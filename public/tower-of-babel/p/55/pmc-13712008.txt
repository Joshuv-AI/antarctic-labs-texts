The modulation of the blood–brain barrier by focused ultrasound stimulates oligodendrogenesis
Authors: Kate Noseworthy, Joseph Silburt, Alessia Apa, Luis Fernando Rubio-Atonal, Sheng-Kai Wu, Carol Schuurmans, Kullervo Hynynen, Isabelle Aubert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFocused ultrasound (FUS) combined with intravenous microbubbles (MB) enables precise and reversible modulation of the blood–brain barrier (BBB) to enhance the delivery of therapeutics from the blood to targeted brain areas. Beyond this application, we discovered over a decade ago that FUS-BBB modulation, without the addition of exogenous therapeutics, activates endogenous regenerative events, including hippocampal neurogenesis. Here, we investigate the effects of FUS on oligodendrogenesis, a key process for myelination and white matter integrity. In adult mice, we targeted FUS-BBB modulation unilaterally to the hippocampus. The proliferation of oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (OPCs) was quantified at 1, 4, 7, and 10 days post-treatment and myelinating oligodendrocytes were assessed at 30 days. At 1 and 4 days post-sonication, the proliferation of hippocampal OPCs increased by 6.8-fold and 2.3-fold, respectively; this resulted in a 5.3-fold increase in myelinating oligodendrocytes one month later. Next, we tested the robustness of FUS-induced oligodendrogenesis using an independent experimental design and targeting the striatum in a separate cohort of mice. The proliferation of striatal OPCs increased by 3.9-fold at 7 days post-FUS. This led to a 5.2-fold increase in oligodendrogenesis 30 days post-treatment, as observed in the hippocampus. Finally, we found that treatments at the same FUS parameters but without MB and without altering the BBB, did not lead to the proliferation of OPCs or oligodendrogenesis. Therefore, with these FUS parameters, MB-induced BBB modulation emerged as a key factor that promoted oligodendrogenesis. Given the long-validated application of FUS-BBB modulation for drug delivery, the additional stimulation of oligodendrogenesis broadens the therapeutic potential of this modality for white matter repair.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40478-026-02364-1.Keywords: Blood–brain barrier, Focused ultrasound, Oligodendrocyte progenitor cells, Oligodendrocytes, Oligodendrogenesis

Introduction

The transient modulation of the blood–brain barrier (BBB) induced by the interactions of non-invasive focused ultrasound (FUS) with intravenous microbubbles (MB) [1] has been established in animal models and in patients with glioblastoma [2–5], Alzheimer disease (AD) [6–9], amyotrophic lateral sclerosis [10] and Parkinson’s disease [11, 12].

Besides delivering intravenous therapeutics to the brain [6, 13–19], FUS-BBB modulation can trigger regenerative effects. For example, FUS-BBB modulation targeted to the hippocampus increases hippocampal neurogenesis through the proliferation of neural progenitor cells (NPCs), leading to the generation of immature neuroblasts and mature neurons [20–24]. FUS-BBB modulation has also been shown to prevent age-related dendrite loss [25] and to improve dendritic complexity and cognition in rodent models of AD [20]. The regenerative effects induced by FUS-BBB modulation on different cell populations offer the potential of multimodal treatment strategies for brain disorders.

The goal of the current study is to investigate the underexplored impact of FUS on oligodendrocytes and their progenitors, namely oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (OPCs). OPCs are distributed throughout the central nervous system (CNS) [26–28]. Oligodendrogenesis, which refers to the process by which OPCs differentiate into myelinating oligodendrocytes, is critical to ensure adequate myelination under physiological conditions and remyelination in pathological contexts. Oligodendrocytes not only contribute to myelin sheath formation but they also participate in key myelin-independent aspects of development, function, and maintenance [29–31].

We designed experiments to test whether the transient modulation of the BBB by FUS and MB in different brain regions promotes (1) the proliferation of OPCs in adult mice, and (2) the maturation and survival of newly generated OPCs. To date, the impact of FUS on the proliferation of OPCs, a required step to the process of oligodendrogenesis, has not been elucidated. By capturing the proliferative, differentiative and maturation phases of oligodendrogenesis, the present study offers insight into how FUS can be harnessed to enhance the brain’s regenerative potential. While FUS combined with MB is well established for transiently modulating the BBB, recent evidence suggests that FUS alone, without BBB modulation, can also influence neural cell proliferation and neurogenesis [32]. Given these findings, we sought to determine whether FUS, at parameters typically used for BBB modulation but without MB, could by itself impact oligodendrogenesis. The results revealed that in the hippocampus and striatum, FUS increased OPC proliferation and oligodendrogenesis only in the presence of MB and evidence of BBB permeability. These findings highlight the potential of FUS-BBB modulation in increasing oligodendrogenesis, contributing to other FUS-induced regenerative events of brain repair. This approach represents a compelling therapeutic avenue for brain disorders, particularly those with white matter pathologies such as AD [29, 33, 34] and multiple sclerosis [35].

Methods

Animals

Male C57BL/6 J mice (n = 56) were used at 2.5 – 3.5 months of age. The mice were housed on a 12-h light/dark cycle and had free access to food and water. All procedures were conducted in accordance with guidelines established by the Canadian Council on Animal Care and protocols approved by the Sunnybrook Research Institute Animal Care Committee.

Experimental design

To assess the effects of FUS-BBB modulation on the proliferation of OPCs, mice (n = 36) received a unilateral treatment to the left hippocampus. Mice were sacrificed at 1, 4, 7, 10, and 30 days (D) post-FUS. Mice sacrificed at 30D received bromodeoxyuridine (BrdU) (50 mg/kg, i.p.) twice daily, 2 h apart, for the first four days post-FUS (Supplementary Fig. 1A). An independent experimental paradigm assessed the influence of FUS-BBB modulation on oligodendrogenesis in a second brain region, the striatum. Mice (n = 12) received a unilateral FUS treatment to the left striatum, and animals were sacrificed at 7D or 30D post-FUS. Mice received 5-ethynyl-2'-deoxyuridine (EdU; 50 mg/kg, i.p.) once daily for the first seven days post-FUS to label cell proliferation (Supplementary Fig. 1B). EdU was selected for these experiments due to its Click-iT chemistry-based detection, which avoids the DNA denaturation required for BrdU immunostaining and thereby preserves tissue integrity and antigenicity, allowing for improved morphological preservation and compatibility with additional immunolabelling. BrdU and EdU are thymidine analogues that incorporate the DNA during the S-phase, labelling the same dividing cell population [36]. To evaluate the impact of BBB modulation on inducing oligodendrogenesis, mice (n = 8) received a unilateral FUS treatment to the left hippocampus. Mice received EdU as described above and were sacrificed 30D post-FUS.

Focused ultrasound guided by magnetic resonance imaging

In anesthetized mice (5% isoflurane initially, reduced to 2%), depilatory cream was used to remove fur from the head, and a 26-gauge angiocatheter was inserted into the tail vein. Mouse brains were mapped using T2-weighted (T2w) MRI axial scans (7.0-T MRI [Bruker]) and four 1 mm hippocampal focal spots, or two 1 mm striatal focal spots were selected for FUS-BBB permeabilization. Coinciding with FUS sonication, mice received a tail vein injection of Definity MB (0.02 ml/kg; Lantheus Medical Imaging) and Gadovist (0.2 ml/kg, Schering AG). FUS treatment was conducted using a preclinical MRI-guided system (an in-house developed prototype of LP100, FUS Instrument Inc., Toronto, ON, Canada), which utilizes an in-house manufactured, spherically focused transducer (1.68 MHz for hippocampal targeting or 1.78 MHz for striatal targeting, 75 mm diameter, 60 mm radius of curvature) with standard BBB modulation parameters outlined in Table 1. Pressure calibration for the transducer was performed using a planar fibre optic hydrophone (active tip diameter = 10 μm; Precision Acoustics Ltd., Dorset, UK). Transducer movement within the tank of degassed, deionized water was controlled with a motorized positioning system (3 degrees of freedom). These transducers have the same physical size and shape and thus produced similar ultrasound field distributions. Therefore, the frequency difference can be considered insignificant from the mechanical point of view. MRI spatial coordinates were co-registered to the transducer positioning system to allow targets to be chosen from structural MR images.

Acoustic pressure was incrementally increased while scattered signals from the MB were monitored with a piezoceramic receiver, capable of detecting ultraharmonic emissions (Supplementary Fig. 2A) [37]. During each burst, hydrophone signals (Supplementary Fig. 2B-F) were captured (capture length = 11 ms, sampling rate = 20 MS/s) using a 14-bit scope card (ATS460; AlazarTech, Pointe-Claire, Quebec, Canada). At each target, the first ten bursts were acquired without MB in circulation to establish baseline values. Once the magnitude of ultraharmonic emissions passed the threshold (mean of baseline plus 10 SD of the mean; triggering peak negative pressure, PNP), PNP was dropped to 25% in the hippocampus, 50% in the striatum, and maintained at this level for the remainder of sonication [37]. Based on prior experiments it was determined that 25% was the most appropriate target level for the hippocampus and 50% for the striatum. Both scaling levels have been demonstrated to result in BBB modulation with normal tissue matrix and minimal-to-no extravasations, while still producing controlled, transient glial responses aligned with stable-cavitation regimes [37–39]. Based on ex vivo mouse skull insertion-loss measurements, the in-situ pressure at the focus in the brain was estimated to be 87 ± 7% of that measured in water [40]. In all experiments, the reported pressures refer to the in-situ PNP estimated in the brain based on the measurements in water with a calibrated hydrophone. In experiments evaluating the impact of BBB modulation on enhancing oligodendrogenesis, FUS was applied at an estimated constant pressure of 0.52 MPa in situ, and only half the mice received intravenous MB as described above.

Tissue collection

At experimental endpoints, mice were deeply anesthetized with ketamine (150 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg), or isoflurane (5%) and were transcardially perfused with ice-cold saline followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in 0.1 M phosphate buffer. Whole brains were removed and postfixed in 4% PFA for 24 h, then transferred to 30% sucrose in 0.1 M phosphate buffer for a maximum of 72 h. Whole brains were mounted to a sliding microtome and sectioned into 40 µm-thick sections. Sections were stored in cryoprotective glycerol in multi-well plates at.

-20 °C.

Immunofluorescence staining

Four serial hippocampal sections (1:24) and six serial striatal sections (1:8) were analyzed. Sections were stained using standard immunofluorescence staining protocols. Phosphate-buffered saline (1X) was used for rinsing. Antigen retrieval was performed using 10 mM sodium citrate at 80 °C for 30 min or Tris–EDTA buffer at 70 °C for 1 h, and 5% donkey serum with 0.3% Triton-X was used for blocking. For BrdU staining, sections were processed for antigen retrieval in 2 N HCl at 37 °C for 30 min, rinsed, and rehydrated with 0.1 M borate buffer (pH = 10.0) for 10 min. EdU staining was performed using the Click-iT EdU Cell Proliferation Kit for Imaging (Thermofisher) as per the manufacturer's instructions. Sections were incubated with primary antibodies for 24 h at 4 °C. Primary antibodies included goat anti-glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP, Santacruz Biotech, cat: sc-6170; 1:250), rabbit anti-ionized calcium-binding adapter molecule-1 (Iba1) (Wako, cat: 016–20001, 1:1000), rat anti-Ki67 (ThermoFisher, cat: 14–5698-82, 1:400), goat anti- oligodendrocyte transcription factor 2 (OLIG2, R&D Systems, cat: AF2418; 1:500), rabbit anti-tubulin polymerization promoting protein (TPPP, Abcam, cat: ab92305, 1:400), rat anti-BrdU (Abcam, cat: ab6326; 1:400), guinea pig anti-breast carcinoma amplified sequence (BCAS1, Cedarlane, cat: 445 004, 1:400), rabbit anti-aspartoacylase (ASPA, Proteintech, cat: 13,244–1-AP, 1:400). Thereafter, sections were rinsed and incubated in secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature (Jackson Immunoresearch; 1:200) or overnight at 4 °C (1:400). Sections were subsequently rinsed and mounted on slides in polyvinyl alcohol-DABCO.

Cellular quantifications

For the first experimental paradigm evaluating FUS-induced oligodendrogenesis in the hippocampus, cells were counted at 63 × oil objective (NA = 1.4) on a Zeiss AxioImager M2 microscope. Early proliferation was assessed between 1 and 10D post-FUS by quantifying proliferating OPCs (OLIG2+/Ki67+ cells) and proliferating non-oligodendrocyte lineage cells (OLIG2−/Ki67+ cells) in the hippocampus between 1 and 10D post-FUS.

To assess the fate of proliferating cells labeled during the early post-FUS period, mice that received BrdU injections over the first 4 days post-FUS were analyzed after 30D. With this birthdating paradigm, BrdU incorporation was used to identify and quantify new cells derived from proliferating progenitors, including myelinating oligodendrocytes (TPPP+/OLIG2+/BrdU+ triple-positive cells), pre-myelinating oligodendrocytes (TPPP−/OLIG2+/BrdU+ cells) and non-oligodendrocyte lineage cells (TPPP−/OLIG2−/BrdU+ cells). Since BrdU labeling was limited to the early post-FUS period and was diluted with subsequent cell divisions, this approach preferentially labeled OPCs that exited the cell cycle or underwent limited divisions. Actively cycling OPCs that continued to proliferate extensively would have diluted BrdU below detection thresholds by 30D. Finally, the granular and subgranular cell layers were excluded when counting proliferating non-oligodendrocyte lineage cells between 1 and 10D post-FUS and newborn non-oligodendrocyte cells at 30D to avoid inclusion of the hippocampal neurogenic niche, which contains a high density of proliferating neural progenitor cells.

Total cell counts were calculated from four slices per animal, taken a distance of 960 µm apart (1:24 sampling), and spanning a total of 2,920 µm within the hippocampus; the average was multiplied by 72, which represents the number of 40 µm slices between and including the first and last sampled slice. Only cells with a nucleus greater than 7 µm, corresponding the average size of the oligodendrocyte nucleus [41], were counted.

For the second experimental paradigm evaluating FUS-induced oligodendrogenesis in the striatum, single plane 20 × coronal images of the striatum were obtained using a Zeiss Z1 Observer/Yokogawa spinning disk (Carl Zeiss) microscope. OPCs (defined as OLIG2+/EdU+ double-positive cells), pre-myelinating oligodendrocytes (defined as TPPP−/OLIG2+/EdU+ cells), myelinating oligodendrocytes (defined as TPPP+/OLIG2+/EdU+ triple-positive cells) and non-oligodendrocyte lineage cells (defined as TPPP−/OLIG2−/EdU+ cells) were quantified using a custom pipeline (Supplementary Table 1) in Cell Profiler 3.1.9. This pipeline was validated against manual counts by an experimenter blind to the experimental conditions using one image per mouse for a total of 12 images. As mature myelinating oligodendrocytes are post-mitotic, EdU⁺ myelinating oligodendrocytes reflect cells that differentiated from proliferating progenitors labeled during the EdU exposure period. Out of the total number of cells that were analyzed (both positive and negative), 0.69% of EdU+ cells, 0.35% of OLIG2+/EdU+ cells, 0.48% TPPP+/OLIG2+/EdU+ cells were falsely determined as positive cells for their respective staining. Total cell counts were calculated from six slices per animal, taken a distance of 320 µm apart (1:8 sampling), and spanning a total of 1,640 µm within the striatum; the average was multiplied by 40, which represents the number of 40 µm slices between and including the first and last sampled slice. Only cells with a nucleus greater than 7 µm, corresponding the average size of the oligodendrocyte nucleus [41], were counted.

In experiments evaluating the glial response to FUS-BBB modulation, and the impact of FUS-BBB modulation in promoting oligodendrogenesis, BCAS1- and ASPA-positive oligodendrocytes were quantified from 40 µm-thick z-stack images of the hippocampus obtained using a Leica Stellaris 5 confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems GmbH, Germany) with a white light laser (WLL). Tissue was imaged using a 20 × air objective (HC PL APO 20x/0.75 CS2, Leica Microsystems GmbH, Germany) at a 1024*1024px resolution. Image pre-processing and thresholding, from which binary masks were created, were performed using BioVoxxel’s Voronoi Threshold Labeler (2D/3D) to segment labeled cells with the corresponding markers of interest. Objects/cells were also filtered by size in which objects/cells smaller than 7 µm, corresponding the average size of the oligodendrocyte nucleus [41], were not included in the binary mask. This approach allowed for enhanced object separation and more accurate identification of overlapping cells. Cells of interest were identified by the overlap of markers using the Image Calculator “AND” feature, and quantified using the 3D Manager plugin in FIJI [42] (Supplementary Table 2–4). Total cell counts were calculated from six slices per animal, taken at a distance of 240 µm apart (1:6 sampling), spanning a total distance of 1240 µm within the hippocampus; the average was multiplied by 30 which represents the number of 40 µm slices between and including the first and last sampled slice.

Characterization of reactive microglia and astrocytes

We identified reactive microglia and astrocytes using the unsupervised machine learning workflow MORPHIOUS, as described in detail by Silburt and Aubert (2022) [43]. Briefly, 40um-thick coronal brain sections stained with antibodies against GFAP and Iba1 were imaged with a Zeiss Z1 Observer/Yokogawa spinning disk (Carl Zeiss) microscope to create z-stack images of the striatum. Maximum intensity projections were generated in Fiji/ImageJ [42] and the polygon tool was used to delineate the striatum as a region of interest (ROI). Images were background-subtracted by 50 units and underwent one round of despeckling. Separate MORPHIOUS classifiers were trained on whole-striatal images of control, homeostatic microglia and astrocytes from the naïve striatal sections. After training, the classifiers were used to identify reactive microglia and astrocytes in FUS treated striatal sections. The features used to evaluate microglia morphology included area, mean intensity, the fractal dimension (D), number of cells, average nearest neighbour distance (NND), average soma size, average soma circularity, cellular perimeter, number of branches, branch length, branch junctions, triple branch points and branch ends. Astrocytes were evaluated using area, mean intensity, cellular perimeter, number of branch junctions, branch ends, branch pixels and triple points. Features were z-score normalized and transformed via principal component analysis with enough principal components to retain 99% of the variance (9 principal components for microglia, 5 principal components for astrocytes).

A tenfold cross validation was used to identify MORPHIOUS parameters, which resulted in the detection of no reactive clusters in the untreated striatal sections while maximizing the number of clusters detected in the FUS treated sections. Optimal MORPHIOUS parameters for detecting microglia were nu: 0.11, gamma: 0.25 min cluster size 22. Optimal MORPHIOUS parameters for detecting astrocytes were nu: 0.16, gamma 0.2, min cluster size: 23.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism was used for statistical analysis. Because the raw data exhibited heteroscedasticity and deviation from normality, values were log10-transformed prior to parametric analyses to stabilize variance and better approximate a normal distribution of residuals [44]. Subsequently, a two-way ANOVA followed by a Tukey post-hoc analysis on log10-transformed data was used to model the time-dependent and treatment-associated changes in OPC proliferation (Fig. 2) and to assess the effects of BBB modulation on cell proliferation and oligodendrogenesis. To evaluate subject-specific increases in OPC proliferation post-FUS (Supplementary Fig. 3), compare FUS-treated and untreated oligodendrogenesis at 30D post-FUS in the hippocampus (Fig. 3), and FUS-treated and untreated OPC proliferation (Fig. 4) and oligodendrogenesis (Fig. 5) in the striatum, a one-tailed, paired student’s t-test was performed on log10-transformed data. We used a one-tailed statistical test because we had a clearly defined a priori directional hypothesis that FUS would increase oligodendrogenesis based on prior reports that FUS-BBB modulation stimulates glial and neural progenitor proliferation [20, 23, 24]. One-tailed tests offer greater power to detect effects in a prespecified direction (i.e., reduced Type II error) when that direction is supported by prior empirical data under comparable experimental conditions[45, 46]. For clarity, raw data are displayed in Figs. 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, but all statistical analyses were conducted on log10-transformed values to meet parametric assumptions; reported p-values correspond to the transformed data.

Results

FUS-BBB modulation promotes hippocampal oligodendrogenesis

To assess the potential of FUS-induced oligodendrogenesis, mice received a single application of FUS, unilaterally targeting the hippocampus. Animals were sacrificed at 1, 4, 7 or 10D post-FUS and brain sections were stained with the oligodendrocyte-lineage marker, oligodendrocyte transcription factor 2 (OLIG2, green) [28] and the proliferative marker, Ki67 (red) (Fig. 2A-I). Proliferating OPCs (OLIG2+/Ki67+) were quantified at 1, 4, 7, and 10D post-FUS (Fig. 2J), comparing the FUS-treated (triangles) and untreated (circles) hippocampi.

The average number of proliferating OPCs was 6.8-fold higher in the FUS-treated (4491 ± 2402; mean ± SD) compared to the untreated (657 ± 133) hippocampi at 1D (Fig. 2J, ****p < 0.0001) and 2.3-fold at 4D in the FUS-treated (2543 ± 388) compared to the untreated side (1094 ± 222) (Fig. 2J; **p = 0.003). Remarkably, in all animals, the number of proliferating OPCs was elevated in the FUS-treated hippocampus relative to its contralateral, untreated side at 1D and 4D post-FUS (Supplementary Fig. 3). No significant differences were detected in the number of proliferating OPCs between FUS-treated and untreated hippocampi at 7D and 10D. A significant interaction was found between time post-FUS and FUS-treatment on the proliferation of OPCs (F(3,24) = 8.08, ***p = 0.0007).

The number of proliferating non-oligodendrocyte cells (OLIG2−/Ki67+) was 32-fold higher in the FUS-treated hippocampi (10,233 ± 6028) compared to the untreated side (320 ± 111) at 1D (Fig. 2K; ****p < 0.0001). No significant differences were observed in the number of proliferating non-oligodendrocyte lineage cells between FUS-treated and untreated contralateral hippocampi at 4D, 7D, and 10D.

Next, we asked whether proliferating OPCs post-FUS differentiated into myelinating oligodendrocytes, which are known to express tubulin polymerization promoting protein (TPPP) [47, 48]. Animals were injected with BrdU starting at 1D and ending at 4D post-FUS to label cells that entered the S phase at least one time during the 4-day injection period. BrdU+ (red) cells in sections also stained with TPPP (green) and OLIG2 (blue) were counted at 30D in untreated (Fig. 3A, B) and FUS-treated (Fig. 3C-E) hippocampi.

The results show that at 30D after FUS-BBB modulation, the number of newly derived, pre-myelinating oligodendrocytes (TPPP−/OLIG2+/BrdU+) was 4.7-fold higher in treated (5472 ± 1164) compared to untreated (1161 ± 188) hippocampi (Fig. 3F; ***p = 0.0004). Similarly, derivative myelinating oligodendrocytes (TPPP+/OLIG2+/BrdU+) increased by 5.3-fold in the treated (882 ± 222) compared to untreated (167 ± 61) hippocampi (Fig. 2G; **p = 0.003). Finally, the number of non-oligodendrocytic cells (TPPP−/OLIG2−/BrdU+) that were birthdated during the early labelling period was 5.7-fold higher in the treated (20,624 ± 2011) compared to untreated (3632 ± 6470) hippocampi (Fig. 3H; ***p = 0.0006). These data demonstrate that FUS significantly increases cell proliferation, including that of OPCs, and promotes the progression of oligodendrocyte lineage cells through differentiation and maturation.

FUS-BBB modulation stimulates OPC proliferation and oligodendrogenesis in the striatum

Next, we carried out a set of independent experiments to assess whether FUS-BBB modulation induces oligodendrogenesis in the striatum, a non-neurogenic brain region, contrasting to the hippocampus, which is neurogenic in the adult brain. After unilateral FUS-BBB modulation, we used different methodologies to quantify proliferative cells. EdU was injected intraperitoneally, once daily, from 1 to 7D, a period of robust proliferation post-FUS [43, 49] to label cells that entered S phase at least one time during the injection period. Animals were sacrificed 2 h following the last EdU injection on day 7.

The colocalization of OLIG2 (green) and EdU (red) was used to label OPCs in brain sections containing the FUS-treated (Fig. 4A-D) and untreated contralateral (not shown) striatum. 7D after FUS-BBB modulation, the number of cells that proliferated and incorporated EdU (EdU+) was 9.2-fold higher in the FUS-treated (8527 ± 4244) compared to the untreated (926 ± 273) striatum (Fig. 4E; ***p = 0.0006). A 3.9-fold increase in the number of OPCs that cumulatively incorporated EdU during the 7-day labeling paradigm (OLIG2+/EdU+) was found in the FUS-treated (2437 ± 1117) compared to untreated (631 ± 122) striatum (Fig. 4F; ***p = 0.0007). Cells that were classified as proliferating, non-oligodendrocyte lineage cells (OLIG2−/EdU+) were 21-fold higher in the FUS-treated (6090 ± 3328) compared to untreated (295 ± 201) striatum (Fig. 4G; ***p = 0.001). Thus, FUS-BBB modulation induces a robust increase in cell proliferation in the striatum, which includes a significant number of proliferating OPCs, as well as other cell types.

To evaluate striatal oligodendrogenesis using a pulse-chase lineage tracing paradigm, animals were administered EdU from 1 to 7D post-FUS and sacrificed after 30D. At this timepoint, co-staining was performed to identify myelinating oligodendrocytes (TPPP, green), pre-myelinating oligodendrocytes (OLIG2, blue), and cells with a history of proliferation (EdU, red). Comparisons were made between untreated and FUS-treated striatums (Fig. 5A-E). To further validate the identity of myelinating oligodendrocytes, additional immunostaining was done using established markers for early myelinating oligodendrocytes, i.e., BCAS1[50], and mature myelinating oligodendrocytes, i.e., ASPA [51] (Supplementary Fig. 4).

The number of pre-myelinating oligodendrocytes with a proliferation history (TPPP−/OLIG2+/EdU+) averaged 676 ± 315 in the FUS-treated striatum and 452 ± 114 in the untreated striatum; however, this difference was not statistically significant (Fig. 5F; p = 0.09). In contrast, the analysis of dividing cells in the striatum revealed a significant 5.2-fold increase in the number of newly-generated myelinating oligodendrocytes (TPPP+/OLIG2+/EdU+) in the FUS-treated (1179 ± 727) compared to the untreated (230 ± 140) striatum (Fig. 5G; **p = 0.005). A 7.5-fold increase in the number of proliferating, non-oligodendrocyte lineage cells (TPPP−/OLIG2−/EdU+) was also observed in the FUS-treated (3387 ± 2231) compared to the untreated (452 ± 114) striatum (Fig. 5H; *p = 0.01). These data demonstrate that FUS-BBB modulation significantly enhanced cell proliferation of oligodendrocyte and non-oligodendrocyte lineages, as well as oligodendrocyte differentiation and maturation in the striatum.

BCAS1 and ASPA immunostaining confirmed the presence of myelinating oligodendrocytes in the striatum. Specifically, the number of newly generated, early-myelinating oligodendrocytes (BCAS1+/EdU+) in the FUS treated striatum was 1363 ± 1488 compared to untreated striatum 299 ± 71 (p = 0.052). Dividing cells in the striatum led to a significant 6.0-fold increase in the number of mature myelinating oligodendrocytes (ASPA+/EdU+) in the FUS-treated (1979 ± 1449) compared to the untreated (330 ± 109) striatum (*p = 0.02).

Together, these findings indicate that FUS-BBB modulation not only enhances cell proliferation but drives the differentiation and maturation of newly generated oligodendrocytes in the treated striatum.

Astrocyte and microglial proliferation and responses to FUS-BBB modulation in the striatum

Given the observed increase in non-oligodendroglial proliferating cells following FUS (Fig. 2K, 3H, 4G, 5H), we next investigated whether astrocytes and microglia contributed to the population of dividing cells in the striatum. To assess glial proliferation at 7D post-FUS, tissue was co-labeled with the DNA synthesis marker EdU (Fig. 6A, yellow) and the cell-cycle marker Ki67 (Fig. 6A, magenta), together with GFAP (Fig. 6A, red) and Iba1 (Fig. 6A, green) to identify astrocytes and microglia, respectively.

EdU labeling was used to visualize cells that entered the S phase at least one time during the 7-day injection period (Supplementary Fig. 1B) while Ki67 immunostaining identified proliferating cells that were actively cycling at the time of sacrifice (i.e., 7D post-FUS). EdU+/Ki67+ double positive cells represent a population that divided during the EdU labeling period and remained actively cycling at the time of analysis. Ki67−/EdU+ cells are a population of cells that divided during the EdU labeling window but subsequently exited the cell cycle (i.e., cells that have likely begun differentiation or maturation). In contrast, Ki67+/EdU− are cells that were actively proliferating at the time of sacrifice but did not undergo DNA synthesis during the EdU exposure period.

FUS-BBB modulation in the striatum led to a significant 2.7-fold increase in the number of EdU⁺ cells (13,888 ± 5504) compared to the untreated, contralateral striatum (5153 ± 829) (Fig. 6B, **p = 0.005). Additionally, there was a significant 11.3-fold increase in the number of EdU+ cells that colocalized with GFAP (GFAP+/EdU+) in the FUS-treated (3601 ± 1760) compared to untreated (318 ± 97) striatum (Fig. 6C, **p = 0.001). FUS-BBB modulation in the striatum led to a significant 2.8-fold increase in the number of EdU+ cells that colocalized with Iba1 (Iba1+/EdU+) (2901 ± 1790) compared to the untreated, contralateral striatum (1044 ± 300) (Fig. 6D, **p = 0.003).

Striatal FUS-BBB modulation did not lead to statistically significant increases compared to the contralateral untreated striatum in Ki67+ counts (2991 ± 813 vs. 2427 ± 220; Fig. 6E, p = 0.15), proliferating astrocytes (GFAP+/Ki67+; 196 ± 82 vs. 162 ± 76; Fig. 6F, p = 0.19), or proliferating microglia (Iba1+/Ki67+; 776 ± 342 vs. 605 ± 140; Fig. 6G, p = 0.18).

The combined use of Ki67 and EdU provides temporal resolution of proliferative dynamics and lineage progression that cannot be achieved using either marker alone. FUS-BBB modulation in the striatum led to a significant 19.6-fold increase in the number of GFAP+/Ki67−/EdU+ cells in the FUS-treated (3421 ± 1747) compared to untreated (176 ± 46) striatum, representing astrocytes that proliferated and then exited the cell cycle (Fig. 6H, ***p = 0.0004). Similarly, FUS-BBB modulation in the striatum led to a significant 4.2-fold increase in the number of Iba1+/Ki67−/EdU+ cells in the FUS-treated (2257 ± 1484) compared to untreated (535 ± 225) striatum, indicating that microglia proliferated early and then exited the cell cycle (Fig. 6I, ***p = 0.0007).There was no significant difference in the number of Ki67+/EdU− cells that colocalized with GFAP (GFAP+/Ki67+/EdU−; Fig. 6J) or Iba1 (Iba1+/Ki67+/EdU−; Fig. 6K) between the FUS-treated and untreated striatum.

Taken together, these data indicate that FUS-BBB modulation induces a transient proliferative response in microglia and astrocytes, resulting in the generation of new glial cells that exit the cell cycle, without evidence of sustained or ongoing glial proliferation by 7D post-FUS.

Previous work has established that FUS-BBB modulation induces transient microglial and astrocytic reactivity [49, 52–55]; however, significant gaps remain in elucidating the function of this reactivity. We use the term “reactive gliosis” defined by Escartin et. al. (2021) to define the process whereby, in response to pathology, astrocytes and microglia engage in molecularly defined programs involving changes in transcriptional regulation, as well as biochemical, morphological, metabolic, and physiological remodeling, which ultimately result in gain of new functions or loss or upregulation of homeostatic ones [56]. To quantify post-FUS microglial and astrocytic reactivity and begin to assess its involvement in oligodendrogenesis observed post-FUS, we employed MORPHIOUS, a machine learning protocol capable of identifying discrete regions of interest corresponding to morphologically reactive microglia and astrocytes [43].

In FUS-treated tissues stained with GFAP, we characterized a single type of cluster for reactive astrocytes, which we termed proximal astrocytes (Fig. 7A, yellow outline). At 7D post-FUS, proximal astrocytes showed an increased mean GFAP intensity compared to untreated cells (Fig. 7B, ***p < 0.0001) and occupied 27% of total striatal area (Fig. 7C). Relative to untreated cells, proximal astrocytes exhibit increased total branch length (Fig. 7D, 820 µm compared to 210 µm, ****p < 0.0001), and number of branches per cell (Fig. 7E, 110 compared to 40, ****p < 0.0001). We subsequently asked whether the astrocytic reactivity could be related to the increase in proliferating OPCs post-FUS. We found a significant correlation (Fig. 7F, R2 = 0.85, p < 0.0001) between the normalized integrated density (i.e., GFAP mean intensity * percent cluster area) and the proliferation of OPCs at 7D post-FUS, suggesting that the astrocyte reactivity could be modulating the increase in oligodendrogenesis observed post-FUS.

In FUS-treated tissues stained with Iba1, FUS induced two gradations of reactive microglia, termed proximal (Fig. 7G, yellow outline) and focal (Fig. 7G, blue outline) microglia, which presented with moderate and substantial changes in morphology, respectively. Microglia within FUS treated striatal sections that were not recognized as reactive are termed distal microglia. At 7D post-FUS, proximal and focal microglia clusters occupied 35% and 27% (Fig. 7I) of the striatum, respectively. Compared to distal untreated microglia, focal microglia demonstrated increased cellular density per 100 µm (Fig. 7J, 10.2 compared to 5.8, ****p < 0.01) and increased soma size (Fig. 6K, 150.0 µm2 compared to 97.0 µm2, ****p < 0.0001). We found a strong association (Fig. 7L, R2 = 0.71, p = 0.0006) between the normalized integrated density of Iba1 and the proliferation of OPCs at 7D post-FUS, suggesting that the microglia reactivity could be modulating the increase in oligodendrogenesis observed post-FUS.

The impact of FUS with and without microbubbles on oligodendrogenesis

To explore the potential contribution of BBB modulation and MB involvement in FUS-induced oligodendrogenesis, we compared outcomes in animals treated with FUS combined with MB (FUS + MB), at parameters known to induced BBB modulation, to FUS at the same parameters but without MB (termed FUS alone). FUS was targeted unilaterally to the hippocampus using four focal spots (Supplementary Fig. 5A).

In both groups of animals, ultrasound was generated using a 1.78 MHz transducer and applied at an estimated constant pressure of 0.60MPa in vivo = 0.52 MPa in situ corresponding to a mechanical index (MI) of 0.4. A MI of 0.4 falls within reported safe and effective range (0.4–0.7) used in preclinical studies for FUS-BBB modulation with MB, enabling transient BBB modulation [53, 57–59]. FUS applied at a MI of 0.45 in the absence of MB is characterized as insufficient to induce BBB modulation [57]. We evaluated changes in the properties of the BBB following FUS application by visualizing Gadolinium contrast enhancement on post-FUS T1w images. BBB modulation was confirmed in the FUS + MB group, while no enhancement was observed in animals that received FUS alone, indicating that at these FUS parameters, MB were required to induce BBB modulation (Supplementary Fig. 5B). No indication of edema, hemorrhage, vascular injury, or neuronal damage was visible in either condition in post-FUS T2* MRI (Supplementary Fig. 5C) or Nissl-stained sections (Supplementary Fig. 5D). This data is supported by our earlier published animal studies [37, 39, 60–62] and current clinical studies have demonstrated that the control method produces reliable BBB permeability enhancement without tissue damage.

Animals received daily intraperitoneal injections of EdU from 1 to 4D post-FUS as previously described. At 30D post-treatment, brains were stained and analyzed for EdU+ cells (Fig. 8A-B, yellow) and TPPP+ (Fig. 8A-B, red)/OLIG2+/(Fig. 8A-B, cyan)/EdU+ triple-positive cells to assess myelinating oligodendrocytes.

A two-way ANOVA was conducted to assess the effects of treatment (FUS-treated vs. untreated) and BBB modulation (FUS + MB vs. FUS alone) on cell proliferation (Fig. 8C). The analysis revealed a significant interaction between FUS and BBB modulation (F(1,16) = 7.3, **p = 0.004) indicating that the effect of FUS on cell proliferation depends on the modulation of the BBB (i.e., FUS in combination with MB). There were also significant main effects of treatment, (F(1,16)=5.9, *p = 0.01) and BBB modulation (F(1,16) = 6.3, *p = 0.013). In the FUS + MB group, we observed a significant increase (**p = 0.003) in the number of EdU+ cells (5486 ± 3103; Fig. 8C, gray bars and triangles) compared to animals receiving FUS alone (1776 ± 253; Fig. 8C, white bars and circles). In line with our previously reported results, animals in the FUS + MB group demonstrated a significant increase (**p = 0.002 in the number of EdU+ (yellow) cells (5486 ± 3103; Fig. 8C) compared to the untreated, contralateral hippocampus (1830 ± 502; Fig. 8C), representing an internal control for MB administration. There was no significant difference in the baseline level of EdU+ cell proliferation between groups (p = 0.97).

A two-way ANOVA assessed the effects of treatment (FUS-treated vs. untreated) and BBB modulation (FUS + MB vs. FUS alone) on oligodendrogenesis (Fig. 8D). The analysis revealed a significant interaction between FUS-treatment and BBB modulation (F(1,16) = 6.3; *p = 0.02), indicating that the effect of FUS on oligodendrogenesis depends on the modulation of the BBB (i.e., FUS combined with MB). There was also a significant main effect of FUS treatment (F(1,16) = 6.8; **p = 0.0096). In the FUS + MB group, we observed a significant increase (*p = 0.03) in the number of newly-generated myelinating oligodendrocytes (TPPP+/OLIG2+/ EdU+; 1437 ± 666; Fig. 8D, gray bars and triangles) compared to those receiving FUS alone (665 ± 93; Fig. 8D, white bars and circles). In line with our previously reported results, animals in the FUS + MB group demonstrated a significant increase (**p = 0.006) in the number of newly-generated myelinating oligodendrocytes (TPPP+/OLIG2+/EdU+; 5486 ± 3103; Fig. 8D) compared to the untreated, contralateral hippocampus (1830 ± 502; Fig. 8D), representing an internal control for MB administration. There were no significant differences in the baseline numbers of myelinating oligodendrocytes with a proliferation history (TPPP+/OLIG2+/EdU+) between groups (p = 0.95). Together, this data indicates that FUS-BBB modulation triggered both a proliferative response and oligodendrogenesis, suggesting that FUS alone, at the parameters used in this study, is insufficient to promote oligodendrogenesis.

To further examine the relationship between the extent of BBB modulation and cellular response, we performed a simple linear regression (Fig. 8E) analysis between the degree of BBB modulation, measured by contrast enhancement, and the number of EdU+ cells. This analysis revealed a strong positive linear relationship (R2 = 0.89; F(1,8) = 69.10; ****p < 0.0001). Similarly, a simple linear regression (Fig. 8F) assessing the relationship between the degree of BBB modulation and the number of myelinating oligodendrocytes (TPPP+/OLIG2+/EdU+) revealed a significant positive linear relationship (R2 = 0.92; F(1,8) = 100.7; ****p < 0.0001). Together these data indicate that the degree of BBB modulation strongly predicts proliferation and oligodendrogenesis.

Discussion

FUS-BBB modulation induces oligodendrogenesis

FUS-BBB modulation has been shown to increase adult hippocampal neurogenesis [20, 23]. Furthermore, FUS-BBB modulation transiently activates astrocytes and microglia in rodent models [52], with beneficial effects that can lead to brain repair such as reducing amyloid pathology for up to two weeks in the TgCRND8 mouse model of AD [20, 21, 23, 43, 52, 63, 64]. Recently, FUS-BBB modulation treatment was shown to arrest the growth and formation of vascular lesions within the CNS that cause neurological symptoms [59]. Here, we describe novel findings demonstrating that FUS-BBB modulation promotes oligodendrogenesis.

Specifically, FUS-BBB modulation induced a significant proliferation of OPCs which resulted in increases in oligodendrogenesis of 5.3-fold in the hippocampus and 6.7-fold in the striatum. We identified a window of oligodendrogenesis in response to FUS-BBB modulation by evaluating the proliferation and maturation of OPCs over one month in the hippocampus. We found that OPC proliferation peaks at 1D and 4D post-FUS and that it is no longer significant at 10D.

Region-specific responses in oligodendrocyte maturation following FUS-BBB modulation

The stimulation of oligodendrogenesis in both the hippocampus and striatum suggests that OPC proliferation and differentiation share common cellular responses to FUS-BBB modulation across these regions. Nevertheless, we observed regional differences in OPC maturation. In the hippocampus, 86% of oligodendrocytes that proliferated and incorporated BrdU from 1 to 4D post FUS remained immature (i.e., pre-myelinating) 30D post-FUS (i.e., maturation rate of 14%). In contrast, 60% of oligodendrocytes in the striatum that proliferated and incorporated EdU from 1 to 7D post-FUS had differentiated into myelinating oligodendrocytes by 30D. One explanation for such regional differences in maturity may be attributed to the source of OPCs. OPCs in the striatum are derived from both resident OPCs and neural progenitor cells in the subventricular zone (SVZ) that retain continuous oligodendrogenesis capacity into adulthood and differentiate into oligodendrocytes under physiological and pathological conditions [65–67]. In the adult hippocampus, oligodendrogenesis is restricted to subregions of the CA3 and CA4 [68]. Furthermore, OPCs may react to the unique microenvironment created in each brain region [69], leading to differences in OPC maturation [69–71]. Indeed, the subgranular zone in the hippocampus is an active zone of adult neurogenesis, with a strong signaling bias towards neuronal lineage commitment and inhibitory signals for oligodendrocyte maturation. Moreover, white matter OPCs are more primed for differentiation compared to gray matter OPCs, which are more likely to remain in a “standby” state [72].

Region- and tissue-specific responses to FUS-BBB modulation, including in gray and white matter [73], warrant further investigation in both healthy and disease models to elucidate underlying mechanisms and identify potential regenerative opportunities. This is particularly important given that OPC behaviour is highly sensitive to local niche signals, which may differentially shape their responses to FUS-BBB modulation across brain regions.

Glial cell responses to FUS-BBB modulation and their potential role in regeneration

The time window of post-FUS oligodendrocyte proliferation reported herein is consistent with previous reports of an acute inflammatory response induced by FUS-BBB modulation [1–3, 53, 74, 75]. While the degree and type of inflammation observed post-FUS are highly dependent on the FUS parameters used (i.e., degree of vascular permeability, PNP, type and dose of MB) [54, 75–78], acute inflammation in the brain can result in a range of effects, some of which are protective [79–81]. For example, FUS-BBB modulation induces neuroblast formation within 1 week of sonication in rodent models of health and disease [20, 23, 24]. This may also be the case for FUS-BBB modulation exposures where inflammatory processes could conceivably be involved mechanistically in the outcome, such as phosphorylated tau and amyloid β plaque clearance [52, 82]. Additionally, while significant increases in the expression of inflammatory markers have been reported in FUS-treated tissue using similar parameters to those employed herein [61], the increases appear to be short-lived [39] and do not reach levels previously shown to result in long-term, overt tissue damage [53, 83]. Together, these data suggest that FUS-BBB modulation induces a transient window of inflammatory responses wherein the brain mobilizes glial and neuronal regenerative responses.

Using MORPHIOUS, a machine learning workflow that detects clusters of reactive glia [43], we characterized astrocyte and microglia reactivity at 7D following a single treatment of FUS-BBB-modulation in the striatum. By 7D post FUS-BBB modulation, reactive microglia occupied 35% of the FUS-treated striatum. This data is consistent with previous reports of transient microglia reactivity in FUS-treated tissue. Specifically, Kovacs et al., (2017) reported increases in Iba1 immunofluorescence as early as 1 h post-FUS, with no differences to the contralateral hemisphere by 24 h [53]. Jordão et al., (2013) reported increased protein expression of Iba1 at 4 h post-FUS, with peak expression by 4D. By 15D, no significant differences between FUS-treated and untreated hemispheres could be detected [52]. Comprehensive single-cell sequencing has been used to characterize the distinct response of microglia to FUS-BBB modulation in mice [55]. We observed astrocyte reactivity at 7D post-FUS, with reactive astrocytes occupying 27% of the FUS-treated striatum. Previous work quantifying the time-course of astrocyte reactivity using similar FUS parameters as in the current manuscript found that FUS-BBB modulation induces the reactivity of astrocytes by 4 days [52], with resolution by 30D [84].

The characterization of OPC proliferation following FUS-BBB modulation in the current manuscript, alongside previously reported temporal profiles of reactive microglia and astrocytes [49, 52, 53, 85, 86] aligns with proposed mechanisms in which reactive glial cells release trophic and signaling factors that enhance oligodendrogenesis [87, 88]. Indeed, studies examining brain macrophage responses to FUS-induced BBB opening have reported elevated expression of chemokines and cytokines [55, 89]. Further, recent data by Noel et al. [90] suggests that FUS-BBB modulation induces a reactive phenotype of hippocampal astrocytes that could contribute to OPC differentiation between 2- and 7-days post-FUS. However, the specific pathways in which reactive astrocytes influence OPCs remains to be determined.

In the hippocampus and striatum, FUS-BBB modulation also promoted the proliferation of cells identified as non-oligodendrocytes (i.e., lacking expression OLIG2). Our group and others have previously found that FUS-BBB modulation increases the number of hippocampal neural progenitor cells [20–24, 63], endothelial cells [38, 85], and microglia [49, 53, 55, 92]. Specifically, using a familiar FUS-BBB modulation paradigm to target the hippocampus, Silburt et al. demonstrated microglia proliferation at 1 and 4D post-FUS [49], occurring within similar timeline as the gain in OPCs. At the parameters employed in the current study, FUS-BBB modulation led to a significant increase in the number of astrocytes and microglia that had entered the S-phase during the 7-day EdU labelling period. It is important to note that the majority of these cells were not Ki67+ at the time of sacrifice, hereby suggesting that by 7D post-FUS, astrocytes and microglia had exited the cell cycle and are no longer proliferative. This data is supportive of a transient proliferative response of glial cells. Thus, several cell populations are known to proliferate in response to FUS-BBB modulation, and they can contribute to the increase in the number of non-oligodendrogenic cells that we observed.

Influence of stimulation parameters on FUS-mediated cellular responses

Our results demonstrate that at the parameters used in this study, BBB modulation by FUS and MB promotes OPC proliferation and maturation, leading to the complete process of oligodendrogenesis. In this paradigm, FUS serves as a propagating pressure wave that exerts both mechanical and mechanoelectrical forces through radiation pressure [32]. The interaction of FUS with MB has an added effect of BBB modulation due to cavitation [18]. Our findings are in line with previous studies at similar FUS parameters and regiments reporting that BBB modulation leads to cell proliferation and neurogenesis [22, 23, 32], including in animal models of disease [20, 21, 24, 63].

These findings do not rule out that additional mechanisms could be involved in FUS-mediated neurogenesis and oligodendrogenesis. Notably, ultrasound exposures designed to modulate the BBB can both enhance vascular permeability and induce neuromodulation either from the ultrasound or MB exposure itself [93–95]. Ultrasound neuromodulation is a fast-growing subfield in therapeutic ultrasound [18], and two recent comprehensive reviews describe in detail the history, technology, biological effects and plausible mechanisms underlying FUS-neuromodulation, from molecular-interactions to system-wide effects [96, 97].

A recent study by Wang et al. [98] highlights the neuromodulatory potential of low intensity pulsed ultrasound (LIPUS), applied for 14 consecutive days, to induce the maturation of oligodendrocytes. In contrast to FUS-BBB described in our study, LIPUS protocols are performed without MB, use higher duty cycles and longer burst lengths, hereby exposing cells to more sustained mechanical energy which can directly influence ion channel gating, membrane capacitance, and mechanosensitive pathways without disrupting the BBB [92, 99, 100]. Similarly, Blackmore et al. [32] show FUS-induced cell proliferation and neurogenesis in the absence of BBB modulation (i.e. FUS without MB), further highlighting the neuromodulatory effects of low-intensity ultrasound. It remains to be determined whether oligodendrogenesis occurred under these experimental conditions. Kline-Schroder et al., 2023 [101], identified differential cellular responses of neuroimmune cells, specifically microglia and macrophages, in response to FUS with or without enhanced vascular permeability. FUS + MB was shown to upregulate chemokines and cytokines, interferon associated genes, and downregulate homeostatic microglia genes, relative to mice receiving MB alone or FUS alone. These findings underscore that cellular responses to FUS are highly dependent on stimulation parameters, including MB administration and vascular permeability. The impact and mechanisms of different FUS treatments on regenerative events in the brain remain to be fully explored and elucidated in this rapidly emerging field of translational research [17, 18].

Enhancing endogenous oligodendrogenesis as a therapeutic strategy

The recognition of oligodendrocytes, OPCs, and myelin as contributing factors for the pathogenesis and progression of neurodegenerative diseases and as cellular targets of traumatic brain and spinal cord injury [102, 103], has highlighted the need to develop therapeutic strategies aimed at promoting myelination by enhancing endogenous oligodendrogenesis [33]. Current preclinical strategies have ranged from small molecule drugs [104] to cell replacement strategies [105]. Previous chemical screening approaches have been used to identify small molecules, such as clemastine [106] or RNS60 [107], that enhance oligodendrocyte differentiation and/or myelination of existing OPCs [108, 109], but does not expand the OPC population itself. Similarly, recent data suggests a role of LIPUS in promoting oligodendrocyte maturation in a model of ischemic stroke (i.e., ~ 1.3-fold increase) [98], without expanding the OPC population. As remyelination depends mainly on the generation of new oligodendrocytes from the adult OPC pool rather than on pre-existing mature oligodendrocytes, therapeutic strategies must not only promote OPC differentiation but also ensure expansion and preservation of the endogenous OPC population [48]. Thus, we present FUS-BBB modulation as a novel strategy that augments endogenous oligodendrogenesis (i.e., a 5.3-fold increase in the hippocampus, a 6.7-fold increase in the striatum) and therein expands both OPC and myelinating oligodendrocyte populations [48]. While the current study was designed to characterize early cellular responses to FUS-BBB modulation, specifically oligodendrogenesis and maturation marker expression, definitive structural and functional measures of remyelination have yet to be elucidated and provide an exciting opportunity for future work.

Limitations

Firstly, the experiments were done only in healthy 2.5–3.5-month-old male mice, due to availability within the timeframe required. We are cognisant that age, sex, and disease state can strongly influence several biological readouts. Of note, we conducted preliminary studies that demonstrated increased oligodendrogenesis in response to FUS-BBB modulation in males and females TgCRND8 mice, at early- and moderate-stages of amyloidosis, and in non-transgenic littermates of both sexes [110]. Future work is needed to validate our results in a larger cohort of aged, female, and disease-model mice, to assess generalizability.

Secondly, a key limitation of our study lies in the inherent challenge of isolating the yet undefined variables that contribute to the stimulation of oligodendrogenesis following FUS-BBB modulation, making it difficult to conclude whether BBB permeability itself is required for this response. However, Mooney et al. 2016, evaluated whether the modulation of the BBB, the specific pressure amplitudes of FUS, and/or the intravenous administration of MB are necessary for the enhancement of neurogenesis. The study demonstrated that only FUS applied at ~ 0.78 MPa pressure amplitude with MB, promoted hippocampal neurogenesis and was associated with increased BBB permeability. FUS applied at higher (1.56 MPa, 3.0 MPa) and lower (0.39 MPa) pressure amplitudes, regardless of MB administration, did not result in increases in neurogenesis, hereby suggesting that FUS-mediated neurogenesis is dependent upon a transient permeabilization of the BBB. We appreciate that alternate mechanisms may be involved in FUS-mediated oligodendrogenesis and intend to explore this in future investigations.

Thirdly, the complex and temporally dynamic biological responses elicited by a single FUS-BBB modulation event complicate the precise delineation of the mechanisms that support oligodendrogenesis, spanning proliferation to lineage maturation stages. BrdU/EdU pulse-chase labeling provides only a temporally restricted and indirect assessment of lineage progression, with potential bias due to incomplete labeling and dilution of the signal with continued cell division. Future studies incorporating single-cell and spatial omics approaches could overcome these limitations by enabling unbiased resolution of oligodendrocyte lineage states and differentiation trajectories, while also providing mechanistic insight into region-specific responses.

Finally, while the current study characterized early oligodendrogenesis responses to FUS-BBB modulation, definitive structural and functional measures of remyelination have yet to be elucidated and provide an exciting opportunity.

This study provides a foundational first step and underscores the need for broader research, including additional FUS parameters, to define the events and mechanisms uniquely engaged by FUS-induced BBB modulation relative to other FUS modalities that may drive oligodendrogenesis and the plasticity of other cell types.

Conclusions

FUS-BBB modulation, traditionally viewed as a tool for delivering intravenous therapeutics to the brain, stimulates OPC proliferation and promotes oligodendrogenesis in the hippocampus and striatum, suggesting the engagement of a regenerative program spanning in different brain regions. Future studies will clarify whether FUS-BBB–mediated oligodendrogenesis occurs in additional brain regions and elucidate the mechanisms driving this response.

Considering that oligodendrogenesis is disrupted in AD [34, 111, 112] and in other neurodegenerative conditions [113], the ability of FUS-BBB modulation to stimulate this process highlights its potential relevance. promise as a regenerative strategy to support white matter repair and functional recovery. Although the precise mechanisms underlying oligodendrogenesis induced by FUS-BBB modulation remain to be defined, our results uncover a promising regenerative approach for brain disorders affecting the white matter[110].
