Attitudes toward death in nursing staff: the role of resilience
Authors: Silvia Arribas-García, Eleni Petkari, Joana Jaureguizar, Teresa Sánchez-Gutiérrez, Isabel Morales, Sandra Doval
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNurses frequently care for patients at the end of life, yet many lack adequate preparation to manage the associated emotional challenges. Attitudes toward death influence both the quality of end-of-life care and nurses´ own psychological well-being. Although resilience has been identified as a potential protective factor, its role in shaping attitudes toward death among nursing professionals remains insufficiently explored. This study examined the role of resilience in attitudes toward death and analyzed personal and occupational factors associated with these attitudes prior to the COVID-19 pandemic.MethodsThis observational cross-sectional study included 742 nursing professionals working in Intensive Care, Palliative Care and Oncology units across seven public hospitals in Spain. Participants completed the Death Attitude Profile-Revised, the Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale and an ad-hoc questionnaire assessing sociodemographic and professional variables between June 2018 and April 2019. Multivariate analyses of variance, correlation analyses, mediation and moderation analyses, and principal component analysis were conducted.ResultsNeutral-Acceptance was the most predominant attitude toward death (M = 5.68, SD = 0.90), followed by Fear-of-Death (M = 3.70, SD = 1.39) and Death-Avoidance (M = 3.53, SD = 1.59). Attitudes toward death were significantly associated with professional category, age, prior training in death and bereavement, healthcare setting, and resilience (all p<.05). Resilience correlated positively with Neutral-Acceptance and negatively with Fear-of-Death and Death-Avoidance. Mediation analyses showed that resilience fully mediated the relationships between several professional factors and key death attitudes, with suppression effects observed in some models.ConclusionsResilience and prior training in death and dying appear to play an important role in shaping nurses´ attitudes toward death. Integrating resilience-enhancing strategies and structured death education into both academic curricula and continuing professional training may contribute to nurses´ emotional well-being and to the quality of end-of-life care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12904-026-02062-8.Keywords: Attitude to death, Nurses, Psychological resilience, Palliative care units, End-of-life care, Death education

Introduction

Death is an inherent and unavoidable aspect of nursing practice and represents a major emotional and professional challenge for nurses, particularly in settings such as Intensive Care, Palliative Care, and Oncology. It encompasses psychological, social, cultural and economic aspects that significantly affect patients, relatives, and formal caregivers [1]. Within palliative care, care is oriented toward relief of suffering, meaning-making, and accompaniment at the end of life, which demands not only technical competencies but also emotional skills and reflective attitudes toward death to ensure compassionate and high-quality end-of-life care [2, 3].

Attitudes toward death (ATD) constitutes a multidimensional construct encompassing cognitive, emotional, behavioural, and spiritual dimensions. The model proposed by Wong et al. [4] describes five dimensions: Fear-of-Death – characterized by anxiety and fear emotions –, Death-Avoidance – a tendency to avoid discussing or confronting death –, and three forms of Death acceptance – Neutral-Acceptance (viewing death as a natural and inevitable part of life), Approach-Acceptance (seeing death as a gateway to a better afterlife), and Escape-Acceptance (preferring death over suffering) [5]. These attitudes are known to influence both the quality of end-of-life care and nurses’ psychological well-being [2, 6, 7]. Empirical evidence indicates that many nurses simultaneously recognize death as a natural process while experiencing fear and avoidance, which may generate internal conflicts [3, 8] and emotional distress, despair, helplessness, anger or depression [9, 10]. In contrast, greater acceptance of death has been associated with lower distress and more adaptive professional functioning [11, 12].

Despite the relevance of ATD, findings regarding its determinants remain inconsistent. Sociodemographic variables, such as age and gender, show contradictory associations with fear-based and acceptance-oriented attitudes. Some studies report that female nurses show more fear-driven attitudes [13, 14] or report the opposite [3], while others suggest that male nurses display more accepting attitudes [8, 15]. Some studies also indicate that younger nurses show greater Death Acceptance [16], whereas others associate youth with higher Death-Avoidance or greater Fear-of-Death [4, 17]. Such inconsistences may reflect complex interactions among personal experience, cultural narratives, and the nature of clinical exposure encountered during the different stages of professional development.

Similarly, occupational factors yield mixed results. Nurses working in Intensive Care Units (ICU) or Oncology, and those with less professional experience tend to report higher fear and avoidance [3, 17, 18], whereas working in palliative setting and greater clinical exposure have been associated with increased acceptance and emotional readiness [19]. However, recent studies suggest that frequent exposure to death does not uniformly promote adaptive attitudes and may, in some cases, hinder emotional adjustment, highlighting substantial variability across contexts [15].

Professional training in death and dying has been more consistently linked to adaptive ATD. Nurses with formal education in end-of-life care demonstrate greater acceptance, reduced fear, and enhanced emotional resilience [20], which in turn contributes to higher-quality care and ethical practice [8, 21–23].

In addition, resilience has emerged as a key protective factor in this contexts. Richardson’s meta-theory of resilience [24] defines it as a dynamic process of biopsychospiritual reintegration following disruption due to stress or adversity. Conceptualized as a multifaceted process shaped by individual, contextual, and relational resources [25, 26], resilience enables nurses to regulate emotions, integrate stressful experiences, and maintain psychological well-being when facing repeated exposure to death [27–29].

In this sense, the Psychological Immunity-Psychological Elasticity (PI-PE) model by IJntema et al. [30] highlights two core mechanisms: stressor-specific tolerance and narrative construction, with four potential adaptive outcomes: sustainability (resistance), recovery (rebound resilience), transformation (psychological reconfiguration), and thriving (flourishing). Higher resilience levels have been associated with greater Neutral-Acceptance and lower Fear-of-Death and Death-Avoidance [7, 31], as well as improved job satisfaction and well-being [32, 33]. Moreover, resilience may mediate the relationship between personal meaning, professional factors, and ATD, facilitating healthier emotional adaptation in end-of-life care settings [29, 34]. Therefore, fostering resilience and reflective engagement with death should be prioritized in nursing education and clinical supervision to safeguard both caregiver wellbeing and care quality.

Although the relevance or ATD and resilience in nursing practice is well established, the mechanisms through which personal and occupational factors interact with resilience to shape ATD in pre-pandemic clinical contexts remain insufficiently understood. In particular, evidence regarding the mediating role of resilience is limited and inconsistent. Accordingly, this study aims to examine, using pre-pandemic data [1], how nurses´ ATD are associated with sociodemographic and professional characteristics across high-mortality clinical settings, including Palliative Care units; [2] the relationship between resilience and specific dimensions of ATD; and [3], the extend to which resilience statistically mediates the associations between selected professional factors and ATD. A clearer understanding of these relationships may inform the development of targeted educational and organizational strategies to support nurses´ well-being and improve the quality of end-of-life care.

Methods

Study design and participants

This observational, cross-sectional study used a convenience sample of n = 742 nursing professionals from seven public hospitals of the Basque Health Service (Spain). Participants included Registered Nurses (RN) with Bachelor of Science in Nursing degrees and Auxiliary Nursing Care Technicians (ANCT) working in Intensive Care (n = 380), Oncology (n = 237), and Palliative Care (n = 125) units. Nurses in Primary Care services and nursing students were excluded.

The sample was predominantly female (90.4%), with 56.9% under 45 years and 70.2% holding Bachelor of Nursing degrees. Most professionals (74.1%) had over 5 years hospital experience, though only 38.1% had specific death and bereavement training. Overall resilience levels were medium-high (M = 28.43, SD = 6.02), with 24.7% showing high resilience. Complete sample characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Statistical power analysis

Sample size was determined via a priori power analysis for MANOVA [35] (power = 0.80, α = 0.05, medium effect size f² = 0.06), indicating a minimum requirement of n = 688. Our final sample (n = 742) exceeds this threshold. Full power analysis details are provided in Supplementary Material S1.

Procedure

Nursing managers and supervisors of ICU, Palliative Care and Oncology units at each participating hospital were initially contacted. Following approval, eligible nursing staff attended one-hour informational sessions within each unit. Participants received paper-and-pencil questionnaires with envelopes for anonymous submission into opaque, sealed boxes. To accommodate high rotation among nursing personnel, informational sessions were repeated fortnightly. Recruitment was conducted from June 2018 to April 2019, adhering to STROBE guidelines for reporting observational research.

Instruments

Sociodemographic and professional variables. Data about gender, age, professional category, healthcare setting, years of experience and prior training related to death and/or bereavement were collected through an ad-hoc questionnaire.

Attitudes toward death. The Death Attitude Profile-Revised scale (DAP-R) [4] Spanish validated version [36] was used. This multidimensional questionnaire consists of 32 items rated on a seven-point Likert scale (1 = totally disagree to 7 = totally agree), measuring five ATD: Fear-of-Death (seven items) and Death-Avoidance (five items), considered negative attitudes; Neutral-Acceptance (five items), Approach-Acceptance (10 items) and Escape-Acceptance (five items), considered positive attitudes [5]. Participant scores are calculated by combining dimensions with highest mean scores to form the pattern reflecting their attitude toward death. Internal consistency reliability for each DAP-R subscale was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha coefficients from the Spanish validation study [36], as our dataset contains aggregated subscale scores. All subscales demonstrated acceptable to excellent reliability: Fear-of-Death (α = 0.87), Death-Avoidance (α = 0.84), Neutral-Acceptance (α = 0.75), Approach-Acceptance (α = 0.93), and Escape-Acceptance (α = 0.81).

Resilience. The Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC-10) [37] Spanish version by Notario-Pacheco et al. [38] was used. It consists of 10 items scored on a Likert scale (0 = totally disagree to 4 = totally agree). Higher scores indicate higher resilience levels (range 0–40). Cronbach’s alpha was α = 0.85.

Data analysis

Data analyses were performed using Python (version 3.8) with NumPy, Pandas, SciPy, Statsmodels, and Scikit-learn libraries. Missing values were handled through Random Forest-based iterative imputation. Normality testing was based on skewness and kurtosis values (< 2 and < 7 respectively) [39, 40], supported by the Central Limit Theorem for large samples.

Descriptive statistics included means, standard deviations, frequencies, and percentages. Pearson and Spearman correlation coefficients assessed associations between variables. For healthcare settings, dummy variables were created for each of the three settings (Intensive Care, Oncology, and Palliative Care), enabling inclusion of all settings in multivariate analyses and specific pairwise comparisons. Each setting was coded as a binary variable (0 = not working in that setting, 1 = working in that setting), with participants belonging to exactly one setting. When reporting results, we emphasized comparisons between Intensive Care and Palliative Care due to their contrasting care philosophies (curative vs. end-of-life focused) and because prior literature has highlighted distinct death attitudes between these specific settings [3, 41].

One-way multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) examined differences in ATD by independent variables (gender, age, professional category, healthcare setting, seniority, prior training, and resilience). Bonferroni correction was applied for post-hoc tests.

Resilience scores were dichotomized at the third quartile (Q3 = 32.00) to create two groups: low-medium resilience (≤ Q3, n = 559, 75.3%) and high resilience (> Q3, n = 183, 24.7%). This approach was chosen for several reasons: first, it provides clinical interpretability—the Q3 cutoff identifies the top 25% of nurses with high resilience, providing a clear threshold for targeting interventions. Second, contemporary resilience models conceptualize outcomes categorically rather than dimensionally [24]. Third, the resilience distribution showed significant deviation from normality (Shapiro-Wilk W = 0.973, p < .001, skewness = -0.651, kurtosis = 0.990), suggesting distinct subpopulations rather than a smooth continuum [42]. Fourth, sensitivity analysis demonstrated high concordance between continuous and dichotomized approaches—5 out of 5 attitudes showed identical significance patterns with comparable effect sizes (see Supplementary Material S2 for detailed comparison).

Mediation analyses used the bootstrap method [43] with 5,000 iterations and 95% bias-corrected accelerated confidence intervals to test whether resilience mediated relationships between professional factors and death attitudes. For each model, we report path a (IV→Resilience), path b (Resilience→DV), and indirect effect (a×b) with 95% CI. Mediation was significant when 95% CI excluded zero.

Hierarchical regression analyses with centered variables examined whether resilience moderated relationships between personal/occupational factors and ATD. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) identified underlying patterns in attitudes toward death. Independent samples t-tests and one-way ANOVAs with Tukey post-hoc tests compared attitudes across groups. Data adequacy for PCA was assessed using the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure and Bartlett test of sphericity. The overall KMO was 0.576 (mediocre; Kaiser, 1974), with individual variable KMO values ranging from 0.491 to 0.735. Bartlett test was significant (χ² = 667.10, df = 10, p < .001), indicating the correlation matrix differed significantly from an identity matrix. The correlation matrix determinant (0.405) was well above the critical threshold of 0.00001, indicating no multicollinearity. While the modest KMO suggests PCA is technically permissible, results should be interpreted recognizing that the five death attitudes maintain substantial unique variance, consistent with the DAP-R multidimensional theoretical framework.

Statistical significance was set at p<.05, with effect sizes reported using partial eta squared (η²) for MANOVA, Cohen’s d for t-tests, and R² for regression models.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Research and Ethics Committee of the Basque Ethics Committee for Research (CEIm-E PI2018066) and conducted according to the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and subsequent amendments. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. Participant anonymity and confidentiality were ensured.

Results

Descriptive analysis of attitudes toward death

Nursing professionals showed highest scores in Neutral-Acceptance (M = 5.68, SD = 0.90), followed by Fear-of-Death (M = 3.70, SD = 1.39) and Death-Avoidance (M = 3.53, SD = 1.59). Lower scores were reported for Escape-Acceptance (M = 3.28, SD = 1.51) and Approach-Acceptance (M = 2.83, SD = 1.41) (Fig. 1).

Correlations between variables

Resilience showed weak negative correlations with Fear-of-Death (r=-.22, p<.01) and Death-Avoidance (r=-.14, p<.01), and a weak positive correlation with Neutral-Acceptance (r=.16, p<.01) (Fig. 2), indicating that more resilient nursing professionals appear to accept death as natural and experience less fear and avoidance.

Among sociodemographic and professional variables, age correlated positively with Approach-Acceptance (r=.15, p<.01) and Escape-Acceptance (r=.21, p<.01). Professional category correlated positively with Death-Avoidance (r=.11, p<.01), Approach-Acceptance (r=.12, p<.01), and Escape-Acceptance (r=.10, p<.01). Among attitudes toward death, strong positive correlations were found between Fear-of-Death and Death-Avoidance (r=.59, p<.01), and moderate correlations between Approach-Acceptance and Escape-Acceptance (r=.42, p<.01). Fear-of-Death and Death-Avoidance both showed moderate negative correlations with Neutral-Acceptance (r=-.37 and r=-.35, respectively, p<.01) (Fig. 3).

Mediating role of resilience

Bootstrap mediation analyses (5,000 iterations) examined resilience as mediator. Professional category showed a significant association with resilience (a = 2.17, p < .01), with ANCTs showing higher resilience. Resilience significantly mediated relationships with Fear-of-Death (indirect = -0.72, 95% CI [-1.15, -0.37]), Death-Avoidance (indirect = -0.51, 95% CI [-0.88, -0.20]), and Neutral-Acceptance (indirect = 0.26, 95% CI [0.10, 0.47]). For prior training, training was associated with lower resilience (a = -1.28, p < .01), resulting in significant mediation for Fear-of-Death (indirect = 0.42, 95% CI [0.11, 0.79]), Death-Avoidance (indirect = 0.24, 95% CI [0.05, 0.51]), and Neutral-Acceptance (indirect = -0.14, 95% CI [-0.29, -0.03]) (Table 4).

Moderating role of resilience

Moderation analyses examined whether relationships between personal/occupational factors and attitudes toward death varied depending on resilience levels. Only one significant moderation effect was identified: resilience moderated the relationship between professional category (ANCT) and Escape-Acceptance, although the effect size was minimal. Simple slope analysis showed the relationship between being an ANCT and Escape-Acceptance was slightly stronger for professionals with high resilience (B = 1.94) compared to those with low resilience (B = 1.93).

Principal component analysis of attitudes toward death

Principal Component Analysis identified two main components explaining 67.27% of variance in death attitudes. The first component (38.91% of variance) was heavily loaded with Fear-of Death (.857) and Death-Avoidance (.797), with negative loading for Neutral-Acceptance (-.619), representing negative/avoidant orientation toward death. The second component (28.35% of variance) was primarily loaded with Escape-Acceptance (.830) and Approach-Acceptance (.734) (Fig. 6).

Discussion

This study examined the relationships between personal and professional characteristics, resilience, and attitudes toward death among nursing professionals working in high-mortality clinical settings, including palliative care, using pre-pandemic data. The findings contribute to the palliative care literature by clarifying how resilience relates to death-related attitudes and by identifying potentially modifiable factors relevant to nurses´ well-being and the quality of end-of-life care.

Neutral-Acceptance emerged as the most prevalent attitude toward death, suggesting that many nurses conceptualize death as a natural and inevitable aspect of life. This form of acceptance appears to coexist with an ongoing motivation to live meaningfully [44], rather than reflecting emotional comfort. Similar patterns have been described previously in palliative and oncology settings, where acceptance-oriented attitudes often coexist with emotional vulnerability. For example, Cybulska et al. [3] reported high levels of both Neutral-Acceptance and Fear-of-Death and low avoidance. In the present study, Neutral-Acceptance was accompanied by moderate levels of both Fear-of-Death and Death-Avoidance. These findings suggest that acceptance may function primarily as a cognitive stance that enables continued professional functioning despite unresolved emotional responses to death. Such strategies may serve as mechanisms of self-preservation in emotionally demanding contexts.

Building on this understanding, a particularly important finding concerns Escape-Acceptance, which reflects a tendency to view death as a release from suffering. In this study, Escape-Acceptance loaded together with Approach-Acceptance in the principal component analysis and was more strongly endorsed by older nurses and auxiliary nursing care technicians. Nevertheless, overall levels of Escape-Acceptance were low in our sample, indicating that this attitude was not predominant among nursing professionals.

Although traditionally classified as a form of death acceptance, Escape-Acceptance requires careful ethical and clinical consideration in palliative care contexts. It may reflect a realistic acknowledgement of the limits of medical interventions and a compassionate orientation toward alleviating suffering. However, higher endorsement may also be associated with moral distress or compassion fatigue, suggesting that sustained exposure to suffering can erode emphatic capacity, emotional resilience, and professional well-being [45, 46]. From a clinical perspective, markedly elevated levels of Escape-Acceptance may warrant reflective inquiry into professionals’ emotional states, coping resources, and organizational support structures. Therefor, Escape-Acceptance should not be assumed to represent an adaptive outcome in itself within palliative care.

The low endorsement of Escape-Acceptance observed in the present study appears consistent with the predominance of Neutral-Acceptance. Together, this pattern suggests that nurses tended to conceptualize death as a natural process, while remaining oriented toward presence, relational engagement, and accompaniment. Such a configuration aligns with core palliative care values, in which relieving suffering coexists with affirming the value of life until death.

The coexistence of cognitive and emotional responses to death aligns with Wong´s model, and is supported by both Meaning Management Theory (MMT) [44] and Terror-Management Theory (TMT) [47]. While MMT emphasizes the integration of death into a meaningful life narrative, TMT highlights avoidance and denial as strategies to alleviate mortality awareness anxiety and terror. According to TMT, high Approach-Acceptance could foster symbolic immortality and more positive attitudes toward death. However, participants in both this study and Xu et al. [8] reported the lowest levels on Approach-Acceptance, implying that, in the absence of the protection of a solid belief in a better afterlife, death itself becomes a central concern. These reports support the findings of Kraitenberger et al.´s [48], who concluded that such beliefs might not reduce anxiety and fear, but rather exacerbate them. Thus, Death-Avoidance may operate as a plausible alternative form of protection, allowing nurses to counteract distressing emotions, thereby reinforcing TMT´s principles.

Nonetheless, effective coping in nursing practice requires more than avoidance. According to MMT, integrating death into one´s worldview allows nurses to focus on life, not just on death, and construct professional meaning. In this sense, higher Neutral-Acceptance enables higher quality patient-focused care and more efficient emotional regulation [49].

Neither gender nor working in oncology-related settings affected nurses´ approaches to death. However, nurses working in Palliative Care Units reported lower Fear-of-Death and Death-Avoidance than those in Intensive Care Units, suggesting that cultivating compassionate workplace cultures may facilitate more open engagement with death [3, 13]. This difference in unlikely to be explained solely by frequency of death-exposure; rather, it may reflect the underlying care philosophy of palliative care, which explicitly legitimizes open communication about death and dying, suffering, and meaning. Such findings reinforce the importance of organizational culture and reflective practice in shaping death-related attitudes.

Professional experience did not impact ATD. However, older nurses showed both greater Approach and Escape-Acceptance, suggesting that life experience itself, rather than work tenure, appears linked to greater reflection on life quality or life-after-death meaning. Results align with Gama et al. [13] and Cybulska et al. [3], suggesting an emphasis on quality of life over longevity, perhaps related to personal death experiences both in and outside workplace [50].

Both professional category and prior training in death and dying are associate with ATD variance. Non-degree professionals and those without death training reported greater Death-Avoidance compared to Registered Nurses. This aligns with Xu et al. [8] where nursing trainees receiving specific death education exhibited greater Neutral-Acceptance. Similarly, Cheong et al. [51] demonstrated that targeted workshops significantly decreased both Fear-of-Death and Death-Avoidance after 6 weeks. These results underscore to need for continual integration of death education into nursing curricula.

Death training is associated with higher reflection and emotional engagement, which may facilitate deeper empathic insight and making avoidance less feasible, but not necessarily reducing discomfort. While Registered Nurses scored lower in Approach-Acceptance, the metric increased following specific death-training, suggesting that no work experience is often associated with more rational, detached attitudes, whereas comprehensive on-job training appears linked to more meaningful, adaptive approaches, aligning with previous research [8, 16, 21, 52].

Resilience emerged as a key factor associated with more adaptive ATD, so negative events may be perceived as opportunities for cultivating strengths and self-growth [24]. Higher resilience was linked to greater Neutral-Acceptance and lower Fear-of-Death and Death-Avoidance. No significant interaction effects were observed among sociodemographic and professional variables and Neutral-Acceptance, except for resilience, which acted as a protective factor for palliative healthcare professionals, supporting conceptualizations of resilience as a dynamic resource that facilitates emotional regulation and meaning-making in the face of repeated exposure to death [53].

The findings are consistent with the Psychological-Immunity/Psychological-Elasticity model (PI-PE) [30], which positions tolerance and narrative construction as essential for adaptive coping with death and dying patients. Tolerance, developed through trained exposure, can be stressor-specific; therefore, tolerance can be specific to some stressors but not others. Building adaptive self-narratives is pivotal to integrating painful experiences, echoing Meaning Management Theory. In relation to Neutral-Acceptance, resilience enables nurses to maintain positive attitudes despite sickness or death proximity, making each moment worthy of living [29, 44].

Mediation analyses suggested that resilience statistically mediated the associations between professional category and prior training and several dimensions of ATD. However, these findings must be interpreted with caution. Given the cross-sectional design, mediation should not be understood as evidence of causal pathways or temporal ordering. Rather, the analyses identify plausible relational patterns indicating that resilience may function as a linking factor within a broader network of personal and professional characteristics.

In light of these findings, several practical implications emerge for nursing education, clinical practice, and healthcare organizations, particularly in high-mortality and emotionally demanding settings, such as Palliative Care units. First, palliative care education should extend beyond purely technical end-of-life competencies to incorporate structured opportunities for reflective practice, emotional processing, and narrative meaning-making. Longitudinal and experiential education in death and dying, integrating case-based learning and clinical supervision, may support nurses in developing coherent professional narratives rather than relying on avoidance.

Second, resilience should be conceptualized not solely as an individual attribute but as a relational and organizational resource embedded in everyday clinical practice. Regular ethical deliberation, peer-support spaces, and structured debriefings following patient deaths may help nurses process emotionally demanding experiences and reduce the risk of maladaptive coping strategies, such as emotional disengagement.

Third, the differential patterns observed across professional categories and age groups highlight the need for inclusive and tailored educational strategies that address the specific needs of all members of palliative care teams. Interventions sensitive to varying levels of training, experience, and decision-making autonomy may be particularly important for preventing emotional overload and mitigating the risk of maladaptive forms of death acceptance, especially among professionals with less formal training or lower organizational power.

Finally, at the organizational level, fostering supportive workplace cultures that validate emotional experiences, encourage dialogue about death, and provide protected spaces for reflection is essential in palliative care contexts, where exposure to suffering is continuous. Such environments may enhance resilience, reduce moral distress and compassion fatigue, and contribute to sustained professional well-being, while also strengthening the quality, humanity, and ethical sensitivity of end-of-life care provided to patients and families.

Strengths and limitations

This study contributes to existing literature given the paucity of publications on ATD and resilience in nursing staff. The large sample size, including professionals from different healthcare settings in different hospitals, with diversity in professional categories and death training degrees, ensures findings’ generalizability. Rigorous statistical analyses performed allow robust conclusions about how resilience plays major roles in nurses’ coping with patient death. Significantly, the study was conducted before COVID-19 pandemic outbreak, providing baseline data unaffected by subsequent large-scale mortality and associated frustration, helplessness and other psychological impacts.

However, several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. First, its cross‑sectional design precludes causal inferences, regarding the relationships among variables. Moreover, mediation analyses should be interpreted as statistical models of association rather than evidence of causal mechanisms, given the cross-sectional design. Longitudinal studies are therefore needed to examine how resilience and death attitudes evolve over time and in response to professional experiences and targeted interventions.

Second, the convenience sampling strategy, while necessary for recruiting professionals across multiple hospital units, may limit representativeness and reduce generalizability to the broader nursing workforce. While the large sample size and inclusion of multiple hospitals strengthen external validity, nurses working in other clinical contexts—such as primary care or low-mortality units—may exhibit different patterns of attitudes toward death and resilience. Third, several potentially influential psychosocial variables—such as religiosity or spiritual beliefs, baseline anxiety and general mental health, personality traits, perceived social support, and the frequency or subjective emotional intensity of exposure to patient death—were not included. These factors may interact with resilience and significantly shape attitudes toward death. Future studies should incorporate a broader psychosocial framework to capture the multidimensional nature of death attitudes more comprehensively. Additionally, as data were obtained through self‑report questionnaires, responses could be affected by social desirability bias, particularly in relation to emotionally sensitive constructs such as fear of death or avoidance behaviors. Participants may have underreported negative attitudes or distress due to professional norms emphasizing emotional control. The inclusion of qualitative methods or observational data in future research could provide a more nuanced understanding of how nurses experience and manage death in clinical practice.

In summary, future research should replicate this study post-pandemic to examine shifts in attitudes toward death and resilience building processes and to assess the long-term impact of large-scale crises on nurses´ emotional adaptation. Employing randomized sampling, longitudinal designs and additional psychosocial measures would clarify causal pathways and capture the dynamic evolution of death attitudes and resilience over time.

Conclusion

This study enhances understanding of how resilience and sociodemographic and professional variables relate to attitudes toward death among nursing professionals working in high-mortality settings, including palliative care. The findings highlight the complexity of death-related attitudes in nursing practice and underscore the role of both individual and contextual factors.

Although causal inferences cannot be established, resilience and prior education in death and dying appear to be associated with more adaptive attitudes toward death, particularly greater Neutral-Acceptance. In palliative care contexts, fostering resilience and reflective engagement with death may support nurses´ emotional well-being and ethical sensitivity, and capacity to provide compassionate end-of-life care.

These insights point to the need for institutional strategies that actively promote reflective death education, psychological support, and resilience-building initiatives for nursing staff. By addressing emotional demands collectively rather than individually, healthcare organizations may cultivate a culture of openness, mutual care, and self‑care which benefits both professionals and patients, enhancing the quality, humanity and ethical responsiveness of end‑of‑life care.
