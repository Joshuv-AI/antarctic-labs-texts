ATF4 promotes immune evasion in oral squamous cell carcinoma by suppressing autophagic PD-L1 degradation
Authors: Kui-Ming Wang, Lin-Zhou Zhang, Jia-Jie Liang, Zao Wang, Xiao-Le Wang, Jie Zhang, Gang Chen, Wei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmune checkpoint blockade targeting programmed death-1/programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-1/PD-L1) has revolutionized cancer therapy. However, its efficacy is frequently limited by primary and acquired resistance. While inflammatory signals transiently upregulate PD-L1 transcription, post-translational regulation is crucial for its sustained expression in chronically stressed tumors. Whether and how tumor-intrinsic stress-response pathways control PD-L1 stability to promote immune evasion remains incompletely understood.MethodsUsing human oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) specimens, syngeneic mouse models, single-cell RNA sequencing, and genetic and pharmacological perturbations, we dissected the role of the unfolded protein response effector ATF4 in regulating PD-L1 stability and antitumor immunity. Its therapeutic potential was further evaluated in immunocompetent mice treated with anti-PD-1 therapy.ResultsWe identified ATF4 as a tumor-intrinsic driver of immune evasion. In malignant cells, ATF4 induced reactive oxygen species (ROS), which activated the AKT-mTOR pathway and suppressed autophagy, thereby stabilizing the PD-L1 protein independently of inflammatory cues. Genetic ablation of ATF4 or pharmacological inhibition of ROS-AKT-mTOR signaling restored autophagic flux, reduced PD-L1 levels, and enhanced CD8+ T-cell infiltration and function. When combined with PD-1 blockade, ATF4 targeting further suppressed tumor growth. Clinically, ATF4 expression was inversely correlated with CD8+ T-cell infiltration and autophagy markers, positively correlated with PD-L1 levels, and predicted a poor response to immunotherapy in patients with OSCC.ConclusionsOur findings establish ATF4 as a stress-responsive regulator of PD-L1 proteostasis, directly linking tumor-intrinsic stress adaptation to immune checkpoint stabilization and therapy resistance. Targeting the ATF4-ROS-AKT-mTOR axis may represent a promising strategy to overcome resistance to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade.Keywords: Head and Neck Cancer, Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor, Immunotherapy, Tumor microenvironment - TME

Introduction

Despite major advances in cancer research, malignancy remains a leading cause of global mortality.1 Growing recognition of the dynamic interplay between tumors and the immune system has ushered a new therapeutic era defined by cancer immunotherapy.2 3 Central to this progress is immune checkpoint blockade targeting the programmed death-1 (PD-1)/programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) axis.4 5 Tumor cells frequently evade immune surveillance by upregulating PD-L1,6 7 which engages PD-1 on T cells to suppress antitumor immune responses.8 9 Monoclonal antibodies disrupting this interaction have produced durable clinical benefit in multiple solid malignancies.10 11 However, both primary and acquired resistance remain prevalent, ultimately limiting overall response rates.12

The challenge of therapeutic resistance is further compounded by the complex biology of PD-L1 itself. PD-L1 upregulation is observed across a wide spectrum of malignancies,13 14 where it not only directly suppresses local T-cell activity but also contributes to the establishment of an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME).15 Importantly, PD-L1 is not restricted to the tumor cell surface, and it is also present on tumor-derived small extracellular vesicles from various cancers,16 including melanoma,17 lung cancer,18 and oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC),19 thereby extending its immunoregulatory effects beyond cell-cell contact. Together, these observations highlight that PD-L1 function is governed not only by its transcriptional induction but also by mechanisms controlling its stability, trafficking, and subcellular localization. Defining the regulatory networks that coordinate these processes is essential for advancing our understanding of tumor immune evasion and for developing strategies to broaden and sustain responses to immunotherapy.

This regulatory landscape is shaped by the TME, a dynamic and heterogeneous ecosystem that critically shapes tumor progression and therapeutic response.20 Rapid malignant expansion imposes substantial intrinsic stress on tumor cells, including nutrient deprivation, hypoxia, and proteotoxic stress.21 22 To adapt to these conditions, tumor cells engage the unfolded protein response (UPR), an evolutionarily conserved endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-initiated signaling program that restores proteostasis and supports cellular survival.23 24 Emerging evidence indicates that UPR signaling extends beyond cell-autonomous stress adaptation to modulate antitumor immunity and immune surveillance.25 26 This convergence of intrinsic stress responses and immune regulation has thus become an area of increasing interest. Nevertheless, the mechanisms by which specific UPR transducers interface with immune checkpoint control, particularly in regulating PD-L1 expression and stability, remain incompletely characterized. This limitation constrains the rational development of combination strategies that integrate ER stress modulation with immune checkpoint blockade.

To address this gap, we performed a systematic profiling of UPR-associated components in clinical tumor specimens. This analysis revealed a consistent upregulation of the PERK-ATF4 signaling axis in malignant cells, with ATF4 emerging as a prominently enriched transcriptional regulator. Functional interrogation demonstrated that ATF4 depletion markedly reduced PD-L1 protein abundance without affecting PD-L1 messenger RNA (mRNA) levels, indicating a post-transcriptional mode of regulation. Loss of ATF4 attenuated AKT-mTOR signaling through a reduction in intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby relieving mTOR-dependent suppression of autophagy and promoting autophagic degradation of PD-L1. Consequently, ATF4 depletion led to a coordinated reduction in both cell-surface PD-L1 expression and the release of PD-L1-containing small extracellular vesicles (sEVs). These changes rendered malignant cells more susceptible to cytotoxic T cells-mediated killing and substantially enhanced responsiveness to anti-PD-1 therapy across multiple murine tumor models. Collectively, these findings identify ATF4 as a central mechanistic node linking UPR signaling to PD-L1 homeostasis and immune evasion, and highlight the PERK-ATF4 axis as a potential target for rational combination strategies to overcome resistance to immune checkpoint blockade.

Methods

Tissue samples and patient cohort

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical guidelines approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Hospital of Stomatology, Wuhan University. All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. Primary tumor specimens were collected from patients with OSCC during surgery at the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery, School and Hospital of Stomatology, Wuhan University. Clinical staging was performed in accordance with the guidelines of the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC 2018) on the TNM (tumor, node, metastasis) system. Diagnosis was rigorously confirmed by two independent pathologists through clinicopathological correlation and immunohistochemical profiling. We also included samples from a completed clinical trial. In that study, patients with stage III-IVA OSCC were assigned to receive either anti-PD-1 immunotherapy or anti-PD-1 plus TPF (docetaxel, cisplatin, 5-fluorouracil) chemotherapy.27 Given the availability of the samples, the present study included 46 patients from this trial, comprising anti-PD-1 immunotherapy (n=24) and anti-PD-1 plus TPF chemotherapy (n=22). Pathological response was assessed post-treatment, and patients were stratified into responders (>50% tumor regression) and non-responders (≤50% regression) according to histopathological review. Based on this criterion, the response rates were 16.7% (4/24) in the immunotherapy group and 90.9% (20/22) in the combination group. Treatment efficacy was evaluated radiologically based on the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors V.1.1. All patients’ information is shown in online supplemental tables 1 and 2.

Cell lines and cell culture

Human OSCC cell lines (CAL27, HSC3), human embryonic kidney cells (HEK293T), human oral keratinocytes (HOK) and mouse melanoma cells (B16F10) were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Cell Box). The mouse oral squamous cell carcinoma line MOC2 was maintained as recommended by the author.28 All cells were incubated at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere.

Generation of ATF4 knockdown and overexpression cell lines

Stable ATF4 knockdown cell lines were generated using lentiviral shRNA constructs targeting ATF4. Control cells were transduced with non-targeting shRNA. For overexpression studies, cells were transduced with lentiviral vectors encoding full-length ATF4 or empty vector controls. Stable populations were selected using puromycin and validated by immunoblotting.

Chemical inhibitors and drug treatments

For pharmacological inhibition experiments, cells were treated with the following reagents at the indicated concentrations and durations: Protein synthesis inhibitor cycloheximide (CHX, 200 µg/mL),29 autophagy inhibitor hydroxychloroquine (HCQ, 50 µM, 24 hours),30 proteasome inhibitor Z-Leu-Leu-Leu-al (MG132, 10 µM, 24 hours),31 AKT inhibitor MK2206 (4 µM, 24 hours),32 33 mTOR inhibitor rapamycin (200 ng/mL, 24 hours),34 35 ROS scavenger N-acetyl-cysteine (5 mM, 24 hours).36 Recombinant human interferon-gamma (IFN-γ, 200 ng/mL, 48 hours) was used where indicated.17 37 All inhibitor concentrations were based on prior literature and did not affect cell viability. All inhibitors’ information is shown in online supplemental table 3.

Immunohistochemistry

For immunohistochemistry (IHC), 4 µm thick paraffin-embedded tissue sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated. Antigen retrieval was performed in sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0). After blocking, sections were incubated with primary antibodies at 4°C overnight, followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (Mxb Biotechnologies). Immunoreactivity was visualized using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB, Mxb Biotechnologies), and nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin (Servicebio). Stained slides were scanned using a digital slide scanner and analyzed with image analysis software.

Protein expression levels were quantified using both H-score and integrated optical density (IOD) methods. The H-score was calculated by multiplying the percentage of positively stained cells at each staining intensity by the corresponding intensity value. The formula is as follows: (percentage of strongly positive-stained cells) × 3 + (percentage of moderately positive-stained cells) × 2 + (percentage of weakly positive-stained cells) × 1). For IOD analysis, positive staining areas were selected, and the integrated optical density was measured and normalized to the corresponding area (IOD/area).

Western blot analysis

Cells were lysed in radio immunoprecipitation assay buffer (RIPA buffer, Beyotime) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (MedChemExpress). Protein concentrations were determined using an enhanced bicinchoninic acid assay (Beyotime). Equal amounts of protein were resolved by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (Millipore). Membranes were blocked for 1 hour at room temperature and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C, followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Jackson). Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and quantified by densitometric analysis using ImageJ. Band intensities were normalized to the corresponding loading controls. All primary antibodies’ information is shown in online supplemental table 3.

Cell immunofluorescence

For immunofluorescence analysis, cells were cultured on 8 mm glass coverslips (CITOGLAS), fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Servicebio), and permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 (Beyotime). After blocking, samples were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C, followed by incubation with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies (Abbkine) in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, Beyotime). Images were acquired using a spinning disk confocal microscope with a 100×oil-immersion objective (SPINSR, Olympus). Colocalization analysis was performed using ImageJ. All primary antibodies’ information is shown in online supplemental table 3.

EdU assay

Cell proliferation was evaluated using the 5-ethynyl-2'-deoxyuridine (EdU) assay kit (Beyotime) per manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates (5,000 cells per well) and cultured for 24 hours. Subsequently, they were incubated with pre-warmed 2×EdU working solution for 2 hours, followed by fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde (Servicebio). After permeabilization with 0.3% Triton X-100 (Beyotime), EdU incorporation was detected via a Click reaction. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Beyotime), and images were captured using a fluorescence microscope for quantitative analysis (Nikon, ECLIPSE Ti2).

Multiplex immunohistochemistry

Multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC) was performed on consecutive 4 µm thick paraffin-embedded tissue sections using a commercial Opal multiplex IHC kit (Akoya Biosciences, #NEL801001KT). Slides were baked, deparaffinized, and rehydrated, followed by antigen retrieval in EDTA buffer (pH 9.0) using microwave heating. After blocking, sections were subjected to sequential cycles of staining with primary antibodies, HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, and Opal fluorophores, with microwave-based antibody stripping performed between each staining cycle. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Beyotime), and slides were mounted using Fluoromount G (SouthernBiotech, #0100-01). Whole-slide multispectral images were acquired using the Vectra 3 Automated Quantitative Pathology Imaging System (Akoya Biosciences). All primary antibodies’ information is shown in online supplemental table 3.

Flow cytometry

Single-cell suspensions were prepared from cultured cells, tumor tissues, peripheral blood, or lymph node as indicated. Cells were harvested, washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and stained under the following conditions. For surface marker staining (membrane PD-L1, CD3, CD8), cells were incubated with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies for 30 min at 4°C in the dark. For intracellular staining (total PD-L1, Ki-67), cells were fixed and permeabilized using the Foxp3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set (Invitrogen, #00-5523-00) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, followed by incubation with the indicated antibodies. Data were acquired on a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter) and analyzed using FlowJo software (V.10.9). All antibodies’ information is shown in online supplemental table 3.

In vivo assay

All animal care and experimental protocols performed were approved by animal experimental ethical inspection of laboratory animal center, Huazhong Agriculture University (HZAUMO-2025-0244). All C57BL/6 and BALB/c mice (aged 6–8 weeks) were maintained under pathogen-free conditions in the animal care facilities. All animals were maintained at room temperature (22–25°C) and with free access to food and water on a 12 hours light/dark cycle. All mice were used after 1 week of acclimatization to the facility. To establish subcutaneous tumor models, mice were inoculated in the dorsal flank with 1× 10⁶ MOC2 cells or 5×105 B16F10 cells. Tumor dimensions were measured every other day, and volume was calculated as (length×width²)/2. Mice were euthanized when tumor burden reached 1.5 cm in the longest dimension or at predetermined experimental endpoints. For survival analysis, mice were monitored until humane endpoints were reached.

For immunotherapy studies, tumor-bearing mice were treated with anti-PD-1 antibody or isotype control agents every 3 days. Tumor growth, tumor weight and survival were analyzed.

Single-cell RNA sequencing and data analysis

Tumor tissues obtained from surgical resection were weighed, placed in tissue preservation solution, and maintained at 2–4°C during transport for single-cell RNA sequencing. Single-cell transcriptomic data were processed and analyzed using the Scanpy (RRID: SCR_018139, V.1.9.3) toolkit. Low-quality cells and genes were excluded based on standard quality-control metrics, and gene expression counts were normalized. Dimensionality reduction was performed by principal component analysis, followed by two-dimensional visualization using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection. Cell clusters were identified using the Leiden clustering algorithm and annotated according to canonical marker genes, with additional reference-based annotation using CellTypist (RRID: SCR_024893, V.1.5.2). Tumor cells were identified based on high expression of squamous lineage and epithelial markers, including KRT5, KRT14, PERP, SFN, and S100A2. Immune cells were annotated by expression of pan-leukocyte and immune-associated genes such as PTPRC, LCP1, SRGN, LAPTM5, and CD74. Stromal cells were defined by enrichment of mesenchymal and fibroblast-associated markers, including SPARC, IGFBP7, IFITM2, IFITM3 and VIM. Malignant epithelial cells were then stratified into ATF4High and ATF4Low groups according to ATF4 expression levels.38 Differentially expressed genes between ATF4High and ATF4Low tumor cells were identified. Gene Ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were subsequently performed on the differentially expressed genes to characterize biological processes and signaling pathways associated with ATF4 activity in tumor cells.

Isolation of cell culture-derived sEVs

Cells were cultured in medium supplemented with 10% sEV-depleted FBS. Bovine sEVs were removed from FBS by ultracentrifugation at 120,000×g for 18 hours using a Beckman Optima XPN ultracentrifuge. Cell culture supernatants were collected after 48–72 hours and subjected to differential centrifugation. Briefly, supernatants were centrifuged at 3,000×g for 40 min to remove cells and debris. The cleared supernatants were then ultracentrifuged at 120,000×g for 70 min at 4°C.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

PD-L1 levels on cell culture-derived sEVs were measured by ELISA. Briefly, a 96-well ELISA plate (BioLegend) was pre-coated with anti-PD-L1 capture antibody (clone 5H1-A3, Millipore) overnight at 4°C. Purified sEVs isolated from conditioned media were quantified, and 100 µL of PBS containing 25 µg of sEV protein was added to each well, followed by overnight incubation at 4°C. Following extensive washing, biotinylated anti-PD-L1 detection antibody (clone MIH1, eBioscience) was added and incubated for 1 hour at room temperature. After washing, signal was developed using standard ELISA detection reagents, and absorbance was measured according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A standard curve was generated using recombinant human PD-L1 protein (R&D Systems, Cat #156-B7) to quantify PD-L1 concentrations in sEV samples.

RNA isolation and real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction analysis

Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Takara) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and RNA concentration was determined using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Reverse transcription was performed using 250 ng of total RNA per reaction. Quantitative real-time PCR was carried out using SYBR Green Master Mix (ABclonal) on a LightCycler 480 system (Roche) under standard cycling conditions. Gene-specific primers were designed with Primer-BLAST and synthesized by Sangon Biotech (online supplemental table 3). Relative gene expression levels were calculated and normalized to GAPDH as an internal control.

Co-immunoprecipitation assays

For co-immunoprecipitation (IP) assays, cells were lysed in IP-RIPA buffer (Beyotime) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein lysates (1 mg per sample) were then incubated with antibody-conjugated agarose beads (Sigma) under rotation at 4°C for 4 hours. Following five washes with IP-RIPA buffer on a rotator, bound proteins were eluted by boiling in 2×SDS buffer for 5 min at 95°C.

Detection of intracellular types of reactive oxygen species

Intracellular ROS levels were measured using the fluorescent probe 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA, Beyotime, S0033S). Cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1×106 cells per well and cultured in triplicate. After trypsinization, cells were collected, resuspended in PBS, and incubated with 10 µM DCFH-DA for 25 min at 37°C in the dark. Cells were then washed once with PBS to remove excess probe. Intracellular ROS oxidize DCFH-DA to the fluorescent compound dichlorofluorescein. Fluorescence intensity (excitation, 488 nm; emission, 515–545 nm) was quantified by flow cytometry using a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter), with 1×104 cells analyzed per sample. Of note, DCFH-DA has limitations in specificity, as it can be oxidized by various reactive oxygen species and does not distinguish between different ROS types.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (V.9.3.1, GraphPad Software). Data were presented as mean±SD. Differences between two groups were assessed using two-sided unpaired t-test. Survival curves were compared using the log-rank test. Correlations were evaluated using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. Tumor growth curves in mouse experiments were analyzed by two-way analysis of variance. A p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

ATF4 elevation is associated with tumor progression and predicts poor prognosis

To identify which branch of the UPR is preferentially activated in tumors, we analyzed the expression levels of the three major UPR sensors:39 ATF6, IRE1α, and PERK in normal oral mucosa (OM, n=25) and OSCC (n=60). Notably, PERK showed the most pronounced upregulation among the three UPR sensors, with both the highest fold increase in H-score and the most statistically significant difference compared with OM (online supplemental figure S1A,B). Consistent with PERK activation, its key downstream effector ATF4 was markedly elevated in OSCC specimens (figure 1A). This observation was further validated by western blot analysis, which confirmed higher ATF4 protein levels in OSCC tissues relative to matched OM controls (figure 1B).

We further compared OSCC cell lines with non-malignant HOK. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed a pronounced increase in the mean fluorescence intensity of ATF4 in OSCC cells (figure 1C). To evaluate the clinical relevance of ATF4 expression, we assessed its correlation with disease progression. Tumor ATF4 levels are significantly higher in patients with advanced clinical stage (figure 1D). Moreover, elevated ATF4 expression correlated with poorer overall survival, indicating its prognostic value in OSCC (figure 1E). Collectively, these findings establish ATF4 as a marker of tumor aggressiveness and poor prognosis in OSCC.

ATF4 inhibition in tumor cells unleashes antitumor immunity

Activation of the PERK-ATF4 branch promotes tumor growth and malignant progression.21 However, whether tumor cell-intrinsic ATF4 actively modulates antitumor immunity remains unclear. To investigate this, we generated Atf4 knockdown (KD) cells using a mouse OSCC cell line MOC2 (figure 2A). ATF4 depletion did not significantly affect tumor cell proliferation in vitro (online supplemental figure S2A,B). However, subcutaneous implantation of Atf4-KD cells into immunocompetent C57BL/6 mice markedly suppressed tumor growth (figure 2B–2C) and prolonged host survival (figure 2D). The discrepancy between the lack of an intrinsic growth defect in vitro and the pronounced tumor suppression observed in immunocompetent hosts raised the possibility that the antitumor effect of Atf4 depletion is mediated by host immune components. To test whether this effect depended on adaptive immunity, we repeated the experiment in T lymphocyte-deficient nude mice. In contrast to the pronounced growth inhibition observed in immunocompetent mice, implantation of Atf4-KD tumor cells into nude mice had no significant effect on tumor growth (online supplemental figure S2C,D), indicating that the antitumor effect of ATF4 ablation is dependent on T-cell mediated adaptive immunity.

Given the critical role of CD8+ T cells in antitumor immunity,20 we next characterized the immune landscape within Atf4-KD tumors. mIHC revealed elevated infiltration of CD45+ immune cells and a pronounced expansion of proliferating Ki-67+CD8+ T cells, accompanied by reduced tumor cell numbers and decreased tumor proliferative activity (figure 2E–2F). To determine whether this Atf4 dependent immune modulation extended beyond the local TME, we assessed systemic T-cell responses. Flow cytometric analysis demonstrated enhanced proliferation of CD8+ T cells in both the blood and lymph node of mice bearing Atf4-KD tumors (online supplemental figure S3A–C), suggesting that tumor-intrinsic ATF4 influences systemic antitumor immunity. Collectively, these data establish tumor-intrinsic ATF4 as a negative regulator of CD8+ T-cell expansion and proliferative competence, thereby restraining effective antitumor immune surveillance.

ATF4 depletion potentiates antitumor immunity through PD-L1 downregulation

To elucidate the underlying mechanism ATF4 shapes the immunosuppressive TME, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) of OSCC tissues. Unsupervised clustering identified tumor, immune, and stromal cell compartments (figure 3A–3B). Stratifying tumor cells by ATF4 expression revealed that ATF4High tumor cells downregulated gene signatures linked to T-cell activation, cytotoxicity, and leukocyte migration (figure 3C), while showing enrichment for JAK2-STAT3 signaling pathway, mTOR signaling pathway, and PI3K-AKT signaling pathways (figure 3D). Notably, ATF4High tumor cells exhibited elevated expression of the immune checkpoint ligand PD-L1 (CD274) and activation of the PD-1 checkpoint pathway (figure 3D), suggesting a direct link between ATF4 and PD-L1-mediated immunosuppression. We next functionally validated this association in vitro. ATF4-KD reduced PD-L1 protein levels and concurrently increased major histocompatibility complex (MHC)-I expression (figure 3E–3F and online supplemental figure S4A,B), without affecting other immune regulators such as B7H3, B7H4, and galectin-9 (online supplemental figure S4A,B). Conversely, ATF4 overexpression elevated PD-L1 (figure 3G–3H). Flow-cytometric analysis confirmed that ATF4 regulates PD-L1 at both total protein and cell-surface levels (online supplemental figure S5A–I). Given that PD-L1 is a well-established IFN-stimulated gene and is robustly induced by IFN-γ released by activated CD8+ T cells within the TME,40 we next examined whether ATF4-dependent regulation of PD-L1 persists under inflammatory conditions. We found that ATF4 mediated PD-L1 regulation was independent of the inflammatory cytokine IFN-γ, as ATF4 knockdown suppressed PD-L1 expression even under IFN-γ stimulation (online supplemental figure S6A–D).

In addition to controlling PD-L1 stability within tumor cells, PD-L1 can also be exported via tumor-derived sEVs to exert immunosuppressive effects beyond the local tumor niche.16,19 Given prior evidence that tumor-derived sEVs can secrete PD-L1 to mediate systemic immune evasion,17 we asked whether ATF4 governs PD-L1 secretion via sEVs. Accordingly, ATF4-KD reduced the colocalization of PD-L1 with the sEV marker CD9 and the vesicular trafficking regulator Rab27a (figure 4A–4D). In line with this observation, ATF4 depletion significantly decreased the level of PD-L1 carried by sEVs (figure 4E–4H), whereas ATF4 overexpression enhanced sEV PD-L1 release (figure 4I–4L). Consistently, in a syngeneic mouse tumor model, Atf4 knockdown significantly reduced circulating sEV PD-L1 levels (online supplemental figure S7A,B), further supporting that ATF4 promotes PD-L1 secretion via sEVs in vivo. These data indicate that ATF4 coordinates both the expression and vesicular secretion of PD-L1. Together, these results establish ATF4 as a key regulator of PD-L1 expression and sEV secretion, providing a mechanistic basis for its role in tumor immunosuppression.

ATF4 sustains PD-L1 stability by restraining autophagic degradation

To delineate the mechanism underlying ATF4-mediated PD-L1 upregulation, we first sought to distinguish between transcriptional and post-translational control. Depletion of ATF4 did not affect PD-L1 mRNA expression (figure 5A and online supplemental figure S8A), indicating regulation primarily at the protein level. Consistent with this, cycloheximide chase assays revealed a marked acceleration of PD-L1 protein degradation following ATF4 knockdown (figure 5B–5C), suggesting that ATF4 stabilizes PD-L1 post-translationally. Given that scRNA-seq revealed suppression of autophagy-related gene signatures in ATF4High tumor cells (figure 3D), we next investigated whether autophagy contributes to PD-L1 stabilization. ATF4 depletion increases autophagic flux, as reflected by elevated LC3B and decreased p62 protein levels (figure 5D). Moreover, immunofluorescence revealed significantly enhanced colocalization of PD-L1 with LC3B-positive autophagosomes on ATF4 depletion (figure 5E–5F), directly linking ATF4 to the autophagic degradation of PD-L1. To functionally validate the role of autophagy, we employed pharmacologic inhibitors. Treatment with autophagy inhibitors (HCQ), but not proteasome inhibitors (MG132), fully restored PD-L1 protein levels in ATF4 knockdown cells (figure 5G and online supplemental figure S8B), confirming that autophagy is the primary degradation route of PD-L1 regulated by ATF4.

To further explore how PD-L1 is recognized for autophagic degradation, we performed co-immunoprecipitation assays. PD-L1 specifically interacted with p62, but not with NBR1 or NDP52, in 293T cells (online supplemental figure S9A). This interaction was significantly enhanced in ATF4 knockdown CAL27 cells (online supplemental figure S9B). Consistently, immunofluorescence showed increased colocalization of PD-L1 with p62 on ATF4 depletion. These findings identify p62 as the selective autophagy receptor mediating PD-L1 degradation when ATF4 is suppressed (online supplemental figure S9C).

Consistent with observations from murine models and in vitro systems, ATF4 expression in human OSCC samples was significantly inversely correlated with intratumoral CD8+ T-cell density (online supplemental figure S10A,B), suggesting an association between elevated ATF4 expression and an immune-excluded tumor phenotype. In parallel, ATF4 levels positively correlated with PD-L1 protein abundance (online supplemental figure S10C,D), supporting a role for ATF4 in sustaining immune checkpoint expression in clinical tumors. Moreover, ATF4 expression exhibited a significant inverse correlation with the autophagy marker LC3B (online supplemental figure S10E,F), indicating that ATF4 may suppress autophagic activity in vivo. Collectively, these clinical data support a model in which ATF4 sustains elevated PD-L1 protein levels in human OSCC by restraining autophagic degradation, thereby promoting tumor immune evasion.

ATF4 sustains PD-L1 expression by activating the ROS-AKT-mTOR axis to restrain autophagic degradation

We next sought to define the upstream signaling pathways through which ATF4 suppresses autophagy. Differentially expressed genes were subjected to KEGG pathway analysis, which revealed significant enrichment of the JAK2-STAT3 signaling pathway, mTOR signaling pathway, and PI3K-AKT signaling pathway in ATF4High tumor cells (figure 3D). Western blot analysis revealed that ATF4 knockdown markedly reduced phosphorylation of AKT and mTOR, without affecting JAK2-STAT3 signaling (online supplemental figure S11A,B). ATF4 overexpression robustly activated AKT-mTOR signaling. Pharmacologic AKT inhibition (MK2206) abolished mTOR activation, concomitantly restoring autophagic flux and promoting PD-L1 degradation (figure 6A–6B and online supplemental figure S11C). In parallel, mTOR inhibition similarly accelerated PD-L1 degradation in ATF4-overexpressing (OE) cells without altering AKT phosphorylation (figure 6C–6D and online supplemental figure S11D), establishing mTOR as a critical downstream effector through which ATF4 suppresses autophagy to stabilize PD-L1.

We next sought to identify the upstream signal linking ATF4 to AKT-mTOR activation. Given the established crosstalk between the UPR and ROS,41 we quantified intracellular ROS levels. ATF4 depletion significantly reduced ROS accumulation, whereas ATF4-overexpression increased ROS accumulation, implicating ROS as an upstream mediator (online supplemental figure S12A,B). Critically, scavenging ROS in ATF4-OE cells abrogated AKT-mTOR activation and reinstated autophagy-dependent PD-L1 degradation (figure 6E–6F and online supplemental figure S12C).

To validate this axis in vivo, we analyzed Atf4-KD tumors. Consistent with efficient Atf4 depletion (figure 2A), these tumors exhibited reduced Atf4 protein levels accompanied by decreased phosphorylation of AKT and mTOR (online supplemental figure S13A,B). Concomitantly, levels of the autophagy adaptor p62 were reduced (online supplemental figure S13C), while expression of the autophagy marker LC3B was increased (online supplemental figure S13D), indicating enhanced autophagic flux. Notably, PD-L1 protein abundance was significantly diminished in Atf4-deficient tumors (online supplemental figure S13E). Collectively, these findings define a tumor-intrinsic ATF4-ROS-AKT-mTOR signaling axis that suppresses autophagy and stabilizes PD-L1, thereby reinforcing immune checkpoint-mediated tumor immune evasion.

Targeting ATF4 sensitizes tumors to anti-PD-1 therapy

We next sought to determine whether tumor ATF4 levels correlate with clinical response to anti-PD-1 therapy. We analyzed pretreatment tissues from 46 patients with OSCC enrolled in a randomized phase II trial (NCT04649476),27 in which patients received either neoadjuvant anti-PD-1 immunotherapy or anti-PD-1 combined with TPF chemotherapy. Elevated ATF4 expression in tumors effectively distinguished non-responders from responders (figure 7A) and was associated with significantly poorer disease-free survival (figure 7B), supporting ATF4 as a potential biomarker of resistance to PD-1 blockade.

To directly assess whether targeting ATF4 could overcome therapeutic resistance, we next established in vivo intervention models. Given that ATF4-mediated stress adaptation and PD-L1 regulation are not tissue restricted, we employed both OSCC and melanoma mouse models to evaluate the therapeutic impact of combining tumor-intrinsic ATF4 suppression with anti-PD-1 therapy. In mice bearing Atf4 knockdown tumors, Atf4 suppression alone significantly restrained tumor growth (figure 7C–7F). Notably, combination treatment with anti-PD-1 further enhanced tumor control compared with either intervention alone (figure 7C–7F), resulting in improved therapeutic efficacy and prolonged survival (figure 7G–7H). Together, these clinical correlative analyses and preclinical intervention studies indicate that tumor-intrinsic ATF4 contributes to resistance to PD-1 checkpoint blockade and that attenuation of ATF4 signaling can sensitize tumors to immunotherapy.

Discussion

Tumor immune evasion arises from a complex interplay between extrinsic cues within the TME and intrinsic stress-adaptive programs in tumor cells.23 42 In this study, we identify ATF4, a core downstream effector of the PERK branch of the UPR,39 as a tumor-intrinsic regulator that links chronic cellular stress to immune suppression. We demonstrate that ATF4 stabilizes PD-L1 protein by suppressing autophagy-dependent degradation through an ROS-AKT-mTOR signaling axis, thereby sustaining immune checkpoint signaling independently of inflammatory cues. By integrating single-cell transcriptomics, mechanistic analyses, and in vivo immunotherapy models, our findings establish ATF4 as a key molecular conduit. It translates chronic stress adaptation into immune checkpoint stabilization, CD8+ T-cell dysfunction, and resistance to immune checkpoint blockade. These findings highlight stress signaling as an actionable dimension of cancer immunoregulation.

Accumulating evidence highlights the context-dependent yet functionally convergent roles of ATF4 in immunosuppression across diverse cellular compartments within the TME.26 42 Consistent with our study, ATF4 drives tumor growth in immunocompetent mice but not in nude mice across lung cancer and melanoma,43 underscoring a conserved immune-dependent mechanism. In human fibrosarcoma, ATF4 knockdown suppresses tumor growth primarily by disrupting amino acid metabolism, particularly through downregulation of asparagine synthetase, accompanied by an initial surge in protective autophagy.44 Beyond cancer cells, ATF4 also exerts cell type-specific effects within the TME. In CD8+ T cells, chronic ATF4 activation driven by hypoxia and metabolic stress induces mitochondrial dysfunction and terminal exhaustion, thereby limiting responsiveness to anti-PD-1 therapy.22 In the myeloid compartment, PERK-ATF4 signaling in macrophages promotes immunosuppressive M2 polarization and proliferation via enhanced serine biosynthesis,45 and drives a glycolytic phenotype in monocyte-derived macrophages within glioblastoma.46 Our previous work further demonstrated that PERK-ATF4 activation in macrophages contributes to an immunosuppressive microenvironment in lymph node of patients with oral cancer.47 Additionally, CHOP, a key downstream effector of ATF4, is essential for the immunosuppressive function of myeloid-derived suppressor cells.48 Beyond immune cells, ATF4-mediated PHGDH expression in endothelial cells supports pathological angiogenesis and restricts T-cell infiltration.49 More recently, tumor-intrinsic ATF4 has been implicated in non-cell-autonomous immune evasion via neuroimmune circuits that suppress tumor-draining lymph node immunity.50 Here, we report that PERK-ATF4 activation in tumor cells further amplifies immunosuppression by impeding autophagic degradation of PD-L1. Collectively, these findings establish the PERK-ATF4 axis as a central regulator of tumor immune evasion across multiple cell types and cancer contexts.

ROS is integral to the TME, serving as both metabolic byproducts and signaling intermediates that regulate antigen presentation, immune checkpoint stability, and immune cell function.51 Sustained ATF4 activation under chronic stress has been linked to cellular redox imbalance,41 as prolonged ATF4 signaling disrupts mitochondrial function and promotes ROS accumulation in both hepatocytes and tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes.22 52 Genetic manipulation of ATF4 in tumor cells revealed that ATF4-dependent ROS accumulation activates AKT-mTOR signaling, consistent with the well-established role of ROS in activating this pathway.53 Prior studies have shown that mitochondrial redox imbalance can activate PI3K-AKT signaling.54 Here, we identify a tumor-intrinsic ATF4-ROS axis that suppresses autophagy and stabilizes PD-L1 in an AKT-mTOR-dependent manner, coupling stress adaptation to immune evasion. Notably, ROS scavenging disrupted this signaling cascade, restored PD-L1 degradation, and enhanced antitumor immunity, positioning ROS as a key downstream effector of ATF4 in tumors.

As the most important immune checkpoint molecule, PD-L1 stability has emerged as a key determinant of immune checkpoint activity in tumors.6 Although IFN-γ-driven transcription mediates rapid PD-L1 induction,55 accumulating evidence indicates that post-translational mechanisms controlling PD-L1 turnover are essential for sustaining immune suppression under prolonged immune pressure.56 PD-L1 stability is regulated at multiple levels, including ubiquitination, glycosylation, and lysosomal trafficking. For instance, CMTM6 preserves PD-L1 expression by limiting ubiquitin-mediated degradation,57 whereas inflammatory signaling through the IL-6-JAK1 axis enhances PD-L1 stability via phosphorylation-dependent glycosylation.58 In addition, lysosomal and autophagy-related pathways have been implicated in PD-L1 turnover, as mTOR inhibition can promote TFEB (nuclear transcriptional factor EB)-dependent PD-L1 degradation and enhance antitumor T-cell responses.59 Consistent with these regulatory mechanisms, we identify a stress-responsive mechanism in which tumor-intrinsic ATF4 restrains autophagic flux via the ROS-AKT-mTOR axis, limiting PD-L1 degradation and sustaining immune checkpoint expression. Notably, ATF4 knockdown not only reduced PD-L1 but also increased MHC-I expression, suggesting a potential link between PD-L1 regulation and antigen presentation. Recent studies have shown that PD-L1 possesses intrinsic E3 ubiquitin ligase activity that promotes β2-microglobulin (β2m) degradation and reduces surface MHC-I.60 Thus, ATF4 knockdown may indirectly enhance MHC-I expression by alleviating PD-L1-mediated β2m degradation.

The identification of ATF4 as a tumor-intrinsic driver of immune evasion carries important translational implications. Unlike immune checkpoint ligands that are primarily induced by inflammatory cues,40 ATF4 activation reflects intrinsic stress states common to solid tumors, including hypoxia, nutrient deprivation, and proteotoxic stress. Consistent with this distinction, elevated tumor ATF4 expression stratified non-responders to PD-1 blockade and correlated with poorer survival, supporting its potential utility as a predictive biomarker of immunotherapy resistance. Although direct pharmacologic inhibition of ATF4 remains challenging, multiple upstream components of the PERK arm of the UPR and ER stress-adaptive signaling are pharmacologically tractable.23 39 These findings provide a mechanistic rationale for combining ER stress-targeting strategies with immune checkpoint blockade, particularly in tumors characterized by high intrinsic stress signaling and immune exclusion.

One limitation of this study is the relatively small clinical sample size, which may limit the generalizability of our findings and warrants validation in larger, multicenter cohorts. In addition, the precise molecular mechanism by which ATF4 regulates ROS remains to be fully elucidated. Furthermore, although genetic approaches demonstrate that ATF4 depletion enhances immunotherapy efficacy, the development of pharmacological inhibitors targeting ATF4 will be essential for clinical translation.
