Radiation‐Associated Aortocolonic Fistula in Urothelial Carcinoma: Multidisciplinary Management and Case Report
Authors: Matthew Lee, Anil K. Pillai, Christopher Fan, Qi Cai, Kristi Baker, Jue Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTUpper tract urothelial carcinoma (UTUC) is an uncommon and aggressive malignancy that often presents as an invasive disease. After receiving systemic therapy, stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT) offers a non‐surgical option for consolidative local tumor control by delivering radiation doses in limited fractions. While SBRT is generally well tolerated in the treatment of UTUC, there is a small but serious risk of radiation‐associated complications. We present a case of a 67‐year‐old male with metastatic UTUC of the renal pelvis treated with chemotherapy and immunotherapy. Due to persistent disease at the primary site, he underwent consolidative SBRT using a PULSAR (Personalized Ultra‐fractionated Stereotactic Adaptive Radiotherapy) approach to his left kidney mass. Weeks later, he developed a renocolic fistula that was complicated by sepsis. Despite a diverting colostomy and antibiotics, he re‐presented with hemorrhagic shock due to an aortocolonic fistula that required emergent endovascular aortic repair (EVAR). This case illustrates a rare but serious complication of consolidative SBRT in a patient with extensive retroperitoneal malignancy. Multidisciplinary planning and close surveillance are critical to ensuring the safe integration of SBRT in the management of metastatic UTUC.

Introduction

Upper tract urothelial carcinoma (UTUC) is a relatively rare malignancy of the renal pelvis and ureters, accounting for approximately 5%–10% of all urothelial cancers and fewer than 10% of renal malignancies. UTUC is known to have an aggressive clinical course, with 25%–30% of patients presenting with metastatic disease [1]. For patients with metastatic UTUC, platinum‐based chemotherapy and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) are the mainstays of treatment [2]. In those with a favorable response or stable disease after systemic therapy, consolidative treatment with surgery is frequently considered and has been associated with improved survival [3].

For patients who are not candidates for consolidative surgery, stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT) offers an alternative for local control. SBRT delivers high doses of radiation over a limited number of fractions to ablate tumor tissue [4]. Although generally well tolerated, the high dose radiation of SBRT can lead to serious toxicities, including ulceration, fibrosis, necrosis, and fistula formation [5, 6]. We report a case of a 67‐year‐old male with metastatic UTUC of the kidney who was treated with consolidative SBRT after being deemed a poor surgical candidate due to extensive residual disease and iliopsoas muscle involvement. He subsequently developed a renocolic fistula complicated by sepsis, which progressed to a life‐threatening aortocolonic fistula that required emergent endovascular repair. This case highlights a rare but serious complication of SBRT in the setting of renal malignancy and underscores the importance of comprehensive, multidisciplinary planning and post‐treatment vigilance.

Case History/Examination

A 67‐year‐old male with a medical history of hypertension, nephrolithiasis, and recurrent urinary tract infections presented to the hospital with worsening left flank pain. CT imaging revealed left xanthogranulomatous pyelonephritis with a large staghorn calculus, left renal enlargement, and a rim‐enhancing lower pole mass extending into the left psoas muscle. Imaging also showed local invasion (Figure 1), and histopathological evaluation of the biopsy confirmed the diagnosis of high‐grade urothelial carcinoma (Figure 2). Subsequent staging scans revealed bilateral lung nodules suspicious for metastatic disease.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment

His case was reviewed at a multidisciplinary tumor board with urology, radiology, radiation oncology, medical oncology, and pathology. Urology deemed his tumor unresectable, and the consensus was to initiate palliative chemotherapy. He was started on carboplatin and gemcitabine due to impaired renal function, and he transitioned to cisplatin‐based chemotherapy after his renal function improved. Three months later, imaging revealed disease progression, so he was started on second‐line enfortumab vedotin and pembrolizumab. Following 11 cycles of therapy, PET/CT imaging demonstrated resolution of the metastatic disease but persistent FDG‐avid disease of the primary renal lesion, with continued extension along the iliopsoas. Given the isolated progression, he was referred for consolidative therapy. Due to the extent of residual disease and muscular invasion, he was deemed a poor candidate for surgery. He underwent SBRT to the left renal mass, delivered as 50 Gy in five fractions on a PULSAR schedule (Personalized Ultrafractionated Stereotactic Adaptive Radiotherapy, one fraction per month), completing all planned treatments.

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

Two months after completing SBRT, the patient was admitted with fevers, fatigue, and abdominal pain. Imaging revealed a necrotic retroperitoneal fluid collection with fistulous communication between the left renal mass and the sigmoid colon, consistent with a renocolic fistula (Figure 3A,B). Blood cultures were positive for Escherichia coli and Fusobacterium, and he was treated with a diverting transverse loop colostomy and intravenous antibiotics. He was then discharged home with a plan for outpatient antibiotic therapy for 4 weeks. Four days after discharge, he returned to the hospital with complaints of abdominal pain and bleeding into his ostomy. On admission, he was found to have hemorrhage and septic shock. CT angiography revealed a new fistulous tract between the aortic bifurcation and the necrotic retroperitoneal mass, which was still connected to the sigmoid colon (Figure 3C,D). This was consistent with an aortocolonic fistula. Following this discovery, he underwent an emergent endovascular aortic repair (EVAR). Despite initial stabilization, his clinical condition continued to deteriorate over the following days, and he was transitioned to comfort care.

Discussion

Vascular‐enteric fistulas are a rare but catastrophic complication of radiation therapy for malignancies involving the gastrointestinal tract and retroperitoneum [7, 8]. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of an aortocolonic fistula following SBRT in a patient with UTUC. Our patient's complex clinical course, including infection, fistula formation, and hemorrhagic shock, highlights the risk of high‐dose radiation in anatomically sensitive regions. This case adds to the limited literature on radiation‐induced vascular‐enteric fistulas and underscores the need for heightened awareness when treating unresectable retroperitoneal tumors near the bowel and major vessels.

While SBRT is a valuable alternative for consolidative therapy in patients who are not surgical candidates, its ablative nature carries a risk of serious gastrointestinal (GI) and vascular complications. Grade 3 and 4 GI adverse events, including ulceration, perforation, mucositis, and fistula formation, can be seen in up to 15% of cases [9]. However, fistula formation remains rare, with an estimated incidence between 2% and 4% following SBRT [6, 10]. Fistulas following SBRT have been documented in tumors involving the pancreas, liver, kidneys, and other retroperitoneal structures [11, 12]. Large tumors in close proximity to critical structures are more prone to severe toxicity from SBRT [13]. In our patient, his tumor closely abutted both the sigmoid colon and aorta, significantly raising the risk of fistula formation.

Beyond the proximity of the irradiated tumor to the bowel and aorta, there were several additional risk factors for our patient to develop a fistula. First, our patient had a longstanding history of nephrolithiasis and recurrent urinary tract infections, which likely led to the development of xanthogranulomatous pyelonephritis (XGP). XGP is an aggressive variant of chronic pyelonephritis associated with recurrent urinary obstruction and parenchymal destruction [14]. Although XGP can lead to fistula formation in rare cases, the renocolic fistula in our case developed shortly after completing SBRT. Subsequent imaging demonstrated necrosis along the iliopsoas within the irradiated field. This timing and anatomical distribution of the injury suggest that SBRT was the precipitating factor. Moreover, our patient had an invasive tumor that extended into adjacent tissues, including the iliopsoas muscle (Figure 1). Tumor invasion into nearby organs has been shown to be a risk factor for fistula formation following SBRT [15]. Together, these elements created a vulnerable environment that likely compromised tissue healing and amplified our patient's risk of a radiation‐induced injury.

To mitigate the risk of radiation‐induced injury, dose‐fractionation strategies are critical. Regimens with a lower dose per fraction (e.g., 8–10 fractions) can reduce toxicity compared to the standard 3–5 fraction SBRT schedules, but this may result in decreased local control [10, 16]. Our patient was treated with a PULSAR SBRT regimen, which delivers high‐dose fractions spaced weeks apart to allow normal tissue recovery and adaptive replanning [17]. This approach has been explored in preclinical and early clinical studies as a strategy to mitigate toxicity while maintaining tumor control [17, 18]. Although the colon was not directly targeted, its close proximity to the left renal mass made it an organ at risk. Consensus guidelines recommend limiting the colon SBRT dose to 38 Gy in 5‐fraction regimens to minimize toxicity [19, 20]. In this case, 50 Gy in 5 fractions was used to maximize local control of the invasive renal mass, but it is possible that adjacent bowel received excess exposure. Despite the use of a risk‐adapted PULSAR strategy, our patient developed a vascular‐enteric fistula, underscoring that even advanced fractionation techniques cannot fully eliminate the risk of SBRT toxicity in high‐risk patients.

To better understand this complication, we conducted a targeted review of published cases of radiation‐induced vascular‐enteric fistulas (Table 1). Most reported cases involved gastrointestinal or retroperitoneal malignancies treated with conventional external beam radiation therapy (EBRT), intraoperative radiation therapy (IORT), or both [8, 21, 22]. Conventional EBRT utilizes small daily fractions of radiation (typically 1.8–2 Gy) over weeks [23]. It has a larger radiation field compared to SBRT, which may cause more diffuse late patterns of toxicity such as progressive ischemia, fibrosis, and ulceration [23, 24]. By contrast, IORT delivers a single, very high dose (commonly ~10–15 Gy) directly to the tumor bed during surgery [25]. While the bowel can often be physically displaced or shielded, the high dose means any unintentionally included bowel or vascular segments are at risk for toxicity. In a Johns Hopkins series, grade 3–4 radiation complications occurred in 34% of patients, highlighting the risk of high single‐fraction dosing [25]. SBRT falls between these modalities because it offers greater precision than EBRT and avoids the extreme single‐fraction dosing of IORT. However, its ablative per‐fraction dose carries a risk of complications, as discussed earlier.

This case also emphasizes the importance of a comprehensive, multidisciplinary approach to managing radiation‐induced fistulas, which can be progressive and life‐threatening. SBRT‐induced fistulas are often the result of progressive tissue injury, developing over weeks to months after treatment. In this context, even minor melena, hematochezia, or hematemesis could represent a “herald bleed,” a warning sign before major hemorrhage in aortocolonic fistulas [26]. Similar to our patient who presented with hemorrhagic shock, each of the 14 additional cases presented with signs of gastrointestinal bleeding such as hematemesis, melena, or syncope (Table 1). Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for fistula formation in patients presenting with gastrointestinal bleeding, abdominal pain, or sepsis following retroperitoneal SBRT. Early recognition of a vascular‐enteric fistula is crucial but challenging, as these lesions are often rapidly progressive and may have variable symptoms. For instance, in a retrospective study by Shanmugam et al., all six patients with radiation‐induced vascular‐enteric fistulas were diagnosed only postmortem [22]. In our case, CT angiography was instrumental in discovering the patient's aortocolonic fistula, as it demonstrated direct extravasation of contrast between the abdominal aorta and sigmoid colon (Figure 3C,D). While prior reports (Table 1) used arteriography to detect bleeding, modern practice favors CT angiography as the imaging of choice to detect vascular‐enteric fistulas due to its non‐invasive nature and ability to guide urgent intervention [27].

Traditionally, aortocolonic fistulas were managed with open surgical repair, but endovascular approaches are increasingly utilized due to their association with lower short‐term mortality in recent studies [28]. Our patient was treated with EVAR, which initially stabilized his hemodynamics. However, he ultimately succumbed to complications related to his fistula. Despite the lower risk of short‐term mortality, studies suggest that EVAR alone may be insufficient, particularly when the fistula involves an infectious or inflammatory component [29, 30]. Open surgical repair allows for direct debridement and resection of involved bowel and has been associated with lower rates of persistent infection [30]. Some literature advocates for a staged approach, using EVAR as a bridge to definitive open repair once the patient is hemodynamically stabilized [29]. Regardless of modality, the prognosis remains poor. Of the 14 reviewed cases, 12 resulted in death, despite aggressive management such as embolization, laparotomy, and endovascular repair (Table 1). These findings highlight the importance of early diagnosis and rapid multidisciplinary intervention when treating vascular‐enteric fistulas.

Given the high mortality associated with vascular‐enteric fistulas, preventative strategies are essential. Radiotherapy planning should aim to minimize exposure to adjacent organs and vascular structures with adaptive dose‐fractionation techniques, bowel displacement, or shielding. For high‐risk patients with tumors abutting bowel or vessels, early post‐treatment radiologic surveillance with CT angiography may help detect mucosal injury or evolving fistulas before catastrophic bleeding occurs [27]. Educating patients to promptly report gastrointestinal bleeding or new abdominal pain is equally important as it can facilitate early recognition of herald bleeds and timely intervention.

Conclusion

This case highlights a rare but potentially fatal complication of consolidative SBRT in the treatment of UTUC. As the use of SBRT expands for local control of genitourinary malignancies, clinicians should remain vigilant for the potential formation of vascular‐enteric fistulas. Early recognition of herald bleeds, prompt imaging, and emergent intervention through multidisciplinary collaboration are crucial to managing radiation‐induced fistulas effectively. Future research into the prevention, early detection, and optimal management of radiation‐induced complications will be important to maximize outcomes in patients undergoing SBRT in anatomically sensitive regions.
