Clusters of adolescent pregnancies and neonatal deaths in São Paulo state, Brazil: a population-based spatial analysis with a socioeconomic approach
Authors: Carina Nunes Vieira e Oliveira, Tulio Konstantyner, Daniela Testoni Costa-Nobre, Ana Sílvia Scavacini Marinonio, Mandira Daripa Kawakami, Rita de Cássia Xavier Balda, Milton Miyoshi, Adriana Sanudo, Kelsy C N Areco, Paulo Bandiera-Paiva, Rosa Maria Vieira de Freitas, Monica La Porte Teixeira, Bernadette Cunha Waldvogel, Carlos Roberto Kiffer, Maria Fernanda B de Almeida, Ruth Guinsburg
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveAdolescent pregnancy is a global issue. Early childbearing is strongly linked to poverty and negative health outcomes, including increased neonatal death risk. This study explores spatial patterns of adolescent pregnancies and neonatal deaths and their association with socioeconomic characteristics.DesignThis population-based study used spatial analysis techniques to investigate the geographical distribution of adolescent pregnancies, socioeconomic characteristics and neonatal mortality rate (NMR).SettingThe 645 municipalities of State of Sao Paulo, Brazil.ParticipantsAll live births to mothers residing in the State of Sao Paulo, Brazil, between 2004 and 2020.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe socioeconomic indicators used were: municipal human development index and per capita income (PCI). Spatial patterns were assessed for spatial autocorrelation (Moran’s I, LISA), and smoothed using local Bayesian estimation. Spearman’s correlation was used to ascertain the relationship between the percentage of live births to adolescent mothers and socioeconomic indexes. This calculation was also undertaken between different maternal age groups of NMR.ResultsThe study analysed over 10 million live births, with 14.3% attributed to adolescent mothers. Spatial analysis revealed significant clustering of adolescent pregnancies, strongly associated with lower socioeconomic indicators. NMR also exhibited spatial clustering, particularly after smoothing. Statistically significant differences were observed in PCI medians between high–high and low–low clusters for adolescent births. High and low incidence areas of NMR, both in all maternal ages and stratified by adolescent and non-adolescent mothers, demonstrated considerable overlap.ConclusionThe results indicated the existence of clustering areas of adolescent pregnancy and neonatal deaths and suggested that the prevalence of births to adolescent mothers is not distributed equally and is higher in lower socioeconomic developed areas.

Background

Pregnancy in adolescence represents a significant global issue with the potential to result in a range of social, economic and health-related consequences that are often undesired.13 Despite the overall decline in the number of teenage mothers over the past few decades, low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs) continue with elevated numbers. In 2020, over 97% of all adolescent pregnancies occurred in LMICs.4

Several factors are associated with an elevated probability of teenage pregnancy and the positive correlation between early childbearing and poverty is well-established.2 5 6 Adolescent pregnancy is affected not only by familial circumstances but also by the environmental context of the neighbourhood in which the adolescent resides.6

As adverse results to pregnancy in adolescence, it is worth noting social stigma and school dropout, which can lead to lower educational and financial achievements.3 However, the adverse consequences of adolescent pregnancy are not limited to the mother, with the neonates born to these mothers at an elevated risk of undesirable outcomes, with neonatal death being a significant one.7 8

The prevention of neonatal deaths has been a global agenda for many years, as was seen in the Millennium Development Goals and in more recent years, in the Sustainable Development Goals and in the creation of the Every Newborn Action Plan.911 One of the most effective ways to reduce neonatal mortality rates (NMRs) is to enhance access to and the quality of care for mothers and newborns, particularly in circumstances where there is an elevated risk of unfavourable health outcomes, such as in adolescent mothers.11 12

Therefore, in order to achieve the goals of preventing adolescent pregnancies, providing adequate healthcare for those who do become pregnant and reducing neonatal deaths, it is essential to identify the areas where these pregnancies are more prevalent. Moreover, the identification of the existing distribution of discrepancies in NMR across and within countries represents a crucial initial step in addressing the inadequacies in healthcare provision.12

The aim of this study was to investigate spatially the occurrence of clustering areas of adolescent pregnancy and neonatal deaths and to explore its association with the socioeconomic characteristics of municipalities.

Methods

Study design and participants

This is a population-based study using spatial analysis by area, including all live births to mothers residing in the State of Sao Paulo, Brazil, between 2004 and 2020. Sao Paulo is in the southwest of Brazil, has a population of 44,411,238 according to the 2022 Brazilian Census, and has the highest gross domestic product in the country.13 The state has 645 municipalities with important social and economic inequities.14 The inclusion criteria were to have been born alive in the State of Sao Paulo during the study period and the exclusion criteria were live births with unknown maternal age or unknown municipality of maternal residence.

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Sao Paulo Federal University (protocol number 2,580,929).

Since this study was conducted exclusively with information or data already available in aggregate form, without the possibility of individual identification, informed consent was not required according to Brazilian Regulations (CONEP 466 resolution).

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

Data collection

The adolescent pregnancies and neonatal deaths data for all study years were extracted from databases of death and live births certificates provided by the Fundação Sistema Estadual de Análise de Dados (SEADE Foundation), the official institution responsible for the management of these records in the State of Sao Paulo. This institution encompasses 99.8% and 99.5% of all recorded deaths and births, respectively, within the state.15

The SEADE Foundation performed a deterministic linkage of death and live birth records to identify the birth information for all infants who died within 365 days after birth. A database was constructed by integrating the infant death certificates into the live birth certificates, using the common variables between the two datasets and all deaths considered in the study were linked to birth certificates.16

The following data were extracted from the birth certificate: municipality of maternal residence, maternal age, number of prenatal visits, type of pregnancy, type and location of delivery, time and date of birth, gestational age, birth weight, newborn sex, and presence of congenital anomalies. From death certificates were retrieved the time, date and age of death, and municipality where the infant was born.

The geographic coordinates and spatial files in shape file format (.shp) of the State of Sao Paulo and its respective municipalities were obtained from the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics.17

The measures of socioeconomic characteristics used were the municipal human development index (MHDI) and the per capita income (PCI), which were extracted from different databases for the year 2010, as follows. The MHDI measures human development at the municipal level, ranges from 0 to 1, and consists of three dimensions of human development: longevity, education and income.18 PCI, the total annual income of an area divided by the number of inhabitants of that area, is an economic and social indicator that quantifies the income of each individual within a given population. It is one of the indicators used to assess the development of a region or country.19 The data for these indicators was obtained from national public databases and is for 2010, the closest year to the middle of the study period for which the two indicators were available simultaneously.20 21

Definitions

The following definitions were used: adolescence was defined as the period from 10 to 19 years old, in accordance with the definition provided by the WHO22; neonatal deaths were defined as deaths among live births occurring between 0 and 27 completed days after birth.10

Inadequate prenatal care was defined as having fewer than seven prenatal care visits, in accordance with the recommendations established by the Brazilian Ministry of Health in 2002 and in use for the entire study period.23 This cut-off does not align with the WHO recommended minimum of eight visits since 2016.24 However, this practice is currently in effect in Brazil and has remained unaltered throughout the study period.

The population was stratified into two groups by maternal age: adolescent mothers and non-adolescent mothers. The adolescent mother group was divided into two categories: younger adolescents for mothers aged 10–14 years, and older adolescents for mothers aged 15–19 years.25

NMR was defined as the number of deaths of neonates aged <28 days for every 1000 live births.10 For the purposes of the present study, NMR was divided into two subgroups according to the maternal age of the neonate. The NMR of neonates born to adolescent mothers was defined as the number of neonates born to adolescent mothers who died aged <28 days for every 1000 live births, and the NMR of neonates born to non-adolescent mothers as the number of deaths from neonates born to non-adolescent mothers aged <28 days for every 1000 live births.

Spatial and statistical analysis

First, the geographical database was created, using the municipality unique identifier to link the layers. The result was a single layer combining the complete State of Sao Paulo with each municipality with data of live birth rates, NMR, MHDI and PCI for each municipality. From this layer, different views were generated for exploring the data from each municipality.

Initially, the spatial weights matrix was defined using a first-order Queen contiguity criterion, considering municipalities that shared a common boundary or vertex as neighbours. This matrix was applied to calculate both global and local spatial autocorrelation indicators (Moran’s I and LISA).

Subsequently, maps with quintile distributions were used to visualise the crude rate of live births to adolescent mothers (total group and subgroups), NMR of neonates born to all maternal ages, NMR of neonates born to adolescent mothers, NMR of neonates born to non-adolescent mothers, PCI and MHDI.

First-order effects were explored for each of the aforementioned rates for each municipality using spatial moving average (μi), calculated for each municipality based on rates associated with neighbouring municipalities. This approach was used to identify emerging trends and spatial patterns.

The same rates were explored for each municipality by second-order effects of local spatial dependency and autocorrelation by applying global Moran indicators (I) and Local Indicators of Spatial Association (LISA), which generated LISA cluster (or Moran) maps.26 Clusters were considered if LISA cluster maps demonstrated a correlation of rates between municipalities exceeding the 95% confidence level.

For the rates of live births to adolescent mothers and neonatal mortality, a smoothing technique using a local empirical Bayesian estimator was used to reduce the influence of outlier areas. Subsequently, the global Moran indicators and LISA were used to assess the spatial autocorrelations of the adjusted rates.26

In addition, a visual comparison was implemented of the overlapping areas between live births from adolescent mothers and socioeconomic indexes, as well as between NMR of neonates born to all maternal ages, NMR of neonates born to adolescent mothers and NMR of neonates born to non-adolescent mothers.

Furthermore, Spearman’s correlation coefficient (ρ) was used to calculate the correlations between the percentage of live births to adolescent mothers and PCI and MDHI. This analysis also encompassed the calculation of correlations between NMR of neonates born to all maternal ages, NMR of neonates born to adolescent mothers and NMR of neonates born to non-adolescent mothers. These calculations were performed in accordance with the non-parametric distribution characteristic of the following variables: PCI, live births to younger adolescent mothers and the three groups of NMR.27

All statistical analysis was conducted using the software package Stata/MP V.18.0 (Stata Corp LLC, College Station, TX, USA) and the TerraView software V.5.7.0/2024 (Instituto Nacional de Pesquisas Espaciais, São José dos Campos, Brazil).

Results

From 2004 to 2020, there were 10,265,105 live births in the State of Sao Paulo, Brazil. Of these, 4869 (0.05%) were excluded from the final population of this study due to either unknown maternal age (4347 cases) or unknown maternal municipality of residence (522 cases). The final population of this study consisted of 10,260,236 live births, with 14.3% of the total births attributed to adolescent mothers and 0.6% of the adolescent births occurring to younger adolescent mothers.

During the study period, there were 81,451 neonatal deaths in the State of Sao Paulo (0.79%). Of these, 23 cases were excluded from the study because the infants were born outside Sao Paulo state. Of the neonatal deaths, 18.7% were from neonates born to adolescent mothers.

The maternal and neonatal characteristics were presented in two groups based on maternal age. Adolescent mothers were more likely to receive inadequate prenatal care and were less likely to have multiple gestations. Infants born to adolescent mothers exhibited elevated rates of low birth weight and prematurity (table 1).

Discussion

The present study showed clusters of adolescent pregnancies, particularly in the South, Southwest and Northwest regions of the State of Sao Paulo. Additionally, the study illustrated the existence of notable disparities in socioeconomic indicators across the state, with discernible clusters of high or low values for the two indicators used. Moreover, a visual comparison of the maps of the prevalence of births to adolescent mothers and socioeconomic indicators revealed an inverse association between prevalence of births to adolescent mothers and PCI or MDHI status. In accordance with these findings, the percentage of live births to adolescent mothers exhibited an inverse significant relationship with the PCI or MDHI, as determined by Spearman’s correlation coefficient.

Global and national data demonstrate an association between an increased prevalence of adolescent pregnancies and a lower socioeconomic status, in accordance with the findings of the present study.4 28

In Brazil, an upper middle-income country with a continental demographic profile, the prevalence of adolescent pregnancy varies, with regions of lower socioeconomic development having higher rates.28 29 Martinez and Roza used spatial analysis to assess the relationship between the distribution of adolescent births in Brazil and the human development index, with similar conclusions.30 This negative correlation between a country’s income and the number of adolescent mothers is a pattern also observed globally.4

The reasons that contribute to a pregnancy during adolescence are numerous and the aforementioned relationship with lower socioeconomic status is well documented as a significant one.246 However, a few other factors associated with early onset of sexual activity and the subsequent risk of early childbearing are worthy of mention. These include the lack of an adequate parental support, the presence of a single-parent family structure, adolescent poor educational performance, low self-esteem and risky behaviours such as substance and alcohol abuse.5 31 Furthermore, a family history of teenage pregnancy plays a significant role in perpetuating the cycle of poverty and inadequate family support, which, in turn, increases the likelihood that a child born to an adolescent mother will become an adolescent mother herself.1 31

In addition, it is important to highlight the influence of the community in shaping individual behaviour.32 The characteristics of the neighbourhood in which an adolescent resides have been found to have an independent association with the likelihood of that adolescent becoming pregnant.5 Neighbourhoods exhibiting social disorganisation (elevated rates of crime, unemployment and lack of adult supervision), coupled with a paucity of resources for child development (such as playgrounds, childcare facilities, parks and after-school programmes), are associated with elevated rates of teenage pregnancies.31 33

This study had some other important results. It suggested that there was a spatial dependence for NMR and for studied subgroups of NMR (NMR among neonates born to adolescent mothers and NMR among neonates born to non-adolescent mothers) before smoothing techniques were applied. However, it was only after applying local Bayesian estimation that the second-order effects showed a significant global Moran index. This allowed the identification of clusters more precisely. This application as a smoothing technique to address random spatial fluctuations in the mortality data was crucial, given that neonatal deaths are often considered rare occurrences, with numerous municipalities reporting no cases. Prior to the implementation of this approach, the presence of outliers served to dilute the effects of the identified clusters.26 34

A discrepancy was identified in NMR across the 645 municipalities under investigation, and on visual comparison between maps of NMR and the studied subgroups of NMR, it was observed that, in most cluster areas, there was an overlap between regions with high and low incidences of those rates. Furthermore, the Spearman correlation revealed a positive and significant relationship between NMR and the studied subgroups of NMR, thereby corroborating the outcomes of the spatial analysis.

The spatial analysis of the general distribution of NMR within the State of Sao Paulo was previously performed for a 5-year period, comparing macro-regions instead of municipalities, with the clusters identified showing some similarity with the cluster found in this study.35

The overlapping clusters of NMR and the studied subgroups of NMR in this study, as well as the results of the Spearman correlation, are indicative of the fact that the phenomenon of elevated NMR among neonates born to adolescent mothers in a particular region is directly associated with the same underlying factors that contribute to the overall high NMR of the region.

In fact, most neonatal deaths are due to preventable and treatable conditions, including neonatal infections, birth asphyxia and complications associated with prematurity.11 12 In addition, quality of care is directly related to neonatal outcomes, and according to UNICEF, 61% of neonatal deaths are due to poor quality of care.36

Improving neonatal survival has been a global goal for decades, and good results have been achieved.911 However, the reduction in NMR has been uneven between and within countries, with the poorest areas falling short of targets. Sub-Saharan Africa has the highest NMR, about 11 times higher than in the region with the lowest mortality, Australia and New Zealand.37

A critical step in reducing preventable neonatal deaths is to improve access to and quality of care for mothers and newborns, especially during the perinatal period, including pregnancy, childbirth and the newborn’s first week of life.11 12 In addition, it is imperative to ensure that every woman and newborn is reached to reduce inequities and improve health assistance globally.11 Community and primary care-based strategies are an essential component of a successful approach. It is imperative that inequities across and within countries be addressed as a matter of priority, with investments aimed at achieving equilibrium and strengthening the overall system.12 It is important to acknowledge the significance of these strategies and recommendations in the context of adolescent pregnancy, as it has been well-established that adolescent pregnancy is a risk factor for neonatal death.3 8

In this context, it is reasonable to assume that an essential preliminary step in achieving the global goals of reducing adolescent pregnancy and neonatal deaths is to identify discrepancies in their existing distribution. The use of geographical information systems and spatial analysis can facilitate a more localised assessment of these problems. Maps provide an additional level of insight into data analysis, facilitating the visualisation of complex patterns and relationships within public health issues.38 39 Furthermore, this facilitates the formulation of evidence-based public health strategies to better approach these events.

The present study is not without its limitations. The most significant of these was the use of data from a single year for socioeconomic indicators, which was a consequence of the absence of data for the indicators used in the other years of the study. This is the result of the fact that, within the context of Brazil, it is noteworthy that data at the municipal level is only available with significant time spans. However, given that the year under consideration was almost midway through the study period, it is considered that the data used offer insight into the socioeconomic landscape of the period. Also, spatial analyses were used to detect geographical patterns and socioeconomic associations; however, the methods applied do not allow for conclusions on the spatial dependence structure between these variables.

A second and an important limitation of this study was that temporal variation was not considered in the spatial analysis. The aggregation of data over the 16-year period, while intended to stabilise rates and strengthen spatial cluster detection, precludes the assessment of temporal trends and may ignore a second source of correlation. As a result, CIs may be narrower than expected, potentially leading to overestimation of statistical significance.

Another limitation is the use of municipalities as the unit of analysis, which may have masked the spatial disparities that exist within municipalities.

Despite these problems, to our knowledge, this is the first approach to the distribution of NMR within municipalities in the State of Sao Paulo and the first study to use spatial analysis to evaluate the distribution of NMR among neonates born to adolescent mothers. The analytical process used in this study can be replicated in other geographical areas, and the resulting data can be used to guide public health policies with the objective of reducing adolescent pregnancy and NMR, aligned with sustainable development goals targets for maternal and child health.

In conclusion, the evidence presented in this study indicates the existence of clustering areas of adolescent pregnancy and neonatal deaths. Also, the results suggested that the prevalence of births to adolescent mothers is not distributed equally and is higher in lower socioeconomic developed areas. Moreover, in the State of Sao Paulo, the concentration of NMR in neonates born to adolescent mothers is analogous to that of general mortality, indicating that the quality of care is an important factor in understanding and addressing the issue.
