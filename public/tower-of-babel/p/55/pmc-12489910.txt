Patterns and causes of supranuclear vertical gaze palsy: A retrospective, single-institutional study in 113 patients
Authors: Daniela Kern, Katharina Raber, Gerit Wünsch, Petra Schwingenschuh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights:•The most common causes of SVGP are vascular and Parkinsonian disorders.•Downward SVGP is predominantly found in PSP.•Upward SVGP has diverse causes: e.g. neurodegenerative, inflammatory, congenital.

Introduction

Supranuclear vertical gaze palsy (SVGP) is characterized by an impairment of conjugate vertical eye movements due to lesions affecting the supranuclear pathways. Its differential diagnosis is broad and includes vascular, neurodegenerative, metabolic, autoimmune, inflammatory and neoplastic disorders. The onset of SVGP can be acute, as in midbrain stroke and hemorrhage, or chronic, as in movement disorders or metabolic diseases.

Assessment of SVGP may also be helpful in the differentiation of Parkinsonian disorders. Although SVGP is a hallmark symptom of progressive supranuclear gaze palsy (PSP), it can manifest in multisystem atrophy (MSA), corticobasal syndrome, and spinocerebellar ataxias (SCA)[[1], [2], [3], [4]]. Moreover, a moderate restriction in the upward gaze is commonly seen among elderly persons and patients with Parkinson's disease [5,6]. A restriction of the downward gaze, however, is a typical sign for SVGP in PSP [2,4]. Furthermore, oculomotor apraxia and non-supranuclear gaze palsy like external ophthalmoplegia can mimic SVGP [7].

The cerebral regions responsible for vertical eye movements are located primarily in the upper brainstem and include the rostral interstitial nucleus of the medial longitudinal fasciculus, the interstitial nucleus of Cajal, the posterior commissure and the medial longitudinal fasciculus [3,8]. Consequently, SVGP is a symptom of lesions of the upper brain stem, in particular the dorsal midbrain. Depending on the location and extent of the damage, SVGP may also coexist with other eye movement disorders such as convergence paresis, nystagmus, pupil abnormalities, or skew deviation.

The bedside oculomotor examination constitutes an essential part of the routine neurological examination and may be helpful not only in the differential diagnosis but also in localizing the lesion. It should include ocular misalignment, range of eye motion, involuntary eye movements, nystagmus, saccades, smooth pursuit, the vestibulo-ocular reflex (VOR), and vergence eye movements [1].

In chronic conditions associated with SVGP, the earliest clinical manifestation is typically a slowing of vertical saccades, which precedes overt vertical gaze restriction. Particularly in the early stages of the disease, SVGP can often be overcome by the VOR in contrast to infranuclear and peripheral gaze palsies [2,9].

This retrospective cohort study analyzed patients presenting with SVGP to our neurology department, aiming to (1) characterize the spectrum and frequency of potential etiologies and (2) identify concomitant clinical features associated with SVGP phenotypes.

Methods

We conducted a single-center retrospective study based on the electronic data collection of patients with SVGP in inpatient and outpatient care in the University Hospital for Neurology Graz between January 2012 and June 2022. Before collecting the data, we carried out a literature review to identify disorders associated with SVGP and potential differential diagnosis. On the basis of this literature review, we selected search terms to identify all patients with SVGP and mimics of SVGP in the electronic data collection system. The following terms were included in the search: “vertical saccadic dysfunction”, “upward saccadic dysfunction”, “downward saccadic dysfunction”, “dorsal midbrain syndrome”, “pretectal syndrome”, “Parinaud syndrome”, “PSP”, “progressive supranuclear gaze palsy”, “Richardson syndrome”, “Niemann-Pick”, “Gaucher”, “IGLON5″. We then analyzed the medical records of all filtered patients for diagnoses and clinical characteristics.

SVGP was diagnosed by a neurologist on bedside examination. Additional structured neuro-ophthalmologic assessments were available in 30 patients. Only patients with upward, downward or bidirectional SVGP were included in the analysis. Patients with reduced saccadic velocity alone were excluded. Although this usually precedes SVGP, it might have been overlooked or misinterpreted during bedside examination due to the absence of standardized testing. Furthermore, all non-supranuclear etiologies of vertical gaze palsy, i.e. external ophthalmoplegia or oculomotor apraxia, were excluded (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Flowchart of patient inclusion and exclusion.

Statistics

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 27.0, SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL). We used absolute and relative frequencies to compare groups. Assumptions for normal distributions were tested using the Shapiro-Wilk-Test. Group differences were analyzed using the Chi-square test or Fisher's exact test, independent samples t-test and Mann-Whitney-U test, depending on the distribution of the data. Correlation analyses were performed using the Spearman's rank correlation coefficient. P-values < 0.05 were considered significant.

Ethics committee

The study protocol was reviewed and accepted by the institutional review board of the Medical University of Graz. This study conforms with the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

The study sample consisted of 113 patients with an age range between 29 and 91 years (mean age: 67.5 years; SD: 14). Of these, 58.4 % were male (n = 66) and 41.6 % were female (n = 47). In addition to neurological assessments, ophthalmological evaluations were available for 30 patients (26.5 %).

The most common etiologies of SVGP were Parkinsonian disorders, accounting for 44.2 % of cases (n = 50), and vascular disorders, accounting for 38.1 % (n = 43). The remaining 17.7 % (n = 20) comprised ataxia syndromes (n = 2), other neurodegenerative disorders (n = 8), autoimmune and inflammatory disorders (n = 3), lysosomal storage diseases (n = 2), neoplastic lesions (n = 3) and others (n = 2) (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Etiologies: Frequency distribution of etiologies of supranuclear vertical gaze palsy of all included patients (n = 113).

Parkinsonian disorders

The mean age at onset of SVGP in patients with atypical Parkinsonian syndromes including progressive supranuclear gaze palsy (PSP), multiple system atrophy Parkinsonian type (MSA-P) and corticobasal syndrome (CBS) was 73 years (SD 10). This was younger than in patients with Parkinson's disease, who had a mean age of onset of SVGP of 79 years (SD 12). However, this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.077).

Within the SVGP cohort, a higher proportion of females than males was observed among patients with PD (female: n = 5; male: n = 2), whereas atypical Parkinsonian syndromes were more common in males (female: n = 11; male: n = 30). This sex distribution difference was statistically significant (p = 0.033). In patients with Parkinsonian disorders, SVGP predominantly affecting downward gaze was more prevalent (n = 28) than SVGP affecting only upward gaze (n = 16). In five patients the direction of the SVGP was not specified, so these patients were excluded from further analysis. While all patients with downward SVGP had a diagnosis of atypical parkinsonism, patients with upward SVGP alone were more likely to have a diagnosis of PD (n = 5, 71.4 % of PD patients) than atypical parkinsonism (n = 11, 26.8 % of atypical parkinsonism patients) (Fig. 3). This association was statistically significant (p = 0.004). Other oculomotor symptoms in patients with Parkinsonian disorders and SVGP were horizontal gaze palsy (n = 5), oculomotor apraxia (n = 2), nystagmus (n = 1), ptosis (n = 2) and intranuclear ophthalmoplegia (n = 1). Two of the three patients with MSA-P had upward SVGP and one had downward SVGP. The patient with CBS had downward SVGP. Neither the patients with MSA-P nor with CBS had other oculomotor symptoms. Vascular disorders

In both ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke, the main localization of the lesion was the mesencephalon (n = 20). Other localizations were the thalami (n = 10), the pons (n = 2) and the cerebellum (n = 3). Four patients had a cerebral small vessel disease resulting in SVGP with (n = 2) and without (n = 2) vascular parkinsonism.

The mean age of patients with ischemic stroke was 64 years (SD 14) and the mean age of patients with hemorrhagic stroke was 56 years (SD 13) (p = 0.110). Patients with hemorrhagic stroke and SVGP were predominantly male (female: n = 1, male: n = 9), in contrast to ischemic stroke, which showed a balanced ratio between males and females (f: n = 14, m: n = 15). However, this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.061).

In patients with vascular disorders, SVGP predominantly affecting upward gaze was more prevalent (n = 35) than SVGP affecting downward gaze (n = 3). The underlying pathologies of patients with downward SVGP were acute basilar artery thrombosis, intracerebral bleeding in the mesencephalon with hydrocephalus and severe vascular parkinsonism. The direction of SVGP was not associated with the type of vascular disorder (hemorrhagic vs. ischemic) (p = 0.421). In four patients the direction of SVGP was not mentioned, therefore these patients were excluded from this analysis. Associated oculomotor symptoms in patients with vascular disorders and SVGP were nystagmus (n = 14), monocular palsy (n = 9), skew deviation (n = 7), intranuclear ophthalmoplegia (n = 2), ptosis (n = 2) and anisocoria (n = 1).

Other diseases

Predominantly upward SVGP was observed in all patients with ataxia syndromes (n = 2), Gaucher disease (n = 1), hypoxic encephalopathy (n = 1), normal pressure hydrocephalus (n = 1), Alzheimer’s disease (n = 2), multiple sclerosis (n = 2), anti-IgLON5 disease (n = 1), neoplastic lesions (n = 3) and in one of two patients with Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease (CJD). In contrast, downward SVGP was observed in the second patient with CJD, in the patient with Niemann Pick disease type C (NPC), the patient with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) and both patients with frontotemporal dementia (FTD). Additional horizontal gaze palsy was present in the patient with anti-IgLON5 disease, as well as in one patient each with CJD (50 %) and FTD (50 %), one patient with a neoplastic lesion (33.3 %) and the patient with congenital gaze palsy (See supplementary material for all oculomotor symptoms in this group).

The patient with CJD and downward SVGP and the two patients with FTD exhibited hallmark symptoms of PSP in the neurological examination, mainly axial rigidity and postural instability. The patient with CJD and upward SVGP did not show any PSP-like symptoms, but ataxia and myoclonus.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort of 113 patients presenting with SVGP, we provide a comprehensive overview of the etiological spectrum, demographic characteristics, and associated clinical features. Our findings underscore the heterogeneity of SVGP and highlight important diagnostic considerations for clinicians.

Consistent with previous reports, parkinsonian disorders − particularly PSP − emerged as the most frequent cause of SVGP, accounting for nearly half of all cases in our cohort. Vascular etiologies, predominantly ischemic and hemorrhagic strokes involving the mesencephalon and thalami, represented the second most common group. Because of their typically acute onset, they can usually be distinguished from neurodegenerative causes. This aligns with the known anatomical substrates of vertical gaze control and emphasizes the importance of careful neuroimaging in the diagnostic workup of SVGP.

The mean age at onset and the sex distribution varied across etiological groups. Notably, patients with atypical Parkinsonian syndromes developed SVGP at a younger age compared to those with PD, although this difference did not reach statistical significance. The observed male predominance in atypical parkinsonian syndromes may reflect underlying disease epidemiology, given that some studies have found PSP to be more common in men. However, the data is inconsistent with some smaller studies also describing and equal sex distribution or a slight female predominance [[10], [11], [12]]. Similar to PD [13], estrogen might have a protective effect against developing PSP [14]. Notably, PD is slightly more common in males too. However, a systematic review on PD prevalence reported a significant male predominance only in the age group 50 to 59 years. Among patients older than 60 years, no significant sex difference was observed [15]. As the mean age of patients with PD in our cohort is 79 years, we do not expect a sex difference. Nonetheless, because of the small sample size the observed female predominance in our cohort may also be influenced by referral or selection bias.

Regarding the direction of SVGP, upward SVGP had a relatively broad spectrum of differential diagnoses, whereas predominantly downward SVGP was most commonly found in patients with atypical parkinsonism, specifically in PSP. The presence of additional ocular motor symptoms supported the diagnosis of PSP.

The key anatomical structures involved in the vertical gaze control are the rostral interstitial medial longitudinal fasciculus (riMLF), the interstitial nucleus of Cajal (INC), and the posterior commissure (PC). Because these nuclei are situated in a very compact area of the midbrain, combined oculomotor deficits are common. The riMLF plays a central role in generating vertical saccades. It projects bilaterally to the elevator muscles, but unilaterally to the depressor muscles. As a result, partial lesions more often lead to downward gaze palsy.

In PSP neurodegeneration typically involves the riMLF, leading primarily to downward gaze palsy. The INC is essential for vertical gaze-holding and projects to vertical motoneurons via the posterior commissure (PC). An inactivation of the INC results in a restricted range of vertical eye movements. Lesions affecting the dorsal riMLF and the PC primarily result in upward gaze palsy. Given their anatomical location within the midbrain, vascular lesion and obstructive hydrocephalus are among the most frequent underlying causes of the dorsal midbrain syndrome [3,8,16].

In retrospect, three patients (one with CBS, two with FTD) of our cohort should be reclassified as PSP. The patient with CBS exhibited unilateral limb apraxia, but also SVGP predominantly affecting downgaze. Therefore, he can be diagnosed with possible PSP with predominant CBS (PSP-CBS). 20 % of patients with PSP-CBS show SVGP in early stages of the disease and 50 % overall [18]. Moreover, patients with CBS and corticobasal degeneration (CBD) can also develop SVGP, but usually in later stages of the disease [17,18].

Two patients of our cohort were diagnosed with frontotemporal dementia (FTD). Although they had FTD as their primary diagnoses, they also fulfilled the criteria for probable PSP with predominant frontal presentation (PSP-F), as they presented with downward SVGP in the first years of their disease. Both, the behavioral variant of FTD and primary progressive aphasia can be a clinical feature of PSP-F as well as frontotemporal lobar degeneration (FTLD) [19]. Conversely, in FTLD SVGP may develop over the course of the disease, but typically not within the first three years [20].

In conclusion, the presence of SVGP, particularly in the early stages of the disease, is a strong indicator of PSP, even if a different etiology is initially suspected. Consequently, longitudinal neurological reassessment is necessary in order to make the diagnosis.

Despite being the hallmark symptom of PSP, SVGP can also present in other diseases that mimic PSP. In our study 18 patients (15.9 %) did not exhibit Parkinsonian or vascular disorders as underlying etiologies. The majority of these patients had upward SVGP, whereas only three presented with downgaze involvement. The corresponding diagnoses for downward SVGP were CJD, ALS, and NPC. The patient with CJD additionally exhibited horizontal gaze palsy and a PSP-like phenotype characterized by parkinsonism and postural instability, a constellation that has been reported as a rare manifestation of CJD [21]. The patient with ALS had a bulbar onset of the disease but did not display any additional PSP-like symptoms. Although ALS only rarely imitates PSP and eye movements are typically preserved until very late, there are cases with early SVGP in ALS with bulbar onset [22,23]. In contrast, downward SVGP is a hallmark symptom of NPC and usually occurs early in the course of the disease. The patient with NPC in our cohort presented with up- and downward SVGP, which usually develops as the disease progresses [2,24].

Although this study provides an oversight of differential diagnosis and the characteristics of SVGP that can be seen in neurological patients, it has several limitations. First, it was a single center, retrospective analysis and the relatively small numbers in some diagnostic subgroups may have limited the statistical power to detect additional associations. Secondly, only 30 patients were seen by an ophthalmologist or had an orthoptic evaluation. Most patients were only examined by a neurologist. Because of the retrospective design, there was no examination protocol and the quality of the oculomotor evaluation varied. In ten patients (Parkinsonian disorders: n = 5, vascular disorders: n = 4, others: n = 1) SVGP was only mentioned without a detailed description of directionality or associated oculomotor findings. Therefore, these patients had to be excluded from further analysis. The absence of comprehensive assessment in ten patients may have biased the results toward more classic PSP-presentations, which are generally easier to recognize. Furthermore, the lack of confirmatory genetic testing in some cases of suspected hereditary ataxia may have introduced classification bias. Studies that incorporate both clinical and biologically confirmed diagnosis are warranted. This includes autopsy-based studies as well as future biomarker studies, once such biomarkers are available.

Conclusion

In summary, SVGP is a clinically and etiologically heterogeneous syndrome. Parkinsonian and vascular disorders are the most common causes, but a wide range of other neurodegenerative, inflammatory, congenital, and neoplastic conditions must be considered. While upward SVGP has a relatively broad differential diagnosis, downward SVGP is predominantly found in patients with PSP. However, atypical presentations and mimics of PSP highlight the importance of careful characterization of oculomotor findings, in conjunction with neuroimaging and ancillary testing. Future prospective studies with standardized oculomotor assessments including video-oculography, larger sample sizes, and biomarker-supported diagnosis are warranted to further elucidate the diagnostic and prognostic significance of SVGP phenotypes.
