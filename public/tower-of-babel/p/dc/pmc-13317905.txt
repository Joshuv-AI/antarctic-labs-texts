Open Pelvic Ring Injury with Extended Morel-Lavallée Lesion Complicated by Fungal Infection Necessitating Hemipelvectomy
Authors: Vera Jaecker, Sven Märdian, Julius Krüger, Wenzel Schöning, Philipp Pickerodt, Martina Gaßner, Ulrich Stöckle, Marcel Niemann, Tazio Maleitzke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Case:A 30-year-old woman sustained a complex open pelvic ring injury with urogenital and rectal injuries and a severe Morel-Lavallée lesion after getting stuck between a train platform and an arriving train. Orthopaedic trauma management included extensive debridements, temporary internal and external fixation, staged plate osteosynthesis, and negative-pressure wound therapy. Progressive soft-tissue necrosis due to mucormycosis necessitated a hemipelvectomy. At the 1-year follow-up, the patient had returned to independent living.Conclusion:Complex open peripelvic injuries, complicated by mucormycosis infection and subsequent hemipelvectomy, have a high risk of mortality. We outline multidisciplinary surgical and antimicrobial strategies potentially effective in similar scenarios.

Open pelvic ring injuries are rare, but debilitating injuries, associated with significant morbidity and mortality1. As part of the often-complex soft-tissue trauma, Morel-Lavallée lesions (MLL) can occur, and the risk of septic complications increases drastically when pelvic ring injuries are accompanied by MLL2.

MLL are internal degloving injuries often caused by high-energy trauma3,4. Tangential forces separate the skin and subcutaneous tissue from the underlying fascia, disrupting perforating blood and lymphatic vessels, which results in hemolymphatic fluid accumulation5.

We present the case of a complex open pelvic ring injury complicated by a severe MLL and fulminant mucormycosis infection, necessitating a hemipelvectomy and extensive soft-tissue reconstruction. The patient survived this life-threatening injury and subsequent complications which have previously only been reported as fatal6, and returned to independent living.

The patient was informed that data concerning the case would be submitted for publication, and she provided consent.

Case Report

While crossing train tracks, a 30-year-old woman got stuck at waist level between a train platform and an arriving train.

On initial assessment, she was breathing spontaneously but was hemodynamically unstable (90/60 mm Hg) with a Glasgow Coma Scale of 13. Initial treatment included 1 L of saline, ketamine, and midazolam. A pelvic binder was applied due to a suspected unstable open pelvic injury.

During the primary survey (level I trauma center), the patient developed ventricular fibrillation requiring 12 minutes of cardiopulmonary resuscitation until return of spontaneous circulation. A left-sided tension pneumothorax and right-sided pneumothorax were treated with bilateral chest tubes. Extensive soft-tissue injuries of the pelvis, urogenital and rectal regions, and both thighs suggested severe MLL (Figs. 1-A and 1-B). Radiographs showed bilateral C-type pelvic fractures and a left subtrochanteric femur fracture (Fig. 1-C). Focused Assessment with Sonography for Trauma revealed minimal intraperitoneal fluid. Owing to persistent hemodynamic instability, further diagnostics were interrupted and the patient was transferred to the emergency operating room (Fig. 2-A) for hemorrhage control.

Pelvic stabilization was achieved using a pelvic C-clamp (posterior) and a hip-spanning supraacetabular-femoral external fixator (anterior). Additional temporary K-wire fixation of the pelvic ring was necessary for stability (Figs. 2-B and 2-C). A median laparotomy excluded intraperitoneal gross bleeding but revealed extensive retroperitoneal trauma. A bladder rupture was sutured, and the ruptured urethra was catheterized. A protective ileostomy, double-barrel sigmoidostomy, and distal rectal closure were performed due to a rectal rupture. Extraperitoneal pelvic packing and provisional soft-tissue reconstruction completed damage control surgery on postadmission day (PAD) 0. Polytrauma diagnostics were completed (Fig. 2-C), without revealing further relevant injuries. On PAD 1, definitive fixation of the anterior pelvic ring was performed using a 16-hole reconstruction plate. Early stabilization was chosen to provide mechanical support for a staged pelvic floor reconstruction. Following extensive debridement, the vagina was reconstructed and negative pressure wound therapy (NPWT) of the urogenital and perineal regions was initiated. The patient was extubated on PAD 2 and was alert, fully oriented, and able to participate in treatment decisions. On PAD 7, invasive mucormycosis (Mucor circinelloides) was diagnosed based on microbiological sampling (deep tissue cultures), prompted by clinical and paraclinical signs of progressing infection (Figs. 3-A, 3-B and 3-C). A partial sacrectomy, resection of the vagina, atypical dorsal hysterectomy, and resection of the left proximal femur were performed on PAD 8 (Fig. 3-D), to limit septic progression.

PAD 7. Extensive peripelvic skin necrosis (lateral [Fig. 3-A], ventral [Fig. 3-B], and dorsal [Fig. 3-C]). Internal fixation of the anterior pelvic ring with a 16-hole reconstruction plate, partial sacrectomy and resection of proximal femur on PAD 8 (Fig. 3-D). PAD = post-admission day.

We performed daily serial debridements and NPWT dressing changes (Figs. 4-A through 4-D and 5-A, 5-B and 5-C). Antifungal and antibiotic therapies were adjusted according to microbiological findings (Fig. 6). Despite aggressive surgical and antifungal management, infection progressed to the gluteal muscles, left hemipelvis, and iliac vessels. Given the angioinvasive nature of the infection and ongoing tissue necrosis, a hemipelvectomy was performed on PAD 13 following a multidisciplinary case conference and consultation with the patient and her family. Hardware removal was considered but not pursued due to the aggressive nature of the fungal infection that had extended beyond the bone and plate osteosynthesis.

Regular multidisciplinary case conferences guided treatment adaptations.

After 6 weeks, the patient was able to mobilize using forearm crutches. She was fitted with a hemipelvectomy prosthesis but primarily used crutches or a wheelchair (Figs. 9-A, 9-B and 9-C). On PAD 55, she was discharged to a rehabilitation facility with oral antibiotic and antifungal therapy (Fig. 6). At the 1-year follow-up, the patient had moved back to her shared apartment on the fourth floor and resumed her university education and career.

Discussion

This case illustrates therapeutic challenges associated with complex open pelvic ring injuries combined with MLL and severe urogenital and rectal trauma. Despite further complications including an angioinvasive mucormycosis infection and a subsequent hemipelvectomy, such an injury is potentially survivable.

Unstable pelvic ring injuries are associated with lower 1-year mortality when treated at level I trauma centers7. Nevertheless, open pelvic ring injuries carry high rates of surgical site infection and mortality8, particularly when sustained in combination with MLL2. Although such injuries are rare9, available evidence suggests improved clinical outcomes following extensive debridement, dead-space reduction, NPWT, and skeletal stabilization compared with conservative treatment10. In the present case, early definitive stabilization on PAD 1 was chosen to provide structural support for a staged pelvic floor reconstruction and due to a lack of feasible alternatives. To date, evidence for the optimal timing to fix open pelvic injuries is lacking11.

MLL result in vascular disruption and compromised tissue perfusion, predisposing to infection12. Mucormycosis is a potentially lethal angioinvasive fungal infection caused by Mucorales species commonly found in soil and decaying organic matter13. Mucormycosis infections typically affect immunosuppressed patients14, yet cases following severe trauma in otherwise healthy patients have been reported15,16. Treatments include aggressive surgical debridements and intravenous amphotericin B14. Mortality rates remain however high, with approximately 40% with deep tissue involvement and up to 70% when disseminated. Mortality rates decrease when surgery and antifungal therapy are combined17.

To our knowledge, only one other case has been published describing a mucormycosis infection secondary to a peripelvic MLL in a 22-year-old patient following a railway accident in India. The injury pattern appeared similar to our case, yet the patient died of sepsis on PAD 6. The open book injury was initially treated conservatively and treatment was delayed by secondary transfer to a trauma center6. Differences in health care settings limit direct comparisons. Yet early surgical and multidisciplinary management may have contributed to the favorable outcome in the present case.

Hemipelvectomy was performed on PAD 13 as the mucormycosis infection progressed. By then, the patient had received amphotericin B and daily debridements for nearly a week. The remaining myocutaneous tissue from the amputated leg used for soft-tissue reconstruction appeared viable and noninfected. Yet, contamination of flaps and grafts cannot be excluded.

In a case series of 14 hemipelvectomies in 13 patients with combat-related injuries, 5 hemipelvectomies were performed due to angioinvasive fungal infections. The surviving patients (11/13) required an average of 44 operations and had a median hospital stay of 113 days. Only 1 patient regained community ambulation, which was partly explained by the fact that the other 12 patients had sustained contralateral lower extremity amputations18. In contrast to our case, none of the described cases18 suffered from an MLL. In our case, a total of 17 operations were required with a combined surgery time of 54 hours and an admission time of 56 days.

Split-thickness skin grafts harvested from the refrigerated amputated limb healed without complication and avoided donor-site morbidity. Experimental data indicate that such grafts may be safely stored for up to 1 week under refrigeration19.

Long-term psychological impacts of major physical trauma combined with severe septic complications and a subsequent hemipelvectomy are difficult to assess. In a small retrospective case series of patients who underwent limb-preserving surgery or amputation for traumatic hemipelvectomies, quality-of-life scores after a mean of 12 years were better in patients who underwent amputation20. Our patient received early support from a clinical psychologist. There is evidence that early psychological interventions reduce post-traumatic stress, anxiety, and depression in critically ill ICU patients21. The patient had regular and scheduled visits from family and friends, a concept that may help reduce physiological stress in critically ill patients22.

Conclusion

This case exemplifies the relevance of specialized trauma care that can typically only be provided at level I trauma centers. With a multidisciplinary treatment approach, regular case discussions, and situation-based adaptations, survival and social reintegration are possible following complex and potentially lethal injuries and make all the efforts worth it.
