Effects of perovskite thickness and electrode selection on carrier transport and performance in hybrid transparent-electrode perovskite solar cells
Authors: Dessalegne A. Tsehay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have attracted considerable attention because of their excellent light-harvesting capability and efficient charge transport characteristics. In this theoretical study, the effects of absorber thickness (300–800 nm) and the selection of front and back contact electrodes on the performance of PSCs were investigated based on Poisson electrostatics, drift–diffusion transport, and carrier recombination theory. Four device architectures, namely ITO/perovskite/Al, Al/perovskite/Cu2S, graphene/perovskite/Cu2S, and Cu2S/perovskite/graphene, were analyzed. The results reveal an optimum absorber thickness of 600–700 nm, where the balance between optical absorption and carrier transport maximizes device efficiency. Among the investigated structures, Cu2S/perovskite/graphene exhibited the highest power conversion efficiency of 26.7%, followed by graphene/perovskite/Cu2S (24.3%), Al/perovskite/Cu2S (20.7%), and ITO/perovskite/Al (17.4%). The enhanced performance of graphene-based devices is attributed to improved charge extraction and reduced recombination losses. These findings highlight the importance of absorber thickness optimization and contact engineering for high-efficiency PSC design.

Introduction

As global energy demand continues to rise, interest in renewable energy sources has increased as a means of meeting future energy needs. Furthermore, accelerating climate change driven by greenhouse gas emissions has made the transition to low-carbon, renewable energy sources increasingly urgent.1 Renewable energy sources such as wind, geothermal, solar, and biomasses are central to sustainable energy development. Among these, solar energy is particularly abundant; the solar radiation reaching the earth's surface exceeds total energy consumption by more than 10 000 times, making it a highly promising resource for meeting future energy demands.2 To harvest solar energy efficiently and at low cost, new photovoltaic material must be developed.

Perovskite solar cells have emerged as one of the most promising photovoltaic technologies due to their high absorption coefficient, long carrier diffusion length and tunable bandgap.3–5 Their high absorption and efficient charge generation make perovskite a premier candidate for next generation thin-film photovoltaics. Their remarkable optoelectrical properties enable efficient light harvesting and charge separation, resulting in rapid improvement in device performance over the past decade. Recent advances have further demonstrated the importance of carrier localization phenomena and minority-carrier lifetime enhancement in semiconductor heterostructures for improving charge-collection efficiency and suppressing recombination losses.14 In addition, defect passivation strategies have proven highly effective in reducing trap-assisted recombination and improving long-term device stability.15 Simultaneously, advances in optical absorption engineering and light–matter interaction control have enabled more effective photon utilization and enhanced photogenerated carrier production.16 These developments collectively reinforce the potential of perovskite materials as a premier platform for high-efficiency thin-film photovoltaic technologies.

Beyond providing electrical conduction pathways, these materials significantly influence charge-transfer processes, carrier transport kinetics, and interfacial electronic properties.17 Recent studies on hybrid transport conductive electrodes have shown that electrode semiconducting behavior and charge-related functional properties can substantially modify charge extraction and transport efficiency.18 Likewise, investigations of ultrathin ion gel-based device architecture and interface-engineering structures have revealed the critical role of interfacial transport mechanisms in determining device performance.19 Theoretical analyses of semiconductor surface interactions further indicate that interface states, charge redistribution, and surface-induced electric fields can strongly influence carrier dynamics and transport behavior across heterojunction interfaces.20

However, device performance is not determined solely by the perovskite absorber layer. It also depends strongly on the materials used as contacts and charge-transport layers, which influence how efficiently charge carriers are extracted and how much charge recombination occurs within the device.6–8 In a typical perovskite solar cell, the absorber layer is placed between two contacts that collect and transport charge carriers. Common contact materials include metals such as Al, transparent conductive materials such as ITO, carbon-based materials such as graphene, and P-type semiconductors such as Cu2S.

These electrodes introduce distinct work functions that directly control band alignment and electrostatic potential distribution across the device. As a result, the built-in potential and internal electric field profile governs charge separation, drift-diffusion transport, and carrier collection efficiency. At the same time, interfacial band misalignment can induce strong band bending, carrier accumulation, and enhanced recombination losses, which reduce the overall power conversion efficiency.9–11 Despite extensive experimental and numerical progress, a unified analytical framework connecting fundamental electrode properties macroscopic device efficiency remains elusive. This gap motivates the development of a comprehensive physics-based model-coupling Poisson's equation with drift-diffusion transport and recombination theory- to systematically analyze multi-electrode perovskite heterostructure.12,13

To address this challenge, the present work develops a unified drift–diffusion model for asymmetric heterojunction configurations formed by graphene, ITO, Cu2S, and Al electrodes on either side of the perovskite absorber. The framework provides a self-consistent description of electrostatic potential distributions, electric-field profiles, carrier transport, and interfacial charge dynamics within the heterostructure. By systematically evaluating the influence of electrode work-function differences on band alignment, built-in potential formation, and charge extraction, the model establishes quantitative relationships between interface electrostatics and key photovoltaic performance metrics. The resulting framework offers a predictive tool for electrode selection and device optimization, providing design guidelines for the development of high-performance perovskite heterostructure solar cells.

Result and discussion

To evaluate the intrinsic charge-transport properties of the perovskite absorber, this study consider absorber's thickness in the range of 300–800 nm rather than ultra-thin layers. In thicker absorbers, photogenerated carriers must travel longer distances before reaching the contacts, making carrier transport more dependent on diffusion and therefore more sensitive to recombination losses, defect states, and interface quality. Consequently, thicker layers provide a more realistic assessment of material quality and device performance. In contrast, ultra-thin absorbers (∼100 nm) can experience strong electric-field-assisted carrier extraction, which may artificially suppress recombination effects and mask the influence of material defects. Instead, the built-in potential is determined by the doping concentration (NA and ND), the intrinsic carrier concentration (ni2), and the operating temperature. Therefore, changes in absorb thickness do not directly modify the magnitude of the built-in potential. However, absorber thickness affects the spatial distribution of the built-in potential, electric field profile, depletion width coverage, and consequently the degree of band bending across the device.

The ratio is used here as a geometric transport parameter. As increases, a larger fraction of the absorber lies outside the depletion region, causing carrier collection to rely more heavily on diffusion. Consequently, recombination losses become increasingly significant.

The effect of perovskite absorber thickness on band bending and carrier transport is shown in Fig. 1 for the ITO/perovskite/graphene and graphene/perovskite/ITO device structures. The results indicate that the electric field strength decreases as the absorb thickness increases from 300 to 800 nm. This demonstrates that thinner absorbers support stronger internal electric fields, while thicker absorbers exhibit weaker field distribution across the device. This behavior reflects a transition in the carrier transport mechanism. In thin absorbers, the depletion region extends over most of the device, enabling drift-dominated transport and efficient charge collection. In contrast, thicker absorbers contain a significant quasi-neutral region where the electric field is weak, and carrier transport occurs mainly through diffusion. Since diffusion transport involves longer carrier path, it increases the likelihood of recombination losses. This trend is consistent with semiconductor heterostructure transport theory, where reduced depletion coverage leads to weaker drift assistance and increased diffusion-limited behavior.14

The depletion coverage parameter (χ = t/W) quantifies the ratio of absorber thickness (t) to depletion width (W) and describes the transition from drift-dominated transport (χ < 1), through mixed drift–diffusion transport (χ ≈ 1–2), to diffusion-dominated transport (χ > 2).

To describe this transition, the dimensionless parameter is introduced as a geometric indicator of depletion coverage. This parameter does not represent relative recombination directly. Instead, it quantifies the ratio between absorber thickness and depletion width, indicating how much of the absorber lies outside the space-charge region. When χ ≈ 1, the absorbers are nearly fully depleted, and carrier transport is mainly driven by the built-in electric field, resulting in efficient charge separation. As χ increase (χ > 1), a larger fraction of the absorber becomes electrically neutral, and carrier transport becomes diffusion-dominated, which increases transport distance and enhances recombination probability.

A comparison between device structures shows that the ITO/perovskite/graphene configuration (Vbi = 1.0 V) exhibits slightly stronger electric fields and lower χ values than the graphene/perovskite/ITO structure (Vbi = 0.95 V). This indicates improved depletion coverage and more efficient carrier separation in the former case. This behavior agrees with studies showing that electrode work-function differences strongly influence band alignment, built-in potential, and interfacial charge extraction efficiency.18,20

Overall, the results demonstrate that absorber thickness is the dominant factor controlling the internal electric-field distribution and transport regime in perovskite heterostructures. Increasing thickness shifts the device from drift-dominated to diffusion-dominated transport, while electrode asymmetry provides only a secondary modulation of carrier collection efficiency.

The variation of band bending and carrier transport with perovskite thickness is shown in Fig. 2 for Cu2S/perovskite/graphene and graphene/perovskite/Cu2S device structures. The results indicate a systematic decrease in electric field strength as the absorber thickness increases from 300 to 800 nm, suggesting that the internal electrostatic potential becomes more spatially distributed in thicker absorbers. This behavior is consistent with semiconductor surface and heterojunction theory, where increased physical separation leads to reduced field intensity due to weaker potential gradient confinement.20

From carrier-transport perspective, this reduction in field strength implies a gradual weakening of electrostatic driving forces responsible for charge separation. Similar behavior has been reported in functional electrode–semiconductor systems, where transport efficiency strongly depends on how effectively the built-in field penetrates the absorber volume.17 In thin absorbers, strong field penetration ensures rapid separation of photogenerated carriers, whereas in thicker absorbers, incomplete field coverage leads to spatial regions where carrier motion is governed primarily by diffusion. This transition has also been observed in hybrid transparent conductive electrode systems, where electrode-induced modulation of band alignment directly affects field distribution and extraction efficiency.18

The corresponding increase in the parameter χ = t/W reflects this reduced electrostatic control across the absorb thickness. This interpretation is consistent with studies on carrier localization and transport in semiconductor heterostructures, which show that when device dimensions exceed the effective depletion length, carriers increasingly occupy quasi-neutral regions with longer transport pathways and reduced collection efficiency.14 In such conditions, carrier accumulation and spatial separation effects become more pronounced, increasing the likelihood of recombination losses due to extended residence time within the absorber.25–29

Furthermore, the slight difference observed between Cu2S/perovskite/graphene (Vbi = 0.90 V) and graphene/perovskite/Cu2S (Vbi = 0.85 V) structures is attributed to electrode-induced asymmetry in interfacial band alignment. Similar effects have been reported in theoretical analyses of semiconductor surface interactions, where work-function mismatch governs charge redistribution and modifies internal electrostatic potential profiles.31 Additionally, studies on charge-transfer processes in functional electrodes confirm that even small variations in interfacial energetics can significantly influence carrier extraction and field distribution across the device.17

Overall, the results are in strong agreement with previous experimental and theoretical findings, confirming that absorber thickness primarily governs electrostatic field distribution and depletion coverage, while electrode selection provides secondary tuning of interface potential and carrier transport efficiency.

The observed decrease in the average built-in electric field (and corresponding band bending) with increasing absorber thickness is consistent with established studies of semiconductor heterojunctions. As the device thickness increases, the fixed built-in potential is distributed over a larger spatial region, effectively reducing the field's intensity due to weaker electrostatic confinement, as shown in Fig. 3.31 This reduction in field strength leads to a gradual transition from drift-dominated transport in thin absorbers to diffusion-dominated transport in thicker devices, where carrier collection relies increasingly on concentration gradients rather than field-driven drift.17

The increase in the “relative recombination” parameter with thickness follows trends widely reported in perovskite and hybrid optoelectronic devices, where thicker active layers often exhibit higher effective recombination losses due to longer carrier transport paths and reduced electric-field assistance. This is consistent with carrier localization and minority-carrier transport analyses in semiconductor heterostructures, which show that when device thickness exceeds the depletion width, carriers spend more time in quasi-neutral regions, increasing the probability of recombination during diffusion-limited transport.14 Similar thickness-dependent degradation in charge collection efficiency has also been reported in hybrid transparent conductive electrode systems, where reduced field penetration leads to weaker extraction efficiency and increased carrier accumulation.30

Furthermore, the slight difference observed between Al/perovskite/graphene and graphene/perovskite/Al structures is consistent with studies on electrode-induced band alignment effects, where work-function asymmetry modifies interfacial electric fields and carrier extraction efficiency. The higher built-in potential configuration shows improved field strength and reduced recombination tendency, in agreement with theoretical investigations of semiconductor surface interactions and charge-transfer processes at interfaces.17,31 These studies confirm that electrode ordering plays a secondary but non-negligible role in tuning internal field distribution, while absorber thickness remains the dominant factor governing transport behavior.

Fig. 4 represents the influence of perovskite layer thickness on band bending and relative recombination for the Al/perovskite/Cu2S and Cu2S/perovskite/Al device structures. As the thickness increases from 300 to 800 nm, the electric field decreases significantly in both structures, indicating weaker bands bending at larger thicknesses. This behavior follows the inverse relationship between electric field and device thickness, since the built-I potential is distributed over a longer distance. The Al/perovskite/Cu2S configuration consistently exhibits a slightly higher electric field due to its larger built-in voltage (Vbi = 0.70 V) compared to the Cu2S/perovskite/Al structure (Vbi = 0.65 V). Stronger electric fields at lower thickness enhance charge separation and carrier extraction efficiency.

In contrast, the relative recombination increases steadily with increasing thickness for both structures. Thicker perovskite layers increase the carrier transport distance and the probability of carrier trapping and defect-assisted recombination. The Cu2S/perovskite/Al structure shows slightly higher recombination throughout the thickness range, which is consistent with its weaker internal electric field and reduced carrier extraction capability. Overall, the results demonstrate a trade-off between absorber thickness and electrical performance: while thicker layers may improve optical absorption, excessive thickness reduces the internal electric field and enhances recombination losses. These findings suggest that an optimized intermediate thickness is necessary to achieve balanced charge transport and minimized recombination in perovskite-based devices.

The results in Fig. 5 demonstrate that the incorporation of Cu2S and graphene layers significantly enhances device performance compared to conventional ITO-based configuration. Among all investigated structures, the graphene|perovskite|Cu2S device exhibits the highest performance, achieving a Jsc of approximately 22.3 mA cm−2, Voc of 1.20 V, FF of 0.83, and PCE of 22%. In contrast, the Al/perovskite/ITO and ITO/perovskite/Al structures show relatively lower photovoltaic parameters and efficiency and efficiencies. Overall, the results indicate that graphene- and Cu2S-based architectures improve charge transport, reduce recombination losses, and enhance the overall efficiency of perovskite solar cells.

Results in the above tables indicate that increasing the perovskite absorbers thickness from 300 to 800 nm strongly influences the photovoltaic characteristics of the investigated structures. Similar trends have been reported in several SCAPS-1D studies, demonstrating that absorber thickness governs the balance between optical absorption and carrier recombination. The effects of absorber thickness and the choice of front and back contact electrodes on PSC performance are discussed in the following sections.

Short-circuit current density (Jsc)

A commonly used absorption model is Jsc(t) = Jmax(1 − e−αt) with Jmax = 24 mA cm−2 and α = 0.004 nm−1. The short-circuit current density increases continuously with absorbers thickness owing to enhanced photon absorption and increase electron–hole pair generation. For example, the graphene/perovskite/Cu2S device exhibits an increase in Jsc from approximately 18.87 mA cm−2 at 300 nm to 26.07 mA cm−2 at 800 nm. This behavior agrees with previous SCAPS studies, which reported that thicker absorbers improve spectral response and enhance photocurrent generation because of increased light harvesting.

Open-circuit voltage (Voc)

Open-circuit voltage increases slightly with thickness and subsequently reaches saturation. Initially, the higher carrier concentration increases the quasi-Fermi level splitting, thereby enhancing Voc. However, beyond the optimum thickness, additional recombination limits further improvement.

Similar observations were reported by several SCAPS-1D authors who showed that increasing absorber thickness eventually increases reverse saturation current and carrier accumulation, resulting in Voc saturation or even a slight decrease.

Fill factor (FF)

A practical approximation is with FF0 ≈ 0.85. The fill factor decreases gradually as thickness increases. This reduction originates from the longer transport distance that photogenerated carriers must travel before collection, which increases transport losses and recombination probability. Comparable reductions in FF with increasing absorber thickness have been reported in SCAPS simulations of inverted and conventional perovskite solar cells.

An increase in absorbers thickness produces larger transport resistance because carriers travel longer distances. Consequently, the series resistance increases with thickness, reducing charge extraction efficiency.

Although Rs is often not explicitly tabulated in many SCAPS reports, the reduction in FF and carrier collection efficiency observed in thick absorbers is generally attributed to increasing series resistance and transport limitations.

The shunt resistance decreases with increasing thickness due to the larger volume of material and increased density of defects and grain boundaries. These imperfections provide leakage paths that facilitate non-ideal current flow. The same phenomenon has been associated with increased defect-assisted recombination and poorer charge extraction in thick absorber layers.

Recombination with current density (J0)

The recombination current density increases as the absorber thickness increases. A thicker absorber contains a larger active volume and therefore more defective states. Several studies have shown that excessively thick absorbers act as recombination centers, reducing carrier collection efficiency and limiting overall device performance.

Power conversion efficiency (PCE)

The PCE initially increases with thickness because of improved photon absorption and higher photocurrent generation. Maximum efficiency is achieved at approximately 600–700 nm. Beyond this optimum thickness, the increase in recombination losses outweighs the gain in photocurrent, causing efficiency to decline.

This optimum thickness range is in excellent agreement with numerous SCAPS investigations. A recent study24 reported maximum performance at an absorber's thickness of 600 nm. Similarly, Vega et al.25 identified 600 nm as the optimum thickness for achieving maximum efficiency. Other reviews concluded that the optimum absorber thickness for single-junction perovskite solar cells generally lies within the range of 400–600 nm.

Summary

Among the investigated device architectures, the Cu2S/perovskite/graphene configuration exhibited the highest power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 26.7%, followed by graphene/perovskite/Cu2S (24.3%), Al/perovskite/Cu2S (20.7%), and the conventional ITO/perovskite/Al structure (17.4%). The superior performance of the Cu2S/perovskite/graphene device is attributed to favorable energy-level alignment, enhanced carrier transport, lower series resistance, and reduced interfacial recombination, which collectively improve the open-circuit voltage (Voc), short-circuit current density (Jsc), and fill factor (FF). The incorporation of graphene as a highly conductive electrode promotes efficient charge extraction and minimizes resistive losses, thereby enhancing the overall device performance.

The results further indicate that increasing the absorber thickness enhances light absorption and carrier generation, leading to an increase in Jsc and a slight improvement in Voc. However, excessive absorber thickness introduces additional transport limitations and recombination losses, resulting in increased series resistance and recombination current density, accompanied by reductions in fill factor and shunt resistance. Consequently, the power conversion efficiency reaches its maximum at an optimum absorber thickness of approximately 600–700 nm, where the trade-off between optical absorption and charge transport is optimized.

Overall, the theoretical analysis based on Poisson electrostatics, drift–diffusion transport, and carrier recombination theory demonstrates that both absorber thickness optimization and contact electrode engineering play crucial roles in determining the photovoltaic performance of perovskite solar cells. The results highlight the potential of graphene-based electrodes for improving charge transport and suppressing recombination losses, identifying the Cu2S/perovskite/graphene architecture as the most promising configuration among the structures investigated for the development of high-efficiency perovskite solar cells.
