Persistent left superior vena cava with retrograde flow and absent coronary sinus in a child with ventricular septal defect and patent ductus arteriosus
Authors: Rashmi Kishore, Rajesh Babu Gudipati, Palaparthi Sairam, Thomas Mathew, Suman Y. Vyas, Nitin K. Rao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCoronary sinus (CS) defects are rare congenital cardiac anomalies that occur in isolation or with other congenital heart diseases. Persistent left superior vena cava (LSVC) is a relatively common entity that usually drains into the CS, is of no hemodynamic consequence, and is easily diagnosed on echocardiography by a dilated CS and an antegrade flow toward the heart. However, a combination of LSVC and CS defect may reverse its flow direction and CS dilation may be absent. In the absence of echocardiographic clues, the reversed flow can be easily misdiagnosed for other structures such as the more common vertical vein of anomalous pulmonary venous connection or the rarer levoatrial cardinal vein. Here, we report a 2-year-old boy with ventricular septal defect, patent ductus arteriosus, absent CS, and a persistent LSVC with retrograde flow producing a hemodynamically significant pretricuspid left-to-right shunt along with its diagnostic challenges. He underwent successful surgical closure of these defects and had been asymptomatic on follow-up.

INTRODUCTION

Coronary sinus (CS) defects are rare congenital anomalies of the cardiac venous system that occur either in isolation or with other congenital heart diseases (CHDs).[1] Meanwhile, persistent left superior vena cava (LSVC) is a relatively common entity with an incidence of 0.3%–0.5% in the general population and 3%–10% in CHDs.[2] A dilated CS and an antegrade flow are almost confirmatory for a persistent LSVC on echocardiography. When associated with CS defects, LSVC can lead to a right-to-left shunt with cyanosis or a left-to-right shunt with reversed flow, as per the type of CS defect. The absence of echocardiographic features can cause it to be confused with an anomalously draining pulmonary vein or the rare levoatrial cardinal vein (LACV).

Herein, we report a 2-year-old boy with ventricular septal defect (VSD), patent ductus arteriosus (PDA), absent CS, and persistent LSVC with retrograde flow, along with its diagnostic challenges.

CASE REPORT

A 2-year-old boy, born out of third-degree consanguineous marriage at term by normal vaginal delivery with uneventful antenatal and perinatal history, presented at the age of 8 months with an incidentally detected murmur. A pansystolic murmur was heard at the left lower sternal border on auscultation. Weight was 5.8 kg, and room air oxygen saturation was normal (96%–98%). Echocardiography revealed a moderate-sized perimembranous VSD with an interventricular gradient of 50 mmHg, restricted by septal aneurysm, a small PDA, and normal-sized cardiac chambers. The right ventricular systolic pressure (RVSP) by tricuspid regurgitation (TR) jet was 30 mmHg. The suprasternal view showed a cephalad venous flow adjacent to the aorta, which was assumed to be an anomalously draining left upper pulmonary vein [Figure 1]. The rest of the pulmonary veins appeared to be draining into the left atrium (LA). He was started on diuretics and was kept on follow-up as he was asymptomatic. At 2 years of age, he was admitted for a lower respiratory tract infection and faltering weight gain with a body weight of 8 kg. Repeat echocardiography showed the development of pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) with RVSP of 40 mmHg measured by TR jet, and the interventricular gradient across the VSD was 40 mmHg. There was no significant chamber dilation. Chest X-ray showed mild pulmonary congestion and cardiomegaly with a cardiothoracic ratio of 0.6. The electrocardiogram showed right axis deviation and biventricular hypertrophy. It was decided to close the VSD, and cardiac catheterization was performed for hemodynamic assessment of PAH and delineation of pulmonary venous anatomy. Pressure studies revealed elevated pulmonary arterial pressure with a mean of 33 mmHg [Table 1]. Oximetric calculations showed Qp/Qs of 2.8 [Table 2]. Angiography revealed a moderate-sized VSD of 7 mm with two fenestrations and a small PDA [Figure 2]. On selective cannulation of the vertical vein, the wire entered the LA and left ventricle instead of a pulmonary vein [Figure 2]. The left pulmonary vein angiogram showed normal drainage into LA without any connection to the vertical vein. Hence, the possibility of LACV was considered. A computed tomography (CT) angiogram was done, which also showed normal pulmonary venous drainage and an additional vein arising from the LA and draining into the right SVC through the innominate vein [Figure 3]. The parents opted for surgical repair of the shunt lesions and ligation of the vein instead of device closure. Intraoperatively, the anomalous vein was found coursing anterior to the left pulmonary artery (LPA) and was connected inferiorly to LA. Atresia of the CS orifice (ACSO) was noted along with absent CS. Individual coronary venous tributaries were draining directly into the posterior LA wall. These findings led to identifying the venous structure as a persistent LSVC. The vein was ligated, PDA transfixed, and VSD closed. Postoperatively, the child made an excellent recovery and was discharged on postoperative day 4. At the 6-month follow-up, he remained asymptomatic with normal growth and development, and there were no residual shunts and resolution of PAH on echocardiography.

DISCUSSION

Persistent LSVC occurs due to failure of regression of left-sided anterior and common cardinal veins, which join the left horn of sinus venosus, the embryological precursor of CS.[1] Hence, in 92% of cases, LSVC drains into the CS, whereas in the remaining 8%, it drains into the LA, either directly or indirectly through an unroofed CS (UCS) [Figure 4a and b].[2] Congenital CS abnormalities may be asymptomatic with accidental detection on CT, magnetic resonance imaging, and catheterization or, rarely, may alter cardiac hemodynamics, producing clinical symptoms along with the associated CHD.[1]

Mantini et al. classified CS defects into four types: (a) enlargement of CS, (b) absent CS, (c) atretic ostium, and (d) hypoplasia [Table 3].[3] CS septal defects or UCS occur when the intervening wall between the CS and LA is absent, producing an interatrial shunt. Although not included as a separate entity in the above classification, UCS can be seen with a dilated CS or ACSO. LSVC is commonly associated with 63%–75% of UCS cases.[14] Further categorization has been done as per the site and size of unroofing as I – complete unroofing, II – unroofing of the middle portion, and III – terminal portion unroofing and presence/absence of LSVC (a or b).[4] In complete UCS, individual coronary tributaries drain directly into the posterior wall of the LA or right atrium (RA), and LSVC, if present, appears connected to the roof of LA. It is associated with CHDs such as atrial septal defect (ASD), atrioventricular canal defects, hypoplastic left heart syndrome (HLHS), single ventricle, systemic and pulmonary venous abnormalities, and heterotaxy syndromes, especially asplenia.[5]

Raghib et al. first described this developmental complex of persistent LSVC, absent CS, and a coronary ostial defect (CS ASD) in 1965 [Table 3, type IIA].[5] His cases had antegrade LSVC flow with varying degrees of desaturation due to the admixture of desaturated LSVC blood in LA [Figure 4c]. Our case, though similar, differs due to the absence of cyanosis, retrograde LSVC flow, and ACSO instead of an ASD, suggesting agenesis of CS as the primary defect. The presence of a bridging vein allowed blood to follow the pressure gradient from LA to RA, producing a pretricuspid left-to-right shunt in the absence of an actual ASD and contributing to the development of PAH [Figure 4d]. The small amount of deoxygenated CS blood still draining into LA was insufficient to produce clinically significant desaturation. This defect has not been included in the original Mantini’s classification but can be placed as a subtype of absent CS [Table 3, type IIB]. Raghib et al. suggested the failure of the left atriovenous fold to completely separate LA from the sinus venous as the basic developmental anomaly. Miraldi et al. gave an alternate theory of right atrial isomerism for a persistent LSVC and absent CS.[6]

Only a handful of case reports describing absent CS and persistent LSVC, with or without additional CHDs such as VSD, scimitar syndrome, and coronary artery fistula, have been published.[67] Few report complications from paradoxical emboli, brain abscess, and stroke due to intermittent right to left shunt through LSVC.[89] Hence, ligation of the vein seems prudent when the bridging vein is present, which averts the complication of left upper limb edema. Retrograde flow in LSVC also occurs with isolated ACSO, where it vents the CS blood, and its ligation is invariably fatal here [Figure 4e]. In our case, the coronary tributaries drained directly into LA instead of LSVC and hence could be safely ligated. Coronary venous blood also drains into LA in ACSO when associated with UCS [Figure 4f].

Important differentials of an LSVC are the vertical vein of a partial or total anomalous pulmonary venous connection and LACV, both of which show retrograde, cranially directed blood flow. The former can be distinguished from LSVC by the absence of a normal pulmonary venous connection to LA, which is maintained in LSVC. The latter can be distinguished by the relative position with respect to LPA, with LACV coursing posterior to LPA and LSVC anterior.[2] Furthermore, the presence of CS defects indicates that the structure is LSVC, given its common embryological origin.[10] LACV invariably occurs with left-sided obstructive lesions such as HLHS and mitral stenosis providing an alternate route for the egress of the pulmonary venous blood.[10]

Hence, persistent LSVC, though a relatively common entity, can be misdiagnosed if associated with CS defects, which alter the hemodynamics and direction of flow. Appropriate diagnosis can be made by analyzing the pulmonary arterial and venous anatomy and the presence of associated defects.
