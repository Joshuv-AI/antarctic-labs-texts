Low minimal alveolar concentration general anesthesia for pain control in percutaneous radiofrequency ablation: Influence on long-term outcomes of renal cell carcinoma
Authors: Yu Jeong Bang, Byung Kwan Park
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe study aims to retrospectively examine performance of general anesthesia in percutaneous radiofrequency ablation (RFA) of renal cell carcinoma (RCC) and to assess the long-term outcomes.Materials and MethodsBetween September 2012 and August 2019, 87 patients (68 males and 19 females; mean age, 61 years) with biopsy-proven T1a RCC underwent computed tomography-guided RFA under general anesthesia. Anesthetic time, minimal alveolar concentration (MAC), and post-anesthetic complications were recorded. Primary effectiveness and local tumor progression (LTP)-free and metastasis-free survival rates were calculated. Major complications following RFA were assessed. A Kaplan–Meier analysis was used to determine the long-term survival rate.ResultsGeneral anesthesia and RFA were performed with 100% technical success. The mean time of general anesthesia was 127 minutes (range, 63–248 minutes). The mean MAC was 0.88±0.15. There was no complication related to general anesthesia. Primary effectiveness was 100%, and the 5-year LTP-free or metastasis-free rate was 97.7% (85/87). One major RFA complication occurred in a patient with ureter stricture that was detected during a follow-up examination.ConclusionsLow-MAC general anesthesia during RFA procedures is appropriate for precise RCC targeting. This type of pain control could influence the long-term outcomes of RCC patients.

INTRODUCTION

Percutaneous radiofrequency ablation (RFA) is recommended as an alternative treatment for renal cell carcinoma (RCC) patients who are poor surgical candidates due to a high risk of postoperative morbidity or mortality [123]. This minimally invasive treatment provides relatively good longterm outcomes, with 5-year recurrence-free survival rates ranging from 88%–92% [4567]. However, several investigations have reported that percutaneous cryoablation achieves slightly higher recurrence-free survival rates, ranging from 93%–100% [891011]. The outcome difference could stem from many factors including procedure pain, ablation margin, and urothelial injury. Most important, RFA induces greater pain during the procedure than cryoablation. Frequently, conscious sedation alone is not effective in terms of pain control and patient immobility during RFA procedures, and it did not achieve better outcomes than general anesthesia [1213]. We hypothesized that general anesthesia might contribute to improving the long-term outcomes of RCC patients. However, details about general anesthesia induction, maintenance, and recovery in percutaneous RFA of RCC are scarce [121415].

The purpose of this study was to demonstrate the performance of general anesthesia in percutaneous RFA and to assess the long-term outcomes of those RCC patients.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This retrospective study was approved by Institutional Review Board of Samsung Medical Center (approval number: 2024-11-130). The informed consent requirement was waived because of the retrospective design.

1. Subjects

Between September 2012 and August 2019 at Samsung Medical Center, 217 patients underwent image-guided biopsy in a renal incidentaloma prior to percutaneous RFA (Fig. 1). All tumors were detected using ultrasound, computed tomography (CT), or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) performed for unrelated reasons. The inclusion criteria were a single solid mass, T1a (<4 cm) RCC, sporadic RCC, and long-term (≥3 years) follow-up. The exclusion criteria were short-term (<3 years) follow-up (n=33), insufficient histological diagnosis (n=31), synchronous or metachronous RCC (n=30), benign renal tumor (n=20), cystic RCC (n=6), non-RCC malignant tumor (n=4), hereditary RCC (n=3), and T1b or higher (≥4 cm) RCC (n=3). After the application of those criteria, 87 patients were included in the study population. Their male to female ratio was 68:19, and their median age was 61 years (range, 26–83 years) (Table 1). Among the patients, 14 (16.1%) were classified as American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I, 66 (75.9%) as ASA physical status II, and 7 (8.0%) as ASA physical status III [1617]. The median size of their RCCs was 2.0 cm (range, 1.1–3.9 cm). The numbers of exophytic and endophytic tumors were 34 and 53, respectively. The RCC subtypes were clear cell in 69, papillary in 8, chromophobe in 6, and unclassified in 4. The median follow-up period was 67 months (range, 36–142 months). During follow-up, 6 patients died because of RCC (n=1) or non-RCC diseases (n=5). The median R.E.N.A.L. Nephrometry Score was 8 (range, 4–10) [18].

2. RFA procedures

All patients underwent general anesthesia for pain control just prior to their RFA procedures. One of two CT scanners (Aquilion or Aquilion ONE; Cannon Medical Systems) was used to guide the percutaneous RFA. Each RCC mass was targeted with a 15-gauge (n=19) or 17-gauge (n=68) single electrode (Proteus; STARmed) that was internally cooled with normal saline circulated by a pump. Two grounding pads were pasted on the backs of each patient’s thighs. The length of the uninsulated electrode was controlled every 0.5 cm and ranged from 0.5 to 3.0 cm [19].

3. Anesthetic techniques

General anesthesia for patients undergoing RFA is a standardized part of our institutional protocol. No premedication was given to any of the patients. Upon entering the intervention theater, supplemental oxygen and standard ASA monitoring were applied. General anesthesia was induced with intravenous thiopental sodium (4–6 mg/kg) and rocuronium (0.6–0.8 mg/kg), followed by tracheal intubation. After intubation, an arterial catheter was placed in the radial artery for continuous blood pressure monitoring. Anesthesia was maintained with sevoflurane in a 1:1 oxygen-to-air mixture, and the sevoflurane concentration was adjusted to maintain hemodynamic stability. Intravenous fentanyl was administered as needed during noxious stimuli such as intubation or ablation. Hemodynamic agents were used to maintain the mean arterial blood pressure and heart rate within 20% of their pre-induction values. Additional doses of rocuronium were administered at the discretion of the attending anesthesiologist. At the end of the procedure, sevoflurane was discontinued, and the neuromuscular blockade was reversed with pyridostigmine. Later in the study period, institutional protocols evolved: propofol (1.5–2 mg/kg) replaced thiopental sodium for induction, and sugammadex (4 mg/kg) supplanted pyridostigmine for neuromuscular blockade reversal.

4. Data analysis

To analyze variables related to general anesthesia, we extracted the type and dose of anesthetics and the anesthesia duration from electronic medical records. Anesthesia maintenance is expressed as age-adjusted minimal alveolar concentration (MAC) [20]. Anesthetic and procedure durations were recorded, along with the specific anesthetics used for induction, maintenance, and reversal. Data on anesthesia-related adverse events, including postoperative delirium and postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), were also extracted. Additionally, analgesic requirements in the postanesthesia care unit (PACU) were investigated; and recovery time, anesthesia-related cost, and hospital stay were recorded.

To analyze technical data, patient positions were recorded to determine whether general anesthesia was possible. We also recorded the number of RFA cycles, sessions, and electrode repositions performed. RF duration was recorded to identify how long it took to complete all ablation cycles. The length of the uninsulated electrode used to ablate each tumor was measured.

To analyze treatment outcomes, residual tumor, technical effectiveness, and local tumor progression (LTP) were assessed. Residual tumor was defined as a focal lesion that was enhanced within the tumor at the first follow-up CT or MRI. Technical effectiveness was defined as no enhancement visible at the first follow-up examination. LTP was defined as a lesion in which a focal enhancement was detected on a second or later follow-up image. Primary and secondary effectiveness rates were also assessed. The primary effectiveness rate was defined as the percentage of RCCs treated in one RFA session. The secondary effectiveness rate was defined as the percentage of RCCs successfully treated in two or more RFA sessions. Vein thrombosis, lymph node metastasis, and hematogenous metastasis were also assessed on follow-up images. Various long-term survival rates were calculated.

To analyze renal function changes, serum creatinine (sCr) and the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) were compared before and after RFA. eGFRs were calculated from the modification of diet in renal disease equation [21]. sCr and eGFRs were measured prior to RFA and at the last outpatient visit.

To analyze treatment complications, major and minor complications were recorded according to the Cardiovascular and Interventional Radiological Society classification for interventional radiology [22]. Major and minor complications were defined when the grade was 3 or higher and 2 or lower, respectively [22].

5. Statistical analysis

All continuous data were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed data are expressed as mean±standard deviation, and non-normally distributed data are presented as median with range. Categorical variables are reported as number (%).

A Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was used to calculate the 5-year LTP-free, metastasis-free, cancer-specific, and overall survival rates. sCrs or eGFRs before and after RFA were compared with a paired t-test. All statistical analyses were performed using commercially available software (PASW Statistics, version 17; IBM Corp.). A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Anesthetic and procedure times were 127 minutes (range, 63–248 minutes) and 81 minutes (range, 30–203 minutes), respectively (Table 1). For induction, thiopental sodium was used in 58 patients at a mean dose of 4.8±0.5 mg/kg. Propofol was administered in 26 patients at a median dose of 1.9 mg/kg (range, 1.6–3.8 mg/kg). Etomidate was exclusively used in 3 patients, primarily those at risk of hemodynamic instability, at a mean dose of 0.21±0.04 mg/kg. Neuromuscular blockade was achieved using either rocuronium or cisatracurium. Rocuronium was used in 68 patients at a median dose of 0.90 mg/kg (range, 0.60–2.05 mg/kg). Cisatracurium was administered at a mean dose of 0.23±0.43 mg/kg to 19 patients with reduced eGFR. Maintenance anesthesia was achieved using sevoflurane, with a mean MAC of 0.88±0.15. To reverse the neuromuscular blockade, 60.9% of patients (n=53) received pyridostigmine, and 39.1% (n=34) were given sugammadex. Adverse events in the PACU are presented in Table 1; delirium in 1 patient (1.1%), PONV in 11 patients (12.6%), and respiratory depression in 1 patient (1.1%). All complications were transient, and patients recovered uneventfully with conservative management. The median recovery time and hospital stay were 56 minutes (range, 50–64 minutes) and 34 hours (range, 30–36 hours), respectively. For all RCC patients, only 5% of their costs were related to low-MAC general anesthesia.

After general anesthesia was induced, 44 patients were placed in the prone position, 20 in posterior oblique, 18 in anterior oblique, and 5 in decubitus. No patients were positioned supine, and general anesthesia was possible in all of them, regardless of position. The median number of RFA cycles, their duration, and electrode repositions were 10 (range, 3–40), 20 minutes (range, 4–73 minutes), and 2 (range, 0–10), respectively. The median length of the uninsulated electrode was 3 cm (range, 2–3 cm). All patients underwent only one session to complete tumor ablation. Technical effectiveness was 100% (87/87) on the first follow-up images, with no residual tumor found in any case. Only two cases had LTP, and they underwent radical nephrectomy and/or chemotherapy because of vein thrombosis (n=1) and lymphatic (n=1) or hematogenous (n=1) metastasis. Primary effectiveness was 100% (87/87), so secondary effectiveness was not relevant (Fig. 2). During follow-up, 6 patients died due to RCC (n=1) or coexisting morbidities (n=5). The 5-year LTP-free, metastasisfree, cancer-specific, and overall survival rates were 97.7% (85/87), 97.7% (85/87), 98.9% (86/87), and 93.1% (81/87), respectively (Fig. 3).

The median levels of sCr before and after RFA were 0.91 mg/dL (range, 0.45–11.53 mg/dL) and 1.02 mg/dL (range, 0.51–13.43 mg/dL), respectively (p=0.3163). However, the median levels of eGFR before and after RFA were 81.4 mL/min/1.73 m2 (range, 4.4–130.6 mL/min/1.73 m2) and 73.6 mL/min/1.73 m2 (range, 3.5–117.2 mL/min/1.73 m2) (p=0.0184).

A major complication was detected in one patient, who had ureter stricture in a follow-up examination, and the involved kidney became atrophic. Minor complications were detected in 22 cases: hematoma (n=18), hematuria (n=2), and urine leakage (n=2). Those complications resolved spontaneously without transfusion or intervention.

DISCUSSION

Our study showed successful treatment of all included RCCs with percutaneous RFA under low-MAC general anesthesia. Primary effectiveness was 100%, with no residual tumor found at the first follow-up examination in any patient, resulting in little LTP and good long-term outcomes due to complete pain control. No major complications or permanent sequelae occurred following general anesthesia.

General anesthesia ensures patient comfort by eliminating painful stimuli and alleviating the discomfort associated with specific positions (e.g., lateral decubitus or prone). Moreover, it enhances procedural accuracy and ease by providing absolute immobility and facilitating controlled breath-holding. These advantages are critical for optimal procedural conditions, ensuring consistent positioning, and minimizing motion [2324].

Unlike general anesthesia for surgery, anesthesia for RFA does not require deep anesthesia or a profound neuromuscular blockade. Instead, it suffices to maintain unconsciousness and suppress spontaneous breathing. For brief noxious stimuli, a temporary increase in anesthesia depth or a small opioid dose is typically sufficient. General anesthesia and opioid use are associated with complications such as respiratory depression, PONV, and delirium [252627]. In our patient group, the frequency of complications was very low, and all observed complications were mild, presenting as transient symptoms in the recovery room that were fully resolved without further intervention. This can be attributed to the avoidance of deep anesthesia and minimal use of opioids during RFA. Overall, 20 cases (12.6%) of PONV were reported, with frequencies comparable to or lower than those typically observed with general anesthesia for surgical procedures [2829].

Many investigations have reported that conscious sedation is mainly performed to control pain during RFA procedures [4630]. The primary RFA effectiveness in those studies was 86%–96%, and their recurrence-free survival rate range was 86%–94% [4630]. Those treatment outcomes are inferior to those reported here. The difference could be due to many factors, including tumor size, tumor location, histologic grade, pain control, and ablation margin. However, those variables in the previous studies were similar to those in our study, except pain control. The median tumor size was 2–3 cm in all studies, and most of the RCCs had a high probability of being low grade (I–II). Tumor locations were a mix of exophytic and endophytic RCCs in the previous research, but it provided no clear statement about the proportions. Therefore, pain control seems to be a key factor in the difference in treatment outcomes. If pain is not controlled, it is difficult for patients to follow radiologist orders or endure procedures. Instead, they frequently ask the radiologist to hurry or stop the RFA due to intolerable pain. In addition, elderly patients find it difficult to maintain specific positions on the CT table for a long time, and those who have dementia, arthritis, or hearing loss rarely cooperate with radiologist orders during RFA procedures. Therefore, general anesthesia should be performed in those situations. Another advantage over conscious sedation is that general anesthesia enables radiologists to precisely re-locate an RF electrode and resume ablation by means of pain control. Interventional radiologists ask patients to hold their breath under conscious sedation or monitored anesthesia care when placing an electrode. Frequently, the depth of breath is not constant, and precise targeting is difficult for radiologists. In contrast, a tumor can be targeted precisely under general anesthesia because patient breath can be maintained at the same level. In our patients, the ventilator was never stopped when a tumor was targeted or monitored. A CT scan was performed at the end of expiration to confirm electrode placement or ablation progress. When the heart rate and blood pressure were elevated at the beginning of RFA cycles, inhalant anesthetics or anti-hypertensive agents were used to control the vital signs. Kim et al. [12] reported that general anesthesia decreased the frequency of residual tumor or LTP, which was higher when an RCC was treated with RFA under conscious sedation.

Our primary effectiveness and LTP-free survival rate are not inferior to those of percutaneous cryoablation reported by many investigations [891011]. Their primary effectiveness and LTP-free survival rate were 96%–100% and 91%–100%, respectively [891011]. The outcome differences between RFA and cryoablation include many factors such as pain degree, ablation margin, ablation size, and urothelial injury. Most important, the pain incurred during cryoablation procedures is mostly tolerable or weak for patients, and conscious sedation alone is sufficient for pain control. Pain tolerance is one of the factors that influence long-term outcomes. However, patients who are old or have severe arthritis, dementia, or hearing loss struggle to follow radiologist orders even during cryoablation. General anesthesia is needed to complete cryoablation in those cases.

Our study has several limitations. First, we did not conduct a randomized controlled trial to compare how general anesthesia and other types of anesthesia influence long-term outcomes. Further comparisons of anesthesia techniques will be needed to validate the necessity of general anesthesia. No intra-procedural targeting accuracy metrics, breath-hold variability data, or objective pain-related treatment interruptions are reported. Second, general anesthesia is more expensive and can induce more numerous complications than conscious sedation or monitored anesthesia care. However, incomplete pain control can lead to incomplete ablation and LTP, resulting in additional RFA sessions. Also, if patients are not well controlled during RFA procedures, it is difficult to precisely target a tumor and achieve sufficient ablation to cover an RCC and tumor margin. As a result, the cost of general anesthesia needs to be weighed against its effectiveness. Third, our study was conducted retrospectively. Therefore, the possibility of selection bias cannot be completely excluded. Fourth, RFA is outdated compared with microwave ablation and cryoablation. Fifth, more than half of our study population had endophytic RCCs. Sixth, although we briefly discuss anesthesia-related complications, we did not systematically evaluate patient satisfaction, post-procedural recovery time, or quality of life.

CONCLUSIONS

The pain incurred by RFA is well controlled even when general anesthesia is performed with low MAC. Patients with old age or co-existing morbidities recovered without sequelae, primary effectiveness was extremely high, and LTP was low due to precise tumor targeting. Therefore, low-MAC general anesthesia during RFA can influence the long-term outcomes of RCC patients.
