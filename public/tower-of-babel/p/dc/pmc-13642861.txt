Hypoxia-induced exosomal LNCOC1 orchestrates bladder cancer progression via the YBX1/PIK3CA axis and serves as a potential liquid biopsy biomarker
Authors: Haoxiang Xu, Chenglin Yang, Qiangfeng Fu, Shengtao Zeng, Yue Yang, Zhi Cao, Jiahang Wang, Yuansong Xiao, Xiaoming Zhang, Wei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bladder cancer (BCa) is a prevalent genitourinary malignancy characterized by limited specific diagnostic and therapeutic options. Although hypoxia is known to drive tumor progression via exosome-mediated communication, the specific oncogenic roles of hypoxia-induced exosomal long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) remain poorly elucidated. Here, we profiled exosomes from normoxic and hypoxic BCa cells using high-throughput sequencing, identifying LNCOC1 as a significantly upregulated lncRNA under hypoxic conditions. Mechanistically, hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF1A) directly binds to the LNCOC1 promoter to drive its transcription and subsequent exosomal encapsulation. Upon transfer to recipient cells, exosomal LNCOC1 promotes BCa cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. It acts as a nuclear scaffold, directly recruiting the transcription factor YBX1 to the PIK3CA promoter, thereby upregulating PIK3CA expression and hyperactivating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. Clinically, elevated LNCOC1 levels in tumor tissues and circulating plasma exosomes from 96 BCa patients positively correlated with advanced tumor grade, metastasis, and poor prognosis. Furthermore, circulating exosomal LNCOC1 demonstrated moderate diagnostic efficacy in distinguishing BCa patients from healthy controls (AUC = 0.789) and metastatic from non-metastatic cases (AUC = 0.765). Collectively, these findings elucidate a novel HIF1A/LNCOC1/YBX1/PIK3CA regulatory axis, highlighting circulating exosomal LNCOC1 as a preliminary candidate biomarker for BCa diagnosis that warrants further validation in independent cohorts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s13402-026-01257-8.

Introduction

Bladder cancer (BCa) ranks among the most prevalent genitourinary malignancies [1, 2], accounting for an estimated 600,000 new cases and 220,000 deaths worldwide in 2022 [2]. Despite continuous advancements in therapeutic strategies, the lack of highly specific diagnostic and treatment modalities frequently leads to delayed detection, high recurrence rates, and poor overall prognosis [3, 4, 5]. Consequently, the early diagnosis and effective clinical management of BCa remain formidable challenges.

The concept of tumor hypoxia, initially proposed by Thomlinson in 1955 [6], is now widely recognized as a fundamental hallmark of cancer, significantly affecting approximately 50–60% of solid tumors [7, 8, 9]. Although physical boundaries within tumor tissues are often indistinct, the tumor microenvironment (TME) is generally characterized by three distinct functional zones: the normoxic, hypoxic, and necrotic regions [10, 11, 12, 13]. Accumulating evidence demonstrates that hypoxia exacerbates tumor progression by stimulating exosome release and modulating their molecular cargo [14, 15]. Thus, hypoxia-induced exosomes function as critical intercellular signaling mediators, transmitting hypoxic stress from oxygen-deprived zones to normoxic tumor cells, thereby profoundly influencing malignant behavior.

Exosomes facilitate intercellular communication and modulate tumor progression by delivering diverse nucleic acid cargoes, including circular RNAs (circRNAs), long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), and microRNAs (miRNAs) [16]. LncRNAs—transcripts exceeding 200 nucleotides that lack protein-coding capacity—are instrumental in cancer development through diverse epigenetic and post-transcriptional mechanisms, such as functioning as competitive endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs), assembling ribonucleoprotein complexes, and interacting with RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) [17, 18, 19]. Nevertheless, the precise oncogenic functions of most exosomal lncRNAs in driving cancer proliferation and metastasis remain poorly elucidated. Specifically, a critical unresolved question is how hypoxia-induced exosomal lncRNAs regulate the crosstalk between hypoxic and normoxic tumor cell populations to mediate BCa progression within the TME.

To address this, we systematically profiled and compared exosomes secreted by BCa cells under both hypoxic and normoxic conditions. We discovered that hypoxia-induced exosomes were significantly enriched with LNCOC1, which potently promoted the development of malignant phenotypes in recipient BCa cells. Clinically, LNCOC1 exhibited elevated expression in both BCa tumor tissues and patient serum exosomes, with its upregulation strongly correlating with unfavorable clinical outcomes. While previous studies have characterized LNCOC1 as an upregulated lncRNA that enhances cellular proliferation and metastatic potential in ovarian carcinoma [20], its specific role in BCa remained unknown. In this study, we identified a novel oncogenic function for LNCOC1 and elucidated its underlying molecular mechanism. Under hypoxic stress, hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF1A) directly binds to the LNCOC1 promoter, driving its transcription and subsequent exosomal secretion. Upon internalization by normoxic tumor cells, this exosomal LNCOC1 recruits the transcription factor YBX1 to the PIK3CA promoter, thereby promoting PIK3CA transcription. The ensuing upregulation of PIK3CA hyperactivates the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway, ultimately fueling BCa malignancy. Collectively, these findings uncover a novel epigenetic regulatory axis and highlight the potential clinical application of circulating exosomal LNCOC1 as a preliminary candidate biomarker for BCa diagnosis and prognosis.

Materials and methods

Cell culture under normoxic and hypoxic conditions

The human BCa cell lines T24 and EJ were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). Cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (VivaCell, China). Cells were maintained under standard normoxic conditions in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ and 20% O₂. Once the culture density reached 70–80%, the standard medium was replaced with exosome-free medium. To establish hypoxic conditions, cells were transferred to a Tri-Gas CO₂ Incubator (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) flushed with a specialized gas mixture to maintain 1% O₂, 5% CO₂, and 94% N₂ for 48 h. Parallel normoxic control cells were maintained in a standard CO₂ incubator for the same duration.

Clinical samples

A total of 96 peripheral blood plasma samples from patients with BCa and 48 samples from healthy control participants were obtained from the General Hospital of Southern Theater Command. Additionally, 96 surgically excised BCa tissue specimens were acquired. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Ethics Committee of the General Hospital of Southern Theater Command (No. NZLLKZ2025072) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Exosome isolation and characterization

Exosome isolation and characterization were performed in accordance with the Minimal Information for Studies of Extracellular Vesicles (MISEV2018) guidelines [21]. For cell line-derived exosomes, culture supernatants were subjected to sequential differential centrifugation: 300 × g for 10 min, 2,000 × g for 10 min, and 10,000 × g for 30 min to remove cells and debris. The supernatant was then ultracentrifuged at 100,000 × g for 70 min twice at 4 °C to pellet exosomes. For clinical samples, plasma-derived exosomes were enriched from 1 mL of pre-filtered (0.8 μm) plasma using Exosupur columns (Echo Biotech, China) combined with size-exclusion chromatography. The isolated exosomes were resuspended in PBS. Morphology was observed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Hitachi HT7700). Particle size distribution and absolute quantification (particles/mL) were analyzed via Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis (NTA) using a NanoSight NS300 instrument (Malvern Panalytical). Exosome-specific surface markers (CD63, TSG101, Flotillin-1) and the negative marker (Calnexin) were validated by Western blotting using equivalent amounts of exosomal protein.

Cell transfection

Gene silencing reagents targeting human HIF1A (siHIF), PIK3CA (siPIK3CA), and YBX1 (siYBX1), alongside a non-targeting control siRNA (siNC), were synthesized by RiboBio (Guangzhou, China) and transfected at a final concentration of 50 nM. Lentiviral constructs expressing shRNAs against LNCOC1 (shLNCOC1) and a scrambled control (shNC) were also obtained from RiboBio. For viral transduction, T24 and EJ cells were incubated with recombinant lentiviruses (LV-LNCOC1 or LV-shNC) in polybrene-supplemented medium for 12 h, followed by a medium replacement. Transduction efficiency was verified 72 h post-infection via fluorescence microscopy. For overexpression assays, the respective vectors (pcDNA-LNCOC1, pcDNA-YBX1, pcDNA-PIK3CA) and the empty vector (pcDNA3.1) were introduced at 1.6 µg/well in 12-well plates. All transient transfections were performed using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) with Opti-MEM (Gibco) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Oligonucleotide sequences are detailed in Table S1.

Exosome pretreatment and standardization

To ensure accurate comparisons and adjust for differential exosome secretion rates under varying oxygen tensions, all in vitro functional assays were standardized based on the total exosomal protein concentration, quantified using a BCA assay kit. Recipient BCa cells (T24 and EJ) were cultured in medium supplemented with 1% exosome-depleted FBS and pretreated with a standardized dose of 10 µg/mL exosomes from the respective experimental groups for 48 h prior to functional evaluations. An equal volume of PBS served as the negative control.

Cell proliferation assay

Cell viability was initially assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay. BCa cells (1 × 10³/well) seeded in 96-well plates were incubated with CCK-8 reagent (Beyotime, China) for 2 h, after which the absorbance was measured at 450 nm. For colony formation assays, cells (1 × 10³/well) were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured for 10 days. The resultant colonies were fixed and stained with 1% crystal violet for quantitative analysis. Cellular proliferative capacity was further evaluated using an EdU labeling kit (Epizyme, China). Cells cultured in 12-well plates (1 × 10³/well) for 48 h were subjected to a 2-hour EdU labeling phase. Subsequently, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100, and counterstained with Hoechst 33,342. The proliferation index was defined as the ratio of EdU-positive cells (green) to the total number of Hoechst-positive nuclei (blue).

Cell migration and invasion analysiscell

Cell motility was evaluated utilizing wound healing and Transwell assays. For wound healing, confluent BCa cell monolayers, pretreated with standardized exosomes, were artificially scratched and imaged at 0 and 24 h to monitor migration. Transwell assays were performed using Matrigel-coated (for invasion) or uncoated (for migration) inserts (Corning, NY, USA). Exosome-pretreated cells suspended in serum-free medium were seeded into the upper chambers, while the lower chambers were filled with medium containing 10% FBS as a chemoattractant. Following a 24-hour incubation, cells that traversed the membrane were fixed with formaldehyde, stained with crystal violet, and quantified. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Western blotting

Total protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, China) and quantified via a BCA assay kit (Beyotime, China). Equivalent amounts of protein samples were resolved by SDS-PAGE and subsequently transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). After blocking with 5% nonfat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with specific primary antibodies diluted in TBST.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC), immunofluorescence (IF) and RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) assays

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) assays were performed on human BCa tissue sections, as previously described [22]. Immunofluorescence (IF) and RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) assays were utilized to ascertain the subcellular localization of YBX1 and LNCOC1 in T24 and EJ cells, respectively, adhering to established protocols [22, 23]. The Cy3-conjugated LNCOC1 probes were synthesized by RiboBio (Guangzhou, China). To examine LNCOC1 expression patterns in clinical specimens, FISH analysis was conducted on human BCa tissue Sect. [24]. For the clinical correlation analysis involving the 96 BCa patient cohort, LNCOC1 and HIF1A expression levels were quantified from individual patient tissue sections. Specifically, LNCOC1 relative expression was determined by measuring the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of representative FISH fields using ImageJ software. Concurrently, HIF1A abundance was assessed via IHC by calculating the mean optical density (MOD) of the positively stained areas.

Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) and subcellular fractionation assay

Total RNA was extracted utilizing TRIzol reagent (Beyotime, China), followed by cDNA synthesis using the HiScript III RT SuperMix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Target gene expression was quantified by real-time PCR using AceQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, China). Relative expression levels were normalized to β-actin and U6 (primer sequences are provided in Table S2). Furthermore, the PARIS Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was employed for subcellular RNA fractionation to analyze the nucleocytoplasmic distribution of LNCOC1, following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Exosomal lncRNA high-throughput sequencing and analysis

Freshly isolated exosomes derived from T24 cells cultured under normoxic and hypoxic conditions were subjected to lncRNA sequencing by Guangzhou Tenng Technology Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China). Briefly, low-input RNA amplification was performed using 2–4 µL of exosomal RNA with the RNALib Single-Cell Whole Transcriptome Amplification Kit (11–12 cycles). The amplified cDNA was purified via magnetic beads and quantified using the QuantiFluor dsDNA System. Subsequently, sequencing libraries were constructed from 1 ng of cDNA using the Lifeint Transpose DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (13 amplification cycles). Following magnetic bead purification and QuantiFluor dsDNA quantification, the fragment size of the library was evaluated using a Qsep100 S2 cartridge. Libraries carrying specific adapters [N101(i5) and N9XX(i7)] were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform in paired-end 150 bp (PE150) mode.

For bioinformatics analysis, raw sequencing data were subjected to stringent quality control. The expression levels of lncRNAs in each sample were quantified and normalized to Transcripts Per Million (TPM). Differential expression analysis was performed utilizing the DEGseq software package. Differentially expressed lncRNAs (DELs) were identified based on the strict threshold criteria of |log2(Fold Change)| > 2 and P < 0.05.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

The wild-type (WT) or mutant (MUT) binding regions within the 2.0 kb upstream promoter sequences of LNCOC1 and PIK3CA were cloned into pGL3 vectors to generate pGL3-LNCOC1 and pGL3-PIK3CA, which were then transfected into BCa cells. For PIK3CA promoter analysis, cells were co-transfected in 96-well plates with 0.2 µg pcDNA-YBX1, 0.2 µg pcDNA-LNCOC1, 50 nM YBX1-siRNA, or 50 nM LNCOC1-siRNA alongside respective controls. For LNCOC1 promoter tests, cells were co-transfected with the HIF1A overexpression plasmid (or empty vector) under normoxic conditions to isolate the direct transcriptional activation by HIF1A. Alternatively, cells were co-transfected with HIF-1α siRNA and subsequently subjected to normoxic or hypoxic conditions. Luciferase activity was measured in accordance with the protocols outlined in the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, USA). The assay employs Renilla luciferase expression as an internal control to standardize experimental results.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)

ChIP assays were performed using the EZ-ChIP™ kit (Millipore, MA, USA) with specific antibodies against HIF1A and YBX1 (Abcam, UK). In brief, formaldehyde-crosslinked cells were lysed and sonicated to shear chromatin into 300–500 bp DNA fragments. The chromatin lysates were then immunoprecipitated with antibody-coated microwells. Following crosslink reversal, the enriched DNA fragments were purified and quantified via qPCR. Input DNA and normal IgG served as controls to confirm interaction specificity. Three biological replicates were analyzed.

Interaction prediction and RNA pull-down assays

RNA-protein interaction studies were conducted utilizing the Pierce™ Magnetic RNA-Protein Pull-Down System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Briefly, in vitro transcribed full-length LNCOC1 sense and antisense RNAs were 3’-end labeled with biotinylated cytidine bisphosphate and immobilized on streptavidin-coated magnetic beads. T24 cell lysates were incubated with the RNA-bound beads for 2 h at 4 °C under continuous rotation. After stringent washing, bound proteins were eluted and visualized via silver staining, and differentially expressed protein bands were excised for mass spectrometry identification. Furthermore, to precisely map the interaction interface between LNCOC1 and YBX1, the catRAPID algorithm (http://service.tartaglialab.com/page/catrapid_omics2_group) was employed [25]. Based on the predicted interaction propensity scores, truncated LNCOC1 sequences were designed: Fragment 1 (1-300 nt), Fragment 2 (1500–2000 nt), and Fragment 3 (4800–5300 nt). These truncated sequences were transcribed in vitro, biotin-labeled, and subjected to the identical RNA pull-down procedure described above, followed by Western blotting to validate YBX1 binding efficiency.

RNA binding protein immunoprecipitation (RIP)

The RIP test was performed with the EZ-Magna RIP™ Kit (Millipore, Billerica, MA) in accordance with the usual procedure, with anti-YBX1 antibodies (Millipore, Billerica, MA) employed for specific RNA-protein complex isolation. Following immunoprecipitation, the captured RNAs were reverse transcribed and analyzed by qRT-PCR to determine their relative abundance.

In vivo murine models

The in vivo functional evaluation of exosomes was conducted using two mouse models. For xenograft tumor assessment, four-week-old male BALB/c nude mice (four per group) received a subcutaneous injection of 5 × 10⁶ T24 cells resuspended in 100 µL of PBS into the right flank. Once tumor volumes reached approximately 100 mm³, the animals were randomly allocated into four experimental groups. The mice in each group received 100 µL of PBS containing 1 × 10⁸ exosome via intravenous delivery every third day. Tumor progression was tracked until study completion three days post-sixth injection, when tumors were harvested and weighed. For metastasis evaluation, nude mice (three per group) were given 1 × 10⁶ T24 cells plus 1 × 10⁸ exosomes in 100 µL PBS through a vein, followed by weekly exosome treatments. After six injections, metastasis was quantified using bioluminescent imaging (Bruker in vivo imaging system) before euthanasia. Lung tissues were then excised for H&E staining and metastatic nodule counting. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care Committee of Southern Theater Command General Hospital (No. NBZQZYY2024055).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (version 26.0) and GraphPad Prism (version 9.0). Quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent experiments. Before parametric testing, data distribution was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test for normality and Levene’s test for homogeneity of variance. For normally distributed data, two-group comparisons were analyzed using the two-tailed Student’s t-test. For comparisons among multiple groups, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test was performed. Non-parametric tests were applied when data did not meet normality assumptions. The χ² test was used to analyze categorical clinical variables. Survival analyses were evaluated using the Kaplan-Meier method with log-rank tests. For univariate and multivariate analyses, the Cox proportional hazards regression model was utilized, incorporating covariates such as age, gender, tumor grade, and TNM stage. Statistical power for in vivo animal studies was predetermined based on preliminary data to ensure adequate sample sizes (power > 0.8 at α = 0.05). A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Hypoxia-induced exosomes enhance the proliferation and metastasis of BCa cells in vitro

Tumor hypoxia, a fundamental hallmark of solid malignancies, significantly drives cancer progression [7–9]. To investigate the impact of hypoxia-induced exosomes on BCa cell behavior, we successfully isolated exosomes from T24 and EJ cell lines. TEM confirmed their classic lipid bilayer morphology and revealed enhanced exosome secretion under hypoxic conditions (Fig. 1A). Western blot analysis verified the robust expression of exosomal marker proteins (CD63, CD83, TSG101, and Flotillin-1) and the absence of the negative control calnexin across all groups (Fig. 1B), ensuring high-purity isolation. NTA demonstrated a typical size distribution ranging from 50 to 150 nm in diameter (Fig. 1C). Consistent with the TEM observations, hypoxic BCa cells yielded significantly higher nanoparticle concentrations than their normoxic counterparts (Fig. 1D).

Following the confirmation of successful exosome extraction, we utilized PKH67 fluorescent labeling to demonstrate that these secreted vesicles were readily internalized by recipient T24 and EJ cells (Fig. 1E). To evaluate their functional impact in vitro, we conducted a comprehensive series of phenotypic evaluations. Compared to the PBS control and normoxic exosomes, treatment with hypoxia-derived exosomes markedly augmented the proliferative, migratory, and invasive capacities of both BCa cell lines, as evidenced by CCK-8, colony formation, EdU, wound healing, and Transwell assays (Fig. 1F-J).

Exosomal LNCOC1 is induced by HIF1A under hypoxia and highly expressed in BCa patients

Exosomes function as crucial intercellular mediators by transporting diverse regulatory nucleic acids, notably long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), to modulate the TME [16, 26–28]. To identify the specific exosomal lncRNAs responsible for driving BCa malignancy under hypoxic stress, we performed high-throughput lncRNA sequencing on exosomes derived from T24 cells cultured under both normoxic and hypoxic conditions (Fig. 2A). Transcriptomic profiling revealed substantial hypoxia-induced alterations, identifying 319 differentially expressed exosomal lncRNAs (log2FoldChange > 2, P < 0.05), including 199 upregulated and 120 downregulated transcripts (Fig. 2B). Given that hypoxia generally enhances exosome secretion, we focused our investigation on the top five most significantly upregulated candidates. Subsequent qRT-PCR validation across both T24 and EJ cell lines confirmed that LNCOC1 was the most abundantly enriched lncRNA in hypoxia-induced exosomes (Fig. 2C).

To elucidate the upstream regulatory mechanisms driving this enrichment, we investigated whether the hypoxia-responsive transcription factor HIF1A modulates LNCOC1 expression. ChIP assays demonstrated that HIF1A physically associates with the LNCOC1 promoter region under both normoxic and hypoxic conditions (Fig. 2D). In silico sequence analysis using UCSC and JASPAR databases identified three putative hypoxia-responsive elements (HREs: CGACGCGCGC, GGACGCGCGG, and GCACGCGCGC) within this promoter (Fig. 2E). Dual-luciferase reporter assays further confirmed that HIF1A overexpression significantly augmented the luciferase activity of reporters containing the wild-type HRE1 and HRE2 sequences, whereas reporters with mutant HREs exhibited no response (Fig. 2F). This firmly establishes that HIF1A directly transactivates LNCOC1. Consistently, targeted HIF1A knockdown drastically suppressed the hypoxia-induced upregulation of LNCOC1 within both the intracellular compartment and the secreted exosomes (Fig. 2G-I). Collectively, these data demonstrate that HIF1A directly induces the transcription and subsequent exosomal packaging of LNCOC1 under hypoxic conditions.

To validate the clinical relevance of the HIF1A/LNCOC1 axis, we analyzed tumor tissues from a cohort of 96 BCa patients. Clinically, elevated LNCOC1 expression positively correlated with advanced histological tumor grade and was significantly higher in patients presenting with metastasis compared to those with localized disease (Fig. 2J-K). Furthermore, LNCOC1 levels exhibited a strong positive correlation with HIF1A expression across these BCa specimens (Fig. 2L-N). These clinical findings robustly support our in vitro data, implying that hypoxia-driven HIF1A upregulates LNCOC1 in human tumors to fuel BCa progression.

Hypoxia-derived exosomal LNCOC1 promotes BCa cells malignant biological behavior in vitro and in vivo

To elucidate the functional role of hypoxia-induced LNCOC1, we generated stable LNCOC1-knockdown and overexpression BCa cell lines using lentiviral vectors (Figure S1A-B). After selecting the most efficient knockdown construct (sh-LNCOC1#1), we quantified LNCOC1 levels in exosomes derived from these engineered cells. Following co-culture, recipient T24 and EJ cells exhibited significantly higher intracellular LNCOC1 abundance when treated with hypoxia-derived exosomes compared to those treated with normoxic exosomes (Fig. 3A). Crucially, LNCOC1 accumulation was markedly diminished in cells treated with LNCOC1-depleted hypoxic exosomes (hypoxic sh-LNCOC1) relative to the hypoxic negative control (hypoxic NC) group (Fig. 3B). Comprehensive in vitro functional evaluations demonstrated that hypoxic exosomes significantly promoted BCa cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. Notably, the specific depletion of LNCOC1 from these hypoxic exosomes virtually abolished these oncogenic effects (Fig. 3C–F).

To further validate this oncogenic role, we employed a complementary gain-of-function approach by engineering normoxic BCa cells to overexpress LNCOC1. Subsequent in vitro assays revealed that treatment with exosomes isolated from these cells (Normoxic LNCOC1-OE exosomes) significantly enhanced the proliferation, migration, and invasion of recipient BCa cells compared to control normoxic exosomes (Figure S2A-F). This reinforces the evidence that exosomal LNCOC1 acts as a potent driver of BCa progression.

To evaluate the in vivo impact, we established subcutaneous xenograft and experimental lung metastasis murine models. Mice received systematic intravenous administration (six tail vein injections, once every three days) of the specified T24-derived exosome preparations. At the 30-day predefined endpoint, the volume and weight of the subcutaneous tumors were significantly increased in the hypoxic exosome group. In stark contrast, the removal of LNCOC1 from the hypoxic exosomes effectively eliminated this tumor-promoting capability (Fig. 3G-I). Furthermore, no substantial changes in body weight were observed across any of the groups, indicating an absence of overt systemic toxicity (Fig. 3H). Consistent with these findings, the lung metastasis model revealed significantly fewer metastatic lesions and nodules in mice treated with hypoxic sh-LNCOC1 exosomes compared to the hypoxic NC cohort (Fig. 3J-L). Aligning with our in vitro data, these in vivo results serve as a robust proof-of-concept, highlighting the oncogenic and pro-metastatic potential of systemically administered exosomal LNCOC1.

LNCOC1 activates the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway by upregulating PIK3CA

To decipher the downstream molecular mechanisms of LNCOC1, we performed RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) on T24 cells overexpressing LNCOC1 (LNCOC1-OE) and control cells. Transcriptomic profiling identified 695 upregulated and 187 downregulated differentially expressed genes (Fig. 4A). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that LNCOC1 overexpression significantly altered multiple cancer-related pathways, predominantly the PI3K/AKT, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, and JAK-STAT signaling pathways (Fig. 4B). Given its prominent enrichment, we prioritized the PI3K/AKT axis for further investigation. Subsequent qRT-PCR validation of core pathway components identified PIK3CA as the most significantly upregulated gene in LNCOC1-OE T24 cells (Fig. 4C). Furthermore, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) robustly confirmed the enrichment of the PI3K/AKT signaling signature upon LNCOC1 overexpression (Fig. 4D). These findings strongly suggest that LNCOC1 drives PI3K/AKT pathway activation by upregulating PIK3CA.

At the protein level, Western blot analysis demonstrated that PIK3CA expression was markedly elevated in T24 cells treated with hypoxia-derived exosomes compared to those exposed to normoxic exosomes (Figure S3A-B). Crucially, this induction was completely abolished when cells were treated with LNCOC1-depleted hypoxic exosomes (hypoxic sh-LNCOC1) (Figure S3C-D), confirming that exosomal LNCOC1 is responsible for PIK3CA upregulation. To further validate this regulatory axis, we evaluated the expression of PIK3CA and downstream effector proteins (p85α, AKT, and p-AKT). LNCOC1 overexpression concomitantly enhanced the levels of PIK3CA, p85α, and phosphorylated AKT (p-AKT), while total AKT expression remained unaffected. Conversely, targeted silencing of PIK3CA effectively reversed these LNCOC1-induced molecular alterations (Fig. 4E and S2E). Collectively, these results demonstrate that LNCOC1 relies on PIK3CA upregulation to hyperactivate the PI3K/AKT signaling cascade in BCa cells.

LNCOC1 requires PIK3CA to promote BCa cell proliferation, migration, and invasion

Next, we investigated whether exosomal LNCOC1 drives BCa malignancy specifically through the upregulation of PIK3CA. Comprehensive phenotypic evaluations, including CCK-8, EdU, Transwell, and wound healing assays, demonstrated that co-transfection with siPIK3CA significantly attenuated the enhanced proliferative, migratory, and invasive capacities induced by LNCOC1 overexpression (Fig. 4F-I). These findings confirm that PIK3CA is functionally indispensable for LNCOC1-mediated BCa progression.

LNCOC1 interacts with the transcription factor YBX1

The biological function of a lncRNA is intrinsically dictated by its subcellular localization [29]. Cellular FISH and subcellular fractionation assays revealed that LNCOC1 is predominantly enriched in the nucleus of BCa cells (Fig. 5A-B). Given this pronounced nuclear presence, we focused our investigation on its potential role in transcriptional regulation.

Because nuclear lncRNAs frequently modulate gene expression via physical interactions with RBPs [30], we performed an in vitro RNA pull-down assay coupled with silver staining in T24 cells. This assay revealed a distinct cluster of 40–55 kDa proteins specifically associated with LNCOC1 (Fig. 5C). Subsequent mass spectrometry (MS) identified the 50 kDa transcription factor YBX1 as the primary interacting candidate (Fig. 5D). We orthogonally validated this physical interaction through targeted RNA pull-down followed by Western blotting, as well as via RIP assays (Fig. 5E-F).

Recognizing that exosomes frequently package and transfer functional RNA fragments rather than intact full-length transcripts, we sought to map the specific essential binding interface between LNCOC1 and YBX1. In silico prediction using the catRAPID algorithm identified a high-affinity binding hotspot at the 5’ terminus (1-243 nt, interaction propensity > 50) and a secondary hotspot near the 3’ end (4900–5260 nt), separated by a non-binding cold spot(Figure S4A-D). Guided by these predictions, we synthesized biotin-labeled truncated LNCOC1 probes for in vitro RNA pull-down. Consistent with the bioinformatic modeling, Western blot analysis demonstrated that YBX1 was abundantly precipitated by Fragment 1 (1-300 nt) and to a lesser extent by Fragment 3 (4800–5300 nt), whereas Fragment 2 (1500–2000 nt) completely failed to bind(Figure S4E). This suggest the 5’ terminal fragment of LNCOC1 as the essential domain mediating its physical interaction with YBX1. Furthermore, immunofluorescence (IF) analysis confirmed the precise nuclear co-localization of LNCOC1 and YBX1 in both T24 and EJ cells (Fig. 5G).

To bridge this interaction to downstream signaling, sequence analysis of the PIK3CA promoter revealed a putative YBX1-binding motif (Fig. 5H). Clinical data mining via the ChIPBase database (TCGA BCa cohort) and the analysis of our 96-patient BCa tissue cohort both demonstrated a strong positive correlation between PIK3CA and YBX1 expression (Fig. 5I-J). However, LNCOC1 expression did not correlate with overall YBX1 abundance (Fig. 5K). Integrating these spatial, physical, and clinical findings, we hypothesize that LNCOC1 functions not by altering YBX1 expression, but by acting as a molecular scaffold to directly recruit YBX1 to the PIK3CA promoter.

LNCOC1 promotes PIK3CA transcription by recruiting YBX1 to PIK3CA promoter

To explicitly validate the regulatory relationship between YBX1 and PIK3CA, we modulated YBX1 expression in BCa cells. Western blot analysis demonstrated that YBX1 knockdown significantly suppressed PIK3CA expression, whereas YBX1 overexpression markedly enhanced it (Fig. 10A-B). Subsequent chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)-qPCR assays utilizing a YBX1-specific antibody revealed a significant enrichment of YBX1 at the PIK3CA promoter compared to the IgG control (Fig. 10C). To confirm the functional consequence of this physical binding, we performed dual-luciferase reporter assays. Overexpression of YBX1 substantially elevated the luciferase activity of the wild-type PIK3CA promoter (PIK3CA-WT) construct, but failed to transactivate the mutant promoter (PIK3CA-MUT), confirming a highly specific transcriptional activation (Fig. 10D).

Crucially, we investigated whether LNCOC1 dictates the binding efficiency of YBX1 to this promoter. ChIP-qPCR analysis showed that silencing LNCOC1 drastically reduced YBX1 enrichment at the PIK3CA promoter, an effect that was successfully rescued by concomitant YBX1 overexpression. Conversely, LNCOC1 overexpression augmented YBX1 binding, which was subsequently abrogated by siYBX1 co-transfection (Fig. 10E). This dynamic recruitment pattern perfectly mirrored the responsive changes observed in PIK3CA promoter-driven luciferase activity (Fig. 10F) and downstream PIK3CA protein expression levels (Fig. 10G-H).

Functionally, phenotypic rescue experiments demonstrated that the suppressive effects of LNCOC1 knockdown on the proliferation, migration, and invasion of T24 cells were effectively reversed by YBX1 overexpression (Figure S5A-D). In contrast, YBX1 silencing abrogated the enhanced malignant capabilities driven by LNCOC1 overexpression in EJ cells (Figure S5E-H). Importantly, neither the knockdown nor the overexpression of LNCOC1 altered total YBX1 mRNA or protein levels (Figure S6A-D), confirming its exclusive functional role as a structural scaffold rather than an upstream regulator of YBX1 expression.

Finally, to directly establish the mechanistic link between exosomal LNCOC1 transfer and downstream PIK3CA regulation, we performed ChIP and dual-luciferase reporter assays on recipient T24 cells treated with distinct exosome populations. Consistent with our intracellular transfection models, cells treated with hypoxic exosomes exhibited significantly enhanced YBX1 enrichment at the PIK3CA promoter and consequently increased WT promoter transcriptional activity compared to those treated with normoxic exosomes. Strikingly, the specific depletion of LNCOC1 from these hypoxic vesicles (Hypoxic sh-LNCOC1 exosomes) effectively abrogated this enhanced YBX1 recruitment and promoter activation. Conversely, in a complementary gain-of-function approach, treatment with normoxic LNCOC1-overexpressing exosomes (Normoxic LNCOC1-OE exosomes) robustly mirrored the hypoxic effects, forcefully driving YBX1 recruitment and WT PIK3CA transactivation. Crucially, none of these specific exosome treatments could activate the MUT PIK3CA promoter lacking the YBX1 binding motif (Figure S7A-B). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the extracellular delivery of LNCOC1 acts as a critical upstream mediator that specifically dictates YBX1-driven PIK3CA transcriptional activation.

Circulating exosomal LNCOC1 emerges as a preliminary candidate biomarker for BCa

Given that tumor-derived exosomal lncRNAs can enter the peripheral circulation [32, 33], we evaluated the clinical translational potential of circulating exosomal LNCOC1. We isolated plasma-derived exosomes from 96 BCa patients and 48 healthy individuals utilizing a combination of size-exclusion chromatography and ultrafiltration. The successful isolation was rigorously validated: TEM imaging revealed the characteristic lipid bilayer morphology of the vesicles (Fig. 11A), NTA demonstrated a typical size distribution ranging from 50 to 150 nm (Fig. 11B), and Western blot analysis confirmed the abundant presence of exosome-associated markers (CD63, TSG101, and Flotillin) (Fig. 11C). Subsequent qRT-PCR analysis revealed that circulating exosomal LNCOC1 levels were significantly elevated in BCa patients compared to healthy controls (Fig. 11D). Furthermore, its concentration was markedly higher in plasma exosomes derived from patients with metastatic disease than in those with localized tumors (Fig. 11E), a pattern that perfectly aligns with our observations in tissue specimens (Fig. 2J-N).

To thoroughly assess its clinical significance, the 96 BCa patients were stratified into high-expression (n = 48) and low-expression (n = 48) cohorts based on the median expression level of plasma exosomal LNCOC1. Clinicopathological analyses revealed that elevated exosomal LNCOC1 levels significantly correlated with aggressive disease features, including larger tumor size (P = 0.002), advanced histological grade (P = 0.022), higher tumor T stage (P = 0.033), and the presence of lymph node metastasis (P = 0.001) (Table S3). Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards models further substantiated its prognostic relevance (Tables S4 and S5). To evaluate its diagnostic efficacy, Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed. Consistent with our rigorous evaluation, exosomal LNCOC1 exhibited a moderate discriminatory capacity, yielding an Area Under the Curve (AUC) of 0.789 for distinguishing BCa patients from healthy individuals, and an AUC of 0.765 for differentiating metastatic from non-metastatic cases (Fig. 11F-G). Finally, Kaplan-Meier survival analyses demonstrated that BCa patients harboring high levels of circulating exosomal LNCOC1 experienced significantly poorer overall and disease-free survival (Fig. 11H-I). Collectively, these findings suggest that circulating exosomal LNCOC1 holds substantial promise as a preliminary candidate biomarker for BCa diagnosis and prognostication.

Discussion

BCa remains a leading malignancy of the urinary tract [1, 2]. Despite continuous advancements, current clinical management strategies often lack sufficient specificity, underscoring the pressing need for enhanced early detection techniques and more targeted therapeutic interventions to improve patient outcomes [3–5]. Within the TME, hypoxia is a well-established driver of BCa progression [7–9]. Accumulating evidence indicates that hypoxic stress accelerates tumorigenesis by stimulating the release of exosomes and modulating their molecular cargo, a mechanism highly consistent with our observations [34–36]. While emerging studies suggest that exosomal lncRNAs serve as crucial mediators of intercellular communication [26–28], their precise roles in orchestrating crosstalk between hypoxic and normoxic BCa cells remain largely elusive. This study addresses this critical gap by elucidating the regulatory functions and underlying mechanisms of hypoxia-induced exosomal lncRNAs in BCa progression. Through the application of lncRNA sequencing, we found that LNCOC1 is the most significantly up-regulated lncRNA in hypoxia-induced exosome samples. Mechanistically, HIF1A directly binds to HREs in the LNCOC1 promoter, driving its transcription and subsequent packaging into exosomes. Additionally, through tissue ISH and IHC, we observed that LNCOC1 expression was elevated in BCa tissues relative to normal tissues, and HIF1A expression exhibited a positive correlation with LNCOC1. These findings suggest that, under hypoxic conditions, HIF1A upregulates LNCOC1 expression to promote BCa progression. Additionally, we detected LNCOC1 in serum exosomes from BCa patients and healthy controls. Serum exosomal LNCOC1 demonstrated moderate discriminatory ability for BCa patients and correlated with unfavourable prognosis, indicating its potential to serve as a diagnostic biomarker candidate for BCa.

Through high-throughput lncRNA sequencing, we identified LNCOC1 as the most significantly upregulated transcript in hypoxia-induced exosomes. Mechanistically, we demonstrated that HIF1A directly binds to the HREs within the LNCOC1 promoter, driving its transcription and subsequent exosomal packaging. Clinically, utilizing FISH and IHC on human BCa tissue sections, we observed that LNCOC1 expression was significantly elevated relative to normal tissues and positively correlated with HIF1A abundance. These findings establish that hypoxia-driven HIF1A upregulates LNCOC1 to fuel BCa progression. Furthermore, we detected elevated LNCOC1 levels in plasma-derived exosomes from BCa patients compared to healthy controls. Exhibiting a moderate discriminatory capacity and a strong correlation with unfavorable patient prognosis, circulating exosomal LNCOC1 emerges as a promising preliminary candidate biomarker for BCa diagnosis.

LNCOC1, initially identified as an ovarian cancer-associated lncRNA, has been implicated in driving the malignant phenotypes of ovarian cancer, endometrial carcinoma, and melanoma [20, 37, 38]. However, its specific oncogenic role in BCa was previously unexplored. Importantly, our mechanistic findings reveal a notable divergence from previously published paradigms regarding its primary mode of action. For instance, Tao et al. reported that LNCOC1 predominantly functions as a cytoplasmic ceRNA, sponging miR-34a and miR-34c to promote ovarian cancer progression [20]. In stark contrast, our cellular FISH and subcellular fractionation assays demonstrated that LNCOC1 is highly enriched within the nucleus of BCa cells. Rather than acting as a cytoplasmic miRNA sponge, nuclear LNCOC1 promotes BCa progression by serving as a molecular scaffold, physically interacting with the RBP YBX1 and recruiting it directly to the PIK3CA promoter to activate transcription.

This significant mechanistic divergence aligns with the well-established consensus that the functional roles and subcellular distributions of lncRNAs are highly tissue-specific [19, 29]. The differential localization of LNCOC1 is likely shaped by the unique TME of BCa. Specifically, hypoxic stress—a central driver of BCa progression in our model—can significantly alter nucleocytoplasmic transport dynamics. Furthermore, the highly tissue-specific repertoire and relative abundance of interacting proteins, such as YBX1, in BCa cells may tether LNCOC1 within the nucleus to execute targeted transcriptional regulatory roles. This critical comparison highlights the multifaceted, context-dependent, and tissue-specific nature of LNCOC1 in cancer biology, suggesting that its underlying molecular mechanisms cannot be generalized across different malignancies.

Our comprehensive in vitro and in vivo evaluations corroborated that hypoxia-derived exosomes markedly potentiate BCa proliferation, migration, and invasion—effects that were largely abolished upon LNCOC1 depletion. These findings indicate exosomal LNCOC1 as a potent contributor to the malignant BCa phenotype. To delineate the downstream effector pathways, we integrated RNA sequencing with KEGG and GSEA analyses, identifying the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway as the primary target activated by LNCOC1 via PIK3CA upregulation. The PI3K/AKT cascade is a master regulator of diverse oncogenic processes, governing cellular growth, survival, metabolic homeostasis, angiogenesis, and motility [39–44].

Because the regulatory mechanisms of lncRNAs are intrinsically dictated by their subcellular distribution [29], the predominant nuclear localization of LNCOC1 strongly pointed toward a role in chromatin organization or transcriptional control rather than cytoplasmic mRNA modulation. Current paradigms indicate that nuclear lncRNAs frequently exert their functions by acting as scaffolds for DNA or RBPs [30]. Following this rationale, our RNA pull-down, mass spectrometry, and RIP assays successfully identified the transcription factor YBX1 as a robust binding partner of LNCOC1. Importantly, a unique feature of exosomal communication is the frequent packaging and transfer of regulatory RNA fragments rather than intact full-length transcripts. By integrating catRAPID bioinformatic predictions with truncated RNA pull-down assays, we successfully mapped the essential YBX1-binding interface to the 5’ terminal fragment (1-300 nt) of LNCOC1. The robust binding capacity of this specific fragment provides a crucial molecular basis explaining how fragmented LNCOC1 packaged within exosomes can still efficiently recruit YBX1 to target promoters in recipient cells. This structural insight further refines our understanding of the specificity of exosomal lncRNA-mediated gene regulation within the TME.

YBX1, a highly conserved member of the DNA/RNA-binding cold shock domain protein family, is a well-documented oncogenic factor [45, 46]. It is frequently overexpressed in various human malignancies and serves as a reliable predictor of unfavorable prognosis [47–50]. Recent studies have highlighted its versatile roles in tumor progression; for instance, YBX1 can interact with MNX1 to stabilize PD-L1 mRNA, thereby promoting tumor immune escape [51], and can recruit CHMP4C to catalyze the m5C methylation of caspase-8 transcripts, effectively blocking necroptosis to drive prostate cancer advancement [52]. Our findings uncover a novel facet of YBX1 biology in BCa, demonstrating that LNCOC1 facilitates its specific recruitment to the PIK3CA promoter to enhance transcriptional activation. Importantly, YBX1 overexpression rescued the suppressive phenotypes induced by LNCOC1 knockdown, whereas YBX1 depletion attenuated the oncogenic effects of LNCOC1 upregulation. Given that total YBX1 expression remained unaltered following LNCOC1 modulation, our results unequivocally suggest that LNCOC1 acts as a critical upstream molecular scaffold, harnessing the oncogenic transcription factor YBX1 to drive PIK3CA expression and BCa progression.

While the broad paradigm of exosome-mediated signaling in the hypoxic TME is well-recognized, our study provides several specific conceptual advances regarding BCa progression. Most notably, we elucidate a novel nuclear scaffold function for exosomal LNCOC1, which is fundamentally distinct from its previously reported cytoplasmic ceRNA mechanisms in other malignancies. By directly recruiting YBX1 to the PIK3CA promoter, LNCOC1 establishes a specific epigenetic regulatory axis that drives PI3K/AKT hyperactivation. Moreover, by validating the elevated presence of exosomal LNCOC1 in patient plasma and correlating it with metastatic progression, our study successfully bridges the gap between TME communication mechanisms and non-invasive liquid biopsy applications. Collectively, these findings provide novel insights into exosomal lncRNA signaling and effectively complement previously reported oncogenic pathways in BCa.

Although our findings highlight the translational potential of circulating exosomal LNCOC1, we acknowledge several limitations regarding its clinical application. First, its independent diagnostic performance is moderate (AUCs of 0.789 and 0.765). Second, our clinical cohort is relatively small (n = 96) and derived from a single institution. Therefore, the current data support exosomal LNCOC1 primarily as a preliminary candidate biomarker rather than a definitive diagnostic tool. Future studies involving independent, large-scale, and multi-center cohorts are essential to validate its clinical utility. Additionally, given the profound heterogeneity of BCa, a single exosomal transcript may not suffice for independent clinical decision-making. Future efforts should focus on establishing a multiplex diagnostic panel that combines exosomal LNCOC1 with existing clinical parameters to achieve the high sensitivity and specificity required for routine application. Finally, the interpretation of our in vivo exosome experiments should be carefully moderated. While our systemic administration model provides a robust proof-of-concept for the oncogenic potential of exosomal LNCOC1, the intravenous injection of purified exosomes may generate supraphysiological concentrations that do not perfectly recapitulate the continuous, localized release of endogenous vesicles within the natural TME. Consequently, the exact extent to which endogenous tumor-derived exosomes mediate these metastatic effects under strictly physiological conditions remains to be fully elucidated in future studies.

Conclusion

In summary, this study demonstrates that hypoxia-induced exosomal LNCOC1 is significantly upregulated in BCa tissues and patient serum, which closely correlates with poor clinical prognosis. Functionally, exosomal LNCOC1 enhances BCa malignant phenotypes in vitro and provides in vivo proof-of-concept for its oncogenic potential. Mechanistically, hypoxia-induced HIF1A drives LNCOC1 transcription, which subsequently promotes PIK3CA expression by directly recruiting YBX1 to its promoter. These findings elucidate a novel HIF1A/LNCOC1/YBX1/PIK3CA regulatory axis in BCa. Furthermore, circulating exosomal LNCOC1 emerges as a preliminary candidate biomarker and a potential therapeutic target, though its clinical utility warrants future validation in independent, large-scale cohorts.
