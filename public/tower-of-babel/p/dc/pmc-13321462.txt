Factors Associated With Optimal Patient‐Clinician Communication Among Cancer Survivors
Authors: Keilan Johnson, Catherine G. Pratt, Sofia W. Viana, Jenna N. Whitrock, Alique Topalian, Melinda Butsch Kovacic, Jack Kues, Christina Pinkston, Jianmin Pan, Shesh Rai, Robert M. Van Haren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionPatient‐clinician communication impacts the well‐being and medical outcomes of cancer survivors. However, there is a gap in current literature regarding patient‐specific factors influencing optimal communication between people with cancer and their clinicians.MethodsTo better understand factors associated with optimal clinician communication among cancer survivors, survey responses from the Health Information National Trends Survey Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results database were analyzed using logistic regression.ResultsOf the 1123 cancer survivors responding to the survey, 46% (n = 518) were males, 87% identified as white (n = 973), and the average age was 71 years. Patient‐clinician communication was reported to be best when patients asked questions (73%) and poorest when dealing with feelings of uncertainty (47%). Descriptive analyses demonstrated differences in selected communication domains among Hispanic participants and racial minority groups. However, race and ethnicity were not independently associated with patient‐clinician communication in adjusted analyses.ConclusionsHigher educational attainment and shorter time since diagnosis were associated with optimal patient–clinician communication experiences. Descriptive analyses suggested differences in overall communication score distributions across racial and ethnic groups; however, race and ethnicity were not independently associated with patient–clinician communication in adjusted analyses.

Introduction

The American Cancer Society estimated that over two million new cancer cases will be diagnosed in the United States in 2025 [1]. Advances in medical science offer improved and curative treatments for a multitude of cancers, contributing to a reduction in mortality rates [1, 2]. Despite this encouraging progress, cancer care remains a complex, often fragmented process for patients requiring a multidisciplinary approach reliant upon complex inter‐specialty care coordination [3]. Patient satisfaction, psychosocial adjustment, collaborative decision‐making, and adherence to treatment have been shown to be associated with the quality of patient‐clinician communication [4].

Patient‐clinician communication plays an integral role in patient well‐being and medical outcomes because of its intimate relationship with patients' experiences. Poor communication between patients and their clinicians can have a variety of negative consequences, including fear of abandonment [5, 6], feelings of hopelessness [7], and clinician mistrust [8, 9]. Previous research among individuals with breast cancer found high rates of discordance between patients and clinicians regarding the content, emotional support, and perceived importance of discussions during oncologic consultation visits [10]. A meta‐analysis of qualitative studies identified sincerity, normality, hope, involvement, and autonomy as key values among patients with cancer [11]. Furthermore, care coordination, information delivery, and decision‐making were specific themes independently related to both patient preferences and patient‐clinician communication [11]. Any perceived deficits in these sentiments have been shown to decrease a patient's willingness to undergo cancer screenings [12] and receive treatment following a cancer diagnosis [13, 14].

While there are more people surviving cancer, disparities in outcomes persist. A disproportionately higher rate of cancer deaths occurs among Black and American Indian/Alaska native populations [15]. Additionally, less than 16 years of education and residing in a non‐metropolitan area, both factors associated with lower health literacy among individuals with cancer, have been identified as pertinent risk factors associated with increased cancer mortality [15, 16, 17]. These disparities are likely multifactorial and may reflect not only structural and socioeconomic barriers but also differences in patient‐clinician communication and trust within the healthcare system. A prior study done by Islami et al. suggests that trust also plays a unique role in patient‐clinician communication, especially among Hispanic and other individuals facing socioeconomic or health literacy barriers [15]. Despite these disparities and the known role of patient‐clinician communication, there is relatively little research regarding patient‐specific factors influencing optimal communication between patients with cancer and their clinicians. This study seeks to identify demographics and clinical factors associated with optimal patient‐clinician communication among cancer survivors responding to the Health Information National Trends Survey (HINTS).

Methods

Health Information National Trends Survey

A retrospective cross‐sectional analysis of previously collected survey responses from the HINTS 5 Cycle 4 Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database was performed. HINTS‐SEER is a survey‐based project developed by the National Cancer Institute that links HINTS survey methodology with selected SEER registry sites to oversample cancer survivors. The sample design for HINTS 5 Cycle 4 (2020) consisted of a single‐mode mail survey, using the Next Birthday Method (who answers a survey when more than one eligible person lives in the same household) for respondent selection [16]. This pilot project was created by the National Cancer Institute to oversample cancer survivors for HINTS by using data from SEER registries. Across all survey cycles (2003–2020), the HINTS program has enrolled more than 110,000 participants from the U.S. population. The present study was restricted to respondents who self‐reported a history of cancer and met the study inclusion criteria. Our final sample was 5,762, representing only 9% of the total participant pool [16]. The collaboration with SEER registries increased the sample size of survivors and ensured participant cancer incidence and survivability data were available. A full description of this project's purpose, methods, and survey instruments is documented in the HINTS‐SEER Methodology Report [16]. The present study was reviewed and approved as exempt by the University of Cincinnati Institutional Review Board (IRB#2024–1182).

Cancer survivors' participants were recruited via the United States Postal Service (USPS) between January 11 and August 20, 2021. The HINTS‐SEER survey included standard HINTS items assessing patient‐clinician communication, which have been used across multiple survey cycles. A subset of these items was analyzed in the present study. The survey utilized a 4‐point Likert scale for questions related to patient‐clinician communication. A sample of the survey is provided in the Supporting Information (Figure S1). The present analysis included survey questions assessing patients’ perceptions of patient‐clinician communication, including their ability to ask questions, involvement in decision‐making, and understanding the next steps in their care plan. Other questions assessed healthcare clinicians' acknowledgment and discussion of patient feelings, clarity of explanations, and time spent (Figure S2). Responses were dichotomized into ‘always’ vs. ‘usually/sometimes/never’ to define optimal vs. non‐optimal patient‐clinician communication, consistent with prior analyses of HINTS communication items and to facilitate interpretation of communication quality outcomes.4 Race and ethnicity were categorized as non‐Hispanic White and Hispanic/other racial and ethnic minority groups, consistent with the available sample size and distribution of participants within the HINTS‐SEER dataset. These categories were used in descriptive analyses and regression models.

Inclusion criteria from the HINTS‐SEER database for the present study were invasive cancer diagnosis, older than eighteen years of age, last contact with SEER registries no earlier than January 1, 2016, and a date of cancer diagnosis prior to 2018. Survivors whose only diagnosis was non‐melanoma skin cancer were excluded from the study since these cancers are associated with distinct clinical courses and survivorship experiences compared with other invasive malignancies. Implicit stratification involved sorting eligible cancer survivors by race/ethnicity and years since diagnosis prior to systematic sampling, to improve representativeness across key subgroups. For the present study, we further excluded non‐response to any patient‐clinician communication questions or responders with fewer than five complete responses to the seven patient‐clinician communication questions (Figure 1).

Statistical Analysis

Cohort demographics (e.g., age, sex, race, employment status, insurance, education level, and income ranges) were summarized with means and standard deviations for continuous variables and counts and percentages for categorical variables. Seven question responses related to patient‐clinician communication were summed based on individual responses (1 = “always,” which was defined as optimal; 2 = “usually”; 3 = “sometimes”; and 4 = “never,” defined as nonoptimal). In addition to the item‐specific logistic regression analyses, descriptive subgroup analyses were performed using the summed communication score. These analyses examined the distribution of overall communication scores across racial and ethnic groups and were intended to provide descriptive context rather than infer independent associations. To identify factors associated with patient‐clinician communication, univariate and multivariable logistic regression models comparing optimal vs. non‐optimal responses for each question were assessed using a statistical significance set at p < 0.05. Each analysis used complete case analyses, defined as a respondent answering the investigated patient‐clinician question. Our sample is defined in Figure 1. Statistical analyses were performed with SAS software (version 9.4) [17]. Consort flowcharts were created with the consort package in R version 4.4.1. The sampling weights are not considered in the analysis, and results may not be expanded to the entire population.

Results

A final cohort of 1123 cancer survivors was included in the study. The analyzed cohort consisted of 46% (n = 518) male participants with an average age of 70.9 years (standard deviation of 11.5 years) (Table 1). The majority of the participating survivors identified as White (87%, n = 973), while Hispanic, Asian, Black, and Pacific Islander ethnicities were analyzed separately. Most participants were insured (98%, n = 1104), retired (66%, n = 746), and had higher levels of education (some college: 21%, college graduate: 28%, postgraduate: 29%). The most common cancers represented were prostate (23%, n = 261), breast (23%, n = 257), and melanoma (10%, n = 110). Other prevalent cancers included the corpus uteri, thyroid, non‐Hodgkin lymphoma, and kidney. Most survivors had received cancer treatment (92%, n = 1032) and had been diagnosed with local (67%, n = 756) or regional (23%, n = 259) disease.

Regarding patient‐clinician communication, fewer than half of participating survivors (47%) reported that the clinicians always helped them to deal with uncertainty about their health or health care (Table 2). Fifty‐one percent reported that their feelings and emotions were always given attention. More than half (56%) reported feeling that their clinicians spent enough time with them. Over 70% of survivors reported they were always given a chance to ask clinicians about their health‐related questions. A similar proportion of survey respondents reported that explanations provided by clinicians were always shared with them in ways that they could understand. Two‐thirds (66%) reported that clinicians always involved them in decisions regarding their health care. Similarly, 67% reported that they were always provided with the information needed to manage their health.

Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses identified time since diagnosis, registry site location, and education as factors associated with specific patient–clinician communication items (Table 3). Compared to those with more recent cancer diagnoses (0–5 years since diagnosis), those diagnosed 11 or more years earlier were less likely to report that clinicians always gave adequate attention to their feelings and emotions (MV OR: 0.65, 95% CI 0.46–0.91; p = 0.01). Additionally, compared to breast cancer survivors, individuals with other cancer types (other than prostate cancer) were less likely to report clinicians helping them deal with uncertainty about their health or health care (MV OR: 0.69, 95% CI (0.49, 0.98), p = 0.04). Education levels, particularly at the college or postgraduate levels compared to those with less than high school, were associated with higher levels of reporting the clinicians involved patients in decisions (MV OR: 1.72, 95% CI (1.14, 2.61), p = 0.01), clear explained the next steps (MV OR: 1.55, 95% CI (1.02, 2.35), p = 0.04), and explained things clearly (MV OR: 1.97, 95% CI (1.29, 3.03), p = 0.002). Patient sex, employment status, and race and ethnicity were not associated with patient‐clinician communication.

Discussion

This study is among the first to use the HINTS‐SEER database to understand patient‐clinician communication. The findings highlight clinician strengths and weaknesses regarding communication with patients with cancer. Cancer survivors report that most clinicians commonly communicate necessary and comprehensible information, ensure adequate time for questions, and involve patients in decision‐making for their cancer care. Still, Hispanic and other patients with increased time since diagnosis were more likely to report that they did not always have the opportunity to ask all their health‐related questions and that their clinicians did not give adequate attention to their feelings and emotions. While open communication techniques empower cancer survivors and increase their autonomy during treatment [4, 5], it is important for clinicians to be aware of patient‐related factors contributing to the perception of poor communication. Strategies to improve clinician communication in non‐optimal patient–clinician encounters may help close the gap in cancer care in patient populations that experience cancer disparities [4].

This study also identified several aspects of patient–clinician communication that may benefit from improvement. Specifically, cancer survivors most frequently report deficiencies in communication related to addressing feelings of uncertainty, acknowledging emotions and fears, and spending sufficient time with patients. These findings suggest that emotional support remains an important unmet need among cancer survivors. Recognition of the emotional impact of a cancer diagnosis and its influence on patients' perceptions of their healthcare team is critical for effective communication and high‐quality cancer care. Individuals affected by cancer commonly experience fear, shock, and uncertainty, which may contribute to anxiety, depression, and an increased risk of suicide [18, 19]. Emotional care is therefore fundamental to helping patients cope throughout treatment and survivorship [20]. Taken together, these findings suggest that clinicians may not consistently address the emotional and psychosocial concerns of cancer survivors, highlighting an opportunity to further integrate emotional support into patient‐centered cancer care.

Patients with greater time since diagnosis may report different experiences on specific patient‐clinician communication items, particularly less frequent reports that clinicians gave adequate attention to their feelings and emotions, which could reflect transitions away from specialized oncology teams to other healthcare providers who may be less focused on addressing cancer‐related emotional and psychosocial needs. As stated previously, cancer affects individuals far beyond diagnosis and treatment. One prospective, population‐based study found that patients with a history of cancer experience a decreased quality of life in comparison to healthy controls, even over time [21]. Our study serves to remind clinicians that cancer is a life‐altering diagnosis that demands long‐term optimization of communication skills.

Numerous studies have shown that clinician empathy and compassionate communication are strongly associated with improved patient satisfaction [21, 22, 23]. Empathy and sympathy are particularly important among Hispanic and other populations that have historically experienced mistrust in the healthcare system [24, 25, 26, 27, 28]. We previously conducted a prospective analysis of patients being offered surgical resection for stage 1 lung cancer, in which a substantial proportion declined recommended surgery (21%). This decision was associated with Black race, lower income, and poorer patient–clinician communication, with qualitative findings highlighting communication quality, trust, health literacy, fear, and shared decision‐making as central influences on treatment decisions [29]. The present population‐based analysis using the HINTS‐SEER database extends these findings by demonstrating differences in selected patient–clinician communication domains among racial and ethnic groups. Although descriptive differences were observed across racial and ethnic groups, with Hispanic participants and participants from racial minority groups less likely than non‐Hispanic White participants to report that clinicians always gave them the opportunity to ask all the health‐related questions they had. Race and ethnicity were not independently associated with patient–clinician communication in adjusted analyses [30, 31]. These findings suggest that differences in communication experiences may exist across patient populations and warrant further investigation. Future studies should evaluate the potential contributions of factors such as health literacy, language concordance, educational attainment, and healthcare access to better understand and address communication barriers in cancer care.

In addition, the findings of this study can also be interpreted within the framework of Epstein and Street's patient‐centered communication model, which describes six core communication functions in cancer care: Fostering healing relationships, exchanging information, responding to emotions, managing uncertainty, making decisions, and enabling patient self‐management. Several HINTS communication items map closely onto these domains. For example, patients' perceptions that clinicians did not explain information in an understandable way reflect challenges in information exchange, while reports that clinicians did not address feelings and emotions correspond to deficiencies in responding to emotions. Similarly, lower ratings related to involvement in care discussions may reflect barriers in shared decision‐making. Interpreting these findings within an established patient‐centered communication framework highlights that communication disparities may extend across multiple dimensions of the clinical interaction rather than representing isolated communication deficits [32, 33].

There are several limitations in the present study to consider. The participants involved in the study were nearly homogeneous, with a large majority identifying as non‐Hispanic White, insured, educated, and retired. The lack of heterogeneity within the cohort is especially concerning, considering the known racially associated disparities in cancer outcomes that exist. This raises the question of whether survey‐based survivorship databases are truly representative of cancer populations. One explanation could be a lack of participation in research studies by minorities. Conversely, specific minorities are more likely to die from cancer than other races and ethnicities. As such, this survey‐based survivorship database may or may not be an appropriate reflection of the cancer survivor population. The presence of statistical significance despite such a homogeneous sample also denotes the potential for a greater magnitude of findings with a more diverse population. Also, the HINTS survey relies on self‐reported data, which may be subject to recall bias. Furthermore, the communication questions broadly referenced interactions with “all doctors, nurses, or other healthcare professionals,” rather than a specific clinician or clinical encounter. As a result, participants may have based their responses on multiple interactions across different healthcare settings, potentially introducing variability in how communication experiences were interpreted and recalled. Additionally, although this study evaluated how sex, race, registry site, and time since diagnosis relate to patient‐clinician communication, the specific dichotomous grouping of measures (“always” vs. “usually”, “sometimes”, and “never”) may have influenced our findings, and alternative categorizations (e.g., grouping ‘always/usually’ vs. ‘sometimes/never’) may yield different interpretations. Furthermore, the survey data potentially introduces sampling bias as discussed above.

Conclusions

This study is among the first to use the HINTS database to identify factors associated with optimal patient–clinician communication among cancer survivors. Overall, these findings highlight the importance of effective patient–clinician communication in shaping patients' understanding, engagement, and experiences with cancer care. Notably, cancer survivors reported lower ratings for communication related to emotional support and managing feelings of uncertainty, underscoring the need for clinicians to address the emotional and psychosocial dimensions of survivorship in addition to providing medical information. Multivariable analyses identified education level and time since diagnosis as important factors associated with patient–clinician communication. Survivors with higher educational attainment were more likely to report involvement in decision‐making, understanding next steps in care, and receiving information in a way they could understand. In contrast, survivors further removed from their cancer diagnosis were less likely to report that clinicians adequately addressed their feelings and emotions, suggesting that communication needs may evolve throughout the survivorship trajectory. Descriptive subgroup analyses of the overall communication score suggested differences in the distribution of non‐optimal communication experiences among Hispanic and other participants. However, race and ethnicity were not independently associated with patient–clinician communication in adjusted analyses. These findings may reflect the influence of factors such as health literacy, educational attainment, and language barriers on communication experiences. Additional research is needed to better understand how these factors interact and to identify strategies that promote equitable, patient‐centered communication for all cancer survivors.
