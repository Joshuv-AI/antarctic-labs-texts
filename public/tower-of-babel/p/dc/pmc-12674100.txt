Redox‐Engineered Co─Cu─Zr Perovskites for Durable Anion Exchange Membrane Electrolysis
Authors: Logeshwaran Natarajan, Ta Thi Thuy Nga, Minjae Kwon, Pandian Mannu, Chung‐Li Dong, Sungho Lee, Dong Jin Yoo, Young Jun Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe oxygen evolution reaction (OER) remains a critical bottleneck in water electrolysis and air‐battery systems, requiring electrocatalysts with both high activity and long‐term stability. Herein, Ba(ZrxCuyCo1‐x‐y)O3‐δ (BZCC), a novel mixed‐metal perovskite synthesized is introduced via a low‐temperature hydrothermal method, which integrates multiple catalytic functionalities for efficient OER in alkaline media. The catalyst simultaneously activates the adsorbate evolution mechanism (AEM) and the lattice oxygen oxidation mechanism (LOM), driven by the redox flexibility of Co3+/Co4+. The incorporation of Cu2+/Cu3+ modulates the electronic structure to enhance intrinsic activity, while Zr4+ contributes to structural robustness against alkaline corrosion. The generation of oxygen vacancies (δ) through Cu/Co redox cycling, stabilized by Zr4+, facilitates OH− adsorption and promotes continuous O2 evolution. BZCC delivers a low overpotential of 238 mV at 10 mA cm−2 and demonstrates excellent durability, showing only a 116 mV increase in operating potential after 200 h. When applied in a single‐cell anion exchange membrane water electrolyzer (AEMWE), the BZCC‐based anode achieves a current density of 1.0 A cm−2 at 2.07 V, maintaining stable operation without performance degradation over 25 h. This combination of redox flexibility, electronic modulation, and structural stability positions BZCC as a scalable, high‐performance electrocatalyst for sustainable AEMWE.

Introduction

The accelerated depletion of fossil fuels and the growing demand for clean energy have driven the urgent development of sustainable energy technologies. Among them, water electrolysis, metal‐air batteries, and fuel cells represent promising platforms for carbon‐neutral energy conversion and storage.[ 1 , 2 , 3 ] Anion exchange membrane water electrolyzers (AEMWEs) combine the low cost of alkaline water electrolysis with the high efficiency of zero‐gap proton exchange membrane water electrolyzers (PEMWEs), making them a promising technology for green hydrogen production.[ 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 ] In alkaline media, earth‐abundant transition metals such as Co, Ni, and Fe can serve as efficient oxygen evolution reaction (OER) catalysts, offering a clear cost advantage over PEMWEs as they allow the use of non‐noble metals.[ 9 , 10 ] Nevertheless, the practical performance of AEMWEs remains constrained by sluggish OER kinetics,[ 11 , 12 ] since currently available catalysts provide insufficient activity and durability, leading to relatively low efficiency and limited operational lifetime. Therefore, developing low‐cost electrocatalysts that couple high intrinsic activity with long‐term stability is crucial to advance AEMWE technology.

Perovskite‐type transition metal oxides with the general formula ABO3 have emerged as compelling candidates, owing to their structural tunability, compositional flexibility, and high catalytic activity, especially in alkaline environments.[ 13 , 14 ] In these materials, the A‐site is typically occupied by rare‐earth or alkaline‐earth metals (e.g., Ba, Sr, La), while the B‐site hosts redox‐active transition metals (e.g., Co, Fe, Cu), which are primarily responsible for catalytic function. The OER activity is governed by two distinct yet complementary mechanisms: the adsorbate evolution mechanism (AEM) and the lattice oxygen oxidation mechanism (LOM). In AEM, surface‐bound oxygen intermediates (OH*, O*, OOH*) are adsorbed on B‐site cations, and catalytic activity is dictated by the Sabatier principle, wherein optimal binding strength—correlated with eg orbital filling (ideally ≈1.0) and the position of the d‐band center – maximizes reaction rates.[ 15 ] In contrast, LOM involves direct participation of lattice oxygen (O2−) and oxygen vacancies (Vo), which can couple with surface species to bypass the high‐energy OOH* formation step. This mechanism often enables a reduction in η by 50–100 mV compared to AEM alone, thereby significantly enhancing activity.[ 16 ] Performance tuning can be further achieved by increasing metal‐oxygen covalency, adjusting the oxygen p‐band closer to the Fermi level, and minimizing Coulomb repulsion within eg orbitals, offering a versatile design space for the development of high‐performance OER electrocatalysts.[ 17 , 18 , 19 ]

Recently, several studies have demonstrated that electronic‐structure‐engineered double and triple perovskite oxides, such as PrBa0.5Sr0.5Co1.5Fe0.5O5+δ,[ 14 ] La1‐xSrxCoO3‐δ,[ 18 ] Ba0.5Sr0.5Co0.8Fe0.2O3‐δ,[ 20 ] SrNb0.1Co0.7Fe0.2O3‐δ,[ 21 ] BaCo0.5‐xFe0.5‐xZrxYxO3‐δ,[ 22 ] SrCoO3/CaCoO3,[ 23 ] can simultaneously activate both AEM and LOM, exhibiting outstanding OER performance. In these systems, Co‐based B‐site cations serve as major active sites for adsorbates and lattice oxygen participation. Nevertheless, structural degradation due to cation leaching in alkaline conditions remains a limiting factor. In contrast, zirconium‐based perovskites offer superior structural robustness and chemical stability under harsh alkaline environments.[ 24 , 25 ] Qiao et al. demonstrated that Zr4+ co‐doping in SrCo0.8Fe0.2O3₋δ nanosheets significantly enhanced OER activity and stability in alkaline media by stabilizing the perovskite lattice.[ 26 ] These findings highlight the potential of Zr4+‐stabilized perovskites combined with redox‐active dopants (e.g., Co, Cu) to develop high‐performance, durable OER electrocatalysts.

Herein, we report the synthesis of a novel mixed‐metal perovskite, BaZr0.5Cu0.25Co0.25O3‐δ (BZCC), via an autoclave‐assisted hydrothermal method. The material is designed to precisely control phase, composition, and morphology through a dissolution‐recrystallization growth mechanism. The incorporation of Co3+/4+ and Cu2+/3+ into the Zr4+ lattice in a 0.5:0.25:0.25 molar ratio activates synergistic redox coupling, promoting both AEM and LOM. Charge compensation between lower‐valence Co/Cu and Zr4+ induces the formation of oxygen vacancies, thereby facilitating lattice oxygen participation and improving catalytic efficiency. BZCC exhibits an exceptionally low η of 218 mV at 10 mA cm−2, surpassing single‐metal analogs such as BaZr0.5Cu0.5O3‐δ (BZCu) and BaZr0.5Co0.5O3‐δ (BZCo). The structural integrity of the perovskite is confirmed by a near‐ideal Goldschmidt tolerance factor of 1.02, supporting its phase stability. Notably, the catalyst maintains robust performance during 200 h at 100 mA cm−2 with negligible morphological degradation post‐operation. Furthermore, zero‐gap anion exchange membrane water electrolyzer (AEMWE) employing BZCC achieves a high current density of 1.0 A cm−2 at a cell voltage 2.07 V, comparable to the commercial counterparts. This study provides a novel strategy for the replacement of noble metals in AEMWE applications.

Result and Discussion

Morphology and Microstructure Characterizations

Figure 1 illustrates the synthetic route for both single and mixed‐metal perovskite structures. In particular, a novel mixed‐metal perovskite BZCC was synthesized via a facile autoclave‐assisted hydrothermal method, enabling precise compositional control and nanoparticle formation. The morphological evolution of barium zirconate follows a dissolution‐regrowth mechanism, beginning with nucleation and progressing to the formation of well‐defined polyhedral crystals under optimized alkaline conditions.[ 27 ] In this study, Cu and Co cations were rationally incorporated into the B‐site of the zirconate lattice and effectively coupled with oxygen intercalation, forming a structurally unique BZCC phase.[ 28 ] The schematic diagram of the proposed unit cell highlights B‐site ordering and possible structural configuration (Figure S1, Supporting Information). The composition was deliberately selected, as a 50% B‐site substitution has been demonstrated to promote abundant oxygen vacancy formation, advantageous for electrochemical processes, while the equimolar incorporation of Cu and Co was intended to enable the concurrent operation of AEM and LOM pathways.[ 29 , 30 ] The detailed reaction mechanism for the hydrothermal synthesis of BZCC is described in the Experimental Section. The X‐ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of prepared BZCC, BZCo, and BZCu perovskites are indexed to the cubic Pm‐3m space group (No. 221) of BaZrO3 (JCPDS No: 00‐006‐0399),[ 31 ] consistent with the optimized bare barium‐zirconate (BZ) sample (Figure 2a). Substitution of B‐site cations with Co and Cu is evidenced by slight diffraction peak shifts, indicating changes in lattice parameter. These shifts are consistent with reference patterns for Cu‐Zr (JCPDS No: 00‐040‐1322) and Co‐Zr (JCPDS No: 00‐036‐1095), confirming successful incorporation into the BZCC structure.[ 32 ] The close resemblance of BZCC to the BZCo and BZCu patterns further supports effective Co and Cu substitution. The dominant reflections correspond to the perovskite phase, with no major impurity phases detected. A weak additional reflection at ≈24° observed in some doped samples can be assigned to a trace BaCO3 phase, with an estimated intensity below 3% of the main peaks, which may occasionally form during low‐temperature hydrothermal synthesis due to CO2 uptake.[ 33 ]

The structural stability of the perovskite system can be evaluated using the Goldschmidt tolerance factor, defined as t=rA+rO2(rB+rO), where rA, rB, and rO represent the ionic radii of the A‐site, B‐site, and oxygen ions, respectively.[ 22 ] In BZCC, Ba2+ occupies the A‐site, while Zr4+, Cu2+, and high‐spin Co3+ occupy the B‐sites. The calculated tolerance factor for BZCC is t = 1.016, which falls within the ideal range for cubic perovskite stability, indicating a structurally favorable configuration. Similarly, the Goldschmidt tolerance factors for BZCo, BZCu, and BZ were calculated as 1.047 (assuming high‐spin Co3+), 1.002, and 1.003, respectively. Physical surface roughness and nanoscale ridges can enhance OH− ion transport and electron transfer by modulating the local surface energy. The morphological features of BZ were confirmed by field emission scanning electron microscope (FE‐SEM) imaging, which showed the formation of well‐developed perovskite grains (Figure S2, Supporting Information). Elemental composition and spatial distribution of Ba, Zr, and O were further verified by EDX elemental mapping. Notably, BZCC exhibited a uniform dice‐like morphology with well‐dispersed Co and Cu dopants, suggesting effective substitution at the B‐sites (Figure S3, Supporting Information). To gain further structural insight, high‐resolution transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was performed. As shown in Figure 2b, BZCC exhibited well‐defined lattice fringes. The corresponding high‐angle annular dark‐field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF) and annular bright‐field scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) images revealed atomic‐level ordering, confirming the incorporation of Co and Cu into the perovskite lattice (Figure 2c). Figure 2d presented an atomic‐resolution STEM image with clearly periodic and symmetric lattice fringes, supporting the crystallographic stability inferred from the calculated tolerance factor. Figure 2e shows a SAED pattern with distinct diffraction features consistent with a crystalline cubic perovskite phase. Elemental mapping via energy‐dispersive X‐ray spectroscopy (EDS) in Figure 2f–j confirms the uniform distribution of Ba, Zr, O, Cu, and Co throughout the sample, supporting the hypothesis of homogeneous doping at the atomic scale. Comparative TEM and EDX analyses of BZCo and BZCu samples were provided in Figures S4 and S5 (Supporting Information), with further discussion available in the Supporting Information.

Chemical composition and bonding configurations were investigated using X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). Figure S6a (Supporting Information) shows the high‐resolution Ba 3d spectra of BZCC, BZCo, BZCu, and BZ, which were deconvoluted into two major spin‐orbit components: 3d5/2 and 3d3/2 , consistent with previous reports.[ 34 ] Compared to pristine BZ, the samples containing transitional metal exhibited slight positive shifts in binding energy, suggesting the formation of oxygen vacancies or changes in the metal‐oxygen covalency induced by Co or Cu incorporation. Due to the overlapping binding energies of Co 2p and Ba 3d, a detailed analysis was performed for Co 2p3/2 and Co 2p1/2 , along with Ba 3d5/2 and 3d3/2 , in BZCC and BZCo samples. Figure S6b (Supporting Information) shows that the Zr 3d peak is also deconvoluted into 3d5/2 and 3d3/2 components. Compared to pristine BZ, the Co‐ and Cu‐incorporated samples exhibited a slight negative shift (≈0.9 eV) in the 3d5/2 peak, indicating increased electron density and a more reduced chemical environment around the Zr4+ ions.[ 35 , 36 ] This shift suggests partial reduction of Zr4+ to Zr3+, reflecting enhanced surface reductions and electronic defect formation, which may facilitate charge transfer to Co/Cu active sites and improve OER kinetics by stabilizing OOH* intermediates. Figure S6c (Supporting Information) showed the O 1s spectra deconvoluted into Olat (lattice oxygen, ≈529.1 eV), Ovac (vacancy‐related defect oxygen, ≈530.7 eV), and Oads (surface ‐OH or adsorbed oxygen).[ 37 ] The slightly lower Ovac fraction and higher Oads fraction in BZ indicate a larger contribution from surface ─OH/adsorbates, whereas the BZCC, BZCu, and BZCo samples exhibit comparable vacancy‐related features. This trend supports the presence of similar oxygen‐vacancy populations in the Co‐ and Cu‐containing perovskites. These results collectively confirm the successful formation of mixed‐metal perovskites (BZCC, BZCo, and BZCu), highlighting their catalytic relevance in enhancing OER activity through electronic modulation and defect engineering. Low‐temperature electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) measurements of BZCC were carried out to verify the formation of oxygen vacancies. A pronounced signal at g ≈2.0053 was observed, which can be attributed to unpaired electrons associated with oxygen vacancies (Figure S7, Supporting Information).[ 38 ]

To gain further insight into the atomic and coordination environments of the synthesized perovskites, X‐ray absorption spectroscopy was employed, including X‐ray absorption near‐edge structure (XANES), Fourier‐transformed extended X‐ray absorption fine structure (FT‐EXAFS), and wavelet‐transformed EXAFS (WT‐EXAFS). Figure S8a (Supporting Information) presents the Ba L3‐edge XANES spectra of BZCC, BZCo, BZCu, and pristine BZ. The absorption edge (≈5242 eV) and the post‐edge features remain largely unchanged upon Co/Cu doping, indicating that the oxidation state of Ba2+ is preserved.[ 39 ] The Ba L3‐edge of BZCC shifts to lower energy compared with that of BZCo and BZCu, indicating a slightly lower oxidation state, which reflects the formation of oxygen vacancies. The Ba L3‐edge FT‐EXAFS spectra showed a pronounced first‐shell Ba─O peak at ≈2.3–2.5 Å, followed by a broad second‐shell signal in the 2.5–3.5 Å range, attributed to overlapping Ba‐M (e.g., Ba─Zr, Ba─Co, and Ba─Cu) scattering paths due to their similar bond lengths (Figure 3a).[ 40 ] These assignments are further corroborated by WT‐EXAFS analysis, which clearly resolves the spatial contributions from Ba─O and Ba─M interactions (Figure 3b,c; Figure S8b,c, Supporting Information).[ 41 ] Additionally, the bond length of Ba─O reduced in the BZCC catalyst compared to that of other catalysts, suggesting the existence of more oxygen vacancies, which could lead to enhancing the overall electrochemical performance of the BZCC catalyst. The Co K‐edge XANES spectra were analyzed to determine the oxidation state. The main absorption peak of CoO, representative of Co2+, appears near ≈7725 eV (Figure S9a, Supporting Information). In contrast, BZCo and BZCC show absorption edge and main absorption peak positions shifted toward higher energies, indicating the presence of higher oxidation states (Co3+).[ 42 ] Moreover, the main peak intensity of BZCC is lower than BZCC, suggesting a higher electron density around Co sites in BZCC due to fewer unoccupied states. Figure 3d shows the corresponding FT‐EXAFS spectra, showing a strong Co─O peak at ≈1.45–1.55 Å and broader features in the 2.5–3.5 Å range assigned to Co─Co/Zr and Co─Co/Cu scattering, confirming successful incorporation of Co into the B‐site lattice.[ 43 ] WT‐EXAFS maps further support these findings by resolving Co─O, Co─Zr, and Co─Cu coordination environments (Figure 3e,f; Figure S9b, Supporting Information).[ 44 ] Again, the contraction in the Co‐O and Co‐Co bond length in BZCC compared to BZCo further indicates the presence of oxygen/cationic vacancies. Similarly, Cu K‐edge XANES spectra were obtained to evaluate the oxidation state of Cu in BZCu and BZCC (Figure S10a, Supporting Information). The XAS spectral features of BZCu and BZCC are compared with Cu2O and CuO standard references that are indicative of Cu1+ and Cu2+ states, respectively. However, BZCu and BZCC exhibit edge shift to higher energies, demonstrating the presence of a higher oxidation state of Cu (Higher than Cu2+ state). The peak shift is more significant in BZCC than that of BZCu, likely due to the strong electronic interactions between Cu and Co, which can be attributed to the charge transfer from Cu to Co sites, as evidenced by the increased electron density at Co sites in BZCC observed in Figure S9 (Supporting Information).[ 45 ] The FT‐EXAFS spectra reveal a dominant Cu─O peak at 1.5–1.6 Å, along with additional contributions in the 2.5–3.6 Å range corresponding to Cu─Zr and Cu─Co scattering paths, indicating successful incorporation of Cu into the B‐site (Figure 3g).[ 46 ] These coordination environments are further resolved by WT‐EXAFS maps (Figure 3h,i; Figure S10b,c, Supporting Information). Compared to the reference Cu foil, the BZCC spectrum exhibits more diffuse and lower‐R signals, reflecting a disordered and heterometallic coordination environment.[ 47 ] Similar to the FT‐EXAFS Co R‐space spectra, the bond length of Cu‐O reduced in the BZCC catalyst yet again confirms strong electronic interactions with Zr and the formation of oxygen vacancies.

Electrochemical OER Evaluation

The electrocatalytic OER performance and structural stability of the synthesized perovskite catalysts—BZCC, BZCo, BZCu, and pristine BZ—were evaluated in 1.0 M KOH using a standard three‐electrode configuration. As shown in Figure 4a, the iR‐corrected Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) curves indicate that BZCC exhibits the lowest overpotential of 218 mV at a benchmark current density of 10 mA cm−2, outperforming commercial IrO2 (255 mV) and other reference catalysts, including BZCo (260 mV), BZCu (295 mV), and BZ (420 mV). This superior activity is attributed to the flexible valence states of Co (Co2+/Co3+/Co4+), which optimize eg orbital occupancy, as well as to the synergistic electronic interaction between Co and Cu, which likely weakens the M─OH bond and reduces the overpotential.[ 48 ] The Tafel plots further confirm the kinetic advantages of BZCC, showing a Tafel slope of 60.1 mV dec−1, significantly lower than those of BZCo (67.5 mV dec−1), BZCu (71.2 mV dec−1), and BZ (119.0 mV dec−1), indicating faster OER kinetics (Figure 4b).[ 49 , 50 ] These results suggest that BZCC follows a dual‐pathway OER mechanism involving both the conventional AEM and the LOM. The presence of oxygen vacancies in the BZCC lattice is expected to facilitate OH− adsorption and reduce the energy barrier for the OOH* formation, thereby accelerating the overall reaction rate.[ 17 ] To clarify whether the BaCO3 impurity observed in the XRD patterns contributes to OER activity, BZ samples synthesized with different reaction times were investigated. Notably, a weak BaCO3 reflection appeared only in the 24 h sample, while the 12 h and 48 h samples remained free of this feature (Figure S11a, Supporting Information). Electrochemical analysis revealed that the catalytic performance improved with increasing reaction time, correlating with the structural development of the perovskite phase rather than the presence of BaCO3 (Figure S11b,c, Supporting Information). These results confirm that BaCO3 is a non‐contributing by‐product, whereas the intrinsic OER activity originates from the well‐crystallized perovskite structure.

To investigate the surface and interfacial properties of the catalysts, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was performed under open‐circuit potential conditions. Figure S12 (Supporting Information) shows the Nyquist plot of BZCC, BZCo, BZCu, and BZ, fitted using an equivalent circuit model. Notably, the BZCC exhibits a substantially lower charge‐transfer resistance of 1.35 Ω, which is 2.8 and 4.1 times lower than the BZCo and BZCu, respectively. This enhancement is attributed to Cu‐induced electronic modulation, which weakens the Co─O bond and facilitates faster interfacial charge transport.[ 49 ] Consequently, BZCC also demonstrates reduced diffusion resistance, enabling more efficient OH− ion transport than BZ. Further evaluation of surface catalytic activity was conducted by estimating electrical double‐layer capacitance (Cdl) from cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements in the non‐faradaic region at various scan rates (Figure S13, Supporting Information). The linear fits of current density versus scan rate for BZ, BZCu, BZCo, and BZCC are shown in Figure S14a–d (Supporting Information).[ 51 ] The corresponding electrochemically active surface areas (ECSAs), calculated from the Cdl values, are summarized in Figure S14e (Supporting Information). Among all samples, BZCC exhibits the highest ECSA of 70.5 cm2, outperforming BZ (23.1 cm2), BZCo (55.1 cm2), and BZCu (58.8 cm2), indicating a significantly larger density of accessible active sites.

The turnover frequency (TOF), defined as the number of oxygen molecules produced per active site per second, was also evaluated for all samples (Figure 4c). This parameter is critical for assessing intrinsic catalytic activity and guiding the optimization of energy‐demanding water oxidation processes.[ 16 ] At 1.65 V vs reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE), BZCC exhibits a TOF of 0.038 s−1, which is higher than other samples, BZ (0.001 s−1), BZCu (0.016 s−1), and BZCo (0.034 s−1), demonstrating its superior catalytic efficiency and the feasibility of the mixed‐metal perovskite structure for alkaline OER.[ 52 ] Figure 4d further compares the OER performance of BZCC with that of previously reported perovskite and transition‐metal‐based catalysts. The data highlight the remarkable activity of BZCC and underscore its strong potential for application in future energy conversion technologies. A detailed comparison of overpotential, Tafel slope, and TOF values for the reported catalysts was provided in Table S1 (Supporting Information).

Significance of Perovskites OER mechanisms

The co‐existence of AEM and LOM on perovskite surfaces can be rationalized using various electronic descriptors, including the nature of B‐site transition metal cation, oxygen binding energy, eg orbital occupancy, metal d‐band center, and oxygen p‐band center relative to the Fermi level (Figure 5a).[ 53 ] A Fermi level located near the O 2p band facilitates the formation of oxygen vacancies, which in turn enhances metal‐oxygen covalency and improves OER performance.[ 54 ] The extended AEM and LOM reaction pathways are schematically illustrated in Figure 5b,c, with a detailed explanation provided in Supporting Information.[ 55 ] In the AEM pathway, the OER proceeds via a stepwise adsorption of water molecules and the sequential formation of OH*, O*, and OOH* intermediates on the metal site, followed by O2 evolution and active site regeneration via proton–electron transfer (Figure 5b).[ 56 ] In contrast, the LOM pathway involves direct lattice oxygen participation: surface hydroxylation leads to the formation of peroxo‐like O─O bonds (e.g., O─M─OO), and the subsequent release of O2 occurs through lattice oxygen coupling, leaving behind oxygen vacancies that are later refilled (Figure 5c). This pathway circumvents the high‐energy OOH* intermediate and provides a lower‐barrier alternative to AEM, particularly in systems with strong metal–oxygen covalency and mobile lattice oxygen.[ 56 ] Recent studies have demonstrated that the relative dominance of AEM or LOM is determined by the binding strength of oxygen intermediates and the nature of the B‐site dopant, with weakly binding cations (e.g., Cu) favoring LOM and strongly binding cations (e.g., Co) favoring AEM.[ 57 , 58 ] The BZCC system incorporates both Cu and Co, thereby enabling concurrent AEM–LOM pathways. Zhou et al. further reported that Cu doping in Co‐based catalysts promotes lattice oxygen vacancy formation, thereby facilitating the LOM pathway, while Co intrinsically stabilizes the AEM pathway.[ 59 ] The BZCC system incorporates both Cu and Co, thereby enabling concurrent AEM–LOM pathways.

Figure 5d shows the long‐term stability test results of BZCC, BZCu, and BZCo in 1.0 M KOH. Among these, BZCC exhibits the earliest onset potential (0.67 V), which is ≈1.24‐fold lower than BZCu and BZCo. It also retains excellent stability over 200 h at a high current density of 100 mA cm−2, which is attributed to a synergistic interplay of lattice strain, oxygen vacancy dynamics, and structural integrity induced by Co‐Cu co‐doping.[ 60 ] Post‐stability LSV measurements confirm that all samples maintain high catalytic activity, with BZCC exhibiting the most stable performance (Figure 5e–g). This suggests that the electronic environment surrounding Ba2+ ions plays a critical role in stabilizing the lattice structure. Furthermore, post‐mortem TEM analysis of BZCC reveals distinct lattice fringe patterns, indicating well‐preserved structural integrity after prolonged operation (Figure S15, Supporting Information).

Following the demonstration of high OER activity in a half‐cell system, the BZCC catalyst was further tested in a full‐cell AEMWE setup to assess its practical applicability.[ 61 ] Figure 6a presents a schematic illustration of the AEMWE setup, with detailed membrane electrode assembly (MEA) fabrication procedures provided in the Experimental Section described in the Experimental Section and corresponding images shown in Figure S16a–c (Supporting Information).[ 62 ] The anode was prepared by coating BZCC catalyst onto a Ni substrate, while the cathode employed a commercial Pt/C (Pt: 20 wt.%) catalyst at a comparable loading. As shown in Figure 6b, the BZCC ‖ Pt/C configuration exhibited outstanding performance, achieving a current density of 1.0 A cm−2 at 2.07 V in 1.0 M KOH at 40 °C, outperforming the benchmark IrO2 ‖ Pt/C cell. This high activity is attributed to the readily accessible active sites in BZCC, which effectively enable both the AEM and LOM pathways, yielding performance comparable to that of commercial Ir/O2.[ 63 ] Stability under various current densities was assessed, where the BZCC ‖ Pt/C cell displayed a significantly lower overpotential increase compared to the IrO2 ‖ Pt/C counterpart, highlighting its superior operational stability (Figure 6c).[ 64 ] Furthermore, long‐term durability was verified at a constant current density of 500 mA cm−2 over 25 h in 1.0 M KOH (Figure 6d). After initial activation, the BZCC catalyst exhibited only an 8.2% drop in current density, indicating excellent durability under practical conditions. The evolved H2 and O2 gases were visually observed and collected during cell operation, as illustrated in Figure S16d–e (Supporting Information). Finally, the overall AEMWE performance of the BZCC ‖ Pt/C system was compared with state‐of‐art perovskite‐based catalysts (Figure 6e; Table S2, Supporting Information), further demonstrating its promise for scalable alkaline water electrolysis.

Conclusion

A novel mixed‐metal perovskite catalyst, BZCC, was successfully synthesized via a simple hydrothermal method and exhibited outstanding electrocatalytic performance toward the OER. Comprehensive physicochemical and electrochemical analyses revealed that the redox activity of Co2+/Co3+ and Cu2+, coupled with abundant oxygen vacancies, facilitated a dual reaction mechanism involving both the AEM and the LOM. The BZCC catalyst delivered a low overpotential and excellent durability under high current density conditions, surpassing commercial IrO2‐based references. To evaluate its practical viability, the catalyst was integrated into a full‐cell AEMWE, where the BZCC ‖ Pt/C achieved a current density of 1.0 A cm−2 at 2.07 V in alkaline media, with stability comparable to that of commercial systems. These results highlight BZCC as a cost‐effective and scalable OER catalyst, offering strong potential for the advancement and commercialization of AEMWE technologies.

Experimental Section

Materials and Chemicals

Barium chloride (BaCl2, 99.99% trace metal basis), cobalt (II) chloride hexahydrate (CoCl2, 99.5% trace metal basis), iridium oxide (IrO2), commercial Pt/C, zirconium (IV) hydroxide (Zr(OH)4, 97%), copper (II) chloride (CuCl2, 99%), Pluronic F‐127, potassium hydroxide (KOH, 85%, pellets), polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) binder, N‐methylpyrrolidone (NMP), and dimethylformamide (DMF) were purchased from Sigma‐Aldrich. Ethanol and acetone were purchased from Samchun Chemicals. All chemicals were used without further purification. All solvents and electrolytes were prepared using ultrapure Milli‐Q water (18.2 MΩ cm at 25 °C).

Synthesis of BZCC, BZCo, BZCu, and BZ Perovskites

The BZCC electrocatalyst was synthesized via a hydrothermal method. First, 6 mmol of BaCl2 was dissolved in deionized water, and 3 mmol of Zr(OH)4 was added to the solution under magnetic stirring for 1 h at room temperature. Separately, 1.5 mmol of each CuCl2 and CoCl2 were dissolved and mixed slowly into the mixture, followed by an additional 1 h of stirring. Subsequently, 50 mg of Pluronic F‐127 was introduced as a morphology‐directing agent. To adjust the pH, 50 mL of KOH solution was added dropwise under constant stirring. The resulting solution was transferred into an autoclave and subjected to hydrothermal treatment at 160 °C for 48 h. The obtained precipitate was collected by centrifugation, washed thoroughly with deionized water and ethanol, and dried overnight in a vacuum oven. For comparison, BZCo and BZCu were synthesized using CoCl2 and CuCl2, respectively, following the same procedure. BZ was synthesized under similar conditions. No post‐hydrothermal calcination was applied, and the powders were characterized in their as‐synthesized form, consistent with previous studies reporting direct crystallization of perovskites under hydrothermal conditions.[ 65 , 66 , 67 ]

Physicochemical Characterizations

The crystal structure and phase patterns were examined using XRD (X'pert‐MRD, Pro Philips). Morphological features were analyzed using STEM and TEM (JEM‐ARM 200F), and FE‐SEM (SUPRA, ZEISS). The elemental distribution was determined via EDS under HAADF‐STEM mode. Surface chemical states were analyzed using XPS (K‐Alpha, Thermo Fisher Scientific). XANES and EXAFS analyses were conducted to determine oxidation states and local coordination environments using beamlines BL17C (TLS) and BL32A (TPS).

Electrochemical Characterizations

OER performance was evaluated using a conventional three‐electrode system. The working electrode was prepared by mixing 4.0 mg of electrocatalysts, 0.8 mg of Vulcan XC‐72 carbon black, and 0.5 mg of PVDF in an appropriate amount of DMF. The resulting slurry was sonicated and drop‐cast onto a 1 cm2 area of nickel foam, followed by drying under vacuum overnight. A saturated Ag/AgCl electrode and a graphite rod were used as the reference and counter electrodes, respectively. All electrochemical measurements were performed using a Gamry 600 potentiostat under ambient conditions. The reference potential was converted to the RHE using the equation (1) ERHE=EAg/AgCl+0.197+0.059pH

LSV was conducted at a scan rate of 1 mV s−1, and CV was measured at 50 mV s−1. EIS was carried out in the frequency range of 100 kHz to 0.01 Hz with an amplitude of 10 mA. Cdl was measured by CV curves recorded at scan rates ranging from 20 to 100 mV s−1 in the non‐faradaic region. ECSA was determined using the following equation: (2) ECSA=CdlCs where Cs is the specific capacitance of the substrate area (40 µF cm−2) in 0.1–1.0 M KOH.[ 68 ] The stability property of the electrocatalysts was measured by a long‐term stability test at 100 mA cm2.

AEMWE Fabrication Techniques

Real‐time catalytic performance and degradation tests were conducted through an AEMWE setup. The active MEA was prepared using the catalyst‐coated substrate (CCS) method. The catalyst ink slurry was formulated by dissolving an appropriate amount of prepared BZCC, commercial 20 wt.% Pt/C, and IrO2 in a mixture of 2.6 mL isopropanol (IPA), 200 µL deionized (DI) water, and 120 µL of 5 wt.% anion ionomer solution. After thorough mixing and fine grinding, the slurry was sonicated for 2 h to achieve a homogeneous catalyst ink. This ink was then coated onto a Ni substrate at a loading ratio of 2 mg cm−2. Meanwhile, a commercial FAA‐3‐50 Fumion anion exchange membrane (Fumatech) was pre‐treated and kept in 1.0 M KOH solution for activation.[ 69 ] The MEA was assembled by combining the catalyst‐coated Ni substrate and the activated anion membrane, then secured in the AEM cell with a uniform torque pressure of 5 Nm. The cell setup utilized a 1.0 M KOH electrolyte solution, preheated to 45 °C before feeding into the cell. During operation, the detected cell temperature was 40 °C, which was reported as the operating temperature in this study.
