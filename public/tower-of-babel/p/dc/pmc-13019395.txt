Modification of Heterogeneous Catalyst Support with Hydrogen Peroxide and Its Application in the Degradation of Methylene Blue Wastewater
Authors: Jianjun Zhao, Leifeng Jia, Xianfeng Zhang, Muxin Liu, Anping Xu, Xiaoxuan Zhang, Jing Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This work focuses on the green modification of heterogeneous catalyst supports. To carry out the modification, a 30 wt % hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) solution was used, and two catalysts, Cu/H2O2–Boehmite (modified with H2O2 solution) and Cu/Boehmite (unmodified with H2O2 solution), were prepared. And then, the prepared catalysts were characterized by the X-ray powder diffraction technique, N2 adsorption–desorption experiments, scanning electron microscopy, energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy, and contact angle measurements. Meanwhile, the catalytic activities of the catalysts were investigated by the degradation of methylene blue (MB) wastewater. The results show that compared with Cu/Boehmite, the active Cu species on the surface of the Cu/H2O2–Boehmite catalyst exhibit better dispersion, a larger BET surface area and pore volume, and significantly enhanced hydrophilicity. The catalytic activity tests showed that the Cu/H2O2–Boehmite catalyst exhibited superior performance in the degradation of MB wastewater compared to the Cu/Boehmite catalyst, and the corresponding chemical oxygen demand removal rate reached 63% within 60 min in this study. Therefore, for catalysts with the same content of active components, modifying the support with a H2O2 solution is conducive to enhancing the catalytic activity.

Introduction

Methylene blue (MB) dye wastewater must be treated before being discharged into natural water bodies due to its characteristics such as high chromaticity and poor biodegradability. , At present, the methods for degrading such wastewater include photocatalytic degradation, biologically activated carbon adsorption, advanced oxidation process (AOPs), and so forth. − Among these methods, the AOPs method has the advantages of low operating cost and can degrade complex organic compounds in wastewater into CO2, H2O, and other small molecules. Meanwhile, the method will not bring secondary pollution to the environment and has attracted people’s attention. ,,

In AOPs of organic matter, hydroxyl radical (•OH) can be generated in the water body under certain conditions. Meanwhile, •OH has strong oxidation ability and can decompose organic pollutants in water body. ,− In the AOPs process, Fenton reagent (composed of transition metal ions and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2)) is used to degrade organic wastewater, which has the advantages of mild operating conditions (reaction can be performed at room temperature) and good degradation effect and has attracted more attention. The reaction mechanism of •OH generation is as follows: ,− 1 Mn++H2O2→M(n+1)++HO−+OH· 2 M(n+1)++H2O2→Mn++HO2·+H+

(M = Fe, Cu, etc.)

•OH further degrades organic pollutants in wastewater, eventually producing small molecules such as CO2 and H2O. The corresponding reaction mechanism is as follows: − 3 OH·+organics→intermediates→H2O+CO2+...

The degradation of organic wastewater by Fenton reagent includes a homogeneous Fenton method and a heterogeneous Fenton method. As is known, the catalyst activity of the homogeneous Fenton process is good, but metal ions will dissolve into the water body during the reaction process, causing secondary pollution to the water body. Compared to the homogeneous Fenton method, the heterogeneous Fenton catalyst has a lower catalytic activity, but the heterogeneous Fenton catalyst is recyclable without secondary pollution to the water body, which has attracted the attention of a large number of researchers. ,,, In recent years, researchers have been working on the development of heterogeneous Fenton catalysts with better activity.

Heterogeneous Fenton catalysts are usually composed of two parts: the active component and the support. In the research on the active component, Fe and Cu, as two common nonprecious metal transition elements, are often used as the active components of these catalysts. For these two elements, copper not only outperforms iron in the redox cycle but also can effectively mineralize and decompose organic pollutants in wastewater.

In the research on catalyst support, current research attention has focused on carbon-based, silicon-based, and aluminum-based materials. Among these candidates, Boehmite (AlO­(OH)) has garnered considerable attention owing to its outstanding thermal stability and high specific surface area. , On the one hand, the basic hydroxyl groups and exposed coordinatively unsaturated aluminum ions in the Boehmite structure exhibit surface densities of 4.5 and 0.25 μmol m–2, respectively, endowing Boehmite with the potential to act as a model support. On the other hand, Boehmite possesses excellent hydrophilicity, which enables it to adsorb both water molecules and organic pollutant molecules in aqueous systems; this facilitates the contact between organic pollutant molecules and the catalyst, thereby further accelerating the degradation and mineralization of organic pollutants.

As a key component of heterogeneous catalysts, the support significantly affects the catalytic activity through its physical and chemical properties. Therefore, modifying the catalyst support to improve its physical and chemical properties and further enhance the catalytic activity has become an important research direction. However, conventional support modification methods often involve introducing new elements into the catalyst, which may have adverse effects on catalytic activity. The primary innovation of this study lies in the modification of the catalyst support without introducing additional chemical elements, which can simultaneously enhance the catalytic activity.

As a catalyst preparation strategy, in this study, Boehmite modified with H2O2 and Boehmite without H2O2 modification were used as the catalyst support, with Cu as the active component, the Cu/H2O2–Boehmite catalyst, and the Cu/Boehmite catalyst was prepared by the equal volume impregnation method. And the activities of the prepared catalysts were evaluated by the chemical oxygen demand (COD) removal rate during the degradation of methyl blue wastewater. Further, the relationship between the activity of the catalyst and the effect of H2O2 treatment on support performance was studied.

Experimental Section

Chemicals and Reagents

Analytical grade Boehmite was purchased from Zibo Hengqi Powdered Advanced Material Co., Ltd., China. Analytical grade copper nitrate (Cu­(NO3)2·3H2O) was purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. Analytical grade hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) was purchased from Tianjin Fu Chen Chemical Reagent Factory, China. Analytical grade methylene blue (C16H18ClN3S·3H2O) was purchased from Tianjin Yongda Chemical Reagent Co. Ltd. Analytical grade potassium dichromate (K2Cr2O7) was purchased from Shanghai Zhanyun Chemical Co., Ltd., China. All of the reagents were used as received without further purification.

Catalyst Preparation

First, the commercially available Boehmite was crushed and screened with 40–60 mesh. 5 g of 40–60 mesh Boehmite was placed in 20 mL of 30% (wt %) H2O2 solution, stirred for 5 min, and then kept for 2 h for filtration. The step was repeated three times. The filtered Boehmite particles were dried in a 120 °C thermostatic drier box. Second, using the equal volume impregnation method, a certain amount of Boehmite treated with or without H2O2 solution was used as the support, and Cu­(NO3)2 was used as the precursor to prepare the catalyst, so that the Cu content in the catalyst accounted for 10% (wt %) of the support weight. Then, the impregnated samples were dried at 120 °C in a drying oven. Subsequently, the dried samples were calcined at 800 °C in an atmosphere for 4 h. The prepared catalysts were labeled Cu/H2O2–Boehmite and Cu/Boehmite, respectively.

Catalyst Characterization

The phase analysis of the prepared catalysts used the D/max-RA X-ray diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation made by the Rigaku Company in Japan. The corresponding test conditions are as follows: tube voltage of 40 kV, tube current of 40 mA, scanning speed of 10°/min, and scanning range of 10–80°.

The specific surface area and pore size of the prepared catalysts were analyzed by a Micromeritics Tristar II 3020 physical adsorption apparatus (USA) at 77 K. The specific surface area was calculated by the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method, and the pore volume and pore size of the catalyst were analyzed by the desorption curve and the Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) method. The sample was degassed at 300 °C for 3 h before the test.

The morphology of the samples was analyzed by scanning electron microscopy using a Thermo Fisher Scientific microscope equipped with secondary electron mode testing. Under high vacuum conditions, when the acceleration voltage is 20 kV, the elemental mapping is determined by X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) using a Thermo Fisher Scientific scanning electron microscope and an Oxford detector (X-Max).

The contact angle between the catalyst and water were measured using the Attension Theta Flex optical contact Angle measuring instrument (Biolin Scientific Co., Sweden). That is, a glass slide was placed horizontally, double-sided tape was stuck on the slide, a certain amount of powder was pressed on the slide, then it tightly was pressed with another slide, and then a water drop test was conducted.

Catalytic Activity Measurements

The activity of the catalyst is evaluated by measuring the rate of COD removal of MB wastewater by the catalyst. The catalytic degradation of MB wastewater was measured in a 150 mL three-necked glass flask containing 50 mL of the MB wastewater (100 mg/L) with a pH value. Then, a certain mass of catalyst and a certain volume of H2O2 solution were added to the three-necked glass flask and stirred; the reaction temperature was kept at 30 °C. After reaction for 60 min, the COD value of MB wastewater was measured with 20 mL of the supernatant, the corresponding COD of MB wastewater was determined in accordance with the national standard “Water QualityDetermination of Chemical Oxygen DemandDichromate Method” (HJ 828-2017). The COD removal rate is calculated as follows: 4 CODremoval=COD0−CODtCOD0×100 In the formula, COD0 and COD t represent the COD value in MB wastewater at the initial time and 60 min after the reaction, respectively.

Statistical Analysis

All experimental data of pollutant degradation efficiency and COD removal rate were obtained from at least triplicate independent experiments to ensure the reliability of the results. And the mean values and standard deviations were calculated to characterize the data distribution. All of the experimental data were graphically processed using the Origin 2021 software (OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, Massachusetts, USA).

As presented in Table , the main diffraction peaks of both Cu/Boehmite and Cu/H2O2–Boehmite catalysts shift toward lower angles. In accordance with the Bragg’s law, this shift indicates an increase in the d-value corresponding to the main crystal plane. This phenomenon is due to the fact that Cu ions/atoms intercalated into the interlayer or lattice gaps of Boehmite, which expanded the lattice structure and further increased the interplanar spacing. Therefore, the Cu intercalation effect was successfully proved by the application of Bragg’s law.

By comparing the XRD patterns of the three samples, it can also be seen that the diffraction peaks of both Cu/H2O2–Boehmite and Cu/Boehmite samples are generated by Boehmite; no Cu phase diffraction peaks were detected in either of the two samples. This may be attributed to the fact that during the catalyst preparation process, some small copper species either intercalated into different crystal planes of Boehmite or existed in a highly dispersed state on the surface of Boehmite. Clausen et al. studied Cu/SiO2 catalysts and found that small particles Cu of will shift into the pore of support in the preparation process of 10% Cu/SiO2 catalyst by means of the QEXAFS/XRD combined online detection method. This conclusion is consistent with that of this study. It can be inferred that the high dispersion of the active components in this study is conducive to enhancing the activity of the catalyst during the degradation of MB wastewater.

Figure shows the adsorption and desorption isotherm curves of Boehmite support and Cu/Boehmite and Cu/H2O2–Boehmite catalyst samples. It can be seen that both the Cu/Boehmite catalyst and Cu/H2O2–Boehmite catalyst have similar adsorption and desorption behaviors for N2, and their adsorption isotherms belong to type IV isotherms. The isotherms give a linear absorption by capillary condensation when P/P 0 below 0.4, and the curves show a hysteresis phenomenon when P/P 0 is higher than 0.4, which is a characteristic of mesoporous materials (2–100 nm). However, the adsorption isotherm of the Cu/H2O2–Boehmite catalyst sample is slightly higher than that of the Cu/Boehmite catalyst sample, indicating that the specific surface area of the Cu/H2O2–Boehmite catalyst sample is higher than that of the Cu/Boehmite catalyst sample.

On the other hand, compared with the Cu/Boehmite catalyst, the Cu/H2O2–Boehmite catalyst has a larger specific surface area and pore volume while having a smaller average pore size. This difference is mainly attributed to the different microstructure evolutions during the support preparation process. The Cu/Boehmite catalyst was not cleaned during the support preparation, and a large number of fine particles were still retained in its pores. When the active component Cu is loaded, Cu species enter the pores that have been partially filled by particles, causing secondary clogging of the pores. Therefore, ultimately, the BET surface area and pore volume of this catalyst are both smaller than those of the Cu/H2O2–Boehmite catalyst, while the average pore size is larger.

Figure shows that for the Cu/Boehmite catalyst, the actual content of the active component Cu in the catalyst is inconsistent with the theoretical value; on the contrary, the Cu content in the Cu/H2O2–Boehmite catalyst is relatively consistent with the theoretical value. The reasons for this phenomenon are as follows: For the Boehmite support, its point of zero charge (PZC) value is relatively high (7.7–9.4). Catalytic Activity

The preparation method of the catalyst affects its properties and further affects the catalytic activities. MB wastewater, an organic wastewater, is not easily degraded by conventional methods (such as biochemical methods) and is often used as a reaction model for the degradation of dye wastewater. , In some cases, the intermediate products produced in the degradation process will bring greater harm to the environment. COD, as an important parameter to represent the content of organic compounds in wastewater, means that how many organic molecules in the wastewater can be completely oxidized into small inorganic molecules, such as CO2 and H2O (mineralization). Therefore, COD removal rate can be used as an evaluation parameter to measure the degradation degree of MB wastewater. , In this study, the degradation performance of MB wastewater by Boehmite, Cu/Boehmite, and Cu/H2O2–Boehmite catalysts was reflected by the COD removal rate.

After treating the Boehmite support with H2O2 solution, the dispersibility of the active component Cu, the BET surface area and pore volume of the catalyst, as well as the hydrophilicity of the catalyst all changed, thereby further affecting the catalytic activity. , Figure shows the influence of the catalytic system on the COD removal rate of MB wastewater when the reaction time is 60 min, the dosage of the catalyst is 0.5 g, the reaction temperature is 30 °C, and the initial pH of MB wastewater is 3.0.

COD removal rate in MB wastewater using different catalytic systems. (1) Boehmite; (2) Cu/Boehmite; (3) Cu/H2O2–Boehmite; (4) 100 μL H2O2 + Boehmite; (5) 100 μL H2O2 + Cu/Boehmite; and (6) 100 μL H2O2 + Cu/H2O2–Boehmite.

It can be seen that when no H2O2 was added to the reaction system (catalytic system 1–3), the COD removal rate of the three catalysts for MB wastewater was very low. According to reaction mechanisms () and (), without the addition of H2O2, there was no •OH which led to MB degradation in the reaction system. The low COD removal rate was due to a small amount of physical adsorption of MB by the catalyst in the reaction system.

As can be seen from Figure (catalytic systems 4–6), when 100 μL of H2O2 solution was added to catalytic systems 4, 5, and 6, respectively, the COD removal rate of MB wastewater was significantly improved after 60 min. Compared with reaction system 4, reaction systems 5 and 6 have a higher COD removal rate from MB wastewater. This indicates that Cu is the active component in this catalyst, although the specific surface areas of Cu/Boehmite and Cu/H2O2–Boehmite catalysts are smaller than those of Boehmite. From reaction mechanisms () and (), it can be known that the absence of Cu will hinder the generation of •OH in the reaction system, resulting in a low COD removal rate in the system composed of H2O2 and Boehmite.

It can also be seen from Figure that the COD removal rate of catalytic system 6 (composed of H2O2 and Cu/H2O2–Boehmite) is higher than that of catalytic system 5 (composed of H2O2 and Cu/Boehmite), which is due to the change in the surface properties of the Cu/H2O2–Boehmite catalyst. For the Cu/H2O2–Boehmite catalyst, a higher BET specific surface area, larger pore volume, and highly dispersed Cu species have a smaller steric hindrance, which helps to generate more •OH in the reaction system. The enhanced hydrophilicity can accelerate the contact between the catalyst and the pollutant molecules in the water body and further lead to more degradation of MB in the wastewater, ultimately resulting in the mineralization of MB in the wastewater. After 60 min of reaction, the COD removal rate of MB wastewater by the Cu/H2O2–Boehmite catalyst reached 63%.

The pH value (that is, the concentration of H+) drastically affects the catalytic activity in the reaction system according to reaction mechanisms () and () of the heterogeneous Fenton reaction. Figure shows the effect of initial pH on the COD removal rate of MB wastewater using the Cu/H2O2–Boehmite catalyst, and the corresponding reaction conditions are as follows: 0.5 g of Cu/H2O2–Boehmite catalyst, the reaction temperature was 30 °C, the dosage of H2O2 solution was 100 μL, and the reaction time was 60 min.

Effects of the initial pH of MB wastewater on the COD removal rate using the Cu/Boehmite and Cu/H2O2–Boehmite catalysts.

It can be seen that the COD removal rate of the MB wastewater was slightly lower when the initial pH was 2.0. According to the reaction mechanism (), the higher H+ concentration prevents the decomposition of H2O2 and affects the generation of •OH, which resulted in reduction of catalytic activity. When the initial pH was 3.0, the COD removal rate was the highest for the degradation of MB wastewater and the corresponding COD removal rate was 63%. When the initial pH was higher than 3.0, the COD removal rate of the MB wastewater decreased significantly. According to the reaction mechanism (), if the initial pH was high, the excessive HO– in the wastewater prevent the generation of •OH, which affected further the degradation of MB in the wastewater (the reaction mechanism ()). In this study, when the initial pH of MB wastewater was 3.0, the COD removal rate was the highest compared to that of the others, which reached 63%. Atique-Ullah et al. investigated the effect of pH on degradation of 30 μmol/L MB wastewater using the Mn3O4 nanocatalyst and found that the MB removal was superior when the initial pH of MB wastewater was 3.0, which was agreed with the results of this study, namely, the catalytic activity was superior in degrading MB wastewater when the initial pH of the reaction system was 3.0.

The addition of H2O2 in the reaction system dramatically affected the degradation of the MB wastewater. Figure shows the influence of the addition of H2O2 on the performance of the Cu/H2O2–Boehmite catalyst when the reaction temperature was 30 °C, the dosage of the catalyst was 0.5 g, the reaction time was 60 min, and the initial pH of MB wastewater was 3.0.

Effects of the H2O2 dosage on the COD removal rate using the Cu/Boehmite and the Cu/H2O2–Boehmite catalysts.

It can be seen that the COD removal rate was very low when the addition amount of H2O2 was 0. The reason is that without the addition of H2O2, according to the mechanism of Fenton-like reactions () and (), there is no •OH that leads to MB degradation in the reaction system. With the increase of H2O2 addition, •OH in the reaction system gradually increased, and the catalytic activity gradually increased in the degradation of MB wastewater. When the addition amount of H2O2 was 100 μL, the catalytic activity reached the maximum. Further addition of H2O2 did not lead to any significant change in the catalytic activity. This phenomenon shows that when the dosage of the Cu/H2O2–Boehmite catalyst was 0.5 g, the suitable dosage of H2O2 was 100 μL in this reaction system. Meanwhile, when the amount of H2O2 added was less than 100 μL, •OH produced in the system was not enough to make the catalytic activity reach the maximum. When the addition amount of H2O2 was higher than 100 μL, the excessive addition of H2O2 will consume •OH generated in the reaction system, resulting in a slight decrease in the catalytic activity. ,

The catalytic mechanism represents a key aspect of this study. In the reaction system, the Cu/H2O2–Boehmite catalyst demonstrated excellent catalytic activity. The primary reason for this phenomenon is presumably that under the catalytic effect of Cu, H2O2 decomposes at the Cu active sites (Cu2+/Cu+), thereby generating highly oxidizing hydroxyl radicals (•OH). The corresponding reaction mechanism is described as follows: 5 Cu++H2O2→Cu2++OH·+OH− 6 Cu2++H2O2→Cu++HO2·+H+

•OH can efficiently attack the surrounding MB molecules, The corresponding reaction is as follows: 7 OH·+MBmolecules→intermediates→H2O+CO2+...

The catalyst prepared using the modified Boehmite support has a larger specific surface area and pore volume as well as better hydrophilicity. It can adsorb more MB molecules. Therefore, the Cu/H2O2–Boehmite catalyst exhibits better catalytic activity.

This work serves as a continuation and further expansion of our research group’s previous investigations into the modification of catalyst supports (ref ). In ref , the modification of the support with polyethylene glycol (PEG) potentially led to the residual carbon atoms in the as-prepared catalyst owing to the introduction of PEG. In the present study, hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) solution was employed for the treatment of the catalyst support; given that H2O2 is completely decomposed during the subsequent drying and calcination steps of the catalyst fabrication process, the entire modification procedure for the catalyst support is free of the introduction of any exogenous chemical elements. The findings of this work offer a novel strategy for the modification of the catalysts. This study focuses on the optimization of catalyst preparation protocols and their corresponding impacts on the catalytic process. The practical application of such modified catalysts in the treatment of real wastewater will be the major direction of our future efforts.

Conclusions

Cu/Boehmite and Cu/H2O2–Boehmite catalysts were prepared by the equal-volume impregnation method using the Boehmite without H2O2 solution treatment and the Boehmite treated with H2O2 solution as supports and Cu as the active components, respectively. The prepared catalysts were used to degrade MB dye wastewater, and the corresponding degradation degree was evaluated by means of the COD removal rate. The two prepared catalysts were characterized using XRD, BET, SEM–EDX, and contact angle measurements. The results show that compared with the Cu/Boehmite catalyst, Cu exists in a more dispersed state in the Cu/H2O2–Boehmite catalyst. Meanwhile, the Cu/H2O2–Boehmite catalyst has a larger BET surface area, larger pore volume, and better hydrophilicity, and these properties are conducive to the degradation of MB dye molecules in wastewater. For the Cu/H2O2–Boehmite catalyst, when the reaction temperature was 30 °C, the initial pH of wastewater was 3.0, and the volume of H2O2 solution added to the wastewater was 100 μL; the COD removal rate of MB wastewater is 63% after reaction for 60 min. The catalytic activity is superior to that of the Cu/Boehmite catalyst. This conclusion further indicates that modifying the surface of the support with H2O2 solution is a feasible method, thus avoiding the introduction of foreign elements.
