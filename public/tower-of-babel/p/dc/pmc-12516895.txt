Unmasking the role of constipation in treatment-refractory enterobiasis in Bangladeshi children
Authors: Md Benzamin, Md Ziaur Rahman Chowdhury, Pranto Chakroborty, Akhlaq Ahmed, Md Moulud Hussen, Rajib Kanti Dey, ASM Bazlul Karim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHelminthiasis remains a prevalent health issue among the pediatric population in both developed and developing countries. Recurrent enterobius vermicularis infection is a particularly distressing and sometimes embarrassing condition for children. While sporadic cases are common, repeated infections despite proper hygiene and regular deworming are uncommon and warrant further investigation.MethodsThis prospective observational study was conducted in Sylhet, Bangladesh between January 2022 and December 2024. All consecutive children presenting with treatment-refractory enterobiasis were enrolled. Comprehensive clinical evaluations and relevant investigations were performed to identify potential underlying or contributory factors. Treatment strategies were tailored accordingly, and clinical response was assessed based on symptom resolution.ResultsA total of 114 children were included, with a mean age of 88.25 ± 37.5 months and a male-to-female ratio of 1.65:1. All children presented with perianal itching and visible passage of worms (100%). Additional symptoms included abdominal pain (64%), anorexia (51%), vomiting (24%), nausea (16%), frequent small-volume defecation (9%), increased frequency of micturition (9%), nocturnal enuresis(7%), and encopresis (7%). Notably, constipation was present in 96% of cases—41% with occult and 55% with overt constipation (p < 0.0001), all of which had either been previously unrecognized or inadequately managed. Following the administration of appropriate laxative therapy along with antihelminthic therapy, significant symptomatic improvement was observed (p < 0.0001) with minimal failure rate (3%).ConclusionConstipation frequently coexists in patients with treatment-refractory enterobius vermicularis infections and proper management of constipation is essential for successful therapeutic outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-025-04335-7.Keywords: Constipation, Treatment-refractory, Enterobiasis, Perianal itching

Introduction

Enterobius vermicularis, commonly known as pinworm, is one of the most prevalent helminthic infections worldwide. It is a nematode (roundworm) that appears as tiny, thread-like, whitish worms approximately 1 cm in length. Female worms have a distinctive pin-like tail at the posterior end [1]. Humans are the only natural host for this parasite.Transmission typically occurs through direct hand-to-mouth contact, especially when contaminated fingers transfer eggs from the perianal region to the mouth. After ingestion, the eggs hatch in the stomach and upper small intestine. The larvae then migrate to and reside in the cecum, appendix, and surrounding areas of the gut. Within 1 to 2 months, they mature into adult worms. Gravid females migrate—primarily at night—to the perianal area to lay thousands of eggs that adhere to the skin. These eggs are highly sticky and become infective (embryonated) within a few hours. This nocturnal migration causes intense itching in the perianal region, promoting autoinfection as individuals scratch and inadvertently ingest the eggs.The eggs can also be transferred to clothing, bedding, bathroom surfaces, toys, furniture, and dust, facilitating fecal-oral transmission to self and others [2]. Infection is most common in children, particularly those of preschool and kindergarten age [3].

A recent meta-analysis reported substantial variation in the pooled, weighted prevalence of Enterobius vermicularis infection among children across different regions: Europe (24.9%), South America (14.3%), Asia (13.0%), Africa (2.0%), and North America (1.9%). Stratification by study population indicated the highest prevalence in primary school children (34.7%), followed by children in the general population (20.2%), those attending medical clinics (14.1%), kindergarten children (11.2%), preschool children (8.2%), and individuals from random population samples (2.6%) [4]. At the country level, reported prevalence rates were notably high in India (61%), England (50%), Thailand (39%), Sweden (37%), and Denmark (29%) [5]. Despite its widespread nature, the condition is often overlooked due to its frequently mild or asymptomatic (40%) nature [6]. The most common clinical sign is perianal and perineal itching, especially at night [5, 7, 8]. There are several highly effective and well-tolerated antihelminthic agents available for the treatment of enterobiasis, including mebendazole, pyrantelembonate, and albendazole. These medications can achieve eradication rates exceeding 90% [9]. However, despite comprehensive preventive measures—such as treating all household members simultaneously, maintaining proper hand hygiene, trimming fingernails, daily perineal cleaning, controlling pica behavior, regularly washing bed linens in hot water, and wearing long pajamas instead of nightgowns—recurrent enterobius vermicularis infection are still frequently observed. Although recurrent infections are common, data on recurrence rates remain limited. One study by Lohiya et al. reported a 12% reinfection rate among adults with developmental disabilities [10]. In cases of chronic recurrent infection, a prolonged treatment strategy, such as a “pulse scheme” lasting up to 16 weeks, is recommended [9].

In our routine clinical practice, we have encountered several unresolved cases of chronic, recurrent enterobius vermicularis infection—termed “treatment-refractory enterobius vermicularis infection” cases—that persist infection despite all recommended interventions. Many of these patients also had constipation that was either unrecognized or inadequately treated. These “treatment-refractory enterobius vermicularis infection/enterobiasis” can cause significant distress in children and considerable anxiety for their caregivers. This study was undertaken to explore the clinical profile of treatment-refractory enterobiasis and assess its association with constipation.

Methods

This prospective observational study was conducted in the Department of Pediatrics, Sylhet MAG Osmani Medical College, and at Dr. Benzamin’s Pediatric Liver Research Centre and Nutrition Clinic, Mount Adora Hospital, Sylhet, Bangladesh, from January 2022 to December 2024. All consecutive children diagnosed with “treatment-refractory enterobius vermicularis infection” and referred to these centers were included. Children with comorbid conditions, including hirschsprung disease, inflammatory bowel disease, hypothyroidism, spina bifida, or other neural tube defects, as well as those presenting with red flag signs indicative of an organic etiology of constipation, were excluded from the study due to the potential impact of these factors on treatment outcomes.

Diagnosis of enterobiasis

Enterobiasis was diagnosed based on the presence of adult worms in the perianal region or passage of worms (Fig. 1, Video 1), as directly observed by the researchers or reported by the parents.

Definition of “treatment-refractory enterobiasis”

Treatment-refractory enterobiasis was defined as persistent infection in patients who had received mebendazole or albendazole, along with all standard supportive measures, administered at 14-day intervals for a total of 16 weeks, without symptomatic improvement.

Data collection and clinical evaluation

Detailed clinical history and thorough physical examination were performed by the primary investigator and recorded using a predesigned standardized data sheet. Previous investigations conducted before referrals were also reviewed. During physical examination, the following findings were documented: presence of fever, mouth ulcers, thyroid abnormalities, growth parameters, skin findings, abdominal examination, neurological signs (tone and reflexes of the lower limbs), vertebral spine abnormalities, abdominal distension, perianal region, and others red flag signs suggestive of organic causes of constipation [11].

Diagnosis of constipation

Functional constipation was diagnosed based on the Rome IV criteria [12]. In the absence of a prior history of constipation, digital rectal examination (DRE) was performed to determine the presence of hard stool in the rectum. For children in whom DRE could not be completed due to resistance or distress, a plain abdominal X-ray was obtained to evaluate for occult constipation.

Diagnostic Criteria for Functional Constipation: Must include 2 or more of the following occurring at least once per week for a minimum of 1 month with insufficient criteria for a diagnosis of irritable bowel syndrome:1. Two or fewer defecations in the toilet per week in a child of a developmental age of at least 4 years 2.At least 1 episode of fecal incontinence per week 3.History of retentive posturing or excessive volitional stool retention 4.History of painful or hard bowel movements 5. Presence of a large fecal mass in the rectum 6.History of large diameter stools that can obstruct the toilet. After appropriate evaluation, the symptoms cannot be fully explained by another medical condition [12].

Occult constipation (OC), a subtype of constipation, was defined as the absence of typical complaints or history of constipation, but with evidence of hard stool on rectal examination or fecal impaction on abdominal X-ray [13]. X-rays were assessed using the Leech scoring system, which divides the abdomen into three colonic segments and scores fecal loading (Fig. 2). A total score between 8 and 15 was considered diagnostic of significant constipation [14]. X-ray reporting was performed by a single radiologist from the study center, who was not involved in any aspect of case selection.

Treatment protocol

For cases diagnosed with constipation or occult constipation, magnesium hydroxide was administered at a dose of 2 ml/kg/day for disimpaction, followed by 1 ml/kg/day for maintenance over 2 months, and then gradually tapered over one month. Disimpaction was considered achieved when there were at least two consecutive brown, loose stools without any large lumps. Effective maintenance therapy was defined as achieving one to two soft bowel movements per day [15]. All parents were thoroughly informed about the disimpaction process and instructed on the appropriate timing for initiating and following up with maintenance therapy during the study period. Antihelminthic treatment with albendazole was administered after disimpaction and repeated after 14 days. Family members were treated simultaneously and instructed to adhere strictly to hygienic practices including proper hand washing, fingernail trimming, daily perineal cleaning, hot washing of bed linens, and changing nightwear daily.

Follow-up and outcome assessment

During treatment, mothers/caregivers were instructed to observe the perianal area at night for signs of worm migration. Patients were followed up monthly for four months, and the perianal region was directly examined by the investigator. The treatment endpoint was defined as the resolution of perianal itching and the absence of visible worms in the perianal area or passage of worms.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS), version 27. Clinical symptoms and treatment outcomes were assessed at the end of the maintenance therapy for constipation, in combination with scheduled albendazole administration. Treatment responses before and after the intervention were compared using McNemar’s test and the Chi-square test. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 114 children were enrolled in the study, with a mean age of 88.25 ± 37.5 months. The male-to-female ratio was 1.65:1. The primary presenting complaints in all participants (100%) were perianal itching (Fig. 3) and passage of worms. Additionally, in 12 female patients, worms were regularly removed from the vaginal area during nighttime observation. Common other associated symptoms included: abdominal pain in 73 children (64%), crying at night(video 2)in 73 children (64%),anorexia in 58 children (51%),nausea in 18 children (16%), frequent defecation with small-volume stool in 10 children (9%), defecation immediately after meals in 10 children (9%), increased frequency of micturition in 10 children (9%), nocturnal enuresis in 8 children (7%), encopresis in 8 children (7%),(see Table 1).Approximately 18 children (16%) had received traditional treatments such as herbal remedies, garlic, or nimleaves in addition to antihelminthics, prior to visiting the study centers. Among the children, 110 (96%) were found to have constipation—55% had overt constipation and 41% had occult constipation, which was statistically significant (p < 0.0001). Constipation had consistently remained untreated or inadequately managed prior to the presentation. Four patients without constipation, all were female.All children with identified constipation received treatment with adequate laxatives in combination with albendazole at recommended intervals. Four children without constipation were excluded from the final analysis, as all remaining patients had received constipation management. At the 4-month follow-up, 97% of patients showed significant symptom improvement, with no recurrence of enterobiasis and a 3% treatment failure rate. (See Table 2)

Discussion

In routine pediatric and gastroenterology outpatient practice, enterobiasis (enterobius vermicularis infection) is a common condition. While typically mild, chronic and recurrent cases are not uncommon and can often be refractory to standard treatment protocols. In our study, we analyzed 114 cases of treatment-refractoryenterobiasis, and notably, 96% of these children had underlying constipation, which had been previously unaddressed or inadequately treated.The hallmark symptom of symptomatic enterobius vermicularis infection is perianal itching, particularly at night, caused by the nocturnal migration of gravid female worms to the perianal region [5, 7, 8]. Consistent with this, 100% of children in our study presented with perianal itching and passage of worms during nighttime.Sleep disturbances, particularly crying at night, are also commonly associated with enterobius vermicularis infection [16]. We observed this in 64% of our patients, indicating significant discomfort and sleep disruption. Other associated symptoms such as abdominal pain, anorexia, nausea, increased frequency of micturition, and nocturnal enuresis were frequently reported. These symptoms are likely multifactorial, potentially arising from both constipation and pinworm infestation [17–20].In certain cases, E. vermicularis may mimic or coexist with other gastrointestinal disorders. It has been associated with abdominal pain resembling acute appendicitis, and may cause appendicitis [21, 22]. Additionally, enterobiasis can occasionally present with features overlapping chronic inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) [23]. In one patient in our study, colonoscopy was performed under suspicion of IBD, but E. vermicularis was identified instead (video 3).We also observed that symptoms such as encopresis, defecation immediately after meals, frequent defecation with small-volume stools, nocturnal enuresis and abdominal distension occurred in approximately 7–10% of patients. These clinical features are strongly suggestive of occult constipation [24–27]. In female children, migration of adult worms to the vaginal or vulvar area can lead to vaginal irritation, inflammation, and mucoid discharge [5, 28]. We identified a few such cases in our cohort, where worms were removed from the vaginal region during nocturnal observation. It’s important to differentiate enterobiasis from other conditions that can mimic its presentation. For example, intestinal myiasis may be misdiagnosed as pinworm infestation, leading to ineffective treatment with antihelminthics [29]. Similarly, in infants, cow’s milk allergy can be a significant cause of recurrent perianal pruritus [30]. In our study, however, each case was diagnosed by direct visualization of adult worms in the perianal region, which helped rule out these differential diagnoses.Traditional treatments such as herbal and ayurvedicremedies are used in South Asian countries, including Bangladesh [31]. In our study, 18% of children had received such traditional treatments like raw garlic and neem leaves,, including homeopathic and herbal medications before enrolled in this study, but none showed any clinical improvement.This use of traditional remedies reflects the strong influence of cultural practices on health-seeking behavior in the study population. Several effective antihelminthic agents are available for the treatment of enterobiasis, including mebendazole, albendazole, and pyrantelpamoate. These drugs are typically administered as a single-dose therapy, with a second dose repeated after two weeks to prevent auto-infection and ensure the elimination of newly developed adult worms. As enterobiasis often affects entire households, it is essential that all family members be treated simultaneously to prevent re-infection. In cases of recurrent infection, a “pulse regimen”—repeating doses at regular intervals over an extended period—is recommended [9]. In our study, all included children had already undergone pulse regimen therapy with poor or no response prior to enrollment. Notably, there is a scarcity of data in the literature regarding treatment-refractory enterobiasis. One case report describes a child who continued to pass worms despite fortnightly albendazole for 6 months, with no definitive resolution [32]. Our study found a significant association between treatment-refractory enterobius vermicularis infection and constipation. When constipation was identified and treated adequately, all cases showed complete symptom resolution. This highlights a critical and previously underexplored link. There is a lack of comparable studies evaluating how constipation may impact treatment outcomes in enterobiasis. In vivo studies have demonstrated that reduced gastrointestinal motility significantly impairs helminth clearance, whereas normal or enhanced motility is critical for effective parasite expulsion [33–37]. One possible explanation is that sluggish bowel motility delays the expulsion of dead worms and eggs, allowing them to remain in the rectum and perianal region. This increases the chances of autoinfection and retroinfection [1, 38].However, eight cases (four with constipation and four without) did not respond to treatment, raising the possibility of antihelminthic drug resistance or ectopic infections, especially vagina may have served as an ectopic reservoir for enterobius vermicularis [32, 39–41], although we were unable to confirm these hypotheses. We fully acknowledge the absence of a control group as a major limitation of our study. Another key limitation is the relatively small sample size and the fact that the study was conducted in a single geographic region, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. Symptom severity was not assessed and duration of follow up was short. Another point, our study did not perform routine vaginal sampling or gynecologic evaluation for genital tract infestation in female patients. We recommend conducting a well-designed study with an appropriate control group with longer follow-up to strengthen causal inference and clarify the role of constipation in treatment-refractory enterobiasis.

Conclusion

Constipation was commonly observed among patients presenting with treatment-refractory enterobius vermicularis infections, suggesting a potential association that warrants further investigation. Identifying and managing underlying constipation, including occult forms, may be essential for the successful eradication of enterobius vermicularis infection. We recommend that clinicians assess for constipation early in the treatment course, particularly in children with repeated relapses following standard therapy, rather than delaying evaluation until prolonged treatment failure occurs.
