Yigongsan alleviates diarrhea in laying hens through cecal microbiota modulation and restoration of intestinal barrier related responses via EGFR PI3K AKT signaling
Authors: Shuai Zhang, Yinuo Zhang, Jianyu Lv, Sijuan Huang, Yucheng Fu, Chao Han, Zhihui Hao, Yipeng Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiarrhea in laying hens impairs productive performance and intestinal health. Yigongsan (YGS), a classical traditional Chinese medicine formula, has been used clinically for gastrointestinal disorders; however, its therapeutic effects and underlying mechanisms in diarrheal laying hens remain unclear. This study evaluated the efficacy of YGS at different doses and explored its potential mechanisms in naturally diarrheal laying hens. A clinical intervention trial was conducted in laying hens with diarrhea. Hens received low-dose YGS (0.53 g/kg) or high-dose YGS (2.12 g/kg) for 7 d. Productive performance, ileal histopathology, inflammatory cytokines, oxidative stress-related indices, barrier-related markers, EGFR/PI3K/AKT pathway-related genes and proteins, and cecal microbiota composition were assessed using an integrated strategy including network pharmacology, histological analysis, gene and protein expression assays, and 16S rRNA gene sequencing. High-dose YGS showed broader and more consistent beneficial effects than low-dose YGS. It improved diarrhea-associated phenotypes, including body weight and egg production, alleviated ileal mucosal injury, and increased the villus height/crypt depth ratio. High-dose YGS also increased the mRNA expression of TJP1 and Occludin, reduced inflammatory responses and oxidative stress in serum and ileal tissues, and was associated with modulation of EGFR/PI3K/AKT pathway-related gene expression, increased PTEN expression, decreased PI3K and AKT phosphorylation, and normalization of the Bcl-2/Bax balance. In contrast, the low-dose treatment produced only partial improvements in several indices. Microbiota analysis showed no significant changes in α-diversity, but suggested shifts in β-diversity, microbial composition, and predicted microbial functions after YGS treatment, particularly in the high-dose group.In conclusion, YGS, especially at the high dose, alleviated diarrhea-associated pathological changes in laying hens and improved intestinal homeostasis. These effects may be associated with attenuation of inflammation and oxidative stress, restoration of barrier-related responses, modulation of EGFR/PI3K/AKT signaling, and restructuring of cecal microbiota composition.Keywords: Yigongsan, Laying hen, Gut microbiota, Intestinal barrier function, EGFR/PI3K/AKT pathway

Introduction

Diarrhea in laying hens is a common gastrointestinal disorder caused by multiple factors, including environmental stress, nutritional imbalance, and pathogen infection. It often leads to increased fecal moisture, reduced feed efficiency, and decreased egg production, thereby causing substantial economic losses in the poultry industry (Xiao et al., 2023). Unlike experimentally induced diarrhea models, naturally occurring diarrhea under field conditions usually reflects the combined influence of multiple stressors rather than a single defined trigger. In addition, diarrhea is closely associated with intestinal mucosal barrier disruption, excessive inflammatory responses, and gut microbiota dysbiosis (Zhang et al., 2022). Although antibiotics are commonly used for the management of diarrhea, their prolonged use has raised concerns regarding antimicrobial resistance and drug residues, highlighting the need for safer and more sustainable alternatives (Chen et al., 2025). Yigongsan (YGS) is a classical traditional Chinese medicine formula that has long been used for gastrointestinal disorders. Given its traditional application and potential regulatory effects on intestinal function, YGS may represent a promising alternative strategy for alleviating diarrhea in laying hens (Guan et al., 2024).

In this study, YGS was selected as a candidate intervention based on its traditional use and reported pharmacological activities (Huang et al., 2025). YGS is derived from the Sijunzi Decoction, with the addition of Citrus reticulata peel, which is traditionally used to improve digestive function and gastrointestinal regulation. From a biomedical perspective, this formulation may help support intestinal homeostasis by improving digestive tolerance, modulating inflammatory responses, and maintaining epithelial barrier function (Falduto et al., 2022). The key pathological feature of diarrhea is dysregulated intestinal water and electrolyte transport, often associated with tight junction disruption and inflammatory mediated hypersecretion (Gao et al., 2026). All the main components in YGS, namely glycyrrhizin, isoglycyrrhizin, hesperidin, as well as atractylideol I were absorbed into the plasma and widely distributed in the spleen, liver and kidneys (Wang et al., 2024). Studies have confirmed that YGS can significantly improve tissue water metabolism disorders by regulating the expression of water channel proteins (AQPs), providing a direct molecular biological basis for strengthening the spleen and eliminating dampness to halt diarrhea (Zeng et al., 2024). Moreover, long-term diarrhea can cause laying hens to enter a state similar to chronic anemia, and YGS has been proven to have significant anti-inflammatory and nutritional consumption improvement effects, which are helpful in restoring the damaged physical condition and laying performance of cows (Huang et al., 2025). Polysaccharides from Codonopsis pilosula and Atractylodes macrocephala improved intestinal morphology and microbial composition in broilers (Zhou et al., 2025; Ping et al., 2022); and Glycyrrhiza uralensis polysaccharides enhanced growth performance, immunity, and beneficial cecal microflora in broilers, collectively supporting a role for YGS-derived bioactive compounds in mitigating diarrhea-associated intestinal dysfunction (Zhang et al., 2024) .

Although there is sufficient pharmacological evidence, it is not yet fully clear how YGS regulates the complex "host-microbe" interaction network to treat diarrhea. Multiple signaling pathways, including NF-κB, MAPK, mTOR, and PI3K/AKT, participate in the regulation of intestinal inflammation, epithelial barrier integrity, oxidative stress responses, and cell survival. In our preliminary network pharmacology analysis, several hub targets related to growth factor and phosphoinositide signaling, including EGFR, PIK3CA, PIK3CB, PIK3CD, PIK3R1, SRC, HRAS, NRAS, PDGFRA, and PDGFRB, were identified from the overlapping targets between YGS-related compounds and diarrhea-associated genes. KEGG enrichment analysis further highlighted PI3K-Akt signaling as a candidate pathway. The EGFR/PI3K/AKT signaling pathway is a key hub that regulates cell survival and inflammatory responses (Cao et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2022), and its pathological over-activation is closely related to intestinal barrier damage (Zheng et al., 2025). Dietary supplementation with Glycyrrhiza uralensis extract has been shown to benefit growth performance, antioxidant status, immune function, and intestinal morphology in broiler chickens, as well as modulate cecal microbial richness and community composition, indicating that bioactive compounds in licorice can positively influence poultry gut health and host–microbe interactions (Li et al., 2024). Given the dynamic bidirectional communication between gut microbiota and epithelial signaling networks, elucidating whether YGS exerts its anti-diarrheal effects through modulation of microbiota EGFR/PI3K/AKT intestinal barrier” regulatory axis is of significant scientific and clinical importance. Therefore, the present study integrates network pharmacology prediction with in vivo validation to systematically investigate the dose-dependent effects of YGS on intestinal microbiota composition, barrier function, and EGFR/PI3K/AKT pathway activation. This work aims to clarify the mechanistic basis of YGS in repairing intestinal barrier injury and to provide experimental evidence for the precise and rational application of traditional Chinese medicine formulations as sustainable alternatives to antibiotics in poultry production.

Materials and methods

Preparation of YGS and UHPLC-MS/MS analysis

All herbal materials used in this study were purchased from Beijing Tongrentang Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd. The composition of YGS is shown in Table 1. Briefly, the crude herbs were decocted twice with distilled water at a ratio of 1:12 (w/v) at 100 °C for 2.5 h for each extraction. The combined filtrates were concentrated under reduced pressure using a rotary evaporator to a final crude drug concentration of 1 g/mL and stored at 4 °C until use.The chemical constituents of YGS were qualitatively characterized by UHPLC-MS/MS. Detailed chromatographic and mass spectrometric conditions, as well as compound identification procedures, were performed according to our previous study (Huang et al., 2025).

Network pharmacology analysis

Targets related to the identified YGS constituents were predicted using the SwissTargetPrediction database. To reduce false-positive results and improve confidence in target prediction, only the top 100 predicted targets, ranked by probability score, were retained for each compound. Diarrhea-related targets were collected from the GeneCards, OMIM, and TTD databases. As these resources are primarily based on human and mammalian data, we recognize that species differences may exist between mammals and chickens. Therefore, these genes were used as candidate targets for network construction, and experimental validation was performed in laying hens to evaluate the potential relevance of these targets to poultry diarrhea. The overlapping targets between YGS-related targets and diarrhea-related targets were identified using Venny 2.1.0.A protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed using the STRING database with a confidence score of > 0.9, and the network was visualized using Cytoscape 3.9.1. The top 10 targets ranked by bottleneck score were selected as candidate hub targets for subsequent enrichment analysis. Subsequently, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed using the DAVID database to explore the potential biological functions and signaling pathways associated with the overlapping targets.

Experimental animals and design

Screening criteria for diarrhea in laying hens

To ensure consistency in animal selection, diarrheal and healthy laying hens were screened according to the Manual for Diagnosis and Control of Poultry Diseases and the Standardization of Poultry Farming in China (Memon et al., 2021). Diarrheal hens were selected when they exhibited depression, ruffled feathers, a reduction in feed intake of ≥15%, increased water consumption, a decrease in egg production of ≥10% for 3 consecutive days, and loose or watery feces with a moisture content of ≥70% lasting for at least 3 days. Healthy control hens showed normal mental status, smooth feathers, stable feed and water intake, egg production fluctuation of ≤3%, and firm granular feces with a fecal moisture content of ≤60%. All hens weighed 1.4 to 1.6 kg, were free of other apparent organic diseases, and had not received antibiotics within 2 weeks before enrollment (Griffin et al., 2022).

Experimental grouping and administration

A total of 90 clinically diarrheal and 30 healthy Hy-Line Brown laying hens were selected from a commercial poultry farm in Wuji County, Shijiazhuang, China. All hens were from the same flock, were 40 weeks old, and were maintained under identical housing and management conditions with free access to feed and water. After a 7-day acclimation period, the healthy hens were assigned to the control group (CON, n = 30), whereas the diarrheal hens were randomly divided into three groups (n = 30 per group): untreated diarrheal model group (MOD), low-dose YGS group (YD), and high-dose YGS group (YG). Hens in the YD and YG groups received YGS aqueous extract by oral gavage at 0.53 and 2.12 g/kg/day, respectively, for 7 consecutive days, while hens in the CON and MOD groups received the same volume of sterile distilled water. The basal diet was a corn–soybean meal-based diet, and its composition and nutrient levels are shown in Table 2. All procedures were approved by the Ethical Review Committee of Laboratory Animal Welfare and Animal Experimentation of China Agricultural University (Approval No. AW0215202-2-04).

Dosage calculation

The theoretical chicken-equivalent dose of YGS was calculated by body surface area normalization based on the routine adult human clinical dose (Griffin et al., 2022). Using a human dose of 75 g/day, a standard adult human body surface area of 1.73 m², and an estimated body surface area of 0.037 m² for a 1.5-kg laying hen, the converted dose was calculated as 1.60 g/day, corresponding to approximately 1.06 g/kg/day. This value was used as the reference equivalent dose for dose selection. Accordingly, the low-dose group received 0.53 g/kg/day, corresponding to 0.5 × the equivalent dose, whereas the high-dose group received 2.12 g/kg/day, corresponding to 2 × the equivalent dose. An additional group receiving the exact equivalent dose of 1.06 g/kg/day was not included in the present study; this point has been clarified as a limitation of the dose-response design.

Sample collection and processing

Body weight was recorded at day 0 and day 7, and egg production was monitored during the 7-day experimental period. After 7 days of treatment, hens were humanely euthanized in accordance with the AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals, using anesthesia followed by cervical dislocation. Blood samples were collected, and serum was separated by centrifugation at 3,000 rpm for 15 min and stored at −80°C until analysis.After euthanasia, the liver, oviduct, and ovary were excised, rinsed with sterile saline, blotted dry, and weighed for calculation of organ indices. Follicle number was also recorded. Mid-ileal tissues were collected, with one portion fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, and the remaining portion stored at −80°C for subsequent molecular analyses. Cecal contents were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C for 16S rRNA gene sequencing.

Body weight measurement and egg production rate

Body weight of each hen was measured on Day 0 (before the start of treatment) and on Day 7 (at the end of the administration period) using a calibrated digital scale. Egg production was recorded at the group level rather than on individual hens. Each experimental group contained 30 hens. The egg production rate was calculated using the formula: Egg Production Rate (%) = (Total Daily Eggs / Number of Laying Hens) x 100%.

Organ index determination

To evaluate organ-related changes, the liver, oviduct, and ovary were collected and weighed after euthanasia. Organ indices were calculated using the following formula: Organ Index (%) = (Organ Wet Weight / Live Body Weight) x 100%.

Histopathological examination and morphometric analysis

Mid-ileum tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 hours, dehydrated, and embedded in paraffin. Sections (5 μm) were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Villus height and crypt depth were measured using ImageJ software. Six well-oriented villi and their corresponding crypts were measured in each group, and the villus height/crypt depth (V/C) ratio was calculated to evaluate ileal mucosal morphology. The original numerical data for villus height, crypt depth, and V/C ratio are provided in Supplementary Table S4.

Determination of oxidative stress-related indices

Serum and ileal tissue homogenates were used for the determination of oxidative stress-related indices. Ileal tissues were homogenized in saline at a ratio of 1:9 (w/v) and centrifuged, and the resulting supernatants were collected for analysis. The activities of total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC; catalog no. A015-2-1) and total superoxide dismutase (T-SOD; catalog no. A001-3-2), as well as the levels of malondialdehyde (MDA; catalog no. A003-1-2) and glutathione (GSH; catalog no. A006-2-1), were measured using commercial assay kits purchased from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was measured using a microplate reader.

Determination of inflammatory cytokines by ELISA

The levels of interleukin-6 (IL-6; catalog no. SEKCN-0161), interleukin-1β (IL-1β; catalog no. SEKCN-0153), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α; catalog no. SEKCN-0006), and interleukin-10 (IL-10; catalog no. SEKCN-0097) in serum and ileal tissue homogenate supernatants were quantified using commercial ELISA kits purchased from Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Absorbance was read at 450 nm, and cytokine concentrations were calculated based on the corresponding standard curves.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from ileal tissues using Trizol reagent. RNA purity (A260/A280 = 1.8∼2.0) and integrity were verified. cDNA was synthesized using a reverse transcription kit. Specific primers for TJP1, Occludin, EGFR, PIK3CA, PIK3R1, AKT1, PTEN, Bcl-2, Bax, and β-Actin were designed and synthesized (Table 3). RT-qPCR was performed using a SYBR Green kit on a thermal cycler. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2^-(∆∆Ct) method.

Western blotting

Ileal tissues were lysed in RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors on ice. After centrifugation, the supernatants were collected and total protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay kit. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with 4% fat-free milk and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against p-AKT (Ser473), AKT, p-PI3K, and PI3K-p85. After incubation with the corresponding HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, protein bands were detected using ECL reagent. Band intensities were analyzed using ImageJ software, and the phosphorylation levels of AKT and PI3K were expressed as the p-AKT (Ser473)/AKT and p-PI3K/PI3K ratios, respectively.

16S gut microbiota analysis

Total DNA was extracted from 24 cecal content samples (6 per group) and subjected to quality assessment. The V3-V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified using barcoded primers and Pfu high-fidelity DNA polymerase. Amplicons were purified, quantified, and sequenced on an Illumina platform. Raw reads were processed in QIIME2 or VSEARCH for ASV/OTU delineation. Downstream analyses included α- and β-diversity, taxonomic profiling, differential feature analysis, and functional pathway prediction based on KEGG/MetaCyc databases .

Integrated correlation analysis of microbial taxa and host phenotypic indices

Integrated correlation analysis was performed to assess the relationships between selected microbial taxa and host phenotypic indices. Host variables included productive performance, ileal morphology, inflammatory cytokines, oxidative stress-related indices, barrier-related markers, and EGFR/PI3K/AKT signaling-related variables. Pearson correlation analysis was used to calculate pairwise correlations among host phenotypic variables, and Mantel tests were further conducted to evaluate the associations between selected microbial taxa and host phenotypic variables. All analyses were performed in R (version 4.5.2).

Data analysis

All experimental data were analyzed using SPSS 26.0 statistical software, and were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD). The differences between groups were analyzed using one-way ANOVA, and multiple comparisons were performed using Duncan's method. A difference was considered significant if P < 0.05, and extremely significant if P < 0.01. Graphs were drawn using GraPhPad Prism 9.0, R language, and Cytoscape 3.9.1 software.

Results

YGS reduced clinical symptom scores in naturally diarrheal laying hens

To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of YGS at the clinical level, clinical symptom scores were assessed before treatment and after the 7-day administration period. Ten hens from each group were evaluated using a standardized scoring system based on fecal consistency, mental status, feather condition, feed intake, and water consumption. The detailed scoring criteria are provided in Supplementary Table S3. As shown in Supplementary Table S3, the CON group maintained low clinical symptom scores throughout the experiment, whereas the MOD group showed persistently high scores after 7 days. Compared with the MOD group, YGS treatment reduced the total clinical symptom score, with a more evident reduction observed in the YGSG group than in the YGSD group. These findings indicate that YGS alleviated diarrhea-associated clinical symptoms in naturally diarrheal laying hens.

Chemical characterization revealed multiple representative constituents of YGS

To characterize the chemical basis of YGS, UHPLC-MS/MS analysis was performed, and a total of 218 compounds were identified in the formula (Table S1). The five herbal materials included in YGS are shown in Fig. 1A. Representative total ion chromatograms obtained in the positive and negative ion modes are presented in Fig. 1B, showing multiple detectable peaks across the retention time range and indicating the chemical complexity of the formula.

Among the identified compounds, hesperidin, isoliquiritigenin, liquiritin, narirutin, nobiletin, tangeretin, glycyrrhizin, and hesperetin were selected as representative constituents for structural presentation (Fig. 1C). Chemical classification further indicated that the identified constituents could be grouped into multiple categories, including alkaloids and N-heterocycles, amino acids and nitrogenous metabolites, coumarins and related benzopyrones, flavonoids and phenolics, lipids and fatty acyls, nucleosides and nucleotides, organic acids and small polar metabolites, phenylpropanoids and aromatic acids, quinones and related phenolics, sugars and sugar acids, terpenoids and saponins, and xenobiotics/contaminants (Fig. 1D). These results indicate that YGS is a multicomponent herbal formula and provide the chemical basis for the subsequent network pharmacology analysis.

Network pharmacology analysis suggested PI3K/AKT-related signaling as a candidate pathway underlying the anti-diarrheal effects of YGS

To explore the potential molecular mechanisms of YGS against diarrhea, network pharmacology analysis was conducted based on YGS-related targets and diarrhea-associated genes. A total of 719 YGS-related targets and 1,743 diarrhea-related genes were collected, and 260 overlapping targets were identified by intersection analysis (Fig. 2A), suggesting that these genes may represent candidate targets of YGS against diarrhea.

(D) KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of the hub genes, highlighting PI3K-Akt signaling as a candidate pathway. (E) Functional classification of the enriched KEGG pathways. (F) Target–function enrichment network linking the hub genes to representative biological processes.

A protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was then constructed using the overlapping targets (Fig. 2B). Further bottleneck analysis identified ten hub genes, including PIK3CA, PIK3CB, PIK3R1, PIK3CD, EGFR, SRC, HRAS, NRAS, PDGFRA, and PDGFRB (Fig. 2C), indicating that growth factor-related and phosphoinositide signaling nodes may be involved in the pharmacological actions of YGS.

KEGG enrichment analysis showed that these hub genes were significantly enriched in several pathways, including EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor resistance, Rap1 signaling pathway, regulation of actin cytoskeleton, phospholipase D signaling pathway, and PI3K-Akt signaling pathway (Fig. 2D). KEGG pathway classification further indicated that the enriched pathways were mainly associated with environmental information processing, cellular processes, and human disease-related pathways (Fig. 2E). In addition, the target–function network linked the hub genes to biological processes such as phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase signaling, phosphatidylinositol-mediated signaling, protein tyrosine kinase activity, platelet-derived growth factor receptor signaling, vascular endothelial growth factor signaling, and wound healing (Fig. 2F). Collectively, these bioinformatic results suggest that PI3K/AKT-related signaling may participate in the anti-diarrheal effects of YGS, and this pathway was therefore selected for subsequent in vivo validation.

YGS alleviates diarrhea-associated phenotypic deterioration and ileal histopathological injury in naturally diarrheal laying hens

To evaluate the therapeutic effects of YGS in naturally diarrheal laying hens, healthy hens and naturally diarrheal hens were assigned to four groups: healthy control (CON), untreated diarrheal model (MOD), low-dose YGS-treated (YGSD), and high-dose YGS-treated (YGSG) groups. Representative H&E staining of ileal tissues showed that the MOD group exhibited marked villus atrophy, crypt hyperplasia, and mucosal structural disruption compared with the CON group, whereas these histopathological changes were partially alleviated by YGS treatment, particularly in the YGSG group(P < 0.01) (Fig. 3A), the original HE section images are presented in the supplementary materials. Consistent with the histological observations, the villus height/crypt depth (V/C) ratio in the ileum was significantly decreased in the MOD group relative to the CON group, while high-dose YGS treatment significantly restored the V/C ratio(P < 0.01). The low-dose treatment showed only a limited improvement (Fig. 3B). These findings indicate that YGS, especially at the high dose, alleviated diarrhea-associated ileal morphological injury.

Naturally diarrheal hens also exhibited impaired productive performance, as evidenced by a reduced egg production rate and body weight loss during the 7-day observation period. YGS treatment improved these phenotypic indices to varying degrees, although the magnitude of recovery differed among parameters and was not uniformly dose-dependent (Fig. 3C,D). In addition, diarrhea was associated with alterations in liver index, oviduct index, ovary index, and follicle number, and YGS partially ameliorated these changes (Fig. 3E). Collectively, these results suggest that YGS alleviated diarrhea-associated phenotypic deterioration and ileal histopathological damage in naturally diarrheal laying hens, with a more pronounced protective effect of the high dose on ileal morphology.

YGS attenuates inflammatory cytokine imbalance in the ileum and serum of naturally diarrheal laying hens

Naturally diarrheal laying hens exhibited marked inflammatory dysregulation in both the ileum and serum (Fig. 4). Compared with the CON group, the MOD group showed significantly increased levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α, together with a marked decrease in the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10, in both ileal tissue homogenates and serum (P < 0.01), indicating the presence of both local and systemic inflammatory responses.

Effects of YGS on inflammatory cytokine profiles in the ileum and serum of naturally diarrheal laying hens. (A) Concentrations of IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-10 in ileal tissue homogenates. (B) Concentrations of IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-10 in serum. Data are shown as mean ± SD (n = 6). **P < 0.01 indicates statistically significant differences between the indicated groups.

YGS treatment alleviated this inflammatory imbalance to different extents. In the ileum, the YGSG group showed a pronounced reduction in IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α levels, along with a significant increase in IL-10, compared with the MOD group (P < 0.01). Similar trends were also observed in serum (Fig. 4A,B). The YGSD group showed partial improvement in several cytokine indices, but its effects were generally weaker than those of the YGSG group. Collectively, these results indicate that YGS attenuated both intestinal and systemic inflammatory responses in naturally diarrheal laying hens, with the high-dose treatment showing a more evident anti-inflammatory effect.

YGS attenuates oxidative stress in the ileum and serum of naturally diarrheal laying hens

Naturally diarrheal laying hens exhibited marked oxidative imbalance in both the ileum and serum (Fig. 5). Compared with the CON group, the MOD group showed significantly decreased levels of T-AOC, T-SOD, and GSH, together with a marked increase in MDA, in both ileal tissue homogenates and serum (P < 0.01), indicating enhanced local and systemic oxidative stress.

YGS treatment alleviated these oxidative alterations to different extents. In the ileum, the YGSG group showed increased T-AOC, T-SOD, and GSH levels and decreased MDA content compared with the MOD group(P<0.01). Similar trends were also observed in serum (Fig. 5A,B). The YGSD group also improved several oxidative stress indices, although the magnitude of recovery was generally weaker than that observed in the YGSG group.Collectively, these results indicate that YGS attenuated oxidative stress in naturally diarrheal laying hens, with the high-dose treatment showing a more pronounced antioxidant effect.

YGS restores EGFR/PI3K/AKT-related transcription, suppresses PI3K/AKT phosphorylation, and regulates apoptosis- and barrier-related genes

To further validate the molecular changes associated with YGS treatment, the mRNA expression of EGFR/PI3K/AKT-related genes, apoptosis-related genes, and tight junction-related genes was measured in ileal tissues, and the phosphorylation status of PI3K and AKT was further examined by Western blot (Fig. 6).

Compared with the CON group, the MOD group showed markedly reduced mRNA expression of PIK3CA, PIK3R1, AKT1, and EGFR, whereas PTEN exhibited an opposite pattern and was significantly increased (Fig. 6A). YGS treatment reversed these transcriptional alterations to different extents, with the YGSG group showing a more pronounced restoration toward the control level. With respect to apoptosis-related genes, the MOD group exhibited significantly decreased Bcl-2 expression and increased Bax expression(P<0.01), indicating enhanced pro-apoptotic signaling. YGS treatment reversed these changes, especially in the YGSG group, which showed increased Bcl-2 and decreased Bax expression relative to the MOD group (Fig. 6B). Similarly, the mRNA expression of the tight junction-related genes TJP1 and Occludin was significantly reduced in the MOD group(P<0.01), whereas YGS treatment restored their expression, with a stronger effect observed in the YGSG group (Fig. 6C).

At the protein level, Western blot analysis showed that the phosphorylation levels of PI3K and AKT (Ser473) were markedly increased in the MOD group (Fig. 6D). Densitometric analysis further confirmed that the p-PI3K/PI3K and p-AKT (Ser473)/AKT ratios were significantly elevated in the MOD group and were reduced after YGS treatment(P<0.01), particularly in the YGSG group (Fig. 6E). The YGSD group showed a partial reduction in p-PI3K/PI3K, whereas its effect on p-AKT (Ser473)/AKT was limited. The original results of the WB experiment are presented in the supplementary materials.

Collectively, these results indicate that YGS restored EGFR/PI3K/AKT-related transcriptional alterations, suppressed aberrant PI3K/AKT phosphorylation, and was accompanied by favorable changes in apoptosis and barrier-related gene expression in the ileum of naturally diarrheal laying hens.

YGS modulated cecal microbial community structure in naturally diarrheal laying hens

To determine whether YGS treatment was associated with changes in cecal microbial communities, 16S rRNA gene sequencing was performed on cecal contents collected from the four groups. As shown in Fig. 7A, No significant differences were observed in alpha-diversity indices among the four groups(P>0.05) , suggesting that naturally occurring diarrhea and YGS treatment did not markedly alter overall cecal microbial richness or evenness. In contrast, beta-diversity ordination analysis suggested groupwise differences in microbial community composition among the four groups (Fig. 7B). Although alpha diversity remained largely unchanged, the spatial distribution of samples indicated that diarrhea and YGS treatment were associated with shifts in cecal microbial structure. Notably, the YGSG group appeared closer to the CON group than did the YGSD group in the ordination plots. The Venn diagram showed that 37 taxa were shared among all four groups, whereas 8, 5, 7, and 7 unique taxa were detected in the CON, MOD, YGSD, and YGSG, respectively (Fig. 7C). At the phylum level, the cecal microbiota was mainly dominated by Bacteroidetes and Firmicutes, while the relative abundance of several minor phyla varied among groups (Fig. 7D). Compared with the CON group, the MOD group appeared to show a higher Firmicutes-to-Bacteroidetes ratio, whereas the YGSG group showed a tendency toward a lower ratio than the MOD group. Collectively, these results indicate that YGS treatment was associated with changes in cecal microbial community composition rather than marked alterations in alpha diversity.

Discussion

The pathogenesis of diarrhea in laying hens is a multifactorial cascade involving gut microbiota dysbiosis, systemic oxidative stress, intestinal barrier collapse, and aberrant immune signaling (Zhang et al., 2025). As the poultry industry transitions away from antibiotic growth promoters, there is an urgent need to decipher the molecular mechanisms of natural alternatives that can restore intestinal homeostasis. This study provides the first evidence that YGS, a classic Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) formula, alleviates diarrhea in laying hens through a strictly dose-dependent mechanism. Specifically, we demonstrate that high-dose YGS (YGSG) orchestrates a holistic recovery by restructuring the gut ecosystem-suppressing proteolytic and succinate-producing pathobionts and consequently inhibiting the overactivation of the EGFR/PI3K/AKT signaling axis, thereby repairing the intestinal epithelial barrier.

Study findings indicate that high-dose YGS administration produced more consistent improvements in productive performance, visceral organ indices, and diarrhea-associated clinical manifestations, including persistent diarrhea and production suppression. In contrast, YGSD showed limited effects on productive performance and organ-related phenotypic parameters, although partial improvements were observed in several inflammation, oxidative stress, and barrier-related indicators. These findings suggest that the therapeutic efficacy of YGS was dose-dependent to some extent, with the high-dose treatment exerting broader and more stable protective effects than the low-dose treatment.

At the histological level, the diarrhea model (MOD) exhibited severe villus atrophy, crypt hyperplasia, and a plummeted Villus/Crypt (V/C) ratio. This structural collapse is inextricably linked to oxidative stress. Environmental stressors prevalent in intensive farming, such as heat stress (Beume et al., 2018), induce the accumulation of Reactive OxYGSGen Species (ROS) in the poultry gut (Belote et al., 2023). Excessive ROS attack mitochondrial membranes in enterocytes, triggering cytochrome c release and activating the caspase-dependent apoptotic pathway, which ultimately degrades tight junction proteins. Our data showed that YGSG treatment significantly upregulated the activities of antioxidant enzymes (T-SOD, GSH-Px) and reduced MDA levels. By scavenging ROS (Aryal et al., 2025), YGS cuts off the upstream signal for apoptosis, preserving the physical integrity of the villi. Furthermore, the upregulation of TJP1 (ZO-1) and Occludin in the YGSG group confirms the restoration of the paracellular barrier (Li et al., 2024), preventing the "leaky gut" phenomenon responsible for systemic inflammation and nutrient malabsorption.

A pivotal finding of this study is the identification of the EGFR/PI3K/AKT signaling pathway as the core molecular target of YGS. While the PI3K/AKT pathway is physiologically essential for cell survival and metabolism (Bang et al., 2023), its role in chronic intestinal inflammation is distinctively pathological.

Recent studies indicate that under conditions of chronic damage or infection, the EGFR/PI3K/AKT pathway is often maladaptively overactivated (Zhang et al., 2018). This hyperactivation inhibits autophagy and promotes a response that paradoxically drives inflammation-associated hyperplasia and suppresses the expression of negative regulators like PTEN (Mukherjee et al., 2021). In our study, the MOD group exhibited significantly decreased mRNA expression of EGFR, PIK3CA, and AKT1, while PTEN was significantly increased, accompanied by a decreased Bcl-2/Bax ratio, indicating an inflammation-driven apoptotic state.

YGSG treatment effectively cooled down this overactive pathway, downregulating the phosphorylation cascade and upregulating the tumor suppressor PTEN. This mechanism mirrors the action of other potent anti-inflammatory phytochemicals (e.g., Paeonol(Liu et al., 2020; Ma et al., 2023), Peach gum (Niu et al., 2025), which have been shown to alleviate colitis by blocking the PI3K/AKT/NF-κB axis. By inhibiting this pathway, YGS shifts the cellular program from pro-inflammatory survival to homeostatic repair, allowing for the re-establishment of a functional epithelial barrier.

The lack of significant differences in alpha diversity between healthy and diarrheic hens does not undermine the validity of the diarrheal phenotype, which was defined by objective clinical and fecal parameters, including fecal moisture, fecal consistency, and symptom scores. Alpha diversity reflects overall richness and evenness, but diarrhea-associated dysbiosis may involve shifts in specific taxa, relative abundance, microbial interactions, or functional potential without significantly altering α-diversity. Additionally, the cecal microbiota of adult laying hens is relatively stable, and α-diversity indices in this compartment may be less sensitive to diarrhea-related changes occurring in other intestinal segments. Therefore, our findings suggest that YGS intervention primarily induced compositional remodeling of the cecal microbiota rather than broad changes in microbial richness or evenness. The MOD exhibited a marked expansion of Paraprevotella, a taxon endowed with robust proteolytic activity and an extensive repertoire of peptidase‑encoding genes (Patra et al., 2022). Under dysbiotic conditions, these bacteria vigorously degrade host mucin glycoproteins and dietary proteins, liberating cytotoxic metabolites including ammonia and branched‑chain fatty acids. These metabolites compromise intestinal mucosal integrity, directly incite epithelial damage, and perpetuate a state of persistent intestinal inflammation (Huang et al., 2023). YGSG intervention significantly diminished the relative abundance of Paraprevotella, thereby attenuating proteolytic stress and preserving the structural and functional integrity of the intestinal mucus barrier. Flavonoids such as hesperidin and nobiletin may selectively enrich beneficial gut bacteria, including Lactobacillus and Akkermansia, promoting mucosal barrier integrity and anti-inflammatory effects (Huang et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2023). Together, these microbiota changes may functionally contribute to alleviating diarrhea and restoring intestinal homeostasis in laying hens .

A pivotal observation was the phenomenon of dysbiotic recovery evident in the YGSD. Although low‑dose YGS elevated beneficial Lactobacillus, it concurrently elicited an aberrant overgrowth of Succinivibrionaceae, a major bacterial family responsible for luminal succinate production (Pope et al., 2011). As an emerging pro‑inflammatory metabokine, succinate engages the SUCNR1 (GPR91) receptor expressed on macrophages and dendritic cells, thereby driving M1 macrophage polarization and enhancing IL‑1β secretion via the HIF‑1α signaling axis (Zeng et al., 2024). YGSD provided mild prebiotic support, promoting beneficial bacteria such as Lactobacillus but insufficiently controlling opportunistic taxa like Succinivibrionaceae, which may have limited its overall therapeutic effect. By contrast, YGSG fostered precision microbiome restoration by concomitantly suppressing Paraprevotella and Succinivibrionaceae while enriching Lactobacillus, thereby establishing a homeostatic and anti‑inflammatory intestinal microenvironment conducive to mucosal repair.

Integrated analyses of gut microbial profiling, histopathology, and molecular signaling suggest that YGS modulates a microbiota–metabolite–EGFR/PI3K/AKT axis in a dose-dependent manner. In untreated diarrheal hens, the cecal microbiota showed enrichment of the mucin-degrading genus Paraprevotella, which likely compromised the mucus barrier and contributed to ileal mucosal damage and reduced villus-to-crypt ratios. These findings highlight the interplay between dysbiotic gut microbiota and intestinal epithelium as a key determinant of diarrhea severity in laying hens. Mechanistically, this physical breach facilitates the translocation of luminal pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), particularly lipopolysaccharides (LPS) derived from overgrown Gram-negative taxa, into the lamina propria (Carneiro et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2023). Accumulated LPS intensely activates Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) signaling on host immune and epithelial cells. Previous in vitro and in vivo studies have demonstrated that TLR4 activation triggers the transactivation of the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) via matrix metalloproteinase-dependent cleavage of membrane-bound ligands, thereby linking innate immune sensing directly to the dysregulation of epithelial growth pathways (Tang et al., 2020) and toxic metabolites such as ammonia to interact with the intestinal epithelium; meanwhile, opportunistic pathogens release metabolites including succinate and uracil analogues that aberrantly activate host signaling receptors, which together trigger the EGFR/PI3K/AKT (Liu et al., 2025). Concurrently, another pivotal finding was the aberrant overrepresentation of the succinate-producing family Succinivibrionaceae in the dysbiotic gut. Notably, this condition was exacerbated rather than corrected by the YGSD, yet it was effectively suppressed by the YGSG. This opportunistic overgrowth likely elevated luminal succinate concentrations, a metabolite increasingly recognized as a potent pro-inflammatory signal in the intestinal microenvironment. By engaging the SUCNR1 (GPR91) receptor expressed on mucosal macrophages and the intestinal epithelium, excess succinate primes the NLRP3 inflammasome and promotes M1 macrophage polarization, driving a robust pro-inflammatory cytokine response (Bauset et al., 2022; Connors et al., 2018). This mechanism is supported by our enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) data, which showed significantly elevated levels of IL-1β and TNF-α, along with increased oxidative stress (MDA accumulation) in both serum and ileal tissues of the MOD. These inflammatory mediators likely act in a paracrine manner, synergizing with direct EGFR transactivation to maintain hyperphosphorylation and pathological overactivation of the intracellular PI3K/AKT signaling cascade. Prolonged activation of this compensatory, inflammation-driven survival pathway is accompanied by an increase in PTEN expression in the MOD group, which may represent a feedback response. YGS treatment tends to normalize PTEN levels, helping to restore epithelial homeostasis, reduce dysregulated apoptosis (as indicated by the altered Bcl-2/Bax ratio), and protect tight junction proteins (TJP1 and Occludin), ultimately alleviating intestinal barrier dysfunction and improving egg production performance. Dietary supplementation with an adequate, high dose of YGS effectively interrupted this vicious pathogenic cycle through synergistic dual interventions at both the gut ecological and host pharmacological levels. Microbiologically, high-dose YGS reshaped the dysbiotic architecture by restricting the proliferation of Paraprevotella and preventing the succinate-driven opportunistic bloom of Succinivibrionaceae, thereby significantly reducing the luminal burden of proteolytic enzymes and pro-inflammatory metabolic stimuli. Furthermore, this precision microbial remodeling successfully restored the population of beneficial Lactobacillus, which supports the recovery of core amino acid and energy metabolic pathways as indicated by our KEGG/MetaCyc functional prediction analysis. The restoration of Lactobacillus and associated commensals inherently promotes the luminal production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), such as butyrate. Because SCFAs are established histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitors (Chen et al., 2019) known to transcriptionally activate PTEN expression in intestinal epithelial cells (Wang et al., 2007), This finding provides a highly plausible upstream mechanistic association for the significant upregulation of PTEN mRNA observed in the ileum of hens treated with high doses of YGS. This endogenous SCFA-PTEN interaction acts as a natural biological brake on the PI3K/AKT axis. At the direct pharmacological level, our UPHPLC-MS/MS and network pharmacology analyses identified 218 bioactive compounds in YGS, including specific flavonoids from Citrus reticulata and saponins from Codonopsis pilosula, which possess documented independent inhibitory properties against the EGFR/PI3K/AKT cascade (Chen et al., 2023). Corroborating these bioinformatic predictions, our in vivo qPCR validations confirmed that high-dose YGS significantly downregulated the mRNA expression of EGFR, PIK3CA, and AKT1, indicating that the systematically absorbed phytochemicals can directly target and suppress this hyper-activated epithelial signaling pathway even in the presence of residual inflammatory stimuli. Taken together, these findings reveal that YGS operates far beyond the limited scope of a traditional antimicrobial agent or a single-target pathway inhibitor; rather, it functions as a holistic, multi-target intestinal modulator. By synergistically correcting microbiota-derived inflammatory metabolic stimuli from the lumen and directly modulating host epithelial signaling responses from within the tissue, an adequate therapeutic dose of YGS restores tight junction integrity, inhibits pathological mucosal apoptosis, and fundamentally alleviates diarrheal symptoms.

Furthermore, our integrated Spearman correlation analysis and Mantel test provided statistical validation for the proposed "microbiota -EGFR/PI3K/AKT- intestinal barrier" regulatory axis. The high intrinsic correlation among laying hen phenotypes indicated that YGS exerted a comprehensive alleviative effect on diarrhea in this species. Our findings confirmed that oxidative stress alleviation and pro-inflammatory cytokine inhibition were closely associated with the restoration of tight junction proteins (TJP1 and Occludin) and downregulation of PTEN. The Mantel test elucidated the link between intestinal microbial community alterations and host intracellular signal transduction. Specifically, the significant enrichment of Lactobacillus abundance was strongly correlated with the functional recovery of laying hens, highlighting its key role in intestinal mucosal repair and pathway inhibition. Conversely, the robust statistical association between opportunistic pathogens (Paraprevotella and Succinivibrionaceae) and aberrant activation of EGFR, PIK3CA and AKT1 strongly demonstrated that these taxa actively drove the pathological cascade. This comprehensive correlation not only statistically explained the clinical failure of low-dose YGS intervention caused by Succinivibrionaceae bloom, but also fundamentally confirmed that high-dose YGS exerts its therapeutic effect via the microbiota-driven EGFR/PI3K/AKT signaling pathway.

This study has several limitations. The diarrheal hens were selected based on fecal moisture, fecal consistency, and clinical symptom scoring under field conditions, but pathogen-specific diagnostic tests were not performed. Therefore, bacterial, viral, or parasitic contributions to naturally occurring diarrhea could not be completely excluded. Future studies should include bacterial culture, PCR or RT-PCR detection of major poultry pathogens, and fecal parasitological examination to more precisely classify the etiology of diarrhea and to further validate the therapeutic effects of YGS in defined diarrhea models.

Conclusions

In summary, YGS treats laying hen diarrhea through a strictly dose - dependent mechanism. High - dose YGS orchestrates a holistic recovery by rebalancing the gut microbiota (preventing Succinivibrionaceae overgrowth) and inhibiting the EGFR/PI3K/AKT pathway to repair the intestinal barrier. New intestinal health markers are provided, which lays the foundation for the development of next - generation targeted antiveterinary drugs that regulate "microbiota - receptor" communication. These findings underscore the importance of dosage optimization in TCM clinical applications.However, this study lacks direct causal verification between fecal microbiota transplantation and targeted metabolomics. Core targets were not confirmed in an in vitro cellular model.
