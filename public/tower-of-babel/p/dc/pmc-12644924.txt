World Dementia Council Update
Published: 2025

In recent years, the dementia field has seen remarkable progress in the development, understanding, and application of biomarkers for Alzheimer's disease and related dementias. These advancements present an opportunity and responsibility to share lessons learned across researchers and the broader scientific community. To facilitate this information sharing, the World Dementia Council (WDC), with support from the Alzheimer's Drug Discovery Foundation (ADDF), held its first session in a new dialogue series exploring innovation in dementia on October 31, 2025. This meeting convened international experts to explore challenges and opportunities surrounding breakthroughs in biomarkers and their potential to influence research, diagnosis, and treatment.

INTRODUCTION

Following opening remarks from Lenny Shallcross, Executive Director of the WDC, Dr. Laura Nisenbaum, Executive Director of Drug Development at ADDF and co‐chair of the meeting, described the pivotal role of biomarkers in advancing Alzheimer's and dementia research. Biomarkers have transformed the field, enabling the first disease‐modifying therapies and accelerating progress through various initiatives. Dr. Nisenbaum also noted recent breakthroughs in blood‐based and digital biomarkers, including artificial intelligence (AI)–driven tools. Despite these advances, there are still many areas of unmet need, including greater understanding around biomarkers for co‐pathologies and the underlying pathobiology of dementia, including inflammation, metabolism, and vascular health. Addressing these gaps and gaining a greater insight into the processes that influence dementia, will allow for more individualized and patient‐centered treatment in the future.

CHALLENGES AND INNOVATIVE APPROACHES IN CLINICAL RESEARCH

Dr. Michael Weiner, Professor in Residence in Radiology at the University of California San Francisco, outlined key challenges and innovative approaches in clinical research. Among the major challenges he identified were the high costs of clinical trials and the lack of diversity among trial participants, who are often affluent, highly educated, and unrepresentative of the broader population. Individuals from lower socioeconomic backgrounds frequently face limited access to health care and have comorbid conditions that can negatively affect brain health, making current trial results and diagnostic tools less generalizable to these populations. To address these disparities, the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) is launching the TEAMS ADNI project to enroll 200 individuals with lower socioeconomic status recruited through a network of federally qualified health centers. These participants will undergo a streamlined ADNI protocol, including assessments, blood tests, and magnetic resonance imaging/positron emission tomography (MRI/PET) scans, aiming to build a more representative and inclusive research cohort. Dr. Weiner also highlighted the use of AI tools, including the development of a Voice Clinical Dementia Rating (VCDR), which uses automated voice agents to interview caregivers or patients to help screen and assess patients. These and other similar tools have the potential to support various functions of clinical trials, from screening and consenting to monitoring and scheduling, ultimately leading to reductions in cost and improved efficiency and accessibility.

THE BIOLOGICAL INTERPRETATION OF ALZHEIMER'S DISEASE BIOMARKERS

Dr. Randall Bateman, the Charles F. and Joanne Knight Distinguished Professor of Neurology at WashU Medicine, described the importance of understanding the specific molecular processes underlying dementia biomarkers. Currently, the limited understanding of the structural and molecular differences of biomarkers, particularly tau, may pose challenges for their application in clinical and therapeutic settings. Not all forms of tau are the same, each form changes at different stages and correlates differently with amyloid, atrophy, hypometabolism, tangles, and clinical progression. There also remains a gap in knowledge regarding biomarkers of other pathological processes, such as neuroinflammation and non‐Alzheimer's neurodegeneration that must be addressed. Dr. Bateman emphasized that not all tau is created equal; different tau species reflect distinct biological processes, and understanding these molecular mechanisms may highlight how tau contributes to disease pathology. Ongoing research is needed to continue to explore the relationships between biomarkers and underlying brain pathology, including amyloid plaques and tau tangles. Understanding this will lead to improved knowledge, diagnosis, and treatment developments in Alzheimer's disease.

NEW APPROACHES TO BIOMARKER COLLECTION PROTOCOLS

Dr. Henrik Zetterberg, Professor of Neurochemistry at the University of Gothenburg, presented the innovative DROP‐AD project, which aims to improve access to and ease of biomarker testing. Traditional methods rely on venous draws, limiting access for many patients. However, DROP‐AD introduces a simpler approach using capillary finger pricks, removing the need for venipuncture that can be guided by the caregiver. DROP‐AD uses capillary dried blood and plasma spots that can be processed and analyzed through a specialized machine to detect biomarkers. Results from the project have shown detectable levels of phosphorylated tau‐217 (p‐tau217) and other Alzheimer's biomarkers comparable to that of venous draws. Both patients and caregivers reported positive experiences, and follow‐up studies confirmed the reproducibility of the results. To further enhance protocol, the team is refining sample collection to reduce issues like hemolysis and intermixing of interstitial fluid, and is testing alternative collection methods to the finger prick to improve patient comfort and experience. DROP‐AD represents a promising step toward broader, more inclusive biomarker testing.

CLINICAL IMPLEMENTATION AND PATIENT CONSIDERATIONS

Dr. Charlotte Teunissen, Professor of Neurochemistry at Amsterdam UMC, shared her insights on the clinical implementation of biomarkers. Clinical implementation of biomarkers, considered the fifth phase in biomarker development, is increasingly important, especially with the development of new therapies. Dr. Teunissen emphasized that proper clinical implementation requires addressing key questions: its impact on care systems, decision‐making, patient‐reported outcomes, and integration with digital biomarkers. To explore these issues, Dr. Teunissen's team surveyed providers about Alzheimer's blood biomarkers in the clinical setting. Providers highlighted the need for clear result interpretation, accuracy, treatment availability, and provider education. Providers also noted a number of facilitating and hindering factors to biomarker implementation. Key facilitating factors included timely diagnosis, improved communication with patients and relatives, and the cost‐effective and non‐invasive nature of biomarkers. Hindering factors included a lack of clear guidelines, a potential increase in referrals and further testing, and a potential burden placed on patients and providers. The team also assessed patient attitudes, revealing strong support for blood‐based testing, even in the absence of treatment. These findings highlight the need to train and educate primary care providers to ensure effective and informed implementation.

DISCUSSION

Following the presentations, Dr. Stacie Weninger, President of F‐Prime Biomedical Research and co‐chair of the meeting, opened the discussion, expressing her excitement around the progress being made in biomarker development and therapeutics, while emphasizing the need for consistency in measurement and understanding how biomarkers vary across diverse populations, considering factors such as socioeconomic status, race, ethnicity, and education. She also stressed the importance of identifying biomarkers for other conditions like Parkinson's and frontotemporal dementia. Participants spoke of the need to equip primary care providers with tools and resources to appropriately counsel amyloid‐positive patients, particularly those who are cognitively normal and not yet eligible for treatments. This raises the importance of a deeper molecular understanding of biomarkers and better education for both providers and the public. Participants agreed that biomarkers should be treated as tools, not standalone diagnostics, and must be interpreted alongside clinical context by a provider. Other key points included the need to improve dementia screenings in primary care, address specialist shortages, and educate primary care providers on dementia. The session closed with excitement for the future and the continued need for data sharing, collaboration, education and equitable implementation.
