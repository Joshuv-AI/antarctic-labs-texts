Depression and apathy: examining an overlooked dimension
Authors: Elvira Anna Carbone, Renato de Filippis, Alessia Scalzo, Emanuela Calabretta, Stefano Lodari, Marianna Rania, Giulia Menculini, Alfonso Antonio Vincenzo Tortorella, Cristina Segura-Garcia, Pasquale De Fazio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundApathy is a significant yet frequently overlooked aspect of Major Depressive Disorder (MDD), often confused with anhedonia, and with the potential to influence clinical outcomes and quality of life regardless of the severity of depressive symptoms. Despite its importance, the relationship between apathy and depression remains underrepresented in current research. This study aims to assess the prevalence of apathy in a sample of outpatients affected by MDD and to explore its clinical, cognitive, and functional correlates.MethodsOutpatients with MDD (n = 154) during a clinical stability phase have been assessed using the Apathy Evaluation Scale-Clinician version (AES-C), Dimensional Apathy Scale (I-DAS), MADRS, BDI-II, MMSE, PANSS, EQ-5D-3 L, and CGI-S. Patients were categorized into two groups according to the presence/absence of apathy (AES-C score ≥ 42). Groups comparison and a stepwise logistic regression were run to identify clinical differences between groups and the predictors of apathy.ResultsApathy was detected in 48% of the sample. Patients with apathy scored higher on depression scales, had lower cognitive performance, more negative and general psychopathology, greater clinical severity, and reduced quality of life. Significant differences emerged in initiation and executive apathy. Logistic regression identified MADRS item 8 “inability to feel” and BDI-II item 12 “loss of interest” as the factors showing the strongest association with apathy.ConclusionApathy is a prevalent and clinically relevant feature in MDD, associated with greater psychopathology and functional impairment. Findings support the need to routinely assess apathy in clinical setting, even during symptomatic stability phases. Introduction

Apathy is a multidimensional construct described as reduced motivation, goal-directed behaviour, and a lack of emotional responsiveness [1]. It was first described by Marin over 30 years ago as “a disorder of diminished motivation not attributable to diminished levels of consciousness, cognitive impairment, or emotional distress”. Since the first description, several definitions have been proposed [2].

Apathy is commonly observed across a range of psychiatric, neurological, and physical disorders and is increasingly recognized as a distinct clinical syndrome, rather than merely a symptom of underlying conditions. This multidimensional framework applies across the lifespan and clinical populations, although age may influence the severity or expression of specific domains. It may include impairments across affective, cognitive, and behavioural domains: (a) “Emotional apathy” (or emotional blunting) refers to a lack or reduction in empathy, spontaneous emotions, emotional reactivity or emotional intensity, and symptoms of indifference; (b) “behavioural apathy” refers to a reduction in an individual’s willingness to engage in or sustain voluntary goal-directed physical activity (sluggishness) and the need for external prompts to engage in daily living tasks; (c) “cognitive apathy” refers to a reduction in spontaneous ideas or intellectual curiosity; decreased interest in problem solving; (d) “social apathy” refers to a reduction in the initiative to propose or engage in social activities or interactions that represent a significant decline from the individual’s previous level of functioning [3].

Apathy is frequent among patients with major depressive disorder (MDD) [3, 4] but often under-recognized. Although it shares features with depressive symptoms such as anhedonia and psychomotor retardation, apathy is considered a unique construct, both clinically and neurobiologically [4, 5]. Anhedonia is defined as the inability to feel pleasure from activities that were pleasant, leading to isolation and detachment. It is remarkable that apathy can significantly worsen functioning and quality of life in this population and is associated with reduced treatment adherence, reduced responsiveness to therapy, poor clinical outcome, higher rate of hospitalization, and increased overall mortality [6–12]. Despite its impact, no pharmacological treatments are currently approved to target apathy. Antidepressants (ADs) are commonly used even if evidence suggests that they are largely ineffective in addressing symptoms of apathy [5, 13]. Moreover, certain psychotropic medications may surprisingly induce or exacerbate apathy. Notably, selective serotonin uptake inhibitors (SSRIs) have been implicated in “apathy syndrome” characterized by emotional blunting and reduced initiative [14, 15]. This syndrome, often dose-dependent, has also been reported in individuals with obsessive-compulsive disorder, who typically require higher doses of ADs, thereby raising the risk of a vicious cycle of apathy [16]. Although apathy has been extensively studied in neurological and psychiatric disorders [17–21], in MDD it remains still less frequently assessed with dedicated, validated tools, particularly in patients considered clinically stable. Moreover, few studies have comprehensively combined clinician-rated and self-report measures or examined apathy at the item level in relation to depressive symptoms, cognitive performance, and functional outcomes within a single cohort.

Based on the above, the primary aim of this study is to explore the prevalence and multidimensional features of apathy in a sample of individuals with MDD during a clinical stability phase. A secondary aim is to examine the relationship between apathy and depressive symptoms at the item level, as well as cognitive performance and functional outcome, using a combination of clinician-rated and self-report measures. While previous studies have applied factor analytic approaches to identify item-level association in depression scales [22, 23], our study extends this work by evaluating specifically, integrating multiple instruments, and considering clinically stable patients. We hypothesize that apathy is a clinically relevant and detectable feature of MDD and that its presence is associated with greater psychopathological severity.

Methods

Participants

We enrolled outpatients aged 18 to 75 years, with a diagnosis of MDD according to Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision (DSM-5 TR) criteria [24], at the Psychiatric Unit of the University Hospital Renato Dulbecco, Catanzaro (Italy), between January 2022 and December 2024 and during a clinical stability phase.

Patients were considered clinically stable if, during the 3 months prior to the enrolment, they reported no relapse, clinical worsening or hospitalization and either maintained stable dosage of their pharmacological treatment or had changes in their dose of psychotropic medications of < 50%. Clinical stability did not imply full symptomatic remission; rather, patients could still present residual depressive symptoms provided that their overall clinical status remained unchanged within this timeframe. Relapse and clinical worsening during the 3 months prior to enrolment were determined based on clinical documentation (medical records and clinician reports) and patient interviews. Relapse was defined as any re-emergence of depressive symptoms leading to treatment adjustment, hospitalization, or clinical intervention. Clinical worsening was assessed by clinician judgment, considering notable increases in symptom severity or functional impairment compared with the preceding three months.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) difficulty understanding semi-structured questions; (2) current and/or last six months abuse of alcohol and psychotropic substances (excluding tobacco); (3) use of cannabis/tetrahydrocannabinol and regular use of over-the-counter medications; (4) diagnosis of neurodegenerative disorders (e.g. Parkinson’s disease, Alzheimer’s disease, Huntington’s disease, others form of dementia) or a documented history of significant head injury (defined as loss of consciousness > 30 min or requiring emergency evaluation).

All participants were informed of the study’s objectives, procedures, and anonymity. Each participant signed a written informed consent declaring their willingness to participate freely in the study. The study protocol and procedures comply with the ethical principles set out in the revised version of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethical Committee of “Regione Calabria, sezione Area Centro (approval No. 02/2022)”.

Procedures

The assessment included a clinical interview performed by trained psychiatrists. The psychiatric diagnosis was established based on the structured clinical interview for the DSM-5 TR [24]. Sociodemographic, clinical and psychometric data were collected. To enhance diagnostic accuracy and account for potential discrepancies between clinician and patient ratings, both clinician-observed (MADRS/AES-C) and patient-experienced (BDI-II/I-DAS) dimensions of depression and apathy were assessed, capturing complementary perspectives on symptom severity.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize sociodemographic and clinical data. Participants were categorized into groups based on the AES-C cut-off score (≥ 42 for clinically significant apathy). The AES-C was used to define apathy status due to its robust psychometric properties, wide use in clinical research, and availability of a validated cut-off (≥ 42) for categorizing clinically significant apathy [1]. Between-group comparisons were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, given the non-parametric distribution of the data. A significance threshold of p < 0.05 was adopted. Further comparisons were conducted at the item level for MADRS and BDI-II scores, applying the Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons.

A stepwise logistic regression was then performed to identify predictors of apathy, including only variables that showed significant between-group differences. Antidepressant therapy entered as a covariate (yes/no) to control for potential treatment effects.

Odds ratios were calculated for each predictor. Multicollinearity was assessed to ensure model validity, and highly correlated variables were addressed accordingly. For non-parametric comparisons, data are presented as median and interquartile range (IQR), and effect sizes are reported as rank biserial correlations. Multicollinearity among predictors included in the logistic regression was assessed using Variance Inflation Factor (VIF). All VIF values ​​were below 2 indicating no significant multicollinearity among the predictors. Where applicable, internal consistency (Cronbach’s α) was calculated on the study sample to ensure adequate psychometric reliability. All analyses were performed using JASP software (Version 0.16.4, 2022).

Results

A total of 154 patients were included in the study. The median age of participants was 61 years (IQR = 16). They majority were women (~ 70%), married (~ 64%), and 32% were employed. The median duration of illness was 4 years (IQR = 9). Only 14% were smokers. Suicide attempts and hospitalizations were reported in a very low percentage of cases (7% and 10%, respectively). Around 96% were being treated at the time of assessment, with the most used drugs being ADs (88%), benzodiazepines (54%), antipsychotics (APs) (43%) and mood stabilizers (22%). Among ADs, SSRIs were the most frequently used class (63%), while among APs, atypical ones prevailed (40%). Table 1 describes the socio-demographic characteristics of the sample (Table 1). Participants with apathy were significantly older than those without apathy (p = 0.017). No statistically significant group differences emerged across socio-demographic or treatment-related characteristics, but significant differences were observed across clinical, cognitive, and functional domains. Striking differences were observed in all subdomains of the I-DAS. Patients with apathy scored significantly higher in the Executive (p < 0.001), Initiation (p < 0.001), and Emotional (p = 0.017) subscales, as well as on the total score (p < 0.001). Greater symptom severity was observed in the apathy group either from clinician’s (MADRS p < 0.001) or patient’s perspective (BDI-II p < 0.001), and on CGI-S (p < 0.001). Cognitive performance was significantly lower in the apathy group (MMSE p = 0.013). Regarding psychotic symptoms, no significant difference was found in the Positive subscale of PANSS (p = 0.538). However, the apathy group showed significantly higher scores in the Negative (p < 0.001), General (p < 0.001), and Total scales (p < 0.001). Lastly, self-perceived quality of life, measured by the EQ-5D-3 L, was significantly lower in patients with apathy (p < 0.001).

Each item of the MADRS and BDI-II scales was then considered separately. Patients with MDD and apathy scored significantly higher on several individual items of the MADRS and BDI-II than those without apathy (Table 3; Detailed item-level normalized bar charts for MADRS and BDI-II are provided in the Supplementary Data). After applying Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons (p < 0.005 for MADRS; p < 0.0024 for BDI-II) the following items remained significant. On the MADRS, significant differences were observed in apparent sadness (item 1), reported sadness (item 2), concentration difficulties (item 6), lassitude (item 7), inability to feel (item 8), and pessimistic thoughts (item 9). On the BDI-II, patients with apathy scored significantly higher on sadness (item 1), pessimism (item 2), loss of pleasure (item 4), disconformity with oneself (item 7), self-criticism (item 8), suicidal thoughts (item 9), loss of interest (item 12), indecision (item 13), loss of energy (item 15), difficulties in concentration (item 19), and tiredness or fatigue (item 20).

Discussion

This study explored the prevalence and clinical correlates of apathy in a sample of patients with MDD without neurodegenerative disorders, highlighting its associations with depressive symptomatology, cognitive functioning, and quality of life. Almost half of the sample met the cut-off for clinically apathy, as measured by the AES-C. Similar prevalence rates have been reported in prior studies, with estimates ranging from 30% to 45% in individuals with depression [35–37]. Results indicate that apathy is a highly prevalent and clinically relevant phenomenon even in a population of individuals with clinical stability. Our findings corroborate and extend previous literature suggesting that apathy is not simply a result of depressive severity but may represent a definite and multidimensional construct in MDD [17, 35, 36].

In our study, patients with apathy exhibited significantly greater severity in psychopathology, as evaluated by both clinician-rated (MADRS, CGI-S) and self-report (BDI-II) measures. These patients also presented impaired cognitive performance (MMSE) and lower levels of self-perceived quality of life (EQ-5D-3 L).

Cognitive impairments may partly explain the higher prevalence of apathy observed in this group [35, 36]. Indeed, it is documented the overlap between apathy, depression, and impairments in executive functioning [37, 38]. A further consideration is the age difference observed between patients with and without clinically significant apathy. While apathy is defined as a transdiagnostic construct not restricted by age, age-related factors may modulate its expression or detection. Apathy may also influence the quality of life (QoL). Research demonstrated that apathy was linked to lower QoL scores independent of depressive symptoms and cognitive impairment and may persist even without clinical depression or cognitive deficits, highlighting apathy’s distinct impact on perceived well-being [39].

Of note, the most pronounced differences between groups were observed on the I-DAS, especially in the initiation and executive subdomains. These findings suggest that deficits in goal-directed behaviour, motivational drive, and executive functioning are central features of apathy in MDD. The emotional apathy subscale was also significantly elevated in patients with apathy, though to a lesser extent, highlighting the heterogeneity of the construct [40]. The increase of Negative and General subscales of PANSS may reveal the overlap between apathy and negative symptoms such as emotional withdrawal, poor rapport, and lack of spontaneity, and motivational deficits [1]. This multidimensional profile supports the idea that apathy consists of distinct but overlapping domains (i.e., cognitive, behavioural, and emotional) and suggest that different mechanisms underlie these forms of apathy, hypothetically suggesting a shared but distinct neurobiological substrate [40–43]. Contrary to some prior evidence [5, 7, 9, 12, 44], apathy was not associated with longer illness duration, higher hospitalization rates, or specific pharmacological treatments, including antidepressants, antipsychotics, or mood stabilizers. This may indicate that the clinical stability of our sample or the effectiveness of current treatments in alleviating depressive symptoms do not completely address apathy. However, it is important to note that the relationship between antidepressant use - particularly SSRIs - and apathy is complex, with evidence suggesting both potential improvement and induction of motivational deficits depending on individual and pharmacological factors [45–50]. While the absence of association with pharmacological drugs in our sample should not be interpreted as definitive evidence, it may provide tentative support for the theoretical and biological distinction between apathy and depression [17, 51].

The item-level analyses of the MADRS and BDI-II further support this dissimilarity, showing that patients with apathy scored significantly higher on items related to affective (e.g., “inability to feel,” “loss of pleasure,” “loss of interest”) and cognitive (e.g., “indecision”, “concentration difficulty,” “pessimistic thoughts”) domains. To note, items related to somatic domain (e.g., “changes in appetite”, “reduced sleep”) were not different between patients with and without apathy. Indeed, the logistic regression found significant correlation only with MADRS item 8 (“inability to feel”) and BDI-II item 12 (“loss of interest”) that emerged as the strongest predictors of apathy in our sample. Although these items may appear conceptually similar, they identify distinct features. The MADRS item 8 “inability to feel” assesses the clinician’ evaluation of patient’s inability to experience emotions, particularly positive affect, and suggests a core feature of emotional blunting or affective anhedonia (“liking”). In contrast, BDI-II item 12 “loss of interest” concerns the individual’s conscious recognition of diminished drive or interest toward social or recreational activities or behavioural/motivational anhedonia (“wanting”) [52, 53]. The presence of both symptoms among patients with apathy suggests that apathy in MDD may clinically involve not only a reduced capacity to produce affective responses but also a disturbance of goal-directed behaviour that are core components of apathy in MDD. These findings may also have biological implications. Several studies investigated the neurobiological underpinnings of apathy and most of them supported the central role of the meso-cortico-limbic circuit, pre-frontal cortex, and anterior cingulate cortex [54–59]. While these neural correlates provide a neurobiological context, the primary relevance of our results lies in the psychometric domain: specific depressive items (e.g., MADRS item 8, BDI-II item 12) may serve as early indicators of apathy, supporting the need for routine assessment with dedicated scales in patients with MDD even during a phase of clinical stability. Moreover, as apathy is associated to poorer cognitive performance and QoL, it may represent a key diagnostic marker and treatment target in this population, encompassing psychotherapeutic, pharmacological, and neuromodulatory interventions. In this light, clinical and neural correlates highlight apathy’s role in motivational processing independently of hedonic experience, suggesting that apathy may reflect a broader, higher-order construct encompassing motivational deficits beyond anhedonia, and could be more closely associated with the emergence of mild cognitive impairment. While these constructs are distinct but overlapping, their hierarchical relationship remains a subject of ongoing investigation.

The results of the present study should be read in light of some limitations. First, the cross-sectional study design precludes to infer about causality. The lack of a control group without MDD and apathy and in full clinical remission may reduce the generalizability of the results. Nevertheless, the use of both well-validated tools and a combination of self-report and clinician-rated measures provided a broad understanding of apathy. The study also examined the relationship between apathy and specific depressive scale items, further enriching our understanding of this relationship. Moreover, it involved a large MDD sample in a real-world context and it compared MDD patients without neurodegenerative disorders. Another relevant limitation in our research is the clinical stability of the included patients, treated with antidepressant drugs potentially able to determine apathy. Importantly, clinical “stability” does not necessarily correspond to full symptomatic remission, and residual symptoms may still influence group differences. Additionally, although antidepressant therapy was included as a covariate, the heterogeneity in medication type and dosage, as well as the use of low-dose antipsychotics - which could not be adequately modeled due to variability in prescription patterns - may have influenced apathy-related outcomes. This limitation should be overcome by future studies with a prospective design, including drug-naive patients ideally at the first depressive episode, and/or accounting for ADs class effects and treatment variables (e.g., low-dose antipsychotics). Finally, future research should incorporate structural and functional neuroimaging to better understand the underlying mechanisms of apathy in MDD.

Conclusion

In conclusion, apathy is often reported among patients with MDD and associated with greater psychopathological severity, cognitive impairment and lower QoL. The identification of “inability to feel” and “loss of interest” as strong predictors plays a central role for emotional attenuation or affective anhedonia (“liking”) and behavioural/motivational anhedonia (“wanting”) in apathy, suggesting its distinct construct from depression. Because both apathy and depression negatively impact patients’ quality of life, it is critical to regularly assess apathy to improve clinical outcomes in individuals with MDD. Taken together, these findings suggest that apathy might represent a core bridging dimension between anhedonia and cognitive symptoms, positioning it as a potential nuclear construct linking emotional and executive dysfunctions. Importantly, whether apathy reflects a state-dependent phenomenon - fluctuating with mood episodes - or a more stable trait-like characteristic remains to be clarified. Longitudinal and neurobiological studies are needed to clarify the temporal dynamics and mechanistic underpinnings of apathy in MDD, which will inform targeted interventions and improve patient outcomes.
