Cicada rib-inspired tough films through nanoconfined crystallization for use in acoustic transducers
Authors: Jiajun Mao, Jia Yan, Ziyu Wang, Teng Ke, Jingsong Peng, Mingzhu Li, Qunfeng Cheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Acoustic transducers require films that demonstrate both toughness and fatigue resistance, presenting notable challenges when achieved through conventional nanoscale reinforcing strategies. Here, we found that the rib structure of a cicada’s tymbal exhibits exceptional toughness and fatigue resistance, attributed to its unique architecture composed of alternating soft and stiff polymer layers. Inspired by this rib structure, we developed a robust artificial rib film (ARF) using a nanoconfined crystallization strategy that involves the deposition of soft polyethylene oxide and stiff phenol formaldehyde. The ARF demonstrates a toughness amplification factor twice that of the cicada’s tymbal rib and exhibits an exceptionally long fatigue life. The nanoconfined crystallization restricts molecular motion and disperses external forces within the crystalline structure, thereby enhancing its mechanical properties. These improvements enable the ARF to outperform commercial polymer films as an acoustic transducer, achieving 2.7 times increase in frequency response and 2.2 times increase in displacement amplitude.

INTRODUCTION

Films used in acoustic transducers are characterized by their mechanical robustness and minimal thickness, which are crucial for achieving highly sensitive frequency responses and vibrational amplitudes. Common materials for these films include polymers, metals, and ceramics (1–5). Among these, polymers such as polyethylene terephthalate (PET), polyetherimide, poly(ether-ether-ketone) (PEEK), and polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) are primarily used in commercial transducers due to their cost effectiveness and ease of processing (2, 3). While metals and ceramics excel over polymers in terms of mechanical properties, such as higher Young’s modulus, and can provide superior frequency responses (4, 5), they often struggle to deliver high strength, toughness, and fatigue resistance concurrently. This limitation restricts acoustic transducers’ capacity to achieve highly sensitive frequency responses and prolonged service lives.

Recent advancements have led to the development of nanoscale reinforcing strategies, including the integration of carbon nanotubes (CNTs) (6), graphene (7–9), MXene (10, 11), and nanowood (12), aimed at enhancing the acoustic performance of these films. These innovations have improved the mechanical properties of films in acoustic transducers, resulting in better frequency responses. However, challenges such as the inhomogeneous dispersion of nanomaterials and weak interfacial interactions between components have hindered the full potential of these nanoscale reinforcing strategies (13). Consequently, creating high-performance acoustic transducers continues to pose a notable challenge.

Nature showcases various vocal systems, with multiple organisms producing sounds through distinct physiological structures (14). By understanding nature’s sound production mechanisms, we could notably enhance the performance of acoustic transducers. Cicadas, for instance, are well known for their loud and stable sounds, which occur at frequencies exceeding 50 times per second and can be sustained for extended periods (15). While research on sound production mechanisms often highlights the role of muscles (16), the high-quality sound produced by cicadas is primarily attributed to the remarkable toughness and fatigue resistance of the ribs in their tymbals (17, 18). Therefore, to overcome the limitations of traditional films used in acoustic transducers, particularly their inability to produce stable sound, it is vital to uncover the structure-property relationships of these ribs.

In this study, we reveal that the rib in the tymbal of cicadas demonstrates exceptional toughness and fatigue resistance due to its unique structure composed of alternating soft and stiff polymer layers. The Young’s modulus of the stiff layer is 1.6 times higher than that of the soft layer. Moreover, the nanoconfined crystallization of chitin within the ribs enhances mechanical properties by restricting molecular motion within the crystalline regions (19, 20). Inspired by this natural design, we have developed a robust and tough artificial rib film (ARF) using a nanoconfinement strategy, achieved through the layer-by-layer deposition of soft polyethylene oxide (PEO) and stiff phenol formaldehyde (PF). The Young’s modulus of PF is 5.5 times higher than that of PEO, and the proximity of PF layers induces nanoconfined crystallization in the PEO layers. The ARF exhibits a high toughness amplification factor (TAF) and an extended fatigue life, both attributed to the variation in Young’s modulus between layers and the nanoconfined crystallization of PEO. Furthermore, the ARF demonstrates superior frequency response and displacement amplitude, outperforming commercial films currently used in acoustic transducers.

RESULTS

The structure and mechanical performance of the cicada’s rib

Cicadas (Fig. 1A) are known for producing high-quality sounds throughout the summer. This sound production is enabled by the coordinated action of muscles and ribs within their tymbals (15). The displacement for tymbal of natural cicada is 11.5 ± 2.1 nm/V at its fundamental resonant frequency of 10,872.5 ± 312.6 Hz (fig. S1). When the tymbal muscles contract and pull on the ribs, the ribs flex inward, creating resonant sounds amplified by large air sacs in the chest and abdomen. The tymbal (Fig. 1B) is located at the junction of the abdomen and thorax. It consists of alternating long and short ribs connected by an elastic membrane rich in resilin (fig. S2). A detailed cross-sectional view of the rib shows a periodic lamellar structure (Fig. 1C), comprising thick layers of chitin and thin layers of resilin (Fig. 1D). Likewise, the resilin-rich membrane (RRM) also exhibits a layered structure (fig. S3, A and B). Unlike structural materials such as nacre (21), which is composed of discontinuous aragonite flakes, the rib and RRM have a continuous layered architecture. The thickness of the rib layers ranges from 150 to 450 nm, with an average thickness of about 260 nm, which is less than the average thickness of RRM layers (~319 nm; fig. S3, C and D). In addition, the layer thickness in the rib is significantly less than that of nacre layers, which average around 500 nm.

Thermogravimetric and elemental analyses indicate that the tymbal consists entirely of organic materials, with no inorganic components (fig. S4). While several all-organic tissues, such as the beak of the Humboldt squid (22), the veins of dragonfly wings (23), and the basitarsal compound slit sensilla of scorpions (24), have been recognized for their exceptional mechanical properties, the relationships among their composition, structure, and mechanical performance remain insufficiently explored. Fluorescence spectroscopy was used to further investigate the composition of the rib. Red fluorescence from chitin, stained with Congo red under 561-nm laser irradiation (fig. S5A), and weak blue autofluorescence from resilin under 405-nm excitation (fig. S5B) were observed (25, 26). The relative content of chitin, resilin, and water in the rib and RRM was quantified using the alkaline peroxidation method (fig. S5, C and D) (27). The ribs contain 62.6 wt % chitin, 9.1 wt % resilin, and 28.3 wt % water, whereas the RRM has significantly less chitin (19.4 wt %) but higher amounts of resilin (42.7 wt %) and water (37.9 wt %) compared to the ribs. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) further reveals a layered structure in the ribs and RRM (Fig. 2, A and B, and fig. S6). In localized regions of the HRTEM images, the well-ordered components in both the ribs and RRM are identified as α-chitin (Fig. 2, C and D, and fig. S6) (28).

To analyze the distribution of resilin within the rib, we compared the morphology of the rib, papain-treated rib (P-rib), and sodium hydroxide (NaOH)–treated rib (S-rib) (29, 30). The cross-sectional morphology of the untreated rib reveals a distinct layered structure (fig. S7A). In contrast, the P-rib exhibits numerous voids between the chitin layers (fig. S7B), indicating that resilin acts as a binding mortar connecting the chitin layers. A similar observation was made in the S-rib, where the resilin between the chitin layers was largely dissolved (fig. S7C). Atomic force microscopy (AFM) was used to further confirm the alternating chitin and resilin layered structure of the tymbal. As shown in Fig. 2 (E and F), the layer stacking pattern displays gaps resulting from the differences in Young’s modulus between the ribs and the RRM. The Young’s moduli of the chitin layer and the resilin layer in the rib were measured to be 5.41 ± 0.58 and 3.38 ± 0.67 GPa, respectively (Fig. 2G). Notably, the Young’s modulus of the chitin layer in the rib is lower than that in the RRM (6.12 ± 0.87 GPa), while the Young’s modulus of the resilin layer in the rib is slightly higher than that in the RRM (3.25 ± 0.63 GPa). The Young’s modulus of the chitin layer is 1.6 times higher than that of the resilin layer in the rib, which is lower than the modulus ratio (1.9) observed in the RRM. Such a difference in modulus between the two components is a common feature in natural structural materials, including aragonitic CaCO3 and organic materials in nacre (21), cellulose and lignin in ginkgo seed shells (31), and hydroxyapatite and collagen in bones (32). This alternating layered structure, characterized by notable modulus differences, is highly advantageous for enhancing toughness and fatigue resistance.

The tensile stress-strain curves of the rib and the RRM are shown in Fig. 2H. The ultimate tensile strength of the rib in the tymbal is 31.7 ± 4.3 MPa, which is 2.2 times higher than that of the RRM (14.5 ± 2.3 MPa). In contrast, the toughness of the RRM (6.5 ± 1.1 MJ/m3) is 3.3 times greater than that of the rib (2.1 ± 0.1 MJ/m3). The TAF is an index to evaluate toughening efficiency. The TAF is defined as the ratio of the fracture toughness of the rib to that of its primary chitin component. According to reference data (33, 34), the TAF of the rib reaches 1.8. This enhancement is attributed to the nanoconfined crystallization of chitin, which restricts molecular motion and disperses external forces within the crystalline structure, thereby improving its mechanical properties (28). Due to the Young’s modulus of the stiff layer being several times higher than that of the soft layer, the stiff layer undergoes compression while the soft layer experiences tension under stress (35). This stress distribution allows for greater deformation and enhances elastic energy storage in the stiff layer before fracture. Both the rib and RRM exhibit a fracture morphology characterized by “zigzag” step-type cracks and layer pullout (figs. S8 and S9), consistent with the described toughening mechanism (36). In addition, interfacial interactions within the rib, driven by covalent and hydrogen bonds between resilin and chitin, play a substantial role in energy dissipation (37). The fatigue resistance of the RRM is greater than that of the rib (Fig. 2I), which can be attributed to the higher resilin content in the RRM, enabling greater elastic energy storage.

Fabrication of the ARF

Inspired by the cicada’s rib structure, which features alternating stiff and soft layers, we demonstrated a robust and durable ARF through layer-by-layer deposition of soft PEO and stiff PF. In a typical preparation process (Fig. 1E), a PEO solution is initially deposited to create a hydrophilic layer that gradually smooths the surface. This is followed by the application of prepolymer PF onto the PEO layer. We sequentially form a layered PF-PEO (LPP) film by repeating these steps. The LPP is then immersed in a boric acid solution to promote cross-linking. The partially cured PF and PEO have hydroxyl groups on their surfaces that form coordination bonds with boric acid (fig. S10, A and B) (38). A new peak corresponding to B-O-C at 1120 cm−1 emerges in the Fourier transform infrared spectrum of the ARF (fig. S10C), confirming the formation of B-O-C linkages between the PF and PEO layers. Furthermore, the hydroxyl ratio in the ARF decreases compared to that in the LPP, indicating the involvement of hydroxyl groups in bonding with boric acid (fig. S10, D and E). The boron ester bonds between the PF and PEO layers mimic the interactions of chitin-binding proteins found in insects (37). After cross-linking, the ARF becomes transparent (Fig. 1F) and showcases an alternating layered microstructure (Fig. 1G). The layer thicknesses of PF and PEO in the ARF are ~500 and 25 nm (fig. S11), respectively, closely aligning with the thicknesses of the chitin and resilin layers in the cicada’s rib. Furthermore, Young’s modulus of the PF layer is 3.21 ± 0.46 GPa, which is 5.5 times higher than that of the PEO layer (0.58 ± 0.08 GPa) (fig. S12). This difference in modulus is significantly greater than the 1.5-fold difference observed in the rib. We can create a series of high-performance organic films by engineering films with a modulus difference of 1.6 times or more between layers. This microscale morphology, consistent with the ribs, is beneficial for achieving the high toughness and fatigue resistance mechanisms observed in the ARF.

Mechanical properties of the ARF

To elucidate the role of each structural element and interfacial interaction in the bioinspired design, we prepared pure PF films, pure PEO films, PF-PEO blend films (a mixture of PF and PEO in a ratio similar to the ARF but lacking a layered structure), and LPP films without interfacial interactions. The ARF demonstrates the highest tensile strength at 158.6 ± 7.9 MPa, which is 1.3, 7.7, 2.4, and 1.2 times higher than pure PF, pure PEO, PF-PEO blend, and LPP films (Fig. 3A). In addition, the ARF exhibits a superior toughness of 9.3 ± 1.2 MJ/m3, which is 3.6, 5.5, and 1.8 times higher than pure PF, PF-PEO blend, and LPP films. Moreover, the ARF exhibits higher fatigue resistance than the LPP and PF-PEO blend films (Fig. 3B). The ARF fractured at 45,303 cycles under 60% of maximum tensile strength, outperforming LPP (27,865 cycles) and the PF-PEO blend (4846 cycles). This can be attributed to the energy dissipation facilitated by the soft PEO layers and enhanced interlayer interactions. Pure PF films display a flat fracture surface, while PEO films exhibit rougher surfaces (fig. S13). Furthermore, crack deflection is observed at the fracture surface of the LPP. Compared to single-component films, the LPP benefits from the stiff PF layer bearing the primary load, while the soft PEO layer serves as a ductile shear-banding layer to dissipate energy. In addition, the fracture surface of the ARF shows layer pullout and curled fracture edges (Fig. 3, C and D, and fig. S14), indicating stronger interfacial interactions compared to the LPP, which enhances stress transfer efficiency between layers. These results underscore that the alternating stiff-soft layered structure and the interfacial interactions between the PF and PEO layers are critical for the biomimetic design. To further illustrate the effect of interfacial interactions on energy dissipation, we prepared two types of films (fig. S15A): one by spin-coating a PF layer onto a flat PEO layer (PF-PEO film) and the other by cross-linking borax onto the PF-PEO film (PF-PEO-borax film). We found that the energy required to peel the PEO layer from the borax–cross-linked PF-PEO-borax film is 1.7 times higher than that for the PF-PEO film, highlighting the notable enhancement in interfacial interactions achieved through borax cross-linking (fig. S15B, C).

In addition to interfacial interactions, the concentration of the polymers plays a critical role in determining the performance of the ARF. We optimized the ARF’s performance by systematically adjusting the concentration of PEO. Four types of ARF were prepared with PEO concentrations of 6, 8, 10, and 12 mg/ml, designated as ARF-6, ARF-8, ARF-10, and ARF-12, respectively (fig. S16). As the PEO concentration increases, the thickness of the PEO layer in the ARF also increases. ARF-8, with a PEO concentration of 8 mg/ml, demonstrates the highest tensile strength and toughness (fig. S17). When the PEO concentration is too low, the soft layer is not sufficiently thick to effectively store energy from shear deformation. Conversely, when the PEO concentration is too high, damage tends to localize within the thicker, soft layer rather than transferring to the adjacent stiff layer.

Three types of ARF were similarly prepared with PF concentrations of 100, 200, and 400 mg/ml, designated as ARF-100, ARF-200, and ARF-400, respectively (fig. S18). As the PF concentration increases, the thickness of the PF layer in the ARF also increases. ARF-200 exhibits the highest tensile strength and toughness at a PF concentration of 200 mg/ml (fig. S19). Neither lower nor higher PF content enhances the stress-transfer efficiency between the PF and PEO layers. At a fixed PEO concentration of 8 mg/ml and a PF concentration of 200 mg/ml, four types of ARF were fabricated with varying borax content. The ARF with a borax concentration of 10 mg/ml demonstrates the highest mechanical properties (fig. S20). Ultimately, the optimal ARF attained a tensile strength of 158.6 MPa and toughness of 9.3 MJ/m3, using PEO, PF, and borax concentrations of 8, 200, and 10 mg/ml, respectively.

Fracture mechanism of the ARF

The proposed fracture mechanism of the ARF can be summarized in three stages (Fig. 3E). Initially, when an external load is applied to the ARF, stress concentration and irreversible deformation occur in the rigid PF layer. Subsequently, the PEO layer, which acts as a ductile shear-banding layer, mitigates crack propagation under sustained external loading, contributing to energy dissipation. Last, as the PEO layer elongates, the hydrogen and covalent bonds between the layers break, further dissipating energy. The ARF exhibits zigzag-shaped cracks, and the fracture edges are curled (Fig. 3, C and D, and fig. S14), indicating high-stress transfer efficiency between the PEO and PF layers.

To further validate these mechanisms, we used finite element analysis (FEA). Based on the experimental results, the model was simplified to a regular continuous-phase layered structure. A two-material interface model and the maximum principal stress failure criterion from the extended finite element method were used to simulate crack propagation. During the initial stretching phase, the stiff PF layer was the first to experience failure, leading to the sliding of internal polymer chains. The FEA results reveal that the stiff layer primarily bears the load, while microcracks propagate along the soft PEO layer (Fig. 3F). A deep red color at the interface indicates that the propagation of the crack tip is delayed when it encounters the interface. The strong interface between the stiff and soft phases also effectively promotes load transfer. As the covalent bonding between the PF and PEO layers begins to fracture, the crack experiences gradient deflection. Further stretching completely disrupts the covalent bonding networks, resulting in notable energy dissipation. The tensile stress-strain curve obtained from the nanoscale model structure closely aligns with the experimental curve of the ARF, and the experimental tensile strength, Young’s modulus, and failure strain are 158.6 MPa, 3.2 GPa, and 9.8%, respectively, which are consistent with the simulated values of 159.2 MPa, 2.8 GPa, and 10.1% (fig. S21).

We also prepared another bioinspired phenol formaldehyde-borax-polyvinyl alcohol (PF-B-PVA) film, using polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) as the soft layer, to investigate the role of modulus variation in enhancing toughness. Boron ester bonds were introduced between the PF and PVA layers (fig. S22). Samples with varying PVA concentrations display a layered structure similar to that of the ARF (fig. S23). The modulus of PVA is 3.7 ± 0.4 MPa, which is closer to that of PF. The PF-B-PVA film achieves a tensile strength of 167.4 ± 8.1 MPa, comparable to that of the ARF (fig. S24). However, the toughness of the PF-B-PVA film is 5.3 ± 0.5 MJ/m3, lower than that of the ARF (fig. S24A). This difference arises because the Young’s modulus of PF is 5.5 times higher than that of PEO in the ARF, whereas the Young’s modulus of PF is similar to that of PVA in the PF-B-PVA film. Unlike the ductile behavior of PEO in the ARF, the PVA layer behaves as a brittle layer, resulting in a flat fracture surface for the PF-B-PVA film (fig. S24, B to E). Simulation results (fig. S24F) further confirm that the crack in the PVA layer of the PF-B-PVA film does not delay but propagates linearly along the initial crack direction.

Nanoconfined crystallization of the ARF

We found that, in addition to the contribution of modulus differences to the mechanical properties of the ribs, the crystallization of nanoconfined chitin also plays a critical role. Polymeric materials under nanoconfinement, influenced by size effects, exhibit substantially different thermodynamic and mechanical properties compared to their bulk counterparts (39–41). In the bioinspired design of the ribs, we used a nanoconfinement strategy using layer-by-layer assembly methods. This nanoconfined crystallization has often been overlooked in previous layer-by-layer assembly approaches due to a lack of focus on size effects (42). PEO layers can display various morphologies, such as two-dimensional (2D) spherulites, stacked in-plane lamellae, or single crystals, when their thickness is reduced to ~1 μm, 100 nm, and 20 nm, respectively (43). By carefully controlling the concentration of PEO, rotation speed, and rotation time, we can precisely regulate the thickness of the PEO layer. This approach prepared ARF with PEO layers measuring 800, 150, and 25 nm in thickness (fig. S25), designated as ARF-I, ARF-II, and ARF-III, respectively (process conditions are listed in table S1).

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) indicates that the spacing between PF layers notably influences the mobility of PEO chains. Figure 4A presents the initial melting thermograms of PEO, PF, ARF-I, ARF-II, and ARF-III films. The PEO film exhibits a sharp melting endotherm with a peak melting temperature of 73°C, while the PF film shows a broad melting endotherm with a peak at 49°C. As the thickness of the PEO layer decreases, the melting peak shifts to a lower temperature, suggesting that the reduced spacing between PF layers constrains the thermal motion of the polymer chains. In the absence of confinement, PEO films typically form chain-folded lamellae and organize into spherulites, with no distinct peak observed in 1D small-angle x-ray scattering slices (fig. S26). However, as the thickness of the PEO layer is reduced from 800 to 150 nm and eventually to 25 nm, the ARF transitions from 2D spherulites to stacked lamellae and ultimately to a single-crystal structure (Fig. 4B and fig. S26).

As the PEO thickness decreases from 800 to 150 nm and further to 25 nm, the crystallinity increases from 2.11 to 7.36 and 8.21%, respectively. This enhanced crystallinity results in a corresponding rise in Young’s modulus, reaching 320, 510, and 580 MPa for the respective thicknesses (Fig. 4C and fig. S27). The mechanical properties of the ARF films also change with PEO layer thickness. ARF-III exhibits the highest tensile strength with a PEO layer thickness of 25 nm. In contrast, ARF-II, with a 150-nm thick PEO layer, displays the greatest toughness (Fig. 4D). The mobility of PEO is restricted in ARF-III, enhancing the entanglement interaction at the interface, which results in increased tensile strength. Furthermore, the ARF-III exhibits a 20% higher strength compared to LPP-III and demonstrates a 43% improvement over ARF-I (fig. S28). These results indicate that nanoconfinement-induced crystallization contributes more significantly to mechanical enhancement than interfacial interactions. This nanoconfinement strategy parallels the nanoconfined crystallization of chitin in ribs, where molecular motion is reduced, and external forces are dispersed within the confined structure, thereby improving the film’s mechanical properties.

In nanoconfined crystallization, the annealing temperature is critical (40). The in situ crystallization process illustrated in fig. S29 demonstrates the effect of temperature on the nanoconfinement of ARF. At low temperatures, molecular motion is restricted, making it challenging for polymer chains to overcome the high energy barriers associated with crystallization (44, 45). As the temperature increases, enhanced molecular motion facilitates the breakdown of defective structures and reduces their size, creating favorable conditions for forming ordered structures. However, as the temperature rises, excessive thermal motion can disrupt the assembly structure within the film, leading to disorder (45). The Herman’s orientation function is calculated using the azimuthal intensity distribution of PEO (120). As the temperature increases, a transition is observed in the PEO regions from a disordered state to an in-plane flake orientation (Fig. 4E and fig. S30). PEO’s temperature-dependent distances and diffraction peak intensities further suggest that an optimal temperature promotes polymer stacking and enhances crystallinity. During the layer-by-layer assembly process of ARF, the best crystallization characteristics are achieved at an annealing temperature of 80°C.

Acoustic properties of the ARF-based acoustic transducer

The ARF demonstrates substantial potential for application in acoustic transducers, as its high mechanical performance and low density enhance frequency response and displacement amplitude. The fundamental resonant frequency of the ARF was measured and compared to that of commercial films used in miniature acoustic transducers (Fig. 5A and fig. S31). The ARF-prepared acoustic transducer exhibits a fundamental resonant frequency of 1341 Hz, which is 2.93, 2.37, 1.54, 1.29, and 1.23 times higher than that of commercial diaphragms made of PET, PEEK, aluminum (Al)/magnesium (Mg) alloy, graphene/PET, and CNT/PET, respectively (Fig. 1H and table S2). Generally, Young’s modulus and the thickness of the film determine the quality of sound output in transducers, while fatigue resistance ensures long-term stable operation. The ARF achieves superior mechanical performance and reduced thickness compared to commercial films (fig. S32) through nanoconfinement and a notable modulus difference between layers. These characteristics enable efficient sound output and long-term stability in sound propagation through the ARF. As shown in fig. S33, the ARF exhibits a 3-dB bandwidth of ~200 Hz (below 1 kHz) and a sensitivity of 2.6 mV/Pa at its fundamental frequency. This indicated that ARF diaphragms are suitable for strictly narrowband applications. The PVDF and lead zirconium titanate (PZT) diaphragms are widely used in microelectromechanical system and ultrasonic devices (46–51). The ARF has been compared with state-of-the-art PVDF or PZT diaphragms as listed in table S3. The results demonstrate that the ARF’s bandwidth is comparable to PVDF but narrower than PZT (which exceeds 1 kHz), and its sensitivity surpasses some of PVDF while remaining below Mg-PVDF and PZT. In addition, compared to MXene/PVDF, the ARF exhibits comparable bandwidth but lower sensitivity (11). When benchmarked against nanowood diaphragms, its bandwidth is comparatively reduced (12).

The performance of transducers is notably influenced by mechanical properties, with the thickness of the PEO layer playing a crucial role in determining the mechanical properties of the ARF. The acoustic performance of ARF with varying PEO layer thicknesses was systematically evaluated (fig. S34). As shown in Fig. 5 (B and E), the ARF films with PEO layer thicknesses of 25, 110, 450, and 900 nm exhibit fundamental resonant frequencies of 1341 ± 38, 953 ± 24, 392 ± 21, and 213 ± 12 Hz, respectively. The corresponding displacement values are 175.3 ± 16.8, 122.6 ± 12.3, 65.4 ± 6.7, and 88.3 ± 5.4 nm/V. As the PEO thickness decreases, the ARF demonstrates a substantial increase in crystallinity and improved mechanical properties (Young’s modulus and tensile strength), resulting in both a higher fundamental resonance frequency and greater displacement amplitude. The trend in displacement and frequency observed in finite element simulations of the thin film vibrations align well with the experimental data (Fig. 5D). As the thickness of the PEO layer decreases, the confined crystallization of the interlayer restricts the movement of polymer chains, thereby enhancing the film’s mechanical properties and resulting in higher response frequencies. The statistical data on thickness variation and mechanical/acoustic spread (table S4) reveal no notable differences of film-to-film variability. Moreover, the mechanical robustness of ARF does not rely on the testing frequency (fig. S35).

The audible sound produced by the ARF-based transducer was recorded with a mobile phone (Fig. 5C) and analyzed using digital audio editing software (Adobe Audition). The ARF-based transducer is capable of reproducing high-quality music, such as Hotel California. The sound wave recorded from the ARF closely matches the original sound waveform, demonstrating ARF’s potential as a diaphragm material for acoustic transducers. This exceptional performance is due to the ARF’s simultaneous achievement of high strength, toughness, and fatigue resistance at a low thickness. Furthermore, scalable manufacturing of ARF could be achieved by combining a roll-to-roll assisted blade coating process with a continuous cross-linking for commercial transducers (52, 53).

DISCUSSION

In summary, we found that the ribs in the cicada’s tymbal exhibit remarkable toughness and fatigue resistance due to the notable modulus difference between layers and the presence of crystalline chitin. Young’s modulus of the stiff layer is 1.6 times higher than that of the soft layer, enabling the stiff layer to support the load while the soft layer inhibits crack propagation. Inspired by this natural design, we successfully replicated this structure in a polymer film using soft PEO and stiff PF, with the Young’s modulus of PF being 5.5 times higher than that of PEO. In addition, we used a nanoconfinement strategy to achieve single-crystal formation in the PEO layer. The toughness and fatigue life of the ARF are greatly enhanced through energy dissipation mechanisms driven by interlayer interactions and the nanoconfined crystallization of the PEO layer. This nanoconfinement effect ultimately leads to enhanced performance of ARF as an acoustic transducer. The ARF exhibits a frequency response and displacement amplitude up to 2.7 and 2.2 times higher than those of commercial polymer films. Our successful replication of all-organic lamellar composites presents a promising pathway for the design of advanced acoustic transducers.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sample preparation

Fresh tymbal specimens were obtained from commercially sourced adult cicadas in China. The specimens were cryopreserved at −20°C in a temperature-controlled freezer to maintain ultrastructural integrity. Before dissection, the cryopreserved samples were allowed to equilibrate to ambient temperature until no solid ice crystals remained within their bodies. Subsequently, the tymbal was carefully dissected from the cicada’s thorax, and the muscle-connected structure attached to the tympanum was clipped with tweezers. The tymbal was then rinsed with deionized water to remove interstitial contaminants. The edges of the tymbal were trimmed using microsurgical scissors and soaked in saline for experimental analysis. The rib structures and RRM were also dissected using a microscalpel on a sterilized glass plate.

Alkaline peroxidation

Specimens for compositional analysis were treated with an alkaline solution (54). First, the tymbal samples were weighed, freeze-dried, and then reweighed to determine their water content. Next, the dried specimens were immersed overnight at 70°C in a solution consisting of 92.5% (v/v) deionized water, 5% (v/v) 30% hydrogen peroxide, and 2.5% (v/v) 10 M NaOH. This peroxidation treatment was repeated until complete depigmentation was achieved. Posttreatment residuals were collected through vacuum filtration, washed with deionized water, and dried to a constant mass for gravimetric analysis.

Bioinspired preparation of LPP films and ARF

Square glass substrates (2.5 cm by 2.5 cm) were sequentially cleaned using ultrasonic treatment in ethanol, acetone, isopropanol, and deionized water, with each cleaning cycle lasting 10 min. A homogeneous PEO solution (chloroform, 8 mg/ml) was prepared by stirring overnight to fabricate a typical film. The PEO solution was then deposited onto the center of the glass slide and spun at 3000 rpm for 60 s to create a uniform PEO layer. After ambient drying, a PF prepolymer solution (150 mg/ml in ethanol) was spun onto the substrate at 3000 rpm for 60 s. Thermal curing at 60°C for 10 min facilitated the formation of the PF layer. LPP was achieved through iterative deposition cycles. The ARF was prepared by immersing LPP in a boric acid solution (10 mg/ml) for 1 hour, followed by drying in an oven at 60°C for 2 hours. Last, the ARF was annealed at 80°C to promote the crystallization of PEO.

Preparation of PF, PEO, PVA, and PF-B-PVA film

PF, PEO, and PVA films were fabricated using an iterative spin-coating method (3000 rpm, 60 s per cycle, acceleration: 500 rpm/s) on glass substrates with their respective solutions: PF prepolymer (150 mg/ml in ethanol), PEO (8 mg/ml in chloroform), and PVA (50 mg/ml in deionized water). Each deposition cycle involved dispensing ~100 μl of solution onto precleaned glass substrates, conducting a total of 10 cycles under controlled ambient conditions.

For PF-B-PVA composite films, a PVA-borax complex solution was initially spun onto a precleaned glass substrate at 3000 rpm for 60 s. After the ambient drying of the PVA-borax layer, a prepolymer layer of PF was deposited at the same speed and duration. The coated substrate was subsequently dried at 60°C for 10 min, during which the curing process of PF occurred. The PF-B-PVA film was produced through iterative deposition cycles.
