Reigniting engagement: A multidisciplinary, authentic case‐based approach to anatomy and physiology education for medical imaging students
Authors: Nicolene Jooste, Asma Shaikh‐Kader
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnatomy and physiology are foundational to health science education, yet student engagement in these modules often declines during later years of study. This discursive article aims to describe a teaching intervention which increased student engagement and provide educator reflection of implementation and improvement over time. The teaching intervention, implemented as a semester‐long, multidisciplinary case‐based portfolio, designed to re‐engage second‐year students with anatomy and physiology content. Centered on a simulated patient case, the intervention scaffolded a series of tasks—ranging from reflective journaling to image interpretation and evidence‐based decision‐making—that progressively increased in complexity. The design aimed to foster cognitive integration, critical thinking, and professional identity development by situating anatomy and physiology knowledge within authentic clinical reasoning scenarios. The intervention drew on constructivist, authentic, and situated learning theories and was iteratively refined over three academic years. Reflective teaching practice, peer assessment, and collaborative task structures supported metacognitive growth, while diverse modalities addressed variability in student learning preferences. Key outcomes included increased student engagement, stronger multidisciplinary connections, and enhanced evaluative judgment as perceived and reflected on by the educators. However, challenges related to academic integrity, group dynamics, and educator workload highlighted the need for improved scaffolding and institutional support. Future directions include collaboration with clinical specialists to enhance authenticity, and partnerships with academic literacy and library teams to strengthen support for applied reasoning tasks. This intervention offers a replicable model for integrating clinical relevance into undergraduate anatomy and physiology education, supporting deeper learning and professional readiness within foundational disciplines.

INTRODUCTION

Anatomy and physiology remain cornerstone modules in the health sciences, providing critical knowledge that informs clinical reasoning and patient care. Despite their foundational importance, undergraduate engagement in these modules often decreases in the second year of study in our experience, especially within curricula that balance academic learning with work‐integrated learning (WIL) components. This disengagement is particularly puzzling considering that students enrolled in health sciences programs are expected to consistently apply anatomical and physiological knowledge in their future clinical roles. As such, the pedagogical challenge lies not merely in content delivery, but in cultivating relevance, motivation, and professional identity among students navigating both theoretical and applied domains.

In response to this challenge, contemporary pedagogical frameworks advocate for the integration of authentic learning and multidisciplinary education, both of which are essential in preparing students for the complexity and uncertainty of real‐world healthcare environments. 1 , 2 Authentic learning engages students in meaningful, context‐rich tasks that mirror professional practice and encourage the development of 21st‐century competencies such as critical thinking, collaboration, and digital literacy. 3 , 4 , 5 In alignment with constructivist and situated learning theories, these practices foster deep engagement by allowing students to actively construct knowledge through socially mediated and experiential encounters. 6 , 7 , 8

This emphasis on social construction of knowledge is further reinforced by collaborative learning literature. Effective collaboration in higher education is most successful when students colabor on open‐ended, complex tasks that demand shared ownership, promote interdependence, and require peer regulation and mutual support. 9 , 10 The design of the teaching activity described in this article drew on these principles to evoke deeper learning and student agency by embedding meaningful clinical relevance and collaborative knowledge construction.

In contrast, Otto et al. 11 highlight that students differ in their preferred learning strategies. Some rely heavily on rehearsal and other solitary approaches that may not align with constructivist or collaborative methods. Others demonstrate a collaborative learning profile, showing a preference for deep learning and peer engagement. Recognizing this diversity in learning profiles and the contextual interruptions caused by WIL alternation, the strategy adopted in this module was mindful of the need to scaffold student engagement—starting from psychologically safe, low‐stakes reflective activities and building toward high‐stakes collaborative and applied assessment. In this way, the activity supported vertical integration of skills across the semester while promoting horizontal integration across health science disciplines.

The hidden curriculum, comprising the tacit acquisition of professional norms and values, served as a powerful undercurrent to this approach. 12 , 13 Intentional inclusion of reflective and collaborative practices sought to surface these hidden elements, supporting the development of professional identity, communication skills, and metacognitive awareness. Reflection was central to the design, both as a pedagogical strategy and as a developmental goal. Drawing from Schön's framework, 14 students engaged in both reflection‐on‐action (e.g., after peer or self‐assessments) and reflection‐in‐action (e.g., during image‐based interpretation and discussion), reinforcing clinical reasoning through iterative knowledge construction.

The intervention aimed to develop students' ability to reason, not merely recall. Silldorff and Robinson 15 describes this as a shift from “content‐focused” to “logic‐focused” instruction, where critical thinking, defined as the integration of mechanistic understanding and application, is essential for predictive reasoning in novel clinical scenarios. By incorporating tasks that required students to connect anatomy and physiology content with real‐world outcomes (e.g., diagnosis, treatment, patient education), the intervention aimed to foster adaptive logic and minimize reliance on rote memorization. This was especially vital in helping students interpret physiological responses, visualize anatomical structures, and assess treatment risks, as highlighted in the S1.

The anatomy and physiology module within the Department of Human Anatomy and Physiology provides a unique context for such innovation. Students from four distinct health science disciplines participate in shared foundational content, taught by subject specialists who may not share the same professional endpoint as their students. This disciplinary disconnect, while challenging, also presents an opportunity: to reposition anatomy and physiology as a multidisciplinary scaffold—an integrative space where clinical reasoning, collaborative learning, and academic identity can coalesce.

Recognizing that foundational knowledge must be integrated both vertically (across academic years) and horizontally (across disciplines and professional roles), the teaching strategy was explicitly designed to address the often‐observed separation between academic content and clinical reasoning. 16 The structured, evolving case portfolio encouraged students to make connections between anatomy, physiology, clinical presentation, and patient care. Thereby supporting the development of cognitive integration necessary for diagnostic accuracy and lifelong clinical competence.

The strategy described in this article was thus developed not simply as an assessment enhancement, but as a pedagogical intervention aimed at re‐engaging second‐year students with anatomy and physiology through a sustained, authentic, and multidisciplinary case‐based learning activity. The approach explicitly addressed the observed decline in motivation and aimed to bridge the academic‐clinical divide by anchoring learning activities in simulated clinical reasoning, reflective practice, and collaborative inquiry. Additionally, the approach aimed to highlight the importance of applied anatomy and physiology during patient education, diagnosis, and treatment.

By integrating authentic assessment with elements of the hidden curriculum, reflective practice, collaborative engagement, and multidisciplinary learning, this intervention responds directly to the evolving expectations of health science education. It aligns with global calls for curricula that not only impart knowledge but also develop the adaptive expertise, ethical sensibility, and collaborative skillsets demanded in modern healthcare settings. 17 , 18 What follows is a detailed account of the context, rationale, and structure of this intervention, as well as the pedagogical insights gained by the educators through its iterative implementation over three academic years (2021–2023).

DESCRIPTION

Educational context and rationale

The teaching and assessment strategy described in this article was developed within the Department of Human Anatomy and Physiology. The module in which the intervention was implemented provides foundational anatomy and physiology content to undergraduate students enrolled in four professional programs within the Department of Medical Imaging and Radiation Sciences: Diagnostic Radiography, Diagnostic Ultrasound, Nuclear Medicine Technology, and Radiation Therapy.

The multidisciplinary nature of this arrangement underscores one of the central educational challenges—and opportunities—of anatomy and physiology teaching. While the educators themselves are subject specialists in anatomy and physiology, the students are ultimately being trained for clinical professions that rely on the integration of anatomical and physiological knowledge within applied contexts. This inherent disciplinary separation between educators and students necessitates a deliberate effort to bridge the gap between foundational knowledge and clinical application. It also means that anatomy and physiology, by its very nature, are well‐suited to serve as a multidisciplinary connector, offering opportunities to tailor learning experiences that are both profession‐specific and broadly relevant to the health sciences.

Adding to this complexity is the structure of the academic year. Students spend alternating 2‐week blocks in either clinical training sites or on‐campus learning as part of a WIL model. This alternating schedule creates logistical barriers to continuity in teaching, collaboration, and student engagement. Students are often in “clinical mode” during WIL blocks, making it difficult for them to consistently engage with academic content. Thus, any intervention aiming to increase learning engagement must be sufficiently compelling, time‐conscious, and aligned with clinical relevance to maintain student attention.

The intervention was conceived not merely as an academic enhancement but as a response to a growing disengagement among second‐year students. Despite the consensus among educators and clinicians on the importance of anatomy and physiology to clinical practice, 16 , 19 students often demonstrated low motivation and minimal engagement. The strategy that emerged from this context aimed to remedy this by anchoring learning in authentic, multidisciplinary, and collaborative activities that mirrored real‐world clinical thinking and teamwork.

Intervention overview: Multidisciplinary case‐based learning activity

The instructional intervention took the form of a continuous learning activity, structured around a simulated patient case that unfolded over the course of the semester. This approach was chosen to simultaneously address multiple pedagogical goals: deep content engagement, development of 21st‐century skills (critical thinking, collaboration, academic and digital literacy), and improved multidisciplinary relevance.

The case centered on Mr. John Smith, a retired construction worker, who first presented with a suspicious lesion on the ear. As the semester progressed, the case evolved to reflect a likely progression of squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), encompassing anatomical and physiological systems in a logical clinical sequence: integumentary, lymphatic and immune, male reproductive, and urinary systems. Each system formed the basis for one task in the portfolio. These tasks varied in format—ranging from reflective writing and group analysis to image interpretation and evidence‐based multiple‐choice questions (MCQs)—and were scaffolded to increase in complexity and stakes.

From the outset, the intervention aimed to promote authentic learning by placing students in a position where they had to think and act like professionals. The tasks were designed to reflect actual clinical reasoning processes, often under conditions of partial information, and required students to apply anatomical and physiological knowledge in ways that mimicked real‐world scenarios. Importantly, these tasks were not evaluated solely on factual correctness but also on the quality of reasoning, the integration of knowledge, and the ability to communicate findings clearly and professionally.

As students progressed through each task, they built a cumulative understanding of the patient's condition, requiring them to recall, reinterpret, and extend their anatomical and physiological knowledge across different contexts. This method reinforced both vertical and horizontal integration of content and skillsets, mirroring the multidisciplinary collaboration expected in clinical environments.

Task 1 required students to engage with reflective questions relating anatomical and physiological structures to clinical observations. Initial student hesitancy, as observed by educators, led to the addition of participatory‐only grading, creating a psychologically safe environment to formulate and share reasoning. Anonymizing class engagement via Mentimeter increased inclusivity, especially during the remote‐learning phase of the pandemic.

Following WIL, students returned to an updated case for Task 2: Mr. Smith developed swollen nodules suggestive of metastasis. Working in self‐selected groups, students responded to targeted questions on metastasis mechanisms, immune response, and lymphatic drainage.

Students completed a two‐page group report, followed by a memorandum‐led class discussion and self‐assessment (Table 1). This allowed reinforcement of good answering techniques and aligned expectations for higher‐stakes assessments.

For Task 3, groups received anonymized pelvic radiographs and used knowledge of male pelvic anatomy to identify abnormalities. The emphasis was not on accurate pathology identification but on forming plausible, anatomy‐informed deductions. Peer evaluation using a structured rubric encouraged accountability and developed evaluative judgment (Table 1).

This was the most multidisciplinary of all tasks, bridging imaging skills with anatomical reasoning—a particularly authentic activity for the students' professional paths.

The final case scenario (Task 4) introduced chemotherapy for Mr. Smith's metastatic SCC. Students were provided two journal articles detailing nephrotoxic risks of treatment and were tested via MCQs. Questions required students to synthesize anatomy and physiology knowledge with evidence from literature and evaluate patient‐specific considerations (e.g., alternative therapies and herbal supplements).

This task bridged academic reading skills, evidence‐based practice, and content integration, preparing students for clinical reasoning and research literacy (Table 1).

DISCUSSION

Reflection and refinements over time

The multidisciplinary case‐based portfolio was designed as a living pedagogical strategy—one that could evolve through iterative cycles of reflection, adaptation, and refinement. Across its 3‐year implementation (2021–2023), the intervention was continuously shaped by educator observations, logistical constraints, and the realities of student performance. These changes highlight the importance of reflective teaching practice and the complexity of innovating within large, multidisciplinary cohorts.

Educators observed increased student discomfort with applying knowledge to open‐ended clinical scenarios, rather than recalling information during Task 1. To reduce observed student anxiety and increase their engagement, the reflective tasks were adjusted to reward participation rather than correctness. This significantly improved student willingness to share ideas and created a safer environment for exploratory thinking, particularly when supported by anonymous platforms such as Mentimeter. These tools, first introduced during remote delivery phases necessitated by COVID‐19, were retained in subsequent in‐person iterations due to their demonstrated value in surfacing diverse perspectives and enabling more inclusive discussion.

As the intervention evolved, logistical refinements were also necessary. The group‐based submission in Task 2, while rich in student reasoning, proved difficult to manage at scale. Free‐text submissions required extensive time to mark, especially in classes exceeding 100 students. To address this, the activity was moved to the LMS with tighter word limits, automatic similarity checking via Turnitin, and class‐wide feedback sessions using structured memoranda. These adaptations helped maintain feedback quality while reducing the administrative burden on educators.

Peer and self‐assessment, first met with detected skepticism, gradually gained legitimacy as marking rubrics were refined and transparency increased. These assessment components helped cultivate evaluative judgment and professional accountability, although moderation remained essential to uphold consistency and mitigate over‐ or under‐assessment by peers. Task 3, involving image‐based oral presentations, exemplified the integration of multidisciplinary content with professional relevance. However, coordinating presentations and managing peer evaluations, particularly in hybrid learning contexts, added substantial complexity to the assessment logistics.

Over time, the strategic scaffolding of complexity and assessment stakes was revealed to be both a strength and a source of tension. While it was designed to build cognitive resilience and academic maturity, some students remained resistant to tasks that demanded active synthesis and reasoning. Particularly in Tasks 2 and 4, a proportion of students attempted to bypass these demands by replicating answers from previous years or relying on shared documents distributed informally. This type of academic dishonesty, where students sought to replicate expected outputs without internalizing the underlying reasoning, undermined the purpose of the intervention. It ran counter to the scaffolded design intended to foster deeper understanding and authentic engagement with clinical scenarios.

This behavior attested to a strategic circumvention of the learning process—an attempt by some students to prioritize grades over growth. This highlighted a misalignment between student motivations and the educational goals of the activity. The risk of such behavior is likely amplified in the current landscape, where generative artificial intelligence (AI) tools may further tempt students to outsource the very reasoning and integration the intervention aimed to develop.

Ultimately, while the case‐based portfolio demonstrated pedagogical merit and yielded many valuable teaching insights, it became increasingly difficult to sustain in its original form. The time burden on educators, both in the setup of complex case materials and the moderation of multilayered assessments, compounded the challenges of managing academic integrity. These factors, combined with the growing prevalence of strategic avoidance behaviors, led to the decision to discontinue the activity after its third implementation.

Nonetheless, the experience offered deep insights for educators into student learning processes, curricular pressure points, and the nuanced interplay between motivation, assessment design, and professional identity formation. It underscored the need for constant reflection, collaborative teaching support, and institutional resourcing when attempting ambitious, multidisciplinary innovations. Although this particular intervention may not continue, its foundational principles (i.e., authentic learning, multidisciplinary integration, and reasoning over recall) remain vital to the evolution of anatomy and physiology education.

Engagement and motivation

One of the most immediate outcomes of the intervention was a noticeable shift in student engagement, particularly in early‐stage tasks. The authenticity of the learning experience—anchored in a simulated clinical case that evolved across the semester—offered students a sense of purpose and relevance that traditional anatomy and physiology approaches often lack. The character of Mr. Smith functioned as a simulated patient, providing a narrative thread that scaffolded engagement while humanizing academic content. This is consistent with findings by Grijpma, 14 who argues that meaningful learning emerges when students see content as connected to real‐life professional practice.

The design capitalized on both behavioral and cognitive forms of engagement. 20 The effort associated with behavioral engagement was introduced in a scaffolded approach, with initial low‐stakes reflection (Task 1) easing students into the activity. Later tasks (Tasks 3 and 4) required students to apply complex reasoning and judgment, which increased behavioral engagement aspects and introduced cognitive engagement. Despite this, some students appeared to engage behaviorally (completing tasks) without fully committing to the cognitive demands, illustrating the well‐documented disconnect between engagement types. 20 , 21

Skill development

The multidisciplinary portfolio was not simply content‐focused; it explicitly developed key professional capabilities such as critical thinking, collaboration, and communication. Across tasks, students were required to interpret images (Task 3), synthesize literature (Task 4), and reason through pathophysiological scenarios. This aligns with Silldorff and Robinson, 15 who emphasize that fostering critical thinking in anatomy and physiology requires scaffolding students' ability to understand content and move beyond pure memorization.

Importantly, communication and peer evaluation were embedded in tasks through oral presentations and peer/self‐assessments. These elements supported metacognitive skill development and evaluative judgment, which are often underemphasized in traditional anatomy and physiology instruction. 10 Moreover, the scaffolding of task complexity mirrored the developmental arc of reflective practice, 22 progressing from understanding to reflection and, in some cases, toward critical reflection.

Multidisciplinary value

Given the interdisciplinary cohort of medical imaging students, the activity was designed to be both inclusive and professionally relevant across different specializations. While traditional anatomy and physiology education often follows a medical student centric model, this intervention allowed each profession to situate anatomical and physiological knowledge within their own clinical contexts. For example, the radiograph interpretation task (Task 3) was especially relevant to diagnostic radiography and ultrasound students, enhancing perceived relevance and motivation. 19

Otto et al. 11 note that students bring varied learning strategies to anatomy education—ranging from solitary rehearsal to collaborative problem‐solving. By designing tasks that drew on both individual (Task 4) and collaborative (Task 2–3) learning strategies, the intervention sought to accommodate and gradually extend students' preferred modes of engagement. This flexibility is critical in multidisciplinary settings with high diversity in learner backgrounds and learning profiles. 23 , 24

Hidden curriculum

The activity also created deliberate spaces for the development of professionalism, teamwork, and ethical reasoning—elements typically associated with the hidden curriculum. While these competencies are rarely assessed directly, they emerged organically through the design features of the tasks. For example, peer evaluation required students to balance honesty with diplomacy, while case‐based reasoning demanded ethical judgment regarding patient care options.

As Pestka 25 suggests, integrating reflective practice into broader curricula may strengthen such developmental outcomes. Though formal attitudinal data were not collected, educator reflections support that these unassessed, yet critical, competencies were tacitly addressed and, in many cases, surfaced for class discussion. However, it should be noted that the hidden curriculum operates subtly and is not always visible or accessible to every student in the same way. These lessons are often unspoken or implied; there is no certainty that all students will recognize or interpret them as intended. 26 This variability highlights the need for intentionality and awareness when incorporating the hidden curriculum into educational design.

Theoretical integration

The design of this intervention draws heavily from constructivist, authentic, and situated learning theories. It meets all five of Driscoll's 18 , 27 conditions for constructivist instruction: (1) complex and relevant learning environments (e.g., evolving case scenario), (2) social negotiation (e.g., group analysis and peer evaluation), (3) multiple perspectives (e.g., integration of imaging, literature, with anatomy and physiology knowledge), (4) ownership of learning (via open‐ended, student‐driven inquiry), and (5) self‐awareness of knowledge construction (e.g., self‐ and peer assessments).

The intervention also reflects Schön's notions of reflection‐in‐action and reflection‐on‐action as highlighted by Grijpma, 14 as students engaged in real‐time clinical reasoning and later revisited their decisions through class debriefings, feedback sessions and self‐assessment. Situated learning principles are similarly present, with students participating in authentic, profession‐relevant tasks that align closely with their future clinical environments. 6

Challenges and limitations

Despite its merits, the intervention was not without limitations. Foremost among these was the substantial time demand placed on educators. The complexity of the tasks, combined with the need for feedback, moderation, and rubric refinement, made the intervention difficult to sustain at scale. Academic dishonesty, through recycled responses and group file‐sharing, posed a threat to the validity of the assessment and undercut the pedagogical goals of reasoning and reflection. This aligns with Boud and Bearman's 17 caution that current assessment practices may fail to support the collaborative and relational nature of learning.

Further, unequal group participation was observed. While peer assessments mitigated some disparities, students with passive learning profiles or weaker communication skills often contributed less visibly, reflecting concerns raised by Xu et al. 20 and Scager et al. 10 Additionally, some students struggled with the nontypical format and preferred more traditional assessment forms—suggesting the need for better preparatory scaffolding in future iterations.

Lastly, the absence of student attitudinal data limited the ability to empirically evaluate student perceptions of the activity. As Vedi 28 and Pestka 25 argue, while task structure is crucial, student feedback provides essential insight into the effectiveness of collaborative learning environments. Future studies should incorporate both qualitative reflections and quantitative metrics to better evaluate learning outcomes and inform refinement.

CONCLUSION AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS

This intervention, designed specifically for the anatomy and physiology context within a medical imaging curriculum, offers valuable insights into how authentic, multidisciplinary case‐based learning can enhance student engagement, reasoning, and reflective capacity in anatomy and physiology education. While the activity was originally custom designed to address the unique needs of the cohort and curriculum, the decision to now share detailed supplementary materials marks a deliberate move toward open educational practice. By doing so, we aim to promote collaboration among educators and support the re‐use, adaptation, and evolution of effective teaching strategies across similar contexts.

Several directions for future refinement and research have emerged. First, the intervention highlighted the need for more explicit academic and assessment literacy support, particularly in helping students interpret and respond to applied anatomical and physiological questions with precision. Partnering with institutional learning development units, including academic literacy advisors, writing centers, and library professionals, may enhance students' ability to navigate source material, structure responses, and engage critically with scientific literature. This would be particularly valuable for tasks that involve evidence‐informed reasoning or engagement with peer‐reviewed clinical research.

Second, while the case‐based format introduced clinical reasoning in a simulated manner, collaboration with clinical educators and imaging specialists presents a key opportunity to strengthen the authenticity and discipline‐specific relevance of future iterations. Engaging professional colleagues in the design or feedback stages of such activities could further ensure that anatomical and physiological learning remains well aligned with the expectations and decision‐making processes of real‐world clinical practice.

Finally, the intervention would benefit from formal evaluation through qualitative and/or mixed‐methods research, particularly with respect to students' perceptions of engagement, perceived relevance, skill development, and preparedness for clinical reasoning. While educator reflections offered valuable insight into student responses and implementation challenges, gathering direct student feedback would provide a more robust understanding of the educational impact and inform future pedagogical adaptations.

In summary, this teaching intervention reinforces the importance of integrating authentic, clinically oriented learning into undergraduate anatomy and physiology education. Through open sharing of structured tasks and reflective design rationale, we hope to contribute meaningfully to the evolving practice of anatomy and physiology teaching—supporting a wider community of educators in cultivating deep, applied learning grounded in the anatomical and physiological sciences.
