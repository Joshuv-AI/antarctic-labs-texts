When leads are not an option: Optimizing subcutaneous implantable cardioverter-defibrillator and leadless pacemaker interaction
Authors: Lucía Canales Muñoz, Paula Sánchez-Somonte, Sergio Castrejon-Castrejon, Marcel Martínez-Cossiani, Omar Al Razzo, Jose Luis Merino
Published: 2025

Introduction

The subcutaneous implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (S-ICD) is an effective therapy for preventing sudden cardiac death in appropriately selected patients.1 As a fully extrathoracic and extravascular system, it avoids the risks associated with transvenous leads, such as fracture or endocarditis.2 However, inappropriate shocks—often owing to cardiac oversensing, particularly T-wave oversensing—remain a significant limitation.3

To mitigate this, the SMART Pass sensing algorithm uses a 9-Hz high-pass filter that improves the R:T wave ratio, reducing the incidence of inappropriate therapies.2, 3, 4 Although effective, SMART Pass can be automatically deactivated under certain conditions, such as extreme bradycardia or transient reductions in QRS amplitude.5 This case illustrates the clinical implications of such deactivation in a patient with an S-ICD and a leadless pacemaker and how tailored device programming resolved the issue.

Case report

A 54-year-old man with cardiac sarcoidosis developed dilated cardiomyopathy and nonsustained ventricular tachycardia. In 2012, he received a single-chamber implantable cardioverter-defibrillator. Seven years later, he presented with a device pocket infection, requiring complete system extraction. Reimplantation was planned, but venography revealed complete thrombosis of the superior vena cava (Figure 1). Given the lack of transvenous access, an S-ICD was selected after confirming appropriate sensing in all vectors during screening. The device was successfully implanted, including induction and successful termination of ventricular fibrillation. Sensing was deemed optimal in the primary vector (ring–case) (Figure 2). In August 2023, the patient experienced syncope without recorded arrhythmias or shocks. The electrocardiogram showed alternating bundle branch blocks, and an electrophysiological study confirmed infra-Hisian conduction disease. Owing to persistent superior vena cava occlusion, a leadless VDD pacemaker was implanted via the femoral vein, with a lower rate limit of 30 bpm. After implantation, the S-ICD appropriately detected paced QRS complexes in all sensing vectors, and the secondary vector (tip–case) was selected for final programming because it provided the most favorable R:T ratio (Figure 3).Figure 3Electrocardiogram with native rhythm (A) and paced rhythm from a leadless pacemaker (B) with proper sensing by a subcutaneous implantable cardioverter-defibrillator. In November 2024, the patient received an inappropriate S-ICD shock during moderate physical activity. Device interrogation revealed automatic SMART Pass deactivation owing to sinus bradycardia, which led to intermittent T-wave oversensing since a few months earlier (Figure 4). When the patient performed physical exercise, sinus tachycardia occurred with a physiological increase in T-wave voltage, leading to continuous oversensing, reaching the therapy zone, and causing inappropriate discharge (Figure 5). During admission, a stress test was performed with the therapies disabled and SMART Pass initially deactivated, which confirmed dynamic T-wave oversensing during sinus tachycardia. Based on the QRS morphology observed during exertion, a new sensing template was acquired. The pacemaker was reprogrammed with a higher base rate (from 30 to 50 bpm) and deactivation of the atrioventricular conduction mode switch, aiming to prevent further SMART Pass deactivation. Remote monitoring during follow-up confirmed that the algorithm remained active, with stable sensing and no recurrence of inappropriate therapies.

Discussion

Combining an S-ICD with a leadless pacemaker is a promising solution for patients lacking transvenous access, but it poses technical challenges. Studies support appropriate S-ICD detection of paced rhythms and pacemaker function after shock.6 Still, oversensing of pacing artifacts and undersensing of ventricular fibrillation during continuous pacing are concerns.

The SMART Pass algorithm effectively reduces inappropriate shocks by filtering low-frequency components, especially T waves.4 However, its autodeactivation—triggered by low QRS amplitude and prolonged R-R intervals—may expose patients to oversensing.5,7 In this case, bradycardia deactivated SMART Pass, allowing dynamic T-wave oversensing during exertion and resulting in an inappropriate shock. Increasing the pacing rate and deactivating bradycardia-promoting algorithms restored algorithm functionality and sensing stability.

Dynamic T-wave oversensing owing to increased voltage during tachycardia is a known phenomenon, particularly in physically active individuals.8 Stress testing can unmask this risk and guide personalized device programming. The SMART Pass has demonstrated utility in mitigating this issue, supporting its role in patients with high levels of physical activity.4

Conclusion

Although the combination of an S-ICD and a leadless pacemaker is not ideal, it remains a viable option for selected patients with no venous access. This case underscores the importance of individualized programming—particularly maintaining active SMART Pass filtering—to prevent inappropriate shocks. Programming strategies should include optimizing base pacing rates and minimizing bradycardia-promoting algorithms. Proactive follow-up and stress testing are essential to ensure safety and long-term efficacy in this complex device pairing.
