Mapping white matter microstructure at term age to motor outcomes at 2 years in very preterm infants: a multicentre cohort study
Authors: Ankita Joshi, Wei Jia, Junqi Wang, Hailong Li, Mekibib Altaye, Nehal Parikh, Lili He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo characterise white matter microstructural differences within very preterm (VPT; <33 weeks’ gestational age) infants that develop motor impairment in the first 2 years of life.DesignCohort study, VPT infants (Cincinnati Infant Neurodevelopment Early Prediction Study) recruited from five level III/IV neonatal intensive care units in the greater Cincinnati area between September 2016 and November 2019.SettingMulticentre study; participants received MRI at term-equivalent age at Cincinnati Children’s Hospital Medical Center and were followed-up at 2 years corrected age to assess their motor performance. Patients Infants born before 33 weeks were eligible.Main outcomes and measuresComposite motor scores at 2 years corrected age (CA) on the Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development, III. Fractional anisotropy (FA) along the white matter tracts was used to measure white matter microstructure. Motor impairment was defined as Bayley-III motor score <85.Results247 controls and 84 impaired infants were included in the study. Compared with the controls, infants with motor impairment were characterised by location-dependent credible group differences and significantly lower FA in both sensorimotor and non-sensorimotor tracts. A location-specific significant positive association between FA and Bayley scores in the impaired group was observed in multiple sensorimotor tracts and non-sensorimotor tracts. No significant associations were found between FA and Bayley scores in the controls.ConclusionVPT infants developing motor impairments show altered inter and intrahemispheric connectivity, with early indications of impaired visual-motor, sensorimotor and thalamo-cortical connectivity, extending beyond sensorimotor tracts.

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Very preterm infants (<33 weeks’ gestation) have a high risk of motor impairment in the first 2 years of life. Diffusion MRI links white matter microstructure to early motor outcomes, with frequent signals in the corpus callosum and corticospinal tracts. Findings across studies are mixed, driven by differing methods, small samples and region selections. Whole-tract averages can mask location-specific effects; along-tract variability is important. The role of non-sensorimotor pathways in early motor outcomes remains underexplored.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

In this cohort study of 331 very preterm (VPT) infants, widespread, location-dependent reductions in fractional anisotropy were observed across multiple sensorimotor and non-sensorimotor white matter tracts. Among those with motor impairment, location-dependent fractional anisotropy was significantly associated with the Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development Motor composite scores.

Introduction

Preterm birth affects over 10% of pregnancies globally,1 with very preterm (VPT) infants (<33 weeks’ gestation) at a higher risk for cerebral palsy and motor impairments, impacting about 40% of these children.2 The mechanisms linking VPT birth to motor impairment are unclear, complicating primary prevention strategies.3 Currently, early intervention is the only effective secondary prevention.4

Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), an MRI technique, allows non-invasive exploration of the brain’s white matter (WM), revealing characteristics of axonal directionality and associated WM membrane permeability.5 Prior studies have related DTI metrics to motor outcomes in preterm cohorts.6–10 While these studies highlight the importance of key sensorimotor tracts, such as the corpus callosum (CC) and corticospinal tract (CST),11 the specific tracts or methods analysed often differ, contributing to variability in findings.7 11–13 For example, one study12 specifies regions of the CC, such as the rostral body, anterior and posterior midbody and isthmus in relation to the motor outcome, while another study14 generalises the role of the CC without detailing subregions. Findings regarding CST vary, with some studies showing bilateral associations with motor outcomes,13 and others7 only on the right. These inconsistencies could be due to the limited statistical power of small cohort studies or may arise because existing studies often examine only averaged diffusion parameters, such as fractional anisotropy (FA) across entire tracts15 or in some cases across the whole brain,16 producing a single mean value and variance per tract or per subject. However, diffusion parameters vary considerably along individual WM tracts, and neurobiological evidence emphasises the importance of analysing the within-tract variability.17 Furthermore, while sensorimotor tracts are well-studied, the role of non-sensorimotor tracts in motor development remains underexplored, despite their potential to indirectly influence motor function through cognitive, perceptual and behavioural processes.18

Therefore, a comprehensive analysis of both sensorimotor and non-sensorimotor tracts combined with along-tract methods could provide a detailed understanding of WM alterations and their associations with motor outcomes. This study leverages the largest US population-based cohort of 392 VPT infants with MRI data and motor outcomes from the Cincinnati Infant Neurodevelopment Early Prediction Study.19 Without prespecification of the tract of interest, we conduct a data-driven analysis of 50 plausible WM tracts as defined by a recently developed comprehensive approach20 comprising both sensorimotor and non-sensorimotor regions. For the first time to our knowledge, rather than averaging diffusion parameters across an entire tract, we use along-tract analysis to evaluate these metrics at multiple segments along the tracts, providing a more detailed view of the tract’s microstructural properties. Our objective was to use DTI analysis techniques to specifically investigate WM microstructural alterations related to motor development in VPT infants. Our primary aim was to identify between-group WM microstructural differences along tracts at term equivalent age (TEA) that distinguish VPT infants with and without motor impairment at 2 years. Our secondary aim was to assess the within-group association between location-specific WM microstructure and motor performance scores.

Methods

Research design, setting and participants

We conducted a prospective, multicentre cohort study of 392 infants born VPT recruited from five level III/IV neonatal intensive care units (NICU) in the greater Cincinnati area between September 2016 and November 2019. Other hospitals approved the study based on an established reliance agreement. Infants were enrolled in the study during their NICU stay between the ages of 32 and 38 weeks postmenstrual age (PMA) following written informed consent by a parent or guardian. Infants were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) known chromosomal or congenital anomalies affecting the central nervous system; (2) cyanotic heart disease; or (3) hospitalisation and mechanical ventilation with greater than 50% supplemental oxygen at 45 weeks’ PMA.

Clinical assessment

Motor outcomes were assessed using the Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development–Third Edition (BSID-III, Bayley scores).21 Assessments were scheduled for BSID-III testing between 22 and 26 months corrected age. For this study, we used a categorical approach, defining infants with Bayley scores <85 (−1 SD) as impaired and those with scores ≥85 as controls.

Neuroimaging protocol and analysis

MRI of the brain was obtained between 39 and 45 weeks PMA during natural sleep using a single 3T Philips Ingenia scanner and a 32-channel head coil located at Cincinnati Children’s Hospital. Technical details on MRI sequence parameters and the preprocessing are provided (online supplemental method 1).

White matter tract segmentation and tractometry

For every subject, 50 WM tracts were dissected using TractSeg,20 22 a deep-learning-based automated WM tract segmentation tool (figure 1). A complete list of all 50 tracts included in this study and their acronyms is given (online supplemental table 1). After segmentation, we performed along-tract tractometry, wherein each tract was divided into 100 equidistant segments and FA was averaged per segment, yielding 100 FA values per tract per subject (online supplemental method 2).

Statistical analysis

We conducted χ2 tests to examine group differences in sex, gestational age (GA), birth weight and PMA. Significance was set at two-tailed p<0.05.

We modelled FA as a smooth function of segment position using Bayesian function-on-scalar regression (FOSR) (online supplemental method 3). For each tract, the response was the FA profile (100 segments) per infant per tract; predictors were group (impaired vs control), PMA at scan, GA and sex. We report the posterior mean and 95% credible interval for the group-difference function βgroup(t) = FAimpaired − FAcontrol (negative values indicate lower FA in the impaired group). Credible segments are those where the entire 95% credible interval lay outside a region of practical equivalence of ±0.02 FA. A secondary, exploratory check, averaging FA values in all credible segments is reported in online supplemental method 4.

Within each group (impaired, control), we computed partial Pearson correlations between segment-wise FA (segments 1–100 along each tract) and the Bayley scores, adjusting for GA, PMA and sex. Multiplicity along a tract was controlled with a permutation-based family-wise error (FWE) procedure (1000 permutations) that estimates a tract-specific significance threshold for the minimum p value across the 100 correlated segments. A segment was declared significant only if its p value was below the tract’s FWE threshold. We report only tracts with at least one FWE-surviving segment.

The FOSR model was fitted using the refund package in R. All other analysis was done using Python V.3.11.

Results

Cohort characteristics

Of the original cohort of 392 infants, 61 infants were excluded (54 infants had missing Bayley scores (86% follow-up rate), the remaining 7 subjects failed the preprocessing pipeline). 331 VPT infants with accurate tractometry data were involved in the following analysis. Baseline characteristics were similar for sex and PMA at scan, whereas the impaired group had lower GA and birth weight than controls (table 1).

Discussion

This large cohort study investigated WM microstructural alterations related to motor development in a cohort of 331 VPT infants. Three main findings emerged. First, we identified the tracts that showed credible differences in FA between the impaired and control groups. Second, significant location-dependent FA reductions were consistently observed along all the identified tracts in the impaired group (table 2, figure 2). This means that the differences in FA are not evenly distributed but occur in specific, localised segments of the tracts, indicating that certain regions within the WM pathways are more affected than others. Third, within the impaired group, we identified the locations along the WM tracts where FA at TEA significantly correlated with the Bayley scores at 2 years (figure 3).

Impaired interhemispheric and intrahemispheric communication

The CC is a major interhemispheric commissure, important for communication of sensory, motor and high-order information. Previous studies have shown reduced FA to be significantly associated with motor outcomes of VPT infants in specific regions of CC such as the splenium or the posterior midbody regions.23–25 Other studies26 27 generalise the role of the CC without detailing individual subregions. In older cohorts, various research groups have reported reduced FA in the posterior segment of the CC for 11-year-old to 12-year-old children28 29 and 12-year-old to 17-year-old adolescents30 born preterm. These findings suggest that the alterations seen at term may persist into childhood and adulthood. Our findings align with these previous studies, indicating significantly lower FA found within all regions of the CC in the impaired group. We also found significant positive correlations between FA and motor outcomes in the anterior midbody, posterior midbody, isthmus and splenium regions of the CC. The posterior end of CC (splenium) is part of the commissural fibre network that connects the right and left hemispheres and is also associated with motor function. Given that the myelination of the CC develops from the posterior end to the anterior end,31 impairment to intercortical connectivity suggests that the posterior end of the CC is the most vulnerable for motor impairment due to VPT birth.

The SLF is part of the long association fibre network that connects the posterior temporal lobe to the motor association cortex in the frontal lobe. Previous research has not specifically examined the individual components of SLF (I, II, III), but has reported lower FA in the entire SLF as being highly associated with the Bayley scores at 2 years6 and 3 years.10 However, two other studies also studying FA values with motor outcomes, with a follow-up before 24 months, did not identify SLF as significant.13 26 In the current study, we found significantly lower FA values in bilateral SLF-I, SLF-II and the left SLF-III tracts. We also found a significant correlation of the FA in the left SLF-I and SLF-III tracts with motor outcomes at 2 years.

The cingulum is perceived as the primary interconnecting apparatus in the medial aspect of the cerebral hemisphere. The cingulum connects the cingulate cortex to the parahippocampal gyrus and is involved in motor control, particularly related to cognitive aspects of motor function such as motivation and attention to movement.32 Our findings of reduced FA in the cingulum are consistent with previous studies.6 14

These findings altogether align with previous research27 suggesting that both interhemispheric and intrahemispheric communication are impaired.

Impaired connectivity to the pons and bilateral CST involvement

The FPT tract is part of the corticopontine-cerebellar pathways, carrying motor information from the frontal cortex to the pons (a structure in the brainstem involved in motor control and sensory analysis), and eventually to the cerebellum for motor coordination, travelling down through the internal capsule, specifically in the anterior half of the posterior limb. Our study is the first to examine FA along the FPT tract bilaterally, identifying specific areas that significantly correlate with the Bayley scores in the impaired group (figure 3).

The parieto-occipital pontine (POPT) tract connects the parietal and occipital lobes to the pons. The parietal lobe’s role in sensorimotor integration and spatial awareness can impact motor function and coordination, especially in body orientation and movements in space. This specific tract is rarely studied in relation to motor neurodevelopment outcomes in preterm infants; however, studies have linked early-life adverse respiratory exposure specifically to the parietal lobe dysmaturation and neurodevelopmental phenotype of motor delay at follow-up.33 Severe WM injuries in the parieto-occipital regions, which include the POPT, are associated with a higher incidence of cerebral palsy.34 Our results present novel findings in the study of the POPT tract, revealing specific regions of the left POPT tract that are significantly associated with the Bayley scores at 2 years. Because the POPT originates in parietal/occipital regions (visuospatial processing) and projects to pontine–cerebellar circuits, alterations in POPT microstructure could compromise cerebellar contributions to motor coordination, affecting early motor performance. Although visuomotor integration deficits are typically linked to corticospinal tract injury, our findings indicate that POPT abnormalities may also play a role, consistent with reports that non-sensorimotor pathways influence early motor function.

CST is the primary motor pathway responsible for voluntary motor control. Our findings are consistent with previous studies that report significant associations between the FA of the posterior limb of the internal capsule (PLIC, a CST region of interest) and Bayley outcomes.25 Our findings emphasise the importance of assessing both the left and right CST when studying motor outcomes, as WM integrity on both sides may significantly influence motor skills (table 2). These results contrast with studies11 35 which reported no significant differences in FA between normal and adverse Bayley scores. They also differ from a study7 which found significant differences only in the right PLIC. These discrepancies might be a result of different analysis approaches or our larger cohort size.

Impaired connectivity in the cerebellar peduncles

The tracts running through the cerebellar peduncles, which project to and from the spinal cord, pons, cerebral cortex and cerebellum, are crucial for refining motor movements, learning new motor skills and converting proprioceptive information into balance and posture. A previous functional MRI study indicated less developed cerebellar networks in the VPT group, and another study36 showed FA of the middle cerebellar peduncle (MCP) to be significantly correlated with Bayley scores at 18 months. Our investigation extends these findings by demonstrating significant correlation of FA in the middle regions of the MCP (between the pons and the cerebellum) and in bilateral ICP with Bayley scores at 2 years (figure 3).

Impaired motor-sensory integration

The superior thalamic radiation (STR) tract relays motor information from the thalamus to motor and sensory regions in the parietal lobe, particularly the primary motor and somatosensory cortices. Impairment on both left and right of the STR (table 2) suggests disruption in motor-sensory integration, potentially leading to challenges in coordinating sensory input with motor responses across both hemispheres. Previous studies have shown significant differences in diffusion metrics in STR among VPT infants with and without cerebral palsy (CP).9 Our findings indicate the involvement of both left and right STR, highlighting the bilateral role of this tract in basic motor control and sensory integration. While previous studies correlated diffusion metrics with severe motor outcomes like early CP diagnosis, our results indicate that microstructural changes in the STR can also detect milder motor impairments, as reflected by Bayley scores at 2 years. This finding is unique and suggests that microstructural changes in STR observed at term may have the potential to detect milder motor impairments that, while less severe than CP, may nevertheless benefit from early intervention.

Early evidence of impaired visual-motor integration

Previous studies have identified the association between lower FA of the optic radiation (OR) tract and poorer motor outcomes, but typically at a later age. A study found that lower FA in the OR was linked to poorer copying scores in the Beery-Buktenica Developmental Test of Visual-Motor Integration-V among preterm infants with very low birth weight examined at 18–22 years of age.37 Another study also associated FA in the OR tract with motor outcome at 11 years of age.38 Our study suggests this association could already be observed at term. We found a significant positive correlation between FA in both the left and right OR to the Bayley scores (figure 3). Our results could thereby reflect the visual dimension of the performed motor tasks, which is likely, since the visual information mediated via the OR tract is also significantly involved in fine motor functioning.39

Early evidence of impaired thalamic-cortical connectivity

Thalamo-premotor (T_PREM), thalamo-parietal (T_PAR) and thalamo-occipital (T_OCC) are tracts connecting the thalamus with various cortical regions, namely, the premotor, parietal and occipital areas, that carry sensory and motor information to and from the cortex. A previous study by Thompson et al40 reported that poor connectivity in the thalamic tracts shows motor impairments in VPT children at 7 years of age. Our current study shows significant correlations with FA within the impaired group along T_OCC (bilateral) and T_PAR (right) (figure 3). Significantly reduced FA is also observed in T_PREM (bilateral) (table 2). The current study shows that the relationship between thalamic-cortical connectivity and motor impairment demonstrated by others in infants born prematurely is already evident at TEA.

Strengths and limitations

This study leverages a large, multicentre, regional cohort of 392 VPT infants and evaluates 50 WM tracts including both the major sensorimotor and non-sensorimotor tracts. The major strength of the study is a targeted analysis within tracts, revealing more detailed information about the underlying associations. The current study analyses a large number of tracts; however, it still fails to capture the full complexity of the WM network. For example, the current tractography pipeline is unable to reconstruct the fornix, which has been associated with motor development in preterm infants in previous studies.6 14 Next, the analyses focused only on FA, as this is the most important and most used metric in literature. Additional diffusion metrics, such as median, apparent and radial diffusivity, may provide complementary insight. Additionally, this study used DTI, which does not account for crossing fibres as effectively as higher order diffusion MRI models. Furthermore, the lack of control for cognitive and language outcomes limits the ability to determine the true independent association between WM microstructure and motor impairment. Lastly, tracts were segmented using TractSeg and therefore may have some erroneous segmentations, and as such, any conclusions about the underlying structures should be taken with caution.

Conclusion

This multicentre cohort study used along-tract analysis for the first time in VPT infants to compare FA in VPT infants with and without motor impairment according to the Bayley scores at 2 years of age. Our results suggest that infants in the impaired group show location-dependent FA reductions along both sensorimotor and non-sensorimotor WM tracts, altered interhemispheric and intrahemispheric connectivity along with early indications of impaired visual-motor, sensorimotor and thalamo-cortical connectivity.
