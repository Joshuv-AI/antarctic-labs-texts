Lamin A/C protects chromatin accessibility during mechanical loading in human skeletal muscle
Authors: Saline Jabre, Emeline Cherchame, Natalia Pinzón, Eline Lemerle, Marc Bitoun, Catherine Coirault
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSkeletal muscle nuclei (myonuclei) are subjected to high mechanical stress which plays a critical role in muscle tissue integrity and plasticity. Here we investigated the role of lamin A/C in dampening the effects of acute mechanical stretch on chromatin states and its downstream effects on gene expression.MethodsWe studied control and lamin A/C-deficient human myotubes both at baseline and following a mechanical stress mimicking acute muscle exercise. Chromatin accessibility and transcriptional responses were assessed using ATAC-seq (assay for transposase-accessible chromatin with sequencing) and RNA-seq, respectively.ResultsWe found that stretch-induced nuclear deformations in lamin A/C-deficient myotubes but not in controls, and was associated with a widespread increase in chromatin accessibility, mainly affecting promoter regions. Concordantly, mechanical stress also increased the levels of H3K4me3 euchromatin marks and decreased heterochromatin-associated H3K27me3 in A-type lamin-deficient myotubes. Additionally, mechanical stress led to the downregulation of transcriptional pathways involved in histone deacetylation, DNA methylation, and muscle differentiation, while pathways related to cytokine activity, extracellular matrix organization, and cell adhesion were upregulated.ConclusionsOverall, lamin A/C deficiency amplifies the chromatin response to mechanical stress, leading to enhanced promoter accessibility and activation of stress DNA damage-related gene pathways. These findings underscore the role of lamin A/C in maintaining chromatin stability under mechanical strain.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-025-02437-z.Keywords: Nuclear mechanotransduction, Mechanical loading, Chromatin, Lamins, Muscle cells

Introduction

Mammalian muscle fibers contain hundreds to thousands of nuclei (myonuclei) that are subjected to high levels of repetitive mechanical stress during muscle contraction and relaxation. Both muscle plasticity and the preservation of genomic integrity in these low-turnover myonuclei are essential for adapting to mechanical demands and maintaining muscle function [1, 2]. A finely tuned regulatory system that governs myonuclear responses to various mechanical stresses is therefore essential for sustaining muscle fiber homeostasis and promoting hypertrophy. Depending on the fiber type, time scale, and force magnitude, healthy myofibers respond through nuclear deformation, activation of mechanosensitive signaling pathways such as YAP/TAZ, chromatin remodeling, and transcriptional reprogramming [2, 3].

The nuclear lamina, a structural meshwork underlying the inner nuclear envelope, serves as a key determinant of the nuclear mechano-response [4–8]. It is mainly composed of lamin proteins including B-type lamins (lamins B1 and B2 encoded by the LMNB1 and LMNB2 genes) and A-type lamins, (lamins A and C, encoded by the LMNA gene) [9] A- and B-type lamins display differential expression patterns across human tissues and perform distinct cellular functions [10, 11]. B-type lamins are constitutively expressed by cells during development and are essential for organogenesis [10]. Whereas, A-type lamins are enriched in differentiated cells [11, 12]. In mechanically active tissues such as skeletal muscle, A-type lamins are highly expressed, where they increase nuclear stiffness and protect the structural integrity of the cell [13]. Mutations in the LMNA gene are responsible for several muscle disorders, including Emery-Dreifuss muscular dystrophy (EDMD) and lamin-related congenital muscular dystrophy (LCMD), underscoring their critical role in skeletal muscle function [14]. Defects in A-type lamin in affected individuals are characterized by muscle weakness [8], altered nuclear morphology [15] and impaired skeletal muscle hypertrophy in response to overload [16].

Together with chromatin, A-type lamins are key determinants of nuclear stiffness [13, 17] and their loss severely compromises nuclear integrity under increased nuclear stress [18]. Moreover, chromatin tethering to the nuclear lamina through lamina-associated domains (LADs) is a major regulator of high-order chromatin organization and epigenomic states [14]. Chromatin bound to the nuclear lamina is generally enriched in heterochromatin marked by di- and tri-methylation of histone H3 at lysine 9 (H3K9me2/me3) [14], an important regulator of gene expression during myogenic differentiation [19]. Beyond the nuclear periphery, A-type lamins also exist in a soluble, highly dynamic complex throughout the nucleoplasm [20] where they interact with both hetero- and euchromatin [20, 21], thereby regulating gene expression in a context-dependent manner [21].

Emerging evidence indicates that external mechanical forces can shape epigenetic landscapes and chromatin accessibility in stem cells [5, 22], with profound implications for tissue morphogenesis. However, the role of lamins A/C in orchestrating these epigenetic responses to mechanical stimuli in differentiated muscle cells remains poorly understood. To address this gap, we investigated how lamin A/C deficiency influences chromatin organization and gene expression in muscle cells subjected to mechanical stress. Our findings reveal that the absence of A-type lamins leads to a global increase in chromatin accessibility following mechanical stretching. These results shed new light on the mechanosensitive role of lamins A/C in maintaining chromatin integrity in differentiated muscle cells.

Materials and methods

Human muscle cell culture

Immortalized human muscle precursors were obtained from the MyoLine cell culture platform at the Center for Research in Myology (https://recherche-myologie.fr/technologies/myoline/). Briefly, primary muscle precursor cells were isolated from a paravertebral biopsy of a healthy control subject (female, 12 year-old) and immortalized as previously described [23]. Cells were cultured in growth medium consisting of 1 part 199 Medium to 4 parts DMEM (Life Technologies, Saint Aubin, France) supplemented with 20% fetal calf serum (Life technologies), 5 ng/mL hEGF (Life Technologies), 0.5 ng/mL βFGF, 0.1 mg/mL dexamethasone (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), 50 μg/mL fetuin (Life Technologies), 5 μg/mL insulin (Life Technologies), and 50 μg/mL Gentamycin (Gibco™, Life Technologies). Upon reaching 90% confluence, myoblasts were switched to differentiation medium (DMEM with 50 μg/mL gentamycin) to induce myotube formation. Gentamycin was removed prior to siRNA transfection. Myotubes were collected 72 hours post-differentiation.

siRNA transfection

siRNA transfections were performed using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Life Technologies) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, at 24 and 48 hours after induction of differentiation using 50 nM siRNA. The siRNA sequences (Eurogentec, Belgium) are listed in Table 1. Target gene or protein expression was analyzed 72 hours after the first transfection (Fig. 1a). No cytotoxic effects were observed following siRNA treatment.

Mechanical stretch

Immortalized muscle precursor cells were seeded onto 6-well BioFlex plates (Flexcell International Corporation, USA) coated with Matrigel Growth Factor Reduced Basement Membrane Matrix (1/10 dilution with DMEM), differentiated into myotubes and eventually subjected to siRNA as described above (Fig. 1a). After 68 hours of differentiation myotubes were subjected to 4 hours of equibiaxial cyclic stretch (10%, 0.5 Hz). Cells were then either fixed for immunocytochemistry or collected for downstream analyses (total differentiation time: 72hours). The cyclic strain protocol was selected based on its established ability to mimic a moderate, physiologically relevant exercise regimen [24] and to trigger at least in control myotubes, mechanosignalling pathways, actin cytoskeleton rearrangement and reorganization and changes in gene expression [16, 25, 26] without inducing damage [26]. Replicate control samples were maintained under static conditions with no applied cyclic stretch.

Immunocytochemistry and image analysis

Myotubes were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 20 minutes at room temperature (RT), permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100, and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100. F-actin was labeled using Phalloidin-Alexa 568 (ThermoFischer Scientific, France). Primary antibodies used for immunostaining are listed in Table 2. Nuclei were stained with Hoechst (ThermoFischer Scientific, France) and Vectashield anti-fade solution (#H-1200; Vector Laboratories, USA) was used as the mounting medium.

Images were acquired using a Nikon Ti2 spinning disk confocal microscope with a Yokogawa CSU-W1 spinning disk head coupled to a Prime 95 sCMOS camera (Photometrics, Tucson, USA), operated via Metamorph software (Molecular Devices, UK). Super-resolution imaging was performed using the LiveSR module (Gataca Systems, Massy, France). Fluorescence intensity quantification was performed using Fiji software (version 1.41). To quantify the YAP/TAZ nucleocytoplasmic (N/C) ratio, the mean gray value was measured separately in the nucleus—defined by a Hoechst-based threshold—and in a selected region of the cytoplasm. The amount of cell fusion was determined from the fusion index and defined as the number of nuclei within myosin heavy chain (MyHC) positive myotubes divided by the total number of nuclei. Only MyHC positive myotubes with two or more nuclei were considered. Z-stacks of images were acquired for 3D images of nuclei and analyzed using IMARIS software (Oxford Instruments, Singapore).

To assess chromatin heterogeneity, the coefficient of variation (CV =σ/µ where σ represents the standard deviation of the Hoechst intensity values and µ represents the mean value of intensity of the nucleus) of Hoechst intensity was calculated across at Least 50 nuclei per condition. For each nucleus, a line transect was drawn across the nuclear diameter, and fluorescence intensity profiles were extracted using Fiji’s “Plot Profile” function [27].

SDS-PAGE and protein analysis

For total protein extraction, myotubes were lysed in 0.5M Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 1.5M NaCl, 2.5% deoxycholic acid, 10% NP-40 and 10 mM EDTA RIPA buffer with added protease inhibitors (25 μg/ml Aprotinin, 10 μg/ml Leupeptin, 1 mM 4-[2-aminoethyl]-benzene sulfonylfluoride hydrochloride, and 2 mM Na3VO4). Histone extraction from chromatin was performed with a specific histone extraction kit (Abcam, ab113476) according to manufacturer’s instructions. Samples were loaded into a 4-20% Mini-Protean TGX Stain-Free Gels (Bio-Rad, USA). Electrophoresis was conducted for 90 min at 100 Volts on a BioRad® system. Proteins were then transferred to nitrocellulose membranes, with the Trans-Blot Turbo semi-dry Transfer System from BioRad. After 1 hour of blocking with 5% milk or BSA in PBS (pH 7.4), membranes were incubated overnight with primary antibodies (Table 2) at 4°C. HRP-conjugated-secondary antibodies were applied for 1 hour at RT with gentle agitation in blocking buffer. Signal detection was performed via ECL (Immobilon Western chemiluminescent HRP substrate, Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA) using a CCD-based system (Vilber Lourmat, France) or a G-box system with GeneSnap software (Ozyme, Saint-Quentin, France). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ (1.53, https://imagej.nih.gov/ij).

RNA-seq and quantification of gene expression

mRNA was isolated using the RNeasy mini kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) with proteinase K, according to manufacturer’s instructions (n = 4 to 9 samples/group). First-strand complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from purified mRNA using M-MLV Reverse Transcriptase in the presence of RNaseOUT Recombinant Ribonuclease Inhibitor (Invitrogen). Quantitative PCR was performed with Takyon ROX SYBR MasterMix dTTP (Eurogentec) on a 7300 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). Gene-specific primers were synthesized by Eurogentec (Liège, Belgium).

Primers sequences (designed by Primer-BLAST,NCBI) and synthesized by Eurogentec are listed in Table 1. Relative expression levels of target genes were normalized to the housekeeping gene RPLP0.

RNA-seq libraries were generated and sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 platform (2x100bp, ~ 44 million paired-end reads/sample) at the Genotyping/Sequencing Platform (iGenSeq Paris Brain Institute). Bioinformatic analysis was conducted at the Data Analysis Core platform (DAC, Paris Brain Institute, Paris, France, RRID:SCR_026138). Raw reads were assessed with FastQC [28]. Poor-quality sequences were removed, and adapters were trimmed with fastp using default parameters [29]. Illumina DRAGEN bio-IT Platform (v3.8.4) was used for mapping the reads against the reference genome hg38, and for quantifying the reads based on GENCODE v.37. Library orientation, library composition and coverage along transcripts were evaluated with Picard tools. Following analysis, data visualizations were conducted with R shiny private software named Quby (https://dac.institutducerveau-icm.org). First, edgeR (v3.38.0) [30] was used to calculate normalized counts per million (CPM) values. Next, only genes with a CPM >1 in at least 2 samples were retained. Differential analysis was performed using the GLM framework likelihood ratio test from DESeq2 (v. 1.34) [31]. The resulting p values were adjusted using the Benjamini and Hochberg’s approach to control the False Discovery Rate (FDR). A threshold of absolute log2 fold change >1 and FDR < 0.05 was used to label genes as significantly differentially expressed (up or down). The functional enrichment analysis was conducted using gProfiler (version e111_eg58_p18_f463989d) with Benjamini-Hochberge FDR correction method and a significance threshold of 0.05 [32] (https://biit.cs.ut.ee/gprofiler/page/citing). An over-representation analysis (ORA) on differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was conducted. Pathways with adjusted p-value < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

Assay for transposase-accessible chromatin with sequencing (ATAC-seq)

The ATAC-seq experiment was performed using the ATAC-seq kit of Active Motif (Waterloo, Belgium) according to manufacturer’s instructions. Part of the pipeline was based on the ENCODE recommendation (https://github.com/ENCODE-DCC/atac-seq-pipeline), from reads trimming to peaks annotation. Quality of raw data was evaluated with FastQC [28]. Poor quality sequences (minimum Length: 50 bp) and adapters were trimmed or removed with trimmomatic tool [33], with default parameters, to retain only good quality paired reads. Paired-end reads were mapped to the human reference genome (build hg38) with Bowtie2 default parameters (maximum fragment Length 2000 bp) [34, 35]. Reads mapping to mitochondrial DNA were excluded from the analysis. Duplicates and reads in Encode blacklist regions were discarded with the Picard tools. Peaks were called using the MACS2 program [36] with the option call peak and call-summits options. Peaks annotations were obtained from the HOMER hg38 database [37]. Then, the summits were extended by ± 250 bp and merged by condition, using the mergePeaks.pl script in the Homer tool with a maximum distance to merge set at 250 bp. Peaks with at least two replicates (if available) were considered reproducible and were retained as consensus peaks (dx.doi.org/10.17504/protocols.io.36wgq326olk5/v1) [38]. In total 114,122 consensus peaks were present across all samples and conditions. Annotation of the consensus peaks was performed using Homer with default parameters. We generated a peak count matrix using FeatureCounts tool [39] based on the gtf file produced from the consensus peaks. The edgeR bioconductor package [30] (v3.28.0) was used to calculate normalized counts per million (CPM) counts (v3.38.0). Data was filtered on a CPM threshold > 1 on at least 3 of 4 samples per group. Differential accessible regions (DARs) analyses was performed using the GLM framework likelihood ratio test from DESeq2 (v. 1.34) [31]. The resulting p values were adjusted using the Benjamini and Hochberg’s approach to control the False Discovery Rate (FDR). DAR analysis was conducted with the criteria of log2 fold change > 0.5 and threshold FDR < 0.05. DAR annotations were performed with ChIPseeker [40] and assigned to nearest or overlapping gene, exon, intron, promoter, 5′ untranslated region (UTR), 3′ UTR, and other genomic features. The number of reads mapping to the reference genome, after deduplication and excluding mitochondrial chromosomes, ranged between 6 and 22 million reads across the samples. ATAC-seq quality metrics (FRiP, TSS enrichment, sub-nucleosomal score, library complexity) are provided in Suppl Fig. 1a-c and Suppl. Table 1. Furthermore, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) of consensus peaks from LMNA-deficient and Ctl groups are shown in Supplementary Fig. 1d. Distribution of DARs along chromosomes was analyzed with hg38 ideograms coordinates and ChIPseeker R package [40]. Karyoplots with DARs coverage were computed with karyoploteR package [41]. Functional enrichment was performed via gProfiler (features within 300 kb of TSS, p < 0.05).

Integration of ATAC-seq and RNA-seq

Integration was performed for comparisons between LMNA-deficient vs Ctl cells, and between stretch and unstretched LMNA-deficient cells. We performed a post hoc integration of DEGs and DARs meeting the FDR < 0.05 threshold. DEGs and DARs were intersected based on gene-peak overlaps to identify coordinated changes in chromatin accessibility and transcription.

Additional statistical analysis

Except where otherwise noted (ATAC-seq/RNA-seq) data are presented as violin plots or mean values ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical tests and plotting were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 10, GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). All statistical testing was conducted by ANOVA followed by multiple comparison tests or two-tailed unpaired t-tests. A p-value below 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Lamin A/C Deficiency alters nuclear shape and chromatin organization in human muscle cells

Multinucleated human muscle cells, i.e., myotubes were analyzed 72 hours after confluent myogenic precursor cells were treated with either scrambled control (Ctl) or lamin A/C targeting (LMNA-deficient) siRNA (N≥3 for all experiments, Fig. 1a). Treatment with LMNA-specific siRNA significantly reduced the levels of the corresponding protein (Fig. 1b), mRNA (Fig. 1c) and lamin A/C immunostaining (Fig. 1d). As previously reported [42], lamin A/C depletion impaired myoblast fusion, as indicated by a significantly reduced fusion index in LMNA-deficient compared to Ctl myotubes (Fig. 1e, f). In addition, LMNA-deficient myotubes exhibited nuclei with abnormal morphology, including chromatin protrusions, nuclear envelope invaginations [42, 43] and increased heterogeneity of DNA distribution across the nucleus (Fig. 1g-i) [42] Futhermore, immunostaining for ɣH2AX revealed elevated levels of DNA damage in siLMNA nuclei compared with controls (Fig. 1j), consistent with previous studies [43].

Chromatin compaction and transcriptional output are altered in lamin A/C deficient myotubes

Given the critical role of lamins in maintaining nuclear architecture and chromatin organization, we hypothesized that LMNA deficiency would be associated with broader changes in chromatin accessibility. To test this hypothesis, we performed an assay for transposase-accessible chromatin with high-throughput sequencing (ATAC-seq). In static conditions a total of 541 regions exhibited significant differences in chromatin accessibility between LMNA-deficient and Ctl myotubes (Fig. 2a, Suppl. Table 2), which we refer as differentially accessible regions (DARs). Of these, 364 were more accessible ('up' DARs) and 177 regions were less accessible ('down' DARs) (Fig. 2a, Suppl. Table 2), indicating a general trend toward increased chromatin openness in LMNA-deficient myotubes.

Over-representation analysis (ORA) showed that significant “up” DARs were associated with genes involved in cytoskeleton organization, apoptosis and signaling regulation. In contrast, significant down DARs mapped to histone-modifying activity, protein binding and membrane related functions (Fig. 2b). Most DARs were located in intronic regions, followed by intergenic and promoter areas (Fig. 2c,d), with minimal representation in centromeric and telomeric regions (Suppl. Table 3, Suppl Fig. 2). After adjusting for gene density, DARs were evenly distributed across chromosomes (Fig. 2d).

Changes in the accessibility of DARs can significantly alter gene expression by impacting transcription regulation, enhancer-promoter communication, and chromatin architecture. To evaluate the functional consequences of these chromatin accessibility changes, we conducted RNA-seq on Ctl and LMNA-deficient myotubes. PCA of all expressed genes revealed distinct transcriptional profiles between LMNA-deficient and Ctl myotubes (Suppl Fig. 3a). Differential expression analysis identified 876 of 13,925 genes (~6.3%) as differentially expressed in LMNA-deficient myotubes compared with Ctl, with 446 (~3.2%) upregulated and 430 (~3.1%) downregulated genes (FDR < 0.05 and log2 FC = 1, Fig. 3a). Detailed sets of upregulated and downregulated genes are provided in Suppl. Table 4. A selection of differentially expressed genes observed with RNAseq data were successfully validated using RT-qPCR (Fig. 3b). ORA showed that up-regulated genes were enriched in pathways related to cell adhesion, extracellular matrix organization, active transcription, chromatin regulation and membrane dynamics (Fig. 3c). Conversely, downregulated genes were associated with contractile fiber, integrin signaling activation and protein binding, the basic unit of coiling DNA that serves as a general repressor of genes and transcription [44] (Fig. 3c). Therefore, A-type lamin deficiency tends to promote chromatin accessibility with substantial impact on genes known to play an important role in muscle differentiation and chromatin organization.

To further characterize the consequences of chromatin accessibility on the transcriptional output, we first confirmed the correlation between open chromatin regions and gene expression (Suppl Figure 4a,b). Next, we performed a post hoc integration of DARs and DEGs (Fig. 3d,e). We identified 154 genes showing concordant changes in both chromatin accessibility and gene expression (Fig. 3d), including 25 associated with promoter regions (Fig. 3e). Notably, promoters of genes involved in adipocyte differentiation (e.g., TMEM 120B, KLF2) were more accessible and upregulated, whereas key regulators of muscle differentiation (CD81 and CHRNA1) were less accessible and downregulated.

Since histone modifications are central to chromatin remodeling and gene expression [45], we examined histone marks associated with euchromatin using immunofluorescence. The histone mark staining reveals some heterogeneity among nuclei (Fig. 4). LMNA deficiency in static myotubes was associated with increased levels of the euchromatin marks H3K4me3 and H3K4ac compared to static Ctls (Fig. 4a-d), suggesting again a more open chromatin conformation. Surprisingly, LMNA deficiency also led to elevated levels of the constitutive heterochromatin mark H3K9me3 —known to maintain chromatin structure, gene silencing, and overall genomic stability [46] and the facultative heterochromatin mark H3K27me3, —which represses inappropriate gene expression during cell differentiation —compared to Ctls (Fig. 4e-h) [47]. Overall, our data show that lamin A/C modulates chromatin accessibility and gene expression in differentiated human muscle cells. These findings point to a complex dysregulation of chromatin structure and epigenetic control in the absence of A-type lamins.

A-type lamin deficiency disrupts nuclear mechanoresponses in myotubes

We next explored the effects of acute mechanical stretch on Ctl and LMNA-deficient myotubes. As previously reported, mechanical stretch altered nuclear shape in LMNA-deficient myotubes, as evidenced by an increased nuclear aspect ratio, whereas no significant nuclear deformation was observed in Ctls (Fig. 5a,b). The nuclear area increased following mechanical stretch in Ctrl, but decreased in LMNA-deficient myotubes (Fig. 5c). To assess whether this reduction in nuclear size was associated with altered nucleocytoplasmic shuttling, we analyzed the nucleo-cytoplasmic localization of YAP, a critical transcriptional co-regulator of mechanosensitive signaling pathways [48]. As expected [48, 49], mechanical stretch increased the nucleo-cytoplasmic ratio of YAP in Ctl myotubes (Fig. 5d,e). In contrast, LMNA-deficient myotubes showed a significant decrease in this ratio following stretch exposure (Fig. 5d,e), consistent with findings in myotubes derived from lamin-related congenital muscular dystrophy [16]. These results highlight the essential role for lamin A/C in regulating nucleo-cytoplasmic trafficking and maintaining proper mechanotransduction.

We then examined changes in chromatin accessibility in response to mechanical stretching. In Ctl cells, only 42 regions showed significant changes in accessibility (Fig. 6a). However, in LMNA-deficient myotubes, 200 chromatin regions were affected after stretching, including 117 regions with increased accessible chromatin (up-DARs) (Fig. 6b, Suppl. Table 5). These up-DARs were linked to genes involved in the SMAD protein complex, cytoskeleton organization, fat cell differentiation, cell adhesion, DNA binding regulation, and positive gene expression regulation (Fig. 6c). Conversely, the 83 regions of less accessible chromatin (down-DARs) showed no significant enrichment for known pathways.

At the transcriptomic level, only 7 DEGs were found in Ctls cells following stretch exposure (Fig. 6d). In contrast LMNA-deficient myotubes exhibited a pronounced transcriptional response, with 485 genes upregulated and 149 downregulated after stretch (Fig. 6e, Suppl. Table 6). ORA analysis revealed that the significant upregulated genes were predominantly involved in cytokine activity, extracellular space, cell adhesion and transmembrane transport.. Additionally, pathways associated with histone deacetylation (HDACs), DNA methylation, the extracellular matrix, contractile fiber and myofibrils were found to be downregulated (Fig. 6f).

Integration of the changes in chromatin accessibility and gene expression data identified 45 genes with concordant deregulations (Fig. 7a), including 16 associated with promoter regions (Fig. 7b). Among these, 15 genes exhibited an increase chromatin accessibility (up-DARs) that was concordant with up-regulated DEGs (Fig. 7b). Notably, these included HMG2a (high mobility group AT-hook 2), encoding a DNA-binding chromatin modifier that affects chromatin structure and gene transcription, and genes involved in extracellular matrix regulation (TIMP3, HAS1, COL8A1) and cell death/apoptosis (PAWR, EMP1) (Fig. 7b). Interestingly, both chromatin accessibility (DAR) and RNA levels of CKMT2, a mitochondrial creatine kinase gene associated with dropped head syndrome, were decreased (Fig. 7b). These results indicate that LMNA-deficient myotubes respond to mechanical stretch with a skewed transcriptional program favoring non-muscle-specific stress-related pathways and reduced capacity for chromatin remodeling and gene silencing.

Importantly, comparison between stretched LMNA-deficient and stretched Ctl myotubes revealed the most extensive differences in chromatin accessibility, with 1,739 regions becoming more accessible (up-DARs) and 1,092 regions becoming less accessible (down-DARs) (Fig. 8a, Suppl. Most of these more accessible regions were located in promoter regions, within 1 kb to 2–3 kb upstream of transcription start sites (Fig. 8b,c). These more accessible chromatin regions were enriched for pathways involved in DNA damage response, cellular stress responses, cell cycle regulation, cytoskeletal organization, and major signaling pathways such as Wnt and SMAD (Fig. 8d). In contrast, down-DARs did not significantly map to any known pathway, suggesting a more diffuse or non- specific reduction in chromatin accessibility. As observed under static conditions, the intensity levels of both euchromatin and heterochromatin marks remained significantly higher in stretched LMNA-deficient myotubes compared to stretched Ctls (Fig. 9a-h). LMNA deficiency leads to a heightened and dysregulated chromatin response to mechanical stress, characterized by a widespread promoter opening and activation of pathways involved in DNA damage and cellular stress responses. Such defects likely impair the maintenance of muscle cell identity and may contribute to the reduced capacity for hypertrophic growth observed in lamin-related congenital muscular dystrophy [16].

Discussion

The main finding of this study is that lamin A/C plays a crucial role in protecting chromatin from aberrant opening and increased accessibility during acute mechanical stretch in human myotubes, in addition to its established roles in mechanosensitive signaling and nuclear integrity. Through this function, lamin A/C helps maintain lineage fidelity and prevents inappropriate gene expression in differentiated cells exposed to mechanical cues.

The nature, timing, magnitude, and impact of force-induced nuclear mechano-adaptation likely depend on the characteristics of the mechanical input. Although myotubes exist only transiently in vivo during development, in vitro muscle cell differentiation remains a valuable model for studying muscle physiology and pathology. We found that control human myotubes sense and respond to dynamic mechanical stimuli, as evidenced by YAP signaling activation upon cyclic stretch. However, under our experimental conditions, mechanical stretch had limited effects on chromatin accessibility and gene transcription in control myotubes (Fig. 6a,d). This suggests that adaptive mechanisms restrict large scale chromatin remodeling during short-term stress. These observations align with previous findings demonstrating the well-established critical role of the nuclear lamina in buffering mechanical perturbations over both short and long timescales to adapt the nucleus to mechanical perturbations [13, 50, 51].

Indeed, lamin-A/C is known to sense mechanical strain and responds to structural and mechanical changes in the cellular environment by reinforcing the nuclear lamina, increasing stiffness, and preserving nuclear shape [13, 50]. Conversely, mechanical stress leads to lamin A/C depolymerization and decreased nuclear stiffness, maintaining a 'mechanostat equilibrium' [8].

Interestingly, cell differentiation increases nuclear stiffness and dampens stretch-induced nuclear deformation [52], primarily due to structural remodeling of lamin A/C [12, 53, 54] and chromatin condensation [55]. As a result, differentiated myonuclei are expected to be less deformable and more resistant to reorganization under mechanical stress than progenitor cell nuclei. Futhermore, developmentally regulated nuclear mechanics are thought to contribute to both the establishment and maintenance of lineage specification [52], which is particularly relevant in mechanically active tissues such as skeletal muscle [6, 43]. By contrast, LMNA mutations such as those found in laminopathies like L-CMD lead to loss of lamin A/C expression, increased nuclear deformability and greater susceptibility to mechanical damage [16, 43] as well as DNA instability [43]. Similarly, we found that acute mechanical stretch in LMNA-deficient myotubes likely exceeds their mechanobiological tolerance, resulting in cellular damage and/or mal-adaptative phenotype transformation.

In LMNA -deficient myotubes, mechanical stretch triggered a significantly broader increase in chromatin accessibility compared to controls with more genomic regions becoming “open” and greater accumulation of active histone marks such as H3K4me3 and H3K4ac. These findings were consistent with transcriptomic shifts (see Figs. 6b,c,e,f and 8) and aligned with previous observations of global chromatin reorganization following LMNA knockdown [56].

Interestingly, the chromatin response in LMNA-deficient myotubes was strikingly different from the mechano-adaptive processes reported in embryonic cells [5, 22]. In stem cells, mechanical loading typically leads to increased chromatin condensation, a general enrichment of the repressive histone mark H3K27me3, a switch from constitutive (H3K9me) to facultative (H3K27me3) heterochromatin, resulting in increased chromatin condensation levels [5, 22]. Such mechano-adaptive processes result in rapid stiffening of the nucleus through chromatin structural strengthening and stabilization, at least partly independent of lamin A/C [5]. In contrast, following mechanical stretch, LMNA-deficient myotubes exhibited elevated levels of the active histone mark H3K4me3, reduced H3K27me3, and global increase in H3K9me3 (Fig. 9), suggesting enhanced transcriptional readiness rather than repression (Fig. 6). Several points should be considered when interpreting histone immunolabelling. First, histone immunolabelling intensity reflects relative, not absolute, levels of euchromatin or heterochromatin. Second, the timing of epigenetic modifications varies: for instance, H3K9me3 can respond rapidly to mechanical stretch, resulting in a transient decrease, reflecting immediate chromatin relaxation, [57]while H3K27me3 accumulates more gradually over time, [58] reinforcing long-term transcriptional repression and mechanoadaptive chromatin remodeling. Third, although H3K9me3 and H3K27me3 statistically co-localize with compacted chromatin structure, up to 30% of H3K27me3 foci may localize within transcriptionally active euchromatin [46].

The chromatin deregulation observed in LMNA-deficient myotubes may, at least in part, result from disrupted function of the Polycomb group (PcG) proteins- epigenetic repressors that ensure the proper transcriptional repression of genes involved in development and differentiation processes [59, 60]. Lamin A/C is essential for PcG-mediated repression [47]. Therefore, one possibility is that lamin A/C-depleted myotubes are more susceptible to instability of PcG protein-mediated transcriptional repression, which may promote chromatin deregulation under mechanical stretch.

Chromatin domain organization and its epigenetic changes are key factors in laminopathic diseases, with consequences during cell differentiation [61–63]. Lamin A mutations have been shown to cause both gain-of-function effects, such as generating promiscuous LADs, and dominant-negative effects resulting from the inability to initiate and spread LADs [61]. Importantly, LAD-associated gene regulation relies on diverse chromatin regulators, [61, 62] some of which function independently of lamina binding [64, 65]. However, the dynamics of lamin-chromatin regulations under mechanical stress remain largely unexplored. Mechanical stretch could indirectly affect LADs by altering nuclear morphology or chromatin mobility. Unfortunately, no direct methods currently exist to assess mechanical strain specifically on LAD-associated chromatin. It is still unclear whether mechanical cues are transmitted from the cytoskeleton to particular LADs.

Overall, we observe strong concordance between changes in chromatin accessibility (DARs) and gene expression (DEG), with most DARs aligning with corresponding gene expression changes. However, some discordance was noted. For instance, nine promoters exhibited increased accessibility yet were associated with downregulated genes. This discordance may reflect the involvement of repressive factors or chromatin remodelers and suggest post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms that dampen mRNA levels despite open chromatin at the promoter. However, we cannot exclude limitations in assigning DARs to the nearest gene, which may not always be the true regulatory target. Furthermore, increased chromatin accessibility in LMNA-deficient myotubes was enriched in promoter regions and correlated with upregulation of genes involved in DNA damage response, stress signaling, and adipogenesis. In contrast, key muscle differentiation pathways, including those governing myofibril and contractile fiber genes were downregulated following mechanical stretch in siLMNA myotubes. The failure of LMNA-deficient myotubes to activate typical muscle-specific transcription programs upon stretch suggests a shift from muscle lineage maintenance toward stress and repair signaling _a hallmark of muscle wasting diseases. Additionally, the upregulation of non-muscle-specific and inflammatory pathways (e.g., cytokine activity, ECM remodeling) further supports the emergence of a pathological remodeling program with compromised differentiation to the skeletal muscle lineage. Notably, the induction of HMG2A and TIMP3 genes linked to chromatin dynamics and fibrosis, respectively is particularly significant.

Lamin-related congenital muscular dystrophy (L-CMD), the most severe form of muscle laminopathies presents with early-onset muscle wasting beginning [66], and is characterized by a pronounced loss of A-type lamin expression [66]. Patients show impaired muscle hypertrophy in response to mechanical stress [16]. In this study, we reported exacerbated chromatin response to mechanical stress in LMNA-deficient myotubes, evidence by widespread promoter activation and induction of DNA damage and stress-response pathways. These findings extend our understanding of the nuclear lamina’s function beyond its structural role, highlighting its critical involvement in chromatin regulation under mechanical challenge. This insight may have important implications for elucidating the pathophysiology of L-CMD and the increased susceptibility to mechanical damage seen in affected individuals.
