Role of Exercise Stress Elastography for Investigating Shortness of Breath
Authors: Takahiro Sakamoto, Kazuto Yamaguchi, Seita Yamasaki, Kazuaki Tanabe
Published: 2026

A 79-year-old man presented with exertional dyspnea and no evidence or history of liver disease that would affect stiffness. Exercise stress echocardiography using an ergometer in the semi-supine position (25 W initially, increased by 10 W after 2 min, terminated for leg fatigue), combined with shear-wave elastography (ARIETTA 65LE LV, FUJIFILM Healthcare Corporation), proved useful for evaluating his dyspnea. Elastography was performed using a right intercostal approach in the semi-supine position, with breath-holding at end-expiration, within 1 min post-exercise, with ≥5 valid measurements averaged. A successful elastography measurement was defined as a measurement with a net effective shear wave velocity ≥50%, which represents the device-specific quality metric.

The patient was found to have bradycardic atrial fibrillation. His heart rate increased from 40 beats/min at rest to 58 beats/min at peak stress, while blood pressure rose from 143/79 to 152/69 mmHg. His Borg dyspnea/leg fatigue scale score reached 15/17, although oxygen saturation remained 99%. Echocardiography showed moderate mitral regurgitation (MR) and mild tricuspid regurgitation (TR). The MR did not change between rest and stress; however, the TR escalated from mild to moderate (Figure A). The patient’s inferior vena cava (IVC) diameter, its respiratory variability, and the TR pressure gradient (TRPG) were unaffected by exercise, but elastography showed a rising estimated right atrial pressure (RAP: −5.8 +6.7 × ln[liver stiffness]), from 8.8 mmHg at rest to 14.4 mmHg immediately after exercise (interquartile range/median ratio: 19% at rest, 7% immediately after exercise) (Figure B).1Figure.(A) Increasing tricuspid regurgitation (TR) by exercise stress. (A) Increasing tricuspid regurgitation (TR) by exercise stress. (B) Estimation of right atrial pressure (RAP) using exercise stress elastography.

Liver elastography quantifies liver stiffness and correlates with RAP.1 To our knowledge, this is the first report describing exercise stress elastography. Because RAP can increase during exercise, exercise-induced pulmonary hypertension is under-recognized without an accurate estimated RAP.2 In this case, exercise stress elastography may reflect an increase in RAP undetected by IVC parameters. This may better reflect RAP and, when combined with peak TRPG, may also better reflect pulmonary artery pressure than conventional IVC-based methods used during exercise.
