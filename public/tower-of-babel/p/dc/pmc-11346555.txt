Factors influencing antibody response after COVID-19 recombinant protein vaccination in adults: A cross-sectional observational study, in Chongqing, China
Authors: Jianqiao Li, Jiawei Xu, Yu Liu, Lei Chen, Linling Yu, Xiao Xiao, Qing Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe factors affecting the antibody responses to the ZF2001 vaccine remain unknown. To address this, we conducted a cross-sectional serological study in the real world. Adults with no prior SARS-CoV-2 infection history and received three doses of ZF2001 vaccine were invited to our study in the early stages of the COVID-19 epidemic in Chongqing between 7 April 2021 and 17 November 2021. A questionnaire survey was conducted to obtain demographic characteristics, health information, and the frequency of lifestyles at the time of enrollment. A total of 266 eligible subjects aged 18 to 86 years, with a median age of 56.00 (IQR: 34–66) participated. 68.80% of them were female. Hypertension (13.16%) and diabetes (6.02%) were common comorbidities. Serum samples were collected at one month after the third dose of ZF2001 vaccination, and serological testing was conducted using the Pseudovirus-Based Neutralization Assay. The chi-square test was employed to compare seropositivity rates, and the Mann-Whitney U test or the Kruskal–Wallis test was used to analyze the neutralizing antibodies level in stratified groups. Subsequently, univariate and multivariate linear regression analyses were conducted to identify the influencing factors. We observed that seropositivity rates was 76.32%, with 95% confidence interval (95%CI) 70.85%–81.03%, and geometric mean titer (GMT) was 120.26, with 95%CI 100.38–144.08. Age, diabetes, and frequently of alcohol were negative associations with antibody response (β = -0.2021, 95% CI: −0.2507 to −0.1535, β = -0.2873, 95% CI: −0.5590 to −0.0155, β = -0.2082, 95% CI: −0.3419 to-0.0746, P < 0.0001, P = 0.0384, P = 0.0024). Conversely, the -interval between 1 and 2 dose and frequently of tea were positive associations with antibody response (β = 0.1369, 95% CI: 0.0463 to 0.2275, β = 0.0830, 95% CI: 0.0106 to 0.1554, P = 0.0032, P = 0.0247). Overall, the ZF2001 vaccine-induced antibody response was influenced by a multifactor that may provide a reference for the development of personalized antigen vaccines and vaccination strategies in the future.

Introduction

Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) is an infectious disease caused by the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus-2 (SARS-CoV-2), which has caused great human health and economic losses worldwide.1,2 World Health Organization (WHO) announced that it would no longer classify COVID-19 as a public health emergency of international concern in May 2023, but the COVID-19 is still considered a global health threat.3 To prevent the COVID-19 pandemic, multiple technology platform vaccines (inactivated virus, viral vectors, protein subunits, mRNA) were developed at an unprecedented speed and were given an Emergency Use of Approval. Studies have shown these vaccines were an effective intervention to prevent and control COVID-19 infection, severe symptoms, and death.4–8 Of these, the ZF2001 (Zifivax) vaccine was approved for emergency use in March 2021 and played an important role in the COVID-19 pandemic in China.

Currently, vaccinology remains empirical in many respects, with the same dose being used for almost every disease and individual.9,10 However, vaccine-induced antibody responses exhibited significant variations among individuals. Prior research had shown that various factors including age, sex, obesity, hypertension, and smoking habit can impact the production of neutralizing antibodies (Nabs) after vaccination with mRNA COVID-19 vaccines.11–16 Furthermore, factors such as vaccine type, vaccine dose, and vaccination schedule can also affect the antibody response.17 Currently, the factors influencing the antibody response to the ZF2001 vaccine have not been fully investigated yet and remain unknown.

The aim of the study was to explore factors influencing antibody responses one month after ZF2001 vaccinations with three doses in Chinese adults in the real world.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

Our study complied with Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement: guidelines for reporting observational studies.18 STROBE Checklist: cross-sectional studies (see S1). As of 3 November 2021, a total of 583 confirmed local cases of COVID-19 had been reported in Chongqing, China, with the infection rate approximately 583/3,204,0000, indicating low SARS-CoV-2 infection rate during the study period.

Between 7 April 2021 and 17 November 2021, we randomly selected 3 (Yubei Districts, Yuzhong Districts, and Jiulongpo Districts) out of 39 Districts in Chongqing, and then 7 (Jiefangbei, Jiulong, Xiejiawan, Yangjiaping, Zhongliangshan, and Longshan) out of 127 vaccination hospitals in these districts. Subjects were randomly sampled by age from all ZF2001 vaccine recipients in each hospital. We had invited the participants via telephone and recruitment notice, and if willing to participate, arrived at the vaccination hospital one month after the third dose of vaccination.

Participants

Individuals aged ≥ 18 years were included in the study: (1) had no prior SARS-CoV-2 infection history. (2) only received three doses of ZF2001 vaccine. (3) willing to provide serum sample one month after the third dose. Those who were excluded: (1) had SARS-CoV-2 infection or evidence of SARS-CoV-2 nucleic acid positivity during the study period. (2) immunocompromised. (3) pregnant. (4) could not provide a serum sample on time.

Referring to previous reference literature,19 the standard deviation (SD) of Nabs was approximately 3.00 to estimate the sample size, with a significance level α = 0.05 and precision β = 0.4. We calculated the sample size to be 217 by using the PASS15.0 software (NCSS, LLC. Kaysville, Utah, USA), and considering the proportion of rejections, the sample size was increased by 20% to determine a sample size of 261.

Vaccination

All volunteers received three doses of ZF2001 vaccination in our study. The information would be recorded in the Chongqing Immunization Information Management System (CQIIMS) after each dose of vaccination. The ZF2001 vaccine is a protein subunit vaccine that used the tandem-repeat SARS-CoV-2 spike receptor-binding domain (RBD) dimer as the antigen, which is produced in Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cells and then adjuvated with aluminum hydroxide.20,21 According to the vaccine package insert, the ZF2001 vaccine should be administered by intramuscular injection into the deltoid muscle of the upper arm, and the primary immunity schedule consisted of three doses (0, 1, 2 months or 0, 2, 6 months).

Data collection

We designed a questionnaire to collect data on the subject’ demographic characteristics, health information, and frequency of the lifestyles, including sex, age, height, weight, SARS-CoV-2 infections history and comorbidities, date of vaccination, date of blood collection, frequency of alcohol, frequency of smoke, frequency of tea, and frequency of exercise. Before the serum samples collection, the doctor would query the subject and fill in questionnaire the information (see S2). Among them, the comorbidities for those aged ≥ 60 years was derived from China Primary Care Services to reduce recall bias, and the vaccination information was obtained from the CQIIMS.

We investigated a total of 13 variables, of which continuous variables were stratified. Age was categorized into 6 groups by 10-year intervals. BMI (kg/m2) was calculated and classified into four categories based on the Chinese BMI classification for underweight (<18.5), normal weight (18.5–24), overweight (24–28), and obese (≥28). Days to vaccination between doses were divided into 4 subgroups by adding 7-day intervals, and days between the third dose vaccination and blood collection were divided into 4 groups based on 10-day intervals. The frequency of lifestyles refers to the times in a specific time period. Lifestyle factors (frequency of alcohol/smoke/tea/exercise) were defined as never (never or almost never), occasionally (1–3 times per month or on a specific holiday), and usually (≥1 times per week). The quantity and type of lifestyles were not investigated in the study.

Serum sample collection

Serum samples were collected approximately one month (we define as 25–60 days) after receiving the three dose ZF2001 vaccine, We collected 5 ml of venous blood from each volunteer and stored the serum samples at ultra-low temperature (−30°C).

Pseudovirus-based neutralization assay (PBNA)

All serum samples were tested for Nabs against the Wuhan-1 strain using the PBNA, which is based on a pseudotyped vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV) production system in the BSL-2 biosafety laboratory. The PBNA for “SARS-CoV-2” was established by the National Institutes for Food and Drug Control of China (NIFDC).22,23 The 50% tissue culture infectious dose (TCID50) was determined by the transduction of pseudovirus into Huh7 cells (JCRB). Specifically, 100 µL of human serum samples at different concentrations (1:30 ~ 1:2430) were mixed with 50 µL supernatants containing 650 ~ 1300 TCID50 SARS-CoV-2 pseudovirus. The mixture was incubated for 1 h at 37°C, supplied with 5% CO2. After the incubation, 100 µL of Huh7 cell suspension (3 × 105 cells/ml) were then added to the mixtures of pseudoviruses and diluted serum for an additional 20 ~ 28 h incubation in the 37°C, 5% (vol) CO2 incubator. Then, 150 µL of supernatant was removed, and 100 µL of luciferase detection reagent was added to each well. After 2 min of incubation, each well was mixed 10 times by pipetting, and 150 µL of the mixture was transferred to a new white microplate. Luciferase activity was measured using a microplate luminometer (PerkinElmer). Then, the infection inhibition rates of each dilution of the sample were calculated according to the relative luminescence unit (RLU) values which were measured by a 96-well microplate reader after adding firefly luciferase substrate to the detection well as follows: inhibition rate = [1–(average RLU) of sample – average RLU of CC/(average RLU of VC – average RLU of CC)]×100%, CC = cell control, VC = virus control).22 Based on the inhibition rate results, the half-maximal effective concentration (EC50) of each sample was calculated by the Reed–Muench method.22 The lower detection limit was 3022,23, with a value of EC50 ≥ 30 defined as positive, if the antibody titer was below the detection limit, half the detection limit was assigned. We had established the internal validation and controls.23 The key control indicators were the coefficient variation (CV) % for duplicate wells ≤ 30% for sample dilutions, and the CV% of the RLU in the VC wells ≤ 30%.

Statistical analysis

A stratified statistical descriptive was conducted on all data collected. Categorical variables were described using frequency and percentages, and non-normally distributed continuous variables were described using median and interquartile range (IQR). Seropositivity rates and GMT with 95% CI of Nabs titers were calculated in this study. The seropositivity rates for categorical variables were analyzed using the Chi-square test. For non-normally distributed continuous variables, Spearman's correlation test was employed to determine the correlations between risk factors and Nabs titers. The GMT of the stratified groups were compared using the Mann – Whitney U test or the Kruskal–Wallis test. Finally, univariate and multiple linear regression model was employed to identify influencing factors and estimate the regression coefficient (β), with log10-transformed Nabs titers as the dependent variable, and all 13 variables as independent variables. We did a sensitivity analysis by generalized linear regression models, converting categorical independent variables into continuous variables and excluding variables with p > .05 in the results of univariate analyses. Statistical analyses and plotting were performed using GraphPad Prism version 8.0.2. The bars above show GMT for each group, with error bars indicating 95% CI, and N represents sample size. Two-tailed p values less than 0.050 were considered statistically significant, NS: P > 0.05, **p < .01, ***p < .001, ****p < .0001.

Seropositivity rates

We observed that 76.32% (95% CI: 70.85%–81.03%) of the serum samples tested positive for Nabs titers. The seropositive rates were significantly lower rates with aging, especially in the elderly aged ≥ 60 years who exhibited significantly lower seropositive rates (p < .0001). Regarding comorbidities, the seropositivity rates were lower in individuals with hypertension, diabetes, and other diseases than in those without these comorbidities (p < .0001, p = .0002, p = .0197). In terms of vaccination schedule, the seropositivity rates gradually increased with the length of the interval and the interval sample collection (p < .0001, p = .0062, p < .0001). Regarding the frequency of exercise, individuals who exercise usually have lower seropositivity rates (p < .0001). Neutralizing antibodies titers induced by vaccination

We observed that the GMT of Nabs was 120.26 (95% CI: 100.38–144.08) among all participants. The females exhibited higher GMT compared to males; However, the sex difference was not statistically significant (p = .0826) (Figure 1a). A negative correlation was observed between age and Nabs titers, with Nabs titers decreasing with aging (p < .0001) (Figure 1b). Specifically, a gradual decrease in GMT of Nabs titers was observed across age groups from 18–29 years to ≥70 years, with a significantly lower GMT in individuals aged ≥60 years compared to those aged 18–59 years (p < .0001) (Figure 1c). In terms of comorbidities, the individuals with diabetes, hypertension, and other diseases exhibited a lower GMT than those without these conditions (p < .0001, p < .0001, p = .0113) (Figure 1d). Furthermore, a negative correlation was observed between BMI and Nabs titers in those aged 18–59 years, while a positive correlation was observed in those aged ≥60 years. However, neither of these correlations was statistical significance (P = .1265, p = .7795) (Figure 1e). Lastly, a comparison of GMT among stratified BMI groups revealed no statistically significant differences. (p = .6096) (Figure 1f) (See S3). We observed a positive correlation of vaccination schedule and Nabs titers, with a longer interval between doses, the higher the titer of Nabs (p < .0001, p = .0138) (Figure 2a–b). Similarly, a positive, though less significant correlation was observed between the days of serum sample collection and Nabs titers (p = .2639) (Figure 2c). Analyzing the GMT across stratified groups on the interval of 1–2 dose, we observed a gradual increase in GMT for intervals of 28–39 days, 40–49 days, 50–59 days, and ≥ 60 days (p < .0001) (Figure 2d). The highest GMT was observed within intervals of 40–49 days between 2 and 3 doses followed by a gradual decline. However, there was no statistically significant difference (p = .0616) (Figure 2e). Similarity, the peak GMT was observed within 41–48 days for stratified blood sample groups, with a subsequent gradual decrease (p < .0001) (Figure 2f). Association of Nabs titers with influencing factors

Univariate regression analyses showed that significant differences in Nabs titers with age (p < .0001), hypertension (p < .0001)，diabetes (p < .0001), other diseases (p = .0087), interval of 1–2 doses (p < .0001), interval of blood collection (p < .0001), and frequency of exercise (p < .0001). While, multivariate analysis showed that age (β = -0.2021, p < .0001), diabetes (β = -0.2873, p = .0384), and alcohol ≥ 1time per week (β = -0.2082, p = .0024) decreased neutralizing antibody production, whereas longer days of vaccination between doses 1–2 (β = 0.1369, p = .0032,) and drinking tea ≥ 1time per week (β = 0.0830, p = .0247) increased antibody production. Sensitivity analyses

We conducted sensitivity analyses by three models: generalized linear regression models, transforming the type of independent variables into continuous variables (age, days to 1–2 doses of vaccination, days to 2–3 doses of vaccination, and days to blood collection), and screening variables (only included the variables with p < .050 as the independent variables(excluding frequency of exercise) into the multiple linear regression). It was found that the results of the three models with statistically significant independent variables were almost consistent and stable. (See S4-S6).

Discussion

Our study preliminarily explored the factors affecting antibody response to ZF2001 vaccines in the real world. It indicated that one month after administration of the ZF2001 vaccine with three doses induced the humoral immune response, with positive seropositivity rates of 76.32% and GMT of 120.26 (95% CI: 100.38–144.08) for all participants. Our findings enrich the results of phase III clinical trials of the ZF2001 vaccine and age, diabetes, interval of 1–2 doses vaccination, frequency of alcohol, and frequency of tea were statistically associated with neutralizing antibody responses, which provide the reference for the development of personalized antigen COVID-19 vaccines and vaccination strategies in the future.

Previous studies have shown that females generally have higher antibody responses to vaccines and experience more adverse events than males after vaccination.24 Mechanisms of sex differences in vaccine-induced immunity may be related to sex steroid hormones, genetic and epigenetic regulation, and microbiome.25 As with other findings on mRNA COVID-19 vaccines,11,13,26 we did not observe sex differences in antibody responses. The possible reason is that our study population was mostly female, with an unbalanced sex distribution.

Studies on COVID-19 mRNA vaccine have shown that aging affects the production27–30 and we’ve noted a significant effect on antibody response post-vaccination, particularly in the elderly aged ≥60 years with a significantly reduced vaccine response. This may be attributed to immunosenescence. Immune senescence was generally defined as age-related changes in the immune system that result in increased susceptibility to infectious diseases and decreased response to vaccination.31 As the production of T cell-derived antibodies and the generation of B lymphocytes decline with age, the antibody response to infectious agents and after vaccination is inadequate.32 Elderly individuals, especially those with comorbidities, are often excluded from clinical trials.33 Yet the elderly with comorbidities are at higher risk of severe illness and death from COVID-19 infection, and those are most in need of vaccine protection. We observed lower titers of Nabs in individuals with diabetes compared to those without diabetes, and the results were consistent with the previous COVID-19 vaccine studies.34,35 The mechanism may be related to the fact that patients with hyperglycemia and insulin resistance had a reduction in the number of circulating helper T-cells, the CD4þ to CD8þ lymphocyte ratio, lymphocyte proliferative response, impaired monocyte or macrophage, and defects in antigen presentation.36

Another factor influencing vaccine response was the vaccine schedule. We observed higher titer of Nabs with longer 1–2 days of vaccination, whereas there was no significant association with longer days between 2 and 3 doses. This is inconsistent with the results of a previous study on the ZF2001 vaccine that the participants with an extended interval between the second and third doses (doses at 0, 1, and 4–6 months) showed higher neutralizing titer than those with shorter interval (doses at 0, 1–2 months).37 It may be related to the longer antibody maturation.

We also observed that the frequency of alcohol is a negative predictor, and the frequency of tea was positively associated with the production of Nabs after ZF2001 vaccination. A possible explanation for the effect of alcohol habits on antibodies response is that low doses of alcohol may inhibit antigen-induced B-cell proliferation,38 and teas have been shown to exhibit various pharmacological activities such as antioxidant, anticancer, hypoglycemic, antibacterial, antiviral, and neuroprotective effects.39

We did not observe statistical associations between BMI, hypertension, other diseases, smoking frequency, and exercise frequency with antibody response, and further validation of these factors in cohort study is needed in the future.

There are some limitations to this study. Firstly, the absence of an unvaccinated control group and pre-immunization samples for Nabs testing were limited to fully assess the vaccine-induced antibody responses. Secondly, the plaque reduction neutralization test (PRNT), the gold standard for neutralizing antibody testing40 was not used due to time-consuming and laborious, low throughput, and must be performed under Biosafety Level 3 (BSL-3) laboratory conditions. Instead, we used the PBNA as an alternative. However, the differences between pseudoviruses and live viruses, as well as various uncertainties in the experimental process may lead to varying antibody levels between PBNA and live virus tests result. Nonetheless, studies have shown that the potency of PBNA is good correlated (R = 0.9781) with the live virus neutralization assay41 and further validation is needed. Thirdly, all subjects were from vaccination hospitals，despite our inclusion and exclusion criteria, there may still be volunteer bias. We tried to be as comprehensive as possible in collecting influencing factors, but there may still be some unidentifiable confounding factors that may have led to false associations between certain factors and Nabs. Moreover, some of the data such as SARS-CoV-2 infection history relied on participants’ self-reports，which may have resulted in the inclusion of asymptomatic infections and recall bias, potentially influencing the outcome. To further validate the influencing factors on Nabs, we will consider, increasing the sample size, adopting a more rigorous random sampling method, and more reliable questionnaires to reduce these errors in the future.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study showed that antibody responses to ZF2001 vaccines are affected by multifactor in the real world. However, given some of the uncertainties and limitations, evidence from multi-center studies is still needed to develop further vaccination strategies. We suggest that the relevant authorities actively advocate booster vaccination for these high-risk populations, appropriately Adjust the days between vaccination doses, and strengthen the promotion of healthy lifestyles and interventions to maximize the protective effect of the vaccine.
