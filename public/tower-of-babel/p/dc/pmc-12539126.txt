Platelet-rich plasma alleviates endoplasmic reticulum stress via the IGF1R-PI3K-AKT pathway in temporomandibular joint osteoarthritis: an in vivo clinical trial
Authors: Shasha Liu, Lin Sun, Jingke Fu, Bin Cai, Yongqiang Hao
Published: 2025

Background

Temporomandibular joint (TMJ) osteoarthritis (OA; TMJ-OA) is an important subtype of TMJ disorders, accounting for 18.01–84.74% of cases [1]. It is characterised by degenerative changes in TMJ [2], causing pain, joint sounds, limited mouth opening, and, in severe cases, facial deformities, thus substantially impacting patients’ quality of life [3].

The pathological features of TMJ-OA include synovial inflammation and joint cartilage destruction [4]. To establish an animal model for investigating the pathogenesis and potential therapies of TMJ-OA, intra-articular injection of monosodium iodoacetate (MIA) has been demonstrated to induce joint inflammation and concomitant osteoarthritic pathological changes, such as cartilage degradation, in the rat TMJ [5]. Currently, TMJ-OA is mainly treated with nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, physical therapy, and other measures to control synovitis and relieve pain. Direct therapies targeting the repair of joint cartilage damage are lacking [6]. Chondrocytes are critical for maintaining articular cartilage homeostasis, and their functional disorder is the main cause of cartilage destruction during OA [7]. Endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS) is an important pathophysiological process in OA, which can induce chondrocyte apoptosis, accelerate cartilage degeneration, promote the expression of proteases, lead to the degradation of the extracellular matrix, aggravate cartilage damage, and activate the inflammatory response, further aggravating joint injury [8]. Therefore, alleviating ERS in chondrocytes may represent a new and effective therapeutic strategy for TMJ-OA. A critical pathway known to regulate cell survival and mitigate apoptosis is the insulin-like growth factor 1 receptor (IGF1R)/phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (AKT) signalling pathway [9, 10], which plays a pivotal role in maintaining chondrocyte homeostasis and has been implicated in the pathogenesis of OA [11]. Furthermore, the PI3K/AKT pathway acts as an upstream regulator of ER stress, and its activation can lead to the inhibition of ER stress [12].

Platelet-rich plasma (PRP), a highly concentrated plasma derived from blood centrifugation, contains a platelet concentration three to five times higher than that of normal human plasma [13]. Due to its rich composition, PRP has garnered considerable attention in regenerative medicine [14]. PRP is abundant in bioactive substances, including various growth factors (GFs; such as platelet-derived GF, transforming GF-beta [TGF-β], insulin-like GF-1 [IGF-1], vascular endothelial GF, and epidermal GF), as well as cytokines [15]. These components contribute to the therapeutic potential of PRP. For instance, GFs in PRP can promote chondrocyte proliferation and cartilage matrix synthesis, thereby aiding in the repair of damaged cartilage tissue. Moreover, PRP influences cartilage thickness by inhibiting the apoptosis of osteoarthritic chondrocytes in vivo [16]. Transcriptome sequencing has further revealed that PRP influences key cellular processes in IL-1β-induced inflammatory condylar chondrocytes, and its beneficial effects are associated with the activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway and upregulation of IGF1R expression [17].

Clinical studies have demonstrated the efficacy of PRP. A single injection of PRP into the lower joint cavity of the TMJ effectively alleviates symptoms in patients with TMJ-OA and exerts radiographically confirmed reparative effects on the condylar bone [18, 19]. Extensive basic and clinical research, particularly concerning knee OA, indicates that PRP effectively improves symptoms and repairs cartilage defects in OA [20, 21]. Additionally, studies on knee osteoarthritis have found that multiple (3) PRP injections lead to a better long-term improvement in joint function compared to a single injection [22, 23]. However, research on PRP treatment for TMJ-OA is still relatively limited, and there have been no reports on the relationship between the number of injections and their efficacy. Crucially, while the therapeutic effects of PRP are recognized, the specific molecular mechanisms by which it alleviates ERS in condylar chondrocytes via the IGF1R/PI3K/AKT pathway remain underexplored.

Given the promising regenerative properties of PRP, further investigation into its mechanisms of action is warranted. This study aims to investigate the therapeutic effects of PRP on TMJ-OA, specifically focusing on its potential to alleviate ERS in condylar chondrocytes, and its impact on cartilage protection and condylar bone repair. We hypothesise that PRP will mitigate ERS-induced chondrocyte apoptosis and promote cartilage integrity by activating the IGF1R/PI3K/AKT signalling pathway, offering a novel therapeutic strategy for TMJ-OA. We also propose the hypothesis that three PRP injections into the joint cavity are effective for treating TMJ-OA, and the efficacy is superior to a single injection treatment. This hypothesis was then verified through both animal and clinical trials.

Methods

Isolation of primary condylar chondrocytes, induction of inflammatory chondrocytes, and preparation of PRP

Four-week-old female Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats were euthanised using an anaesthetic overdose under sterile conditions. Following dissection, condylar cartilage was collected from the TMJs. Primary condylar chondrocyte extraction was performed according to a previously described protocol [19]. Chondrocytes were stimulated with 10 ng/mL of IL-1β (PeproTech, Cranbury, NJ, USA) for 24 h to induce inflammation.

Whole blood samples were collected from 8-week-old healthy rats, and 2.5% sodium citrate anticoagulant was added at a 1:10 volume ratio. To prepare PRP, we referred to previous studies by Yin et al. and Xu et al. The PRP was prepared using a double centrifugation method. First, the whole blood was centrifuged at 160 × g for 10 min. The resulting supernatant plasma was then collected and centrifuged again at 250 × g for 15 min to obtain pure PRP [24, 25]. PRP was activated using thrombin (Hakon Pharma, Hangzhou, China) before use. The PRP protein concentration was determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and standardised. For the in vitro experiments, protein concentrations of 50 and 500 µg/mL were used. The concentration used was determined and verified based on a previous study [19] and a preliminary dose-response CCK-8 assay for cell viability (Figure S1). For in vivo experiments, undiluted PRP was directly injected into the TMJ cavity.

Detection of PRP effects on chondrocyte apoptosis using flow cytometry

Chondrocytes (5 × 105/well) were seeded in six-well plates and incubated overnight at 37 °C until approximately 70–80% confluence was achieved. Chondrocytes were divided into four groups: control (normal cell culture medium) and three experimental groups (10 ng/mL of IL-1β and PRP at concentrations of 0, 50, and 500 µg/mL), and collected using centrifugation at 300 ×g for 5 min after trypsin digestion. Chondrocytes were stained using the Annexin V-FITC/PI Cell Apoptosis Detection Kit (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) and apoptosis was detected within 1 h using a flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA). FlowJo Version 10.8.1 (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) was used for the data analysis and plotting. The proportion of apoptotic chondrocytes in each group was then calculated and compared.

Detection of PRP effects on genes related to ERS using reverse-transcription–quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Chondrocytes were divided into four groups, as described in the previous section. Subsequently, 1 mL of RNAiso Plus (TAKARA Bio, Shiga, Japan) was added to each well after co-cultivation for 24 h. Total RNA was extracted according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA purity and concentration were measured using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) to determine the OD260/OD280 ratio.

RNA was processed using ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Master Mix with gDNA Remover (TOYOBO, Osaka, Japan) and THUNDERBIRD Next SYBR qPCR Mix (TOYOBO), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Real-time PCR amplification was performed using a LightCycler 480II (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). The PCR cycling conditions included an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 amplification cycles comprising denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s and extension at 60 °C for 30 s. The Ct values of the samples were measured. GAPDH was used as an internal control and the ΔCt method was used for quantification and is presented as fold change. The genes related to cell proliferation, apoptosis, and migration examined here included Hspa5, Eif2ak3, Atf6, Casp12, Ddit3, and Xbp1. Primer sequences were obtained from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China; Table 1).

Detecting ERS and IGF1R/PI3K/AKT pathway changes in condylar chondrocytes after PRP treatment using Western blotting and RNA sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from condylar chondrocytes treated with 10 ng/mL of IL-1β and IL-1β plus 500 µg/mL of PRP for 24 h and subjected to RNA sequencing. Paired-end sequencing data for the samples were obtained using an Illumina platform. The DESeq2 software [26] was used to normalise the gene counts for each sample (using BaseMean values to estimate expression levels), calculate fold changes, and perform differential significance testing using a negative binomial distribution. Differentially expressed protein-coding genes were screened based on fold change and significance testing results. Kyoto Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis was performed on the differentially expressed genes, followed by gene enrichment analysis for the ERS and PI3K/AKT pathways.

Chondrocytes were seeded in a 10-cm cell culture dish and divided into four groups, as described above. Total protein was extracted after co-culture for 24 h. Western blotting detected GRP78, p-PERK, p-IRE, ATF6, CHOP, BAX, IGF1R, PI3K, p-PI3K, AKT, and p-AKT expression levels.

In vivo experiments to investigate the effects of PRP on condylar chondrocytes in rats with TMJ-OA

Ethical approval for this study was granted by the Ethics Committee of the Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine Affiliated Ninth People’s Hospital. The study was conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines, ensuring the rigor and reproducibility of our research, particularly by using randomization and blinding to minimise bias. A TMJ-OA rat model was established using the MIA injection method [5, 27]. MIA induces persistent inflammation and increases inflammation-related cytokine expression, leading to OA-like pain and degenerative changes.

Sample size was determined using PASS software version 15.0.5 (Power Analysis and Sample Size, NCSS, Kaysville, UT, USA). The calculation was based on the pain threshold at one week post-treatment, with a two-sided significance level of α = 0.05 and a power of 1-β = 0.90. Based on a previous study [5] and our preliminary experiments, we estimated the effect size of PRP on TMJ-OA rats to be 3.6 g. Accounting for a maximum anticipated mortality rate of 20%, a final sample size of 9 animals per group was determined, for a total of 27 animals. To minimise the risk of selection bias, we implemented a simple randomization procedure. Animals were randomly allocated to their study groups using a random number generator. The process was conducted by an investigator who remained blinded to the group allocations.

Twenty-seven eight-week-old female SD rats were randomly divided into three groups: control (healthy rats), normal saline (NS), and PRP, with nine rats in each group. All rats underwent general anaesthesia before the TMJ injection. Compound aesthetic (10 mL) was prepared with 0.4 mL of dexmedetomidine hydrochloride (Orion, Finland), 1.6 mL of Zoletil50 (Virbac, France), and 8 mL of NS, with a 0.2 mL/100 g body weight dosage. Rats in the NS and PRP groups were injected with MIA to establish a TMJ-OA model. Treatment commenced two weeks after modelling, with the NS and PRP groups receiving 50 µL of NS and PRP, respectively, through intra-articular injections once weekly, for a total of three injections. Pain behaviour was assessed weekly after treatment initiation, and TMJ samples were collected weekly from three rats per group after cervical dislocation under general anaesthesia.

Rats were housed at 20–25 °C with a 12:12-h light/dark cycle and free access to food and water.

Pain behavioural testing

Pain thresholds were assessed using Von Frey filaments. This method measures the threshold intensity of force that induces head withdrawal (HWT) in rats when applied to the TMJ area [28]. Assessments were conducted weekly.

Histological and immunohistochemical analysis

The TMJ of the rats was fixed, paraffin-embedded, decalcified, and sectioned for histological evaluation and immunohistochemical staining. Haematoxylin and eosin staining was used to assess histological changes in the condylar process and to measure cartilage thickness, whereas Safranin O-fast green and toluidine blue staining were used to evaluate cartilage matrix loss. The Mankin histological scores were used to assess the degenerative conditions of the articular cartilage [29]. Immunohistochemical staining detected representative markers of the PI3K/AKT pathway, including p-PI3K and p-AKT. Immunohistochemical results were semi-quantitatively compared using the immunoreactive score (IRS) [30].

Effects of PRP on condylar chondrocyte ERS and apoptosis under PI3K/AKT blockade and impact of siRNA-mediated Ig1fr knockdown on PI3K/AKT signalling

LY294002 (Cayman Chemical, Ann Arbor, MI, USA) was used to block the PI3K/AKT signalling pathway. The chondrocytes were divided into four groups: normal control, IL (10 ng/mL of IL-1β), PRP (10 ng/mL of IL-1β + 500 µg/mL of PRP), and LY (10 ng/mL of IL-1β + 500 µg/mL of PRP + 10 µM LY294002). Flow cytometry apoptosis was performed as previously described. Gene expression was measured using RT-qPCR. Western blotting was used to detect GRP78, p-PERK, p-IRE, ATF6, CHOP, and BAX protein expression levels.

Igf1r was knocked down using siRNA. Chondrocytes were seeded in a 10-cm culture dish and divided into four groups: normal control (IL; 10 ng/mL of IL-1β), PRP (10 ng/mL of IL-1β + 500 µg/mL of PRP), and siRNA (10 ng/mL of IL-1β + 500 µg/mL of PRP + siRNA). Total protein was extracted as previously described, after co-culturing for 24 h. Western blotting was used to detect PI3K, p-PI3K, AKT, and p-AKT protein expression levels.

Clinical exploration of the efficacy of single and multiple PRP injections for TMJ-OA

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Ninth People’s Hospital, affiliated with the Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine and was registered at the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (registration ID: ChiCTR1900023013). All procedures were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was received from the patients.

Patients aged 18 to 40 years with a pain intensity score of 4 or higher on the Numerical Rating Scale (NRS), diagnosed with degenerative joint disease according to the Diagnostic Criteria for Temporomandibular Disorders [31], who had completed MRI and CBCT examinations before enrollment, and provided signed informed consent were included in this study. Exclusion criteria encompassed patients who had undergone joint lavage or intra-articular injection therapy within the preceding 3 months, those with sepsis, severe thrombocytopenia, platelet dysfunction, infection in the joint area, or a history of prior temporomandibular joint (TMJ) surgery. The criteria also included patients with other conditions that would render them unsuitable for participation, such as neurological diseases, immune system diseases, tumors, or cognitive impairment, as well as those unable to complete the full follow-up.

Thirty-two patients with TMJ-OA who visited the TMJ rehabilitation clinic between May 2019 and May 2021 were enrolled in this study. Sixteen patients received a single PRP injection, whereas the remaining patients received three PRP injections (one per month). Follow-up assessments were conducted 1, 3, and 6 months after treatment. The evaluation parameters included pain intensity (numeric rating scale), maximum active mouth opening, joint sound score [17], jaw functional limitation scale (JFLS) score [32], and cone-beam computed tomography imaging.

Statistical analysis

Different individuals conducted the experiment, outcome assessment, and data analysis, with the latter two blinded to the group assignments. GraphPad Prism version 9 (GraphPad Software, USA) was used for statistical analyses and data visualisation. One-way analysis of variance was used for multiple group comparisons, and the Mann–Whitney U test for two independent samples was used for comparisons between groups. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

PRP alleviates ERS and inhibits apoptosis in inflamed condylar chondrocytes

Transcriptomic sequencing analysis revealed that PRP addition downregulated multiple ERS-related genes, including Hspa5 and Eif2ak3, and endoplasmic reticulum pathway apoptosis-related genes, including Casp12 and Bax (Fig. 1a). RT-PCR confirmed the results of transcriptomic analysis (Fig. 2b). The flow cytometry results for apoptosis showed that IL-1β could induce chondrocyte apoptosis while inducing inflammation, and PRP could reduce apoptosis in inflamed chondrocytes. Moreover, with increasing PRP concentrations, the effect of reducing apoptosis became stronger (Fig. 1c, d). Western blotting showed that IL-1β activated ERS during inflammation induction, whereas PRP reduced ERS, and with increasing PRP concentration, the ERS reduction effect became stronger (Fig. 1e, f).

IGF1R/PI3K/AKT axis is activated when PRP acts on inflamed condylar chondrocytes

KEGG enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes obtained from transcriptomic sequencing revealed activation of the PI3K/AKT signalling pathway, and the heat map showed upregulation of multiple genes in the PI3K/AKT pathway (Fig. 2a, b). Western blotting confirmed that IL-1β-induced inflammation in condylar chondrocytes and downregulated the IGF1R/PI3K/AKT axis, whereas PRP reversed this result, and high concentrations of PRP had a better upregulating effect on the IGF1R/PI3K/AKT axis (Fig. 2c, e). After using siRNA to block the IGF1R, the effect of PRP on PI3K/AKT pathway regulation disappeared, indicating that the effect of PRP on PI3K/AKT pathway upregulation in inflamed condylar chondrocytes depended on IGF1R activation (Fig. 2d, f).

PRP alleviates ERS and inhibits apoptosis in inflamed condylar chondrocytes, depending on the activation of the PI3K/AKT signalling pathway

Flow cytometry analysis indicated a decreased antiapoptotic effect after blocking the PI3K/AKT pathway with LY294002 (Fig. 3a, b). Western blotting showed that the effect of PRP in reducing ERS in inflamed condylar chondrocytes disappeared (Fig. 3c, d). RT-PCR showed that the effect of PRP on the downregulation of ERS-related genes had disappeared (Fig. 3e).

PRP can improve pain behaviour and condylar pathology in rats with TMJ-OA, and the PI3K/AKT pathway is upregulated by PRP in condylar chondrocytes

In the NS injection treatment group, at 1 week (after one injection), the condylar surface exhibited proliferative changes typical of the acute phase of OA, with uneven thickening of the cartilage layer and increased extracellular matrix. Primary pathological changes included cartilage layer thinning and extracellular matrix loss in the second and third weeks (after two to three injections). In contrast, the PRP group consistently exhibited proliferative changes in the cartilage layer over a 1–3-week period, with a relatively smooth condylar surface (Fig. 4a). The heatmap distribution of the Mankin scores demonstrated that the PRP group had better scores than the NS group at 1–3 weeks (Fig. 4b). HWT measurements indicated that the facial pain threshold in the PRP group was consistently higher than that in the NS group 1–3 weeks after injection (Fig. 4c).

Immunohistochemical staining and IRS score results of rat condylar sections demonstrated higher p-PI3K and p-AKT expression in the PRP group than those in the NS group (Fig. 4d–f).

Three PRP injections are more effective than a single injection

The effects of three PRP injections versus a single injection were investigated in 32 patients with TMJ-OA. Age, sex ratio, disease duration, and affected-side distribution showed no significant differences (Table 2). The three-injection group exhibited improved pain intensity and JFLS scores compared to those of the single-injection group at the 6-month follow-up. At the 3- and 6-month follow-ups, the three-injection group exhibited improved joint sound scores compared with those of the single-injection group (Fig. 5). Furthermore, the radiographic improvement rates at the 6-month follow-up were 75 and 84.6% in the single- and multiple-injection groups, respectively. Multiple PRP injections are more beneficial than a single PRP injection for long-term pain and mandibular function improvement, and are more effective in improving joint sounds for more than 3 months.

Discussion

PRP effectively repaired cartilage damage in TMJ-OA, primarily by mitigating ERS in condylar chondrocytes while simultaneously inhibiting apoptosis. PI3K/AKT pathway activation is crucial, and IGF1R is a key target for the effect of PRP on condylar chondrocytes, with PI3K/AKT pathway activation depending on the IGF1R. Animal and clinical trials have also revealed that multiple PRP injections are more beneficial for repairing condylar bone cartilage damage in TMJ-OA, pain relief, and functional improvement.

Excessive and prolonged ERS can lead to chondrocyte apoptosis [8], and CHOP serves as a critical regulator of this process [33]. Consistent with these findings, our results show that PRP significantly reduced the mRNA and protein expression of ERS-related genes, such as PERK and CHOP, in inflamed condylar chondrocytes. This demonstrates that PRP alleviates ERS and inhibits apoptosis in TMJ-OA. Furthermore, our findings align with the work of Yang et al. [34], who reported that the IRE1/mTORC1/PERK pathway coordinates autophagy and apoptosis in osteoarthritic TMJ chondrocytes.

GFs in PRP influence IGF1R-related signalling pathways [35], with IGF-1 directly activating it. A genetic study conducted in 2021 identified IGF1R as a novel genetic signal associated with OA and highlighted its potential as a therapeutic drug target [36]. PI3K/AKT is a crucial signalling pathway that regulates physiological functions, such as cell proliferation, growth, survival, and metabolism [37]. It promotes chondrocyte proliferation and inhibits apoptosis, exerting a protective effect against OA, making it a potential therapeutic target for OA treatment [11]. IGF1R and the PI3K/AKT pathway are closely related. IGF1R, a receptor tyrosine kinase, can be activated by IGF-1 to recruit and activate PI3K, which activates AKT, initiating the PI3K/AKT signalling pathway [38]. Our findings are consistent with this mechanism; we demonstrated that PRP activates the PI3K/AKT pathway and that this activation is dependent on IGF1R. Furthermore, our results indicated that PRP’s cartilage repair effect was significantly attenuated after the PI3K/AKT pathway was blocked, providing direct evidence that this pathway is crucial for PRP’s therapeutic effects.

The PI3K/AKT signalling pathway can inhibit ERS through multiple mechanisms, including the inhibition of IRE1α activity, regulation of Bcl-2-family protein expression, and PTEN expression [39]. A study on the treatment of spinal cord injury with natural medicines also found that the drugs attenuated ERS and inhibited neuronal apoptosis by activating PI3K/AKT [40]. The feasibility of targeting IGF1R/PI3K/AKT to reduce ERS and apoptosis is further supported by a traumatic brain injury study showing that intranasal insulin reduced microglial apoptosis via PI3K/AKT-ERS [41].

We also validated and compared the clinical efficacy of single and triple PRP injections to treat TMJ-OA. Our results indicated that PRP can improve clinical symptoms visible in radiographic imaging results of patients with TMJ-OA, consistent with previous research [17, 18, 42]. In addition, to the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to explore the differences in efficacy between single and multiple PRP injections in TMJ-OA. We found that multiple PRP injections were more effective than a single injection in improving clinical symptoms and imaging outcomes in patients with TMJ-OA. This finding is consistent with previous research on knee OA [22, 23]. However, due to our small sample size, this finding requires further validation in larger-scale clinical trials.

A major limitation of PRP in clinical applications is that it requires autologous platelets, necessitating individual blood samples. This arises from the conditions required for PRP preparation and restricts its use in patients with systemic issues, such as tumours, autoimmune diseases, or blood disorders. Furthermore, regarding our clinical study, the small sample size (n = 16 per group) limits the strength of the conclusions drawn from the comparison of single versus multiple PRP injections. This should be considered a limitation of our study. Therefore, future research should focus on purifying PRP components, such as isolating immunogen-free PRP-derived exosomes (PRP-Exos) [43], and conducting detailed analyses of the protein and microRNA contents of PRP-Exos to identify and isolate the active components. These efforts could significantly expand the therapeutic potential of PRP.

Conclusions

This study provides compelling evidence that PRP effectively repairs cartilage damage in TMJ-OA. Our findings demonstrate that PRP primarily exerts its therapeutic effects by ERS in condylar chondrocytes, thereby inhibiting apoptosis. A critical discovery is the central role of the PI3K/AKT pathway activation, which is dependent on the IGF1R, in mediating the beneficial effects of PRP on condylar chondrocytes. Furthermore, both animal and clinical observations from this study highlight that multiple PRP injections are more advantageous than single injections for repairing condylar bone cartilage damage, alleviating pain, and improving functional outcomes in TMJ-OA.
