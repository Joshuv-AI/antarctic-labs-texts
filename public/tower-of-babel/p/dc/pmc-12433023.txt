Association of stress hyperglycemia ratio with clinical outcomes in patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and type 2 diabetes mellitus in intensive care units: a retrospective study
Authors: Jinzhan Chen, Ayun Chen, Ning Zhang, Zhisheng Chen, Jinxian Huang, Hongni Jiang, Congyi Xie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCo-morbidity of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) corresponds to increasing severity and adverse clinical outcomes. This study intends to investigate the association of stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR) with adverse outcomes in critically ill patients with COPD and T2DM.MethodsCritically ill patients diagnosed with COPD and T2DM from 2008 to 2019 were retrospectively included from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV; version 2.2). They were assigned into three groups based on SHR tertiles, and all-cause in-hospital mortality (ACIHM) and length of intensive care unit (ICU) stay (LOIS) were the primary and secondary outcomes, respectively. The association of SHR with adverse outcomes was explored by Logistic regression, generalized linear regression, and restricted cubic spline (RCS) curve analyses, and its robustness was verified by subgroup analyses.ResultsTotally 1,781 patients with COPD and T2DM were included. Logistic regression analyses revealed that after adjusting for all confounders, the risk of ACIHM increased by 51% in T3 compared with T1 [odds ratio (OR) =1.51; 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.06–2.16]. RCS curve analyses showed a statistically significant linear association between SHR and ACIHM (Pnonlinear=0.58). As shown by generalized linear regression analyses, each unit increase in SHR was associated with a 0.68-day increase in LOIS after adjusting for all confounders (β=0.68; 95% CI: 0.12–1.2). According to subgroup analyses, SHR and ACIHM had no statistically significant interaction except for the marital status subgroup (Pinteraction=0.04).ConclusionsA high SHR is associated with an increase in ACIHM and LOIS in critically ill patients with COPD and T2DM. Assessment of SHR may contribute to better risk stratification and prognostic prediction for such patients.Keywords: Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR), clinical outcome

Highlight box

Key findings

• The stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR) is independently associated with all-cause in-hospital mortality (ACIHM) and length of intensive care unit (ICU) stay (LOIS) in critically ill patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). A 1-unit increase in SHR correlates with a 58% higher risk of ACIHM and an additional 0.68 days in ICU.

What is known and what is new

• Hyperglycemia and glucose variability are established risk factors for poor outcomes in critically ill patients.

• This study adds new evidence showing that SHR is a strong, independent predictor of both ACIHM and LOIS in critically ill COPD and T2DM patients, providing further insights into risk stratification in this population.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• SHR may serve as a practical prognostic marker in ICU settings, helping to identify high-risk patients and guide individualized treatment. Early interventions targeting SHR may improve clinical outcomes. Further prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings.

Introduction

Co-morbidity of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), two common chronic diseases, has gained increasing attention as the global burden of chronic diseases increases recently. Insulin resistance and/or pancreatic β-cell hypofunction leading to persistent elevation of blood glucose is the major pathogenesis of T2DM (1). Globally, approximately 537 million adults suffered from diabetes mellitus (DM) in 2021, and this number is projected to rise to 783 million in 2025 (mostly T2DM), with a global prevalence rate of 12.2% (2). COPD, a respiratory disease featuring persistent airflow limitation, is closely related to long-term smoking and exposure to air pollution, which is the third major cause of death globally (3). DM is a common comorbidity in COPD patients, with a prevalence rate of approximately 15–17% in patients with stable COPD (4). In addition, COPD simultaneously affects about 10% of patients diagnosed with DM (5,6). To sum up, COPD and T2DM interact with each other to increase disease severity and cause adverse clinical outcomes (7,8). Therefore, assessment of the condition of patients with COPD and T2DM is critical for optimizing personalized treatment strategies and predicting clinical outcomes.

Stress hyperglycemia refers to a relative increase in blood glucose levels secondary to inflammation and neurohormonal disorders under stress, which may directly contribute to adverse outcomes by inducing endothelial dysfunction and oxidative stress (9,10). The stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR) is an estimated value of relative blood glucose based on the admission glucose/hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) ratio, with HbA1c as the baseline glucose, which more accurately reflects the acute hyperglycemic state than absolute blood glucose levels (11). Critically ill patients are more susceptible to stress hyperglycemia due to excitation of the sympathetic nervous system, release of multiple cytokines, insulin resistance, and use of glucocorticoids (12). In recent studies, SHR has been identified as a biomarker for high-risk groups for some critical diseases (10,12-15). Acute exacerbation of COPD (AECOPD) is often accompanied by enhanced systemic inflammatory responses, which may induce or worsen stress hyperglycemia (16). Under stressful conditions, significant fluctuations in blood glucose may also occur in DM patients (9,17). The impact of stress hyperglycemia on the prognosis of T2DM or COPD has been investigated (9,13,18), but few studies are available on SHR and its specific impact on clinical outcomes in patients with COPD and T2DM.

This study, using the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV), intends to explore the association of SHR with clinical outcomes [all-cause in-hospital mortality (ACIHM) and length of intensive care unit (ICU) stay (LOIS)] in ICU patients with COPD and T2DM. Considering the interplay of the two diseases, the importance of synergistic management is underscored. The findings of this study may help identify high-risk patients, and provide a scientific basis for developing targeted glycemic control strategies and ameliorating clinical outcomes. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2024-2221/rc).

Methods

Study population

Data sources

MIMIC-IV (version 2.2) (19), a large, publicly accessible dataset containing over 50,000 deidentified ICU patient records from Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC, Boston, MA, USA) between 2008 and 2019. Demographic data, vital signs, test results, and International Classification of Diseases, 9th and 10th Revisions (ICD-9 and ICD-10) encoded diagnoses could be obtained from the MIMIC-IV. Upon completion of the training, one author (C.X.) was granted access to the MIMIC-IV (certification No. 62802839). No informed consent was required since the data in the MIMIC-IV had been de-identified. Ethics Committee approval was waived because of the public and anonymous nature of the MIMIC. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Study population

From the MIMIC-IV, 4984 patients with T2DM (25000, 25002, 25010, 25012, 25020, 25022, 25030, 25032, 25040, 25042, 25050, 25052, 25060, 25062, 25070, 25072, 25080, 25082, 25090, 25092, E11) and COPD (4912, 496, J44) identified by ICD-9 and ICD-10 were included. Patients were included if they had both COPD and T2DM diagnoses recorded, irrespective of whether these were the principal or secondary diagnoses at ICU admission. Patients were divided into three groups based on the SHR tertiles: low SHR group (T1), moderate SHR group (T2), and high SHR group (T3). The low SHR group (T1) was used as the control group. These groups were matched for key clinical characteristics, including age, gender, and comorbidities (COPD and T2DM). The primary distinction between the groups was SHR levels. This division allowed for a focused analysis of the effect of SHR on clinical outcomes while minimizing the impact of other clinical variables. This approach is consistent with other studies that have used SHR tertiles to assess the relationship between hyperglycemia and patient outcomes (10,20). Only the first hospitalization record was considered in patients with multiple hospitalizations. Those patients aged below 18 years at admission (n=0), not admitted to ICU (n=1,987), and with missing data on blood glucose (n=2), HbA1c (n=1,074), and categorical variables (marital status: n=73, race: n=67) were excluded. Finally, 1,781 patients were included and assigned into three groups based on SHR tertiles. The screening procedure for patients is shown in Figure 1.

Data extraction

Demographic data [age, gender, marital status, race, and body mass index (BMI)], vital signs [systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), heart rate (HR), respiratory rate (RR), and peripheral blood oxygen saturation (SpO2)], laboratory indicators [red blood cell (RBC) count, hemoglobin (HGB), white blood cell (WBC) count, platelet (PLT) count, international normalized ratio (INR), total bilirubin (TBIL), albumin (ALB), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine (Cr), creatine kinase (CK), potassium (K), sodium (Na), partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2), partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PaCO2), base excess (BE), lactic acid (Lac), and HbA1c], comorbidities [myocardial infarction (MI), asthma, and pneumonia], therapeutic measures [mechanical ventilation (MV), norepinephrine, and respiratory fluoroquinolones], disease severity scores on admission [Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II), and Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA)], ACIHM, and LOIS were extracted from studies using pgAdmin4 software and Structured Query Language (SQL). The covariates selected for this study were based on their clinical relevance and supported by previous literature demonstrating their association with ICU outcomes (10,12,14,21). To further ensure the appropriateness of the selected covariates and assess potential multicollinearity, we calculated the generalized variance inflation factor (GVIF) for all covariates. The detailed variance inflation factor (VIF) analysis results are provided in Table S1. We included HbA1c as a variable in the study to reflect glucose control at the time of ICU admission. However, MIMIC-IV does not provide detailed management information for COPD and T2DM, limiting our ability to assess the specific impact of treatment strategies for these conditions on ICU outcomes. Respiratory fluoroquinolones were specifically assessed because they are commonly prescribed for the treatment of severe lower respiratory tract infections among ICU patients with COPD, a major population in this study. Additionally, recent studies have reported that fluoroquinolones, particularly moxifloxacin and levofloxacin, can significantly affect glucose metabolism, leading to dysglycemia, which makes their inclusion clinically relevant when analyzing the association between SHR and patient outcomes (22,23). We utilized three disease severity scores upon ICU admission: GCS, SAPS II, and SOFA. These scores were selected because they are commonly used in ICU settings to evaluate the overall severity of illness and organ dysfunction in critically ill patients. Information on comorbidities was extracted using the ICD coding system. Two investigators (H.J. and C.X.) checked the reliability of data extracted.

The SHR was calculated as the ratio of plasma glucose (mg/dL) to a formula involving HbA1c (%): {plasma glucose (mg/dL)/[28.7 × HbA1c (%) − 46.7]} (11). This formula was adapted from previous studies that validated SHR as an indicator of stress hyperglycemia in critically ill patients. The SHR units are dimensionless, as it is a ratio of plasma glucose and HbA1c levels, both of which are standardized clinical measures.

Handling of outliers and missing values

Variables with more than 20% missing values were excluded, and multiple imputation was performed for those with less than 20% missing values. Outliers were winsorized at 1% and 99% levels.

Outcomes

ACIHM and LOIS were the primary and secondary outcomes, respectively.

Statistical analysis

Normally distributed continuous variables were described by mean ± standard deviation and analyzed by t-test. Non-normally distributed continuous variables were described by median (interquartile range) and analyzed by Mann-Whitney U tests or Kruskal-Wallis tests. Categorical variables were presented as n (%) and subjected to χ2 tests or Fisher exact tests. To assess multicollinearity, we calculated the GVIF for all covariates included in our multivariable models. The adjusted GVIF[1/(2 × Df)] values were all below 5, indicating low multicollinearity. The detailed VIF analysis results are provided in Table S1. Variables with a VIF ≥5 were excluded. The association of SHR with ACIHM and LOIS was explored by Logistic regression and generalized linear regression analyses. Trend test analyses were carried out to observe the linear trend between SHR and clinical outcomes. Besides, Model 1 was not adjusted for covariates; Model 2 was adjusted for demographic variables (gender, age, marital status, race, and BMI); Model 3 was further adjusted for vital signs, laboratory indicators, comorbidities, therapeutic measures, and disease severity scores based on Model 2. This stepwise approach of adjusting covariates is a commonly used strategy in clinical research, as it allows for the assessment of the impact of demographic factors (Model 2) before considering the broader clinical factors and disease severity (Model 3). Model 2 provides valuable information about the independent effect of demographic variables (such as age, gender, marital status, race, and BMI) on the outcomes. By first evaluating the demographic factors, this method helps provide a clearer understanding of the individual contributions of different factors to the outcomes, ensuring a more thorough interpretation of the results. In the main analysis, we included individual comorbidities (MI, asthma, pneumonia) as covariates and did not use aggregate indices such as the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) or Elixhauser Comorbidity Index. However, to address reviewer concerns, we conducted a supplementary sensitivity analysis in which we additionally incorporated the CCI alongside the covariates in Model 3 to assess the robustness of our findings (see Table S2). Restricted cubic spline (RCS) curve analyses were conducted to investigate the nonlinear relation between SHR and ACIHM. The consistency in the association between SHR and adverse clinical outcomes across subgroups defined by age, gender, marital status, race, MI, asthma, pneumonia, MV, norepinephrine, and respiratory fluoroquinolones was detected by subgroup analyses to identify their potential interaction. All statistical analyses were conducted by R 4.4.0. A two-sided P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

As shown in Table 1, there were 1,378 (77.4%) cases aged ≥60 years, 962 (54.0%) male cases, 852 (47.8%) married cases, and 1,269 (71.3%) Caucasian cases. Three hundred and thirty-two (18.6%), 529 (29.7%), and 965 (54.2%) cases developed MI, asthma, and pneumonia, respectively. Nine hundred and sixty-one (54.0%), 459 (25.8%), and 239 (13.4%) cases were given MV, norepinephrine, and respiratory fluoroquinolones, respectively. ACIHM was observed in 278 (15.6%) cases, and the median LOIS was 2.66 days. The patients were assigned into a low SHR group, a moderate SHR group, and a high SHR group, with median SHR of 0.67, 0.91, and 1.33, respectively. The three groups were divided according to tertiles of SHR values, with the lowest one-third of subjects assigned to the low SHR group (T1), the middle one-third to the moderate SHR group (T2), and the highest one-third to the high SHR group (T3). The high SHR group had a higher proportion of elderly (≥60 years), male, and Caucasian cases, higher levels of WBC, ALP, BUN, Cr, Lac, SAPS II scores, and ACIHM, longer LOIS, and lower levels of DBP, RBC, HGB, PLT, ALB, and Na than the low SHR group.

Association of SHR with clinical outcomes in patients with COPD and T2DM

The results of logistic regression analyses on the association of SHR with ACIHM in patients with COPD and T2DM are displayed in Table 2. After no covariates were adjusted in Model 1, the risk of ACIHM increased by 79% for each unit increase in SHR as a continuous variable [odds ratio (OR) =1.79; 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.32–2.42; P<0.001], whereas it increased by 60% in the high SHR group (T3) compared with the low SHR group (T1) with increasing SHR as a categorical variable (OR =1.60; 95% CI: 1.18–2.18; P=0.003), with a statistically significant difference (Ptrend=0.002). Similar results were observed after demographic variables were adjusted in Model 2. After adjusting for all confounders in Model 3, each unit increase in SHR was associated with a 58% increase in the risk of ACIHM (OR =1.58; 95% CI: 1.11–2.26; P=0.01), and the risk of ACIHM increased by 51% in the high SHR group (T3) compared with the low SHR group (T1) (OR =1.51; 95% CI: 1.06–2.16; P=0.02) (Ptrend=0.02). Besides, RCS curve analyses showed a statistically significant linear association between SHR and ACIHM after adjusting for all confounders (Poverall=0.03, Pnonlinear=0.58) (Figure 2).

Discussion

This is the first retrospective study exploring the association of SHR with ACIHM and LOIS in critically ill patients with COPD and T2DM. According to the analysis of the medical records of 1,781 patients with COPD and T2DM in the MIMIC-IV, a high SHR was associated with an increase in ACIHM and LOIS. Therefore, assessment of SHR may contribute to better risk stratification and prognostic prediction for such patients.

Findings in previous studies

Due to their simplicity and affordability, admission glucose and HbA1c are routinely assessed in hospitalized patients, and their ratio (SHR) is a better predictor for stress hyperglycemia than any single indicator (11). The association of SHR with adverse clinical outcomes in critically ill patients has been explored, and the ability of SHR to assess mortality in critical illness (24), acute MI (25), acute coronary syndrome (20), sepsis (12), and acute ischemic stroke (26) has been demonstrated. Ding et al. found that SHR is independently associated with all-cause mortality in DM patients in a U-shaped way, and that the HR of all-cause mortality significantly increases under SHR >0.87 after adjusting for potential confounders (9). To sum up, SHR may serve as a prognostic marker for disease progression and mortality in different populations.

COPD and T2DM interact with each other to result in adverse clinical outcomes (7). The impact of blood glucose on adverse outcomes in patients with COPD and T2DM has been explored. For example, Yang et al. found that a large glycemic disparity and modified SHR are associated with the onset of acute respiratory failure in DM patients admitted with AECOPD, and that HbA1c-adjusted glycemic variables (including SHR) can be used as indicators of disease severity in these patients (18). We also noted an association between SHR and adverse clinical outcomes, i.e., both the risk of ACIHM and LOIS increased with increasing SHR as a continuous variable, whether or not confounders were adjusted; an association between a high SHR as a categorical variable and an increased risk of ACIHM could still be observed, whether or not confounders were adjusted, with a linear trend. Although SHR was analyzed as a continuous variable in the regression models for completeness, we acknowledge that the categorical comparison across tertiles (T2 and T3 vs. T1) provides more clinically interpretable results, given the relatively narrow range of SHR values observed in the cohort. In addition, RCS curve analyses were utilized to assess the linear relation between SHR and ACIHM in patients with COPD and T2DM, and a significant linear relation was revealed (Pnonlinear=0.58). These findings carry important implications for clinical practice and patient care. However, another study holds that neither HbA1c nor HbA1c-adjusted glycemic variables can serve as predictors for disease severity or short-term mortality in DM patients admitted with AECOPD (27). Such a discrepancy may be attributed to differences in the study population and sample size: 156 general patients from tertiary referral hospitals in Greece were included in the above study, whereas this paper included 1,781 critically ill patients admitted to ICU from the MMIC-IV.

Mechanism of association between SHR and adverse clinical outcomes

This study suggests that a high SHR is associated with higher ACIHM and longer LOIS in critically ill patients with COPD and T2DM. The association of a large glycemic disparity with an increased risk of developing multiple organ dysfunction syndrome and acute respiratory failure has been verified previously (28). Adverse clinical outcomes resulting from a high SHR may be related to the fact that ICU patients suffer enhanced hepatic gluconeogenesis and insulin resistance due to the activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis and the increased release of steroid hormones, epinephrine, glucagon, and growth hormone, thereby facing a higher risk of stress hyperglycemia (10-12). As has been verified in several experimental and clinical studies, acute hyperglycemia can have a direct effect on acute or chronic inflammation through various mechanisms (29-31). An increase in ACIHM associated with a higher SHR may be attributed to the inflammatory response induced by blood glucose fluctuations. High levels of blood glucose can facilitate the release of pro-inflammatory factors interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and the overproduction of reactive oxygen species, worsening inflammatory responses and oxidative stress. As a result, pancreatic β-cells are damaged and peripheral tissue insulin sensitivity is decreased, ultimately creating a vicious circle (32). Acute fluctuations in blood glucose may trigger endothelial dysfunction, fibrinolysis impairment, and PLT activation, increasing the risk of thrombosis and inducing disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), infection, and cardio-cerebrovascular accidents (33,34). In addition, the immune system is disrupted due to the inhibitory effect of hyperglycemia on T-lymphocyte, immunoglobulin, and complement functions. Hyperglycemia even facilitates glycosylation of pulmonary connective tissue, decline in pulmonary elastic resistance, respiratory muscle weakness, and susceptibility to pathogens, thus increasing the risk of pulmonary infections and prolonging the length of stay (35).

Subgroup analyses

The association of SHR with ACIHM was compared in critically ill patients with COPD and T2DM stratified by various factors. An interaction was found between SHR and marital status, especially in the single and divorced subgroups. Presumably, the susceptibility of single or divorced populations to stress hyperglycemia may be indirectly influenced by higher psychological stress, unhealthy lifestyles, different coping mechanisms, socioeconomic factors, and differences in healthcare access. In contrast, individuals with a stable marriage can achieve better blood glucose management, reducing the risk of stress hyperglycemia. In addition, a significant association of SHR with ACIHM was observed in the MI subgroup, consistent with previous findings (9,17-20,24-36). It is worth noting that SHR displayed a more prominent association with ACIHM in patients using respiratory fluoroquinolones, a class of antibiotics commonly used in respiratory medicine (37). This observation aligns with recent evidence showing that fluoroquinolone use, especially moxifloxacin and levofloxacin, can affect blood glucose regulation and increase the risk of both hypoglycemia and hyperglycemia, particularly in diabetic or critically ill patients (22,23). The possible reason is that fluoroquinolones can interfere with glucose metabolism, thus increasing blood glucose fluctuations in critically ill patients (38,39). It can be inferred that more aggressive glucose monitoring may be warranted in critically ill patients who must use fluoroquinolones. Besides, SHR and LOIS showed no significant interaction in each subgroup, suggesting the robustness of results.

Limitations

This study also had some limitations. First, this single-center retrospective study is insufficient to clarify the causality, so more multicenter prospective studies are required in the future. Second, SHR may also be affected by other factors despite multivariate adjustment and subgroup analyses, leading to potential data bias. Third, the findings should not be generalized indiscriminately to other countries or ICU centers due to the inherent limitations of the MIMIC-IV, and more study populations are still needed to enhance the credibility and representativeness of the findings. Fourth, although HbA1c was used as a measure of glucose control at ICU admission, the MIMIC-IV database lacks detailed information on the management of COPD and T2DM. This represents a significant limitation, as better management of these conditions could substantially impact ICU outcomes. Fifth, the MIMIC-IV database lacks detailed information about the pre-hospital status of patients, such as the control of T2DM or the severity of COPD prior to ICU admission, which could significantly influence ICU outcomes. This lack of data granularity limits our ability to fully assess the effect of disease management on ICU outcomes. Finally, due to limitations in the MIMIC-IV database, we were unable to obtain data on the total hospital stay duration, and our analysis was limited to ICU-specific length of stay. This constraint should be considered when interpreting the results. Future research should aim to integrate more detailed treatment data, including pre-hospital management, to comprehensively evaluate the relationship between disease management and ICU outcomes.

Conclusions

A high SHR is associated with an increase in ACIHM and LOIS in critically ill patients with COPD and T2DM. In high-risk groups, SHR may serve as an important predictor for adverse events and a valuable tool for risk assessment and prognostic management. In the future, multicenter prospective studies can be conducted to elucidate the mechanism linking SHR to adverse clinical outcomes in critically ill patients with COPD and T2DM.
