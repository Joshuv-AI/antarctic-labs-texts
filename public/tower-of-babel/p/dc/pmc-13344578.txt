Successful Treatment of Adult Epstein–Barr Virus‐Associated Hemophagocytic Lymphohistiocytosis With Etoposide Guided by Plasma Epstein–Barr Virus DNA Monitoring: A Case Report
Authors: Kuniaki Maehara, Masuho Saburi, Katsuya Kawano, Takahiro Kusaba, Shogo Urabe, Eiichi Ohtsuka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) is a life‐threatening hyperinflammatory syndrome. Epstein–Barr virus (EBV)‐associated HLH is a major subtype of secondary HLH, requiring prompt diagnosis and treatment. However, treatment is particularly challenging in patients with severe coagulopathy and hepatic dysfunction. A previously healthy 23‐year‐old woman presented with a 2‐week history of high‐grade fever and cervical lymphadenopathy. Cytopenia, elevated lactate dehydrogenase levels, marked transaminase elevations, hyperferritinemia, and disseminated intravascular coagulation were observed. EBV‐associated HLH was diagnosed based on bone marrow hemophagocytosis, an elevated whole blood EBV‐DNA level (6.63 log IU/mL), and serological findings consistent with primary EBV infection. Initial treatment with methylprednisolone and cyclosporine A was followed by weekly oral etoposide, with careful monitoring in the setting of severe hepatic dysfunction. Clinical symptoms and laboratory abnormalities improved within 3 weeks of starting etoposide. Plasma EBV‐DNA became undetectable during treatment, and oral etoposide was discontinued after the third weekly course on Day 18, with a total cumulative dose of 750 mg, corresponding to approximately 450 mg/m2. The patient showed no evidence of relapse at the 6‐month follow‐up, with sustained negativity of plasma EBV‐DNA. This case suggests that timely etoposide‐based therapy may be feasible in severe EBV‐HLH when response and toxicity are carefully monitored.Trial RegistrationThe authors have confirmed clinical trial registration is not needed for this submission.

Introduction

Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) is a life‐threatening hyperinflammatory syndrome characterized by fever, cytopenia, hepatosplenomegaly, hyperferritinemia, and transaminase elevations, resulting from excessive immune activation [1, 2]. HLH is classified into two categories: familial (primary) HLH and secondary HLH. Secondary HLH, which is more common in adults, is frequently triggered by infections, malignancies, or autoimmune disorders and often progresses rapidly with a high mortality rate [2, 3]. The HLH‐94 and HLH‐2004 protocols, consisting of immunosuppressive therapy with etoposide, have been widely used for the treatment of both familial and secondary HLH [4, 5]. Etoposide plays a crucial role in both the induction and maintenance phases of HLH treatment, particularly in Epstein–Barr virus‐associated HLH (EBV‐HLH). However, the management of HLH can be challenging in patients with severe coagulopathy and marked hepatic dysfunction, especially with regard to the feasibility and safety of etoposide administration. A case of EBV‐HLH in a young adult who was successfully treated with early clinical diagnosis and prompt initiation of immunosuppressive therapy with etoposide is presented.

Case Report

A previously healthy 23‐year‐old woman presented to our hospital with a 2‐week history of high‐grade fever and cervical lymphadenopathy. Her general condition deteriorated to an ECOG performance status of 3 due to persistent fever and marked fatigue. On admission (Day 1), complete blood cell counts showed cytopenia with a white blood cell count of 2940/µL (including 3% atypical lymphocytes), hemoglobin of 15.4 g/dL, and a platelet count of 37,000/µL. Laboratory evaluation demonstrated elevated liver enzyme levels (alanine aminotransferase 280 U/L, γ‐glutamyl transpeptidase 372 U/L), an elevated total bilirubin level of 2.6 mg/dL, marked hyperferritinemia (25,062 ng/mL), and elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH; 2144 U/L), soluble interleukin‐2 receptor (34,328 U/mL), and C‐reactive protein (7.1 mg/dL). Coagulation studies showed severe coagulopathy, with a prothrombin time‐international normalized ratio of 1.44, activated partial thromboplastin time of 71.4 s, fibrinogen of 123 mg/dL, and fibrin/fibrinogen degradation products of 24.1 µg/mL, consistent with disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Based on these findings, HLH was strongly suspected. Computed tomography showed hepatosplenomegaly and nonspecific lymphadenopathy involving the cervical, supraclavicular, axillary, and para‐aortic regions. Bone marrow aspiration demonstrated increased atypical lymphocytes (17.4%) and macrophages (4%) with evidence of hemophagocytosis on May–Giemsa staining (Figure 1). Hematoxylin and eosin staining showed increased CD8‐positive T‐lymphocytes and CD68‐positive macrophages exhibiting hemophagocytosis (Figure 2). Flow cytometric analysis of bone marrow showed an expanded population of T‐cells positive for CD2, CD3, CD7, CD8, and T‐cell receptor (TCR)‐αβ, and negative for CD4 and TCR‐γδ (Figure 3). Chromosomal analysis showed a normal karyotype. The patient fulfilled the HLH‐2004 diagnostic criteria [4]. The EBV‐DNA level in whole blood was markedly elevated at 6.63 log IU/mL. Serological testing was consistent with primary EBV infection (viral capsid antigen [VCA]‐IgM 1:10, VCA‐IgG 1:80, EBV nuclear antigen < 1:10). Southern blot analysis for EBV‐DNA in peripheral blood was inconclusive due to poor sample quality, and clonality could not be demonstrated. Screening for other viral infections, including cytomegalovirus and varicella‐zoster virus, as well as autoimmune diseases (antinuclear antibodies, anti‐double‐stranded DNA antibodies, and myeloperoxidase anti‐neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies), yielded negative results. Based on these findings, the patient was diagnosed with EBV‐HLH.

The patient's clinical course is shown in Figure 4. Treatment was initiated with methylprednisolone (1 g/day for 3 days, followed by tapering) on Day 1, and oral cyclosporine A (6 mg/kg/day) was added on Day 2. Although the patient became afebrile by Day 3, her fibrinogen level decreased to 32 mg/dL due to DIC. From Day 3 to 14, she required frequent transfusions of red cells, platelets, and fresh frozen plasma, along with a 6‐day course of recombinant human thrombomodulin. On Day 4, serum ferritin and LDH levels increased further to 100,320 ng/mL and 5439 U/L, respectively, accompanied by severe cytopenia (white blood cell count of 990/µL, hemoglobin of 11.0 g/dL, and platelet count of 24,000/µL). Given the progressive disease activity in the setting of marked liver dysfunction (total bilirubin 5.2 mg/dL, alanine aminotransferase 440 U/L, γ‐glutamyl transpeptidase 649 U/L), oral etoposide was initially administered on Day 4 at 175 mg, corresponding to approximately 100 mg/m2 based on a body surface area of 1.61 m2, to assess tolerability. On Day 5, serum LDH (4537 U/L), alanine aminotransferase (377 U/L), and ferritin (73,600 ng/mL) levels decreased, and an additional dose of etoposide (75 mg, approximately 50 mg/m2) was administered, resulting in a total dose of approximately 150 mg/m2 over two consecutive days. Oral etoposide was subsequently administered weekly at 250 mg, corresponding to approximately 150 mg/m2. Total bilirubin peaked at 12.6 mg/dL on Day 9 and subsequently decreased. Serum LDH and ferritin levels peaked on Day 4 and decreased gradually. Her general condition improved markedly in parallel with laboratory improvement. Plasma EBV‐DNA became undetectable by Day 15. Oral etoposide was discontinued after the third weekly course on Day 18, with a total cumulative dose of 750 mg, corresponding to approximately 450 mg/m2. Her platelet count recovered to 206,000/µL by Day 22, and white blood cell counts and liver enzyme abnormalities normalized by Day 26. The patient was discharged on Day 27. Plasma EBV‐DNA remained undetectable on follow‐up evaluations on Days 32, 50, and 120. Cyclosporine A and prednisolone were gradually tapered and discontinued on Day 148. Six months after disease onset, the patient showed no evidence of relapse.

Discussion

This case illustrates a young adult with EBV‐HLH complicated by rapidly progressive DIC and severe hepatic dysfunction, who was successfully treated through early diagnosis and prompt initiation of immunosuppressive therapy with etoposide. Notably, serial monitoring of plasma EBV‐DNA levels played a pivotal role in assessing treatment response and guiding the duration of etoposide therapy, thereby minimizing cumulative drug exposure.

Recent reviews have emphasized that secondary HLH should be regarded as a heterogeneous hyperinflammatory syndrome rather than a single disease entity, and that management should be individualized according to the underlying etiology and disease severity [2, 6, 7]. Owing to the rarity of HLH, delays in diagnosis and treatment are common in adults and are associated with poor clinical outcomes. Previous studies have demonstrated that early etoposide administration improves survival, even in adults [7, 8]. In the present case, prompt initiation of treatment in parallel with comprehensive diagnostic evaluations may have contributed to a good outcome without progression to irreversible organ failure. However, etoposide administration can be challenging in cases with severe coagulopathy or hepatic dysfunction due to concerns regarding etoposide‐related toxicity. In HLH, hepatic dysfunction may reflect severe hyperinflammatory disease activity itself, rather than impaired drug tolerance alone. Therefore, excessive reduction or delay of etoposide may compromise disease control, particularly in rapidly progressive EBV‐HLH. In the present case, oral etoposide was initially administered at approximately 100 mg/m2 on Day 4 to assess tolerability and early treatment response. After this initial administration, hepatic dysfunction showed no further deterioration, and decreases in serum LDH and ferritin levels were observed the following day. These findings suggested that the hepatic dysfunction was mainly associated with active HLH. Therefore, an additional dose of approximately 50 mg/m2 was administered on Day 5, resulting in a total dose of approximately 150 mg/m2 over two consecutive days. This cautious approach allowed etoposide to be administered in a patient with severe, life‐threatening HLH while balancing the need for disease control against concerns regarding treatment‐related toxicity. Although this dose was approximately equivalent to the standard etoposide dose used in the HLH‐2004 protocol, the route of administration differed from the standard intravenous regimen. Because oral etoposide has lower and variable bioavailability than intravenous etoposide, systemic exposure after oral administration may be lower and less predictable than that achieved with the same nominal intravenous dose [9]. In our clinical setting, oral etoposide was immediately available and was selected as a practical option to avoid delaying etoposide‐based therapy in a patient with rapidly progressive HLH. Intravenous etoposide remains the standard route of administration in HLH‐2004‐based treatment. Nevertheless, the use of oral etoposide may be considered in selected clinical situations, such as when prompt initiation of intravenous therapy is not feasible, provided that treatment response and toxicity can be carefully monitored.

Recent studies have highlighted the clinical utility of plasma EBV‐DNA quantification as a surrogate marker of disease activity and treatment response in EBV‐HLH. In a recent retrospective pediatric cohort study, 7 of 20 patients achieved sustained negativity of plasma EBV‐DNA during treatment, which was associated with a good prognosis. In contrast, sustained elevation of EBV‐DNA levels during therapy has been associated with refractory disease [10]. In the present case, plasma EBV‐DNA became undetectable on Day 15 of treatment and remained undetectable, in parallel with the improvement of systemic inflammation and organ dysfunction. Based on virological and clinical improvement, oral etoposide was discontinued after the third weekly course, resulting in a cumulative dose of approximately 450 mg/m2, substantially lower than that typically recommended in standard protocols [4, 5]. Although etoposide‐based therapy remains the standard approach, its use in adult secondary HLH requires careful consideration because of potential toxicity [6, 7]. Reducing cumulative exposure to etoposide is particularly important in young patients because of the risk of long‐term adverse effects, including therapy‐related myeloid neoplasms [11, 12]. Although fixed‐duration therapy remains the standard approach, this case suggests that treatment intensity and duration might be adjusted according to virological and clinical responses, provided that careful monitoring is ensured. Importantly, results from the pediatric cohort also suggest that re‐elevation of plasma EBV‐DNA levels does not necessarily indicate disease relapse in all cases, and that management decisions should be guided by careful assessments of the overall clinical manifestations, rather than EBV‐DNA levels alone [10].

Assessment of the cellular compartment harboring EBV is also important in EBV‐HLH. Although EBV infection is classically associated with B cells, EBV‐HLH may involve EBV‐infected T‐cell or NK‐cell populations [13]. In the present case, flow cytometric analysis showed an expanded CD8‐positive T‐cell population in the bone marrow, suggesting a possible association with T‐cell activation. However, the EBV‐infected cellular compartment could not be directly identified because cell‐sorted EBV‐DNA analysis and EBER in situ hybridization combined with immunophenotyping were not performed. In young patients with EBV‐HLH, evaluation for primary or familial HLH should also be considered in addition to assessment of the EBV‐infected cellular compartment, and such evaluation generally requires genetic testing for familial HLH‐associated genes as well as functional assays of cytotoxicity [7]. In the present case, EBV‐HLH was clinically diagnosed based on serological findings consistent with primary EBV infection and the overall clinical presentation. However, because the patient was a young adult, an underlying primary HLH or inherited immune dysregulation could not be completely excluded, and further evaluation for primary HLH would have been desirable.

This case report has several limitations. First, the duration of follow‐up was relatively short. Second, clonality assessment was not performed, and, therefore, the presence of an underlying clonal lymphoproliferative disorder could not be definitively excluded. In addition, the EBV‐infected cellular compartment was not directly identified. Third, a detailed evaluation for primary HLH was not performed. Therefore, adult‐onset primary HLH or underlying inherited immune dysregulation could not be completely excluded. Finally, most of the available evidence regarding EBV‐DNA‐guided management is derived from pediatric cohorts, and its applicability to adult patients remains to be fully elucidated.

In conclusion, this case highlights the importance of prompt diagnosis and timely initiation of etoposide‐based therapy in EBV‐HLH with life‐threatening conditions, including severe coagulopathy and hepatic dysfunction. In addition, serial monitoring of plasma EBV‐DNA levels may be useful for assessing treatment response and for optimizing the duration of therapy, potentially allowing reduction in cumulative drug exposure. Further studies in adult EBV‐HLH are required to clarify the role of response‐guided treatment strategies.
