Integrating AlphaFold2 with physics-based ensemble docking for high-efficiency nanobody discovery
Authors: Yinghao Guo, Renfang Guan, Lunde Jin, Jiayu Zhang, Wei Wu, Rui Sun, Bei Li, Ruireng Cao, Hang Jia, Lulu Liu, Wenjing Xing, Jian Ma, Bin Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nanobodies, the single-domain counterparts of traditional antibodies, are approximately one-tenth the size yet retain the ability to bind their target antigens tightly and specifically. A major practical bottleneck in developing functional nanobodies is the panning process required to identify them from the vast immunized cDNA libraries derived from camelids. To overcome this bottleneck, we developed a computational framework that progressed from next-generation sequencing (NGS)-derived candidate nanobody sequences to predicted structures using AlphaFold2, and prioritized nanobodies based on predicted binding energy scores. The nanobody-antigen binding poses were predicted using an ensemble docking strategy, which was selected over single-structure docking to better account for antigen conformational flexibility. We demonstrated that a physics-based docking method followed by MM/GBSA re-scoring delivered favorable performance in recovering native nanobody-antigen binding poses, outperforming sequence-only AlphaFold3 in our preliminary benchmark test. Applied to three antigen systems—Mesothelin (MSLN), PD-1, and Nectin-4—our computational workflow successfully prioritized candidate nanobodies. At least seven out of ten (70%) of the top-ranked candidates for each target exhibited strong binding by flow cytometry, with ELISA and surface plasmon resonance (SPR) further confirming nanomolar-level binding for representative candidates. Additionally, compared with conventional random-selection-based monoclonal clone picking, our workflow improved hit recovery while reducing redundancy, enabling the identification of functional nanobodies across a broader range of NGS copy-number ranks rather than only the most abundant post-panning clones. These results support the practical utility of the framework for enriching functional nanobodies from experimentally pre-enriched NGS-derived pools.

Introduction

Nanobodies, also known as VHH antibodies, are the variable domains of heavy-chain-only antibodies naturally occurring in camelids. With a molecular weight of approximately 15 kDa, which is about one-tenth that of a conventional IgG, nanobodies are the smallest known functional antigen-binding fragments, yet they maintain high stability, solubility, and specific target recognition.1 These unique biophysical properties, coupled with their ease of recombinant production and superior tissue penetration, have established nanobodies as powerful tools in diagnostics, molecular imaging, and targeted therapeutics.2 Despite their considerable promise, the rapid and efficient generation of target-specific nanobodies remains a significant technical hurdle. The conventional discovery pipeline is labor-intensive and time-consuming.3,4 It typically begins with the construction of a large cDNA library from immunized animals, with library diversity often reaching 109–1010 individual clones.5 High-affinity binders are then retrieved from this vast pool through iterative rounds of in vitro display technologies, such as phage display.4,6 While effective, this empirical panning process is stochastic, often biased toward highly enriched clones, and may yield a limited number of unique sequences after weeks of work, making the overall discovery cycle typically span a year or more.3 Consequently, there is a pressing need for strategies that can accelerate this bottleneck by enriching functional and diverse candidates prior to costly experimental validation.

Computational methods offer a compelling avenue to guide and accelerate nanobody screening. Recent advances in machine learning have led to sequence-based models that predict nanobody-antigen binding from embeddings or k-mer profiles,7 as well as hybrid tools that combine Rosetta energy functions with classifiers.8 In parallel, computational protein-design strategies have been increasingly applied to antibody and protein engineering. For example, integrated computational pipelines have been developed to design high-affinity nanobodies with expanded genetic codes,9 and comprehensive protein-design protocols have been used to identify hotspot residues, resistance mutations, and adaptive signatures in pathogen proteins.10 These studies highlight the growing potential of computational approaches for engineering binding proteins and predicting functionally important residues. However, many sequence-based or classifier-driven approaches remain partly black boxes in nature, lacking mechanistic interpretability and struggling to generalize to novel antigen systems. Conversely, the revolution in protein structure prediction via AlphaFold2 (AF2) now provides reliable, de novo models of nanobodies and their targets,11 opening the door to structure-based biophysical screening. However, reliably modeling the critical CDR3 loops and performing accurate nanobody-antigen docking remain challenging, as highlighted by recent benchmarks.12,13 A generalizable, physics-informed framework that integrates high-quality structure prediction with binding-energy estimation to prioritize candidates from experimentally enriched NGS-derived repertoires is still lacking.

To bridge this gap, we developed a comprehensive computational pipeline that synergizes AF2-based structural modeling with physics-based docking and scoring to efficiently prioritize nanobody candidates from next-generation sequencing (NGS) data Fig. 1. Our method addresses key limitations of prior approaches by implementing an ensemble docking strategy to account for antigen flexibility. In a re-docking benchmark, physics-based docking followed by MM/GBSA re-scoring showed favorable performance in recovering native nanobody-antigen binding poses. Under the sequence-only AlphaFold3 setting tested here,14 this structure-based docking workflow was more suitable for downstream pose generation and re-scoring. This optimized framework enables the direct ranking of hundreds of candidates from an immune library by their predicted binding free energy.

Productive nanobody generation from immune libraries is often bottlenecked by screening, which seeks to identify rare functional nanobodies from cDNA libraries containing 106 to 108 clones. In this work, we present a computational framework that greatly accelerates candidate selection. This framework first identifies nanobody amino acid sequences via NGS and converts candidate sequences into structures using AlphaFold2 (ref. 11). The binding affinities of candidate nanobodies to their cognate antigens are then computed and ranked via a customized docking strategy. This strategy utilizes the physics-based HDOCK18 to obtain nanobody-antigen binding poses, while incorporating an ensemble docking approach to account for antigen conformational flexibility. Finally, MM/GBSA-calculated binding free energies are used to re-rank binding poses and prioritize candidates for experimental validation. We demonstrate that this computational workflow can identify functional nanobodies within 10 days with a ∼70% success rate across three independent systems, significantly outperforming traditional random picking strategies in terms of both speed and sequence diversity.

We validated this pipeline on three therapeutically relevant antigen systems: Mesothelin (MSLN),15 PD-1 (ref. 16), and Nectin-4 (ref. 17). The top 10 computationally ranked candidates for each antigen were experimentally tested, yielding at least 7 out of 10 candidates for each target showing target-dependent binding by flow cytometry. This is a substantial improvement over random selection or traditional panning. This work establishes a robust, generalizable, and mechanistically interpretable strategy that leverages advanced structure prediction and physics-based simulation to transform the efficiency and rationality of nanobody discovery.

Methods

Next-generation sequencing (NGS) and sequence filtering

For all three antigen systems, three rounds of phage-display panning were performed before sequencing. No sequencing was performed after rounds 1 or 2. NGS sequencing was specifically performed on the round-3 enriched output pools. The enriched round output pools were subjected to a single round of Illumina paired-end amplicon sequencing after PCR amplification of the VHH-encoding inserts using flanking primers and KAPA HiFi HotStart ReadyMix. Raw base-calling data were converted into FASTQ files using the Illumina base-calling/demultiplexing pipeline, and raw pass-filter reads were summarized for each library. After length filtering, complete VHH sequences were collapsed into unique full-length clonotypes and further pre-filtered using the copy number ≥ 100 criteria. Before filtering, the three antigen systems (MSLN, PD-1, and Nectin-4) have 519 688, 325 849, and 252 345 unique VHH sequences, respectively, and filtering to retain only sequences with copy number ≥ 100 yields 365, 433, and 254 sequences that were then subjected to our computational pipeline (see summary in Table S1). Because the unfiltered NGS output pools contained a large number of unique VHH sequences, we applied this copy-number cutoff of ≥100 to define a computationally tractable candidate set for structural modeling, docking, and MM/GBSA re-scoring. This threshold was chosen as a practical balance between retaining sequence diversity and maintaining feasible computational cost, rather than as a definitive biological boundary distinguishing binders from non-binders. Cutoff-sensitivity analysis showed that lowering the threshold markedly increased the number of retained unique sequences and would therefore substantially expand the downstream computational workload (Table S2). We acknowledge that lower-frequency sequences may also contain true binders, and future implementations could combine abundance filtering with CDR3- or full-length VHH-based clustering to select representative candidates from lower-abundance sequence families.

Nanobody structure prediction using AlphaFold2

AlphaFold2 (ref. 11) was locally deployed following the installation procedures provided in the public repository https://github.com/kalininalab/alphafold_non_docker. Starting from the NGS derived sequence pool that contains candidate nanobodies whose copy numbers are ≥100, protein sequences were extracted and subjected to structure prediction. For each sequence, AlphaFold2 generated five structural models, and the top-ranked model was used for downstream analysis. Nanobodies are small and also feature a highly conserved fold; therefore, the AF2 predicted nanobody structures are usually with high confidence, as has been noted by other AF2-based nanobody structure prediction.19 To illustrate this conserved structural basis, we added a schematic showing the characteristic framework and CDR organization of nanobodies, prepared based on previously described features of camelid antibodies and nanobodies20 (Fig. S1A). Consistently, we showed in Fig. S1B that >95% of the predicted nanobodies have per-residue pLDDT score >90, demonstrating the high structural quality of the nanobody structures predicted by AF2. Under the hardware configuration used in this study, AlphaFold2 modeling required approximately 15 min per VHH candidate. The corresponding computational cost is summarized in Table S3.

Antigen structure prediction via AF2 plus molecular dynamics (MD) simulations

Unlike nanobodies, antigen structures are usually more heterogeneous, and may contain intrinsic structural flexibility. For antigen structure preparation, the initial structure was predicted via AF2 from amino acid sequence, and then subjected to 3 × 1 µs atomistic MD simulations for conformational refinement and sampling. For the MD simulations, the structure was described by the Amber FF19SB force field21 and solvated in an OPC water box22 with a 10 Å buffer. NaCl ions were added into the system to neutralize system's charge and maintain 0.15 M physiological ion concentration, and the Joung/Cheatham monovalent ion parameters were used for ions.23 The system was first energy minimized by steepest descent algorithm for 200 steps followed by conjugate gradient algorithm for 9800 steps. It was then heated from 0 to 100 K in the NVT ensemble over 800 ps and from 100 to 300 K in the NPT ensemble over 2 ns. During the heating stage, the backbone of the protein was restrained by a harmonic constraint with a force constant of 5 kcal mol−1 Å−2. A 1 ns equilibrium simulation with the force constant reduced to 1 kcal mol−1 Å−2 was then performed to further relax the system. The equilibrated system was used to initiate three independent 1 µs production runs. As shown in Fig. S2A, S5A and F, the antigen structures sampled stable conformational states during the simulation course, with backbone RMSDs plateauing after approximately 1 µs. During the simulations, the SHAKE algorithm24 was used to restrain the lengths of bonds involving hydrogen atoms. The hydrogen mass repartition operation,25 which redistributes the masses of heavy atoms to their bonded hydrogens, was performed to enable a 4 fs time step. The Langevin thermostat was used during the simulations and the equilibration and production runs were performed in the NPT ensemble at 300 K. The non-bonded interaction cutoff was 10 Å. All simulations were performed using the Amber22 package.26

To obtain representative antigen conformations, hierarchical agglomerative clustering was performed using CPPTRAJ,27 based on backbone heavy-atom RMSD metric. A total of 10 clusters were generated for each antigen system, and representative structures from the first three clusters were taken for downstream ensemble docking with candidate nanobodies. This incorporated antigen conformational flexibility while focusing on the dominant conformational states sampled in the equilibrated trajectories. Under our computational setup, antigen MD simulation required approximately 80 h per 1 µs simulation. Detailed runtime information is provided in Table S3.

Docking of predicted nanobody structures to antigen

After preparing the structures of candidate nanobodies and their cognate antigen, docking was performed to obtain their binding poses. Prior to docking our candidate nanobodies, we first performed a re-docking experiment using reported nanobody-antigen co-crystal structures from the SAbDab database28 to benchmark the performance of various docking methods.

We firstly analyzed the full-length VHH sequence lengths and CDR3 lengths of the 880 SAbDab VHH co-crystal structures. The benchmark dataset showed a broad distribution of CDR3 lengths, ranging from 3 to 26 amino acids, with a median length of 15 amino acids. Full-length VHH sequence lengths were also broadly distributed across the benchmark set. Notably, the tested MSLN VHHs in this study fell within the length-distribution range of the SAbDab-derived benchmark VHHs at both the full-length VHH and CDR3 levels (Fig. S1C and D). To further characterize benchmark sequence features beyond length distribution, we compared amino acid frequencies at both the full-length VHH and CDR3 levels between the SAbDab-derived benchmark VHHs and the tested MSLN VHHs. The two datasets showed broadly comparable amino acid usage profiles in both CDR3 regions and full-length VHH sequences (Fig. S1E and F). We further generated sequence logos using WebLogo29 to compare position-wise conservation patterns in the SAbDab-derived benchmark VHHs and the tested MSLN VHHs. Both datasets showed conserved framework-region features characteristic of VHH domains, while differences were observed in the CDR regions, particularly CDR1 (Fig. S1G and H). Together, these analyses provide additional characterization of the sequence features of the SAbDab-derived benchmark VHHs and the tested MSLN VHHs.

We selected representative physics-based docking tools such as HDOCK,18 ZDOCK,30 and ClusPro,31 as well as the machine-learning-based AlphaFold3-multimer14 for the re-docking benchmark on the SAbDab database. Among these, ClusPro was used via its online server, while the others were deployed locally. For HDOCK, ZDOCK, and ClusPro, docking parameters were set to their default values, and the top 10 predicted binding poses for each nanobody-antigen pair were considered. This choice was intended to ensure standardized and reproducible evaluation of these tools. Since our primary goal in benchmarking these docking tools was to select the established tools for practical use, rather than to develop a more accurate docking program for nanobody-antigen docking, we did not explicitly consider the potential bias brought by the relatively small size of VHHs and their long CDR3 loops. Future work may further improve nanobody-antigen docking by incorporating CDR-guided restraints, flexible CDR3 loop refinement, or ensemble representations of CDR conformations.

In the re-docking experiment, we also re-ranked the top 10 binding poses using MM/GBSA-based binding free energy calculations. For MM/GBSA analysis, each binding pose was first energy-minimized with Amber22 and then used as input for the MMPBSA.py script from Amber22. The Generalized Born model proposed by Onufriev et al.32 was applied, with the ionic strength set to 0.15 M.

After identifying that the HDOCK + MM/GBSA re-scoring strategy performed optimally in recovering the nanobody-antigen co-crystal structures from SAbDab, we applied this strategy to prioritize candidate nanobodies from our NGS-derived repertoire. Notably, the SAbDab benchmark was performed using experimentally resolved VHH-antigen complexes and was primarily designed to evaluate docking pose recovery. Although the favorable benchmark performance supports the rationale of using HDOCK within our workflow, the extension of these results to actual nanobody discovery settings represents an inference rather than a direct demonstration. To account for potential structural flexibility of the antigen, we tested docking against a single AF2-predicted antigen structure as well as against three representative antigen structures sampled by MD simulations (ensemble docking). Each candidate nanobody was docked against each antigen structure to produce 10 ranked binding poses using the HDOCK + MM/GBSA re-scoring strategy.

For each antigen conformation, the three docking poses with the most favorable MM/GBSA binding energies were selected after re-scoring, and their average MM/GBSA value was used as the conformation-specific binding energy. The final score for each nanobody was obtained by averaging the conformation-specific binding energies across the three MD-derived antigen conformations. Nanobody candidates were ranked according to this ensemble-averaged MM/GBSA binding energy, with more negative values indicating more favorable predicted binding. The top 10 candidate nanobodies were selected for flow cytometry assays to verify functional binding to the antigen. We demonstrated that using ensemble antigen structures outperforms the use of a single-structure in identifying functional nanobodies (see Results, Section 3.2). Therefore, we have standardized ensemble docking for incorporating antigen conformational flexibility and the “HDOCK + MM/GBSA” approach for obtaining accurate nanobody-antigen binding poses within our computational framework. In our implementation, ensemble docking required approximately 12 min per VHH candidate, and MM/GBSA re-ranking required approximately 6 min per VHH candidate. These computational requirements are summarized in Table S3. All calculations were performed on a local workstation equipped with an Intel Core i7-13700KF CPU and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4080 GPU. The code for our integrated pipeline is available at a public GitHub repository: https://github.com/bsu233/bslab/tree/main/VHH-Screening.

Construction of cell lines overexpressing antigens

Antigen-overexpressing cell lines were generated using a lentiviral transduction system. Briefly, a lentiviral expression plasmid encoding the target antigen, together with the packaging plasmid gag–pol, and the envelope plasmid VSV-G, were co-transfected into HEK 293T cells using a standard transfection protocol. The culture medium was replaced after transfection, and viral supernatants were collected 48 h later. The harvested supernatants were clarified by centrifugation and filtration, and subsequently used to transduce naïve HEK 293T cells. Transduced cells were subjected to puromycin selection to establish stable cell lines. In parallel, a lentiviral empty vector lacking the antigen insert was processed and used for transduction under identical conditions to generate vector-only control cells. Antigen overexpression and cell surface localization were confirmed by flow cytometry.

Antigen (MSLN) protein expression

The MSLN coding sequence was inserted into an in-house mammalian expression vector. Endotoxin-free plasmid DNA was prepared and transiently transfected into suspension-adapted HEK 293 cells according to the manufacturer's protocol. After seven days of incubation, culture supernatants were harvested, and Fc-fusion proteins were purified by Protein A affinity chromatography (Cytiva). Bound proteins were eluted using a descending pH gradient of glycine buffer (pH 3.5–3.0) and immediately neutralized with 1 M Tris-Cl (pH 9.0) to maintain protein stability. Neutralized fractions were concentrated and buffer-exchanged into PBS (pH 7.4) using 10 kDa molecular weight cutoff ultrafiltration units (Merck). Protein integrity and purity were assessed by SDS-PAGE.

Expression and purification of nanobodies

All nanobodies were expressed using a prokaryotic system. Coding sequences were cloned into the pET-22b expression vector (Comatebio, Changchun, China) by homologous recombination, with each construct including a C-terminal 6× His tag for affinity purification. Verified plasmids were transformed into E. coli Rosetta competent cells (Biomed, Beijing, China), and recombinant proteins were produced under standard induction conditions.

Flow cytometry

Antigen-overexpressing cells and matched vector-control cells were harvested and resuspended in staining buffer to generate a single-cell suspension. For each sample, 1 × 106 cells were aliquoted into individual tubes and incubated with 5 µg purified His-tagged nanobody, corresponding to an approximate final concentration of 100 nM, for 1 h at 4 °C in the dark. Cells were then pelleted by centrifugation and washed once to remove unbound nanobody. Subsequently, an APC-conjugated anti-His secondary antibody (BioLegend) was added, followed by incubation for 1 h at 4 °C in the dark. After washing once and resuspending in staining buffer, samples were acquired and analyzed on a BD flow cytometer according to the manufacturer's instructions. A computationally prioritized candidate was defined as flow-positive when it produced a complete or near-complete positive shift on antigen-overexpressing cells at approximately 100 nM while showing no detectable or only background-level binding to the matched vector-control cells. This paired-cell design provided a target-dependent specificity control under the same cellular background and staining conditions. We note that this flow-cytometry-based criterion represents an operational hit definition for cell-surface binding and does not correspond to a kinetic KD threshold.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

High binding 96-well plates (NEST) were coated with recombinant MSLN (0.2 µg per well) and incubated overnight at 4 °C. After removal of the coating solution, plates were washed six times with PBST (PBS containing 0.05% Tween-20) and blocked with 3% BSA for 2 h at 37 °C. Nanobody samples were prepared at an initial concentration of 1 µM and serially diluted tenfold in PBST. A volume of 100 µL per well was added and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. Plates were washed six times and incubated with 50 µL per well of HRP-conjugated 6*His, His-Tag Monoclonal antibody diluted 1 : 10 000, for 2 h at 37 °C. Following six additional washes, 100 µL of TMB substrate (Thermo Fisher) was added per well and developed for 5 min before quenching with 50 µL of ELISA stop solution (BBI Life Sciences). Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10 to determine minimal binding concentration, endpoint titer and Half Maximal Effective Concentration (EC50).

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR)

Binding kinetics between the MSLN protein and nanobodies were analyzed at 37 °C using a Biacore 8K surface plasmon resonance system (Cytiva). All measurements were carried out in PBST buffer (2 mM KH2PO4, 8 mM Na2HPO4, 136 mM NaCl, 2.6 mM KCl, 0.05% Tween-20, pH 7.4) with a Series S Sensor Chip CM5. The MSLN was covalently immobilized on the chip in 10 mM acetate buffer (pH 5.0) using standard amine-coupling chemistry. Serial dilutions of nanobodies were then injected over the chip surface, while a reference channel without immobilized antigen served as a blank control. After each cycle, the sensor surface was regenerated with 10 mM glycine (pH 2.0) to remove bound analyte and restore baseline response. Association and dissociation phases were globally fitted using a 1 : 1 langmuir binding model, and kinetic constants (ka, kd) were derived from the concentration-dependent responses. The equilibrium dissociation constant (KD = kd/ka) was calculated to quantify binding affinity, where smaller KD values indicate stronger antigen–antibody interactions.

Identification of positive nanobody clones by phage ELISA

After the third round of bio-panning, 96 individual colonies were randomly picked and inoculated into 96-deep-well plates (NEST) containing 2×YT medium supplemented with carbenicillin. Cultures were superinfected with M13K07 helper phage, followed by kanamycin selection, and incubated overnight at 37 °C with shaking to produce phage particles displaying nanobodies. Cells were removed by centrifugation, and phage-containing supernatants were harvested. For phage ELISA, supernatants were diluted 1 : 1 (v/v) with 3% (w/v) BSA in PBS and applied directly as the primary antibody. After the standard ELISA procedure and color development, absorbance at 450 nm was recorded using a microplate reader. Data were processed in GraphPad Prism 10. Clones were defined as positive when the OD450 values obtained in recombinant antigen-coated wells were ≥1.5. Positive clones were subjected to Sanger sequencing, which identified three unique nanobody sequences.

Results and discussion

Physics-based docking with MM/GBSA re-scoring is the optimal strategy for recovering native nanobody-antigen binding poses

A prerequisite for our computational framework is the accurate prediction of nanobody-antigen binding poses, as these structures serve as the foundation for subsequent affinity calculations. Currently, two primary categories of computational methods are available for this task: machine-learning-based methods, represented by AlphaFold3 (AF3),14 and traditional protein–protein docking methods relying on physics-based scoring, such as HDOCK,18 ZDOCK,30 and ClusPro.31 We evaluated these methods to determine which approach yields the highest accuracy for nanobody-antigen complexes.

To this end, we curated a dataset of antigen-nanobody co-crystal structures from the SAbDab database28 and performed re-docking experiments using AF3, HDOCK, ZDOCK, and ClusPro. For ClusPro, we assessed both the standard protein–protein docking mode and the antibody–antigen docking mode (ClusPro-AM)33 (Fig. 2A). Unlike AF3, which requires only amino acid sequences as input, the physics-based methods utilized the resolved crystal structures of the binding partners. For AF3 predictions, we performed two replica runs per interaction, generating five poses each, for a total of ten poses per protein–protein interaction (PPI). For the physics-based algorithms, we selected the top 10 poses ranked by their respective default scoring functions. We then compared these predicted poses against the co-crystal structures to evaluate performance (Fig. 2A).

Strategies for recovering nanobody-antigen co-crystal structures. (A) Schematic of the re-docking strategy used to assess physics-based methods and AF3 in recovering resolved nanobody-antigen binding poses. (B) Cumulative success rates of different methods in recovering native binding poses (RMSD ≤ 2.0 Å) as a function of the number of top-ranked poses considered. For AF3, two replicate runs were performed to generate 10 total poses per case. (C) Distribution of RMSD values relative to native binding poses for the Top 1 predicted pose. (D) Impact of re-ranking HDOCK-generated poses using MM/GBSA-calculated binding free energies. (E) Cumulative accuracy of the “HDOCK + MM/GBSA” strategy when considering the Top 1, Top 3, and Top 10 poses across different RMSD thresholds (0 to 10 Å) for defining successful recovery.

Defining successful recovery as a ligand RMSD ≤ 2.0 Å relative to the native pose after alignment on the antigen, we found that the tested docking and structure-prediction methods exhibited different levels of pose-recovery accuracy, with AF3 showing intermediate performance under the sampling conditions used in this study. Specifically, when considering only the top-ranked (Top 1) pose, HDOCK achieved the highest accuracy (52%), followed by ZDOCK (26%), AF3 (18%), ClusPro (1%), and ClusPro-AM (4%) (Fig. 2B). When the inclusion criteria were expanded to the top 10 poses, the success rates of all methods improved marginally, as expected. It should be noted that the AF3 comparison was performed with limited sampling and was included as an exploratory reference rather than a comprehensive benchmark of AF3 performance. Given the stochastic nature of AF-based complex prediction, a rigorous evaluation of AF3 would require substantially expanded sampling, including multiple independent seeds/runs and a larger benchmark set. Therefore, these results only indicate that, under our current benchmark and sampling settings, HDOCK provided the highest Top 1 recovery rate among the tested approaches and was therefore selected as the initial pose-generation method for subsequent MM/GBSA re-scoring.

To further characterize HDOCK's performance, we analyzed the RMSD distribution of the top-ranked nanobody poses relative to the native structures (Fig. 2C). HDOCK exhibited a bimodal distribution: the majority (∼52%) of Top 1 poses achieved an RMSD ≤ 2.0 Å, while the remaining predictions largely fell into the >10 Å range. This indicates that HDOCK tends to be highly accurate when successful, but produces significantly deviant poses when it fails. In contrast, other methods produced a markedly higher proportion of poses with RMSD > 10 Å compared to those ≤2.0 Å, indicating inferior performance relative to HDOCK across both successful and unsuccessful cases. Given HDOCK's superior performance with its default scoring, we investigated whether re-ranking the generated poses using MM/GBSA binding free energies could further enhance accuracy. MM/GBSA is known to provide more rigorous scoring for PPIs, albeit at a higher computational cost.34 As shown in Fig. 2D, re-ranking with MM/GBSA increased the recovery rate of the Top 1 pose to 58%, an absolute improvement of 6% over HDOCK's default scoring. Notably, this benefit was most pronounced within the top 3 poses. When considering all top 10 poses, the default and MM/GBSA scoring functions performed similarly. Thus, MM/GBSA re-ranking effectively improves the selection of near-native poses, particularly among the top-ranked candidates. Finally, to optimize the trade-off between accuracy and computational expense, we determined that considering the top 3 poses from the HDOCK-plus-MM/GBSA strategy offers the best balance for practical applications (Fig. 2E). Protein–protein docking using the HDOCK algorithm combined with MM/GBSA re-ranking recovers nanobody-antigen complex structures with high accuracy.

Ensemble docking accounts for antigen conformational flexibility and outperforms single-structure docking

While the previous section established HDOCK with MM/GBSA re-scoring as the optimal algorithmic strategy, the accuracy of docking predictions also depends heavily on the quality of the input structures. Nanobodies, due to their small size and single-domain fold, are generally modeled with high reliability by AlphaFold2 (AF2). However, AF2-predicted antigen structures often possess low-confidence regions or significant conformational flexibility, and such flexibility is a critical factor in protein–protein interactions (PPIs).35,36 Relying on a single static model may overlook transiently accessible conformational states. In this section, we apply our optimized docking protocol to compare the performance of single-structure docking against an ensemble docking strategy derived from molecular dynamics (MD) simulations.

We used Mesothelin (MSLN),15 a cell-surface biomarker overexpressed in various cancers, as a test case. A nanobody phage display library was constructed from llamas immunized with the MSLN extracellular domain (hereafter “antigen”; Fig. 3A). For single-structure docking, the static AF2-predicted antigen model was used. For ensemble docking, this model was subjected to 3 × 1 µs atomistic MD simulations. Three representative structures from the most populated clusters were selected to represent the antigen's conformational space (Fig. 3B and C).

Ensemble docking accounts for antigen structural flexibility and improves candidate prioritization. (A) MSLN extracellular domain schematic for docking/validation. (B and C) Comparison of single-structure and ensemble docking workflows. (D) Spatial density of center of mass (COM) of docked nanobody poses. (E) Antigen-nanobody contact frequency computed from the top3 energetic poses. (F and H) MM/GBSA energies of the top-10 candidates from single-structure and ensemble docking. (G and I) Flow cytometry validation of the corresponding top-10 candidates on 293T-MSLN cells versus 293T-Vector controls; functional binders are boxed.

The antigen structures exhibited considerable flexibility during three independent 1 µs MD simulations. The three selected representative conformations per antigen differed primarily in regions of high flexibility (Fig. S2 and S5). In the single-structure approach, the predicted binding energy of each nanobody was calculated as the average of the top 3 MM/GBSA-ranked poses. In the ensemble approach, affinity was averaged over nine poses (the top 3 poses against each of the three antigen conformers). For both strategies, candidate nanobodies were ranked by binding free energy, and the top 10 candidates were selected for in vitro expression and flow cytometry based validation.

At the computational level, the ensemble strategy demonstrated a broader exploration of the antigen surface. By plotting the center of mass (COM) of all docked poses, we observed that ensemble docking distributed nanobodies across a wider range of MSLN residues compared to single-structure docking (Fig. 3D). Interestingly, both strategies showed the highest pose density within the concave region of the crescent-shaped MSLN, consistent with a general preference for concave epitopes.37,38 However, the ensemble approach effectively increased the theoretically accessible surface area for binding.

Importantly, while ensemble docking initially explored a broader surface, it exhibited tighter spatial convergence for high-affinity poses. When considering only the top 3 MM/GBSA-ranked poses per nanobody, the contact sites in the ensemble strategy were more spatially concentrated than those in the single-structure strategy (Fig. 3E). This suggests that while MD-generated conformers provide more potential binding sites, the scoring function successfully filters this noise, converging the top-ranked poses onto the most energetically favorable epitopes. Similar phenomena were also observed for the PD-1 and Nectin-4 systems where ensemble docking showed broader initial surface exploration followed by stronger convergence of the top MM/GBSA-ranked poses (Fig. S5D, E, I and J). Nevertheless, the magnitude of this effect may vary among targets depending on antigen architecture, local epitope flexibility, and the extent to which sampled conformers capture binding-relevant states.

To experimentally validate the advantage of ensemble versus single-structure docking, we evaluated the top 10 candidates from each strategy by flow cytometry using a 293T cell line stably overexpressing MSLN (293T-MSLN). Binding specificity was defined by comparison to a matched empty-vector 293T control (293T-Vector) generated with the same expression vector and selection procedure but without the MSLN insert. As shown in Fig. 3G and I, ensemble docking achieved a higher experimental hit rate than single-structure docking in this limited validation set, with 7/10 candidates showing detectable binding compared with 4/10 candidates selected by the single-structure strategy. Notably, the functional nanobodies identified by the single-structure strategy (MS6, MS8) were also recovered by the ensemble strategy (renamed ME2, ME7), indicating that ensemble docking retains “easy” targets while successfully identifying additional binders that require specific conformational states.

These results suggest that ensemble docking may improve the recovery of functional nanobodies by accounting for antigen conformational heterogeneity, although this conclusion requires further validation in larger candidate cohorts. The present comparison provides preliminary but encouraging evidence that incorporating MD-derived antigen conformers can enhance candidate prioritization within the NGS-enriched repertoire. Larger validation cohorts will be required to rigorously assess the statistical significance and generalizability of this improvement.

Quantitative assays confirm the high affinity of predicted nanobodies and the accuracy of the physics-based model

The core of our computational framework is the integration of structural modeling with energetic scoring, designed not only to identify binders but also to rank candidate nanobodies by their predicted affinity. While flow cytometry screening established the ability of the ensemble docking strategy to enrich antigen-binding nanobodies, quantitative characterization was required to assess binding strength and to evaluate the accuracy of the physics-based ranking. We therefore performed ELISA-based affinity measurements to provide an empirical estimate of apparent affinity under equilibrium-like conditions for the seven nanobodies identified by ensemble docking as functional binders to MSLN. All seven nanobodies showed robust, concentration-dependent binding to MSLN in ELISA (Fig. 4A), and their apparent affinities were generally in the nanomolar regime as reflected by fitted EC50 values (Table S4). We note that ELISA-derived EC50 values are apparent binding readouts and do not represent kinetic dissociation constants. Therefore, they should not be interpreted as direct quantitative measures of affinity. Instead, these values provide a practical estimate of relative binding strength among the candidates.

Structural analysis provided additional mechanistic cues beyond affinity measurements. For the top-ranked nanobody ME1, we examined the nine retained poses contributing to its ensemble averaged score. Among the nine retained poses, four clustered into a highly consistent binding site on the MSLN surface, suggesting a preferred and structurally reproducible interaction mode. We therefore visualized the lowest-energy representative from this conserved pose cluster as the putative ME1-MSLN binding configuration (Fig. 4B). To interrogate the molecular basis of this interaction, we mapped the binding interface in the predicted complex and identified two ME1 residues, R57 and R101, positioned at the core of the contact network and forming prominent electrostatic/hydrogen-bond interactions with MSLN (Fig. 4C). We hypothesized that if the predicted binding pose is accurate, disrupting these interactions should significantly reduce affinity. To experimentally test this prediction, we generated the alanine mutants (R57A and R101A), expressed and purified the variant nanobodies, and confirmed their purity by SDS-PAGE (Fig. 4D). As predicted, both mutations resulted in a pronounced rightward shift of the dose–response curves, with substantially increased concentrations required to reach saturation, indicating a marked reduction in apparent binding affinity (Fig. 4E) and surface plasmon resonance (SPR) kinetics further confirmed the affinity decrease (Fig. 4F). This reduction supports that these residues are indeed crucial for binding, but they do not fully validate the complete binding mode or detailed side-chain interaction network. Therefore, the docking models should be interpreted as plausible structural hypotheses for candidate prioritization and mutational design, rather than as definitive experimentally determined complex structures.

Although mutagenesis of the top-ranked MSLN binder supported the functional relevance of selected predicted interface residues, the predicted binding modes were not validated for all recovered binders. We further inspected additional lower-ranked binders, ME8-ME10, but these retained low-energy docking poses were more dispersed and did not converge to a single stable binding orientation (Fig. S3). Therefore, predicted interfaces, particularly for lower-ranked candidates or candidates with heterogeneous docking poses, should be interpreted as testable structural hypotheses requiring further validation by mutagenesis, competition assays, or experimentally resolved complex structures. Additional structural validation beyond the ME1-MSLN complex remains an important direction for future work and will be necessary to further assess the broader accuracy of predicted nanobody-antigen binding modes.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that our framework can enrich high-affinity nanobodies from the candidate library and provide plausible structural hypotheses for antigen-nanobody recognition, including putative interface residues that can guide subsequent experimental validation.

Our computational framework outperforms random selection and traditional screening in efficiency and diversity

To evaluate the robustness and efficiency of our computational pipeline, we compared its performance with two baseline methods using the MSLN antigen as a test case: random selection from an enriched candidate pool, and a traditional experimental screening workflow. First, we established the baseline success rate of random selection. Both the random selection and our computational framework utilized the same initial pool of 365 unique nanobody sequences derived from next-generation sequencing (NGS), filtered by a copy number threshold of ≥100 (Fig. 5A). Ten candidates were randomly chosen without considering sequence abundance from this pool for expression and validation. Of these ten randomly selected nanobodies, only three showed measurable binding to MSLN via flow cytometry, yielding a 30% success rate (Fig. 5B). This was less than half the 70% success rate achieved by our ensemble docking framework (described above). This twofold improvement demonstrates the model's capacity to effectively filter non-binders and enrich for functional candidates within a diverse sequence library.

We next compared our framework with a traditional phage-display screening workflow that relies on experimental enrichment and monoclonal screening without computational pre-ranking (Fig. 5C). After three rounds of bio-panning against MSLN, 96 monoclonal phage clones were randomly picked and subjected to ELISA screening. Eighty-one clones showed positive ELISA signals (OD450 ≥ 1.5). However, subsequent Sanger sequencing revealed extensive redundancy among these positives, collapsing the hits to only three unique nanobody sequences (Fig. S4B). Representative phage ELISA results from 81 randomly selected clones, together with the CDR3 alignment of the three unique sequences, are shown in (Fig. S4B and C), and these nanobodies were recombinantly expressed and purified (Fig. S4D). These three binders were confirmed to be specific for MSLN by flow cytometry. However, this traditional workflow yielded only three unique binders from 96 screened clones and required substantial experimental labor to identify and remove redundant clones. Thus, the key limitation of random monoclonal picking was not the absence of binders, but the inefficient recovery of sequence-diverse candidates from a highly redundant enriched pool. Importantly, these experimentally recovered nanobodies were all associated with high NGS copy-number ranks, reflecting a strong bias toward dominant clones introduced by iterative panning and monoclonal selection. In contrast, the ensemble docking was able to recover nanobodies overlapping with experimentally identified binders, including ME2, while operating independently of clone abundance (Fig. 5E). Moreover, the ensemble-selected nanobodies spanned a broader range of NGS copy-number ranks, indicating that the computational framework mitigates the copy-number bias intrinsic to experimental screening.

To further evaluate whether the computationally prioritized binders represented redundant recovery of closely related clones or sequence-diverse candidates, we compared the CDR3 regions and full-length VHH sequences of the experimentally validated nanobodies (Fig. 5F and G). The validated binders displayed distinguishable CDR3 sequences, with differences in both CDR3 length and sequence composition, and were distributed across multiple full-length VHH sequence clusters. These results indicated that the workflow did not simply recover repeated copies of a single dominant clone. Together with the broader NGS abundance-rank distribution of computationally prioritized binders compared with conventional monoclonal picking (Fig. 5E), this CDR3-level analysis supports the ability of the framework to identify sequence-diverse functional binders from the enriched NGS-derived repertoire.

Generalizability on two additional antigens and potential limitations of the workflow

To demonstrate the robustness and generalizability of our computational framework, we evaluated its performance on two additional antigen systems of high therapeutic relevance: PD-1, a pivotal immune checkpoint receptor in cancer immunotherapy,16 and Nectin-4, an emerging oncology target for antibody-drug conjugates.17 For each antigen, nanobody candidate pools were first obtained by NGS after experimental enrichment, yielding 433 unique sequences for PD-1 and 254 for Nectin-4 after applying the copy-number cutoff of ≥100 (Fig. 6B and F). Without any target-specific parameter tuning, the identical computational workflow, comprising ensemble docking followed by MM/GBSA re-scoring, was applied to rank candidates based on predicted binding energy.

In the PD-1 system, flow cytometry analysis confirmed that 7 out of the 10 top-ranked nanobodies exhibited specific binding to PD-1-expressing cells (Fig. 6D). Similarly, in the Nectin-4 system, all ten top-ranked candidates demonstrated clear binding signals in flow cytometry (Fig. 6H). However, this 100% validation rate should be interpreted cautiously because only ten candidates were experimentally tested. Several non-mutually exclusive factors may have contributed to this result. First, the Nectin-4 round-3 panning output may have been more strongly enriched for genuine binders before computational ranking, thereby increasing the baseline hit probability in the NGS-derived pool. Consistent with this possibility, after applying the same copy-number cutoff, the retained candidate pool was smaller for Nectin-4 than for MSLN or PD-1 (254, 365, and 433 candidates, respectively; Table S2), suggesting a more concentrated post-panning repertoire. In addition, the antigenic surface sampled for Nectin-4 may have been relatively more structurally stable or more compatible with docking/MM/GBSA-based ranking than more flexible or heterogeneous targets. However, these explanations remain hypothetical, and the higher apparent hit rate may also reflect sampling variation due to the small validation cohort. Larger-scale validation across additional candidates and antigen systems will be required to determine whether this result reflects intrinsic target-dependent tractability or stochastic variation.

Notably, the antigen systems evaluated here differ substantially in extracellular domain architecture and structural complexity (Fig. 6A and E), as well as in the copy-number distributions of their NGS enriched candidate pools (Fig. 6B and F). Despite these differences, the computational framework consistently prioritized functional binders with high experimental validation rates. These results support the utility of the structure- and physics-based workflow as a candidate-prioritization strategy for accelerated nanobody discovery, rather than as a definitive predictor of binding outcomes for every antigen system.

We note that the present study did not include a sufficiently large experimentally validated non-binder set to establish a universal MM/GBSA energy threshold or to perform a formal ROC analysis. Therefore, the MM/GBSA score should be interpreted as a relative prioritization metric within experimentally enriched NGS-derived candidate pools, rather than as a validated binary classifier for distinguishing binders from non-binders across the entire repertoire. To assess whether the ranking provided value beyond copy-number enrichment alone, we compared computationally prioritized candidates with randomly selected candidates satisfying the same copy-number cutoff. The higher validation rate of the computationally prioritized group suggests that structure-based ranking contributed additional enrichment beyond the initial abundance filter, although larger-scale binder/non-binder validation will be required for rigorous ROC-based evaluation.

The present workflow was evaluated on three antigen systems and is expected to be most suitable for folded protein antigens with relatively well-defined, structurally stable, and accessible epitopes. Its performance may be more limited for challenging targets with highly flexible epitopes, extensive conformational rearrangements, heavy glycosylation, intrinsically disordered regions, or membrane/context-dependent conformations, as these features can affect antigen structure prediction, docking pose generation, and MM/GBSA-based re-scoring. In the current study, antigen glycosylation was not explicitly modeled during docking and MM/GBSA re-scoring. The extracellular domains of antigens such as PD-1 and Nectin-4 can be glycosylated in vivo, and glycans located near a predicted interface may influence local antigen conformation, sterically shield potential epitopes, or alter epitope accessibility. Therefore, the predicted binding interfaces in this study should be interpreted as protein-only structural hypotheses, and the framework should be viewed as a candidate-prioritization and hypothesis-generating strategy rather than a universally applicable method for definitive structural or affinity prediction. For such challenging targets, future implementations could improve biological realism and predictive reliability by incorporating glycosylation-site mapping, glycan-aware antigen modeling, enhanced conformational sampling, experimentally guided restraints, mutagenesis, epitope mapping, or direct structural validation to assess whether the prioritized nanobodies bind physiologically accessible epitopes.

Another practical limitation is that the present workflow was evaluated using llama immunization-derived VHH libraries. These libraries generally retain the conserved VHH scaffold, and most candidates in our dataset showed high AlphaFold2 modeling confidence. The performance of the workflow should therefore not be directly extrapolated to synthetic or semi-synthetic nanobody libraries, in which CDR loop diversity, especially CDR3 diversity, may be deliberately expanded beyond natural sequence space. Such sequences may yield lower AF2 confidence or less reliable CDR loop conformations, which could in turn affect docking pose generation and MM/GBSA-based ranking. For these libraries, additional quality-control and sampling strategies, such as filtering by AF2 confidence metrics, generating multiple CDR loop conformations, or incorporating CDR-specific refinement, may be required. Future studies will be needed to systematically evaluate the framework on synthetic and semi-synthetic VHH repertoires.

Taken together, despite these limitations, the validation results across MSLN, PD-1, and Nectin-4 demonstrate the practical utility of our structure- and physics-guided workflow for enriching functional nanobody candidates from experimentally pre-enriched NGS pools. By combining sequence abundance filtering with structural modeling, ensemble docking, and MM/GBSA-based re-ranking, the framework provides a scalable prioritization strategy that can accelerate nanobody discovery while preserving clear opportunities for further refinement and target-specific validation.

Conclusion

The rapid expansion of NGS has provided researchers with vast libraries of potential nanobody sequences, yet the ability to efficiently prioritize functional, high-affinity binders from these pools remains a significant bottleneck. Traditional experimental screening, such as phage display panning, is labor-intensive and often biased toward high-abundance clones, while purely sequence-based selection lacks the structural resolution to predict affinity.

In this study, we presented a comprehensive computational framework that integrates structural modeling, molecular dynamics, and physics-based scoring to bridge the gap between sequence data and functional therapeutic leads. A key technical innovation of our work lies in the rigorous evaluation and optimization of the docking-and-scoring protocol. While machine learning approaches like AlphaFold3 have revolutionized structural biology, our systematic re-docking assessment revealed that traditional physics-based docking (HDOCK) coupled with MM/GBSA re-scoring currently offers the optimal strategy for recovering native nanobody-antigen binding poses. Furthermore, we considered structural flexibility in docking by implementing an ensemble docking strategy. By incorporating antigen conformational flexibility derived from MD simulations, our framework successfully identified binders targeting transient or cryptic epitopes that were missed by single-structure docking. Our computational framework not only predicts binding qualitatively but also provides experimentally useful prioritization of candidates with measurable binding strength.

The practical utility of this framework was demonstrated through a benchmark against traditional discovery methods using Mesothelin (MSLN) as a test case. Our pipeline achieved a success rate of 70%, a more than twofold improvement over random selection (30%) and a vast improvement over the high-redundancy output of traditional panning. Crucially, our approach proved capable of mining sequence diversity, identifying high-affinity functional nanobodies from low-abundance NGS clusters that are typically lost in conventional abundance-based screening. The robustness of the framework was further validated by its successful application to two additional therapeutic targets, PD-1 and Nectin-4, where it achieved success rates of 70% and 100%, respectively.

In summary, we have established a robust, generalized, and high-efficiency computational pipeline for nanobody discovery. By combining the speed of NGS with the accuracy of physics-based ensemble scoring, this framework significantly reduces the experimental burden of screening and accelerates the development of diverse, high-affinity nanobody therapeutics.
