Analysis of genetic differences underlying chilling stress tolerance using whole genome Re-Sequencing in walnut (Juglans regia L.)
Authors: Kai Liu, Dejun Liang, Lianlian Ji, Yang Li, Hongxia Wang, Feng Liu, Ruifen Zhang, Yaxin Sang, Junpei Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Walnut (Juglans regia L.) is prized worldwide for both its nutritional value and economic importance, yet it remains vulnerable to cold stress, with significant differences in tolerance among varieties. This study combined physiological analyses with whole-genome resequencing (WGS) to evaluate the cold stress responses of two varieties, ‘Qingxiang’ and ‘Liaoning No.8’. Under chilling stress (0 °C), we measured electrolyte leakage and antioxidant enzyme activity, applying both exogenous methyl jasmonate (MeJA) and the jasmonate inhibitor DIECA. Genomic variations were analyzed using WGS. Results showed that ‘Liaoning No.8’ exhibited superior cold tolerance. Application of MeJA reduced electrolyte leakage by 37% and MDA accumulation by 52% on average, whereas DIECA exacerbated stress-related damage. WGS achieved 16.24–16.26× coverage and identified 2.73–2.78 million SNPs, 378–382k InDels, 25–26k SVs, and 7.2–7.9k CNVs. Twenty genes containing sequence variants showed transcriptional responses under cold stress that were significantly correlated with mutation density (r = 0.62, P < 0.01). One gene, XM_018985465.2, which lacked SNPs in ‘Liaoning No.8’, was expressed 4.2 times more in this variety, suggesting cis-regulatory influence. These findings highlight the role of jasmonic acid signaling in enhancing cold tolerance in walnut and offer genomic insights into its underlying adaptive mechanisms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-11986-z.

Introduction

Juglans regia L., a thermophilic and cold-sensitive deciduous tree, can withstand temperatures as low as −25 °C but grows optimally between 8 and 12 °C [1]. It is cultivated across six continents [2], with China being the top producer, although the United States dominates in market share and pricing [3]. Walnut kernels are nutritionally dense, containing high levels of lipids (mainly triglycerides with > 90% unsaturated fatty acids), proteins, fiber, vitamins (especially vitamin E), and phenolic antioxidants [4]. The high level of unsaturated fatty acids [5] classifies walnuts as a functional food with notable health benefits [6].

Whole-genome resequencing (WGS) involves sequencing individuals or populations by comparing their genomes to a reference genome [7], allowing the detection of genetic variations such as single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), insertions and deletions (InDels), copy number variations (CNVs), and structural variants (SVs). This method helps in analyzing and characterizing genetic traits, predicting genes linked to specific characteristics, and studying evolutionary relationships. WGS is widely used for screening genetic variation, mapping genes, and constructing genetic maps [8].

The evolution of sequencing technology has progressed through several generations. The first generation began with chemical degradation methods (Maxam-Gilbert 1977) [9], which were later replaced by automated Sanger sequencing that combined PCR and capillary electrophoresis [10], but it faced limitations in throughput and cost. The second generation, known as next-generation sequencing (NGS), introduced high-throughput sequencing-by-synthesis (Roche 2004) [11], enabling rapid and cost-effective sequencing of billions of bases [12]. The third-generation sequencing (TGS) advanced this further with single-molecule real-time sequencing [13, 14], addressing the limitations of short reads and often used alongside NGS for enhanced results. The fourth generation features nanopore technology (e.g., Oxford MinION), which offers real-time sequencing with long reads ranging from 13 to 20 kb and ~ 98% accuracy [15]; however, it still faces challenges related to membrane stability and chip durability [16].

Resequencing allows comparison of variations within populations [17], facilitating the efficient detection of variants such as SNPs, InDels, and SVs. This accelerates the identification of genes linked to traits and supports breeding programs [18–20]. Examples include studies on flowering-time genes in sweet cherry (33.91× coverage) [21], domestication genes in Cucurbita [22], and the development of high-density genetic maps in rice (RIL populations) [23].

Low temperature stress significantly affects the survival and productivity of fruit trees [24]. Exposure to low temperatures hampers growth and yield [25] by causing both morphological changes, such as wilting and chlorosis, and physiological damage, including membrane and chlorophyll degradation [24, 26]. As a result, comprehending cold tolerance has become a critical area of research. The availability of a reference genome has facilitated progress—for example, the ‘Chandler’ walnut variety shows a heterozygous SNP distribution (2.2 M SNPs, 4/kb) with reduced heterozygosity on chromosome 13 [27], and genome assemblies from J. microcarpa × J. regia hybrids have enabled SV detection [28, 29]. However, most transcriptomic studies have focused on disease resistance and fruit quality, with relatively little focus on abiotic stress responses, particularly cold tolerance.

This study presents the first whole-genome resequencing of two J. regia walnut genotypes with contrasting responses to cold: J. regia ‘Qingxiang’ (cold-tolerant) and J. regia ‘Liaoning No.8’ (cold-sensitive). By analyzing genomic variations—including SNPs, InDels, CNVs, and SVs—we aim to elucidate the genomic differences underlying their differing cold sensitivities and to identify genetic variations linked to key horticultural traits of economic significance.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

The walnut (Juglans regia L.) varieties ‘Qingxiang’ and ‘Liaoning No.8’ were grafted onto the same rootstocks of potted walnut seedlings.

Effects of MeJA and DIECA on relative electrical conductivity and enzyme activity of walnuts under chilling stress

The leaves of the grafted ‘Qingxiang’ and ‘Liaoning No.8’ walnut plants were treated with 100 µmol of methyl jasmonate (MeJA) or Sodium diethyldithiocarbamate trihydrate (DIECA), a jasmonic acid (JA) biosynthesis inhibitor, until runoff. The entire plants were then subjected to chilling stress (0 °C) for durations of 0, 8, 12, 24, 36, and 48 h. Leaf samples were collected at each aforementioned time point, rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored for later analysis of relative electrical conductivity (REC) and enzyme activity. Additionally, leaves at 0 and 48 h were used for RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq).

Determination of REC and enzyme activity

Relative electrical conductivity (REC) was assessed using Tian’s method [30] and calculated as the ratio A1/A2, where A1 was the electrical conductivity of the material being evaluated, and A2 was the reference conductivity.

The activities of antioxidant enzymes (Superoxide dismutase (SOD), Peroxidase (POD), Catalase (CAT), and Malondialdehyde (MDA)) were determined with commercial assay kits (SolarBio, China), following the manufacturer’s instructions: SOD - BC0175, POD - BC0095, CAT - BC0205, and MDA - BC0025.

Library construction and whole genome resequencing

Genomic DNA was sheared into ~ 350 bp fragments using a Covaris ultrasonicator (). The resulting DNA library was then amplified through clustering on a flow cell. Quality control of the library involved several steps: fragment size selection was done with Agencourt SPRIselect, quantification was performed using a Qubit 3.0 fluorometer, and size distribution was analyzed with the Caliper LabChip® GX/NGS 3 K system. High-quality libraries were sequenced on an Illumina platform (PE150 configuration), generating 150 bp paired-end reads.

Data quality control

Raw sequencing data obtained from the Illumina platform underwent base calling to produce raw reads. These quality-filtered reads were then aligned to the reference genome (Juglans regia assembly GCF_001411555.2; NCBI) using the BWA-MEM tool (parameters: mem -t 4 -k 32 -M) [31]. Following alignment, PCR duplicates were identified and marked using the SAMtools rmdup [32].

Variation detection results and annotations

SNP calling

Single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were identified as genomic positions exhibiting single-base substitutions (transitions or transversions). Initial SNP calling was performed using SAMtools mpileup (parameters: -m 2 -F 0.002 -d 1000). To ensure high-confidence variants, SNPs were filtered based on two criteria: A read depth ≥ 4 supporting the variant allele and a mapping quality (MQ) score of ≥ 20.

InDel detection

Insertions and deletions (InDels; ≤50 bp) were detected using SAMtools mpileup, applying the same parameters described in Sect. 2.6.1. InDels located within coding-regions or splice sites were specially flagged, as they have the potential to disrupt normal gene function by causing frameshift mutations (indel lengths non-divisible by 3) or by altering protein translation.

CNV detection

Copy number variations (CNVs) were identified using CNVnator [33] (parameter: -call 100). This tool detects genomic segments with duplications or deletions based on read-depth divergence from the reference genome.

SV detection

Large-scale structural variants (SVs) such as insertions (INS), deletions (DEL), inversions (INV), intra-chromosomal translocations (ITX), and inter-chromosomal translocations (CTX) were detected using the BreakDancer tool [34]. The detection criteria included discordant paired-end read alignments, deviations in insert size, and a minimum of two supporting read pairs.

Functional annotation

Variant functional consequences were annotated using ANNOVAR [35] based on genomic coordinates such as chromosome number, start and end positions, and reference and alternate alleles. The annotations covered several categories: Gene-based (e.g., exonic, splicing); Region-based (e.g., conserved regions); Filter-based (e.g., population frequency); and Disease association databases.

Genetic variation of genes related to target traits

Based on published literature, we selected twenty candidate genes associated with the response to chilling stress. We then screened these genes for SNPs and InDels, integrating transcriptomic profiling of the stress response. Additionally, we performed transcriptional expression analysis of the candidate genes. This comprehensive approach allowed us to investigate genetic variations within the candidate genes and evaluate the potential functional consequences of the mutations detected.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted to assess genomic variation and its functional implications between the walnut cultivars ‘Qingxiang’ and ‘Liaoning NO.8’. Total genomic variants, including SNPs, InDels, SVs, and CNVs, were compared using t-tests. To detect biases in the distribution of functional variant types (e.g., non-synonymous SNPs, frameshift InDels), chi-square tests were employed. Fisher’s exact tests were used to evaluate cultivar-specific enrichment of high-impact variants (stop-gain/loss), while Pearson correlation analyses were conducted to determine the relationship between variant densities in candidate genes and their transcriptional expression levels (measured as FPKM) under cold stress conditions. Additionally, sliding-window (100-kb) analyses were performed to calculate chromosomal variant densities, which were then visualized via heatmaps and circular plots. All statistical tests applied a significance threshold of α = 0.05, with multiple-hypothesis correction using false discovery rate (FDR) control.

Results

Evaluation of REC and antioxidant enzyme activity in two walnut varieties under chilling stress

The walnut cultivars ‘Liaoning NO.8’ and ‘Qingxiang’, both grafted onto identical rootstocks, were subjected to chilling stress at 0 °C for 0, 8, 12, 24, 36, and 48 h. Leaf samples were collected to assess REC and antioxidant enzyme activity.

Both cultivars exhibited a gradual increase in REC with prolonged chilling exposure (Fig. 1), but ‘Liaoning NO.8’ consistently had significantly lower REC values compared to ‘Qingxiang’ at all timepoints (P < 0.05), suggesting better tolerance to chilling. Treatment ‘Liaoning NO.8’ and ‘Qingxiang’ with 100 µmol MeJA significantly decreased REC compared to untreated controls, while application of DIECA, an inhibitor of jasmonic acid synthesis, caused an increase in REC (P < 0.01). MDA levels increased steadily in both cultivars under chilling stress (Fig. 2A). After 48 h, the ‘Qingxiang’ cultivar showed a 2.13-fold increase in MDA, while ‘Liaoning NO.8’ experienced a 3.00-fold increase. At all timepoints throughout the chilling stress period, ‘Qingxiang’ consistently exhibited significantly higher MDA levels than ‘Liaoning NO.8’ (P < 0.05). Treatment with MeJA led to an average MDA reduction of 37%, whereas DIECA treatment caused an average MDA increase of 52%. POD activity in ‘Liaoning NO.8’ showed a sustained increase (Fig. 2B), reaching a peak of 6.85 times the control at 48 h. In contrast, ‘Qingxiang’ displayed a transient increase, peaking at 3.78 times the control at 36 h before declining. MeJA application mirrored the cultivar-specific trends, while DIECA induced irregular fluctuations. Across all measurements, POD activity remained significantly higher in ‘Liaoning NO.8’ than in ‘Qingxiang’ (P < 0.01). CAT activity peaked at 24 h in both cultivars, with ‘Liaoning NO.8’ exhibiting significantly higher peak levels (2.74× vs. 2.23× the control in ‘Qingxiang’; P < 0.05) (Fig. 2C). MeJA further elevated CAT activity (2.6–3.57× the control), while DIECA reduced it. Overall, ‘Liaoning NO.8’ maintained significantly higher CAT activity compared to ‘Qingxiang’ (P < 0.05). SOD activity followed trends similar to those observed for CAT in both cultivars, and treatment responses mirrored the patterns observed in POD/CAT activity (Fig. 2D). Collectively, these physiological data indicate that ‘Liaoning NO.8’ has greater chilling tolerance than ‘Qingxiang’. Quality assessment of sequencing data

DNA libraries for Juglans regia cultivars ‘Liaoning NO.8’ and ‘Qingxiang’ were constructed using the Illumina TruSeq DNA Library Prep Kit, following the manufacturer’s instructions. The process included end repair, dA-tailing, adapter ligation, size selection, and PCR amplification (Table 1). Sequencing was carried out on an Illumina platform (PE150 configuration), generating 24.46 Gb of raw data. After rigorous quality control (removal of adapters, low-quality bases (Phred score < 20), and N-rich reads), 24.31 Gb of high-quality clean data (99.37% retention) were obtained for downstream analysis. Each sample exhibited adequate sequencing depth (9.46–12.72 Gb/sample), high base quality (Q20 ≥ 94.67%; Q30 ≥ 86.51%), and normal GC content (37.37–37.75%), confirming successful library construction and sequencing reliability for variant detection.

Mapping statistics

High-quality reads were aligned to the Juglans regia reference genome (Table 2) using the BWA-MEM tool (parameters: mem -t 4 -k 32 -M). PCR duplicates were subsequently marked with SAMtools rmdup. Alignment metrics reflected strong sequence homology and data uniformity: mapping rates ranged from 95.51 to 96.92% across all samples, coverage depth was 16.24–16.26× (excluding N-regions) (Fig. 3), and genome coverage (≥ 1×) was > 95.52%. The reference genome size was 572,946,670 bp. These optimal alignment metrics—including a mapping rate of > 95.5%, uniform depth distribution, and > 95.5% coverage depth—support the reliability of the dataset for downstream variant detection and analysis.

SNP detection and annotation

SNPs were identified as single-base substitutions—either transitions or transversions—using SAMtools mpileup (parameters: -m 2 -F 0.002 -d 1000), with stringent filtering criteria (minimum read depth ≥ 4 and mapping quality ≥ 20) (Table 3, Fig. 4). Comparative genomic analysis showed that ‘Liaoning No.8’ had significantly fewer SNPs (2,729,743) than ‘Qingxiang’ (2,782,312; P < 0.01). Despite this difference in SNP count, both cultivars exhibited comparable genomic distribution patterns, with most SNPs located in intergenic regions (67.96% vs. 68.11%), followed by intronic regions (15.71% vs. 15.62%). Notably, ‘Liaoning No.8’ harbored a slightly higher number of high-impact variants, including 268 exonic stop-loss mutations (compared to 274 in ’Qingxiang’) and 776 frameshift InDels (compared to 767 in ‘Qingxiang’), indicating potential functional advantages in stress-response genes. Data quality (Fig. 5) was confirmed based on read depth distributions, SNP quality scores, and inter-SNP distance frequencies, validating the dataset for genetic studies in Juglans regia.

InDel detection and annotation

InDels (< 50 bp) were detected using SAMtools mpileup (parameters: -m 2 -F 0.002 -d 1000). When located in coding regions, these variants can have significant functional consequences: frameshift InDels disrupt the reading frame and are subject to stronger selective pressure compared to non-frameshift variants. Distribution analysis showed that both ‘Liaoning NO.8’ (220,640 InDels) and ‘Qingxiang’ (218,495 InDels) shared similar patterns: (1) predominance in intergenic regions (59.58% vs. 59.73%), (2) moderate intronic representation (20.81% [77,048] vs. 20.37% [75,548]), (3) relatively high frequencies of non-frameshift InDels in exonic regions (31.57% del/30.67% ins vs. 30.61% del/30.9% ins), and (4) moderate proportions of frameshift variants (20.43% del/16.03% ins vs. 20.29% del/16.85% ins), with stop-gain or stop-loss variants being negligible. Analysis of InDel lengths revealed a genome-wide occurrence of 1-bp InDels (> 30%), an enrichment of 3-bp InDels within CDS (> 30%), and maximal inter-cultivar divergence at 1-bp length class (Figs. 7 and 8; Table 4).

SV detection and annotation

SV analysis detected large-scale fragment alterations (≥ 50 bp), including insertions (INS), deletions (DEL), inversions (INV), and translocations in the Juglans regia cultivars ‘Liaoning NO.8’ (26,231 SVs) and ’Qingxiang’ (24,984 SVs) using the BreakDancer software. SV identification was based on discordant paired-end alignments and insert size deviations, with variants filtered for ≥ 2 supporting read pairs and DEL/INS/INV annotated by ANNOVAR (Table 5; Fig. 9). The key findings included: 1) CTX translocations were predominant in ‘Liaoning NO.8’ and ’Qingxiang’(54.93% [13,723] vs. 51.44% [13,494]),) most SVs were located in intergenic regions (46.52% vs. 47.91%), 3) splice-site variants were the rarest (0.08% vs. 0.06%), and 4) > 60% of SVs were larger than 1,200 bp, with maximal inter-cultivar divergence occurring in this size class (Fig. 10).

CNV detection and annotation results

CNVs were identified using CNVnator (-call 100) through read-depth divergence analysis against the Juglans regia reference genome, with ANNOVAR-annotated variants (Table 6, Fig. 11). The ’Liaoning NO.8’ variety exhibited a higher number of CNVs (7,932) than ‘Qingxiang’ (7,155), including 2,287 duplications (31.22 Mb) compared to 1,987 duplications (27.64 Mb) and 5,645 deletions (74.77 Mb) versus 5,168 deletions (73.62 Mb). Most CNVs were found in intergenic regions (49.4% [3,920] vs. 50.6% [3,621]), indicating a notable enrichment in non-coding genomic segments.

Analysis of gene variation and differences in transcription expression levels

Twenty genes were randomly selected from genomic regions exhibiting varying densities of genetic variants (e.g., XM_018985465.2 showed 0 SNPs in ‘L8’ compared to 1 in ‘QX’) to investigate the relationship between genotype and gene expression (Table 7). Transcriptomic profiling after 48 h of cold stress demonstrated distinct expression patterns (Fig. 13), with transcriptional responses showing a significant correlation with gene-specific mutation density (r = 0.62, P < 0.01). These findings indicate that both cis-regulatory elements and coding-sequence variations may influence differences in gene expression in response to cold stress.

Discussion

Our study demonstrated that the enhanced cold tolerance of Juglans regia ’Liaoning No.8’ is achieved through the coordinated regulation of antioxidants and jasmonic acid (JA) signaling—a mechanism commonly seen in various plant species [36–39]. Physiological profiling confirmed that ’Liaoning No.8’ maintained significantly lower REC and MDA levels, along with stronger and more sustained activities of POD, CAT, and SOD enzymes under chilling stress compared to ’Qingxiang’. Importantly, treatment with MeJA improved cold adaptation in both cultivars, while application of DIECA exacerbated cold damage. These results highlight the pivotal role of JA in walnut cold tolerance, consistent with observations in watermelon [40] and melon [41], but reported here for the first time in perennial trees.

Whole-genome resequencing uncovered extensive structural differences between the cultivars. The cultivar ’Liaoning No.8’ showed a lower total number of SNPs (2,729,743) compared to ‘Qingxiang’ (2,782,312) (P < 0.01), but had a higher count of functionally impactful variants in critical regions, including exonic variants; with fewer stop-loss mutations (268 vs. 274) and non-synonymous SNPs (56,348 vs. 56,802). In terms of CNV distribution, 49.4% were enriched in intergenic regions (compared to 50.6% in ’Qingxiang’). SV size varied, with > 60% of SVs exceeding 1,200 bp and showing the greatest inter-cultivar differences. These genomic features likely contribute to varied stress response mechanisms. Among twenty randomly selected genes with differing variant burdens, transcriptional responses after 48 h of chilling significantly correlated with mutation density (r = 0.62, P < 0.01; Fig. 13). For example, gene XM_018985465.2, which had no SNPs in ‘Liaoning NO.8’ versus one in ’Qingxiang,’ showed a 4.2-fold higher induction in ‘Liaoning NO.8’. Another gene, XM_035694326.1, with 9 SNPs in ’Liaoning NO.8’ and 10 in ‘Qingxiang,’ was expressed constitutively only in the cold-tolerant cultivar.

This study reveals that both cis-regulatory variants and coding mutations collectively influence cold-responsive gene expression in walnut—a pattern previously observed in pomelo [41] and Brassica [42], but reported here for the first time in walnut. Three major contributions arise from our research. First, we identified a crosstalk between JA signaling and antioxidant pathways, demonstrating that JA signaling orchestrates cold adaptation in walnut by modulating antioxidant enzymes such as SOD, POD, and CAT, thereby offering a physiological basis for targeted breeding efforts. Second, we established linkages between genetic variants and cold tolerance phenotypes, highlighting intergenic CNVs and SVs near stress-response genes, as well as non-synonymous SNPs in antioxidant enzyme genes, as promising candidate regulators of observed tolerance differences. Third, we characterized the germplasm, identifying ’Liaoning NO.8’ as a valuable genetic resource for walnut improvement, showing similarities to cold-adapted Cucurbita morphotypes [22] but with distinct variant patterns. Although resequencing has accelerated trait discovery in many crops [18–20, 43, 44], this study presents the first integrated genomic and physiological model for cold adaptation in walnut. Future research should focus on validating candidate genes (e.g., XM_018954845.2 with 17 SVs in ‘Liaoning NO.8’) using CRISPR to confirm causal relationships between the identified variants and cold tolerance phenotypes.

Conclusion

This study presents the first comprehensive whole-genome analysis of genetic variation underlying abiotic stress tolerance in walnut (Juglans regia) cultivars. By combining physiological assessments under chilling stress, we established that the cultivar ‘Liaoning NO.8’ showed greater cold tolerance than ’Qingxiang’, evidenced by significantly lower REC and MDA accumulation, as well as consistently higher activities of antioxidant enzymes (POD, CAT, SOD). These physiological traits were further modulated by JA signaling, where treatment with exogenous MeJA enhanced antioxidant responses, while the application of DIECA increased cold stress damage. Whole-genome resequencing revealed numerous high-impact genetic variants, including SNPs, InDels, SVs, and CNVs, with twenty randomly selected mutation-bearing genes exhibiting differential transcriptional responses under cold stress. Collectively, these results offer a genomic basis for elucidating cold-adaptation in walnuts, identify candidate genetic variants for marker-assisted breeding of cold-tolerant cultivars, and provide a valuable framework to harness genetic diversity for improving walnut germplasm.
