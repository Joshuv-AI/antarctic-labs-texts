Hypoxia‐Induced Lineage Plasticity in Neuroblastoma: Advancing Signature Interpretation and Clinical Translation
Authors: Jimmy Carter Osei, Aya Abukwaik, Matteo Di Giovannantonio, Louis J. Koizia, Benjamin H. L. Harris
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Chaves et al. [1] provide an important contribution to understanding the role of hypoxia in driving lineage plasticity in neuroblastoma. They identify a 94‐gene mesenchymal (MES)/hypoxia signature, in which higher scores were associated with improved overall survival in retrospective cohorts of patients with high‐risk neuroblastoma [2]. This perhaps surprising finding is particularly noteworthy given its potential implications for immunotherapy [1]. We suggest a few ways in which this work could be extended to strengthen its clinical interpretation.

The authors propose that the in vitro switching mechanism extends to patient tumors where high bulk MES/hypoxia scores likely reflect hypoxia‐induced lineage plasticity [1]. However, bulk transcriptomics cannot easily distinguish this hypothesis from other possible explanations. Some tumors may simply contain a higher proportion of mesenchymal (MES) cells. In addition, the signal may partly arise from stromal cells such as cancer‐associated fibroblasts, macrophages and other cell types that express collagen, extracellular matrix and TNF‐α pathway genes represented within the 94‐gene set. Single‐cell and spatial transcriptomic datasets that are now available for neuroblastoma may yield further important insights [3, 4]. In particular, the spatial atlas from Patel et al. [4] could potentially be reanalyzed to examine MES/hypoxia expression across different cell types. The very strong correlation between the MES/hypoxia signature and the pure MES signature (r = 0.92) [1] also raises an important question: whether the MES signature alone would produce the same prognostic separation observed in the hypoxic subgroup.

The choice of hypoxia signature and summary score used to define the hypoxic subgroup may influence study findings. A recent comprehensive analysis of 70 hypoxia gene‐expression signatures and various scoring methods has demonstrated that performance varies substantially across different contexts, whilst also highlighting the importance of benchmarking against random length‐matched gene sets to help distinguish true biological signal from structural artifact [5]. Testing whether the prognostic association remains consistent when alternative published hypoxia signatures or scoring approaches are used would strengthen the robustness of these findings. Further complementary survival analyses using methods such as multivariable Cox and logistic regression models across the current and additional clinical validation datasets may also be informative.

Overall, Chaves et al. [1] have opened an important line of research in neuroblastoma biology. Extending this work would provide a stronger basis for MES/hypoxia‐guided immunotherapy development and patient stratification in future studies.
