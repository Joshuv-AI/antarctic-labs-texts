All-Arthroscopic Membrane Autologous Chondrocyte Implantation for Femoral Cartilage Defects
Authors: Zirong Huang, Wenhan Yang, Hanyu Yao, Mingjin Zhong, Kan Chen, Wenzhe Feng, Weimin Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMembrane autologous chondrocyte implantation (m-ACI) has become an increasingly applied and effective method for treating cartilage defects. Traditional open m-ACI procedures prolong patient recovery time and increase the risks of trauma and knee stiffness. With the application of fibrin glue and improvements in surgical skills, the procedure has evolved from traditional open surgery to an all-arthroscopic technique. This transition has made m-ACI a minimally invasive, cosmetically favorable surgical procedure, which facilitates faster recovery and is increasingly promoted.

With an aging population and the increasing incidence of sports injuries, the frequency of cartilage defects has significantly increased as well.1 Articular cartilage, as a critical component of the knee joint, plays a key role in reducing friction, improving movement fluency, and cushioning pressure. However, because of the lack of nerve and blood vessel distribution within the cartilage tissue, its self-healing and repair capacity is relatively limited.2,3 Membrane autologous chondrocyte implantation (m-ACI) has been widely applied clinically as an effective treatment for cartilage defects. Follow-up studies by Ebert et al.4 showed that m-ACI provides excellent clinical scores, high patient satisfaction, and sufficient graft survival rates at 10 years postoperatively, demonstrating the durability of the repaired tissue.

Although m-ACI has many advantages in cartilage repair, its potential complications and risks cannot be ignored. Common intraoperative complications, such as infection, bleeding, and hematoma, may affect postoperative recovery and cartilage regeneration.5 Therefore, minimizing surgical trauma and promoting early recovery are critical. Fibrin glue, widely used because of its biosealing, hemostatic, and adhesive properties, supports cartilage cell migration and proliferation.6 It promotes various cellular responses through thrombin, enhancing cell migration, proliferation, and survival.7,8 The application of fibrin glue allows scaffolds to be easily fixed to the defect base, eliminating the need for sutures and enabling faster, less-invasive techniques through arthroscopy.

Surgical Technique

Step 1: Patient Positioning and Preparation

The patient is placed supine on the operating table, ensuring free flexion and extension of the surgical-side knee over the edge of the table. Standard sterile preparation and draping are performed on the lower limb. A tourniquet is used to exsanguinate the limb and is then inflated.

Step 2: Diagnostic Arthroscopy and Cartilage Harvest

A lateral portal is established, and an arthroscope is inserted to perform a comprehensive diagnostic evaluation of the knee joint. The cartilage defect is identified, and a cannulated needle is introduced through the medial patellar tendon. By flexing and extending the knee, the cannulated needle is guided to the defect, and the medial portal is established. Large detached cartilage fragments are removed using a curette until the normal cartilage boundary is visible. Unlike the commonly recommended technique of harvesting cartilage from the intercondylar notch or the superolateral trochlear ridge, our approach uses cartilage from the transitional zone of the defect as seed cells for culture. The edges of the cartilage defect are trimmed with a curette device to create a regular defect shape with smooth, sloped, and even edges. During debridement, the calcified cartilage layer at the defect base is removed, but the subchondral bone must be preserved. Synovial tissue obstructing the view is cleared with a shaver, and hemostasis is achieved using radiofrequency. The defect size is measured under arthroscopy using a flexible ruler, and photographs are taken for documentation (Fig 1). At least 250 to 350 mg of cartilage tissue is required. If the amount of cartilage obtained is insufficient, additional cartilage can be harvested from non−weight-bearing areas of the knee. The harvested chondrocytes are sent to Guanhao Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China) for culture. After 3 to 4 weeks, the patient undergoes a second implantation procedure.

Diagnostic arthroscopy and cartilage harvest procedure. (A) The arthroscope is introduced through the lateral portal, revealing a flap of delaminated cartilage with frayed surrounding cartilage. (B) A curette is inserted through the medial portal to debride the cartilage defect, creating a smooth, flat, and slightly beveled regular margin. (C) A trimmed flexible ruler is introduced through the medial portal to measure the maximum transverse diameter of the cartilage defect. (D) After adjusting the orientation of the ruler, the maximum longitudinal diameter of the cartilage defect is measured.

Step 3: Preparation of Cartilage Defects

The second procedure is performed through the original portal, and the cartilage defect is re-examined. Unnecessary debridement is avoided to minimize synovial bleeding and maintain a clear arthroscopic view. The defect size is re-measured using a flexible ruler. The cultured m-ACI is retrieved from the medium, and its size and shape are trimmed using a tissue scissor to match the defect dimensions (Fig 2). The graft size is kept slightly smaller than the defect to avoid protrusion beyond the edges, which could impair fibrin glue adhesion and cause graft detachment. The m-ACI is marked with a sterile pen to distinguish its smooth articular surface (facing the joint) from its rough side (facing the bone).

Preparation of the m-ACI graft. (A) The entire m-ACI construct is removed from the culture medium. Using a surgical marker, the intraoperatively measured transverse and longitudinal diameters of the cartilage defect are marked on the m-ACI. The smooth surface of the m-ACI is also marked, indicating the side that will face the articular cavity after implantation. (B) The m-ACI is trimmed to the appropriate size using surgical scissors for subsequent use. (m-ACI, membrane autologous chondrocyte implantation.)

Step 4: m-ACI Implantation

The arthroscopic inflow is closed, and joint irrigation fluid is aspirated using negative suction. Dry the defect with a mass made of rolled-up gauze in the form of a peanut until the surface is clean and free of blood clots or excess fluid. Fibrin glue is applied to the defect area using an applicator (Fig 3), ensuring complete but thin coverage to prevent adhesion to surrounding tissues. The trimmed m-ACI is delivered through the skin incision to the defect area, with the marked side facing the joint. The graft is spread evenly using hooks and forceps. If necessary, a third portal may be established to facilitate the procedure. A thin layer of fibrin glue is applied around the m-ACI, and the joint is kept static for 5 minutes to allow the glue to solidify. Excess fibrin glue adhering to surrounding tissues is carefully removed to prevent graft detachment during joint movement (Fig 4). The knee is repeatedly flexed and extended to confirm graft stability. The wound is closed, bandaged, and the knee is immobilized with a brace in full extension.

m-ACI implantation procedure. (A) The patient’s right knee is positioned flat on the operating table. The arthroscope is introduced through the lateral portal. The m-ACI graft is grasped with forceps and delivered into the joint through the medial portal. (B) After the m-ACI is positioned and flattened onto the cartilage defect, a fibrin glue applicator is introduced through the medial portal, and the defect area is gradually covered with fibrin glue. (m-ACI, membrane autologous chondrocyte implantation.)

m-ACI fixation procedure. (A) Fibrin glue is applied to the dried cartilage defect, forming a thin, even layer over the defect surface. (B) The m-ACI graft is spread and flattened over the defect using a probe or curved forceps to ensure complete coverage. (C) Additional fibrin glue is applied to the surface of the implanted m-ACI, covering the entire graft. (D) The graft is left undisturbed for 5 minutes to allow the fibrin glue to set. (m-ACI, membrane autologous chondrocyte implantation.)

Postoperative Rehabilitation

Rehabilitation is divided into three phases. During the first 0 to 2 weeks, early rehabilitation involves knee immobilization with a brace in full extension. Exercises include ankle pumps and isometric quadriceps contractions. From 2 to 6 weeks, pain-free knee flexion exercises are initiated with continuous passive motion machine assistance. During the 6- to 12-week phase, partial weight-bearing is allowed with crutches if the defect is in a non−weight-bearing zone. For defects in the weight-bearing zone, the protocol from the 2- to 6-week phase is continued, with gradual weight-bearing introduced after 12 weeks. Return-to-sport criteria vary and depend on magnetic resonance imaging findings and knee function during follow-up.

Discussion

Compared with traditional open m-ACI, the all-arthroscopic approach offers significant advantages in terms of reduced trauma, postoperative pain, hospital stay, and scarring.9, 10, 11 Studies have shown shorter hospital stays, lower complication rates, faster recovery of knee mobility, and better functional outcomes with arthroscopic m-ACI.11,12 Although midterm outcomes, imaging quality, and failure rates are similar between the 2 approaches,13 the minimally invasive nature of arthroscopic m-ACI reduces damage to surrounding tissues, lowering the risk of postoperative complications such as infection and stiffness.11,12

The use of fibrin glue in m-ACI has significantly simplified the fixation process compared with traditional suturing methods. Fibrin glue allows for secure attachment of the cell-seeded collagen membrane to the cartilage defect without the need for sutures, reducing surgical time and minimizing trauma to the surrounding cartilage and soft tissues.14, 15, 16 Clinical studies have shown that fixation with fibrin glue alone results in high early postoperative graft adherence rates, with most patients achieving complete attachment and effective defect coverage within the first month after surgery.17 This technique also enables arthroscopic or minimally invasive approaches, making it feasible to treat lesions in anatomically challenging locations where suturing would be difficult or impossible.14 Overall, the adoption of fibrin glue for m-ACI fixation improves procedural efficiency, decreases surgical morbidity, and supports favorable cartilage repair outcomes.

Arthroscopic m-ACI maximizes joint capsule and surrounding tissue integrity, offers superior cosmetic results for young patients, and reduces infection and scarring risks. The technique requires advanced surgical skills, including precise implantation and hemostasis in a nonirrigated environment. Despite these challenges, the procedure reflects the growing trend of minimally invasive techniques in cartilage repair, particularly for young patients prioritizing aesthetics and function.
