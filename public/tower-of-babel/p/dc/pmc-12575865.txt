The early therapeutic efficacy of robot-assisted laparoscopic radical prostatectomy with combined anterior and posterior approach: a cohort study
Authors: Fan Feng, Yuanshan Cui, Xinghe Pan, Xiang Dong, Wenliang Ma, Jun Pan, Weidong Gan, Hongqian Guo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study aims to compare the perioperative outcomes, postoperative urinary control rates, and positive surgical margin (PSM) rates of robot-assisted laparoscopic radical prostatectomy using a combined anterior and posterior approach (AP-RARP) with those of the Retzius-sparing (RS-RARP) and standard anterior (anterior-RARP) approaches in prostate cancer treatment. We collected and analyzed medical data from prostate cancer patients who underwent one of these three approaches. No statistically significant differences were observed in operative time, intraoperative blood loss, transfusion rates, anastomotic leakage, or postoperative infection rates among the groups. PSM rates were comparable across the three groups in patients with T2 stage disease, whereas AP-RARP and anterior-RARP demonstrated lower PSM rates compared to RS-RARP in patients with T3a stage disease. The RS-RARP group demonstrated lower immediate and 1-month postoperative continence rates than the AP-RARP and anterior-RARP groups. At 3 months postoperatively, continence rates in both RS-RARP and AP-RARP groups were lower than those observed in the anterior-RARP group, with no significant differences among the groups at 6 months postoperatively. In conclusion, AP-RARP appears to be a viable surgical option for prostate cancer treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-21851-4.

Introduction

Radical prostatectomy remains the primary treatment modality for non-metastatic prostate cancer. In 2000, Binder et al. performed the world’s first robot-assisted laparoscopic radical prostatectomy (RARP) using the da Vinci robotic system1, laying the foundation for the advancements in surgical techniques and approaches, which subsequently demonstrated superior therapeutic efficacy compared to traditional laparoscopic surgery2. In the same year, Guy V et al. introduced the combined anterior and posterior approach to radical prostatectomy, referred to as the “Montsouris technique”, and implemented it on five patients using robotic assistance the following year3,4. However, understanding of the anatomical and functional aspects of structures such as the prostate fascia, vascular nerves, and related tissues was limited at the time, with scant literature addressing these considerations. Consequently, there have been few subsequent reports on this approach. In 2010, Galfano et al. proposed a posterior approach to robot-assisted laparoscopic radical prostatectomy, later termed Retzius-sparing robotic-assisted radical prostatectomy (RS-RARP)5.

Since 2016, our urology center has incorporated RS-RARP into practice. RS-RARP has shown notable advantages, including enhanced surgical field visibility during dissection of the prostate’s posterior wall and improved early postoperative continence due to preserved urinary control-related structures6,7. However, this technique entails a lengthy learning curve, with challenges in urethral suture reconstruction, especially for less experienced surgeons8. Its broader application has also been hindered by a relatively high positive surgical margin (PSM) rate9. Over the past three years, our team has refined the Montsouris technique by integrating the advantages of both anterior and posterior approaches, performing a substantial number of robot-assisted laparoscopic radical prostatectomies using this combined anterior-posterior (AP-RARP) approach. In this study, we detail our technique, compare it to other established approaches, and present preliminary outcomes.

Results

Intraoperative and postoperative outcomes

All 233 surgeries were completed successfully with no statistically significant differences observed in operative time, intraoperative blood loss, transfusion rate, persistent anastomotic leakage, infection rates, or postoperative Gleason scores among the three groups (P > 0.05). Intraoperative transfusions were required in one case each from the RS-RARP group and anterior-RARP groups. Four cases of postoperative anastomotic leakage occurred, all of which resolved within two weeks post-surgery. Seven cases of postoperative infections were recorded across the groups, all of which responded to treatment with cephalosporins or piperacillin/tazobactam and resolved within one week. Urethral catheters were removed for all patients on postoperative days 12–14, and abdominal drains were subsequently removed the following day after confirming the absence of urinary fistulas (Table 1).

Tumor control

At the three-month postoperative follow-up, 10 patients had PSA levels ≥ 0.1 ng/mL, with no statistically significant differences among the three groups (Table 1). In cases with T2 tumors, the positive surgical margin (PSM) rates were comparable across groups. However, in T3a cases, the AP-RARP and anterior-RARP groups exhibited slightly lower PSM rates than the RS-RARP group, a difference that was statistically significant (P < 0.05) (Table 2).

Continence

At the time of catheter removal and one month post-surgery, a significantly higher percentage of patients in the RS-RARP group had regained continence compared to those in the AP-RARP and anterior-RARP groups (P < 0.05), with no significant difference observed between the latter two groups. Three months post-surgery, continence recovery rates in both the AP-RARP and RS-RARP groups were significantly higher than in the anterior-RARP group (P < 0.05), while there was no statistically significant difference between the AP-RARP and RS-RARP groups (P > 0.05). By six months post-surgery, continence rates had equalized, showing no significant differences among the three groups (P > 0.05) (Table 3).

Discussion

Radical prostatectomy has long been recognized as one of the most technically demanding urological surgeries in laparoscopic practice. The introduction of robotic surgical systems has significantly reduced the complexity of prostatectomy, lowered perioperative complication risks, and facilitated the development of diverse surgical approaches and strategies10–12.

Postoperative urinary incontinence remains a common complication of radical prostatectomy. Robotic-assisted surgical techniques have been demonstrated improved postoperative urinary control rates13. Continuous advancements in RARP techniques have aimed at preserving and enhancing urinary and erectile functions, including the standardization of anatomical procedures14, optimization of suture techniques15, preservation of the puboprostatic ligament, nerve-sparing16, and bladder neck preservation17, among others. Similarly, the development of RS-RARP was an endeavor to further protect urinary function. Although definitive evidence of RS-RARP’s benefits on postoperative urinary control is not yet conclusive18, a substantial body of research supports its role in enhancing early postoperative urinary control7,19. Our center has performed RS-RARP for the past seven years and has observed that the Retzius-sparing approach significantly improves early urinary recovery in patients with localized prostate cancer20.

In 2000, Guy V et al. introduced the Montsouris technique, a laparoscopic-assisted radical prostatectomy that combines anterior and posterior approaches3. Their study at that time provided a detailed and in-depth analysis of this technique. However, it was noted that all procedures involved opening the Denonvilliers fascia and performing an extrafascial dissection. As our understanding of prostate anatomy has evolved, more diverse and adaptable approaches to fascia dissection have been developed. These advancements aim to reduce residual tumor presence while preserving neurovascular bundles, thereby optimizing postoperative urinary control and erectile function recovery. In our modified approach, we incorporated elements from the RS-RARP technique during the initial surgery phase. We began by addressing the seminal vesicles and vas deferens through the posterior wall of the bladder, selectively dissecting the posterior wall of the prostate using either an intrafascial or extrafascial approach. This posterior access offered clearer anatomical visibility compared to anterior-RARP, as the absence of urine enhanced the view of the prostate’s posterior wall. Following this, we transitioned to the classic anterior approach, which is technically simpler and advantageous for addressing the anterior and lateral walls and bladder neck of the prostate. After transecting the posterior bladder neck and opening the vesicoprostatic muscle, we were able to visualize the previously dissected posterior wall, effectively mitigating the challenges often encountered with posterior wall dissection in the anterior RARP approach. Furthermore, our modifications extended to several other procedural elements, including division of the prostate’s vascular pedicle, urethral anastomosis, and selection of the specimen retrieval incision site.

In classical anterior-RARP, posterior dissection of the prostate is typically performed after opening the bladder neck, necessitating traversal of the gap between the bladder neck and prostate. This approach often requires extensive lateral dissection to adequately expose the surgical field. In addition, bleeding and urine leakage can aimpair visibility, posing potential risks to the neurovascular bundles along the posterior and lateral prostate. By contrast, in AP-RARP, the posterior prostate is accessed through the Douglas pouch at the outset, providing clear exposure to the posterior surgical field. This approach streamlines the procedure and may contribute to improved early postoperative urinary control outcomes in AP-RARP compared to anterior-RARP.

When compared to anterior-RARP, the working space in RS-RARP is relatively restricted during urethral anastomosis reconstruction, adding to the procedural complexity. This technique requires a longer learning curve, particularly for surgeons with limited experience in anterior approach surgeries8. The technical demands not only make it challenging to master but may also extend operative time. In contrast, AP-RARP mitigates these difficulties by initially dissecting the posterior prostate and subsequently completing the remaining steps through a familiar anterior approach, thus simplifying the procedure significantly.

In anterior-RARP surgery, bleeding and urine leakage are frequently encountered during dissection of the seminal vesicles and vas deferens after opening the bladder neck, often impacting the surgical field visibility. Although patients with stages above T3a were excluded from this study, based on our surgical experience, AP-RARP provides enhanced visibility that facilitates a smoother procedure, especially in cases involving seminal vesicle invasion or intestinal involvement.

PSM remain a critical concern in prostatectomy. As noted in prior studies, the International Society of Urological Pathology (ISUP) score, Prostate Imaging Reporting and Data System (PI-RADS) score, and PSA were independent risk factors for PSM21. In recent years, a randomized controlled trial by Dalela et al. comparing anterior-RARP and RS-RARP suggested potential advantages for RS-RARP; however, the study found no statistically significant differences in PSM rates between the two surgical approaches for low- and intermediate-risk prostate cancer6. High-risk patients were not included in their analysis. As RS-RARP techniques have matured, many surgeons have begun applying RS-RARP to high-risk cases22. An increasing number of studies have since emerged. For instance, Barakat et al. conducted a meta-analysis of previous studies, demonstrating that in patients with ≤ pT2 tumors, RS-RARP was associated with a significantly higher PSM rate compared to standard RARP (relative risk [RR] = 1.39; 95% confidence interval [CI]=[1.01–1.91]). In patients with ≥ pT3 tumors, RS-RARP also showed a trend towards higher PSM, although without statistical significance (RR = 1.36; 95% CI=[0.74–2.50])9. Conversely, another meta-analysis by Phukan et al. reported no significant differences in PSM rates across any T stage23. In our study, the modified AP-RARP demonstrated a significantly lower PSM rate in T3a patients compared to RS-RARP. For T2 patients, although not statistically significant, a trend towards lower PSM was observed. Our exclusion criteria—omitting patients with ≥ T3b staging and positive lymph nodes, - may contribute to the slight variance in outcomes compared to other studies.

Patients and methods

General information

This manuscript presents a single-center retrospective cohort study. Formal ethical approval was not required under local policy, and all procedures adhered to relevant guidelines and regulations established by the Ethics Committee of Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital. The study was retrospectively registered with Clinical Trials (NCT 06020287, August 31, 2023), and all participants provided written informed consent. Clinical data from 233 patients treated at our urological center between September 2020 and May 2022 were retrospectively analyzed. The inclusion criteria as follows: (1) prostate cancer confirmed through pathological examination of biopsy specimen; (2) radical prostatectomy performed by a single surgeon; and (3) pathological TNM staging ≤ T3a. Exclusion criteria included: (1) prior neoadjuvant endocrine or radiation therapy; (2) clinical and pathological TNM staging ≥ T3b and N1; (3) evidence of distant metastasis prior to surgery; and (4) pre-existing urinary incontinence. During this period, the surgeon performed a total of 391 RARP procedures, with an average monthly case volume of 18.6 surgeries. From September 2020 to February 2021, consistent with the past seven years’ practice, 89 procedures were performed using the anterior-RARP approach. Beginning in March 2021, the surgeon adopted and mastered the RS-RARP technique in collaboration with our center, completing 126 RS-RARP procedures between February and October 2021. From August 2021 to May 2022, the surgeon further explored the AP-RARP technique, performing 136 procedures with this approach. To minimize the impact of the learning curve on study outcomes, the first 50 cases of both RS-RARP and AP-RARP were excluded from the statistical analysis. Fourteen patients were lost to follow-up within six months postoperatively (Fig. 1).

Surgical procedure

Patient preparation

Patients underwent routine preoperative evaluations to rule out any surgical or anesthetic contraindications. They were instructed to fast from midnight prior to surgery, and general anesthesia was administered. Bowel preparation was not performed. Heparin anticoagulation was administered only to patients identified as high risk for thrombosis. The abdominal area was shaved from the costal margins to the pubic bone.

Positioning and accessing

Patients were positioned in a steep Trendelenburg position. Following the induction of general anesthesia, the surgical site was sterilized and draped. All procedures were performed using the Vinci Si® robotic platform (Intuitive Surgical, Inc., Sunnyvale, CA, USA). Port placements were as follows: the second viewing scope was inserted one finger width above the umbilicus; the first robotic arm port was positioned approximately 7 cm to the left of the umbilical midline; the robotic third arm port was placed about 7 cm to the right of the midline; and the fourth robotic arm port was located around 8 cm lateral (or slightly above) the third arm at the level of the umbilicus. Two auxiliary trocars were positioned - one about 8 cm lateral to the first arm port, and the other approximately 6 cm above the midpoint between the first and second arm ports (Fig. 2). The abdominal cavity was insufflated to a pressure of 12 mmHg.

AP-RARP (supplementary material, video)

Dissection of the vas deferens and seminal vesicles: using a transperitoneal approach, the peritoneum was incised transversely at the level of the Douglas pouch (Fig. 3a), allowing identification and dissection of the bilateral vas deferens and seminal vesicles (Fig. 3b).

Operational steps of AP-RARP. (a) opening the peritoneum at the level of the Douglas pouch; (b) dissecting the bilateral vas deferens and seminal vesicles; (c) dissecting the posterior prostate; (d) opening the endopelvic fascia on both sides; (e) separating the prostate-bladder junction; (f) opening the bladder neck and meeting with the previously opened space on the back of the prostate; (g) dividing the vascular pedicles of both sides; (h) separating the urethra; (i) anastomosing of the bladder neck and urethra.

Dissection of the posterior prostate: for patients with localized low or intermediate-risk prostate cancer, an intrafascial dissection technique was used to preserve the bilateral neurovascular bundles (NVBs). In cases with suspected extracapsular extension on preoperative MRI or based on intraoperative assessment, single-sided NVB preservation was attempted. For patients with suspected residual prostate cancer or bilateral extracapsular extension on imaging, NVBs reservation was not undertaken. The intrafascial dissection technique involves following the plane between the Denonvilliers fascia and the prostate capsule to the apex of the prostate (Fig. 3c). In contrast, the interfascial/extrafascial dissection technique involves dissecting along the posterior aspect of the prostate in the fatty tissue behind the Denonvilliers fascia, extending around the rectum.

Exposure of the orostate anterior surface and suture of the dorsal venous complex (DVC): a high incision was made along the midline of the umbilical ligament and extended laterally along both lateral umbilical ligaments, opening the peritoneum, and expanding it bilaterally to access the retropubic space. The fatty connective tissue was cleared to expose the puboprostatic ligament and the prostate. The endopelvic fascia was incised bilaterally, either on the inner side (for intrafascial or interfascial dissection) or the outer side (for extrafascial dissection) (Fig. 3d). The puboprostatic ligament was then detached, and the DVC was ligated.

Opening of the bladder neck: the junction between the prostate and bladder was separated from superficial to deep, allowing for the urethral opening (Fig. 3e). The No. 4 arm was used to provide upward traction on the catheter. The bladder neck was opened to reveal the vesicoprostatic muscle, and dissection was proceeded until it connected with the previously accessed posterior space behind the prostate (Fig. 3f).

Dissection of the lateral prostate: the interfascial dissection technique was primarily used for the lateral prostate, dissecting down to the vascular pedicles along the space between the prostatic fascia and the endopelvic fascia. The intrafascial dissection technique involved following the plane between the prostatic capsule and the prostatic fascia, though in approximately half of the cases, fusion between the prostatic capsule and fascia prevented separation. The extrafascial dissection technique dissected along the space between the endopelvic fascia and the levator ani muscle. Hem-o-lok clips were applied to occlude the vascular pedicles, which were then divided with scissors, ensuring hemostasis and preserving the NVBs (Fig. 3g).

Separation of the urethra: traction on the urethra was released, and the posterior DVC was dissected near the suture. The urethra was then dissected at the prostate apex using scissors, with particular care taken to preserve the urethral sphincter and achieve complete prostate mobilization (Fig. 3h).

Anastomosis of the bladder neck and urethra: a 3 − 0 absorbable suture was used for continuous anastomosis of the bladder neck and urethra, beginning from the 6 o’clock position (Fig. 3i). If necessary, the bladder neck was narrowed by additional suturing before anastomosis.

RS-RARP and anterior-RARP

RS-RARP was performed using a transperitoneal approach as detailed in our previous study24. Anterior-RARP was performed under general anesthesia, also utilizing a transperitoneal approach in alignment with methods described by Montorsi et al.25. In all RS-RARP procedures, an intrafascial or interfascial dissection technique was employed with preservation of NVBs. NVB preservation in anterior-RARP followed principles similar to those used in AP-RARP.

PLA

PLA was performed after prostatectomy in patients with high-risk prostate cancer and those with a suspected risk of lymph node metastasis based on imaging assessments. The surgical technique was consistent with methods described by Bertram et al.26.

Evaluation indicators

The primary outcomes of the study were PSM rates and urinary continence recovery. Secondary outcomes included operating time, intraoperative blood loss, intraoperative and postoperative transfusion, postoperative infections (including incisional, intrapelvic, or urinary tract infections within two weeks post-surgery), anastomotic urinary leakage (confirmed by cystography), and other complications. Additionally, the postoperative pathological Gleason score and PSM were recorded, and pathological TNM staging was assessed based on histopathological results. Urinary continence was evaluated immediately after catheter removal and subsequently at 1, 3 and 6 months post-surgery with continence recovery defined as the use of 0–1 pad per day.

Statistical methods

Data analysis was conducted using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences version 26.0 (IBM SPSS, Armonk, NY). Normally distributed quantitative data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, with between-group comparisons conducted using one-way ANOVA, followed by LSD post-hoc pairwise comparisons. Non-normally distributed quantitative data were presented as medians (minimum, maximum), and analyzed with the Kruskal-Wallis rank sum test, followed by Bonferroni-adjusted post-hoc comparisons. Categorical data were presented as frequencies (%), with between-group comparisons, performed using the χ2 test. A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Conclusions

AP-RARP integrates the advantages of anterior-RARP and RS-RARP, enhancing early postoperative urinary control compared to anterior-RARP and reducing PSM rates relative to RS-RARP. This approach presents a viable surgical option for prostate cancer treatment. However, as a single-center retrospective study, this research is subject to biases associated with a smaller sample size, regional homogeneity, and variations in surgical timing. Further multi-center clinical studies are warranted to validate these findings.
