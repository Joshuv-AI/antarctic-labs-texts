Shunt Reversal After Transcatheter Tricuspid Valve Replacement
Authors: Hazem Omran, Volker Rudolph
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

Kreyden et al. present a compelling and, to date, first-reported case of hypoxemia due to right-to-left shunt reversal through a patent foramen ovale (PFO) following transcatheter tricuspid valve replacement (TTVR) with the 52-mm EVOQUE system.1 The case highlights an important and likely underrecognized complication of transcatheter tricuspid interventions that warrants greater awareness as the field of structural heart interventions continues to expand. We congratulate the authors for this interesting case report. However, a closer examination of the reported clinical and hemodynamic data reveals a more nuanced pathophysiology than a straightforward right ventricular (RV) failure after TTVR.

Complexity of the Hypoxemia: More Than Just Shunt Physiology

While the authors attribute the patient's hypoxemia primarily to right-to-left shunting through the PFO as a consequence of post-TTVR RV failure, the clinical trajectory suggests a more complex mechanism. The patient initially presented with profound hypoxemia requiring 10 L of supplemental oxygen to maintain a saturation of 86%, yet upon arrival to the emergency department, her saturation improved to 94% on only 6 L via nasal cannula. Pure right-to-left intracardiac shunt physiology would not be expected to respond to supplemental oxygen alone, as deoxygenated blood bypasses the pulmonary circulation entirely. This fluctuation suggests a mixed etiology in which intrapulmonary factors play a contributing role alongside the intracardiac shunt.

Indeed, the patient had significant pulmonary comorbidities, including pulmonary fibrosis with WHO group 3 pulmonary hypertension and a remote history of pulmonary embolism. These conditions cause ventilation-perfusion mismatch and diffusion impairment—both oxygen-responsive mechanisms that would layer on top of any intracardiac shunt component. While the immediate improvement in oxygen requirements from 10 to 2 L after PFO closure strongly supports a hemodynamically relevant right-to-left shunt, the residual oxygen requirement of 1-2 L at follow-up—attributed to baseline pulmonary fibrosis—confirms that the hypoxemia was not solely shunt-mediated.

The Hemodynamic Paradox: Normal Pressures, Profound Hypoxemia

Perhaps the most intriguing aspect of this case is the disconnect between hemodynamic data and clinical presentation. Right-heart catheterization revealed a mean right atrial (RA) pressure of only 4 mm Hg and a pulmonary capillary wedge pressure of 3 mm Hg. While the RA-to-left atrial gradient favored right-to-left shunting, a difference of merely 1 mm Hg seems insufficient to explain the degree of hypoxemia observed. Furthermore, an RA pressure of 4 mm Hg is within normal limits and does not align with the proposed mechanism of RV failure.

However, acute kidney injury, hyponatremia, and a markedly elevated pro-B-type natriuretic peptide of 5,429 pg/mL suggest neurohormonal activation consistent with right-heart failure.2 The failure to respond to diuresis with worsening renal function further supports that this was not merely a decompensation of underlying heart failure with preserved ejection fraction. One possible explanation is that hemodynamic measurements obtained during right-heart catheterization on high-flow oxygen supplementation may have transiently improved pulmonary hemodynamics and underestimated true RA pressures during episodes of significant desaturation.

Mechanistic Considerations: Reduced RA Compliance After Tricuspid Regurgitation Elimination

The authors correctly note that the shunt reversal was not flow-driven given the absence of intravalvular or paravalvular regurgitation. This points toward a pressure-driven mechanism. But how does one reconcile normal RA pressure with clinically significant right-to-left shunting? Recent literature demonstrates that TTVR results in immediate and complete cessation of tricuspid regurgitation, with an associated acute increase in total RV afterload. In an RV already compromised by pulmonary fibrosis, pulmonary hypertension, and intrinsic dysfunction, this abrupt hemodynamic shift may be poorly tolerated.2 Successful tricuspid regurgitation elimination may acutely decrease RA compliance, meaning that even modest pressure elevations—or phasic pressure swings during the cardiac cycle—could be sufficient to drive intermittent right-to-left shunting across the PFO.3

It is worth emphasizing that right-heart catheterization provides only a snapshot of mean pressures, whereas PFO shunting may occur preferentially during specific phases of the cardiac cycle—particularly during atrial contraction—when transient RA-to-left atrial pressure gradients may exist despite low mean pressures. This dynamic component may explain both the hemodynamic paradox and the variable response to supplemental oxygen.4

Broader Implications: Screening and Systematic Assessment

Despite the apparently low incidence of right-to-left shunt through PFO after TTVR, this complication is associated with significant morbidity and could increase mortality if not appropriately managed. As the volume of transcatheter mitral and tricuspid interventions continues to grow, this phenomenon may have been overlooked in many patients in the absence of systematic assessment. This concern extends beyond patients with pre-existing PFO. Many patients undergoing mitral transcatheter edge-to-edge repair have iatrogenic atrial septal defects (iASDs) from transseptal puncture that could behave similarly. Morikawa et al. demonstrated that 5% of patients post-MitraClip developed right-to-left shunting through iASD, and notably, only approximately one-third of those developed clinically relevant hypoxemia.5 The presence of severe tricuspid regurgitation, pulmonary hypertension, renal dysfunction, and elevated B-type natriuretic peptide were identified as risk factors for R-L shunt development.

Taken together, this case and the emerging literature support a call for systematic baseline assessment of interatrial shunt physiology in patients undergoing tricuspid valve interventions. This may include echocardiographic evaluation with bubble study both before and after the procedure. Close monitoring in the days following intervention is particularly warranted, as this case illustrates that clinically significant shunt reversal may present in a delayed fashion. In patients identified as high risk for RV dysfunction—particularly those with pre-existing pulmonary disease, pulmonary hypertension, or baseline RV impairment—concurrent PFO or iASD closure should be considered at the time of the index procedure to mitigate this potentially life-threatening complication.
