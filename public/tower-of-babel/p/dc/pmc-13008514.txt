Aortic Root Thrombosis in LVAD Patients
Authors: Adhvithi Pingili, Tarun Dalia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey words: aortic root thrombosis, aspiration thrombectomy, LVAD, management

We read about this interesting case of aortic root thrombus (ART) management by Dr Kazem et al1 with great enthusiasm. ART is an uncommon yet underrecognized complication in patients with continuous-flow left ventricular assist devices (LVAD). As per Carey et al2, who studied 197 primary implantation HM3 LVAD patients, 19 (9.6%) patients had ART. Multiple factors contribute to ART, including LVAD-induced left ventricular unloading and reduced preload from right ventricular failure, which decrease blood flow across the aortic valve, favoring stasis and clot formation.1, 2, 3, 4, 5 Others might be due to the surgical technique, affecting flow dynamics, and insufficient anticoagulation.2,3 ART is associated with serious adverse events, including myocardial infarction from coronary artery occlusion, ventricular arrhythmias like in this case, stroke from systemic thromboembolism, aortic valve insufficiency leading to the need for an aortic valve intervention, need for device exchange, and mortality.1, 2, 3 A few established ways to treat ART in the literature include intensification of systemic anticoagulation, systemic thrombolysis, valve replacement, and device exchange.4,5 Nonetheless, these approaches carry substantial risk like bleeding risk associated with increased anticoagulation and systemic thrombolysis, particularly in early postoperative period while valve replacement and device exchange carry procedural complication, such as stroke.1,4

Kazem et al1 applied percutaneous aspiration thrombectomy to manage acute ART, a technique well established in other thrombotic conditions, including pulmonary embolism, myocardial infarction, and acute limb ischemia, but novel in this setting. One recent case publication also utilized a similar technique of aspiration thrombectomy in LVAD patients and had good results.6 While this approach is promising, mechanical thrombectomy carries its own risks, most notably systemic thromboembolism. The size of the thrombus is a critical factor, as large thrombi increase the likelihood of fragmentation during the procedure, raising the risk of distal embolization and incomplete clot removal. The authors partially addressed this by using a cerebral embolic protective device, but this alone does not eliminate the risk of systemic embolism elsewhere. Other risks include worsening aortic valve insufficiency due to catheter manipulation of the aortic root and dissection of the coronary ostia. A careful preprocedural assessment of thrombus size and morphology using imaging is therefore essential to guide patient selection and procedural planning. In addition, the role of percutaneous aspiration thrombectomy in chronic aortic root thrombosis is unknown yet. Nonetheless, cases like this demonstrate the use of established techniques in a new patient population. In addition, they pave pathways for less-invasive treatment options for acute ART in LVAD patients, which is very timely given the overall increase in the number of LVAD patients.

It is important to note that none of the management options of ART described in the literature have been studied in large patient populations, emphasizing the need to individualize therapy and study outcomes in larger cohorts. Future studies should aim to explore adjustments in LVAD speed to allow intermittent aortic valve opening and the impact of the outflow graft anastomosis technique in relation to the ascending aorta to prevent ART.
