The establishment of a universal standard for both viral antigen and nucleid acid detection based on digital PCR
Authors: Manyu Li, Siyuan Yang, Tingting Ma, Alimire Abulikemu, Chunlei Zhang, Xun Cheng, Jing Li, Jia Lu, Pengfei Wang, Yong Guo, Donglai Liu, Linghang Wang, Haiwei Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundViral antigen rapid detection tests (Ag-RDTs) and PCR-based nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs) are essential for diagnosing infections like SARS-CoV-2. However, unlike nucleic acids which can be quantified precisely in copies, viral antigens lack a standardized unit of measurement, hindering precise analytical evaluation of Ag-RDTs and comparison with NAATs.MethodsWe established a universal national standard for SARS-CoV-2 antigen and nucleic acid based on an Omicron BA.1 strain, inactivated using β-propiolactone (BPL). Its concentration was assigned via multi-laboratory digital PCR (dPCR). Clinical samples were tested with Ag-RDTs and NAATs. We also compared the impact of heat and BPL inactivation on detection using dPCR, qPCR, and sequencing. The standard was then used to evaluate the limits of detection (LoDs) of commercial Ag-RDTs and NAATs using a common unitage.ResultsClinical samples’ results showed that antigen positivity was correlated with higher nucleic acid titers. Both BPL inactivation and heat inactivation maintained comparable nucleic acid titers, but BPL inactivation preserved better antigen activity. The national standard concentration was assigned as 1.04 × 108 Unit/mL (standard uncertainty: 3.48 × 106 Unit/mL). Using this standard, NAATs exhibited lower LoDs than Ag-RDTs, though some Ag-RDT sensitivity approached NAAT levels. Most commercial assays met or exceeded their claimed LoDs, with all claimed NAAT LoDs falling within 2-fold of measured values. The standard enabled direct cross-format LoD comparison.ConclusionWe established and validated a BPL-inactivated universal national standard specifically designed to overcome the difficulties in quantifying antigen and enabling direct sensitivity comparison between SARS-CoV-2 Ag-RDTs and NAATs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12985-025-02954-w.Keywords: Standard, Virus, Antigen, Nucleic acid, SARS-CoV-2

Background

Viral antigen rapid detection tests (Ag-RDTs), such as severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) Ag-RDTs, have been widely used around the world, not only in low- and middle-income countries, but also in high-income regions, and recently in China as well [1, 2]. While PCR-based nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs) have been considered the gold standard for the diagnosis of virus infection which offer great sensitivity to detect minuscule amounts of viral RNA, Ag-RDTs provides an attractive alternative that’s faster, lower in cost and instrument-free [1]. These advantages of Ag-RDTs have been leveraged by many countries in an effort to improve public health management, although having an inferior limit of detection (LoD) compared with NAATs [3].

High-quality and stable supply of Ag-RDTs that allow the rapid identification of potentially infected individuals and therefore minimize its transmission will be crucial for public COVID-19 management [3, 4]. However, unlike that for NAATs, WHO international standard (IS) for SARS-CoV-2 antigen has not been established until 2022, making it difficult to precisely evaluate its analytical performance, especially the LoD of Ag-RDTs during the early stage of pandemic. This suggests that the development of antigen standard reference is complicated and time-consuming. Commercially available reference standards for SARS-CoV-2 diagnostic tests varied on their inactivation methods used, such as heat, gamma-irradiated, or beta-propiolactone (BPL) inactivated, and on the units used on viral titers, such as genome copies/mL, PFU/mL or TCID50/mL. These variations impose great challenge when comparing among commercialized tests, especially between Ag-RDTs and NAATs, which is pivotal for making scientifically informed decision on assay uses in accordance with public health policy.

Quantification cycle (Cq) values that indicate viral genome copies by NAATs have been reported to correlate with patient outcome and proposed to guide patient management, and adopted as performance reference for other diagnostic assays [5]. For example, WHO proposed that Ag-RDTs should reach a minimum sensitivity of 80% in the intended population with Cq values < 25–30 by NAATs [6]. Furthermore, a linear correlation between the number of viral genome copies and nucleocapsid protein (the most commonly used analyte for Ag-RDTs) had been suggested by a previous study showing each viral particle comprised an average of 26 ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complexes [7]. However, conversion between different reporting units of LoDs and comparing assay performance between NAATs and Ag-RDTs have been challenging and brought great confusion.

Thus, in this study, we aimed to establish a universal national standard for both SARS-CoV-2 antigen and nucleid acid detection based on digital PCR and validate its use in performance evaluation of Ag-RDTs and NAATs assays.

Methods

Clinical samples

During August 2023 to September 2023, a total of ten SARS-COV-2 infected patients were enrolled in this study. Their throat swabs were collected for SARS-COV-2 antigen and nucleic acid tests for one consecutive week starting from the confirmation of infection. Then, these samples were tested by two antigen test kits which all used immunochromatographic colloidal gold method, and five nucleic acid test kits which used digital PCR (dPCR), qPCR and isothermal microfluidic chip method, respectively. The Antigen was tested on-site, while nucleic acid was sent to the laboratory for testing. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Ditan Hospital, Capital Medical University (DTEC-KY2023-018-01).

The impact of heat inactivation and BPL inactivation on nucleic acid detection and sequencing

To optimize the inactivation methods, a SARS-CoV-2 culture (Omicron strain) was processed as following described: uninactivated, inactivated at 56℃ for 30 min, inactivated at 56℃ for 60 min, inactivated at 65℃ for 60 min, inactivated by BPL (SERVA Electrophoresis GmbH, Heidelberg, Germany) at final volume ratio of 1:4000 for 48 h, inactivated by BPL for 48 h followed by 4℃ for 48 h, inactivated by BPL for 48 h followed by 4℃ for 72 h, inactivated by BPL for 48 h followed by −70℃ for 48 h. Then viral total RNA was extracted with the QIAamp Viral RNA Mini Kits (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Their virus titers were detected by dPCR using the TD-1 system (TargetingOne, Beijing, China).

Five SARS-CoV-2 cultures, including cultures of SARS-CoV-2 Beta, WT, Delta, Gamma, and Omicron strains, were provided by Sinovac Biotech Ltd and were subjected to heat inactivation and BPL inactivation, resulting in a total of 10 samples. These samples were lyophilized into 3 mL vials with 0.5 mL per vial and stored at − 20 °C. The nucleic acids of these samples were extracted by BG-Abot-96 Nucleic Acid Extraction System (BioGerm, Shanghai, China) following manufacturer’s instructions.

The dPCR was performed using Naica crystal digital PCR system (Stilla Technologies, Villejuif, France). The primers were listed in Supplementary Table 1.

The qPCR was performed by the 2019-nCoV Nucleic Acid Detection Kit (qPCR method) (BioGerm, Shanghai, China) following manufacturer’s instructions.

The Sanger sequencing was targeted 1000 bp regions of the ORF1a/b and N genes that covered the detection areas of dPCR and qPCR. The bidirectional sequencing was performed.

In the next-generation sequencing, whole genome sequencing was performed for each sample using the Illumina NovaSeq6000 platform, with an expected sequencing data output of 6 Gb and PE150bp sequencing mode. First, FastQC and fastp software were used to perform data quality control and low-quality filtering on the obtained raw data. The actual data yield, proportion of COVID-19 data, coverage and average depth were calculated to evaluate the data quality of each sample. The filtered data were assembled using SPAdes software, and N50 and N90 values for each sample were calculated to assess the assembly quality. Variant detection for each sample was performed using samtools and bcftools software to differentiate the variant strains of each sample. Finally, evolutionary analysis (maximum likelihood method) for all samples was conducted using FastTree software.

Preparation of national standard candidate

A SARS-CoV-2 culture (Omicron BA.1 hCoV-19/Hong Kong/HKU-691/2021, GISAID accession number EPI_ISL_7138045) was provided by the Wuhan Institute of Biological Products Co., Ltd. The strain was cultured with Vero cells (ATCC CCL-81) at 37 ℃ in a 5% carbon dioxide incubator. The cultured viruses were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 min at 4 ℃ and the resulting supernatant was collected. The supernatant obtained after centrifugation was inactivated by incubating with BPL (SERVA Electrophoresis GmbH, Heidelberg, Germany) at final volume ratio of 1:4000 for 48 h at 4 ℃, and then hydrolyzed at 37℃ for 2 h. After inactivation, the concentration of the supernatants was determined using a digital PCR detection kit (TargetingOne Corporation, China). The SARS-CoV-2 stocks were diluted to approximately 1 × 108 copies/mL in a universal buffer (10 mM PBS buffer pH 7.5, 1% human serum albumin, 0.1% trehalose). Bulk preparations were lyophilized into 3 mL vials with 0.5 mL per vial and stored at − 20 °C.

Viral RNA extraction and One-Step RT-dPCR for value assignment

The RNA extraction process was performed in duplicate for the quantification of the reference standard candidate. To quantify the SARS-CoV-2 reference standard candidate using the digital PCR method, three participants used the TD-1 system (TargetingOne, Beijing, China), and the other four participants used the Naica system (Stilla Technologies, Villejuif, France), OsciDrop Flex system (OS-300, Maccura, Chengdu, China), Starry 10 K system (DAAN Gene, Guangzhou, China), and MicroDrop-100 system (Forevergen, Guangzhou, China).

Evaluation of LoDs of commercial antigen and nucleic acid diagnostic assays

The quantified reference standard was serially diluted and applied to seven antigen diagnostic assays and LoD probit regression analysis for seven nucleic acid diagnostic assays. In the nucleic acid diagnostic assays, a total of at most 10 concentration levels were tested with multiple replicates per concentration according to the manufacturer’s instructions with an additional 10 replicates of viral transport medium as the blank control. Probit regression analysis of 95% hit rates was performed with SPSS PASW Statistics v18.0 statistical software package (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using the SPSS PASW Statistics v18.0 statistical software package (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL). The coefficient of variation (CV) was calculated by dividing the population standard deviation by the average value. Data between different groups were compared using Student’s t-test. A P-value of < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Clinical characteristics of SARS-COV-2 infected patients

During August 2023 to September 2023, a total of ten SARS-COV-2 infected patients were enrolled in this study. Their throat swabs were collected for SARS-COV-2 antigen and nucleic acid tests consecutively for one week.

Two antigen test kits and five nucleic acid test kits were used, which were named kit A to G. Kit A to E were nucleic acid test kits. Kits A, B, C utilized the qPCR method and D used the dPCR method, while kit E used the isothermal microfluidic chip method. Nucleic acid test kits A, B, and E only reported qualitative results (positive or negative), whereas kits C and D reported quantitative results (copies/mL). Kits F and G were antigen tests kits, which all employed the immunochromatographic colloidal gold method and only reported qualitative results (positive or negative).

The results showed that nucleic acid test kits A, B, C, and D exhibited good consistency (Fig. 1). However, the sensitivity of nucleic acid test kit E was lower than that of the other four kits. In patients 1, 4, 6, and 7, when their nucleic acid levels were below 4 log10 copies/mL, kit E yielded negative results. In patients 8, 9, and 10, when their nucleic acid levels were below 5 log10 copies/mL, kit E showed negative results. These findings suggested that the isothermal microfluidic chip method (kit E) did not outperform qPCR in detecting SARS-CoV-2 nucleic acid.

By detecting SARS-COV-2 antigen, we found that antigen test kit G showed better sensitivity and stability than kit F (Fig. 1). We then compared the nucleic acid and antigen results. The findings showed that positive antigen results were usually associated with higher nucleic acid titers, while negative antigen results were often associated with lower nucleic acid titers. Thus, we assumed that SARS-CoV-2 antigen results correlate with SARS-CoV-2 nucleic acid levels.

Different inactivation methods had varying effects on the detection of SARS-CoV-2 antigen and nucleic acid

Our previous study demonstrating in contrast to heated inactivation, BPL inactivation had little effect on the detection by Ag-RDTs [8]. Since that prolonged storage after BPL inactivation may affect the virus titer, in this study we first optimized the inactivation methods. After different inactivation processing, the virus titers were tested by dPCR. The results showed that none of these inactivation methods led to significantly different results compared to uninactivated samples (Supplementary Fig. 1). Thus, we chose inactivation at 56℃ for 60 min and inactivation by BPL for 48 h for the following experiments.

To compare the impact of heat and BPL inactivation methods on nucleic acid detection, after heat inactivation and BPL inactivation of the SARS-CoV-2 cultures, the detection results were then evaluated using different techniques such as dPCR, qPCR, next-generation sequencing, and Sanger sequencing. A total of five SARS-CoV-2 cultures, each belonging to a different strain, were prepared, and each culture was separately inactivated by heat and BPL.

The dPCR and qPCR results showed that, despite the use of different SARS-CoV-2 strains, the titers of BPL-inactivated samples were significantly higher than those of heat-inactivated samples (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2). However, the differences between these two inactivation methods were less than 0.5 log10 copies/mL. The detection of both ORF and N genes yielded the same results. These results suggested that BPL inactivation had less effect on the detection of SARS-CoV-2 nucleic acid.

Establishment of a universal national standard for standardization of both SARS-CoV-2 antigen and RNA assays

Since we found that the SARS-CoV-2 antigen results correlate with SARS-CoV-2 nucleic acid levels in clinical samples, we aimed to develop a national standard which allows cross-assessment of sensitivity between Ag-RDTs and NAATs. A standard candidate was provided to seven laboratories equipped with five different dPCR platforms. The dPCR assays targeting the ORF1ab and N regions were performed by these participants. The quantification results were reported by the participants and analyzed to determine the consensus true value of the virus concentrations.

The results from all participants showed a high degree of consistency on a coarse scale with means of 1.27 × 108 copies/mL (8.1 log10 copies/mL, CV = 3.5%) and 1.33 × 108 copies/mL (8.1 log10 copies/mL, CV = 2.1%) for the concentrations of ORF1ab and N gene, respectively (Fig. 3). As previously described [9], the value of this standard candidate was calculated. The arithmetic mean of concentration of ORF1ab gene and N gene was used to calculate the virus concentration. The standard uncertainty of the reference standard was obtained by combining of the standard uncertainties of ORF1ab and N genes. The concentration of virus particles is determined with a reporting universal unit of 1.04 × 108 Unit/mL (U/mL) with a standard uncertainty of 3.48 × 106 U/mL. This unit is calibrated based on nucleic acid copy number detected by dPCR, as 1 unit corresponds to 1 copy of SARS-CoV-2 nucleic acid.

Limit of detection evaluated using the universal standard for both SARS-CoV-2 antigen rapid detection tests (Ag-RDTs) and nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs)

This universal standard allowed us for an assessment across multiple commercial Ag-RDTs and NAATs kits. First, we applied the reference standard with consensus true value to evaluate the LoDs of seven commercial SARS-CoV-2 antigen diagnostic kits. The methods of these kits included colloidal gold, latex bead, immunofluorescence (IF) and chemiluminescence (CL). Using the reference standard established in this study, we measured LoDs of these seven kits and compared them with their claimed LoDs. Our results showed that three can meet or exceed the average of LoD, and four cannot (Fig. 4).

Then, we also applied the reference standard to evaluate the LoDs of seven commercial SARS-CoV-2 NAATs assays. The tests covered reverse-transcription loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP), metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS), reverse-transcription cross-priming amplification (CPA), dPCR and PCR. Using the reference standard established in this work, we measured LoDs for all target genes of seven diagnostic assays based on probit regression analysis of 95% hit rates (Figs. 4 and 5). Our study demonstrates that most diagnostic assays included in the study can meet or exceed the claimed sensitivity and are reliable for SARS-CoV-2 nucleic acid detection. All 12 claimed LoDs fall within the 2-fold region of the measured LoDs. The results showed that the reference standard is suitable for the assessment of various NATs and allows for comparable LoD studies of various detection methodologies.

Previously, we have shown that BPL inactivation can avoid the impact of heat-inactivation on Ag-RDTs detection [8]. Our results showed that BPL inactivated standard can also be used for the evaluation of sensitivity of various NAATs assays.

We found that the LoDs of NAATs were generally lower than that of Ag-RDTs (102 to 104 U/mL and 104 to 106 U/mL, respectively), more so with clinical site used than home tests (Fig. 4). In the Ag-RDTs, the CL-based assay showed better sensitivity than assays used colloidal gold, latex bead and IF. In the NAATs, we found that the LoDs of dPCR and PCR were lower than the LoDs of mNGS and RT-LAMP.

Surprisingly, we found similar sensitivity even between Ag-RDTs and NAATs in some cases (Fig. 4), emphasizing the importance of cross-format assay evaluation when making approvals for specific uses, and for making strategic decisions on using different tests for public health management.

Discussion

A critical challenge in evaluating viral Ag-RDTs has been the difficulty in precisely quantifying antigen concentration due to the lack of a standardized, universally accepted unit, unlike nucleic acids which can be quantified as copies. This has hindered the precise analytical assessment of Ag-RDTs and the direct comparison with NAATs. In this study, we established a new approach for the quantitative reference material of SARS-CoV-2 antigens based on the dPCR nucleic acid quantification method. This national standard not only reflects the performance differences between SARS-CoV-2 antigen kits but also allows for comparison with SARS-CoV-2 nucleic acid kits. The establishment of such a national standard can provide regulatory agencies with accurate product performance information, and the performance claims based on this national standard can also offer users a point of reference for selection. During the development of SARS-CoV-2 antigen reagents, both the TCID50 value and the value annotated by this national standard can be offered, which can be utilized in quantity traceability based on dPCR.

We used two antigen test kits and five nucleic acid test kits to detect clinical samples of SARS-CoV-2-infected patients. We found that the titers of SARS-CoV-2 nucleic acid were correlated with SARS-CoV-2 antigen results. The positive antigen results were usually associated with higher nucleic acid titers. Due to different forms of N protein in clinical samples [10], the correlation between antigen detection results and viral RNA loads may demonstrate variability. Valiente et al. also found that SARS-CoV-2 NCap antigen was correlated with RNA concentration [11]. They measured the absolute N gene concentration by reverse transcriptase digital PCR (RT-dPCR). By using an ultrasensitive and quantitative antigen assay, Pollock et al. showed that log-transformed antigen concentrations correlated tightly with RNA Ct values [12]. Thus, we can assume that there is a certain relationship between SARS-CoV-2 nucleic acid and SARS-CoV-2 antigen concentrations.

Thus, we established this national standard for both SARS-CoV-2 nucleic acid and SARS-CoV-2 antigen detection. We used dPCR to assign its value as 1.04 × 108 U/mL with a standard uncertainty of 3.48 × 106 U/mL. This unit is calibrated based on nucleic acid copy number detected by dPCR, as 1 unit corresponds to 1 copy of SARS-CoV-2 nucleic acid. According to Yao et al. [7], each viral particle comprised an average of 26 ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complexes. Thus, 1 unit is also assumed to be comprised of an average 26 SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid protein. Several studies have evaluated the performance of antigen assays based on the copies or Ct results of SARS-CoV-2 nucleic acid [13–17].Currently, most SARS-CoV-2 antigen detection assays were based on antibody detection of the SARS-CoV-2 N protein [18]. In 2020, Yao et al. investigated the molecular landscape of the SARS-CoV-2 virus and found that RNPs were comprised of N proteins and RNA. Their results showed that an average of 26 of RNPs was identified in each virion, which means there was a proportional relationship between the N antigen and the nucleic acid copy number in purified virus [7]. Therefore, it is viable to assign values to SARS-CoV-2 antigen reference materials based on nucleic acid copy number.

Recently, several studies have reported that the sensitivity of the SARS-CoV-2 antigen tests against the Omicron variant has decreased [13, 19]. It has been shown that the poorer performance of Ag-RDTs against Omicron variant may be related with the mutations of N protein [20]. In this case, using recombinant N antigen as a reference material cannot fully reflect the test’s ability to detect clinical samples. Thus, assigning values of the national standard based on copy number can mitigate the effects of N protein mutations, enabling a more accurate assessment of the detection capability of antigen assays.

We also compared the effect of different inactivation methods on the detection of SARS-CoV-2 nucleic acid and antigen. Our previous study has shown that the BPL inactivation method did not affect the performance of national reference panels for SARS-CoV-2 antigen detection kits [8]. In this study, we analyzed the difference of impact of different inactivation methods on nucleic acid detection. By using dPCR and qPCR, we found that the nucleic acid titers of different SARS-CoV-2 variants were less affected by the BPL inactivation.

By using this national standard inactivated by BPL, we have compared its application in various commercial Ag-RDTs and NAATs kits. In the Ag-RDTs, assays used CL showed the lowest LoD. The CL assay can offer higher sensitivity and increased output, and supports automated operation. But it is not suitable for self-testing or use in resource-limited regions. We found that the national standard can be used in assessing the Lod of nucleic acid and antigen assays based on different methods. This suggests that combining SARS-CoV-2 nucleic acid and antigen reference materials into a single standard could be more convenient and facilitate direct comparisons between nucleic acid and antigen assays, which can be used in the development of in vitro diagnostics standard during the early phase of emerging infectious disease outbreak.

There are several limitations of this study. First, clinical samples should be tested for validation in further independent studies. A future large-scale clinical study is warranted to further validate the clinical correlation and utility of this standard. Second, more research is still needed on the purification of virus cultures to make the assignment of values more accurate.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we established a universal national standard with a reporting universal unit of U/mL, which allows cross-assessment of sensitivity between Ag-RDTs and NAATs. We used different inactivation ways and assign its value by dPCR method. Also, this national standard has been validated for use in performance evaluation of Ag-RDTs and NAATs assays.
