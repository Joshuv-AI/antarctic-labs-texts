APPL1 concentration as a potential biomarker in early-onset preeclampsia: correlations with adiponectin-leptin dysregulation and adverse neonatal outcomes
Authors: Qianmeng Zhu, Yuzhen Zhou, Yan Huang, Xiaoli Chi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPreeclampsia (PE) is a severe pregnancy complication associated with metabolic dysregulation. APPL1 (adaptor protein, phosphotyrosine interacting with PH domain and leucine zipper 1) is a critical adaptor protein in adiponectin (ADP) and insulin signaling. Its role in preeclampsia (PE) remains underexplored. This study investigates APPL1 as a potential biomarker for early-onset PE and its links to adipokine imbalance, glycolipid metabolism, and neonatal outcomes.MethodsA case-control study enrolled 116 pregnant women (45 PE vs. 71 controls) from a single center. Serum APPL1, ADP, and leptin (LEP) levels of all 116 participants were measured. Glycolipid metabolism indices and neonatal outcomes were collected. The correlation was analyzed by simple linear regression, and the logistic regression was used to evaluate the predictive effects of APPL1, ADP and LEP on PE.Results(1) Prepregnancy BMI (22.15 ± 1.80 vs. 21.03 ± 1.99 kg/m2, p = 0.0028), APPL1 (82.65 ± 8.27 vs. 62.21 ± 12.41 pg/mL, p = 0.0047) and LEP (12.60 ± 2.83 vs. 8.66 ± 1.77 ng/mL, p = 0.0004). (2) APPL1 positively correlated with ADP only in PE (r = 0.0921, p = 0.0456). (3) Logistic regression identified APPL1 (OR = 1.405, p = 0.003) and LEP (OR = 3.618, p = 0.006) as PE risk factors, with ADP as protective (OR = 0.299, p = 0.003). (4) APPL1 showed extensive relationships with glycolipid dysregulation in PE (p < 0.05). (5) In PE, APPL1 inversely predicted adverse neonatal outcomes, including neonatal birth weight (r=-0.0149, p = 0.0396), ponderal index (r=-0.0198, p = 0.0024) and Apgar score (r=-0.0368, p = 0.0225).ConclusionsElevated APPL1 is a potential and novel biomarker for early-onset PE, and reflecting adiponectin-leptin dysregulation, glycolipid metabolic disturbances, and adverse neonatal prognosis. Its integration into clinical prediction models warrants further validation.Keywords: Preeclampsia, APPL1, Adiponectin, Leptin, Biomarker

Introduction

Preeclampsia (PE) is a pregnancy-specific, multi-system progressive disorder typically occurring after 20 weeks of gestation [1, 2], and is clinically classified into early-onset (diagnosed before 34 weeks) and late-onset subtypes based on the gestational age at diagnosis. PE is one of the most common complications in pregnancy, with a global prevalence ranging from 2% to 8% [2, 3], which seriously threatens the health of pregnant women and fetuses. Characterized primarily by hypertension and proteinuria, it represents one of the leading causes of maternal and perinatal mortality [3, 4]. Its pathological features include placental hypoperfusion, systemic vascular endothelial injury, and hyperactive inflammatory responses [1, 5]. The etiology of PE includes maternal, placental, fetal pathological factors and adverse environmental factors during pregnancy. In recent years, with in-depth research into the pathophysiological mechanisms of PE, accumulating evidence indicates that metabolic dysregulation may play a potential role in the onset and progression of PE [6–8]. Aberrations in glycolipid metabolism are not only closely associated with PE development [6], but may also impact fetal growth and neonatal outcomes.

APPL1 (adaptor protein, phosphotyrosine interacting with PH domain and leucine zipper 1) is a highly conserved adaptor protein that serves critical functions in insulin signaling and adiponectin (ADP) signaling pathways. It acts as a key positive regulator of glucose homeostasis [9–11]. Although the roles of ADP and leptin (LEP) in PE have been partially elucidated [12–15], the expression pattern, clinical significance, and relationship with metabolic disorders of APPL1, the core downstream signaling molecule of ADP, remain undefined in PE. Whether APPL1 could serve as a novel biomarker or therapeutic target for PE requires further exploration [16].

The primary objective of this study was to investigate the relationship between maternal serum APPL1 levels and the risk of early-onset PE, while concurrently exploring its potential interactions with established clinical parameters (adiponectin-leptin dysregulation, glycolipid metabolic disturbances, and adverse neonatal prognosis). We attempted to identify APPL1 as a novel biomarker and provide mechanistic insights into the metabolic dysregulation characteristics of early-onset PE. This understanding may provide information for future diagnosis and treatment strategies targeting APPL1 related pathways to achieve early prediction, mitigate disease progression, and improve neonatal outcomes.

Materials

Inclusion criteria

PE: women aged 20 to 34 who can be diagnosed as early-onset PE according to Diagnosis and treatment of hypertension and pre-eclampsia in pregnancy: a clinical practice guideline in China (2020) in the Suzhou Hospital of Integrated Traditional Chinese and Western Medicine from January 2023 to February 2024. Control: gestational age-matched healthy pregnant women in the same period.

Exclusion criteria

① Other pregnancy complications: such as gestational diabetes mellitus, placental abnormality, abortion, and premature delivery, etc.; ② Severe organ dysfunction: such as severe hepatic and renal insufficiency; ③ Other systemic diseases: such as severe immune system disorders, neurological diseases, cardiovascular diseases, and coagulation dysfunction, etc.; ④ Fetal abnormalities: including fetal malformations, intrauterine growth restriction, fetal demise, and other adverse pregnancy outcomes; ⑤ Infectious diseases: such as genital tract infections and intrauterine infections, etc.; ⑥ In the 3 months before pregnancy, used drugs that are prohibited or with caution in pregnant women, such as anti-psychotic, anti-epileptic, anti-tubercular, anti-tumor drugs, etc.; ⑦ In the 6 months before pregnancy, received allogeneic blood transfusion, transplantation, stem cell therapy and immunotherapy; ⑧ Incomplete data: inability to obtain complete clinical records or follow-up data; ⑨ Other special circumstances: such as the patient’s inability or unwillingness to sign an informed consent form, and multiple gestations, etc.

Diagnostic criteria of PE

After 20 weeks of gestation, the pregnant woman presents with a systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg and/or a diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg, accompanied by any one of the following: proteinuria quantification ≥ 0.3 g/24 h, or urine protein-to-creatinine ratio ≥ 0.3, or random urine protein ≥ (+) (the method of examination when protein quantification is not available); or in the absence of proteinuria, accompanied by involvement of any one organ or system, such as the cardiovascular, pulmonary, hepatic, or renal systems, or abnormal changes in the hematologic, gastrointestinal, or neurologic systems, or placental-fetal compromise.

In this study, PE was further divided into mild-preeclampsia (MPE) and severe-preeclampsia (SPE). In pregnant women with PE, the presence of any of the following manifestations is indicative of SPE (others are MPE): ① Uncontrolled persistent hypertension: Systolic blood pressure ≥ 160 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 110 mmHg; ② Persistent headache, visual disturbances, or other central nervous system abnormalities; ③ Persistent upper abdominal pain with signs of subcapsular hepatic hematoma or hepatic rupture; ④ Abnormal liver function: Elevated levels of alanine aminotransferase or aspartate aminotransferase; ⑤ Renal impairment: Proteinuria > 2.0 g/24 h; oliguria (urine output < 400 mL in 24 h, or < 17 mL/h), or serum creatinine level > 106 µmol/L; ⑥ Hypoproteinemia with ascites, pleural effusion, or pericardial effusion; ⑦ Hematologic abnormalities: Persistent thrombocytopenia with platelet count < 100 × 109/L; microangiopathic hemolysis, manifested by anemia, elevated serum lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) level, or jaundice; ⑧ Cardiac failure; ⑨ Pulmonary edema; ⑩ Fetal growth restriction, oligohydramnios, fetal demise, or placental abruption.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study design, protocol and informed consent form were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Suzhou Hospital of Integrated Traditional Chinese and Western Medicine and all participants freely signed theinformed consent upon enrollment in the study. The approval number is 2023(022).

Sample collection and processing

Blood samples are collected using evacuated tubes containing a clot activator and are allowed to stand for 15–30 min to permit spontaneous coagulation. Immediately centrifuge at 4℃ for 3,000 r/min for 15 min, and then use ELISA to determine the levels of APPL1 (PA5-18643, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., US), ADP, and LEP (MM-1535H1, MM-0084H1, Jiangsu Meimian Industrial Co., Ltd., CN). At the same time, collected information for research from medical records that do not contain personal identification. Including prepregnancy body mass index (pBMI), systolic pressure, diastolic pressure, mean arterial pressure (MAP), fasting blood glucose (FBG), fasting insulin (FINS), triglyceride (TG), total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein (LDL-C), neonatal birth weight (NBW), ponderal index, and Apgar score (at 5 min after birth). Further calculate Homeostasis Model Assessment (HOMA) indices: HOMA-IR (Insulin Resistance index) = FPG × FINS/22.5; HOMA-IS (Insulin Sensitivity index) = 1/(HOMA-IR); HOMA-β (β-cell function index) = 20 × FINS/(FPG − 3.5).

It should be particularly noted that the maternal serum samples used for analysis in this study were collected during routine prenatal examinations in the mid-trimester (20 to 28 weeks for the diagnosis of PE) at the obstetric outpatient clinic. The samples utilized were residual serum remaining after completion of clinically indicated prenatal screening tests. Further considerations, women diagnosed with, or at high risk for, conditions like PE in the third trimester (over 28 weeks gestation) are routinely admitted to the inpatient obstetric unit for closer monitoring and management. Residual serum samples from these inpatient admissions were not included in the current study cohort to maintain consistency in the sample source (outpatient clinic) and timing (20 to 28 weeks). A clearer explanation is that all the above indicators come from the mid-trimester (20 to 28 weeks).

Statistical analysis

The whole process of research and analysis were carried out under the Triple Blind, and each group was distinguished by code. For data that followed a normal distribution and had homogeneous variances, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for between-group comparisons, and the least significant difference (LSD) method was applied for multiple comparisons. If the data did not have homogeneous variances, Tamhane’s T2 method in a nonparametric test was chosen. The correlation was analyzed by simple linear regression, and the logistic regression was used to evaluate the predictive effects of APPL1, ADP and LEP on PE. Data that followed a normal distribution were described statistically as mean ± standard deviation (x ± s). General clinical data did not obey normal distribution and were statistically described in quartiles (p25, median, p75). A p-value less than 0.05 indicated a statistical difference, while a p-value less than 0.01 indicated a statistically significant difference.

Application program or software

Excel 16.6 (Microsoft Corporation, released 2016) was used for data collection and the preliminary processing. SPSS 22.0 (IBM, released 2019) was used for statistical analysis. Prism 9.0 (Graphpad, Inc., released 2020) is used for visualization of statistical results.

Results

General statistical description

According to the inclusion and exclusion criteria in the experimental design, a total of 181 cases were selected to meet the requirements. After 65 cases were excluded according to exclusion criteria, a total of 116 analyzable cases were obtained (Fig. 1), including PE (MPE 31 cases and SPE 14 cases) and CON (71 cases). Indicators used for analysis are divided into three parts, including monitoring index (APPL1, ADP and LEP), metabolism index (FPG, FINS, TG, TC, HDL-C and LDL-C) and neonatal outcome (gestational age at delivery, BNW, PI and AS). Of these, the level of BMI (22.15 ± 1.80 vs. 21.03 ± 1.99 kg/m2, p = 0.0028), systolic pressure (149.91 ± 8.36 vs. 110.42 ± 9.53 mmHg, p < 0.0001), diastolic pressure (102.78 ± 7.99 vs. 75.31 ± 7.27 mmHg, p < 0.0001), LEP (40.40 ± 9.29 vs. 87.01 ± 5.73 mmHg, p < 0.0001), APPL1 (82.65 ± 8.27 vs. 62.21 ± 12.41 pg/mL, p = 0.0047), LEP (12.60 ± 2.83 vs. 8.66 ± 1.77 ng/mL, p = 0.0004), HDL-C (1.12 ± 0.21 vs. 1.55 ± 0.38 mmol/L, p < 0.0001), LDL-C (3.15 ± 1.00 vs. 2.71 ± 0.68 mmol/L, p = 0.0040) and gestational age at delivery (p < 0.0001) were significantly different between PE and CON (Table 1). Furthermore, we performed additional calculations on the monitoring indices. Compared to the Control, the expression level of APPL1 was increased by 32.9% (82.65 vs. 62.21), ADP was decreased by 26.7% (8.58 vs. 11.71), and Leptin was increased by 45.5% (12.60 vs. 8.66) in the PE.

Correlation analysis of APPL1, ADP and LEP between PE and CON

The differences of APPL1, ADP and LEP were compared respectively. The level of APPL1 in MPE (p < 0.0001) and SPE (p < 0.0001) were significantly higher than that CON (Fig. 2A). Similarly, the level of LEP in MPE (p < 0.0001) and SPE (p < 0.0001) were significantly increased (Fig. 2B). However, ADP in MPE (p < 0.0001) and SPE (p = 0.0013) were decreased (Fig. 2C). Consistently, there were no significant difference among APPL1 (p = 0.1388), ADP (p = 0.4658) and LEP (p = 0.4699) between MPE and SPE. According to the above, our follow-up study did not distinguish the degree of preeclampsia (PE). Furthermore, the relationship between APPL1, ADP and LEP in PE or CON were analyzed by simple linear regression. There was a weak positive linear correlation between APPL1 and ADP in PE (r = 0.0921, 95%CI = 0.0019 to 0.1824, p = 0.0456), but this correlation disappeared in CON (p = 0.1428) (Fig. 2D&G). Consistently, there was no linear relationship between APPL1 and LEP, whether in PE (p = 0.6662) or CON (p = 0.3957) (Fig. 2E&H). Just as we all know, there was a significant correlation between ADP and LEP, no matter PE (r = 0.5081, 95%CI = 0.2035 to 0.8126, p = 0.0016) or CON (r = 0.1847, 95%CI = 0.0029 to 0.3664, p = 0.0465) (Fig. 2F&I), although it was weak in CON. In conclusion, the levels of APPL1, ADP and LEP in preeclampsia changed significantly, and there is a linear correlation between APPL1 and ADP only in preeclampsia.

Predictive value of APPL1, ADP and LEP in preeclampsia

Analyzing the effect of APPL1, ADP and LEP on preeclampsia by regression of binary logistic. Firstly, the validity of the overall regression model is analyzed by likelihood ratio test (p < 0.001) and Hosmer-Lemeshow test (p = 1.000), and the results showed that this model has a good fitting degree. Then the multicollinearity analysis between variables was carried out, and the variance inflation factor (VIF) was all less than 3 (Fig. 3A). Based on the above premise, the highest OR was found in LEP (OR = 3.618, 95%CI = 1.441 to 9.085, p = 0.006), followed by APPL1 (OR = 1.405, 95%CI = 1.126 to 1.753, p = 0.003) and the lowest in ADP (OR = 0.299, 95%CI = 0.134 to 0.667, p = 0.003) (Fig. 3B). This part of the results shows that LEP and APPL1 are risk factors for preeclampsia, while ADP is a protective factor. In other words, the higher LEP, APPL1 and the lower ADP indicate the occurrence of preeclampsia.

Correlation analysis of APPL1 and glycolipid metabolism indices

The relationship between APPL1 and glycolipid metabolism indices were analyzed by simple linear regression. In the patients with PE, APPL1 had a linear positive correlation with FPG (r = 0.0158, 95%CI = 0.0073 to 0.0242, p = 0.0005), FINS (r = 0.2058, 95%CI = 0.1007 to 0.3110, p = 0.0003), HOMA-IR (r = 0.0505, 95%CI = 0.0244 to 0.0766, p = 0.0003) and LDL-C (r = 0.0911, 95%CI = 0.0668 to 0.1155, p < 0.0001), respectively (Fig. 4A-C&I). In addition, APPL1 had a linear negative correlation with HOMA-IS (r=−0.0070, 95%CI=−0.0106 to −0.0033, p = 0.0004) and HDL-C (r=−0.0135, 95%CI=−0.0201 to −0.0069, p = 0.0002), respectively (Fig. 4D&H). Nevertheless, APPL1 had no linear relationship with HOMA-β (p = 0.0704), TG (p = 0.2798) and TC (p = 0.4897) (Fig. 4E-G). Similar results were found in control pregnant women, which were as follows. APPL1 had a linear positive correlation with FPG (r = 0.0096, 95%CI = 0.0039 to 0.0153, p = 0.0013), FINS (r = 0.0555, 95%CI = 0.0026 to 0.1083, p = 0.0398), HOMA-IR (r = 0.0168, 95%CI = 0.0060 to 0.0276, p = 0.0028) and LDL-C (r = 0.0281, 95%CI = 0.0168 to 0.0394, p < 0.0001), respectively (Fig. 5A-C&I). Additionally, APPL1 had a linear negative correlation with HOMA-IS (r=−0.0034, 95%CI=−0.0060 to , p = 0.0120), HOMA-β (r=−16.27, 95%CI=−30.3600 to −2.1790, p = 0.0243) and HDL-C (r=−0.0115, 95%CI=−0.0183 to −0.0047, p = 0.0013), respectively (Fig. 5D-E&H). Consistently, APPL1 had no linear relationship with TG (p = 0.9246) and TC (p = 0.8967) (Fig. 5F-G). To sum up, there is a credible correlation between APPL1 and glycolipid metabolism indices, and this correlation is increased in PE.

Correlation analysis of APPL1 and neonatal outcome indices

This study mainly collected three neonatal outcome indicators, including neonatal birth weight, ponderal index and Apgar score. The relationship between APPL1 and neonatal outcome indices were analyzed by simple linear regression. In the patients with PE, APPL1 had a linear negative correlation with neonatal birth weight (r−0.0149, 95%CI=−0.0290 to −0.0007, p = 0.0396), ponderal index (r=−0.0198, 95%CI=−0.0321 to −0.0074, p = 0.0024) and Apgar score (r=−0.0368, 95%CI=−0.0681 to −0.0054, p = 0.0225), respectively (Fig. 6A-C). On the contrary, APPL1 had no linear relationship with neonatal birth weight (p = 0.5110), ponderal index (p = 0.7956) and Apgar score (p = 0.4513) in the control pregnant women (Fig. 6D-F). These results show the potential value of APPL1 in predicting the neonatal outcome of patients with PE.

Discussion

It is well known that PE is one of the most common complications in pregnancy, which seriously threatens the health of pregnant women and fetuses. The incidence of PE in China is about 2.7%, and the stillbirth rate is about 4.6% [4, 17]. Compared with healthy pregnancy, the risk of death of PE patients and their offspring increased by 29% (HR = 1.29, 95%CI = 1.20 to 1.38) and 188% (HR = 2.88, 95%CI = 1.79 to 4.63) respectively [18]. The incidence of PE pregnant women, fetal death and premature delivery with medical indications increased significantly, which were 3.73 times, 3.12 times and 4.51 times higher than those of non-PE pregnant women respectively [19]. Because PE has the characteristics of multiple factors, mechanisms and molecular pathways, at present, the clinical treatment principle is “symptomatic treatment combined with timely termination of pregnancy”, but the effect is not satisfactory. Based on above reasons, the prevention and accurate prediction of PE is particularly important, but there is still a lack of biological markers for clinical detection.

With the in-depth understanding and research of PE, it breaks through the traditional concept that PE is regarded as a purely placental disease. Currently, the etiology of PE can be divided into four categories [1, 20–22]: maternal, placental, fetal pathological factors and adverse environmental factors during pregnancy. Among them, maternal metabolic abnormality is an important risk factor, including glucose, lipid, energy and iron metabolism [23–25], as well as microorganisms [26]. All these information suggests that there may be potential biomarkers in many metabolic indices, which can predict the incidence and severity of PE to some extent, at least in pregnant women with metabolic abnormality. Based on our findings, APPL1 is considered to be a field worthy of expectation and further research.

Our results support an relationship between APPL1 and early-onset PE, which is expected to provide a new direction for clinical screening, prevention and treatment of PE. While APPL1 (initially termed DIP13α) was first characterized in kinase signaling contexts [27–29], its critical role in adiponectin pathways—now implicated in PE pathogenesis—makes it a compelling biomarker candidate. APPL1 participates in insulin signal transduction pathway through interaction with AKT (protein kinase B, PKB) and phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K) [27], and it is directly related to glucose transporter type 4 (GLUT4) translocation and glucose uptake caused by insulin [27, 30]. APPL1 is also involved in the crosstalk between insulin signal transduction pathway and adiponectin signal transduction pathway, which is a new mechanism of adiponectin-related insulin sensitivity effect [10]. Therefore, it is not difficult to find the correlation between APPL1 and adiponectin in PE patients of our sample, not to mention the close relationship between adiponectin and leptin. APPL1, mainly derived from adipose tissue [10], acts as a central mediator of adiponectin signaling and insulin sensitivity, and its dysregulation directly links to the metabolic perturbations observed in PE—particularly in pregnancies complicated by overweight/obesity, insulin resistance (IR), or dyslipidemia. This tissue-specific prominence underscores the clinical value of monitoring circulating APPL1 levels as a dynamic biomarker in high-risk pregnancies. Clarify that therefore, our results cannot support conclusions about the specific impact of overweight/obesity, and that such a relationship remains a hypothesis to be tested in a future study specifically designed with a cohort encompassing a broader BMI range.

Since leptin and adiponectin have been found to be correlated with PE, APPL1, as the core hub molecule of glucose and lipid metabolism, deserves to be expected for its potential. Serum leptin is mainly produced by adipocytes, but placenta is also an important source during pregnancy [31]. Previous reports showed that the level of leptin in pregnant women with preeclampsia increased, which was consistent with our research results. This is because IR is more serious in PE patients, and it is considered that it may cause inflammatory reaction and vascular endothelial injury, which eventually promotes the occurrence of PE [32]. Furthermore, it has been reported that higher leptin are observed in early-onset PE or in larger gestational weeks [31, 33]. In addition, adiponectin is the most adipose tissue-specific protein, which has anti-inflammatory and insulin-sensitizing effects [34], and Angelidis found that it also has an effect on placental angiogenesis in PE [35]. In our sample, although ADP levels were lower in the PE group, the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.3955). The non-significant ADP difference may reflect methodological limitations: unlike Ida which identified ADP in PE within a prepregnancy obesity cohort, our design did not stratify by prepregnancy BMI [13]. Earlier work Maria using screening-phase BMI similarly reported null findings [12], suggesting pre-gestational metabolic characteristics are critical for detecting ADP-PE relationships [34, 36]. There is no doubt that APPL1 shows the correlation with many glycolipid metabolism indices, such as FPG, FINS, HOMA-IR, HOMA-IS, LDL-C and HDL-C in our results, and this correlation increased in PE.

Based on APPL1 as an important regulator of insulin and adiponectin-signaling pathways, its clinical value in many diseases and pathological processes has been determined, including type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) [37], gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) [38], polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) [39] and IR [40]. More specifically, diabetes-induced pathological vascular remodeling may be prevented by interventions promoting APPL1 nuclear translocation [41]. Additionally, available evidence suggests that reduction of APPL1 in granulosa cells could be involved in the development of PCOS [42]. Furthermore, IR can be observed in the APPL1 knockout mice indicating that APPL1 sensitizes insulin signaling pathway by acting at the downstream effector of the insulin receptor [43]. APPL1 not only participates in insulin and adiponectin signal transduction pathway and its metabolic effect, but also plays an important role in cell proliferation, apoptosis, intracellular transport, chromatin remodeling and other physiological processes [28]. Our results provide a basis for APPL1 to predict the neonatal outcome of preeclampsia patients: APPL1 was observed to be correlated with neonatal birth weight, ponderal index and Apgar score, but this relationship disappeared in non-PE pregnant women. We further clarify that establishing causality—whether APPL1 dysregulation is a driver or a consequence of preeclampsia, and its interaction with specific placental pathologies—remains a crucial objective for future mechanistic investigations.

This study is a single-center case-control study, but it has the defects of small sample size and single region, which constrains the statistical power for subgroup analyses and increases the risk of Type II errors. It is also a limitation that more clinical indicators (UtA-PI, PAPP-A, and PlGF) and potentially relevant biomarkers (sFlt-1 and PlGF) are not included in the design stage. We specifically list them here to facilitate peer reference and improve the research that has not yet started to be implemented. Our observation of a statistically significant but clinically negligible APPL1-ADP correlation underscores a critical design limitation: The analysis should have specifically targeted overweight/obese PE pregnancies where adipocytokine dysregulation is most pronounced. Our unstratified cohort lacked power to detect meaningful biological relationships in this pathway. Importantly, the lack of a statistically significant difference in ADP levels between the PE and control groups in our study (p = 0.3955) must be interpreted in the context of a significant methodological constraint. To minimize the potential confounding effect of advanced maternal age, a known independent risk factor for PE, on glycolipid metabolic parameters, the study was initially designed to enroll patients within the age range of 20–34 years. It must be acknowledged that this design choice limits the generalizability of our findings to the broader obstetric population. As prepregnancy BMI was not an inclusion criterion or a stratification factor during participant recruitment, its potential confounding effect on ADP levels could not be adequately controlled for in this analysis. This omission in the initial design likely obscured a potential underlying relationship, consistent with findings observed in cohorts specifically enriched for or stratified by obesity [13]. The inability to account for prepregnancy BMI represents a key limitation affecting the interpretation of our ADP results. In addition, while statistically significant, these weak correlations suggest APPL1 explains limited variance in metabolic parameters. Our study was not powered to establish APPL1 as a standalone diagnostic tool. Rather, these preliminary data position APPL1 as a component biomarker warranting integration into future multivariable prediction models. It is necessary to design a large number of high-quality multi-center, large-sample, multilevel, prospective clinical cohort studies and animal/cells model studies are still needed to further clarify the biological process of APPL1 in PE.

In conclusion, our results support that APPL1 is a risk factor for PE. It is related to the glycolipid metabolism indices of patients, which indicates the neonatal outcome of their offspring. Therefore, APPL1 is considered to be a field worthy of expectation and further research. In the future, multi-center clinical research is encouraged to clarify the effectiveness and feasibility of APPL1 as a biomarker of preeclampsia, and to provide a scientific basis for the early application of APPL1 in clinics.
