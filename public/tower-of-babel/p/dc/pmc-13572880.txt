Making sense of nature-based flood mitigation: A levers framework for conceptualizing and communicating natural flood management (NFM)
Authors: Kirstie Fryirs
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNatural flood management (NFM) offers a sustainable alternative to traditional flood mitigation by harnessing natural processes to slow and attenuate floods. This paper introduces a biogeomorphic levers framework to conceptualize NFM across three spatial scales; channels and reaches, valleys and corridors, and tributaries and catchments. By identifying geomorphic and vegetative elements that can be manipulated to enhance hydraulic roughness and deliver flood mitigation, the framework provides a practical tool for implementation and communication. The article provides an overview of the challenges of scaling NFM for different flood magnitudes and offers a reality check for practitioners and decision-makers. This forum reframes NFM as a nature-based, multi-scalar strategy for the 21st century.Keywords: geomorphology, river rehabilitation, roughness, floodplain wetlands, nature based solutions

A timely debate

In many parts of the world, society must adapt to a future where climate change will increase the intensity and variability of floods and exponentially increase the cost of post-flood recovery (Dottori et al. 2018, Kirkpatrick et al. 2024). Debate rages about the role of climate change on the frequency, intensity, extent, and magnitude of these natural disasters, and the effectiveness and suitability of current emergency response, planning, adaptation , and mitigation measures (Johnson et al. 2020, Garvey and Paavola 2021, Kreibich et al. 2022, Rutherford et al. 2024, Ripple et al. 2025).

This situation has been exposed through recent experiences of extreme flooding in many parts of the world including Europe, Asia, North America, and Australia (Best et al. 2022, Ripple et al. 2025). In some places, this has triggered independent inquiries and commissions to investigate current practice and make recommendations to government and society about alternative solutions for implementation (e.g., O'Kane and Fuller 2022). By way of example, in eastern Australia, record-breaking flooding during a series of La Nina events in 2021, 2022, and 2025 was some of the largest and most widespread, impacting tens of thousands of people and businesses. The 2021–2022 floods are the costliest disaster in Australia’s recorded history with insured losses of ~$6.41 billion (ICA 2022). Since the Black Summer fires of 2019–2020, the New South Wales (NSW) Government has spent $9.5 billion on disaster relief and recovery, a more than 1000% increase compared to the previous six-year average (NSW Government, 2025–26 Budget). By 2050, Australia’s annual extreme weather cost is likely to be $32.5 billion (ICA 2022).

The recommendations of the NSW Government independent inquiry into the 2022 floods “flipped on its head” the notion that traditional river and flood engineering continue as the first-choice mitigation solution. Instead, this inquiry called for implementation of “nature-based flood mitigation … using floodplains as assets … and letting watercourses largely flow naturally rather than implementing engineering barriers such as flood levees and mitigation schemes to stop floods” (e.g., O'Kane and Fuller 2022, p. 30). It also calls for a “landscape restoration strategy…for nature-based flood mitigation and adaptation which would see large-scale native revegetation and wetland restoration,” and reconsideration of land use planning and types of development on floodplains (e.g., O'Kane and Fuller 2022, p. 36).

This situation in Australia provides just one example whereby recurrent flooding has brought into sharp focus the need to re-examine flood mitigation, adaptation, and preparedness strategies (Johnson et al. 2020, Best et al. 2022). Traditional flood protection methods often involve large, engineered structures such as detention basins, levees, bypasses, weirs, embankments, and dams that are costly to construct, expensive to maintain, and linked to poor environmental outcomes (Wingfield et al. 2019, Ellis et al. 2021). Such measures also provide communities with a false sense of permanent protection from flooding (Harries 2008, Birkholz et al. 2014, Ding et al. 2023). Other solutions are needed. As a result, attention is being placed on the potential to design and add space-to-flood, nature-based solutions to the flood mitigation and adaptation toolkit. Natural flood management (NFM) is a high-profile nature-based solution for achieving sustainable flood mitigation, while also enhancing the health and resilience of riverine ecosystems (World Bank 2018, Broomby et al. 2025). In some parts of the world, it is called nature-based solutions for flood management, natural infrastructure, or hybrid flood management if combined with traditional grey engineering approaches (Skidmore and Wheaton 2022, Bezak et al. 2024, Prinsley et al. 2025, Vann et al. 2025).

NFM emphasizes rehabilitation of natural hydrological pathways, processes, and corridors to provide important flood regulation services alongside water quality, biodiversity, aesthetic, and cultural improvements (Iacob et al. 2014, 2017, Hartmann et al. 2019, Ellis et al. 2021). As such, international policy makers and scientists are increasingly interested in NFM as a complementary, or replacement, flood mitigation technique (Collentine and Futter 2018, Wingfield et al. 2019, 2021) and are embedding it in climate change adaptation and flood mitigation strategies (e.g., Iacob et al. 2017, Kay et al. 2019). Realizing NFM on-the-ground and mitigating flood risk is going to require that we apply all the nature-based tools we have in the toolbox (Prinsley et al. 2025).

Recommendations from independent inquiries, and a groundswell of interest in nature-based solutions, globally, are giving a green light to implement NFM in-practice. However, turning NFM into a reality on-the-ground remains a significant challenge, particularly at the scale needed to deliver mitigation, and to convince communities and practitioners that NFM is a viable flood mitigation solution and tool for the 21st century. In this perspective, I provide a conceptual framing of cross-scalar NFM, highlight how NFM can be realistically delivered, what outcomes can be expected, and where challenges lie.

Conceptualizing NFM from a biogeomorphic perspective

In this paper, NFM is defined as a nature-based solution to flood mitigation that uses natural biophysical processes to slow floods down, reduce erosive power, and attenuate them downstream (Dixon et al. 2016, Lane 2017, Waylen et al. 2018, Wingfield et al. 2019, Black et al. 2021). Several key measures are commonly used to detect NFM signals; changes in flood wave celerity, peak-to-peak travel time, and flood wave attenuation.

Flood wave celerity is defined as the speed at which a flood wave propagates downstream (m/s) (Chow 1959). Peak-to-peak travel time is the time taken for the peak of a flood to pass between two points along a river channel (hours and minutes). If flood wave celerity is decreasing over time, then the travel time between two gauges is increasing and floods are getting slower.

Attenuation of flow is defined as the difference in peak stage height (or discharge) between two points along a river channel. If attenuation is increasing over time, then more flow is being placed in storage (e.g., on floodplains) between the two gauge locations and slowly being released as the flood peak passes and the flood wanes, reducing downstream peak stage height (or discharge) (Asselman et al. 2022). If attenuation is occurring, then flood levels are getting lower.

In its purest form, delivery of NFM requires that floods become both slower and lower (figure 1). Although this may be achievable in some settings, elsewhere this is a challenging and potentially unrealistic objective. However, in many places NFM is still regarded as successful if floods are only becoming slower as such floods deliver significant benefits to the environment and society and provide a more realistic and achievable option on-the-ground (figure 1).

The conundrums of communicating and delivering NFM.

Slower floods have reduced erosive and destructive power and create additional time for evacuations or emergency response during disasters, all of which are critical safety considerations and priorities for communities. Slower floods are less flashy, tend to have a longer duration, and therefore water will reside in the landscape and inundate floodplains for longer. Water may back-up or pond in certain parts of the catchment resulting in locally higher water levels. These areas will take longer to “dry out.” These processes create important opportunities for soil replenishment through fine-grained material deposition, moisture rehydration, groundwater recharge, and floodplain ecosystem (e.g., wetland) rejuvenation (Short et al. 2019, Yimer et al. 2024). Floods that are slower tend to cause less severe river change meaning that the physical structure of rivers can recover more quickly with less intervention. Lower floods (created by attenuation) result in downstream land being less severely inundated, reducing the time it takes for these areas to “dry out” (figure 1). To achieve flood lowering requires that water be stored or detained in the upstream catchment and slowly released after flood peaks have passed. This may create higher upstream water levels.

The NFM processes presented in figure 1 often create decision-making, trade-off, and communication conundrums, as interventions may deliver flood mitigation benefits for some although simultaneously generating disbenefits for others. Ultimately, whether NFM is adopted, and how these trade-offs are negotiated, is a societal decision in any given place. Numerous reviews have considered the evidence for NFM and its effectiveness as a flood mitigation solution (Bagheri-Gavkosh et al. 2025, Zhu et al. 2025). Evidence is mounting that NFM can be delivered at local, reach, and small catchment scales and for minor and moderate flooding (Wilkinson et al. 2019, Black et al. 2021). However, the challenge escalates as spatial area and flood magnitude increase. Some have questioned whether NFM is even possible at large catchment scales and for major or catastrophic flooding (Dadson et al. 2017, Kreibich et al. 2022, Xiao et al. 2022).

There is still much to be learnt about how to deliver NFM in reality (Wells et al. 2020, Wingfield et al. 2021). Proof-of-concept work is still required to identify which flood “types” (magnitude-frequency) can be realistically mitigated, the quantum of mitigation that can be realistically delivered on-the-ground, the scale at which nature-based solutions and mixes of them can be used to deliver a mitigation effect, and the extent to which communities and agencies can commit to and resource the implementation of NFM over the short, medium, and long-term (Black et al. 2021, Hill et al. 2023). However, this should not delay on-ground action, particularly in places where there are solid foundations on which to work (Fryirs et al. 2023, Zhang and Fryirs 2024).

This paper takes a different approach to conceptualizing the reality of NFM by framing it through a biogeomorphic, multi-scalar lens (Schanze 2017, Nesshöver et al. 2017, Hartmann et al. 2019, Wohl et al. 2025a). It uses the analogy of levers that can be realistically pulled by those implementing NFM on-the-ground. Because this is a biogeomorphic perspective (Fryirs 2024), the levers are the physical and vegetative structures and processes that can be adjusted or manipulated at the local and reach scales to enhance roughness, and the physical and vegetative structures and processes that can be better utilized at the valley and (sub)catchment scales to manipulate flow and flood routing and dynamics.

In simple terms, delivering NFM means working to enhance the physical and vegetative roughness of rivers to achieve hydrological recovery (figure 2) at various scales. Geomorphic recovery is defined as a noticeable improvement in the physical structure and function of river channels and floodplains (Fryirs and Brierley 2016). This improvement in geomorphic condition can come about through unassisted (natural) or assisted (rehabilitation) means. Vegetative recovery is defined as a noticeable improvement in the quantity and structure of instream, riparian, and (sub)catchment vegetation. When geomorphic and vegetative recovery are occurring, more roughness is created (figure 2). As a result, hydrological recovery can be triggered or enhanced, defined as a slowing and lowering of minor, moderate, and major floods (aka NFM).

What levers can be pulled to deliver NFM on-the-ground?

Rivers perform geomorphic work (Lisenby et al. 2018). Whether geomorphic work is done depends firstly on the total potential energy that is available in the system (Simon 1992). The use of that energy to do work is expressed as the relative amount of energy expended versus energy conserved at any particular location, such that erosion and/or deposition thresholds are, or are not, breached (Wolman and Gerson 1978, Cost and O’Connor 1995). To mitigate floods and change their hydrology through the implementation of NFM requires that resisting forces that consume energy outweigh impelling or erosive forces at a range of spatial scales (Brierley and Fryirs 2005).

Using a geomorphic lens, NFM is conceptualized as a set of levers that can be pulled at three different scales; channel and reach scale, valley and corridor scale, and tributary and catchment scale (figure 3). To deliver NFM, implementation will require increasing riparian and instream hydraulic roughness at channel and reach scales, using floodplains as sediment and water regulation assets at valley and corridor scales, and desynchronizing tributary and trunk stream flood peaks at tributary and catchment-scales (figure 3).

Levers that can be used to deliver NFM.

Pulling Lever 1 at channel and reach scales

At the finest scale (channel and reach) boundary resistance is created by grain and bedform roughness, and channel resistance is created by channel roughness and planform configuration (figure 4) (Brierley and Fryirs 2005). These multiple forms of riparian and instream roughness provide NFM Lever 1 (figure 3). Grain and bedform roughness have a frictional effect on the channel bed. Grain roughness refers to the calibre (size) and organization of bed materials, and bedform roughness is the features developed in and on the bed material, such as ripples and dunes (Church 1992). Both grain and bedform roughness exert hydraulic drag and therefore create energy loss (Knighton 1998). Channel resistance refers to the morphology of the channel and its configuration (planform) on the valley bottom.

Channel roughness includes the shape and irregularity of the channel that may be created by vegetation or wood, or complex banks (figure 4a, b; Montgomery and Piégay 2003). Irregular and “messy” (Wohl 2016) rivers expend significantly more energy than relatively smooth channels. Channel size also plays a role with smaller channels being more effective at dissipating energy over floodplains. Figure 4a shows the distribution of woody and non-woody vegetation along the instream and riparian zone of a laterally unconfined river. Different vegetation forms have different drag effects on flow (Nepf 1999). Figure 4b shows the bank and channel roughness of a cross-section and the Manning’s n as flow stage increases. Energy is lost when flow occurs along rough bank irregularities, and around vegetation and other structural elements such as wood (Wohl et al. 2019).

Planform roughness includes the number of channels, their sinuosity, and their capacity to adjust laterally on the valley bottom (figure 4a). It also includes the geomorphic unit (landform) assemblage at the reach-scale (figure 4c; Fryirs and Brierley 2022). Rivers that have multiple channels or channels that are sinuous (meandering), a diversity of geomorphic units, and can adjust on the valley bottom, that is, have space to move, will expend more energy as flow occurs around landforms and at bends (Nanson and Huang 2017). Figure 4c shows the geomorphic unit diversity and planform for a reach of partly confined river. Energy is lost when flow occurs around bedrock bends, over and around a complex assemblage of geomorphic units, and over the gravel bed of this river reach (Hey and Thorne 1986).

Pulling Lever 2 at valley and corridor scales

At the intermediate scale (valley and corridor) the extent of the valley bottom determines the longitudinal pattern floodplains and the distribution of floodplain wetlands. Both floodplains and their associated wetlands act as flood regulation and mitigation “assets,” providing NFM Lever 2 (figure 3). A valley bottom comprises the active channel and contemporary floodplain which determines the valley setting of a river and the extent of flood inundation (Fryirs et al. 2016). Three types of valley setting can occur, confined where the channel abuts the valley bottom margin > 85%, partly confined where the channel abuts the valley bottom margin between 10% and 85% and laterally unconfined where the channel abuts the valley bottom margin by < 10% (Fryirs et al. 2016, Khan and Fryirs 2020). Confined valleys may contain occasional floodplain pockets, partly confined valleys may be dominantly bedrock controlled or have slightly more space for planform adjustment, and laterally unconfined valleys will have continuous floodplains and channel(s) that are either continuous or discontinuous/absent (Brierley and Fryirs 2005, Fryirs and Brierley 2010). The sequence of valley settings along corridors determines where valley-scale basins and pinch points occur that control the distribution of flow expansion and contraction zones and therefore the extent to which a valley is enhancing flood flow or dissipating flood flow (Miller 1995, Thompson and Croke 2013, Wohl et al. 2025b). Energy is dissipated where channels abut valley margins, where floods spill on to floodplains or enter broad valley bottoms after being confined. Backwater processes that are often observed during floods occur at the valley-scale. For example, backwater effects can occur behind bedrock spurs, upstream of pinch points and in confluence zones (Benda et al. 2004).

The configuration and shape of a valley bottom also determine the occurrence of floodplains and wetlands that act as natural water storage and detention basins (Fryirs et al. 2016, Wohl et al. 2025b). Different floodplain types occur along a continuum of energy (as determined by discharge and slope), valley confinement, and sediment calibre/texture (Nanson and Croke 1992). In high energy settings, coarse grained floodplains are typically found whereas in lower energy settings, mixed-load and fine-grained floodplains are formed. Floodplains will also have different shapes and sizes, from small occasional pockets to more regularly alternating pockets, to continuous surfaces (Brierley and Fryirs 2005). On floodplains, different types of wetlands can occur including backswamps, cutoffs, palaeochannels, trapped tributary fills, lagoons, unchannelized swamps or valley fills, and chains of ponds (Brierley and Fryirs 2005). The size and position of these wetlands on the valley bottom (distal or proximal), and their sedimentary and vegetative characteristics influence the functional role they play during overbank flows of different magnitudes (Åhlén et al. 2022). These attributes determine their water holding and release capacity, with different effects created depending on the assemblage and critical mass of floodplain wetlands that occur along any given valley bottom and along a corridor (Blanchette et al. 2022).

Pulling Lever 3 at tributary and catchment scales

At the coarsest scale (tributary and catchment) flow and flood routing are controlled by the interplay between geomorphological characteristics of a catchment and the hydrology of rainfall and storm events of varying spatial and temporal magnitude, intensity, and tracking. The interaction between these factors in tributaries, collectively determine from where runoff and flow are (or are not) generated which subsequently impacts the travel time, celerity, peak magnitude, and attenuation effects of floods along a receiving trunk stream (Lane 2017, Pattison et al. 2014). At this scale, (de)synchronizing tributary and trunk stream flood peaks provide NFM Lever 3 (figure 3).

Catchment morphology and drainage network structure characteristics such as size, shape, pattern, and tributary-trunk stream relationships are a key control on flow and flood routing dynamics (Benda et al. 2004, Asselman et al. 2022, Arash et al. 2023, 2025). Overlayed on this, land use, particularly (sub)catchment vegetation cover, and soil characteristics determine the extent to which the landscape acts as a “sponge.” These characteristics determine the rate, volume, and timing of runoff and flow from different tributary subcatchments, controlling when discharge is contributed to a trunk stream (Pattison et al. 2014, Lane 2017, Awaad et al. 2025). If tributary peak flows are temporally aligned and closely spaced (i.e., synchronous) their cumulative effect amplifies the downstream flood peak (figure 5e top panel). Tributary desynchronization occurs when peak flows from one or more tributary subcatchments are delayed and/or lowered (such as tributary peaks 3 and 4 in figure 5e bottom panel), resulting in delayed peaks and altered flow celerity and/or attenuation along the trunk stream (figure 5e Lismore and downstream peaks). This desynchronization can be achieved by using all three levers in rehabilitation of key tributary subcatchments.

(De)synchronization can also occur as a result of differences in the characteristics of the rain or storm event and its tracking over tributaries that occur at different locations in a catchment. For example, if a storm tracks over, or is concentrated on, more than one major tributary, then tributary position within the catchment will determine the form of the trunk stream flood peak. If the tributaries join the trunk stream at closely spaced confluences, then the trunk stream peak will be accentuated (figure 5e top panel). If, however, the tributary confluences are widely spaced, then contributions to the trunk stream flood peak will be minimal and diffuse. Other factors such as the timing of the rain or storm event within a longer-term sequence of events also need to be considered given that antecedent soil moisture and shallow aquifer volume are key controls on the “sponge” capacity of a catchment and processes such as infiltration and overland flow (Lane 2017, Woessner 2020, Awaad et al. 2025, Han et al. 2025).

The reality check: translating the concept to on-ground, at-scale implementation

Although NFM is a valuable flood mitigation tool in a river managers toolkit, it is not a panacea. It is likely to have greatest effect and benefit as part of a complementary and integrated set of solutions for flood mitigation (Ellis et al. 2021). In this regard, collective work is needed on front facing, community, government and industry engagement to conceptualize NFM and its suitability and usability as a flood mitigation and adaptation tool, although challenging firmly held engineering philosophies of flood mitigation, flood risk, and flood protection (Howgate and Kenyon 2009, Iacob et al. 2017, Wells et al. 2020, Bark et al. 2021). For example, Bark et al. (2021) show that many stakeholders in the UK view NFM as a “no-brainer”; based on cost-effectiveness, social and environmental benefits, and failure of the dominant flood risk management and protection paradigm.

When proposing or implementing NFM as a flood mitigation tool, it is critical that practitioners set realistic expectations about the magnitude-frequency of floods they are aiming to mitigate and match that with the scale at which NFM needs to be implemented (Thaler et al. 2023). Conversely, honesty is needed if NFM cannot be realistically delivered in a given target catchment (Wells et al. 2020). Contextualizing the risk and the cost: benefit of implementation (or no action) will require careful consideration (Short et al. 2019, Ruangpan et al. 2024, Broomby et al. 2025) . Especial care is needed to clearly communicate to stakeholders, particularly communities impacted by flooding, department treasuries, or policy makers (Wingfield et al. 2021, Carter et al. 2024, King et al. 2025). Part of the challenge lies in clearly explaining the processes that are occurring during NFM (figure 1) and the timeframes over which NFM can be realistically delivered.

Using levers as an analog to conceptualize and communicate NFM provides a clear framework for practitioners to consider what can be realistically implemented on-the-ground. It helps make decisions about which levers can be realistically pulled, the quantum to which each lever needs to be pulled and the mix of levers that need to be pulled to produce a mitigation effect in any given target catchment. This then determines the extent to which NFM can be delivered at a range of spatial scales and for a range of floods of varying magnitude and frequency. The levers framework provides a reality check for determining knowledge gaps, implementation techniques, prioritization, and communications strategies.

The operation of the lever framework will vary in different catchments depending on the characteristics and condition of each of the geomorphic and vegetative elements operating at each of the three scales and the hydrological characteristics of the system. This will determine the extent to which a flood mitigation signal can be detected (or delivered) for different magnitude-frequency of flood. The types of techniques that could be used will also vary in different places depending on the “tools” that are available, the level of intervention required, the scale at which intervention is needed, the resourcing available (both financial and human capital), and the willingness of community, governments, and agencies to adopt and implement NFM as a nature-based solution (Nesshöver et al. 2017, Martin et al. 2025, Russell et al. 2026).

To achieve a noticeable mitigation of minor floods may only require that Lever 1 be pulled (figure 3). A minor flood is defined here as a water level that causes inconvenience (BoM 2026). Some low-lying areas next to river channels are inundated. Minor roads may be closed, and low-level bridges and other infrastructure submerged (BoM 2026). On-ground implementation requires consideration of how to use all the geomorphic and vegetative roughness tools in the toolkit. Identifying what type of roughness needs to be established, where it needs to be enhanced and at to what level/density is critical. Nature-based interventions that can be used to pull Lever 1 include in-channel and riparian replanting, the installation of woody structures and/or leaky weirs, modifications to in-channel geomorphic structure or planform through assisted or unassisted means, or deployment of local runoff attenuation features (RAFs) (Nicholson et al. 2020, Sholtes and Doyle 2011, Prinsley et al. 2025).

To mitigate moderate floods both Levers 1 and 2 may need to be pulled (figure 3). A moderate flood is defined here as one where the area of inundation is larger and main traffic routes may be affected (BoM 2026). Some buildings may be affected above floor level and other infrastructure inundated. Evacuation may be required (BoM 2026). To pull Lever 2 requires understanding of the valley bottom configuration and the distribution of confined and unconfined reaches along corridors. It also requires understanding of the types, locations, and condition of floodplain assets, their morphological and water detention/retention characteristics, and how much “critical mass” of floodplain assets is needed to perform a flood mitigation function. Critically it requires that land tenure and landowner agreements are in place to allow for the “use” of floodplain assets as natural flood detention and release basins during floods. Nature-based interventions that can be used to pull Lever 2 include alterations to channel size and roughness to reconnect floodplains and wetlands such as backswamps and lagoons, enhancing flood flow through paleochannels and cutoffs, enacting space to move, flood and erode programs, restoring existing natural wetlands and constructing new wetlands using nature-based designs (Tang et al. 2020, Bogdan et al. 2022, Yang et al. 2024, Prinsley et al. 2025).

Evidence is emerging that NFM can be delivered for minor and moderate floods using Levers 1 and 2 (figure 3). For example, several case studies have shown that working at the channel and reach scale by focusing on planting riparian vegetation and installing wood can increase flood storage by 15–71%, delay flood peaks by 0.5–17 hours, and reduce peak flows by 5%–50% (Anderson et al. 2006, Pattison et al. 2014, Dixon et al. 2016, Metcalfe et al. 2017, Follett et al. 2024). Others have shown that corridor scale “regreening” can have a noticeable effect on flood wave celerity and flood peak travel times (Cohen et al. 2022, Fryirs et al. 2023). Others have shown that floodplain wetland restoration can reduce peak flow by up to 25% (Acreman et al. 2003, Wu et al. 2020) and that natural clusters of small wetlands in small catchments can reduce surface runoff by 15%–30%, enhance soil water retention by 30%–50% and decrease flood peak water levels by 10%–25% (Yang et al. 2024). Additionally, the co-benefits of floodplain inundation and wetland restoration are numerous, including enhancing fine-grained deposition and replenishment of floodplain soils, augmenting soil moisture rehydration processes, increasing surface water-groundwater interaction, and recharging long term groundwater and aquifer water storage, all of which have the potential to store the greatest amount of water for longer, minimizing future drought conditions that inevitably follow periods of flooding (Short et al. 2019, Woessner 2020, Yimer et al. 2024).

To mitigate major floods will require that a mix of levers be pulled at multiple scales, in different places, to varying degrees, and with enough critical mass to achieve a noticeable effect (figure 3). A major flood is defined here as one where large areas are inundated and many buildings may be affected above floor level (BoM). Properties and towns are likely to be isolated, major rail, and traffic routes closed and utility services affected, and evacuation may be required (BoM 2026). Major floods also include those that reach extreme levels. Although pulling Lever 3 is conceptually appealing it is often considered a prima facie high-risk proposition. This underscores the need for place-based research, particularly catchment-scale hydrological modeling using continuous simulation rather than design events, to confirm whether it is even possible to (de)synchronize the storage and release time of water during floods in the target catchment (Pattinson et al. 2014, Xiao et al. 2022). Care is needed to understand whether interventions that alter flow celerity or attenuation in one tributary will change (de)synchroneity functions occurring elsewhere in the catchment, potentially having unintended consequences on flood risk elsewhere (Lane 2017).

If NFM is possible at this scale, many have proposed the use of a multi-technique, fit-for-purpose approach that is tailored to the target catchments’ physical characteristics and hydrology (i.e., the right NFM in the right place; Pattison et al. 2014, Bagheri-Gavkosh et al. 2025). This will require that the focus shift from opportunistic, small-scale interventions to policy-level initiatives that affect an entire catchment. Interventions at the scale of the entire catchment need to optimize the balance between working in one key subcatchment or on smaller adjustments in multiple tributaries (Pattison et al. 2014). Options include alterations to land use and land management, improvements to soil health to increase infiltration, implementation of landscape-scale measures such as reafforestation or agroforestry, and the installation of packages of RAFs to slow the flow at source (Bathurst et al. 2018, Cooper et al. 2021, Quinn et al. 2022, Kingsbury-Smith et al. 2023, Follett et al. 2024, Bagheri-Gavkosh et al. 2025). Some research has shown that working in small catchments (<30 km2) and in the headwaters using reafforestation and RAFs can reduce peak flow by 30% (Wilkinson et al. 2019, Nicholson et al. 2020, Black et al. 2021). However, when using Lever 3 it is critical to acknowledge that as flood magnitude (and catchment area) increases it is likely that the scale-effect will become evident (Lane 2017) meaning that the effect of strategies implemented at smaller, local scales will be “washed out” and diminish, and either more or larger landscape-scale interventions will be needed (Bloschl et al. 2007, Lane 2017, Dadson et al. 2017).

Concluding questions for river managers and flood prone communities

Building flood-resilient fluvial corridors to deliver NFM means rivers can withstand more destructive floods and recover more quickly from them (figure 1) (Iacob et al. 2014), ultimately reducing the financial and environmental cost of river repair post-flooding. To scale these benefits, local actions must be embedded within a broader, corridor and catchment-scale strategy (Agnew and Fryirs 2022). Prioritising interventions that offer the highest return on investment is essential (Dixon et al. 2016, Agnew et al. 2024, Broomby et al. 2025). Every reach contributes to the cumulative effectiveness of flood mitigation corridors (figure 3). Dramatically upscaling business-as-usual rehabilitation, particularly where structural and process-based interventions are already underway, could provide a foundation for embedding NFM more widely (Schanze 2017). Where geomorphic, vegetative, and hydrological recovery is already occurring, let the river do the work, just help it along (Buffin-Bélanger et al. 2015, Fryirs and Brierley 2021, Zhang and Fryirs 2023, 2024). Working with the river means fully adopting a recovery-enhancement approach to river management (Fryirs et al. 2018, 2021, Fryirs and Brierley 2021, Fryirs 2024) and using nature-based solutions as a key part of a modern-day flood mitigation toolkit.

To implement NFM will require strategic, broad-scale, corridor-based instream and riparian management, a rethink about how to utilize floodplain assets during floods, and how whole-of-catchment processes can be used (figure 3). Future work must identify where each NFM lever can be most effectively pulled and what on-ground techniques can further enhance resilience in already recovering systems. Ultimately, the use of NFM raises two critical considerations for river managers and flood prone communities (figure 1). First, is there a preference for slower, less erosive floods that cause less damage and for which evacuation time is longer and post-flood recovery time is shorter? And second, will communities be able to accept that flood levels may be higher and water will reside in the system and on floodplains for longer, recognizing that this will have significant co-benefits down the track? Ultimately it is a societal decision as to whether NFM is adopted and how inherent conundrums and trade-offs are negotiated and resolved in any given place.
