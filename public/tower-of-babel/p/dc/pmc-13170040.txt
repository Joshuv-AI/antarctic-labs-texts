Determination of Trichomonas vaginalis, certain bacterial and fungal positivity, and risk factors in female patients with urinary and genital complaints
Authors: Türkan Mutlu Yar, Emine Şamdancı, Seda Keskin, İpek Balıkçı Çiçek, Ülkü Karaman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to determine the regional prevalence of Trichomonas vaginalis infection in Turkey and to evaluate the distribution of urogenital findings including T. vaginalis, bacterial vaginosis, Candida albicans, Candida glabrata, acute cystitis, coccobacilli, calcium oxalate crystals, lactobacilli, and hematuria among women presenting with urogenital complaints in and around Ordu province. In addition, sociodemographic and socioeconomic factors associated with these conditions were explored, and their predictive contribution was assessed using a machine learning–based multilayer perceptron (MLP) model.MethodsSmear samples were collected from 236 women attending the Gynecology Outpatient Clinics of Ordu University Training and Research Hospital. Diagnostic evaluation was performed using direct microscopy, Giemsa staining, culture methods, and Papanicolaou (Pap) staining. Sociodemographic data including age, marital status, education level, economic status, place of residence, employment status, spouse’s education and employment status, household composition, and level of knowledge regarding infectious diseases—were obtained using a structured questionnaire. Associations and potential risk factors were evaluated using multivariate analyses and an MLP neural network model.ResultsOverall, T. vaginalis positivity was detected in 24.6% (n = 58) of cases using direct microscopy, staining, and culture methods. In Pap-stained cervical smears, T. vaginalis was identified in 12.7% (n = 30), bacterial vaginosis in 23.7% (n = 56), Candida albicans in 13.6% (n = 32), coccobacilli in 14.0% (n = 33), and lactobacilli in 7.2% (n = 17) of samples. A statistically significant association was observed between Pap staining results and T. vaginalis positivity (p < 0.001). The MLP model achieved an accuracy of 77.1% in the classification of bacterial vaginosis. High negative predictive values were observed for several conditions, including T. vaginalis (97.1%), whereas positive predictive values were lower for some infections, such as Candida (53.1%). Variable importance analysis indicated that age was the most influential factor for T. vaginalis and Candida albicans, level of knowledge regarding infectious diseases for bacterial vaginosis, and employment status for hematuria.ConclusionThis study provides region-specific data on the prevalence of T. vaginalis, bacterial vaginosis, and fungal infections among women with urogenital complaints and demonstrates a substantial infection burden in this symptomatic population. Pap smear staining was found to be associated with T. vaginalis positivity and may serve as a supportive diagnostic indicator. The MLP model suggested that sociodemographic factors may contribute to infection prediction, highlighting the potential role of machine learning as a complementary analytical approach alongside conventional statistical methods. These findings may support future efforts in risk stratification and targeted public health interventions for urogenital infections.

Introduction

Trichomonas vaginalis is a flagellated protozoan parasite that remains one of the most common non-viral sexually transmitted infections (STIs) worldwide, with substantial regional variability in reported prevalence rates among women. Recent studies have continued to demonstrate its endemic presence in diverse populations, with prevalence estimates often between 3% and 5% in general clinical settings using molecular or sensitive diagnostic techniques [25].

Trichomoniasis in women is associated with a range of adverse sexual and reproductive health outcomes, including vaginitis symptoms and an increased risk of acquiring other STIs. Molecular diagnostic approaches such as real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) assays have shown high sensitivity and specificity in detecting T. vaginalis compared with traditional methods, highlighting limitations of microscopy and culture [3].

Women presenting with vaginal and urogenital complaints often have infections caused not only by T. vaginalis but also by bacterial vaginosis (BV) and vulvovaginal candidiasis (VVC). In surveillance studies investigating multiple etiologies of vaginitis, BV and VVC are frequently more prevalent than trichomoniasis and commonly co-occur, suggesting overlapping clinical presentations and shared risk factors [12].

Bacterial vaginosis itself reflects disruption of the normal vaginal microbiota, typically characterized by reduced Lactobacillus dominance and overgrowth of anaerobic taxa. Molecular profiling studies have detailed distinct microbial community state types associated with BV, underscoring its complex microbiological nature and relevance to urogenital health [20].

Conventional cytological techniques such as the Papanicolaou (Pap) test are widely utilized for cervical cancer screening but are not considered diagnostic for trichomoniasis due to limited sensitivity; however, T. vaginalis may be incidentally detected during cytological evaluation, warranting follow-up with validated diagnostic tests. Beyond infective agents, an increasing body of literature has emphasized the role of sociodemographic and socioeconomic factors, such as age, education level, and access to care, in the epidemiology of urogenital infections. These factors may interact in complex ways with clinical and microbiological outcomes, motivating the use of analytical approaches capable of modeling multi-dimensional relationships beyond linear assumptions [12].

Machine learning approaches, including artificial neural networks like the Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), are being explored as supportive analytical tools in medical research, offering the potential to elucidate complex patterns and associations in clinical and epidemiological datasets when used alongside conventional statistical analyses rather than as replacements for established diagnostic criteria [3].

In this context, the present study aimed to investigate the prevalence of T. vaginalis, BV, and Candida albicans among women with urogenital complaints in the Ordu region, to assess the distribution of sociodemographic and socioeconomic characteristics within this population, and to explore potential associations between these factors and microbiological outcomes with the support of an MLP-based analytical approach.

In this study, we aimed to determine the prevalence of T. vaginalis, bacterial vaginosis, Candida albicans, and other microbiological findings among women with urogenital complaints; to evaluate the distribution of sociodemographic and socioeconomic characteristics; to assess the relationship between Pap smear findings and microbiological results; and to explore the potential contribution of an MLP model in identifying complex risk patterns.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study was designed as a cross-sectional observational study conducted among female patients presenting with urogenital complaints at the Gynecology Outpatient Clinics of Ordu University Education and Research Hospital, Turkey. Data collection was carried out between January 2023 and May 2023, following ethical approval (Decision No: 2023/136).

Study population

A total of 236 women who attended the gynecology outpatient clinic during the study period and met the inclusion criteria were enrolled. Patients with incomplete clinical or laboratory data were excluded from the analysis.

Inclusion criteria

Women aged ≥ 15 years who presented to the gynecology outpatient clinic with urinary and/or genital complaints and from whom complete clinical, laboratory, and questionnaire data were available were included in the study.

Exclusion criteria

Patients who were pregnant, menstruating at the time of sampling, had received systemic or local antibiotic or antifungal treatment within the previous two weeks, or had incomplete laboratory or sociodemographic data were excluded from the analysis.

Sample collection and laboratory diagnosis

Vaginal swab samples were obtained from the posterior vaginal fornix under sterile conditions. The presence of Trichomonas vaginalis was evaluated using direct wet mount microscopy, Giemsa staining, and culture methods (CPLM medium).

In addition, cervical cytology samples collected during the same visit were stained using the Papanicolaou (Pap) method and examined for T. vaginalis, bacterial vaginosis, Candida albicans, coccobacilli, and lactobacilli. Daldal et al., [9], Eripp, [11], Özbilgin, [19], Taylor, [23], Toker, [24]. Pap-stained cervical smears were evaluated according to established cytological criteria. The presence of Trichomonas vaginalis was identified based on characteristic morphological features such as pear-shaped organisms with eccentric nuclei. Bacterial vaginosis was assessed cytologically by the presence of clue cells, reduction of lactobacilli, and increased coccobacillary flora. Candida species were identified by the presence of pseudohyphae or yeast forms. It should be noted that Pap smear findings were considered as supportive cytological indicators rather than definitive diagnostic criteria and were interpreted alongside microscopy and culture results. Direct wet mount microscopy was performed immediately after sample collection to detect motile Trichomonas vaginalis trophozoites. While wet mount examination is a rapid and cost-effective method, its limited sensitivity is well recognized; therefore, it was complemented by Giemsa staining and culture methods to improve diagnostic reliability.

Sociodemographic data collection

Sociodemographic and socioeconomic data were collected using a structured questionnaire, including age, marital status, educational level, employment status, economic status, place of residence, household structure, and level of knowledge regarding infectious diseases. Karaman, [14], Karaman, [15]. Variables considered as potential predisposing factors included age, marital status, educational level, employment status, economic status, household structure, presence of other women in the household, and level of knowledge regarding infectious diseases. These factors were selected based on their reported association with urogenital infections in previous epidemiological studies.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Associations between variables were assessed using Pearson’s chi-square test or Yates-corrected chi-square test, where appropriate. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0.

Machine learning analysis

A Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) neural network model was developed to explore complex, non-linear relationships between sociodemographic variables and microbiological outcomes. Model performance was evaluated using 10-fold cross-validation and reported using accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV metrics.

Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) neural network

Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) Neural Network is the most widely used type of artificial neural network and is specifically employed for solving complex problems like classification, regression, and prediction. Such an MLP has one input layer, one or several hidden layers, and one output layer. The input layer, being a data-receiving unit, sends the accepted data to the next layer of the network and includes each node representing a feature from among the features constituting the collected data set. 5 A activations Hidden layers are intermediate layers in a neural network to learn the relationships from input data to output, and the nonlinear relationships are modeled by activation functions at each node. The output layer of a model which generates the final predictions of the model is usually shaped based on the type of target variable [22].

MLPs are composed of fully connected layers, meaning that each node in a layer is connected to all nodes of the previous layer. The model predicts the output with forward propagation and calculates the deviation between prediction and real value via the error function. This discrepancy is then reduced using backpropagation, through which the weights of the network are being updated. The activation functions (ReLU, sigmoid, or tanh) in MLP allow it to learn beneath non-linear and complex relationships within the data used for training [5].

A statistically significant association was observed between Trichomonas vaginalis positivity determined by direct microscopy, Giemsa staining, and culture methods and Pap smear staining results (p < 0.001). Among patients who tested negative for T. vaginalis, 83.98% (n = 173) were Pap smear–negative, while 16.67% (n = 5) were Pap smear–positive. In contrast, among patients who tested positive for T. vaginalis, 83.33% (n = 25) were Pap smear–positive.

When sociodemographic and socioeconomic characteristics were evaluated according to Pap smear staining status, no statistically significant differences were observed between the groups with respect to age (p = 0.934), marital status (p = 0.341), economic status (p = 0.596), education level (p = 0.183), spouse’s education level (p = 0.335), place of residence, employment status (p = 0.196), spouse’s employment status (p = 0.150), household structure (p = 0.866), presence of other women in the extended family (p = 0.820), or level of knowledge regarding infectious diseases (p = 0.505).

The age distribution was similar in both groups, with the majority of participants belonging to the 40–50 and ≥ 50 age categories.

Modeling and evaluation

In this study, a Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) neural network model was developed to classify the status of smear samples as being Pap-stained or not. The independent variables for the model were determined as: Trichomonas vaginalis, bacterial vaginosis, Candida albicans, coccobacillus, lactobacillus, employment status, age, knowledge status of infectious diseases, spouse’s employment status, education level, spouse’s education level, settlement unit, marital status, economic status, presence of another woman in the extended family, and household living conditions. The modeling study was conducted to understand the effects of these variables on the dependent variable and to predict the dependent variable.

During the model training process, outputs were calculated using the forward propagation method, and the differences between the predicted and actual values were measured using a loss function. Subsequently, the backpropagation algorithm was applied to iteratively update the network’s weights, thereby minimizing the error. Throughout this process, regularization techniques were employed to prevent the model from overfitting (Botalb et al., 2018).

Regarding overall accuracy, the highest performance of the MLP model was observed for the oxalate and hematuria classes, with accuracy rates of 99.6% (95% CI: 98.7–100) for both. High accuracy was also achieved for lactobacillus (96.6%; 95% CI: 94.3–98.9), coccobacillus (94.5%; 95% CI: 91.6–97.4), and Trichomonas vaginalis (94.1%; 95% CI: 91.1–97.1). Accuracy values for Candida (90.7%; 95% CI: 87.0–94.4) and acute cystitis (90.3%; 95% CI: 86.5–94.0) were slightly lower. The lowest accuracy was observed for bacterial vaginosis (77.1%; 95% CI: 71.8–82.5).

Balanced accuracy analysis demonstrated high performance for hematuria (97.2%; 95% CI: 95.1–99.3) and oxalate (99.8%; 95% CI: 99.2–100), followed by coccobacillus (90.7%; 95% CI: 87.0–94.4) and acute cystitis (89.3%; 95% CI: 85.4–93.3). In contrast, balanced accuracy for bacterial vaginosis was lower (69.9%).

Sensitivity values were highest for oxalate (100%) and hematuria (94.4%). Sensitivity for T. vaginalis was 78.6% (95% CI: 59.0–91.7), for Candida 70.8% (95% CI: 48.9–87.4), for coccobacillus 85.7% (95% CI: 67.3–96.0), and for acute cystitis 88.2% (95% CI: 63.6–98.5). The lowest sensitivity was observed for bacterial vaginosis (51.4%; 95% CI: 39.3–63.3).

Specificity was generally high across all classes, reaching 100% (95% CI: 98.3–100) for hematuria and remaining above 92% for oxalate, lactobacillus, coccobacillus, and Candida.

Negative predictive values exceeded 98% for oxalate, lactobacillus, and hematuria. The NPV was 97.1% (95% CI: 93.8–98.9) for T. vaginalis, 96.6% for Candida, 99.0% for acute cystitis, and 98.0% for coccobacillus, while bacterial vaginosis showed a lower NPV (80.6%).

Positive predictive values varied across classes. PPV was highest for hematuria (100%; 95% CI: 80.5–100), followed by coccobacillus (72.7%; 95% CI: 54.5–86.7) and T. vaginalis (73.3%; 95% CI: 54.1–87.7). Lower PPV values were observed for bacterial vaginosis (66.1%), Candida (53.1%), acute cystitis (41.7%), lactobacillus (54.5%), and oxalate (50.0%).

The variable importance analysis (Table 5) revealed that each clinical parameter is associated with different demographic and socioeconomic factors, and the importance levels of these factors vary significantly depending on the pathogen.

For T. vaginalis, the most determining factor was age (100%), followed by employment status (97.9%) and spouse’s employment status (92.3%) (Fig. 1).

For bacterial vaginosis, the most critical variable was the knowledge level of infectious diseases (100%), while settlement unit (95.6%) and education level (88.5%) were identified as other important variables (Fig. 2).

Regarding Candida albicans infection, age (100%) and employment status (99.6%) stood out as the primary determinants, with settlement unit (84.1%) ranking third (Fig. 3).

For the detection of coccobacillus, age (100%), economic status (99.4%), and household living conditions (99.0%) were identified as the three most important factors (Fig. 5).

For the presence of lactobacillus, the most critical factor was the knowledge level of infectious diseases (100%), followed by age (73.9%) and employment status (70.2%) (Fig. 7).

When all of Figs. 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 and 8 are evaluated together, it can be seen that the variable importance distributions differ markedly for each clinical parameter. This situation indicates that infections or clinical findings cannot be explained by a single sociodemographic factor; on the contrary, each diagnosis has its own unique risk profile.

In addition, variable importance analyses for acute cystitis and oxalate are presented in Figs. 4 and 6, respectively, showing similar patterns of association with sociodemographic factors.

Furthermore, these findings demonstrate that, despite the absence of significant relationships detected by classical statistical tests between Pap staining and sociodemographic variables (Table 3), the machine learning model was able to identify some of these same variables as strong predictors. The lack of significance in simple bivariate comparisons suggests that these variables may influence clinical outcomes through non-linear or multivariate interactions.

This situation emphasizes that artificial intelligence models reveal not only statistical relationships but also predictive power. Therefore, in urogenital infections, which involve complex biological and behavioral processes, machine learning approaches offer significant advantages. They can capture multivariate interactions that are not easily observable, identify risk factors not apparent in classical analyses, and support clinical decision-making.

For this reason, it is recommended that clinicians and public health experts utilize advanced analytical tools to identify such subtle relationships, which may be clinically critical even though they cannot be detected by simple statistical tests.

Discussion

The aim of this study was to determine the frequency of Trichomonas vaginalis, Bacterial vaginosis (BV), Candida albicans and other vaginal infections among women with urogenital symptoms. We also tested the capability of sociodemographic details to predict an infection status, with a multilayer perceptron (MLP) neural network model. Results contribute to the epidemiological knowledge of the study population and facilitate diagnostic and analytical strategies.

The current study found that 24.6% in T. vaginalis presence. While a significant number of people tested negative, these results show a modest infection rate among symptomatic women. The prevalence of T. vaginalis is associated with sociocultural and lifestyle factors. Previous literature has indicated that T. vaginalis is predominantly detected in individuals: 16 to 35 years; those ages correspond to times of high sexual activities [26]. In addition, increased prevalence rates in those who are more involved in extramarital sexual intercourse and have limited access to health control measures were found [6].

The prevalence observed in this study is generally consistent with reports from different regions of Türkiye. Previous studies have reported T. vaginalis detection rates of 8.6% [10], 4.9% [1], 4.2% [2], 1.81–2.18% [7], and 9.37% [21]. In contrast, substantially higher prevalence rates have been documented in specific risk groups, such as sex workers, where rates up to 42.4% have been reported [4, 8]. These findings indicate that sociodemographic characteristics, sexual behavior patterns, and regional factors play a decisive role in the epidemiology of T. vaginalis.

The detection rate of T. vaginalis by Pap smear was 12.7% in the present study. This low positivity rate is reasonably low due to the poor sensitivity of cytological tests for T. vaginalis diagnosis. As reported elsewhere, microscopy, culture and molecular tests are more definitive diagnostic options. However, the significant association identified between Pap smear findings and T. vaginalis positivity (p < 0.001) suggests that cytological examination may provide supportive findings, although it should not be considered a definitive diagnostic method.

In relation to other vaginal infections, bacterial vaginosis was diagnosed in 23.7%, C. albicans in 13.6%, coccobacilli in 14.0%, and lactobacilli (7.2%). These results indicate that alterations in vaginal flora are a frequent cause of clinical symptoms. Screening of BV (9–24%) and C. albicans (9.8–42%) in other studies was similar to the rate we observed in the present study but not identical with ours [13, 16, 17, 18, 27]. While the detection frequency of C. albicans was low, care should be taken in interpreting the results given the significant clinical relevance of this yeast.

One of the new things in our study is that we tested the sociodemographic variables by an MLP neural network model. However, it is surprising that some of these elements were not found to be associated with outcome levels in standard statistical analyses despite drawing out many such non-significant associations (e.g., T. vaginalis infection: age; bacterial vaginosis: knowledge about infectious diseases; hematuria: current employment status). These results imply that machine learning methodology is able to identify complex (non-linear) relationships that may not be easily ascertainable by traditional statistical techniques. However, these findings are also hypothesis-generating and need to be interpreted with caution.

As for the model performance measures, high accuracies of oxalate and hematuria classifications were obtained. The high NPV for T. vaginalis indicates that the model might be useful to exclude infection. In contrast, the low sensitivity for BV suggests that our model might not be very sensitive to predict this condition and it could be necessary other predictors or new statistical methods. Furthermore, positive predictive values vary by type of infection suggesting clinical follow-up must be included in the interpretation of model results to prevent misclassification.

From the perspective of public health, this study demonstrates that infection risk may be a function of education status, unemployment status and knowledge about infectious diseases. The large percentage of people without sufficient knowledge about infectious diseases reveals the requirement for focused educational and preventive campaigns, especially in rural or underprivileged populations.

There are several limitations to the current study. First, the diagnosis of bacterial vaginosis was based on cytological findings rather than standardized criteria such as Nugent scoring, which may have affected diagnostic accuracy. Second, molecular diagnostic methods such as PCR were not used, which may have led to underestimation of certain infections. In addition, the cross-sectional design of the study limits the ability to establish causal relationships. Finally, the use of Pap smear findings as supportive indicators rather than definitive diagnostic tools should be considered when interpreting the results.

In summary, the findings of this study suggest that standard diagnostic tools may be inadequate for an overall view on urogenital infections. The combination of clinical, cytologic and sociodemographic data with multivariate analysis techniques like machine learning models may provide additional information to aid in targeted diagnostic and public health strategies.

Conclusion

The work described here important contributed regional burden of disease statistics, as it measured the prevalence T. vaginalis, that of bacterial vaginosis (BV), Candida albicans and other types of vagina infections will be present in women with urogential complaints. The results show a demographic variation, characterized by a T. vaginalis infection pattern among the study participants with respect to age, marital status, economic bracket and education level. These findings emphasize the importance of prevention strategies, which may include raising awareness and health education among high-risk groups, as well as improving access to health care among lower and middle socioeconomic populations, strengthening medical resources in disadvantaged areas.

A significant correlation (p < 0.001) was found between T. vaginalis positivity and Pap stain findings. These findings suggest that Pap smear evaluation may provide supportive cytological information; however, it should not be considered a definitive diagnostic method. In contrast, no significant association was observed between Pap staining and bacterial vaginosis (p = 0.584) or C. albicans infection (p = 0.139), indicating that the diagnostic value of Pap smear is limited compared to microscopy and/or culture methods.

The machine learning analysis with an MLP NN model showed that sociodemographic and socioeconomic variables might explain the prediction of some urogenital infections. The high NPV, in particular for T. vaginalis, indicates that the model may have a role in ruling out infection under certain circumstances. However, differences between infection types in relation to model performance indicate the requirement for additional optimization and validation before the applicability of such analyses can be generally recommended.

In summary, the findings have indicated that the use of a machine learning analytic model may be able to supplement traditional statistical analysis by identifying interactions between sociodemographic characteristics and combined urogenital infections. The association of these contemporary risk factors may underscore the need for combined strategies involving screening, education promotion and increasing access to health service delivery to decrease burden of infection and improve women’s health.
