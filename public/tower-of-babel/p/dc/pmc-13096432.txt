Postmenstrual age-adjusted brain volumes and their association with early neurodevelopmental outcomes in very low birth weight infants
Authors: Moon-Yeon Oh, Sol Kim, Min Soo Kim, Jeongmin Shin, Sae Yun Kim, Sook Kyung Yum, Soo-Ah Im, Young-Ah Youn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVery low birth weight (VLBW) infants are at a higher risk of developmental abnormalities, making early prediction essential for timely intervention. Brain MRI performed at near-term age is one of the key tools for predicting neurodevelopmental outcomes. This study aimed to investigate the association between Postmenstrual Age-Adjusted brain volume, as measured by MRI, and neurodevelopmental outcomes in VLBW infants at corrected 12 to 18 months of age. Brain MRIs from 118 infants were analyzed. Brain volumes were adjusted to a postmenstrual age of 40 weeks, and z-scores were calculated to categorize the infants into groups with larger and smaller brain volumes. Neurodevelopmental assessment using the Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development, Third Edition, was performed in 75 infants. After adjusting for sex, cerebellar volume showed significant associations with all domains of development—cognitive, language, and motor. Total brain and cerebral volumes were significantly associated with language and motor outcomes, while differences in cerebellar volume between groups were particularly associated with language development. These findings underscore the importance of cerebellar growth, along with global and cerebral brain development, in shaping early neurodevelopmental trajectories of VLBW infants.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42039-4.Keywords: Prematurity, Very low birth weight, Brain volume, Neurodevelopmental outcomeSubject terms: Magnetic resonance imaging, Brain imaging, Neonatology, Preterm birth

Introduction

The incidence of developmental delays in preterm infants is approximately 20%. However, neurodevelopmental impairments in very low birth weight (VLBW) and very preterm infants have been reported in 20–50% of cases1–3 which is markedly higher than the estimated 1–10% observed in the general pediatric population4,5. Importantly, even in the absence of major brain lesions on neuroimaging, the incidence of developmental delays in VLBW infants has been reported to exceed 20%6. Early prediction of these neurological disabilities is crucial, as it would enable early intervention and potentially improve outcomes. However, predicting developmental delays during the neonatal period remains challenging, prompting ongoing efforts to forecast the long-term outcomes for preterm infants receiving care in neonatal intensive care units.

Brain ultrasound is one of the commonly used tools for predicting neurodevelopmental outcomes7. However, with advancements in the accessibility and quality of neonatal brain MRI, there has been increasing emphasis on using MRI for predicting the prognosis of VLBW infants during the neonatal period8. Qualitative MRI is considered a more pragmatic and feasible tool in this context, as quantitative MRI requires additional analysis with specialized software pipelines that are not widely available in most clinical settings. Nevertheless, recent advances in imaging technology and analytic pipelines have led to growing efforts to investigate neurodevelopmental outcomes using quantitative MRI metrics. Among these quantitative approaches, measuring brain volumes and assessing brain condition through these measurements have emerged as significant approaches for prognosis9.

Several studies have reported an association between brain volume—particularly cerebellar volume—and neurodevelopmental outcomes10–13. Reduced cerebellar volume at term-equivalent age in very preterm infants was related to early neurodevelopmental outcomes, and this association was largely mediated by concomitant white matter injury14. The cerebellum, which constitutes approximately 10% of total brain weight, has extensive neural projections to the cerebral cortex15. It is known to be associated with neurodevelopment, particularly in motor and cognitive outcomes11,16.

Advancements in segmentation techniques have enabled more detailed classification of brain structures, such as white matter, deep nuclear gray matter, and cortical gray matter10,12,17. However, rapid and accurate segmentation of the neonatal brain remains challenging because of incomplete myelination. For this reason, alternative approaches, such as using easily measurable transcerebellar diameter to assess its association with developmental outcomes, have also been explored13. In clinical practice, identifying high-risk infants for delayed developmental outcomes is often critical for earlier intervention. Therefore, the use of simplified and intuitively measurable brain volumes, adjusted for postmenstrual age (PMA) may improve their practical applicability. Volumetric indices such as total brain and cerebellar volumes, which can be readily derived from semi-automated segmentation of routine MRI scans, can be further normalized for PMA to account for rapid brain growth during this period, thereby improving the accuracy of volumetric comparisons.

This study aims to investigate the association between PMA-adjusted brain volume at near-term age and neurodevelopmental outcomes in VLBW infants. By evaluating the relationship between brain volume measurements and neurodevelopmental outcomes, this study aims to enhance the prediction of long-term neurodevelopmental impairments in this vulnerable population.

Materials and methods

Study population

The study population consisted of VLBW infants who underwent brain MRI at a single tertiary referral university hospital with a level IV neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) between July 2013 and December 2019. A total of 521 VLBW infants were hospitalized during the study period. Of these, 125 VLBW infants who underwent brain MRI were initially enrolled in this retrospective cohort study (Fig. 1). Because there are currently no standardized guidelines for performing brain MRI in VLBW infants18, the decision to perform imaging was made at the discretion of the attending neonatologist with parental consent, and MRI was more likely to be obtained in infants with abnormal cranial ultrasound findings or concerning clinical conditions. From 2018 onward, brain MRI was implemented as a local institutional protocol for most VLBW infants at our center to support early detection of developmental abnormalities. Seven infants who had undergone ventriculoperitoneal (VP) shunt surgery were excluded to minimize confounding effects on brain volume assessment.

Study flow diagram. VLBW, very low birth weight; VP shunt, ventriculoperitoneal shunt; Bayley III, Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development, Third Edition.

The brain MRIs of these 118 infants were processed using advanced 3D software tools to obtain precise volumetric measurements across different regions. Subsequently, PMA-adjusted z-scores for brain volume were extracted from these MRI datasets. Among these, 75 infants underwent neurodevelopmental assessment with the Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development, Third Edition (Bayley III), at 12 to 18 months of corrected age. The association between PMA-adjusted volumetric z-scores and Bayley III outcomes in these 75 infants was then evaluated.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of Seoul St. Mary’s Hospital, The Catholic University of Korea (KC23RASI0057). Due to the retrospective nature of the study, the Institutional Review Board of Seoul St. Mary’s Hospital, The Catholic University of Korea waived the need of obtaining informed consent.

Definitions

The major diseases included in the study were defined as follows: Pulmonary hypertension was defined by the need for nitric oxide, sildenafil, or iloprost for a period of up to 1 week after birth. Bronchopulmonary dysplasia was diagnosed if oxygen requirement >21% at 36 weeks postmenstrual age19. Necrotizing enterocolitis was defined as grade II or higher according to Bell’s classification20. Severe intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH ≥ III) was defined according to the Papile classification, which includes ventricular dilatation and parenchymal involvement.21.

Anthropometric measurements for the newborns were obtained either at the time of birth or within one week after birth, and the most recent measurements taken within one week prior to discharge were used as the anthropometric data at the time of discharge. The INTERGROWTH-21st standard was used to standardize weight, head circumference, and height measurements according to postmenstrual weeks22. Neurodevelopmental outcomes were assessed using Bayley III. Developmental delay was defined as a composite score < 85 in each respective domain (cognitive, language, and motor).

Brain MRI and volumetry

Neonatal brain MRI scans were performed between 35 and 53 weeks of corrected age, primarily around 40 weeks, either after administering chloral hydrate syrup or during natural sleep. Imaging was conducted using a Siemens 3T MAGNETOM Vida MRI system (Siemens, Erlangen, Germany) with 64-channel phased array head coil.

For quantitative assessment (total brain volume, cerebral volume, cerebellar volume, and brain stem volume), MR images were uploaded to dedicated 3D workstation (Aquarius iNtuition, TeraRecon Inc., Durham, NC). Axial FLAIR and T2-weighted MR images (slice thickness 3 mm; field of view 14 × 14 cm2; matrix 256 × 256) were annotated using a previously described 3D program23,24. Two technicians, blinded to clinical outcomes, performed semi-automatic volumetric measurements, which were subsequently reviewed and confirmed by a pediatric radiologist. Volume metrics were extracted from the segmented images, and segmentation accuracy was verified through 3D reconstruction. As illustrated in Fig. 2A, annotation was performed on three orthogonal two-dimensional planes, and the software automatically recognized boundary differences from adjacent tissues to reconstruct three-dimensional volumes. The reconstructed volumes were then used for measurement and could be visually verified, as shown in Fig. 2B. To ensure internal validity, segmentation accuracy was visually confirmed by the pediatric radiologist. In addition, inter-rater reproducibility was assessed in 10 randomly selected cases and demonstrated excellent agreement (intraclass correlation coefficient > 0.95).

Statistical analysis

Measurements of brain volumes (total brain, cerebrum, cerebellum, brainstem, and ventricle) were collected at different PMAs. To examine their relationship with PMA, Pearson correlation coefficients (r) with 95% confidence intervals were calculated, and linear regression models of the form Volume = β0 + β1 × PMA were fitted for each region. Using the fitted regression, brain volumes at the target PMA of 40 weeks (280 days) were estimated. To adjust each measured value, we first calculated the residual (observed_value – predicted_value at the infant’s PMA) and then added this residual to the predicted value at 40 weeks. This yielded the adjusted brain volume, formally expressed as adj_value = observed_value – predicted_value + target_predicted_value, where observed_value is the measured volume at the PMA of MRI, predicted_value is the expected volume at the same PMA estimated from the regression model, and target_predicted_value is the predicted volume at 40 weeks from the same model. To facilitate comparison across individuals, adjusted brain volumes were further standardized into z-scores: z = (adj_value – mean)/SD.

These standardized measures were then examined in relation to Bayley III cognitive, language, and motor scores composite scores. Correlation and regression analyses were performed with sex included as a covariate to control for sex-related differences. In addition, infants were categorized into high- and low-volume groups based on z-scores (≥ 0 vs. < 0) for each brain region. Group comparisons of Bayley III scores were performed using linear regression for continuous outcomes, and comparisons of developmental delay rates were conducted using logistic regression, with sex adjustment applied in all models. Results are presented as mean ± SD for continuous variables and number (percentage) for categorical variables, with sex-adjusted p-values reported.

Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and compared between groups using Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using χ² or Fisher’s exact test. All statistical analyses were two-tailed, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Post hoc power analysis indicated that the sample size (N = 75) provided 0.57 power for group comparisons (Cohen’s d = 0.5) and 0.10–0.52 power for correlations (r = 0.10–0.31), suggesting limited ability to detect small-to-moderate effects. All analyses were conducted using R version 4.2.2 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, http://www.r-project.org).

Results

During the study period, a total of 521 VLBW infants were admitted, with a mean gestational age of 28.0 ± 2.8 weeks and a mean birth weight of 1015.9 ± 286.8 g. Among them, 125 infants underwent brain MRI. Infants in the MRI group had higher mean gestational age and birth weight compared with those who did not undergo MRI, likely reflecting the fact that MRI could only be performed in infants who survived to a clinically stable condition near term-equivalent age. The MRI group also showed higher rates of maternal chorioamnionitis, confirmed PVL, sepsis, moderate BPD, and a longer length of hospital stay. In contrast, the non-MRI group had higher rates of severe IVH (grade ≥ III) and substantially higher mortality (Supplementary Table 1). After adjusting the measured total brain volume to a corrected full-term age of 40 weeks, subjects were categorized into groups based on positive and negative z-scores, indicating larger and smaller brain volumes, respectively. The clinical characteristics of the two groups are shown in Table 1. The group with smaller total brain volume had a lower gestational age and birth weight at birth. Overall, the smaller brain volume group had lower body weight, head circumference, and height at discharge, indicating poorer physical growth. Additionally, this group exhibited higher incidences of pulmonary hemorrhage, pulmonary hypertension, sepsis, bronchopulmonary dysplasia, periventricular leukomalacia, and intracranial hemorrhage. They also experienced longer durations of mechanical ventilation and hospitalization, as well as a higher PMA at discharge.

Among the 118 infants, 75 underwent the Bayley III assessment at corrected 12–18 months of age. To clarify the characteristics of infants who completed neurodevelopmental follow-up, we compared clinical variables between infants who underwent Bayley III assessment and those who did not. Gestational age was lower and maternal age was higher in the Bayley III–assessed group compared with the non-assessed group. However, no other significant differences were observed between the two groups. Further, the incidence of brain lesions such as intraventricular hemorrhage, periventricular leukomalacia, and intracranial hemorrhage did not differ significantly between the two groups. At discharge, the degree of growth, including head circumference at both admission and discharge, was similar between the groups. The length of hospital stay and the timing of MRI scans were not significantly different. (Supplementary Table 1)

Brain MRI was performed at PMAs ranging from 35 to 53 weeks, with most being conducted before 45 weeks. The average age at MRI was 86.9 ± 34.5 days, with a mean PMA of 40.5 ± 3.9 weeks. All measured brain volumes tended to increase with PMA, as summarized in Fig. 3; Table 2. The correlation analysis indicated strong positive correlations between PMA days and the volumes of the Total brain, Cerebrum, Cerebellar, and Brainstem regions, with coefficients ranging from 0.67 to 0.79. The Ventricle volume showed a weaker correlation with a coefficient of 0.23. Linear regression models quantified these relationships, showing, for instance, an average increase of 2.6 mL per day for the total brain volume (SE = 0.19) and predicting a volume of 352.76 mL at 40 weeks PMA. The weekly volume increase was 18.17 mL, with a 95% confidence interval of 15.61 to 20.74mL. Similar trends were observed for other brain regions, highlighting differential growth patterns across brain regions as a function of PMA days.

The analysis of the relationship between total brain volume and regional volumes (cerebrum, cerebellum, brainstem), adjusted to a PMA of 40 weeks, and the Bayley III assessment results conducted at corrected ages of 12 to 18 months, is presented in Fig. 4; Table 3. Linear regression analyses were performed with sex included as a covariate. The cerebellar volume demonstrated statistically significant associations across all domains—cognitive, language, and motor—and showed the strongest correlations compared to other brain regions. In addition, total brain and cerebrum volumes were significantly associated with language and motor scores, whereas no significant associations were found for the brain stem. This suggests that cerebellar growth may play a crucial role in neurodevelopmental outcomes in very-low-birth-weight infants, while global and cerebral volumes also contribute to language and motor development. Our analysis explored the relationship between brain volume sizes, segmented by z-score into high and low volume groups, and developmental outcomes assessed by the Bayley III scales, particularly focusing on regions showing significant differences. (Table 4) The cerebellar volume showed a statistically significant correlation with language scores, suggesting that smaller cerebellar volume may predict poorer language development. However, no other brain regions demonstrated a statistically significant correlation with cognitive, language, or motor developmental scores when comparing high and low volume groups.

Discussion

In this study, cerebellar volume showed significant associations with all domains of neurodevelopment—cognitive, language, and motor—at 12–18 months of corrected age, and these associations remained significant after adjusting for sex. Total brain and cerebrum volumes were also significantly associated with language and motor outcomes, whereas no significant associations were found for the brain stem. Cerebellar volume demonstrated the strongest and most consistent associations; however, the differences compared with total brain and cerebrum volumes were modest. These findings suggest that both cerebellar and global brain growth contribute meaningfully to early neurodevelopment in VLBW infants.

Traditionally regarded as a center for motor control, the cerebellum also plays a crucial role in language processing and development25. It is interconnected with language regions in the cerebral cortex, including Broca’s area, through dense reciprocal connections26. These connections enable the cerebellum to participate in both the motor and cognitive aspects of language processing. Damage to the cerebellum is known to impair language fluency, grammar processing, and the ability to identify and correct language errors25. The cerebellum develops over an extended period, making it vulnerable to early insults that can influence language development. Research indicates that a reduction in cerebellar volume, particularly in the posterolateral regions, is associated with language delays27. Due to its extensive connections with the cerebral cortex’s language areas, a reduction in cerebellar volume can lead to language delays and deficits, emphasizing the cerebellum’s significant role in language and cognitive development., Shah et al. also reported that reduced cerebellar volumes in very preterm infants were associated with early neurodevelopmental outcomes, but this relationship was mediated by concomitant white matter injury, underscoring the importance of considering cerebral white matter integrity when interpreting cerebellar findings14.

Our results highlight that cerebellar volume showed the strongest and most consistent associations with neurodevelopmental outcomes, a finding in line with previous studies11,13. However, we also observed that total brain and cerebrum volumes contributed to language and motor development, which aligns with other reports suggesting that global and cerebral volumes, particularly white matter, are associated with neurodevelopment28–32. These studies have reported that total brain volume or cerebrum volume, particularly white matter, is significantly associated with neurodevelopment, especially language development10,12.

The post hoc power analysis indicated that our study had a statistical power of 0.57 for detecting medium-sized effects in group comparisons and 0.10 to 0.52 for detecting correlations. This suggests that the sample size (N = 75) may have limited our ability to detect meaningful differences and associations, particularly for small-to-moderate effects. Thus, while the limited power may have constrained detection of additional associations in other brain regions, the consistency of cerebellar findings strengthens confidence in their clinical relevance. Future studies with larger sample sizes are recommended to improve statistical power and validate these findings, as well as to further assess the clinical and developmental implications of both cerebellar and global brain growth in VLBW infants. Among the 118 patients who underwent MRI, only 75 received the Bayley III assessment at corrected 12–18 months. When the brain volume adjustments were performed on the entire cohort of MRI patients, the clinical characteristics of the overall cohort and those who underwent the Bayley III assessment were very similar, with no statistically significant differences except for gestational age and maternal age.(Supplementary Table 1) Therefore, it was deemed appropriate to apply the z-scores calculated from the entire cohort of 118 patients to those who completed the Bayley III assessment. Additionally, having a larger sample size for the brain volume adjustment likely increased the accuracy of estimating changes and predictions of brain volume with advancing gestational age.

The study results indicated that infants with smaller brain volumes had lower gestational ages, lower birth weights, and a higher incidence of neonatal morbidities associated with prematurity. Additionally, while there were no significant differences in weight, head circumference, and height z-scores at admission, all growth indicators showed significant differences at discharge. Smaller brain group infants also had longer hospital stays and were discharged at a later PMA. These findings suggest that both prenatal and postnatal factors, including preterm-related morbidities and growth during the NICU stay, play a crucial role in brain development and may ultimately influence neurodevelopmental outcomes.

Brain lesions can affect brain volume and are associated with developmental disabilities. Studies that did not exclude the presence of brain lesions have shown that regional brain volume at term-equivalent age significantly influences developmental outcomes at two years of age30. In our study, we excluded patients who required a VP shunt due to increased intracranial pressure that could affect post-MRI brain growth. However, infants with preterm-related brain lesions, such as periventricular leukomalacia or intraventricular hemorrhage, were not excluded, as these conditions would have influenced brain volume before MRI measurement. Our study focuses on the association between brain volume at the time of MRI, including the impact of preceding brain injuries, and neurodevelopmental outcomes, rather than isolating individual pathological conditions.

In this study, we adjusted the brain volumes of 118 infants based on MRI results, normalizing these values to the PMA of 40 weeks. Brain MRI was performed over a relatively wide range of PMAs, and the relationship between PMA and brain volume was modeled using a linear approach, which may have influenced the accuracy of the findings. However, previous studies have demonstrated that brain volume growth in the neonatal period follows a relatively linear trajectory with age29,33,34. Additionally, we selected a linear modeling approach to improve the clinical applicability of brain volume estimation in preterm infants. This adjustment allowed us to derive standardized brain volume measurements, calculate z-scores, estimate brain volume growth rates in preterm infants, and provide expected brain volume values at term-equivalent age.

Various studies have reported average brain volume values measured by MRI at term-equivalent age, with total brain volume ranging from 270 to 462 mL and total cerebellar volume from 16.1 to 24.2 mL35–40. Although no universally established reference range exists for preterm infant brain volume, Romberg et al.33 reported mean values of 379 mL (SD 72 mL) for total brain volume and 21 mL (SD 6 mL) for cerebellar volume at term-equivalent age in a systematic review. In our study, the estimated total brain volume at 40 weeks PMA was 352.76 mL, and the cerebellar volume was 18.26 mL. These findings suggest that the brain volume measurements in our study closely align with previously reported values, supporting their validity and clinical relevance. In addition, the weekly growth rate of total brain volume in our cohort (18.2 mL/week) was comparable to that reported in term infants by Holland et al. (≈ 22 mL/week during the third trimester)41. It also fell within the range observed in longitudinal studies such as Knickmeyer et al. (10–15 mL/week on average during the first postnatal year)42. Cerebellar growth in our study (1.5 mL/week) was likewise consistent with previous reports41,42. The slightly lower overall volumes compared to some published norms may reflect both the vulnerability of VLBW infants and potential selection bias, as MRI was more often performed in clinically high-risk infants31. Taken together, these results indicate although absolute brain volumes were somewhat reduced, the observed growth trajectories remained consistent with established developmental patterns, supporting the robustness of our findings.

This study has several limitations. First, brain MRI was not performed in all VLBW infants, and the decision to obtain MRI depended on clinical judgment and parental consent, as no standardized guidelines exist for routine screening in this population18. Because only infants who survived to a clinically stable condition near term-equivalent age could undergo MRI, survival bias was unavoidable. MRI was also more frequently performed in infants with abnormal cranial ultrasound findings or concerning neurological conditions, contributing to selection bias. To evaluate this bias, we compared infants who did and did not undergo MRI (Supplementary Table 1). The MRI group had higher gestational age and birth weight, reflecting greater survival to the time of imaging, but also showed higher rates of maternal chorioamnionitis, PVL, sepsis, and moderate BPD—conditions that commonly prompt neuroimaging. In contrast, the non-MRI group had substantially higher rates of severe IVH and markedly higher mortality, likely because many critically ill infants died before MRI could be performed. These findings suggest that the MRI cohort represents infants who survived to later PMAs and includes both lower overall illness severity (due to survival) and higher prevalence of morbidities that triggered MRI. This limits the representativeness of the MRI cohort relative to the entire VLBW population; however, the primary aim of our study was to evaluate associations between brain volume and neurodevelopment among infants who underwent imaging. Thus, while selection and survival biases should be considered when interpreting generalizability, they are unlikely to substantially affect the internal validity of the observed associations.

Second, only about 64% of infants with brain volume measurements completed Bayley III assessments, reflecting the retrospective study design and the follow-up limitations common in the Korean clinical setting6. Although incomplete follow-up remains a limitation, comparisons between infants with and without Bayley data (Supplementary Table 2) showed no major differences in key perinatal characteristics, aside from slightly lower gestational age and higher maternal age in the tested group. This small gestational age difference may have contributed to marginally lower developmental scores but is unlikely to have introduced substantial bias. Importantly, the primary aim of this study was to obtain reliable PMA-adjusted brain volume estimates, which were derived from the full MRI cohort. Therefore, while residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded, the incomplete follow-up is unlikely to meaningfully affect the identified associations between brain volume and neurodevelopmental outcomes.

Finally, in analyses adjusted for sex, the associations between brain volumes and Bayley III scores became more pronounced, suggesting that sex is an important factor when evaluating neurodevelopmental outcomes. However, due to our limited sample size, we were unable to examine sex-specific growth trajectories in greater detail, and this remains an area for future research. Previous studies have also reported that male neonates generally have larger brain volumes than female neonates43, indicating that sex may influence brain size and should be considered when interpreting our findings.

One of the primary goals of brain MRI in preterm infants is to predict neurodevelopmental outcomes and identify high-risk infants for early intervention. In this context, the novelty of our study lies in applying a semi-automatic volumetric measurement that is relatively simple to perform, combined with PMA-adjusted reference modeling, to provide a clinically practical framework for interpreting neonatal brain volume. Previous studies have attempted to use simpler markers, such as transcerebellar diameter, but these approaches did not adjust for MRI timing, which limits their reliability13. By contrast, our method incorporates PMA adjustment, enabling more accurate comparisons across infants scanned at slightly different PMAs. This methodological approach not only improves reliability but also makes the relative brain size more intuitive to recognize and interpret in clinical practice. Our results highlight that, after sex adjustment, cerebellar volume showed consistent associations with all three Bayley III domains (cognitive, language, and motor), while total brain and cerebral volumes were significantly associated with language and motor outcomes. Moreover, in group-based comparisons, infants in the low cerebellar volume group demonstrated significantly lower language scores. This finding suggests that smaller cerebellar volume can serve as an early marker to identify infants at high risk of language delay, supporting its utility for early risk stratification in the NICU setting.

This study therefore proposes a practical approach using simplified volumetric measurement and timing adjustment to improve the clinical applicability of brain volume assessments. By focusing on relative brain size alone, our method demonstrates that it is possible to identify high-risk groups without the need for complex, resource-intensive imaging pipelines. Another strength of this approach is that the adjusted and standardized brain volumes can be intuitively understood, thereby increasing accessibility for clinicians beyond research environments. Importantly, our framework is not intended to replace qualitative MRI, which provides valuable information on structural abnormalities, but rather to complement it by offering a simple and standardized quantitative tool that can enhance interpretation and risk stratification in clinical practice. In the future, we hope to establish an AI-based prediction model to identify infants at high risk for neurodevelopmental impairments. While our approach offers valuable insights, we acknowledge the limitation of not analyzing long-term developmental outcomes, which future research should address. Further studies with larger datasets are needed to refine PMA adjustments and validate this framework across diverse populations. In addition, future methodological improvements may involve replacing semi-automatic volumetry with fully automated, AI-driven segmentation techniques, which could further enhance accuracy, reduce observer dependence, and facilitate widespread clinical implementation. Ultimately, our findings support the potential of simplified, PMA-adjusted volumetry as a clinically applicable method to enhance early identification of high-risk infants for neurodevelopmental impairments.
