EVA-centric QUBO optimization for active and reactive power coordination in DLMP-driven distribution systems
Authors: Vansh Suri, Neelu Nagpal, Pierluigi Siano, Ravi Sharma, Saurabh Agarwal, Wooguil Pak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis research presents a bilevel optimization framework enhanced with quadratic unconstrained binary optimization (QUBO) for the reactive demand response (DR) driven by the locational marginal price (DLMP) of electric vehicle aggregators (EVA) in renewable integrated distribution systems. The methodology fundamentally reverses the traditional power system hierarchy by positioning the EVA as the Stackelberg leader and the distribution system operator (DSO) as the follower, acknowledging the growing influence of electric vehicles as dominant flexible resources. The proposed framework introduces the first comprehensive approach that simultaneously optimizes both active power charging schedules and reactive power provision through QUBO-enhanced spectral clustering with multimetric validation. It also introduces a novel post-optimization disaggregation methodology that distributes cluster-level decisions to individual vehicles while minimizing charging interruptions. The comprehensive evaluation of the IEEE 33 bus distribution system with 1000 electric vehicles demonstrates quantifiable economic benefits that include a 24.70% reduction in DSO costs, a 75.93% reduction in EVA payments, and 39.55% total system cost savings compared to conventional approaches. The technical improvements include a 4.64% reduction in power loss, 9.86% improvement in voltage deviation, 6.49% improvement in the load factor, and 86.11% effectiveness of the voltage support. QUBO optimization achieves a 100% success rate with 72,000 binary variables while introducing reactive power revenue streams generating $47.20 per day, while maintaining 98.7% customer satisfaction. The quantum-inspired optimization approach positions the framework for future enhancement as quantum computing technologies mature, providing immediate benefits through algorithms capable of handling the computational complexity of large-scale EV integration scenarios. The methodology has been compared against Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) and Genetic Algorithm (GA), and the QUBO problem has been simulated using a Linprog, a solver in Yalmip toolbox in MATLAB, and compared against a commercial solver, Gurobi, for computation analysis. The results establish performance benchmarks for advanced EV integration strategies, providing validation data for regulatory frameworks and investment decisions in distribution systems with high penetration of electric vehicles and renewable energy.Keywords: QUBO optimization, Bilevel programming, Reactive demand response, Distribution locational marginal pricing, Spectral clustering, Smart grid optimizationSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The increasing adoption of electric vehicles (EVs) and renewable energy sources is challenging traditional grid operations1,2. At the same time, studies show that EVAs can improve the flexibility of the grid by aggregating distributed EV resources to provide key services, including stability support, renewable integration, and demand response (DR) programs3,4. Thus, research has transitioned from treating EVs solely as controllable loads to recognizing them as strategic distributed energy resources that can influence system-wide operations5. Electric vehicle aggregators (EVAs) have been conceptualized as intermediaries that aggregate individual EV flexibility to participate in various grid services such as peak shaving, valley filling, and voltage regulation6–9 with proper coordination among multiple EVs. Coordinated charging and discharging of EVs has facilitated multi-time scale demand response, contributing to both short-term operational flexibility and long-term system planning2,10. The EVAs have established their potential as active grid support resources rather than passive loads by providing ancillary services, particularly for frequency regulation and voltage support11,12. Furthermore, EVA participation on an incentive basis is enabled by dynamic pricing mechanisms that serve as key tools for encouraging engagement in DR programs. Among common pricing strategies: Time-of-Use (TOU), real-time pricing (RTP), and compensation-based approaches13–15, dynamic pricing models help reduce peak demand and support renewable integration by promoting load shifting to optimal periods16. In previous research, active power DR has received extensive attention, but reactive power management by aggregators has remained significantly underexplored in the literature. The few studies addressing reactive power services have demonstrated substantial potential for voltage support and power factor improvement11,17,18. DLMP for reactive power has been proposed as a mechanism to incentivize EV participation in voltage regulation services, though practical implementation frameworks are limited. The integration of four-quadrant EV chargers capable of bidirectional reactive power flow represents an emerging capability that could significantly enhance grid support services. Jangid et al.17 have demonstrated the practical implementation of reactive DR from EV aggregators DLMP, showing how coordinated active and reactive power optimization can improve both voltage stability and grid economics17. However, the literature reveals that such comprehensive approaches remain limited, with most studies focusing exclusively on active power management for energy arbitrage purposes.

The EV-grid integration has identified hierarchical control architectures as essential frameworks to develop better coordination among multiple stakeholders. Among all, clustering has been a key component in EV aggregation as it reduces computational complexity by grouping vehicles with similar charging patterns, energy requirements, and temporal availability, transforming individual optimization problems into manageable cluster-level decisions. This dimensionality reduction enables efficient problem formulation while preserving the heterogeneous characteristics of different EV types, ensuring that the aggregator can provide coordinated grid services without losing the flexibility needed for individual vehicle constraints. Additionally, clustering facilitates better load balancing and voltage support by strategically grouping EVs based on their reactive power capabilities and grid connection locations. Bilevel or multilevel optimization approaches with Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions have been used to integrate competing objectives while maintaining computational tractability18. The Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM) has been particularly successful in enabling distributed EV scheduling while preserving privacy and reducing computational burden11,19. Mean-field game theory has been applied to handle large EV populations, providing theoretical foundations for scalable coordination mechanisms20. Existing studies have predominantly focused on Stackelberg game models to establish the hierarchy of DSOs, EVAs, and EV users, shifting levels depending on the specific research focus6,20,21. Stackelberg-theoretic frameworks have been extensively applied to model the hierarchical interactions between EVAs and system operators, positioning EVAs as leaders or followers in the decision-making process, with studies showing varying approaches to hierarchy definition6,8,16. The effectiveness of Stackelberg models in achieving optimal pricing and scheduling strategies while balancing stakeholder interests has been consistently demonstrated across different system configurations and scales.

Recent work has investigated advanced optimization methodologies, adopted to address the computational complexity of large-scale EV integration. Mixed-integer linear and nonlinear programming approaches have been widely applied to EV scheduling problems, with studies reporting successful handling of discrete charging decisions and binary operational variables9,10. Stochastic programming techniques have been employed to address uncertainties in EV availability, energy requirements, and market prices22,23. Game-theoretic approaches beyond Stackelberg models have been explored, including non-cooperative games, potential games, and mechanism design frameworks24,25. These approaches address strategic interactions among multiple EVAs or between EVAs and other market participants, though the complexity of multi-party game equilibria presents ongoing challenges for practical implementation. The application of emerging approaches, such as quantum-inspired algorithms and QUBO formulations, to practical problems is still limited. These techniques have shown theoretical promise for handling the combinatorial complexity of large-scale EV scheduling problems, but empirical validation in realistic power system contexts is still evolving11,26. The transition from classical to quantum-inspired optimization represents a frontier area with significant potential for future development.

Clustering algorithms have been identified as essential tools for managing large EV populations while maintaining computational tractability. Spectral clustering has emerged as the preferred method, consistently outperforming traditional k-means approaches to capture EV behavioral patterns and operational characteristics10,13,17. An effective clustering reduces computational complexity while improving scheduling accuracy and DR participation rates. Self-Organizing Map (SOM) neural networks and fuzzy clustering approaches have been applied to capture uncertain EV characteristics and user behavior patterns7,10. These methods show particular effectiveness in handling heterogeneous EV populations with varying arrival times, energy requirements, and charging preferences. However, the dynamic nature of EV availability and mobility patterns presents ongoing challenges for static clustering approaches.

Identified gaps and research opportunities

The literature review reveals several significant limitations that constrain the practical applicability of current research. Assuming perfect information of EV availability, and user behavior represents a significant departure from realistic operating conditions, potentially undermining the robustness of proposed strategies22,23. Further, computational scalability remains a concern, with many optimization models facing exponential complexity growth as EV population sizes increase. While clustering approaches address this challenge partially, the trade-offs between computational efficiency and solution optimality are not comprehensively analyzed across different system scales11,24. The integration of multiple EV aggregators operating in competitive markets represents an underexplored area, with limited research on coordination mechanisms and market efficiency implications. The literature has inadequately addressed communication infrastructure requirements, data privacy concerns, and cybersecurity implications of distributed EV control systems19,27.

Most existing studies have treated clustering as a preprocessing tool rather than embedding it within the optimization framework, thereby limiting the adaptive capabilities of aggregation strategies. This highlights the need for integrated clustering–optimization approaches to enhance scalability and adaptability in EV management. Recent literature indicates several emerging trends that will likely shape future research directions. The integration of machine learning approaches for predicting EV behavior and optimizing dynamics is gaining attention, with early studies showing promise for improved forecasting accuracy and adaptive control19. The development of quantum-inspired optimization techniques represents a frontier area with potential for addressing the combinatorial complexity of large-scale EV coordination problems.

The transition toward prosumer-centric models where EVAs take leadership roles in market interactions represents a paradigm shift from traditional utility-controlled operations. This evolution reflects the growing influence of distributed energy resources and the need for more flexible grid operation paradigms6. The integration of EV resources with smart city infrastructure represents an interdisciplinary opportunity to optimize both mobility and energy system efficiency. The literature establishes a strong foundation for EVA research while identifying critical gaps that require attention for practical deployment. The convergence of advanced optimization techniques, comprehensive modeling, and real-world validation represents a pathway toward realizing the full potential of EV aggregation in future power systems.

Unlike conventional concepts where the DSO acts as the leader by setting DLMPs and the EVA follows as a price-taker, the proposed study fundamentally reverses the hierarchical relationship by positioning the EVA as the Stackelberg leader, enabling proactive market participation and enhanced system efficiency through reactive power services. This strategic reversal acknowledges the increasing penetration of EVs in distribution networks and their potential to provide valuable grid support services beyond simple energy consumption. The proposed framework recognizes EVs as active participants in the electricity market, capable of making strategic decisions that influence system-wide operations. By positioning the EVA as the leader, the methodology captures the reality of future power systems where distributed energy resources, particularly electric vehicles, will play increasingly dominant roles in grid operation and planning. The methodology addresses the growing influence of EVs as dominant flexible resources capable of shaping distribution system operations through coordinated active and reactive power provision.

Proposed methodology

This section presents a comprehensive QUBO-enhanced bi-level optimization framework for DLMP-driven active and reactive demand response from EVAs. The methodology begins by aggregating individual EVs into manageable clusters using QUBO-enhanced spectral clustering, which groups vehicles based on their charging patterns, energy requirements, arrival and departure times, and power capabilities. This process is formulated as a binary optimization problem that assigns each vehicle to exactly one cluster while balancing intra-cluster similarity with cluster sizes. The resulting clusters inform infrastructure planning by identifying groups of EVs with similar charging characteristics, which guides decisions on the number of charging stations, socket capacity allocation, and scheduling of charging resources. This, in turn, enables the EVA to right-size its facilities, optimize strategic planning, and make informed bids in the electricity market by clarifying peak demand requirements, temporal utilization patterns, energy procurement needs, and reactive power capabilities for ancillary services.

Next, a hierarchical decision-making structure is established, with the EVA acting as the leader, making strategic decisions on when and how much power each cluster should consume for both charging and voltage support services to the grid. Core optimization occurs through an iterative bilevel game, in which the EVA first solves a QUBO problem to determine optimal charging and reactive power schedules for each cluster (derived from spectral clustering), considering estimated electricity prices and operational constraints. The QUBO formulation converts continuous power decisions into discrete binary choices by discretizing power levels and time periods–for example, deciding whether a cluster operates at low, medium, or high power during specific intervals. Once the EVA determines the cluster-level schedules, a post-optimization disaggregation process translates these aggregate decisions into individual vehicle schedules, ensuring that each vehicle meets its energy requirements while minimizing charging disruptions. The DSO then responds by solving its own QUBO optimization problem, using the EVA’s power demands as inputs to optimize generator dispatch, voltage control, and grid operations, minimizing system costs while maintaining power balance and voltage constraints.

The iterative process continues as the DSO calculates new DLMPs based on the system conditions resulting from the EVA’s decisions (active and reactive demand schedules), incorporating factors such as power losses, congestion, and voltage support needs into the pricing signals. These updated prices are then fed back to the EVA, which re-optimizes its cluster schedules according to the new economic signals. This back-and-forth continues until the price estimates converge to a stable equilibrium, where neither party has an incentive to change its strategy. Throughout this process, the QUBO formulations are solved using quantum-inspired simulated annealing algorithms, which explore the binary solution space by gradually reducing randomness to converge on optimal solutions. At the same time, constraint violations are prevented through penalty terms embedded in the QUBO matrices.

Conclusion

This research has successfully developed and demonstrated a QUBO-enhanced bilevel optimization framework for DLMP-driven reactive demand response from electric vehicle aggregators (EVA) in renewable-integrated distribution systems. The proposed methodology represents a paradigm shift in power system operations by positioning the EVA as the Stackelberg leader, contrary to the traditional approach where the Distribution System Operator (DSO) leads the decision-making process. This fundamental change highlights the growing importance of electric vehicles (EVs) as flexible resources in modern grids, capable of actively shaping system operations rather than merely responding to system operator directives. The QUBO-based framework introduced here offers a comprehensive solution to optimize both active and reactive power provision simultaneously. This dual optimization enhances grid stability, reduces power losses, and improves voltage regulation, ultimately leading to more efficient grid management. By integrating QUBO-enhanced spectral clustering, we manage large numbers of EVs, grouping them into clusters for scalable and computationally tractable solutions. The effectiveness of this methodology is demonstrated through significant improvements in both economic and technical performance. Specifically, the proposed framework achieves a 24.70% reduction in DSO operational costs, a 75.93% reduction in EVA payments, and a 39.55% reduction in total system costs compared to conventional methods. Additionally, technical improvements such as a 4.64% reduction in power losses, a 9.86% improvement in voltage deviation, and a 6.49% enhancement in load factor highlight the robustness of the approach. Moreover, the reactive power services provided by the coordinated EVs contribute significantly to voltage support, further enhancing the stability of the distribution network. The introduction of new revenue streams, such as reactive power revenue ($47.20/day), underscores the economic potential for EV aggregators. These benefits, coupled with customer satisfaction rates above 98%, demonstrate that the proposed framework is not only technically sound but also practically viable for widespread deployment. In conclusion, this research paves the way for the future integration of electric vehicles in renewable-integrated grids, offering a promising solution for improving grid efficiency, stability, and sustainability.

Future scope

While this research introduces a powerful and efficient framework for integrating electric vehicles (EVs) into distribution systems, there are several avenues for further research and development. One of the key areas for future work is improving the scalability of the proposed QUBO-enhanced bilevel optimization framework. As quantum computing technologies continue to evolve, the quantum-inspired optimization approach used in this study has significant potential for enhancement. With advances in quantum hardware, the framework could be refined to handle larger, more complex power systems. This would allow for even greater efficiency in optimizing EV scheduling and reactive power services, potentially revolutionizing the way we manage grid operations. Furthermore, the EV clustering methodology, while effective, can be improved. Future work could explore more sophisticated clustering algorithms that adapt in real-time to system conditions, such as changes in renewable generation, load profiles, or operational constraints. A dynamic clustering approach could enhance the flexibility and responsiveness of the system, making it even more suitable for unpredictable grid scenarios, especially as renewable energy penetration continues to increase. Another area for enhancement is the disaggregation process that translates cluster-level decisions to individual EV schedules. While the current approach minimizes charging interruptions and meets energy requirements, future research could focus on further improving the efficiency and customer experience by incorporating additional factors, such as vehicle types, state of charge, and owner preferences. A more personalized approach could lead to even greater satisfaction, further increasing EV participation in demand response programs. In addition to these improvements, future research could expand the scope of this methodology to address other grid-related challenges. For example, integrating the framework with energy storage systems (ESS) and demand-side management (DSM) strategies could create more holistic and adaptive grid solutions. The ability to balance both active and reactive power in real-time could also be further explored by integrating advanced forecasting models and predictive analytics, enabling better anticipation of grid conditions and optimizing power flows before issues arise. Finally, the application of this framework could extend to other energy systems beyond the distribution network, such as microgrids, and could be applied in systems with high levels of distributed generation. As more regions move towards decentralized energy systems, the proposed approach could serve as a key enabler for the management and optimization of such systems, ultimately contributing to the broader goals of energy sustainability, resilience, and decarbonization.

In conclusion, the proposed framework provides a solid foundation for the future integration of electric vehicles into renewable-integrated grids. With further refinement and the incorporation of emerging technologies, this methodology has the potential to play a pivotal role in shaping the next generation of smart grids and decentralized energy systems.
