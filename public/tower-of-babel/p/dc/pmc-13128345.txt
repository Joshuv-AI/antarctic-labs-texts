Synthesis of 2‐Aryl(alkyl)benzimidazole Hydroxamic Acids as HDAC Inhibitors with Anti‐Angiogenesis Properties
Authors: Yi‐Ting Liu, Cheng‐Ta Lai, Shih‐Wei Wang, Shan‐Chi Liu, Yi‐Lin Hwang, Yi‐Chen Tsai, Chi‐Yeh Lee, Yu‐Wei Lai, Juei‐Yu Yen, Po‐Chuan Wang, Hsueh‐Yun Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study synthesized a series of C2 and N1‐substituted benzimidazole hydroxamic acid (6‐19) as HDAC inhibitors. Among them, most of 2‐aryl(alkyl)benzimidazole hydroxamic acids (11‐16) exhibited HDAC6 activity and slight HDAC1 (or HDAC2) activity. In addition, they also showed anti‐angiogenic activity and inhibited the growth of tested cancer cells. For instance, compound 13 has a GI50 of 3.9 μM against EPCs and inhibited the growth of HCT‐116, SK‐Hep‐1, and PC‐3 cells with GI50 values of 1.3, 4.2, and 7.5 μM, respectively. The exact mechanism underlying the anti‐angiogenic effects of these compounds remains unknown, though this study gives an insight into how future HDAC inhibitors that aim to target blood vessel formation can be designed.

Introduction

Epigenetic regulation, an effect of gene expression without changing DNA sequence, plays a crucial role in cellular regulatory processes. One of the regulations that is comprehensively studied is histone acetylation and deacetylation, which are modulated by the balance of histone acetyltransferases (HATs) and histone deacetylases (HDACs) [1]. Removing the acetyl groups from the lysine tails of core histone by HDACs results in “compacted” DNA state and repression of DNA transcription. Recent studies reveal that dysregulation of HDACs, causing the destruction of this balance, gives an impetus to the development of several diseases [2]. The mammalian HDAC family is comprised of 18 isozymes, which are further divided into four classes (I, II, III, and IV) and possess different functions. Zn2+‐dependent enzymes include class I (HDAC1, 2, 3, and 8), class IIa (HDAC4, 5, 7, and 9), class IIb (HDAC6 and 10), and class IV (HDAC11), while class III (sirtuins 1–7) are NAD+‐dependent enzymes [3]. Since the first generation pan‐HDAC inhibitors were approved by FDA, there have been clinical reports about cardiotoxicity and hematotoxicity, primarily due to off‐target effects originating from poor selectivity [4]. Recently, researchers have focused on the development of selective HDAC inhibitors, specifically HDAC6 inhibitors. Although HDACs are mainly involved in the deacetylation of histones in the nucleus, due to nuclear export signaling (NES), HDAC6 can translocate out of the nucleus to the cytoplasm, where numerous non‐histone proteins, such as α‐tubulin, heat shock protein (HSP‐90), Ku70, and tau protein, are located. The ability to affect additional substrates makes HDAC6 exceptionally important to the physiological functions of human body [5]. Furthermore, HDAC6 has proven itself as a promising drug target for cancer, CNS disorders, and immune disorders [6]. Several HDCA6 inhibitors, such as ACY‐1215 and ACY‐241, are undergoing clinical trials for multiple cancer in monotherapy or combination therapy [7, 8].

Blood vessel formation, also known as angiogenesis, is crucial to the growth and survival of cells. Tumor cells, like normal cells, require a supply of oxygen and nutrients and removal of waste to grow and progress. “Starving tumor cells to death” has been a promising strategy of defeating tumors. Over the past few decades, there are an abundance of anti‐angiogenic agents, which have been discovered, such as Sorafenib and Bevacizumab. These anti‐angiogenic agents target the vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)/VEGF receptor‐2 (VEGFR‐2) axis. It has been reported that VEGF acts as a survival factor in tumor cells and is upregulated by hypoxia. Previously, Kim et al. found that HDAC1 is hypoxia‐inducible and thus opens a new pathway for HDAC inhibitors to also serve as anti‐angiogenic agents [9]. In our previous effort, we utilized structural hybridization by combining the structure of Sorafenib and characteristics of typical HDAC inhibitors to create potent HDAC/VEGF inhibitors [10]. The fact that HDAC inhibitors are able to possess anti‐angiogenesis properties is an inspiration for this work.

Tubastatin A (1, Figure 1), a potent HDAC6 inhibitor with neuroprotective functions [11], features typical components of HDACi, which are cap (tetrahydro‐γ‐carboline), linker (p‐tolyl), and zinc‐binding group (hydroxamic acid). Due to its remarkable HDAC6 selectivity, tubastatin A provides a promising structural template for the development of selective HDAC6 inhibitors. The following are some examples utilizing tricyclic or bulkier motifs as cap groups. In addition to tetrahydrocarboline, Butler et al. published carbazole‐capped compound (2) inhibiting HDAC6 with an IC50 value of 19 nM [11]. Vögerl and coworkers explored a series of phenothiazine‐based benzhydroxamic acids and identified compound 3 possessing an azaphenothiazine as a selective HDAC6 inhibitor (HDAC6 IC50 = 5 nM) [12]. Sellmer et al. synthesized a series of tetrahydro‐β‐carboline‐based compounds having tetracyclic bulky heads and discovered Marbostat‐100 (4) showing potent HDAC6 inhibition with a Ki value of 0.7 nM [13].

Structures of reported HDAC inhibitors (1‐5).

Our previous study exploiting ring‐opening strategy on tubastatin A identified indole‐based hydroxamate (5) as a potent HDAC6 inhibitor [14]. In this study, we utilized an alternative strategy of ring transformation by dissociating the lateral ring of tricyclic core of reported HDAC6 inhibitors (Figure 2), followed by isosteric replacement of indole with benzimidazole, leading to a series of 2‐(cyclo)alkylbenzimidazole and 2‐aryl(alkyl)benzimidazole hydroxamic acids (6‐16). Moreover, Choi et al., as well as Chen et al., have developed benzimidazole‐based HDAC inhibitors with the zinc‐binding group at the C2 position [15, 16]. To better understand the structure–activity relationship, we also developed compounds with a reverse substitution pattern (17‐19). All synthesized compounds were evaluated for HDAC and anti‐angiogenic activities to elucidate their biological profiles and mechanisms of action.

Biological Evaluation

HDAC Isoform Inhibition

The synthesized compounds (6–19) and reference compound trichostatin A were examined for HDAC isoform inhibitory activity (Table 1). Based on the substitution pattern, all synthetic hydroxamic acids (6–19) are grouped into three categories, which are C2‐alkyl chain containing (6–10), C2‐aryl/C2‐arylalkyl moiety containing (11–16), and reverse groups (17–19). A glance at the result indicated that all compounds exhibit distinct HDAC6 inhibitory activity, as compared with that of HDAC1 and HDAC2. This result revealed that the strategy utilized in this study (Figure 2) helps provide compounds with HDAC6 inhibitory activity. Notably, substituents primarily affect the activity of HDAC1 and HDAC2, thereby influencing the selectivity for HDAC6. Among the group of 2‐alkyl chain containing compounds (6–10), compound possessing an n‐propyl group (6) showed the most potent and selectivity against HDAC6. The IC50 value of compound 6 against HDAC6 is 11.7 nM and the selectivity ratio over HDAC1 and HDAC2 is more than 855‐fold. The further modification of the n‐propyl group resulted in different extents of influence on activity. For instance, the increase of length (7) and compound with a branched substitution (8) resulted in 2‐fold decrease of HDAC6 activity to that of 6. Ring closure of 8, namely compound 9, led to 2‐fold decrease of HDAC6 activity but a distinct increase of HDAC1 activity. When it comes to HDAC1 activity, the cyclic motif at C2 position, including cycloalkyl, aryl, and arylalkyl, seems to contribute to the increase of HDAC1 activity. The comparison of 9 and 10 revealed the increase of the size of saturated ring resulted in a slight decrease of HDAC activity. The conversion of cyclohexyl (10) to phenyl moiety (11) resulting in increased activity toward HDAC1, HDAC2, and HDAC6. Compound 11 exhibited HDAC1, HDAC2, and HDAC6 inhibitory activity with IC50 values of 538, 2442, and 24.8 nM, respectively. Compounds with C2‐aryl/C2‐arylalkyl moiety (11–16) not only exhibited distinct HDAC6 activity but also displayed HDAC1 activity. Moreover, the distance between phenyl ring with benzimidazole impacted on enzymatic activity as well. The increase of the linkage length between benzimidazole and phenyl moiety (11, 14, and 16) resulted in a decrease of HDAC1 activity and an increase of HDAC6 activity with different extent, overall, which led to the rise of HDAC6 selectivity. Among compounds 11–16, compound 14 exhibited potent HDAC6 inhibitory activity with an IC50 value of 9.3 nM, and its HDAC6 selectivity ratio over HDAC1 and HDAC2 is 461 and more than 1075, respectively. The reverse type of compounds (17–19), with the exception of 17, showed moderate HDAC6 inhibitory activity but neglected activity against HDAC1 and HDAC2. It assumed that the presence of phenyl motif contributes to HDAC6 activity for the reverse type of compounds. With respect to the influence of the substitutions of phenyl ring on HDAC activity, it is different between C2‐aryl/C2‐arylalkyl moiety containing compounds (12–15) and reverse type of compounds (18 and 19) The comparison of two sets of compounds (11‐13 and 14‐15) indicated that ‐F and ‐OCH3 groups led to a slight decrease in HDAC1 and HDAC6 activity. Unlike the influence of fluorine atom on HDAC activity of C2‐aryl/C2‐arylalkyl containing compounds, the fluorine atom contributed 2‐fold increase of HDAC6 activity (18 vs. 19).

Docking Study and Superimposition of HDAC1 and HDAC6

A glance at the results shown in Table 1 showed that cycloalkyl (9 and 10) and aryl/arylalkyl (11‐16) at C2 position contributed to the enhancement of HDAC1 inhibitory activity. To understand the impact of these groups on HDAC1 activity, we conducted docking study and superimposed HDAC1 (PDB ID: 5ICN) and HDAC6 (PDB ID: 5EDU) (Figure 3). The superimposed result showed that HDAC6 consists of hydrophobic amino acids near the catalytic site, whereas HDAC1 possesses some polar and aromatic ring‐containing amino acids (Asp, His, and Tyr) in this region. This explains the diminished HDAC1 activity of 6 and 8, due to the lack of hydrophobic interaction resulting from small volume of C2‐susbtitution. In contrast, the docking study indicated that the phenyl group of compound 11 interacted with Tyr204 and Phe679 of HDAC1 and HDAC6, respectively (Supporting Information, Figure S1). Due to this additional interaction, compounds with cyclic substituent at C2 position have increased HDAC1 inhibitory activity.

Anti‐Angiogenic and Anticancer Effects

In targeted therapy, angiogenesis inhibitors suppress tumor progression by obstructing the formation of blood vessels that supply nutrients and oxygen to the tumor. Endothelial progenitor cells (EPCs) play a critical role in tumor angiogenesis and metastasis by activating the angiogenic switch, contributing both structurally and functionally to neovascularization within the tumor microenvironment. To explore the potential angiogenesis inhibitory effects, compounds 6–19 were evaluated against human EPCs (Table 2). An interesting finding was that compounds bearing C2‐aryl/C2‐arylalkyl group (11‐16), with the exception of 15, have increased anti‐angiogenic activity than other compounds. Among them, compound 13, which has a 4‐methoxyphenyl motif, expressed the strongest anti‐angiogenic effect with a GI50 value of 3.9 ± 0.5 μM. Angiogenesis is a complex process involving endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and capillary‐like tube formation. In this study, EPCs were treated with 10 μM of compound 13 for 24 h. The results demonstrated that compound 13 inhibited EPC migration and tube formation, achieving potency comparable to the positive control, Sorafenib. In contrast, tubastatin A, an HDAC6 inhibitor, did not exert any significant inhibitory effect on EPC tube formation or migration, as shown in Figure 4. The relationship between structure and EPCs activity is not consistent with that of HDAC activity, and it seems like that HDAC1 and HDAC2 inhibitory activities correlated with EPCs activity. For instance, despite the absence of HDAC6 selectivity, compound 13 showed distinct activity toward HDAC1 and HDAC2 compared with other synthesized compounds, with IC50 values of 1.5 and 2.5 μM, respectively. Taking a glance at compounds 11 and 16, showing moderate EPCs inhibitory activity, they also displayed HDAC1 and 2 activities. The correlation of HDAC inhibition with anti‐angiogenic activity is complicated and still under investigation; however, some literature may help to explain. For instance, Yoo's study indicated inhibition of HDAC1 reduced the acetylation level of HIF‐1α, an important angiogenesis‐related factor, which led to its degradation [17]. In addition, HDAC2 recruitment by KLF‐4 at VEGF promoter was found to correlate with suppression of VEGF expression in Ray's work [18]. In light of our previous work on phenylurea hydroxamic acids having HDAC and VEGFR‐2 inhibitory activity [10], we also tested the VEGFR‐2 inhibition of compounds 6, 11–14, and 16. The result showed that they have no activity against VEGFR‐2 (supporting information Table S1). Therefore, 2‐aryl(alkyl)benzimidazole hydroxamic acids in this study could help understand the detailed mechanisms and pave the avenue to HDACis with anti‐angiogenic effect.

Table 3 summarizes the antiproliferative activity of compounds 6–19 against colorectal cancer cells (HCT‐116), hepatocellular carcinoma (SK‐Hep‐1), and prostate cancer cells (PC‐3). The correlation of structure with anticancer effect is similar to that of anti‐angiogenic effect, which is compounds bearing C2‐aryl (11, 12, and 13) or C2‐arylalkyl (14, 15, and 16) exhibited anti‐proliferative activity against three cancer cell lines. In other words, compounds showing anti‐angiogenesis properties tend to display anticancer function. Compound 13, which showed the most potent anti‐angiogenic activity, is also the most potent one in inhibiting the growth of tested cancer cells. Compound 13 inhibited the growth inhibitory effects of HCT‐116, SK‐Hep‐1, and PC‐3 cells with GI50 values of 1.3, 4.2, and 7.5 μM, respectively.

In the cytotoxicity assay toward normal cells, the seven most potent compounds (6, 11, 12, 13, 14, 16, and 19), together with a positive control (tubastatin A), were evaluated using MRC‐5 human lung fibroblasts. The results showed that four compounds (11, 12, 14, and 16) exhibited GI50 values of approximately 20 μM, whereas the other three compounds (6, 13, and 19) displayed GI50 values greater than 30 μM. Taken together, these results indicate that this series of compounds exerts selective anti‐angiogenic and anticancer activities compared with normal cells. Notably, Compound 13 may exhibit an approximately ten‐fold selectivity, while the remaining compounds show more than a three‐fold selectivity difference (Supporting Information, Table S2).

Conclusion

This study developed a series of benzimidazole hydroxamic acids based on the strategies of ring transformation and isostere swapping. Most of synthesized compounds possess a certain degree of HDAC6 activity and selectivity, which revealed that the strategy utilized in this study (Figure 2) indeed led to expected results. Structure‐activity relationships revealed that C2 position can tolerate a variety of substituents, such as aryl and arylalkyl groups, without significantly decreasing the HDAC activity and selectivity. Compound 6, possessing a C2 n‐propyl group, displayed remarkable inhibitory activity toward HDAC6, with it being 855‐times more selective than both HDAC1 and HDAC2. The appearance of cycloalkyl (9 and 10), aryl (11‐13), and arylalkyl (14‐16) at C2 position possibly contributed to the increase of HDAC1 or HDAC2 activity. It is noteworthy that compounds with HDAC1 activity also displayed anti‐angiogenic activity and tend to suppress the growth of cancer cells. For instance, compound 13 that bears a 4‐methoxyphenyl ring on the C2 position has a GI50 of 3.9 μM against EPCs. It also inhibited the growth of HCT‐116, SK‐Hep‐1, and PC‐3 cells with GI50 values of 1.3, 4.2, and 7.5 μM, respectively. The exact mechanism by which how these compounds have anti‐angiogenetic effects remains unknown and would require further studies on animal models. Nevertheless, 2‐aryl(alkyl)benzimidazole hydroxamic acids are established as promising leads for future drug design on novel angiogenesis inhibitors.

Docking Study

The HDAC1 (PDB ID: 5ICN) and HDAC6 (PDB ID: 5EDU) protein structures were downloaded from the Protein Data Bank repository [19]. The protein structures and small molecules were prepared with default settings using Schrödinger Release 2019–3. Molecular docking was performed within the same software. The superimposition of HDAC1 and HDAC6 shown in Figure 3 was generated using ChimeraX [20].

Biology

Cell Culture

HCT‐116 (RRID: CVCL_0291), SK‐Hep‐1 (RRID: CVCL_0525), and PC‐3 (RRID: CVCL_0035) human cancer cell lines were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC; Manassas, VA, USA). Normal human lung fibroblasts (MRC‐5; RRID: CVCL_0440) were obtained from the Bioresource Collection and Research Center (BCRC; Hsinchu, Taiwan). All cell lines were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37°C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2. Human CD34+ EPCs (RRID: CVCL_4W34) were isolated and maintained as previously described [21].

Assay for Anticancer Effect

Human cancer cells were seeded onto 96‐well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well. Overnight, cells were treated with tested compounds for 48 h. Then, anticancer activity was determined by an SRB assay based on our previous work [22].

Assay for Anti‐Angiogenic Effect

For EPCs’ cell growth assay, the methods used for cultivation and cell growth assessment of EPCs were based on our previous reports [10, 23].

HDAC Inhibition Assays

Enzyme inhibition assays were conducted by the Reaction Biology Corporation, Malvern, PA. Compounds were dissolved in DMSO and tested in at least 10‐dose IC50 mode with 3‐fold serial dilution starting at 10 μM. HDAC control compounds were tested in a 10‐dose IC50 with 3‐fold serial dilution starting at 10 μM.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 5.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). For comparisons among more than two groups, one‐way ANOVA was conducted, followed by Bonferroni's post hoc test. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
