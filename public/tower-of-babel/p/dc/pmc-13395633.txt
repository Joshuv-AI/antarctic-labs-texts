Advancing Geriatric Care in Japan: Integrating the 5Ms Framework With Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment for Enhanced Clinical Practice in a Super‐Aged Society
Authors: The CGA and 5Ms Working Group of the Japan Geriatrics Society, Tomomichi Sakai, Taku Fujimoto, Yuji Yamada, Tatsuya Hosoi, Naoki Tomita, Hiroyuki Kadoya, Yumi Chiba, Mariko Otsuka, Akitoshi Hara, Hiroyasu Akatsu, Kenji Sekiguchi, Keisuke Maeda, Ken Shinmura, Katsutoshi Furukawa, Satoru Ebihara, Soichiro Shimizu, Ken Sugimoto, Sumito Ogawa, Hiroyuki Umegaki, Koichi Kozaki, Koichi Yamamoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundJapan faces unprecedented demographic challenges as the world's most rapidly aging country, with 29.4% of the population aged ≥ 65 (2025) and projections estimating 38% by 2065. While Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment (CGA) is the gold standard for geriatric care, its implementation in Japan remains limited to geriatric specialists, creating a significant gap in delivering optimal care to Japan's growing older adult population.ObjectiveTo propose an integrated model that combines the 5Ms framework (Mind, Mobility, Medications, Multicomplexity, Matters Most) with CGA to enhance accessibility, practicality, and continuity of geriatric care in Japanese healthcare systems.MethodsThis position statement synthesizes evidence on CGA effectiveness and the educational and clinical utility of the 5Ms, evaluates cultural considerations in Japan, and proposes a three‐tiered implementation model.ResultsThe integration of CGA and the 5Ms framework creates a tiered assessment continuum that adapts to clinical contexts, facilitates team‐based care, and improves quality and continuity across settings. The model supports education, quality improvement, and culturally sensitive care planning.ConclusionsThe integration of the 5Ms and CGA offers a practical framework to formalize, connect, and strengthen geriatric care already being delivered across Japanese healthcare settings. The Japan Geriatrics Society supports this model as a guiding structure for phased implementation, workforce development, and ongoing evaluation to enhance the delivery of person‐centered geriatric care in Japan.

Introduction

Japan stands at the forefront of global aging, with 29.4% of its population aged 65 or older as of 2025, and projections indicating this figure will rise to approximately 38.4% by 2065 [1]. Moreover, the population aged ≥ 85 is expected to nearly double between 2020 and 2040, significantly increasing the demand for complex, individualized healthcare [2]. This demographic transition presents a profound challenge to healthcare systems, necessitating innovation in how care is structured, delivered, and evaluated.

Among evidence‐based approaches to geriatric care, Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment (CGA) has been consistently recognized as the gold standard. CGA is a multidimensional, interdisciplinary evaluation process that addresses medical, psychological, functional, and social aspects of aging [3, 4, 5]. Multiple randomized controlled trials and meta‐analyses, including Cochrane reviews, have demonstrated that CGA improves survival, reduces institutionalization, and enhances functional outcomes in older adults [3, 5, 6]. Importantly, previous reviews have shown that CGA is cost‐effective in primary care through reduced emergency visits and hospitalizations and improves organizational efficiency through better resource allocation and streamlined care coordination [7, 8]. These organizational benefits might help offset initial time investment and address workload concerns among healthcare professionals.

In Japan, CGA has been systematically incorporated into specialized geriatric care settings, and the Japan Geriatrics Society (JGS) has recently published the CGA‐Based Healthcare Guidelines 2024 to promote its standardized implementation nationwide [9].

However, CGA is predominantly confined to geriatric specialists and academic centers [10], leaving a considerable gap in care quality for older adults in primary care not involving geriatricians and specialist care outside geriatrics. Access to geriatricians and geriatric education is extremely limited in Japan; geriatric departments are scarce in acute care hospitals, including university hospitals, and there are only 1800 board‐certified geriatricians (equivalent to 0.4% of all physicians). The number of universities with medical schools offering geriatric training programs is below 30% and has been declining [11]. In the United States, although the shortage of geriatricians is similarly severe, 45% of medical schools require clinical exposure to geriatric medicine, and optional geriatrics activities are available at 76% of schools [12]. However, challenges in geriatric education remain substantial in both countries.

In parallel, the Geriatrics 5Ms framework, introduced by Tinetti and colleagues in 2017 [13], has gained traction internationally as a simplified yet comprehensive way to conceptualize core domains of geriatric care. The 5Ms—Mind, Mobility, Medications, Multicomplexity, and Matters Most—were developed to enhance accessibility and scalability of geriatric principles, especially for non‐geriatric healthcare providers. In North America, educational initiatives using the 5Ms have improved knowledge retention, confidence, and interdisciplinary collaboration among medical trainees and teams [14, 15].

This position statement argues that CGA and the 5Ms are not competing paradigms but complementary tools grounded in the same principle: the need for multidimensional, person‐centered care for older adults [16, 17]. We propose an integrated CGA‐5Ms model tailored to Japan's healthcare system and cultural context—designed to scale across diverse care settings while preserving the clinical rigor of CGA. This framework is intended for a broad range of stakeholders involved in the care of older adults, including physicians, nurses, pharmacists, therapists, dietitians, care managers, social workers, and other frontline care workers, as well as educators, organizational leaders, and healthcare policymakers.

This integration complements the World Health Organization (WHO)'s Integrated Care for Older People (ICOPE) framework [18], which focuses on community‐based health promotion and early intervention, whereas CGA‐5Ms addresses clinical medical assessment and treatment (Figure 1). Together, these approaches create a comprehensive continuum from health maintenance to medical care for Japan's aging population.

Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment (CGA) in Japan

CGA is a multidimensional, interdisciplinary process that systematically evaluates older adults' medical conditions, functional abilities, cognitive status, psychological well‐being, social support, and environmental circumstances [3, 4]. It generates individualized care plans designed to improve outcomes and maintain independence.

The efficacy of CGA has been repeatedly demonstrated. A Cochrane review reported that hospitalized older adults who received CGA were more likely to be alive and at home after discharge compared with those receiving standard care [3]. Meta‐analyses confirm benefits in mortality reduction, functional preservation, and reduced nursing home admission [5, 6]. These findings underpin the global status of CGA as a foundational geriatric intervention.

In Japan, CGA has been promoted by the Japan Geriatrics Society, which released the CGA‐Based Healthcare Guidelines 2024—a comprehensive framework for assessing and managing older adults using standardized instruments and interdisciplinary collaboration [9]. However, CGA remains significantly underutilized in clinical practice due to barriers such as its time‐intensive nature, workforce shortages, resource constraints, and challenges in workflow integration, a pattern also reported internationally [19, 20]. A nationwide retrospective cohort study found that only approximately 29% of hospitalized older adults with common geriatric conditions underwent CGA during admission [10]. The likelihood of receiving CGA varied by age, diagnosis, and region—older patients and those treated in metropolitan areas were more likely to receive CGA, whereas regional hospitals showed limited uptake [10].

Moreover, Japanese studies have shown that CGA is associated with better clinical outcomes. In a large‐scale study of older stroke patients, CGA implementation was linked to lower in‐hospital mortality, shorter lengths of stay, and increased use of rehabilitation services [21]. Another nationwide study found that CGA resulted in a modest but significant reduction in polypharmacy among hospitalized patients with ischemic stroke [22].

Evidence Gap

While CGA effectiveness in Japanese clinical settings is well‐documented for specific conditions, large‐scale studies evaluating long‐term outcomes and cost‐effectiveness across diverse healthcare settings remain limited and require dedicated research programs.

The 5Ms framework, introduced by Tinetti, Huang, and Molnar in 2017 [13], distills the essential domains of geriatric care into a simple and memorable structure:

This framework emphasizes person‐centered care and facilitates interdisciplinary communication across all levels of healthcare providers. It has been widely adopted in educational settings, where studies have demonstrated its utility in improving learners' confidence and knowledge. A workshop applying the 5Ms for interprofessional trainees significantly increased comfort with geriatrics and enhanced understanding of team roles [15]. A Delphi consensus further confirmed its relevance in defining core competencies for medical education [14].

The 5Ms also serve as the foundation for the Age‐Friendly Health Systems (AFHS) movement in North America. AFHS implementations, based on a subset of the 5Ms (typically the “4Ms”), have been associated with improved care quality metrics, such as more consistent documentation, better medication reviews, and higher patient satisfaction [23, 24].

Evidence Gap

While the educational benefits of the 5Ms are well‐documented, large‐scale randomized controlled trials evaluating direct clinical outcomes remain limited and are currently ongoing. Most current studies focus on process measures and educational metrics rather than definitive clinical endpoints.

The utility of the framework in education and process improvement is well‐supported, providing a scalable and flexible entry point for geriatric principles, particularly in non‐geriatric and busy frontline clinical settings where it is difficult to perform a full CGA due to the diverse geriatric skill levels of each staff member and significant time constraints.

Integrating the 5Ms within a CGA framework builds upon this evidence by offering a structured, team‐oriented approach to assessment and care planning, while using the 5Ms—supported primarily by educational and implementation‐focused evidence—to structure and support, rather than replace, CGA.

Despite evidence of the effectiveness of CGA, its low uptake is hindered by multiple barriers. Workforce limitations are particularly acute in Japan, with a limited number of geriatricians and limited geriatric training in medical schools 11. Time constraints represent another major challenge: CGA is substantially more time‐intensive than standard consultations [30]—incompatible with standard consultation structures in high‐volume clinical settings. Lack of training outside geriatric specialties means most clinicians lack familiarity with structured geriatric assessment tools 12. Additional barriers include insufficient reimbursement systems and limited interdisciplinary team availability. This gap emphasizes the need for pragmatic, scalable solutions that retain CGA's multidimensionality while enabling broader implementation.

Proposed Integration Model

We propose a three‐tiered integration model that flexibly applies the 5Ms framework within the structure of CGA, aligning assessment intensity with clinical context, available resources, and patient complexity. This model is informed by existing CGA, the 5Ms framework, and ICOPE concepts, and prior implementation experience; however, it should be understood as an evidence‐informed model rather than a fully validated framework.

Tier 1: 5Ms‐Based Screening and Initial Assessment

Purpose: Rapid identification of key issues.

Setting: Primary care, emergency departments, general medical wards.

Personnel: All healthcare providers working within interprofessional care teams for older adults.

Duration: 15–30 min.

Outcome: Identification of needs requiring further evaluation.

This tier allows non‐geriatric clinicians within interprofessional care teams, including physicians, nurses, and other care staff, to screen older adults using the 5Ms as a structured checklist. When multiple domain issues or an acute decline are identified, the patient should escalate to Tier 2 (full CGA), thereby enabling appropriate referral for full CGA. This screening is designed to be delivered through task‐sharing within existing workflows as an intake interview and an admission assessment. In extremely time‐constrained settings, a minimal pathway using brief screening questions about high‐yield triggers that cover each 5Ms domain, such as recent falls history or functional decline, cognitive or behavioral concerns, polypharmacy, multimorbidity, or a mismatch between current care and patient priorities, can be used. If staff resources are limited, it may be a feasible option to use a pre‐visit self‐administered questionnaire. In routine practice, escalation or handoff is typically initiated by the clinician performing the initial intake or admission assessment—most commonly nurses, or other frontline staff members—and communicated to the responsible physicians or care team through existing documentation or briefing processes. This approach reflects common interprofessional practices in acute and community settings. While the core role of this tier is early identification of geriatric complexity, Tier 1 may also include focused initial assessments for selected domains when clinically indicated. Tier 1 ensures early recognition of geriatric syndromes and connects with community‐based ICOPE health promotion initiatives.

Tier 2: CGA‐Enhanced Evaluation

Purpose: Comprehensive, multidomain assessment.

Setting: Geriatric services, complex care units.

Personnel: An interdisciplinary team trained in geriatric medicine.

Duration: 60–120 min.

Outcome: Individualized care plans with targeted interventions.

In this tier, each M is expanded into structured assessments aligned with CGA instruments [9].

Tier 3: 5Ms‐Structured Communication and Follow‐Up

Purpose: Ongoing care coordination and monitoring.

Setting: All healthcare settings.

Personnel: All healthcare providers working together as a team to care for older patients.

Duration: Ongoing.

Outcome: Continuity of care and value‐based communication.

Summarizing CGA results using the 5Ms enables consistent language across transitions in care, from hospitals to outpatient settings and home care.

In this integrated model, ICOPE and CGA‐5Ms are complementary frameworks used in parallel across different care settings. ICOPE's six intrinsic‐capacity domains (cognition, psychological, hearing, vision, mobility, vitality) can be cross‐referenced with the 5Ms framework. Community‐based ICOPE assessments and clinic‐based 5Ms evaluations inform each other bidirectionally: functional decline detected in the community may prompt clinical assessment (Tier 1 or 2), while clinical 5Ms findings guide community follow‐up through ICOPE monitoring. Essential handoff data include functional status, medication list, and patient priorities. This interoperability, embedded within Japan's Community‐based Integrated Care System (CICS), ensures seamless continuity.

This tiered structure supports scalable implementation while preserving the integrity of CGA and clinical value. These tiers are not strictly sequential but flexible and adaptable. They can be applied iteratively or selectively based on clinical context, patient needs, and local resource constraints, with implementation tailored to each site's workflow.

Here is an example of this integration model. A 78‐year‐old man living alone presents to the emergency department (ED) with head injury from recurrent falls. Tier 1 (ED screening): A 5‐min 5Ms screen identifies hearing loss, cognitive concerns, and frailty indicators (falls, weight loss), prompting admission. Tier 2 (Inpatient CGA): Full CGA confirms moderate hearing loss, mild dementia, and severe malnutrition; social withdrawal due to fall anxiety explains clinic discontinuation. Patient's priority: remain home to care for his bonsai (ikigai). Tier 3 (Transition): Care plan includes hearing aid, nutrition support, home safety modifications, and fall prevention. Home care team coordinated through CICS; 5Ms‐structured discharge summary ensures continuity with community providers.

Cultural Adaptation for Japan

The successful integration of 5Ms and CGA in Japan must respect cultural norms and decision‐making practices while leveraging unique Japanese concepts that enhance care quality.

Family‐Centered Decision‐Making

The “Matters Most” domain should be expanded to encompass not only patient preferences, but also family values, which are central in Japanese healthcare. Studies have shown that shared decision‐making with family is often preferred by older Japanese adults [31, 32].

For example, in Advance Care Planning (ACP), Japanese patients often defer decisions to family, and physicians are expected to balance individual autonomy with family harmony [31, 32]. Thus, 5Ms‐based assessments must incorporate structured tools and communication models that allow inclusion of family perspectives while respecting the patient's voice.

Incorporating Ikigai in the “Matters Most” Domain

The Japanese cultural concept of Ikigai, or “life's purpose,” should be considered when assessing the “Matters Most” domain as a prompt for goal‐setting or ACP conversations to ensure culturally appropriate understanding of patient values and goals [33, 34].

Respect for Hierarchy and Role Clarity

Japan's medical culture values a hierarchical structure. Implementation must strike a balance between empowering all professionals to participate in geriatric assessments and ensuring that they are adequately trained. Clearly defined roles—physicians as facilitative leaders, nurses as continuity anchors, therapists and pharmacists as domain experts—will facilitate acceptance [28]. It is important to recognize, however, that the “care coordinator” role varies across Japanese care settings: depending on context (community‐based integrated care, home care, long‐term care facilities, hospital‐to‐community transitions), nurses, care managers, medical social workers, or other professionals may lead coordination. This contextual variability should be acknowledged in implementation planning. Interprofessional learning can be supported through structured mechanisms such as case conferences, shared documentation templates, and standardized communication protocols, which foster shared mental models of comprehensive geriatric care across disciplines and settings.

Building on Existing Strengths

Japan has a robust primary care system, high standards for clinical documentation, and a growing workforce interested in geriatrics. The integration model should leverage Japan's Community‐based Integrated Care System (CICS), which enables older adults to remain in their familiar communities until the end of life, even when they require care. CICS will serve as the institutional base for this integration. Japan's digital health infrastructure can be utilized to track assessment quality and outcomes, potentially accelerating nationwide implementation. Coordination between the CGA‐5Ms framework and ICOPE community health promotion programs will be established to ensure seamless care transitions within the CICS.

Implementation Strategy

As most older adults in Japan receive care in community‐based settings, academic centers are not positioned as primary care delivery sites but as implementation hubs that support dissemination, training, and evaluation across frontline facilities.

Phase 1: Foundation Building

This phase will focus on establishing the core infrastructure needed to effectively implement the CGA‐5Ms model in Japan. A Japan‐specific CGA‐5Ms protocol and educational materials will be developed, ensuring cultural, clinical, and institutional alignment with Japanese practice standards. Training will then be delivered to pilot teams at selected academic centers to ensure understanding of the model and cultivate local leadership. Subsequently, these teams will conduct pilot projects to validate the model's feasibility and collect initial data on implementation processes and patient‐centered outcomes. Implementation science frameworks will be employed to guide data collection, evaluate implementation fidelity, and inform strategies for subsequent expansion.

Phase 2: Expansion and Refinement

In this phase, the model will be expanded and refined. The model validated at academic institutions will be introduced into surrounding regional hospitals, outpatient clinics, home care agencies, and long‐term care facilities, expanding its scope of application. The frontline implementation will be primarily driven by professionals responsible for the longitudinal care of older adults, including general internists, nurses, and care managers. Implementation process measures (e.g., completion rates, time efficiency, interprofessional collaboration) and patient‐centered outcomes will be refined based on evidence from pilot projects. Educational integration will be pursued through incorporation of the 5Ms framework into medical and nursing curricula, beginning with pilot institutions and gradually expanding toward national adoption.

Phase 3: Standardization and Optimization

This phase aims for nationwide dissemination of the integrated CGA‐5Ms model, building on the regional implementation established in Phase 2. As part of the standardization process, structured CGA‐5Ms assessment templates will be integrated into electronic health record (EHR) systems to facilitate documentation, data sharing, and quality monitoring. Furthermore, ongoing support will be provided to promote outcome evaluation at each facility and drive continuous quality improvement to optimize care delivery. Continuous education programs will be offered alongside implementation support. International collaboration with the Age‐Friendly Health Systems (AFHS) initiative in the United States will be promoted to facilitate knowledge exchange, share implementation strategies, and contribute to the global advancement of age‐friendly care frameworks.

This strategy enables stepwise adoption, incorporating feedback and evidence at each stage, while developing a comprehensive CGA‐5Ms integration framework that enhances geriatric care delivery across all healthcare settings and aligns with Japan's health care system, where most geriatric care is delivered by non‐geriatric specialists.

Educational Integration

Medical Student Education

Introducing the 5Ms and CGA concepts early in training promotes familiarity with geriatric principles. Studies have shown that integrating the 5Ms into medical curricula improves knowledge structure and retention [25]. Curricular mapping has demonstrated that the 5Ms align well with existing geriatric learning objectives [25].

Physicians‐In‐Training and Allied Health Training

Residency and specialty training should incorporate progressive competencies, beginning with 5Ms‐based screening, advancing toward full CGA. Interprofessional workshops have shown improvement in trainees' readiness to deliver geriatric care [15].

Continuing Medical Education

Practicing physicians and staff should be offered CME opportunities in the integrated CGA + 5Ms model. Programs should include practical applications, such as role‐play, case review, and documentation tools. Online modules and mentorship from geriatric leaders can enhance reach.

Given the severe shortage of geriatricians in Japan, educational programs will utilize scalable approaches such as online learning modules, train‐the‐trainer models, and teleconference‐based outreach education to extend training beyond academic centers.

These indicators should be collected longitudinally and reported back to clinical teams as part of continuous improvement cycles [23, 27]. To ensure feasibility and sustainability in routine practice, a minimal measurement set is recommended: 2–3 process indicators (e.g., completion rates, timeliness) and 2–3 patient‐centered outcome indicators (e.g., quality of life, functional independence). This core set balances evaluation rigor with practical data collection burden. Additionally, implementation outcomes should be monitored during early‐phase rollout to guide iterative refinement: adoption (uptake by sites and clinicians), acceptability (stakeholder satisfaction), feasibility (workflow fit), and fidelity (adherence to core components). Monitoring these metrics will enable data‐driven adaptation to support successful scale‐up.

Addressing Implementation Challenges

Resource Constraints

The primary barrier to introducing the CGA‐5Ms model would be limited geriatric expertise, time, and personnel, particularly in frontline healthcare settings. The tiered model allows implementation to be scaled to local capacity. Furthermore, remote consultations and standardized digital assessment tools can help mitigate workforce shortages and facilitate wider adoption in areas with a shortage of geriatric experts.

Training Requirements

Another significant challenge is the lack of systematic education on the 5Ms framework, which may lead to inconsistencies in implementation. To promote uptake, institutions should develop modular, competency‐based curricula tailored to different professional roles and care settings. This approach will help bridge the knowledge gap and reduce variation in clinical practice. Moreover, designating and educating implementation leaders (so‐called 5Ms champions) at each facility will enhance the feasibility of integrating the CGA‐5Ms model into routine clinical practice.

Technology Integration

Integrating the CGA‐5Ms workflow directly into existing electronic health records (EHR) systems inevitably presents practical challenges, as it generates fragmented and duplicated information, increasing the burden on clinicians. By incorporating structured EHR templates aligned with 5Ms and auto‐populated fields for commonly used CGA instruments, care consistency will be ensured through centralized information, promoting care efficiency by reducing duplication.

Evidence Gap

Research on optimal technology integration strategies for CGA‐5Ms systems is in the early stages and requires dedicated implementation science studies.

Future Research Directions

Effectiveness Studies

Large‐scale randomized controlled trials are needed to compare integrated CGA + 5Ms versus traditional care on clinical outcomes and cost‐effectiveness. These studies should include diverse clinical settings and patient populations representative of Japanese healthcare.

Implementation Science Research

Research should identify best practices for training, documentation, and sustainability across institutions, with particular attention to cultural adaptation for the Japanese healthcare context.

Policy and Health Economics Research

Research is needed to evaluate optimal reimbursement strategies for 5Ms implementation and their impact on healthcare utilization, cost‐effectiveness, and quality outcomes. Given the success of CGA fee (additional reimbursement for performing CGA) in promoting comprehensive geriatric assessment adoption in Japan, similar economic evaluation studies should examine the potential effects of 5Ms‐specific reimbursement on implementation rates, care quality, and long‐term healthcare costs.

Evidence Gap

Systematic research programs addressing these areas are currently limited and require coordinated multi‐institutional collaboration and dedicated funding support.

International Collaboration and Knowledge Sharing

Japan's experience with integrating the 5Ms framework and CGA provides valuable opportunities for international collaboration. As other aging societies face similar challenges, Japan can serve as a model for successful implementation while learning from international experiences.

JGS should actively engage in international professional organizations, research collaborations, and knowledge‐sharing initiatives to advance the global understanding of optimal geriatric assessment approaches in collaboration with the National Center for Geriatrics and Gerontology.

Limitations and Future Challenges

This position statement acknowledges several limitations. First, direct comparative effectiveness research on the integration of CGA‐5Ms approaches and traditional methods is limited. Second, optimal implementation strategies may vary significantly across different healthcare settings and regions within Japan. Third, the cultural adaptations proposed require empirical validation through systematic research.

Future challenges include ensuring sustainable financing for integrated programs, maintaining quality standards across diverse implementation sites, and adapting to evolving demographics and healthcare technologies. Long‐term success will depend on continuous evaluation, adaptation, and commitment from healthcare leaders, policymakers, and frontline providers.

Digital health technologies offer significant opportunities for 5Ms implementation in Japan. Electronic Health Record (EHR) systems could automatically construct 5Ms assessments from existing clinical data, while Personal Health Record (PHR) integration would enable patient‐contributed functional status and preferences. Mobile health applications, remote monitoring, and AI‐assisted decision support could facilitate continuous 5Ms tracking and help non‐geriatric healthcare providers interpret results. However, successful implementation requires addressing digital literacy, privacy concerns, system interoperability, and ensuring equitable access to technology across Japan's diverse healthcare landscape.

Conclusions

The integration of the Geriatric 5Ms framework with Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment offers a practical framework to formalize, connect, and strengthen geriatric care practices already emerging across Japanese healthcare settings. This approach honors the established excellence of CGA while addressing the practical challenges of implementing high‐quality geriatric care across Japan's diverse healthcare settings.

By creating a flexible, scalable, and culturally appropriate approach to geriatric assessment, this integration can support broader access to comprehensive, person‐centered care that addresses their complex health needs while respecting their values and life purpose. The success of this initiative depends on sustained commitment from healthcare leaders, comprehensive training programs, and ongoing research to refine and optimize implementation strategies.

This CGA‐5Ms integration model complements WHO's ICOPE framework, creating a comprehensive continuum where ICOPE addresses community‐based health promotion and prevention, while CGA‐5Ms provides clinical medical assessment and intervention. Together, these frameworks provide Japan with a comprehensive approach to aging care, spanning from health maintenance to medical treatment.

JGS is well‐positioned to support the dissemination and refinement of this integrated approach, drawing upon its deep expertise in geriatric medicine while embracing innovative approaches that can expand the reach and impact of geriatric care. Through this integration, Japan can establish itself as a global leader in geriatric care delivery, providing a model that combines clinical excellence with cultural sensitivity and community engagement for other aging societies worldwide.

In Japan's super‐aged society, there is a growing need for scalable approaches that support high‐quality geriatric care while honoring cultural values and promoting community well‐being. The integration of the 5Ms framework with CGA offers a practical approach that builds on established practice while supporting adaptation to the challenges of our demographic future.
