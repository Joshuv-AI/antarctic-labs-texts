Shear bond strength of laser-sintered orthodontic appliances bonded to enamel using various bonding protocols: an in vitro study
Authors: Alexander Schwärzler, Caroline Gugler, Patrick Chitan, Andreas Schedle, Erwin Jonke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis in vitro study compared the shear bond strength (SBS) and failure mode of laser-sintered orthodontic metal specimens bonded to enamel using different bonding protocols (with or without enamel sandblasting and four adhesive systems).MethodsTwo hundred and forty bovine teeth were randomly allocated to no sandblasting (control) or sandblasting (50 µm, 1 bar) and bonded with 3D-printed Co-Cr discs using either light-curing orthodontic adhesives (Transbond XT, Transbond LR) or dual-curing resin cements (RelyX Unicem 2 Automix, Panavia SA Cement Universal Automix) (2 × 4 design; n = 30/group). After SBS testing, the failure mode was documented. Welch's ANOVA was used to compare SBS across groups (S, C, and subgroups); p < 0.05 was considered significant.ResultsDual-curing adhesives (RX, PA) showed higher SBS than light-curing adhesives (XT, LR) (p = 0.001). The highest SBS was observed in RX (33 MPa), and the lowest in XT (18 MPa). A surface treatment × adhesive effect was found, although not statistically significant (p = 0.21). Failures occurred mainly on the metal side (> 50%; 140/240).ConclusionsDual-curing adhesives showed higher SBS when bonding laser-sintered Co-Cr appliances to bovine enamel, with failures predominantly occurring on the metal side. Sandblasting of enamel did not increase SBS under the conditions tested. As SBS values exceeded the clinically acceptable range, additional enamel sandblasting may not be clinically necessary.Clinical significanceFor bonding laser-sintered Co–Cr appliances, dual-curing adhesives may provide clinically acceptable SBS, but higher values could increase enamel damage risk during debonding. Enamel sandblasting showed no SBS benefit in this protocol.

Introduction

Implementing digitized processes in orthodontic workflows offers multiple benefits and opportunities for diagnostics [1–3], treatment planning [4–7], and the customization of end products through Computer-Aided Design (CAD) and Computer-Aided Manufacturing (CAM) [8–10]. CAD/CAM technology facilitates customizing orthodontic appliances because of automated software processes and reduces resources like materials, equipment, space (e.g., external orthodontic laboratories), and time. In a digital workflow, the design and creation of orthodontic devices happen in a virtual space, offering a digital library of construction elements and automated adaptations to the digitized patient's dental arch [11].

A wide range of materials can be used for dental CAM products, including polymers, ceramics, and various metal alloys. For orthodontic care, resin-based materials and Co-Cr alloys are preferred due to their mechanical properties, biocompatibility, and lower costs [12]. Manufacturing techniques for dental devices can be categorized as subtractive and additive. Additive manufacturing (AM) is progressively replacing subtractive manufacturing (SM) due to numerous advantages. AM enables the construction of complex geometries that were not possible with SM because of the angulation and size limitations of milling machines. Additionally, AM reduces material waste, decreases tool wear, eliminates the need for machining components such as drills, saws, and milling machines, and lowers costs [13]. An additive process for printing metal dental devices is powder bed fusion (PBF) technology. Metal-based CAM appliances can be produced using different types of PBF, e.g., direct metal laser sintering (DMLS), direct metal laser melting (DMLM), and electron beam melting (EBM). During the manufacturing process, a metal powder alloy is deposited in layers by a coater and fused by a laser beam according to a stereolithography (STL) file. The STL file contains the framework design of the virtually created device, and its cross-sections represent each layer of the metal powder alloy. DMLS, DMLM, and EBM use the same principles but differ in temperature and laser beam type [14]. For orthodontic metal CAM devices, DMLS and DMLM are the most commonly used [15]. CAM can be performed chairside in an orthodontic office, but it is mostly limited to non-metal-based materials because of the high cost of infrastructure and the precautions required during metal processing [15, 16]. In this study, specimens were produced by DMLS.

Enamel conditioning influences the bonding of metal orthodontic appliances and typically involves cleaning and polishing, followed by acid etching, with optional aluminium oxide sandblasting to increase micromechanical retention. Bonding can be achieved using conventional light-curing orthodontic adhesives or dual-curing/self-adhesive resin cements. In everyday clinical practice, adhesion failures are observed after inserting 3D laser-sintered orthodontic metal appliances. Evidence on bonding 3D-printed/laser-sintered Co–Cr appliances to enamel is limited, particularly regarding how modern dual-curing cements perform compared with conventional orthodontic resins and how these protocols influence failure mode.

Therefore, this in vitro study compared the shear bond strength and failure mode of laser-sintered Co–Cr orthodontic metal specimens bonded to enamel using four adhesive systems, with and without enamel sandblasting.

The null hypotheses were that:

(i)Sandblasting enamel surfaces does not lead to higher SBS compared to the control (no sandblasting of the enamel surfaces).

Sandblasting enamel surfaces does not lead to higher SBS compared to the control (no sandblasting of the enamel surfaces).

(ii)There is no difference in SBS between dual and light-cured adhesives.

There is no difference in SBS between dual and light-cured adhesives.

Materials and methods

The laboratory experiment was performed at the Competence Center of Dental Materials at the University Clinic of Dentistry, Medical University of Vienna. No human participants or human tissue were involved. Ethical approval/IRB review and consent were not required because bovine teeth were obtained as by-products of routine commercial slaughtering in accordance with national guidelines; no animals were sacrificed for research, and no procedures were performed on live animals. No a priori power calculation was performed. The sample (n = 240; n = 30/group) size was determined based on data reported in a previously published study by Franz et al., using relevant parameters from that study to guide the calculation [17].

The applied testing method was executed and documented according to the recommendations of the 2009 Academy of Dental Materials conference [18].

Source of bovine teeth

Bovine incisors used in this study were obtained as by-products from a slaughterhouse (not specifically slaughtered for research purposes). The teeth were collected post-mortem from animals slaughtered for commercial food production, and no animals were sacrificed solely for the purposes of this research.

Preparation of bovine teeth

A total of 240 bovine incisors, characterized by a relatively flat buccal surface and the absence of enamel defects, were stored in a 0,5% chloramine-T solution at 5 °C for at least two weeks. After storage, the teeth were fixed in small plastic moulds using a two-component epoxy resin (EpoFix Resin, Struers GmbH, Sarasota, Florida, USA). Under a laboratory exhaust hood, they were dried for at least 12 h, then ground and polished with a polisher (Metaserv 2000 Grinder/Polisher, Buehler, Lake Bluff, Illinois, USA). All teeth were ground with two polishing papers of different grain sizes (P400 and P1200, CarbiMetTM, Buehler, Lake Bluff, Illinois, USA) [19, 20]. After polishing, a flat enamel window (≥ 5 mm2) was prepared and left exposed; only the remainder of the tooth was embedded for stabilization.

The latter was accomplished by preparing each specimen with a fine grinding paper (P1200) for 10 s as a last step (Fig. 2).

Close-up of a tooth specimen after polishing

After polishing and rinsing the specimens under water for 20 s, the 240 bovine teeth were divided into two equally distributed main groups. The teeth in group S were sandblasted (Spot blasting Unit P-G 400, Harnisch + Rieth GmbH, Winterbach, Germany) with Al2O3-particles (Spezial-Edelkorund, Harnisch + Rieth GmbH, Winterbach, Germany) with a grain size of 50 μm, at a pressure of 1 bar for 10 s, at a distance of 5—10 mm. The teeth in group C remained without surface pretreatment (Fig. 1).

3D laser-sintered metal specimens

Co–Cr discs (Remanium Star CL, Dentaurum, Ispringen, Germany) were designed in CAD and exported as STL files. The discs had a diameter of 4 mm and a thickness of 2 mm. The build was oriented so that the bonding face was not in contact with supports; supports were placed exclusively on the sidewall (Fig. 3), then removed post-processing to maintain a standardized bonding surface. All metal discs received an identical sandblasting treatment (Al2O3-particles, distance of 5—10 mm) but with a pressure of 2 bar as suggested by the manufacturer's instructions for the used adhesives. After sandblasting, the metal specimens were cleaned by an ultrasonic bath (Sonorex digitec, BANDELIN electronic GmbH & Co. KG, Berlin, Germany) for 10 min.

Specimens produced by laser -sintering technology (laserCUSING®, Concept Laser GmbH, Lichtenfels, Germany)

Attachment of specimens to enamel

Right before bonding the metal discs to the bovine teeth, all enamel surfaces were pretreated with 35% phosphoric acid (3 M Unitek Etching Gel, 3 M, Monrovia, CA, USA) for 30 s and rinsed under water for 30 s. The etched and rinsed surface was gently air-dried until a chalky enamel morphology appeared. The metal specimens were conditioned with a metal primer (Reliance Orthodontic Products Inc., Itasca, IL, USA) for 30 s, followed by surface sealing with a light-cure sealant resin (Light BondTM Sealants, Reliance Orthodontic Products Inc., Itasca, IL, USA). Afterwards, the adhesives (XT, LR, RX, and PA) were applied to the pretreated metal discs in a randomised sequence. When XT and LR were applied, a thin layer of Transbond XT primer (3 M, Monrovia, CA, USA) was layered on the enamel surface before attaching the metal disc to the tooth. When RX and PA were applied, no additional pretreatment was necessary. For all adhesive systems, standardised conditions regarding light-curing and pressure were set. The intensity of a light-curing unit (1600mW/cm2, Ortholux Luminous Curing Light, 3 M Unitek, Monrovia, CA, USA) was verified with a measuring device integrated into the LED light's charger. All adhesive agents were light-cured from three different directions for 15 s. Since RX and PA are dual-curing adhesive resin composite cements, the manufacturers' recommendations for self-curing time were followed (6 min for RX, 5 min for PA). During curing, a constant pressure of ~ 25 g/mm2 was applied with a custom-made device. The bonded specimens were stored in an incubator (Heraeus incubator B6200, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) simulating an oral-like environment at 37 °C with a relative humidity of 100% for 24 h.

Shear bond strength measurements

A Zwick universal testing machine (Type 1486, Zwick Roell, Ulm, Germany, illustrated in Fig. 4) was used to conduct SBS measurements. No additional ageing was performed before SBS testing. The luted specimens were fixed in a guillotine-like device according to ISO 10477:2020 [21] using a flat loading head, positioned flush at the Co–Cr disc–enamel interface so that the load was applied parallel to the bonding interface until failure. The shear bond test was conducted with a crosshead speed of 0,8 mm/min. A flat disc bonding surface was selected according to ISO 10477:2020 [21].

Illustration of the shear bond testing device

Failure mode

Failure modes were documented macroscopically using a digital camera (Canon EOS R5, Canon Inc., Japan) with a 100-mm macro lens (Canon RF 100 mm f/2.8L, Canon Inc., Japan), supplemented by a visual and tactile assessment with a dental probe. Failure mode was categorized as (1) predominantly metal–adhesive interfacial failure, (2) predominantly enamel–adhesive interfacial failure, or (3) mixed/cohesive failure, based on established failure-mode approaches [22].

No cohesive failure was detected on the bovine teeth enamel. For metal-sided failures, at least 75% of the metal surface needed to be free of attachment material; in turn, for tooth-sided failures, at least 75% was required to cover the metal surface. Other findings were considered mixed failures (Fig. 5). The frequency of each failure mode was documented.

Failure mode of adhesion: a Metal-sided, b Mixed, c Tooth-sided

Statistical methods

IBM SPSS Statistics (v 29; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used. Normality of SBS data was assessed using Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests and visual inspection. Homogeneity of variances was evaluated with Levene's test; as heteroscedasticity was detected, SBS comparisons were performed using Welch's ANOVA (α = 0.05). Pairwise post-hoc comparisons were conducted using the Games–Howell procedure (variance-robust). In addition, the effects of surface treatment (sandblasted vs control) and adhesive type (XT, LR, RX, PA), including their interaction, were evaluated within the factorial design. Failure mode distributions were analyzed descriptively and compared between groups using χ2/Fisher's exact test as appropriate (α = 0.05). Two specimens were not assessable for failure mode (N = 238).

Results

Shear bond strengths

There was no significant difference (p = 0.21) in shear bond strengths between group S (sandblasted specimens) and the controls (Fig. 6). The SBS of the dual-curing self-adhesive resin composite cements (RX and PA) was significantly higher (p < 0.001) than that of the light-curing adhesives (Table 3). No significant difference (p > 0.05) was detected between RX and PA, as well as between XT and LR (Figs. 7 and 8). Table 4 presents the descriptive data of all eight subgroups.

Discussion

This study found that enamel sandblasting did not significantly increase shear bond strength (SBS) of laser-sintered metal specimens bonded to bovine teeth. Dual-curing adhesives produced higher SBS than light-curing adhesives, with failures predominantly on the metal side.

The first null hypothesis was accepted, as sandblasting of enamel surfaces did not lead to significantly higher SBS compared with the control group without enamel sandblasting. The second null hypothesis was rejected, as SBS differed between dual-cured and light-cured adhesives, with dual-cured adhesives showing higher SBS values.

One problem observed in orthodontic care is the failure of adhesion of 3D printed metal-based orthodontic appliances after they have been attached in a patient’s mouth. Multiple factors, like poor bonding technique, surface preparation of the interfaces, inaccuracy of CAD/CAM orthodontic devices, material properties, and used attachment material, influence the potential for adhesive failure after bonding. The clinical application of CAD/CAM orthodontic is established and has been broadly investigated regarding accuracy and reliability [23–25].

Shear bond strength testing was selected because it is a widely used method for evaluating orthodontic bracket adhesion and allows direct comparison with established clinical thresholds. Dual-curing resin cements were included because their chemical polymerization component may achieve higher conversion beneath opaque metal surfaces where light penetration is limited [26, 27].

Sandblasting

The lack of improvement with sandblasting aligns with recent evidence. A 2017 meta-analysis found no benefit of sandblasting prior to etching for orthodontic bracket bond strength (p = 0.22) [28]. Similarly, a 2026 study by Osipove et al. confirmed sandblasting had no significant effect on bond strength (p > 0.05) [29].

Li et al. demonstrated that dual-curing systems combining light and chemical polymerization are essential for obtaining higher initial bond strengths by ensuring complete polymerization under the bracket. Metal-side failures suggest adequate enamel preparation but potential limitations in metal surface treatment [30].

The effect of sandblasting on increasing SBS by enlarging the surface has been investigated on enamel and other dental materials like dentin, zirconia, metal alloys, and composites [26–32].

Sandblasting did not increase SBS, likely because conservative parameters (50 µm, 1 bar) and subsequent phosphoric acid etching already provided sufficient enamel micromechanical retention. The high rate of metal-sided failures (58.33%) further suggests that the metal–adhesive interface, rather than enamel, was the limiting factor. This aligns with findings by Patcas et al. and Daratsianos et al., who also reported no SBS improvement when sandblasting was followed by acid etching [31, 32].

In contrast, Dirie et al. observed a notable advantage of sandblasting when combined with acid etching in both laboratory settings (SBS) and clinical environments (failure rates) [33]. For sandblasting, they used a particle size of 90 μm and a pressure of 4,14 bar, whereas, in the present study, a less invasive procedure with a 50 μm particle size and a pressure of 1 bar was applied. In a study by Halpern et al., using a self-etching primer significantly improved the SBS when a pressure of 4.8 bar was applied. Increasing particle size enhanced this effect [34]. In studies where acid etching with phosphoric acid was used as a separate conditioning step, additional sandblasting did not increase SBS, which aligns with our study [35–37].

Adhesives

The significantly higher SBS of dual-curing cements compared to light-curing adhesives aligns with findings by Li et al., who attributed this to superior chemical polymerization beneath opaque metal where light penetration is limited, though Vicente et al. reported lower SBS for RelyX Unicem than Transbond XT, indicating material-specific variability [30]. According to the manufacturers` suggestions (a3M Unitek, Monrovia, CA, USA and bKuraray Noritake Dental Inc., Okayama, Japan), XTa and LRa are explicitly indicated for orthodontic care, whereas RXa and PAb are rather recommended for long-term restorations. Still, all of them are applicable for attaching metal and, therefore, part of the study. Takahashi et al. evaluated resin cements on additively manufactured and cast Co–Cr alloys and identified a methyl methacrylate (MMA) cement as a clinically favourable option for laser-sintered orthodontic appliances. Their 1-day SBS values (25.9 and 23.5 MPa) are within a similar range to the dual-curing cements in the present study (RX: x̄ = 32.20 MPa; PA: x̄ = 27.62 MPa), supporting our finding that dual-curing systems can provide high initial bond strength to Co–Cr [38].

Various findings were reported regarding the minimal bond strength for clinical orthodontic movements. Reynolds et al. [39] stated a minimum shear bond strength of 5.9–7,8 MPa, Örtendahl and Thilander [40] of 4 MPa, and McCourt et al. [41] of 10 MPa. On average, all four adhesives used in this study surpass these minima.

High SBS of adhesive agents is usually aimed at in dentistry as it minimises unintentional debonding. Still, in orthodontics, at some point, debonding of metal is intended to remove the appliances. Increased SBS makes the debonding procedure more difficult and raises the risk of enamel damage like microfractures, enamel loss and scratched enamel surfaces after intentional debonding [42]. A maximal bond strength of 14 MPa has been suggested to reduce the risk of enamel damage during debonding [43]. In the present study, mean SBS values, and in some groups the maximum values, exceeded this threshold, indicating that several bonding protocols may produce bonds stronger than is considered desirable for safe clinical removal. Although no macroscopic enamel trauma was observed after debonding, this does not exclude microcracks or subtle enamel loss; therefore, these high SBS values should be interpreted with caution and balanced against debonding safety. Future studies should evaluate alternative conditioning strategies, particularly metal-side treatments (e.g., airborne abrasion, silica coating, or surface texturing/design modifications), as failures occurred predominantly at the metal–adhesive interface.

Failure mode

The failure mode findings should also be considered. The presence of mixed or cohesive failures may indicate that the bond strength was already sufficient and that failure did not occur solely because of weak enamel adhesion. This may help explain why additional sandblasting did not significantly affect SBS.

The predominance of metal-sided failures (58.33%) despite the significantly higher SBS of dual-curing cements suggests that even with stronger adhesive bonding to enamel, the metal–adhesive interface remains the limiting factor, and increased bond strength does not shift the failure site toward the enamel. This is consistent with Nawrocka et al., who reported that fractures occurred predominantly between the bracket base and adhesive regardless of SBS values or etching protocol, and with Hellak et al., who recommended dual-curing resins specifically for metal-to-metal bonding due to their superior adhesion to metallic surfaces [44, 45].

From a clinical perspective, this failure pattern is favourable, as metal-sided debonding leaves the enamel surface largely intact and reduces the risk of iatrogenic damage during appliance removal [46].

Clinical significance

Dual-curing resin cements may improve the reliability of bonding for laser-sintered Co–Cr orthodontic appliances compared with conventional light-curing orthodontic adhesives. However, because many protocols produced SBS values exceeding commonly cited upper limits for safe debonding, careful removal techniques are required to minimise enamel damage. Enamel sandblasting with 50 µm aluminum oxide at 1 bar provided no SBS benefit in this study and may be omitted from the bonding protocol under these conditions.

Limitations

This was an in vitro study using bovine enamel and a standardised, polished bonding area, which may not fully represent clinical enamel conditions. The inherent variability of bovine enamel and small, unavoidable differences in specimen preparation and testing may also have influenced SBS outcomes. Ageing was limited to the storage protocol applied prior to testing, so long-term durability under thermal and mechanical cycling cannot be inferred. Failure modes were assessed macroscopically; microscopic enamel alterations and interfacial morphology were not evaluated, for example, by Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM), and therefore, subtle enamel damage cannot be excluded.

Conclusion

Sandblasting the enamel before bonding the laser-sintered orthodontic device showed no SBS benefit under the parameters tested. The dual-curing self-adhesive resin composite cements RX and PA show higher SBS than two conventional orthodontic adhesives. They might build a more reliable bond between the enamel surfaces and the metal alloys. With increased SBS, precautions should be taken when debonding the orthodontic appliance due to the heightened risk of enamel damage.
