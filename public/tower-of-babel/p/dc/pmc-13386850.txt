Air pollution, socioeconomic status and the risk of incident rheumatic heart disease: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Shuyang Meng, Jiacheng Ying, Yuwei Jin, Baojie Hua, Shuxiao Ren, Peiyang Luo, Yun Hong, Qihong Yang, Feitian Ni, Jing Guo, Jiayu Li, Xiaohui Sun, Cuiqing Liu, Ding Ye, Yingying Mao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe relationship between long-term ambient air pollution exposure and socioeconomic status (SES) with rheumatic heart disease (RHD) has been rarely investigated.MethodsWe conducted a population-based cohort study involving approximately half a million participants based on UK Biobank. Long-term exposure to five air pollutants (PM2.5, PM10, PMcoarse, NO2, and NOx) was estimated by using land-use regression models. An overall SES indicator was derived by using latent class analysis based on household income, education level, and employment status. Cox proportional hazards models, together with mediation and interaction analyses were utilized to assess the associations of air pollution and SES with incident RHD.ResultsDuring a median follow-up of 13.6 years among 404,317 participants, a total of 7,944 incident RHD cases were identified. Higher SES was associated with a reduced risk of incident RHD (Medium vs. Low, HR = 0.77, 95% CI: 0.74–0.81; High vs. Low, HR = 0.65, 95% CI: 0.60–0.71; P for trend = 2.29 × 10–35). Statistically significant associations were also observed between incident RHD and PM2.5 (HR: 1.10, 95% CI: 1.07–1.13, per IQR), PM10 (HR: 1.04, 95% CI: 1.01–1.06, per IQR), NO2 (HR: 1.09, 95% CI: 1.06–1.12, per IQR), and NOx (HR: 1.07, 95% CI: 1.05–1.10, per IQR). Mediation analyses suggested that individual air pollutants statistically explained 1.2% to 10.6% of the association between SES and RHD risk. Furthermore, significant interactions were found between nitrogen oxides (NO2 and NOx) and SES on RHD risk. When considering joint effect, the HR was ~ 60% higher among individuals with high air pollution exposure and low SES compared to those with low air pollution exposure and high SES.ConclusionsLong-term exposure to air pollution was associated with an increased risk of incident RHD. Notably, individuals with lower-SES exhibited greater susceptibility to the detrimental effects of air pollution exposure on RHD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27873-5.

Introduction

Rheumatic heart disease (RHD) is a chronic condition characterized by valve damage resulting from an abnormal immune response to group A streptococcal infection [1]. This disease causes at least 200,000 to 250,000 premature deaths every year and contributes to cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, becoming one of the major causes of cardiovascular death in developing countries [1, 2]. Socioeconomic status (SES) is considered as an important factor of RHD occurrence, which affects hygiene, access to medical care, and living conditions, with lower SES populations supposed to experience a higher burden of the disease [1, 3, 4]. With socioeconomic progress and enhanced living standards, the incidence of RHD has declined in wealthier nations, whereas it still persists as a significant health challenge in low-resource settings [1, 2, 5]. An increasing rate of wealth inequity is seen in countries such as US and UK, as the health gap between wealthy and deprived areas continues to grow [6]. Effective efforts are therefore required to address the socioeconomic inequities in RHD risk.

Air pollution has emerged as a major global health threat, with significant implications for respiratory and cardiovascular health. Studies have established a direct negative impact of air pollution on the respiratory system and its role as a major contributor to cardiovascular disease (CVD) [7–11]. Socioeconomic disadvantage has been consistently linked with higher exposure to ambient air pollution, as low-SES populations are more likely to live near major roads or industrial areas [12]. Growing evidence suggests that SES not only determines pollution exposure levels but also modifies susceptibility to its cardiovascular effects [13]. For instance, the adverse impact of long-term PM₂.₅ exposure on cardiovascular mortality is stronger among individuals from socioeconomically disadvantaged communities [14]. Recent studies further highlight that SES disparities can amplify the health risks associated with chronic pollution exposure through differential access to healthcare, housing quality, and baseline comorbidities [15]. Given these interconnected factors, this study considers SES as a primary social determinant of RHD and aims to elucidate the role of long-term air pollution as a critical environmental pathway. Specifically, we investigate whether air pollution acts as a mediator or an effect modifier in the relationship between SES and incident RHD. By uncovering these links, we can better address the underlying mechanisms and develop targeted environmental interventions to reduce health inequities mitigate the burden of RHD.

Methods

Study population

The UK Biobank recruited approximately half a million participants aged 38–73 years between 2007 and 2010 from 22 assessment centers across England, Scotland, and Wales. Full study design and data collection methods have been previously described [16]. Figure S1 shows the detailed inclusion and exclusion flow chart of the current study. Among the 502,355 participants available in the UK Biobank, we excluded withdrawals, participants with previous RHD at baseline, and those with missing information on air pollution data, socioeconomic factors, and other covariates. Finally, 375,521 participants were included in the PM dataset, and 404,317 participants were included in the NOx dataset.

Assessment of SES

Health insurance, household income, education level, and employment status were used to measure SES according to previous studies [17–19]. Given the implementation of the National Health Service in the UK, a publicly funded healthcare system with the goal of delivering comprehensive, universal, and free services, we did not include health insurance as a component of socioeconomic status in the current study based on the UK Biobank [20]. Thus, an overall SES variable was created using latent class analysis based on the three aforementioned variables (household income, education level, and employment status). Three latent classes were identified, which were labeled for low, medium, and high SES according to the characteristics of each class (supplementary methods).

Assessment of air pollutants

The annually derived mean estimates of ambient air pollutants, including fine particular matter (PM) with diameters less than 2.5 μm (PM2.5), coarse PM with diameters between 2.5 and 10 μm (PMcoarse), PM with diameters less than 10 μm (PM10), nitrogen dioxide (NO2) and NOx, were obtained from the UK Biobank for the year 2010 by using land-use regression (LUR) models developed by the ESCAPE (European Study of Cohorts for Air Pollution Effects) project [21, 22]. LUR Synthesized ESCAPE monitoring data and participants’ residential addresses provided at baseline, along with Geographic Information System-derived predictor variables (e.g., traffic volume, population density, terrain features, and land usage), to evaluate the spatial discrepancies in air pollutant concentrations. The leave-one-out cross-validation demonstrated strong model performance for PM2.5, PMcoarse, PM10, NO2, and NOx (cross-validation R2 = 77%, 57%, 88%, 87%, and 88%, respectively). Further information on the development and validation of the ESCAPE LUR models can be found elsewhere [21, 22]. The pollutants were classified as low or high pollution levels based on median values.

Outcome ascertainment

The outcome of interest for this study was incident RHD, which was ascertained by linking to hospital inpatient data defined by the 10th Revision of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10) codes I05-I09 (chronic rheumatic heart diseases). The event date was defined as the date of the first hospital-recorded diagnosis of RHD and was used as the incident date in all analyses. Participants were followed up from the enrollment until the time of RHD diagnosis or censoring. Censoring was defined as the time of death, loss, or the end of follow-up, whichever came first.

Calculation of covariates

The current study incorporated demographic variables including age (years, continuous), gender (female, male), and ethnicity (white, non-white); lifestyle factors including body mass index ([BMI], underweight: less than 18.5, healthy weight: between 18.5 and 25, overweight: between 25 and 30, obesity: over 30), smoking status (current, previous, never), drinking status (current, previous, never), physical activity (regular and irregular), waist circumference (WC, in centimeters, continuous), and a healthy diet score (ranging from 0 to 7 points). For precise elucidation of these covariates, detailed explanations can be found in Table S1.

Statistical analysis

Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for incident RHD associated with air pollutants and SES. Schoenfeld residuals method was utilized to assess the proportional hazard assumption, and no evidence was found contradicting the assumption (all P values > 0.05). Stratified analysis by SES was further performed to assess associations of air pollutants with incident RHD across different socioeconomic subgroups. The measures on multiplicative and additive interaction were previously described [23]. Two indices including the relative excess risk due to the interaction (RERI) and the attributable proportion due to the interaction (AP) were employed to depict the additive scales between air pollutants and SES. The CI would include 1 if there was no multiplicative interaction, while it would include 0 if there was no additive interaction. Mediation analyses were carried out to investigate whether the effect of medium or low vs. high SES on the risk of RHD is mediated by a potentially indirect effect through exposure to air pollutants.

The mediation analyses were performed using the regression-based counterfactual mediation framework implemented in the R package CMAverse. For the primary time-to-event mediation analysis, we specified a Cox proportional hazards model for the outcome and a linear regression model for the mediator. All mediation models were adjusted for the same baseline covariates: age, sex, ethnicity, smoking status, drinking status, BMI, physical activity, diet, and WC. A causal interpretation of natural direct and indirect effects requires strong assumptions, including no unmeasured confounding of the SES–pollution and pollution–RHD relations, no exposure-induced mediator–outcome confounding, and correct model specification. These assumptions are not fully testable in an observational setting; therefore, we interpret the mediation proportion primarily as the proportion of the SES–RHD association statistically explained by air pollution under the specified models rather than as definitive causal mediation. To address interpretational concerns related to the non-collapsibility of hazard ratios in Cox models, we conducted a sensitivity analysis on a collapsible risk scale by defining a 10-year binary endpoint and fitting the outcome model using a log linear regression model.

To assess joint effects of SES and air pollutants on RHD risk, we stratified the participants into six subgroups according to SES (low, moderate and high) and air pollutants categories (low and high), and then estimated the HRs of incident RHD within each subgroup compared with those with high SES and low concentrations of air pollutants. Stratified analyses were also conducted for age (< 60 vs. ≥60), sex (male vs. female), BMI (obesity vs. non-obesity), drinking status (never vs. previous or current) and smoking status (never vs. previous or current).

In addition, we performed several sensitivity analyses. First, we repeated the analyses after excluding RHD events which occurred during the first three years of the follow-up to test the robustness of the associations. Second, we restricted the analyses to participants who had lived in the same place of residence for at least 10 years to alleviate the misclassification of air pollution exposures. Third, we repeated the analyses by replacing SES with individual socioeconomic factors (household income level, education and employment status), all of which were mutually adjusted for in the model.

Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.1. Cox proportional regression, and latent class, and interaction analyses were performed using R packages, including the “survival”, “poLCA”, “CMAverse” and “interactionR” packages. All P values were two-sided, and a P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Population characteristics

During a median follow-up period of 13.6 years, 7,727 RHD events were observed in the PM data set, and 7,944 RHD events were observed in the NOx data set. The age at first recorded RHD event was predominantly in later life, with most events occurring between 60 and 70 years and a peak at approximately 65 years. Table 1 shows the baseline characteristics of the participants in the analytical cohort. The mean annual average estimates for PM2.5, PM10, PMcoarse, NO2, and NOx were 9.98 μg/m³ (SD, 1.05), 16.21 μg/m³ (SD, 1.89), 6.42 μg/m³ (SD, 0.90), 26.57 μg/m³ (SD, 7.54), and 43.87 μg/m³ (SD, 15.38), respectively. The mean age of the participants with complete covariates was 56 years and ~ 53% of them were female, with the majority being the white race (96%) and having higher SES more than medium levels (73%).

Associations between air pollution and incident RHD

As shown in Table 2, long-term exposure to air pollution was associated with an elevated risk of incident RHD. After adjustment for age, sex, BMI, ethnicity, smoking status, drinking status, diet, physical activity, WC and SES, high concentrations of PM2.5 (HR: 1.10, 95% CI: 1.07–1.13, P = 5.00 × 10− 12, per IQR) and PM10 (HR: 1.04, 95% CI: 1.01–1.06, P = 8.35 × 10− 4, per IQR) were associated with an increased risk of incident RHD. However, no statistically significant association between PMcoarse and RHD was observed (HR: 1.01, 95% CI: 0.99–1.03, P = 0.252). Thus, PMcoarse was not considered in the subsequent analyses. In the NOx data set, both NO2 (HR: 1.09, 95% CI: 1.06–1.12, P = 9.55 × 10− 9, per IQR) and NOx (HR: 1.07, 95% CI: 1.05–1.10, P = 6.84 × 10− 10, per IQR) were found to be associated with an increased RHD risk. Similar associations were found as well when using categorical variables (Table S2).

Mediation analysis of air pollution on associations of SES with incident RHD

Before conducting mediation analysis, we first validated the association of SES with incident RHD. Higher SES was found to substantially mitigate the risk of incident RHD (Medium vs. Low, HR = 0.77, 95% CI: 0.74–0.81; High vs. Low, HR = 0.65, 95% CI: 0.60–0.71; P for trend = 2.29 × 10− 35) (Table S3), which was consistent with prior findings. Table 3 presents the results of mediation analysis. When comparing medium SES to high SES, the proportion of RHD risk explained by PM2.5 was 3.53% (95% CI: 0.93%-6.13%, P = 0.008), by PM10 was 1.42% (95% CI: 0.28%-2.57%, P = 0.015) and by NOx was 1.37% (95% CI: 0.37%-2.38%, P = 0.007) under the specified model. When comparing low SES to high SES, the mediation pattern was found to be more distinct for PM2.5 (proportion mediated: 10.56%, 95% CI: 7.01%-14.10%, P < 0.001) and NOx (proportion mediated: 5.97%, 95% CI: 3.62%-8.33%, P < 0.001), with NO2 demonstrating a notable shift in significance (proportion mediated: 4.89%, 95% CI: 2.95%-6.84%, P < 0.001). To address concerns related to the non-collapsibility of hazard ratios and the interpretation of HR-scale mediation, we additionally conducted a sensitivity analysis on a collapsible scale by estimating mediation effects for the 10-year cumulative incidence (risk) (Table S4). The overall mediation pattern remained largely consistent with the primary analysis, and notably, the NO₂-mediated proportion for Medium vs. High SES became statistically significant in the 10-year risk-based analysis (proportion mediated: 2.88%, 95% CI: 1.10%-4.67%, P = 0.002).

Interaction and joint analysis of air pollution and SES with incident RHD

As shown in Fig. 1, no evidence on the interactions between PM and SES on RHD risk was found, whereas both multiplicative and additive interactions were found between nitrogen oxides (NO2 and NOx, high category vs. low category) and SES (low vs. high). For example, the multiplicative scale was 1.17 (95% CI: 1.00-1.36) and RERI was 0.23 (95% CI: 0.07–0.40) for high NOx exposure when low SES was compared with high SES, indicating a 17% higher risk due to multiplicative interaction and a relative excess risk of 23% due to additive interaction, accounting for 14% (4%-24%) of the risk of RHD among participants with both high NOx exposure and low SES. To put it the other way, the associations between nitrogen oxides and risk of incident RHD were stronger within subgroups of a low SES. That is, take NOx for instance, the HRs for individuals among high NOx category compared with those among low category were 0.99 (95% CI: 0.86–1.14, P = 0.887) within those of high SES, 1.07 (95% CI: 1.01–1.15, P = 0.033) within those of medium SES, and 1.15 (95% CI: 1.07–1.23, P = 9.70 × 10− 5) within those of low SES. Similar patterns were found for the associations between NO2 and risk of incident RHD.

Discussion

In this large population-based prospective cohort study, ambient air pollution, including PM2.5, PM10, NO2 and NOx, were found to be associated with increased risk of incident RHD, and statistically explained 1.2% to 10.6% of the associations between SES and RHD risk. Significant interactions were found between nitrogen oxides (for both NO2 and NOx) and SES for both multiplicative and additive scales, and the associations between nitrogen oxides and RHD risk were stronger among those of low SES. Furthermore, when examining the joint effect of air pollution and SES, the risk of incident RHD was ~ 60% higher among those with high air pollution exposure levels and low SES. Existing studies on the associations between long-term air pollution exposure and RHD risk were limited. One retrospective study, focusing on the relationship between early-life domestic air pollution and RHD, found a strong correlation between exposure to domestic pollution in infancy and subsequent adult RHD mortality—an index of chronic RHD prevalence in the study (relative risk per SD increase in pollution 1.17; 95% CI: 1.13–1.21, P < 0.001) [24]. A more recent time-stratified case-crossover study conducted in Guangzhou, China, yielded similar results, showing that 4-day average concentrations of PM1, PM2.5 and PM10 were associated with the risk of RHD mortality [25]. In this large cohort study involving approximately half a million participants, we assessed the impact of long-term exposure to five air pollutants on the incidence of RHD. Consistent with findings from prior studies, our results revealed statistically significant associations between PM2.5, PM10, NO2, and NOx exposure and an increased risk of incident RHD. It is noteworthy that the HR for PM2.5 was higher than that for PM10 and PMcoarse in the current study, which aligns with emerging evidence suggesting higher health risks associated with PMs of smaller aerodynamic particle size [26, 27]. Air pollution may influence the development and progression of RHD through several interrelated biological mechanisms. Early-life exposure to ambient pollutants has been associated with higher susceptibility to respiratory infections, including group A streptococcal infections that trigger rheumatic fever, potentially increasing the risk of initial autoimmune cardiac injury [24]. In later life, chronic exposure to particulate matter and nitrogen oxides may exacerbate systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and endothelial dysfunction, thereby accelerating fibrotic remodeling or calcification of rheumatic valves [28]. Airborne pollutants can also disrupt immune regulation and amplify autoimmune inflammatory responses, potentially reactivating subclinical rheumatic lesions [29]. Given that most RHD cases in this cohort likely reflect infections decades earlier, current adult exposures are more plausibly acting as accelerators or triggers of disease manifestation rather than as the initial cause [30].

In addition to air pollution, our study considers SES as a primary social determinant of RHD. we confirmed previous findings regarding the association between SES and RHD risk. Numerous prior studies have underscored the important role of socioeconomic factors in the pathophysiology of RHD [31–34]. In our current investigation, we employed latent class analysis to construct a comprehensive SES indicator by amalgamating three socioeconomic metrics: household income, education level, and employment status. Our results, offering prospective insights, revealed a 35% (HR: 0.65; 95% CI: 0.60–0.71) lower risk of incident RHD among individuals with high SES compared to their low SES counterparts. While this association is well-established, the core objective of our subsequent analysis was to identify modifiable environmental links, such as air pollution, that explain or exacerbate these socioeconomic inequities.

Given the geographical variability inherent in both SES and air pollution, we further explored their interaction, joint effect, and whether air pollution mediates the association between SES and RHD. In models simultaneously adjusting for SES and air pollution, the associations between air pollutants and RHD risk remained statistically significant, highlighting the robust influence of air pollution on RHD risk independent of socioeconomic factors. Furthermore, our mediation analyses indicated that 1.2% to 10.6% of the associations between SES and RHD risk were explained by individual air pollutants. Both multiplicative and additive interactions were found between nitrogen oxides and SES on RHD risk, suggesting that the interactive effect of low SES and high nitrogen oxides exposure was greater than the multiplication or sum of the two individual effects. In other words, lower-SES individuals exhibited greater susceptibility to the adverse effects of air pollution exposure on RHD, as evidenced by the results of stratification analyses by SES. Considering these results, it can be speculated that air pollution not only acts as a partial pathway of SES on RHD but also modifies the influence of SES on RHD. When assessing the joint effect of SES and air pollution, the risk of incident was approximately 60% higher in individuals with high air pollution exposure and low SES compared to those with low air pollution and high SES. Effective measures should be adopted to address the air pollution issue and mitigate the inequality in the SES effect on RHD, particularly for those with poor socioeconomic conditions.

These patterns are broadly consistent with prior evidence that socioeconomically disadvantaged populations tend to live in more polluted environments and may experience greater health impacts at similar exposure levels [35, 36]. In cardiovascular research, SES-related disadvantage has been reported to amplify pollution–health associations, potentially through differences in baseline health, cumulative stress, and access to prevention and care [15, 37, 38]. Importantly, our interaction results should be interpreted as model-based effect-measure modifiers rather than definitive evidence of causal or biological interaction, and residual confounding—particularly by urbanicity, healthcare accessibility, and co-exposures (e.g., traffic-related noise)—cannot be excluded.

The stronger SES modification observed for NO₂/NOₓ, but not for PM₂.₅/PM₁₀, may have several non-mutually exclusive explanations. Nitrogen oxides are traffic-related pollutants with pronounced spatial gradients, increasing the likelihood that high exposure co-occurs with socioeconomic disadvantage [39]. NO₂/NOₓ may also better capture near-road exposure contrasts (and related co-exposures such as traffic noise), which could contribute to apparent heterogeneity across SES strata [40]. In contrast, PM₂.₅ and PM₁₀ are often more regionally homogeneous which can attenuate interaction signals. Finally, unmeasured neighborhood characteristics (e.g., green space, crowding, area-level deprivation) may differentially correlate with NO₂/NOₓ compared with PM, offering alternative explanations for pollutant-specific interaction patterns without implying distinct causal mechanisms [41, 42].

The major strengths of the current study include the large sample size and long follow-up time of UK Biobank, use of uniform data collection protocols, consideration of a comprehensive socioeconomic indicator and the mediational, interactive, and joint effect of air pollution and SES on the risk of RHD. Several limitations merit consideration. First, air pollution exposures were assigned using 2010 estimates and may not capture time-varying exposure during follow-up; however, restricting analyses to participants living at the same address for ≥ 10 years yielded materially similar results. Second, despite extensive covariate adjustment, residual confounding remains possible; unmeasured regional differences in baseline incidence or diagnostic practices may also persist, and we did not adjust for recruitment center to avoid potential over-adjustment of spatially clustered exposures. Third, mediation analyses require strong, unverifiable assumptions and the temporal ordering of SES–pollution–RHD cannot be fully confirmed; while baseline SES preceded the 2010-based pollution assignment for most participants, temporality cannot be definitively established in this observational setting; thus, the “proportion mediated” should be interpreted as the proportion of the SES–RHD association statistically explained by air pollution under the specified models, rather than definitive causal mediation. Finally, RHD ascertainment from inpatient ICD codes may introduce diagnostic heterogeneity and incomplete capture of incident disease, although age-stratified analyses were consistent with the main findings.

Conclusions

In this population-based prospective cohort study, exposure to ambient air pollution was intimately associated with an increased risk of incident RHD. Interactions were observed between nitrogen oxides (NO2 and NOx) and SES regarding RHD risk, and the impact of SES on RHD was partially explained by air pollution exposure under the specified mediation model. These findings highlight the necessity for government intervention in managing air pollution issue to mitigate disparities in RHD risk arising from socioeconomic inequalities, particularly among those with poor socioeconomic status.
