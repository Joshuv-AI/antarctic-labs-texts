Extracellular Vesicles Released by Picornavirus‐Infected Cells Modify Antiviral Immune Cell Responses
Authors: Xinyi Pei, Janine T A T Stienstra, Margarida Viola, Renee G C Maas, Kyra A Y Defourny, Erik Mul, Saskia C A de Jager, Joost P G Sluijter, Frank J M van Kuppeveld, Esther N M Nolte‐‘t Hoen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTNaked viruses of the family Picornaviridae commonly spread via lysis of infected cells. Recent studies indicate that, prior to cell lysis, naked viruses are packaged and released inside extracellular vesicles (EVs). This has been considered a pro‐viral mechanism because EV membranes protect enclosed virions from neutralizing antibodies. Here, we explored whether immune cells can respond to picornavirus‐induced EVs. Using two different picornaviruses, we demonstrate that EVs from virus‐infected cells stimulate human immune cells to produce the antiviral cytokine type I IFN and chemotactic cytokine IP‐10. Virus‐containing EVs trigger higher cytokine levels than similar amounts of naked virus. Monocytes are the predominant cell type to interact with and respond to virus‐induced EVs. By blocking virion‐receptor interactions we demonstrate that both EV‐enclosed virions and additional host/virus components in EVs aid in immune cell stimulation. This EV‐induced response may provide protection prior to lytic virus release and development of the humoral immune response. We further demonstrate that EV‐enclosed viruses, in contrast to naked virus, cannot be opsonized by neutralizing antibodies. This prevents enhanced uptake of EV‐enclosed virus and ensuing pro‐inflammatory cytokine production by immune cells needed for viral clearance. This work underscores the role of virus‐induced EVs in host antiviral responses to picornavirus infections.Keywords: cardiomyocytes, cytokine production, extracellular vesicles, immune cells, infection, picornavirus

Introduction

The family Picornaviridae, a group of non‐enveloped single‐stranded RNA viruses, comprises a large number of human and animal pathogens. Vaccines and antiviral therapies against these viruses are largely unavailable, which urges the need for a better understanding of their life cycle. Picornaviruses have long been thought to release progeny viruses exclusively through the lysis of infected cells. Recent studies unveiled that viruses can exploit extracellular vesicles (EVs) originating from host cells to escape infected cells and facilitate transmission to new cells (van der Grein et al. 2018). EVs are lipid bilayer enclosed particles secreted by almost all cell types and play important roles in intercellular communications by transferring proteins and nucleic acids to acceptor cells (Colombo et al. 2014, Yáñez‐Mó et al. 2015). The release of EV‐enclosed virus particles has been observed by us and others across various non‐enveloped viruses, including the picornaviruses poliovirus, encephalomyocarditis virus (EMCV), coxsackievirus B3 (CVB3), enterovirus 71 (EV71) and hepatitis A virus (HAV) (Moulin et al. 2021; van der Grein et al. 2019; Gu et al. 2020, Feng et al. 2013, Altan‐Bonnet 2016, Fu and Xiong 2023). Previously, we demonstrated that the picornavirus EMCV strongly stimulates the release of EVs, of which specific subsets can spread the infection to new cells (Van Der Grein et al. 2019). In addition, we showed that one of the viral non‐structural proteins was necessary for the induction of EV‐enclosed virus release via a pathway known as secretory autophagy (Defourny et al. 2024). These findings suggest that viruses can actively steer the packaging and release of virus particles in EVs by infected host cells. Several lines of evidence support the idea that the release of EV‐enclosed viruses is mostly a pro‐viral mechanism that enhances viral spreading. First, we and others demonstrated that the host EV membrane provides a protective shield for virus particles, thereby inhibiting the binding of viral capsids by neutralizing antibodies that play an important role in preventing infection of susceptible cells (van der Grein et al. 2019, Feng et al. 2013, Mao et al. 2016, Chapuy‐Regaud et al. 2017, Nagashima et al. 2017). Additionally, it has been suggested that EVs can encapsulate multiple virus particles, thereby enhancing transmission efficiency (Chen et al. 2015, Santiana et al. 2018). Moreover, the enclosure of virus particles in EVs changes the interaction with target cells and may allow spreading of viruses to cells that do not carry the specific virus receptor (O'Hara et al. 2020). Finally, picornavirus infections alter the type of host proteins and microRNAs incorporated in EVs (van der Grein et al. 2022 and reviewed in Raab‐Traub and Dittmer 2017), which could influence the efficiency of EV‐enclosed virus to enter cells and replicate (van der Grein et al. 2022, Fu et al. 2017). Potential beneficial effects of these EVs in the host antiviral responses have hardly been explored. Hence, an important question is whether immune cells can detect and respond to these virus‐induced EVs.

Immune responses to picornavirus infections have mainly been characterized by detection of neutralizing antibody production and assessment of antiviral and pro‐inflammatory cytokine production. In vivo studies revealed elevated levels of antiviral type I/III interferon (IFN), IFN‐γ induced protein 10 (IP‐10), and pro‐inflammatory cytokines tumour necrosis factor alpha (TNF‐α) and interleukin 6 (IL‐6) in mice infected with picornaviruses such as EMCV, CVB3, and Theiler's murine encephalomyelitis virus (TMEV) (Gitlin et al. 2006, Antoniak et al. 2013, Rosenthal et al. 2012, Jin et al. 2007). These cytokines can play crucial roles in antiviral responses to picornaviruses. Type I IFN restricts viral replication primarily by inducing IFN‐stimulated genes (ISGs), which interfere with various stages of viral infection (Schoggins and Rice 2011). TNF‐α limits the replication of viruses, and promotes the infiltration of macrophages, dendritic cells (DCs), natural killer (NK) cells, and neutrophils to control and clear infections (Mohd Zawawi et al. 2023). IP‐10/CXCL10, a chemotactic cytokine produced by monocytes, T lymphocytes, and endothelial cells, recruits T cells to infection sites and facilitates viral clearance. In vitro studies more specifically addressed whether picornaviruses can productively infect immune cells and induce activation. In vitro cultured monocytes or differentiated macrophages exposed to picornaviruses, including CVB3, EMCV and human rhinovirus (HRV), were shown to upregulate the production of type I IFN, IP‐10, and TNF‐α (Siednienko et al. 2012, de Marcken et al. 2019, Satoh et al. 2010, Laza‐Stanca et al. 2006, Karta et al. 2014, Henke et al. 1991). In both the in vivo and in vitro studies, a potential role of EVs in virus‐induced immune activation was not considered for several reasons. First, the broad confirmation and scientific acceptance that picornaviruses can exist in naked and EV‐enclosed forms has only been achieved in recent years. Second, it is technically difficult to separate EVs and viruses because these entities share many biophysical and biochemical properties (van der Grein et al. 2018). Nevertheless, there is accumulating evidence that EVs released under stress conditions including metabolic stress, inflammation, or infection, can transfer specific cargos and have strong immune‐activating and‐regulating properties (Beninson and Fleshner 2014). To better understand the interplay of picornaviruses and our immune system, it is important to unravel the relative contributions of EVs and naked picornaviruses to immune cell activation and cytokine production.

In this study, we used the picornaviruses EMCV and CVB3 to investigate the impact of picornavirus‐induced EVs on cytokine production by human immune cells. We designed strategies to identify the specific role of EVs from infected cells, and control for the immune effects of constitutively released EVs. Moreover, we applied methodologies to compare the capacities of naked viruses and EV‐enclosed viruses to transfer viral components to immune cells and induce the production of antiviral and pro‐inflammatory cytokines. Our results demonstrate that picornavirus‐induced EVs have the capability to stimulate immune cells to produce type I IFN and IP‐10. Importantly, we validated our data obtained with EVs from immortalized cell lines using human induced pluripotent stem cell‐derived cardiomyocytes (hiPSC‐CMs) (Buikema et al. 2020), a cell type that is naturally affected by EMCV and CVB3 during viral myocarditis. We identified monocytes as the primary immune cell subpopulation to bind and/or take up and respond to virus‐induced EVs. While EV‐enclosed viruses induced production of type I IFN and IP‐10, we demonstrate that EV‐enclosed viruses cannot be opsonized by neutralizing anti‐capsid antibodies produced during the humoral immune response. Our data illustrate that picornavirus‐induced EVs can initiate innate antiviral cytokine production, but hamper antibody‐dependent immune responses that are essential for viral clearance.

Materials and Methods

Cells and Viruses

Human cervical carcinoma cells (HeLa‐R19) and baby hamster kidney cells (BHK‐21) were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (Rockville, MD, USA), and buffalo green monkey cells (BGM) from the European Cell Culture Collection (ECACC). All cell lines were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; high glucose, GlutaMAXTM, ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; GE Healthcare Bio‐Sciences, Chicago, IL, USA; Serana, Pessin, Germany), 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin (Gibco, Paisley, United Kingdom) and cultured in a humidified incubator at 37°C and 5% CO2. hiPSC line was obtained from the Stanford Cardiovascular Institute Biobank (donor SCVI‐273, Sendai virus reprogrammed, PBMCs, female) and cultured in DMEM/F12 (ThermoFisher Scientific) supplemented with the essential eight (E8) growth factors (ThermoFisher Scientific) in a Matrigel (Corning, Somerville, MA, USA) coated (1:400 for 24 h.) plate. To improve cell survival, 10 µM of ROCK inhibitor Y‐27632 (STEMCELL Technologies, Vancouver, BC, Canada) was included in the hiPSC maintenance media for the first 24 h. after cell passaging. hiPSCs were differentiated into cardiomyocytes as reported earlier (Maas et al. 2021). hiPSC and hiPSC‐CMs were kept at 37°C with 5% CO2, and all culture media used were supplemented with 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin (Gibco).

EMCV virus stocks were produced by transfection of BHK21 cells with in vitro RNA transcripts of the previously described infectious cDNA clone pM16.1, which contains a copy of the EMCV genome with a shortened poly‐C tract (Duke and Palmenberg 1989). CVB3 virus stocks were produced by transfecting runoff transcripts of the p53CB3/T7 infectious clone containing CVB3 strain Nancy in BGM cells (Wessels et al. 2005). Virus‐containing samples were harvested after observing virus‐induced cytopathic effect (CPE) and subjected to three consecutive freeze‐thaw cycles. Cell debris was removed by centrifugation for 30 min at 3000 × g and thereafter virus was concentrated from cell‐free culture supernatants by high‐speed ultracentrifugation through a 30% sucrose cushion at 25,000 rpm (80,000 × g) for 16 h. in a SW32 rotor (k‐factor 321) (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA). After removing the supernatant and purification on a 30% density gradient sucrose cushion, virus stocks pelleted at the bottom of the tube were resuspended in PBS and frozen at −80°C. For Figures 3B and onwards, we used highly purified EMCV/CVB3 naked viruses (depleted from host membrane fragments, EVs, and FBS contaminants). These virus stocks were obtained by transfection as described above, followed by the removal of DMEM containing 10% FBS after 24 h. post transfection. The cells were washed with PBS (+Ca2+/+Mg2+) once and supplemented with plain DMEM without FBS and Pen/Strep. Virus‐containing samples were harvested after observing virus‐induced CPE and subjected to three consecutive freeze‐thaw cycles. Cell debris was removed by centrifugation for 30 min at 3000 × g and the cell‐free supernatants were filtered by a vacuum filtration unit (0.2 µm). The culture supernatants were loaded on top of a 30%/50% density gradient sucrose cushion for ultracentrifugation at 32,000 rpm (127,000 × g) for 22 h. in a SW32 rotor (k‐factor 200) (Beckman Coulter). After removing the supernatant and 30%/50% density gradient sucrose cushion, pure naked virus stocks pelleted at the bottom of the tube were resuspended in PBS and frozen at −80°C.

PBMC Isolation and Monocytes

Human buffy coats were provided by the Sanquin blood donation facility (The Netherlands). Donors had different blood types: A (datapoints labelled red, yellow, or black), B (labelled green), and 0 (labelled blue). All donors were rhesus positive (Rh+). The buffy coat was diluted and gently mixed with the same volume of DPBS or Hanks’ Balanced Salt Solution 1× +Ca2+/+Mg2+ (HBSS) (ThermoFisher Scientific) at room temperature. The diluted blood samples were slowly layered onto a Lymphoprep cushion (Axis‐Shield, Oslo, Norway) or Ficoll‐PaqueTM layer (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA) and centrifuged at 400 × g for 25 min at RT with low acceleration and deceleration. After centrifugation, the plasma/buffer was removed as much as possible and the interface containing the PBMC fraction was harvested into a 50 mL tube containing 1% FBS/PBS or 1% FBS/HBSS. PBMCs were washed 3 times at 200 × g for 20 min and passed through a 100 µm cell strainer. All PBMCs were aliquoted and cryopreserved in 90% FBS+10% DMSO. For use in experiments, cells were thawed, diluted in 10 mL Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI)‐1640 (Gibco) medium (+10% FBS, +1% Pen/Strep) and centrifuged at 200 × g for 5 min at RT. The cells were resuspended in RPMI‐1640 medium (+10% FBS, +1% Pen/Strep) at a concentration of 1 million cells/mL and rested overnight in a humidified incubator at 37°C and 5% CO2. For experiments with purified monocytes, we made use of a pooled batch of monocytes from 40 healthy donors (0+, datapoints labelled purple) provided by Sanquin Research Amsterdam (The Netherlands).

Antibodies

The following antibodies were used for western blotting: mouse‐anti‐CD9 (1:2000, clone HI9a; BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA), mouse‐anti‐Flotillin‐1 (1:1000, clone 18/Flotillin‐1; BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA), rabbit‐anti‐LC3 (1:1000, polyclonal; MBL international, Woburn, MA, USA), rabbit‐anti‐Calnexin (1:1000, clone N3C2; GeneTex, Irvine, CA, USA), HRP‐coupled goat‐anti‐mouse secondary antibody (1:8000, cat# A2304; Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MA, USA), and goat‐anti‐rabbit secondary antibody (1:10000, cat# A4914; Sigma‐Aldrich). For flow cytometry, the cells were stained with Brilliant Violet 421 anti‐human CD56 (NCAM) (clone HCD56; BioLegend), Brilliant Violet 421 anti‐human CD14 (clone M5E2; BioLegend), PE/Cyanine7 anti‐human HLA‐DR (clone L243; BioLegend), PE/Cyanine7 anti‐human CD19 (clone HIB19; BioLegend), PerCP/Cyanine5.5 anti‐human CD16 (clone 3G8; BioLegend), APC anti‐human CD11c (clone BU15; eBioscience, San Diego, CA, USA), APC anti‐human CD3 (clone UCHT1; BioLegend), and PE anti‐human CXCL10 (IP‐10) (clone J034D6; BioLegend).

EV Isolation

Cells were infected with EMCV/CVB3 at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10. Cells were washed three times with PBS (+Ca2+/+Mg2+) at 1 h post infection and supplemented with DMEM containing 10% EV‐depleted FBS and 1% Pen/Strep. EV‐depleted FBS was prepared by pre‐diluting the FBS 1:3 in plain DMEM, after which it was ultracentrifuged for 16–20 h at 28,000 rpm (100,000 × g) in an SW32 rotor (k‐factor 262) and 30 mL of supernatant was passed through a 0.22 µm filter. Cell supernatants were harvested at the indicated timepoints during the pre‐lytic phase of infection (7–8 h post infection). During this phase, minimal cell death is observed, as assessed by flow cytometry or using assays measuring leakage of the intracellular enzyme LDH into the extracellular space (van der Grein et al. 2019, Defourny et al. 2024). At the indicated timepoints after infection, cell culture supernatants were harvested and centrifuged at 200 × g for 10 min and twice at 500 × g for 10 min at 4°C to remove cells and cell debris. EVs were isolated from supernatants by ultracentrifugation pelleting for 65 min at 28,000 rpm (100,000 × g) in an SW40 rotor (k‐factor 144.5) or by polyethylene glycol (PEG) precipitation o/n (10% PEG6000, 75 mM NaCl, 5 mM EDTA, 75 mM Tris‐HCl). No differences in EV function were observed using these two different methods for EV concentration (data not shown). EV pellets were resuspended in 0.1% aggregate‐depleted BSA/PBS (cleared from protein aggregates by ultracentrifugation at 100,000 × g for 16 h.). Then, EVs were mixed with iodixanol (OptiPrepTM; Axis‐Shield) to a final concentration of 45% and overlaid with a linear gradient of 40%–5% iodixanol in PBS. Density gradients were centrifuged at 39,000 rpm (192,000 × g) for 16 h. in a SW40 rotor (k‐factor 144). Gradient fractions of 1 mL were collected from the top and their densities assessed by refractometry. EVs at density of 1.06–1.10 g/mL or naked viruses at density of 1.18–1.21 g/mL were diluted with 0.1% aggregate‐depleted BSA/PBS and re‐concentrated by ultracentrifugation pelleting for 90 min at 28,000 rpm (100,000 × g) in an SW32 rotor (k‐factor 262).

Virus Titration

Virus titers of virus‐induced EVs or naked viruses were analysed by performing end‐point dilution assay. BHK‐21 (EMCV) or HeLa‐R19 cells (CVB3) were seeded in 96‐well plates and were infected with 3 to 5‐fold serial dilutions of prediluted density gradient fractions on the next day. The virus titers based on Cell Culture Infectious Dose 50 (CCID50) values were calculated 3 days after infection using the Spearman‐Karber calculation method.

High Resolution Flow Cytometric Analysis of EVs

To characterize EV release at the single particle level, EVs were labelled with 30 µM CFSE (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) for 1 h at RT after ultracentrifugation pelleting or PEG precipitation, followed by purification via density gradient centrifugation. 50 µL of individual gradient fractions were vigorously mixed 1 to 1 (vol/vol) with 4% PFA and fixed in the dark for 30 min at RT. Samples were diluted 1:20 in PBS immediately prior to high‐resolution flow cytometric analysis on a BD Influx flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) or Cytek Aurora with Enhanced Small Particle (ESP) Detection (Cytek Biosciences, Fermont, CA, USA) using an optimized configuration for small vesicle measurement, as previously reported (van der Vlist et al. 2012, Nolte‐’t Hoen et al. 2012). In short, thresholding was applied on fluorescence generated by CFSE‐labelled EVs passing the 488 nm laser. The threshold level was set to allow an event rate of ≤ 10 events per second when measuring PBS. The fluorescently labelled EVs from virus‐infected and mock cells disappear upon treatment with 0.1% Triton (van der Grein et al. 2019). Fluorescent 200 nm polystyrene beads (FluoSpheres, Invitrogen) or Cytek Quality Control beads (Cytek Biosciences) were used to calibrate the fluorescence, forward (FSC) and side (SSC) scattered light settings. Samples were acquired in a fixed time of 30 s (Influx) or fixed volume of 20 µL (Aurora) to compare EV concentrations in parallel samples. Data analysis was performed using FlowJo v10.8.1 software (FlowJo LLC, Ashland, OR, USA).

Flow Cytometric Analysis of Cells

10 µg/mL Brefeldin A (BioLegend) was added to PBMCs for the last 3 h of stimulation. Hereafter, 2–4 × 105 PBMCs were incubated with FcγR block (BioLegend) for 10 min on ice and stained with cell surface labelling antibodies for 25 min at 4°C. The PBMCs were washed once with PBS/0.5% BSA/0.02% azide (PBA), once with PBS and resuspended in Cytofix/Cytoperm solution (BD Biosciences) for 20 min at 4°C. After fixation and permeabilization, the cells were washed and stained with antibodies against IP‐10 for 30 min at 4°C. After washing two times with Perm/Wash buffer and one time with PBA, the PBMCs were fixed in 1% PFA, followed by analysis using a BD FACS Canto II (BD Biosciences) or Cytek Aurora (Cytek Biosciences). Data analysis was performed using FlowJo v10.8.1 software (FlowJo LLC).

Western Blot

EVs were isolated from DNase I treated supernatant of infected cells by ultracentrifugation pelleting for 65 min at 28,000 rpm (100,000 × g) in an SW32 rotor (k‐factor 262) or SW40 rotor (k‐factor 144.5) and lysed in RIPA buffer (40 mM Tris‐HCl pH 8, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 1% Triton X‐100, 150 mM sodium chloride, 0.1% sodium dodecyl sulphate) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) prior to mixing with 5× sample buffer. Cell lysates were lysed in RIPA buffer for 30–40 min on ice and spun by centrifugation at 15,000 × g for 15 min to remove cell debris. The protein concentration of whole cell lysate was determined using a Pierce BCA assay kit (ThermoFisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Proteins were denatured by incubating 4 min at 98°C, loaded on sodium dodecyl sulphate polyacrylamide gels by electrophoresis (SDS‐PAGE) to separate proteins based on molecular weight, and transferred to 0.2 µm polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Trans‐Blot Turbo Transfer pack, Bio‐Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) using the Trans‐Blot Turbo Transfer system (Bio‐Rad). Membranes were blocked with 0.2% (w/v) fish skin gelatin (FSG; Sigma‐Aldrich) in TBS (20 mM Tris Base, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.6) + 0.1% tween (TBS‐T) for 1 h at RT, followed by incubation with primary antibodies o/n at 4°C. Membranes were washed 5 times in 0.2% (w/v) FSG in TBS‐T followed by incubation for 45 min at RT with secondary antibodies. After 3 times washing in TBS‐T and ≥2× washing in TBS, membranes were developed using ECL solution (Clarity Western ECL Substrate, Bio‐Rad) for detection on a Bio‐Rad ChemiDoc imager. Images were analysed by Image Lab software (Bio‐Rad).

In Vitro Stimulation of PBMCs With EVs and Viruses

Normalization of Mock and Virus‐Induced EVs

EVs from infected or non‐infected cells were isolated by PEG precipitation o/n and resuspended in 0.1% aggregate‐depleted BSA/PBS. The purified EVs were separated by density gradient ultracentrifugation and concentrated via ultracentrifugation pelleting for 90 min at 28,000 rpm (100,000 × g) in an SW32 rotor (k‐factor 262). 50 µL of each EV fraction (1.06–1.10 g/mL) was mixed vigorously 1:1 by volume with 4% PFA and fixed in the dark for 30 min at RT. Samples were diluted 1:20 in PBS immediately prior to high‐resolution flow cytometric analysis. Numbers of mock and virus‐induced EVs were normalized based on the condition with the lowest EV counts, after which an equal number of mock and virus‐induced EVs (approximately 3.5 × 108 EVs per condition) was added to 1 × 105 of PBMCs in 150 µL culture volume per well in a flat bottom 96‐well plate. Due to limited availability of EVs from non‐infected (mock) hiPSC‐CMs, three times less mock than CVB3‐induced EVs from hiPSC‐CMs were added to PBMCs.

Normalization of Naked Virus and Virus‐Induced EVs

Virus‐induced EVs were isolated by PEG precipitation o/n and resuspended in 0.1% aggregate‐depleted BSA/PBS. The purified virus‐induced EVs were separated from naked virus by density gradient ultracentrifugation, concentrated via ultracentrifugation pelleting for 90 min at 28,000 rpm (100,000 × g) in an SW32 rotor (k‐factor 262), and resuspended in 50 µL. RNA was isolated from 2 µL of the total volume of virus‐induced EVs and pure naked virus stock and used for RT‐qPCR‐based quantification of viral genomic RNA levels as described below. After calculating viral RNA levels in each condition, virus‐induced EVs or pure virus stock containing viral RNA equivalent to Ct = 18 for EMCV or Ct = 17 for CVB3 were added to 1 × 105 of PBMCs in 150 µL culture volume per well in flat bottom 96‐well plates.

After stimulation of PBMCs with EVs or viruses for 24 h, the supernatant was collected for cytokine measurement using LEGENDplex (see below; BioLegend). Cells were washed once with PBS and harvested by centrifugation for 5 min at 500 × g for RT‐qPCR analysis (see below).

Array‐Based Cytokine Quantification (LEGENDplex)

The culture supernatants of PBMCs were harvested after 24 h of stimulation, centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 min to remove cells, and stored at −20°C until analysis. Supernatants were analysed using a LEGENDplex (Cat. 740390; BioLegend) bead‐based cytokine detection assay according to manufacturer's instructions. For this study, we selected the ‘human anti‐virus response panel’ that allows simultaneous quantification of 13 human cytokines related to antiviral immune responses. Beads were analysed on a BD FACS Canto II (BD Biosciences) or Cytek Aurora (Cytek Biosciences). Following the manufacturer's instructions, beads were separated by size and fluorescence (reporter channels PE, 575 nm and APC, 660 nm) into cytokine‐specific gates, and the fluorescence intensity per bead population was assessed. The concentration of cytokines (in pg/mL) was calculated based on a Human Anti‐Virus Response Panel Standard Curve using the LEGENDplex data analysis software (BioLegend). For each cytokine in every individual experiment, a limit of detection (LoD) was calculated. The average of each LoD is displayed with a dashed gray line in the corresponding graphs. All measured values ranging from 0 pg/mL to the LoD are presented as datapoints on the LoD value for each pof the individual experiments.

The thermal cycling conditions were composed of an initial denaturation step at 95°C for 5 min, 45 cycles at 95°C for 10 s, 60°C for 5 s and 72°C for 30 s. Virus‐Host Receptor Blocking Assays

To assess the efficacy of recombinant CAR Fc chimera protein to block CVB3 infection of susceptible cells, CVB3 virus stock was pre‐incubated with 1.6, 4 and 10 µg/mL of recombinant human CXADR (CAR) Fc chimera protein (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) at RT for 1 h and the effect on CVB3 infectivity was assessed by end‐point dilution in HeLa‐R19 cells.

To assess the effect of virus‐receptor blocking on immune cell activation by virus‐induced EVs, EVs normalized on viral RNA were incubated with 1:1000 diluted anti‐EMCV capsid antibodies (kind gift from Ann Palmenberg) (Dorobantu et al. 2015) or isotype control antibodies (clone Poly29108; BioLegend), or with 4 µg/mL of recombinant human CAR Fc chimera proteins or control Fc fusion proteins (cat# 773004; BioLegend) for CVB3 for 1 h at RT. Hereafter, the EVs were added to 1 × 105 of PBMCs, which were pre‐treated with FcγR block (anti‐human CD16/FcγRIII, clone 3G8 and anti‐human CD64/FcγRI, clone 10.1; BioLegend) at 37°C for 20 min, in a culture volume of 150 µL in a flat bottom 96‐well plate. After incubation of PBMCs with stimuli for 24 h, the supernatant was collected for cytokine measurement via LEGENDplex (BioLegend). Dose‐dependent blocking assays were performed with a 1:200, 1:1000, and 1:5000 dilution range for anti‐EMCV capsid antibodies, or 1.6, 4, and 10 µg/mL concentration range for CAR Fc chimera proteins. The isotype control antibodies and control Fc fusion proteins had the lowest dilution or highest concentration used.

Antibody Opsonization Assays

EMCV‐induced EVs and naked viruses, normalized based on viral RNA content, were incubated with anti‐EMCV capsid antibodies (Dorobantu et al. 2015) or with isotype control antibodies (BioLegend) for 1 h at RT. EVs and naked viruses were added to 1 × 105 of PBMCs in a culture volume of 150 µL in a flat bottom 96‐well plate. After incubation of PBMCs with stimuli for 24 h, the supernatant was collected for cytokine measurement via LEGENDplex (BioLegend). Dose‐dependent opsonization assays were performed with a 1:2500, 1:5000 and 1:10000 dilution range for anti‐EMCV capsid antibodies. The isotype control antibodies had the lowest dilution used.

Statistical Analysis

Data display and analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism version 10 (GraphPad Software, CA, USA). All datapoints in Figures 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 are considered biological replicates from independent experiments, because for each datapoint EV batches were prepared from separate cultures of infected cells. Results

EMCV‐Induced EVs Trigger Antiviral Cytokine Production in PBMCs

We previously reported that infection with the picornavirus EMCV induces the release of a heterogenous population of EVs during the pre‐lytic phase of infection and that the virus‐induced EVs can transfer infection to new cells (van der Grein et al. 2019, 2022). Here, we addressed whether such virus‐induced EVs can activate immune cells to produce antiviral and inflammatory cytokines (research strategy explained in Figure 1). The virus‐induced EVs can be separated from naked viruses based on differences in buoyant density (1.18–1.21 g/mL for naked viruses versus 1.06–1.10 g/mL for EVs) (van der Grein et al. 2019). EMCV infection increases the quantity of EVs carrying CD9, CD63 and Flotillin‐1, as assessed by high‐resolution flow cytometry and western blot analyses (Figure S1A,C,D), and we confirmed the infectivity of these EVs (Figure S1B). EVs isolated from EMCV‐infected or uninfected (mock) cells were quantified by high‐resolution flow cytometry, after which PBMCs from different donors (indicated with colours) were incubated with equal numbers of EMCV‐induced EVs or EVs from mock cells. Inclusion of this mock EV control is important because recent studies indicated that EVs from tumorigenic cell lines, such as HeLa‐R19, can trigger the production of pro‐inflammatory cytokines including IL‐6, macrophage inflammatory protein‐1 (MIP‐1), and monocyte chemoattractant protein‐1 (MCP‐1) (Cocozza et al. 2023). After 24 h, we analysed the production of 13 antiviral and/or pro‐inflammatory cytokines relevant in antiviral immune responses. EMCV‐induced EVs specifically promoted the production of the antiviral cytokines IP‐10 and IFN‐α (Figure 2A). No significant differences were observed between induction of IFN‐β, IFN‐γ, IFN‐λ1, IL‐λ2/3, IL‐6, TNF‐α, IL‐12, and IL‐1β by EMCV‐induced EVs versus mock EVs (Figure S2). Secreted type I IFN may be rapidly depleted from the supernatant by binding to their specific receptor in an autocrine and paracrine manner (Michael Lavigne et al. 2021). Therefore, we validated the production of IFN‐α and IFN‐β in response to virus‐induced EVs at the transcriptional level by RT‐qPCR (Figure 2B). These data indicate that both mock EVs and EMCV‐induced EVs induce production of pro‐inflammatory cytokines in PBMCs, but that only the EVs from infected cells can elicit production of the antiviral cytokines type I IFN and IP‐10.

EMCV‐Induced EVs Trigger Higher Levels of Cytokine Production Than Naked EMCV

During infection with EMCV, virus particles are released in two different forms: as naked virus and as virus packaged in EVs. We next investigated whether EVs and naked virus released from EMCV‐infected cells have a similar capacity to induce antiviral cytokine production by immune cells. We collected the purified EMCV‐induced EVs from the 1.06–1.10 g/mL fractions of the density gradient and EMCV naked virus from the 1.18–1.21 g/mL fractions (Figure S1A,B), and re‐concentrated them by ultracentrifugation. Next, we assessed the amount of viral RNA in the virus‐induced EVs and naked virus samples using RT‐qPCR. To compare cytokine production in response to virus‐induced EVs or naked virus, PBMCs were incubated with different doses of both virus materials containing various amounts of viral RNA. The production of IP‐10 and IFN‐β in these cultures was measured after 24 h using RT‐qPCR analysis (Figure 3A). We observed a dose‐dependent production of IP‐10 and IFN‐β in response to both virus‐induced EVs and naked virus. However, the virus‐induced EVs triggered significantly higher levels of cytokine production than naked virus.

The naked virus fractions isolated from the gradient can be contaminated with protein complexes released by infected cells that co‐sediment at the 100,000 × g ultracentrifugation step and co‐occur in the same 1.18–1.35 g/mL density fractions (Cavicchi et al. 2018). We therefore prepared a highly purified naked virus stock (see the Materials and Methods section) and confirmed that this virus stock was free of EV‐enclosed viruses (Figure S3A). Next, we selected one dose of virus material, Ct = 18 (fold change vs. lowest dose = 181), and assessed cytokine production by PBMCs from three different donors treated with either EMCV‐induced EVs or pure EMCV naked virus (normalized for viral RNA content) at the protein and mRNA level (Figure 3B,C). The combined data confirmed that virus‐induced EVs trigger production of IP‐10 and type I IFN, and that levels induced by EVs exceed those induced by naked viruses. We investigated whether this difference in cytokine production in PBMCs exposed to virus‐induced EVs or naked viruses was due to differences in active viral replication, which may lead to viral dsRNA intermediates recognized by the cytoplasmic sensors MDA‐5 or RIG‐I. However, our results indicate a lack of efficient RNA replication upon incubation with either EMCV‐induced EVs or naked viruses (Figure 3D). This suggests that the higher cytokine response triggered by virus‐induced EVs is not due to a stronger capacity to facilitate viral replication. Overall, these data demonstrate that EMCV‐induced EVs evoke antiviral cytokine production in immune cells, suggesting that EVs produced by infected cells have a role in shaping the antiviral immune response during picornavirus infections.

Data Validation Using a Human Picornavirus and a Cardiomyocyte Cell Model

With the methodology set up using EMCV as a model, we next validated our findings using a human clinically relevant picornavirus, CVB3. CVB3 is a causative agent of viral myocarditis, pancreatitis, and meningitis in humans (Rotbart 2000, Tam 2006, Mamana et al. 2023). Similar to EMCV, CVB3 infection induced a strong increase in the release of EVs carrying CD9, CD63, and Flotillin‐1 (Figure S4A,C). Likewise, the CVB3‐induced EVs carried virus particles and were able to transfer the infection to new cells (Figure S4B). Analogous to EMCV‐induced EVs, CVB3‐induced EVs specifically promoted the production of the chemotactic cytokine IP‐10 by immune cells (Figure 4A), although the absolute concentration was lower than that triggered by EMCV‐induced EVs. The levels of IFN‐α and IFN‐β induced by EVs from CVB3‐infected cells were much lower than those induced by EVs from EMCV‐infected cells and did not exceed the levels induced by mock EVs (Figure 4A,B). Similar to EMCV‐induced EVs, the production of other measured cytokines (IFN‐β, IFN‐γ, IFN‐λ1, IFN‐λ2/3, IL‐6, TNF‐α, IL‐12p70 and IL‐1β) did not exceed those induced by mock EVs (Figure S5). Exposing PBMCs to either CVB3‐induced EVs or highly purified CVB3 virus stock (Figure S3B) confirmed that the EVs and naked virus triggered no or very low production of type I IFN, but that the EVs triggered higher production of IP‐10 than the naked viruses (Figure 4C). As is the case for EMCV, CVB3‐induced EVs or naked viruses do not actively replicate inside PBMCs (Figure 4D). Collectively, these data suggest that CVB3, similar to EMCV, induces the release of EVs that can evoke IP‐10 production in immune cells.

We aimed to further validate our findings in a non‐tumorigenic, physiologically relevant cell model. Hereto we selected hiPSC‐CMs, which is an in vitro model reflecting terminally differentiated human cardiac muscle cells (Lodrini et al. 2020). hiPSC‐CMs have previously been used as a cell model for viral myocarditis research to study the infection dynamics of CVB3 (Sharma et al. 2014). Similar to our observations in HeLa cells (van der Grein et al. 2019, Defourny et al. 2024), infections of hiPSC‐CMs with EMCV and CVB3 led to increased production of EVs and the release of EV‐enclosed virus particles in the pre‐lytic phase of the infection (Figure S6A–C). We added similar numbers of EVs from non‐infected (mock) hiPSC‐CMs or hiPSC‐CMs infected with EMCV (Figure 5A) or CVB3 (Figure 5B) to PBMCs and performed cytokine analysis. We confirmed the previous data obtained in the HeLa cells, showing that EVs from EMCV‐infected hiPSC‐CM induced IP‐10 and IFN‐α production, and that EVs from CVB3‐infected hiPSC‐CM induced IP‐10 production. Overall, our findings on the immune cell activating properties of EMCV‐ and CVB3‐induced EVs from HeLa cells recapitulate the functions of virus‐induced EVs produced in cardiomyocytes that are pathophysiologically relevant in EMCV and CVB3 infections.

Monocytes Take up and Respond to Virus‐Induced EVs

Next, we investigated which of the different immune cells among the total population of PBMCs interact with and respond to the EMCV/CVB3‐induced EVs. Hereto we labelled virus‐induced EVs from HeLa cells with CFSE and incubated these with PBMCs for 24 h. Using flow cytometry, we compared the binding/uptake of fluorescently labelled EVs by monocytes, DCs, T cells, B cells, natural killer T (NKT) cells, and NK cells that we identified based on cell type specific markers (shown in Figure S7). Monocytes had the strongest capacity to bind and/or internalize both EMCV‐induced EVs (Figure 6A) and CVB3‐induced EVs (Figure 6B). Using intracellular cytokine staining of PBMCs and flow cytometric analysis, we observed that a high proportion of monocytes produced IP‐10 in response to EMCV‐induced EVs (Figure 6C). Similar results were confirmed for one donor stimulated with CVB3‐induced EVs (Figure 6D). Using EMCV‐induced EVs as a test case, we investigated whether the cytokine response observed in complete PBMCs could be attributed to EV‐induced effects in isolated monocytes without the context of other immune cells. Purified monocytes pooled from 40 donors were incubated with equal numbers of EMCV‐induced EVs or mock EVs and assessed for cytokine production after 24 h. (Figure 6E). We confirmed that EMCV‐induced EVs but not mock EVs elicited the production of IP‐10 and IFN‐α in isolated monocytes. Collectively, these findings underscore the ability of monocytes to recognize virus‐induced EVs and interact with these EVs, triggering the production of IP‐10 and type I IFN.

EV‐Induced Production of Type I IFN but Not IP‐10 is Abrogated by Blocking Virus‐Receptor Interactions

Various components of virus‐induced EVs may trigger cytokine responses in immune cells. First, these EVs may deliver complete viral particles or viral components (Altan‐Bonnet 2016), which may trigger type I IFN responses. Second, EVs may contain host signalling components derived from the (stressed) infected cells, like damage‐associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) (Collett et al. 2018), which may trigger a pro‐inflammatory cytokine/chemokine response. To gain initial insight into this process, we investigated whether cytokine production induced by EVs from EMCV‐ or CVB3‐infected cells was dependent on the interaction of virus particles with host virus receptors. Naked forms of EMCV and CVB3 require interaction with their respective entry receptors, ADAM9 (Baggen et al. 2019)/VCAM‐1 (Huber 1994) and CAR (He et al. 2001), to transfer infectious viral RNA to the cytoplasm of susceptible cells. This process can be blocked by pre‐treatment of viruses with neutralizing antibodies against EMCV capsid (van der Grein et al. 2019, Vlaspolder et al. 1988a, 1988b) or soluble CVB3 receptor CAR (Goodfellow et al. 2005) (Figure S8A). Immune cells, including monocytes, also express receptors for both EMCV (Namba et al. 2001, Wang et al. 2018) and CVB3 (Shin et al. 2023, Long et al. 2016). This raises the question of whether blocking virus‐receptor interactions during incubation of PBMCs with virus‐induced EVs could inhibit the production of type I IFN (EMCV‐induced EVs) and IP‐10 (EMCV‐ and CVB3‐induced EVs). To address this, virus‐induced EVs were pre‐incubated with either EMCV neutralizing antibodies or soluble CAR proteins and subsequently added to PBMCs for 24 h, after which cytokine production and EV uptake was assessed. Incubating EMCV‐induced EVs with antibodies or CVB3‐induced EVs with soluble CAR did not affect uptake of these EVs by PBMCs (Figure S8B,C). EMCV neutralizing antibodies reduced IFN‐α production by immune cells exposed to EMCV‐induced EVs (Figure 7A), indicating that virion‐receptor binding is necessary for inducing type I IFN production. However, for both EMCV‐ and CVB3‐induced EVs, the production of IP‐10 by the immune cells could not be inhibited by blocking virus‐receptor interactions (Figure 7A,B). The addition of higher concentrations of blocking agents also did not block IP‐10 production triggered by EMCV‐ and CVB3‐induced EV (Figure 7C,D). These findings suggest that the combination of viral and host components in picornavirus‐induced EVs can trigger immune cells to produce various cytokines with a role in the antiviral immune response.

Antibody Opsonization Enhances Antiviral Cytokine Responses Induced by Naked Virus but Not EV‐Enclosed Virus

Naked virus particles released after lytic cell death trigger the humoral arm of the immune response to produce capsid‐recognizing antibodies, which are a major factor in the protection against picornavirus infections (Lasrado et al. 2021, Bogaerts and Durville‐van der Oord 1972, Muir et al. 1991, Rossi et al. 1991). Such (neutralizing) antibodies can opsonize virus particles, which not only prevents infection of new susceptible cells, but also enhances uptake by myeloid cells via Fcγ receptors (Junker et al. 2020). This can enhance virus degradation and induce production of pro‐inflammatory cytokines that are crucial for further immune stimulation and viral clearance. Here we tested how addition of neutralizing antibodies affected cytokine production by PBMCs exposed to naked virus or virus‐induced EVs. We tested this for EMCV, since for this virus a neutralizing antibody is available (Dorobantu et al. 2015). The data in Figure 8 and the dose‐response curves in Figure S9B show a strongly enhanced production of IL‐6, TNF‐α, IL‐10, IL‐12, and IFN‐γ elicited by naked viruses combined with the antibody. This is likely the result of FcγR and/or TLR triggering upon uptake of antibody‐opsonized naked virus particles by the immune cells (Junker et al. 2020, Vogelpoel et al. 2015, Wang et al. 2015, Tugues et al. 2015, Liu et al. 2005). In contrast, EV‐enclosed viruses combined with the antibody did not elicit significant levels of these cytokines in PBMCs (Figure 8), while the EV‐virus stimulated production of type I IFN and IP‐10 was not affected by the antibody (Figure S9A). These data indicate that EVs can prevent opsonization of enclosed virus particles, thereby hampering subsequent FcγR/TLR mediated induction of pro‐inflammatory cytokines important for inducing antiviral immune defence and subsequent viral clearance.

Discussion

We here presented evidence that EVs released by picornavirus‐infected cells triggered immune cells to produce the antiviral (chemotactic) cytokines IP‐10 and type I IFN, which have a known role in combatting picornavirus infections. Intriguingly, virus‐induced EVs induced more cytokine production than naked virus. This immune activation evoked by virus‐induced EVs was observed for both EMCV (genus Cardiovirus) and CVB3 (genus Enterovirus) and was confirmed in cardiomyocytes, which are natural target cells of EMCV and CVB3 during viral myocarditis. Among PBMCs, monocytes were identified as the cells most efficiently interacting with and responding to virus‐induced EVs. Interactions of virus particles with the host virus entry receptors played a role in EV‐induced triggering of type I IFN but not IP‐10 production. This suggests that various components of the virus‐induced EVs may contribute to eliciting cytokine production in immune cells. Despite their potency to trigger an innate antiviral response, the EV‐enclosed viruses could not be opsonized by neutralizing antibodies, thereby hampering FcγR‐mediated triggering of inflammatory cytokines needed for viral clearance. The data provide first evidence that both EV‐enclosed and naked picornaviruses interact with immune cells, but elicit different responses relevant for antiviral immune defence.

To study functional differences between virus‐induced and mock EVs, we normalized by the numbers of EVs, assessed by high‐resolution flow cytometry. Our data clearly indicated that not only virus‐induced EVs but also mock EVs can elicit cytokine release by immune cells. This confirms earlier observations by the group of Prof. Théry, who demonstrated that EVs from tumorigenic cell lines such as HeLa have immune‐stimulatory potential due to the presence of endogenous retroviruses (Cocozza et al. 2023). However, we observed that EVs from non‐infected, non‐tumorigenic cardiomyocytes also induced inflammatory cytokine production in PBMCs (data not shown). This corroborates earlier findings that EVs from cardiomyocytes enhance the pro‐inflammatory functions of monocytes (Almeida Paiva et al. 2019). This underlines the importance of including control EVs from non‐infected cells to identify unique properties of EVs from infected cells.

EMCV and CVB3 infections have been documented to induce immune activation and cytokine production in in vivo murine infection models (Siednienko et al. 2012, de Marcken et al. 2019, Satoh et al. 2010). Several studies also demonstrate the importance of type I IFN (Antoniak et al. 2013, Weithauser et al. 2013, McCartney et al. 2011) and IP‐10 (Yang et al. 2021) in combatting these viral infections. IP‐10 can bind to its receptor CXCR3, which is expressed on a range of immune cells including T cells, NK cells, monocytes, DCs, and macrophages. Triggering of CXCR3 can promote several immune cell effector functions, including T cell adhesion and IL‐12 and IFN‐γ production, as well as DC maturation and cross‐presentation (Yang et al. 2021, Groover and Richmond 2020, Liu et al. 2011, Krathwohl and Anderson 2006). These IP‐10‐driven processes are important for recruiting effector T cells, NK cells, and other relevant immune cells to the site of virus infection (Yang et al. 2021). Less is known about which cells secrete IP‐10 and type I IFN in vivo and how cytokine production is triggered in these cells. In vitro studies showed that monocytes and macrophages exposed to EMCV and CVB3 produced antiviral and pro‐inflammatory cytokines, including IP‐10, TNF‐α and IL‐6 (Siednienko et al. 2012, de Marcken et al. 2019, Henke et al. 1991, Henke et al. 1992). Specifically, exposure to EMCV, but not CVB3, induced type I IFN production in human and mouse monocytes/macrophages (de Marcken et al. 2019, Satoh et al. 2010). In the above‐mentioned studies on immune cell activation by EMCV and CVB3, virus stocks were not cleared from EVs or secretion products resulting from the induction of virus‐induced cell stress. Our study is the first in‐depth comparison of the immune activating potential of virus‐induced EVs versus naked viruses. We demonstrate that virus‐induced EVs are more potent in eliciting type I IFN and IP‐10 in human PBMCs. One potential explanation is that EVs may contain multiple virions (Chen et al. 2015, Santiana et al. 2018), which increases the viral load to individual immune cells and potentially induces a stronger immune response. In addition, EVs not only carry virions but also infection‐induced host stress factors, which may enhance antiviral cytokine release by immune cells. For example, mitochondrial DNA in EVs released during infection with Kaposi's sarcoma‐associated herpesvirus (KSHV) was found to stimulate interferon‐stimulated genes (ISGs) and type I IFN production (Jeon et al. 2019). Our data further indicate that monocytes are the main responders to virus‐induced EVs. Monocytes are known to participate in the development of CVB3‐induced myocarditis (Yip et al. 2023). After CVB3 infection, monocytes are recruited to infected tissues, where they can differentiate into macrophages to phagocytose pathogens and mediate pro‐inflammatory and antiviral cytokine production, such as IP‐10 (Yip et al. 2023). IP‐10 can recruit NK cells to the heart in CVB3‐induced myocarditis (Yuan et al. 2009). Our observations in hiPSC‐CMs are an important validation that also EVs from virus infected‐cardiomyocytes can activate immune cells to produce cytokines that are relevant in the antiviral response observed during viral myocarditis.

Mechanisms underlying type I IFN production in susceptible host cells in response to picornavirus infection are well described. This requires the interaction of virus and entry receptor, followed by the release of viral RNA into the cytosol and viral replication, where recognition of dsRNA by RIG‐I‐like receptors (RLRs) RIG‐I and MDA‐5 induce phosphorylation of IFN regulatory factors (IRF)‐3/7 that drive type I IFN production (McNab et al. 2015). Type I IFN induction in monocytes/macrophages exposed to EMCV has also been shown to involve RLRs and IRF7 (Siednienko et al. 2012, Satoh et al. 2010). What remains to be resolved is how virus‐containing EVs trigger cytokine responses in immune cells. EV‐enclosed viruses may interact with target cells in different ways (Figure 9A). EVs may bind to the plasma membrane of target cells or are internalized into endosomes/phagosomes. They can signal via interaction with receptors on target cell membranes or by delivering their luminal contents into the cytosol upon fusion with the target cell. Alternatively, the EV membranes can be degraded in endosomal/lysosomal compartments due to acidification and the presence of proteolytic enzymes (Rivera‐Serrano et al. 2019). In our current study, we demonstrate that in immune cells, the type I IFN production induced by EVs from EMCV‐infected cells required interaction between the EMCV virion and its host receptor. For this process to occur, the EV‐enclosed virions need to be internalized into endosomes, where low pH and enzymes such as hydrolases and lipases can degrade the EV membrane. The released virions can interact with virus receptors (e.g., CAR) on the endosomal membrane, which facilitates the transfer of viral RNA into the cytosol and triggering of the type I IFN response. This pathway has also been proposed for the spread of EV‐enclosed hepatitis A and E viruses to susceptible cells, which was shown to require endosomal acidification (Feng et al. 2013, Yin et al. 2016). We did not detect significant levels of type I IFN production in PBMCs exposed to CVB3‐induced EVs or naked virus, even though these EVs were taken up by the immune cells and monocytes are known to express host receptors of CVB3. This is likely due to stronger inhibition of host antiviral type I IFN responses, for which picornaviruses have developed various mechanisms (Feng et al. 2014).

Hypothetical scheme of antiviral cytokine production triggered by virus‐induced EVs. (A) EVs secreted by virus‐infected cells contain cargo from viral and host origin. Through different routes, these EVs bind to/are taken up by monocytes. Upon internalization into the endosomal system, EV membranes may be degraded by low pH or proteolytic enzyme cleavage in these compartments. As a result, EV cargo molecules including virions and host/viral RNAs can be released in endosomes. Here they can directly trigger pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) on the endosomal membrane, leading to production of type I IFN and/or IP‐10. Interactions between viral capsid and viral entry receptors in endosomes facilitate the release of viral RNA into the cytosol. Viral RNA not enclosed in capsid and other EV cargo may also enter the cytosol upon fusion of EVs with the target cell plasma membrane or endosomal membranes. Production of type I IFN may be triggered through viral single‐stranded (ss) or double‐stranded (ds) RNA recognition by PRRs inside the cytosol. Other EV cargo may trigger different PRRs and induce immune transcription factors, which leads to the production of IP‐10. (B) Left panel: EV‐enclosed viruses are resistant against neutralization and opsonization by anti‐capsid antibodies. Uptake of these EVs in monocytes is similar in the absence or presence of antibodies and leads to the production of only type I IFN and IP‐10, which is likely not sufficient for viral clearance. Right panel: naked viruses opsonized with anti‐capsid antibodies are taken up by monocytes and other immune cells via interaction with the FcγR, which triggers a cascade of cytokine production. Together with the other cell types in the PBMC population, type I IFN, IFN‐γ, IL‐6, TNF‐α, IL‐10, IL‐12 and IP‐10 are produced in response to the opsonization. This will lead to clearance of naked viruses.

In addition to type I IFN, IP‐10 was produced by monocytes in response to EVs from both EMCV‐infected and CVB3‐infected cells. A large number of studies have highlighted the importance of this chemotactic cytokine in multiple RNA virus infections, aiding in virus clearance or enhancing disease pathogenesis (reviewed in Elemam et al. 2022). CVB3‐induced EVs triggered a lower amount of IP‐10 than EMCV‐induced EVs. One potential explanation is that IP‐10 production is enhanced by pathways involving type I IFN (Kokuzawa et al. 2023), which is missing in the CVB3 condition. We observed that the production of IP‐10 elicited by both EMCV‐ and CVB3‐induced EVs could not be inhibited by blocking of virion‐receptor interactions. This suggests that components other than virions are responsible for IP‐10 induction in immune cells (Figure 9A). Potential host components that may trigger IP‐10 production are histones, which can be associated with the surface of EVs (Nair et al. 2018) and are released from cells under various stress conditions (van der Grein et al. 2022, Singh et al. 2024). Extracellular histones can target monocytes and be recognized by TLR4/myeloid differentiation factor 2 complex, leading to IP‐10 production (Westman et al. 2015). It is also possible that EVs from CVB3 infected cells contain components that trigger the NLR family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome. Previous studies showed that inflammasome activation occurs in macrophages infiltrating the heart during CVB3‐induced viral myocarditis (Bao et al. 2019). In some viral infections, activated inflammasomes are correlated with IP‐10 production (Sefik et al. 2022, Chang et al. 2020).

EV‐enclosed viruses, which are released early in infection prior to virus‐induced cell lysis, may provide an early line of defence against picornaviruses. However, our data indicate that these EV‐associated viruses cannot be opsonized by neutralizing antibodies that are produced by the adaptive immune system later during infection. As a result, EV‐associated viruses do not undergo FcγR‐mediated uptake by immune cells and cannot trigger enhanced production of TNF‐α, IL‐6, IL‐12 and IFN‐γ (Figure 9B). These cytokines are produced via the collaborative action of FcγRs and TLRs, for example, upon recognition of opsonized pathogens (Junker et al. 2020, Vogelpoel et al. 2015, Kim et al. 2025) and are important to recruit and activate various immune cells to the site of infection, supporting viral clearance (Zhang et al. 2023). Our data may corroborate recent findings that injection of crude preparations of CVB3‐induced EVs in mice led to higher production of pro‐inflammatory cytokines and a more severe onset of myocarditis compared to injection with naked virus (Fu et al. 2023). The higher production of IL‐6, TNF‐α and IL‐1β in response to CVB3‐EVs compared to naked virus is congruent with our in vitro data, but this in vivo study lacked the important control for pro‐inflammatory effects of mock EVs. The in vivo experiments demonstrated that EV‐enclosed virus was cleared less efficiently than naked virus and caused wider spread of infection and increased disease burden. We provide initial evidence that this reduced clearance may be due to the inability of EV‐enclosed viruses to become opsonized by neutralizing antibodies, preventing FcγR‐mediated uptake by immune cells and induction of cytokines important for clearance (Figure 9B). Our current study increases our understanding of the immunological mechanisms that underlie host antiviral responses to picornavirus infections. Picornavirus infections lead to the production of EV‐enclosed and naked virus particles. These particles both interact with immune cells but play diverse roles in antiviral defence. The lack of neutralization and opsonization of EV‐enclosed virus particles is highly relevant for future research on chronic infections with picornaviruses.
