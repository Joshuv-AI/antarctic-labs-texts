Nonserogroup-1 Legionella pneumophila Pneumonia: Navigating Diagnostic Challenges
Authors: Lara K. Rotter, Julian A. Villalba, Claressa E. Lucas, Melisa J. Willby, Marwan M. Azar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A 59-year-old woman with newly diagnosed immune thrombocytopenia treated with steroids and rituximab presented with a cavitary lung lesion. Extensive work-up, including bronchoalveolar lavage and transthoracic lung biopsy, were unrevealing. Molecular testing on lung tissue ultimately diagnosed nonserogroup-1 Legionella pneumophila, underscoring the utility of advanced diagnostics in clinical conundrums.

Case Report

A 59-year-old woman from the northeastern United States, vacationing in the western United States, presented to a hospital with 1-week of widespread blood-filled blisters on skin and oral mucosa. Her platelet count was 1 × 1000/μL (reference: 150–420 × 1000/μL] and her C-reactive protein level was 116.74 mg/L (reference, <10.0 mg/L). Other blood test results were normal.

Lesions were attributed to immune thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP). Extensive testing for other causes, including cytomegalovirus, varicella zoster virus, Epstein–Barr virus, HIV, SARS-CoV-2, hepatitis C virus, hepatitis B virus, parvovirus B19, Rickettsia rickettsii, Ehrlichia species, Borrelia burgdoferi, and immune thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura were negative. Imaging studies showed no abnormalities.

Treatment included methylprednisolone (125–250 mg intravenously every 6 hours), prednisone (1 mg/kg bodyweight orally daily), rituximab (1000 mg intravenously every 4 weeks), and romiplostim (1 mcg/kg bodyweight). Within 3 weeks, her platelet count rose to 132 × 1000/μL. She completed one course of rituximab and romiplostim (~1 mcg/kg bodyweight) treatment was continued while tapering prednisone therapy to 5 mg daily, maintaining stable platelet levels.

Seven weeks postpresentation, the patient reported dyspnea and pleuritic right lower chest pain. She did not report back pain, dizziness, tachycardia, fever, or viral symptoms. Her vital signs were as follows: temperature, 36.6 °C; blood pressure, 118/76 mm Hg; heart rate, 93 beats/min; respiratory rate, 18 breaths/min; and 96% oxygen saturation on room air. Computed tomography angiography of the chest revealed a large pulmonary cavity in the right lower lung, bilateral faint ground-glass opacities, and scattered bilateral subsegmental atelectases (Figure 1, A–C). She was admitted for further evaluation. Noninvasive testing—including blood cultures, HIV antigen/antibody, urine Legionella and Streptococcus antigen, serum cryptococcal antigen, serum Coccidioides serologies and urine Coccidioides antigen, MTB QuantiFERON-TB Gold In-Tube test, and serum galactomannan antigen—were negative.

A 1,3-β-d-glucan test returned positive (153 pg/mL [reference, <60 pg/mL]). Bronchoscopy with bronchoalveolar lavage yielded negative stains and cultures for bacteria, fungi, acid-fast bacilli, Nocardia spp., Legionella spp., and GeneXpert MTB.

Empirical treatment with amoxicillin-clavulanic acid (875–125 mg twice daily orally) for bacterial aspiration pneumonia and fluconazole (400 mg daily orally) for coccidioidomycosis was initiated. Fluconazole treatment was discontinued after 1 week owing to alopecia. Symptoms did not improve and repeat Coccidioides test results were negative.

Five weeks postadmission, a repeat computed tomography scan of the chest showed improved ground-glass opacities but persistent right lower lung consolidation (Figure 1, D–F). Amoxicillin-clavulanic acid treatment was discontinued after 6 weeks. An interventional radiology–guided core-needle biopsy of the right lower lung lesion demonstrated focal organizing acute lung injury. Stains for Grocott methenamine silver and acid-fast bacilli yielded negative results, with no evidence of malignancy. Cultures for bacteria, fungi, acid-fast bacilli, and Nocardia spp. remained negative.

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded lung tissue tested positive for Legionella pneumophila via use of broad-range polymerase chain reaction (PCR) at University of Washington. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention testing confirmed L. pneumophila, but not serogroup-1, by multiplex real-time PCR (Figure 2, A–D). Immunohistochemistry for L. pneumophila serogroup-1 was negative. Rare bacilli were seen on Warthin–Starry stain. Further molecular studies were hindered by tissue exhaustion. Azithromycin (500 mg orally daily, 1000 mg loading dose) therapy was prescribed for 2 weeks. Symptoms resolved and follow-up computed tomography scans showed improvement in right lung consolidation (Figure 1, G–I).

Discussion

This case describes an uncommon presentation of subacutechronic cavitary pneumonia caused by nonserogroup-1 L pneumophila in an adult receiving steroids and rituximab for ITP. L pneumophila, a gram-negative bacterium, accounts for up to 9% of community-acquired pneumonia cases and leads to 18 000 hospitalizations annually in the United States (1). L pneumophila comprises 15 or more serogroups, with serogroup-1 being the most common, accounting for more than 70% of cases (2). While serogroup-1 infections typically result in moderate to severe pneumonia, the clinical presentation of nonserogroup-1 infections varies and may depend on both the infecting serogroup and host factors (3).

L pneumophila binds to complement factor C3 and receptors CR1 and CR3, aiding invasion of monocytes and macrophages (4). Intracellular replication is facilitated by avoiding phagolysosome fusion, creating an endoplasmic reticulum–like vacuole and disrupting eukaryotic signaling pathways via molecular mimicry (4).

Legionella infection is primarily controlled by T cell immune responses, posing increased risks for T-cell–deficient individuals (5). High-dose steroids, as received by our patient, impact T cell immunity via nuclear factor kappa inhibition, heightening legionellosis risks (5). The impact of rituximab, a B-cell-depleting antibody, is unclear, given the primarily intracellular habitat of Legionella (6). Since Legionella relies in part on opsonization with antibodies and subsequent Fc-receptor binding for phagocytosis, humoral deficiency theoretically decreases risk for infection, although animal models suggest that antibodies offer some protection (6). The humoral response to Legionella infection may contribute to thrombocytopenia. Antibodies generated against Legionella epitopes form immune complexes that interact with platelet surfaces, activating platelets and complement, potentially causing thrombocytopenia as a manifestation of legionellosis. (7). Thrombocytopenia has been observed in L. pneumophila infections (8), primarily in severe cases of serogroup-1 infection, suggesting critical illness as the main cause.

Our patient developed severe thrombocytopenia before being diagnosed with nonsevere pneumonia. Immune thrombocytopenic purpura involves autoantibodies against platelet membrane glycoproteins and is often precipitated by inflammatory or infectious events, prompting consideration of legionellosis as a possible trigger (8). However, this was deemed unlikely because the average incubation period for serogroup-1 L pneumophila is 2 to 10 days and pulmonary symptoms did not arise until 7 weeks postadmission for ITP (9).

Diagnosing nonserogroup-1 L pneumophila presents a diagnostic challenge. Legionella urine antigen does not detect nonserogroup-1 L pneumophila or non-pneumophila species (such as L longbeachae and L micdadei). Biopsies may yield negative results for Legionella despite an active infection owing to the limitations of current diagnostic tests (2). Other methods, such as respiratory cultures and direct fluorescent antibodies, lack sensitivity, whereas direct-specimen PCR is sensitive and specific but not widely available. Serology is impractical, requiring collection of acute and convalescent samples 4 weeks apart (10). In our case, Legionella urine antigen testing and Legionella bronchoalveolar lavage cultures were negative, whereas PCR indicated L pneumophila without specifying the serogroup.

Despite extensive negative test results, a high suspicion for atypical infection persisted owing to no alternative diagnoses (including malignancy), the patient’s immunocompromised state, and lack of response to empirical therapies. L pneumophila is a key pathogen to consider, especially given its potential for severe disease and the limitations of biopsy and culture methods in detecting it (2). Mycoplasma pneumoniae and Chlamydia pneumoniae are other common causes of atypical pneumonia, often presenting with extrapulmonary manifestations such as rash or hemolytic anemia. Zoonotic pathogens such as Chlamydia psittaci, Coxiella burnetii, and Francisella tularensis should be considered with relevant exposures (11).

Infection with L pneumophila should be included in the differential diagnosis for immunocompromised patients with cavitary pneumonia given the pathogen’s potential for severe and atypical presentations in this population (12–14). Collaborating with experts, including the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, was pivotal in confirming the diagnosis. The specific source of Legionella infection was unclear and may have been health care associated during hospitalization for ITP.

This case illustrates an atypical presentation of legionellosis due to nonserogroup-1 L pneumophila, underscoring the importance of following a stepwise diagnostic approach that considers advanced diagnostics and leverages local and national expert input to detect difficult-to-diagnose pathogens such as nonserogroup-1 L pneumophila. Effective antibiotics improve pneumonia within 3 to 5 days (15). In cases of refractory pneumonia, adding a macrolide for atypical coverage should be considered early.
