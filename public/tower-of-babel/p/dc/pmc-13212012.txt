Assessment of liver regeneration in living donor liver transplantation recipients using computed tomography volumetry-an institutional experience
Authors: Sanket Solanki, Abhishek Jayant, Kishan Singh Rawat, Sri Aurobindo Prasad Das, Samiran Nundy, Naimish N Mehta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgrounds/AimsEarly liver graft regeneration after living donor liver transplantation (LDLT) is critical for patient outcomes, yet prospective volumetric data from Indian recipients remain limited. INTRODUCTION

While living donor liver transplantation (LDLT) is increasingly performed in Asian centers, prospective volumetric data on graft regeneration in Indian recipients remain scarce. Given ethnic differences in body composition and disease etiology, regenerative patterns may differ from those observed in international cohorts [1]. Clinical and experimental data show that partial liver grafts undergo rapid volumetric expansion in the early postoperative period. The A2ALL study demonstrated that adult LDLT recipients achieve approximately 93% of their predicted standard liver volume (SLV) by three months [2]. However, it remains unclear whether this timeline applies to ethnically diverse populations. Indian LDLT recipients have a notably different demographic profile compared to Western cohorts: the mean body mass index (BMI) typically ranges from 23 to 25 kg/m2 (compared to 27 to 29 in North America), and cryptogenic cirrhosis (40%) predominates over alcoholic liver disease [3,4]. These differences raise the possibility that regenerative kinetics and the factors driving hypertrophy may vary in Indian recipients. Early hepatic regeneration is influenced by multiple factors, including portal venous flow, hepatocyte growth factor signaling, and the metabolic demand imbalance between graft mass and recipient needs [5,6]. The degree of initial graft-to-body size mismatch (reflected by graft-to-recipient weight ratio [GRWR] and recipient BMI) likely affects the intensity of these regenerative signals [7]. How these mechanisms specifically manifest in Indian recipients, and whether recipient body composition and disease etiology modulate the regenerative response, remains incompletely characterized.

We conducted this prospective study to quantify early graft regeneration in adult LDLT recipients at a single Indian center using computed tomography (CT) volumetry [8] and to identify clinical predictors of this process. Specifically, we aimed to: (1) measure graft volume on postoperative days 7 and 30; (2) correlate regeneration with recipient body metrics, liver disease severity, and graft factors (including GRWR, lobe, and steatosis); (3) compare regeneration between key subgroups (e.g., right vs. left lobe, low vs. high GRWR); and (4) determine how closely regenerated volumes approach India-specific SLV estimates by day 30.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

Between September 2017 and April 2019, a cohort of thirty-four consecutive patients undergoing LDLT at Sir Ganga Ram Hospital, New Delhi, India was prospectively enrolled in this study after obtaining informed consent. This convenience sample included all consecutive eligible recipients during the study period. Patients with acute liver failure and pediatric transplant recipients were excluded (Fig. 1). Ethical approval was granted by the institutional review board, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants (IRB no. EC/08/15/885).

Shows the study flow diagram indicating the study methodology. CT, computed tomography.

Preoperative clinical history, demographic details, and laboratory investigations were recorded for all patients. Each patient underwent either left lobe or right lobe LDLT performed by the same surgical team. Grafts were classified as steatotic if preoperative unenhanced CT imaging demonstrated liver attenuation ≤ 40 Hounsfield units (HU) or a liver attenuation index (LAI; defined as the difference in attenuation between the liver and spleen) ≤ 5 HU. Grafts with liver attenuation > 40 HU and LAI > 5 HU were categorized as non-steatotic [9]. The graft weight was measured intraoperatively after flushing the graft with a preservative solution. The branches of middle hepatic vein (MHV) were reconstructed with autologous vein grafts or polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) grafts where applicable. Arterial, portal venous, and biliary reconstructions were performed using standard techniques. None of the 34 patients experienced major intraoperative complications. The GRWR was calculated based on the intraoperative graft weight. CT angiography or non-contrast CT scans of the abdomen were performed on postoperative days 7 and 30 to calculate recipient and graft volumes. The CT scans were conducted using a 128-slice Philips Ingenuity CT scanner (Philips Healthcare), and the graft volume was assessed on a dedicated post-processing workstation (GE Advantage Workstation; GE HealthCare) using a pencil-based method with 5 mm sections [10-12]. Measurements were performed by a single, experienced abdominal radiologist, with repeat assessments conducted in 10 patients (29%) to evaluate intra-rater reliability (intraclass correlation coefficient = 0.96). Volumetric assessments were performed without knowledge of recipient clinical variables and time points. Due to the absence of routine intraoperative portal hemodynamic measurements at our center, these parameters could not be incorporated into the regression models. Additionally, the postoperative imaging in this study concentrated on volumetry and did not include a standardized protocol for the systematic assessment of MHV patency or the quantification of congestion volume.

Calculated variables include [13,14]:

SLV (cm3) = 1,000.9 × body surface area (BSA) – 319.9

GRWR = (graft weight in kg/recipient weight in kg) × 100

BMI = weight (kg)/height (m2)

BSA = √[weight (kg) × height (cm)/3,600]

Primary outcomes included changes in graft volume at days 7 and 30, measured as absolute gain in cm3 and relative percentage increase compared to baseline, as well as the percentage of predicted SLV achieved by day 30. Secondary analyses examined regeneration patterns based on GRWR category, graft lobe (right vs. left), recipient age strata, BMI categories, and the presence of mild steatosis. Baseline characteristics are reported as mean ± standard deviation for continuous variables and as proportions for categorical variables. Paired t-tests were used to compare within-patient changes in graft volume across time points. Between-group differences (e.g., right vs. left lobe grafts; GRWR < 0.8 vs. ≥ 0.8) were assessed using unpaired t-tests or one-way ANOVA, with non-parametric alternatives (Mann–Whitney U) applied when distributional assumptions were not satisfied. Stepwise multiple linear regression was employed to identify independent predictors of day-30 graft volume, retaining variables with p < 0.05. Model performance was summarized using the coefficient of determination (R2). Analyses were conducted using SPSS v30.0 (IBM Corp.), with Microsoft Excel utilized for data handling.

RESULTS

Thirty-four adult recipients were included, as shown in Table 1 (mean age 47.6 ± 10.3 years; 82% male). The mean BMI was 25.0 ± 5.5 kg/m2, and the mean BSA was 1.74 ± 0.25 m2. The mean model for end stage liver disease (MELD) score was 19.5 ± 5.8. The indications for transplantation were cryptogenic cirrhosis in 41.2%, hepatitis B virus in 23.5%, alcoholic liver disease in 14.7%, hepatits C virus in 11.8%, and other etiologies in 8.8%. Right-lobe grafts were used in 76.5% of cases (n = 26), while left-lobe grafts were used in 23.5% (n = 8). Macro-steatosis was absent in 76.5% of the recipients and mild (< 30%) in 23.5%. The mean graft weight was 607.9 ± 137.9 g, with a mean GRWR of 0.93 ± 0.24 (range 0.60–1.77). Using an India-specific formula, the recipients’ predicted SLV averaged 1,424 ± 248 cm3. No major intraoperative complications or early graft failures occurred.

Graft volume changes and early regeneration

Mean graft volume increased from 607.9 ± 137.9 cm3 at baseline to 957.3 ± 175.7 cm3 on day 7, and 1,293.4 ± 247.0 cm3 on day 30 (Table 2, Fig. 2). This reflects an absolute gain of 349.5 ± 116.8 cm3 (+62% ± 30%) by day 7 and 685.4 ± 200.3 cm3 (+111% ± 44%) by day 30. All increases compared to baseline were highly significant (day 7 vs. day 0, p < 0.001; day 30 vs. day 0, p < 0.001). Growth was most rapid during the first week, with an additional mean increase of approximately 336 cm3 between days 7 and 30 (p < 0.001). By one month, grafts reached an average of 93% ± 22.7% of the predicted SLV, with no cases of early graft failure.

Multivariate predictors of one-month graft volume

Stepwise multiple linear regression identified recipient BMI and GRWR as the only independent predictors of day 30 graft volume. The final model was:

Day 30 graft volume (cm3) = 152.4 + 29.4 × BMI + 435.9 × GRWR

This model explained 34.9% of the variance in day 30 volume (adjusted R2 = 0.349; overall p = 0.0013). Each 1 kg/m2 increase in BMI was associated with an additional 29 cm3 of graft volume (Fig. 3), while each 0.1 increase in GRWR predicted an increase of 43–44 cm3. No other baseline variables enhanced the model once BMI and GRWR were included. Both of these adjusted effects were statistically significant (regression coefficient for BMI: β = 29.4 ± 7.5, p < 0.001; for GRWR: β = 435.9 ± 170, p = 0.015).

Scatter plot with regression analysis showing the correlation of body mass index (BMI) with postoperative day 30 graft volume.

In practical terms, larger recipients and those who received a larger graft relative to their weight tended to have a greater absolute liver volume by one month, after controlling for other factors. No other baseline variables (e.g., age, sex, or MELD score) significantly improved the model once BMI and GRWR were included. Together, BMI and GRWR accounted for about one-third of the variability in 30-day volume, suggesting that other unmeasured factors contribute to the remaining variation. This finding aligns with prior studies indicating that recipient body size and graft size are major determinants of early post-transplant liver volume recovery. Clinically, these results suggest that while even small-for-size grafts can undergo substantial regeneration, the starting graft mass and the recipient’s habitus establish the baseline for liver volume present by one month.

Subgroup analyses of regeneration

Left-lobe grafts exhibited significantly higher proportional growth compared to right-lobe grafts (174% ± 41% vs. 103% ± 34%, p < 0.001), although both achieved comparable absolute volumes by day 30 (p = 0.56), as shown in Table 3. Younger recipients (19–41 years) demonstrated greater proportional regeneration than older recipients (56–64 years; 160% ± 32% vs. 109% ± 38%, p = 0.01). Grafts with a GRWR < 0.8 showed a trend toward higher proportional growth (137% ± 55% vs. 110% ± 41%; p = 0.07 for parametric, p = 0.04 for non-parametric). Mild macro-steatosis (< 30%) did not negatively affect proportional growth (127% vs. 117%, p = 0.55). Recipients with a BMI > 27 kg/m2 achieved significantly larger absolute volumes by day 30 (1,462 ± 232 cm3) compared to leaner recipients, supporting the regression model that identifies BMI as an independent predictor.

Exploratory analyses of regeneration patterns

A detailed exploratory evaluation was conducted to better characterize the relationship between starting graft size and regenerative behavior. Fig. 4 illustrates this inverse correlation: a scatter plot of initial graft volume vs. percent increase reveals a clear negative trend (slope downward), highlighting the liver’s compensatory hyperplasia in response to reduced graft mass.

Scatter plot showing the inverse correlation between initial graft volume and the percentage volume increase by day 30. Each point represents a patient. A clear negative trend is seen (dashed regression line): smaller grafts (left side of x-axis) tend to undergo greater relative growth (higher y-axis values). For example, grafts around 300–400 cm3 often more than double in size (> 100% increase), whereas larger grafts (~700–800 cm3) generally exhibit < 100% increase. This illustrates the compensatory regeneration capacity of smaller grafts.

Despite the differences in proportional growth, the absolute day 30 volumes remained within a relatively narrow range, indicating that grafts of varying starting sizes tended to converge toward a similar physiological target volume by one month.

Initial graft volume showed a strong inverse correlation with percent volume increase by day 30 (r ≈ −0.67, p < 0.001), consistent with compensatory hyperplasia. Quartile analysis revealed that “high regenerators” (≥ 142% gain) had smaller initial grafts (468 cm3 vs. 691 cm3, p = 0.001), were younger (40 vs. 52 years, p = 0.03), achieved larger absolute gains (875 vs. 523 cm3, p = 0.004), yet had comparable day-30 volumes (1,342 vs. 1,214 cm3; p > 0.3).

Overall, these findings show a clear pattern of liver graft regeneration after LDLT. Early growth primarily depends on initial graft size and the recipient’s physiological capacity. Smaller grafts in younger, metabolically active recipients undergo greater proportional hypertrophy, allowing even undersized grafts to reach adequate volume by one month. In contrast, larger recipients with larger grafts achieve higher absolute volumes but smaller relative gains due to less deficit to correct. All grafts regenerated substantially, with predictable variation based on graft and host factors. Clinically, this means low-GRWR left-lobe grafts in younger recipients can be expected to grow rapidly, while larger grafts in older recipients follow a slower course [15]. Anticipating these patterns enables more individualized postoperative management, including tailored nutritional and immunosuppressive strategies to support early recovery. DISCUSSION

In this prospective Indian LDLT cohort, we observed rapid and substantial graft regeneration within the first month. The graft volume increased by approximately 62% on postoperative day 7 and by approximately 111% by day 30, at which point the grafts reached about 93% of the recipient’s SLV. This swift early hypertrophy is consistent with classical observations (Table 4) that most liver volume restoration occurs in the first week after transplantation [16-22].

Marcos et al. [16] reported that living-donor right lobe recipients nearly doubled their graft mass within the first 7–14 days (an 87% increase at week 1), achieving a total increase of around 119% by one month. Our findings closely parallel these results, highlighting that partial grafts can quickly meet the recipient’s metabolic demands. Notably, by one month, our patients reached approximately 93% of their predicted total liver volume, which is comparable to the ~93% (±18%) volume reconstitution at 3 months reported in the multi-center A2ALL study [2]. This suggests that regeneration in our cohort may have occurred slightly earlier, aligning with reports indicating that grafts may even transiently exceed full liver volume. For instance, Haga et al. [17] documented that grafts can surpass 100% of SLV by two months post-LDLT before stabilizing at approximately 90% of SLV by six months. Similarly, Marcos et al. [16] observed a decrease in graft size from +119% at 30th day, to +99% at 60th day. This overshoot-realignment pattern likely reflects the liver’s robust but self-limiting regenerative response during the early postoperative period.

Our analysis identified recipient BMI and GRWR as independent predictors of graft volume at one month, explaining roughly 35% of the variability. This aligns with prior studies indicating that larger recipient size and initial graft volume are associated with greater absolute liver volume at three months [2]. Importantly, we found an inverse relationship between initial graft size and the proportional growth achieved: smaller grafts exhibited significantly higher relative hypertrophy by day 30 (Pearson r ≈ –0.67). This phenomenon is well-documented in the literature. Byun et al. [19] observed that grafts with lower GRWR underwent more vigorous regeneration, achieving a graft mass approximately 1.7-fold of the initial mass by two weeks. In contrast, GRWR itself correlated negatively with the regeneration rate (r ≈ –0.41). Similarly, a recent volumetry study by Zhang et al. [22] noted a significant inverse correlation between the initial graft volume fraction (initial graft volume [IGV]/SLV) and subsequent liver regeneration ratios (r ≈ –0.60). Mechanistically, small-for-size grafts experience relative portal hyper-perfusion, which can enhance early regenerative signaling. Byun et al.’s study [19] directly linked higher portal vein flow per 100 grams of graft to accelerated hepatic regrowth, while Cheng et al. [18] reported that greater portal inflow (indicated by spleen size) was associated with higher six-month regeneration ratios. These data support the idea that when graft mass is low, hemodynamic and shear-stress stimuli are amplified, driving hepatocyte proliferation—a compensatory response that allows smaller grafts to “catch up” in volume. Notably, none of our patients experienced graft failure or persistent small-for-size dysfunction, indicating that even grafts at the lower acceptable GRWR threshold (≈ 0.8%) can regenerate sufficiently, provided immediate function is maintained. Furthermore, Marcos et al. [16] similarly found no functional disadvantage in grafts with GRWR < 1% compared to those with GRWR > 1% in the early postoperative course, reinforcing that a graft meeting minimal weight criteria can be viable with timely hypertrophy.

It is important to recognize that early increases in CT-derived graft volume may not accurately reflect true hepatocyte mass. Factors such as edema, congestion, and inflammation can temporarily increase liver volume during the first 1–2 weeks, even when the liver is under functional stress. Chae et al. [20] found that recipients with early allograft dysfunction (EAD) had larger relative liver volumes than those without EAD at day 7 and day 21. This suggests that early volumetric expansion can occur alongside biochemical dysfunction and does not necessarily indicate healthy regeneration. Since we did not systematically correlate serial volumetry with liver function tests or use a formal definition of EAD, our findings should be interpreted as evidence of restored anatomical liver mass rather than definitive proof of functional recovery.

Our subgroup analyses provide additional insight into the factors influencing regenerative dynamics. Recipients of left-lobe grafts, who initially had smaller graft masses, demonstrated significantly higher proportional growth compared to those with right-lobe grafts (~174% vs. ~103% increase by one month in our series). As a result, their absolute liver volumes by day 30 were comparable to those of right-lobe recipients, despite the initial disparity. This finding aligns with previous reports indicating that left-lobe grafts can be successfully used in adults when properly selected [23]. Cheng et al. [18] noted that while left-lobe recipients tended to have slightly lower regeneration ratios on average (85% vs. 91% of SLV at 6 months), both left and right grafts ultimately achieved sufficient volume reconstitution. In fact, Soejima et al. [23] reported that by one year post-LDLT, recipients of left lobe grafts reached a graft-to-SLV percentage of approximately 88%, comparable to those with right lobes, due to the left lobe’s accelerated growth. Our data at one month confirms that this convergence occurs early, indicating that a left lobe can effectively compensate if the initial graft meets a safe minimum size. These results support the strategy of using left lobe grafts to enhance donor safety when feasible, as robust regenerative capacity can mitigate the recipient’s small-for-size risk. Additionally, we observed that younger adult recipients (aged 19–41) experienced a more pronounced percentage volume gain than older patients (56–64 years). Although our sample was not large enough for a definitive age-stratified analysis, this trend is biologically plausible. Liver regenerative capacity is known to decline with age; younger livers respond to proliferative cues more vigorously, likely explaining the superior growth in our younger subset [24,25]. This aligns with general clinical experience (e.g., younger living donors regenerate faster than older donors) and suggests that recipient age may modestly influence regeneration kinetics, an area deserving further study. Another notable finding is that moderate hepatic steatosis did not impair regenerative outcomes. All grafts in our study with less than 30% macro-steatosis exhibited regeneration profiles indistinguishable from those of non-steatotic grafts. This supports the observations of Marcos et al. [16], who found that mild donor steatosis (under 30%) had no adverse effect on graft volume restoration or function. In practical terms, our data and that of others support the current practice of accepting living donors with low-grade fatty livers, as these grafts can regenerate normally in the recipient. Of course, severe steatosis (> 30%) remains a concern due to functional and recovery issues. However, our results reinforce that the upper limit of “acceptable” fat content does not intrinsically limit regenerative capacity in the early post-transplant period [26]. Donor age is another factor that may influence regenerative capacity but was not systematically captured in our study database. Several studies have suggested that older donor age is associated with slower early hypertrophy and a greater propensity for functional impairment after partial hepatectomy or LDLT. For instance, Kim et al. [27] reported that grafts from donors ≥ 50 years demonstrated reduced volumetric recovery and higher peak bilirubin levels compared to grafts from younger donors, despite similar baseline characteristics. Similarly, Haga et al. [17] found that older donors exhibited delayed normalization of liver volume compared to younger donors in a Japanese living donor cohort.

Overall, our findings align well with the existing literature on early liver graft regeneration, while providing context specific to an Indian LDLT cohort. The kinetics and magnitudes of graft hypertrophy we observed—approximately a two-thirds increase by week 1 and doubling by one month—are consistent with those reported in American, European, and East Asian populations. This suggests that the fundamental mechanisms of liver regeneration are conserved across diverse settings. We confirmed key predictors of regenerative volume, specifically recipient size and graft size, and demonstrated a trade-off where smaller grafts regenerate proportionally more. This trade-off highlights the importance of careful donor-recipient matching: while a smaller graft can thrive through hyper-regeneration, extremely small grafts increase the risk of perioperative dysfunction.

Based on our findings, we would also like to propose a preliminary decision framework:

• Low-BMI recipients (< 23 kg/m2): may tolerate slightly lower GRWR (e.g. ≥ 0.6) due to their enhanced relative regenerative capacity.

• Standard-BMI recipients (23–27 kg/m2): aim for the traditional GRWR ≥ 0.8, which appears appropriate for most average-build patients.

• High-BMI recipients (> 27 kg/m2): consider targeting a higher GRWR (≥ 1.0) or employing pre-transplant metabolic optimization to ensure sufficient functional mass, given their greater demands.

One limitation of our study was the modest sample size, which resulted in insufficient power to exclude moderate differences in regeneration between the groups. Therefore, the subgroup comparisons should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Additionally, intraoperative portal hemodynamic data, including inflow modulation—factors that critically influence regeneration—were unavailable. The follow-up period was limited to 30 days, preventing an assessment of long-term remodeling or functional recovery. Furthermore, donor-related variables such as donor age, liver stiffness, and comorbidities were not prospectively and systematically captured in our study database. Consequently, our findings should be interpreted with the understanding that unmeasured donor factors, including age, may partially explain the inter-patient variability in regeneration. Volumetric data were also restricted to two postoperative time points, providing snapshots rather than continuous growth trajectories. Nevertheless, our standardized imaging protocol and consistent surgical technique help minimize variability and provide reliable early volumetric insights.

Prospective studies that incorporate portal hemodynamic measurements are needed to define the inflow thresholds that influence regeneration. Longer follow-up will clarify the durability and functional relevance of early hypertrophy. Multi-center Indian cohorts can validate these findings and refine SLV formulas [28]. Integrating volumetry with functional metrics and exploring regenerative augmentation strategies [29]—including nutritional, pharmacologic, perfusion-based, and predictive modeling approaches—may further optimize graft selection and outcomes.

In conclusion, this study shows that early liver graft regeneration after adult LDLT in an Indian cohort is rapid, robust, and aligns with global experiences. Even small grafts can achieve nearly complete volume restoration within one month through compensatory hyperplasia. The recipient’s BMI and the GRWR were identified as key factors influencing early graft volume, highlighting the importance of evidence-based optimization in graft selection and perioperative care. While the study is limited by the lack of portal hemodynamic data and a short follow-up period, its findings provide valuable population-specific insights into LDLT physiology. Ongoing innovation, careful hemodynamic management, and personalized clinical strategies will further improve outcomes for both donors and recipients.
