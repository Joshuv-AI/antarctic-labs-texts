Single-cell immune landscape of acute kidney injury after cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass
Authors: Tingting Wang, Yuanyuan Yao, Jiayi Sun, Juan Wu, Xinyi Liao, Ping Yang, Qinjuan Wu, Zhenzhen Cheng, Wentong Meng, Lei Du, Min Yan, Jiyue Xiong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesAcute kidney injury (AKI) occurs in 19–42% of patients with cardiac surgery under cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB). Clarifying their landscape of immune cells may help discover the mechanisms and thus the treatment and preventive strategies.MethodsThis prospective, single center study recruited adult patients scheduled for cardiac surgery. The primary outcome was AKI. Blood samples were harvested from the central vein for single-cell RNA sequencing, flow cytometry and protein microarrays to characterize the landscape of immune cells. Here we compared the single-cell RNA sequencing of 42,362 immune cells in the blood of 4 patients before and after cardiac surgery, depending on whether they experienced AKI or not.ResultsWe observed an increased degree of stronger activation and cytotoxicity of CD8+ and CD4+ T cells, upregulation of CCL5 and downregulation of IL-10 in patients who experienced AKI than those who did not. Analysis of cell-to-cell communication linked AKI to greater postoperative interaction between CCL5 CD8+ T cells and CCR1 on monocytes, as well as between ICAM1 on monocytes and the complex of ITGAL and integrin β2 on T cells. Furthermore, we also found a positive association between AKI and IL-16 signal as well as stronger signaling involving macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF).ConclusionsTo our knowledge, this is the first prospective study to integrate single-cell RNA-seq, flow cytometry, and protein microarray in cardiac surgery-related AKI. Our analysis identified distinct immune cells and identified key biomarkers, including CD69, CD28, CCL5, ICAM1, IL-16 and MIF as candidate pathway.

Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI) affects 19–42% of patients who undergoing cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass, and 5% may even require renal replacement therapy [1–4]. Old age, female sex and various comorbidities increase the risk of such injury [5,6], which appears to involve hypoperfusion, ischemia-reperfusion injury, neuro-humoral activation, nephrotoxins, and mechanical factors [7,8]. These factors may drive AKI through inflammatory responses. During surgery, ischemia–reperfusion injury and exposure of blood to the cardiopulmonary bypass circuit may induce the opening of mitochondrial permeability transition pores in the kidneys, leading to the production of reactive oxygen species and upregulation of pro-inflammatory transcription factors such as nuclear factor (NF)-κB and HIF-1α [9,10] that ultimately injure or kill cells [11,12]. The change in gene transcription upregulates various pro-inflammatory factors, such as interleukin (IL)-1, IL-6, C-C motif chemokine 2 (CCL2) and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α [13,14]. These factors activate and recruit to the kidney various types of immune cells, including CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, monocyte-macrophages, and CD11b/CD18 (Mac-1) neutrophils [6,15]. This recruitment involves the upregulation of intercellular adhesion molecules, such as ICAM1, in immune cells [16,17].

Less clear, however, is the landscape of immune cells changes and its role in AKI, despite the emphasis on perioperative organ protection in Enhanced Recovery After Surgery guidelines for cardiac surgery [18]. Here we combined single-cell RNA sequencing [19], flow cytometry and protein microarrays to characterize the landscape of immune cells and their gene expression in patients undergoing on-pump cardiac surgery [20], determine how the landscape and transcriptome changes during such surgery and try to elucidate their association with AKI.

Methods

Patients

In this single-center prospective study, patients scheduled for cardiac surgery under cardiopulmonary bypass at our medical center were recruited between May 2022 and March 2023 according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria (Supplementary methods). The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of West China Hospital of Sichuan University (2022-262) and registered at Clinicaltrials.gov (NCT05400356). Trial registration (NCT05400356) was completed on June 1, 2022, prior to first enrollment on June 1, 2022. All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. All participants were enrolled, and all clinical procedures were performed at West China Hospital; collaborating institutions contributed to data processing and analysis only. This study was in line with the STROCSS criteria [21] and the STROBE statement [22].

Patients were classified as experiencing AKI within 7 days after surgery, based on the appearance of any of the following [23]: (1) an increase in serum creatinine of ≥ 0.3 mg/dl (≥ 26.5 μmol/l) within 48 h after surgery, (2) an increase in serum creatinine to a level ≥ 1.5 times the level at baseline during the previous week.

Diagnosis of AKI and other assessments of patients were performed by qualified researchers not directly involved in their medical care.

Biospecimen processing by third-party service providers complied with our institutional review board protocol and applicable regulations on human genetic resources and cross-border data transfer. No identifiable data left the host institution.

Blood sampling and analysis

Before surgery and at the end of cardiopulmonary bypass, blood from the central vein was sampled (10 mL) into sodium citrate-coated tubes. Adverse event monitoring was performed, and no additional clinical risks beyond the standard of care were identified in the study blood samples. Single-cell RNA sequencing (10X Genomics) (n = 4 patients), multichannel spectral flow cytometry (n = 120 patients), and protein microarrays (QAH-INF-3) (n = 45 patients) were used to characterize the landscape of immune cells and their gene expression in patients who underwent on-pump cardiac surgery (Figure 1). Blood samples (5 mL) from four patients who experienced AKI and who did not were subjected to single-cell RNA sequencing on the 10X Genomics Chromium system and Single-Cell 5′ kit (version 2) (Pleasanton, California, USA). The immune cells extracted from blood of 120 patients were analyzed using multichannel spectral flow cytometry on the FACSLyric flow cytometer (BD Bioscience, Franklin, New Jersey, USA). Plasma from 45 patients was assayed for cytokines using the Quantibody Human Inflammation Array 3 (Catalog No. QAH-INF-3, RayBiotech, Norcross, GA, USA). Details of the various procedures are provided in Supplementary methods.

Overview of the strategy for analyzing immune cell types in the blood of patients before and after on-pump cardiac surgery. CPB, cardiopulmonary bypass; scRNA-seq, single-cell RNA sequencing.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed statistically using R 4.1.0 (www.r-project.org) and results were visualized using GraphPad Prism 9.5.1. Continuous data were tested for normality and reported as mean ± SD if normally distributed or as median (interquartile range) if not. Differences between patients who experienced AKI or not were evaluated for significance using the Mann-Whitney U test or t test. Categorical data were reported as n (%), and intergroup differences were assessed using Fisher’s exact test or the chi-squared test.

The ability of certain indices to predict AKI in our cohort was assessed in terms of the area under receiver operating characteristic curves. Correlations were examined through Pearson correlation analysis. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

All patient data were de-identified using coded identifiers. Analyses were performed exclusively on a secure, password-protected server with access restricted to authorized study personnel.

Analysis of differential gene expression

Within each immune cell cluster, differentially expressed genes were identified utilizing the bimod test within the ‘FindMarkers’ function of Seurat. Such genes were defined as those that (1) showed a log2(fold difference) ≥ 0.26 and p < 0.01 between patients who experienced AKI or not, and (2) were expressed in more than 1% of cells from the two patient groups. Potential functions of these differentially expressed genes were explored in terms of enrichment in Gene Ontology terms (www.geneontology.org) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathways (https://www.genome.jp/kegg/). Enrichment was defined as significantly greater representation of genes under a term or pathway than the genome background based on the hypergeometric test at a significance level of p < 0.05. Enrichment of gene sets in terms and pathways was assessed using the ‘clusterProfiler’ function in R.

Communication among immune cell types

Potential interactions among immune cell types were investigated using CellPhoneDB (version 3, www.cellphonedb.org). Interactions were surmised when a receptor on one cell cluster and the corresponding ligand on another cell cluster were expressed at sufficient levels [24], and the strength of interactions was semi-quantitated by averaging the expression levels of receptor and ligand. Only ligands and receptors that were expressed in at least 10% of cells in the analysis were included. Pairwise comparisons between clusters were conducted by randomly permuting cluster labels for all cells 1000 times, with the P value for each receptor-ligand interaction calculated based on a null distribution assumption.

Results

Here we compared the single-cell RNA sequencing of immune cells in blood of 4 patients before surgery and after cardiopulmonary bypass depending on whether they experienced postoperative AKI or not (Figure 2A). In the validation phase, we prospectively enrolled and analyzed 120 patients (51.67% men) who underwent valve surgery and/or coronary artery bypass grafting, 17 of whom experienced AKI of differing severities within 7 days after surgery. The mean body mass index of the cohort was 24 kg/m2, 81% were assigned preoperatively to class III on the American Society of Anesthesiologists scale, and nearly half were assigned preoperatively to classes III or IV on the New York Heart Association scale. After quality control, single-cell RNA sequencing of 42,362 peripheral blood mononuclear cells identified five major immune cell types based on the technique of uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) and t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE): T cells, monocytes, natural killer cells, B cells and platelets (Figure 2B; Supplementary Figures S1 and S2). UMAP visualization revealed clustering and overlapping among cell types, highlighting both similarities and differences across treatment groups (Figure 2B,C).

Cell annotation showed significant transcriptome differences, particularly in T cells and monocytes after surgery (Figure 2D). A gene expression heatmap indicated high expression of genes like CD3D, CD3E, CD8A, and FCGR3A in T cells and monocytes after surgery (Figure 2E). Proportion analysis confirmed varying cell type proportions, notably a higher T cell proportion in patients who experienced AKI, accounting for 83.56% of peripheral blood mononuclear cells after surgery (Figure 2F,G).

CD8+T cells activation to pro-inflammation in AKI before surgery and enhanced after CPB

Among 31,866 T cells in our analysis, we identified six subpopulations: CD4+ T cells (CD3D+CD3E+ CD4+), accounting for 42.68% of all T cells; CD8+ T cells (CD3D+CD3E+CD8A+CD8B+), 26.24%; natural killer T cells (CD3D+CD3E+CD4-CD8-KLRD1+), 19.84%; GDT cells (TRDC+TRGC1+), 7.08%; mucosa-associated invariant T cells (SLC4A10+), 3.79%; and proliferative T cells (MKI167+TOP2A+), 0.37%. These indicate that CD4+ T and CD8+ T cells exhibit the highest percentage and most notable changes postoperative (Figure 3A,B and Supplementary Figure S3). Transcriptome profiling distinguished four subpopulations of CD8+ T cells: CD8+GNLY+ T cells (CD8A+GNLY+GZMB-), accounting for 53.10% of all CD8+ T cells; CD8+ Tn cells (CD8+CCR7+SELL+ANXA1-), 27.86%; CD8+ Tcm cells (CD8+CCR7+SELL+GZMK+ANXA1+), 12.94%; and CD8+ GZMK+ T cells (CD8A+GZMK+), 6.11% (Figure 3C,D and Supplementary Figure S4).

Among CD8+ T cells, we detected 336 genes that were differentially expressed before surgery between patients who experienced AKI or not and 314 differentially expressed after bypass (Supplementary Table S1), which we reduced to a final set of 57 genes upregulated and 85 downregulated during surgery by excluding the preoperative genes (Figure 3E). Genes differentially expressed before surgery and after bypass were enriched in Gene Ontology terms related to cellular response to TNF-α, while genes differentially expressed after bypass were enriched in terms related to regulation of Th1 cytokines, lymphocyte chemotaxis, IL-10 regulation, and pro-inflammatory NF-κB signaling [5] (Figure 3F). CD69 were expressed at higher levels in patients with AKI than in those without (Figure 3G).

We evaluated CD8+ T cells using a customized gene set that included scores for exhaustion, activation, cytotoxicity, and naive states. Patients who experienced kidney injury showed the elevated activation genes like CD69 and CD28, as well as cytotoxicity genes such as GNLY and GZMB, indicating cells were in an active or effector state to enhance the cell-killing ability. In addition, the naive score was lower in patients who experienced kidney injury, suggesting a shift from naive to activated and effector states (Supplementary Figure S5A and Supplementary Table S2).

Using an appropriate gating strategy (Supplementary Figure S6A), we used flow cytometry to analyze expression of CD69 and CD28 on CD8+ T cells before surgery and at the end of cardiopulmonary bypass in the blood of 120 patients (103 non-AKI patients, 17 AKI patients). CD69 activates CD8+ T and other immune cells [25,26], while CD28 provides a co-stimulatory signal to activate CD8+ T cells and induce their proliferation and cytokine production [27], which could secrete cytokines, create immune memory, and modulate immune responses [28]. Compared to patients without kidney injury, those who experienced injury showed higher expression of CD69 on their CD8+ T cells before surgery and after bypass (3.05 vs 0.60%, p = 0.028; 2.30 vs 0.93%, p = 0.009; Figure 3H) and higher expression of CD28 on their CD8+ T cells after bypass (51.30 vs 36.40%, p = 0.024; Figure 3I). Similar results were obtained when we examined expression of CD69 and CD28 specifically on the T cells (Supplementary Figure S6B,C). These changes in expression were not due to changes in the relative abundance of T cells globally or CD8+ T cells specifically (Supplementary Figure S6D,E).

Gene set enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes linked AKI to postoperative enrichment of positive chemotaxis of CD8+ T cells (Figure 3J). After bypass, CCL5 were expressed at higher levels in patients who experienced kidney injury than in those who did not (Figure 3G) and our bioinformatic analysis suggested that the increased CCL5 production originated in the CD8+ subpopulations GZMK+, GNLY+, and Tcm (Figure 3K). Using protein microarrays from 45 patients, we confirmed higher postoperative levels of CCL5 in plasma from patients who experienced kidney injury (6 patients) than in plasma of those who did not (39 patients) (Figure 3L), CCL5 recruits T cells, eosinophils and basophils to sites of inflammation [29]. Bioinformatic analyses suggested that in the differentiation trajectory from CD8+ Tn to CD8+ GZMK+ T, CD8+GNLY+ T and CD8+ Tcm, AKI was associated with increased production of CD8+GNLY+ T cells (Supplementary Figure S7A,B).

Next we focused on the GNLY+ subpopulation of CD8+ T cells because it was the most abundant and is known to kill target cells effectively [30]. Between patients who experienced AKI or not, expression of 213 genes differed before surgery and expression of 320 genes differed after it (Supplementary Table S3), leading to a final set of 72 upregulated and 135 downregulated genes by excluding the preoperative genes (Supplementary Figure S8A). Genes differentially expressed before surgery and after bypass were enriched in the Gene Ontology terms related to inflammatory responses, while genes differentially expressed after bypass were enriched in terms related to NF-κB signaling, IL-10 regulation, and interferon-g (Supplementary Figure S8B). For example, the pro-inflammatory genes CX3CR1 and XCL2 were upregulated, while three genes involved in IL-10 signaling (BCL3, STAT3, TIGIT) were downregulated in patients who experienced AKI relative to those who did not (Supplementary Figure S8C). Using protein microarrays, we confirmed lower level of IL-10 in plasma from patients who experienced kidney injury (21.03 vs 58.39 pg/mL, p = 0.021; Supplementary Figure S8D).

In the second most abundant subpopulation of CD8+ T cells, Tcm cells [5], 85 genes were upregulated and 169 were downregulated after bypass in patients who experienced AKI relative to those who did not (Supplementary Figure S8E,F). These genes were enriched in terms related to platelet activation, focal adhesion, apoptosis, and signaling by TNF or chemokines. Genes differentially expressed in the third most abundant subpopulation of CD8+ T cells, GZMK+T cells, were enriched in terms related to signaling mediated by HIF, TNF or chemokines (Supplementary Figure S8G,I), suggesting greater pro-inflammatory activity in patients who experienced AKI. The GZMK+ subpopulation can, like GNLY+ cells, kill target cells effectively [31].

Greater protein synthesis in CD4+ T cells

Transcriptome profiling distinguished four subpopulations of CD4+ T cells: CD4+ Tn (CD4+CCR7+SELL+ANXA1-ANXA2-), accounting for 44.93% of all CD4+ T cells; CD4+ Tcm (CD4+CCR7+ANXA1+ANXA2+), 37.87%; CD4+ Tem (CD4++CCR7-ANXA1+ANXA2+), 13.78%; and CD4+ Treg (FOXP3+CTLA4+), 3.43% (Figure 4A,B and Supplementary Figure S9). Genes differentially expressed in CD4+ T cells before surgery between patients who experienced AKI or not were enriched in Gene Ontology terms related to mitochondrial electron transport and granulocyte chemotaxis (Figure 4C,D and Supplementary Table S1). In contrast, genes differentially expressed after bypass were enriched in terms related to cytoplasmic translation, structural constituent ribosomal activity, and NF-κB signaling (Figure 4D). Consistently, gene set enrichment analysis linked kidney injury to enrichment in cytoplasmic translation (Normalized Enrichment Score = 1.57, p = 0.010) and structural component ribosome activity (Normalized Enrichment Score = 1.28, p < 0.001; Figure 4E). Kidney injury was also associated with upregulation of CCL5, CX3CR1, and HLA-A (Figure 4F), implying enhanced immune inflammatory responses. Tem cells appeared to be the CD4+ subpopulation primarily responsible for CCL5 production (Figure 4G). These cells, also referred to as CD4+ effector memory T cells, produce interferon-γ and IL-2 [32].

We evaluated CD4+ T cells using a customized gene set that included scores for exhaustion, activation, cytotoxicity, and naive states. Before surgery, patients who experienced kidney injury showed higher activation and cytotoxicity scores compared to those who did not. Activation genes like CD69 and CD28 were elevated, indicating cells were in an active or effector state, engaging in immune functions. In contrast, patients who experienced kidney injury exhibited lower levels of exhaustion and naive scores, indicating that these cells retain effective function and are not substantially impacted by immunosuppression (Supplementary Figure S5B and Supplementary Table S2).

Next, we used flow cytometry to analyze expression of CD69 and CD28 on CD4+ T cells. Before surgery, CD4+ T cells from patients who later experienced AKI expressed significantly higher levels of CD69 than the corresponding cells from patients who did not experience injury (7.83% vs 1.96%, Figure 4H). The observed difference in CD69 expression was not due to changes in the relative abundance of CD4+ T cells (Supplementary Figure S6F). They were much more abundant before surgery, and expressed higher levels of CD28, in patients who experienced AKI than in those who did not (Figure 4I,J).

Bioinformatic analyses suggested that in the differentiation trajectory from CD4+ Tn to CD4+ Treg, CD4+ Tem and CD4+ Tcm, AKI was associated with increased production of CD4+ Tem (Supplementary Figure S7C,D).

Neutrophil aggregation by monocytes aggravated in AKI patients

Transcriptome profiling distinguished two subpopulations of monocytes: classical monocyte (FCN1+CD14+SELL+S100A8+) engulf and destroy invading microbes while secreting inflammatory cytokines and chemokines [33], and nonclassical monocyte (FCN1+FCGR3A+FCGR3B+CX3CR1+) detect and remove pathogens and damaged cells, contribute to immune surveillance, as well as aid in tissue repair and regeneration [34] (Figure 5A,B and Supplementary Figure S10). AKI was associated with significantly higher expression of monocytes after bypass (2.90 vs 2.20%, Figure 5C). Relative to patients who did not experience AKI, those who did showed upregulation of 138 genes in monocytes and downregulation of 122 before surgery, as well as upregulation of 112 and downregulation of 381 after bypass (Figure 5D and Supplementary Table S1). The genes were enriched in Gene Ontology terms related to neutrophil aggregation, macrophage migration inhibitory factor binding, and S100 protein binding (Figure 5E).

We conducted customized gene set scoring on monocytes and discovered that patients who experienced kidney injury had higher pro-inflammatory, anti-inflammatory, and antigen presentation scores compared to those who did not. The upregulation in pro-inflammatory genes like IL6 and CCL5 indicates a pro-inflammatory state and acute inflammatory response. Elevated anti-inflammatory genes such as CD163 and CD274 suggest immunomodulatory or tissue repair roles. Higher expression of antigen-presenting genes like CD80 and HLA-DRA implies monocyte differentiation into specialized antigen-presenting cells involved in T cell activation (Supplementary Figure S5C and Supplementary Table S2).

Next, we used flow cytometry to analyze expression of CD80, CD163, CD274 and HLA-DRA on monocytes and subpopulations of monocytes. Compared to patients who did not experience kidney injury, those who experienced injury showed higher expression of CD80 on their monocytes after bypass (35.75 vs 5.94%, Figure 5F), promoting T cell activation and proliferation [35]. Proportions of monocytes expressing CD163 and CD274, implying immunosuppression [6,36], were significantly higher levels of patients who experienced AKI than those who did not, both before surgery (CD163+, 55.15% vs 32.70%) and after bypass (CD163+, 53.30 vs 41.00%; CD274+, 30.35 vs 16.00%; Figure 5G,H). CD274 supports immunotolerance [37], so the link between AKI and CD274 upregulation is consistent with the link between injury and CD163 upregulation mentioned above.

Relative abundance of non-classical monocytes was significantly lower among patients who experienced acute kidney failure than among those who did not before surgery (10.11 vs 15.95%, Supplementary Figure S11A). On the other hand, non-classical monocytes in patients who experienced kidney injury expressed significantly higher levels of CD80 after bypass than the corresponding cells from patients who did not experience kidney injury (Supplementary Figure S11B). In fact, post-bypass expression of this protein was associated with kidney injury in our cohort with an AUC of 0.767 (Supplementary Figure S11C). Proportions of non-classical monocytes expressing HLA-DR, which participate in antigen presentation [38], were significantly higher levels of patients who experienced kidney injury than those who did not, both before surgery (40.55 or 22.95%) and after bypass (14.30 vs 4.49%; Supplementary Figure S11D). Unlike non-classical monocytes, classical monocytes did not differ significantly in relative abundance between patients who experienced AKI or not (Supplementary Figure S11E). Nevertheless, classical monocytes expressed significantly higher levels of CD80 after bypass in patients who experienced kidney injury than in those who did not (Supplementary Figure S11F). In fact, post-bypass expression of CD80 was associated with kidney injury in our cohort with an AUC of 0.738 (Supplementary Figure S11C).

Consistently, kidney injury was associated with upregulation of ICAM1, CD74, S100A8 and S100A9 (Figure 5I), including upregulation of ICAM1 on neutrophils confirmed by flow cytometry (Figure 5J), where the molecule helps neutrophils adhere to blood vessel walls and enter surrounding tissues [16]. Using protein microarrays, we confirmed higher postoperative levels of ICAM1 in plasma from patients who experienced kidney injury than from those who did not (Figure 5K). Bioinformatic analyses suggested that in the differentiation trajectory from classical monocytes to non-classical monocytes, AKI was associated with decreased production of non-classical monocytes (Supplementary Figure S7E,F).

Altered communication among monocytes with T cells to promote inflammation

Bioinformatic assessment of potential interactions among CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells and monocytes suggested that AKI was associated with stronger interactions between CD8+ T cells and monocytes before surgery, but with stronger interactions of monocytes with CD4+ and CD8+ T cells after bypass (Figure 6A). These alterations in intercellular interactions imply altered signaling pathways [39].

When we analyzed how these changes in intercellular communication might affect signaling pathways (Figure 7A), we found that AKI was associated with stronger interactions of classical and non-classical monocytes with CD4+ Tem cells (Figure 7B) and CD8+GNLY+ T cells (Figure 7C) after on-pump cardiac surgery. Discussion

Using single-cell RNA sequencing combined with flow cytometry and protein microarray technology, we comprehensively evaluated the kinetics of perioperative immune cell populations and inflammatory cytokines before and after cardiopulmonary bypass in order to identify the perioperative immunology and inflammatory pathways associated with AKI [42]. By elucidating novel perioperative immunological mechanisms underlying cardiac surgery-related AKI, our findings extend the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO)-based framework and may stimulate future research on pathogenic molecules and processes. Ultimately, this could facilitate earlier detection and more precise risk stratification, and enhanced patient management [23].

Previous scRNA-seq studies in sepsis, transplantation or ischemia-reperfusion-associated AKI have predominantly concentrated on infection-driven systemic inflammation or hypoxia-induced tubular injury [43–45]. In contrast, AKI associated with cardiac surgery represents a distinct clinical setting characterized by sterile inflammation and additional surgery-specific insults [46,47]. Beyond ischemia-reperfusion injury, CPB induces immune activation by exposing blood to extracorporeal circuits, leading to cytokine release, activation of neutrophils and macrophages, and subsequent inflammatory infiltration of the renal parenchyma. Moreover, surgical manipulation may dislodge atherosclerotic plaques or emboli, resulting in renal microvascular obstruction and complement activation, exacerbating kidney injury. These distinctive mechanisms highlight the fact that the immune landscape of cardiac surgery-related AKI differs that of from previously studied contexts, underscoring the novelty of our study in mapping this process using single-cell transcriptomics.

Our study associated AKI with the activation of CD69+CD8+ T and CD28+CD8+ T cells, indicating significant immune cell activation [26]. Gene set enrichment analysis was consistent with the known ability of on-pump cardiac surgery to upregulate cytokines and chemokines that activate lymphocytes, monocytes, and neutrophils, and recruit them to the renal parenchyma. This process, in turn, upregulates proinflammatory transcription factors such as NF-κB, whose target genes contribute to inflammation and infiltration of renal parenchymal, ultimately leading to AKI [6]. Our results associate AKI with the pro-inflammatory cytokines CCL5 and anti-inflammatory IL-10 [29,48–50], consistent with the enhanced activation and proliferation of CD8+ T cells. These observations identified specific immune subsets and ligand–receptor interactions as potential translational biomarkers of AKI.

In addition to CD8+ T cell activation, we observed significant changes in CD4+ T cells, particularly in their metabolic activity. The transcriptomic analysis is consistent with the concept that activated T cells require enhanced protein production to support their effector functions. Among the subpopulations of CD4+ T cells, we observed a shift toward CD4+ Tem. This aligns with previous studies showing that CD4+ Tem cells are highly inflammatory and capable of sustaining cytokine secretion, which could contribute to prolonged immune activation in AKI. The observed upregulation of CX3CR1 and HLA-A in CD4+ T cells further supports a pro-inflammatory phenotype, indicating a potential mechanism by which these cells contribute to AKI. Our analysis confirmed that AKI is associated with a pro-inflammatory state in the circulatory system.

Our results link AKI to the upregulation of CD80+, CD163+, CD274+ and HLA-DRA in monocytes. CD80 and HLA-DRA delivers essential costimulatory signals required for T cell activation and survival through their interaction with CD28 [51]. CD163 exerts anti-inflammatory effects [36,52], while interaction between CD274 on monocytes and its receptor PD-1 on T cells suppresses T cell activation and proliferation [37]. This finding is consistent with previous evidence in sepsis and systemic inflammation, which demonstrates that CD274/PD-1 signaling plays a role in transient immunosuppression and T cell exhaustion, potentially influencing organ injury and repair processes [53,54]. In our study, the upregulation may represent a compensatory mechanism aiming to limit hyperinflammatory responses triggered by CPB, sterile inflammation, and surgical stress. Notably, non-classical monocytes, which are involved in immune surveillance and tissue repair, were significantly reduced in patients with AKI, suggesting a shift toward a more inflammatory monocyte population.

Our results also link kidney injury to upregulation of ICAM1 (CD54) on neutrophils and monocytes, which helps the cells aggregate, adhere to blood vessel walls and invade surrounding tissues [55,56], part of the processes known to contribute to kidney injury in early stages [57]. Indeed, downregulating ICAM1 in animal models can mitigate AKI [58]. Our findings rationalize further exploration of ICAM1 as a translational biomarkers of AKI.

Our analysis of communication among immune cell types confirmed our assays based on flow cytometry, associating AKI with enhanced interaction between CCL5 on CD8+ T cells and CCR1 on monocytes. This interaction enhances local immune responses, recruiting monocytes, T cells, eosinophils and neutrophils, while upregulating secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-6 and IL-1β [59–61]. Our communication analysis associated AKI with strengthened interaction between ICAM1 on monocytes and the integrin αLβ2 complex, ITGAL and SPN on CD4+ and CD8+ T cells. The interaction of ICAM1 with the complex of ITGAL and integrin β2 facilitates antigen presentation to T cells, stimulating their activation and proliferation [62–64]. Interestingly, interactions involving ANXA1, which is known to mediate anti-inflammatory responses, were stronger in patients who did not experience kidney injury, suggesting a potential protective mechanism that is disrupted in AKI. This imbalance in pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory signaling may contribute to sustained immune activation and renal injury.

Lastly, our analysis of gene expression linked AKI to upregulation of IL-16, which stimulates T cell chemotaxis [65,66], and upregulation of MIF, which is a pro-inflammatory cytokine that sustains immune responses [67,68]. We hypothesize that the upregulation of IL-16 helps drive processes that increase risk of AKI, whereas the upregulation of MIF is a compensatory response aiming to curb tissue injury. Several studies have suggested that MIF protects against AKI after on-pump cardiac surgery [67] and against kidney injury in mouse models [69]. These results propose testable hypotheses that IL-16/MIF signaling pathways may have translational potential. We ensured methodological rigor and reproducibility by adhering to the STROCSS criteria, thereby enhancing the integration of our findings into future surgical research [70]. Our study provides novel insights into the perioperative immunology underlying cardiac surgery-associated AKI, with significant implications for both perioperative nephrology and precision medicine. We have identified immune biomarkers, including CD69, CD28, CCL5, and ICAM1, and observed the activation of IL-16/MIF signaling pathways during cardiac surgery as detailed in Table 3. From a perioperative nephrology perspective, these findings present opportunities for early immune monitoring and the development of kidney-protective strategies. In the context of precision medicine, they advocate for the advancement of individualized risk prediction and targeted interventions, which could potentially enhance outcomes for patients undergoing cardiac surgery under CPB. Our findings also provide critical insights for anesthesiologists and cardiac surgeons. The identified immune signatures may facilitate early intraoperative risk stratification and guide precision management strategies, such as tailored fluid therapy or anti-inflammatory interventions. This highlights that AKI is a systemic inflammatory event, necessitating a collaborative perioperative approach to improve outcomes.

This study has some limitations. One was the relatively small single-center cohort and the limited number of cases of advanced AKI (stages II–III). This restricted our statistical power to examine the associations between AKI stages and outcomes, such as renal replacement therapy and mortality. Additionally, the small sample size increases the risk of overfitting and may reduce the robustness of the bioinformatics conclusions. To enhance reproducibility and confirm the clinical applicability, future multicenter studies with prospective modeling and larger sample sizes are essential.

Despite these limitations, our study provides strong evidence that links the risk of AKI after on-pump cardiac surgery to alterations in the relative abundance and gene expression of various immune cell types. These alterations can be examined through simple blood sampling and flow cytometry, which may create new possibilities for the early detection or even prediction of AKI, and may improve patient management before and after surgery. Notably, although the single-cell RNA-seq cohort was limited to four patients, we validated key immune alterations in a substantially larger prospective cohort of 120 patients using flow cytometry, thereby strengthening the robustness and clinical relevance of our findings. Moreover, by identifying immune biomarkers such as CD69, CD28, CCL5, ICAM1, IL-16, and MIF, our findings have the potential to be directly integrated into perioperative risk prediction models and immune monitoring strategies, offering a practical framework for precision nephrology in the surgical context. In addition, the involvement of IL-16 and MIF signaling pathways highlights candidate pathway warranting mechanistic validation. Our research not only formulates testable hypotheses regarding specific molecules, genes, and pathways implicated in AKI following on-pump cardiac surgery but also serves as a translational conduit for individualized risk stratification and targeted therapeutic interventions.

Although our study provides novel immunological insights into cardiac surgery-associated AKI, further research is essential to translate these findings into clinical practice. First, multicenter validation in larger and more diverse cardiac surgery cohorts is required to confirm the robustness and generalizability of the identified biomarkers. Second, the integration of these immune markers into predictive risk models could improve perioperative risk stratification and support precision nephrology. Third, experimental studies are required to assess the functional roles of IL-16 and MIF in animal or translational models, which may provide preclinical evidence of their potential as candidate pathways and warrant mechanistic validation. Collectively, these directions will help bridge discovery-level insights into clinical translation of perioperative immunology.
