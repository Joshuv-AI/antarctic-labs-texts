An automated plasma‐based proteotyping immunoassay for APOE ε4 zygosity classification in Alzheimer's disease
Authors: Scott Levin, Brian Engel, Corey Carlson, Jeremiah Hinson, Mark Holland, Dan Figdore, Hyun Kook Lim, Laurent Tiers, Kinal Bhatt, Ben Schlichtmann, Katie Hoffmann, Ye Eun Bae, Miklos Szabo, Sylvain Lehmann, Yoo Hyun Um, Alicia Algeciras‐Schimnich
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONDetermining apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 allele status, a key genetic risk factor for Alzheimer's disease (AD), requires molecular genotyping infrastructure not widely accessible beyond specialized centers.METHODSA fully automated high‐throughput apoE E4 proteotyping immunoassay was evaluated for clinical performance (460 participants across three cohorts) and analytical validity. Concordance with polymerase chain reaction (PCR)‐based genotyping and measures of analytical validity were reported.RESULTSThe apoE E4 immunoassay demonstrated 99.6% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 98.4% to 99.9%) concordance with PCR‐based APOE ε4 genotype results across the pooled clinical cohort; 100.0% (95% CI: 97.1% to 100.0%) in those with AD (N = 127) and 99.4% (95% CI: 97.8% to 99.8%) in those without AD (333). The assay met analytical validity criteria for E4 isoform specificity, interference, precision, and stability.DISCUSSIONThe apoE E4 immunoassay demonstrated high concordance with PCR‐based genotyping and robust analytical validity, offering an accessible alternative for APOE ε4 zygosity assessment.Highlights A novel high‐throughput plasma‐based proteotyping immunoassay for APOE ε4 zygosity classification was developed and evaluated for clinical performance and analytical validity.The apoE E4 immunoassay demonstrated high concordance (99.6%) with PCR‐based APOE ε4 genotyping across a diverse international cohort, and a robust analytical profile.An apoE E4 immunoassay may offer a more cost‐effective and accessible alternative to DNA genotyping approaches currently used for AD risk evaluation and anti‐amyloid treatment decisions. Keywords: Alzheimer's disease, apoE E4, APOE ε4, apolipoprotein E, immunoassay

BACKGROUND

The apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 allele is the strongest genetic risk factor for Alzheimer's disease (AD). 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 The ε4 variant confers increased risk for AD and earlier onset of disease. 6 , 7 Heterozygous individuals (one ε4 allele) exhibit a three‐ to fivefold increased risk and homozygous individuals (two ε4 alleles) a nine‐ to 15‐fold increased risk for late‐onset AD. 5 , 6 , 8 , 9 APOE variants have implications for multiple aspects of brain biology and play a central role in AD pathogenesis. 6 Clinically, APOE ε4 allele status guides anti‐amyloid therapy decisions, as carriers face increased risk for amyloid‐related imaging abnormalities (ARIA) from cerebral edema or hemorrhage. 10 , 11 , 12 Despite this growing importance, current approaches to APOE ε4 assessment rely almost exclusively on DNA‐based genotyping. These molecular genotyping approaches, such as polymerase chain reaction (PCR) or mass spectrometry, demand specialized laboratory infrastructure and expertise, making them more costly and difficult to deploy compared to plasma‐based immunoassays. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17

To address these limitations, a scalable, high‐throughput apoE E4 proteotyping immunoassay could reduce reliance on complex DNA genotyping and improve testing access for individuals being considered for anti‐amyloid treatment, especially in primary care settings. 18 , 19 , 20 Plasma‐based biomarkers are increasingly used for AD diagnosis, reducing the need for cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) sampling or positron emission tomography (PET) imaging. 18 , 21 , 22 , 23 An apoE E4 immunoassay could enable integrated workflows (reflex testing or multi‐biomarker algorithms) using a single blood draw to assess both amyloid pathology and APOE ε4 status for treatment eligibility. Unlike DNA genotyping, an immunoassay approach measures apoE isoforms in plasma, reflecting both genetic determinants and downstream protein expression levels that directly contribute to AD pathogenesis. 24 The objective of this study was to evaluate the clinical performance and analytical validity of the research use only (RUO) apoE E4 immunoassay (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA) for APOE ε4 zygosity classification across diverse cohorts. 25

METHODS

Design and participants

Participants were pooled from three study cohorts to evaluate clinical performance: the Davos Alzheimer's Collaborative (Republic of Korea), 26 the Mayo Clinic (United States), 27 and the Montpellier Institute for Neurosciences (France). 28 The Davos cohort was composed of participants receiving care at the Catholic Brain Health Center at Yeouido St. Mary's Hospital, who completed a magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), PET, blood sampling, and detailed clinical/neuropsychological evaluation. The Mayo Clinic cohort consisted of individuals who received amyloid PET or CSF AD biomarker testing as part of their routine clinical evaluation. The Montpellier cohort included individuals receiving care at the Montpellier Neurological and Clinical Research Memory Center. Collectively, the pooled cohort included participants across the cognitive spectrum, from subjective cognitive decline and cognitively unimpaired individuals through mild cognitive impairment to dementia. Diagnoses included AD, frontotemporal lobar degeneration, Lewy body spectrum disorders, vascular dementia, and other neurological and psychiatric conditions. However, diagnostic composition varied across cohorts: Davos excluded non‐AD neurodegenerative disorders and significant comorbidities, Mayo consisted predominantly of AD cases, and Montpellier included the most diverse set of neurological conditions. More detailed descriptions of each cohort may be found in the Supporting Information (Cohort Descriptions). Analytical validity was assessed in a separate set of 10 plasma samples from cognitively unimpaired participants obtained from two biospecimen repositories (BioIVT 29 and Cantor BioConnect 30 ). Ethical review boards approved each of these source study protocols, under which participants provided written or oral consent.

ApoE E4 immunoassay

The apoE E4 chemiluminescent immunoassay is a multiplex of two 2‐step sandwich assays using paramagnetic particles coated with anti‐pan‐apoE (total of all apoE isoforms) monoclonal antibody (mAb) and complementary anti‐pan‐apoE mAb/alkaline phosphatase conjugate or anti‐apoE E4 mAb/alkaline phosphatase conjugate. Samples and reactants are incubated and washed before a chemiluminescent substrate is added. The light generated is processed through an algorithm that provides a ratio of apoE E4 relative light units (RLUs) to pan‐apoE (RLUs), reflecting apoE E4 and pan‐apoE protein levels. The apoE E4 RLUs/pan‐apoeE RLUs (apoE E4 ratio) is translated to an assessment of APOE ε4 zygosity based on a preliminary threshold fixed across all cohorts: ≤0.54 non‐carrier, 0.55 to 4.54 heterozygous, and ≥4.55 homozygous. ApoE E4 ratios and corresponding zygosity were determined for all participants using the RUO apoE E4 immunoassay provided by Beckman Coulter (Brea, CA, USA). 25

Clinical performance measurements and analyses

PCR‐based APOE genotyping served as the reference standard for immunoassay comparison in the clinical performance cohort. APOE genotyping was performed using standard PCR‐based methods that identify two key single‐nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) (rs429358 and rs7412) to determine ε2, ε3, and ε4 alleles, 31 enabling classification into ε4 zygosity groups (non‐carrier, heterozygote, homozygote). The genotyping protocols involved DNA extraction from blood samples followed by either TaqMan SNP assays (Davos) or PCR amplification with restriction enzyme digestion and gel electrophoresis analysis (Mayo, Montpellier). The specific methods for performing PCR for each cohort are described in the Supporting Information (Cohort Descriptions).

Participants’ demographics (age, gender), PCR‐based genotype, and diagnosis of AD (AD or non‐AD) were reported. Participants were classified with a diagnosis of AD based on an automated assessment of amyloid burden on PET (Davos), clinical evaluation with an amyloid biomarker measurement from PET or from CSF ptau181/Aβ42 (Mayo), or clinical evaluation with CSF Aβ42/Aβ40 (Montpellier). The precise methods for determining AD versus non‐AD status by cohort may be found in the Supporting Information (Cohort Descriptions). Concordance between PCR‐determined and immunoassay inferred APOE ε4 zygosity was computed with corresponding 95% confidence using the Wilson interval method. 32 Comparisons of continuous variables between groups were performed using the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test with Bonferroni correction.

Analytical validity measurements and analyses

The apoE E4 assay's analytical validity was established by evaluating E4 isoform specificity, interference, precision, and stability. Results were evaluated against analytical criteria designed to ensure consistent classification of APOE ε4 zygosity (non‐carrier, heterozygote, homozygote) despite analytical variations, with <20% deviation from controls applied, aligned with other novel AD biomarkers. 33

ApoE E4 assay specificity was confirmed by spiking a known E4‐negative K2 EDTA plasma sample with recombinant apoE E2 and E3 proteins at final concentrations of 0, 10, 25, or 50 µg/mL. Spiked samples and PBS controls were analyzed in duplicate, and apoE E4 signals were compared to the unspiked sample to assess cross‐reactivity.

Interference was evaluated by spiking apoE E4‐positive and negative plasma samples with potential endogenous interferents and common drugs per Clinical Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) EP07 guidelines. 34 Apoe E4 assay ratios were compared to equal volume or neat controls across interferents.

The precision of the apoE E4 immunoassay was evaluated per CLSI EP05‐A3 35 using two reagent lots on two analyzers. Six native K2 EDTA plasma samples with variable apoE E4 status were tested daily for 5 days (five replicates per run, two runs per day) to determine the within‐run, between‐run, and total coefficient of variation (CV) and standard deviation (SD).

Sample stability was evaluated under refrigerated (4°C), room temperature, and freeze/thaw conditions. Samples under refrigerated (4°C) and room temperature conditions were tested at 0, 1, 5, 7, and 13 days of incubation. Aliquots of samples under freeze/thaw conditions were frozen at −70°C, then thawed for 30 min at room temperature for up to five total freeze/thaw cycles. Statistical analyses for clinical performance and analytical validity were performed using Python version 3.8 and JMP version 16.1.0, respectively.

RESULTS

The apoE E4 immunoassay was evaluated for clinical performance on 460 participants (pooled cohort) and analytical validity across 10 plasma samples. Clinical performance cohort participants had a mean age of 74 (SD = 7.8), with 269 (58.5%) female and 127 (27.6%) diagnosed with AD (Table 1). Analytical validity was measured across 10 plasma samples, all from non‐AD participants.

Clinical performance

ApoE E4 immunoassay zygosity classification and PCR‐determined APOE genotypes for the clinical performance cohort are shown in Table 1. The cohort included 253 (55.0%) non‐carriers, 171 (37.2%) heterozygotes, and 36 (7.8%) homozygotes, with 219 (47.6%) ε3/ε3 and 160 (34.8%) ε3/ε4 genotypes being the most common.

The apoE E4 ratios ranged from 0.00 to 7.82 (Figure 1A) with clear differentiation between APOE ε4 non‐carriers (median 0.00 [interquartile range: 0.00 to 0.00]), heterozygotes (2.30 [2.04 to 2.58]), and homozygotes (6.14 [5.67 to 6.64]), all between‐group comparisons p < 0.001. This was driven by large differences in apoE E4 isoform RLUs (Figure 1B) (all between‐group comparisons p < 0.001). The pan‐apoE‐specific signal (Figure 1C) showed differences between non‐carriers versus both heterozygotes (p < 0.001) and homozygotes (p < 0.001), but no difference between heterozygotes versus homozygotes (p = 0.086).

The apoE E4 immunoassay demonstrated 99.6% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 98.4% to 99.9%) concordance with PCR genotype results across the pooled clinical performance cohort (Table 2). There was 100.0% (95% CI: 97.1 to 100.0%) concordance in the AD group and 99.4% (95% CI 97.8% to 99.8%) in the non‐AD group. The immunoassay demonstrated complete concordance for both the non‐carrier (100.0%; 95% CI: 98.5% to 100.0%) and homozygote (100.0%; 95% CI: 90.4% to 100.0%) subgroups and 98.8% (95.8% to 99.7%) for the heterozygote subgroup. A total of two discordant results occurred; both were PCR‐determined heterozygotes classified as non‐carriers by the apoE E4 immunoassay in the non‐AD group within the Davos cohort. Thus, cohort‐specific concordance was 99.3% (97.6% to 99.8%) for Davos, 100.0% (94.4% to 100.0%) for Mayo, and 100.0% (96.1% to 100.0%) for Montpellier.

ApoE E4 assay specificity was confirmed by comparing a known E4‐negative control sample to samples spiked with increasing concentrations of apoE E2 and E3 analytes. The apoE E4 assay ratio showed minimal differences (<3.0%) between control and spiked samples, well below the <20% criteria (Table 3), demonstrating E4 isoform specificity (Table S1).

Interference was evaluated by spiking an apoE E4‐negative and E4‐positive samples with potential interferents and common drugs and then comparing apoE E4 assay ratios to controls. Interferents showed low impact (≤20% difference criteria) on apoE E4 assay ratios (Table S2).

Assay precision (within‐run, between‐run, and total) was evaluated using six apoE E4 classified samples: two non‐carriers, three heterozygotes, and one homozygote (Figure 2). The CV and SD were low for non‐carrier E4‐negative samples (SD ≤ 0.05 criteria) and heterozygous or homozygous E4‐positive samples (CV ≤ 20% criteria), as seen in Figure 2 and Table S3.

DISCUSSION

The APOE ε4 genotype remains a critical risk factor for AD and is strongly associated with increased risk for ARIA in patients receiving anti‐amyloid therapies. 1 , 2 , 9 , 10 , 12 In this study, we evaluated an apoE E4 immunoassay for clinical performance and analytical validity, representing one of the first and most robust assessments of this assay type for use in clinical practice. 36 , 37 The assay demonstrated 99.6% concordance with PCR genotype results across a diverse cohort and showed a robust analytical profile. The observed concordance with APOE ε4 zygosity is consistent with recent evaluations of chemiluminescent apoE E4 immunoassays (98.2% to 100.0% concordance) 36 , 37 , 38 , 39 and turbidimetric immunoassays (98.1% to 100%) 40 , 41 and exceeds those reported for a nucleic acid‐linked immunoassay (95.7%). 37 A fully automated high‐throughput apoE E4 immunoassay has the potential to provide a cost‐effective, accessible alternative to DNA genotyping approaches for APOE ε4 zygosity classification. 17

DNA genotyping using PCR or mass spectrometry requires extensive laboratory infrastructure and expertise, presenting barriers to widespread clinical implementation. PCR genotyping necessitates dedicated multiroom laboratory facilities with a unidirectional workflow design to prevent contamination and specialized equipment such as thermal cyclers and gel electrophoresis systems. 13 Mass spectrometry demands even greater resources, requiring matrix‐assisted laser desorption/ionization time‐of‐flight platforms, robotics, and intensive climate‐controlled laboratory space. 14 , 15 , 16 Both methods need highly trained personnel with specialized expertise, creating substantial cost and adoption barriers for routine clinical laboratory implementation. Cost comparisons from non‐AD indications reflect these increased demands, suggesting PCR‐based genotyping costs two to five times more than immunoassay methods, with variability in this relationship based on platform type, existing infrastructure integration, and test volume. 17 , 42 , 43 , 44 , 45 , 46

Testing barriers combined with the complexity of delivering genetic information, including inconsistent insurance coverage and fragmented workflow, have created access disparities for lower socioeconomic, inadequately insured, and rural populations. 47 , 48 , 49 , 50 , 51 , 52 To address the large‐scale needs of APOE disclosure in AD clinical trials, specific programs have been established as alternatives to the traditional genetic counseling and disclosure procedures. 53 An apoE E4 immunoassay for AD risk assessment is more analogous to standard blood tests and generates limited gene‐related information (restricted to APOE ε4 zygosity classification only), which may support patient privacy and reduce genetic counseling requirements compared with comprehensive DNA‐based testing. However, results still carry genetic risk implications and may require targeted counseling in some clinical contexts. Overall, by decreasing laboratory costs and complexity, minimizing genetic counseling requirements and streamlining workflow (AD biomarker panel with apoE E4 from a single blood draw), an immunoassay approach has the potential to reduce access disparities for AD diagnosis.

Despite high immunoassay concordance with PCR, two discordant test results occurred. Discrepancies may reflect technical limitations of either platform; PCR can be affected by contamination, allelic dropout, or mispriming, while immunoassays may suffer from cross‐reactivity, matrix effects, or antibody affinity differences. Beyond technical errors, proteotype–genotype discrepancies may arise from rare mutations that silence APOE ε4 protein expression. 54 , 55 A recent report described a nonsense mutation in the APOE ε4 allele, which produced an individual who was genetically ε4‐positive (genotype) but had an E4‐negative proteotype 54 ; this is consistent with the two discordances observed in this study (heterozygote genotype, non‐carrier proteotype). Overall, APOE genotyping assesses two SNPs (rs429358 and rs7412) defining ε2, ε3, and ε4 alleles but does not capture protein expression differences. Proteotyping directly quantifies circulating apoE E4 protein, reflecting genetic and post‐genetic influences that may better approximate biological effect and disease risk. Unlike DNA‐based methods, an apoE E4 immunoassay requires no extraction or amplification, enabling rapid automated testing with minimal contamination risk. While APOE ε4 genotyping remains the gold standard for risk stratification, protein‐level measurement may offer complementary biological information relevant to cerebrovascular and metabolic pathways. The assay could inform treatment decisions, monitoring strategies, clinical trial enrichment, and research opportunities to understand how apoE E4 levels modulate AD progression and biomarker interactions.

Beyond AD diagnosis and treatment, apoE proteotyping for APOE ε4 zygosity classification has broad clinical utility. APOE ε4 status influences cognitive trajectories in Parkinson's disease, dementia with Lewy bodies, and chronic traumatic encephalopathy, serving as a stratification tool for prognosis and clinical trial enrollment. 56 , 57 , 58 , 59 , 60 Outside of neurodegenerative disease, APOE variants affect lipid metabolism, cardiovascular risk, cholesterol levels, atherosclerotic disease, and response to lipid‐lowering therapies. 61 , 62 , 63 APOE ε4 has also been implicated in cerebral amyloid angiopathy, stroke outcomes, infectious disease, and immune responses. 64 , 65 , 66 , 67 Thus, a scalable apoE E4 immunoassay could extend precision medicine applications beyond AD, supporting individualized care across neurology, cardiology, and population health domains.

This study had several limitations. First, participants and data for the clinical performance cohort were derived from broader dementia and AD clinical registries rather than a controlled prospective trial design. Additionally, some measurements lacked standardization across cohorts (e.g., PCR method, AD determination). For example, the Davos cohort determined AD solely through automated quantification of amyloid burden, while the Mayo and Montpellier cohorts relied on clinical evaluations combined with amyloid biomarker status. However, the pooling of these data yielded a larger and more diverse cohort for analysis and reflects real‐world variability in patient selection and AD assessment that mirrors the clinical settings where the apoE E4 immunoassay would be used. Next, because repeat testing was not performed on the discrepant samples, it is unknown whether discordance was due to technical errors or represented a true biological proteotype–genotype difference. Last, the samples tested for analytical validity were separate from the clinical performance cohort, but the scope and scale of testing were aligned with standard laboratory practice guidelines for establishing an immunoassay's analytical validity.

The apoE E4 immunoassay demonstrated high concordance (99.6%) with PCR‐based genotyping and robust analytical validity. An automated high‐throughput immunoassay for apoE E4 may offer a more cost‐effective and accessible alternative to APOE ε4 zygosity classification while maintaining reliable clinical performance.
