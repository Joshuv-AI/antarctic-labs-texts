BPAG1 promotes osteoclastogenesis and postmenopausal osteoporosis via activating ERK signaling pathway
Authors: Yufeng Zhang, Han Zheng, Li Yang, Yi Wang, Liang Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryOsteoclasts (OCs), as the primary cells responsible for bone resorption, play a crucial role in the development of osteoporosis. Yet, how OC differentiation is regulated and involved in the occurrence of OP remains unknown. Here, we notice that BPAG1 is closely related to OC differentiation and osteoporosis. The upregulation of BPAG1 promotes OC differentiation in vitro, whereas its downregulation inhibits this process. Research on the mechanism indicates that BPAG1 can induce OC differentiation via regulating the phosphorylation of ERK. Notably, inhibiting ERK impaired the increase in OC differentiation caused by BPAG1 overexpression. In addition, inhibiting BPAG1 reversed bone destruction in osteoporotic mice. Hence, our research revealed the mechanism by which BPAG1 regulates OC differentiation and bone resorption by activating ERK. Targeting BPAG1 is a potential strategy for treating osteoporosis.Subject areas: biological sciences, Physiology, molecular biology

Introduction

Osteoporosis (OP), a prevalent age-related skeletal disease, poses a major clinical challenge to the elderly population. Osteoporosis, characterized by reduced bone mass and compromised bone microarchitecture, leads to increased bone fragility and a heightened risk of fractures. Given the significant clinical implications of osteoporosis as a primary risk factor for fractures, it is crucial to focus on its prevention and treatment strategies. Osteoporotic fractures of the hip, spine, and forearm are closely related to limited mobility, physical deformity, chronic pain, and disability.1,2,3 Among them, osteoporotic fractures of the hip are highly destructive; 21%–30% of them will die within one year, accounting for 5% of the total etiological mortality rate of patients.4,5 With the increase in life expectancy, most women reach menopause at approximately two-thirds of their average life span. The cessation of ovarian function leads to a sharp drop in estrogen levels in vivo. The decrease in estrogen levels can expedite bone loss, given women’s inherently weaker bone structure. This process begins one year before menopause and lasts for three years. After that, the rate of bone mass loss slows down, yet bone mass loss continues in the following 4–8 years.6 Bone mineral density drops by an average of 10% during menopause, leading to greater bone loss in women, with 20% of bone mass lost within 5–7 years before and after menopause.7 In addition, approximately 25% of postmenopausal women are characterized by rapid bone loss, with a significant increase in the incidence of osteoporosis, as evidenced by the rate of bone loss and the importance of bone resorption markers. Therefore, it is urgent to explore the prevention and treatment strategies for osteoporosis.

Osteoclasts (OCs), which originate from hematopoietic stem cells, are the main functional cells responsible for bone resorption and are associated with monocytes and macrophages.8 The differentiation of bone marrow macrophages (BMMs) into OCs depends on the presence of nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) ligand receptor activator (RANKL) and macrophage colony stimulating factor (M-CSF).9 RANKL is highly expressed in osteoblasts, bone marrow stromal cells, T lymphocytes and B lymphocytes, and activates its receptor on OCs (RANK). RANKL binds to RANK, activates transcriptional regulatory factors, promotes the proliferation, differentiation, fusion, activation, and survival of OCs, thereby leading to bone resorption.10 The high expression level of RANKL in the early stage of menopause in women subsequently promotes osteoclastogenesis and accelerates bone resorption.11 In addition to menopause and sex hormone suppression, prostate cancer or breast cancer also leads to the activation of the RANKL/RANK pathway and excessive bone resorption. Moreover, various hormones and inflammatory cytokines also regulate OC differentiation via RANKL.12,13,14 Further, mutations in the bone marrow immune microenvironment and malignant bone diseases are also highly associated with RANKL, such as rheumatoid arthritis, periodontal disease, myeloma bone disease, and osteolytic bone metastasis.15,16,17,18 Despite the utilization of numerous anti-absorption medications for osteoporosis treatment, such as bisphosphonates, SERMs, and RANKL antibodies, their overall efficacy is often deemed unsatisfactory due to various off-target effects and adverse reactions.19,20,21 In recent years, OC-targeted drugs have opened up new avenues for the treatment of osteoporosis. However, the molecular mechanism of excessive bone resorption is not yet fully understood, which in turn limits the development of new drugs. Therefore, the exploration of the molecular mechanism of osteoclastogenesis can clarify the pathogenesis of osteoporosis and provide another perspective for the treatment of osteoporosis.

BPAG1 consists of 100 exons and is encoded by the dystonin gene (DST), which is a member of the spectraplakin family.22 BPAG1 is expressed in a variety of human cells including selected population of resident macrophages. BPAG1 has multiple biological functions, including ATPase activity23 and the formation of actin filaments.24,25,26 Meanwhile, BPAG1 also plays a crucial role in endoplasmic reticulum stress27 and cellular signal transduction.28 In addition, BPAG1 plays a significant role in the arrangement of the cytoskeleton and intracellular transport,29 which are crucial for OC differentiation. Little is known about its role in OC differentiation and bone absorption.

In this study, we found that BPAG1 was upregulated during osteoclastogenesis and was associated with osteoporosis. Further research has elucidated that BPAG1 enhances OC differentiation by modulating the ERK signaling pathway in vitro and in vivo. Our research reveals BPAG1/ERK axis in osteoclastogenesis and may provide theoretical basis and potential targets for the development of strategies for bone resorption diseases.

Results

BPAG1 is associated with osteoclastic differentiation and osteoporosis

To investigate the role of BPAG1 in the pathogenesis of osteoporosis, we analyzed the data from GSE56815 in the GEO database and found that BPAG1 was upregulated in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) of people with low bone mass (Figures 1A and 1B), suggesting that BPAG1 may promote bone mass loss. We then established a mouse model of ovariectomy osteoporosis and found that compared with the sham operation group, the BPAG1 mRNA in BMMs of the osteoporosis group was upregulated (Figure 1C). Moreover, immunohistochemistry also showed similar results: compared with the sham group, the ovariectomy group had more OCs and higher BPAG1 expression (Figures 1D and 1E).

Osteoclasts are the main bone resorption cells. To explore the potential role of BPAG1 in osteoclastic differentiation, we further analyzed the data of GSE54779 from the GEO database. It was found that BPAG1 mRNA was significantly upregulated in RANKL-induced BMMs (Figures 1F and 1G). Following the established protocol, we induced OC differentiation in RAW264.7 cells and BMMs using M-CSF and receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa-B ligand (RANKL), as detailed in previous studies (Figure S1). During this process, we observed an upregulation of BPAG1 mRNA in both cell types, indicating its potential role in OC differentiation (Figures 1H and 1I). Indeed, immunofluorescence also observed the upregulation of BPAG1 protein in BMMs during OC differentiation (Figures 1J and 1K). These results suggest that BPAG1 may promote osteoclastogenesis and bone loss. In addition, BPAG1 has multiple isomers, including BPAG1a expressed in neural tissue, BPAG1b expressed in muscle tissue, and BPAG1e expressed in epithelial tissue.30 To explore the expression of different isomers in BMM, quantitative reverse-transcription PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed, and the results showed that BPAG1-a was significantly expressed in BMM. The expression of BPAG1-b also showed significant differences. However, compared with the negative control group, its expression level was relatively low. BPAG1-e was almost not expressed, indicating that BPAG1-a is the main isomer expressed in BMM (Figure S2).

BPAG1 promotes OC differentiation in vitro

To verify the potential role of BPAG1 in osteoclastic differentiation, BPAG1 was knocked down via siRNA (Figure 2A; Figure S3A). Tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining indicated that down-regulation of BPAG1 inhibited the OC differentiation ability of BMMs (Figures 2B and 2C). Similarly, compared with si-nc-treated BMMs, si-BPAG1-treated BMMs showed downregulation of OC-specific genes. Including RANK, cell oncogene fos (c-Fos), dendritic cell-specific transmembrane protein, and cathepsin K (Figure 2D). The bone resorption assay showed BMMs with downregulated BPAG1 had lower ability of bone resorption (Figure S3B). Conversely, overexpression of BPAG1 (Figure 2E; Figure S3C) promoted OC differentiation and the expression of OC-specific genes (Figures 2F–2H; Figure S3D). In addition, both upregulation and downregulation of BPAG1 expression did not affect the proliferation or apoptosis of BMM (Figures 2I and 2J). The results suggested that BPAG1 promoted the OC differentiation of BMMs in mice.

BPAG1 regulates OC differentiation via ERK signaling pathway

To reveal the potential mechanism by which BPAG1 promotes OC differentiation, we knocked down BPAG1 in BMMs with siRNA (Figure S3E) and performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq). Principal-component analysis (PCA) was performed to analyze two groups. The results showed significant differences between the control group and the knockdown group, defining different transcriptome profile (Figure 3A). The heatmap and volcano map indicated that the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the control group and the si-BPAG1 group were significantly isolated, with 317 genes upregulated and 477 genes downregulated (Figures 3B–3D). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis revealed that genes related to “cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction,” “osteoclast differentiation,” “PI3K-Akt signaling pathway,” “HIF-1 signaling pathway,” and “rheumatoid arthritis” were significantly enriched (Figure 3D). The HIF-1 signaling pathway and the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway are both directly implicated in OC differentiation, as evidenced by their roles in bone cell metabolism and the maintenance of bone homeostasis (Figures 3E and 3F). The gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of these DEGs (Figure 3G) also indicated that ERK1 and ERK2 cascade, regulation of I-kappaB kinase/NF-κB signaling, negative regulation of MAPK cascade, OC differentiation, and bone resorption were closely related to the downregulation of BPAG1. Furthermore, the results demonstrated that downregulation of BPAG1 could inhibit multiple inflammatory signaling pathways such as ERK1 and ERK2 cascade, regulate the MAPK cascade, OC differentiation, and bone resorption (Figure 3H). The enrichment of OC-related genes in vivo was further analyzed through GOChord. The enriched genes were classified as “osteoclast differentiation,” “negative regulation of MAPK cascade,” “ERK1 and ERK2 cascade,” “bone resorption,” and “regulation of I-kappaB kinase/NF-κB signaling” (Figure 3I). Among them, the ERK signaling pathway plays a significant role in the differentiation process of OCs. Therefore, we speculate that BPAG1 may regulate OC differentiation by influencing the activation of ERK. Taken together, these results suggested that BPAG1 may promotes osteoclastogenesis by regulating the activation of ERK.

BPAG1 regulates OC differentiation by activating ERK

To further explore the regulation of ERK activation by BPAG1, western blot was performed. Indeed, downregulation of BPAG1 inhibited the phosphorylation of ERK (Figures 4A and 4B; Figure S3G), while upregulation of BPAG1 promoted the phosphorylation of ERK in BMMs (Figures 4C and 4D; Figure S3H). These results indicate that BPAG1 regulates the activation of ERK during OC differentiation. To investigate the role of ERK in BPAG1-induced OC differentiation, we inhibited ERK activation in cells overexpressing BPAG1. Inhibition of ERK activation eliminated the enhancement of osteoclastogenesis induced by BPAG1 upregulation, which could be demonstrated by the reduction in TRAP+ cells and expression of OC-specific gene (Figures 4E–4G). These results suggest that BPAG1 promotes OC differentiation by regulating the phosphorylation of ERK.

Inhibiting BPAG1 alleviates bone loss caused by estrogen deficiency osteoporosis

Through the analysis of Micro-CT, Ovariectomy (OVX) mice with downregulated BPAG1 had higher had higher bone mass and Tb.N, and lower Tb.Pf than OVX mice that were administered the si-NC (Figures 5B and 5C). These results suggest that inhibiting BPAG1 can salvage bone loss in estrogen deficiency osteoporosis.

Similarly, the bone mass and Tb.N of OVX mice that inhibited BPAG1 did not significantly decrease compared with the sham operation group, as shown by H&E staining (Figures 5D and 5E). For OVX mice, the number of TRAP-positive cells in the bone marrow cavity was higher than that in the sham group, while there was no significant change between the BPAG1-inhibited OVX mice and the sham group (Figures 5F and 5G). Furthermore, immunohistochemical and western blotting analysis confirmed a reduction in BPAG1 expression in the BPAG1 inhibition group, and the phosphorylation of ERK was inhibited (Figures 5H and 5I; Figures S3I and S3J). These results suggest that BPAG1 may play an important role in the treatment of OVX-induced osteoporosis.

Discussion

Osteoporosis, a prevalent bone disease worldwide, is characterized by reduced bone mass, compromised microarchitecture of bone tissue, and diminished bone strength. Type I osteoporosis, also known as postmenopausal osteoporosis, is characterized by excessive absorption of trabeculae, mainly due to a deficiency of estrogen. For many years, bisphosphonates and other medications have been the cornerstone of osteoporosis treatment, with bisphosphonates being the most commonly prescribed. As osteoporosis is a chronic disease, anti-fracture treatment can last for the rest of a patient’s life. Many anti-absorption drugs lose their activity rapidly after discontinuation, such as denosumab, estrogen, raloxifen, and calcitonin.31 Nitrogen-containing (amino) bisphosphonates (BPs) damage OC function by inhibiting Farnik pyrophosphate synthase, thereby preventing excessive bone resorption.32,33 Existing drugs have off-target effects and potential side effects,19,20 including mandibular osteonecrosis, cardiovascular events and potential infection risks.21,34 Teriparatide, as a therapeutic drug for bone anabolic metabolism, is characterized by its ability to stimulate bone formation and improve the microstructure of cortical and trabecular bones. It affects calcium metabolism by binding to the parathyroid hormone type 1 receptor. In contrast to continuous stimulation, transient stimulation by parathyroid hormone type 1 receptors promotes bone formation, but both types of stimulation may lead to hypercalcemia.35 Our research established a mouse model of estrogen-deficient osteoporosis and found that BPAG1 may be a potential target for treating osteoporosis caused by excessive activation of OCs. Suppression of BPAG1 can impair osteoclastogenesis and address abnormal bone resorption. Micro CT analysis indicated that osteoporotic mice treated with BPAG1 inhibition exhibited increased bone mass and trabecular bone count, surpassing those of the control group and closely matching the levels observed in the sham operation group. Furthermore, pathological analysis revealed that the number of OCs in BPAG1-inhibited osteoporotic mice was relatively low, indicating that BPAG1 can prevent bone loss by damaging OC differentiation. Given the potential side effects associated with osteoporosis treatments, BPAG1 emerges as a promising candidate for future therapeutic strategies against osteoporosis.

Osteoclasts are the main functional cells of bone resorption, which regulated by RNAKL and M-CSF, and eventually adsorb on the bone surface to exert the function of bone resorption. The study of bone remodeling mechanisms has led to the creation of therapies that combat osteoporosis by targeting OCs, such as bisphosphonates, SERMs, and monoclonal antibodies, which effectively reduce bone resorption and the occurrence of osteoporotic fractures. Here, we demonstrate that BPAG1 can induce the occurrence of estrogen deficiency osteoporosis by promoting OC differentiation. It is downregulated in the early stage of OC development, which is regarded as a key stage of OC differentiation.36 Through RNA-seq and subsequent experiments, we found that BPAG1 affects osteoclastogenesis by activating ERK, and identified a BPAG1/ERK axis. Furthermore, we found that inhibiting BPAG1 can treat osteoporosis in mice and improve bone mass loss, indicating that BPAG1 is an effective method for treating postmenopausal osteoporosis.

BPAG1 is expressed in various human cells and regulates ATPase activity23 and the formation of actin filaments.24,25,26 Our RNA-seq and subsequent experiments demonstrated that BPAG1 affects osteoclastogenesis by regulating the phosphorylation of ERK. ERK is a key signaling protein in the MAPK signaling pathway, which can promote OC differentiation and the expression of related genes, and is regulated by multiple transcription factors and signaling molecules.37,38 ERK modulators such as naringin and mangiferin can prevent OC differentiation by inhibiting RANKL-induced activation of NF-κB and ERK.39,40 Nanoscaffolds synthesized based on naringin can also be used for osteoporotic bone healing.41,42 Indeed, inhibiting the activation of ERK can significantly reverse the increase in OC differentiation in BMMs caused by overexpression of BPAG1, indicating that the regulation of OC formation by BPAG1 depends on the activation of the ERK signaling pathway. Furthermore, transcriptome analysis revealed that down-regulation of BPAG1 could induce multiple signaling pathways, including JAK-STAT, HIF-1, and PPAR pathways. These signaling pathways play a very important role in the differentiation process of OCs.43,44 In addition, previous studies have shown that BPAG1 has at least three main forms: BPAG1-e (expressed in epithelial tissue), BPAG1-a (expressed in neural tissue), and BPAG1-b (expressed in muscle tissue).30 Among these isomers, transcriptional variant 1 (BPAG1) represents the longest transcript and encodes the longest subtype. Compared with variant 1, variant 2 (BPAG1-b) is different on the 5′ UTR, lacking a part of the 5′ encoding region and initiating translation at the alternative start codon. This variant also lacks two alternating in-frame and out-of-frame exons in the encoding region. Variant 3 (BPAG1-a) is different on the 5′ UTR, lacking a part of the 5′ encoding region and initiating translation at the alternative start codon. This variant also lacks seven alternating in-frame and out-of-frame exons in the encoding region. Variant 4 (BPAG1-e) has multiple differences in the encoding area and UTR, featuring distinct N-ends and C-ends, and is significantly shorter than variant 1. Our experiments demonstrated that BPAG1-a is significantly expressed in BMM, the expression level of BPAG1-b is relatively low, and BPAG1-e is almost not expressed, indicating that BPAG1-a is the main isomer expressed in BMM. Among them, the expression level of BPAG1-a (variant 3) was close to that of BPAG1 (variant 1, the longest transcript). Therefore, we mainly focus on the role of BPAG1 (variant 1) in OC differentiation.

Bone mass and trabeculae are important factors for bone strength. Osteoclasts, as the main cells for bone resorption, are related to the quantity and thickness of trabeculae. According to our research, BPAG1 controls OC differentiation through the ERK signaling pathway, which is a signaling pathway that may contribute to the development of other therapies for osteoporosis caused by estrogen deficiency.

Limitations of the study

This study reveals the mechanism by which BPAG1 regulates OC differentiation and bone resorption through ERK. Targeting BPAG1 is a potential strategy for treating osteoporosis. Although we discussed the inhibitory effect of BPAG1 on OC differentiation, the impact of BPAG1 on bone development has not yet been studied. Considering the lethality of knockout of BPAG1, it is necessary to use BPAG1-specific knockout mice in future research. Furthermore, due to the presence of multiple isomers of BPAG1, the role of different isomers in osteoclastic differentiation remains to be further studied.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell lines

The mouse macrophage cell line RAW264.7 (ATCC, Cat#TIB-71) and the human kidney epithelial cell line 293T (ATCC, Cat#CRL-3216) were tested for mycoplasma contamination. They were cultured in DMEM containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37°C in an atmosphere of 5% CO2.

BMMs were isolated from 4-week-old male C57BL/6J mice. In brief, the femurs were separated under sterile conditions and the marrow cavity was rinsed with α-MEM (HyClone, Cat# SH30265.01B) containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Cell suspensions were cultured overnight in complete α-MEM at 37°C and 5% CO2. The supernatant was transferred to a new cell culture flask, M-CSF was added to a final concentration of 50 ng/mL, and the cells were cultured for another 3 days.

Animals

All animal experimental procedures were in accordance with protocols approved by Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Wuhan University (WP20240814). Since this study focused on postmenopausal osteoporosis models, female mice were used as experimental animals. Four-week-old female C57BL/6J mice purchased from Si Beifu Biotechnology Co., LTD (Beijing, China).

Method details

Bioinformatics analysis

To explore the potential correlation between BPAG1 and OC-related osteoporosis, GSE54779 from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) repository at the National Center of Biotechnology Information (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) were analyzed by GPL6246 Affymetrix mouse gene 1.0 ST array. This dataset contains three samples of bone marrow mononuclear cells (BMMs) treated with RANKL and M-CSF and three samples of BMM treated with M-CSF alone. Moreover, GSE56815 dataset from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) repository at the National Center of Biotechnology Information (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) were analyzed through GPL96 [HG-U133A] Affymetrix Human Genome U133A Array. This dataset contains 40 samples of BMM from high-bone mass women and 40 samples of BMM from low-bone mass women. All the original data were standardized using the R and Bioconductor (http://www.bioconductor.org) Affymetrix software packages. The DEGs in BMM samples were identified using the Linear array microarray analysis (Limma) software package. V values p < 0.05 and |log2FC| >0.5 were regarded as the thresholds for identifying DEGs.

Cell culture and osteoclast differentiation

The mouse monocyte cell line RAW264.7 (ATCC, Cat# TIB-71) and human kidney epithelial cell line 293T (ATCC, Cat#CRL-3216) were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere.

Isolation, culture and differentiation of bone marrow mononuclear cells (bone marrow macrophages)

As previously mentioned,45 BMM was isolated from 4-week-old male C57BL/6J mice. In short, the femur was separated under aseptic conditions, and the bone marrow cavity was rinsed with α-MEM containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. After the cell suspension was cultured overnight, the supernatant was transferred to a new cell culture flask, M-CSF was added to the final concentration of 50 ng/mL, and then cultured for another 3 days.

For OC differentiation, cells were treated for 6 days with complete α-MEM supplemented with 30 ng/mL M-CSF (R&D Systems, Cat# 416-ML-010) and 50 ng/mL RANKL (R&D Systems, Cat#462-TEC-010) to induce differentiation. The culture medium was refreshed every other day. The drug experiment used 3 nM ERK1/2 inhibitor 1 (MCE, Cat #HY-112287) to inhibit ERK. Osteoclasts induced for 6 days were fixed at room temperature with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, soaked in PBS containing 0.5% Triton X- for 30 min, and stained in TRAP solution (Servicebio, Cat. #G1050). Count cells with two or more nuclei. Statistics are based on the area of OC in the unit field of view. For the bone resorption assay, BMMs were inoculated on a bone resorption assay plate (Cosmo Bio, Cat#CSR-BRA-48P), M-CSF was added to a final concentration of 50 ng/mL, and the cells were cultured for another 1 day. Then, cells were cultured in complete α-MEM containing 50 ng/mL M-CSF and 50 ng/mL RANKL for 6 days. The cells were cleared by soaking in 5% sodium hypochlorite for 3 min. Images were obtained using an inverted microscope system (Olympus, IX73).

Knockdown and overexpression of BPAG1

To knockdown BPAG1, siRNA was transfected for 24 h using the riboFECT CP transfection kit (RiboBio, Cat #R10035.7). The BPAG1 overexpression plasmid was constructed. The plasmids PSPAX2 and PMD2G, along with the overexpression plasmid, were co-transfected into 293T cells to construct lentiviral vectors.46 The supernatant was collected at 48 and 72 h and concentrated (Millipore,Ultracel-100 regenerated cellulose membrane, 15 mL sample volume,Cat# UFC9100) for lentivirus transfection. Lentivirus (MOI = 100) infected BMM cells with 10 μg/ml polystyrene for 24 h. Incubate with 2 μg/ml puromycin for 72 h. PCR was used to detect the knockdown or overexpression level of BPAG1.

Cellular immunofluorescent staining

BMMs were cultured in complete α-MEM containing 30 ng/mL M-CSF and 50 ng/mL RANKL for 0, 2, 4 or 6 days, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, permeated with PBS containing 0.5% Trion-x for 30 min, and DAPI staining was performed. For anti-BPAG1 immunofluorescence staining, cells were fixed and permeated, then blocked with PBS containing 5% BSA. The cells were incubated overnight with the BPAG1 primary antibody (Affinity, Cat# DF6752, 1:200) at 4°C. Then incubate with the secondary antibody for 1 h. Images were captured using a Leica (SP8) confocal microscope and analyzed using LAS X.

RNA extraction and quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cells via TRIzol (Thermo Fisher, Cat#15596026). 200 ng of total RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA via reverse transcriptase kit (Vazyme, Cat#R223). Quantitative PCR was performed by SYBR green mixture (Vazyme, Cat#Q311) and Monad Real-Time PCR instrument (Monad q225).

The primer sequences were as follows:

NFATc1: Forward(F):5′-TATATGAGCCCATCCTTGCCT-3′/Reverse(R):5′-GGCTGCCTTCCGTCTCATAG-3′

RANK: F:5′-CTCCTTGGAAAGCTAGAAGCAC-3′/R:5′-TTCCCTCCCTTCCTGTAGTAAAC-3′

Western blotting

BMMs were cultured in 6-well plates through complete α-MEM medium containing 30 ng/mL M-CSF. Collect cells and extract proteins from by immunoprecipitation lysates (Servicebio, Cat. #G2038) containing protease inhibitor cocktail (MCE, Cat. # HY-K0010, 1:100) and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail I (MCE, Cat. # HY-K0021, 1:100). After standing on ice for 30 min, centrifuge at 13,000 rpm for 15 min to separate the supernatant. Heat at 95°C for 5 min for Western blotting. As mentioned,47 25ug denatured total protein was separated with 10% SDS-PAGE, transferred into the membrane, blocked with 5% milk, and incubated overnight with the primary antibody. The membrane was rinsed three times with TBST for 5 min each time at second day, and then incubated with the specific secondary antibody for 1 h. Tanon-5200 (Tanon, 18000856) and Image-Pro Plus 6.0 were used for the detection and analysis of immunoreactive proteins. Normalize the protein levels to GAPDH or total protein levels.

The proliferation and apoptosis assay

The cytotoxicity of Si-NC or Si-BPAG1 against BMMs was assessed using the MTT assay, a common method for evaluating the viability of cells in response to test substances. Knockdown BPAG1 or control group was inoculated in 96-well plates and incubated with 30 ng/mL M-CSF for 3 days. After that, add 10 μL of MTT buffer to each well. Incubate for another 4 h at 37°C. The absorbance at 492 nm was detected by using the PE Enspire multifunctional microplate reader.

To detect apoptosis, BMM was transfected with Si-BPAG1 or Si-NC respectively and incubated with 30 ng/mL M-CSF for 3 days. The apoptosis of BMM was detected by live and dead cell staining. Calcein-AM (live cells, green) and PI (dead cells, red) incubated the cells for 30 min. Imaging was performed using a fluorescence microscope.

Animal experiments

For the osteoporosis model, as mentioned earlier,48 8-week-old female C57BL/J mice were anesthetized with 1–4% isoflurane to expose the dorsal skin. Subsequently, a 0.5 cm incision was made along the midline of the lower dorsal skin, and the underlying connective tissue was carefully dissected. The ovaries were located beneath the thin muscular layer, and small incisions were made on each side to enter the abdominal cavity. Expose the fallopian tubes and ovaries, determine and place the ovaries back into the abdominal cavity. For sham DEGslopian tubes, gently cut the fallopian tubes with small sterile scissors, remove the ovaries, and place the remaining fallopian tubes back into the abdominal cavity, suturing layer by layer. For the siRNA injection experiment, in vivo si-BPAG1 or si-NC was injected into the femoral bone marrow cavity at a dose of 2 mg/kg each time at the 3rd week after the operation, once every 4 days for 7 consecutive injections.49

All animal researchs procedures were performed in accordance with the protocol approved by the Wuhan University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee.

Micro-CT analysis

All mice were euthanized with CO2, the femurs were separated and evaluated by the SkyScan 1176 high-resolution micro-CT imaging system (Bruker). Set at an isometric resolution of 7 μm, the aluminum filter was set at 0.25 mm, and X-ray energy settings of 55 kV and 200 μA were used for analysis according to the manufacturer’s instructions. NRecon (Bruker) was used to reconstruct the image and CTAn (Bruker) was used for quantitative analysis. CTVol software is used (Bruker Micro-CT) to adjust the 3D model.

Histological analysis

For paraffin sections, the femurs were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h, decalcified with 10% EDTA for 28 days, and then embedded in paraffin. The tissue was sectioned (8 μm) by Leica RM2235 slicer, dewaxed, and subjected to TRAP (Servicebio, Cat. #G1050) and H&E.

Immunohistochemistry analysis

For immunohistochemistry, the sections were dewaxed, treated with citric acid buffer (pH 6.0) three times at 95°C, treated with 3% H2O2 for 20 min at room temperature, blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h at room temperature, incubated overnight with the primary antibody at 4°C, and the samples were washed. The samples were incubated with secondary antibodies using the Polink-2 Plus polymer HRP detection system (ZSGB-BIO, PV6001). DAB (ZSGB-BIO, ZLI-9017) was used for color development. Hematoxylin was used for nuclear staining. After dehydration and fixation, the sections were scanned with an Aperio VERSA 8 scanner.

Transcriptomic analysis

BMMs were isolated from 4-weeks-old mice and cultured with 50 ng/mL M-CSF for 3 days, BPAG1 of BMMs was knocked down by siRNA, and total RNA was extracted using the GenCatch TM extraction kit (Epoch Life Sciences, Cat#1660050). According to the manufacturer’s instructions (Hiseq 2500, Illumina), cDNA libraries were prepared, and sequenced with 2 μg RNA. HISAT250 was used to locate sequencing reads to the mouse genome, and SAMtools51 was used to summarize tag counts at the gene level, allowing only one read per length at each position. Differentially expressed genes were determined by DESeq2.52 The original read count was normalized, and the statistical model was calculated using the probability of hypothesis testing (p-value). Finally, multiple hypothesis tests were corrected to obtain the FDR value. Gene set enrichment analysis was conducted by ClusterProfiler,53 including GO and KEGG. The enrichment analysis was based on the principle of hypergeometric distribution. Among them, the differential gene set was the differential gene set obtained through significant difference analysis and annotated into the GO or KEGG database. The background gene set was all genes obtained through significant difference analysis. It was annotated into the GO or KEGG database. The enrichment analysis results revealed that all differential gene sets in each comparison combination were enriched, with some upregulated and others downregulated. GOplot36 was used for the visualization of GO analysis. The RNA-seq data in this study is available in SRA and the BioProject accession number is PRJNA1295926.

Statistical analysis

Data were expressed as mean ± SD. The results were considered statistically significant if the p value was less than 0.05. The random number method was used for random assignment. Researchers blinded to group assignment during the experiment. The data were analyzed using the appropriate Student’s t test when comparing two groups or one-way analysis of variance by Tukey’s post hoc test when comparing more than 2 groups. All statistical tests were performed using Prism 7.0 software.
