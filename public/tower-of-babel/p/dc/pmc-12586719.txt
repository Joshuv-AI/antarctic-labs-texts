Heterologous sequential immunization using chimeric mRNA and protein vaccines with HA-stem and S-RBD enhanced protective mucosal immunity against influenza and COVID-19
Authors: Yulei Li, Xi Wang, Xi Zeng, Baoli Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Influenza virus and severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), as two highly contagious respiratory pathogens, continue to pose critical challenges to global public health. In our previous research, we successfully developed mRNA and protein vaccines by integrating the HA stem of H1N1 influenza virus with the RBD from SARS-CoV-2, which demonstrated broad protective efficacy against multiple strains of both viruses in mouse models. Here, we compared the immunogenicity and protective efficacy of heterologous sequential immunization regimens based on this chimeric antigen design, employing mRNA vaccine priming followed by intranasal protein vaccine boosting. Our results show that an mRNA vaccine prime followed by an intranasally administered protein vaccine with PICKCa adjuvant boost not only induces robust systemic humoral immunity but also significantly enhances respiratory mucosal immunity. Compared to boosting with an adjuvant-free intranasal protein vaccine, the inclusion of PICKCa adjuvant markedly elevated mucosal IgA levels in both nasal washes and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF). Furthermore, the adjuvanted intranasal protein vaccine boost regimen provided optimal protection against high-dose lethal influenza virus challenge. These findings provide valuable insights for refining immunization strategies to enhance the efficacy of combined influenza-COVID-19 vaccines.

Introduction

Influenza viruses and SARS-CoV-2 are both highly transmissible respiratory pathogens. Seasonal influenza causes millions of severe cases and hundreds of thousands of deaths annually, while the COVID-19 pandemic has resulted in over 7 million deaths1,2. Although existing vaccine strategies have achieved some success in controlling these diseases, they primarily focus on inducing systemic immune responses with insufficient attention given to mucosal immunity3. Mucosal surfaces serve as the primary portals for pathogen entry, making it particularly important to enhance local mucosal immunity for the prevention of respiratory infections4,5. However, current vaccine designs often fail to adequately activate this critical defense mechanism, limiting their overall protective efficacy. Furthermore, seasonal variations and the continuous emergence of new variants pose additional challenges to the effectiveness and durability of existing vaccines6,7.

Heterologous prime-boost vaccination involves the sequential administration of different types of vaccine platforms to optimize immune responses8. This approach can combine the advantages of mRNA vaccines, such as efficient antigen expression and potent cellular immune activation, with the ability of protein vaccines to more accurately mimic the structural complexity of antigens during natural infection, thereby achieving more comprehensive immune protection9. Notably, heterologous prime-boost vaccination has shown great potential in inducing mucosal immunity, promoting the production of IgA antibodies, which are crucial for defending against pathogens transmitted through the respiratory tract10–12. PICKCa is a complex of poly IC-kanamycin-Cacl2. It is the ligand of pattern recognition receptors of TLR3, NOD and RIG-1. Numerous studies have shown that PICKCa adjuvant can significantly enhance vaccine immunogenicity13,14. However, its specific role and effectiveness in the context of heterologous prime-boost regimens remain insufficiently characterized and warrant further investigation.

In our preliminary studies, we conceptualized a chimeric protein subunit vaccine and mRNA vaccine targeting both influenza virus and SARS-CoV-215,16. This design integrates the hemagglutinin (HA) stem of the H1N1 influenza virus with the receptor-binding domain (RBD) from the SARS-CoV-2 Delta variant, forming a stable trimeric structure that can be recognized by neutralizing antibodies. The potential of this approach was demonstrated by providing protection against a range of strains in mouse models. Here, we explore the immunogenicity and protective efficacy of the heterologous sequential immunization protocol using mRNA vaccines and protein vaccines containing HA-stem and S-RBD components. We evaluate in detail the effect of this strategy in increasing systemic and respiratory mucosal antibody levels and its protection against viral challenges, demonstrating that heterologous sequential mRNA vaccine priming followed by intranasal protein vaccine with PICKCa adjuvant boosting immunization significantly enhances mucosal immunity and protective efficacy against both influenza and SARS-CoV-2. This study contributes to addressing the deficiencies in the immune efficacy of existing vaccines, especially in strengthening mucosal immunity, thereby enhancing the overall immune protection level of the population.

Materials and methods

Cells and viruses

Expi293F cells (ATCC), HEK293T cells (ATCC) and Madin–Darby Canine Kidney (MDCK) cells (ATCC) were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2, using Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with1% penicillin–streptomycin and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS).

A/Brisbane/02/2018 (H1N1) wild-type virus (GISAID: EPI_ISL_522424) with 99.7% sequence similarity to A/Victoria/2570/2019 (H1N1) (GISAID: EPI_ISL_417210) in HA stem region was used as the challenge virus. The influenza virus strain was propagated using 9-day-old specific pathogen-free (SPF) embryonated chicken eggs and stored at − 80 °C.

Design, synthesis and preparation of nucleoside-modified mRNA vaccines

Nucleoside modified mRNA vaccines were designed and synthesized as previously described15,17. Briefly, we synthesized the target gene (Genscript, China) and cloned it into the pUC57 vector under the control of the T7 promoter. The plasmid was then linearized by restriction enzyme digestion and used as a template for in vitro transcription with a T7 RNA polymerase kit (Novoprotein, Shanghai, China; E131). In addition, a modified nucleoside-containing mRNA was generated by replacing UTP with 1-methylpseudouridine 5 ‘-triphosphate. The mRNA purified by LiCl precipitation was analyzed using agarose gel electrophoresis, and then stored at − 80 °C until use.

We encapsulated mRNA into lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) through a self-assembly process. The mRNA was dissolved in citrate buffer (50 mM, pH 4.0). The four lipids were dissolved in anhydrous ethanol at molar ratios of 50:10:38.5:1.5 for ionizable lipid, DSPC, cholesterol, and PEG-lipid. Then, the ethanol phase and the water phase were mixed in a microfluidic device at a volume ratio of 1:3. Finally, mRNA-LNP formulations were sterilized with a 0.22 μm filter and stored at 4 °C until use.

Protein expression and purification

Protein subunit vaccines were expressed, purified, and prepared as previously described16. Briefly, the construct was coded optimized, synthesized and cloned into the pCAGGS vector, and transfected into Expi293F cells. The target protein underwent a series of purification steps, and its size and purity were analyzed by SDS-PAGE and analytical gel filtration analyses.

Animals

SPF female BALB/c mice (six to eight weeks old) were purchased from Vital River Laboratories (Beijing, China). All mice were provided with free access to water and standard feed, and were maintained under SPF conditions in at the animal facility of the Institute of Microbiology, Chinese Academy of Sciences (IMCAS). The experimental protocol was reviewed and approved by the Committee on the Ethics of Animal Experiments of IMCAS with the approval number, APIMCAS2022141. All experiments were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. All methods are reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines.

Mouse vaccination and challenge experiments

Eight mice in each group were initially immunized intramuscularly (IM) with 10 µg/100 µL of mRNA-LNP or sham (control). Two weeks later, boosted were given as follows: the mRNA group received the mRNA vaccine (10 µg/100 µL) via the IM route; The IN- PA group received 10 µg in 100 µL of protein subunit vaccine, formulated with PICKCa adjuvant via the IN route; the IN group received 10 µg in 100 µL of protein subunit vaccine without adjuvant via the IN route; The IM- PA group administered 10 µg in 100 µL of protein subunit vaccine, formulated with PICKCa adjuvant via the IM route; the IM group received 10 µg in 100 µL of protein subunit vaccine without adjuvant via the IM route.

Three weeks after booster vaccination, the mice were intranasally inoculated with 50 µL of Influenza A/Brisbane/02/2018 (H1N1) virus containing 20 × the 50% mouse lethal dose (mLD50). To analyze viral load, three mice were randomly selected and euthanized via cervical dislocation after being sedated with isoflurane on day 3 post-challenge, and the left lung lobes were collected for viral titer analysis. The right lung lobes were harvested for histopathological analysis. In addition, the remaining mice were monitored for a 14-day period to record body weight changes and survival rates. Any mice that experienced a reduction in their initial body weight by ≥ 20% were humanely euthanized and defined as dead.

Viral titer analysis

To measure viral load in lung homogenates, the 50% tissue culture infectious dose (TCID50) assay using MDCK cells. In brief, MDCK cells were seeded at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well into 96-well plates and incubated overnight. The cells were washed with PBS and then incubated for 48 h at 37 °C with serial dilutions of the lung homogenates in quadruplicate in DMEM supplemented with 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 µg/mL streptomycin, and 2 µg/mL of l-1-tosylamido-2-phenylethyl chloromethyl ketone-treated trypsin. The TCID50 values were calculated using the Reed-Muench method.

ELISA analysis of antibody titers

Mouse nasal washes and BALF were collected with 200 µL and 1 mL of PBS respectively. The mucosal washes were centrifuged at 500 g for 5 min, and the resulting supernatant was stored at − 20 °C until analysis. Influenza virus HA- and SARS-CoV-2 RBD-specific antibodies were quantified using ELISA. Briefly, ELISA plates were coated with recombinant proteins of interest (HA of A/Victoria/2570/2019 (H1N1) or RBD of Delta variant, 2 µg/mL) overnight at 4 °C. The plates were subjected to a 1 h blocking step using blocking buffer, followed by washing and incubation with serially diluted mouse sera, nasal wash or BALF for 1 h at 37 °C. Plates were then washed and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C with horseradish peroxidase (HRP) conjugated anti-mouse IgG (1:5000) or IgA (1:3000) antibodies (Abcam). Following incubation, plates were washed again and developed with tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrates (Tiangen, China) for 10 min. The reaction was then stopped by adding100 µL of 2 M H2S04, and optical densities (OD) were measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (PerkinElmer, USA).

Histopathology analysis

For histopathology, Mouse lung tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution for duration of 48 h to ensure proper fixation. Subsequently, thin paraffin slides, approximately 3 ~ 4 μm in thickness, were prepared from the samples. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was applied to these slides to facilitate the identification of histopathological changes in the tissues. A pulmonary pathologist reviewed the slides blinded and characterized immune cell infiltration and other related pathology. The characteristics of lung scores 1 to 4 in mice are as follows: score 1: Mild patchy mononuclear infiltration, both in the parenchyma and perivascular regions, with variable reactive pulmonary cells and interstitial changes; score 2: Moderate patchy mononuclear infiltration, both in the parenchyma and perivascular regions, with variable reactive pulmonary cells and interstitial changes; score 3: Mild, dense mixed infiltrates including mononuclear cells, granulocytes, and neutrophils; score 4: Moderate, dense mixed infiltrates including mononuclear cells, granulocytes, and neutrophils.

Statistical analysis

All data plotted with error bars are shown as mean ± SEM. The p values were generated by analysis data with one-way ANOVA or two-way ANOVA with a Bonferroni’s multiple comparisons test using the Prism 9 program (GraphPad Software), and p < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant. The details can be found in the figure legend.

Results

IN-PA boosting immunization induces robust and comprehensive systemic and respiratory humoral immunity

To evaluate whether different immunization strategies induce differences in systemic and respiratory tract local humoral immunity, we initially primed the immune response with an mRNA vaccine (Fig. 1A). Two weeks later, we administered boosting immunization using different vaccines or through various immunization routes. Three weeks post-booster, mouse serum, nasal washes, and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) were collected to detect specific antibodies against influenza virus HA and SARS-CoV-2 RBD via ELISA assays. Our results showed that all vaccinated groups induced high levels of specific serum IgG antibodies (Fig. 1B and F). Analysis of nasal washes and BALF revealed that all vaccinated groups successfully induced local IgG antibody production in the respiratory tract, with notably higher antibody levels observed in the mRNA group and the IN-PA group compared to other vaccine groups (Fig. 1C–D and G–H). Notably, only IN-PA and IN groups exhibited robust serum IgA responses (Fig. 1E and I). Further analysis of PICKCa adjuvant effects revealed that while adjuvanted protein boosting did not enhance IgG induction, it significantly potentiated serum IgA production. In summary, a vaccination strategy involving mRNA priming followed by boosting with adjuvanted protein vaccines can effectively elicit robust and comprehensive systemic and respiratory humoral immune responses.

IN-PA boosting immunization induces strong respiratory mucosal immunity

Respiratory mucosal immunity plays a crucial role in preventing respiratory infections. To evaluate the efficacy of different immunization strategies in inducing respiratory mucosal immunity, we detected specific IgA antibodies against influenza virus HA and SARS-CoV-2 RBD in immunized mice through ELISA experiments. The results showed that the IN-PA group exhibited significantly higher levels of IgA antibodies in nasal washes and BALF compared to other groups (Fig. 2A–D). The mRNA group produced low levels of IgA antibodies in only a few mice, while no IgA antibodies were detected in the IM-PA and IM groups. These findings indicate that an immunization strategy involving mRNA priming followed by boosting with protein vaccines can effectively induce mucosal immune responses, and the PICKCa adjuvant can significantly enhance this effect.

IN-PA boosting immunization provides optimal protection against lethal influenza virus challenge

After analyzing the antibody responses, we evaluated the efficacy of protection against viral challenge with influenza virus in these groups. Previously, we have demonstrated that both the mRNA vaccine and the protein vaccine conferred complete homologous protection in a two-dose regimen in mice without significant weight loss15,16.

To evaluate the protective efficacy of a heterologous sequential immunization strategy with vaccines, we challenged mice with 20 × mLD50 of H1N1 influenza virus, three weeks after the booster vaccination (Fig. 3A). We then monitored the body weight and survival of the mice for 14 days. Among the five unvaccinated control animals, four reached the humane endpoint within 10 days, indicating a severe and lethal disease course (Fig. 3B–C). In contrast, all vaccinated groups showed 100% survival. Notably, mice in the IN-PA group did not exhibit any weight loss post-challenge, while other vaccinated groups experienced an initial drop in body weight followed by recovery. This suggests that protein vaccine boosting with PICKCa adjuvant conferred complete protection against high-dose influenza virus challenge, preventing both mortality and symptomatic disease while exhibiting superior protective efficacy compared to other regimens.

IN-PA boosting immunization significantly reduces viral load and pathological damage in the lungs following a lethal influenza virus challenge

On the third day post-influenza challenge, three mice from each group were euthanized to collect lung tissues for assessing viral load and pathological damage. High titers of viral replication were detected in the lungs of control group mice, whereas all vaccinated groups showed a significant reduction in viral load (Fig. 4A). Notably, in the IN-PA group, two out of the three tested mice completely suppressed viral replication. Similar results were observed in histopathological examinations of lung tissues, where the IN-PA group demonstrated superior protection against lung lesions, with only one of the three mice exhibiting focal mononuclear cell infiltration three days post-infection (Fig. 4B–C).

Discussion

The continuous evolution of influenza viruses and the ongoing emergence of new variants of SARS-CoV-2 highlight that these two respiratory viruses will remain a significant public health risk in the future. Consequently, the development of a combined vaccine targeting both influenza virus and SARS-CoV-2 shows great promise, potentially simplifying vaccination procedures and enhancing vaccination efficiency. In our previous research, we successfully engineered a protein vaccine and an mRNA vaccine by integrating the HA stem of the H1N1 influenza virus with RBD from SARS-CoV-2. These vaccines demonstrated outstanding protective efficacy against different strains of both viruses in mouse models. However, further optimization of immunization strategies to enhance the vaccines’ protective efficacy against the continuously mutating strains of these two respiratory viruses remains to be explored.

Existing studies have indicated that heterologous prime-boost strategies using different vaccine types may enhance vaccine protection by eliciting more diverse and multifaceted immune responses18,19. Tianyang Mao and colleagues has shown that after priming with an mRNA vaccine, boosting with an unadjuvanted intranasal spike vaccine can induce robust systemic and mucosal immunity, providing complete protection against lethal SARS-CoV-2 infection in mice11. Our study also demonstrated similar results, while additionally finding that the use of PICKCa adjuvant significantly enhanced the efficacy of mucosal immunity compared to protein vaccines without adjuvants. This enhancement is likely due to the establishment of a rich reservoir of antigen-specific B and T cells following mRNA vaccination, which are then recruited to mucosal tissues and facilitated to differentiate into IgA-secreting plasma cells by intranasally administered adjuvanted protein boosts20,21. The specific enhancement of the IgA response by adjuvants warrants further investigation into their impact on dendritic cell subsets and T follicular helper cell differentiation within mucosa-associated lymphoid tissues.

In a study on different immunization strategies of influenza vaccine, it was found that after priming with an mRNA vaccine, boosting with a protein nanoparticle vaccine could enhance the cross-protection efficacy of the vaccine, with mucosal immunity playing a crucial role in this process10. In our findings, boosting with an adjuvanted protein vaccine following mRNA priming induced robust systemic humoral and mucosal immune responses. The influenza challenge experiments also demonstrated that this immunization strategy provided optimal protection, resulting in the lowest viral titers in the lungs and minimal pathological damage. It is reasonable to speculate that using the highly conserved stalk region of HA as an immunogen, combined with this optimized immunization strategy, could offer better protection against other influenza virus subtypes, particularly the recently emerged highly pathogenic bovine 2.3.4.4b H5N1 strain.

There are several limitations to the study. Firstly, we only focused on the changes of systemic humoral immunity and respiratory mucosal immunity after immunization with different vaccines, and did not systematically analyze the characteristics of cellular immunity. Secondly, we evaluated protection only against H1N1 influenza virus and not against SARS-CoV-2 or other subtypes of influenza virus. Further studies are needed to assess whether an immunization strategy with an adjuvant intranasal protein vaccine after mRNA vaccine primary vaccination can enhance broad protection. Finally, the protective efficacy of these immunization strategy was not evaluated in hamster or nonhuman primate models to obtain data more closely resembling human responses, due to limited availability of animals at the time of the study.

In summary, our results demonstrate that heterologous sequential immunization using mRNA vaccine and protein vaccines with PICKCa adjuvant containing HA stem and S-RBD chimeric antigens can significantly induce systemic humoral and respiratory mucosal immunity, and provide optimal protection against lethal influenza virus challenge. These findings can help advance clinical translation and adopt more effective sequential immunization strategies, which have broad implications for the control of influenza epidemics and COVID-19.
