Deep learning for incidence rate prediction and radiation risk assessment of solid tumors
Authors: Zhenqiu Liu, Tomoki Nakamizo, Munechika Misumi, Satoru Ono, Igor Shuryak, Robert L Ullrich
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurately estimating radiation risk is crucial for protecting public health, establishing safety standards, and advancing research in radiation safety. Historically, epidemiological studies have relied on parametric risk models, which are simple and interpretable but can yield risk estimates that are highly dependent on model specifications. Deep neural networks (DNNs), in contrast, make fewer assumptions and have driven significant advances across many fields. However, the full potential, limitations, and implications of DNNs for radiation risk assessment remain largely unexplored. In this study, we evaluated the feasibility and practicality of using DNNs for radiation risk assessment, using the RERF Life Span Study (LSS) cohort as our dataset. We found that DNNs offer greater flexibility and model independence, achieving slightly better or comparable performance in predicting tumor incidence rates across predefined metrics. Interestingly, excess relative risk (ERR) estimates from DNNs differed significantly from those of the parametric model, despite similar predictive accuracy for incidence rates. In the parametric models, ERR estimation can be influenced by the specified functional forms for both baseline risk and age-related effect modification. To investigate the source of this discrepancy, we applied SHAP values, which indicated that the DNN identifies radiation dose as the primary contributor to ERR, offering an alternative perspective. Although DNNs have limitations, such as reduced interpretability and challenges in constructing confidence intervals, they may serve as a complementary tool to well-specified parametric models for exploring complex nonlinear patterns and interactions.Keywords: AI/Deep learning, Radiation risk assessment, SHAP values, feasibility and challengesSubject terms: Cancer, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Mathematics and computing, Medical research, Oncology

Introduction

Ionizing radiation (IR) refers to radiations that have sufficiently high energy to eject electrons from an atom or molecule, thereby creating ions. Both electromagnetic radiation (X- and - rays) and particle radiation (alpha-particles, electrons, or protons) belong to ionizing radiation as described in WHO and BEIR VII reports1,2. Exposure to ionizing radiation from natural or man-made sources is unavoidable everywhere. The major sources of natural radiation exposure include air, cosmic rays, food, or water3, while exposure to artificial sources may vary in the population. Medical personnel, nuclear industry workers, individuals living in air-contaminated regions, and patients with medical procedures are more prone to man-made radiation exposures4. Accurately assessing the health risks of radiation is critical for providing a scientific basis for radiation protection and safety.

The Life Span Study (LSS) of A-bomb survivors from Hiroshima and Nagasaki provide a unique source for radiation risk assessment. The LSS cohort contains over 105,427 survivors. It is a large cohort that includes all ages and both genders with a very long follow-up time. Extensive research within this specific cohort5–10 has significantly influenced public decision-making. To date, radiation risk assessments have typically relied on parametric models. They are simple and easy to interpret but may not capture the full complexity of dose-response relationships in the data.

Owing to the exponential growth of data, the revolution of computer hardware, and the improvement of network architecture, deep learning, also known as deep neural network (DNN), is a subset of machine learning. It refers to any kind of differentiable function that can be expressed as a computation graph with multiple layers that may contain a complex structure and nonlinear mapping. DNN assumes that an output is the result of interactions between different factors, and such interactions can be spliced into multiple layers and organized in a compositional or hierarchical way. DNN, at the forefront of modern AI, has led to significant breakthroughs in fields such as natural language processing, computer vision, and speech recognition11–14. Nonetheless, despite the presence of numerous healthcare and medical applications of DNN in the literature15–19, this domain remains relatively nascent, with its full potential, implications, and limitations yet to be comprehensively explored.

In this paper, we investigate the viability and practicality of DNN in radiation risk assessment with RERF Life Span Study (LSS) data, explore both the potential benefits and hurdles of DNN in radiation risk estimation, and discuss the future research directions aimed at overcoming the obstacles of DNN in the field of radiation effects research.

Methods

Datasets: This analysis is based on a person-year table, which is a tabular format used to summarize data on radiation exposure and tumor incidence over time. It is a useful tool for analyzing time-dependent events. The person-year summary data (LSSinc07)5 was originally created from individual-level data in the LSS cohort. Subjects were stratified by attained age, age at exposure, sex, city, NIC (Not in City), and radiation dose. The dependent variable in this analysis is the observed count of tumor incidences in each stratum. We downloaded the data from the Radiation Effects Research Foundation (RERF) website (https://www.rerf.or.jp/en/library/data-en/lssinc07/). After we exclude the records with missing doses, there are 25,570 cells with summarized data from 17,448 solid tumors out of 105,427 survivors and 2,764,735 person-year follow-ups. The potential factors for solid tumor incidence include attained age, age-at-exposure, time-since-exposure, gender, city, NIC, and radiation dose. The person-years in each cell are measured to quantify the total amount of time that a group of individuals has been at risk. The radiation dose is a person-year weighted, adjusted, and truncated DS02 colon dose in the range of Gy20. The related ages are also person-year weighted mean ages in years. There are 59.3% women and 40.7% men with from Hiroshima, from Nagasaki, and NIC.

The deep learning model was trained with Jupyter Lab and PyTorch. The implementation was straightforward, and the software is available in the complementary file. We split the data randomly into training and test data. The model performance was evaluated with the mean negative log-likelihood (NLL) of the test data. We evaluate the model performance with 5-fold cross validation. The dataset was split into five approximately equal-sized folds. Models were trained and evaluated five times, with each fold serving as the test set once, while the remaining four folds were used for training. Because our data consist of aggregated person-year strata from the same cohort, observations are not fully independent. Random splitting at the stratum level may assign related observations to both the training and test sets, introducing dependence and potentially optimistic performance estimates. In this study, cross-validation was used primarily as a practical approach to assess the stability of predictive performance and to reduce overfitting in the DNN, rather than to evaluate generalizability to an independent cohort. Performance metrics from each fold and seed are averaged to estimate the models’ generalization performance. For the DNN model, additional 10% subjects from the training set were held out as a validation set by the model to control overfitting and model complexity. Various metrics are employed for assessing the out-of-sample model performance. Hyperparameter Determination

The number of nodes and layers is determined by the smallest mean negative log likelihood of the test data. Learning rate is set to 0.0001 and the popular Adam optimization algorithm is used for parameter estimation. The algorithm leverages the power of adaptive learning rates and finds individual learning rates for each parameter. The actual learning rate of Adam in each iteration is bounded by the step size hyperparameter (0.0001). The step size is a critical hyperparameter to select. If the step size is too large, the algorithm may fail to converge, while if it is too small, convergence can be extremely slow. We tested step sizes of 0.001, 0.0001, and 0.00001, ultimately selecting 0.0001 as it provided the best balance of fast and stable convergence.

Model Interpretation

Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) values are widely used to interpret machine learning models and assist with variable selection22–24. Originating from game theory, SHAP values quantify the contribution of each variable to a model’s prediction. A prediction is treated as the outcome of a cooperative game, where variables act as “players” and the prediction itself is the “payout.” Shapley values fairly distribute this payout among the variables by evaluating each one’s impact relative to its average contribution. This framework systematically decomposes a model’s output into additive contributions from the input variables, offering clear insights into how each variable influences the prediction. In linear models, variable importance is typically inferred from the magnitude of coefficients; SHAP values extend this interpretability to any model, including those capturing complex nonlinear relationships.

Results

DNN model achieves marginally better performance than the parametric ERR models in predicting tumor incidence using LSS data

We fit four distinct models to the dataset: the null model, linear model, parametric ERR model, and a deep neural network (DNN) model. The null model was estimated using the sample mean, calculated as the total tumor incidence divided by the total person-years of the training data. The linear model is a standard Poisson regression with a log-link function. The parametric ERR model was defined akin to the one employed in the original publication5:

The DNN model consists of three hidden layers with 12, 8, and 4 nodes, respectively. We used ReLU activation for nonlinear transformation and the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.0001. The model was trained using full-batch updates, with a maximum of 3000 epochs and early stopping (patience = 50). We did not perform an exhaustive hyperparameter search, but evaluated several three-layer architectures, including (256, 128, 64), (64, 32, 16), (16, 12, 8), and (12, 8, 4). Performance was comparable across these configurations, while smaller architectures trained faster. The training data were normalized using z-scores for computational stability, and the test data were standardized using the mean and standard deviation from the training set. Model performance evaluation was conducted using five-fold cross-validation. To prevent model performance from depending on a specific data split, we repeat five-fold cross-validation 10 times using different random seeds. The total computational time on a Mac Studio with 128 GB of RAM (CPU only) was 1380.287 s for the DNN, compared to only 14.272 s for the parametric model. Five metrics, RMSE, MAE, MPR, MPDL, and MNLL, were employed for model comparison, where lower values indicate better performance. Both DNN and parametric ERR models are well-calibrated

Model calibration refers to how closely the predicted values match the observed outcomes. We compared the predicted and observed mean tumor incidence rates (per 1000 PYR) within subgroups defined by categorical attained age, age at exposure, and radiation dose in the test data. Attained age and age at exposure were each divided into 5-year intervals, consistent with the original dataset encoding (e.g., 1: 0–5 years; 2: 5–10 years; 3: 10–15 years; and so on). Radiation dose was categorized into nine subgroups. The results of this comparison are presented in Fig. 2.

The predicted and observed mean tumor incidence rates by age at exposure, attained age, and categorical radiation dose of the test data. Left panels (a-c): DNN model; Right panels (d-f): parametric ERR model.

Figure 2 shows that the predicted mean tumor incidence rates from both the DNN (left panels) and parametric (right panels) ERR models closely match the observed mean incidence rates, demonstrating strong fidelity to the underlying trends. Specifically, the observed and estimated tumor incidence rates align well across most covariate categories, except for the oldest attained age and age at exposure groups. The models also show good agreement for lower radiation doses (< 1.5 Gy), although larger discrepancies emerge at doses greater than 1.5 Gy. Notably, the DNN model performs slightly better for categorical attained age and age at exposure, achieving lower RMSEs (0.52 and 0.80) compared to the parametric ERR model (0.84 and 0.86). However, the DNN shows slightly worse alignment for radiation dose, with a higher RMSE (0.71) compared to the parametric ERR model (0.66). Overall, both models are well-calibrated and effectively capture the key patterns in the data.

As shown in Fig. 3, the individual ERRs estimated by the DNN and the parametric ERR model differ significantly, despite their similar performance in predicting tumor incidence rates. The parametric ERR model produces higher individual ERRs estimates than the DNN model, with wider prediction intervals across the dose range.

One possible explanation is that individual ERRs were estimated as part of the tumor risk using a single model. Differences in the functional forms of the baseline risk and effect modification terms may lead to variations in individual ERR estimation. The baseline risks and effect modification terms learned from the data by the DNN and the parametric ERR model may differ, resulting in different ERR estimates. We will further explore this using interpretable SHAP values in the next section.

Note that all comparisons were based on our definition of ERR, defined as the individual excess relative risk relative to baseline risk with all other covariates held fixed, rather than the conventional radiation epidemiology definition of excess relative risk per Gy. Direct comparison of ERR estimates between DNN and parametric models under the conventional definition is challenging. The parametric model provides a population-level ERR per Gy estimate (e.g., 0.426159), whereas estimating ERR per Gy from a DNN model is not straightforward, highlighting a limitation of DNN-based approaches.

Comparison and interpretation of DNN and parametric ERR models using SHAP values

We used SHAP values22 to interpret and compare the DNN model and the parametric ERR model. SHAP values clearly illustrated the importance of each variable (feature) in estimating tumor incidence rates and ERR for both models. A summary plot of the SHAP values, generated with Python and available at https://github.com/shap/, is shown in Fig. 4.

Panels (a–c) (left) show summary plots of SHAP values for the DNN model, and panels (d–f) (right) show corresponding plots for the parametric ERR model, illustrating the contribution of each variable. Panels (a) and (d) are bar charts of tumor incidence rates, (b) and (e) are bar charts of ERR, and (c) and (f) are violin plots of ERR. In the violin plots, the color gradient from blue to red represents increasing feature values, and SHAP values increase from left to right: positive values indicate that a feature increases the predicted tumor rate and ERR, while negative values indicate a decrease. The magnitude reflects the strength of each feature’s contribution for an individual.

As shown in Fig. 4(a) and 4(d), both the DNN and parametric ERR models identified attained age as the most influential variable for predicting tumor incidence rates. In the DNN model, age at exposure and radiation dose were ranked second and third, respectively, whereas in the parametric ERR model, radiation dose and age at exposure held the second and third positions. Despite these slight differences in ranking, both models consistently identified the same top four variables—attained age, age at exposure, radiation dose, and gender—demonstrating their ability to detect the key factors associated with tumor incidence rates.

Despite the similarity in variable importance for tumor incidence rates between the DNN and parametric ERR models, the variable importance for ERR differs substantially, as shown in Fig. 4(b) and 4(e). In the DNN model, radiation dose emerged as the most dominant factor for ERR prediction, while attained age, age at exposure, and sex were also associated with ERR but contributed much less—both compared to radiation dose and to their roles in predicting tumor incidence rates. In contrast (Fig. 4(e)), the parametric ERR model identified age at exposure as the most influential factor for ERR, with radiation dose and attained age ranking second and third, respectively. Notably, age at exposure and attained age showed much larger contributions to ERR in the parametric model, contributing to the observed discrepancy in ERR assessments between the DNN and parametric ERR models. SHAP values help understand how the trained model distributes importance among input variables when making predictions, rather than to infer causal relationships.

The violin plots in Fig. 4(c) and 4(f) illustrate both the direction and magnitude of the associations between ERR and the variables. In Fig. 4(c), higher radiation doses are positively associated with ERR, with larger SHAP values corresponding to higher doses (red on the right). In contrast, attained age and age at exposure show negative associations with ERR, as smaller SHAP values are linked to older ages (red on the left), indicating that survivors with younger attained ages or younger ages at exposure have higher radiation risks. Similarly, Fig. 4(f) shows a positive association between ERR and radiation dose, and negative associations with both attained age and age at exposure. These patterns are consistent across both models and align with previous findings5–10.

What do the SHAP values of ERRs reveal about ERR assessment using DNN and parametric models?

Both the DNN and parametric ERR models estimated ERRs as part of tumor risk assessment using a single Poisson model. Although the DNN and parametric ERR models showed comparable performance in estimating tumor incidence rates, the parametric ERR model produced larger ERR estimates. This difference is primarily due to the significantly greater contributions of age at exposure and attained age, as highlighted in the SHAP value plots (Fig. 4(b)–4(e)). Take a closer look at the formula for individual ERRs of the given covariates estimated by the parametric model:

The model assumes that individual ERRs have a linear relationship with radiation dose, a power function relationship with attained age, and an exponential relationship with age at exposure. More importantly, it assumes that radiation contributes to ERR solely through interactions with age factors, with no independent contribution from radiation dose. These restrictive assumptions in the ERR function may result in age at exposure having a dominant influence on the ERR, potentially leading to a higher ERR estimation as illustrated in Fig. 5(b).

Schematics of radiation risk (ERR) assessment using the DNN model (Fig. 5(a)) and the parametric ERR model (Fig. 5(b)).

Unlike the parametric ERR model, where ERRs are estimated solely through the interactions between radiation dose and age modification terms, the DNN model allows for the independent contribution of radiation dose. It indeed demonstrated that radiation contributes to ERRs both independently and through its interactions with baseline variables (e.g., attained age), as illustrated in Fig. 5 (a). After removing the independent effects of baseline variables on tumor risk, their contributions to radiation risk, acting only through interactions with radiation dose, are substantially smaller than their contributions to overall tumor risk, as illustrated in Fig. 4(b) and 5(a).

It is important to note that SHAP values represent additive contributions to the model’s prediction, distributed across explanatory variables. For example, the risk arising from radiation–modifier interactions may be allocated to the SHAP values of both the dose and the modifiers. Therefore, the SHAP value for radiation dose should not be interpreted as an isolated radiation effect. Nevertheless, from a data-driven perspective, the relatively higher SHAP value associated with radiation dose in the DNN suggests that dose is a primary contributor within the model to the predicted ERR. This provides an alternative perspective to parametric models, in which the risk-modification structure is specified a priori.

Discussions

Despite its promise and ability to capture complex nonlinear dose–response relationships and interactions, the DNN model faces several challenges when applied to radiation risk assessment25:

Data-driven nature

DNNs learn dose–response relationships and estimate ERR directly from data without explicitly incorporating radiation physics, making it difficult to enforce known forms (e.g., linear or linear–quadratic models).

Limited interpretability

DNNs remain “black box” models, making it challenging to interpret predictions and to assess ERR per Gy as defined in radiation epidemiology, where transparency is essential.

Lack of uncertainty quantification

Current DNN analyses primarily compare point estimates of ERR across models without providing confidence intervals. However, uncertainty quantification is essential for radiation risk assessment and decision-making. Although methods such as the Delta method27, bootstrapping, and Bayesian approaches28 exist, deriving confidence intervals from person-year aggregated data is not straightforward. We will explore approaches to address this in future work.

Limitation of cross-validation

DNNs are inherently prone to overfitting due to their large number of parameters. In this study, we used cross-validation to assess predictive stability and mitigate overfitting. However, because the data consist of person-year aggregates from the same cohort, the training and test sets are not fully independent, which may limit generalizability. In future work, we will consider alternative strategies, such as splitting by individuals or time periods when individual-level data are available.

Conclusions and future directions

DNNs excel at predicting tumor incidence (rate) for solid tumors and provide insights into the features influencing ERR. Unlike parametric models, where the functional forms of baseline and modification terms can strongly affect the dose-response relationship, DNNs flexibly learn the dose-response directly from the data without assuming any specific functional form, this is a major advantage. Using SHAP values, DNNs identify radiation dose as the dominant factor for ERR, while a parametric model suggests age at exposure as the dominant factor for radiation ERR. Different modeling frameworks may assign varying importance to variables. The SHAP analysis illustrates how models attribute importance when estimating incidence rates and ERR, rather than providing causal inference, and does not contradict the well-established epidemiologic evidence for age-at-exposure modification of radiation risk. Differences in attribution across models likely reflect variations in model structure and parameterization rather than evidence against established epidemiologic findings.

Several challenges arise when applying DNNs in this area. Despite ongoing initiatives towards achieving interpretability in machine learning22–24, DNNs remain intricate25. It demands substantial computational time and resources. For example, when applied to the LSS data, the DNN model is approximately 97 times slower than the parametric model26 and estimating the confidence interval of a DNN model proves to be a non-trivial task27,28. Nevertheless, the realm of artificial intelligence and machine learning still holds immense potential for advancing our understanding of radiation risk.

Constructing A Parametric Model Guided by DNN

Parametric models, known for their simplicity and ease of interpretation, have become widely adopted in radiation risk assessment. However, any misspecification in the model can introduce bias, leading to inaccurate risk estimations. On the flip side, DNNs are free of strict assumptions and have the potential to unveil novel patterns and relationships that may be overlooked by parametric models. By integrating the strengths of both methodologies, we can develop a parametric model guided by insights from the DNN. The DNN aids in identifying crucial parameters and relationships within the data, forming a robust foundation for constructing a more interpretable and computationally efficient parametric model29,30. Incorporating the intricate patterns discerned by the DNN, the parametric model achieves simplicity while retaining the capacity to capture nuanced features in the data. This integrated approach harnesses the power of DNNs for initial guidance, facilitating the creation of a streamlined parametric model that strikes a harmonious balance between interpretability and predictive performance.

DNN for Low-dose Radiation Risk Assessment

Low-dose ionizing radiation (IR) permeates our daily lives through various sources, such as medical procedures, environmental exposure, and occupational settings. The precise assessment of radiation risks following low-dose exposure holds significant implications for public decision-making and government regulations. However, controversy persists in estimating risks at low doses. In the existing literature, five low dose-response models, namely linear-no-threshold, threshold, supra-linearity, quadratic, and hormesis (sub-linearity), have fervent advocates31–36. Epidemiological studies with parametric models claim support for each hypothesis, ranging from hormesis to supra-linearity, further complicating the issue. DNN models may provide a complementary tool for exploring alternative dose–response patterns with fewer parametric assumptions and for identifying potential nonlinearities or interactions. However, interpretation of low-dose risk remains challenging and depends on the availability and quality of the data.

Collaborations Between Domain Expertise and DNNs

Collaborations between domain expertise and Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) are essential for advancing the field of radiation risk prediction37. Domain experts, such as medical physicists and radiologists, bring in-depth knowledge of the underlying biological processes and intricacies of radiation exposure. Their expertise is invaluable in curating high-quality datasets, defining relevant features, and interpreting the clinical significance of predictions. On the other hand, DNNs have the capacity to learn intricate patterns and relationships within vast datasets, enabling them to uncover complex patterns that may be challenging for human experts to discern. The synergy between domain experts and DNNs allows for the development of more accurate and robust models. Domain experts guide the selection of appropriate architectures, ensure the ethical use of patient data, and provide critical insights into the interpretability of model outputs. This collaborative approach not only enhances the predictive performance of DNNs but also fosters a deeper understanding of radiation risk, ultimately leading to more effective and personalized strategies for patient care.
