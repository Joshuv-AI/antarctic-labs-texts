Pediatric mediastinal tumor unveiled as T-cell prolymphocytic leukemia: diagnostic pitfalls—a case report
Authors: Şule Çalışkan Kamış, Barbaros Şahin Karagün, Fulya Adamhasan, Ayşe Selcan Koç
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundT-cell prolymphocytic leukemia (T-PLL) is a rare and highly aggressive mature T-cell malignancy that predominantly affects older adults. Its occurrence in childhood is exceptionally uncommon and may mimic other neoplasms, such as lymphoma or thymoma, particularly when associated with mediastinal masses or generalized lymphadenopathy.Case presentationA 13-year-old Turkish girl presented with severe respiratory distress. Thoracic imaging demonstrated a large anterior mediastinal mass. In the prevascular mediastinal compartment, the most common tumors include thymoma, germ cell neoplasms, and lymphoma. The initial needle biopsy was of limited diagnostic value due to crush artifacts and demonstrated a T-cell-predominant infiltrate with focal keratin positivity, initially suggestive of a thymic neoplasm. Based on this preliminary interpretation, empiric chemotherapy for presumed advanced thymoma was initiated. However, detailed systemic radiological assessment revealed that the mass was not confined to the mediastinum but also accompanied by widespread lymphadenopathy. Subsequent systemic radiological evaluation revealed widespread lymphadenopathy beyond the mediastinum, prompting an incisional lymph node biopsy. Immunohistochemical analysis revealed a neoplasm lacking an epithelial component (negative for P63, pancytokeratin, and cytokeratin) and composed entirely of lymphoid cells. The tumor cells showed diffuse positivity for CD45, CD3, CD5, CD8, and BCL-2, with a high Ki-67 proliferation index, and were negative for immaturity markers (CD34, TdT, CD33). Taken together, these findings supported a mature T-cell neoplasm most consistent with T-PLL, although the absence of molecular confirmation limited definitive diagnostic certainty. Bone marrow evaluation was non-diagnostic.ConclusionThis case illustrates the diagnostic challenges of pediatric T-PLL and demonstrates that a multidisciplinary correlation of clinical, histological, immunophenotypic, and molecular features—together with repeated biopsy and comprehensive immunophenotyping—can be decisive for timely and accurate diagnosis.

Introduction

The mediastinum, excluding the lungs, contains nearly all major intrathoracic organs and can be affected by a broad spectrum of benign and malignant tumors. Among these, lymphoma represents the most frequently encountered malignancy. Primary mediastinal lymphomas are less common than secondary involvement of systemic lymphomas and typically arise from mediastinal lymph nodes and/or the thymus [1].

Mediastinal masses are rare in the pediatric population and pose significant diagnostic challenges. The most frequent tumors in the prevascular compartment include thymoma, germ cell neoplasms, and lymphoma. In the visceral compartment, lymphoma and congenital cysts may occur, whereas tumors of the paravertebral compartment are predominantly neurogenic in origin. Overall, lymphoma accounts for approximately 50–60% of mediastinal malignancies, with non-Hodgkin lymphoma being the predominant subtype [2].

Patients with mediastinal lymphomas commonly present with symptoms related to mass effect, such as dyspnea, cough, or superior vena cava obstruction. Accurate diagnosis of lymphoma can be particularly challenging, especially in mediastinoscopic or needle biopsies, due to limited sample size and crush artifacts. Therefore, a comprehensive clinicopathologic evaluation integrating clinical presentation, histology, immunophenotyping, and molecular genetic studies is recommended to establish an accurate diagnosis [3].

Case report

A previously healthy 13-year-old Turkish girl presented to the emergency department with progressively worsening dyspnea and chest pain. On physical examination, respiratory distress was evident, and auscultation revealed decreased bilateral breath sounds. Based on radiologic findings, the initial differential diagnosis included lymphoma and germ cell tumor. The patient was hospitalized, and a tru-cut biopsy of the mediastinal mass was planned. Abdominal ultrasonography performed to evaluate disease dissemination revealed multiple hypoechoic hepatic lesions, each measuring approximately 1.5 cm, raising suspicion of hepatic metastasis. Coronal contrast-enhanced thoracic CT more clearly delineated the mediastinal mass as a large, irregular lesion compressing adjacent lung parenchyma and vascular structures (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Coronal thoracic computed tomography image. A tru-cut biopsy of the mediastinal lesion was obtained. Histopathologic examination demonstrated a T-cell–predominant lymphoid infiltrate with focal keratin positivity. These findings were initially interpreted as suggestive of a thymic epithelial neoplasm; however, given the limited tissue sample and presence of crush artifacts, this interpretation was considered provisional. The keratin positivity was later regarded as potentially attributable to residual thymic epithelium or cytokeratin-positive dendritic cells rather than true neoplastic epithelial components. In view of the clinical progression and radiologic evidence of systemic disease, a diagnostic re-evaluation was undertaken. The case was thus initially interpreted as advanced thymoma (Masaoka-Koga stage IVB) based on clinical–pathological discordance.

Whole-body 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography (18F-FDG PET/CT) demonstrated pathological uptake in a confluent nodal mass in the anterior mediastinum, bilateral jugular chains, mediastinal and abdominal lymph nodes. Furthermore, irregular hypodense hepatic lesions and multiple skeletal foci exhibited heterogeneous FDG uptake, consistent with metastatic disease.

Due to worsening respiratory function, systemic chemotherapy was urgently initiated. Given the life-threatening presentation and the absence of a definitive diagnosis at that time, empiric systemic chemotherapy was administered as an urgent, non-standardized approach under diagnostic uncertainty. The patient received a 5-day induction regimen, consisting of cisplatin (60 mg/m2), cyclophosphamide (600 mg/m2), doxorubicin (30 mg/m2), and prednisolone (60 mg/m2/day). This decision reflected the critical clinical status and the lack of established pediatric protocols for such atypical mediastinal presentations.

Although initial clinical improvement was achieved after chemotherapy, a new painful lymph node developed in the left submandibular region, prompting repeat biopsy for diagnostic re-evaluation. Immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis revealed diffuse CD3 and CD5 positivity, high Ki-67 proliferation index, CD4 negativity, and CD8 positivity. Immature markers including TdT, CD34, and CD33 were negative, and epithelial markers (cytokeratin 19, pancytokeratin, p63), which had shown focal positivity in the initial biopsy, were entirely negative in the repeat specimen. These findings were inconsistent with the initial biopsy and argued against metastatic thymic neoplasm, instead supporting a T-cell neoplasm, particularly T-cell prolymphocytic leukemia (T-PLL). To address the potential impact of prior chemotherapy on histological interpretation, the diagnosis was based on the combined assessment of extramedullary histomorphology, immunophenotypic profile (CD3⁺, CD5⁺, CD8⁺, TdT⁻/CD34⁻), and systemic disease distribution rather than on morphology alone. As TCL-1 antibody was not available, correlation of clinical, histologic, immunophenotypic, and molecular genetic features was recommended.

Bone marrow aspirate was subsequently analyzed by flow cytometry. The CD45^dim leukocyte population represented only 0.18% of total cells, with no atypical cells or blasts detected. These results indicated limited marrow involvement at presentation and highlighted the diagnostic value of lymph node biopsy in this case.

Although typical cytogenetic abnormalities such as inv (14) (q11q32) were clinically suspected, molecular or cytogenetic confirmation was not available at diagnosis. Therefore, the final diagnosis was considered most consistent with T-PLL based on clinicopathologic correlation, while acknowledging that the absence of molecular confirmation limits absolute diagnostic certainty. This interpretation aligns with the International T-PLL Study Group criteria and the 2022 WHO classification, which recognize that diagnosis may rely on characteristic morphological and immunophenotypic features when genetic data are unavailable.

Discussion

The presentation of a dominant anterior mediastinal mass without marked lymphocytosis represents an exceptionally rare manifestation of T-PLL and closely mimics thymoma or primary mediastinal lymphoma, thereby creating a significant diagnostic pitfall. Primary thymic epithelial tumors are exceedingly rare in the pediatric population and frequently present diagnostic challenges due to overlapping features with more common mediastinal masses such as lymphoma, germ cell tumors, and leukemias [1, 2]. While thymomas are characterized by neoplastic epithelial cells intermingled with immature T lymphocytes [1, 3], the disseminated disease pattern observed in our patient-including widespread lymphadenopathy, hepatic lesions, and osseous involvement-was atypical for a classic thymoma. Notably, presentation with a dominant anterior mediastinal mass without initial marked lymphocytosis represents an exceptionally rare manifestation of T-PLL and closely mimics thymoma or primary mediastinal lymphoma, thereby posing significant diagnostic challenges. The pediatric mediastinal tumor literature underscores that unusual dissemination patterns should prompt consideration of alternative diagnoses and, when necessary, repeat tissue sampling [4].

In our case, the initial biopsy revealed histopathological findings suggestive of thymic neoplasia. This interpretation was understandable given the histologic overlap between thymic epithelial tumors and lymphoid-rich neoplasms, particularly in small-core biopsies complicated by crush artifacts. The diagnostic challenge in this case reflected inherent sampling limitations and the extreme rarity of pediatric T-PLL rather than diagnostic error. The subsequent emergence of new lymphadenopathy prompted multidisciplinary reassessment-including clinical, radiological, and flow cytometry findings-and ultimately a repeat biopsy, which demonstrated a mature T-cell immunophenotype most consistent with T-PLL.

Immunohistochemistry of the submandibular lymph node demonstrated diffuse positivity for CD3, CD5, and BCL-2, along with a high Ki-67 proliferation index. Immature markers such as TdT and CD34 were negative. This immunophenotypic profile strongly supported a mature T-cell neoplasm compatible with T-PLL, although definitive confirmation would ideally require cytogenetic or molecular correlation [5, 7]. Bone marrow flow cytometry was non-diagnostic due to minimal involvement, further emphasizing the pivotal role of tissue biopsy in establishing the diagnosis.

Although cytogenetic abnormalities such as inv (14) (q11q32) and t (14; 14) (q11; q32) are characteristic of T-PLL, molecular testing was not feasible in this case. Recent literature highlights recurrent ATM mutations, Janus kinase/signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK/STAT) pathway activation, and overexpression of the TCL1 and MTCP1 proto-oncogenes as central drivers in T-PLL pathogenesis [6–8]. Current reviews have emphasized that T-PLL remains a rare and aggressive mature T-cell malignancy with limited treatment options, supporting the need for individualized therapeutic approaches [9]. Rare adolescent cases of T-PLL associated with underlying genomic instability, including ataxia-telangiectasia and JAK3 mutations, have also been reported, further supporting the biological heterogeneity of pediatric and adolescent T-PLL [10]. These insights provide the rationale for therapeutic strategies beyond conventional chemotherapy. Alemtuzumab remains the standard first-line therapy and frequently induces remission, with allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) recommended for eligible patients as consolidation [7, 11]. Pediatric T-PLL, however, presents unique management challenges, as treatment approaches are largely extrapolated from adult protocols, and the absence of pediatric-specific guidelines introduces uncertainty in therapeutic decision-making.

Novel treatment strategies are under investigation. Preclinical and early clinical evidence suggests that BCL-2 inhibitors (for example, venetoclax), Histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitors, Cyclin-dependent kinase (CDK) inhibitors, and Mouse double minute 2 homolog (MDM2) inhibitors may hold promise [12]. Additionally, JAK/STAT pathway inhibitors and Chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T cell) therapies are being explored and may broaden future options for refractory cases [9, 12]. Although T-PLL is generally aggressive, rare cases with a long-smoldering orinitially indolent course have been described, highlighting the clinical heterogeneity of this disease [13]. Aggressive mediastinal lymphomas remain an important differential diagnosis in patients presenting with bulky mediastinal disease and systemic involvement [14].

According to the 2022 World Health Organization (WHO) classification, T-PLL is defined as a distinct mature T-cell leukemia characterized by marked lymphocytosis, splenomegaly, frequent cutaneous involvement, and an aggressive clinical course [6]. Although cytogenetic abnormalities-particularly inv (14) (q11q32) and t (14; 14) (q11; q32)-are highly supportive, WHO 2022 acknowledges that in cases where molecular results are unavailable, diagnosis may rely on characteristic morphological and immunophenotypic features when interpreted in the appropriate clinical context.

Therefore, we describe this case as most consistent with T-PLL based on multidisciplinary clinicopathologic integration rather than as a molecularly confirmed diagnosis. While the absence of cytogenetic confirmation limits absolute diagnostic certainty, this case underscores the importance of repeat biopsy and comprehensive immunophenotyping in atypical mediastinal presentations.

In conclusion, this case contributes to the limited pediatric T-PLL literature and emphasizes the importance of diagnostic re-evaluation in the setting of clinical progression Raising awareness of this rare entity among pediatric hematologists, oncologists, and pathologists is essential to reduce diagnostic delay and improve management of similar complex presentations.

Conclusion

Pediatric T-PLL is an exceptionally rare and aggressive mature T-cell malignancy that may closely mimic other anterior mediastinal masses, particularly thymoma. This case, most consistent with T-PLL based on clinicopathologic correlation, adds to the limited pediatric literature by emphasizing the diagnostic value of integrating histopathological, immunophenotypic, and clinical data, especially when molecular confirmation is unavailable. By increasing awareness of this rare entity, timely diagnostic consideration and informed therapeutic decision-making may be facilitated, potentially improving patient outcomes in similarly complex presentations.
