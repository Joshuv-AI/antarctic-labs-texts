Novel rapid diagnostic system for pathogen identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing: clinical utility in ICU patients with sepsis
Authors: Agata Chalasiewicz, Jakub Śmiechowicz, Patrycja Trzeciak, Ewa Lewczyk, Waldemar Goździk, Barbara Adamik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate a molecular diagnostic system for the rapid identification of pathogens and determination of their antimicrobial susceptibility. By comparing its performance with standard microbiological methods, the study assessed whether the new system could shorten the time to optimal antimicrobial therapy and reliably complement, or potentially replace, conventional diagnostics.MethodsA retrospective study was conducted to evaluate the performance of the Accelerate Pheno system (ACC), a platform for rapid microorganism identification (ID) and antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST), in comparison with standard-of-care (SOC) laboratory methods for positive blood cultures. The study included adult patients diagnosed with sepsis admitted to the Intensive Care Unit (ICU) who had a positive blood culture result.ResultsThe total turnaround time (from ICU blood sample collection to the availability of both ID and AST results) was significantly reduced from approximately 3 days with SOC methods to 29 h with the ACC system (p < 0.001). In 67% of cases clinical decisions regarding antimicrobial therapy were guided by ACC results: de-escalation in 20%, escalation in 37%, and no changed in 43%; in the remaining 33% of cases, decisions were based on SOC results. Pathogen ID using ACC system was successful in 68% of samples, while in 32% of cases no complete result was obtained. Failure to identify pathogens was due to limitations of the detection panel, partial identification in polymicrobial samples, or technical errors of the system. AST comparison between ACC and SOC methods showed full concordance for Gram-positive organisms. Among Gram-negative bacteria, 95% of AST results were concordant, with discrepancies observed in 5% of determinations, including three very major errors, four major errors, and four minor errors.ConclusionsThe evaluated system substantially shortened the time to pathogen identification and susceptibility reporting. Although complete results were not achieved in all clinically relevant cases, the system demonstrated high concordance with standard methods. Despite some limitations, particularly in pathogen coverage and performance in polymicrobial samples, the system proved to be a valuable complementary diagnostic tool. Background

Early diagnosis of sepsis remains serious global health issue despite advances in diagnostic technologies and laboratory accessibility [1]. Detection of sepsis at the very early stage of the disease and timely administration of appropriate antibiotic therapy are critical as each hour of delay increases the risk of mortality [2].

Identification of the causative pathogen in sepsis and determination of its antibiotic susceptibility are most commonly based on culture-dependent microbiological methods, which typically require 2 to 5 days to generate results [3]. To reduce this diagnostic delay and enable earlier adjustment of empirical antimicrobial therapy, several novel laboratory techniques have been introduced [4]. Most of these advanced methods are designed to rapidly analyse microorganisms directly from positive blood culture samples and include technologies such as fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), multiplex polymerase chain reaction (PCR), and matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) [5, 6]. Although these methods accelerate pathogen detection, they have important limitations. Their diagnostic range is restricted to predefined pathogen panels, which may lead to missed or incomplete detection of uncommon or emerging organisms [7, 8]. Most rapid methods focus primarily on pathogen identification and do not directly assess phenotypic antibiotic susceptibility [9, 10]. Even when resistance testing is included, it is typically limited to the detection of resistance genes, leaving other mechanisms undetected and potentially resulting in inappropriate therapy [11]. Polymicrobial infections pose an additional challenge, as many systems are optimized for detecting a single dominant organism, which can mask co‑infecting or less abundant species. While rapid detection supports earlier antimicrobial therapy and improved outcomes, these limitations must be considered to ensure appropriate use [12–14]. It is therefore essential to evaluate their performance in specific patient populations and to regularly compare results with standard-of-care (SOC) methods.

This study aimed to assess the performance of a rapid diagnostic system for pathogen identification (ID) and antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) directly from positive blood culture samples, both in the laboratory and clinical settings. Conducted in a cohort of patients diagnosed with sepsis at ICU admission, the study focused on the concordance analysis between the identification and antimicrobial susceptibility results produced by the rapid diagnostic system and those obtained using standard microbiological methods. Ultimately, this analysis allowed for an evaluation of whether the rapid system could serve as a reliable substitute, or at least an early complement, to standard microbiological diagnostics in the management of septic patients. We hypothesized that implementing a rapid diagnostic system would shorten the time to optimal antimicrobial therapy in patients with bloodstream infections.

Materials and methods

Study design

A retrospective study was conducted to evaluate the performance of the Accelerate Pheno system (ACC) (Accelerate Diagnostics, Denver, CO, USA), a rapid diagnostic tool for microorganism ID and AST, compared to the current standard of laboratory care for positive blood samples from septic patients. The study was conducted at the Department of Anaesthesiology and Intensive Therapy of Wroclaw Medical University and at the Microbiology Laboratory of Wroclaw University Hospital (1470 hospital beds, including 39 ICU beds) in Poland. We retrospectively analyzed medical records of patients treated between April 2019 and December 2020. The study population included all consecutive adult patients diagnosed with sepsis admitted to the ICU who had a positive blood culture. Only the first blood culture from each patient, collected at ICU admission, was included in the analysis. Patients who died before blood culture results became available were excluded from the final analysis; no additional exclusion criteria were applied. The study was approved by the Bioethics Committee of Wroclaw Medical University (approval numbers: KB- 363/2019 and KB-552/2020). Since the ACC system is routinely used for diagnosis of bloodstream infections in hospital laboratories, the Bioethics Committee granted a waiver of informed consent for this study.

According to EUCAST guidelines, the categories “S” (susceptible to antimicrobials), “I” (susceptible, increased exposure, i.e. therapeutic success is likely if exposure to the antimicrobial is increased), and “R” (resistant to antimicrobials) represent three distinct and non-overlapping interpretative categories [15]. We adhered to EUCAST definitions, therefore discrepancies in AST results were classified into three categories:

minor error (MiE): when one method reported susceptible, increased exposure (“I”) and the other reported either susceptible or resistant (“S” or “R”),

minor error (MiE): when one method reported susceptible, increased exposure (“I”) and the other reported either susceptible or resistant (“S” or “R”),

major error (ME): when an isolate deemed susceptible (“S”) by SOC methods was reported as resistant (“R”) by ACC, or when the system identified a microorganism but failed to report its susceptibility,

major error (ME): when an isolate deemed susceptible (“S”) by SOC methods was reported as resistant (“R”) by ACC, or when the system identified a microorganism but failed to report its susceptibility,

very major error (VME), when an isolate considered resistant (“R”) by SOC methods was incorrectly reported as susceptible (“S”) by ACC.

very major error (VME), when an isolate considered resistant (“R”) by SOC methods was incorrectly reported as susceptible (“S”) by ACC.

These definitions are consistent with those used by other authors [8, 16, 17]. However, it should be noted, that we expanded the definition of ME to include instances where the ACC system reported a pathogen ID but did not report a corresponding antibiotic susceptibility result. From a clinician’s standpoint, the absence of an antibiotic susceptibility result has the same practical consequences as a false resistant result - both similarly limit antibiotic treatment options. For this reason, we considered it appropriate to classify both scenarios within the ME category, as they represent “false resistant equivalent” outcomes in terms of their impact on patient care.

Patient management and blood sample collection

All patients included in the study were diagnosed with sepsis/septic shock according to the Sepsis-3 definition on admission to the ICU [18]. Demographic and clinical data of the patients were collected for analysis. The severity of sepsis was determined using the APACHE II (Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II) score on admission to the ICU. The score is made up of 12 physiological variables and 2 disease-related variables, and it is routinely used as a prediction tool for ICU patients [19]. The extent of organ dysfunction was assessed with the SOFA (Sequential Organ Failure Assessment) score daily. The score is routinely used in the ICU for monitoring the severity of sepsis based on the status of the following systems: respiratory (PaO2/FiO2 index), cardiovascular (mean arterial pressure and the dose of vasopressors), hepatic (bilirubin level), coagulation (platelets level), renal (creatinine level/urine output), and neurological (Glasgow coma scale) [20]. All patients in the study received standard treatment, according to the Surviving Sepsis Campaign Guidelines (SSC) [21]. Antimicrobial agents were administered in accordance with SSC guidelines and institutional ICU protocols. Therapy was individualized based on the suspected infection source, patient comorbidities, prior antibiotic exposure, local microbiological epidemiology and risk of multidrug-resistant pathogens. Specifically, for complicated intra-abdominal infections, combination therapy with carbapenem and vancomycin or linezolid, and antifungal agent were usually indicated. For ventilator-associated or hospital - acquired pneumonia, regimens included combination therapy with carbapenem, vancomycin or linezolid, and amikacin or colistin when indicated. Community - acquired pneumonia requiring ICU admission was treated with ceftriaxone plus macrolide or levofloxacin, with additional methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus coverage when risk factors were present. For urinary tract infections, therapy consisted of carbapenem, fluoroquinolone, piperacillin/tazobactam, ceftazidime or cefepime, or combination therapy with aminoglycoside and piperacillin/tazobactam or fluoroquinolone. For catheter-related bloodstream infections, therapy consisted of carbapenem with vancomycin and antifungal agent. In patients with risk factors for Pseudomonas aeruginosa infection, piperacillin/tazobactam, ceftazidime, cefepime, fluoroquinolones, carbapenem, aminoglycosides, alone or in combination were used. Colistin was used in selected patients with hospital acquired infections depending on epidemiology of the hospital ward. Once microbiological results became available, antimicrobial therapy was reassessed and modified accordingly. These modifications were classified as escalation or de-escalation based on antimicrobial spectrum broadening or narrowing following microbiological findings and clinical reassessment.

Upon ICU admission, as part of standard protocol, two blood culture sets (aerobic and anaerobic) were collected from each patient with sepsis or septic shock and promptly sent to the laboratory. Only blood cultures collected at admission were included in the analysis; subsequent cultures obtained later during hospitalization were not analyzed. Therefore, each of the cases included in the final analysis constitutes an independent episode corresponding to the patient’s initial blood collection. For each patient, changes in antimicrobial therapy based on results from either the ACC or SOC methods were documented. These included escalation of treatment when indicated, de-escalation of unnecessary antibiotics, or continuation of the existing therapy. Microorganisms identified in polymicrobial blood samples were considered true pathogens by the attending clinicians, based on a comprehensive evaluation of the patient’s clinical status and laboratory findings, and appropriate therapy was administered.

Pathogen identification and susceptibility testing

Blood samples collected from septic patients upon ICU admission were inoculated into BacT/ALERT FA Plus and BacT/ALERT FN Plus bottles (bioMerieux, Inc., Durham, NC, USA) and transported to the microbiology laboratory via a pneumatic tube system. The microbiology laboratory does not operate 24 h a day; it functions on weekdays from 7:30 AM to 6:30 PM, and on weekends and holidays from 8:00 AM to 6:00 PM. Regardless of delivery time, all samples were promptly incubated upon arrival using the automated BacT/ALERT blood culture monitoring system (bioMerieux, Inc., Durham, NC, USA) by the laboratory staff or on-duty personnel. As part of the standard procedure, Gram staining was performed for each blood sample flagged as positive. The results were immediately reported to the ICU physician, who then decided whether to proceed with testing using the ACC system based solely on the patient’s survival status. The ACC method was performed for each patient who was alive at the time the blood culture signaled positive. For each enrolled patient, pathogen ID and AST were performed in parallel using both the ACC system and SOC methods. Blood was drawn from the same culture bottle at the same time for both methods to ensure consistency (Fig. 1).

SOC procedure: VITEK2 (bioMerieux, Inc., Durham, NC, USA) and MALDI-TOF MS using the MALDI Biotyper system (Bruker Daltonics, Fremont, CA, USA) have been routinely used for the bacteria and yeast ID. For AST VITEK2, broth microdilution method - the gold standard for AST (Erba Lachema, Brno, Czech Republic), and a gradient test method were used (Liofilchem MTS, Roseto degli Abruzzi, Italy). All results were interpreted according to EUCAST (European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing) guidelines, versions 2019 and 2020 [15]. The results were made immediately available to ICU physicians via the hospital’s electronic medical record system.

ACC procedure: the system uses automated fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH) technology combined with morphokinetic cellular analysis to provide rapid bacteria and yeasts ID, as well as AST. The ID results are available within 1.5 h, while AST result, including minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) interpreted according to EUCAST guidelines [15], are provided within 7 h. The ACC system, as based on MIC, does not allow for the determination of AST in samples containing more than one microorganism. All results were communicated to the ICU physician as soon as they became available. The system is capable of identifying 15 distinct bacterial types and species (8 Gram-negative and 7 Gram-positive), as well as two fungal species belonging to the genus Candida. In addition, the ACC system enables antimicrobial susceptibility testing for 21 antibiotics for Gram-negative bacteria and for 9 antibiotics for Gram-positive bacteria. The lists of the microorganisms identifiable by the ACC system and antimicrobial agents for which susceptibility can be determined are provided in the Table S1 and S2 in the Supplement.

Statistical methods

All patients enrolled in the study were included in the analyses. Statistical calculations were performed using Statistica v.13.3 (TIBCO Software Inc., Palo Alto, CA, USA) under a license held by Wroclaw Medical University. Continuous variables are presented as means with standard deviations and ranges (minimum–maximum), while categorical variables are summarized as counts and percentages. The Shapiro–Wilk test indicated non-normal distributions. Accordingly, differences in numerical variables between standard microbiological culture and the rapid diagnostic ACC system were assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using contingency tables and the chi-squared test. Agreement between ACC and SOC was evaluated using standard performance metrics, including sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV. In addition, inter-method agreement for pathogen identification was assessed using the kappa index, which quantifies concordance beyond chance. The confidence interval was calculated using the Clopper - Pearson exact method. The Kaplan - Meier method was used to estimate the probability of remaining without an available test result over time, comparing the ACC and SOC methods. At time zero, all patients were without a result, corresponding to a probability of 1.0. As diagnostic results became available over time, the probability of remaining without an available result decreased, yielding a stepwise Kaplan–Meier curve. Statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05.

Results

Between April 2019 and December 2020, 847 patients were admitted to the ICU, of whom 325 were diagnosed with sepsis. Blood samples for culture were obtained at ICU admission for all septic patients, with 82 (25%) yielding positive results. Due to the retrospective design of the study, all data were obtained from patient medical records. When a patient died before a positive growth signal was detected in the culture bottle, there was no longer any clinical indication to perform rapid molecular diagnostics, including the ACC, and only SOC laboratory procedures were continued. In this cohort, 22 patients died before their blood culture bottles were flagged as positive by the automated BacT/ALERT blood culture monitoring system. Consequently, ACC results were not available for these individuals, and they were excluded from the analysis, resulting in a final study population of 60 patients. Time to obtain identification and antimicrobial susceptibility results: ACC vs. SOC comparison

Blood samples collected from ICU patients were transported to the microbiology laboratory via the hospital’s pneumatic tube system and immediately placed in the incubator. The mean time from sample collection to a positive growth signal in the culture bottle was 22 ± 16 h (range: 7 h to 5 days). Following a positive growth signal, Gram staining was performed within an average of 5 ± 3 h (range: 20 min to 14 h). Importantly, for both the ACC and SOC methods, samples for pathogen identification (ID) and antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) were drawn simultaneously from the same culture bottle, ensuring consistency across methods. All observed delays were primarily attributed to the absence of laboratory staff during night hours (6:00 p.m. to 7:00 a.m.), as the laboratory did not operate night shifts.

Immediately after obtaining the Gram stain results, pathogen ID and AST procedures were initiated in parallel using both the ACC and the SOC methods. From the time of positive growth signal in the culture bottle, pathogen ID results were available after only 7 ± 3 h (range: 2–17 h) with ACC compared to 27 ± 14 h (range: 10–86 h) with SOC (p < 0.001). Similarly, AST results were available significantly faster with ACC: 12 ± 3 h (range: 3–19 h) versus 38 ± 18 h (range: 14 h − 4 days) for SOC (p < 0.001). From the time of Gram staining, pathogen identification (ID) results were available after 2 ± 1 h (range: 2–5 h) using the ACC system, compared with 22 ± 14 h (range: 9–80 h) with SOC methods (p < 0.001).

Similarly, AST results were obtained significantly faster with the ACC system, becoming available after 7 ± 1 h (range: 7–10 h), whereas AST results using SOC required 33 ± 18 h (range: 13–96 h) (p < 0.001)(Fig. 3). Taken together, the mean turnaround time from ICU blood sample collection to the availability of both pathogen identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing results was 29 ± 9 h (range 11–54 h) when using ACC method, and 3 ± 2 days (range: 19 h–9 days) using SOC methods (p < 0.001).

Impact of ACC results on physicians’ decisions regarding antimicrobial therapy

The mean time from blood sample collection at the ICU to physicians’ decisions regarding antimicrobial therapy was 43 ± 33 h (range 11 h to 9 days). The Kaplan - Meier curve illustrates the probability of remaining without an available test result over time, comparing the ACC method with the SOC method (Fig. 4). Analysis of turnaround time to diagnostic result demonstrated that the interval from blood sample collection to result availability was significantly shorter for the ACC method than for SOC (log-rank p < 0.001), indicating a clear difference in turnaround time distributions between the two methods.

The Kaplan - Meier analysis to estimate the probability of remaining without an available test result over time, comparing the ACC and SOC methods

In 40 cases (67%), decisions were guided by ACC results, with a mean turnaround time (from blood sampling in the ICU) of 29 ± 9 h (range 11–54 h). In this subgroup, antimicrobial therapy was de-escalated in 20% of patients, escalated in 37%, and unchanged in 43%. In the remaining 20 cases (33%), decisions relied on SOC results, with a mean turnaround time of 3 ± 2 days (range: 19 h–9 days) (p < 0.001). In this subgroup, antimicrobial therapy was de-escalated in 35% of patients, escalated in 45%, and unchanged in 20%.

Among the 11 discordant results, there were 3 VMEs, 4 MEs, and 4 MiEs. The impact of each error on antimicrobial therapy decisions is detailed below. The first and second VME involved Pseudomonas aeruginosa tested against piperacillin/tazobactam. In both cases, the ACC system reported susceptibility, whereas the SOC methods indicated resistance. In the first case, therapy was escalated to colistin based on the ACC result. In the second case, no modification was made, as colistin had already been initiated as first-line therapy. Piperacillin/tazobactam was not considered in either instance. The third VME involved Enterobacter cloacae, where the ACC system again reported susceptibility to piperacillin/tazobactam, contrary to the SOC result showing resistance. In this case, meropenem was selected as the first-line agent, and piperacillin/tazobactam was not considered. In summary, none of the VMEs in this study ultimately altered patient therapy. Nevertheless, it is important to emphasize that each had the potential to delay appropriate treatment and thereby adversely affect clinical outcomes.

Out of 4 MEs, the first involved Pseudomonas aeruginosa tested against gentamicin: the ACC system reported resistance, whereas the SOC methods indicated susceptibility. Since colistin had already been selected as first-line therapy, gentamicin was not considered for treatment. The next two MEs involved Acinetobacter baumannii tested against colistin. In both cases, the ACC system reported resistance, while the SOC methods indicated susceptibility. As a result, therapy was escalated to include colistin only after the SOC result became available (nearly two days later) in one patient. In the second patient, colistin was introduced earlier, guided by pathogen identification through ACC and SOC results obtained from a previously collected BAL sample. The forth ME involved Serratia marcescens tested against amikacin. Although the ACC system correctly identified the pathogen, it failed to provide a complete AST profile. This error, however, did not influence therapy, and treatment with piperacillin/tazobactam and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole was continued.

Out of 4 MiEs, three involved cefepime susceptibility testing for Serratia marcescens and Enterobacter cloacae. In two cases, the ACC system reported susceptible, increased exposure, whereas the SOC methods indicated susceptibility and in one case the ACC system reported resistance while the SOC methods indicated susceptible, increased exposure. Since cefepime was not considered for treatment, neither MiE affected patient management. The fourth MiEs involved meropenem susceptibility testing for Acinetobacter baumannii. Here, the ACC system reported susceptible, increased exposure, while the SOC methods indicated resistance. In this case, therapy with colistin was continued according to ACC result, and meropenem was not considered.

Although the discordant results observed in this study did not directly affect therapeutic choices, they carried the potential risk of delaying adequate therapy.

Additionally, a subgroup analysis was conducted to evaluate whether carbapenem-based therapy was more likely to undergo de-escalation compared with antipseudomonal β-lactam therapy. Among patients receiving carbapenem-based regimens, therapy was de-escalated in 23%, escalated in 32%, and remained unchanged in 45%, while in those treated with antipseudomonal β-lactams, therapy was de-escalated in 33%, escalated in 44%, and unchanged in 22%. The difference between the two subgroups was not statistically significant (p = 0.463). Furthermore, within the carbapenem-treated subgroup, the overall distribution of therapeutic decisions (de-escalation, escalation, or continuation of the current regimen) did not differ significantly between the ACC and SOC approaches (p = 0.375). A similar lack of difference was observed in the antipseudomonal β-lactam subgroup, where the distribution of therapeutic decisions was also comparable between the ACC and SOC approaches (p = 0.495).

Identification of microorganisms: ACC vs. SOC comparison

Among the 60 positive blood culture samples analyzed with SOC methods, Gram-positive pathogens were identified in 26 cases (43%), Gram-negative pathogens in 30 cases (50%), and yeasts in 9 cases (15%). When analyzed with the ACC system, Gram-positive pathogens were detected in 17 cases (28%), Gram-negative pathogens in 21 cases (35%), and yeasts in 3 cases (5%).

Concordance in pathogen ID between ACC and SOC methods was observed in 68% of patients (N = 41). A summary of ACC identifications that matched SOC results is presented in Table 2. The ACC system failed to provide pathogen ID in 19 patients (32%). This included 5 cases (8%) of polymicrobial infections, where the ACC system did not identify all pathogens; 6 cases (10%) involving technical errors of the ACC system; and 8 cases where the pathogens were not included in the ACC detection panel. As a result, physicians either received no pathogen identification or only partial results in the case of polymicrobial infections.

Among the 60 positive blood samples analysed using both methods, polymicrobial infections were detected in 5 patients (8%) by the SOC methods, but were not detected by the ACC system; Gram staining did not indicate polymicrobial infection, and additional organisms were detected only in culture. Clinically, such partial identification, without any indication of polymicrobial presence, may be more problematic than a complete lack of identification, as it can lead to incomplete or inappropriate antimicrobial therapy. In addition, in eight samples, the pathogens identified by the SOC methods were not included in the ACC detection panel. These off-panel microorganisms were: Candida parapsilosis (n = 2), Providencia rettgeri (n = 1), Stenotrophomonas maltophilia (n = 3), Bacillus spp. (n = 1), and Cutibacterium acnes (n = 1). Furthermore, in six positive blood culture samples, the presence of bacterial or fungal cells was confirmed by Gram staining, and pathogens were successfully identified using the SOC methods but not detected by the ACC system, despite being listed in its detection panel. These included: Staphylococcus epidermidis (n = 2), Enterococcus faecalis (n = 1), Acinetobacter baumannii (n = 1), Candida albicans (n = 1), and Candida glabrata (n = 1). From a clinical perspective, such failures are disappointing, as they delay the time to definitive results until SOC methods are completed. However, they do not typically result in inappropriate antimicrobial therapy adjustments, since no misleading identification is reported. It is worth emphasizing that all pathogens identified by the ACC system were subsequently confirmed by SOC methods, demonstrating the high sensitivity of the ACC system.

In summary, of the 16 distinct pathogen species identified using SOC methods, the ACC system successfully detected 10 of them. Comparison between ACC and SOC results showed that the ACC system yielded 46 true-positive identifications, no false positives, 808 true negatives, and 19 false negatives. The false negatives included: 5 undetected pathogens in polymicrobial samples, 6 due to technical errors of the ACC system, and 8 involving pathogens not covered by the ACC detection panel. Overall, the diagnostic performance of the ACC system demonstrated a sensitivity of 71% (range: 0–100%), specificity of 100%, positive predictive value (PPV) of 100%, and negative predictive value (NPV) of 98% (range: 91–100%). These metrics are based on a pathogen-level comparison of identification results between the tested ACC system and the SOC methods. A detailed summary of these results is provided in Supplementary Table S3. If microorganisms absent from the ACC system’s detection panel but identified by SOC methods were not included in the analysis, the sensitivity of the ACC system would increase to 81%, underscoring the need to broaden its detection menu. However, to accurately reflect real world implementation of the ACC system in ICU settings, failures associated with off panel organisms were intentionally retained. The diagnostic performance of the ACC system based on sample-level assessment demonstrated a moderate sensitivity of 68% (95% CI, 55–80) and very good positive predictive value (PPV) of 100% (95% CI, 91–100). A detailed summary of these results is provided in Supplementary Table S4. Agreement analysis between ACC and SOC for pathogen identification demonstrated a kappa coefficient of 0.65, indicating good agreement beyond chance. This level of concordance supports the reliability of ACC as a complementary diagnostic method while acknowledging method-specific differences.

Among 6 cases (10% of positive samples) classified as technical errors, all presented the same instrument-generated message: “too few cells for analysis.” Although microbial load was sufficient for Gram-stain visualization and for pathogen ID and AST by SOC methods, the ACC system did not recognize the cell count as adequate. The error was consistent across all affected samples, with no variation in the instrument output. In each case, pathogen identification failed on the ACC system but was successfully achieved using SOC methods, with microorganisms visible on Gram-stain and ID/AST completed by conventional culture. Supplementary Table S5 summarizes the pathogens that were detected by SOC methods but missed by the ACC system due to these errors.

In addition, to assess whether the adequacy of the ACC panel varied with illness severity, we performed a subgroup analysis based on the presence of septic shock at ICU admission. In our cohort, 53% of patients presented with septic shock, representing a subgroup with more severe clinical status, as reflected by a significantly higher SOFA score compared with patients without shock (12 vs. 6 points, p = 0.001). The proportion of off-panel organisms did not differ significantly between these groups (9% with shock vs. 14% without shock, p = 0.456). Therefore, in our dataset, the diagnostic usefulness of the ACC system did not appear to be related to the severity of clinical condition in sepsis.

Performance of the ACC system for yeast identification

Special attention was given to the performance of the ACC system in the rapid identification of yeasts from positive blood cultures. The ACC system is theoretically capable of identifying Candida albicans and Candida glabrata. Among the 60 analysed samples, yeasts were detected in 9 by SOC methods (C. albicans in 6, C. glabrata in 1, and C. parapsilosis in 2). In contrast, the ACC system identified C. albicans in only 3 samples. For the 3 samples correctly identified by the ACC system, yeast identification was available after 2 ± 0.7 h (range 2–3 h) from the time of Gram staining, compared with 31 ± 8 h (range 25–40 h) using SOC methods. In the remaining six samples, in which the ACC system failed to provide identification, SOC-based yeast identification became available after 39 ± 10 h (range 26–48 h). Delayed yeast identification in these six cases was associated with delayed antifungal treatment in four patients; two of these patients died from causes unrelated to the fungal infection. In the other two cases, antifungal therapy was not delayed, and one patient died from non–fungal-related causes. Although delayed identification did not correlate with worse clinical outcomes in this cohort, the inability of the ACC system to consistently detect fungemia is concerning, particularly as empiric antimicrobial regimens for sepsis generally do not include antifungal coverage.

In all samples, the isolates showed appropriate growth in the system’s growth control, allowing AST to be performed. During the study period, there was a lack of continuity in the SOC laboratory testing of Staphylococcus spp. susceptibility to daptomycin. However, the ACC system enabled AST for this antibiotic, resulting in a greater number of susceptibility results from ACC system compared to the SOC methods. In total, the ACC system performed 67 susceptibility tests, which were compared with 61 susceptibility results conducted using SOC methods. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing for polymicrobial cultures

According to EUCAST guidelines, AST must be performed using pure bacterial or fungal culture. Therefore, in the case of a positive blood culture containing a mixed microbial population, AST using a rapid phenotypic method (such as the ACC system) is not performed. In the analysed patients’ blood samples, the SOC methods identified five mixed microbial cultures. In each of these samples, the tested ACC system detected only a single bacterial pathogen and proceeded to perform AST for that organism. For Gram-positive bacteria, the results of AST were concordant with SOC; however, for Gram-negative bacteria, discrepancies were observed, as listed above. These findings are concerning, as performing AST from a mixed culture carries a high risk of inaccuracy and may lead to inappropriate therapeutic decisions while awaiting SOC results.

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the performance and clinical utility of the ACC system in both ICU and laboratory settings. Specifically, we assessed (1) its role in guiding antibiotic therapy for septic ICU patients and (2) the concordance of ACC microbial identification and antimicrobial susceptibility results with standard microbiological methods. The novelty of our study lies in its strong emphasis on a practical clinical perspective, which remains underrepresented in the current literature. To reliably assess the clinical usefulness of the ACC system, we examined its diagnostic performance across Gram-positive, Gram-negative, and yeast pathogens directly from positive blood cultures, including mono- and polymicrobial samples. This comprehensive approach reflects the pathogen spectrum encountered in critically ill ICU patients with sepsis or septic shock and ensures a clinically meaningful evaluation of the ACC system. In addition, we identified several additional bacterial and yeast pathogens that should be incorporated into the ACC system’s diagnostic menu to further enhance its utility in routine ICU practice. Our findings demonstrate that the ACC system enables microbial identification from positive blood cultures nearly four times faster than SOC methods and provides antimicrobial susceptibility results more than three times faster, thereby facilitating earlier optimization of therapy in most patients. Although pathogen identification was achieved in the majority of samples, the ACC system failed to deliver complete results in several clinically relevant scenarios: polymicrobial infections, technical errors, and microorganisms not included in the detection panel. Importantly, all results were retained in the final analysis, which we consider a strength of this study, as it provides a realistic appraisal of diagnostic performance and greater clinical relevance. In the following sections, we discuss the implications of our findings with respect to time-to-result, pathogen identification, AST performance, and the overall clinical utility of rapid diagnostic tools in improving patient management.

Time reduction

In sepsis, an excessive immune response combined with inadequate infection control increases the risk of multiorgan failure and death. Delays in recognizing sepsis and initiating appropriate antibiotic therapy markedly raise mortality, making rapid and accurate pathogen identification essential [24, 25]. With rising antimicrobial resistance, rapid AST has become equally critical for guiding optimal therapy. Novel diagnostic methods can overcome the turnaround time limitations of SOC microbiological techniques. The ACC system evaluated in our study, enables direct identification of microorganisms from positive blood cultures using fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) and provides phenotypic AST via morphokinetic cellular analysis. Consistent with previous reports, ACC significantly reduces the time to microbiology results: identification from the positive culture signal was nearly four times faster than SOC (7 vs. 27 h), and AST results were available more than three times sooner (12 vs. 38 h) [4, 8, 16, 26]. This time advantage shows the potential of ACC to accelerate optimal treatment decisions in septic patients with bacteremia. Moreover, it can be assumed that the overall turnaround from blood collection to final results could be further reduced if the laboratory operated continuously, without a night break, as limited laboratory operating hours are a critical factor influencing reported turnaround times.

Clinical performance

Sepsis and septic shock are medical emergencies requiring rapid intervention. The Surviving Sepsis Campaign guidelines recommend administering antimicrobials within 1 h of suspected septic shock or high likelihood of sepsis [21]. Several studies have demonstrated that the use of rapid diagnostic methods in bloodstream infections is associated with earlier initiation of appropriate antimicrobial therapy, shorter hospital length of stay, and reduced mortality [32, 33, 36, 37]. These findings underscore the potential clinical value of rapid diagnostics in patients at high risk of early clinical deterioration. At the same time, the benefits of rapid testing depend on timely availability of results and their integration into active clinical decision-making, which may be limited in cases of early mortality. In the present study, in accordance with established guidelines, blood and other relevant specimens were collected from all septic patients upon ICU admission, and broad‑spectrum antibiotics were initiated. Empirical therapy was individualized, taking into account illness severity, infection source, comorbidities, immunosuppression, local epidemiology, and risk of multidrug resistance. Once microbiological data became available, therapy was reassessed and adjusted - classified as escalation or de‑escalation depending on whether the antimicrobial spectrum was broadened or narrowed. In our study, the ACC system markedly reduced the time to ID and AST results. The mean turnaround time from ICU blood sample collection to availability of both ID and AST results was significantly reduced to approximately 29 h with the ACC system, compared to 3 days with SOC methods, consistent with previous reports [4, 8, 16, 26]. This advantage would likely be even greater in a 24/7 laboratory setting, as the effect of limited laboratory operating hours on reported turnaround times is a critical factor influencing overall diagnostic efficiency.

Early availability of pathogen identification and susceptibility data enabled earlier optimization of therapy in most patients, including escalation for resistant pathogens and de-escalation where broad-spectrum coverage was unnecessary, thereby reducing toxicity and limiting the emergence of antimicrobial resistance. Importantly, the choice of initial empiric regimen (e.g., carbapenem-based or antipseudomonal β-lactam therapy) did not influence the frequency or direction of subsequent therapy adjustments, indicating that the likelihood of de‑escalation was independent of the empiric antibiotic selected. The choice between ACC and SOC approaches did not influence the pattern of antimicrobial decision-making within either treatment subgroups, indicating that both diagnostic strategies resulted in comparable therapeutic modifications regardless of the initial antibiotic class.

Although discordant results did not directly alter therapeutic decisions, they could potentially delay appropriate therapy and affect clinical outcomes. Overall, these findings indicate that the ACC system supports more timely antimicrobial decision-making; however, confirmation with SOC methods remains essential in critically ill patients.

Patient safety

When evaluating novel diagnostic methods for pathogen ID and AST, outcomes such as reduced time to appropriate antimicrobial therapy, earlier de-escalation, and mitigation of therapeutic errors should be interpreted within the framework of patient safety. Our study applied a comprehensive, real-world evaluation of the ACC system, including all blood culture results from an ICU population with sepsis. This inclusive design allows for an assessment of the ACC system under routine clinical conditions and reflects the full microbiological spectrum encountered in everyday ICU practice. Consistent with our results, the ACC system has the potential to shorten the time to appropriate antimicrobial therapy. Its high positive predictive value may support early escalation, whereas de-escalation should remain guided by SOC methods confirmation, particularly in critically ill patients. Evaluating the ACC system under these real-world conditions provides a reliable assessment of its impact on antimicrobial decision-making and strengthens its evaluation in the context of patient safety.

Identification of bacterial pathogens

Although the ACC system substantially reduced the time for microbial identification, complete identification was achieved in only 68% of samples; in the remaining 32%, no result was obtained or, in cases of polymicrobial infection, only a single pathogen was identified. Importantly, the ACC system did not yield any false-positive results, and all pathogens detected were subsequently confirmed by SOC methods, yielding a diagnostic specificity of 100% and a sensitivity of 71%. The reduced sensitivity resulted from failure to identify pathogens in 32% of positive blood culture samples, which was attributed to: (1) microorganisms not included in the ACC detection panel, (2) polymicrobial infections, or (3) technical errors of theACCsystem. Organisms identified by the SOC methods but not covered by theACC panel included Stenotrophomonas maltophilia, Candida parapsilosis, Providencia rettgeri, and Cutibacterium acnes, all of which may represent clinically relevant pathogens in sepsis [27–31]. According to international sepsis guidelines, empiric antimicrobial therapy should be initiated immediately, ideally within 1 h of recognition, in patients with possible septic shock or a high likelihood of sepsis [21]. Empiric regimens in the ICU generally provide coverage for Providencia rettgeri and Cutibacterium acnes; therefore, the absence of these microorganisms in the ACC panel is not a major limitation. In contrast, the inability to detect Stenotrophomonas maltophilia is significant as the drug of choice for S. maltophilia - trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole - is not routinely included in empiric therapy. This Gram-negative rod was identified by SOC method in three of the tested samples, highlighting the clinical relevance of its omission from the ACC detection panel. Similar concerns have been reported by other authors [32–35]. Taken together, although the ACC panel covers a broad range of pathogens, it is insufficient for critically ill septic patients in this cohort. Expansion of microbial coverage and alignment with local microbiological epidemiology are critical, as incomplete detection could compromise timely, targeted therapy and jeopardize patient safety in ICU. At the same time, the pathogen distribution observed in our centre reflects local epidemiology, which may differ from that of other institutions and thus limit the generalizability of our findings. Therefore, further multi-center studies with different epidemiology are needed to confirm these findings.

Identification of yeast pathogens

Data on the usefulness of the ACC system for the rapid identification of fungemia are limited. In this study, we assessed its performance in identifying yeasts in blood samples from septic ICU patients. The ACC panel is restricted to only two yeast species, Candida albicans and Candida glabrata. In our cohort, yeasts were detected in 15% of samples by SOC methods, whereas the ACC system identified yeasts in only 5%. The difficulty of detecting yeasts has been previously highlighted by other authors [36–38]. This limitation is clinically relevant, as empiric antimicrobial regimens for sepsis typically lack antifungal coverage, and guidelines recommend empiric antifungal therapy only for patients at high risk of invasive fungal infection. Thus, the inability of the ACC system to reliably detect fungemia, together with its very limited yeast identification panel, is of particular concern. Moreover, common yeast pathogens, including C. albicans, C. glabrata, C. krusei, and C. parapsilosis, exhibit species-dependent variability in antifungal susceptibility, necessitating different treatment strategies. In the ICU setting, special attention should be given to Candida parapsilosis as a frequent cause of catheter-related bloodstream infections [30, 39]. The restriction of the ACC system to only two yeast species has led some investigators to exclude cases of fungemia from analyses, contributing to the limited evidence base [5, 35, 40]. In our study, failure of the ACC system to detect yeasts delayed initiation of appropriate antifungal therapy; however, this delay did not lead to worse clinical outcomes. The small number of fungemia cases in our cohort limits the strength of these conclusions but highlights the need for further investigation. Overall, the inconsistent detection of yeast pathogens remains a concern, emphasizing the importance of considering local epidemiology when implementing the ACC system. Understanding local epidemiology is essential to ensure that the system’s practical utility aligns with the pathogens most likely to be encountered, and caution is needed when extrapolating our results to other settings.

Identification of polymicrobial infections

Another factor contributing to the reduced sensitivity of ACC testing is its limited ability to detect all pathogens in polymicrobial samples. In previously published evaluations of the ACC system, only a few research groups have included polymicrobial cases in their analyses, while many excluded them altogether or restricted their analyses to Gram-negative organisms [7, 17, 32–34, 37, 41–44]. In the study by Ehren et al., polymicrobial samples that were not visualized by Gram staining were analyzed [5]. Consistent with our findings, the ACC system identified only a single organism in each case of polymicrobial bloodstream infection, whereas SOC methods achieved complete pathogen identification. Similarly, Burnham et al. reported both false-negative and false-positive Candida spp. results in polymicrobial samples [37]. Such inaccuracies pose a substantial risk to patients, potentially resulting in unnecessary antifungal therapy or, conversely, the omission of appropriate treatment. In another study assessing the hypothetical impact of the ACC system on antimicrobial prescribing, complete pathogen identification in polymicrobial bloodstream infections was achieved only in a subset of cases. The authors described a clinically relevant example in which the ACC system detected Escherichia coli but failed to identify MRSA; while therapy for E. coli could have been optimized earlier, the missed MRSA detection carried potentially serious clinical consequences [44]. Overall, the performance of the ACC system in detecting pathogens in polymicrobial bloodstream infections remains insufficiently evaluated in the current literature. This gap has been acknowledged as a limitation by several authors and underscores the need for further dedicated studies [40]. In our study, polymicrobial infections were identified in 8% of samples using SOC methods; in none of these did the ACC system detect all pathogens present, despite their inclusion in the detection panel. Furthermore, AST results were consistently generated for only a single organism, which could misleadingly suggest the possibility of de-escalation therapy. Accurate identification of the true pathogen responsible for sepsis is remains a clinical challenge. Certain organisms, particularly coagulase-negative staphylococci and other skin commensals, may represent contamination rather than true bloodstream infection. Therefore, every measure should be taken to minimize the risk of blood sample contamination during collection. In addition, as part of standard protocol, two blood culture sets (aerobic and anaerobic) were collected from each patient with sepsis or septic shock and promptly sent to the laboratory. True pathogens are typically recovered consistently across multiple sets, whereas contaminants appear sporadically, often in a single bottle. Determining whether an isolate represents a true pathogen requires an integrated assessment of clinical, microbiological, and laboratory data. Ultimately, clinical judgment remains paramount - patients must be treated, not laboratory results alone. In our study, all microorganisms identified in polymicrobial blood samples by SOC were considered true pathogens by the attending clinician based on comprehensive evaluation of the patient’s clinical status and laboratory findings, and appropriate therapy was administered. Given the limited reliability of Gram staining for pathogen identification in positive blood cultures, this shortcoming further undermines user confidence in the ACC system and may discourage de-escalation of empirical antibiotic therapy [45]. Our findings, demonstrating unsatisfactory performance of the ACC system in polymicrobial infections, are consistent with previous reports [16, 17, 37, 44]. It is evident that the ACC system has limitations in detecting polymicrobial bloodstream infections as well as fungal infections, and therefore diagnostic workup must be supplemented with conventional culture. However, it should be emphasized that the ACC system did not produce any false ‑ positive results. Consequently, a recommendation to avoid basing treatment on ACC AST until SOC confirmation would be overly restrictive. In our view, antimicrobial therapy must be initiated promptly and can be modified later if SOC results indicate the need for adjustment.

In summary, correct pathogen identification was achieved in 68% of samples in our study, compared with higher rates reported previously, ranging from 75.7% in Ehren et al. to 98.8% in Banerjee et al. These differences reflect substantial variations in study design, as prior studies often excluded samples with Gram-positive bacteria, fungi, or polymicrobial growth. In contrast, our study encompassed all positive blood culture samples, including both mono- and polymicrobial infections, with Gram-positive pathogens identified in 43% of samples, Gram-negative pathogens in 50%, and fungi in 15%. By including all cases, our analysis provides a more realistic assessment of diagnostic performance of ACC system in ICU settings. This approach is particularly relevant for patient safety, as incomplete or inaccurate pathogen identification leads to inappropriate antimicrobial therapy, delayed treatment, and potentially severe adverse outcomes. These findings highlight the potential of the ACC system as a universal tool for rapid detection of Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria, fungi, and polymicrobial infections, supporting more timely and appropriate clinical decision-making, and underscore the need for further studies evaluating all positive blood cultures. Considering the pathogen identification results discussed above, this also draws attention to the fact that the clinical usefulness of the system may vary according to the local microbial epidemiology.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Antimicrobial susceptibility results were obtained over 3 times faster using the ACC system than with SOC methods (on average 12 vs. 38 h from the time of growth signal in the culture bottle). Numerous publications have similarly demonstrated that the use of the ACC system substantially reduces the turnaround time for antimicrobial susceptibility results [4, 23, 38, 40].

For Gram-positive microorganisms, complete concordance with SOC results was achieved. Importantly, the antibiotic panel provided by the ACC system was adequate for the pathogens identified in this group. A notable advantage of the ACC system is the inclusion of daptomycin in the susceptibility panel, an antibiotic for which routine AST was not performed with SOC methods during the study period. This expands the clinical utility of the ACC system, particularly in guiding targeted therapy for infections caused by Gram-positive organisms.

For Gram-negative bacteria, the ACC system performed 250 individual antibiotic susceptibility determinations, of which the majority of results (95%) were concordant with SOC results. In 5% of cases, discrepancies were observed: 1.2% of isolates were resistant by SOC but reported as susceptible by ACC, 1.6% were susceptible by SOC but reported as resistant by ACC (or when ACC identified the organism but failed to provide susceptibility results), and 1.2% showed differences when one method reported susceptible, increased exposure while the other classified the isolate as susceptible or resistant. These discrepancies involved antibiotics frequently used in the ICU, including meropenem, piperacillin/tazobactam, amikacin, and colistin, and were observed in clinically relevant pathogens such as Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Acinetobacter baumannii, Serratia marcescens, and Enterobacter cloacae. Similar discrepancies have been reported previously, particularly for Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Serratia marcescens, indicating that this issue may warrant further evaluation and potential ACC software updates [38]. Although infrequent, such errors may reduce clinicians’ confidence in relying solely on ACC results to guide antimicrobial therapy. In our cohort, very major errors (VMEs) did not result in inappropriate therapy; however, other reports have documented serious clinical consequences of VMEs in routine practice. Such errors are high-risk because they can lead to inappropriate treatment, adverse patient outcomes, and the spread of resistance. In a large multicenter study of carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales, semi-automated susceptibility testing platforms showed high rates of VMEs and major errors (MEs) [46]. These errors led to ineffective therapy in 7% of patients and were associated with significantly higher mortality (56% vs. 26%).

In our view, an important advantage of the ACC system is its broad panel of tested antibiotics, including relatively new agents such as ceftolozane/tazobactam and ceftazidime/avibactam, as well as aztreonam. These antibiotics were not available in the VITEK2 system during the study period and were therefore not routinely tested with SOC methods. This extended coverage enhances the clinical relevance of the ACC system, especially in settings with high levels of antimicrobial resistance.

An important limitation of the ACC system is the reporting of susceptibility results for pathogens detected in polymicrobial samples. Although designed to detect multiple microorganisms, in our study it failed to provide complete identification in all five such cases. Consequently, AST results were generated for only a single detected organism. According to EUCAST guidelines, AST must be performed on monoculture; therefore, in blood cultures containing mixed microbial populations, phenotypic methods such as the ACC system should not be applied, as they carry a substantial risk of diagnostic error [15]. This limitation further undermines confidence in the ACC system as a standalone method and may represent a significant barrier to its broader clinical adoption, particularly in the ICU, where polymicrobial infections are relatively common [35, 36, 44, 47].

In summary, discrepant findings have been reported for both Gram-positive and Gram-negative microorganisms [17, 36]. Only a few studies have shown complete concordance with SOC methods [16]. This again underscores the importance of evaluating the system within specific patient populations. It should be emphasized that meeting analytical performance thresholds does not fully eliminate the clinical risk of using suboptimal antibiotic therapy. Every discrepancy in results has the potential to influence therapeutic decisions, especially when rapid testing is used to support escalation or de-escalation strategies. Therefore, ACC results can be most safely applied to support early escalation, while de-escalation decisions should preferably rely on confirmatory SOC methods. This distinction between acceptable analytical error rates and potential consequences for individual patient should be considered when implementing novel rapid AST systems in high-risk populations.

Failure to identify pathogens due to technical errors of the ACC system

Another factor contributing to the reduced sensitivity of ACC testing is the occurrence of so-called technical errors. Although such errors are usually reported in published evaluations of the ACC system, they are not always included in performance analyses [17, 26, 38, 42, 43]. In our study, technical errors accounted for pathogen identification failures in 10% of samples. In these cases, the system produced no identification result despite the presence of visible microorganisms on Gram stain, inclusion of the pathogens in the ACC detection panel, and subsequent identification by SOC methods. Similar errors and their potential consequences have been documented by other investigators [32, 36, 37]. These failures diminish the perceived reliability of the ACC system among laboratory and ICU personnel and increase laboratory costs without yielding actionable results. While the absence of a result does not directly misguide therapy, it may delay initiation of appropriate antimicrobial treatment until SOC results become available.

Strengths and limitations of the study

The strength of our study lies in the fact that we included all results in the analysis, including those with yeast isolates, polymicrobial cultures, and cases in which the ACC system did not provide results due to technical errors. This comprehensive approach enhances the validity of the findings and reflects the true clinical applicability of the ACC system.

The study has several important limitations. First, the relatively small number of analyzed patients may amplify the impact of errors and influence performance estimates; further studies with larger ICU cohorts are needed to further validate the potential of the ACC system across all positive blood cultures. Second, the microbiology laboratory did not operate on a 24/7 basis. Consequently, the full potential of the ACC system to reduce time to pathogen identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing could not be fully exploited. It is likely that implementation of such a rapid diagnostic system in a continuously operating laboratory would result in more pronounced improvements in turnaround times and potentially greater clinical benefit. Third, the ACC organism - antibiotic panel is not fully comprehensive, restricting coverage of certain clinically relevant pathogen–drug combinations. As described in the Results section, it was not possible to compare all antimicrobial susceptibility testing results generated by the ACC system with SOC methods. This limitation was due to a lack of continuity in routine SOC susceptibility testing for certain antimicrobial agents during the study period, which restricted direct comparison for all antibiotics included in the ACC panel. Another important limitation is the absence of patient-centred clinical outcome analysis (e.g., mortality, ICU length of stay, duration of organ support), which precludes assessment of whether earlier availability of ACC results translated into meaningful clinical benefit. Future studies with larger sample sizes, specifically designed to evaluate the association between rapid pathogen ID and AST results and clinical outcomes, are warranted. Finally, antimicrobial susceptibility results were compared with standard methods routinely used in the microbiology laboratory rather than with reference (gold-standard) methods. However, reference methods are recommended in microbiological laboratories only in specific cases, as they are more time-consuming and costly [15].

Conclusions

In recent years, the introduction of rapid diagnostic tools and automated laboratory workflows has substantially shortened the time to microbiological results. The ACC system is one of several available platforms that enable earlier recognition of causative microorganisms and antimicrobial susceptibility patterns. It is relatively simple to operate in routine practice, as sample preparation and processing are straightforward, time-efficient, and do not require highly specialized or extensively trained personnel. However, as also emphasized in other scientific reports, the relatively high cost of testing should be taken into account and considered in the context of overall implementation expenditures [3, 4, 40]. While most systems rely on positive blood cultures, culture-free approaches and platforms capable of analyzing specimens other than blood are also employed in laboratories. Based on our findings and previously published data, rapid diagnostic tools cannot fully replace conventional microbiological methods. First, rapid systems are limited by predefined organism and antibiotic panels, restricting their ability to detect less common pathogens and resistance mechanisms outside the panel’s scope. Second, in polymicrobial infections, incomplete detection and partial AST reporting reduce their reliability. Third, although infrequent, discrepancies in antimicrobial susceptibility testing may have significant clinical consequences. Therefore, rapid diagnostics should be considered a valuable adjunct that enhances and accelerates clinical decision-making but does not substitute for standard microbiological methods. With ongoing technological advances, it is anticipated that rapid, accurate infection diagnostics will soon become a routine part of clinical practice, further improving patient outcomes and antimicrobial stewardship.
