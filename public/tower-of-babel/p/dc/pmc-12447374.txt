Biofilm Eradication Using Water‐Soluble Silver(I) Coumarin Complexes
Authors: Erika Mooney, Gordon Cooke, Emma Caraher, Fintan Kelleher, Bernadette S. Creaven
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Water‐soluble photostable coumarin acetate complexes of silver(I) are successfully synthesized and characterized and found to have the ability to eradicate preformed MRSA biofilms. Substitution with short PEG chains at the 4‐position of the coumarin ring allows the subsequent synthesis of water‐soluble coumarin oxyacetate ligands which successfully allowed silver(I) complex formation. The new complexes are characterized by IR and NMR spectroscopy, microanalysis and high‐resolution mass spectrometry where possible. Previous unPEGylated analogs has shown excellent antimicrobial activity against the pathogenic bacteria MRSA, Escherichia coli and Pseudomonas aeruginosa and that activity is maintained in the PEGylated complexes. However, the solubility of the un‐PEGylated analogs is limited to DMSO, and those silver(I) complexes are unstable in ambient light conditions. Both issues are resolved with the new PEGylated complexes reported here and importantly the ability to eradicate preformed biofilms of MRSA is demonstrated. In addition, a triphenylphosphine adduct complex is also synthesized, but this complex has reduced activity compared to the simple PEGylated complex.

Introduction

The relationship between antimicrobial resistance and bacterial virulence is complex and multifaceted, and understanding these dynamics is critical for developing effective strategies to combat bacterial infections. Virulence factors are the microbial elements which enable microbes to accomplish invasion of the host, and while they do not directly lead to the development of an infection, they aid in the survival of bacteria in hostile environments through the formation of biofilms.[ 1 ] Once bacteria establish this organized community, it is 10–1,000 times more tolerant to the effects of antibiotics than sessile bacterial cells.[ 2 ] Two of the most ubiquitous biofilm‐forming bacteria in diverse environments, which are notoriously resistant to antibiotics, are methicillin resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa), while Escherichia coli (E. coli) is also known to form biofilms.[ 3 , 4 ‐ 5 ] The problem posed by the ability of bacteria to evade the bacteriostatic and bactericidal action of many currently used antibiotics has initiated the development of innovative approaches which target the virulence factors associated with these mechanisms of resistance.[ 2 ]

The intricate cell–cell communications within the biofilm are controlled by a system termed quorum‐sensing (QS), allowing bacteria to rapidly adapt to harsh environments and develop resistance to antibiotic therapies.[ 6 ] A virtual study by Zeng et al. in 2008,[ 7 ] determined the coumarin scaffold and decorated coumarin derivatives to be novel drug candidates for microbial QS and biofilm formation inhibitors. Coumarin‐based compounds have been of interest to our research groups for many years, and since that study by Zeng et al. multiple studies have shown that coumarin derivatives effectively target the QS system of clinically important pathogens such as MRSA, P. aeruginosa and E. coli.[ 8 ] The effect coumarin compounds have on the biofilm formation of MRSA, responsible for most nosocomial infections, has also been studied. In a study by da Cunha et al.[ 9 ] the antibiofilm formation capability of the naturally derived coumarin compound “CNM” against methicillin‐susceptible S. aureus (MSSA) and MRSA, showed that this compound decreased the formation of both MSSA and MRSA biofilms, similar to the vancomycin control at a concentration of ≈114 µM. In a separate study,[ 10 ] the synthetic coumarin derivative “DCH” was found to selectively inhibit the MRSA biofilm formation over Gram‐negative bacterial strains tested, in a dose dependent manner, over a concentration range of 0.25–4 µg mL−1. More recently, the effect of coumarin on the biofilm formation of the PA01 strain of P. aeruginosa was investigated along with other Gram‐negative bacteria in a study by Ahmad et al.[ 11 ] They found that coumarin decreased the swimming motility of P. aeruginosa by 80%, at a concentration of 256 µg mL−1 and was not only capable of inhibiting the biofilm formation but could also alter the architecture of the biofilm. In another study,[ 12 ] the QS inhibitory activity of coumarin against multiple clinical isolates of P. aeruginosa was determined. Similar to the results obtained by Ahmad et al.[ 11 ] this study determined that coumarin plays a key role in the downregulation of genes associated with biofilm formation in the QS system. However, it concluded that although coumarin can reduce QS‐regulated virulence and biofilm formation in P. aeruginosa, its effect was strain dependent.

There have been relatively few studies on the inhibition of biofilm formation by coumarin‐derived metal complexes,[ 13 ] though a recent review has highlighted the potential for metal‐based approaches to act as antimicrobial agents against several pathogenic species.[ 14 ] Previously our group published a study on novel Ag(I) coumarin–carboxylate complexes, which were effective against P. aeruginosa biofilms’ formation.[ 15 ] Some of the complexes were ≅ 4–6 times more potent against the biofilm compared to planktonic bacteria, and introduction of phenanthroline as a second ligand to the Ag(I) complexes improved the activity of two of them against PA01 and ATCC 27,853 strains of P. aeruginosa. However, the poor aqueous solubility and photostability of these complexes, and indeed many of the antimicrobial complexes studied by our group in the past 20 years, have limited their development as potential therapeutics. In more recent years we have published a series of studies on silver(I) coumarin complexes whereby adduct formation using carbene or triphenylphosphine ligands has resulted in complexes with improved photostability, solubility in organic solvents, and good antimicrobial activity, but at that time we did not assess the ability of these complexes to inhibit biofilm formation.[ 16 ] In this work we detail the approach taken toward isolating a new series of coumarin‐derived silver(I) complexes with short polyethylene glycol (PEG) chains, which were designed to address a critical challenge posed by our previous studies, lack of aqueous solubility.

PEG is a linear polyether of ethylene glycol and is a nontoxic, nonimmunogenic, nonantigenic, water soluble FDA approved chemical.[ 17 ] Researchers have exploited the chemical properties of PEG to form PEG conjugates, which have enhanced permeability and retention of the drug molecules as well as an improved pharmacokinetic profile, leading to slower excretion from the body.[ 17a , 18 ] Several studies have also highlighted the role of PEGylation strategies in increasing the aqueous solubility of pharmacophores as central to improve therapeutic profile.[ 19 ] First described in the 1970s, PEGylation is defined as the modification of a protein, peptide, or nonpeptide molecule by the linking of one or more polyethylene (PEG) glycol chains.[ 18 , 20 ] Over 30 PEGylated drug products have been granted FDA approval, some of which being the PEGylated LNP‐mRNA‐based vaccines (Comirnaty and Spikevax) used for the immunization of the global population against the recent COVID‐19 pandemic.[ 21 ]

The use of PEGylation strategies to improve the therapeutic profiles of coumarins was investigated by Surya Prakash Rao et al.[ 22 ] who synthesized oligomeric alkoxy‐substituted coumarin compounds and observed that, on increasing of the chain length (n = 2 to n = 5) of the PEG side chains, the aqueous solubility of the compounds increased. In another study, 4‐methylcoumarin derivatives were anchored to long chain PEGs with a MW of ≥ 2000 via an ether bond through the von Pechmann reaction instead of using an ester linkage which is more readily hydrolyzed. By anchoring several coumarin derivatives to this large water‐soluble support, the final water‐soluble compounds were easily isolated via precipitation rather than a lengthy purification protocol.[ 23 ] Thus, to address the problem of nonaqueous solubility of the antimicrobial coumarin‐based complexes, this study investigates synthetic strategies to isolate water soluble coumarin complexes via the use of PEGylation strategies, a strategy less established for increasing the solubility of therapeutic metal complexes. In addition, preliminary investigation into the ability of the novel complexes, together with some of the complexes previously synthesized by our group, to target biofilms formed by important pathogenic bacteria P. aeruginosa, E. coli and MRSA, are reported herein.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis of PEGylated Derivatives of Coumarin‐7‐Oxyaceto Ligands

In our recent paper, the silver(I) complexes of coumarin‐7‐oxyacetate ligands showed excellent antibacterial activity against the three pathogens of interest here.[ 16a ] The introduction of a PEGylated side chain on the coumarin ring of the coumarin‐7‐oxyacetate ligand requires a free hydroxy group, but as shown in Scheme 1 , a free hydroxy group is also needed for alkylation of the coumarin ring prior to the formation of the acid ligating group and subsequent silver(I) complex formation. Thus, to isolate a PEGylated derivative of this type of complex, the 7‐position was first benzyl protected using a procedure described by Gaskell et al.[ 24 ] to prepare 4‐hydroxy‐7‐benzyloxycoumarin (3), via the formation of compound 2.

Synthesis of 4‐(mPEG)n‐coumarin‐7‐oxyacetate derivatives and silver(I) complexes, inset showing reference compounds 15 and 16.

PEGylation of 3 with compounds (mPEG) n ‐4‐methylbenzene sulfonate (n = 2 or 4), prepared via a known procedure with details given in Supporting information S1.1,[ 25 ] was achieved using microwave irradiation to form compounds 4 and 5 in yields of 60% and 81%, respectively. Compounds 4 and 5 were then catalytically debenzylated to give the hydroxy group at position 7 of the coumarin ring. Due to the difference in the solubility profiles of compounds 4 and 5, ascribed to the differing lengths of the PEG chain attached, a 50:50 mix of anhydrous DMF: dioxane was used to form compound 6, and anhydrous methanol was used for compound 7 in yields of 69% and 59%, respectively. Alkylation of 6 and 7 at the 7‐position to form the oxyacetate ethyl esters 8 and 9 resulted in yields of 84% and 92%, respectively, and acidic hydrolysis of these compounds gave the carboxylic acids 10 and 11 in yields of 53% and 69%, respectively.

The results obtained from microanalysis, and HRMS where possible, confirmed the formation of all compounds. The mass tolerance of compounds analyzed by HRMS was below 5 ppm. While the experimental %C and H of compounds 6, 7, and 11 agreed with theoretical values, for compounds 4, 8, and 10, the experimental %C and H results were consistent with the inclusion of one molecule of water in their lattice.

Analysis of compounds 4–11 was further confirmed by NMR spectroscopy. The full assignment of the 1H and 13C signals are summarized in the supporting information, (Tables S2‐3, Supporting Information) and were assigned using the numbering systems given in Figures S4‐5, Supporting Information. For compound 4 and 5, the lack of signals which corresponded to the three methyl protons and four aryl protons of the tosylate group at ≈ 2.42, 7.79, and 7.48 ppm, respectively, as well as the disappearance of the ‐OH signal at 13.36 ppm of compound 3 confirmed their formation. The formation of hydroxy‐substituted compounds 6 and 7, was confirmed through disappearance of the aryl protons, as the singlet at 5.21 ppm (‐CH2) and the appearance of the proton of the hydroxy group at 10.59 ppm. Formation of the ethyl esters 8 and 9 was confirmed via the presence of signals corresponding to the ethyl group in both cases, and then subsequently the disappearance of these protons was then observed on the formation of the carboxylic acids 10 and 11. For all compounds, the signals corresponding to the (mPEG) n functions remained the same. A 1H NMR stack of compounds 3, 4, 6, 8, and 10 is given in Figure S6, Supporting Information, with a similar figure for the (mPEG)4 derivatives given in Figure S7, Supporting Information. Full spectra are given in Supporting Information Figures S8‐47.

Synthesis and Characterization of (mPEG)n Substituted Silver(I) Complexes 12 and 13 and Bis‐TPP Adduct, 14

Scheme 1 also outlines the reactions used to isolate the silver(I) complex of 4‐(mPEG)2‐coumarin‐7‐oxyacetate ligand, which precipitated from the aqueous reaction solution complex 12. Isolation of the analogs (mPEG)4 complex 13 was only achieved by reacting compound 11 with equimolar amounts of silver oxide in anhydrous acetonitrile at 40 °C and only in low amounts. Subsequently, 12 and TPP were reacted in a 1:2 mmolar ratio to form the bis TPP adduct, 14. HRMS analysis of complexes 12 and 13, the latter was isolated as an oil, confirmed their successful formation. Microanalytical data for 12 and 14 confirmed that the experimental values obtained for %C and H agreed with the theoretical values calculated as shown in Table 2 .

NMR Spectroscopic Analysis, Photostability Assessment and Solubility Profile of Compounds 12–14

NMR spectra of all three compounds were recorded in deuterated DMSO and with full data given in Supporting Information Table S4 and Figures S48‐58. The disappearance of the acidic proton at ≈13 ppm in the spectra of ligands 10 and 11, as well as an upfield shift in the signal corresponding to the proton closest to the site of complex formation (H11) from 4.83 ppm to 4.49 and 4.37 ppm for 12 and 13, respectively, evidenced the formation of the complexes. In addition, formation of the bis‐TPP adduct of 12 was confirmed by the addition of signals in the aryl region of the 1H NMR spectrum in complex 14 integrating to 30 protons. Assignments of 13C NMR spectra using DEPT90, 135, HSQC and HMBC experiments and are given in supplementary information, showing complex formation only resulted in small signal shifts in all cases.

The solubility of these complexes (10 mg) in common laboratory solvents (2 mL) was assessed. In comparison to the solubility of the unPEGylated parent compound, which was only soluble in DMSO, compound 12, which bears the (mPEG)2 group at the 4‐position of the coumarin ring, extended the solubility to chloroform, DCM, acetonitrile, ethanol, methanol, and most importantly, water. Complex 13 was soluble in water, methanol, and DMSO. The solubility of 14, its bis‐TPP adduct, was limited to a range of organic solvents only, and given the low yield of complex 13, its TPP adduct was not formed. The photostability of complexes 12 and 14 was assessed by 1H NMR spectroscopy in DMSO over a 96 h period, with the full stability profiles of these complexes given in the Supporting Information, Figures S59−64. In the case of 12, its photostability in D2O was also assessed. Neither of the complexes were observed to degrade in solution over the 96 h timeframe.

Thus, a new synthetic route allowed for the preparation of 4‐[m(PEG) n )(coumarin‐7‐oxyacetate)] ligands 10–11 and silver(I) complexes (n = 2 or 4) 12 and 13. The silver(I) complex 12 and the bis‐TPP adduct 14 were shown to be photostable for up to 96 h in solution. In addition to this, silver(I) complex 12 was aqueous soluble, showing that the introduction of a PEG chains can improve the solubility of coumarin silver(I) complexes significantly. Interestingly, while the bis‐TPP adduct 14, exhibited good solubility in a range of laboratory organic solvents, it was not soluble in aqueous solutions. As the low yield of complex 13 did not allow the formation of the TPP adduct nor allow for its full testing for antimicrobial activity, it was excluded from the following studies.

Determination of Antibacterial Activity of Synthesized Coumarin Silver(I) Complexes at Using the Broth Microdilution Assay

The antimicrobial activity of ligands 10 and 11, complex 12, and its bis‐TPP adduct 14 against E. coli (ATCC‐25,922), P. aeruginosa (PA01 ATCCBAA‐47) and MRSA(ATCC‐43,300) were assessed using the EUCAST guidelines.[ 26 ] Due to precipitation issues with some of the reference compounds, a final test concentration range of 0–128 µg mL−1 was determined. Full data is given in Supporting Information, Tables S5‐S10.

This data could be compared to that previously reported for the analogous unPEGylated ligands and complexes 15 and 16, two complexes shown as an inset in Scheme 1, which were previously identified as having excellent antimicrobial activity.[ 16b ] In those studies, the ligands used to synthesize complex 15 and 16 were inactive against the bacterial strains tested, with MIC50 values > 1024 µg mL−1 and this proved to be the case for the bis‐triphenylphosphino adduct 14 as well as the PEGylated ligands 10 and 11 (Tables S11−13, Supporting Information). Silver(I) complexes 12, 15, and 16 were identified as most effective at inhibiting the growth of E. coli, P. aeruginosa and MRSA in the initial screening study carried out over a large concentration range. For the studies reported here, the antibacterial activities against each individual strain were focused on results obtained for E. coli, P. aeruginosa and MRSA at concentrations of each tested compound at 8–16, 12–32 and 30–66 µg mL−1 respectively. Figures S65‐67, Supporting Information, illustrates the overall activity of the complexes and controls against the three pathogenic bacteria in these concentration ranges and Table 3 indicates the overall MIC values for the complexes and controls tested.

As can be seen from the MIC values in Table 3, all 3 complexes were more effective at inhibiting the growth of E. coli than either of the reference antibiotics. All 3 complexes could also inhibit the growth of P. aeruginosa, with the PEGylated form of complex 16, namely complex 12, showing increased activity relative to its unPEGylated analog. To inhibit the growth of MRSA, a higher concentration of all three complexes was required. Two of the complexes, 15 and 16, inhibited the growth of MRSA at a concentration of 30 µg mL−1 but at this concentration, these complexes were considerably more active than amikacin and erythromycin. However, to inhibit growth of MRSA required a higher concentration of 12 (42 µg mL−1).

Each of the complexes proved to have comparable activity to amikacin against all three strains of bacteria. The studies here, over a focused concentration range, confirmed the antimicrobial activity of complexes 15 and 16 and importantly identified that the formation of the water‐soluble PEGylated derivative complex 12 did not hinder that activity. As the major drawback to the development of complexes 15 and 16 as therapeutic treatments was their insolubility in aqueous media, introducing the PEG functionality of complex 12 overcomes this issue. All the silver(I) complexes showed selectivity toward the more virulent Gram‐negative bacterial strains. The variation in the activity of the complexes against the different bacterial strain types may be due to the permeability of the bacterial cells rather than the interactions with the B subunit of bacterial DNA gyrase enzymes, which is the mode of action of other coumarin‐based antibiotics such as novobiocin, clorobiocin, and coumermycin A1.[ 11 ]

For the MBC testing, MHA plates were prepared as previously described.[ 16a ] MBC was determined by taking 50 μL of the well for the MIC value, and separately from the well above (with higher concentration) and below (with a lower concentration). The results of the MBC determination of the three complexes and the reference antibiotics against E. coli, P. aeruginosa and MRSA revealed that at a concentration of ≥16 µg mL−1, all three complexes proved to be bactericidal against E. coli at relatively low concentrations. This bactericidal activity is directly comparable to that of amikacin and significantly better than erythromycin. In contrast to this, the concentration of the complexes required to exhibit a bactericidal effect on P. aeruginosa was > 32 µg mL−1 for 16, while 15 exhibited this effect at double this concentration. Unfortunately, the bactericidal effect of the three complexes was not comparable to that of amikacin, but it was better than that of erythromycin. Complex 12, did not prove to be bactericidal against P. aeruginosa at the concentrations tested. In addition to this, none of the complexes were shown to have bactericidal action against MRSA.

Biofilm Assays

Complexes 12, 15, and 16 exhibited antibiofilm activity against MRSA (Figure 1A ), however, mature biofilms are deemed more tolerant to the effects of antibiotics and therefore more difficult to treat.[ 27 ] To determine the biofilm eradication capability of the complexes on a preformed biofilm, a biofilm challenge assay was carried out, and the results are presented in Figure 1B. It was found that the complexes and control antibiotics had little to no effect on the preformed biofilms of E. coli and P. aeruginosa over the test concentration range (data not shown). However, the complexes did exhibit significant activity against MRSA in both the prevention and challenge assays. Compounds 12, 15, and 16 showed significant antibiofilm activity against MRSA during the prevention assay, with concentrations ≥16 µg mL−1 markedly inhibiting biofilm formation over 24 h and near‐complete eradication observed at concentrations ≥128 µg mL−1 (Figure 1A). Notably, in the challenge assay, where biofilms were allowed to mature for 24 h prior to treatment, concentrations ≥256 µg mL−1 of these compounds were capable of almost completely eradicating established biofilms. This striking activity against mature biofilms highlights the potential of these compounds as therapeutic candidates for the treatment of persistent biofilm‐associated infections.

Such efficacy suggests a possible mode of action involving disruption of the extracellular polymeric substance matrix, although this remains to be confirmed. Further mechanistic investigations will be necessary to elucidate the precise interactions at play. Nonetheless, considering the well‐documented clinical challenge of eradicating established biofilms, particularly those formed by MRSA, these compounds represent promising leads for the development of novel antibiofilm therapeutics.

Previous studies have revealed that coumarin derivatives can disrupt the QS system of bacteria and subsequently inhibit biofilm formation.[ 28 ] In addition to this, silver(I) ions and silver‐nanoparticles (silverNPs) have been shown to possess the ability to inhibit the biofilm formation of several bacterial strains and reduce infection, especially in regard to bio‐medical implants.[ 29 ] As well as this, studies show that silverNPs have the ability to disrupt the production of the extracellular polymeric matrix subsequently decreasing the adhesion ability of bacteria, which is critical to the formation of biofilms.[ 30 ] While these studies could be suggestive of the mechanism by which the silver(I) complexes tested in this study may exhibit antibiofilm formation activity against the strains tested, further studies to determine their MOA are required.

Conclusion

In previous years we had successfully isolated a number of very active silver(I) complexes of coumarin‐based ligands but despite repeated synthetic strategies, including adduct formation with TPP and N‐heterocyclic carbenes, we were unable to isolate water soluble complexes. This work successfully isolated water‐soluble coumarin‐based silver(I) complexes whose antimicrobial activity against pathogenic bacteria was confirmed. In all, three new coumarin silver(I) complexes were isolated for this study using a new PEGylated ligand as the ligating group in all three complexes. Whilst the formation of a triphenylphosphine hybrid complex rendered the complex insoluble in aqueous solution, the isolation of photostable water‐soluble PEGylated coumarin oxyacetate silver(I) complexes was successfully achieved. The broth microdilution method was used to assess the bacteriostatic and bactericidal activities of silver(I) complexes 15 and 16, two complexes which had shown excellent antimicrobial activity in preliminary studies, as well as the PEGylated complex 12 and its bis‐TPP adduct 14, against E. coli, P. aeruginosa and MRSA. From this study, complexes 12, 15, and 16 were identified as exhibiting the most effective antimicrobial activity against all three test strains. All three complexes were shown to be selectively more active at inhibiting the growth of the Gram‐negative bacteria E. coli and P. aeruginosa in comparison to the Gram‐positive bacterium MRSA. However, a study of the bactericidal activity of the most active complexes revealed that these complexes only had a bacteriostatic effect on MRSA at the concentrations tested, while all three complexes exhibited bactericidal activity against E. coli and P. aeruginosa at concentrations as low as 16 and 32 µg mL−1 respectively.

A study on the ability of the complexes isolated to prevent biofilm formation identified that silver(I) complexes 12, 15, and 16 were effective inhibitors of the biofilm formation of MRSA. The same complexes exhibited poor inhibition of P. aeruginosa and E. coli biofilms but results here have shown the complexes were able to eradicate the preformed biofilm of MRSA, with complexes 15 and 12 exhibiting the most potent activity.

Overall, it can be concluded that the silver(I) complexes, 12, 15, and 16 have therapeutic potential as novel antimicrobial agents against several ESKAPE pathogens, namely E. coli, P. aeruginosa and MRSA. Of the four complexes, 12, due to its solubility properties, solution, and photostability profile, holds the most potential to be developed as a novel antimicrobial therapy, as it overcomes the significant drawbacks associated with previously synthesized coumarin‐based silver(I) complexes. Future studies will focus on MOA of biofilm inhibition of the most promising compounds, together with adjuvant studies and stability evaluation in biological media. The compounds investigated here have good photostability in aqueous and DMSO solution, but stability in complex media will need to be established before these complexes can be fully exploited as new therapeutics.

Experimental Section

Equipment

All melting points were recorded on a Stuart Scientific SMP1 melting point apparatus. All values were taken up to 300 °C with a temperature ramp of 0.5 °C per minute and were uncorrected. All samples for IR analysis were prepared as KBr discs and infrared spectra recorded in the region of 4000—400 cm−1 using IR Prestige‐21 Shimadzu infrared spectrometer. NMR spectra were obtained using a Bruker Advance III 500 MHz spectrometer, with all chemical shifts reported as parts per million (ppm, δ). This instrument operated at 500 MHz with a typical resolution of 0.28 Hz for 1H NMR and at 125 MHz with a typical resolution of 0.45 Hz for 13C NMR. All spectra were either recorded in DMSO‐d6 or CDCl3. Signal assignments were aided using standard spectral experiments such as DEPT 90, DEPT 135, and HSQC. The percentage compositions (%C, %H, %N) of some compounds were determined at the Microanalytical laboratory, University College Dublin, using an Exeter CE440 elemental analyzer. Other compounds were analyzed at the Microanalytical laboratory in NUI Maynooth using a Flash EA1112 series NC analyzer, with Eager 300 software. Analysis of PEGylated compounds were performed using an Agilent Technologies 6530 Accurate Mass Q‐TOF LC/MS system tuned in positive mode at 100—2500 M/Z and data was interpreted using Mass Hunter Software packages. Samples for analysis were dissolved in mobile phase using HPLC grade solvent and analyzed on a C18 Phenomenex column using either acetonitrile: water: 0.1% acetic acid or methanol: water: 0.1% formic acid mobile phases with a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min. Injection volume was set to 1.0 µL. Silver(I) complexes were analyzed by continuous infusion using a 50:50 methanol: water mobile phase. Synthesis of PEGylated compounds was performed using a CEM Discover 2.0 microwave synthesizer. Reaction suspensions were stirred at the highest mixing speed under irradiation at 140 °C for 25 min.

Synthesis

All reagents were purchased from Sigma, TCI Chemicals, Fisher Scientific or Fluorochem and used without further purification. General Details of Bacterial Strains Used for Biological Studies

The E. coli (ATCC‐25,922) and methicillin resistant S. aureus (ATCC‐43,300) test strains used were obtained from frozen stocks of our collaborators in UCD while the P. aeruginosa (PA01 ATCCBAA‐47) test strain was obtained from frozen stocks from the microbiological laboratory in Technological University Dublin, Tallaght. Fresh frozen stocks of each of the test strains were made by culturing each strain individually in tryptone soy broth (TSB) and storing them with 10% glycerol at −80 °C. Prior to their use in an experiment, they were passaged twice on tryptone soy agar plate.

General Details of Bacterial Strains Used for Biological Studies: Preparation of Microtiter Test Plates for the Broth Microdilution Assay

Sterile 96‐well microtiter plates were preloaded with silver(I) complexes and control substances up to 5 days before use, sealed, and stored at −20 °C. Plates were thawed at 37 °C immediately prior to the assay.

Silver(I) complexes were screened for antibacterial activity using broth microdilution across 0–1,024 μg mL−1 in triplicate to determine the optimal test range. For each assay, complexes were prepared at 0–256 µg mL−1 in solution, and 50 µL was dispensed into designated wells. Control wells received serial dilutions of an antibiotic reference compound, DMSO, or ethanol, also in Mueller–Hinton broth (MHB; 50 µL). Triplicate MHB‐only wells served as negative controls.

Test strains were cultured overnight in 10 mL MHB at 37 °C, then subcultured into 100 mL fresh MHB and grown to mid‐log phase (OD600 = 0.6–0.8). Cultures were diluted to 1 × 106 CFU mL−1, and 50 µL was added to each well, yielding a final volume of 100 µL, 5 × 105 CFU mL−1 inoculum, and compound concentrations of 0–128 µg mL−1.

Plates were incubated aerobically at 37 °C for 24 h with mild agitation (≈65 rpm). Growth was measured via OD600 using a plate reader. Experiments were performed in triplicate on separate days, with six technical replicates per complex and triplicate controls per run.

General Details of Bacterial Strains Used for Biological Studies: General Method for Biofilm Inhibition Assay

Test strains were cultured in TSB to support robust biofilm formation. An initial 10 mL overnight culture was incubated at 37 °C for 18 h with agitation (180 rpm), followed by subculture into 100 mL fresh TSB and growth to mid‐log phase (OD600 = 0.6–0.8). Cultures were then diluted to 1 × 106 CFU mL−1.

Sterile, round‐bottomed 96‐well polystyrene plates were preloaded with 50 µL of each test complex or control (0–2,048 µg mL−1), followed by 50 µL of bacterial suspension (final 5 × 105 CFU mL−1; compound range 0–1,024 µg mL−1). Plates were incubated statically at 37 °C in a humidified chamber for 24, 48, or 72 h.

At each time point, nonadherent cells and medium were removed. Wells were washed thrice with deionized water and air‐dried for 45 min. Adherent biofilms were stained with 150 µL of 0.1% crystal violet for 30 min at room temperature, washed, and air‐dried for 15 min. Bound dye was solubilized with 200 µL of 95% ethanol containing 0.05% Triton X‐100. Biofilm biomass was quantified by OD600 measurement.

All assays were performed in triplicate using independent cultures. Each compound was tested in three technical replicates; controls were assessed in duplicate.

General Details of Bacterial Strains Used for Biological Studies: General Method for the Biofilm Challenge Assay

For this assay, the test strains were cultured using the same method as for the inhibition assay. Sterile, polystyrene round‐bottomed 96‐well microtiter plates were inoculated with the test organism (50 µL, 1 × 106 CFU mL−1) and diluted 1:2 with fresh TSB to a final inoculum concentration of 5 × 105 CFU mL−1. The plates were incubated in a humidified incubator at 37 °C for 24 h. After 24 h, the preformed biofilms were exposed to each test complex, alongside antibiotic controls, over a concentration range of 0–1,024 µg mL−1. The plates were then incubated in the same humidified incubator at 37 °C for a further 24 h. After this time, the biofilm washing and staining procedure, as described above, was used, and the OD of each well measured at 590 nm using a multiplate reader. An N of 3 with 3 replicates each were tested per complex, while 2 replicates per N were used for the antibiotics.
