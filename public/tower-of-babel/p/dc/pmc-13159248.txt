Metabolic reprogramming of CAR-T cells: a multi-pronged strategy to conquer the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment
Authors: Zhi Zheng, Zhichao Chen, Zhiwei Zhang, Zhi Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
CAR-T cell therapy has demonstrated remarkable success in hematologic malignancies, yet its application in solid tumors continues to face formidable challenges posed by metabolic dysregulation within the tumor microenvironment (TME). The TME impairs CAR-T cell effector function and persistence through mechanisms such as nutrient deprivation (e.g., glucose scarcity), disrupted lipid metabolism, and the accumulation of immunosuppressive metabolites (e.g., kynurenine). This review systematically outlines multidimensional strategies to enhance CAR-T cell efficacy through metabolic reprogramming: in glucose metabolism, approaches such as ex vivo metabolic priming, genetic engineering, and pharmacological modulation improve glucose uptake and utilization; in lipid metabolism, domain selection, gene modification, and combinatorial interventions optimize fatty acid oxidation and memory phenotype maintenance; in amino acid metabolism, engineering and pharmacological inhibition alleviate essential amino acid depletion and metabolite-induced immunosuppression. Collectively, these strategies endow CAR-T cells with greater metabolic adaptability, laying the foundation for breakthroughs in tumor therapy. Looking ahead, multi-pathway synergistic interventions, dynamically controllable metabolic regulation, and personalized metabolic adaptation are poised to become central pillars in the development of next-generation CAR-T cells.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-026-02864-6.

Introduction

CAR (chimeric antigen receptor)-T cell therapy is an adoptive immunotherapy approach wherein T cells are genetically modified to display synthetic receptors that selectively recognize antigens expressed on tumor cells. The expression of these synthetic chimeric receptors on the T cell surface enables them to localize and engage malignant targets with precision [1]. CAR-T cell therapy marks a major breakthrough in cancer immunotherapy, demonstrating high efficacy and sustained clinical benefit in individuals with hematologic cancers [2]. Nonetheless, translating the success of CAR-T cell therapy from hematologic malignancies to solid tumors has proven challenging, largely due to factors such as antigenic diversity, suboptimal T cell functionality and persistence, restricted tumor infiltration, and the immunosuppressive nature of the tumor microenvironment (TME) [3, 4].

Dysregulated metabolic pathways have been increasingly recognized as key contributors to the initiation and advancement of solid malignancies. For example, early mutations in the metabolic enzyme isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) lead to the accumulation of the oncometabolite D-2-hydroxyglutarate (D2HG). This accumulation drives oncogenesis by inducing epigenetic hypermethylation and blocking cellular differentiation—a well-documented initiating event in gliomas and other malignancies. Within the TME, metabolic stressors—such as nutrient deprivation and hypoxia—are critical drivers of CAR-T-cell exhaustion. Tumor cells within the TME exhibit a strong dependence on glycolytic metabolism—a phenomenon known as the Warburg effect—resulting in excessive glucose consumption and a substantial reduction in glucose availability for CAR-T cells [5]. Since glycolytic metabolism serves as the primary energy source for effector T cells, its restriction downregulates the expression of the glucose transporter GLUT1, thereby impairing T cell activation, proliferation, and cytotoxic function [6]. In addition, dysregulated lipid metabolism within the TME has been implicated in T cell impairment. Lipid utilization plays a vital role throughout the CAR-T cell lifecycle, particularly in supporting the metabolic demands of memory T cells, which is crucial for their persistence and therapeutic potency [7]. The accumulation of metabolic byproducts such as lactate, adenosine, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) within the TME also exerts multifaceted suppressive effects on T cell functionality [8]. Accordingly, reprogramming the glycolytic and lipid metabolic pathways of CAR-T cells through genetic engineering or pharmacological intervention represents a promising strategy to enhance their persistence, effector function, and antitumor activit.

Metabolic adaptability is therefore a decisive factor for CAR-T cell survival and efficacy within the hostile TME. Metabolic reprogramming has emerged as a cornerstone in the development of next-generation CAR-T therapies. By strategically rewiring core metabolic pathways—encompassing glycolysis, lipid, and amino acid metabolism—CAR-T cells can be endowed with superior metabolic fitness and durability. This holistic enhancement is imperative for unleashing their full antitumor potential and achieving breakthroughs in treating solid tumors.

The essential role of glycolytic metabolism in CAR-T cell function and its reprogramming strategies

Under physiological oxygen levels, cellular energy production typically involves cytoplasmic glycolytic metabolism coupled with mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation. In contrast, hypoxic environments shift energy reliance toward glycolytic metabolism. Notably, cancer cells preferentially engage glycolytic metabolism for ATP generation even under normoxic conditions [9]. The intensified metabolic demands of tumor cells deplete glucose resources and lead to excessive lactate accumulation, which acidifies the microenvironment. This lactate-induced stress compromises T cell proliferation and effector capabilities, thereby placing CAR-T cells at a metabolic disadvantage [10]. During tumor engagement, CAR-T cells upregulate glycolytic flux to support their rapid proliferation and cytolytic function [11]. Glycolytic metabolism is not merely an auxiliary energy source but serves as a central metabolic pathway that supports the rapid proliferation, acquisition of cytotoxic function, and maintenance of the effector phenotype in activated CD8⁺ effector T cells and CAR-T cells [11]. The following section synthesizes recent advances to systematically elucidate strategies for reshaping glycolytic metabolism in order to overcome the metabolic constraints faced by CAR-T cells (Fig. 1).

Ex vivo metabolic priming: rewiring glycolytic metabolism during manufacturing

CAR-T therapy is a form of cell-based treatment, more precisely defined as autologous live-cell immunotherapy engineered through genetic modification. Moreover, CAR-T therapy functions as a living drug, with its efficacy contingent upon cellular persistence and survival [12]. Therapeutic T cell expansion is typically conducted under metabolically intensive culture conditions designed to maximize cellular proliferation. However, conventional ex vivo expansion protocols often yield T cells with impaired metabolic and functional profiles, exhibiting greater dependence on aerobic glycolytic metabolism compared to their in vivo counterparts. This approach compromises the metabolic flexibility and in vivo persistence of CAR-T cells [13]. Therefore, by modulating the expansion process, it is possible to generate metabolically fitter CAR-T cell products with enhanced memory phenotypes—a strategy already being explored in cutting-edge research.

Using naive and previously activated resting CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells isolated from 6- to 8-week-old mice, Ashley V. Menk et al. demonstrated that proximal T cell receptor (TCR) signaling modulated glucose metabolism by phosphorylating and activating pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 1 (PDHK1) [14]. This kinase suppressed pyruvate dehydrogenase activity, thereby limiting the mitochondrial import of pyruvate and promoting its conversion to lactate [14]. Dichloroacetate (DCA), a compound formerly used in the treatment of lactic acidosis, acted as an inhibitor of PDHK1 [15–17]. Building upon prior discoveries, Andrew T. Frisch and colleagues conducted in-depth studies revealing that modulation of glycolytic metabolism with DCA during T cell expansion enhanced mitochondrial function, preserved stem-like characteristics, and improved antitumor activity in murine TCR-transgenic and human CAR-T cells [13]. DCA-treated cells exhibited reduced dependence on glucose and instead utilized physiologically abundant carbon substrates present in serum. Furthermore, DCA exposure facilitated mitochondrial-to-chromatin metabolic flux, leading to increased histone acetylation at genomic loci associated with cellular longevity [13]. DCA-conditioned T cells exhibited a rapid quantitative advantage following adoptive transfer in immunocompetent C57BL/6 mice bearing B16-F10 melanoma tumors; within 24 h post-infusion, their representation exceeded that of control cells by up to 8-fold (mean ratio 3:1) across peripheral blood, spleen, lymph nodes, and tumor sites [13]. A recent study proposed an advanced strategy to enhance CAR-T cell performance by implementing in a nutrient-rich environment during manufacturing. This method, termed metabolic priming (MP), involved initial T cell activation under low-glucose and low-glutamine conditions, followed by expansion in a nutrient-rich environment. Such priming effectively reduced terminal differentiation and promoted the development of a central memory phenotype with superior persistence [18]. MP-optimized anti-GD2 TRAC-CAR T cells exhibited diminished glycolytic activity and enhanced oxidative metabolism, as indicated by elevated NAD(P)H activity. Concurrently, the secretion of inflammatory cytokines such as IFN-γ and IL-2 was attenuated, potentially mitigating toxicity and improving metabolic adaptability. This ex vivo metabolic reprogramming translated into robust in vivo antitumor efficacy, with MP CAR-T cells demonstrating improved persistence and tumor clearance in an immunodeficient NSG mouse xenograft model bearing CHLA-20 neuroblastoma tumors [18]. As a glycolytic inhibitor, 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2DG) was found to impair antitumor T cell activity, given that glycolytic metabolism fuels rapid clonal expansion and underpins key effector functions, notably the production of IFN-γ. However, a recent study revealed that short-term exposure to low-dose 2DG exerted potential immunostimulatory effects on activated human T cells derived from peripheral blood mononuclear cells [19]. This treatment not only enhanced IFN-γ secretion but also downregulated the exhaustion marker programmed death 1 (PD1) and upregulated the early activation marker CD69 [19]. Ex vivo metabolic reprogramming strategies—such as MP, or the use of DCA and low-dose 2DG—enhance mitochondrial function, limit terminal differentiation, enrich memory phenotypes, and improve in vivo persistence and antitumor efficacy of CAR-T cells by modulating glycolytic activity. These approaches represent key innovations in optimizing CAR-T cell manufacturing.

In addition to modulating culture medium composition and incorporating metabolic agents, adjusting the strength of T cell activation signals during manufacturing has also been identified as an effective strategy for metabolic reprogramming [20–22]. Researchers discovered that gradually reducing the amount of αCD3/inducible costimulator activation beads to attenuate TCR/costimulatory signal strength yielded Th17 cells with superior metabolic profiles [23]. These cells exhibited diminished glucose dependence, shifted toward more efficient central carbon metabolic pathways, and accumulated the key metabolite phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP). This distinct metabolic state translated into enhanced effector function; when redirected with a mesothelin-specific CAR, these Th17 cells demonstrated markedly improved clearance of human mesothelioma. Notably, overexpression of the PEP-regulating enzyme PCK1 rescued the functional deficits induced by strong activation signals, directly underscoring the central role of metabolic reprogramming [23]. Beyond its efficacy, this approach significantly reduced reagent consumption, offering a cost-effective and clinically translatable strategy—representing a simple yet powerful advancement in CAR-T cell manufacturing. Together, these ex vivo metabolic reprogramming strategies highlight the importance of optimizing culture conditions to enhance CAR-T cell functionality.

Genetic engineering strategies: enhancing glycolytic capacity

Beyond exogenous modulation of culture conditions, direct genetic engineering of CAR-T cell metabolic pathways—such as overexpressing key genes involved in glucose metabolism—can equip these cells with metabolic advantages to outcompete tumor cells within the nutrient-deprived TME. A central focus of this strategy involves enhancing glucose uptake by upregulating glucose transporters (GLUTs), thereby enabling T cells to prevail in the competition for this critical nutrient.

Among the metabolic targets, GLUT1 has received particular attention. To overcome nutrient competition within the TME—primarily driven by tumor—researchers have employed a genetic engineering strategy to enforce GLUT1 expression in CAR-T cells. In multiple immunodeficient NSG mouse xenograft models—specifically systemic NALM6 acute lymphoblastic leukemia, a K7-CD19 renal cell carcinoma metastasis model, and orthotopic U251 glioblastoma—GLUT1 overexpression markedly enhanced the antitumor efficacy of two clinically relevant CAR constructs (19-28z and IL13Rα2-BBz). Mechanistically, this enhancement was attributed to the promotion of stem-like memory phenotypes, upregulation of genes associated with glycolytic metabolism and mitochondrial respiration, and reduction of exhaustion markers [24]. In a more in-depth study aimed at directly testing the hypothesis that glucose availability limits effector CAR-T cell function, researchers performed mechanistic investigations by stably overexpressing the glucose transporter GLUT1 in primary human CAR-T cells [25]. GLUT1 overexpression significantly increased glucose uptake, glycolytic activity, oxidative phosphorylation, and glycolytic reserve. It also induced broad metabolic reprogramming, including enhanced glutathione-mediated resistance to ROS and elevated inosine accumulation. Functionally, these metabolic adaptations were associated with reduced T cell exhaustion and increased Th17 differentiation. Upon tumor challenge, GLUT1-overexpressing CAR-T cells secreted higher levels of proinflammatory cytokines and exhibited augmented cytotoxicity in vitro, while demonstrating superior tumor control and sustained persistence in mouse models [25]. Moreover, the application of genetic engineering strategies in both solid and hematologic malignancies has further validated their broad utility. In hepatocellular carcinoma, GPC3-targeted CAR-T cells overexpressing either GLUT1 or acylglycerol kinase enhanced anti-apoptotic capacity via activation of the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway, resulting in improved in vivo persistence and antitumor efficacy [26]. To address the metabolic challenges in acute myeloid leukemia, T cells co-expressing GLUT1 and mitochondrial transcription factor A competitively deprived leukemic blasts of glucose, thereby reversing T cell dysfunction and effectively reducing leukemia burden [27]. Notably, genetic interventions extend beyond direct overexpression of metabolic genes. Evidence indicated that overexpression of microRNA-143 also enabled effective metabolic reprogramming. By targeting GLUT1, miR-143 modulated glycolytic metabolism to promote memory T cell differentiation, reduce apoptosis, and ultimately enhanced the antitumor activity of CAR-T cells [28].

In response to the severe glucose-restricted microenvironment characteristic of solid tumors, researchers have further elucidated the distinct roles and therapeutic potential of various GLUT isoforms. Notably, not all GLUT proteins function equally under low-glucose conditions. Although CAR-T cells typically express high levels of GLUT1, its low affinity for glucose limits its ability to restore cellular function under nutrient-deprived conditions [29–31]. In contrast, enforced expression of GLUT3, which possesses the highest glucose affinity among GLUT family members, significantly enhanced CAR-T cell activation, effector function, and antitumor efficacy both in vitro and in vivo [32]. Mechanistic studies revealed that GLUT3 overexpression potentiated PI3K/Akt signaling and improved mitochondrial adaptability and oxidative phosphorylation, offering a novel strategy to boost CAR-T cell performance in solid tumor settings [32]. Similarly, in solid tumor models, GLUT5 expression enhanced the effector function of macrophages, CD8⁺ T cells, and CAR-T cells under glucose-restricted conditions in vitro. GLUT5-expressing T cells exhibited elevated fructolytic activity and demonstrated superior antitumor efficacy in both syngeneic and human xenograft mouse models, with effects further amplified upon fructose supplementation [33]. Collectively, these findings highlight that metabolic engineering via GLUT5 enables immune cells to effectively utilize fructose, thereby strengthening antitumor immunity within glucose-deprived TME [33].

In addition to optimizing nutrient acquisition, genetic engineering was also employed to neutralize metabolic barriers within the TME. A representative example involved IDH-mutant cancers, where accumulation of the oncometabolite D2HG created a profoundly immunosuppressive milieu [34, 35]. D2HG directly impaired CAR-T cell expansion, cytokine production, and central memory formation, while increasing glucose uptake—processes that are metabolically demanding and reliant on intact energy pathways. To overcome this challenge, CAR-T cells were engineered to overexpress D2HGDH, the enzyme responsible for degrading D2HG, thereby effectively eliminating this metabolic obstacle [36]. Under high D2HG conditions, D2HGDH-overexpressing CAR-T cells restored their functional potency, including robust metabolic activity, and exhibited superior antitumor efficacy in vivo [36]. This strategy underscored that restoring a permissive metabolic environment was as critical as enhancing nutrient uptake for optimal CAR-T cell performance. In summary, genetic engineering of GLUTs and metabolic enzymes represents a promising avenue to enhance CAR-T cell efficacy in nutrient-poor TME.

Pharmacological modulation in vivo: systemic and direct interventions

Pharmacological modulation in vivo can also intervene in the reprogramming of glycolytic metabolism in CAR-T cells, offering another promising avenue to enhance CAR-T cell therapy. This strategy aims to reshape the immunosuppressive TME or intrinsically reprogram CAR-T cell metabolism by administering small-molecule drugs systemically or locally after infusion, thereby overcoming immunosuppressive barriers and preventing T cell exhaustion.

A representative approach to modulating intrinsic T cell metabolism involves targeting mitochondrial IDH2. A compound screening study identified that enasidenib, an FDA-approved IDH2 inhibitor, markedly promoted the generation of memory CAR-T cells in vivo and preserved their cytotoxic activity against leukemic targets [37, 38]. Mechanistically, IDH2 activity suppressed the pentose phosphate pathway—a critical branch of glucose metabolism responsible for antioxidant production—thereby intensifying oxidative stress in CAR-T cells, especially under nutrient-limited conditions [38]. Furthermore, inhibition of IDH2 elevated cytosolic acetyl-CoA levels and facilitated histone acetylation at genomic loci associated with memory cell differentiation. This metabolic-epigenetic reprogramming enhanced CAR-T cell adaptability and persistence, suggesting that enasidenib co-administration significantly improved therapeutic outcomes in preclinical models [38]. Another innovative strategy involves directly activating key glycolytic enzymes within T cells. Activation of pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2) was shown to reprogram mitochondrial metabolism in both CD8⁺ T cells and CAR-T cells, thereby significantly enhancing their effector functions [39–41]. Multi-omics analyses revealed that PKM2 agonism altered one-carbon metabolism and reduced methionine availability, leading to hypomethylation of both nuclear and mitochondrial DNA. This epigenetic shift promoted mitochondrial biogenesis and functional capacity. As a result, CD8⁺ T cells exhibited improved recall responses and heightened antitumor activity [42]. Notably, this approach demonstrated strong synergy with anti-PD1 therapy in preclinical models. PKM2 agonists not only boosted effector T cell activation but also decreased the intratumoral frequency of FoxP3 regulatory T cells (Tregs), effectively reshaping the immune landscape to favor tumor eradication [42]. In contrast to the previous two systemic strategies, the third approach focuses on directly dismantling key immunosuppressive mechanisms within the TME. Oxamate, an inhibitor of lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA), exemplifies this strategy [43, 44]. It was co-injected with CAR-T cells to target the excessive lactate accumulation in glioblastoma. Elevated lactate not only acidified the TME but also upregulated the expression of ectonucleotidases CD39/CD73 and CCR8 in immune cells via histone H3K18 lactylation, thereby promoting immunosuppression [43]. This created a multifaceted barrier. By inhibiting LDHA, oxamate reduced lactate production in tumor cells. In both orthotopic and subcutaneous BALB/c nude mouse xenograft models utilizing patient-derived glioblastoma stem cells (51 A and 66 A), the co-administration of oxamate and EGFRvIII-targeted CAR-T cells led to a less immunosuppressive TME, characterized by reduced infiltration of CAR-Treg cells. This metabolic reprogramming of the TME significantly enhanced CAR-T cell infiltration and immune activation, thereby improving tumor control [43].

In summary, in vivo pharmacological modulation—whether through systemic administration or direct targeting of the TME—offers a potent and clinically translatable strategy to enhance CAR-T cell therapy. By alleviating metabolic constraints, counteracting epigenetic suppression, and neutralizing soluble inhibitory factors, these small-molecule interventions can be seamlessly integrated with existing CAR-T protocols. Their goal is to overcome the immunosuppressive barriers of tumors and prevent T cell exhaustion, ultimately eliciting more durable antitumor responses.

The critical role of lipid metabolism in CAR-T cell function and its reprogramming strategies

Metabolic remodeling occurs throughout the T cell life cycle. Unlike glycolysis—which primarily supports the acute metabolic demands of effector functions—lipid metabolism serves as a critical metabolic foundation for sustaining long-term persistence and promoting memory phenotypes in CAR-T cells [45, 46]. Under physiological conditions, quiescent naive T cells and long-lived memory T cells primarily rely on mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation (FAO) to meet their energy demands—a metabolic program that supports their longevity and self-renewal capacity [47, 48]. However, the TME disrupts lipid metabolic homeostasis through multiple mechanisms. For instance, tumor-derived fibroblast growth factor 21 activated the AKT-mTORC1 signaling axis, leading to elevated cholesterol biosynthesis in CD8⁺ T cells and impairing their cytotoxic function [49]. In lipid-deprived TME, the metabolic needs of memory T cells become constrained. Prior studies reported that gastric adenocarcinoma cells upregulated fatty acid-binding proteins, creating competition with tissue-resident memory T cells for limited lipid resources. This lipid deprivation triggered apoptotic pathways in T cells, highlighting that restricted lipid availability critically undermines their survival and persistence [50]. Moreover, tumor-associated macrophages induced CD8⁺ T cell exhaustion through cholesterol efflux, directly impairing the cytotoxicity of CAR-T cells [51]. The intrinsic design of CAR-T cells also profoundly influences their lipid metabolic programming. For example, CAR constructs containing the 4-1BB costimulatory domain promoted a preference for FAO and memory phenotypes, whereas the CD28 domain favored glycolysis and effector phenotypes [52]. Therefore, reprogramming the lipid metabolic pathways of CAR-T cells through targeted interventions aims to enhance their metabolic adaptability and survival advantage within hostile TME. Genetic engineering strategies: augmenting lipid metabolic fitness

To endow CAR-T cells with superior metabolic adaptability against lipid-deprived TME, direct genetic engineering strategies represent the most fundamental and proactive form of intervention. These approaches range from selecting endogenous signaling elements to introducing exogenous key regulatory genes, with the central aim of reprogramming the lipid metabolic pathways of CAR-T cells.

Strategic selection of costimulatory domains represents a foundational layer of metabolic programming. A pioneering study by Kawalekar et al. demonstrated that the choice between CD28 and 4-1BB signaling domains intrinsically determined the metabolic fate and persistence of CAR-T cells [52]. CAR constructs containing the 4-1BB domain (BBζ) induced a phenotype resembling central memory T cells. These BBζ CAR-T cells exhibited enhanced respiratory capacity, increased mitochondrial biogenesis, and a strong reliance on FAO [52]. Mechanistically, this was reflected by elevated uptake of isotope-labeled fatty acids and significantly upregulated mRNA expression of key FAO enzymes, including the rate-limiting mitochondrial enzyme carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 A and the lipid transporter fatty acid-binding protein 5 [47, 52]. In contrast, CD28ζ-based CAR-T cells favored an effector memory phenotype and primarily relied on aerobic glycolysis [52]. This intrinsic metabolic reprogramming provides a compelling explanation for the superior persistence observed in clinical 4-1BB CAR-T products, underscoring the importance of domain selection as a primary genetic engineering decision to enhance lipid metabolic adaptability.

In addition to intrinsic signaling elements, engineering CAR-T cells to overexpress key metabolic genes also shows significant therapeutic efficacy. A groundbreaking study identified transgelin-2 (TAGLN2), a cytoskeletal protein, as a key regulator of lipid metabolism in T cells [53–55]. TAGLN2 interacted with FABP5 to facilitate its localization to the cell surface, thereby optimizing fatty acid uptake and mitochondrial respiration in activated CD8⁺ T cells [56]. Critically, the study found that endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress within the ovarian cancer microenvironment suppressed TAGLN2 expression in tumor-infiltrating T cells, impairing their function. Engineering CAR-T cells to overexpress TAGLN2 reversed this suppression and enabled the cells to bypass the detrimental effects of tumor-induced ER stress [56]. These modified cells maintained high levels of TAGLN2 and surface FABP5 upon tumor infiltration, which correlated with an increased central memory T cell subset, enhanced production of IFN-γ, TNF, and granzyme B, and ultimately led to improved control of metastatic lesions and significantly prolonged survival in syngeneic, immunocompetent C57BL/6 mouse models bearing aggressive intraperitoneal ovarian tumors (e.g., ID8-Defb29/Vegfa and PPNM cell lines) [56]. This approach reveals a novel immunosuppressive mechanism within the TME and establishes TAGLN2 engineering as an effective strategy to enhance lipid metabolism and antitumor potency in solid tumors.

Moreover, a highly innovative metabolic reprogramming strategy has recently emerged: the co-expression of Foxp3. Although Foxp3 is the master regulatory factor of immunosuppressive Tregs, which paradoxically thrive within the TME, Niu et al. ingeniously exploited only its metabolic properties [57–59]. They constructed CAR-TFoxp3 cells by co-expressing Foxp3 with a third-generation CAR. Remarkably, these cells underwent significant metabolic reprogramming, characterized by downregulated aerobic glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation, alongside upregulated lipid metabolism [60]. This shift was driven by the interaction between Foxp3 and the mitochondrial fission factor Drp1, which regulated mitochondrial dynamics. Importantly, in-depth analyses confirmed that CAR-TFoxp3 cells did not acquire the immunosuppressive functions of Tregs. Instead, Foxp3-mediated metabolic adaptation led to reduced expression of exhaustion markers, enhanced cytotoxic capacity, and sustained long-term immune surveillance, ultimately resulting in potent antitumor efficacy in a humanized immune system mouse model (HSC-engrafted NSG mice) bearing subcutaneous Huh7 hepatocellular carcinoma xenografts [60]. This study demonstrated a sophisticated strategy of “borrowing” the metabolic advantages of other T cell lineages without adopting their suppressive functional phenotype.

Finally, engineering key regulators of mitochondrial metabolism provides a more comprehensive approach to enhancing metabolic flexibility. Based on evidence that tumor-infiltrating T cells suppress PGC-1α, a key transcriptional coactivator of mitochondrial biogenesis, one study designed an Akt-insensitive, high-activity variant of PGC-1α (PGC-1αS571A) [61]. Introducing this engineered PGC-1α into EGFR-targeting human CAR-T cells induced comprehensive metabolic reprogramming. Transcriptomic analysis revealed upregulation not only of genes related to mitochondrial biogenesis but also of those involved in effector functions [62]. Functionally, these cells exhibited increased mitochondrial mass, enhanced respiratory capacity, and elevated FAO. While conventional CAR-T cells failed to control A549 solid tumors in mice, cells expressing PGC-1αS571A demonstrated significant tumor control and prolonged survival [62]. Notably, these benefits are achieved without altering the expansion or apoptosis dynamics of CAR-T cells during manufacturing.

In summary, genetic engineering provides a powerful toolkit to fundamentally enhance the lipid metabolic adaptability of CAR-T cells. Whether through strategic selection of costimulatory domains, enhancement of lipid transport via TAGLN2, adoption of Treg-like metabolic programs through Foxp3, or comprehensive improvement of mitochondrial function via PGC-1α, these strategies share a common goal: to reverse the metabolic impairments imposed by the TME and thereby promote the persistence and efficacy of CAR-T cells in solid tumors.

Pharmacological modulation and combination therapies

In addition to directly engineering CAR-T cells themselves, pharmacological modulation of the TME, systemic metabolic regulation, and combination with other therapies represent another key approach to enhancing CAR-T cell efficacy. These strategies offer strong clinical translational potential and can be flexibly integrated with existing CAR-T products.

FLASH radiotherapy is an emerging technique that delivers radiation at ultra-high dose rates and attracts attention for its reduced toxicity to normal tissues [63–65]. Recent studies revealed that its potent antitumor effect was closely associated with the reprogramming of lipid metabolism in tumor-associated macrophages. In a genetically engineered, immunocompetent mouse model of spontaneous medulloblastoma (Math1-Cre; SmoM2fl/fl on a C57BL/6 background), FLASH radiotherapy significantly promoted the polarization of tumor-associated macrophages toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype compared to conventional radiotherapy. Mechanistic investigations showed that conventional radiotherapy induced ROS production, which activated peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ) and its downstream target gene arginase 1, thereby enhancing the immunosuppressive function of macrophages. In contrast, FLASH radiotherapy suppressed the expression of lipid oxidation enzymes and the generation of oxidized low-density lipids, effectively reducing PPARγ activity and reversing the immunosuppressive state of macrophages. This favorable reprogramming of macrophage lipid metabolism created a more supportive microenvironment, markedly improving GD2 CAR-T cell infiltration and activation, and ultimately enabling previously unresponsive medulloblastoma to respond to CAR-T therapy [66]. This study innovatively integrates physical therapy with metabolic modulation of cellular therapy, offering a new paradigm for solid tumor treatment.

Targeting intrinsic immunometabolic checkpoints in T cells represents another important strategy. CH25H was identified as a key enzyme responsible for the production of 25-hydroxycholesterol (25HC), which effectively inhibited effector trogocytosis between cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) and tumor cells [67]. This process not only led to tumor antigen loss and immune evasion but also triggered CTL exhaustion and fratricide. The same pathway was suppressed in CAR-T cells. Restoring CH25H expression using TAK981 sumoylation inhibitor, or co-expressing CH25H during CAR construction, effectively maintained 25HC levels, significantly inhibited trogocytosis, and preserved CAR-T cell functionality and viability, thereby markedly enhancing their antitumor efficacy in preclinical models [68]. This study reveals a novel mechanism by which the TME disrupts lipid metabolism in T cells and proposes new approaches to overcome resistance through pharmacological or genetic intervention of this pathway.

Inducing ferroptosis in tumor cells represents an emerging strategy for combination therapy. Ferroptosis is a form of iron-dependent, lipid peroxidation–driven programmed cell death [69, 70]. Studies showed that ROR1-targeted CAR-T cells secreted IFN-γ, which synergized with ferroptosis inducers such as RSL3. This synergy was reflected in the immune activation of CAR-T cells promoting specific lipid metabolic reprogramming within tumor cells, particularly increasing levels of phosphatidylcholine species enriched in diacyl-polyunsaturated fatty acid tails (PC-PUFA2), which are prone to lipid peroxidation [71]. The accumulation of PC-PUFA2 further upregulated the expression of acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4, thereby strongly driving lipid peroxidation and the induction of ferroptosis [71]. Therefore, combining CAR-T therapy with ferroptosis inducers enables dual mechanisms—immune-mediated and metabolism-driven cytotoxicity—to synergistically target tumors, resulting in a “1 + 1>2” antitumor effect.

Moreover, various small-molecule metabolites and inhibitors also show potential in modulating CAR-T cell function. For example, the addition of α-ketoglutarate (αKG) during in vitro culture reprogrammed mitochondrial metabolism and lipid homeostasis, inhibited the differentiation of CAR-T cells into immunosuppressive Treg, and maintained their inflammatory phenotype, thereby enhancing their antitumor activity in vivo [72]. Berbamine (BBM), a natural alkaloid extracted from the traditional Chinese herb Coptis chinensis, was shown to enhance lipid metabolism and mitochondrial respiration in CAR-T cells, promote their differentiation toward a memory-like phenotype, and consequently improve their in vivo persistence and anti-leukemia activity [73]. Additionally, inhibition of stearoyl-CoA desaturase 1 reduced esterified cholesterol levels in CD8⁺ T cells, directly enhanced their effector function, and synergized with CAR-T therapy or immune checkpoint inhibitors [74].

In summary, pharmacological modulation and combination therapy strategies empower CAR-T cells through multiple mechanisms: FLASH radiotherapy remodels the immunosuppressive microenvironment at a systemic level; targeting the CH25H–25HC axis or inducing ferroptosis directly enhances tumor clearance; and metabolic regulators such as αKG and BBM optimize CAR-T product quality during the cell culture phase. These approaches complement genetic engineering strategies, collectively reprogramming the lipid metabolic pathways of CAR-T cells.

Adaptation to tissue-specific and metabolite-rich microenvironments

The metabolic adaptability of CAR-T cells can be actively enhanced through genetic engineering or combination therapies, but it also manifests in their passive adaptation and response to distinct tissue-specific microenvironments and the unique metabolites within them. Understanding these context-dependent metabolic challenges is crucial for optimizing the application of CAR-T therapy across diverse cancer settings.

Clinical sample analysis from patients receiving CD19 CAR-T therapy revealed that CAR-T cells underwent dynamic metabolic reprogramming based on their anatomical location [75]. The study found that CAR-T cells infiltrating the central nervous system (CNS) microenvironment exhibited distinct metabolic adaptation compared to those in peripheral blood. In response to the low lipid availability characteristic of the CNS, these CAR-T cells upregulated the expression profile of genes related to lipolysis and displayed enhanced memory-like features [75]. Mechanistically, this adaptive shift was likely driven by high IL-15 and low TGF-β signaling within the CNS microenvironment. Importantly, expanding CAR-T cells in vitro under conditions simulating the CNS microenvironment (using cerebrospinal fluid) preconditioned them for improved metabolic adaptability and anti-leukemic activity [75]. This study is the first to reveal that CAR-T cells exhibit spatiotemporal immunometabolic plasticity in patients, offering a groundbreaking concept for enhancing their efficacy through ex vivo metabolic preconditioning—such as manufacturing CAR-T cells under conditions that simulate the target tissue microenvironment.

On the other hand, specific metabolites within the TME can directly suppress CAR-T cell function. For example, key metabolites derived from the gut microbiota—short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs)—were shown to significantly modulate the function of anti-ROR1 CAR-T cells in a chip model simulating human intestinal adenocarcinoma [76]. Among them, propionate and butyrate exhibited strong immunosuppressive effects: they inhibited histone deacetylases, promoted the differentiation of CAR-T cells toward a FoxP3⁺ regulatory T cell–like phenotype, and upregulated exhaustion markers such as PD-1 and TIM-3, ultimately reducing CAR-T cell infiltration, cytotoxicity, and cytokine release. In contrast, acetate and pentanoate tended to promote a pro-inflammatory Th17 phenotype. This study underscored the critical yet often overlooked role of the local metabolic environment—particularly diet and microbiota-derived metabolites—in determining the functional state of CAR-T cells [76]. When administering CAR-T therapy for gastrointestinal tumors or other regions enriched in SCFAs, it is essential to consider the impact of these metabolites and potentially counteract their immunosuppressive effects through microbiota modulation or the combined use of metabolic inhibitors.

Therefore, the metabolic plasticity of CAR-T cells within specific tissues, along with their sensitivity to local metabolites, collectively indicates that integrating metabolic preconditioning strategies with interventions tailored to distinct microenvironments is a critical direction for optimizing CAR-T therapy efficacy in the future.

The critical role of amino acid metabolism in CAR-T cell function and its reprogramming strategies

Compared to glucose and lipid metabolism, amino acid metabolism possesses unique characteristics: it not only contributes to energy production, signal transduction, and biosynthetic precursors, but also directly establishes potent metabolic checkpoints through immunosuppressive metabolites. The consumption of semiessential and essential amino acids by tumor cells and myeloid-derived suppressor cells imposes a competitive constraint on CAR T cells within the nutrient-limited TME. Therefore, reprogramming amino acid metabolism in CAR-T cells holds dual strategic significance: it addresses amino acid scarcity and mitigates suppression by immunosuppressive metabolites (Fig. 1).

Rewiring CAR-T cell intrinsic metabolism to overcome essential amino acid scarcity

The scarcity of essential amino acids in the TME poses a fundamental barrier to the efficacy of CAR-T cells. To overcome this challenge, emerging strategies aim to reprogram their intrinsic metabolism by enhancing amino acid uptake or equipping them with endogenous biosynthetic pathways.

An important strategy is to engineer CAR-T cells to overexpress key amino acid transporters, thereby gaining a competitive advantage in nutrient acquisition. Silvia Panetti and colleagues demonstrated that T cells could be re-engineered to co-express transmembrane amino acid transporters such as SLC7A5 or SLC7A11 alongside CARs [77]. Under conditions of limited availability of their respective amino acids—tryptophan (Trp) or cystine—this modification significantly enhanced CAR-T cell proliferation without compromising cytotoxicity or increasing exhaustion markers. Transcriptomic and phenotypic analyses further revealed that transporter-modified CAR-T cells upregulated the expression and activity of intracellular arginases [77]. This metabolic adaptation was associated with improved mitochondrial respiration and glycolytic flux, ensuring sufficient ATP supply even under amino acid starvation. In acute myeloid leukemia models, the engineered anti-CD33 CAR-T cells exhibited superior in vivo proliferative capacity and markedly enhanced tumor clearance compared to unmodified counterparts [77].

Beyond enhancing uptake, a more advanced strategy is to equip CAR-T cells with the ability to de novo synthesize essential amino acids, thereby achieving metabolic autonomy. This was exemplified in overcoming arginine depletion, a common feature in both hematologic and solid tumors [78–81]. Due to the inherently low expression of arginine resynthesis enzymes—ornithine transcarbamylase (OTC) and argininosuccinate synthase (ASS)—T cells were highly sensitive to arginine-depleted environments [82–84]. Metabolic engineering strategies successfully enabled CAR-T cells targeting various antigens, such as GD2, CD33, and mesothelin, to coexpress functional OTC and/or ASS enzymes [85]. These engineered cells acquired the metabolic capacity to convert ornithine into citrulline (via OTC) or citrulline into argininosuccinate (via ASS), effectively bypassing the blocked arginine biosynthesis pathway [85]. This intervention conferred a strong proliferative advantage to CAR-T cells under low-arginine conditions or tumor-conditioned media in vitro. Critically, the enhanced proliferative capacity translated into potent antitumor activity in vivo, with enzyme-modified CAR-T cells exhibiting improved expansion, prolonged persistence, and superior control of both systemic HL60 acute myeloid leukemia (in NSG mice) and subcutaneous GD2 + neuroblastoma (in Nude mice) xenografts [85]. In summary, strategically reprogramming the intrinsic metabolism of CAR-T cells to overcome essential amino acid deprivation represents a paradigm shift in cellular therapy design.

Reprogramming the immunosuppressive Milieu by targeting the tryptophan-kynurenine axis

Among the numerous metabolically driven immunosuppressive mechanisms within the TME, the Trp–kynurenine (Kyn) metabolic axis stands out as a key regulator of tumor immune evasion. This pathway is primarily driven by indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase 1 (IDO1) in tumor cells, which depletes the essential amino acid Trp and accumulates immunosuppressive Kyn, thereby potently inhibiting T cell function and promoting their exhaustion [86, 87].

The most direct strategy is to pharmacologically inhibit IDO1 to block the production of Kyn. Evidence from gastrointestinal cancer models confirmed the effectiveness of this strategy. In subcutaneous NCG mouse xenograft models of gastric (utilizing AGS and Hs746T cell lines) and pancreatic (utilizing Panc02 and KPC cell lines) adenocarcinoma, tumor cells highly expressed IDO1, which was further upregulated by IFN-γ signaling following CAR-T cell attack, forming a potent negative feedback loop [88]. Studies showed that the IDO1 inhibitor epacadostat significantly reduced Kyn production within the TME. When combined with CLDN18.2-targeted CAR-T cells, this inhibition led to markedly enhanced antitumor responses [88]. Mechanistically, IDO1 blockade increased the production of cytokines such as IL-2 and IFN-γ by CAR-T cells, reduced the expression of exhaustion markers (TIM-3, PD-1, LAG-3), and downregulated the key exhaustion-associated transcription factor TOX. This metabolic-epigenetic reprogramming resulted in improved tumor control and prolonged survival in vivo. Furthermore, the standard lymphodepletion chemotherapy regimen of fludarabine and cyclophosphamide was unexpectedly found to suppress IDO1 expression in tumor cells, thereby enhancing CAR-T cell infiltration and function—revealing a clinically translatable combination strategy [88].

Beyond systemic pharmacological inhibition, more advanced strategies focus on the targeted delivery of metabolic agents to more precisely remodel the TME. A representative example was the use of genetically programmable vesicles (D@aPD-L1 NVs) [89]. These nanovesicles were engineered from cells stably expressing anti–PD-L1 single-chain variable fragments (scFv) on their membranes and were loaded with the glutamine antagonist DON via electroporation [90, 91]. The resulting D@aPD-L1 NVs exhibited dual functionality: first, the anti–PD-L1 scFv enabled specific binding to PD-L1 on tumor cells, thereby blocking the PD-1/PD-L1 immune checkpoint; second, this targeted delivery ensured localized release of DON, disrupting tumor cell glutamine metabolism and reversing the immunosuppressive TME. In lung cancer models, these vesicles demonstrated excellent tumor enrichment. They effectively reduced infiltration of immunosuppressive cells such as M2 macrophages and myeloid-derived suppressor cells, while promoting the recruitment and activation of endogenous CD8⁺ T cells and MSLN-targeted CAR-T cells. The combination of D@aPD-L1 NVs with CAR-T cells resulted in superior tumor clearance and established long-lasting protective immune memory, even against tumor rechallenge. This approach represents a powerful synergy of metabolic reprogramming, immune checkpoint blockade, and targeted nanomedicine [89].

Despite the aforementioned strategies that externally remodel the TME, the most fundamental and durable solution is to genetically arm CAR-T cells themselves with the capacity to neutralize immunosuppressive metabolites. This was achieved by engineering CAR-T cells to overexpress kynureninase (KYNU), an enzyme responsible for degrading Kyn [92]. Studies showed that unmodified CAR-T cells exposed to high levels of Kyn exhibited impaired proliferation, reduced cytotoxicity, and increased exhaustion. In contrast, KYNU-OE CAR-T cells were capable of degrading extracellular Kyn. Notably, these engineered cells not only resisted the suppressive effects of a high-Kyn environment but also demonstrated superior antitumor activity both in vitro and in vivo [92]. In mouse models bearing tumors with IDO1 overexpression—thus creating a Kyn-rich TME—KYNU-OE CAR-T cells maintained a less exhausted phenotype (lower PD-1 expression), exhibited enhanced persistence, and achieved significantly improved survival outcomes compared to conventional CAR-T cells [92]. This “detoxification” approach transforms metabolic barriers into potential advantages, exemplifying how cellular engineering can reshape the TME to favor therapeutic efficacy.

In summary, the Typ–Kyn metabolic axis represents a formidable barrier to CAR-T cell efficacy, particularly in solid tumors. However, a multifaceted strategic framework is emerging to dismantle this obstacle. Pharmacological inhibition of IDO1 offers an easily combinable approach; targeted nanovesicles enable precise metabolic and immunological remodeling of the TME; and equipping CAR-T cells with intrinsic “detoxifying” enzymes such as KYNU provides a cell-autonomous solution. Collectively, these strategies aim to reshape the immunosuppressive metabolic landscape and are essential for unlocking the full therapeutic potential of CAR-T cell therapy against solid malignancies.

Discussion

The limited efficacy of CAR-T cell therapy in solid tumors largely stems from its metabolic disadvantage when competing with tumor cells. This review systematically elucidated that metabolic reprogramming—through genetic engineering, pharmacological intervention, or manufacturing optimization to reshape CAR-T cell glucose, lipid, and amino acid metabolism—was a core strategy for endowing CAR-T cells with metabolic adaptability and overcoming the immunosuppressive TME. Whether by enhancing glucose uptake (e.g., overexpressing GLUT1 or GLUT3), optimizing lipid utilization (e.g., selecting the 4-1BB costimulatory domain or overexpressing TAGLN2), or relieving amino acid constraints (e.g., enabling arginine biosynthesis or degrading Kyn), these approaches shared the common goal of improving CAR-T cell persistence and functionality within nutrient-deprived, metabolite-rich TME.

Based on current research, the field is unfolding several exciting and forward-looking directions. First, multi-pathway synergistic interventions are poised to become the next frontier. Current strategies predominantly focus on single metabolic pathways, yet the immunosuppressive nature of the TME arises from the convergence of multiple metabolic stressors [93]. Moving forward, combining interventions targeting glycolysis (e.g., PKM2 agonists), enhancing lipid oxidation (e.g., PGC-1α engineering), and relieving amino acid checkpoints (e.g., KYNU overexpression) is expected to yield additive or even synergistic effects, forging metabolically “omnipotent” CAR-T cells. Second, dynamic and controllable metabolic reprogramming is key to enhancing safety and precision. Continuous overexpression of certain metabolic genes may pose long-term safety risks [94, 95]. Therefore, developing switchable systems inducible by small-molecule drugs, or integrating response elements into CAR constructs that sense specific TME metabolites—such as elevated lactate or hypoxia—enables spatiotemporal regulation of CAR-T cell metabolic phenotypes, ensuring they are “activated” and gain metabolic advantages only within tumor sites. Finally, personalized metabolic adaptation is poised to become a necessary component of precision medicine. The metabolic profiles of the TME vary significantly across tumor types and even among individual patients [96, 97]. Future research focuses on mapping the metabolic landscape of tumors to guide the selection of the most compatible CAR-T metabolic modifications. For instance, tumors with high IDO1 expression may benefit from CAR-T cells equipped with KYNU-mediated “detoxification” capabilities, whereas tumors with severe lipid scarcity may require CAR-T products enhanced for FAO.

Furthermore, the future of CAR-T cell therapy in solid tumors will increasingly rely on synergistic combinations with novel TME-modulating agents currently in clinical or preclinical development. For instance, the deployment of novel arginase inhibitors (e.g., INCB001158) presents a highly promising avenue to reverse myeloid-driven amino acid depletion, thereby restoring CAR-T cell proliferation and effector function in arginine-starved microenvironments [98–100]. Beyond direct metabolic inhibitors, targeting the broader myeloid and macrophage compartment offers profound synergistic potential. Strategies driving macrophage repolarization or blocking the CD47/SIRPα “don’t eat me” immune checkpoint are emerging as powerful tools to remodel the immunosuppressive landscape [101]. Particularly in stroma-rich or bone-derived malignancies such as osteosarcoma, where the physical and metabolic barriers are notoriously formidable, integrating metabolically enhanced CAR-T cells with immuno-regenerative scaffolds or innate immune checkpoint blockades could fundamentally dismantle the TME [102, 103]. By neutralizing metabolic sinks and reprogramming local innate immunity simultaneously, these combinatorial frontiers represent a critical next step in consolidating the clinical value of next-generation CAR-T therapies.

In conclusion, metabolic reprogramming is no longer merely an auxiliary approach but has become the cornerstone for constructing next-generation CAR-T cells with enhanced potency and durability. By integrating multi-pathway synergistic interventions, advancing dynamic regulatory technologies, and moving toward personalized metabolic adaptation, we are poised to systematically overcome the metabolic barriers imposed by solid tumors. Ultimately, these efforts aim to extend the therapeutic impact of CAR-T cell therapy beyond hematologic malignancies into the broader realm of solid tumors.
