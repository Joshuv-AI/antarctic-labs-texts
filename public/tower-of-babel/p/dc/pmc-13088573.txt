Background

Infections that are not present at the time of hospital admission are referred to as hospital-acquired infections (HAIs). These infections must manifest at least 48 h after hospitalization, within 3 days after discharge, or within 30 days after surgery, except in the case of organ implants and prostheses, where they can occur up to one year post-procedure [1]. The most common types of HAIs are respiratory, bloodstream, urinary tract, and surgical site infections (SSIs) [2]. Given that SSIs occur in 1% to 3% of surgical procedures, they are among the most common types of HAIs [3]. SSIs are wound infections that develop within 30 days after surgery or up to one year if an implant is placed [3]. SSIs place a significant burden on both patients and the healthcare system, as they are associated with the spread of antimicrobial resistance, increased morbidity and mortality, longer hospital stays, and higher healthcare costs [4]. However, it is estimated that modifying certain perioperative factors could prevent up to 60% of SSIs [5].

Identifying the risk factors for SSIs is essential. Over the past 20 years, numerous studies have investigated the relationship between serum vitamin D levels and the risk of developing various types of infections, including SSIs [6]. Due to its immune-modulating properties, including the induction of host-defense antimicrobial peptides, vitamin D indirectly combats a variety of microorganisms. In addition to being present in innate and adaptive immune cells, vitamin D receptors and its activating enzyme, 1-alpha-hydroxylase, are found in numerous organs. The active form of vitamin D enhances macrophages’ ability to fight infections and supports tissue remodeling [7].

The Institute of Medicine defines vitamin D levels as normal when they are above 20 ng/mL and low when they are below 20 ng/mL [8]. Over the past 20 years, numerous studies have investigated the relationship between serum vitamin D levels and the risk of developing various infections [6]. Only a limited number of studies have reviewed the literature to evaluate the potential effects of vitamin D levels and supplementation on the risk of SSIs. To provide the necessary evidence, it is important to critically assess studies in this field.

Over the past 20 years, several studies have investigated the relationship between serum vitamin D levels and the risk of developing various illnesses [6]. Recent literature suggests an association between vitamin D levels and supplementation and the incidence of SSIs across different types of surgeries [7, 9–15]. It is essential to systematically review existing studies to provide evidence that may help reduce the burden of SSIs. This review will synthesize the findings of studies investigating the relationship between vitamin D blood levels and the risk of SSIs.

Methods/design

This systematic review and meta-analysis will be conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines, aiming to investigate the association between preoperative vitamin D deficiency and SSIs across various types of surgeries.

Search strategy

The following four electronic databases will be searched systematically to identify and retrieve relevant articles: MEDLINE (PubMed), Embase, Web of Science, and the Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL). According to a 2017 systematic review, this combination of databases provides the most comprehensive coverage [16]. Google Scholar, ClinicalTrials.gov, the International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (WHO - ICTRP), the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, and manual searches of any additional pertinent articles from the reference lists that were not found by the initial searches will all be used to supplement the search. We will contact the authors of unpublished trials, conference proceedings, and published abstracts to request missing or unpublished study data. Authors will be contacted twice: an initial email will be sent, followed by a second reminder email after one week if no response is received. If no response is obtained after the second contact, the study will be excluded. All excluded studies will be reported in an appendix, together with the reason for exclusion. For each included primary article, an additional citation search will be conducted in Google Scholar using the “cited by” feature. The search will cover the period from the inception of the databases until December 2025 and will be conducted without language restrictions. The search will be repeated prior to the final analysis. Citation management and screening, including the removal of duplicate records, will be performed using EndNote reference management software (EndNote 20, Version 20.4, Thomson Reuters®, New York, NY, USA).

Our PICO question is: “What is the association between vitamin D deficiency versus vitamin D sufficiency and the development of SSIs in patients undergoing surgical procedures?” Based on this question, a search strategy has been designed. The search terms will include pre-determined combinations of variations from the following categories:

Vitamin D, 25-hydroxyvitamin D3, 25-hydroxyvitamin D, 25(OH)D, 25-OH-D, 25-hydroxycholecalciferol, calcidiol, cholecalciferol, calcitriol, hydroxycholecalciferols

Depending on the database, Boolean operators, MeSH terms, keywords, free-text searches, and index terms will be used as appropriate. To ensure high sensitivity, no additional filters will be applied. Relevant studies will be identified using a two-step approach by two authors. First, they will assess the titles and abstracts to determine eligibility, consulting the full text if necessary. Next, they will screen the full texts of the selected articles. At each stage, the authors will discuss and evaluate the included studies. Any disagreements will be resolved through discussion, or, if needed, a third collaborator will act as an adjudicator.

All study types will be included.

If a study is an abstract, a conference proceeding, unpublished, or not openly accessible, it can be obtained through the Qatar University (QU) Library, the interlibrary loan (ILL) service, or by contacting the author directly. Studies will be excluded if the full text is not available. No restrictions will be applied regarding language, publication date, or country of origin. Google Translate will be used as a preliminary tool to review non-English studies [17]. The Translation and Interpretation Department at Qatar University, which provides professional and skilled written translation services, will be contacted to obtain an accurate translation.

The development of SSIs is the primary outcome. SSIs are wound infections that occur within 30 days after surgery or up to one year if an implant is placed [1, 3]. Studies that examine and report the relationship between vitamin D levels and SSIs as an outcome will be considered eligible.

Screening and data extraction

If titles and abstracts do not provide sufficient information, the full text will be examined to determine eligibility. Screening of studies will initially be based on titles and abstracts. To remove duplicates, studies will be managed using EndNote 20.4. Retrieved articles will be recorded in an Excel selection form with a standardized coding system, after which the full texts of the selected studies will be reviewed.

To ensure consistency in systematic screening and selection, the two study investigators (RF and SZ) will pilot the eligibility criteria on a sample of five publications. Following a successful pilot, they will independently read, review, and categorize each study as “eligible,” “potentially eligible,” “not eligible,” or “unclear,” providing a rationale for their decision. Any disagreements will be resolved through discussion until consensus is reached. If disagreements persist, a third collaborator will be consulted.

Studies will be included if they investigate the association between vitamin D levels and SSIs. The selection process will be illustrated using a PRISMA flow diagram.

A standardized data collection form, in the form of an Excel sheet, will be used to extract key data in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines for systematic reviews [18]. All data will be analyzed using the codes assigned to the studies.

To ensure the accuracy and quality of data extraction, the two study authors will independently extract data and then compare their results through discussion.

The two reviewers will independently assess each data entry and resolve any discrepancies through discussion. If disagreements persist, a third collaborator will be consulted. The authors of the original studies will be contacted for clarification or to obtain any missing information.

Assessment of risk of bias in included studies

The Methodological Standards for Epidemiological Research (MASTER) Scale will be used to assess the quality of studies through 36 safeguards grouped into seven domains: equal recruitment (items 1–4), equal retention (items 5–9), equal ascertainment (items 10–16), equal implementation (items 17–22), equal prognosis (items 23–28), sufficient analysis (items 29–31), and temporal precedence (items 32–36). Each safeguard will be scored as 1 if present or 0 if absent. Studies will then be ranked relative to the highest-scoring study, allowing potential biases within the study pool to be identified.

To ensure consistent application, the two raters will evaluate a pilot sample of two studies. Studies will not be excluded based on risk of bias. Each assessment will be documented in writing, and any disagreements will be resolved through discussion with a third collaborator until consensus is reached.

Measures of treatment effect

The included studies will be reviewed narratively and presented with a PRISMA flow diagram and a table summarizing each study. Studies will be categorized according to vitamin D cutoff values. Effect sizes will be reported as pooled odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and a P-value of less than 0.05 will be considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses will be conducted using STATA version 17 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA) with the restricted maximum likelihood estimator. If meta-analysis is not feasible due to insufficient data, the results will be described narratively.

Dealing with missing data

Open-ended questions will be used when contacting study authors for missing or insufficient information, in order to minimize the likelihood of receiving overly positive or biased responses [19].

Assessment of heterogeneity

Heterogeneity will be assessed using the I2 statistic. An I2 value greater than 50% will indicate substantial heterogeneity, while a value of 75% or higher will suggest serious heterogeneity, prompting an investigation of potential sources of between-study variability. These sources may include selective outcome reporting, methodological flaws, small-study effects, or true clinical heterogeneity. If feasible, meta-regression will be performed to examine factors such as vitamin D cutoff, assay type, and type of surgery.

Data synthesis

The included studies will be presented both in tables and narratively, highlighting their characteristics and results. Meta-analyses will be conducted to examine the relationship between vitamin D deficiency and SSIs using the quality effects model, which accounts for systematic errors by adjusting study weights based on their quality rankings. Analyses will be stratified by two vitamin D cutoff values (20 ng/mL and 30 ng/mL), with separate evaluations to determine their impact on SSIs. Pooled odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) will be calculated. Heterogeneity among studies will be assessed using the I2 statistic, with specific thresholds indicating varying levels of heterogeneity. Results will be presented in forest plots. Publication bias will be evaluated using Doi plots and the Luis Furuya-Kanamori (LFK) index, with values indicating different levels of asymmetry.

Confidence in cumulative evidence

The World Health Organization and the Cochrane Collaboration have both adopted the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) framework, which will be used by the two reviewers to assess the quality of the evidence [18, 20]. In the GRADE framework, randomized controlled trials are initially rated as high quality, while observational studies are rated as low quality; however, the evidence will be further evaluated for factors that may decrease or increase its quality [21]. A table generated using GRADEpro (https://gradepro.org/) will summarize the results along with their corresponding GRADE ratings. A high rating indicates that further research is unlikely to change the confidence in the estimated effect. A moderate rating suggests that additional research may have an important impact on the estimate and could potentially change it. A low rating means that further research is very likely to affect the estimate and alter it, while a very low rating indicates considerable uncertainty regarding the effect estimate. A “Summary of Findings” table will be produced to present the results according to the GRADE framework, with reliable evidence typically rated as high or moderate strength.

Patient and public involvement

This study did not directly involve patients. Patient involvement in recruitment or in communicating study results was not applicable, as this protocol pertains to a systematic review and no participant recruitment will occur.

Amendments

Any changes to the protocol will be documented with the date of the adjustment, the type of change, and the rationale for the modification.

Dissemination

The review will be submitted for publication in a reputable journal within this field. Additionally, the results will be presented at relevant conferences.

Discussion

Although many risk factors for SSIs have been identified, the role of vitamin D in their development remains unclear. Given the significant burden SSIs place on patients and healthcare systems, it is essential to determine whether an association exists.

This systematic review and meta-analysis will enhance current knowledge regarding the impact of vitamin D on the risk of SSIs, as we aim to conduct the most comprehensive review to date by including all relevant studies without language restrictions. A rigorous methodology will be employed in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook, and outcomes will be reported following the PRISMA guidelines. As a result, this review is expected to provide valuable evidence that could influence future clinical practice. Publishing the research protocol enhances methodological transparency and reduces the risk of bias, representing a key strength of the study. Additionally, the review will focus exclusively on the role of vitamin D in SSIs, providing surgeons with high-quality evidence to guide, support, and tailor clinical decision-making.

One potential limitation of this study is the variability in study types; consequently, the comprehensiveness of the included studies and the quality of their methodologies will significantly affect the reliability of the results.
