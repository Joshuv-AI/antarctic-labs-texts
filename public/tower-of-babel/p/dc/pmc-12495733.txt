Interferon signaling and STING pathway in head and neck cancers: unlocking immune secrets and therapeutic frontiers
Authors: Kosar Zolfaghari, Mohammad Yazdi, Shaghayegh Mohammadioun, Fatemeh Tajafrooz, Hesam Malekfarnood, Kiarash Saleki, Nima Rezaei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Head and neck squamous cell carcinomas (HNSCCs) rank seventh among the most prevalent malignancies globally. The immune response in cancer conditions governs the course of the disease and clinical outlook. Interferons (IFNs) are a key part of the innate immune system and are essential in modulating the tumor microenvironment (TME). Additionally, IFNs could influence proliferation and affect differentiation, emigration, and death of cancer cells. The cGAS–stimulator of interferon genes (STING) signaling identifies cytosolic DNA and triggers an immune response. Aberrant induction of the cGAS–STING signaling may lead to excess and sustained release of type-I IFN, leading to imbalanced aggregation in various tissues or organs. It is noteworthy that STING signaling has shown a dual role, both promoting tumor resistance and antitumor immunity. Moreover, STING agonists have been investigated in recent studies; however, challenges such as toxicity should be addressed. IFNs have also been used as monotherapy or adjuvant treatment before chemotherapy to increase efficacy against HNSCC tumors. The present review briefly describes IFN signaling with a focus on the STING pathway, and discusses its role in modulating immunotherapy and chemotherapy effects in HNSCC.

Introduction

Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) is a term for mucosal epithelium cancers originating in the mouth, throat, or esophagus [1]. It denotes a class of cancers featuring laryngeal, pharyngeal, and salivary gland involvement. Globally, HNSCC ranks in the top ten in terms of prevalence among cancers. Moreover, HNSCC makes up approximately one in twenty cancer diagnoses and leads to about half a million deaths annually [2].

There are two innate and adaptive components to the immune system. Detecting and responding to cellular danger messages that may be made in cancerous conditions. A vital component of the immune system, interferons (IFNs) could modulate the tumor microenvironment (TME) [3]. Furthermore, they could also hinder proliferation and regulate cancer cells’ differentiation, migration, and apoptosis [4]. These substances are secreted by both innate and adaptive immune cells, functioning as both autocrine and paracrine factors. However, studies suggest a dual role for IFN pathways; hence, using IFNs in cancer treatment requires caution [5].

The antitumorigenic and homeostasis functions of IFN-α and IFN-β are well documented. Other types of IFN-I, including IFN-κ, IFN-ɛ, and IFN-ω, which could also represent such effects, remain an area for further investigation [6]. The underlying mechanisms of IFN production will be discussed later.

In HNSCC, understanding the nuances of STING pathway activation is essential, as it dictates antitumor immunity and response to therapies [7]. Indeed, the clinical development of STING agonists is a therapeutic strategy to enhance antitumor immunity in HNSCC [8]. Recent studies continue to focus on the complex regulatory mechanisms of the STING pathway in cancer, highlighting its differential regulation and potential therapeutic vulnerabilities in HNSCC. Furthermore, the preclinical and clinical data highlight the involvement of STING agonists in augmenting current treatment paradigms for HNSCC [9]. IFN treatments may be given in monotherapy or combined with other HNSCC chemotherapies [10, 11].

In the present review, we discuss the immunopathological and therapeutic implications of IFN signaling in HNSCC, with a particular focus on the STING pathway. We also provide insights for future research.

Among these mechanisms, he cytosolic DNA-sensing pathway known as the Stimulator of Interferon Genes (STING) is pivotal for activating IFN-I responses [12]. Detecting damaged cytosolic DNA activates the STING pathway, often originating from pathogens or cancerous cells. This detection is mediated by cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS), which catalyzes the production of cyclic GMP-AMP (cGAMP) [13]. cGAMP acts as a second messenger upon binding to cytosolic DNA. Then, it binds STING, a membrane-spanning protein primarily residing in the endoplasmic reticulum. This joining event causes a change in the conformation in STING, leading to its activation and translocation to the Golgi apparatus and subsequent phosphorylation and activation of downstream signaling molecules, such as interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3) and TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1). Once activated, IRF3 moves into the nucleus, triggering the transcription of type I interferons and various pro-inflammatory cytokines [14].

IFN signaling in HNSCC has many facets. It has dual roles in mounting an antitumor response, resistance to treatment, interaction with targeted therapies, and modulation of checkpoint molecules [15–18].

Overview of IFN signaling pathways

IFNs are key cytokines that play complex roles in innate and adaptive immunity [19] (Fig. 1). Although the three classes of IFNs (IFN-I, IFN-II, and IFN-III) share some functions, each type has unique receptors, signaling pathways, and biological impacts [19].Earning a deeper understanding of the intricate signaling pathways of IFNs in HNSCC could help design effective therapies. The biological effects of interferons depend on where their receptors are located and how many of them are in the tumor microenvironment. IFNAR1 and IFNAR2 are two types of receptors that type I interferons use to send signals. These receptors are found on many types of blood and epithelial cells. Type II IFN interacts with IFNGR1 and IFNGR2, which are found on most nucleated cells and are more common on immune effectors. Type III interferons work through IFNLR1/IL-10R2, which are mostly found on epithelial barriers. Changes in how receptors are expressed can change how well a person responds to IFN-based therapy and may be used as prognostic markers [20, 21].

Type I interferon

IFN-I influences the activation and recruitment of several immune cell types, which helps to shape the TME. The detection of cytosolic DNA and the induction of IFN-I production depend on the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS)–STING pathway [22, 23]. In HNSCC, DNA from tumors can activate cGAS, producing cyclic GMP-AMP (cGAMP) [24]. cGAMP, in turn, activates STING, which triggers downstream signaling cascades and leads to the secretion of IFN-I [9]. In the HNSCC TME, tumor cells and immune cells can produce IFN-I following STING activation, shaping the interferon repertoire within the tumor [9, 25]. Hence, STING plays a pivotal role in antitumor responses by modulating immune-mediated and tumor-intrinsic processes. Research has demonstrated that lacking IFN-I signaling in HNSCC TME decreases tumor stemness and immunosuppressive mechanisms, such as apoptosis [25]. However, despite the tumor-suppressive role of IFN-I, it can also contribute to creating a tumor-friendly environment [4]. For instance, Ma et al. have shown that IFN-α can potentially suppress the antitumor immunity in HNSCC [4]. Hence, this dual action makes the overall effects of IFN-I in HNSCC more complex and challenging to interpret.

According to studies, given the valuable impacts of the role of the STING pathway in tumor progression, it has become a compelling therapeutic target, driving the development of several pharmacologic STING agonists [7]. Moreover, due to the immunomodulatory roles of IFN-I, combination therapies could be a helpful strategy to boost antitumor immune responses in HNSCC [26]. For instance, a study has shown that inducing the IFN-I signaling combined with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) could better suppress tumor progression in early research stages concerning HPV-related oral cancer [27].

Type II interferon

IFN-γ, the only member of IFN-II, plays a vital role in shaping the TME and enhancing antitumor immunity in HNSCC. IFN-γ signaling starts when it binds to a complex of heterodimeric receptors, IFNGR1 and IFNGR2, present on both HNSCC cells and immune cells within the tumor environment [28]. When a ligand binds, it activates Janus kinases (JAKs), namely JAK1 and JAK2, which are non-receptor tyrosine kinases that bind to the receptor complex. Activating JAK1 and JAK2 phosphorylates Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription 1 (STAT1) on tyrosine residues, prompting its dimerization and movement into the nucleus [29]. The nuclear translocation of phosphorylated STAT1 is essential because it acts as a transcription factor, attaching to specific DNA sequences to trigger the expression of various interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs) [30, 31]. Activating this pathway leads to the expression of numerous genes that play roles in critical biological processes, including cell cycle regulation, apoptosis, growth inhibition, and tumor suppression [32].

Furthermore, recent research has shown an emerging rise in MHC-II gene expression corresponding to increased levels of intra-tumoral IFN-γ production [33]. Different from the upregulation of MHC-II, many tumors, including HNSCC, have low levels of MHC-I, which makes it harder for cytotoxic T lymphocytes to find them. Malignancies in HNSCC evade the immune system by lowering the surface expression of major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I). In order to help the immune system recognize and eradicate cancer cells, these molecules are essential for delivering small fragments of tumor proteins to cytotoxic CD8+ T lymphocytes (CTLs). Reduced MHC-I levels make it more difficult for CTLs to recognize and target tumors, which lowers immune responses and produces less than ideal results from treatments like immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). A variety of factors may cause MHC-I depletion. TAP1/2 and β2-microglobulin are two essential proteins needed for antigen processing and presentation that are lacking in some cancers. Some prevent the synthesis of MHC-I by reacting to immunosuppressive cytokines like TGF-β or by losing vital transcriptional regulators like NLRC5. These changes allow the tumor to grow without the immune system stopping it. Some ways to restore MHC-I expression are to use epigenetic agents, cytokine therapies to lower transcriptional barriers, and to combine these methods with immune checkpoint inhibitors to make tumors more immunogenic. To make immunotherapy work better in HNSCC, it may be necessary to address MHC-I down regulation [34–36]. In HNSCC, the downregulation of MHC-I is a well-known way for the immune system to avoid detection, along with changes to MHC-II. This mechanism makes it harder for cytotoxic T cells to see antigens, which lowers the tumor’s ability to cause an immune response and limits the effectiveness of immunotherapies. Mechanisms include problems with the machinery that processes antigens, transcriptional repression, and inhibition by cytokines. Researchers are looking into how to restore MHC-I expression to make treatments more effective [37, 38].

Genetic, epigenetic, post-transcriptional, and post-translational changes that make the MHC-I antigen presentation pathway less stable make it much easier for HNSCC to avoid the immune system and resist immune checkpoint inhibitors. This downregulation, which happens in 40–90% of human cancers, makes it harder to find CD8+ T lymphocytes and stops them from killing cancer cells, which is often linked to worse clinical outcomes [35, 39]. This suggests that tumor cells could be essential as antigen-presenting cells (APCs), improving tumor recognition by the immune cells in the presence of IFN-γ [40]. Apart from its direct effect on antigen presentation, IFN-γ can also affect the tumor microenvironment (TME) by interacting with innate immune sensing pathways. Furthermore, IFN-γ influences the activation and recruitment of several immune cell types, which helps to shape the TME. The detection of cytosolic DNA and the induction of IFN-I production depend on the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS)–STING pathway [41, 42].

IFN-γ strongly activates cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) and Natural Killer (NK) cells in the HNSCC TME. For instance, NK cell activation produces IFN-γ, which subsequently increases Programmed Death-Ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression on tumor cells and directs the release of chemokines that attract more T cells, establishing a complicated feedback loop within the TME [43, 44]. Chronic exposure to IFN-γ and tumor-intrinsic mechanisms may result in immune evasion. Engagement of PD-L1 with its receptor PD-1 on T-cells leads to T-cell exhaustion, which hinders antitumor immunity [45]. Additionally, the Suppressor of Cytokine Signaling 1 (SOCS1), an essential negative regulator of JAK-STAT signaling, can be elevated in response to IFN-γ. Though SOCS1 was initially identified for its function in forming excessive inflammatory responses, increased expression in tumor cells can reduce IFN-γ signaling, contributing to resistance and immune evasion [46].

Preclinical studies using xenograft models for HNSCC have shown that IFN-γ treatments exert antitumor responses through increasing apoptotic activity and infiltrating CTLs and NK cells in the tumor milieu [47, 48].

Type III interferon

IFN-III (including IFN-λ1, IFN-λ2, IFN-λ3, and IFN-λ4) is vital for its specific receptor expression and localized immunomodulatory roles [49]. Their different but distinct signaling is triggered by binding to a heterodimeric receptor complex formed by IFNLR1 and IL-10R2 subunits [1, 50]. When IFN-λ binds, it predominantly activates the JAK-STAT pathway, phosphorylating STAT1 and STAT3, activating interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs) [51].

The significance of IFN-III is mainly considered for HPV-associated HNSCC. In these tumors, persistent HPV infection is a key driver, and the IFN-III plays a critical role in viral suppression and localized immune activation. Type III IFNs induce antiviral ISGs that directly interfere with the HPV life cycle, potentially limiting viral replication and promoting clearance [52]. High-risk HPVs are known to downregulate ISGs, actively representing a viral immune evasion strategy. Type III IFNs may counteract this by robustly inducing a subset of ISGs in epithelial cells [53].

Furthermore, IFN-III can modulate DC function, which is vital for antitumor surveillance. IFN-III can use plasmacytoid DCs for TLR7 activation, enhancing their capacity to initiate antitumor immune responses [54]. The localized immune modulation by IFN-III offers a potential advantage in HNSCC therapy by limiting systemic inflammatory toxicities while effectively targeting the TME.

Preclinical in vivo studies are supporting the antitumor potential of IFN-III. While direct in vivo evidence in HNSCC models is still developing, studies in other cancer models and related contexts provide valuable insights. For instance, IL-28 treatment has reduced tumor burden and enhanced NK cell cytotoxic activity in murine cancer models [55, 56]. Since NK cells express IFNLR1 and are responsive to IFN-λ stimulation, IFN-III can boost innate antitumor immunity [57]. Furthermore, the enhanced dendritic cell-mediated IFN signaling, particularly IFN-III production by cDC1s, is vital for antitumor surveillance in localized disease settings [58]. These findings underscore the potential of IFN-III as a therapeutic agent in HNSCC, especially for localized HPV-positive tumors where localized immune activation and viral control are paramount. Further research is needed to fully elucidate the in vivo mechanisms in HNSCC and explore optimal therapeutic strategies, potentially combining IFN-III with other immunotherapies or conventional treatments.

Several STING agonists have been tested in human studies, but the results have not been good enough [59]. Increasing evidence shows that the cGAS-STING pathway helps tumors spread and creates a tumor microenvironment (TME) that weakens the immune system [60]. DNA damage caused by therapy turns on the cGAS-STING pathway, which leads to a type I IFN reaction [61, 62].

Both cancers and cells inside cells may be impacted by the activation of cGAS-STING-dependent inflammatory signals downstream of chromosomal instability (CIN). Type I IFNs and IFNAR/STAT1 activation result in the expression of numerous effectors, including genes that inhibit cell growth and cause cell death, which are detrimental to the survival of tumor cells. On the other hand, activating NC-NF-kB signaling through cGAS–STING can lead to IL6/STAT3 signaling and EMT processes, both of which help tumors grow and spread [63]. It is important to note that the different effects of a therapeutic approach targeting cGAS-STING mean that each patient needs a unique treatment plan [64].

Receptor expression patterns in the HNSCC tumor microenvironment

The biological effects of interferons depend on where their receptors are in the tumor microenvironment (TME), the number of receptors present, and how they are modulated. Type I interferons send signals through the heterodimeric receptors IFNAR1 and IFNAR2. These receptors are found on many types of cells, including hematopoietic cells, epithelial cells, and head and neck squamous cell carcinoma tumor cells [65, 66]. IFNAR expression may be decreased in malignant settings by chronic inflammation, oncogenic signaling, or epigenetic silencing, which would hinder the effectiveness of treatment [67].

Type II interferon (IFN-γ) binds to IFNGR1 and IFNGR2, which are found on almost all nucleated cells, but are especially common on immune effector populations [68]. Recent research shows that IFNGR1 is mainly present in small extracellular vesicles (sEVs) that come from HNSCC cell lines. These vesicles change the immune environment by increasing PD-L1 expression in fibroblastic reticular cells [69].

Type III interferons only work with a small group of receptors made up of IFNLR1 and IL-10R2. These receptors are mostly found on epithelial surfaces and certain types of myeloid cells [70]. Changes in the expression or location of IFNLR1 and IL-10R2, whether they are caused by processes inside the tumor or by factors in the tumor microenvironment, can change the strength and quality of type III IFN responses. These changes could have an impact on antiviral defenses, antitumor immunology, and, ultimately, the effectiveness of IFN-based treatments for HNSCC [70].

The therapeutic aspects of HNSCC IFN

It was recently discovered that STING, an intracellular DNA sensor activator of interferon genes, is a key part of the immune system’s capacity to resist viruses and tumors by increasing the production of cytokines. Human papillomavirus (HPV) is known to cause a specific type of HNSCC, but it is unclear how the STING pathway works in a virus-induced tumor environment [9]. Recent studies have started to make these pathways clearer, especially when it comes to HPV-related HNSCC. STING is expressed differently in these cancers than in HPV-negative cases, and HPV-positive cell lines often have a significant problem with signaling through this route. Immunohistochemical tests of human tissue samples have shown that HPV-related cancers have higher levels of STING expression inside the tumors, and this higher expression is linked to better patient survival [9]. Activating the STING signaling pathway may help the immune system fight HNSCC, especially when combined with therapeutic monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) like cetuximab, an EGFR inhibitor. Recent progress in immunotherapy has sparked new interest in HNSCC treatment. At the same time, previous research has shown that targeted therapy with cetuximab, an EGFR-specific mAb, enhances clinical response and survival in some cases when used with chemotherapy or radiation therapy [71]. There has been much research to find out the immunologic mechanism that makes the treatment even more effective. Studies have already shown that cetuximab activates NK cells and releases IFN-γ, which helps dendritic cells (DC) mature and start adaptive immune reactions [72]. On the other hand, it is unclear how the molecules fit within a larger molecular landscape [73].

It is generally agreed that for antitumor immune responses to work after PD-1/PD-L1 blockade, tumor-specific T cells must be reactivated and clonally proliferate in the tumor microenvironment (TME). The different outcomes of cancer immunotherapy can be partly explained by how different the TME is [61, 74].

In HPV-positive HNSCC, viral oncoproteins E2, E5, E6, and E7 can stop the STING pathway from working by breaking down cGAS/STING components, blocking TBK1/IRF3 signaling, and causing epigenetic silencing. Finding strategies to restore STING signaling in HPV-related cancers is crucial because this suppression may reduce the effectiveness of STING-targeted therapy [9, 75].

Two studies were the first to show that anti-PD-1 mAb alone could effectively and safely treat advanced HNSCC. These included the POLARIS-02 study with teraplizumab and the CAPTAIN study with carrilizumab [62]. HPV+ patients have a better prognosis than HPV-negative patients. However, today, treatments fail in one in six of HPV+ HNSCC cases with local recurrence or metastases in other parts of the body [76]. It was discovered that cetuximab had lower overall and tumor-free survival rates in HPV-positive HNSCC than cisplatin. This means that no medicine still works for all patients [77, 78]. So, it is essential to develop new treatments for HNSCC quickly [64].

Radiation therapy is an important part of treating HNSCC because it kills cancer cells directly and boosts the immune system’s ability to fight tumors. Radiation can hurt DNA, which can cause tumor cells to collect cytosolic DNA fragments. The cGAS–STING pathway can then find these pieces. When STING is turned on, it makes type I interferons and pro-inflammatory signals, which help dendritic cells mature and CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes move into the tumor microenvironment and become active. Radiation can boost the immune system, but it depends on STING signaling. It can also make immunotherapies work better. The results suggest that researchers should look into treatment combinations that use radiation to turn on cGAS along with STING agonists or immune checkpoint inhibitors to make treatments more effective [79, 80].

Two types of macrophages are found around tumors: M1 and M2. M1 can kill cancer cells and damage the extracellular matrix, while M2 can help the tumor grow. IL-4 can change M1 to M2, while IFN-γ can change M2 to M1 [81]. In animal models, blocking the gamma isoform of phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3Kγ) has been shown to increase the expression of M1 macrophages [82]. IPI-549 is a PI3Kγ inhibitor that is being studied as a single treatment or in combination with the immune checkpoint inhibitor nivolumab for people with HNSCC in a phase 2 trial (NCT03795610) [83]. It should be noted that future research should avoid using M1/M2 terminology; researchers should instead evaluate these factors as molecular dynamics of microglia [84].

Signaling is a potential way to treat HNSCC. One interesting thing about HNSCC is that 90% of the tumors overexpress epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), which helps the tumor grow. Cancer cells may also collect EGFR by amplifying the gene in 10–58% of cases [85–87].

ISG15 is the initially defined ubiquitin-like protein modifier that is triggered through type I IFN pathways [88]. Necroptosis and cGAS-STING signaling were intrinsically coordinated to produce ISG15, a key regulator of necroptosis-activated metastasis. Furthermore, RAGE was proposed as a novel extracellular ISG15 receptor that influenced the reprogramming of the peri-necroptotic milieu and lymphatic metastasis linked to ISG15. Our findings demonstrated a hitherto unidentified cGAS-ISG15-RAGE-dependent reprogramming of the necroptotic TME, which converts the necroptotic stress into an invasive force to promote the circulation of HNSCC cells. A significant contribution of ISG15 to the development of HNSCC has been established by proving its programmed production via necroptosis-cGAS arrangement and its downstream modulation through RAGE [89].

Interferon-based treatments in HNSCC

The efficiency of IFN-γ and autophagy blockade as a combinational protocol for the therapy of OSCC has recently been studied [90]. In vitro and in vivo studies were conducted to examine the synergistic role of IFN-γ and chloroquine, an autophagy inhibitor. IFN-γ caused OSCC cells to undergo autophagy, apoptosis, and anti-proliferation. Autophagy-associated protein 5 (Atg5) [91], a significant component of autophagosomes, was a prominent finding in IFN-γ-triggered autophagy flux. IFN-γ and chloroquine showed a clear synergistic impact on cell growth suppression and cell death in OSCC cells and xenograft models. These results indicated that IFN-γ-triggered autophagy confers protection. The blockade of autophagy flux enhanced IFN-γ-modulated cell death in OSCC. Dual therapy by IFN-γ and autophagy blockers may deserve further investigation to determine safety and efficacy in other studies [90]. IFN-α could induce dormant cancer stem cells (CSC) in OSCC to accelerate their destruction through traditional chemotherapeutics. A recent animal study showed IFN-α could transcriptionally increase the expression of CD44/ALDH1A1. IFN-α treatment augmented the cytotoxic role of cisplatin, erlotinib, and nimotuzumab in OSCC in preclinical settings [10].

IFN-related chemicals could be used to find new biomarkers. It is being looked into whether pembrolizumab and cetuximab can be utilized individually or in combination with cisplatin and various other chemotherapy medications like 5-fluorouracil and/or docetaxel to treat HNSCC. Recent ex vivo experiments showed substantial heterogeneity in response to cetuximab, pembrolizumab, and combinational treatments with and without IFN-γ activation. Furthermore, the authors interlinked IFN-γ-activated CXCL10/IFN-γ-induced protein (IP-10) secretion and enhanced outcomes in HNSCC patients responsive to IFN-γ as well as pembrolizumab and cetuximab. An IP-10 score was suggested as an independent predictive marker for HNSCC outcome [92]. Retinoic acid and IFN-α could be effective against HNSCC and boost angiogenesis. Researchers found that antiangiogenic agents can synergistically suppress the outgrowth and the vascularization of HNSCC administered into the mouth floor of nude rodents. The antiangiogenic effect was increased by two-fold [93].

Radiation therapy breaks double-stranded DNA, which causes cytosolic DNA to build up and the cGAS–STING pathway to turn on. This process helps make type I interferon, increases the presentation of antigens, and increases the number of immune cells that get into the body. Combining radiation with STING agonists may boost the immune system’s ability to fight tumors and get around resistance [20].

Treatments that use interferon are very good at boosting the immune system. However, they do have many problems because their side effects can make you feel like you have the flu, make you tired, hurt your liver, and even cause cytopenia. These side effects can make patients less likely to finish their treatment and make it less effective. Localized delivery methods, such as nanoparticle carriers, intratumoral injections, or hydrogel-based release systems, can help with this by enhancing IFN’s efficacy in the tumor microenvironment and preventing its spread to the rest of the body. It is easier to take medications without losing their effectiveness when they are pegylated or when the dosage is changed, such as when lower or split doses are used in combination therapy. Interferons have numerous immunostimulatory effects and work well with other treatments, but their widespread receptor expression could cause systemic side effects that limit dose and adherence. On the other hand, STING agonists can cause very localized immune activation when they are aimed at the right spot. However, systemic treatment can cause too much inflammation. Interferons have problems with tolerability, but STING agonists have to deal with problems with delivery and resistance mechanisms, especially in HPV-positive cancers [94]. The body’s inflammatory responses are what make STING agonists bad for you. Researchers are now looking into ways to make antitumor drugs safer. Nanoparticles, antibody-drug conjugates, and molecular engineering are some of these methods. To get better antitumor immunity with the least amount of harm, it is important to change how and at what doses the drugs are given [95, 96].

Challenges through the IFN therapy in the HNSCC

One of the main challenges of the STING agonist is managing toxicity. Activation of the STING signaling triggers abundant excretion of hyperinflammatory cytokines, including type I IFN (e.g., IFN-α, IFN-β) and other IFNs, including IFN-γ, leading to critical adverse events such as fever, chills, and cytokine release syndrome (CRS). In addition to IFN-mediating chemotherapies and immunotherapies, IFN therapies themselves could be associated with various adverse events. The susceptibility to CRS is increased when IFN is given through systemic routes; therefore, enhancing dosing approaches and developing more selective STING inducers to reduce off-target binding are necessary. The heterogeneity in the cancer immune response challenges STING agonists. The difference in the tumors’ genetic makeup and immune environment facilitates the immune response. The determination of predictive markers is necessary to choose cases that STING-targeting pharmacotherapies will most effectively target. Significant regulatory and quality control challenges arise during the engineering and approval process of STING inducers, particularly when production techniques involve delivery systems such as nanoparticles and modified microbes [7, 97, 98].

Insights for the future

IFN-γ resistance poses a clinical difficulty, particularly in relapsing or advanced HNSCC cases. The mechanism behind this resistance is complex, involving the upregulation of SOCS proteins that contribute to JAK-STAT pathway desensitization and potential epigenetic modifications that may influence the expression of STAT1 or related genes [99]. However, this aspect needs further exploration, specifically in HNSCC. Addressing these resistance mechanisms is essential for optimizing IFN-γ-based therapies and strategically combining them with other approaches, like immune checkpoint inhibitors, to boost antitumor immunity and enhance clinical results in HNSCC [100, 101].

There are numerous biological and translational challenges that make it difficult to use STING-based therapies in HNSCC. The immune system in the area around the tumor is different for each patient and lesion. This affects how the STING pathway functions and its effectiveness in agonist therapy. There are still many questions about how well the treatment works. It is still challenging to give STING agonists systemically because of the toxic effects of inflammation. We need to find better ways to do this. At this point, it is crucial to pick the right people because there are no apparent signs of what will happen next. This shows how important it is to use biomarker-driven trial designs to identify the best candidates for STING-targeted therapies [8, 102–104].

Another recommendation is to focus on gene editing technologies with a focus on STING signaling in HNSCC. Mending the STING activation in these patients is a considerable problem; however, multiple approaches are being investigated. Gene-based treatment methods, including CRISPR or viral vectors, could amend mutations in the STING signaling elements, reinstating STING function. As an alternative, combinational treatments combining STING inducers with ICIs or DNA damage response blockers could boost antitumor immunity even in HNSCC lesions with partially deficient STING. Epigenetic treatments that reverse the silencing of genes associated with STING are also being investigated to normalize STING activation. Even though such methods hold promise, additional HNSCC clinical trials are required to verify viability and efficiency in compensating STING signaling deficiencies [64, 87, 105–107].

Because STING agonists cause systemic inflammatory toxicities, more and more people are interested in ways to limit drug exposure to the tumor site. Researchers are looking into ways to limit STING activation to the tumor microenvironment, such as intratumoral injection, nanoparticle delivery methods, and antibody-drug conjugates. These methods could boost antitumor immunity while causing less damage to the rest of the body. Antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) are one of the most appealing ways to target precisely among these methods. Antibody-drug conjugates that contain STING agonists are a promising way to deliver drugs directly to tumor cells, which could improve treatment efficacy and reduce inflammation-related side effects [108–111].

Researchers are looking into extracellular vesicle (EV)-based delivery methods. These methods offer a unique and highly adjustable way to get STING agonists directly into the tumor microenvironment, building on previous therapies that targeted tumors. In the 1970 s, exosomes were discovered by Wolf and colleagues. In later decades, extracellular vesicles (EVs), including exosomes and microparticles, were redefined as a robust means of treatment delivery [112]. Exosomes are tiny, versatile, membrane-bound entities that could be modified to circulate curative factors, including cyclic dinucleotide (CDN), directly to special cells, such as immunological cells in the TME. CDN-loaded exosome STING inducers suggest a revolutionary method for cancer immune-mediating therapy. A good example is “ExoSTING”, a prime instance of a CDN-carrying STING based on exosome inducer explored by Codiak BioSciences. Researchers have engineered to enhance the transportation and activity of the STING signaling activators in cancerous lesions to trigger the natural features of EVs and enhance immunological cells’ targeting and absorption of CDNs. For end-stage HNSCC solid tumors, exoSTING is now being evaluated in stage I/II RCTs (ClinicalTrials.gov-NCT04592484). To strongly activate STING signaling within the TME, the medication is administered into the tumor. Strong immunological responses marked by increased infiltration of T-lymphocytes and other immune effectors into the malignant lesion are produced by the localized and robust activation of STING made possible by the EV-reliant delivery method. The goal of the ongoing clinical trials is to better define exoSTING’s efficacy and safety while also examining its effectiveness in combination with immune-mediating therapies like ICIs. The initial results are promising, indicating that exoSTING could turn into an invaluable addition to the wide range of STING-based malignancy treatments, for cancerous lesions resistant to traditional therapeutics [97, 113].

The intracellular and presentation efficacy of APCs is increased by nanoscale delivery devices that alter the cellular absorption of STING inducers from direct diffusion to endocytosis [114].

Hybrid manganese nanoparticle simultaneously accelerates the destruction of HNSCC malignant stem cells and induces STING signaling to enhance cancer Immunotherapeutics. Conclusions

STING pathways and IFN signaling influence the clinical course of HNSCC. The effect of many chemotherapies and mAb treatments on IFN pathways elements has been explored; however, efforts to maximize efficacy while managing adverse events such as CRS need to be extended. Also, the role of novel therapeutics such as nanoparticles and exosome-based cell-free treatments in mediating STING elements has been explored. These experiments are essential to overcome challenges faced in maximizing the efficacy of solid tumor treatments. IFN therapies have been used as pre-treatment before chemotherapy to enhance effectiveness. Moreover, research suggests better efficacy for chemotherapies and mAbs that activate the STING pathway in addition to their conventional antitumoric characteristic (e.g., enhanced antiangiogenesis). For the future, we suggest that studies attempt to fit the molecular alterations in the STING pathway into a larger landscape. Also, additional studies are recommended to determine the role of STING activation in changing susceptibility to adverse events.
