Development and validation of predictive models for mortality of cases with COVID-19 (Omicron BA.5.2.48 and B.7.14): a retrospective study
Authors: Peifang Li, Huiliang Yang, Jinyu Wu, Yanfei Ma, Ailin Hou, Jiali Chen, Ning Ning
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesWith the emergence of new COVID-19 variants (Omicron BA.5.2.48 and B.7.14), predicting the mortality of infected patients has become increasingly challenging due to the continuous mutation of the virus. Existing models have shown poor performance and limited clinical utility. This study aims to identify the independent risk factors and develop practical predictive models for mortality among patients infected with new COVID-19 variants.DesignA retrospective study.Setting and participantsWe extracted data from 1029 COVID-19 patients in the respiratory disease wards of a general hospital in China between 22 December 2022 and 15 February 2023.Outcome measuresMortality within 15 days after hospital discharge.ResultsA total of 987 cases with new COVID-19 variants (Omicron BA.5.2.48 and B.7.14) were eventually included, among them, 153 (15.5%) died. Non-invasive ventilation, intubation, myoglobin, international normalised ratio, age, number of diagnoses, respiratory rate, pulse, neutrophil count and albumin were the most important predictors of mortality among new COVID-19 variants. The area under the curve of logistic regression (LR), decision tree (DT) and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) models were 0.959, 0.883 and 0.993, respectively. The diagnostic accuracy was 0.926 for LR, 0.918 for DT and 0.977 for XGBoost. XGBoost model had the highest sensitivity (0.908) and specificity (0.989).ConclusionOur study developed and validated three practical models for predicting mortality in patients with new COVID-19 variants. All models performed well, and XGBoost was the best-performing model.

Background

The COVID-19 pandemic has become a global emergency, poses a challenge to the healthcare system and disrupts the international culture, society and economy. According to statistics from the WHO, there have been 775 293 630 cumulative COVID-19 cases until March 2023.1

Since its discovery, COVID-19 has been characterised by high rates of severe illness rate, hospitalisation and mortality. In 2019, the sudden influx of patients into the hospital crashed the medical and health system. Patients experienced respiratory tract involvements ranging from mild sore throat to severe pneumonia and tended to progress rapidly to fatal complications, such as acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) and multiple organ dysfunction.2 3 In the first year of COVID-19, despite the government’s published data on 6 million deaths,4 the WHO5 and the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation6 reported 14.9 million and 18.2 million excess deaths in 2020, respectively.

There are some existing prediction models for risk factors associated with death. At the very start, clinicians directly used the Simplified Acute Physiology Score V.3 (SAPS V.3), a model widely used in intensive care units to evaluate disease severity and predict in-hospital mortality.7 However, the SAPS V.3 has proved to be inaccurate in estimating the mortality of COVID-19 inpatients in various pilots across regions.8 9 Thus, researchers started to build a custom-designed predictive model for COVID-19.

By the initial stage, clinical features extracted from the patient were modelled by reinforcement algorithms, such as logistic regression (LR), Cox regression and gradient boosting decision tree. Schmidt et al discovered that high-sensitive troponin I concentration, fasting glycaemia, immature neutrophils count and high urine protein concentration were predictors for progression from moderate or severe to critical COVID-19 with a multivariable LR model among 190 patients.10 By using the Cox regression model, Atlam et al reported that age, muscle pain, pneumonia and throat pain are the most important predictors of inpatient mortality.11 Many algorithms are available to build helpful predictive models. However, most of them are difficult to interpret and apply in clinical practice. Nowadays, artificial intelligence (AI) models have been developed to predict the outcomes of COVID-19 patients.1113 AI can recognise radiographic images with COVID-19 patterns. However, the imaging features are not obvious in the early stage, which limits the usage of these models. In addition, these models have general limitations, including small cohort sizes, retrospective studies and heterogeneity between populations.

COVID-19 can cause SARS-CoV-2, which belongs to the β-coronavirus family.14 As an RNA virus, the SARS-CoV-2 is easy to mutate, leading to failures in established detection and treatment methods. During the study period, the main new variants of COVID-19 in Sichuan province are Omicron BA.5.2.48 (66.37%) and B.7.14 (22.71%).15 Although Omicron BA.5.2.48 and B.7.14 are weakened and more approaches have been developed to protect against or treat COVID-19, experts are concerned that patients may not have long-lasting protective immunity, and recurrent infection of SARS-CoV-2 may lead to increases in death rates. As of 2023, the number of newly reported death cases is still over 4000 per week globally.1 Considering the cost and effect, we need a tool to identify populations with high risk of progression and mortality.

Given the multiple rounds of infection and mutation, a new and more effective method is required to screen high-risk and potentially high-risk groups for the risk of death on admission. Therefore, we conducted a comprehensive analysis to identify the independent risk factors and develop and visualise predictive models for short-term mortality of COVID-19 pneumonia caused by Omicron BA.5.2.48 and B.7.14.

Methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective study was conducted in the respiratory disease wards of our hospital between 22 December 2022, and 15 February 2023. A total of 1029 COVID-19 patients who tested positive for SARS-CoV-2 by nucleic acid test were included in the study. Four levels of disease severity for COVID-19 according to the Chinese Clinical Guidance for COVID-19 Pneumonia Diagnosis and Treatment (7th version) were defined: mild, moderate, severe and critically ill.16 In this study, we classified the severe and critically as severe cases, while the rest two levels as non-severe cases. Patients who died within 24 hours of admission were given tracheal intubation or tracheotomy before hospital admission, or lost to follow-up after discharge were excluded.

Data collection

Demographic, clinical and laboratory data collected within 24 hours of admission were extracted from electronic medical reports by the research team. Demographic variables included age, gender, height, weight, nation and history of drink and smoking. Clinical data included the severity of COVID-19 disease, the number of diagnoses, the activity of daily living (ADL) measured by Modified Barthel Index,17 vital signs on admission (temperature, pulse, respiratory rate, blood pressure and pain), clinical symptoms (fever, cough, dyspnoea, distress, myalgia, stethalgia and headache), comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, respiratory distress syndrome, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), respiratory failure, phlebothrombosis, anaemia, malnutrition, hypoproteinemia, organ transplant and more). The study also collected information on the modes of respiratory support (non-invasive ventilation, intubation or tracheotomy tube) during the hospitalisation.

Blood test data was collected within 24 hours of admission, including complete blood count (erythrocyte count, haemoglobin, hemameba count, blood platelet count, neutrophil count and lymphocyte count), biochemical parameters (total protein, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), AST/ALT, total bilirubin, albumin, glucose, creatinine, carbamide, glomerular filtration rate (GFR) and lactic dehydrogenase (LDH)), electrolyte level (Na, K, Cl, Ca and Mg), coagulation index (prothrombin time, international normalised ratio (INR), thrombin time and fibrinogen), cardiac biomarkers (myoglobin, creatine kinase MB isoenzyme, troponin, brain natriuretic peptide), blood gas analysis (partial pressure of carbon dioxide, arterial oxygen partial pressure, oxygen saturation), procalcitonin, C reactive protein (CRP) and interleukin 6. The data were reviewed by two researchers, and a third researcher adjudicated any differences and reverified outliers and missing values with electronic medical reports.

Outcome measures

The main outcome was mortality within 15 days after the hospital discharge of participants. Patients with treatment abandoning and unknown survival outcomes who were discharged by themselves were followed up with the outcome 15 days after hospital discharge by phone call.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables, which are normal distribution, were described as mean (SD) and compared using an unpaired, two-tailed t-test. Continuous variables, which do not show normal distribution, were expressed as medians with IQRs and compared using the Wilcoxon rank sum test. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to estimate the normality of data. Categorical variables were reported as absolute values and percentages and compared using the χ2 test and Fisher’s test.

Variables with >20% missing values were deleted. We imputed other missing values by random forest (missForest, R package, V.4.2.1). Because candidate variables exist in high-dimensional complex collinearity, we used the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression model to select the most useful prognostic risk factors of death in COVID-19 patients. LASSO regression constructs a penalty function to select the main variables. Some variables, whose coefficients were compressed to 0 due to no strong correlation with the dependent variable, were eliminated. These independent variables strongly related to the dependent variable were screened and corresponding coefficients were analysed. The complexity of the LASSO regression model was decided by the parameter λ. The range of λ was 0–1, corresponding to different numbers of independent variables. The larger λ was, the greater the penalty for the variables of linear models, and the coefficients of more variables were compressed to 0. Thus, we obtained an ultimate model with fewer variables. So we should select the optimal parameter (λ) by 10-fold cross-validation. The optimal λ value was obtained at the minimum criteria (λ min), where the model performs best. And at the 1 SE of the minimum criteria (λ 1SE), we obtained the simplest and best model. Because after λ reached a certain size, continuing to increase the number of model-independent variables (reducing λ) did not significantly improve model performance. λ 1SE gave a model that had excellent performance but the least number of independent variables.

The variables, screened by LASSO regression, were used to formulate the LR model. Then we formulated a nomogram to visualise the results of the LR model. All variables were included to construct a decision tree (DT) model to explore different predictors. The Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) model was established based on the results of LASSO regression to predict the outcome of hospitalised patients with COVID-19. Further, we used the Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) method to interpret the XGBoost model. SHAP value visually exhibited each feature’s importance and contribution to mortality.

To evaluate the prediction ability of different models, we conducted a confusion matrix of each model to compare the actual values and predictive values and plotted the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. At the same time, we compared the accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, Youden’s index and area under the curve (AUC, 95% CI) of those models.

All statistical analyses, except for XGBoost and SHAP, were implemented via R (V.4.1.3) with R software packages ‘dplyr’, ‘epiDisplay’, ‘gtsummary’, ‘missForest’, ‘glmnet’, ‘rms’, ‘car’, ‘rpart’, ‘partykit’, ‘caret’, ‘InformationValue’ and ‘pROC’. XGBoost and SHAP were carried out by python (V.2.7) with python software packages ‘numpy’, ‘pandas’, ‘xgboost’ and ‘shap’. A p value lower than 0.05 (two sided) was regarded as statistically significant.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

During the study period, 1029 patients with COVID-19 were admitted to our institution. 42 patients were excluded, leaving a total of 987 patients who were eventually included, 153 (15.5%) died and 834 (84.5%) survived. A flow diagram of patients’ selection is shown in figure 1. The median age of the patients was 69 years (IQR: 54–81 years). There were 672 (68.1%) male patients. A total of 497 (50.4%) were classified as severe and 490 (49.6%) as non-severe cases. The majority of the patients had comorbidities, such as hypertension (411, 41.6%), hypoproteinemia (329, 33.3%), electrolyte disturbances (352, 35.7%) and diabetes (251, 25.4%). The most common COVID-19-related symptoms were cough (84.3%) and dyspnoea (59.9%), followed by chest distress (17.9%) and myalgia (9.5%). Factors selection

Due to a high proportion of missing data (27.25%), body mass index (BMI) was excluded from further analysis. Other variables with missing values below 20% were imputed using a random forest model. LASSO regression analysis was then performed to select predictive factors of death in COVID-19 patients (figure 2). The tuning parameter (λ) was selected by 10-fold cross-validation (figure 2A). Two dotted vertical lines were drawn at λ min and λ 1SE. The λ min was a lambda value that yielded the highest AUC, indicating good model performance. On the other hand, the λ 1SE resulted in a model with good performance but a minimum number of variables. Log (λ 1SE) was chosen as the optimal value, which was equal to −3.31. A coefficient profile plot was produced (figure 2B), with a dotted vertical line drawn at the λ 1SE criteria, resulting in 15 non-zero coefficients. The 15 predictive factors were age, number of diagnoses, respiratory rate, pulse, ADL, neutrophil count, albumin, GFR, LDH, INR, myoglobin, CRP, respiratory failure, intubation and non-invasive ventilation. The coefficients of 15 variables were listed in online supplemental table 1.

LR model

Based on the LASSO regression results, an LR model was constructed with a variance inflation factor of less than 2 for the predictive factors. The LR model revealed that age (OR=1.03, p=0.001), number of diagnoses (OR=1.07, p=0.041), respiratory rate (OR=1.13, p=0.002), INR (OR=2.29, p=0.013), CRP (OR=1, p=0.009), respiratory failure (OR=1.98, p=0.027), pulse (OR=1.04, p<0.001), non-invasive ventilation (OR=11.92, p<0.001), intubation (OR=18.48, p<0.001) were independent risk factors, while albumin (OR=0.92, p=0.004) and GFR (OR=0.98, p=0.002) were independent protective factors for the mortality of COVID-19 (online supplemental table 2).

To visualise the results of the prediction model, a nomogram was created using the independently associated factors from the LR analysis. The nomogram transformed the complex regression equation into a visual graph, making it easier for clinicians to interpret and apply the results (online supplemental figure 1). The sum of the assigned number of points for each factor in the nomograms corresponded to the total points, and higher total points were associated with higher mortality. According to nomograms, INR had the greatest impact on total points, while respiratory failure had the smallest impact.

DT Model

The DT model is visible, clinically operable and easy to interpret due to its recursive tree-based decision system. First, we input all variables to build a DT model. When it split 12 times, and the error rate was minimum (xerror=0.673), a certain but complex tree model was built (online supplemental figure 2). Second, we used cross-validation to trim back the full tree. We set the max nodes of split no more than four and chose the smallest error rate to obtain a meaningful and simple DT model (online supplemental figure 3). Patients who are not non-invasively ventilated and intubated have a very low risk of death of 3.4%. However, a high risk of death of 93.8% for intubated patients whose troponin>18.7 ng/mL. Non-invasively ventilated patients whose myoglobin ≥76.6 ng/mL and whose INR level is more than 0.995 had a risk of death of 76.5%.

XGBoost model

The XGBoost ranks the individual variables based on their relative influence, and the top 15 variables are presented in online supplemental figure 4A. The intubation factor had the most potent predictive power, followed by non-invasive ventilation, myoglobin, ADL and INR. However, the rank of feature importance could differ in different algorithms. To visually exhibit each factor’s importance and contribution to mortality, the SHAP algorithm was conducted. Online supplemental figure 4A presented whether that feature was high (in red) or low (in blue) for that observation according to the SHAP value, a higher SHAP value indicates a higher probability of mortality from COVID-19. For each feature, each dot was created according to its positive (red colour) or negative (blue colour) contribution to the model output of each patient, creating the dotted line for each feature of all patients.

Comparisons of models’ performance

The performance of the LR, DT and XGBoost models was compared using confusion matrices (online supplemental table 3) and ROC curves. As shown in table 2, the XGBoost model had the highest AUC of 0.993, followed by the LR model with an AUC of 0.959 and the DT model with an AUC of 0.883. The XGBoost model also had the highest diagnostic accuracy of 0.977, followed by the LR model with an accuracy of 0.926 and the DT model with an accuracy of 0.918. Furthermore, the XGBoost model achieved the highest sensitivity of 0.989 and specificity of 0.908, indicating that it had a better ability to correctly identify patients with COVID-19 mortality risk compared with the other models. These results suggest that the XGBoost model is the most effective model for predicting mortality risk in COVID-19 patients. Discussion

The COVID-19 pandemic has emerged as a global emergency, presenting a significant challenge to the healthcare system and disrupting global culture, society and economy. While various mortality prediction models have been created since the onset of the pandemic, they are often complex and challenging to interpret and apply. Therefore, we undertook a retrospective cohort study to develop more practicable and accurate models of mortality among new COVID-19 variants (Omicron BA.5.2.48 and B.7.14). We used a relatively advanced XGBoost model that was rarely employed in other studies about predicting mortality in COVID-19 patients. Additionally, visualising the model results is also a major strength of this article.

Our study identified several important predictors in our mortality prediction models, including intubation, non-invasive ventilation, myoglobin, number of diagnoses, CRP, INR and age. It is well documented in the literature that patients with intubation or non-invasive ventilation or with higher myoglobin and INR levels have a higher risk of death, which is consistent with our findings.161821 However, those features were studied in isolation in previous studies. In our study, the DT model indicated the relationship among intubation, non-invasive ventilation, myoglobin, INR and mortality and provided threshold values associated with increased mortality. This finding could assist physicians in making better clinical decisions to reduce mortality among COVID-19 patients who require intubation or non-invasive ventilation by controlling the level of myoglobin and INR.

Our study found that older age predicted a higher risk of death, which was consistent with previous studies.2224 The increased risk of mortality in older patients could be attributed to the impairment of the immune system and increased risk for immunopathology.25 We also found that the number of diagnoses was related to death, which had been reported in previous studies.2 22 23 Although gender and BMI were associated with the mortality of COVID-19 patients in other studies,2629 they are included in our models. However, our study showed that the mortality proportion of male patients was higher than that of female patients, providing further evidence to support the hypothesis of male’s vulnerability to COVID-19.20 29 BMI was not input in the model due to its high percentage of missing values (more than 20%) and lack of significant difference between the survival and the non-survival group according to single-variable analysis.

Fumagalli et al conducted a retrospective cohort study to develop a risk score for predicting in-hospital mortality in COVID-19 patients, and their results showed that comorbidities and the number of chronic diseases were independent predictors of mortality.30 Consistent with their findings, we also found that COVID-19 patients with more comorbidities have a higher risk of mortality. The rapidity of COVID-19 progression is largely influenced by the presence of comorbidities and extrapulmonary organ injuries. According to the results of Zaim’s study,31 exposure to SARS-CoV-2 in individuals with comorbidities such as diabetes, cerebrovascular disease and COPD can be detrimental to the lungs, heart, kidneys and liver, leading to ARDS, heart failure, arrhythmias, renal failure and, eventually, mortality. The comorbidities create a vicious cycle of infection, which increases the mortality of COVID-19 patients after admission. Additionally, the number of complications is also significantly associated with significant mortality.

In this study, we applied machine learning algorithms to develop three prognostic models for predicting mortality in COVID-19 patients with new variants. All models performed well in the overall population, with an AUC of 0.878–0.961 and an accuracy of 0.913–0.977. Compared with LR and DT models, the XGboost model performed best in predicting mortality. XGBoost is efficient, flexible and lightweight, and it uses second derivatives to make the loss function more accurate while adding regular terms to avoid overfitting. In addition, most predictive models are challenging to interpret due to their black-box modelling strategies. In this study, we used nomograms to visualise the LR model and plotted the DT model. The SHAP algorithm was used to visually display the importance of each factor in predicting mortality for the XGboost model. These visualisations make the three models more visible, clinically operable and easy to interpret.

There are several limitations to this study. First, as a retrospective study, this research involved filtering data from datasets and excluding patients with missing data for the collected variables, which could potentially introduce selection bias. Second, these models were not externally validated, and their generalisability and implementation across different populations are unknown. Third, the postdischarge follow-up time was relatively brief, so further investigation is needed to determine whether these models can predict the long-term mortality of COVID-19 patients.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed three models to predict the mortality of patients with new COVID-19 variants. All models demonstrated good performance and practicality, with XGboost being the best-performing model.
