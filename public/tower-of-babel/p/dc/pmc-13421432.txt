PET/CT false-negative angioimmunoblastic T-cell lymphoma: a case report and literature review
Authors: Yiling Bai, Yisong Xiong, Hengning Lian, Guangqing Shi, Yue Teng, Lijie Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Angioimmunoblastic T-cell lymphoma (AITL) is a peripheral T-cell lymphoma characterized by heterogeneous clinical and immunologic manifestations. We report a 65-year-old female whose morphologically abnormal superficial lymph nodes were fluoro‐D‐glucose (FDG)-negative on positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT), while deep mediastinal and hilar nodes were hypermetabolic. Initial biopsies of FDG-avid sites were non-diagnostic. Definitive diagnosis was ultimately achieved via excisional biopsy of a superficial node. This case underscores that AITL may exhibit PET/CT false-negativity, highlighting the need for integrating clinical, laboratory, flow cytometry, and histopathologic data to avoid diagnostic delays.

Introduction

Angioimmunoblastic T-cell lymphoma (AITL) is a subtype of peripheral T-cell lymphoma (PTCL) originating from T follicular helper (Tfh) cells, accounting for approximately 1–2% of all non-Hodgkin lymphomas (1). Its clinical presentation is characteristically heterogeneous, encompassing not only lymphadenopathy but also systemic immune dysregulation, including autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA), polyclonal hypergammaglobulinemia, Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) reactivation, and cutaneous manifestations (2, 3). This immunological complexity frequently leads to initial misattribution to infectious or autoimmune etiologies, contributing to diagnostic delay.

2‐deoxy‐2‐[18F] fluoro‐D‐glucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography (18F-FDG PET/CT) has become an important modality for staging and treatment response assessment in lymphoma due to its ability to evaluate metabolic activity and lesion distribution throughout the entire body. Most patients with AITL show widespread FDG-avid lymphadenopathy and organ involvement on PET/CT imaging, however, metabolic heterogeneity is frequently observed. This case report presents a patient diagnosed with AITL, in whom PET/CT imaging revealed hypermetabolic activity in deep lymph nodes. Notably, despite abnormal morphology of superficial lymph nodes (cervical, axillary, and inguinal etc.) observed on ultrasound, no significant FDG uptake was detected in these regions. This lack of avidity led to the initial avoidance of superficial lymph node biopsies, resulting in unsuccessful diagnostic attempts at hypermetabolic deep sites and a subsequent delay in definitive diagnosis. This uncommon imaging manifestation illustrates that, in rare cases, PET/CT metabolic guidance alone may be insufficient for directing biopsy strategy, and underscores the value of integrating multimodal clinical data in the diagnostic evaluation of AITL.

Case presentations

A 65-year-old woman with no significant past medical history initially presented to an outside institute in July 2024 with right-sided pleural effusion. Diagnostic work-up, including thoracentesis, pleural biopsy, and endobronchial ultrasound-guided transbronchial needle aspiration (EBUS-TBNA) of the subcarinal lymph node (station 7), revealed no evidence of malignancy. The pleural effusion resolved following symptomatic treatment, and the patient was discharged without a definitive diagnosis.

In late November 2024, the patient developed recurrent symptoms including productive cough, progressive dyspnea, right-sided pleuritic chest pain, and intermittent skin rash. She was admitted to our institution on December 5, 2024, at which point her clinical course had entered a rapidly progressive phase with multisystem involvement. Laboratory investigations demonstrated severe anemia (hemoglobin 55 g/L) with a strongly positive direct antiglobulin (Coombs) test, elevated indirect bilirubin (24.9 μmol/L), and markedly elevated serum ferritin (538.1 ng/mL), collectively consistent with AIHA. Additional laboratory findings included eosinophilia (0.88 × 109/L), polyclonal hypergammaglobulinemia (elevated IgM, IgG1, and γ-globulin fractions), and detectable EBV DNA. Superficial lymph node ultrasonography revealed bilateral cervical, axillary, and inguinal lymphadenopathy with cortical thickening. Chest CT demonstrated scattered patchy consolidations in the right lower lobe with punctate hypodense foci.

Ultrasound examination demonstrated morphologically abnormal lymph nodes in multiple superficial regions. In the bilateral cervical regions (levels I–IV), enlarged lymph nodes were identified bilaterally, with the largest measuring 13.7 × 6.5 mm on the right and 13.8 × 7.6 mm on the left, showing plump morphology, partially indistinct internal architecture, and internal vascularity on Doppler imaging. In the bilateral axillae, the largest node measured 27.0 × 10.5 mm on the right and 10.6 × 9.5 mm on the left, with loss of normal internal structure and internal blood flow signals. In the bilateral inguinal regions, the largest node measured 24.2 × 8.0 mm on the right and 11.7 × 7.3 mm on the left, with cortical thickening and internal vascularity. Despite these morphological abnormalities, subsequent 18F-FDG PET/CT (December 17, 2024, Figure 1) revealed no abnormal FDG uptake in any of these superficial lymph node regions, with maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax) below 2.5 in all stations, which is the threshold used to define metabolic positivity at our institution. In contrast, PET/CT demonstrated intense FDG avidity in deep thoracic lesions. An irregular soft tissue mass in the right hilar region measured 4.3 × 2.8 cm (CT attenuation approximately 35.0 HU) with markedly elevated FDG uptake (SUVmax 10.2, SUVmean 7.0), causing compression of the adjacent bronchus. Scattered patchy infiltrates, nodules, and consolidative lesions in the ipsilateral lung parenchyma also demonstrated increased FDG activity (SUVmax 10.3, SUVmean 8.7). Multiple enlarged lymph nodes in the right internal mammary region, bilateral hila, and mediastinum (maximum diameter approximately 2.0 cm) showed significantly elevated FDG uptake (SUVmax 10.4, SUVmean 6.0). Multiple small pulmonary nodules in the right lung (maximum diameter approximately 0.7 cm) and all left lung fields showed no abnormal FDG uptake. Guided by the metabolically active lesions, two CT-guided percutaneous lung biopsies were performed, both yielding only fibrotic tissue with inflammatory infiltration and no evidence of malignancy. EBUS-TBNA targeting FDG-avid mediastinal lymph nodes (stations 7 and 11L) was performed, with concurrent flow cytometry. Flow cytometry revealed 2.96% of lymphocytes (0.46% of nucleated cells) expressing an aberrant Tfh phenotype. However, histopathological analysis of the biopsy specimen showed only cartilage and mucinous debris, with no definitive tumor morphology. Concurrent flow cytometry of the pleural fluid (Figure 2) revealed lymphocytes accounting for 81.8% of nucleated cells, with aberrant T-cell populations comprising 7.66% of lymphocytes (5.03% of nucleated cells). Suspecting a systemic hematologic malignancy, bone marrow aspiration and flow cytometry were pursued. Flow cytometry performed on the bone marrow showed lymphocytes accounting for 5.8% of nucleated cells, including 0.6% aberrant T cells with a Tfh phenotype (0.04% of total nucleated cells). Bone marrow biopsy showed hypercellularity but no clear evidence of malignant infiltration. Although concurrent flow cytometry findings in pleural fluid, bone marrow, and mediastinal lymph nodes consistently supported the immunophenotypic profile of AITL, a definitive diagnosis could not be established solely due to the absence of histopathological biopsy confirmation. Although the cervical, axillary, and inguinal lymph nodes demonstrated no abnormal FDG uptake on PET/CT, their morphological abnormalities on ultrasonography prompted the clinical team to recommend excisional biopsy of a superficial lymph node to obtain adequate tissue for definitive histopathological diagnosis. However, the patient declined the procedure for personal reasons and was subsequently discharged without a confirmed diagnosis.

In May 2025, the patient was readmitted to our institution for further evaluation and management. AIHA remained refractory, with a persistently strongly positive Coombs test, and hemoglobin had declined to a nadir of 34 g/L. Following multidisciplinary consultation, partial exchange transfusion was performed, resulting in hemoglobin recovery to 52 g/L. Given the critical and persistent diagnostic uncertainty, the clinical team elected to move beyond the constraints of PET/CT metabolic guidance. On May 8, 2025, excisional biopsy was performed on a cervical lymph node that had previously been deemed FDG-negative on PET-CT. Histopathological examination of the excised specimen supported a diagnosis of AITL. Histopathological examination of the excised cervical lymph node revealed a lymphoproliferative process with effacement of normal nodal architecture. The neoplastic cells displayed morphological features consistent with Tfh cell origin, set within a background of prominent vascular proliferation, expanded follicular dendritic cell meshworks, and a mixed inflammatory infiltrate comprising plasma cells, eosinophils, and immunoblasts. Immunohistochemical staining demonstrated positivity for Tfh-associated markers including PD-1, CXCL13, and Bcl-6, as well as CD3, CD5, CD21, CD43, and CD56. The Ki-67 proliferation index was approximately 60%. Negative markers included ALK-P80, CD10, CD20, CD30, PAX-5, TIA-1, and TdT. Epstein-Barr virus-encoded RNA (EBER) in situ hybridization was positive, indicating EBV involvement within the tumor microenvironment. Taken together, the morphological features, immunophenotypic profile, and EBER positivity supported a definitive diagnosis of AITL.

Following definitive diagnosis, the patient commenced CHOP chemotherapy (cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone) on May 16, 2025, and completed eight cycles in total, with gradual improvement in respiratory symptoms. Restaging ¹8F-FDG PET/CT performed on January 20, 2026 (Figure 3), demonstrated near-complete resolution of the previously hypermetabolic right hilar soft tissue mass and right lower lobe pulmonary lesions, with no residual abnormal FDG uptake identified. No definitive enlargement or abnormal FDG uptake was identified in any of the remaining lymph node stations, including the previously involved bilateral hilar, mediastinal, and superficial nodal regions. No new FDG-avid lesions were identified. Overall, these findings were assigned a Deauville score of 1, consistent with a complete metabolic response (CMR). At the most recent follow-up, the patient remains clinically stable with no evidence of disease progression. The clinical timeline of this case is summarized in Table 1.

Discussion

In the present case, although the patient exhibited multiple characteristic clinical features of AITL, including lymphadenopathy, AIHA, polyclonal hypergammaglobulinemia, the greatest diagnostic challenge arose from the discrepancy between imaging findings and histopathological confirmation. Studies have shown that 18F-FDG PET/CT demonstrates high sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy in the diagnosis of AITL (4). Choi et al. reported widespread FDG avidity across T-cell lymphoma subtypes and advocated for the routine incorporation of PET/CT into their diagnostic workup (5). Similarly, Chen et al. suggested that a negative PET/CT result may reasonably preclude the need for biopsy given the extremely low false-negative rate reported in the literature (6). In the present case, however, marked FDG uptake was confined to deep mediastinal and hilar lymph nodes, while morphologically abnormal superficial cervical, axillary, and inguinal nodes remained metabolically inactive. This pattern led to the initial exclusion of superficial nodes from biopsy consideration, directly contributing to diagnostic delay.

Although the majority of AITL patients exhibit widespread FDG uptake on PET/CT, some cases demonstrate heterogeneous metabolic activity, leading to FDG-negative findings which are influenced by multiple factors, including lesion size, tumor metabolic characteristics, and the immune microenvironment. This “imaging–pathology decoupling” poses a risk for relying solely on PET/CT for lesion localization. Firstly, small lesions or nodules with necrosis or fibrosis are prone to underestimation due to the partial volume effect, resulting in reduced FDG visualization. A systematic review in 2021 indicated that small lesion size, necrosis, and image reconstruction parameters could contribute to false-negative PET results in lymphoma, highlighting the importance of integrating CT imaging for comprehensive assessment (7). It is important to distinguish between two mechanistically distinct forms of PET/CT false-negativity in this context. True biological PET-negativity refers to scenarios in which neoplastic cells exhibit intrinsically low glycolytic activity, resulting in FDG uptake below the detection threshold regardless of lesion size. In contrast, size-related limited detectability arises from the partial volume effect, whereby lesions smaller than approximately 1 cm are subject to standardized uptake values (SUVs) underestimation due to signal averaging with surrounding tissue, independent of the underlying tumor biology. In the present case, the superficial lymph nodes demonstrated SUVmax below 2.5 across all stations on PET/CT, with the largest node measuring approximately 1.0 cm in short axis. Given this size, partial volume effect-related underdetection cannot be excluded as a contributing factor. However, the diffuse involvement of multiple superficial stations and the consistent morphological abnormality on ultrasound suggest that biological factors, including the low neoplastic cell burden relative to the reactive microenvironment, may also have contributed to the observed metabolic inactivity. In the present case, both mechanisms may have operated concurrently, and definitive differentiation between them is not possible on the basis of available imaging data alone. Severely atrophic or structurally compromised lymph nodes, or lesions diffusely distributed in multiple micro-nodules, further increase the likelihood of missed lesions. A study evaluating bone marrow involvement in AITL using 18F-FDG PET/CT indicated that PET-positive bone marrow findings can obviate the need for repeat bone marrow biopsy to confirm involvement (8–10). However, a negative PET/bone marrow result does not definitively exclude the possibility of marrow involvement, suggesting the potential for lesion-level underdetection. Moreover, the intrinsic biological features of AITL are critical contributors to PET/CT false-negativity. Tumor cells in AITL are often masked by abundant inflammatory cells, plasma cells, eosinophils, and EBV-infected B cells, resulting in low actual tumor cell burden, which may exhibit metabolic activity below the PET detection threshold. AITL is characterized by a complex immune microenvironment, in which tumor cells may constitute a minor fraction of nodal tissue amidst abundant reactive elements, including B cells, plasma cells, follicular dendritic cells, and high endothelial venules. This milieu can obscure FDG-avid tumor signals, producing atypical uptake patterns (8). Dynamic interactions between tumor and immune cells may further modulate glucose metabolism, increasing intralesional heterogeneity and decreasing PET/CT sensitivity. Technical factors, including patient positioning, respiratory motion, scan timing, and image acquisition parameters, may additionally contribute to false-negative results (7).

Importantly, AITL is characterized by a prominent and complex tumor microenvironment, in which neoplastic T follicular helper cells often represent only a minor and spatially heterogeneous component of the involved lymph node. Previous studies have demonstrated that FDG uptake in AITL frequently reflects metabolic activation of non-neoplastic cellular components, including reactive T cells, expanded follicular dendritic cell networks, EBV-positive B immunoblasts, and inflammatory infiltrates, rather than true tumor burden (8). Consequently, FDG-avid lymph nodes may lack overt morphological or immunophenotypic evidence of lymphoma, particularly in early or evolving disease stages. Moreover, the patchy distribution of neoplastic cells in AITL predisposes to sampling bias, further contributing to discordance between metabolic imaging and histopathological findings. In this case, serial EBUS-TBNA and CT-guided biopsies of FDG-avid deep nodes yielded only inflammatory or fibrotic tissue, while definitive diagnosis was ultimately achieved through excisional biopsy of a PET/CT-negative superficial node. This observation is anecdotal and should not be interpreted as evidence against the overall reliability of FDG-PET in guiding biopsy decisions in AITL. Rather, it illustrates an important exception that may arise in cases with pronounced metabolic heterogeneity.

From a diagnostic standpoint, this case highlights several important lessons. First, morphological abnormality on ultrasonography should be regarded as an independent indication for biopsy, irrespective of FDG avidity on PET/CT. Second, the consistent identification of aberrant Tfh-phenotype T cells across multiple compartments, including pleural fluid, bone marrow, and mediastinal aspirate, by flow cytometry provided critical supportive evidence that maintained clinical suspicion despite non-diagnostic histopathology. Third, excisional biopsy remains superior to needle-based sampling in AITL, as it preserves nodal architecture essential for definitive diagnosis. While PET/CT-guided biopsy remains the standard approach in AITL, this case illustrates that in exceptional circumstances—particularly when PET-avid lesions repeatedly yield non-diagnostic material and overall clinical suspicion remains high—morphologically abnormal but metabolically inactive nodes may warrant consideration as alternative biopsy targets. This recommendation is based on a single case experience and should be applied with caution, pending validation in larger cohorts. Taken together, these findings underscore that a multimodal diagnostic approach, integrating clinical phenotype, serological markers, flow cytometry, and strategic biopsy site selection, is indispensable for avoiding diagnostic delay in AITL, particularly when PET/CT findings are discordant with the overall clinical picture.
