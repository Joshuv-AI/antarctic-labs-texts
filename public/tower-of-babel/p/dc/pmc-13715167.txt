Can blind box buying be addictive?: Measurement and associations with gambling disorder among Chinese adults
Authors: Jing Zhai, Mengxuan Zhang, Andrea Czakó, Haofeng Ling, Anise M. S. Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBlind boxes are gambling-like products with increasing popularity worldwide. Accumulating evidence has shown that blind box over-purchasing is a rising problem, yet no validated tool exists for its addictive components. Besides, the link between problematic blind box buying and gambling disorder (GD) has never been tested. To fill these knowledge gaps, the current study tested the psychometric performance of the Problematic Blind Box Buying Scale (PBBS) and further examined the associations between problematic blind box buying and GD at both construct and symptom levels using bivariate correlation and network analysis, respectively.MethodsThe sample consists of 1324 Chinese adults (52.3% female, Mage = 33.13, SDage = 6.73) who have experience with blind box buying.ResultsResults from exploratory and confirmatory factor analysis consistently suggested the unidimensionality of PBBS. This scale also showed good internal consistency, convergent validity, and concurrent validity. The positive correlations between problematic blind box buying and GD were found at both construct and symptom levels. Chasing loss was the central node, while the conflict symptom of GD and the relapse symptom of problematic blind box buying were the bridge symptoms in the network of problematic blind box buying and GD.ConclusionsThe current study is the first exploratory investigation of problematic blind box buying and its relationship with GD. It provides evidence for the validity and reliability of PBBS for measuring problematic blind box buying. Moreover, its findings also support the addictive potential of blind box buying and the associated risk with gambling disorder. These findings provide practical insights for the regulation and policy of blind box commerce and GD intervention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-026-05184-z.

Background

Blind box, which refers to the products (e.g., toys, cards, etc.) with random styles contained in an opaque package, is a new type of product that is increasingly popular across all ages [1]. Blind boxes are generally sold in series, with some styles being limited editions and having a lower probability of occurrence. Customers can only discover the exact style in the blind box after purchasing and opening it. Blind boxes are widespread in many countries, including Japan, China, and the USA, with a huge and rapidly growing market [2]. According to the publicly available data from POP MART (i.e., one of the largest blind box companies), it reached a revenue of over 13 million CNY (approximately 1.9 million USD) in 2024, with an annual growth rate of 106.9% [3]. Considering the randomness and uncertain rewards in blind boxes, consumers are vulnerable to risky and excessive purchases and even addiction to blind boxes. Many consumers may feel driven to buy blind boxes repeatedly by the desire for a rare item in those boxes, as well as a sense of “near miss” [4], even spending money or time beyond their means [5]. Potential adverse consequences related to blind box over-buying have also been noted in previous studies. Duan et al. [6] reported that blind box over-engagement was correlated with a higher risk of suicide among adults. In fact, regulators in China have enacted relevant policies to protect blind box consumers and minimize the harms of blind box over-engagement [7], supporting the fact that excessive or problematic blind box buying is not uncommon and warrants research attention on its addictive properties. To our best knowledge, there were no published study took the addiction perspective to investigate problematic blind box buying, probably because no validated assessment tool was available to capture its addiction symptoms. Therefore, the first aim of this study is to address this gap by extending Griffiths’ components model of addiction [8] to understand and assess problematic blind box buying.

Despite the rapidly growing market and consumer base of blind boxes, only two empirical studies on over- or problematic blind box buying were published [1, 6]. One study found that over-engagement was related to higher suicide risk [6], while another reported the positive association of impulsive buying intention with perceived uncertainty and curiosity [1]. Considering that blind box buying is fundamentally a shopping behavior, problematic blind box buying can be conceptualized as a sub-type of problematic shopping/purchase, which is commonly categorized as a behavioral addiction [9]. This study defined it as an addictive pattern of individuals’ blind box buying that adversely impacts other life domains (e.g., job, study, social relationships), and proposed its multiple warning signs from the addiction perspective. According to the components model of addiction [8], behavioral addictions typically exhibit the following common symptoms: tolerance, salience, withdrawal, mood modification, relapse, and conflict, with some behavioral addictions also showing a problem symptom [8]. This framework has been used to understand several rising addictive behaviors, such as social media use [10], work [11], and shopping [12]. Adapting the Bergen Shopping Addiction Scale [12], which was developed grounded on the components model of addiction, can be a cost-efficient approach to provide a valid instrument (i.e., the Problematic Blind Box Buying Scale [PBBS] in this study) to measure the addiction symptoms of problematic blind box buying to facilitate the knowledge of this addictive behavior and its potentially associated harms.

The Bergen Shopping Addiction Scale showed satisfactory psychometric properties with a single-factor structure in prior studies [13, 14]. In line with the Bergen Shopping Addiction Scale, problematic blind box buying is postulated to comprise seven core symptoms (e.g., tolerance), each represented by one item in PBBS. The current study aimed to test the psychometric characteristics of PBBS, including its one-factor structure, internal consistency, and validity. In terms of validity, both convergent and concurrent validities were examined: the former was indicated by any positive correlations with the descriptive norm and attitude of blind box buying, while the latter was evaluated by its positive associations with the purchase expenses and frequency because shopping or gambling frequency and monetary expenditure are consistently correlated with tendencies of shopping addiction or GD in previous research [15, 16].

Considering their shared symptoms and neural basis, behavioral addictions commonly co-occur and are interrelated. Sussman et al. [17] conducted a systematic review of 11 types of addictive behaviors (e.g., alcohol use, gambling, and Internet use) and found a high co-occurrence rate ranging from 10 to 50%, while shopping addiction was found to show a positive correlation with gambling disorder (GD) [18–20]. GD is defined as the recurrent and persistent problematic pattern of gambling that induces individuals’ functional impairments, such as problems in job/study and family conflicts [21]. Within the context of shopping, blind boxes are often considered a gambling-like product and share properties with gambling, such as paying money for a chance to get uncertain and potentially valuable rewards [1, 22]. Moreover, blind boxes typically cannot be returned or refunded merely because the consumers are dissatisfied with the randomized results, and this non-refundable feature is similar to the “no take-backs” feature of gambling [7]. Given the resemblances (e.g., randomized reinforcement processes and irreversibility) between blind box purchases and gambling, problematic blind box buying is also hypothesized to be positively associated with GD in this study.

In addition to the potential correlation between problematic blind box buying and GD at the construct level, it is worthwhile to further explore the underlying interactions among the specific symptoms of them, promoting the understanding of whether and how these two addictive behaviors link [23]. The network theory suggested that mental disorders arise from and are sustained by the interactions among their symptoms [24, 25]. Network analysis, drawing on the network theory, could elucidate pivotal symptom(s) that play the central role in the development and maintenance of the disorder and the bridge role in the associations with other disorders [26]. Hence, the second aim of the present study is to provide empirical evidence regarding the associations between problematic blind box buying and GD at not only the construct but also symptom levels, using bivariate correlation analysis and network analysis [24, 27], respectively. Network analysis allows us to identify crucial symptoms that function as the bridge between problematic blind box buying and GD and promote the understanding of how these two behavioral addictions interact with each other. In practical terms, our findings would facilitate knowledge about the potential hazards of problematic blind box purchases, providing insights for the policy formulation to regulate the increasingly large blind box market and consumer population.

Methods

Participants and procedure

A convenience sampling method was utilized in this study. Data were collected via a crowdsourcing data collection platform (i.e., Wenjuanxing) in China from December 2024 to January 2025. After excluding responders who 1) are not adults (aged < 18 years), 2) completed the questionnaire in less than 10 min, or 3) failed the attention check item (i.e., “Please choose 4 = often if you are reading the question”), the final dataset provided by Wenjuanxing consisted of 1612 participants. Among them, 1324 (52.3% female, age range 18–68, Mage = 33.13, SDage = 6.73) were eligible participants (i.e., having experience of buying blind boxes) for the validation test. In the valid sample of 1324 blind box buyers, 866 had lifetime gambling experience (53.1% female, age range 18–68, Mage = 33.35, SDage = 6.67) whose data were used for further network analysis. In the subsample of 866 participants, 278 (32.1%) were probable GD (i.e., met at least four symptoms of GD). Informed consent was obtained from all participants before the survey commenced. The ethics approval (Reference Number: DPSY/2024–30) was granted by the Department of Psychology of the affiliation of the corresponding author.

Measures

Problematic blind box buying scale

The Problematic Blind Box Buying Scale (PBBS) is an adaptation of the Bergen Shopping Addiction Scale [12]. The adaptation involved replacing the phrase “shopping/buying things” with “buying blind boxes” only. One example item is “I think about buying blind boxes all the time”. Following the ITC Guidelines for Translating and Adapting Tests [28], the PBBS was translated into Chinese by a bilingual psychologist and then back-translated into English by another bilingual psychologist to ensure equivalence and accuracy of the language versions. After that, two Chinese addiction experts reviewed and discussed the translated items to evaluate and confirm their conceptual relevance and appropriateness in the context of blind box buying. The polit test was then conducted in a sample of five blind box buyers to ensure the comprehensibility of this Chinese version scale. This scale consists of seven items, with each representing a core symptom of addiction [8]. All the items are answered on a 5-point Likert response scale, anchored from 0 (completely disagree) to 4 (completely agree). The sum score ranges from 0 to 28, with a higher PBBS score reflecting a higher risk of problematic blind box buying.

Descriptive norm and attitude of blind box buying

Following Fishbein and Ajzen [29], two items (i.e., “Among the people you know, approximately how many percent have bought blind boxes?” and “For me, buying a blind box in the next month is 1 = bad to 7 = good”) were used to measure participants’ descriptive norm of and attitude toward blind box buying, respectively. Higher scores indicate a stronger descriptive norm or a more acceptable attitude toward blind box buying.

Self-reported GD symptoms

Participants were asked to state whether they had experience with each of the nine symptoms of GD (e.g., “Needs to gamble with increasing amounts of money in order to achieve the desired excitement”) based on DSM-5 criteria [21], using a dichotomous response scale. This 9-item measure has been widely used in both Western (e.g., [30, 31] and Chinese (e.g., [32, 33]) gambling studies for assessing probable GD status and tendency. These nine symptoms, using the cutoff of four, were also evaluated and used to screen probable GD cases in Chinese adult gamblers [32, 34]. A higher total score of this measure indicated more self-reported GD symptoms, reflecting a higher risk for probable GD. The internal consistency of this measure was found to be adequate among Chinese adults in this study (KR-20 = 0.70).

Demographics

All the participants were required to report their sex (0 = male, 1 = female) and age (years). Moreover, the frequency of blind box buying (from 1 = once a year to 8 = two to three times per week) and the monthly expense on blind boxes (from 1 = 0CNY to 9 = over 5,000CNY) in the past 12 months were also collected.

Data analysis

The descriptive statistics, bivariate correlations, and the reliability test were conducted using SPSS 29.0. To test the structure and dimensionality of PBBS, we first performed exploratory factor analysis (EFA) followed by confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). Participants were randomly allocated into two subsamples (i.e., sample A and sample B) with 662 participants in each subsample. EFA and parallel analysis were conducted in sample A using SPSS 29.0. Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) Measure of Sampling Adequacy (KMO > 0.80 as meritorious; [35] and Bartlett's test of sphericity (p < 0.05, [36] were used to evaluate the appropriateness of EFA. Then, factor extraction was guided by parallel analysis, with the principal axis factoring method for extraction and the varimax method for rotation [37]. CFA and measurement invariance were conducted in sample B using the lavaan package [38] in R (Version 4.3.2). Skewness was evaluated using an absolute value greater than 2 as an indicator of deviation from normal distribution [39]. Although none of the seven items of PBBS were substantially skewed (|skewness|= 0.18 to 1.68), considering they were Likert-type, the CFA was conducted using the diagonally weighted least squares (DWLS), which is appropriate for ordinal data [40]. A good model fit is indicated by TLI > 0.90, CFI > 0.90, RMSEA < 0.08, and SRMR < 0.08 [41]. According to the published studies regarding the development and validation of the Bergen Shopping Addiction Scale (e.g., [12, 42]), modification indices would be considered for adding residual covariances to improve the goodness of model fit if it did not reach a satisfactory level. The internal consistency, convergent validity, and concurrent validity were tested with the total sample of 1324 participants.

Network estimation for problematic blind box buying and GD was performed using the mgm package [43] and the visualization was produced using the qgraph package [44] in R (Version 4.3.2). The mixed graphical model (MGM) was employed to estimate the network, as this method is suitable for modeling a network including different types of data (e.g., continuous and binary variables) [43]. The centrality of nodes in the network was assessed using strength and one‑step expected influence (EI) as key indices [45]. A higher centrality index indicated a more influential or bridging role of the node. The case-dropping subset bootstrap framework was used to estimate the correlation stability coefficient (CS-coefficient) via the bootnet package [46] to estimate the stability of the network. As suggested by the previous studies, the CS-coefficient is preferred above 0.5 [46]. The edge-weight accuracy at 95% confidence intervals (CIs) was estimated using 1000 bootstraps. A larger CI indicates less accuracy of edge estimation.

Psychometrics of problematic blind box buying scale

Construct validity

The appropriateness of EFA for PBBS with sample A (n = 662) was estimated by a KMO of 0.803 and a significant Bartlett’s test of sphericity (p < 0.001). The parallel analysis and scree plot (please see Figure S1 in Supplementary Materials) suggested the single-factor structure of PBBS. The one-factor model of the PBBS was further tested using CFA in sample B (n = 662). The results of CFA of the initial model were not satisfactory: TLI = 0.801, CFI = 0.868, RMSEA = 0.144, SRMR = 0.067. After allowing two pairs of covariances (i.e., PBB3 with PBB7 and PBB1 with PBB4) based on the modification indices, the model showed a good model fit: TLI = 0.967, CFI = 0.981, RMSEA = 0.059, SRMR = 0.031. All the standardized factor loadings of items are acceptable (0.308 to 0.788). The items of PBBS and their standardized factor loadings are shown in Table 1.

Measurement invariance of PBBS across sexes was examined via multi-group CFA at the configural, metric, and scalar levels. Convergent and concurrent validity

As displayed in Table 2, the positive correlations were observed between problematic blind box buying and both descriptive norms and attitudes toward blind box buying, supporting the convergent validity of PBBS. Besides, the PBBS scores were significantly related to a higher frequency of and larger expenses on blind box buying, which demonstrated the concurrent validity of PBBS.

Internal consistency

All items of PBBS were significantly and positively correlated with each other (r = 0.21 to 0.57, p < 0.001), with a Cronbach’s α of 0.783 in the total sample (n = 1324), indicating its satisfactory internal consistency.

Discussion

In light of the growing population of blind box consumers and the scarcity of research on individuals’ addictive patterns of blind box buying, this study applied the components model of addiction [8] to problematic blind box buying and provided evidence for the psychometric characteristics of PBBS, which was adapted from the Bergen Shopping Addiction Scale [12]. In doing so, it offered a new and practical assessment tool, which is theoretically grounded and psychometrically sound, for problematic blind box buying for future studies. In addition, the current study offered the first empirical evidence demonstrating the positive associations between problematic blind box buying and GD at both the construct and symptom levels. Within the network of problematic blind box buying and GD, chasing loss of GD was the central node, while the conflict (i.e., negative impacts on job/study) of GD and the relapse (i.e., difficulties in cutting down) of problematic blind box buying emerged as the bridge nodes linking these two constructs.

Aligned with previous studies, which reported the one-factor construct of the Bergen Shopping Addiction Scale [12, 13], our results also supported that the one-factor model is better than other models (e.g., the two-factor model) for the 7-item PBBS across sexes among Chinese adults. Nonetheless, the inclusion of two pairs of residual covariances (i.e., conflict with problem and salience with tolerance) suggested that the unidimensional structure of the PBBS may be less stable than we originally expected. The correlated residuals of conflict and problem may reflect their shared associations with negative life experiences related to addiction, while that between salience and tolerance may be attributable to the physiological basis of behavioral addiction [8, 12]. These findings warrant replication studies, particularly given that these modifications were data-driven. In addition, PBBS demonstrated satisfactory internal consistency, indicating its good reliability. Evidence of the convergent validity of PBBS was supported by its positive associations with the descriptive norm and attitude toward blind box buying, while the concurrent validity was reflected by its positive associations with the frequency of and expenses on blind box buying.

This study also provided empirical evidence that blind box buying conforms to the component framework of addiction [8], as all seven symptoms (e.g., salience) were observed, suggesting the addictive potential of blind box buying. Mood modification was the most prevalent symptom in our sample, yet its corresponding item in the PBBS showed a relatively low factor loading. Together with prior research reporting that the mood modification item in the original Bergen Shopping Addiction Scale showed a poor fit [14], this pattern suggested that this item may capture the common emotional values of blind box buying, rather than serve as an effective discriminating indicator of different levels of problematic blind box buying, or even general shopping addiction [14, 47]. Besides, a significant and positive bivariate correlation between problematic blind box buying and GD was found, highlighting the potential risk of problematic patterns of blind box purchase. Their positive association can be explained by the gambling-like reinforcement schedules involved in blind boxes [1, 22]. Although blind boxes are commonly regarded as appealing products to younger and female consumers [48, 49], the non-significant association of problematic blind box buying with age and sex indicated that both males and females, regardless of age, were at similar levels of risk in their adulthood.

The present study not only revealed a positive correlation between problematic blind box buying and GD at the construct level but also their positive interactions underlying their symptoms via network analysis. Specifically, chasing loss played the most crucial role in the network of problematic blind box buying and GD. Consistent with the literature, which suggested that loss chasing is the dominating symptom of and risk factor for problematic gambling [50, 51], our findings also indicated that amplifying their betting to recoup prior losses also plays a crucial role among general gamblers. These findings suggested that chasing loss may serve as a potential precursor or promising screening marker for early identification of disordered gambling [52, 53], which needs more empirical evidence to support.

The conflict symptom of GD and the relapse of problematic blind box buying were the bridge symptoms between these two addictive behaviors. Also, the robust associations between these two symptoms were noted in the network. This pattern implied that the difficulties in reducing one addictive behavior were associated with the negative consequences of another, at least within the relationship between problematic blind box buying and GD. In fact, extant evidence has also documented that functional impairments are related to less abstinence in individuals with substance use disorder [54]. One possible explanation is that functional impairments were considered to reflect the damaged self-control [55], which may make it harder for individuals to stop or cut their engagement in various addictive behaviors despite their earlier attempts/success. The bridging roles of these two symptoms temporally linking addictive behaviors (e.g., blind box buying and gambling) deserve further examination through longitudinal designs.

The strong link between the relapse symptom of problematic blind box buying and the withdrawal symptom (i.e., restlessness without gambling) of GD was also identified in the network, indicating that the feeling of restlessness and irritability when stopping gambling is related to individuals’ difficulties in cutting down on blind box buying. While this association requires further investigation via prospective studies, it might be explained by the substitute addictions [56], denoting that individuals may buy blind boxes as an alternative activity to derive a similar satisfaction when they abstain from gambling. These findings may provide practical implications for the treatment of GD. For instance, mental health professionals could take into account blind box buying as a socially accepted alternative behavior, especially among clients with prior experience with blind box buying.

Limitations

First, the sample in this study is relatively young (with a median age of 33 years old), which may reflect that blind boxes are more attractive to young people [2]. However, we should also note that young adults, given their neurobiological and psychological developmental stage, are more susceptible to addiction behaviors [57], which may inflate the estimation of the prevalence of behavioral involvement and addiction symptoms in the present study. Besides, our sample only includes Chinese adults, which also limits the generalization of our findings due to the different accessibility of blind box buying across countries. Take POP MART as an example, the number of its offline stores in mainland China is nearly three times larger than that overseas [3]. Considering these confounding factors, our findings, especially the prevalences, should be generalized with caution to other regions and populations. We also call for further investigation regarding the psychometric properties of PBBS, as well as the correlates of problematic blind box buying, in other cultural contexts and populations using probability‑based sampling methods. In addition, given the cross-sectional design of the present study, it precludes the evaluation of the test–retest reliability for PBBS and the temporal link between problematic blind box buying and GD. Future longitudinal research is needed to address these issues. Besides, this study assessed concurrent validity using only blind box buying frequency and monthly expenditure. Despite their consistent and positive correlations with behavioral addictions in previous studies [15, 16], frequency and expenditure can also be a result of other contributors, such as disposable income, collecting interest, resale activity, or market engagement, rather than reflecting the pathological level. Therefore, future validation studies of PBBS may also incorporate other clinically meaningful indicators (e.g., functional impairment, perceived loss of control, and psychological distress, etc.) for validity checks. Last but not least, we only tested the relationships between two addictive behaviors (i.e., blind box buying and gambling). For instance, problematic blind box buying may also relate to social media addiction as blind box consumers usually discuss, resell, or exchange blind boxes via social media [58], and meanwhile, frequent blind box advertisements in social media may tempt consumers to buy more [59]. Thus, future studies may further investigate how problematic blind box buying relates to other behavioral addictions to promote a comprehensive understanding of this rising addictive behavior.

Implications

This study also yields several implications. First, our findings facilitated subsequent investigations on problematic blind box buying and its correlates by providing empirical evidence for the applicability and satisfactory psychometric properties of PBBS. Besides, the findings regarding the adverse association between problematic blind box buying and GD underscore the importance of regulating the market of blind boxes as well as mitigating blind box over-engagement and its potential harms, such as gambling-related problems found in this study and suicide risk found by Duan et al. [6]. These findings may offer implications for policymakers regarding the potential need to strengthen the enforcement of existing guidelines (e.g., the Guidelines for Regulating Blind Box Business Activities published by the China State Administration for Market Regulation [CSAMR]) to better protect consumers from excessive purchasing. For instance, practices such as setting expense limits for consumers and prominently disclosing the item probability on the packaging could be considered as possible approaches [60], though the effectiveness of these practices requires further empirical investigation. Also, the findings at the symptom level provide practical insights for future research on GD interventions. For example, the central position of chasing loss in the network implies that it may be a potentially efficient target for designing cost-effective preventive programs for gambling-related problems.

Conclusion

This study represents a pioneering effort in investigating addiction symptoms of problematic blind box buying. It provided not only psychometric support to PBBS for measuring problematic blind box buying but also empirical evidence regarding the construct- and symptom-level relationships of problematic blind box buying with GD. These findings contribute to a deeper understanding of the emerging addictive behavior—blind box buying, and lay the groundwork for future research about its problematic pattern. Given the positive associations of problematic blind box buying symptoms with GD symptoms, regulatory bodies could consider the need and strategies for implementing consumer protection measures. For example, policymakers and stakeholders may consider to implement regulatory policies (e.g., set expense limits for blind box consumers) to promote safer consumption practices and prevent consumers from over-purchasing blind boxes.
