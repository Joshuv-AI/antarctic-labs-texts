Unusual presentation of fungal keratitis and endogenous endophthalmitis in Sézary syndrome: a case report
Authors: Marta Fernández-Gurría, Jennifer Kwan, Nina Dimitropoulou, Navpreet Dhillon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis report describes an atypical presentation of Candida keratitis and subsequent endogenous bacterial endophthalmitis in a patient with Sézary Syndrome undergoing immunotherapy and subsequently chemotherapy.ObservationsA male patient in his late sixties, a rigid gas permeable (RGP) contact lens wearer diagnosed with Sézary Syndrome and undergoing immunotherapy, presented with blurred vision and pain in the right eye. Examination showed a pseudodendritic infiltrate, severe anterior chamber inflammation with hypopyon and B-scan ultrasonography was unremarkable. Herpetic keratouveitis was suspected, and treatment was started with oral acyclovir, topical levofloxacin, and topical dexamethasone, with no improvement after 48 hours. Cultures identified Candida albicans and treatment was swapped to hourly topical levofloxacin, amphotericin B, voriconazole, and systemic voriconazole, which improved the presentation. However, the patient developed complete pupil occlusion and pupillary block with raised intraocular pressure, which was controlled with medical treatment. One month later, after a chemotherapy cycle, the hypopyon recurred despite improvement of the corneal infiltrate, and B-ultrasonography showed vitritis. Vitreous tap and intravitreal injections were performed, and cultures identified Bacillus cereus and Corynebacterium species. The endogenous endophthalmitis resolved with treatment, but the patient developed retinal detachment, which was managed conservatively. Unfortunately, he passed away three months later, despite infection resolution and stabilization of ocular symptoms.ConclusionThis case highlights the complex relationship between systemic immunosuppression, ocular susceptibility and opportunistic endogenous infections, contributing to a challenging clinical course, diagnosis and treatment of patients with Sézary Syndrome. Clinicians should be aware of this risk of atypical and aggressive infectious presentations and carefully monitor these patients after chemotherapy cycles to detect potential complications early.

Introduction

Sézary syndrome and mycosis fungoides (MF) are the two main forms of cutaneous T-cell lymphoma (CTCL), which belong to the group of non-Hodgkin lymphomas. Sézary syndrome is a rare condition, with an incidence rate of 0.21 cases per million persons per year in the United States.1, 2, 3 It is a more aggressive subtype of CTCL, accounting for 3 % of all cases with a median survival of less than 5 years, and is generally considered a late-stage progression of MF, which accounts for 62 % of all cases and has a better 5-year life expectancy of 88–91 %.4,5 It is characterized by generalized erythroderma, lymphadenopathy, and circulating Sézary cells, making it a challenging condition to manage due to its aggressive nature and systemic involvement.3 In the early stages, it shares clinical and histopathological overlap with MF, and is thus definitively diagnosed by the presence of Sézary cells (neoplastic T cells with cerebriform nuclei) in peripheral blood samples.3,6

Although ocular manifestations in CTCL are rare, they present significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. This case further highlights the complexity of managing ocular manifestations in CTCL, particularly fungal keratitis and endogenous bacterial endophthalmitis. Due to the Sézary syndrome and the effects of immunosuppression, the ocular presentations were atypical and more aggressive compared to immunocompetent patients. These rare but serious complications underscore the importance of close monitoring and aggressive management in immunosuppressed individuals to prevent further ocular and systemic deterioration.

Case report

A 68 year-old male, a RGP contact lens wearer diagnosed with Sézary syndrome and undergoing Mogamulizumab (Poteligeo®) immunotherapy, presented with acute blurred vision and pain in his right eye. His medical history included type 2 diabetes mellitus (DM2), hypertension, hyperuricemia and a history of deep venous thrombosis.

Ophthalmic examination showed significant cicatricial ectropion in both eyes. Visual acuity was hand movements in the right eye and 0.2 logmar in the left eye, with intraocular pressures (IOP) of 12 and 11 mmHg, respectively. The right eye presented a small 1mm central pseudodendritic infiltrate, along with severe anterior chamber inflammation and 1 mm hypopyon, and a fibrinous membrane extending from the posterior central cornea, just beneath the infiltrate, to the pupillary margins. Posterior segment structures could not be assessed, but B-mode ultrasonography was unremarkable.

Initially, herpetic keratouveitis was suspected, and acyclovir 800mg 5 times daily, topical dexamethasone 0.1 % preservative-free six times daily, atropine 1 % three times daily, and topical levofloxacin 0.5 % four times daily were started. However, no clinical improvement was seen after 48h. Microbiological cultures identified Candida albicans, and treatment was swapped to hourly levofloxacin 0.5 %, amphotericin B 0.15 %, and voriconazole 1 % drops, in addition to atropine 1 % three times daily and Voriconazole 200mg tablets twice daily, which produced a gradual improvement during alternate-day follow-up.

Two weeks later, he was admitted with confusion, fever, suprapubic tenderness, haematuria, reduced oral intake and abdominal distension. Clinical examination revealed crepitations at the left base of the chest, and a chest X-ray confirmed a lower lobe consolidation. These findings developed shortly after his fifth cycle of Mogamulizumab. Although urine and blood culture were negative, inflammatory markers were elevated (CRP 160 mg/mL) and lymphopenia (0.78 × 109) was noted. He was treated for presumed sepsis and continued on antifungal therapy for fungal keratitis. During this admission, large cell transformation of Sézary syndrome was confirmed, and the patient was switched to Gemcitabine (first cycle).

Ocular symptoms improved, and the patient was monitored weekly after initial stabilization. Six weeks later, he developed a pupillary block and iris bombé with temporal iridocorneal touch due to occlusion pupillae, as shown in Fig. 1, causing a rise in IOP to 38 mmHg. This was managed with maximum medical treatment, including acetazolamide 250mg PO four times daily, iopidine 1 % three times daily, bimatoprost 0.3mg/mL + timolol 5mg/mL once daily, and brimonidine twice daily. A laser iridotomy was not feasible due to ongoing discomfort. One week later, IOP was successfully controlled, the central anterior chamber was formed (Fig. 2), and the patient's condition stabilized. Follow-up was extended to every two weeks, and the topical drop dose was gradually tapered. Visual acuity remained at hand movements due to residual central corneal scarring and complete pupil occlusion, as displayed in Fig. 3, although the anterior chamber inflammation had resolved.

However, shortly after starting a second cycle of chemotherapy (Gemcitabine), the patient developed acute, severe ocular pain and chemosis, with recurrence of anterior chamber inflammation and hypopyon. This episode coincided with ongoing lymphopenia (0.85 × 109), elevated CRP (32mg/L) and neutrophilia (9.78 × 109/L). Urine and blood cultures were negative and transthoracic echocardiography showed no evidence of endocarditis. B-mode ultrasonography revealed new-onset vitritis, as shown in Fig. 4. A vitreous tap was performed for microbiological and cytopathological analysis and intravitreal injections of ceftazidime 2.25mg/0.1mL, vancomycin 1mg/0.1mL, amphotericin B 5 μg/0.1mL and voriconazole 100 μg/0.1 mL were administered. The cytopathological results were normal and microbiology results confirmed the presence of Bacillus cereus and Corynebacterium species, leading to a diagnosis of bacterial endogenous endophthalmitis.

Right eye B-scan ultrasound: The imaging reveals dense hyperechoic mobile opacities within the vitreous chamber (white arrow), findings that are consistent with vitreous debris and suggestive of endophthalmitis. There are no definite signs of retinal detachment at this point.

Following one week of intensive treatment with hourly drops of levofloxacin, amphotericin B, and voriconazole, atropine 1 % three times daily, 750mg ciprofloxacin tablets twice daily, and prednisolone 20mg tablets once daily, the infection was controlled, and dose was tapered to every 2 h.

Nevertheless, just a few days after receiving another dose of chemotherapy, hypopyon recurred. B-scan demonstrated worsening vitreous inflammation with evidence of organization and membrane formation (Fig. 5). Repeat vitreous tap and intravitreal injections were performed, and the frequency of topical treatment was increased. Although vitreous cultures were negative, inflammatory markers were elevated (CRP 54 mg/L) and the patient was found to be profoundly lymphopenic (0.31 × 109/L). One week later, laboratory tests showed further deterioration with a rise in CRP to 139 mg/L, worsening lymphopenia (0.18 × 109) and new-onset neutropenia (0.58 × 109). The patient developed a neutropenic sepsis and blood cultures identified Enterobacter asburiae. Concurrent CT imaging revealed a suspicious splenic infarct, supporting systemic dissemination.

Right eye B-scan ultrasound shows persistent dense, mobile, hyperechoic opacities within the vitreous chamber, along with worsening inflammation and evidence of organization and membrane formation (white arrow).

The patient was initially monitored daily and once a week when his condition improved. But further extension of follow-up visits was not possible due to the patient's ongoing chemotherapy cycles, which significantly increased the risk of infection outbreaks. Therefore, ocular check-ups were scheduled 1–2 days after each chemotherapy cycle to ensure prompt detection and management of potential complications. Visual acuity remained at hand movements, and IOP was 6 mmHg, with no signs of anterior chamber inflammation; however, B-scan showed a retinal detachment. Unfortunately, the patient's underlying condition eventually worsened, and he passed away three months after the initial presentation of ocular manifestations.

Discussion

The ocular manifestations of MF were first documented by Stenson in 1981, who reported ocular findings in 11 out of 30 patients, including eyelid tumors, keratitis, uveitis, and optic atrophy.7 Generally, ocular involvement in CTCL is not common and was found to occur in just 1.95 % of all CTCL patients in a study by Cook et al., however, it is relatively more frequent in Sézary syndrome, with 28.6 % of the ocular cases associated with this condition compared to 45.2 % of ocular cases associated with MF. 8

The most common ocular pathologies seen in cases of CTCL tend to be associated with eyelid disorders such as cicatricial ectropion, blepharitis, and chalazion, which can contribute to dry ocular surface disease.9,10 Corneal changes were reported in only two cases of Sézary syndrome, neither of which involved fungal keratitis. These included bilateral peripheral ulcerative keratitis (PUK) reported by Maywood et al. and a severe case of exposure keratopathy that led to a Pseudomonas corneal ulcer with subsequent perforation despite aggressive therapy described by Livingstone et al.11,12 Our case supports these findings, particularly the presence of bilateral cicatricial ectropion and corneal involvement, as shown in Fig. 3, but represents the first case reported of both Candida keratitis and bacterial endogenous endophthalmitis in the context of Sézary syndrome.

Of note, the cases of Sézary syndrome and MF reported in the literature that presented with corneal pathology were either due to direct infiltration of atypical T cells from corneal limbus or conjunctiva or had exposure keratopathy secondary to eyelid involvement causing lagophthalmos.12, 13, 14, 15 Our patient did not have lagophthalmos despite his cicatricial ectropion, and on initial presentation, he had a centrally located dendritiform lesion without corneal thinning. He developed both fungal and bacterial ocular infections in the setting of an advanced hematologic malignancy and significant systemic immunosuppression. While the initial fungal keratitis was most likely triggered by epithelial barrier breakdown and ocular surface compromise due to cicatricial ectropion and contact lens wear, the possibility of an endogenous fungal source cannot be excluded given the patient's profound immunosuppression. Similarly, the subsequent bacterial endophthalmitis, which occurred in the absence of active keratitis and coincided with systemic infection, strongly suggests an endogenous origin via hematogenous spread. These findings highlight the increased susceptibility of immunocompromised patients to opportunistic endogenous infections. Our findings expand the spectrum of ocular complications of Sézary Syndrome, underscoring the need for careful monitoring and diagnosis of ocular involvement.

Fungal keratitis is typically associated with risk factors such as trauma, corticosteroid use, contact lens wear and immunosuppression, particularly, Candida albicans is more common in individuals with systemic immunosuppression, inadequate tear production, and compromised eyelid closure. 16 These risk factors were clearly present in our patient, who had systemic immunosuppression secondary to Sézary syndrome, Mogamulizumab (Poteligeo®) immunotherapy, followed by Gemcitabine chemotherapy, as well as ocular vulnerability from cicatricial ectropion and RGP contact lens use. Although the clinical presentation was initially misdiagnosed as herpetic keratouveitis, microbiological investigations identified Candida albicans, highlighting the importance of an exhaustive microbiological workup to confirm the etiology of infectious keratitis, especially in immunocompromised patients with predisposing risk factors.

Moreover, our patient's case was complicated by the development of bacterial endogenous endophthalmitis, a rare but devastating complication that can arise after surgical procedures, trauma or through bacteremia, when pathogens enter the eye via the retinal blood vessels.17 Bacillus cereus is a frequent cause of endogenous endophthalmitis and a devastating infection, particularly in immunocompromised individuals, and is associated with rapid progression to severe visual loss if not treated promptly. 18 In our patient, both Bacillus cereus and Corynebacterium species were identified in vitreous samples.

Though rare, intraocular involvement has been described in other studies, such as those by Leitch et al. and Erny et al., and remains a critical consideration in patients with CTCL who present with ocular symptoms.19, 20, 21 The intraocular inflammation described in these cases was evident in the form of anterior chamber cells and flare, hypopyon, vitreous opacities and retinal infiltrates, affecting both the anterior and posterior segments simultaneously. Our patient only developed signs of posterior segment involvement at a subsequent visit, at which time he had already been started on systemic chemotherapy due to worsened systemic condition, and the diagnosis was likely endogenous endophthalmitis secondary to immunosuppression. However, before the start of chemotherapy, he only presented with inflammation confined to the anterior segment. Isolated anterior segment inflammation with hypopyon has previously been described by LaMattina and Becker, although their case presented bilateral involvement, and our patient only had unilateral, recurrent episodes of inflammation.22 Intraocular T-cell lymphoma should remain a differential diagnosis in these patients, especially given the overlapping clinical features between lymphoma and other ocular inflammatory or infectious diseases.

Conclusions

The presence of both fungal keratitis and bacterial endogenous endophthalmitis in our patient highlights the importance of a comprehensive diagnostic approach in the management of complex ocular infections, especially in the context of predisposing conditions such as Sézary syndrome, ocular surface susceptibility and profound immunosuppression. In this case, the patient's immunosuppressive state, exacerbated by Mogamulizumab (Poteligeo®) and subsequent chemotherapy (Gemcitabine), significantly contributed to the development of severe opportunistic infections. This complexity emphasizes the need for prompt microbiological testing, targeted treatment, and ongoing close monitoring to detect early potential complications.

Claim of priority

A literature review conducted on 3rd April 2025, using PubMed with the keywords 'Sézary Syndrome + fungal keratitis' and 'Sézary Syndrome + endophthalmitis' we did not find prior reports of fungal keratitis and endogenous endophthalmitis in the setting of Sézary Syndrome. This underscores the novelty of our case and its potential contribution to the understanding of atypical presentations in ophthalmology.
