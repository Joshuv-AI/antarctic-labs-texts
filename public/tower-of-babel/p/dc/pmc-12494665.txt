Changes in nutritional status in hepatocellular carcinoma patients treated with sorafenib
Authors: Radosław Osmański, Marcin Mardas, Piotr Radomyski, Maria Litwiniuk, Marta Stelmach-Mardas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeMusculoskeletal imbalance is often overlooked in clinical management, although in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) it has been associated with adverse outcomes. We aimed to assess the nutritional status using Computed Tomography (CT) scans, in HCC patients and its association with progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS).MethodsA total of 122 patients were qualified for systemic therapy due to HCC, 73 for sorafenib treatment. The nutritional status was assessed using CT, Body Mass Index (BMI), the prognostic nutritional index (PNI) and on the Nutritional Risk Score (NRS) 2002 scale. The ECOG Performance Status Scale was used to assess the patients’ level of functioning. The classification of the liver failure was described with the Child–Pugh scale. The Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer classification system was used for patients management. Survival analyses were performed.ResultsNRS and BMI were not significantly related to PFS and OS. Nutritional status changed significantly during 6 months of sorafenib treatment; however, the sarcopenia was not detected. The low PNI deteriorates significantly both PFS and OS. Significant differences between median time of survival in patients that lost more than 10% in visceral or subcutaneous fat or muscle mass in comparison to those with stable parameters were noticed: Subcutaneous Adipose Tissue Index (SATI): PFS—9.5 vs 16.4 months, OS—15.95 vs 24.5 months; Visceral Adipose Tissue Index (VATI): 11 vs. 15.75 months; SMI: PFS—11.5 vs 15.75 months, OS—22 vs 24.1 months; PMI: PFS—9.2 vs 16.15 months, OS—14.5vs 24.5 months.ConclusionThe loss in fat and muscle mass in patients undergoing sorafenib therapy for HCC was associated with poorer PFS and OS.Keywords: Muscle mass, Fat mass, Liver, Cancer, Nutritional status

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the most common primary liver malignancy (75%–85% of cases). Currently, HCC is the sixth most frequently diagnosed cancer (4.3% of all cancers worldwide) and the third most common cause of cancer-related deaths (7.8%) with an estimated 865,000 new cases and 757,948 deaths in 2022 in the world [1]. HCC was the 12th most common cause of death in men and 13th in women in Poland [2].

The main risk factors for HCC are hepatitis B and C virus infection, although non-alcoholic steatohepatitis associated with metabolic syndrome or diabetes mellitus is becoming a more frequent risk factor in the West. Other factors include heavy alcohol consumption, smoking, exposure to toxins like aflatoxin and genetic factors [3]. Moreover, in chronic liver disease, repeated damage and regeneration of the organ may lead to fibrosis, cirrhosis and the development of HCC. Currently, targeted therapies and immunotherapy are used in the treatment of HCC [4]. However, from a clinical perspective, therapeutic decisions are based not only on the stage of the disease but also on the patient’s overall fitness level and liver function. [5]. More attention is paid to the diagnosis of malnutrition and sarcopenia in oncological patients using objective imaging methods such as computed tomography (CT) [6]. There are various software tools on the market that enable quick assessment of a patient’s nutritional status. Unfortunately, musculoskeletal imbalance is often overlooked in clinical management, although in HCC it has been associated with adverse outcomes.

The aim of the study was to assess the nutritional status based on CT scans in HCC patients and its association with progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS).

Material and methods

Patient’s population

A total of 122 HCC patients were qualified for systemic therapy between 2012 and 2023 in the Greater Poland Cancer Center in Poznan. The analyzed population included 73 patients receiving Sorafenib at an initial dose of 800 mg per day, with locally advanced or metastatic HCC diagnosed on the basis of a histopathological examination or based on a characteristic image for HCC with liver cirrhosis in CT or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with contrast. The oncological treatment was based on the guidelines of the Polish Society of Clinical Oncology (PTOK) [7] and the European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) [8] that were current at the time of patient treatment.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: age over 18 years, (1) locally advanced or metastatic hepatocellular carcinoma confirmed by histology or cytology. In patients with cirrhosis and lesions ≥ 1 cm in diameter, it is possible to waive the histological or cytological diagnosis provided that a typical image for HCC is obtained in multiphase CT or magnetic resonance imaging with contrast (MRI), with enhancement in the arterial phase (hyperdense lesion) and contrast washout from the lesion in the portal venous or delayed phase (hypodense lesion); (2) no possibility of radical surgical treatment or locoregional therapies or their ineffectiveness; (3) performance status 0–2 according to WHO/ECOG; (4) functional liver status in category A based on the assessment according to the Child–Pugh classification; (5) presence of at least one lesion that can be assessed, according to the currently applicable RECIST criteria; (RECIST 1.1: mRECIST criteria used from 2022 in doubtful situations); (6) absence of significant comorbidities constituting a contraindication to therapy determined by the attending physician based on the relevant Summary of Product Characteristics and guidelines of the Polish Society of Clinical Oncology in the treatment of hepatocellular carcinoma; (7) adequate organ function, determined on the basis of blood laboratory test results, which, in the opinion of the attending physician, allows for the safe commencement of therapy; (8) no contraindications to the use of each drug; (9) in the case of coexistence of other active neoplasms, qualification for treatment must take into account the prognosis related to the coexisting neoplasm;

Exclusion criteria based on: (1) coexistence of another malignant disease except for basal cell carcinoma of the skin, cervical cancer in situ, (2) uncontrolled infection, (3) heart failure higher than NYHA III or IV, (4) pregnancy or breastfeeding; (5) lack of cooperation or non-compliance with medical recommendations, including in particular those concerning periodic check-ups assessing the effectiveness and safety of treatment on the part of the beneficiary or his legal guardians.

Clinical characteristic of patients

BMI was calculated by dividing body weight by height expressed in meters squared, and the risk of malnutrition was assessed according to the Nutritional Risk Score (NRS) 2002 scale in hospital setting. For the assessment of patients’ level of functioning in terms of their ability to care for themselves, daily activity, and physical ability the ECOG Performance Status Scale was used [9]. Additionally, according the Child–Pugh scale the classification of the liver failure was described [10]. The Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) classification system was used for cancer staging and determination of treatment options and patient prognosis [11]. The prognostic nutritional index (PNI) was calculated based on serum albumin (ALB) concentrations and total lymphocyte counts in the blood: PNI = 10 × ALB (g/dL) + 0.005 × total lymphocyte count (/mm3). Lymphocytes and albumins were routinely assessed in certified hospital laboratory. Patients with a PNI < 40 are considered undernourished.

Computed tomography (CT) image acquisition

All CT examinations were conducted at the Greater Poland Cancer Centre using two scanners: the GE Optima CT660 and Siemens Somatom Definition AS. The scans were performed at baseline and then after either every 6 months or based on clinical indications. Both scanners produced axial images with 1.25-mm slice thickness and a soft tissue reconstruction kernel. A single slice from each CT scan, showing the transverse processes of the third lumbar vertebra, was selected by a single radiologist using Siemens syngo.via software. All selected slices were in the axial plane, with the third lumbar vertebra confirmed in the sagittal plane. The measurement of the subcutaneous fat (SF), visceral fat (VF), total muscle (TM) and psoas muscle area (PMA) were done automatically using Coreslicer software. If necessary, the automated categorization of selected anatomical structures by CoreSlicer was manually corrected for individual patients by experienced professionals. The following formulas were used to calculate the: Skeletal Muscle Index (SMI), Psoas Muscle Index; Subcutaneous Adipose Tissue Index (SATI) and Visceral Adipose Tissue Index (VATI).

A muscle mass at the L3 level of < 42 cm2/m2 in men and < 38 cm2/m2 in women and/or a psoas muscle index of < 6.36 cm2/m2 in men and < 3.92 cm2/m2 in women supported a diagnosis of sarcopenia [12].

Statistical analysis

For quantitative variables, descriptive statistics (arithmetic mean, range, standard deviation) were used, and for qualitative variables numerical value and percentage values ​​were used. Depending on the distribution of data, when assessing changes before and during treatment, the following were used: for normal distribution Student’s t test for related variables and non-normal distribution—Friedman’s test for related variables. As we compared patients in a 6-month time frame we choose 10% difference in body composition for analysis [13]. Survival analysis was performed using the log-rank test (Mantel-Cox). Statistical analyses were performed using STATISTICA 13.3 (TIBICO Software Inc., Palo Alto, CA, USA, 2017) and GraphPad Prism 5.1 (GraphPad Software).

Results

More than two third of the population were men characterized by NRS 0–2. The mean BMI value in the study population exceeded 25 kg/m2, which is the lower threshold for the overweight range. The Child–Pugh A was indicated in 96% of patients characterized by a good hepatic function, where the surgical treatment was not implemented (86%). BCLC staging classification was indicated in two third of patients advanced stage (C). According to the ECOG Performance Status Scale 63% of HCC patients included in the study were mostly restricted in physically strenuous activity but were ambulatory and able to carry out work of a light or sedentary nature, e.g., light house work, office work (ECOG = 1). The PNI and serum albumin (ALB) reflect a patient’s nutritional status. Low mean PNI showed insufficient nutritional function in HCC patients (Table 1).

When analyzing BMI changes it seems that the differences between HCC patients that lost > 10% in BMI and those with stable BMI tend to be clinically significant for OS (BMI loss > 10% median survival: 11.8; stable BMI median survival: 23.0; p > 0.075) (Fig. 1e–f) but not for PFS. Taking into account low PNI (< 40) for HCC patients, we have observed significant differences for both PFS (PNI < 40 median survival: 6.25; PNI > 40 median survival: 16.1; p > 0.0158) and OS (PNI < 40 median survival: 15.25; PNI > 40 median survival: 23.0; p > 0.045), which might suggest a high clinical importance of PNI in HCC. If we analyze the SMI parameter, there was a significant effect on disease PFS and OS. The median survival time in HCC patients with a decrease of over 10% in SMI was 11.55 months for PFS and 22 months for OS, and for those with stable SMI: 15.75 months for PFS and 24.1 months for OS respectively (Fig. 2a–d).

The loss of more than 10% in SATI indicated the significant influence on disease PFS with median time of survival: 9.5 months vs 16.4 months in patients with stable SATI and on OS with median time of survival: 15.95 months vs 24.5 months respectively. Similarly, for VATI the significant differences between patients that lost at least 10% of VATI and those with stable VATI were visible in both PFS and OS. For PFS median time of survival if VATI loss exceeded 10% was 11 months vs. 15.75 months for stable VATI and for OS median time of survival was not significant. Discussion

The obtained data confirmed the importance of body composition in effectiveness of oncological treatment and survival. The loss of at least 10% in visceral/subcutaneous fat or muscle mass was significantly associated with poorer PFS and OS in HCC patients treated by sorafenib. Therefore, it is extremely important to provide proper nutrition to patients with HCC in order to support oncological treatment, both antiproliferative and antiangiogenic effects.

A rapid publication of new researchers in HCC that attained predefined primary end points brings new challenges for professionals. Firstly, there is a need to organize the treatment in this group of patients in a standardized way. Secondly, a standard of care for patients with Child–Pugh class B liver disease, especially those without liver decompensation, should be developed [14]. Recently published guideline recommendations clearly indicated either lenvatinib or sorafenib as the preferred strategies for first-line strategies in patients with contraindications for immunotherapies. It seems that in this context there is also room for discussion about the role and importance of proper nutritional status in patients with HCC. There is no golden standard for nutritional assessment in patients suffering from HCC. Usually, in the clinical setting the Patient-Generated Subjective Global Assessment (PG-SGA), Mini Nutritional Assessment (MNA) or NRS is used. The selection of higher accuracy in different scores to predict mortality in HCC population varied between studies, but all help in malnutrition diagnosis [15, 16]. The Global Leadership Initiative on Malnutrition criteria (GLIM) covers the diagnostic criteria for malnutrition and based on e.g. loss in skeletal muscle mass, which further can initiate personalized dietary intervention [17]. The currently analyzed population can be described as overweight (BMI exceeded 25 kg/m2), which reflects the trend of increasing body mass—the obesity pandemic, observed among people living in developed countries [18]. A sedentary lifestyle, stress, smoking, alcohol consumption and a diet rich in fats (including saturated fatty acids) and simple carbohydrates directly define the health situation of patients [19]. In the metanalysis by Gupta et al. [20] it was confirmed that premorbid obesity may be independently associated with a twofold risk of HCC-related mortality. This link was more pronounced in men from Western populations. Additionally, study by Feng et al. [21] disclosed the relationship between BMI-guided transcriptome and HCC. It was stated that overweight/obesity-associated gene signature has a potential for clinical management of HCC patients. But still, as reported Rong et al. [22] BMI cannot be recognized as an independent prognostic factor for the evaluation of the prognosis in HCC and is not associated with postoperative complications except for wound infections. It was pointed by Ouyang et al. [23] that the effects of BMI on OS depended on body compositions. There are some data suggesting that sarcopenic visceral obesity, assessed by selected parameters from CT (i.e. SMI, VFI), is a more objective and accurate prognostic indicator for HCC [24]. The role of Preoperative PNI was highlighted as a significant prognostic indicator in HCC patients undergoing curative hepatectomy, where patients with higher PNI were characterized by improved survival outcomes [25]. In the current study PNI with the cutoff established at the level of 40 was also significantly associated with disease PFS and OS. The formula of PNI calculation gives explanation. On one hand, the ALB concentration is linked with the liver function and has been related to survival outcomes in HCC. On the other hand, the lymphocyte count is recognized as an accessible biomarker of inflammation. Pinato et al. [26] indicated the PNI as a stage independent predictor of OS in HCC suggesting that this index may describe more aggressive disease phenotype via e.g. liver functional reserve and the presence of portal vein thrombosis.

Although, the musculoskeletal imbalance is reported as a predictor of perioperative mortality in patients with HCC, this condition is still overlooked in clinical setting [27]. The PMI was found as an independent prognostic factor for 1-year treatment outcomes in HCC patients treated with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) and tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs). Particularly, patients with low PMI (≤ 5.5409 cm2/m2) were characterized by a higher risk of death at 1 year and at the end of the follow-up period [28]. In the current study, the reduction of 10% in PMI was significantly related to disease PFS and OS in HCC patients. The clinical importance of PMI was confirmed in different studies on HCC [29–31]. As reported Ogushi et al. [29] PMI might be a predictive marker of tolerance to treatment and time to treatment failure (TTF) in HCC patients receiving sorafenib treatment. Authors [29] defined the cutoff PMI values (men: 7.04 cm2/m2; women: 4.40 cm2/m2) determined by receiver operating characteristic curve analysis. Frequencies of all types of severe adverse events were statistically higher in the low PMI group than in the high PMI group. It was also stated by Wu et al. [31] that sarcopenia is an adverse prognostic factor in HCC, where the CT-assessed sarcopenia defined using psoas muscle (PM) was an independent prognostic factor for the poorer prognosis of unresectable HCC after radioembolization. The potential of muscle-defined sarcopenia as a prognostic factor for OS and recurrence-free survival (RFS) was analyzed by Rao et al. [30]. It was stated that sarcopenia defined with the use of PMI, not SMI, may serve as a significant risk factor for RFS and OS in patients with HCC after hepatectomy. In our study the sarcopenia status was not confirmed which might be related to relatively good nutritional status of HCC patients at baseline. Yabuski et al. [32] showed that low SMI is an independent adverse prognostic factor for cumulative recurrence rate (CRR) in patients with BMI ≥ 22 with HCC. In the current study, a 10% loss of SMI was associated with poorer PFS and OS in the analyzed population. In the study by Ouyang et al. [29] sarcopenia, myosteatosis and visceral adipose deposition independently predicted OS, but visceral adipose tissue was protective in OS in HCC receiving immunotherapy. As was highlighted by Zhang et al. [33] low SMI in HCC patients undergoing treatment with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) impacted negatively on treatment response and effectiveness. Lu et al. [34] goes further and suggests that post-transplant muscle loss might help in prognostic stratification of recipients without pre-existing sarcopenia. Cheng et al. [35] showed that myosteatosis and subcutaneous to visceral fat ratio were survival predictors in male patients undergoing surgical resection. It was confirmed in a study by Jang et al. [36]. “Obesity paradox” was partially explained by Zhou et al. [37] study, where high VATI and mid-level BMI were protective for the survival of patients with HCC receiving immunotherapy. Authors indicated that SMI and SMD may be recognized as better predictors for outcomes in patients suffering from HCC and receiving immunotherapy. Imai et al. [38] concluded that skeletal muscle mass decreases significantly during sorafenib treatment and is an independent prognostic factor for HCC. This observation stays in line with our study, where a 10% decrease in VATI and SATI was significantly associated with PFS and OS in the study group. Similar changes in body composition were observed in a study by other authors [39].

There are several limitations of this study. The small sample size limits the dataset for the analysis. Nevertheless, HCC is a rare cancer and as for a single-center study the collected sample size seems to be satisfactory. Objective measurements for body composition assessment changes were used which indicates a good quality of presented data. The data analysis was performed by an experienced radiologist and oncologist, which is another strong point of the presented data. Of course, the planned prospective observational study would benefit from additional information on the analyzed group of HCC patients.

In conclusion, changes in body composition of at least 10% influence significantly the disease PFS and OS and therefore should warrant careful attention from all healthcare professionals. A simple graph, constructed using biochemical data and parameters assessing body composition in CT, could help in predicting survival in HCC patients treated with sorafenib.
