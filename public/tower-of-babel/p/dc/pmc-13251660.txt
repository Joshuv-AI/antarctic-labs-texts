Nurses’ experiences in pediatric cerebral palsy care: Insights from a transitional health system
Authors: Nursultan Abdigapbar, Ejercito Balay-Odao, Joseph Almazan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn transitional healthcare systems, such as Kazakhstan, caring for children with cerebral palsy (CP) places substantial physical and emotional demands on nurses, but their experiences remain largely undocumented in post-Soviet contexts. CP care requires continuous support, complex clinical tasks, and close collaboration with caregivers, so understanding the perspectives of nurses is essential for improving pediatric neurodisability services.AimThis study explored the lived experiences of nurses caring for children with CP in a tertiary hospital in Kazakhstan, focusing on the clinical, emotional, and institutional factors that shaped their practice.MethodsA descriptive qualitative design was used. Twelve nurses were homogeneously purposively sampled, and data were collected through semi-structured, face-to-face interviews conducted for 30–60 min between March and April 2025. Interviews were transcribed and analyzed using Braun and Clarke's six-step thematic framework. Ethical approval was obtained from Nazarbayev University and the University Medical Center ethics committees.ResultsFive themes emerged: (1) significant physical and resource-related challenges; (2) emotional labor, resilience, and risk of burnout; (3) caregiver dynamics involving stress, conflict, education, and collaboration; (4) professional and personal growth through skills development, empathy, and identity formation; and (5) workplace satisfaction shaped by institutional and peer support. Nurses exhibited strong ethical commitment, frequently compensating for systemic limitations.ConclusionThis first exploration of CP nursing experiences in Kazakhstan reveals how cultural norms, institutional gaps, and post-Soviet healthcare legacies influence pediatric neurodisability care. Emotional labor functions both as a vulnerability and a key component of professional identity. Strengthening the assistive infrastructure, psychological support services, and CP specific nurse education is essential for enhancing the care quality and sustaining the pediatric nursing workforce.Keywords: Cerebral Palsy, Central Asia, Nurse, Pediatric Patient, Kazakhstan

Introduction

Cerebral palsy (CP) is among the most common motor disabilities worldwide, with a global prevalence ranging from 1.5 to 4 per 1000 live births, and higher rates often occur in countries with limited access to perinatal and neonatal care, and medical infrastructure.1 According to Novak et al.,2 CP describes a group of permanent disorders of the development of movement and posture, causing activity limitation, which are attributed to non-progressive disturbances that occur in the developing fetal or infant brain. The motor disorders in CP are often accompanied by disturbances of sensation, perception, cognition, communication, and behavior, as well as epilepsy and secondary musculoskeletal problems. Given this wide-ranging clinical complexity, caring for children with CP requires sustained nursing involvement spanning medical, rehabilitative, emotional, and social domains.

Nurses are central to this continuum of care.3 In pediatric hospital settings, nurses provide high-dependency support, such as mobility assistance, feeding, medication administration, and hygiene, while simultaneously acting as educators, advocates, and emotional anchors for families.4 However, delivering this care is both physically demanding and emotionally taxing, particularly in systems with limited infrastructure and insufficient disability focused training.3,4 In these settings, children with CP often lack access to assistive technology, community-based rehabilitation, and consistent caregiver support.5 Nurses must then navigate these structural deficits while managing caregiver expectations and delivering complex, continuous care.6 These realities are further compounded in post-Soviet contexts, where institutional legacies and evolving healthcare reforms intersect to create uniquely transitional systems.7,8 In Kazakhstan, recent reforms have prioritized maternal and child health,7,9 but healthcare delivery remains constrained by healthcare workforce shortages, fragmented disability services, and cultural perceptions of disability that influence caregiver–nurse interactions.10 These professionals experience stress due to the unique nature of children's nursing, and struggle with fear and uncertainty in their new roles.11 However, while these needs are acknowledged in general pediatric contexts, the lived experiences of nurses who specifically care for children with CP remain significantly underexplored in the literature, particularly within transitional or post-Soviet healthcare systems, where structural limitations and evolving professional norms may intensify such stressors.6,9,11 In contrast to research from low and middle-income countries in sub-Saharan Africa,12,13 and Southeast Asia,14,15 where the focus has largely been on infrastructure deficits and caregiver burden, post-Soviet systems like Kazakhstan are characterized by a unique convergence of historical legacies, emerging policy reforms, and professional role ambiguities.7,16,17 These transitional dynamics affect health system structures but also the daily realities of nursing practice, shaping emotional labor, decision-making, and caregiver interactions.

Furthermore, some previous studies focused on the caregiver burden, clinical rehabilitation outcomes, or care models implemented,8,9,18 but few specifically examined the experiences of pediatric nurses, particularly within Kazakhstan. Thus, a notable gap remains in the literature regarding how nurses perceive, navigate, and respond to the physical, emotional, and relational complexities of caring for children with CP. Previous studies in high-income countries explored the experiences of pediatric nurses in technologically advanced and policy-supported environments,19,20 but these contexts differ substantially from transitional healthcare systems. The legacy of a country, evolving care ethics, and systemic fragmentation uniquely shape how frontline workers experience emotional labor, caregiving, and moral decision-making but this intersection remains understudied.

The present study addressed this empirical gap as well as exposing theoretical blind spots in existing nursing frameworks, which often assume stable institutional conditions. The results obtained in the present study make three key contributions regarding pediatric nurses in healthcare institutions. First, this study focused on the underrepresented voices of pediatric nurses in a post-Soviet country, which is a geopolitical and clinical context that has seldom been addressed in previous neurodisability studies. Second, the findings obtained in this study demonstrate that emotional labor in CP care functions a source of strain but also as a core element of professional identity and moral purpose. In transitional healthcare environments, emotional labor frequently substitutes for absent institutional support, revealing a distinctive moral economy of care. Third, institutional conditions were identified such as peer support and access to psychological services that can reduce burnout and strengthen resilience, thereby providing an empirical foundation for workforce interventions tailored to resource-constrained systems.

Methods

Objectives

This study explored the lived experiences of nurses who cared for children with CP in a tertiary pediatric hospital in Kazakhstan. The aims of this study were to identify the physical, emotional, and institutional factors that shape their caregiving roles, and to generate insights that can inform improvements in care delivery, nurse well-being, and system-level responsiveness within pediatric neurodisability services.

Design

This study used a descriptive qualitative approach. Descriptive qualitative research focuses on presenting phenomena as they naturally occur, offering clear and straightforward accounts of participants' experiences without imposing interpretive frameworks or theoretical development.21 This approach is commonly used in areas with limited previous research and aims to capture the perspectives of participants in their own words. This design was selected because it aligned with the study's objective of providing direct and detailed accounts of the experiences and perceptions of nurses regarding their care for children with CP.

Setting

The study was conducted in a tertiary government hospital in Kazakhstan. The institution is one of the country's most comprehensive centers for pediatric health services, providing specialized care for children with complex conditions, including neurodisabilities. The hospital has a total capacity of 550 beds and offers a wide range of specialized services in 31 clinical units, which include pediatric surgery, long-term care, and rehabilitation. These units help to provide care and comfort to patients

Participants

The participants in this study were registered nurses in the current workplace with more than one year of experience caring for children hospitalized with CP and who were willing to participate. The exclusion criteria were nurses with less than 1 year of experience caring for pediatric patients with CP, those on leave, and those who refused to participate in the study. A homogeneous purposive sampling technique was used in this study by selecting those with rich and relevant experience in caring for pediatric disabilities.

The sample size was determined by data saturation. Saturation was monitored using a saturation grid during the data collection phase. No new codes emerged by the 10th interview and the themes had stabilized with sufficient variation in nurse experiences across units by the 12th interview, which indicated thematic redundancy and justified closing recruitment at that point.

Instruments

The main tool used for data collection was semi-structured interviews. Semi-structured interviews allowed us to gather information about the experiences, feelings, and challenges of nurses while caring for pediatric patients with CP. The questions covered different aspects, including demographic information, care for children, types of CP, difficulties, communication, workload, and insights.

The interview questions were developed based on the literature review, and questions were translated into Russian and Kazakh to ensure clarity. To ensure the reliability of multilingual interviews, the interview guide was initially translated into the local language by a bilingual researcher. These translations were then independently back-translated into English by a second bilingual expert. Any discrepancies between the original and back-translated versions were discussed and resolved through consensus among the experts and research team.

Experts evaluated the guide questions for face and content validity to determine whether they could be used to collect data according to the study's objective. Some changes were made to the interview questions to ensure full coverage of the experiences of nurses while caring for children with CP and emotional and psychological difficulties. The guide questions were piloted before they were used in the interviews.

Data collection procedure

Data collection began after receiving ethical approval from the IREC. After obtaining approval, participant recruitment was facilitated through coordination with the hospital's head nurse in the pediatric department. The head nurse identified potential participants who met the inclusion criteria and informed them about the study. Those who expressed an interest were approached by the researcher, provided with an information sheet, and asked to give written informed consent before participation.

Interviews were scheduled at a time that was convenient for each participant in order to respect the busy schedules of nurses and avoid interfering with their duties. The interviews were conducted in each participant's preferred place, such as a private, quiet room within the hospital, to ensure confidentiality and minimize interruptions. Data were collected from March to April in 2025.

The data collection procedure involved semi-structured, face-to-face interviews, which allowed flexibility for participants to share their experiences while also ensuring consistency across interviews. The interview tool consisted of open-ended, predetermined questions designed to explore the experiences of nurses while providing care to pediatric patients with CP. The primary question was: “Can you describe your experience in providing care for pediatric patients with CP?” and follow-up questions were based on the participants' answers. Each interview lasted approximately 30–60 min, depending on the depth of the responses and participant's availability.

During the interview, an audio recorder (such as a digital voice recorder or a secure mobile recording app) was used to accurately capture the responses of participants. In addition, the researcher recorded field notes to document non-verbal cues, contextual details, and preliminary impressions. After each interview, the researcher manually transcribed the audio recording verbatim and analyzed the data.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using Braun and Clark's22 six-step thematic analysis method. Conducting thematic analysis generated valuable insights about the experiences and challenges of nurses while caring for pediatric CP patients. This approach facilitated independence, and rigorous and credible data analysis.

Initially, the researchers familiarized themselves with the data. Each researcher read the transcript file line by line and highlighted meaningful quotations that reflected the significance of each statement. Each researcher then manually coded the data independently. After each researcher manually coded the data, they convened to compare their codes. Any discrepancies in the codes were resolved by reviewing the quotes, and the researchers agreed to the final code. Subsequent codes were then clustered together to develop subthemes. Consequently, codes such as “lack of medicine” and “limited equipment” were merged into a “lack of medical resources” subtheme.

The subthemes were aggregated and organized into broader themes that described significant patterns in the data. This process included locating links between related codes to develop higher-level themes that captured the nurses' experiences. Consequently, themes emerged such as “challenges in caring for children with CP,” “emotional and psychological aspects of care,” “caregiver support and interaction,” “professional and personal growth,” and “satisfaction with the work environment.” The themes were refined until they accurately reflected the data. This process included reviewing the transcripts and member checking to ensure that themes were consistent with participants’ experiences. During the data analysis process, all data, themes, and subthemes were reviewed by experts. Some themes were elaborated, merged, and reorganized to clarify and reduce overflow. For example, the original independent “emotional exhaustion” and “burnout” themes were placed under a common “emotional and psychological aspects in care” theme.

After finalizing the themes and subthemes, they were described to capture the relevance and impact of each theme. Each theme was labeled descriptively to define the essence of the theme. Subthemes were discussed to strengthen the analysis. The last step involved formulating a coherent narrative by connecting the themes to generate the research findings. Direct quotes from participants were included to illustrate key themes and support the analysis. This approach allowed us to remain very close to the data and to describe the experiences of nurses while caring for pediatric patients with CP in detail.

Rigor and trustworthiness

The researcher established prolonged engagement to ensure the credibility of the study, and in order to obtain a deeper understanding of the context where nurses provided care and to capture the perspectives of nurses regarding factors that influenced the coordinated care of pediatric patients with CP. In addition, member checking was implemented when discussing interpretations of the responses with each participant herself to ensure that the findings accurately captured the meanings and experiences they intended to communicate. Peer debriefing was conducted with registered nurses experienced in qualitative analysis to help confirm the interpretations and prevent researcher bias.

To enhance transferability, the researchers collected and presented specific demographic and professional background information regarding the participants, including years of experience and workplace setting. This allowed readers to assess the relevance of the results to closely related settings or groups.

To ensure dependability, the researchers maintained a comprehensive audit trail containing field notes, audio recordings, transcripts, and reflective memos depicting the research process. All stages of data collection and analysis were documented, providing a transparent and traceable research process. Throughout the study, practicing reflexivity supported confirmability. The researchers used a reflexive journal to note personal ideas, assumptions, and possible biases to keep the results grounded in the participants' narratives rather than the researchers’ interpretations. The primary researcher had clinical familiarity with pediatric care but was not employed at the study site, positioning them as a partial outsider. This outsider status minimized social desirability bias during interviews but required continuous reflexivity to interpret cultural dynamics and emotional responses. Reflexive journaling documented instances where personal assumptions or prior clinical exposure may have influenced theme development, ensuring that the final interpretations remained grounded in the participants' narratives.

Authenticity was finally reached through the balanced representation of the voices of all participants, highlighting both common themes and divergent perspectives. The researchers respected each participant's unique experiences and preserved the emotional tone of their stories when reporting the results.

The number of years experience providing care to children with CP ranged from 1 year to 6 months to 11 years. Most participants had applied nursing educational qualifications. Applied nurses had completed a 2- or 3-year nursing program (see Table 1) (see Table 2).

Summary of themes and subthemes.

The findings obtained in this study based on thematic analysis of the experiences of 12 nurses who provided care for pediatric patients with CP are presented in the following text. Analysis of the data identified 86 initial codes for extraction. The initial codes with similar meanings were combined into 13 sub-themes and five major themes were finally identified: (1) challenges in caring for children with CP; (2) emotional and psychological aspects of care; (3) caregiver support and interaction; (4) professional and personal growth; and (5) satisfaction with the work environment. Each theme was supported by subthemes and direct participant quotes to illustrate the lived experiences and perspectives of nurses.

Theme 1: challenges in caring for children with CP

This theme encompasses the difficulties faced by nurses when physically handling CP patients and the resource limitations within healthcare settings.

Physical care and daily medical tasks

Children with CP require physical assistance, such as lifting, positioning, and managing hygiene, and nurses shared that it was physically challenging for them to perform these procedures without assistance. The nursing staff most frequently assisted with transfers, repositioning, and activities of daily living, which often imposed inordinate demands on their musculoskeletal system, thereby predisposing them to musculoskeletal disorders. These caregiving demands were also exacerbated because of a lack of staffing resources and the need for assisting peers, highlighting structural constraints in healthcare institutions.

The nursing staff most frequently assisted with transfers, repositioning, and activities of daily living, which often imposed inordinate demands on their musculoskeletal system, thereby predisposing them to musculoskeletal disorders. “We must feed them ourselves, help them sit, lie, and change their position. This part is physically demanding—we have to lift them …” N1

“These children need total assistance with daily care—feeding through tubes, suctioning, repositioning.” N11

The participants further mentioned that CP children required assistance with eating and taking medications, which could be time-intensive. Administering medication was also very challenging. Children with CP sometimes have difficulty swallowing and require prolonged feeding times that must be carefully monitored, as well as the administration of drugs. These procedures are very time-consuming and place extra pressure on nurses, depriving them of further time to care for different patients.

“Sometimes parents … just don’t give the medication, or the child vomits and spits it out. You need to be there.” N8

“They require very detailed attention—everyday care like feeding, bathing, and changing positions takes more time.” N10

Lack of medical resources

Many nurses described having to adapt due to a lack of equipment, which added extra strain and limited the quality of care. Nurses mentioned that the care they provided to children with CP did not meet the quality standard since the assistive equipment needed to strengthen their muscle activity and ensure patient mobility and comfort was unavailable.

“In the past, we didn’t have wheelchairs, and the hospital beds were not functional.” N1

“They often need special equipment, and we don’t always have what’s needed, like proper wheelchairs or mattresses.” N10

“In our department, we don’t have many wheelchairs. It would be nice to have more.” N3

Parental stress, exhaustion, and challenging behaviors

Nurses shared that emotional and physical burdens also affected the parents of pediatric patients, which negatively affected their ability to take care of their children. Nurses often witnessed parents doing everything for their children with CP. However, a point came when they were exhausted and neglected the child's care, and sometimes missed providing physical exercise for the child.

“A mother stays next to her CP child all the time—lifting them, taking them to the bathroom, feeding them, doing everything, and they mentioned that it’s very tiring for them.” N1

“Parents are often exhausted and struggle to care for their children properly.” N2

Nurses reported that parents were often stressed and became aggressive toward the nursing staff, causing conflicts. This conflict in the working environment contributed to occupational burnout for nurses.

“Over time, the stress builds up, and they take it out on us, the nurses.” N1

“Parents of children with CP are a little different, and they are a little more aggressive.” N8

“It can be challenging when dealing with aggressive parents.” N4

Theme 2: emotional and psychological aspects of care

This theme explores the emotional and psychological experiences of nurses while caring for children with CP. Participants described the need to develop emotional resilience, manage the risk of burnout, and maintain professional boundaries to protect their own well-being. Many nurses reported emotional resilience and maintaining professional boundaries to ensure their well-being.

Emotional resilience and coping mechanisms

Nurses reported that caring for children with CP required a high degree of emotional strength. Many described deliberately regulating their emotions and maintaining composure in order to continue providing quality care. For some, remaining emotionally distanced from patients was a necessary coping mechanism.

“As a nurse who has to work for at least another ten years, I need to remain composed.” N1

“We try not to get attached to the child. We keep our distance.” N8

Despite efforts to stay resilient, several participants acknowledged feeling emotionally overwhelmed at times. Thus, nurses emphasized the importance of psychological support, rest, and personal coping strategies for maintaining their mental well-being.

“After my shift, I always take advice from the psychologist.” N2

“I rest whenever possible and try to keep a positive mindset.” N11

“I also take mental breaks when I feel overwhelmed.” N12

These accounts reflect a combination of emotional strain and active coping efforts, illustrating how nurses balanced compassion with the need to protect their own psychological health.

Maintaining professional boundaries

Maintaining professional boundaries was identified as another protective strategy used by nurses when caring for children with CP. Participants noted that emotional detachment was taught during their training and reinforced in clinical practice as a way to prevent emotional overload.

“I studied psychology for three years … In nursing education, we learn that a nurse is a nurse, and a patient is a patient.” N1

“We must be professionals.” N6

“You can love them, but don’t show it too much because you might feel bad.” N8

While many nurses described challenges with managing their emotions, one participant reported experiencing no significant emotional or psychological difficulties. This perspective highlights individual variability in emotional responses to caregiving.

“No, not really. I see children struggle, but I do not let it get to me.” N1

Theme 3: caregiver support and interaction

Caregiver support and interaction involve providing caregivers with education, emotional care, and collaborating in care due to the needs of children with CP. Emotional care includes empathetic communication, respect for the person's dignity, and practical assistance. Educating parents benefits them and their children with CP by enhancing their overall well-being. It is also important to acknowledge the caregiver's needs, such as preventing burnout and accessing supportive resources, to maintain high-quality caregiving. In addition, collaborating with caregivers helps with understanding personal and cultural values, strengthens trust, and improves the effectiveness of caregiving relationships.

Educating parents on CP care

Nurses emphasized the importance of educating parents because children with CP require complex support, and educating parents ensures that high-quality care is continued at home. Nurses served as key informants for parents, guiding them on supporting their child's mobility, feeding, and medication adherence. This educational role of nurses is essential for enhancing family-centered care, promoting teamwork, and empowerment.

“Nurses also educate parents and caregivers.” N2

“The training explains what kind of care children with cerebral palsy need, and the mother should always be there.” N7

“We focus more on teaching the mother since they are the main caregiver.” N9

Managing caregiver emotions

Nurses frequently encountered emotionally overwhelmed caregivers, indicating that providing caregiving for a child with CP had significant psychological repercussions for families. When parental distress escalated into conflict, nurses viewed emotional management as part of their professional responsibility. However, their responses revealed a reliance on institutional psychological support, suggesting that nurses viewed emotional crises as requiring specialized expertise.

“Sometimes a mother quarrels with the nurses, and then we call a psychologist.” N7

“We cannot argue with them … we have a psychologist in the center.” N2

Collaboration with caregivers

Many nurses emphasized the importance of collaborating with parents to help them better understand their children's needs and the cultural practices that are effective in managing them. Good relationships with parents are built through open communication and mutual support, which enhances the quality of care and reduces the burden on caregivers. This further demonstrates that practical nurse–parent partnerships enhance the quality of care but also the emotional climate in the caregiving environment.

“I also talk to the parents to learn how they care for their children and their practices. They give good advice because they know their child best.” N10

“Mothers are a great help to us. We help the mother when she takes care of the child.” N6

“I talk with the parents to understand their child better. It helps me a lot.” N12

Theme 4: professional and personal growth

Despite the emotional and physical demands, nurses described caring for children with CP as transformative. Their accounts revealed significant developmental gains both clinically and personally, as well as professionally. This theme highlights how challenging care environments can cultivate adaptive skills, foster empathy, and reinforce a professional identity.

Gaining experience and specialized skills

Nurses reported developing clinical and interpersonal aspects of care as they provided neurological, nutritional, and mobility care to children with CP. The uniqueness of caring for children with CP led nurses to improve their skills, such as adaptability and problem solving. Furthermore, nurses noted that this professional development was paramount for their career and job enrichment. The demands of CP care also forced nurses to refine their clinical intuition, adaptability, and critical thinking, contributing to long-term professional growth.

“I am gaining experience and acquiring a lot of knowledge.” N1

“Every patient is unique. We also learn patience …” N2

Learning patience and compassion

Nurses described personal transformations resulting from witnessing children's struggles. These emotional encounters nurtured more profound compassion and empathy, which nurses viewed as essential qualities for their practice. Therefore, CP care was a technical task but also a moral and emotional journey. This subtheme reveals that emotional connections can enrich the caregiving experience and shape a stronger, more empathetic professional identity.

“Patience, calmness, kindness—and it’s a great learning experience.” N1

“It made me more compassionate and more patient.” N10

“They suffer so much, and I feel deep compassion for them.” N2

Adopting a professional attitude

Nurses are committed to providing high-quality care to pediatric patients with CP and expressed a strong sense of duty. They also emphasized the importance of regulating emotions, remaining resilient, and adapting as essential skills that promote good standards in practice.

“I believe it is our duty to care for patients.” N3

“We knew what we were getting into and were ready for any patient with any diagnosis.” N7

“It taught me that nursing is not only about treatment but also about emotional care.” N12

This finding further demonstrates that a sense of moral purpose reinforced their perseverance in the face of challenges, illustrating how a professional identity could buffer against emotional strain and contribute to sustained engagement in CP care.

Theme 5: satisfaction with the work environment

Nurses expressed overall satisfaction with their work setting, which they attributed to institutional support, teamwork, and opportunities for professional fulfillment. Their accounts show how a supportive environment can mitigate stress and foster long-term retention.

Work being rewarding

Nurses described their work as meaningful and personally fulfilling, particularly when they witnessed positive changes in children or felt appreciated by colleagues. This sense of reward functioned as a motivational force, reinforcing their dedication. The nurses considered that providing CP care could have positive emotional rewards, such as seeing patient's progress or receiving recognition, which acted as protective factors against burnout.

“It feels very rewarding to know that I’m helping someone feel safe and cared for.” N10

Institutional support

Institutional support emerged as a major contributor to job satisfaction. Access to psychologists, adequate staffing, peer collaboration, and regular training were viewed as essential infrastructure that enabled nurses to manage occupational stress and deliver high-quality care. These findings highlight the crucial roles played by organizational structures in promoting the well-being of nurses. Nurses reported higher satisfaction and stronger engagement when institutions acknowledged the emotional labor inherent in CP care and provided mechanisms for support.

“We have a psychologist at the center, and I began to work with him.” N8

“After the psychologist's intervention, we usually notice positive changes in caregivers' behavior.” N2

Discussion

This study examined the experiences of nurses who cared for children with CP and identified the significant physical, emotional, and relational demands associated with pediatric neurodisability care. These findings illustrate the challenges related to the physical workload, emotional strain, caregiver interactions, opportunities for professional development, and workplace satisfaction. By interpreting these experiences within the structural and cultural realities of a transitional post-Soviet healthcare system, this discussion extends beyond general descriptions of low-resource settings and contributes to a deeper understanding of how health system transitions shape the daily work of nurses in CP care.

Challenges in caring for children with CP

Nurses reported substantial physical strain associated with lifting, repositioning, and feeding tasks, which were often conducted without adequate ergonomic support. These findings are consistent with the studies by Zhu et al.23 and Lopez et al.24 However, in Kazakhstan, these challenges are amplified by chronic infrastructure gaps, inconsistent staffing, and limited access to assistive devices. Previous studies frequently emphasized the caregiver burden, but the findings obtained in the present study highlight the embodied labor of nurses and show how systemic neglect transforms routine caregiving duties into high-risk physical work. Addressing these concerns requires the provision of equipment but also broader structural investment in pediatric neurodisability care. Safeguarding nurses from injury is essential for sustaining high-quality and compassionate care.

Emotional and psychological aspects of care

Nurses described the emotional exhaustion arising from the need to balance empathy with professional boundaries, and previous research also focused on emotional fatigue among nurses who cared for children with CP.25 However, in transitional health systems, emotional regulation becomes a compensatory response to institutional gaps rather than solely an interpersonal skill. Participants relied on informal coping strategies, peer support, and occasional access to psychological services, reflecting a structural expectation that nurses carry emotional burdens without formal support. One participant reported minimal emotional strain, highlighting individual variability in coping. These insights extend emotional labor theory by demonstrating how cultural norms, developing professional identities, and systemic instability collectively shape affective demands in transitional healthcare environments.

Caregiver support and interaction

Nurse–parent collaboration was a crucial component of CP care. Nurses provided direct education on feeding, medication, and mobility but also supported caregivers’ emotions. These findings are consistent with previous research,20,25, 26, 27 but our data also indicate that conflicts or tension could often arise from structural challenges, such as chronic caregiver fatigue, limited community resources, and the emotional weight of long-term disability care, rather than from communication issues alone. Therefore, strengthening nurse–parent relationships requires systemic interventions, including structured communication training, conflict management skills, and culturally responsive engagement strategies that recognize the burdens carried by caregivers.

Professional and personal growth

Despite the challenges they encountered, nurses reported significant personal and professional growth. Sexton et al.28 noted increased patience and skill refinement among nurses in high-resource settings. However, in Kazakhstan, this growth emerges through navigating system deficiencies, adapting to unpredictability, and maintaining ethical commitment under pressure. Participants described developing moral resilience and enhanced problem-solving skills as they managed complex patient needs with limited institutional support, which reflects a form of “growth through adversity” that is characteristic of transitional health systems. Thus, structured mentorship, reflective practice, and resilience-focused training should be prioritized to transform this survival-based growth into sustainable professional development.29,30

Satisfaction with the work environment

Workplace satisfaction shaped the overall experiences of nurses in CP care. According to Herzberg's two-factor theory,31 both intrinsic motivators (meaning, purpose, and contribution) and extrinsic supports (training and psychological resources) influenced satisfaction. Unlike high-income contexts where compassion satisfaction is reinforced through formal recognition and structured support systems, the nurses in the present study relied heavily on peer solidarity, emotional reciprocity, and informal teamwork. These dynamics served as stabilizing forces, sustaining commitment despite physical and emotional burdens. This finding aligns with global evidence that emphasizes the critical role of supportive team environments in enhancing the well-being and retention of nurses.4,11,32,33 To strengthen workplace satisfaction and reduce turnover, healthcare organizations should implement resilience-building strategies, including embedded psychological support, structured peer mentorship, and recognition systems.3,6,34 Institutionalizing these supports will be essential for maintaining high-quality, compassionate care for children with complex neurodisability needs and sustaining a stable nursing workforce within transitional health systems.

Limitations

This study was conducted at a single tertiary hospital, which may limit the transferability of our findings to broader populations. In addition, all of the participants were female, and thus the lack of demographic diversity may have influenced the range of perspectives captured. The sample size was sufficient for thematic saturation, but may not have captured the full diversity of nursing experiences across different units or shifts. Social desirability bias was possible due to the formal interview setting. Moreover, all participants were female, limiting gender-based analysis. Recruitment was facilitated through the head nurse, who could have introduced selection bias. Thus, the researchers ensured that all eligible nurses were invited to participate, and participation was entirely voluntary. Nurses were assured that their responses would remain confidential and not affect their employment. The sampling technique was homogeneous purposive sampling, which limited the analysis of data by comparing the demographic details of the participants. Thus, it is recommended to conduct the same research using maximum variation sampling techniques to compare the demographic details and perform in-depth analysis of the findings. Finally, researcher bias may have influenced data interpretation, although reflexivity and peer debriefing were employed to strengthen trustworthiness and ensure that the findings reflected the authentic perspectives of the participants.

Conclusion

This study explored the experiences of nurses who cared for children with CP in a tertiary hospital in Kazakhstan and five major themes were identified: caregiving challenges, emotional strain, caregiver interactions, professional growth, and workplace satisfaction. Nurses exhibited resilience and ethical commitment despite systemic limitations, highlighting the physical, emotional, and relational demands of pediatric neurodisability care. These findings emphasize the urgent need for institutional investment in assistive infrastructure, specialized training, and embedded psychological support to reduce burnout and strengthen workforce retention.

The present study provides important insights but its qualitative, single-site design limits generalizability, and the extent to which these experiences reflect broader patterns within Kazakhstan or across post-Soviet and Central Asian contexts remains unknown. Future research should extend these findings through multi-site or comparative regional studies to examine similarities and differences in CP nursing across diverse transitional health systems. By focusing on the perspectives of nurses within a transitional healthcare system, the present study adds to global nursing scholarship and highlights context-specific strategies for improving care quality and sustainability in complex pediatric environments, and its implications are grounded directly in the lived experiences described by participants.

Implications for medical practice

This study has several important implications for clinical practice, nursing education, leadership, and policy in pediatric neurodisability care. Clinically, the findings obtained highlight the urgent need for assistive devices and ergonomic protocols to mitigate the physical strain of caring for children with CP, and to safeguard both the health and care quality of nurses. Educationally, the results support the integration of CP-specific content into nursing curricula, particularly hands-on training in positioning and feeding, and the cultivation of emotional resilience through simulation and reflective practice.

At the organizational level, this study highlights the value of institutional supports, especially peer collaboration and accessible psychological services, as protective mechanisms against burnout. In particular, nurse managers are well placed to foster emotionally supportive work environments that normalize open communication, encourage resilience building, and strengthen professional identity. From a policy perspective, investment in pediatric disability infrastructure, standardized care pathways, and continuous professional development are essential for aligning systemic resources with the lived realities of nurses in transitional health systems, such as that in Kazakhstan. Finally, this study extends theoretical discussions of emotional labor by showing how empathy, self-regulation, and moral identity intersect within structurally constrained environments. These findings demonstrate the need for culturally sensitive strategies that consider societal attitudes toward disability and mental health, ensuring that the well-being of nurses and patient care are advanced together.
