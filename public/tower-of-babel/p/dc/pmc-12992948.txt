Atopic march protocol involving sequential skin and lung inflammation elicited by epicutaneous Staphylococcus aureus and allergen exposure
Authors: Sabrina N Kline, Laine E Feller, Danielle Wills, Rishi Damarla, Nathan K Archer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe prevalence of allergic diseases is rising globally, and these diseases often follow a sequential pattern of disease onset in childhood, beginning with atopic dermatitis, followed by food allergy, asthma, and allergic rhinitis. Here, we present a protocol for modeling this “atopic march” in mice. We describe the steps for inducing atopic dermatitis-like skin inflammation through Staphylococcus aureus epicutaneous exposure. We then detail procedures for eliciting lung inflammation via skin allergen sensitization and subsequent intratracheal allergen elicitation in the lungs.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Kline et al.1Subject areas: Cell isolation, Single cell, Flow cytometry, Health sciences, Immunology, Microbiology, Model organisms

Before you begin

Innovation

Epicutaneous Staphylococcus aureus mouse infection models represent a powerful and innovative tool for studying complex, clinically relevant skin conditions characterized by S. aureus skin colonization under controlled and reproducible conditions. These models are particularly valuable for studying chronic inflammatory skin diseases such as atopic dermatitis, which are associated with systemic immune alterations and multi-organ effects beyond the skin2 that cannot be addressed by in vitro systems. By incorporating an allergen during the skin sensitization and lung elicitation phases, the model described herein also provides a robust platform to investigate the immunological mechanisms of the atopic march.

These protocols can be used to dissect host immune responses using transgenic mice, evaluate therapeutic interventions such as topical and systemic antibiotics or immunotherapies, and explore microbiome-host-pathogen interactions. Additionally, they enable detailed investigation into the molecular pathways and mechanisms underlying the progression of the atopic march and inter-epithelial signaling.

Background

Atopic dermatitis (AD) is a chronic inflammatory skin condition marked by recurring, itchy, and eczematous lesions, and affects an estimated 15–20% of children and 3–10% of adults in industrialized countries.3,4,5,6 It often represents the first step in the “atopic march” – a progression to other allergic diseases including food allergy, rhinitis, and asthma.7,8 Early-life AD, linked to skin barrier dysfunction and inflammation, predisposes to sensitization and allergic responses at other distant epithelial barriers sites.7,9,10 The incidence of allergic diseases is increasing and currently impacts nearly 20% of the global population.11 As such, understanding the mechanisms by which AD progresses to other conditions associated with the atopic march could reveal key therapeutic targets for the prevention and treatment of allergic disease.

Staphylococcus aureus colonization exacerbates AD, with bacterial abundance correlating with disease severity.12,13 Studies in humans and mice link S. aureus skin colonization in AD to increased allergic responses and allergy risk, especially asthma.14,15 As such, this mouse atopic march model can be employed to characterize how epicutaneous S. aureus colonization leads to the development of lung inflammation, thereby modeling the connection between initial AD and subsequent asthma development.

The protocol below describes the specific actions for performing a mouse model of the atopic march comprised of an initial skin sensitization step with S. aureus and cockroach antigen (CrA) allergen, followed by a lung elucidation step via intratracheal CrA administration.1,14 Alternatively, the skin sensitization phase can be performed as a standalone experiment as it is a well-established model of epicutaneous S. aureus-induced, atopic dermatitis-like skin inflammation.1,14,16,17 Finally, detailed procedures to guide sample collection are outlined for both the AD-like skin inflammation model and the atopic march model.

Isoflurane and oxygen tank settings

Mice were anesthetized using an isoflurane gas induction chamber for all procedures except where alternate instructions are specifically noted in the text. Detailed step by step instructions can be found for the set up and maintenance of the gas induction chamber at step 28.

In this isoflurane anesthetic station (Figure 1A), the flow of isoflurane gas is driven by the flow of oxygen from an oxygen tank, through the isoflurane vaporizer (Figure 1B), and the resulting oxygen/isoflurane gas is delivered to the induction chamber and/or an anesthetic nose cone via tubing (Figures 1C and 1D). The percentage of isoflurane gas in the isoflurane/oxygen mixture can be adjusted using the isoflurane vaporizer (Figure 1B). Gas flow to the induction chamber and nosecone can be individually controlled via switches on the isoflurane flow indicator unit (Figure 1C).

Mice

All experimental mice were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions within an accredited animal facility. The choice of mouse strain will be guided by the specific experimental question. We have had good success using female, 6- to 9-week-old C57Bl/6 mice, as well as various transgenic and knock-in reporter strains.1,14,16,17

Step-by-step method details

Cockroach antigen preparation

Timing: 0.5–1 h

The following steps describe the preparation of stock solutions of cockroach antigen protein (CrA) (Stallergenes, Greer laboratories) for use in the atopic march model. Prepared stocks can be stored at −20°C for up to 3 months.

1.Prepare a 2 mg/mL stock of CrA by diluting the allergen extract with sterile PBS to generate a stock whereby the concentration is determined by the protein content rather than total mass.

Prepare a 2 mg/mL stock of CrA by diluting the allergen extract with sterile PBS to generate a stock whereby the concentration is determined by the protein content rather than total mass.

Note: For example, for a vial containing 7.82 mg of dry protein, 3.91 mL of PBS is added to obtain a 2 mg/mL stock.

Note: A 2 mg/ml stock will be used for epicutaneous exposure during induction of AD-like inflammation.

2.Prepare a 0.4 mg/mL CrA stock by performing a 1:5 dilution of the 2 mg/mL CrA stock prepared in step 1 with sterile PBS.

Prepare a 0.4 mg/mL CrA stock by performing a 1:5 dilution of the 2 mg/mL CrA stock prepared in step 1 with sterile PBS.

Note: A 0.4 mg/ml stock will be used for intratracheal administration to elicit allergic lung inflammation during days 10–12 of the atopic march model.

Note: Stocks and aliquots can be made in advance and stored at −20°C for up to 3 months. It is recommended to aliquot the 2 mg/mL and 0.4 mg/mL stocks into 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes containing 270 μL each, as this is sufficient to treat 5 mice at a 50 μL/mouse dose per treatment.

Note: Prepare three times the amount of 0.4 mg/ml stock as 2 mg/ml stock per experiment, as epicutaneous exposure with 50 μL of 2 mg/mL CrA occurs only one time per mouse, whereas administration of 50 μl of 0.4 mg/mL CrA intratracheally occurs 3 times per mouse.

Mouse skin preparation: Shaving

Timing: 5 min per mouse

Mouse skin requires complete depilation for successful epicutaneous S. aureus (+/− allergen) exposure and subsequent development of AD-like skin inflammation. This section describes the initial step of shaving the dorsal mouse fur.

Note: The shaving and depilation steps should be completed 1–2 days prior to S. aureus exposure (day 0) to allow the skin to recover from any irritation associated with Nair chemical exposure.

Note: The shaving of the mouse dorsal fur procedure is available as a video (Methods Video S1) to help visualize the hand grip and mouse positioning we have found work well for this task. Expected outcomes

Epicutaneous S. aureus-induced skin inflammation

Skin inflammation is the primary anticipated outcome at the termination of the model of S. aureus epicutaneous (e.c.) +/− allergen exposure, also referred to as the initial phase of the atopic march development protocol. This skin inflammation recapitulates many features of human AD, including erythema and scaling, increased epidermal thickness, increased immune cell infiltration, and elevated Th2 cytokines.1,14,16,17

In the absence of S. aureus, or with CrA alone on the skin, very little skin inflammation occurs (Figures 10A and 10B). However, significant skin inflammation occurs following S. aureus exposure, characterized by erythema, edema and scaling (Figures 10A and 10B). Histological analysis of the skin should also reveal increased epidermal thickening (Figures 10C and 10D). Factors such as low bacterial viability, improper bacteria inoculum dosage, or poor Band-Aid placement and maintenance may impair induction of skin inflammation, thereby impairing the outcome of the model. At a cellular level, increased infiltration of inflammatory cells such as neutrophils, eosinophils and T cells occurs in the skin, contributing to skin disease severity.1,14,16,17 Along with keratinocytes, these immune cells enhance the inflammatory milieu via secretion and signaling of pro-inflammatory cytokines e.g., IL-36 cytokines.1,14,16,17

Preclinical model of the atopic march

The combined presence of S. aureus and CrA on the skin disrupts local barrier integrity and induces a systemic immune response at other barrier epithelia resulting in a heightened immune response upon allergen elicitation. The expected outcomes of the atopic march model as described here are the induction of skin inflammation as described for the AD-like disease model (Figures 10A–10D) with the addition of increased immune cell infiltration in the BAL, weight loss, lung inflammation and bronchial epithelial thickness as assessed by lung histology1,14 (Figures 10E–10G).

If the mice swallow the CrA rather than inhale it, the progression of the atopic march will be impaired: weight loss is less pronounced and the immune cell infiltrate, particularly neutrophil levels in the lungs and bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL), is significantly reduced.

In the atopic march model described above, we observed a predominantly neutrophilic infiltrate in the lungs, which closely resembled non-T2 neutrophilic asthma–a severe, steroid-resistant asthma subtype.28 Importantly, this model offers valuable insights not only into the progression of allergic diseases but also into potential therapeutic strategies for neutrophilic asthma and other conditions as these findings could also be significant for other inflammatory lung diseases, such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and cystic fibrosis.

This protocol has not yet been evaluated with other asthma-associated allergens possessing intrinsic proteolytic activity, such as house dust mite (HDM) or Penicillium citrinum, which may disrupt epithelial barriers in the skin and lungs and potentially elicit distinct immune responses.29,30

When planning an experiment, appropriate controls should be used until consistent results develop, as the epicutaneous wrapping phase and the administration of CrA via IT can be challenging to master. The continued presence of S. aureus on the skin is crucial for inducing skin dysbiosis and epithelial barrier disruption, necessary for initiating the development of the atopic march.

Histology: Epidermal thickness

Another useful metric for assessing inflammation is quantifying the thickening of the epidermis via histology. Once the skin tissue section has undergone formalin fixation, processing, and paraffin embedding, it can be sectioned on edge and mounted on charged glass slides, followed by staining with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) for visualization of the layers of the skin: epidermis, dermis, hypodermis and muscle. Images of the entire skin section can then be obtained using a bench top microscope with a camera attachment or scanning slides using a digital slide scanner at 20× magnification. Ensure images are obtained that contain an appropriate scale bar for use in subsequent image analysis. Import images to an image analysis software e.g., ImageJ. Calibrate the image scale by entering a known distance and unit based on the scale bar included during image acquisition. Using the “Line” tool, measure the epidermal thickness by drawing a perpendicular line through the epidermis starting at the apical surface of the stratum corneum and extending to the basal surface of the stratum basale. Measure the epidermal thickness at regular intervals (e.g., every 200 μm of skin length, example of black lines in epidermis, Figure 10C) and calculate the average of these measurements per mouse. Plot these mean values as the “epidermal thickness” (Figures 10C and 10D). Do not measure the depth of hair follicles; perform measurements only on the interfollicular epidermis.

Analysis of flow cytometry

Determining neutrophil composition in skin by flow cytometry

After the skin samples have been stained and processed in the flow cytometer, the samples may then be analyzed with software associated with the flow cytometer utilized e.g., MACsquant or FlowJo. The following gating strategies were used to identify skin neutrophil cell populations following live and singlet gating: Myeloid cells were first determined based on CD45hiCD11bhi markers, and neutrophils were subsequently identified as CD11bhiLy6GhiLy6Cint/low. To calculate the number of neutrophils in the 10 mm skin punch collected, the following factors must be considered: the size of the punch biopsy used for flow cytometry (half punch vs full punch), the volume in which the digested skin was resuspended (Step 87) and the volume taken up by the machine to yield the neutrophil cell count. For example, if the cell pellet from a full skin punch (post digestion and mesh filtration) was resuspended in 1 mL of PBS, 200 μL was plated, and 100 μL was taken up by the flow cytometer to yield a neutrophil count, that neutrophil count must be multiplied by 10 to yield the final neutrophil number in a 10-mm skin punch. If half a skin punch was used for flow cytometry, then the neutrophil count would be multiplied by 20 to yield the final neutrophil count per 10 mm skin punch.

Determining neutrophil counts in BAL by flow cytometry

After the BAL samples have been stained and processed in the flow cytometer, the samples may then be analyzed with flow cytometry analysis software e.g., MACSquant or FlowJo. The neutrophil population can be identified from the live singlet population as CD45hi, CD11bhi, Ly6GhiLy6Cint/low. To calculate the absolute number of neutrophils in the BAL, one must consider the volume that the BAL was resuspended in post ACK lysis wash and the volume of sample taken up by the flow cytometer to yield the measured neutrophil cell count. For example, if the BAL was resuspended in 600 μL of HBSS, 200 μL of that was plated, and 100 μL was taken up by the flow cytometer to yield a neutrophil count, that neutrophil count must be multiplied by 6 to yield the final neutrophil number in the BAL (100 x 6 = 600). Detailed flow cytometer gating strategies for BAL immune cell populations are available in previous publications.1,14

Analysis of lung histology

Various assessments of lung histology can be undertaken including an analysis of tissue density as a readout for lung inflammation and epithelial bronchiole thickness as a readout for airway irritation.1,14 Immunohistochemistry or immunofluorescent staining can be performed on histological sections on glass slides to analyze the abundance or spatial location of various cells or proteins of interest. For cellular density to be a meaningful readout, a consistent volume of NBF must be instilled into the lungs to achieve comparable lung inflation between samples. Likewise, adequate lung perfusion to flush red blood cells out of the vasculature is critical if immunofluorescent staining is to be pursued, as red blood cells exhibit autofluorescence that can interfere with signal interpretation and detection, particularly within the green channel.

Lung inflammation

Lung inflammation was calculated by quantifying the density of cellular infiltrates in H&E-stained lung tissue sections. Following fixation in 10% formalin for 16–20 h, whole lungs were sectioned from cranial to caudal into three equal-thickness cross-sections using a straight-edge razor. Tissue sections were oriented cut-side down for processing followed by embedding in paraffin wax and sectioning. The 4 μm-thick sections of lungs were then mounted on charged glass slides and stained with H&E. The slides were scanned using a digital slide scanner at 40× magnification. From these scans, images of the various lobes of the lung were captured at 5× magnification and exported into ImageJ for analysis.

To quantify the degree of inflammation, the cellular density within the lung parenchyma can be analyzed by applying a consistent thresholding method that distinguishes the darker-stained cellular regions, indicative of immune cell infiltration, from the aerated and acellular alveolar spaces. The threshold level should be applied uniformly across all images in a H&E-stained batch to ensure consistency. The proportion of cellular area relative to the total lung area can be calculated to provide a percentage reflecting the extent of lung inflammation based on the cellular density in the lung tissue (Figure 10F).

Bronchiole epithelia thickness

For bronchiole epithelia thickness measurements, images of bronchioles in H&E-stained slides were acquired at 20× magnification, resulting in images of 40–70 bronchioles per slide. Be sure to include an appropriate scale bar in images to allow future size calibration during image analysis. Using ImageJ, the epithelial thickness of each bronchiole was calculated by measuring the height of the mucosal epithelium circumferentially every 15 μm around the bronchiole, resulting in 7–30 measurements per bronchiole depending on the airway size, and calculating the average. The thin black lines in Figure 10E are examples of measurements making up the mean bronchioles thickness for that particular bronchiole. The average epithelial thicknesses of all bronchioles on the slide (40–70 bronchioles per lung slide) were then averaged together to find the average bronchiole thickness for the slide. This is reported as the “bronchiole epithelia” thickness for that mouse (Figure 10G).

Limitations

While the present protocol effectively models key features of atopic dermatitis and the atopic march, there are several limitations that should be considered.

Sex-specific differences

The model described here has been optimized for use in female mice and may not be directly applicable for use in male mice. For example, during skin preparation for flow cytometry, it is recommended that male mouse skin samples incubate for 1 h 15 min in digestion buffer (Step 83) compared to 1 h for female mice. Furthermore, males are reported to develop more severe disease than females in response to S. aureus infections, including skin and soft tissue infections.31,32 Additional optimization steps may be required to define the proper S. aureus inoculum concentration and/or wrap duration during the epicutaneous portion of the model for success in male mice.

Age and hair cycle of mice

This model requires the absence of an undercoat such that hair can be completely removed by shaving and depilation. While this normally corresponds to mice aged between 6 and 9 weeks, hair cycle timing may differ between mouse strains (particularly transgenics), and careful selection of both strain and age is essential for meaningful comparisons across experiments. Moreover, we have not performed this model in older mice and cannot predict the role of age in the ensuing inflammatory response.

Specialized equipment

The procedure requires access to a benchtop isoflurane vaporizer connected to an oxygen tank for safe and reproducible anesthesia, which may not be available in all laboratory settings. We have not optimized this protocol for use with injectable anesthetic agents and cannot comment on their compatibility.

Hair removal effects

Mice must be shaved and treated with a chemical depilatory lotion (Nair) prior to S. aureus ± CrA exposure. This process likely disrupts the native skin barrier and microbiome, introducing variables that may influence the immune response. This protocol has not been validated in naturally hairless (e.g., nude) mouse strains, which may offer an alternative approach.

Artificial induction of skin dysbiosis

The skin is inoculated with a high density of S. aureus to induce dysbiosis and inflammation. While effective for experimental purposes, this method does not fully replicate the gradual and complex microbial shifts observed in human atopic dermatitis, where S. aureus overgrowth often arises in the context of preexisting barrier dysfunctions and inter-microbial dynamics.

Limited skin monitoring

The use of Baid-Aids as wraps during the skin sensitization phase prevents direct observation of skin features during skin inflammation development such as erythema, edema, dryness, or lesion development, thus limiting real-time assessment of epicutaneous responses.

Partial representation of the atopic march

Although this protocol is intended to model the atopic march, it focuses exclusively on epicutaneous sensitization and subsequent lung inflammation by CrA exposure. Other manifestations such as allergic rhinitis and gastrointestinal involvement (e.g., enteritis) have not been assessed in this model.

Proteolytic characteristics of cockroach antigen

The cockroach extract used in both sensitization and elicitation phases contains proteolytic enzymes, which may independently influence epithelial barrier functions and immune responses in both the skin and lung. This inherent bioactivity should be considered when interpreting results, and adequate controls should be included such as epicutaneous exposure to CrA without S. aureus or intratracheal instillation of PBS in lieu of CrA. While we have optimized and validated this protocol for use with CrA, alternate allergens may be substituted (e.g., house dust mite) which may elicit different immune responses. In addition to the endpoints listed previously, further downstream analyses such as RNA sequencing or mass spectrometry may help garner deeper insights into the cellular and molecular responses in the lung during the development of the atopic march.

Low cellular viability for skin flow cytometry

Cellular analysis of skin tissue via flow cytometry is challenging due to the activity of both host-derived and bacterial proteases, which contribute to significant and rapid cell degradation. In our hands, viable cell yields are low, with only approximately 10–20% of cells remaining viable for analysis.

Incomplete profiling of immune responses by BAL analysis

While bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) allows for the analysis of immune cells within airway and alveolar spaces, it does not capture cells residing within the lung interstitium. Moreover, recovery of BAL fluid is inherently incomplete as the full 800 μL of PBS instilled is never fully recovered. A comprehensive assessment of all inflammatory cells residing within the lungs therefore requires post-perfusion lung dissection, enzymatic digestion, and tissue-specific flow cytometry. Alternatively, a histological evaluation of the lung tissue by immunofluorescent and/or Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining could assist in defining the cellular composition of the lung e.g., neutrophils, eosinophils, macrophages and T cells. Although we have not validated it, the BAL supernatant may potentially be analyzed for protein content (e.g., cytokines or proteases) via western blotting or mass spectrometry to help define immune processes being elicited. Although we have primarily used this protocol to assess neutrophilic influx in the BAL and lung, it may be adapted to evaluate other immune cell populations.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Skin cuts during shaving (step 31).

Potential solution

Dirty or blunt blades increase the likelihood of cuts, which can be mitigated with proper care and cleaning of the blades pre- and post-shave. Additionally, since clipper blades become hot after continued use, it may burn or irritate mouse skin thus increasing the incidence of shaving nicks. To alleviate this, always turn off the clippers between mice, and do not have them turned on for longer than 30–45 second increments. Moreover, have extra blades on hand and switch them out often to prevent excessive heating of individual sets of blades. Finally, unsteady hands and improper handling may be another cause of shaver nicks as holding the mouse with too loose a grip will cause the skin to be wrinkled and the blades to catch. Practice proper mouse handling, positioning, and shaving prior to the anticipated experiment to increase confidence and proficiency at holding the mouse with taut skin.

Problem 2

Chemical Nair burns following depilation (Step 37–40).

Potential solution

The first main cause of Nair burns is prolonged exposure to Nair which can be solved by using a timer and not exposing the mouse to Nair longer than 1 minute during the first Nair application, and 20 seconds during the second. Pay close attention to where the Nair is applied as accidental application near the belly, ears or shoulders may go unnoticed when removing the Nair later. The second main cause of Nair burns is due to inadequate rinsing of the Nair in the water bath. Using a new wash rag and replacing dirty wash water with fresh warm water can help. Even a barely visible small layer of Nair can cause devastating burns if not properly removed. Prior to returning the mouse to its housing cage, confirm that no Nair remains on the skin, particularly near the ear, shoulders, sides of the abdomen and bottom of the back. While these locations will not directly affect S. aureus ± CrA induced inflammation, it will lead to excessive scratching behavior, loss of Band-Aid adhesiveness and increased mouse morbidity, thus indirectly affecting the integrity of the experiment.

Problem 3

Mouse-initiated Band-Aid removal during infection.

Potential solution

Due to the nature of the S. aureus-induced skin inflammation, some itchiness experienced by the mice is inevitable, leading to scratching, biting, and chewing often directed at the bandages. These problems are inherent in the model and do not have a direct solution for alleviation. Therefore, Band-Aid maintenance is essential. It is common to have to trim and/or rewrap the exterior Band-Aid of each mouse at least once during the 7-day course. Checking the mice and rewrapping/trimming the wraps every two days is essential. However adequate Band-Aid tightness and positioning during the initial placement (Step 45) is the first step to ensuring long-term Band-Aid integrity. If the Band-Aid wraps are too loose, they will be easily chewed up and removed; if they are too tight, the mouse may suffocate or starve. If the Band-Aids are fully overlapping, the Tegaderm may be removed by the mouse; if the Band-Aids are too far apart, they will not provide sufficient protection to the Tegaderm and gauze pad underneath. The wrapping process is an important aspect of this mouse model and should be practiced ahead of time using shaved and depilated mice.

Problem 4

Mice have difficulty ambulating.

Potential solution

This is often due to poor positioning of the Band-Aids during the infection. The Band-Aid wraps should be positioned around the thorax and cranial abdomen, not the caudal abdomen. If the bandages were placed too tightly, the mice may have trouble moving or breathing. If they are too loose, they can slip backwards, get chewed on, and/or mice can get their feet caught in them when grooming. Regardless, placing some food on the bottom of the housing cage is helpful for the mice to remain nourished.

Problem 5

No skin inflammation when expected.

Potential solution

If inflammation is expected but does not occur, this is almost certainly due to a problem with the bacteria or a problem with the bandage wraps.

If the issue lies with the bacterial quality and quantity, verify inoculum quality by practicing preparation techniques and confirming concentrations via serial plate dilutions. Consider using a fresh bacterial batch, ensuring the correct bacterial strain was used, minimizing time out of the −80°C storage freezer, and confirming virulence using in vitro assays.

The other likely cause relates to the wrapping of the bacteria-soaked gauze pad with the Tegaderm and Band-Aids. Ensure that the gauze pad is strictly centered in the middle of the Tegaderm, and the Tegaderm is well adhered to the mouse skin (not wrinkled or folded onto itself). Additionally, it will not stick to mouse fur, so ensure adequate shaving and depilating (steps 31 and 37–40, respectively). Inadequate Tegaderm and Band-Aid placements may also lead to air entering under the adhesives, thus compromising S. aureus ± CrA exposure. Appropriate controls e.g., inoculating with PBS only, should be incorporated until the technique is mastered and results are consistently as expected.

Problem 6

Skin inflammation when not expected.

Potential solution

In the event of inflammation occurring in negative control mice, skin inflammation may occur if the control reagent e.g., PBS, was contaminated or if the induction chamber or BSC was not properly disinfected prior to handling control mice.

Problem 7

Mouse does not inhale CrA (Step 59).

Potential solution

It is possible that during the intratracheal administration of CrA or other stimulus, the mouse does not inhale but will instead swallow the liquid when the mouse regains consciousness. This most commonly occurs when the mouse is not sufficiently anesthetized. If the CrA is swallowed, return the mouse to the isoflurane chamber and wait until the mouse is deeply anesthetized. This can be determined when the breathing rate slows and thorax movements occur at a rate of 1–2 per second. At this point intratracheal administration of CrA can be re-administered. This technique should be mastered with practice mice prior to starting an experiment. To confirm satisfactory intratracheal administration and successful inhalation, practice mice may be instilled with a colored liquid (e.g., ink solution or PBS with Evans blue) which should turn the lungs black or blue respectively if properly administered.33

Problem 8

Mice dying during intratracheal CrA instillation (Step 59).

Potential solution

If mice are dying during CrA intratracheal administration, it may be due to a punctured larynx or trachea if the pipette tip was pushed into the mouse’s throat. Also, ensure that the mouse tongue is not pulled out too far which could cause pain and subsequent trouble eating. The mouse may also die if too high a volume is administered intratracheally. A volume of 50 μL is well tolerated. Finally, death may be due to prolonged exposure to isoflurane or alternative anesthetic used. Using a timer to track time exposed to the anesthetic can help resolve this problem.

Problem 9

No weight loss when expected.

Potential solution

The mouse may not be inhaling the CrA. Refer to Problem #6 for troubleshooting. Keep a record of successful or failed IT administration during the IT process to see if that may account for the low weight loss recorded.

Problem 10

Lungs do not inflate with PBS or formalin.

Potential solution

If the lungs do not inflate, attempt to reposition the catheter deeper in the trachea. Confirm that the catheter is in the trachea and that you have not punctured the trachea or lungs, as this will prevent successful lavage as PBS or formalin will pool in the thoracic cavity instead. Finally, confirm that PBS or formalin are not leaking out of the catheter insertion site in the trachea, which could happen if the tracheal incision was too large or deep, or if the surgeon’s knot placed around the catheter was too loose and not properly securing the catheter. Any of these may result in PBS or formalin leaking out of the incision and not reaching the lungs. If this occurs, make a new incision closer to the lungs and reinsert the catheter and/or place an additional surgeon’s knot around the catheter to secure it.

If only one side of the lungs is inflating, the catheter has migrated down one of the mainstem bronchi and is no longer in the trachea. Retract the catheter slightly to resolve this. The outline of the catheter should be visible in the trachea to confirm the location of the catheter tip.

Problem 11

Low volume of BAL collected (step 74).

Potential solution

The inflation of the lungs with an appropriate volume of PBS for BAL collection is critical. If too much PBS is used, the lungs will over-inflate and leak PBS into the body cavity, which cannot be recovered.

Over-perfusion of the mouse may also result in fluid coming out of the nostrils, indicating that the perfusion is performed at too high a pressure and with too large of volume. When this occurs, the excess fluid comes out of the pulmonary veins, into the lungs, up the respiratory tree out and out of the nose. This is suboptimal as it prematurely flushes the contents of the respiratory tree out of the nose and thereby impacts future BAL sample content. When inflating the lungs with PBS or formalin, do so slowly and cease injection when the lungs are fully expanded. Lung capacity differs depending on age and mouse weight; however, we have found success in injecting up to 1 mL of PBS or formalin in 6 to 9-week old female mice. Also, it is critical to aspirate the BAL fluid slowly to prevent collapsing the trachea. Once the trachea has collapsed, it is very difficult to re-establish sufficient negative pressure to collect any fluid remaining in the lungs.

Problem 12

Low cell count recovered in BAL.

Potential solution

If low immune cell numbers are detected in the recovered BAL of day 13 mouse lungs, this could be due to the mouse strain being resistant to the development of lung inflammation e.g., mice lacking T cells or neutrophils.1 Alternatively, verify appropriate BAL collection procedures (Problems #9 and #10). Finally, if lung inflammation was expected, a low cell count could be due to ineffective CrA intratracheal instillation (step 59) during days 10–12 (Problem #6).

Problem 13

No lung inflammation when expected.

Potential solution

If little to no lung inflammation is visible by histology, confirm firstly that the mouse experienced expected skin inflammation on day 7 by disease score. If skin inflammation had occurred, the likely cause of the lack of lung inflammation is due to improper instillation of CrA during the intratracheal challenge. Verify that the mouse successfully inhaled the CrA by checking the recorded logs on days 10, 11 and 12, and further confirm that the mouse lost weight. If the mouse inhaled the CrA on all 3 days and lost weight, yet no lung inflammation by histology was apparent, it is likely that this particular mouse is not susceptible to cellular influx in this model of preclinical atopic march.
