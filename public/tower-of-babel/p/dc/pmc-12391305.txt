ABSTRACT
In copper oxides (cuprates) with single CuO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document} layer such as La\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{2-x}$$\end{document}Ba(Sr)\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_x$$\end{document}CuO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_4$$\end{document}, antiferromagnetism coexists with superconductivity at small doping levels x, where chemical disorders are significant. Here, we report that superconductivity occurs in a uniform and fully ordered Néel state in a single-layer cuprate La\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document}CuO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{4+\delta }$$\end{document} with a small amount of excess oxygen \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\delta = 0.015)$$\end{document} as demonstrated by the 139La nuclear quadrupole resonance measurement. A uniform oxygen distribution in the crystal is crucial for achieving microscopic phase coexistence and overcoming the miscibility gap associated with the staging instability; self-organized periodic oxygen arrangement driven by mobile oxygen atoms. This finding prompts the reconsideration of superconductivity in cuprates, highlighting that it can emerge in a robust Néel state that retains sizable magnetic moments and hosts only a small carrier density.

Introduction

High transition temperature superconductivity develops in copper oxides (cuprates) by doping conducting carriers into the Mott-insulating parent material. Unpaired electrons of Cu\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{2+}$$\end{document} ions in the non-doped material are localized due to strong electron interactions, leaving only the spin degrees of freedom active. The additional carriers injected into the Mott insulating state drive the frozen charges to itinerant, leading to a significant impact on the conducting properties1. Superconductivity, which reaches its maximum transition temperature at an optimal doping level of approximately 16%, has drawn significant attention, especially since the superconducting (SC) transition temperature \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_c$$\end{document} exceeds 130 K in Hg-based cuprates2. The SC mechanism has been explained by the Cooper pair formation through antiferromagnetic (AFM) fluctuations near the AFM criticality3. Before reaching the maximum \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_c$$\end{document}, the lightly doped cuprates exhibit puzzling electronic properties, such as pseudogap4, charge density wave5,6, and spin glass states7–10. These complex electronic states highlight the diverse physics of cuprates, but hinder emergence of superconductivity in samples with very low doping levels.

The itinerant carriers are introduced into the Mott-insulating La\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document}CuO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_4$$\end{document} via chemical substitution, disrupting the periodicity of crystalline lattice. A prototypical example is the Sr-substituted La\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{2-x}$$\end{document}Sr\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_x$$\end{document}CuO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_4$$\end{document}11, in which Sr\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{2+}$$\end{document} ions provide conducting holes to the CuO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document} layers deforming simultaneously the crystalline lattice by the mismatch in the ionic radius between Sr\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{2+}$$\end{document} and La\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{3+}$$\end{document}. At low doping level, dilute carriers move through variable range hopping12, but the structural imperfections cause frequent scattering, leading to carrier localization and a disordered ground state. Considerable efforts have been made to preserve a clean electronic state by minimizing the impact on the crystal structure due to doping. The SC state on the disorder-free flat CuO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document} layer has been revealed in the multi-layered cuprates13,14. Although structural disorder in the charge reservoir layers (CRL) causes lattice imperfection in the adjacent outer CuO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document} layers (OL), the inner CuO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document} layers (IL), shielded by OLs, remain intact and maintain the homogeneous electronic state after doping carriers through OLs. Novel electronic properties in the ILs were measured using spectroscopic experiments13. A striking result from the angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy (ARPES) measurement demonstrated the microscopic coexistence of superconductivity and antiferromagnetism in the same CuO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document} layer. Specifically, the SC gap was found on the Fermi surface folded by the periodic AFM spin structure15,16. A pioneering Cu-NMR study estimated the carrier concentrations from the Knight shift measurement and concluded that the SC ground state is already introduced in a sample with only 4% of doped carriers when disorders are eliminated14. NMR study also detected the coexisting SC and AFM states in the multi-layered cuprates, although the Cu-NMR signals from ILs and OLs were not clearly resolved in the AFM state17. These findings manifest that superconductivity appears in the AFM state at small doping, posting a question on the pairing mechanism of high-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_c$$\end{document} superconductivity.

This intriguing coexistence of phases appearing at doping levels smaller than 8% has only been observed in multi-layered cuprates, where the homogeneous ILs are isolated from the CRL14. To capture the dawn of high-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_c$$\end{document} superconductivity and investigate the noticeable SC properties at low carrier concentrations, a homogeneous CuO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document} layer in a single-layer cuprate is needed. In this study, we focus on a very lightly oxygen doped La\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document}CuO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{4+\delta }$$\end{document}, in which excess oxygen is distributed in the LaO layers and donates carriers to the CuO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document} layer with minimal structural impact (Fig. 1a). At higher concentrations, excess oxygen forms a periodic density modulation along the c direction, disrupting the homogeneous electronic state. The modulated structure, defined by the number of layers per period n, is referred to as stage n18. The staging instability restricts oxygen concentrations to integer values of n, obscuring much of the phase diagram due to the miscibility gap. (Fig. 1b) The fractional oxygen concentrations are obtained as the mixture of different stages, which creates fractal structure at micrometer length scale19,20.
