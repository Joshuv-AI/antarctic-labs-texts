Mothers’ fears and concerns about subsequent pregnancy after raising a child with autism: a qualitative study
Authors: Mahla Salarfard, Talat Khadivzadeh, Javad Moghri, Kobra Mirzakhani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn Iran, as globally, autism spectrum disorder (ASD) imposes a significant burden on families, with extensive local research documenting high levels of stress, stigma, and reduced quality of life among mothers as primary caregivers. Building on this established context of caregiver impact, this study explores how these experiences shape a specific consequential decision: the consideration of another pregnancy. This qualitative study aims to explore the dimensions and context of the specific fears and concerns of mothers of children with autism regarding a subsequent pregnancy, to guide the development of targeted psychosocial support.MethodsA qualitative content analysis was conducted using in-depth, semi-structured interviews with 27 participants selected through purposive maximum variation sampling. Data were collected between September 2024 and October 2025 from selected autism-focused schools and rehabilitation centers in Birjand and Mashhad, Iran, and analyzed inductively following Graneheim and Lundman’s (2004) approach. Transcripts were coded and managed systematically with the aid of MAXQDA 2020.ResultsA qualitative content analysis delineated 10 distinct subcategories of fears within two overarching categories. The first, individual dreads and apprehensions (8 subcategories), included fears and concerns of autism recurrence, physical harm to a newborn or the mother, neglecting either child, pregnancy difficulty while mothering an autistic child, having an autistic daughter, worry about the next healthy child’s marriage and family formation, the fear of intensified pressures and long-term worries about sibling relationships (concern and fear of lack of acceptance of one child by the other). The second, social dreads (2 subcategories), centered on the fear of social blame and judgment from others for considering another pregnancy. Collectively, these fears often dampened the desire for further childbearing and resulted in the decision to forgo having more children.ConclusionThis study showed that mothers of autistic children have various fears and concerns when thinking about another pregnancy. These fears often prevent further childbearing and may even cause mothers to decide not to have more children. The study’s findings highlight the need for holistic and compassionate support programs that also address psychological, practical, and social issues.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-026-05190-1.

Introduction

Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) is one of the most prevalent neurodevelopmental disorders defined by lifelong and pervasive impairments in social interaction and communication, alongside restricted and repetitive patterns of behavior, interests, or activities [1–4]. The cause of autism is still unknown; however, extensive research indicates that its development is attributed to a complex combination of characterized genetic and non-genetic risk factors, including environmental, immunological, perinatal, neuroanatomical, and biochemical elements [5, 6]. Data from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) show a substantial rise in ASD prevalence over the past two decades, from 1 in 150 8-year-olds in 2000 to 1 in 44 in 2018 [7, 8]. The most recent 2020 data indicates a further increase to 1 in 36, reflecting a pronounced sex difference with 4% of boys and 1% of girls identified with ASD [9]. Considered one of the most incapacitating neurodevelopmental disorders, ASD imposes a substantial economic burden on society [10].

Beyond these macro-level statistics, the daily reality of raising a child with ASD creates profound and cascading challenges for families [1]. It often forces parents to reduce their work hours, resulting in direct financial loss. Simultaneously, families face immense lifetime costs for care and therapies. The unremitting nature of caregiving imposes a heavy psychological burden on caregivers, leading to a marked decline in their mental health and quality of life. As the primary caregivers, mothers face unique challenges in raising a child with ASD. Compared to caregivers and mothers of typically developing children or those with other conditions like Down syndrome, mothers of children with ASD report higher levels of anxiety, depression, stress, social isolation, and a lower quality of life [11–15].

This global picture of caregiver burden is particularly salient in the Iranian context. A growing body of Iranian research has quantitatively documented the multifaceted challenges faced by families, particularly mothers, of children with ASD. Studies conducted in various Iranian cities have consistently demonstrated that Iranian mothers of children with Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) experience significantly high levels of psychological distress, including anxiety, depression, and stress. Beyond psychological distress, the literature highlights the multifaceted nature of this hardship, including a diminished quality of life, social stigma, marital strain, and severe economic pressure [16–27]. While the outcomes of caregiving are well-described, how significant decisions such as subsequent childbearing are shaped within the context of this intense caregiving experience remains unexplored.

The first author (a PhD candidate in midwifery) had a clinical experience at the health center where she worked that was about subsequent childbearing in a mother of a child with autism, which served as the origin of the present research. During her practice, she encountered a mother of a child with autism who had an unintended pregnancy. In repeated visits during early pregnancy, the mother expressed intense fear about having another child with autism and explicitly asked the midwife-researcher for ways to terminate the pregnancy. At that time, the researcher had no prior knowledge of autism spectrum disorder. The mother’s profound fear and insistence on termination sparked the researcher’s curiosity to learn more about autism. As she reviewed the literature, she came across a few quantitative studies showing that having a child with autism spectrum disorder influences parents’ future reproductive decisions, and some of these studies have demonstrated fertility cessation (reproductive stoppage) among mothers of children with autism [28, 29]. In one such study, in a single sentence, was briefly mentioned that “parental concerns” might be one possible reason for this cessation [28]. However, that quantitative study had not provided any further explanation regarding what these concerns are and what worries these parents have about subsequent childbearing. The plural form “concerns” in that quantitative study, together with the researcher’s clinical experience with the mother of a child with autism, intrigued the researcher: What specific concerns and fears make subsequent childbearing undesirable for these mothers and lead them to avoid pregnancy?. Thus, the researcher decided to conduct a qualitative study to explore the dimensions of these fears and worries of mothers regarding subsequent childbearing — fears and worries that might influence their decisions about further childbearing. Such reproductive decisions and the fears underlying them are not formed in a vacuum but are deeply embedded within and shaped by the broader cultural, social, and religious context in which these mothers live.

Reproductive decisions are universally influenced by cultural and religious contexts [30]. In Iran, several distinctive characteristics make this exploration particularly significant. Iranian society is profoundly “child-loving,” with children holding a central place in family life [31]. Simultaneously, as an Islamic country, religious beliefs strongly shape reproductive attitudes and desired family size [32–34]. These cultural and religious particularities suggest that the fears and worries of Iranian mothers of children with ASD regarding subsequent pregnancies may have distinct features that require exploration within this specific context.

To explore these unexplored experiences within this specific cultural milieu, a qualitative inquiry is necessary. Accordingly, this qualitative study was conducted with the following aims:

To explore the specific dimensions and nature of fears and concerns experienced by mothers of children with autism regarding a subsequent pregnancy.

To explore the specific dimensions and nature of fears and concerns experienced by mothers of children with autism regarding a subsequent pregnancy.

To investigate the contextual factors that shape these reproductive concerns within the Iranian setting.

Method

Study design

This study employs a qualitative conventional content analysis to explore mothers’ experiences in the context of decision-making regarding subsequent pregnancies, with a focus on identifying fears and concerns that influence this process.

Sampling strategies and participant recruitment

Eligible participants were identified and enrolled between September 2024 and October 2025 from autism educational-rehabilitation centers. Data were collected from autism rehabilitation centers and schools in two Iranian provinces—specifically, from the Tavan Afza autism rehabilitation center and the Fereshtegan autism School in Birjand, and from the Afra and Armaghan Nour centers, along with autism schools in Mashhad. The study incorporated the multi-perspective views of mothers of autistic children, their family members, and healthcare providers to ensure a comprehensive understanding of the decision-making context.

This study employed a purposive sampling strategy with maximum variation. To gain a comprehensive understanding of the phenomenon and ensure demographic diversity, participants were selected from a wide spectrum of key characteristics, including socioeconomic status, education level, occupation, autism severity in the child, and time since diagnosis.

To recruit participants for the study, the researcher first secured ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of Mashhad University of Medical Sciences, along with the necessary administrative permits from the university. Subsequently, formal letters of introduction were obtained from the Welfare Organization and the General Department of Education in both Khorasan Razavi and South Khorasan provinces.

Following authorization from these organizations, the researcher visited autism educational-rehabilitation centers in person. During these visits, the researcher presented the official documentation, detailed the study’s objectives, and methodology to the center managers, and formally requested their assistance. The centers were asked to help in identifying and connecting the researcher to mothers who met the eligibility criteria for the study.

The researcher visited the autism centers and schools daily during morning or afternoon shifts to meet with parents who were dropping off or picking up their children. After building rapport and gaining their trust, the study’s purpose and procedures were explained, and the researcher obtained contact information from those who agreed to participate. Subsequently, the researcher contacted the potential participants by phone to arrange a suitable time and place for the interview, as preferred by the mother. Interviews were then conducted in person at various agreed-upon locations, such as the autism centers and schools, the mother’s home or workplace.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The primary participants in this study were mothers who had at least one child diagnosed with autism spectrum disorder, were of reproductive age (15–49 years) and biologically fertile, and had actively experienced the decision-making process regarding a subsequent pregnancy (i.e., had already initiated or completed their decision-making process regarding further childbearing, either decided to have another child or decided not to pursue further pregnancies). To enrich the data and obtain complementary perspectives on mothers’ reproductive decision-making, a small number of close family members and healthcare providers who had been directly involved in this process were also interviewed. These healthcare providers included staff from autism educational and rehabilitation centers, gynecologists, midwives, reproductive health specialists, geneticists, psychologists, psychiatrists, and pediatric neurologists. Their interviews were conducted to provide complementary perspectives and contextualize the mothers’ experiences, while the mothers remained the primary source of data for the study. This multi-perspective approach was not merely a methodological choice but a cultural necessity, given the collectivist nature of Iranian society. In this context, reproductive decisions are rarely made by mothers alone and are often significantly influenced by extended family members as well as by healthcare providers.

Data collection

To gain a nuanced understanding of the fears and concerns surrounding subsequent pregnancy decisions among mothers of children with autism, qualitative data were gathered via semi-structured, in-depth, face-to-face interviews with 27 participants. The participant cohort was specifically composed of 15 mothers, 5 family members, and 7 healthcare service providers, ensuring a multifaceted view of the phenomenon under investigation. The first, second, fifth, eighth, tenth and thirteenth participants were interviewed twice, while the sixth participant was interviewed three times. Finally, data collection was concluded with 35 interviews involving 27 participants.

Given the central role of the researcher as the primary instrument in qualitative data collection, all interviews were carried out by the first author. This approach ensured consistency in the probing and follow-up questioning throughout the study.

Prior to the main data collection, the semi-structured interview guide was pilot-tested to enhance its clarity, relevance, and appropriateness for the target population. This pilot phase consisted of three interviews—one with a mother of an autistic child, one with a family member, and one with a healthcare provider—allowing for initial adjustments to the questioning approach.

The dialogue began by exploring the decision-making journey as a whole, using open-ended prompts like: “I would like to hear about your journey in deciding whether or not to have another child after your experience with your autistic child”, could you tell me about that journey?. Following this initial narrative, more focused questions were posed to explore specific aspects of their decision-making process, particularly the internal conflicts and emotional or practical challenges associated with the prospect of another pregnancy, allowing for the emergence of fears, concerns, and other relevant themes from participants’ narratives. Key questions included: “What factors, circumstances, or thoughts made this decision particularly difficult or challenging for you?“, “What were the biggest dilemmas or internal conflicts you faced while making this decision?“, “When you thought about another pregnancy, what fears, concerns or even nightmares came to your mind?“, “Could you elaborate on what these fears or concerns were mainly about?“, " Could you tell me what factors, experiences, or situations have shaped these fears and concerns or played a role in their development?” and “What do you think has caused these worries to take root in your mind over the years?”. Throughout the interviews, probing questions such as “Could you elaborate further?”, “What did you mean by that phrase/sentence?”, “Do you mean that…?”, “Could you give me an example of this?”, and “Could you explain the meaning of that sentence more precisely?” were used to elicit deeper insights and clarify participants’ narratives. To ensure completeness, each interview ended by inviting participants to share any remaining thoughts or points they wished to add. Each interview lasted between 45 and 106 minutes. The semi-structured interview guide was developed specifically for this study by the research team (The complete interview guide in English is provided in the supplementary file).

To maintain a focus on participants’ authentic perspectives and to mitigate potential interviewer bias, the interviewer systematically recorded and reflected on their own positionality throughout the study to bracket pre-existing views. Furthermore, to cross-verify interpretations and strengthen validity, all interview transcripts and the emergent coding framework were independently reviewed and discussed with a co-author, ensuring that the analysis remained grounded in the participants’ narratives rather than individual preconceptions.

Sampling and interviews continued until saturation was reached, meaning that subsequent interviews yielded no substantively new insights or thematic dimensions relevant to the research questions. This point was determined after reviewing the collected data through iterative discussion and consensus between the two authors.

Data analysis

Qualitative data were analyzed using qualitative content analysis, following the framework proposed by Graneheim and Lundman (2004) [35].

Right after completing each interview, the first author (MS) personally transcribed the audio recordings word-for-word and repeatedly reviewed the material, thereby achieving deep immersion in the participants’ accounts and perspectives. The transcripts were systematically managed and coded using MAXQDA 2020 software. An inductive coding strategy was applied, whereby significant statements and phrases were openly coded to allow themes to emerge directly from the participants’ accounts, free from preconceived classifications.

Through an iterative process of constant comparison, initial codes with shared characteristics were consolidated into distinct subcategories. These subcategories were subsequently synthesized into broader, more abstract categories that encapsulated the core of the participants’ experiences. This analytical journey culminated in the distillation of overarching categories that vividly illuminate the psychosocial dimensions of mothers’ fears and worries when facing another pregnancy after having an autistic child.

The procedure meticulously adhered to the five fundamental steps of content analysis [35]:

Rigor and trustworthiness

To ensure the rigor and trustworthiness of the qualitative data in the present study, we adhered to the five criteria proposed by Guba and Lincoln: credibility, dependability, confirmability, transferability, and authenticity [36, 37].

Credibility

Credibility refers to confidence in the truth of the data and their interpretation [36, 38]. To enhance credibility, the researcher returned the coded transcripts to the participants to check for agreement on the codes and their appropriateness, making corrections where necessary. Additional strategies included allocating sufficient time for data collection, conducting in-depth interviews, prolonged engagement with participants, and repeatedly reading the interview texts and notes. Furthermore, the coding process was a collaborative effort involving both the MS and KM researchers, which allowed for cross-checking and negotiation of codes and categories. In addition, peer debriefing was conducted by providing the interview transcripts, codes, and emergent categories to the supervisors and advisors to review and comment on the accuracy of the data analysis process. Data triangulation was also employed by including diverse groups such as mothers and fathers of children with autism, extended family members and healthcare providers across multiple data collection sites.

Dependability

Dependability refers to the stability of data over time and under varying conditions [36, 38]. To address this, an external auditor was used to review the research process and examine instances of both similarity and difference in interpretation. For this purpose, all research steps, from beginning to end, were described in extensive detail to enable the external auditor to conduct a thorough audit trail.

Confirmability

Confirmability pertains to the objectivity of the findings, meaning the potential for agreement among independent individuals on the accuracy, relevance, and meaning of the data [36, 38]. To achieve this, a detailed report of the research process and the procedures undertaken at each stage was provided. Furthermore, transcripts of several interviews along with the emerged codes and categories, were made available so that other researchers not involved in the study could assess the confirmability of the data.

Transferability

Transferability is comparable to generalizability and concerns the extent to which the qualitative findings can be applied to other groups [36, 38]. To facilitate transferability, the researcher provided a thorough description of the participants’ demographic characteristics, the research context and setting, as well as the participants’ experiences and direct quotations. This allowed readers to evaluate the applicability of the results to other contexts. Additionally, the research findings were presented to individuals who did not participate in the study to gather their opinions on the similarity between the results and their own experiences.

Authenticity

Authenticity refers to the fair and faithful presentation of all facts and findings, capturing the emotional tone and essence of the participants’ lived experiences [37, 38]. A clear description of the findings and emergent themes was provided so that the voices of mothers of children with autism spectrum disorder regarding their decision-making process about childbearing were understood by the reader as they were truly lived.

Categories and subcategories emerging from the data

Individual dreads and apprehensions

This category captures the internal emotional landscape of personal fears, worries, and anxieties that these mothers navigate when considering another pregnancy. These fears and concerns are rooted in daily lived experiences of caring for a child with autism and its consequences; future projections; mothers' beliefs, convictions and expriences; inhibiting social perspectives and discourses; limited or absent social support; adverse financial conditions; and social and cultural context, which often act as powerful and defining barriers. The category is comprised of eight distinct yet interconnected sub-categories:

Fear and worry about autism recurrence (or another disorder)

Fear and concern of autism recurrence emerged as a pervasive challenge, perceived by all but one participant as a significant barrier in their reproductive decision-making, a point that was consistently reiterated and reinforced during interviews with close family members and service providers. This apprehension was rooted in or amplified through multiple sources: the lived experience of caring for a child with autism and its consequences; the mothers’ personal convictions and beliefs; social perspectives and discourses from family and community beliefs about recurrence risk; and direct counsel from healthcare providers advising against further pregnancy. Furthermore, firsthand encounters—such as witnessing another family’s experience with a next autistic child or hearing similar accounts from therapists, teachers, or peers—served to deepen and heighten their dread of recurrence in a future child. For instance:

“I’m so scared, so much. my second son cries for a sister, saying, ‘Mom, bring a girl, we don’t have a sister. but I’m afraid that since this one [current child] has turned out this way, well, it’s a big risk. I say maybe the next one could be like this too. Also, we went to a doctor and he said, ‘That’s enough, don’t have any more, because there’s a risk it might happen again” (37-year-old mother of an autistic child-P2).

“When I speak with these mothers, the conversation often turns to having more children. Without exception, every single one expresses the same fear and worry: what if the next child also has autism? That fear is constantly reinforced by what they see and hear around them. They might interact with a family at the center who has two children with autism, or hear from a teacher, trainer or another parent that someone’s second child has been diagnosed—or is suspected of having—autism (Healthcare provider- P7)”.

Fear of harm from the autistic child to the mother or to the infant

A profound and immediate fear that tangibly shadowed the mothers’ reproductive decisions was the fear of potential physical harm to a newborn or infant by their existing autistic child. This acute dread, stemming from direct personal experiences and observed challenging behaviors, presented itself as a concrete and terrifying barrier to expanding their families. The fear was rooted in the mothers’ daily realities, where they witnessed their children’s difficulties with impulse control, sensory dysregulation, or unpredictable actions and aggressive behaviors, leading them to vividly imagine catastrophic scenarios for a vulnerable new sibling. Beyond the fears for a future newborn, a significant number of mothers described a more immediate physical threat directed at their own bodies during subsequent childbearing. The unpredictable and aggressive behaviors of their autistic children created a profound terror of sustaining injury while carrying another child and during the postpartum recovery period. This fear, for some, was a traumatic memory from a previous pregnancy, and for others, a vivid projection based on daily experiences of being inadvertently harmed.

“One of our biggest fears was that we were afraid if we had a small child of our own, these kids might hurt him, push him, or crush him. That’s why I said, ‘No, let’s not have another child.’ We were worried and scared that God forbid they might poke their fingers in his eyes, or if he was lying down, they might suddenly jump on him. Because I had seen them jumping on our relatives’ kids.” (36-year-old mother of autistic twins-P5).

" I love children dearly. But my fear stems from my son-who has autism-, I’m terrified that if I have another child, he might harm that next child too. Because, for example, even now with his younger sister “A”, who has grown up a lot, he still constantly hits her. he hits her head really hard, or torments her relentlessly. Little “A” has endured so much beating since she was a child that, God only knows… It’s truly been by God’s grace that “A” is still here. Otherwise, with the beatings my son gives her, my child wouldn’t have survived until now.” (29-year-old mother of an autistic child-P10).

“Another thing I’m scared of is that, God forbid, with his aggression and hitting, he might accidentally strike my belly during a pregnancy and harm the unborn baby—because he doesn’t understand. during my last pregnancy, when my son was three years old and I was expecting my next child, he kicked my stomach so much, threw things at my belly, and hit it so often… I really didn’t think our baby would be born healthy.” (30-year-old mother of an autistic child-P8).

Concern about neglecting either child (the autistic or the next one)

Some mothers grappled with a profound ethical and emotional dilemma centered on the concern of caregiving failure, concerned that they would inevitably neglect one child in favor of the other. This concern operated in two directions. On one hand, mothers worried that the immense, around-the-clock demands of a newborn would inevitably force them to neglect the specialized and intensive needs of their existing autistic child. On the other hand, another significant concern was the potential neglect of a future child due to the overwhelming demands of caring for their existing autistic child. The mothers perceived their capacity as a single, exhaustible resource: allocating it to a new infant meant directly depriving their vulnerable previous child, and conversely, dedicating it to the autistic child meant failing the next one.

" I tell myself, even if I somehow get through those nine months, after that, for example, that baby is born. That baby needs care, you have to breastfeed it and look after it, so who do I entrust my son’s care to? Who will handle his needs? There’s no one. I’m worried that with the arrival of a new baby, my autistic child will be overlooked and I will neglect him.” (34-year-old mother of an autistic child-P1).

“You know, another thing that makes me not consider having another child is the worry that, due to the demanding caregiving conditions for my autistic child, I’ll fall short with that next child—I won’t be able to attend to him/her properly or dedicate time to him/her. I am only one person, and my abilities are limited, especially since this already happened with my daughter, and she truly ended up sidelined because of her brother’s condition.” (40 year-old mother of an autistic child-P3).

Concern about pregnancy difficulty while mothering an autistic child

One salient theme identified was the profound worry regarding the physical management of pregnancy alongside constant caregiving responsibilities for a child with autism. This concern was often magnified for mothers who were the primary or sole caregivers, lacked a strong support system, and who had a difficult and challenging previous pregnancy experience.

“I had a very difficult pregnancy. One thing that really worries me is how those nine months of pregnancy are going to end, considering “P’s” condition, where he needs constant 24/7 care. He, for example, now goes to the bathroom — you have to go and wash him. You have to give him his food, and you have to pay attention to him in every way: his toileting, his messes, his occupational therapy, his speech therapy…” (40 year-old mother of an autistic child-P3).

“Getting pregnant when you already have a child like this is really hard, very hard. Especially since I handle everything on my own by myself. The hardest part for me, which is one of the biggest obstacles, is how I’ll be able to run after this child for 9 months with that big belly and take care of all his needs.” (29-year-old mother of an autistic child-P10).

Fear of giving birth to a girl with autism

An additional, salient theme that emerged was the gendered dimension of future pregnancy fears. Participants voiced a particular dread of having an autistic daughter, citing concerns about puberty, vulnerability to abuse, and perceived higher caregiving burdens, which shaped their reproductive decisions.

“All my children are boys, and I truly wish I had a daughter. If my current son were healthy, I might have considered having another child, hoping it would be a girl. But then I think, if it were a girl and she was sick or had autism, it would be even more difficult. Life presents many more challenges for girls – the fear of someone harming them, their menstruation… it’s a greater burden.” (37-year-old mother of an autistic child-P2).

“I see mothers who have autistic girls… I mean, it’s really tough with a girl. A girl gets her period, and the risk of her being harmed or abused is higher. I know one mother who had an autistic daughter and decided to stop at just that one child because of these issues. She used to say, ‘This one autistic daughter is more than I can handle; what if I have another child and it’s another autistic girl?” (28-year-old mother of an autistic child-P12).

Worry about the next healthy child’s marriage and family formation

Some mothers expressed profound anxieties about the long-term social implications for the future of their next healthy children. A significant, culturally rooted concern was the potential stigma affecting marriage prospects and the ability to form their own family, highlighting how the presence of an autistic sibling could extend its shadow over the entire family’s social future.

One thing that often occupies my mind and worries me is this: Let’s say we go ahead and I have another child, and he/she is healthy. One day, that child will want to get married. How much difficulty will they face in finding a marriage partner because they have an autistic brother? Just like we see in movies, when there’s a child with special needs in a family, it’s as if potential suitors are somehow afraid to marry into a family that has autism, intellectual disability, or Down syndrome.” (31-year-old mother of an autistic child-P3).

Fear of intensified pressures following subsequent childbearing

The overwhelming and multi-dimensional burden of caregiving—physical, emotional, psychological, and financial—presented a fundamental barrier to family expansion. Mothers explicitly feared that adding another child would exceed their finite personal capacity on all fronts, leading to a complete breakdown of their well-being and family stability.

Low-income families in particular feared that having another child would further intensify their financial burden, as they would have to divide already scarce resources between an autistic child’s costly therapies (occupational therapy, speech therapy, and medications), the costs of her/his care, and the basic needs of a new baby. On the other hand, mothers who bear the caregiving burden alone and face little or no family support grapple with a different challenge. For them, adding a new child means dividing their already depleted personal energy between an autistic child with exhausting needs and a newborn requiring round-the-clock care. This impossible equation creates a fear of unbearable physical and psychological pressure, and ultimately, complete burnout.

“There might be a mother who genuinely wishes to have another child, but the fear of increased costs and financial pressure prevents her from taking action, especially in families with lower incomes. For instance, in a family where the father is a laborer, how much can he possibly earn to manage both the overwhelming expenses of his autistic child and the costs of another child?” (a family member- P14).

“Honestly, our financial situation is really not good at all, and we’re under a lot of pressure. I’m afraid that if we have another child, we’ll face severe financial strain and get into trouble. As it is, we’re barely keeping up with the expenses of these two children. My son, who is healthy, thankfully doesn’t cost too much, but my daughter with autism—her occupational therapy, speech therapy, diapers, her medications… it’s truly tough for us. Mostly because of these financial reasons, we’re not even thinking about having more children for now.” (33 year-old mother of an autistic child-P3).

Concern and fear of lack of acceptance of one child by the other

Among the concerns raised in participants’ discussions was the worry of mutual non-acceptance and familial estrangement. Parents were concerned both that the healthy child might not accept their autistic sibling, and conversely, that the autistic child might not accept a new sibling.

One mother articulated her anxieties about long-term sibling relationships, stating, “My concern is about the future — whether the children will get along, or if the healthy child will ever truly accept her/his autistic sibling. I worry that he/she won’t accept him and won’t support him when they’re older or that the autistic child won’t accept the next child and will constantly harm him/her.” (36-year-old mother of an autistic child-P5).

One of the mothers I spoke with a while ago at school said, “My younger daughter tells us not to bring her older sister, who has autism, with us when we go out or to parties”. That’s why I’ve also started worrying: what if I have another child and he/she doesn’t accept their autistic brother either? (31-year-old mother of an autistic child-P12).

Social dreads

This category encompasses fears that originate from the perceived or anticipated reactions, judgments, blame, stigma, and pressures of the broader social environment. These are about the blame, judgement and social condemnation mothers anticipate facing from their extended family, acquaintances, and society at large for their reproductive decisions. The two subcategories of this main category are fear of social blame for having another child and fear of being judged by others for having another child.

Fear of social blame for having another child

Another significant deterrent to childbearing was the fear of direct and explicit social blame. Mothers were scared that those around them would openly condemn their decision to have another child. They feared being blamed, and that having another child would not only be viewed as a personal choice but as a culpable error, for which they would be held morally responsible. This fear of being explicitly blamed is captured in the words of Participant 1:

“You know, another thing that makes me not want to have another child is the fear of being blamed by those around me and hearing them say, ‘This child has problems, and you shouldn’t have had another one. You made a mistake.” (34-year-old mother of an autistic child-P1).

Fear of being judged by others for having another child

Participants expressed a deep fear of being judged for their reproductive choices, especially if a subsequent child also had a disability. They feared that others would judge their decision as unwise and wrong. For instance:

“If I have next child and the child has problems, what will others say? Were you blind? For instance, your first child turned out this way, what made you have another? Couldn’t you control yourself? You went and had a second child? Didn’t you suffer enough with the first one? How thick-skinned are you to go through it again…” (30-year-old mother of an autistic child-P8).

Discussion

This qualitative study provides an in-depth exploration of the multifaceted fears and worries that shadow the reproductive decision-making of mothers who have a child with ASD. By foregrounding the voices of mothers, alongside perspectives from family members and healthcare providers, the analysis reveals that the decision to pursue another pregnancy is not merely a personal choice but a profoundly complex psychosocial dilemma. The identification of two overarching categories—“Individual dreads and apprehensions” and “Social dreads”—along with their detailed subcategories, provides a nuanced framework for understanding why many of these mothers ultimately forgo expanding their families. These fears are concrete, lived barriers that often lead mothers to forgo further childbearing, fundamentally altering their life trajectories and family planning. These findings illuminate the heavy psychosocial shadow that autism casts over family planning.

Individual dreads and apprehensions

The most salient and pervasive fear articulated by nearly all participants was that of autism recurrence in a subsequent child. While the exact etiology of ASD remains complex and multifactorial, for these mothers, the lived experience condensed this uncertainty into a terrifying probability—“it happening again.” This fear and concern was shaped or reinforced by several factors: the mothers' own personal convictions and beliefs; the overwhelming lived experience of caring for an autistic child and its consequences; the opinions and views of family and those around them; direct advice from healthcare providers, which was often alarmist or discouraging; and firsthand encounters in the autism community, such as witnessing another family’s experience with a next autistic child or hearing similar accounts from therapists, teachers, or peers.

These findings are strongly rooted in the specific contextual realities of Iranian mothers. A robust body of Iranian research has thoroughly documented the profoundly difficult and exhausting caregiving experience of these mothers, the multiple pressures arising from it, and their caregiving isolation or the limited support from family and relatives in caring for the child with ASD [39–42]. Our findings reveal that this very lived hardship, as documented in Iranian studies, is precisely what gives root to the fear of recurrence. However, other factors also contribute to the formation of this fear, as discussed below.

Our findings also reveal that reproductive decision-making was not merely an individual decision but a deeply participatory process. Participants explicitly said that they consulted extensively with relatives and friends, and that these consultations—together with broader social discourses (healthcare providers and peers)—actively shaped or intensified their fear of recurrence and influenced mothers’ childbearing decisions. It reflects a culturally grounded process: mothers actively seek and weigh collective wisdom, and in doing so, the prevailing social discourses about the risks of having another child with ASD become internalized as their personal truth.

This finding is consistent with a body of Iranian research demonstrating that the attitudes of relatives, friends, and even healthcare providers significantly influence reproductive decisions. For instance, Shakrinejad et al. found that the opinions of relatives and extended family members act as a driving force pushing couples toward or away from childbearing [43]. Similarly, Akbari and Shoa Kazemi reported that in qualitative studies of Iranian mothers, negative attitudes of others toward childbearing and the comments of the husband’s family about the number of children, along with discouraging comments from physicians, were identified as major barriers to childbearing [44]. Furthermore, Makvandi et al. highlighted that negative opinions of relatives toward childbearing, social attitudes surrounding childbearing, and spousal opposition all play critical roles in fertility decisions. As Makvandi et al. note, evidence shows that childbearing is influenced both by individual choice and by values, culture, and social norms [45]. Collectively, these Iranian studies support our finding that the fear of recurrence is not shaped solely by personal experience but is also reinforced by the attitudes and discourses of the social network surrounding the mother and consequently overshadows their childbearing decisions.

In addition, the nature of current knowledge regarding ASD, its inherent limitations (diagnostic, preventive and therapeutic) and the unknown and complex etiology of the condition, can play a central role in shaping or intensifying this fear. Despite advances in genetic science, the field remains unable to provide definitive answers to mothers that transcend probabilistic estimates. This limitation, rooted in the current state of scientific progress, means that even when mothers seek information, they are met with uncertainty rather than certainty.

The dominant theme of "fear and worry about autism recurrence" about autism recurrence resonates strongly with the literature on other genetic, perceived-genetic, and chronic childhood disorders, where the perceived risk of recurrence becomes a central, paralyzing factor in family planning and reproductive decision-making [46–50]. This is corroborated by qualitative research across diverse diagnostic contexts. Studies of parents facing undiagnosed developmental delays highlight how the profound “future uncertainty” and lack of a recurrence risk figure dominate reproductive choices, often leading to a decision to forego further biological children [50]. Similarly, research on families after a diagnosis of Inherited Metabolic Disorders via newborn screening identifies “personal evaluation of recurrence risk” and “perceptions of the disease” as core, fluctuating factors shaped heavily by interactions with healthcare providers [47]. Furthermore, cross-cultural studies on families of children with Down syndrome confirm that reproductive decision-making becomes markedly more complex after the birth of a child with a genetic condition, involving a multitude of familial and ethical considerations [49]. Collectively, these studies underscore that beyond statistical risk, it is the subjective perception of risk—forged by personal experience, familial narratives, and the quality of professional guidance—that fundamentally shapes reproductive pathways and parental well-being.

Beyond recurrence, the study uncovered unique, tangible fears directly stemming from the behavioral phenotypes of ASD: fear of physical harm. The fears about the autistic child harming a newborn, or injuring the mother during pregnancy, arise directly from daily lived experiences with challenging behaviors, aggression, and lack of danger awareness. This finding adds a critical and under-explored dimension to the literature. While the immense caregiving burden on mothers of children with ASD is well-documented [39, 51, 52], these tangible fears- the specific fear for the safety of a future child and the mother’s own physical well-being during pregnancy and after childbirth- are seldom highlighted in the literature on reproductive decision-making but emerge here as critical, practical barriers that overshadow desires for family expansion.

The issue of caregiving justice and anticipated neglect presents a profound ethical dilemma for mothers of children with autism. The fear of neglecting either the existing autistic child or a future child—or creating an unfair dynamic between them—reflects a finite perception of maternal resources. Mothers perceive their time, energy, and emotional capacity as severely limited and already fully allocated. This concern becomes even more acute among mothers who lack spousal involvement or broader familial support and who feel alone in their caregiving circle. For mothers of children with ASD, this is compounded by the child’s intensive, lifelong need for specialized care, turning the thought of another pregnancy into a potential act of injustice toward both children. This dilemma is grounded in a well-established empirical reality characterized by three interrelated factors: first, the universally high levels of caregiver burden documented among parents of children with disabilities [53]; second, the critical role of social support as a key modifier of this burden, where its absence significantly increases psychological distress while its presence acts as a potent protective factor [53, 54]; and third, the mechanism by which robust social support buffers maternal well-being, often by moderating the relationship between caregiving stressors and negative outcomes such as depression. Furthermore, the efficacy of social support is often mediated or amplified by internal psychological resources like resilience and optimism [55, 56]. Consequently, the lack of a supportive network not only exacerbates the objective burden of care but also depletes the very psychological reserves necessary to provide equitable care for more than one child.

Research findings indicate that receiving support—both formal and informal—is among the key factors influencing childbearing decisions [57, 58]. In line with this, the narratives of participants in the present study confirmed this notion, as inadequate formal and informal supports influenced childbearing decisions by contributing to the formation or intensification of the fear of neglecting the autistic child. Participants stated that formal governmental supports—such as financial aid and specialized centers—do exist, yet they described them as inadequate. Even more telling is the state of informal support. Spouses, extended family, and relatives either provide no support at all, or if they do, it is inadequate—leaving mothers to shoulder the entire burden of care alone. Mothers thus perceive their individual capacities as extremely limited due to insufficient support systems, leading them to fear that another pregnancy would inevitably result in neglect of either their existing autistic child or the future child. This finding is consistent with the results of other studies in Iran; there, too, mothers of children with autism spoke of the same lack of adequate support, saying that the support that does exist “is not enough to meet their needs [42, 59–61].

The gendered dimension of fear—specifically, the dread of having an autistic daughter—adds a culturally nuanced layer to these reproductive anxieties. Concerns about puberty, vulnerability to abuse, and increased caregiving burden for girls with ASD reflect how social norms and gendered expectations intensify maternal worries. For instance, a qualitative study by Cridland et al. investigating the experiences of adolescent girls with Autism Spectrum Disorders and their mothers highlighted precisely these gendered challenges. The study identified sex-specific puberty issues, sexual vulnerabilities, and unique difficulties in socializing with neurotypically developing peers as core concerns of adolescence. Furthermore, it emphasized the prolonged, ‘hands-on’ role of parents—a caregiving burden that often falls disproportionately on mothers and extends well into the teenage years [62]. Thus, the fear of raising an autistic daughter is a reflection of the practical and intensified challenges these families navigate, challenges that are profoundly shaped or exacerbated by prevailing gender and social norms.

Finally, long-term existential worries about the impact on a future healthy child’s social prospects (e.g., marriage and family formation), together with concerns and fears that one child may not accept the other, reveal how the shadow of autism extends beyond immediate caregiving into the anticipated family relationships, cohesion, and the family’s broader social future.

Social dreads

Beyond the internal ethical calculus, mothers’ reproductive deliberations were profoundly constrained by a pervasive fear of the external social gaze. This fear manifested in two distinct yet interconnected layers: the anticipation of harsh judgment and, more powerfully, the dread of direct moral blame. Participants articulated a terror of being explicitly condemned for their reproductive desires, encapsulated in an anticipated accusation that frames the birth of a child with disability not as a chance occurrence but as a culpable parental error. The fear extended to being perceived as irresponsible or even selfish, suggesting that choosing to have another child despite existing caregiving burdens is viewed not as an act of hope or resilience, but as a moral failing in foresight or self-control.

This experience of being subject to moral scrutiny over “risky” reproduction is not unique to our participants. Studies on social blame have long shown how mothers’ reproductive choices become public property, often forcing women into silence about stigmatized desires [63]. What makes this dynamic particularly palpable in the Iranian context—and what lends weight to our findings—is a well-established body of local research on stigma, social blame and Judgment. Studies conducted in Iran have consistently documented that families of children with disabilities live under social stigma. Mothers of autistic children, in particular, face blame from relatives and strangers, verbal harassment, and public stares—ultimately resorting to social withdrawal [18, 64]. In other words, the external gaze our participants fear is a documented reality.

However, in the context of autism—a condition with complex, non-deterministic etiology—this blame is particularly paralyzing. It places the onus of a biologically complex outcome squarely on the mother’s shoulders, transforming a personal dilemma into a public verdict on her character and judgment. Consequently, this external pressure does not merely add to maternal stress; it actively silences maternal desire and enforces reproductive isolation, as mothers preemptively internalize societal condemnation and opt out of the conversation altogether, lest their “legitimate thoughts and desires [be] met with disbelief and scorn” .Thus, social fear operates as a potent mechanism of reproductive control, where the anticipation of blame and Judgment becomes a decisive barrier to expanding the family.

Strengths

This study possesses several notable strengths that enhance the credibility and depth of its findings. Primarily, the multi-perspective, qualitative design allowed for a rich, holistic triangulation of data that enriches the understanding of the complex psychosocial phenomenon. By gathering insights not only from mothers but also from key family members and a diverse range of healthcare providers, the study captured the decision-making ecosystem from multiple angles. Furthermore, purposeful sampling with maximum variation—which ensured the inclusion of mothers from different socioeconomic backgrounds, with children across the autism spectrum, and with varying family structures—strengthens the transferability of the core themes to similar settings.

Limitations

While the study provides valuable insights, certain limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. the study is limited by its focus on mothers in two Iranian provinces, which may affect transferability to other cultural or healthcare contexts. Comparative studies across cultures could elucidate how social norms and support systems modulate these reproductive dilemmas.

The conceptual foundation of this study arose from two sources: a clinical experience of one of the authors and insights from prior literature. Accordingly, the research focused on exploring mothers’ fears and concerns regarding subsequent pregnancy decision-making. Although interviews were initiated with broad, open-ended questions and analysis followed an inductive approach, the study’s thematic focus on concerns and fears may have influenced the scope of narratives elicited, potentially limiting the prominence of other dimensions of reproductive decision-making such as hope, resilience or positive expectations.

In addition, all participants had actively experienced the process of deciding about a subsequent pregnancy, enabling exploration of the fears and concerns arising during this period. However, reproductive decision-making is multifactorial and dynamic. Although raising a child with ASD was frequently described as influential, other factors—such as maternal age, marital relationships and economic conditions—may also have shaped these decisions. Therefore, the findings should not be interpreted as indicating that ASD alone determines reproductive choices, but rather as illuminating specific fears within a broader decision-making context.

Conclusion

This study revealed the significant fears and concerns about subsequent childbearing among mothers of children with autism, which influence their decisions about having another child. While raising a child with ASD emerged as a central influence in participants’ narratives, reproductive choices were complex and shaped by intersecting personal, relational, and sociocultural considerations.

The findings underscore the need for consulting and support frameworks. Interventions should incorporate psychosocial support, practical resources, and stigma reduction strategies, thereby fostering informed reproductive decision-making.

From a clinical and policy perspective, several urgent needs emerge

Tailored psychosocial genetic counseling (addressing “fear of recurrence” & limitations of genetic knowledge in Iran)

Genetic counseling in Iran currently focuses mostly on probabilistic statistics of recurrence rates. However, our findings show that the uncertainty surrounding the causes of autism actually intensifies mothers’ fear. Counseling must therefore shift from being purely informational to psychosocial—helping mothers with emotional preparation, coping with ambiguity, and realistic planning. Since some healthcare providers in Iran unintentionally reinforce this fear with alarmist or discouraging remarks, training genetic counselors and specialists in supportive communication is essential.

Strengthening formal and informal support systems (addressing “fear of neglect & overload”)

The fear of neglect reflects a rational calculation by mothers who perceive their limited time and energy as insufficient to care adequately for both an existing child with ASD and a potential newborn. From the perspective of mothers raising a child with autism in Iran, both formal support and informal support are inadequate. Accordingly, several practical supports are suggested. First, expanding the number of specialized care centers for children with ASD is recommended. Second, increasing government financial support for these families is proposed to reduce economic pressure. Third, establishing regular and reliable respite care services is suggested to provide mothers with periodic relief from continuous caregiving. Fourth, training sessions on empathy and collaborative caregiving could be offered within autism care centers, special schools, and through media channels to teach family members how to share the caregiving burden more effectively alongside the mother. Collectively, these measures would directly address the perceived resource scarcity that underlies the fear of neglect and demonstrate to mothers that equitable caregiving is feasible.

Anti-stigma and peer support programs (addressing “social gaze and moral blame”)

Mothers of children with autism fear that others will judge or blame them for considering another pregnancy—that they will be told, “You made a mistake.” And this fear is rooted in a social reality; Iranian research has shown that families of children with disabilities routinely face intense social stigma, including public stares and verbal blame. The following measures could help reduce these pressures and facilitate decision-making for these mothers: First, public education that directly confronts the belief that ‘having a child with autism is the parents’ fault. Second, peer support groups of mothers who have navigated the same reproductive decision—to break the isolation and normalize the experience.
