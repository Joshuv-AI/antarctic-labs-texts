lcUMAPtSNE: use of non-linear dimensionality reduction techniques with genotype likelihoods
Authors: Kerem Uzel, Christine Grossen, F. Gözde Çilingir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUnderstanding population structure is essential for conservation genetics, as it provides insights into population connectivity and supports the development of targeted strategies to preserve genetic diversity and adaptability. T-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) and uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) have proven effective for revealing population genetic structures in human and model organisms using hard-called genotypes, but their application in wild species using genotype likelihoods from low coverage sequencing (as a cost-saving measure) remains underexplored.ResultsHere, we present a Jupyter Notebook-based workflow that facilitates the use of UMAP and t-SNE on genotype likelihood-derived principal components. This workflow is demonstrated using medium to low-coverage whole-genome sequencing data from scimitar-horned oryx, which has been reintroduced into the wild and faces multiple conservation challenges.ConclusionDetailed guidance on hyperparameter tuning and practical implementation is also provided, enhancing the application of these methods in wildlife genetics to potentially support biodiversity conservation. lcUMAPtSNE is designed as a complementary and exploratory dimensionality reduction tool, rather than a replacement or benchmarking framework, and its strengths are demonstrated here using empirical data.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12898-2.

Background

Population genetics seeks to understand genetic relationships within and between populations, informing strategies to preserve biodiversity and adaptability. Key applications include estimating population size, inferring demographic history, identifying population structure, and identifying loci that contribute to adaptive capacity or uncovering the genetic basis of reduced fitness in populations, all critical for effective conservation management, particularly for endangered species [1, 2].

The advent of next-generation sequencing has revolutionized population genetics by enabling genome-wide analyses, even for non-model species with low-coverage sequencing data. Low-coverage sequencing, combined with genotype-likelihood-based methods, provides a cost-effective approach to studying population structure and genetic diversity [3] while addressing uncertainties inherent in low-depth data [4–6].

Dimensionality reduction techniques like principal component analysis (PCA) are widely used to visualize population structure. PCA simplifies genomic data by capturing major variance components, making it a foundational tool in genomics [7]. However, PCA’s linear assumptions may overlook non-linear genetic patterns [8], prompting the development of alternative techniques like t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) [9] and uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) [10]. These non-linear methods can better capture complex relationships, especially when applied after PCA, to reduce computational demands and enhance interpretability [11, 12]. Subsequently, top PCs are used as the input for further dimensionality reduction using t-SNE or UMAP (referred to as PCA-t-SNE and PCA-UMAP, respectively) [9, 13]. In scenarios where genotype data is unavailable, such as in low-coverage whole-genome sequencing, PCs computed based on genotype likelihoods can be effectively incorporated into t-SNE and UMAP analyses. This adaptability further underscores the versatility of these dimensionality reduction techniques in various genomic data contexts.

​​Here, we introduce a Jupyter Notebook-based workflow, lcUMAPtSNE, that leverages genotype likelihood-based principal components for further dimensionality reduction using t-SNE and UMAP. This tool is demonstrated with medium to low-coverage whole-genome sequencing data from scimitar-horned oryx, a species with critical conservation needs. The workflow includes detailed guidance on parameter optimization and implementation, enabling ecologists and conservationists to apply these non-linear dimensionality reduction techniques in wildlife genetics effectively.

Implementation

Description of the workflow

Our Jupyter Notebook [14] based workflow uses Python v3.11.4 [15] and a set of Python libraries to perform t-SNE and UMAP analyses. The workflow begins by decomposing the covariance matrix output of PCAngsd, which incorporates genotype likelihoods and accounts for missing data while reflecting the genetic variance structure of the samples [16]. The covariance matrix is decomposed using NumPy (v1.24.4) [17] to extract eigenvectors and eigenvalues. Then, the principal components are computed by projecting the covariance matrix onto its eigenvectors, which are sorted in descending order of their eigenvalues. Since establishing a set of minimum and maximum number of available PCs was suggested [13], our workflow incorporates a step for the guidance on the selection of principal components (PCs) for the input dataset that is performed by the ‘KneeLocator’ function from the ‘kneed’ Python package (v0.8.5) [18], which identifies the optimal number of PCs using a polynomial fit method to smooth the explained variance curve. This technique identifies the ‘elbow point’ by smoothing the PCs’ explained variance curve with polynomial interpolation and selecting the point where the rate of increase significantly diminishes (Figure S1). Subsequently, user-defined principal components serve as input for dimensionality reduction via PCA-t-SNE and PCA-UMAP, utilizing the scikit-learn (v1.3.0) [19] and umap-learn (v0.5.3) [10, 20] libraries, respectively. For clarity, we refer from here on to the techniques of PCA-t-SNE and PCA-UMAP as t-SNE and UMAP, respectively.

​​Hyperparameter optimization is a critical step in our workflow, conducted through a systematic grid search to fine-tune the settings for both t-SNE and UMAP. For t-SNE, we adjust the perplexity (perp), which influences the number of effective neighbors and is commonly explored within the range of 5 to 50 [9]. For UMAP, we modify two parameters: the number of neighbors (NN, default 15) and the minimum distance (MD, default 0.1) [10, 20]. Because perp and NN both define the neighborhood scale used to construct the embedding, they should be chosen with sample size in mind rather than treated as fixed constants [9, 10, 21]. In practice, smaller datasets are generally explored using smaller to intermediate values, whereas larger datasets may support larger neighborhood sizes; correspondingly, smaller values emphasize local structure, and larger values emphasize broader global structure. The MD parameter controls the compactness of the embedding, with lower values producing tighter clusters and higher values yielding more diffuse point separation [10].

Finally, all visuals of dimensionality reduction analyses are plotted with the seaborn library (v0.11.2) [22]. To ensure accessibility in visual data representation, we used the color palette iwanthue v2.0.0 [23].

To aid practical application, we provide several Jupyter Notebooks enabling users to input their covariance matrix, compute principal components, receive guidance on the minimum number of PCs to retain, and apply t-SNE and UMAP techniques using various pre-determined hyperparameters to explore their effects on the dimensionality reduction outcomes. These notebooks were crafted to manage single or multiple input files (here genotype likelihood files) simultaneously, streamlining the analysis of diverse datasets with varied parameter configurations, thereby enhancing efficiency in handling extensive genomic datasets. Additionally, we provide a step-by-step guideline for users to run lcUMAPtSNE Jupyter Notebooks and explore their own datasets (see Availability and Requirements section below).

Example application: scimitar-horned oryx

The scimitar-horned oryx (Oryx dammah) is a large antelope that once roamed widely across North Africa [24]. However, the iconic animals experienced a precipitous decline in the 20th century due to drought, hunting, and land-use competition [25], leading to their extinction in the wild [26]. Before their disappearance, captive breeding began with fewer than 100 oryxes from Chad, expanding the ex-situ global population to about 15,000 individuals [27]. While 1,000 are in coordinated breeding programs, many reside in places with minimal genetic management [28]. Due to the success of reintroduction programs in Chad, the species was recently downlisted to Endangered by the IUCN Red List [29]. To understand the genetic consequences of different conservation management strategies, Humble et al. [28] sequenced the whole genomes of 49 oryxes from populations with varying genetic management levels, notably from EAZA Ex Situ Programmes (EEP, n = 8), USA (n = 17), and two unmanaged collections in the United Arab Emirates (EAD A, n = 9 & EAD B, n = 15). In their analysis, Humble et al. [28] combined model- and non-model-based population structure methods with genome-wide measures of inbreeding (runs of homozygosity) and genetic load (mutation load). They concluded that these four populations are genetically distinct and with varying degrees of relatedness shaped by their shared bottleneck and subsequent management histories. In particular, their PCA (PC1 vs. PC2) showed that EAD_B was most distinct, EAD_A was separated from the two managed populations, and the managed EEP and USA populations were distinguishable along an additional principal component (PC3).

Whole-genome sequencing data analyses

We obtained the raw whole-genome re-sequencing data of 46 genetically unrelated scimitar-horned oryx individuals from [28] (NCBI BioProject PRJEB37295), which were also utilized as the final dataset in the PCA analysis presented in the referenced study [28] (Table S1). Subsequent data processing was performed using the ATLAS Pipeline v7 [30–32], involving the Gaia, Rhea, and Perses workflows. In Gaia, individual sequencing data underwent quality trimming using TrimGalore v0.6.6 [33] with default settings and were aligned to the Oryx dammah reference genome, SCBI_Odam_1.1 [34] (NCBI RefSeq GCF_014754425.2) employing BWA-MEM v0.7.17 [35] in paired-end mode. Within the Rhea workflow, local re-alignment was conducted using GATK v3.8 [36] (Table S1). Subsequently, the Perses workflow facilitated the merging of forward and reverse reads into single reads per fragment using the tool ATLAS v0.9 [30], avoiding pseudo-duplicated bases where original reads overlapped.

We implemented downsampling with ATLAS [30] (task=downsample) to standardize the coverage across individuals, setting high-coverage samples to an average coverage of 6x while preserving the original coverage levels for lower-coverage individuals in accordance with the methodology outlined in the cited study. We designated the primary dataset as “SO_6x”. In this set, we maintained the 20 samples with coverage below the 6x average and downsampled the remaining 26 to the target of ~6x (Table S1). For subsequent analyses to explore the effect of lower sequencing coverage, we created “SO_2x” and “SO_0.5x”, where we downsampled all samples to about 2x and 0.5x, respectively.

Genotype likelihood estimation and PCA

We estimated genotype likelihoods using ANGSD v0.940 [4] based on the three distinct datasets described above, each characterized by varying levels of average coverage (6x, 2x, 0.5x). Following the methodology of [28], we employed ANGSD [4] using the GATK model (-GL 2) to infer major and minor alleles (-doMajorMinor 1, -doMaf 1). We restricted this analysis to the 28 chromosome-length autosomes (Table S1) and included only sites with Phred quality and mapping scores exceeding 30. We utilized properly paired (-only_proper_pairs 1) and unique reads (-uniqueOnly 1) while retaining only biallelic sites (-skipTrialleleic 1). Sites with read coverage in less than 60% of the samples were excluded (-minInd 30, minimum representation among samples > 60%), and we retained only polymorphic sites with a p-value less than 1e-6 (-SNP_pval 1e-6). We applied thinning for the variant sites, ensuring a minimum distance of 1 Kb between two sites with a custom bash script. Lastly, we performed the principal component analysis with PCAngsd v1.11 [16] by using default settings where the minor allele frequency cut-off was 5%.

t-SNE and UMAP applications using lcUMAPtSNE workflow

We tested various combinations of perp, NN, and MD values across three datasets. For both t-SNE and UMAP, we explored low, intermediate, and high neighborhood sizes relative to the total number of individuals by using perp values of 5, 10, and 23 for t-SNE and NN values of 5, 10, and 23 for UMAP, with the upper value extending to approximately half the sample size as a broad-structure sensitivity check. For UMAP, we additionally evaluated MD values of 0.01, 0.1 (the default), and 0.5 to assess the effect of cluster compactness. These parameters were combined with a set of top PCs determined as follows: for each dataset, we used the elbow method to identify the minimum number of PCs to retain, and also included the maximum number of PCs available. For SO_6x, this corresponded to 6 PCs (minimum) and 46 PCs (maximum; Figure S1A). For SO_2x and SO_0.5x, the elbow method suggested 5 PCs (minimum), and the maximum number was also 46 PCs (Figures S1B & C).

Results and discussion

After quality filtering, an average of 99% of the raw sequencing data from 46 scimitar-horned oryx individuals was retained, and following mapping and deduplication, approximately 81% (ranging from 77% to 87%) of these high-quality reads aligned with the reference genome. The individual-level sequencing coverage varied from around 4.6× to 20.6× (Table S1). Based on different coverage thresholds, three datasets were generated: the SO_6x dataset (coverages between 4.6× and 6×) yielded 1,415,641 variant sites with a minor allele frequency greater than 0.05 and a minimum inter-variant distance of 1 Kb, SO_2x produced 1,516,230 variant sites under the same criteria, and SO_0.5x generated 23,129 variant sites. The PCA conducted on each dataset consistently revealed the same population structure that has been reported previously in Humble et al. [28]. Specifically, the first principal component (PC1) clearly separated the genetically unmanaged population EAD_B from the others, PC2 distinguished the remaining unmanaged population EAD_A from the genetically managed populations (EEP and USA), and PC3 further separated the managed EEP group (Figs. 1A-C, Figures S2A-C). The cumulative variance explained by PC1 and PC2 was 21.9% for the SO_6x dataset, 20.3% for SO_2x, and 19.2% for SO_0.5x, thereby confirming that the underlying genetic structure was robust to variations in sequencing coverage.

The minimum (N = 5) and maximum number of PCs (N = 46) incorporated in the t-SNE and UMAP analyses of SO_6x, SO_2x, and SO_0.5x corresponded to 35.3 and 99.8%, 26.6 and 99.3%, 24.0 and 98.8% cumulative variance explained, respectively (Figure S1). For clarity in the main figures, we retained the intermediate settings (perp = 10, NN = 10, MD = 0.1) coupled with the top PCs obtained with the elbow method unless stated otherwise, as these captured the overall trends observed across the hyperparameter space we explored. See the Supplementary Figures S3-8 for a comprehensive overview of the parameter space we explored.

The t-SNE and UMAP analyses of all three datasets of varying coverages, using the intermediate settings, confirmed four groups representing the four sampled populations (Figs. 2 and 3A and B; Figures S3-8). This pattern is consistent with the population structure reported by Humble et al. [28] and with our PCA results (Figs. 1 and 2; Figure S2), in which EAD_B is most distinct along PC1, EAD_A separates from the managed populations along PC2, and the two managed populations (EEP and USA) are further differentiated along PC3. All three datasets successfully differentiated the two genetically managed groups within a single two-dimensional projection when using t-SNE or UMAP (Figures S3–6). In PCA, this same distinction was also visible, but required examining additional principal components beyond the first two (e.g., PC3; Figure S2). Thus, in this example, the non-linear methods did not change the biological interpretation relative to Humble et al. [28], but rather provided a complementary two-dimensional visualization that integrates structure otherwise distributed across multiple PCs.

For all datasets, as anticipated, altering parameters affected the embeddings (see Supplementary Figures S3-S6). The cohesiveness of the clusters improved with combinations of local parameters. Lowering the perp, NN, or MD values led to clustering at a finer scale, while increasing these values helped to visualize more global patterns, suggesting higher genetic similarity between (and leading to clustering of) one unmanaged population (EAD_B) and the two managed populations than each of these with the other genetically unmanaged population (EAD_A) (Figures S3–6). At 6x and 2x, the increase in the number of PCs did not influence the clustering patterns obtained using either technique (Figures S4 & S6).

For SO_0.5x, both t-SNE and UMAP also separated the two genetically managed populations (Figs. 3A & B; Figures S7 & S8). However, t-SNE analyses provided poorer local structure when compared to UMAP (Figs. 3A & C). Notably, when we increased the number of PCs, the clustering efficacy of UMAP was reduced as opposed to the trends observed with SO_6x and SO_2x (Figs. 3C & D; Figure S8), likely due to the added uncertainty of genotype likelihoods with 0.5x [16]. This further supports the view that these techniques are complementary to PCA rather than replacements.

UMAP and t-SNE have been effectively applied to analyze population genetic structure across various species, including plants, invertebrates, and humans [13, 37–45]. Despite their proven utility, these non-linear dimensionality reduction methods are underutilized in conservation genomics, particularly with genotype likelihoods from low-coverage sequencing—a gap that presents a significant opportunity for advancing research in this field.

We introduce a Jupyter Notebook-based workflow, lcUMAPtSNE, which allows users to apply PCA-initialized UMAP and t-SNE to genotype likelihoods. Demonstrated with the Scimitar-horned oryx dataset across three coverage levels (6x, 2x, 0.5x), this workflow effectively discerns populations and provides enhanced resolution beyond PC3, summarized in a single visualization.

While default parameters like perplexity (perp), number of neighbors (NN), and minimum distance (MD) work well in large human datasets [13, 46], smaller sample sizes in conservation genomics require a customized approach. Our lcUMAPtSNE workflow facilitates this through a grid search, allowing users to explore a small set of low, intermediate, and high perp/NN values relative to the number of individuals in the dataset. As a practical rule-of-thumb, we caution against interpreting very small neighborhood settings in isolation, as these may accentuate noise or fragment structure [21]. Instead, we recommend assessing whether the principal patterns are consistent across a small range of settings and, when they are, using the intermediate setting as a representative visualization, while varying MD to assess how strongly cluster compactness influences the embedding. Importantly, using the elbow method to determine the optimal number of PCs avoids incorporating excess noise, optimizing the analysis for different data characteristics. With the provided online step-by-step guidelines, users can explore their own datasets.

Limitations

An important caveat to note is the necessity of maintaining focus on the biological relevance of clustering results while cautioning against the potential for ‘over-separation’ of genetic groups. Techniques such as UMAP and t-SNE try to find a lower-dimensional representation that preserves the distances between the points in the neighborhood; in other words, they are prone to highlighting similarities while exaggerating differences, which may lead to an ‘over-separation’ of genetically similar individuals. For this reason, embeddings should be interpreted as exploratory summaries, and features that appear only under a single hyperparameter setting should be treated cautiously, as overly local settings may fragment structure or accentuate noise [21]. Accordingly, it is essential to consider the biological relevance of the observed clustering patterns. Also, when population structure is detected, a thorough evaluation of the influences of genetic drift versus local adaptation is crucial for deriving appropriate conservation strategies. Additionally, although PCA initialization may provide insights into the global structure of clusters, the interpretation of global structure with these non-linear techniques remains highly sensitive to parameter adjustments, rendering distances between groups not directly interpretable. Finally, the inherent limitations of PCA, a linear dimensionality reduction method, when used in conjunction with non-linear methods, may sometimes exacerbate data distortion [47]. An alternative approach may involve directly employing a distance matrix based on genotype likelihoods rather than relying on PCA. The Jupyter notebook that we provided is designed for flexible adaptation to incorporate such methodologies, enhancing the analysis of population genetic structure.

Conclusions

In our study, we presented lcUMAPtSNE, an easy-to-implement tool with which users can supplement their PCAs with non-linear dimensionality reduction techniques even for lower coverage datasets, by utilizing genotype likelihoods. In some cases, these techniques can integrate information from multiple PCs into a single visualization, potentially improving interpretability. Additionally, non-linear dimensionality reduction methods might in some contexts accentuate structure driven by rare variants, which are often underrepresented in PCA [48], offering the possibility of detecting more recent or fine-scale differentiation when coverage is not extremely low to allow for rare variant detection. However, PCA remains a robust baseline across coverage levels, and we present t-SNE and UMAP as complementary tools rather than replacements. It is crucial to recognize that both linear and non-linear approaches to dimensionality reduction can result in data distortion, each presenting unique benefits and challenges. As such, careful handling of data and biologically relevant interpretation of the results are imperative.
