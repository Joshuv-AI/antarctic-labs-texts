Improving compliance with curative radiotherapy for cervical cancer in a high-volume centre in India: a bundled workflow (AID-CARE) quality improvement project
Authors: Sreenivasa Rao Bonala, Bala Venkata Subramanian, Cherwin Venati, Henita Selvamary, Naveen Bhargav Talatoti, Gongada Sindhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTimely completion of curative chemoradiotherapy within 56 days is a key determinant of oncological outcomes in cervical cancer; however, achieving this target remains challenging in high-volume public-sector settings in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). At a tertiary public-sector cancer centre in South India, routine clinical observations identified poor compliance with recommended radiotherapy timelines, with delays and treatment defaults occurring at multiple points along the care pathway. In response, a quality improvement project was undertaken to improve treatment timeliness and completion.A retrospective baseline audit confirmed substantial delays and high default rates among patients receiving curative-intent external beam radiotherapy, followed by intracavitary brachytherapy. Root cause analysis identified system-, staff- and patient-related factors contributing to poor compliance. Based on these findings, a bundled workflow intervention (AID-CARE) was developed, incorporating early brachytherapy assessment, planned interdigitation, accelerated schedules for delayed cases, structured patient counselling, staff education and prospective patient tracking. The intervention was implemented using sequential Plan–Do–Study–Act cycles, followed by a prospective re-audit and sustainment phase. The primary outcomes were compliance with an overall treatment time of ≤56 days and treatment default rates assessed over time using run charts.At baseline, only 18.6% of patients completed treatment within 56 days, whereas 46.2% defaulted and 35.2% experienced prolonged treatment duration. Following implementation, compliance with the recommended treatment timeline improved to 70.1%, with a marked reduction in treatment defaults to 6.2%. These improvements were maintained during the sustainment phase. No increase in acute treatment-related toxicity was noted.This quality improvement initiative demonstrates that a structured, workflow-driven and resource-conscious approach can substantially and sustainably improve compliance with recommended radiotherapy timelines for cervical cancer in high-volume public-sector settings, with potential applicability to similar centres in LMICs.Keywords: Compliance, Radiotherapy, Quality improvement, Interruptions

Problem

Our institute is an autonomous tertiary public-sector hospital in South India with dedicated oncology services, delivering radiotherapy to approximately 1100 patients annually, of whom nearly 30% are treated for cervical cancer. Most patients receive care through government-funded schemes and belong to economically disadvantaged backgrounds.

Despite delivery of guideline-concordant definitive chemoradiotherapy, a substantial proportion of patients did not complete treatment within the recommended overall treatment time (OTT) of 56 days. This gap was initially identified through routine clinical observation and departmental discussions, with delays and defaults occurring at multiple points along the care pathway.

These challenges became particularly evident during the post-COVID recovery phase, which exposed vulnerabilities in workflow coordination and treatment continuity. Failure to complete treatment within recommended timelines poses a significant risk of compromised oncological outcomes. In response, we initiated a structured quality-improvement (QI) project under the institutional quality framework to improve treatment timeliness and completion.

Background

Cervical cancer remains one of the most common malignancies among women in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), including India, where most patients present with locally advanced disease.1 The standard treatment is definitive concurrent chemoradiotherapy, comprising external beam radiotherapy (EBRT) delivered over 5–5.5 weeks with concurrent chemotherapy, followed by brachytherapy delivered in 3–5 fractions.2

National and international guidelines recommend completion of the treatment sequence within 56 days, as prolongation of OTT is associated with inferior local control and survival, with each additional day of delay estimated to reduce tumour control by 1%–1.5%.3,6 Despite clear recommendations, audits from India and other LMICs consistently report substantial delays and incomplete treatment in routine practice.7,10 These gaps are attributed to interacting patient-, staff- and health-system-level factors that vary across settings.

Although individual clinical strategies such as interdigitation of EBRT and brachytherapy or accelerated brachytherapy schedules can reduce treatment time, sustained improvement is unlikely with isolated measures alone.11 12 Evidence from high-income settings suggests that context-specific workflow redesign, supported by staff engagement and patient counselling, can reduce treatment delays13; however, such QI approaches remain under-evaluated in LMICs.

Large audits from high-volume centres and international expert reviews highlight that delays and defaults in radiotherapy treatment timelines are multifactorial and require coordinated, multi-component, organisation-level strategies rather than isolated measures,14 15 providing the rationale for this context-specific QI initiative.

Measurement

Population and setting

The study population comprised all consecutive patients with cervical cancer treated with curative-intent chemoradiotherapy followed by intracavitary brachytherapy (ICBT) at our centre. Patients receiving palliative radiotherapy, postoperative radiotherapy or those referred externally for interstitial brachytherapy were excluded from the study.

This QI project was conducted in the Department of Radiation Oncology at Sri Venkateswara Institute of Medical Sciences (SVIMS), Tirupati, India, as part of departmental quality assurance activities aligned with the National Accreditation Board for Hospitals (NABH) framework. The QI team included consultant radiation oncologists, resident doctors, medical physicists, radiotherapy technologists, dedicated brachytherapy nurses and administrative and operational staff.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome indicator was compliance with curative radiotherapy timelines, defined as the completion of planned EBRT followed by ICBT within 56 days.

The two key secondary outcome indicators were OTT and treatment default. OTT was defined as the number of days from the first fraction of EBRT to the final fraction of brachytherapy and was analysed both as a continuous variable (days) and as a categorical variable (≤56 vs >56 days). Treatment default was defined as failure to complete the planned course of EBRT and/or brachytherapy in the absence of documented clinical or medical indications for treatment discontinuation, such as disease progression, severe treatment-related toxicity, intercurrent illness or death.

These measures were selected because they directly reflect treatment timeliness and completion, are strongly associated with oncological outcomes in cervical cancer, and are explicitly recommended in the national and international guidelines.

Baseline measurement

Baseline measurements were obtained through a retrospective audit of patients treated between January and December 2021, following approval by the institutional NABH quality framework. Resident doctors from the QI team independently extracted data from medical records, radiotherapy treatment charts and brachytherapy logs using a predefined data collection template. Any discrepancies were resolved by a consultant radiation oncologist from the QI team.

Process and balancing measures

Process indicators measured during the intervention phase were aligned with individual Plan–Do–Study–Act (PDSA) cycles and included early brachytherapy assessment, use of planned interdigitation and decompensation schedules, delivery of structured counselling at predefined milestones, staff participation in education sessions and early toxicity escalation, and completeness of documentation and reminder calls for scheduled brachytherapy appointments. Acute treatment-related toxicity requiring unplanned treatment interruption was monitored as a balancing measure.

Data collection and analysis

Data collection and analysis were conducted separately for the baseline (pre-audit), prospective implementation and sustainment phases of the study. During the baseline phase, data were retrospectively extracted, as described earlier. During the implementation and sustainment phases, resident doctors prospectively recorded all predefined outcomes and process indicators. Entries were reviewed weekly by a designated QI team member and validated by a consultant radiation oncologist to ensure their completeness and accuracy. All measures were reviewed fortnightly during implementation and discussed at departmental QI meetings.

Baseline and re-audit outcome indicators were compared using χ² tests for categorical variables and Mann-Whitney U tests for continuous variables, with absolute differences and 95% CIs calculated. Run charts were the primary method used to assess changes over time during the prospective implementation and sustainment phases of the project.

Design

The retrospective audit findings were presented during departmental meetings attended by all stakeholders involved in the cervical cancer radiotherapy care. A baseline process map covering the cervical cancer radiotherapy pathway—from registration to completion of EBRT and brachytherapy—was developed to identify workflow bottlenecks contributing to treatment delay.

Root cause analysis was performed using a fishbone diagram (figure 1), which identified patient-, staff-, system- and policy-related contributors to poor compliance. These findings informed the development of a driver diagram (figure 2) linking the project aim.

Based on this analysis, a bundled workflow intervention, the AID-CARE bundle, was developed. The acronym was chosen to reflect two complementary domains: ‘AID’ representing policy- and system-level actions that support timely radiotherapy and ‘CARE’ representing people-focused responsibilities shared by patients and staff to maintain continuity of treatment.

The bundle comprises six integrated components:

A—Assessment (policy/system): early brachytherapy assessment to avoid delays at the EBRT–ICBT transition.

I—Interdigitation (policy/system): planned scheduling of ICBT during EBRT where feasible.

D—Decompensation (policy/system): accelerated brachytherapy schedules (two applications per week) for patients presenting late or with delayed first ICBT.

C—Counselling (people): structured, milestone-based counselling for patients to reinforce treatment timelines and adherence.

A—Awareness (people): staff education on OTT targets, workflow expectations and early toxicity recognition.

RE—Register (people): prospective patient flow and treatment timeline tracking using a departmental OTT Tracking Register, with reminder calls for missed or delayed brachytherapy appointments.

This structured, dual-domain bundle was designed to address the multifactorial causes of treatment delay by combining system-level workflow improvements with patient- and staff-level behavioural reinforcement. The system-level components represented workflow adaptations informed by prior clinical studies11 12 demonstrating feasibility, safety and supported by guidelines emphasising timely completion of radiotherapy.3 4 16 The people-focused components functioned as practical QI measures to strengthen coordination and treatment adherence. Importantly, the intervention represented a modification of workflow and care coordination only, without altering treatment protocols or imposing any additional financial burden on patients.

Strategy

The SMART aim of this project was to achieve at least a 50% improvement in compliance with curative radiotherapy timelines for cervical cancer—defined as the completion of EBRT and ICBT within 56 days—within 12 months of implementing a bundled, workflow-based intervention in our high-volume public-sector radiotherapy department.

To operationalise this aim, we used sequential PDSA cycles to test and refine the individual components of the AID-CARE bundle before full implementation. Each PDSA cycle was applied to consecutively treated, eligible patients with cervical cancer during the defined time period. Although the bundle comprised six interventions, they were introduced in a phased manner. Interventions from earlier cycles were retained and cumulatively implemented, reflecting a stepwise build-up of the workflow. After refinement, the complete bundle was adopted into routine practice and evaluated prospectively, followed by a final cycle dedicated to sustainability monitoring.

Classical PDSA guidance recommends small-scale pilot testing before wider implementation; however, this approach was not feasible in this context. The project was undertaken during the post-COVID recovery period, when workflow disruption and poor baseline compliance posed a risk to oncological outcomes and required prompt system-level improvement. In addition, the cervical cancer radiotherapy pathway is tightly coupled. EBRT and ICBT require coordinated scheduling to maintain continuity and minimise OTT. Therefore, small-subset piloting was impractical and inequitable. Given these contextual constraints, the AID-CARE components were implemented at the service level with progressive integration through successive PDSA cycles. Pragmatic adaptations of PDSA methodology in real-world settings have been recognised in the QI literature.13 17 18

PDSA cycle 1: workflow redesign for early brachytherapy assessment, interdigitation and decompensation (December 2022–March 2023)

Plan: A redesigned radiotherapy workflow was planned to reduce the EBRT–ICBT gap and OTT. The policy change focused on three elements: early brachytherapy assessment during EBRT with advance scheduling, planned interdigitation of ICBT with EBRT, and the use of accelerated brachytherapy schedules (decompensation) for patients at risk of treatment prolongation. The team hypothesised that these measures would minimise unnecessary waiting periods and improve timely treatment completion.

Do: Early brachytherapy assessment was implemented through a mandatory workflow checkpoint. EBRT was not continued beyond 15 fractions unless brachytherapy assessment had been documented, with enforcement by radiotherapy technologists. For patients assessed as suitable for brachytherapy during EBRT, resident doctors initiated advance administrative pre-authorisations to facilitate timely scheduling of interdigitation. For patients identified as being at risk of exceeding OTT targets, a formal decompensation protocol permitting two times per week brachytherapy fractions was implemented.

Study: Early brachytherapy assessment and interdigitation were associated with reductions in the EBRT–ICBT interval and OTT. These were feasible without an increase in acute treatment-related toxicity. Early exposure to brachytherapy also improved patient acceptance of subsequent procedures. However, interdigitation was not feasible in all patients because of anatomical constraints, tumour extent and treatment-related toxicity. In such cases, decompensation schedules were applied where clinically appropriate; however, additional delays persisted owing to machine downtime and patient travel constraints. Although workflow efficiency improved, treatment default rates were not substantially reduced during this cycle. An increase in procedural workload in the brachytherapy operating theatre was observed.

Act: The redesigned workflow was adopted as the standard operating procedure. To address the increased procedural workload, daily brachytherapy operating lists were formalised, procedural hours were extended, and resident support was reallocated. The limited impact on treatment defaults highlighted the need for additional interventions leading to subsequent PDSA cycles focused on patient counselling and behavioural factors.

PDSA cycle 2: structured patient counselling and education (April 2023–May 2023)

Plan: Despite improvements in workflow efficiency, treatment defaults persisted, particularly after EBRT. The team hypothesised that inadequate patient understanding and concerns related to brachytherapy contributed to non-compliance. This cycle aimed to improve treatment adherence through structured patient counselling.

Do: Standardised counselling was implemented at three predefined milestones: the initial outpatient consultation, EBRT initiation (CT simulation and/or first fraction) and brachytherapy assessment or the first brachytherapy fraction. Counselling focused on the importance of completing treatment within 56 days, the curative role of brachytherapy, anticipated logistics, expected treatment timelines and early reporting of treatment-related toxicities. It was delivered in the local language by resident doctors and nursing staff and reinforced during weekly on-treatment reviews.

Study: A reduction in treatment defaults related to fear of brachytherapy and post-EBRT attrition was observed in this study. Patients who received repeated counselling demonstrated improved adherence to the scheduled brachytherapy appointments. However, counselling alone did not fully prevent treatment interruptions related to acute toxicities.

Act: Structured, milestone-based counselling was embedded into routine departmental practice. The persistence of toxicity-related interruptions highlighted the need for improved staff awareness and early toxicity management, leading to the next PDSA cycle focusing on staff education and escalation pathways.

PDSA cycle 3: staff education and early toxicity recognition (June 2023–July 2023)

Plan: Variability in staff awareness of OTT targets, interdigitation protocols and early toxicity recognition was identified. The team hypothesised that focused staff education would reduce avoidable treatment interruptions.

Do: Targeted educational sessions were conducted for residents, radiotherapy technologists and nursing staff, covering the oncological importance of OTT, principles of interdigitation and decompensation, and early identification and escalation of acute toxicities. OTT targets and escalation pathways were incorporated into resident induction and reinforced during monthly academic meetings.

Study: Improved consistency was observed in scheduling practices, application of decompensation protocols and early escalation of toxicity-related concerns. However, occasional missed workflow checkpoints highlighted the need for real-time oversight mechanisms.

Act: Regular staff education was institutionalised. The need for a prospective tracking mechanism to ensure accountability and early corrective action was recognised, informing the subsequent PDSA cycle.

PDSA cycle 4: prospective treatment timeline tracking using a departmental OTT Tracking Register (August 2023)

Plan: Persistent delays related to missed appointments and the absence of real-time oversight highlighted the need for a structured tracking mechanism. The team hypothesised that prospective monitoring of treatment timelines would enable early identification of delays and facilitate timely corrective action.

Do: A prospective departmental OTT Tracking Register was introduced and maintained by resident doctors. The register captured key treatment timeline variables, including EBRT dates, brachytherapy assessment and fraction dates, interdigitation status, OTT and reasons for delay or default (online supplemental file 1). Patients missing scheduled appointments by more than 5 days were proactively contacted. Data were reviewed regularly to enable timely corrective action.

Study: The tracking system enabled early detection of emerging delays and facilitated timely rescheduling. Coordination between EBRT and brachytherapy services improved, with better slot utilisation following advance preparation and formalisation of daily operating lists.

Act: The OTT Tracking Register was embedded into routine departmental workflow and aligned with NABH quality monitoring processes. Following iterative refinement across the preceding PDSA cycles, the bundled AID-CARE workflow was formally integrated into routine practice. A prospective audit was subsequently undertaken to evaluate its impact on predefined process and outcome indicators.

PDSA cycle 5: prospective evaluation of the full AID-CARE bundle (September 2023–September 2024)

Plan: After sequential testing and refinement of individual components in PDSA cycles 1–4, the full six-component AID-CARE bundle was ready for implementation. This cycle marked the transition from sequential testing to a full-bundle implementation. The plan was to adopt the complete bundle into the routine departmental workflow and evaluate its impact prospectively under the SMART aim.

Do: The full bundle—Assessment, Interdigitation, Decompensation, Counselling, Awareness and Registration—was implemented for all eligible patients with cervical cancer beginning September 2023. Residents recorded daily data on outcome and process indicators in the brachytherapy register. Reminder calls and default-tracking processes were fully operationalised.

Study: Prospective monthly run charts showed a marked reduction in OTT, and a higher proportion of patients completed treatment within 56 days. The EBRT–ICBT intervals shortened, and the default rates declined. The operational bottlenecks were addressed through regular coordination meetings.

Act: Minor workflow refinements were made, including improved alignment of the EBRT and ICBT lists and clearer documentation prompts. The full bundle was retained as the standard practice.

PDSA cycle 6: sustainment and institutionalisation (October 2024–March 2025)

Plan: To ensure long-term sustainability, the team planned to monitor performance, reinforce workflow expectations and maintain staff engagement after the initial improvement period.

Do: October 2024 onward, the department continued to track OTT, default rates and process-measure adherence. Staff education sessions were repeated for new residents, and the reminder call system was maintained. Weekly academic meetings included a brief review of the run charts and workflow issues.

Study: Run charts demonstrated stable performance with reduced variation in OTT during the sustainment phase. No significant increase in toxicity-related interruptions or staff workloads was observed. The bundle remained feasible within the existing resources.

Act: The AID-CARE bundle was formally integrated into the routine departmental policy. A quarterly audit cycle was established to ensure continuous oversight and early drift detection.

Results

Baseline and re-audit comparison

In the baseline audit, compliance with the completion of EBRT followed by ICBT within 56 days was 18.6% (49/264). Treatment default occurred in 46.2% (122/264) of the patients, while 35.2% (93/264) completed treatment with a prolonged (OTT>56 days).

During the prospective re-audit period, compliance improved to 70.1% (124/177), representing an absolute increase of 51.5% (95% CI 43.3% to 59.7%; p<0.001). Treatment defaults reduced to 6.2% (11/177), an absolute reduction of 40.0% compared with the baseline (p<0.001). The proportion of patients who completed treatment with prolonged OTT decreased to 23.7% (42/177), corresponding to an absolute reduction of 11.5% (p=0.01).

All improvements were statistically significant, and the predefined SMART aim of achieving greater than 50% improvement in compliance was met. The interventions were well tolerated, with no increase in acute treatment-related toxicity observed. Interdigitation and decompensation strategies were temporally associated with reductions in OTT, whereas structured counselling and reminder systems were associated with reduced treatment defaults.

Sustainment phase performance

During the sustainment phase, 63 patients underwent curative-intent radiotherapy. Of these, 58 patients (92.1%) completed the treatment within 56 days. Prolonged OTT was observed in four patients (6.3%), and one patient (1.6%) defaulted despite repeated follow-up attempts.

Performance during the sustainment period remained stable, with minimal variation, confirming that the improvements achieved during implementation were maintained over time.

Process measures

Brachytherapy assessment by the 15th EBRT fraction was achieved in 87% of the patients (154/177). Planned interdigitation was implemented in 71% of the eligible patients (126/177). Accelerated decompensation schedules were used in 19% of patients (34/177) approaching or exceeding the 56-day threshold.

Structured counselling at all three predefined milestones was delivered to >90% of patients (161/177). Reminder calls for scheduled ICBT appointments were successfully completed in 93% of the instances (165/177). High adherence to this process temporally coincided with improvements in outcome indicators.

Process and outcome variation over time

Run charts (figure 3) constructed during the prospective audit and sustainment phases demonstrated sustained improvement in OTT following the implementation of the AID-CARE bundle. During the early implementation period, the monthly OTT values showed wide variations and frequently exceeded the 56-day benchmark.

Run chart showing monthly median overall treatment time (OTT) (solid red line) and individual patient OTT (blue dots) across implementation and sustainment phases. The dashed horizontal line represents the target median OTT (≤56 days), and the vertical line marks the transition between phases. A reduction in median OTT and improved consistency of individual OTT values are observed following implementation of the AID-CARE bundle.

Following sequential PDSA cycles and cumulative implementation of the AID-CARE bundle from September 2023, a clear downward shift was observed, with monthly values stabilising near the target and extreme delays becoming infrequent. This pattern persisted throughout the sustainment phase (October 2024 to March 2025), accompanied by reduced variation and consistently higher proportions of patients completing the treatment within 56 days. The observed shift and narrowing of spread meet established run-chart criteria for non-random improvement.

Contextual factors and unintended effects

Early brachytherapy assessment led to an increased procedural workload, necessitating operational adjustments, including the extension of brachytherapy operating hours, formalisation of daily operating lists and allocation of additional resident support. These adaptations improved coordination and slot utilisation without compromising patient safety.

Residual delays occurred in a small proportion of patients owing to non-modifiable factors, including treatment-related toxicity, machine downtime, long travel distances and intercurrent illness. No increase in acute treatment-related toxicity or additional patient financial burden was observed following implementation of interdigitation or accelerated brachytherapy schedules; balancing measures were prospectively monitored throughout the implementation period.

Data completeness

The data completeness exceeded 95% across all phases of the project. Missing data were minimal and primarily related to patients who defaulted and could not be contacted despite repeated attempts to reach them. Where available, the reasons for default or delay were documented through chart reviews and telephone follow-ups.

Lessons learnt and limitations

This project generated several important insights relevant to quality improvement in high-volume public-sector radiotherapy settings. First, the baseline measurements revealed a substantial and previously unrecognised gap between the perceived and actual compliance with the recommended OTT. In large tertiary centres, there is often an implicit assumption that curative workflows are functioning adequately. Our findings demonstrate that without systematic measurement, significant delays and defaults can remain hidden. Therefore, routine auditing is essential to make performance visible and to catalyse meaningful improvements.

Second, the baseline audit occurred in the post-COVID period when radiotherapy services were affected by staffing shortages, disrupted referral pathways, increased patient backlogs and altered patient behaviour. These factors likely contributed to the poor baseline compliance. Rather than serving solely as a confounder, this situation highlights the need for resilient, system-based workflows capable of maintaining treatment timeliness during service strain. The bundled intervention remained effective despite these constraints, demonstrating its ability to sustain performance under such conditions.

Third, the project underscored that improving compliance in cervical cancer radiotherapy requires addressing both technical workflow elements (such as early brachytherapy assessment, interdigitation and decompensation schedules) and behavioural factors (such as patient understanding, staff awareness and accountability). Educational interventions alone were insufficient; durable improvements were achieved only after embedding structural safeguards, such as mandatory assessment checkpoints and prospective tracking systems.

Fourth, this project also demonstrated that the provision of government-funded treatment alone did not ensure compliance; structured counselling and patient education were critical to achieving timely treatment completion.

Fifth, the AID-CARE intervention functioned as an integrated, interdependent workflow rather than as isolated components. Early brachytherapy assessment facilitated interdigitation, while decompensation strategies and prospective tracking helped maintain treatment timeliness when delays occurred. These components operate in a coordinated manner, ensuring that delays are anticipated early and managed consistently.

This approach supports application across diverse clinical settings, including lower-volume and resource-constrained centres, where individual components may be tailored to local constraints while maintaining the integrity of the overall workflow. Centres serving socioeconomically disadvantaged or low-literacy populations may require more intensive counselling, but the core bundle remains transferable.

Finally, integrating the AID-CARE intervention into routine departmental practice and aligning it with the institutional quality framework (NABH) helped to sustain improvements beyond the implementation phase. Importantly, this project reinforces that quality improvement in radiotherapy is not a one-time exercise but a continuous process requiring ongoing measurements, feedback and adaptations.

This study has several limitations. As the PDSA cycles were implemented across whole patient cohorts rather than through small pilot testing, the potential influence of temporal and contextual factors cannot be fully excluded. The project used a quasi-experimental quality improvement design with a retrospective baseline audit followed by prospective implementation, re-audit and sustainment phases without a concurrent control group, making it susceptible to secular trends and unmeasured confounders.

Despite these limitations, the magnitude of improvement, its temporal association with the intervention phases, and sustained gains over time support a meaningful intervention effect. As this was a single-centre study conducted in a high-volume public sector institution, direct generalisability may be limited; however, the bundle workflow may be applied across varied clinical contexts with appropriate local adaptation. The smaller improvement in OTT compared with the reduction in treatment defaults largely reflects the influence of non-modifiable factors such as machine downtime and patient travel constraints, highlighting the need for capacity-level or technological solutions beyond workflow redesign.

In future iterations, earlier implementation of prospective tracking and enhanced brachytherapy service planning would be prioritised. Digital dashboards could also be incorporated to optimise workflow and reduce the burden of manual tracking.

Conclusion

Timely completion of curative radiotherapy for cervical cancer remains challenging in high-volume public-sector settings. This quality improvement project demonstrates that a bundled, workflow-driven (AID-CARE) intervention can substantially improve compliance with recommended treatment timelines, reducing treatment defaults and prolonged OTT. These improvements were achieved without increasing toxicity or requiring major additional resources, supporting feasibility within routine clinical practice. Further evaluation is needed to assess long-term oncological outcomes and broader implementation across diverse care settings.
