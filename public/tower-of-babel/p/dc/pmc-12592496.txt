O-glycosylated peptides curtail the aggregation and toxicity of human islet amyloid polypeptide
Authors: Qian Zhang, Xiaohan Mei, Yan Li, Ziyang Wang, Ang Li, Dantong Liu, Sunil Kumar, Deling Kong, Andrew D Hamilton, Chunqiu Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA key pathogenetic pathway of type 2 diabetes (T2D) is pancreatic beta cell failure, losing their capability of producing insulin and regulating blood glucose. Accumulated evidence has demonstrated that the elevated aggregations of human islet amyloid polypeptide (hIAPP), consisting of soluble oligomers and insoluble fibrils, contribute to the beta cell dysfunction and loss. In this work, a focused library of O-glycosylated peptides was designed to target hIAPP monomers. An N-acetylglucosamine modified peptide, SNS, was identified as the most effective inhibitor against hIAPP fibrillation in this library. SNS treatment in beta cells and isolated mouse islets suppressed the hIAPP-induced oxidative stress, cytotoxicity and apoptosis, resulting in beta cell protection and preservation of islet function. Therefore, these results suggest that O-glycosylated strategy will pave a new way for the design of peptide-based antagonists, and shed light on the creation of novel therapeutics for amyloid-related disease.Subject terms: Glycobiology, Peptides

Introduction

Pathological accumulation of misfolded proteins is the hallmark of multiple amyloid-related diseases, with type II diabetes (T2D) being one of the most prominent example1–5. Postmortem analysis reveals that more than 90% of the pancreas in T2D patients present islet amyloid plaques, the major protein component of which is human islet amyloid polypeptide (hIAPP)6. The hIAPP, also known as amylin, is a 37-residue hormone co-secreted with insulin by pancreatic islet β-cells and normally acts as a neuroendocrine regulator to modulate glucose levels7; however, hIAPP tends to form amyloid in the pancreatic islets of Langerhans in T2D by unknow mechanism, which mediates pancreatic β-cell dysfunction and death, thereby leading to the development of T2D8. According to the International Diabetes Federation (IDF), the number of patients afflicted with T2D is approximately 500 million, which is predicted to significantly increase to 643 million by 2030, representing an enormous social and economic burden on society9. Current therapeutic approaches mainly aim to slow down the progression of T2D and are unsuccessful to reverse it10. Therefore, the rapidly rising number of diabetic patients worldwide and the lack of effective treatment highlight the critical importance of developing inhibitors to target and curtail the aggregation of hIAPP at earlier stage, much before the formation of amyloid plaques.

Over the past two decades, many techniques, including X-ray diffraction, NMR, and Cryo-EM, have been widely employed to investigate the aggregation mechanism of hIAPP11–14, which is beneficial for the design of inhibitors that have the ability to slow down hIAPP aggregation or redirect it to a nontoxic aggregating pathway. These studies commonly indicate that hIAPP is spontaneously converted to fibrils through a multistep misfolding process, in which the monomers convert into metastable β-sheet oligomers and termed to be fibrillar structures, although there are models suggested for the different formation of α-helical bundles or β-hairpin dimers as early intermediates15,16. Meanwhile, a series of studies based on structure-function relationship of synthetic hIAPP peptide variants have revealed the influence of particular aggregation-prone residues or regions of the sequence on the aggregation processes, in which residues 14-37 are identified to compose the fibril core17,18 and drive the amyloid formation through self-association at the protein-protein interface. On the basis of these observations, oligopeptides derived from the amyloid forming regions of hIAPP were shown to be important modulators of self-assembly, and some of them, for example peptide fragments ANFLVH (13-18)19, SNNFGA (20−25)20 and NFGAILSS (22−29)21, were noted as inhibitors of hIAPP aggregation. Notably, to further increase the binding affinity and water solubility of oligopeptide inhibitors, a “hot-segment-linking” approach was developed by linking two aggregation-prone regions with hydrophilic tripeptide, resulting in effective suppression of both self- and cross-seeded hIAPP aggregation22. In this regard, de novo designed oligopeptides blocking the aggregation of hIAPP might become promising candidates for T2D therapeutics.

The only known nature mutation in hIAPP is serine-to-glycine substitution at position 20 in hIAPP, which is found with small frequency in Asian populations and associated with an earlier onset of T2D23,24. The near-atomic resolution Cyro-EM structural analysis suggested that Ser20 was located in the left-handled ɑ-helix region of the Ramachandran diagram and adopted a positive ϕ-backbone dihedral angle; however, the replacement of Ser20 by Gly20 resulted in the reduce of steric hindrance in this region, which confer S20G variant greater fibril stability than hIAPP17. On the contrary, we hypothesize that increasing the steric hindrance in such a region might be able to hinder the aggregation of hIAPP; however, to the best of our knowledge, there exist no examples to design hIAPP inhibitors in this regard, leaving this potential therapeutic strategy untapped for T2D treatment.

Inspired by the previous findings that physiologically, integral O-GlcNAcylation Aβ42 was missing in the samples from AD patients compared with the samples of healthy individuals, and the cleavage fragments of Aβ bearing O-glycosylated were shown to reduce significantly in AD patients as well25, herein we introduce glycosylation modification into Ser19 or Ser20 of hIAPP amyloid-prone sequence (LANFLVHSSNNFGAIL) to present a novel class of O-glycosylation peptide inhibitors (OPIs) for curtailing the aggregation and toxicity of hIAPP (Fig. 1a). Beyond our hypothesis that incorporation of glycosylation into OPIs would provide the enhanced steric hindrance to restrain strong inter-molecular interactions between protofilaments26, the flexibility of glycosidic bond might also modulate the comprehensive interaction surface of OPIs/hIAPP complexes via CH−π, H-bonding opportunity, hydrophilicity and charge balancing27–32. Thus, we perform systematic characterization to establish inhibitory capability of OPIs against hIAPP aggregations. We further employ theoretical and numerical models to investigate the microscopic mechanisms. The therapeutic efficacy of inhibitor SNS, the most potent antagonist of hIAPP fibrillation in the library, is validated on the basis of in vitro beta cell model and isolated mouse islet model.

Results

Design and synthesis of O-glycosylation peptide inhibitors

The design of OPIs was based on the structural analysis that hIAPP(12–27) (LANFLVHSSNNFGAIL) was identified as the key amyloidogenic region, characterized by a β-sheet-turn-β-sheet motif, in which hIAPP (12–18) and hIAPP(22–27) forming the β-sheets and hIAPP (19–21) (Ser19-Ser20-Asn21) being the core sites of the turn (Fig. 1a). OPIs were thus derived by linking the two hot segments hIAPP (12–18) and hIAPP(22–27) to each other via tripeptide segments, consisted of one or two O-glycosylated serine residues modified by N-glucose (Glc, L) or N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc, N) (Fig. 1b). For simplicity, SS represented the natural amyloidogenic region of hIAPP (12–27) as a control here, and OPIs containing different O-glycosylated modifications were abbreviated to SLS, LSLS, SNS and NSNS, respectively (Fig. 1c). OPIs and peptide SS were synthesized by solid phase peptide synthesis (SPPS), purified by HPLC and characterized by mass spectrum (Fig. S1).

Antagonism of hIAPP amyloidogenesis by OPIs

Previous studies have shown a significant interaction between hIAPP and lipid membranes, and the interaction is sensitive to the content of anionic lipids33–35. To better simulate the aggregation of hIAPP under physiological conditions, we conducted the kinetic assay in the presence of anionic liposomes (DOPG/DOPC = 3/7), in which the anionic content is closer to the cell membrane36–40. We investigated the inhibitory function of different OPIs towards hIAPP by an established Thioflavin T (ThT) kinetic assay41–44, and the aggregation kinetic was quantified using the reaction midpoint t50, which was determined as the time when the ThT fluorescence intensity reached half of the fluorescence intensity between the baseline and plateau. To eliminate the influence of the OPIs themselves, the ThT fluorescence intensity in the presence of OPI alone was monitored. ThT-based amyloid kinetic assay clearly indicated that all the OPIs did not exhibit any aggregated tendency compared with the dramatically enhanced fluorescent intensity of hIAPP (Fig. S2a). Then, the library of OPIs was screened against hIAPP aggregation, among which SNS was found to be the statistically most effective (Fig. 1d). Further fitting the curves with the Logistic function showed that the aggregation rate of hIAPP was slowed down to varying degrees in the presence of different OPIs, and SNS delayed the aggregation with a significantly longer t50 = 50.5 ± 0.2 min, which was ~ 5-fold higher than hIAPP alone (9.4 ± 0.3 min) (Fig. 1e). It was also found that the antagonist behavior, induced by all the OPIs against the fibril formation of hIAPP, was concentration-dependent (Fig. S2b–d); on the contrary, the peptide SS accelerated the aggregation rate of hIAPP in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. S2e). In addition, the inhibitory function of different OPIs and the promotion effect of peptide SS were also verified by ThT kinetic assay in the absence of anionic liposomes (Fig. S3), indicating that the introduction of O-glycosylation was essential for the inhibiting effect.

Remarkably, OPIs modified by N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc) (SNS and NSNS) exhibited significantly stronger inhibition ability compared to that of N-glucose-modified (Glc) OPIs (SLS and LSLS) (Fig. 1d, e), regardless of whether it was single-site or double-sites modification. The advantage of SNS and NSNS might be attributed to the acetyl group, which probably conduced to enhance the energetically favorable CH-π stacking between hIAPP and the cluster of carbon atoms of the GlcNAc moiety28,45. Meanwhile, the acetyl group with slightly stronger hydrophobicity was hypothesized to get involved in the binding of hIAPP, contributing to an ideal balance between hydrophobicity and hydrophilicity of the complex46. In addition, the ThT intensity of plateau stage in SLS and SNS treating groups was significantly lower than that in LSLS and NSNS groups, meaning that single-site modified OPIs exhibited greater inhibitory ability than double-sites modified OPIs, although t50 value of LSLS or NSNS was a little bit higher than that of SLS and SNS, respectively (Fig. 1d). We speculated that double-sites modification resulted in the excessive steric hindrance, which reduced the binding affinity between OPIs and hIAPP, accompanied by changes in the aggregation-inhibition profile of hIAPP.

To further validate the observations from ThT assay, we firstly investigated the morphology and abundance of hIAPP fibrils in the presence of different OPIs by means of Transmission Electron Microscope (TEM) and Atomic Force Microscope (AFM). The negatively stained TEM image of 15 μM hIAPP incubated in PBS within 5 min did not show any short fibrillar structures or big aggregates (Fig. S4). After 24 h incubation, a characteristic network of amyloid fibers was observed (Fig. 2a), while no mature fibers were formed in the presence of OPIs (Fig. 2b–e). The anti-amyloidogenic activity of OPIs was also visualized by using AFM. hIAPP was eventually converted into extensive fibers after 24 h incubation at room temperature; in marked contrast, co-incubation of hIAPP with OPIs resulted in a sparse population of fibers after 24 h (Fig. S5). We next studied the effect of different OPIs on the conformation change of hIAPP through circular dichroism (CD). The CD spectrum showed that hIAPP transitioned from random coil to β-sheet-rich conformation when the fibrils were formed, characterized by a single minimum around 218 nm, which was consistent with the ThT assays; in contrast, the CD spectrum of hIAPP/OPIs complexes showed an apparent negative peak around 230 nm, which has been reported to be β-sheet or β-turn structures47,48 (Fig. 2f–i). Co-incubation with OPIs mediated the formation of sparsely distributed, short-range twisted β-sheet architectures evidenced by TEM imaging. Taken together, these findings conclusively indicated that OPIs could inhibit the generation of mature amyloid fibrils by disrupting fibril elongation. Given the optimal suppression performance above all OPIs, SNS was used to explore advanced effects and mechanisms in the subsequent studies.

Quantitative kinetic analysis of hIAPP aggregation in the presence of inhibitor SNS

To further investigate the kinetics and thermodynamics of the binding interactions that occurred between hIAPP and SNS, surface plasmon esonance (SPR) and microscale thermophoresis (MST) were employed. Briefly, different solutions at the concentration of SNS ranged from 3.9 to 50 µM were flowed over the immobilized hIAPP surface to determine their binding affinity. SPR sensorgrams exhibited significant adsorption in a concentration-dependent manner, confirming the existence of intermolecular interactions between SNS and hIAPP (Fig. 3a), which was also in good agreement with the results from MST experiments (Fig. 3b). These results revealed that SNS was able to interrupt the primary nucleation by binding with hIAPP monomers during the amyloid aggregation process.

To get deeper insight into the molecular mechanism of SNS against hIAPP aggregation, global kinetic fittings of the ThT curves were performed to associate the macroscopic measurements of hIAPP aggregation with the microscopic mechanisms using the online platform AmyloFit49 (http://www.amylofit.ch.cam.ac.uk), which can generate combined rate constants (k+kn and k+k2, wherein kn, k+ and k2 represents the rate constant of primary nucleation, elongation, and secondary nucleation, respectively) to all the kinetic curves at different concentrations. We initiated the quantitative analysis of hIAPP at initial monomer concentrations (m0) ranging from 8 μM to 32 μM by determining the t50 and plotting it as a function of m0. High concentration dependence of the t50 was observed for hIAPP when fitted by a power-law function (t50 ~ m0γ), in which γ is defined as the scaling exponent that relevant to the reaction orders. Two possible models were determined according to the value of γ for hIAPP (Fig. 3c): ‘multistep secondary nucleation’ and ‘saturating elongation and secondary nucleation’. The former was proved to be the better model for describing the kinetic curves (Fig. 3d), while the latter was powerless to describe the experimental data without regard to the multi-step effect of secondary nucleation (Fig. 3e). Not unexpectedly, another model that include fragmentation and secondary nucleation also failed to reappear the experimental curves, which was consistent with previous reported studies50,51(Fig. 3f).

Remarkably, kinetic curves precisely matched the model for multistep secondary nucleation in the presence of concentration gradient SNS at all times by modulating the combined rate constants corresponding to primary nucleation (k+kn) (Fig. 3g), while matched none of curves of inhibition by reducing the combined rate constants corresponding to second nucleation (k+k2) (Fig. 3h)—revealing that SNS acted particularly to suppress the primary nucleation microscopic step in the aggregation reaction, as opposed to the second nucleation. In particular, the combined rate constant for primary nucleation, k+kn, was reduced as the concentration of SNS increasing, indicating that SNS produced a marked inhibition effect in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 3i). In addition, the combined rate constants (k+kn and k+k2) generated from the global kinetic fittings showed that k+kn and k+k2 were dropped in over two orders of magnitude in the co-incubation with 10 eq SNS relative to hIAPP alone (Fig. 3j), respectively, consistent with its positive inhibitory effect on hIAPP aggregation. The √KM which represented to be the monomer concentration of half-saturation of secondary nucleation was higher in the co-incubation with SNS than hIAPP alone (Fig. 3j), indicating a lower binding affinity of the monomer in the presence of SNS to its fibril surface compared with hIAPP, in accordance with the decreased rate of secondary nucleation for this reaction. This suggested that the chief mechanism responsible for inhibitory function was the consequence of providing a structural template to which amyloidogenic hIAPP conformers could adhere, repressing the primary nucleation preferentially and thereby affecting the secondary nucleation. Inhibitory effects were thus likely mediated by the high specific affinity and binding ability of SNS to hIAPP monomers, while SNS possessing hIAPP-based structural similarity but lacking hIAPP-like toxicity, resulting in the formation of non-toxic assemblies (Fig. 3k).

Seed-catalyzed aggregation of hIAPP inhibited by SNS

In order to further validate the mechanical hypothesis of effective inhibition on secondary nucleation process of hIAPP by SNS, we investigated the inhibitory potential of SNS on the prolongation of hIAPP seeds. In the presence of freshly prepared hIAPP fibrils as seeds (5%, v/v), the aggregation of hIAPP was accelerated by the elimination of the lag phase, accompanied by the t50 reduced from 14.1 ± 0.6 min to 8.5 ± 0.2 min. On the contrary, secondary nucleation was almost entirely suppressed in the presence of SNS, with the t50 extended from 8.5 ± 0.2 min to 58.6 ± 0.2 min, demonstrating the ability of SNS to arrest fibril propagation (Fig. 4a, b). An abundance of seeds-catalyzed hIAPP fibers were also observed under TEM imaging after 24 h incubation (Fig. 4c). In contrast, no elongated fibers were observed in the presence of SNS under the same conditions (Fig. 4b, d). These data support the notion that SNS could block the interaction between hIAPP monomers and seeds, which prevented the elongation of fibrillar structures.

hIAPP-induced beta cell failure suppressed by SNS

The oligomerization of hIAPP has been demonstrated to critically mediate membrane damage through pathogenetic hydrophobic exposure8. To investigate the inhibitory effect of SNS on the membrane disorganization induced by hIAPP aggregates, a membrane leakage model was constructed by encapsulating FRET pair Cy2 (donor) and Cy3 (acceptor) into anionic liposomes, and subsequently treated with hIAPP aggregates or hIAPP/SNS complexes. Due to the membrane disturbance or damage induced by hIAPP aggregates, the distance between Cy2 and Cy3 was increased, resulting in the decrease of FRET ratio; however, the existence of SNS prevented this phenomenon occurring in a dose dependent manner (Fig. S6). It has been reported that the damage of phospholipid membranes was highly related to the exposure of hydrophobic clusters of hIAPP aggregates52. Bis-ANS (4,4’-dianilino-1,1’-binaphthyl-5,5’-disulfonic acid) binding studies53 indicated that SNS could efficiently suppressed the exposure of residues/fragments of hIAPP (Fig. S7). Taken together, all the results indicated that SNS could protect phospholipid membranes from hIAPP-indcued damage by suppressing the exposure of hydrophobic clusters of hIAPP aggregates.

The successful inhibition of hIAPP-induced membrane damage by SNS led us to explore its capability to rescue pancreatic cells from hIAPP amyloidogenesis-associated cytotoxicity. The protective effects of SNS were examined on rat insulinoma INS-1 beta cells, and cell viability was quantified by MTT assay. After 24 h incubation, 1 μM hIAPP reduced cell viability to 43.5 ± 0.6% compared to the cells without hIAPP treatment (control group) (Fig. S8a), while SNS alone did not show any cytotoxicity to INS-1 cells under the same condition (Fig. S8b). Notably, the cytotoxic effect of hIAPP was significantly attenuated by SNS in a dose-dependent manner, demonstrating that SNS was able to modulate hIAPP assemblies to a less toxic state (Fig. 5a). To verify the alleviation effect of SNS aganist hIAPP-induced cytotoxicity more intuitively, the live/dead staining assay was performed to visualize cell viability by confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM). As shown in Fig. S8c, much more dead cells were observed under the treatment of hIAPP compared to control group; however, the hIAPP-induced cell death was restored by co-treatment with SNS, consistent with the MTT assay.

Since it has been noticed that hIAPP-induced cell death was associated with oxidative stress54, we investigated the modulating effect of SNS on hIAPP-induced intracellular accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS). ROS level in INS-1 cells was probed by 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) and quantified by flow cytometry. After the treatment with 1 μM hIAPP, a marked increase of intracellular ROS (~6-fold) was determined by significantly enhanced fluorescence intensity of DCFH-DA compared to the control group. However, the elevated ROS level was dose-dependently reduced by co-treatment of SNS, especially recovered to almost the same level as control group when the concentration of SNS was ten times of hIAPP (Fig. 5b–e).

In further, the cellular uptake and intracellular localization of hIAPP and hIAPP /SNS complex was visualized by CLSM. To this purpose, the N-terminus of hIAPP was covalently labelled with fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC), named as hIAPPF. INS-1 cells were treated with 1 μM hIAPPF with or without SNS. CLSM imaging showed that hIAPPF was mainly located outside of the cells, whereas hIAPPF/SNS complexes were efficiently internalized and mainly colocalized with intracellular lysosomes (Fig. 5f). Subsequent quantitative analysis using Pearson’s correlation coefficient (PCC) revealed pronounced co-localization between hIAPPF/SNS complex and lysosomes compared to that of hIAPP group (Fig. S9). Thus, we concluded that the reduced cytotoxicity of hIAPPF/SNS complex was not only due to the morphology transition, but also dedicated to the internalization of hIAPP into lysosomes, where hIAPP would be potentially degraded into non-toxic segments. While the phospholipid membrane damage and the intracellular oxidative stress induced by hIAPP was effectively suppressed by the application of SNS, the cell viability was not fully restored by SNS evidenced by MTT assay (Fig. 5a). We hypothesized multiple pathways, including cell membrane disruption, ER stress55 and mitochondrial apoptotic pathway56, simultaneously play essential roles in hIAPP-induced cell death.

Effective protection of mouse islet exposed to hIAPP by SNS

Based on the above investigations, we next evaluated the therapeutic effects of SNS on isolated mouse islets exposure to hIAPP, as shown in Fig. 6a. Accumulating evidence have indicated that synthetic hIAPP fibrils or oligomers induced apoptosis in both human and mouse islet beta cells57, which was also observed in our study by TUNEL staining (Fig. 6b, c). Meanwhile, the incubation of mouse islets with hIAPP also significantly reduced the islet beta cell viability identified by live/dead staining (Fig. 6d, e). On the contrary, co-incubation with SNS neutralized hIAPP-induced apoptosis and cytotoxicity as compared to control and hIAPP groups. We next examined the content of endogenous insulin in isolated mouse islets by immunofluorescence staining. Confocal imaging and quantitative analysis showed that the down-regulation of insulin was accompanied by apoptotic beta cell death when mouse islets were exposed to hIAPP, which was totally restored by the co-treatment of SNS (Figs. 6b, S10). In addition, the determination of total insulin content showed that hIAPP-exposure reduced total insulin content by ~45% (Fig. 6e, left panel), while SNS treatment could preserve total insulin content close to the control group.

Furthermore, glucose-stimulated insulin secretion (GSIS) assay was performed to evaluate the insulin secretion of isolated islets by scoring the ratio of insulin release in high glucose (16.7 mM) to that in low glucose (1.6 mM). Compared to the control group, insulin levels stimulated by low glucose and high glucose in the hIAPP-treated group decreased to approximately 16.4% and 7.2% (Fig. 6f), respectively; whereas co-treatment of SNS dramatically improved the insulin secretion, especially under low glucose-stimulated condition (almost equivalent to the control group). Moreover, incubation with hIAPP led to a significantly decrease (~80.1%) in islet stimulation index (from 3.1 ± 0.2 to 1.4 ± 0.2), while co-incubation with SNS resulted in a decrease in stimulation index about 9.5% (from 3.1 ± 0.2 to 2.9 ± 0.1) (Fig. 6g). All these results confirmed the therapeutic effects of SNS on hIAPP-induced islet damage, characterized by reduced cytotoxicity, suppressed apoptosis and intact insulin secretion capability.

Discussion

Clinically, the pathogenesis of Type 2 diabetes (T2D) has not been fully understood yet, but accumulated evidence has indicated that the development and progression of T2D is highly associated with beta cell failure, which results in the insufficient production of endogenous insulin and lost capability to control blood glucose levels and to counteract insulin resistance58. Multiple factors underlie the beta cell dysfunction and loss, one of which is the over accumulation and aggregation of hIAPP. The amyloidogenic hIAPP oligomers and fibrils not only contribute to disruption of cell membranes and cytotoxicity, but also share some pathogenetic pathways with other factors, including oxidative stress59, apoptosis60 and inflammation61,62. Thereby, tuning the rate of hIAPP into non-toxic state by therapeutic strategies will offer an important opportunity for delineating the pathogenic mechanisms as well as combating T2D disease. Herein, we designed and prepared a small library of de novo designed O-glycosylated peptides as highly potent inhibitors of hIAPP. We described that hIAPP fibrils could induce beta cell failure, which could be curtailed by the co-treatment of oligopeptide inhibitor in cultured beta cells and isolated mouse islets models.

Our results proved that these oligopeptides, which were made of two hot-segments from hIAPP linked via O-glycosylated tripeptide spacer, could delay or even inhibit the formation of hIAPP amyloid fibrils. By screening the constructed library, we found SNS exhibited the most significant inhibitory effect on the hIAPP fibrillization although all these O-glycosylated peptides shared the same molecular mechanism that suppressed the β-sheet formation of hIAPP monomer. These observations clearly gave us three suggestions: i) the introduction of glycosylation could enhance the steric hindrance and interfered the intermolecular interactions among hIAPP monomers, in line with previous study that O-glycosylation modification redirected Aβ42 to form less stable fibril polymorphic structure at the molecular level [16]; ii) Compared to N-glucosamine, N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc) provided more comprehensive interactions between O-glycosylated peptide and hIAPP, which might contribute to the increased the binding affinity; iii) Over-modification resulted in the excessive steric hindrance, which inversely reduced the binding affinity, indicated by the decreased inhibitory effects of NSNS and LSLS. Therefore, seeking the balance between enhancing steric hindrance and improving binding affinity is very crucial for the design of O-glycosylated peptide inhibitors.

Dissection of the microscopic events enables us to deeply unveil the detailed mechanisms of inhibitors against amyloid aggregation. Comparing hIAPP with hIAPP/SNS, the global fitting of the ThT curves on the online platform AmyloFit showed reduced rate constants of primary nucleation and secondary nucleation in the presence of SNS, indicating that SNS most likely interacted with hIAPP monomers. Meanwhile, the higher value of √KM in hIAPP/SNS group proved that the interaction between SNS and hIAPP monomers blocked the access of hIAPP monomers to the surface of fibrils, further preventing the elongation of fibrils, which was also confirmed in the amyloid seeding model.

The major pathogenetic mechanism of hIAPP is their cytotoxicity to beta cells, commonly observed in human and hIAPP-expressing mouse islets63. Additional mechanisms by which hIAPP causes the islet dysfunction include cell membrane disruption64, cell apoptosis65, oxidative stress59 and inflammation66,67. In vitro and in vivo data in this work supported the roles of hIAPP in these pathways. Application of hIAPP to phospholipid membranes and beta cells induced the membrane disorganization, elevated intracellular ROS level and resulted in the cell death. Further, in the isolated mouse islets model, hIAPP trigged the beta cell apoptosis, loss and dysfunction, accompanied by the reduced secretion of insulin. Of note, co-treatment with hIAPP and the inhibitor SNS significantly preserved the integrity of phospholipid membranes, reduced the amyloid-induced oxidative stress and cytotoxicity, suppressed the occurrence of apoptosis and preserved the normal function of mouse islets.

In summary, our findings highlight how the O-glycosylation-modified peptide inhibitor can have a remarkable effect against hIAPP aggregation: the glycosylation modification gives rise to enhanced steric hindrance and the rebalancing of multiple interactions, which interrupts the primary and secondary nucleation process of hIAPP. SNS-mediated inhibition in an early phase of hIAPP fibrilization can preserve beta cell and islet function. This is of particular relevance since the currently developed strategies for T2D treatment are rather limited. In essence, the O-glycosylated peptide inhibitors hold the promise to be considered as therapeutic agents for treating amyloid-related disease.

Methods

Materials

Fmoc-amino acids, diisopropylethylamine (DIPEA), CLEAR-Amide Resin, and O-(7-azabenzotriazole-1-yl)-1,1,3,3-tetramethyluronilum hexafluorophosphate (HATU) were purchased from BO MAI JIE Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Thioflavin T (ThT) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Lipids [dioleoylphos-phatidylcholine (DOPC) and dioleoylphosphatidylglycerol (DOPG)] were purchased from TCI. The 96-well plates (black, flat bottom) were purchased from Corning. All commercial materials (Sigma-Aldrich, TCI, Aladdin, J&K, Hwrkchemical, etc.) were used without further purification. All solvents were reagent grade or HPLC grade (Sigma-Aldrich, TCI).

Peptides and peptide synthesis

hIAPP used in the study was purchased from GenScript with purity >98% and used without further purification. hIAPP was dissolved in 1,1,1,3,3,3-hexafluoroisopropanol (HFIP) (1 mg/mL) and kept at room temperature for 1 h with occasional vortexing, which was then aliquoted into small fractions and evaporated by gentle nitrogen gas flow to remove HFIP. Finally, they were lyophilized overnight and stored as a white solid at -80 °C. Fresh stock solution of hIAPP was prepared in ultrapure water. The concentration of hIAPP was calculated using absorbance measurements at 280 nm (ε = 1400 M-1 cm-1) by Thermo Scientific NanoDrop 8000.

All the peptides were synthesized by standard solid phase synthesis (SPPS) on CLEAR amide resin. HATU and DIPEA was used for the coupling reactions. Fmoc-protected group was removed by 20% piperidine in anhydrous DMF. The O-acetyl groups were removed by treatment of hydrazine hydrate (80% in MeOH, v/v) for 45 min twice on resin with shaking. Peptides were cleaved from the resin by a mixture (TFA/H2O/Tis = 95:2.5:2.5, v/v/v) for 4 h at room temperature, and then precipitated in ice-cold diethyl ether, lyophilized and purified by RP-HPLC. All synthetic peptides were characterized by mass spectrometry. Fresh stock solution of OPIs was dissolved in DMSO at a final concentration of 5 mM.

Preparation of anionic liposomes

Anionic liposomes were prepared using DOPC and DOPG at a stoichiometric ratio of 7:3 (DOPC: DOPG). DOPC and DOPG were dissolved in the mixture of chloroform and methanol (3:1, v/v), and then evaporated to form thin film, which was hydrated in 2 mL phosphate buffer. The suspension was then extruded through 100 nm diameter filters (Avanti Polar Lipids) over 20 times and stored at 4 °C.

ThT-based kinetic assay

Inhibitory effects of OPIs on amyloidogenesis of hIAPP were determined using ThT assay. Kinetic assays were conducted on a multi-mode microplate reader from Tecan Spark. Fluorescence intensity was monitored at 485 nm for 300 minutes with 1 min intervals between each read. The stoichiometry ratio of ThT and hIAPP was 1:1, and the final concentration of anionic liposomes was 0.25 mg/mL. Kinetic assays in the presence of OPIs were conducted at the final concentration of DMSO less than 0.5% (v/v). OPIs were added to the wells with ThT and buffer, and mixed gently with a pipette before adding hIAPP. To keep the condition identical, an equal amount of DMSO was added to control group without OPIs.

Bis-ANS fluorescence titration

Bis-ANS (4,4′-dianilino-1,1′-binaphthyl-5,5′-disulfonic acid) fluorescence titration was performed by a F97Pro fluorescence spectrophotometer (Shanghai Lengguang Technology Co., Ltd.). Briefly, solutions of bis-ANS alone (15 µM) and its mixtures with hIAPP (15 µM) or hIAPP/SNS (1/10) complexes were freshly made in 1× PBS (pH 7.4), and the fluorescence spectrums were recorded from 400 nm to 650 nm.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

hIAPP (15 µM) was incubated in PBS (pH 7.4) with or without OPIs (10 eq) in the presence of anionic liposomes. To prepare the samples for TEM, 5 µL of each solution was loaded onto the copper grids of carbon support films (300 mesh) (Zhongjingkeyi Technology Co. Ltd., Beijing, China). Grids were negatively stained with phosphotungstic acid (3%, w/v) for 30 s. TEM micrographs were collected on an FEI Talos L120C G2 transmission electron microscope at 120 kV acceleration voltage.

Atomic force microscopy (AFM)

AFM imaging was performed at 20 °C using a Bruker Dimension Icon atomic force microscopy. hIAPP (15 µM) was incubated in PBS (pH 7.4) with or without OPIs (10 eq) in the presence of anionic liposomes. 10 μL droplets of the samples were spotted onto a freshly stripped surface of mica and dried over night at room temperature. A scanning area measuring 5 μm × 5 μm was performed with 256 scanning lines and the scan rate was set at 1.5 Hz during the test. Flatten module in Nanoscope Analysis software was used to correct signal distortion in AFM images.

Circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy

Measurements were carried out using a Jasco CD Spectropolarimeter J-180. The spectra of samples were recorded from 190 nm to 260 nm with a bandwidth of 1 nm and a read time of 200 nm/min and an average of three repeats. hIAPP solutions (20 μM) were aged for 24 h in the presence or absence of OPIs at 10 eq ratio in 1× PBS (pH 7.4) for CD measurements. Mdeg values were subtracted from the baseline (1× PBS, pH 7.4, without peptides).

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) spectroscopy

A Biacore T200 (GE, Sweden) instrument was used throughout. hIAPP was immobilized onto a CM5 chip by amine-coupling chemistry. The binding affinity test between hIAPP and OPIs was performed by injecting a serial diluted OPIs (3.9–500 µM) in PBS-P+ running buffer at a flow rate of 30 µL min-1, allowing 60 s for association and 60 s for dissociation. All SPR reagents and software were from GE Healthcare.

Microscale thermophoresis (MST)

All microscale thermophoresis measurements (MST) were performed on a Monolith™ NT.115 instrument (NanoTemper Technologies GmbH, Munich, Germany). hIAPP was labelled by Alexa Fluor™ 647 fluorescent dye (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and purified with gravity flow columns PD MiniTrap G-10 (Cytiva, USA). The standard capillaries dedicated to Monolith NT.115 were used (NanoTemper Technologies GmbH, Munich, Germany). Each of the 16 solutions of one titration series was filled into a capillary, which was measured successively to create the respective data points in the experiment. The excitation source (LED) power was 2%, the IR laser power was set to medium, and the system was thermostated at 25 °C. The data was analyzed by MO.Affinity Analysis, and fitted by the quadratic binding equation (NanoTemper Technologies GmbH, Munich, Germany).

Seed-catalyzed kinetic assay

Seeds were prepared by incubating hIAPP (15 μM) in 1×PBS at room temperature for 24 h. The solution was sonicated for 5 min at room temperature to obtain hIAPP seeds. The concentration of seeds was calculated based on the monomeric concentration of hIAPP. For the seed catalyzed aggregation kinetics of hIAPP, 5% (based on the monomeric hIAPP, v/v) seeds were added with ThT in phosphate buffer to the 96-well plate. The aggregation was initiated by the addition of fresh hIAPP solution followed by gentle mixing. Kinetic assays in the presence of OPIs were conducted under the same conditions except that the OPI were added from a stock solution (10 mM in DMSO) to keep the final concentration of DMSO less than 0.5% (v/v).

Membrane leakage analysis

To prepare FRET pair containing liposomes, Cy2 and Cy3 were encapsulated into anionic liposomes (DOPC/DOPG = 7/3), named as Lipo2@3. Lipo2@3 exhibited robust Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET) efficiency, which inversely correlates with membrane structural integrity. hIAPP (10 μM) and SNS with different concentrations were added to lipos2@3 system for co-incubation. Lipo2@3 without the addition of hIAPP and SNS was considered as the negative control. Fluorescence emission spectra were recorded on a multi-mode microplate reader from Tecan Spark.

Cell culture

Rat insulinoma INS-1 cells were purchased from Saiye (Suzhou) Biological Technology Co., Ltd., and authenticated by the vendors. INS-1 cells were tested for mycoplasma contamination and cultured at 37 °C and 5% CO2 in RPMI 1640 media supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin/streptomycin (all from Gibco, New York, USA). Upon reaching ~95% confluence, cells were split (0.25% trypsin-EDTA; Gibco) into fractions, passaged and/or used in experiments. Cells used in experiments were pelleted and resuspended in fresh media with no Trypsin-EDTA.

Cell viability

Cell viability was determined by well-established 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazole-2-yl)-2,5- diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) reduction assay. Cells were plated at a density of 8 × 103 cells per well in 300 µL complete RPMI 1640 growth medium in 48-well plates. After culturing for 24 h in 5% CO2 at 37 °C, the medium was removed, and the cells were washed with 1× PBS (200 µL per well). hIAPP and OPIs were pre-mixed in buffer (50 mM NaPi, 150 mM KCl, pH 7.4) and the mixture was added to the cells in complete medium. The cells were then incubated for an additional 24 h. After removing all the remaining supernatant of each well, cells were labelled in 0.5 mg mL−1 MTT culture medium solution at 37 °C for 4 h and dissolved the formazan crystals in dimethyl sulfoxide. The absorbance value was read at 490 nm by using Tecan Spark microplate reader, and the cell viability was determined as the percentage of MTT reduction as compared to untreated cells. Percent cell viability was calculated as per the following equation:

% Viability = 100 × [(〈S〉-〈B〉)/(〈N〉-〈B〉)]

where〈S〉,〈B〉, and 〈N〉 are the average fluorescence intensities of the sample, blank control, and negative control, respectively. The blank control is a cell-free system designed to normalize background absorbance, consisting solely of complete medium and MTT working solution. The negative control refers to cell-containing systems (untreated cells) cultured in complete medium and exposed to MTT working solution, without any drug treatment.

Live/dead staining assay

INS-1 cells were seeded in confocal petri dishes at a density of 1 × 106 cells per well and cultured for 24 h. The cell medium was then replaced by fresh medium containing hIAPP with/without SNS. After incubation for 24 h, the cells were washed twice with 1× PBS and stained with live/dead viability assay kit diluted in culture media. The live/dead imaging was monitored by a fluorescence microscope. All samples were observed using a confocal laser scanning microscope (TCS SP8, Leica, Germany).

Confocal laser scanning microscope

For CLSM imaging, hIAPP was labeled by fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC), named as hIAPPF. INS-1 cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well in confocal petri dishes (Nest, Wuxi, China). After culturing for 48 h in 5% CO2 at 37 °C, the medium was replaced with complete medium containing 1 μM hIAPP (0.9 μM hIAPP and 0.1 μM hIAPPF), 10 μM SNS, or hIAPP/SNS complex (0.9 μM hIAPP, 0.1 μM hIAPPF and 10 μM SNS), and incubated for 3 h. For lysosome staining, 50 nM LysoTracker Red (Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd, Beijing, China) was added to the culture medium and incubated for 1 h. Thereafter, the medium was removed, the cells were washed with 1× PBS to remove any extracellular hIAPP, OPI or organelle marker, and 300 μL complete medium was added to each chamber in the cell culture dish. Imaging was carried out at the Leica TCS SP8, using a 40× Plan-Apo/1.4-NA oil-immersion objective with DIC capability (Leica, Germany). For all experiments reporting on the uptake of labelled hIAPP, the gain setting for the red channel was kept constant from sample to sample. Image acquisition and processing were achieved using LAS X 3.4.2 and Image J software.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) detecting assay

Reactive Oxygen Species Assay Kit (Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd, Beijing, China) were used to detect the intracellular level of ROS. INS-1 cells were seeded at a density of 1× 105 cells/well in confocal petri dishs (Nest, Wuxi, China) and cultured for 24 h in 5% CO2 at 37 °C. Firstly, the probe 2’,7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA, 5 μmol/L) was co-incubated with cells in serum-free cell culture medium at 37 °C for 30 min, followed by washing with serum-free medium for 3 times to remove residual probes. Then, INS-1cells were treated with complete medium containing hIAPP (1 μM) or the mixture of hIAPP (1 μM) and different doses of OPI (1, 2, 5 or 10 μM) for 2 h. Subsequently, the medium was removed, and the cells were washed by 1× PBS and observed using CLSM (TCS SP8, Leica). Meanwhile, ROS in INS-1 cells were also quantitated by flow cytometry using BD LSR Fortessa (Becton, Dickinson and Company, USA). The cells were treated by trypsin, and resuspended in 1× PBS after being centrifugated. FSC and SSC were defined in order to display the entire cell population. Flow cytometry data was gated according to scatter properties and viability (Fig. S11). Cells gated by this strategy demonstrated statistical significance.

Animals

Male BALB/c mice were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology. All the mice used in the experiments were 8-12 weeks of age with weight at 20-25 g. The mice were maintained at Laboratory Animal Center of Nankai University. They are randomly raised in a cage every 5 individuals at a temperature-, humidity- and light-controlled room. The animal experiments of mice were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Nankai University, under approved IACUC # 2025-SYDWLL-000582. We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use.

Mouse islet-based assay

Mouse islets of Langerhans were isolated and purified from male BALB/c mice by collagenase digestion technique [67]. After isolation, the islets were resuspended with RPMI 1640 medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum and then placed in 6 cm dishes. Under the stereomicroscope, islets were manually picked using a 20 μL micropipette based on stringent morphological criteria: intact architecture, well-demarcated boundaries, smooth surface topography, absence of structural compromise (including fragmentation or vacuolation), and characteristic pale-yellow coloration with dense cellular organization. Selected islets exhibited uniform compaction without signs of amyloid-induced degeneration. Then, the islets were recovered overnight in RPMI 1640 media supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin/streptomycin (all from Gibco) with 5% CO2 at 37 °C. Islets were subsequently counted manually and randomly divided into 12-well cell culture plates (30 islets per well) after removing the inactive ones. Then islets were incubated in fresh medium with/without 10 μM hIAPP in the presence or absence of 100 μM SNS for 24 h. In the experimental design, untreated islets served as the control group. Each experimental condition was replicated in triplicate to ensure statistical robustness. Viability of islets was assessed by TUNEL and live/ dead staining assay. Quantification of islet viability was performed through manual counting of live/dead stained cells, with each dataset derived from 3 biologically independent samples.

For insulin and TUNEL staining, the islets were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (4 °C, 30 min) and permeated with 0.2%Triton X-100 at room temperature for 20 min. Subsequently, the samples were incubated with rabbit anti-insulin antibody (Cat # 3014, Cell Signaling Technology, Shanghai, China) at 4 °C overnight (1:200), followed by incubation with Alexa Fluor 647 goat anti-rabbit IgG (Cat # 33113ES60, Yeasen Biotechnology (Shanghai) Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) for 1 h (1:200) at 4 °C overnight. After insulin immunolabelling, islet cells were incubated with TUNEL reaction mixture (1:49) for 6 h at 37 °C (UElandy, Suzhou, China). After washing with 1× PBST for four to six times, samples were stained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). Finally, immunofluorescence staining images were captured by CLSM (TCS SP8, Leica). Ex vivo pancreatic islets from three independent biological samples underwent three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction, with one representative islet selected per replicate for analysis. Maximum intensity projection (MIP) algorithms were applied to quantify insulin fluorescence intensity across all three islets. β-cell populations were enumerated via DAPI-stained nuclear counting, and normalized functional assessment was performed by dividing total fluorescence intensity by β-cell counts. Untreated ex vivo mouse islets served as experimental controls.

For measurement of the total insulin content, islets were lysed in radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer, and insulin content was measured using a mouse-specific insulin ELISA kit (Elabscience Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China).

After treatment of hIAPP (with/without SNS), Beyo3D™ calcein AM staining solution and Beyo3D™ PI staining solution (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) were used to conduct the live/dead assay of pancreatic islets. All samples were imaged by CLSM (TCS SP8, Leica, Germany).

Glucose-stimulated insulin secretion (GSIS) assay

After incubation with hIAPP (with/without SNS) for 24 h, islets were washed and used for glucose-stimulated insulin secretion (GSIS). Krebs-Ringer bicarbonate buffer (Krebs; supplemented with 0.5% BSA, pH 7.3–7.4) containing 1.6 mM glucose and 16.7 mM glucose was prepared and warmed to 37 °C before use. Islets were pre-treated in Krebs buffer containing 1.6 mM glucose for 1 h, followed by 1 h incubation in low-glucose Krebs buffer (1.6 mM, basal insulin release) and then 1 h incubation in high-glucose Krebs buffer (16.7 mM, stimulated insulin release). Supernatants were collected after each incubation, and islets were rinsed at each solution change with fresh Krebs buffer. Insulin concentration at low and high glucose was measured by a mouse-specific insulin ELISA kit (Elabscience Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China). Insulin secretion was measured and expressed as the glucose stimulated index (GSI), which was calculated by the ratio of insulin concentration at high glucose to low glucose.

Statistics and reproducibility

All experiments were conducted independently at least in triplicate, and the results were presented as the mean ± standard deviation. Comparison between two groups was analyzed by Student’s t-test. For multiple groups, statistical significance was determined using one-way ANOVA or two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons. P < 0.05 was set to represent a statistically significant difference. To ensure reproducibility, each experiment was independently repeated at least three times under identical conditions. Biological replicates were defined as independent experimental trials performed using distinct biological samples. For isolated pancreatic islets experiments, each replicate was conducted using a separate group of animals to minimize individual variability. Experiments without formal statistical testing were still repeated across multiple biological replicates to confirm reproducibility. The exact number of sample sizes for each experiment are provided in the corresponding figure legends.
