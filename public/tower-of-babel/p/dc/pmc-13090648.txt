Early proteomic signatures of impaired maxillary bone remodeling under mechanical stress in type 1 diabetes mellitus
Authors: Luis Fernando Tobias-López, Cynthia Georgina Trejo-Iriarte, Alejandro García-Muñoz, José Francisco Gómez-Clavel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundType 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) compromises the mechanical adaptive capacity of bones, posing challenges in orthodontic treatment and increases periodontal complication risks. Mechanical loading is the main inducer of craniofacial bone adaptation; however, the early proteomic responses of the maxillary bone to stress under diabetic conditions remain unexplored. Therefore, we aimed to characterize the early proteomic signatures of the maxillary bone in T1DM rats subjected to controlled mechanical stress and identify potentially relevant molecular pathways for orthodontic treatment in patients with diabetes.Materials and methodsSixteen male Wistar rats were randomly assigned to four groups: normoglycemic, normoglycemic + mechanical stress, diabetic, and diabetic + mechanical stress. T1DM was induced using streptozotocin, and mechanical loading was applied using a nickel-titanium expansion device for 4 days. Maxillary bone proteins were extracted, separated using two-dimensional gel electrophoresis, and identified using LC-MS. STRING-based protein interaction analysis was also conducted.ResultsNineteen differentially expressed protein spots were identified. STRING analysis revealed significant PPI enrichment and three interconnected functional clusters: cytoskeletal organization proteins (actin, cytoplasmic 2, and tropomyosin β chain), translation/proteostasis (eEF1A1 and apoptosis/β-catenin modulator 14-3-3 ζ/δ), and inflammatory/oxidative stress mediators (S100-A9, galectin-1, and HSPA8).ConclusionsEarly T1DM-induced proteomic changes and mechanical stress impair the maxillary bone profile by reshaping cytoskeletal remodeling, translational regulation, and stress-response pathways during early mechanoadaptation. These findings provide a proteomic framework for understanding how metabolic dysregulation may influence craniofacial bone responses under orthodontic forces.Keywords: Proteomics, Maxillary bone, Type 1 diabetes mellitus, Mechanical stress, Orthodontics

Introduction

Maxilla and mandible are unique skeletal structures with continuous functional activity, high remodeling rates, and rich vascularization. They are constantly exposed to the oral microenvironment via the gingival sulcus and functional mechanical stimuli, such as mastication and occlusal forces.1 The exposure demands a persistent bone turnover to preserve periodontal health and enable orthodontic tooth movement. Disruption of this mechano adaptive capacity may result in delayed tooth movement, compromised periodontal stability, and reduced treatment predictability.2

Mechanical forces, the main stimuli for bone turnover, are mainly sensed by osteocytes, which orchestrate bone remodeling through mechano-transduction pathways, promoting osteogenesis and regulating osteoclast activity in response to loading.3

Type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) compromise orthodontic outcomes by increasing the risk of bone loss and periodontal complications.4 Sustained hyperglycemia and accumulation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs) disrupts skeletal adaptive mechanisms through a coordinated molecular cascade. AGEs stiffens the collagenous bone matrix via non-enzymatic cross-linking and engages the receptor for AGEs (RAGE). Activation of the AGE–RAGE axis triggers oxidative stress and pro-inflammatory signaling pathways, leading to osteocyte apoptosis and impaired mechanotransduction.5,6 Concurrently, inflammatory mediators suppress osteoblast differentiation and favor osteoclastogenesis. Collectively, these alterations shift bone remodeling toward resorption and compromise the anabolic response to mechanical loading.7, 8, 9.

Mechanical force-induced signaling is reduced in patients with T1DM.10 Most previous studies have focused on the long-term structural consequences of diabetes in bone. However, craniofacial bone adaptation to orthodontic forces occurs within days and early molecular alterations may precede irreversible microarchitectural damage.11 In craniofacial tissues, T1DM induces early structural changes in osteocyte lacunae in rats,12 and a deficient bone remodeling response to orthodontic forces.13 Therefore, very early molecular events, rather than late structural deterioration, may underlie impaired mechano-responsiveness. However, the underlying molecular mechanism of this early effect remains unknown.

To investigate these early molecular events, approaches capable of capturing dynamic functional changes at the protein level are required. Proteins are the primary effectors of cellular activity and reflect real-time biological responses more directly than genomic or transcriptomic analyses. Proteomics, the comprehensive characterization of proteins in a tissue at a given time, enables the identification and quantification of structural proteins, signaling mediators, enzymes, stress-response molecules, and established protein-protein interactions (PPIs). Unlike histological or radiographic methods, which detect established structural alterations, proteomic profiling can reveal early molecular changes that precede irreversible microarchitectural damage.

Mass spectrometry, particularly when coupled with two-dimensional polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS), enables systematic protein identification and quantification in complex biological conditions.14 Importantly, MS-based proteomics can discriminate between healthy and diseased states by identifying enriched biological processes through bioinformatic mapping as PPI networks.15 These studies illustrate how clustering and interaction network analyses reveal coordinated molecular signatures rather than isolated protein changes This feature makes proteomics particularly suitable for investigating early adaptive or maladaptive responses to mechanical loading under hyperglycemic conditions.

Proteomic approaches have been applied in vitro to analyze bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells during osteogenic differentiation and osteoblast cultures under different stimuli,16, 17, 18, 19 and in vivo to characterize protein expression in bone tissue under hormonal treatment.20 In craniofacial tissues, extracellular matrix proteomics of the periodontal ligament under orthodontic load has revealed dynamic modifications in collagen and mechano-responsive structural proteins.21 Proteomic analysis of mandibular condylar cartilage under functional stress has identified significant alterations in protein synthesis and signaling pathways related to cartilage-bone adaptation.21 However, proteomic profiling of in vivo maxillary bone subjected to mechanical loading under hyperglycemic conditions has not been reported. This gap limits our understanding of the early molecular mechanisms governing craniofacial bone adaptation in patients with T1DM. Elucidating these early proteomic changes may reveal upstream regulators and potential therapeutic targets before irreversible microarchitectural damage occurs.22

We hypothesized that the combination of hyperglycemia and mechanical load induces a distinct early proteomic signature in the maxillary bone involving pathways related to mechano-transduction, oxidative stress, and cell survival. We aimed to identify and compare early alterations in the proteomic profile of the maxillary bone in early-T1DM rats subjected to maxillary mechanical loading, using two-dimensional polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (2D-PAGE) coupled with mass spectrometry (MS).

Materials and methods

Animal model and experimental groups

Sixteen male Wistar rats (200–250 g) were obtained from the FES Iztacala Bioterium and maintained under controlled temperature (22 ± 2 °C), humidity (50–60%), and a 12 h light/dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and water. The animals were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 4 per group): normoglycemic (N), normoglycemic + mechanical stress (NS), diabetic (D), and diabetic + mechanical stress (DS). All animal procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Facultad de Estudios Superiores Iztacala, UNAM (approval no. 1080).

Mechanical loading protocol

Mechanical stress was applied in the NS and DS groups using a U-shaped nickel-titanium expansion device (0.012 in) anchored between the upper first and second molars, as previously described23 (Fig. 1). Prior to placement, all springs were pre-calibrated using a precision tension gauge to verify the force-activation relationship. At an initial activation of 1.5 ± 0.1 mm, the devices generated approximately 16 gf. They were placed under general anaesthesia (ketamine 60 mg/kg + xylazine 7 mg/kg, i.p.), and positioning was checked daily to ensure stability and correct placement, and animals were monitored daily for body weight, grooming behavior, and food intake to detect potential discomfort or feeding impairment. Loading was maintained for 4 days based on preliminary histological observations, indicating maximal osteocyte lacunar enlargement at this time point without significant gingival or periodontal lesions that could influence proteomic differences between groups.

Experimental orthodontic device. (A) Schematic diagram of nickel-titanium arch expansion device used for maxillary first molar separation in rats, activated with a force of 16 gf, as described by Igarashi et al.22 The device had a 5.5 mm transverse span and 5 mm vertical arms; the lower transverse dimension (x) varied depending on maxilla size. (B) Intra-oral view of the device in situ, showing its placement between the upper first molars.

Tissue collection and protein extraction

The animals were euthanized 4 days after device placement using a pentobarbital overdose. Maxillae were excised, and the soft tissues and teeth were removed to minimize contamination from dental proteins. Bones were frozen at −80 °C. For proteomic analysis, all maxillary samples from each group were ground in liquid nitrogen, and the bone powders were homogenized in isoelectric focusing buffer with protease inhibitors. Supernatants obtained after centrifugation (10,000×g, 4 °C) were used for proteomic analysis. Whole maxillae were processed based on the potential for paracrine and mechano-transductive signaling to influence adjacent bone regions within a short experimental timeframe and obtain sufficient protein yield for 2D-PAGE and liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS).

2D-PAGE and image analysis

Protein samples (200 μg) were hydrated on 7 cm Immobiline DryStrip gels (pH 3–10, GE Healthcare) for 24 h. Isoelectric focusing was carried out on an Ettan IPGphor 3 IEF System (16–20 kV/h for 2:44 h), followed by SDS-PAGE using 15 × 13 cm gels (Bio-Rad). Strips were thawed, placed for 10 min in reducing and alkylating equilibration buffer, and run at 100 V until the bands reached the lower edge of the gel). The gels were stained with colloidal Coomassie Blue G250 and scanned for digital analysis.

Protein spot intensities were quantified after total spot volume normalization using the MELANIE 9 software. (Detailed buffer compositions are provided in Supplementary Methods).

Protein identification by LC-MS/MS

Twenty spots were selected and excised (five spots for each gel), reduced (DTT; Sigma-Aldrich), alkylated (iodoacetamide; Sigma-Aldrich), digested with sequencing-grade trypsin (Promega), and analyzed on an LC-MS system comprising an Accela microflow pump (Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, CA, USA) coupled to an LTQ-Orbitrap Velos mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with a nano-electrospray ionization source. Peptide spectra were searched against the Rattus norvegicus UniProt database using Proteome Discoverer 1.4 (Sequest HT).

STRING (v11) was used for PPI analysis with a high-confidence cutoff (score ≥0.7; only interactions above this score were included). The confidence score is the approximate probability that a predicted link exists.

Statistical analysis

Due to small sample size, statistical comparisons of histomorphometry analyses were performed using non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis followed by a paired Dunn's test.

Due to biological availability, no inferential statistical tests were performed for protein spot differences. Therefore, differences in protein spot abundance between groups were expressed as semi-quantitative fold change (FC) values relative to the N (control) group in each spot protein, additionally represented as log2-transformed FC (log2FC). Accordingly, the reported expression changes should be interpreted as exploratory trends rather than statistically validated differential expression.

Results

Validation of T1DM rat model

Seventy-two hours after STZ injection, all diabetic animals (D and DS) showed glucose levels above 600 mg/dL, whereas those of normoglycemic animals remained at 108 ± 14 mg/dL. Hyperglycemia persisted throughout the experimental period, confirming successful induction of T1DM. Histomorphometric analyses were performed on digital images using ImageJ software. The randomly measured lacunar area in the D (112.3 μm2 ± 22.2) and DS (114.4 μm2 ± 35.6) groups were larger than those in the normoglycemic groups (56.1 μm2 ± 21.9; p < 0.001)((Fig. 2A).

(B) Osteocyte lacunae enlargement after 4 days of mechanical loading. (B-1) Normoglycemic + mechanical stress (NS). (B-2) Diabetic + mechanical stress (DS). Black arrows indicate osteocyte lacunae.

Mechanical loading model validation

The nickel-titanium loading devices produced visible interdental widening without periodontal lesions between the upper first molars in both the NS and DS groups by Day 4. No device detachment or distress signs were observed during the experimental period, suggesting that mechanical loading did not introduce major systemic confounders. Histomorphometric analysis revealed osteocyte lacunae enlargement at this time point, consistent with the mechanical response of NS (70.4 μm2 ± 22.7), and DS groups (114.4 μm2 ± 35.6, p < 0.05) (Fig. 2B).

Proteomic profiling

Four 2D-PAGE gels, each corresponding to one experimental group, were obtained. The gels exhibited a consistent number and distribution pattern of protein spots, with a mean total of 271.25 ± 33.7 spots. Gels 1 (N), 2 (D), 3 (NS), and 4 (DS) contain 301, 228, 261, and 295 total spots, respectively. Representative gel images are shown in Fig. 3.

Identification of differentially expressed proteins

Nineteen of the 20 selected protein spots were successfully processed for LC-MS/MS analysis and 39 proteins were identified (Table 1). Five spots were excluded because of identified blood-derived proteins (e.g., hemoglobin subunits, albumin, and fibrinogen). Notable proteins included cytoskeletal components (cytoplasmic actin 2, tropomyosin β chain, and myosin light chain 1/3), metabolic regulators (elongation factor 1 alpha 1 [eEF1A1] and 14-3-3 ζ/δ), chaperones (heat shock cognate 71 kDa protein [HSPA8]), inflammatory mediators (S100-A9 and Galectin-1 [Gal-1]), and oxidative stress-related proteins (thioredoxin [Trx] and peroxiredoxin-2).

Semi-quantitative protein expression patterns

Relative protein abundance was expressed as FC normalized to the control group (N) and represented as log2-transformed values (log2FC), where 0 indicated no change relative to control (Fig. 4). For descriptive interpretation of magnitude of change, log2FC values ≥ +1 or ≤ −1 were considered indicative of marked upregulation or downregulation, respectively. The heatmap revealed distinct condition-specific expression profiles across structural, regulatory, and stress-related proteins. Red indicates increased abundance and blue indicates decreased abundance compared to control. Proteins associated with cytoskeletal organization and contractile function, including troponin I (fast skeletal) and myosin regulatory components, tended to decrease in the D and NS groups, suggesting potential alterations in structural integrity or contractile regulation under these conditions. Additionally, eEF1A1, a key factor involved in translational elongation and cytoskeletal interactions, showed reduced abundance in D and NS, with partial restoration in the DS group, indicating a possible compensatory response.

Conversely, proteins implicated in stress adaptation and signaling pathways, such as 14-3-3 ζ/δ and heat shock cognate 71 kDa, exhibited increased abundance particularly in the NS and DS groups, consistent with activation of cellular stress-response mechanisms. Fibrinogen beta chain and Gal-1 upregulation in DS further suggests modulation of inflammatory or extracellular matrix-associated processes.

Overall, these semi-quantitative patterns suggest coordinated alterations affecting structural organization, translational regulation, and stress-response pathways under diabetic and mechanical stress conditions.

PPI STRING analysis

STRING enrichment analysis yielded a protein-protein interaction network with significant PPI enrichment (p < 0.05). This p-value reflects the probability that the observed number of interactions among the input proteins occurs by chance compared to a random protein set of similar size. Twenty-four proteins formed interconnected clusters related to cytoskeletal organization, translational control, and stress response. Eight proteins did not integrate into the main network at the selected high-confidence threshold (score ≥0.7), suggesting the absence of strong interaction within this data set (Fig. 5).

Discussion

In this study, we identified early proteomic signatures in the maxillary bones of T1DM rats subjected to controlled mechanical stress using 2D-PAGE coupled with MS. Hyperglycemia and mechanical loading appear to reorganize the early molecular program of adaptation, generating a distinct signature not explained by either factor alone. Key alterations involve cytoskeletal adaptation (cytoplasmic actin 2, tropomyosin β chain, and myosin light chain isoforms), translational control (eEF1A1), apoptosis and survival pathways (14-3-3 ζ/δ), and inflammation and oxidative stress regulators (S100-A9, Gal-1, Trx, and HSPA8).

STRING analysis highlighted a non-random PPI network comprising three main functional clusters: (1) cytoskeletal organization, (2) translation and proteostasis, and (3) inflammatory and stress regulation. These clusters do not operate independently, they appear functionally coupled in shaping early mechano-adaptation.

Cytoskeletal proteins and mechano-transduction

The cytoskeletal cluster (composed of actin isoforms, myosin light chains, troponin I, and tropomyosin) likely contributes to osteocyte and osteoclast shape stability, motility, and alterations during differentiation to adapt to mechanical forces in osteocytes. In this study, selected cytoskeletal proteins, particularly tropomyosin and myosin light chain isoforms, were upregulated in mechanically loaded normoglycemic bone (NS), consistent with their role in osteocyte mechano-sensation and osteoclast motility, possibly via active mechanosensory adaptation. In diabetic bone (D), selective downregulation or imbalance of myosin light chains suggests impaired cytoskeletal reorganization. In the DS group, attenuation of the mechanical-induced increase observed in NS suggests that hyperglycemia impairs the cytoskeletal response to loading. Thus, early diabetes may alter the structural foundation required for optimal mechano-sensation.

Translation control and TGF-β signaling

The eEF1A1 protein, a central player in mRNA translation involved in multiple functions,24,25 showed reduced expression in both the NS and D groups, whereas the DS group exhibited a marked increase. This pattern highlighted translational control as a sensitive early target of metabolic and mechanical cues. TGF-β receptor I phosphorylates eEF1A1, thus influencing osteoblast differentiation,26 and AGEs upregulate TGF-β1 expression in osteocytes.27 The DS pattern may reflect complex interactions between metabolic and mechanical signaling pathways, potentially involving TGF-β–related regulation of eEF1A1, although this mechanism cannot be directly inferred from the present data.

Apoptosis inhibition and β-catenin stabilization

The 14-3-3 ζ/δ protein, recognized for β-catenin stabilization and anti-apoptotic activity,28,29 was upregulated under diabetes and mechanical stress conditions. This pattern is consistent with a potential role in modulating β-catenin stability and cell survival under mechanical and metabolic stress conditions. The attenuated increase in the DS group compared with NS suggests that hyperglycemia may modify the magnitude of this adaptive response.

Inflammatory and oxidative stress mediators

S100-A9 is a calcium- and zinc-binding protein that regulates inflammatory and immune responses. In this study, it was under-expressed in all experimental groups compared to the N group. S100-A9 prevents osteoclastogenesis,30 although it can interact with RAGE on osteocytes to promote RANKL and IL-6 expression.31 This pattern suggests that diabetes may influence inflammatory signaling associated with early bone responses to orthodontic forces.

Gal-1, a known immune regulator, is associated with the osteogenic differentiation potential of bone marrow stem cells and osteoclastic differentiation suppression.32,33 In this study, Gal-1 was upregulated under NS, but its attenuation under diabetes indicated that hyperglycemia could interfere with osteogenic-immunomodulatory signaling.

Trx was detected along with Gal-1 in the protein spots. Trx participates in redox reactions and protects cells against oxidative stress. Oxidants stimulate osteoclastic differentiation by inducing Trx expression and increasing the binding of essential transcription factors during differentiation.34 However, in this study, it was not possible to determine its expression profile.

HSPA8 is a molecular chaperone involved in protein folding and proteome protection from stress. In this study, HSPA8 was overexpressed under mechanical loading and remained elevated in the diabetic-loaded condition, consistent with reports of its upregulation in diabetic conditions and platelets,35 possibly as a reflection of the increased proteostatic demand associated with oxidative stress. However, HSPA8 can induce osteoclastogenesis36; therefore, its increased expression in mechanically stimulated groups may be compatible with a pro-osteoclastogenic microenvironment, although functional validation is required.

Another interesting finding was the presence of peptidyl-prolyl cis-trans isomerase A (PPIA). This protein contributes to protein folding. Guo et al.37 reported that PPIA is necessary for RUNX2 and ALP expression in osteoblasts, and associated with anti-osteoclastic activity. In this study, PPIA was underexpressed in the NS group compared to that in the other groups. Therefore, mechanical stress was associated with reduced PPIA expression in N, which may reflect altered osteoblast–osteoclast regulatory balance. However, PPIA expression under diabetic conditions was similar to that observed in controls.

Collectively, these findings suggest that early T1DM induces a redox-proteostatic shift, altering the balance between structural remodeling, translational activity, and stress-response pathways.

Methodological considerations and limitations

Given the exploratory nature of the study and ethical considerations regarding animal use, a sample size of n = 4 per group was used. Although limited in statistical power, differential expression was based on fold-change analysis combined with MS validation. Regarding the mechanical stress model, although force decay over the 4-day period was not continuously monitored, nickel–-titanium alloys are known for relatively stable force delivery within short timeframes.

The absence of classic bone matrix proteins, such as collagen type I or osteocalcin, was likely due to the extraction methodology that targeted the soluble proteome rather than the insoluble extracellular matrix. This approach successfully captured intracellular and signaling proteins responsive to short-term stimuli, consistent with previous in vivo bone proteomic methods.19,20 The 2D-PAGE proteomic approach could limit sensitivity for low-abundance, highly hydrophobic, and extreme molecular weight proteins. Therefore, the present data likely underrepresents other regulatory molecules expressed at low levels. Additionally, only 20 spots were selected for LC-MS/MS, potentially overlooking biologically relevant low-abundance proteins. Future label-free LC-MS/MS or shotgun proteomics would broaden proteome coverage.

Analysis of the whole maxilla limits spatial and cell-type resolution, as detected proteins may derive from multiple bone-resident and infiltrating cell populations. However, this approach captures integrated proteomic responses across interconnected bone regions, likely hidden by the osteocyte lacunocanalicular network architecture. This allows accurate prediction of bone formation in regions distant from the site of mechanical stimulation,38 as activity originating in embedded osteocytes subsequently exerts action on surface osteoblasts,39 supporting long-range mechanochemical signal propagation within bone. However, studying the entire maxilla allows the detection of a wider range of events and may reflect systemic metabolic effects of diabetes rather than bone-specific mechanoadaptation.

Clinical and research implications

The identified proteins may represent future research candidates to explain mechano adaptive mechanisms aimed at improving orthodontic outcomes in patients with T1DM. However, the present data are exploratory, and functional validation in animal models and human studies is necessary before translational applications. Future studies should have a larger sample size and combine spatially resolved proteomics with functional assays to dissect cell-type–specific responses and extend observation periods to track the temporal evolution of the bone proteome under sustained mechanical and metabolic stress.

Conclusions

This exploratory study provides preliminary evidence that T1DM and mechanical loading alter the early proteomic response of the maxillary bone. Rather than simply suppressing bone adaptation, diabetes may induce a dysregulated mechanoadaptive state characterized by altered cytoskeletal organization, disturbed translational control, and increased proteostatic stress.

The semi-quantitative differential expression of key proteins, such as eEF1A1, 14-3-3 ζ/δ, Gal-1, S100-A9, and HSPA8, suggests thatearly hyperglycemia reshapes, rather than abolishes, mechano-responsiveness. These findings support the need for longitudinal, spatially resolved, and functionally validated proteomic studies to clarify how metabolic dysregulation influences periodontal and bone loss risks in patients with T1DM.
