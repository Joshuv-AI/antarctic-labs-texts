Toward Relationality in Infectious Disease Research
Authors: Elliott M Chemberlin, Kate A Duchowny, Jaidyn Probst, Eugene T Richardson, Sonia T Hegde, Grace A Noppert
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEmerging research increasingly links climate change to infectious disease outcomes, including zoonotic transmission and spillover events and destruction of health-supporting infrastructure (ie, housing, nutrition, sanitation, and healthcare). However, Indigenous communities have understood the interdependence of ecological and human health for millennia. This knowledge is encompassed by relationality, an ontological and epistemological stance that revolves around relationships with relatives (including landscapes, animals, plants, humans, ancestors, and spiritual entities). Relational methodologies prioritize interdisciplinary thinking and reciprocity between learners, subjects of interest, and community. Without exploiting or appropriating knowledge from any specific Indigenous community, we illustrate a generalized concept of relationality that is applicable to infectious disease research. Relational methods reveal historic and ongoing colonialism as fundamental causes of both climate change and infectious disease. These issues will never be fully understood without accounting for colonialism and its entanglements with pathogens and the science that studies them. Climate and health research will be improved through application of relational methods and active repair of ongoing colonial violence.Keywords: relationality, indigenous science, climate change, colonialism, infectious disease

Climate change is a growing concern for infectious disease epidemiologists [1–3]. Rising temperatures, floods, deforestation, extreme weather, violent conflict over resources, precarious migration, and other climate-related phenomena contribute to the spread of pathogens as well as the emergence of novel diseases in human populations [3–8]. As these crises intensify with disproportionate impacts on marginalized populations, Indigenous communities are on the frontlines of climate advocacy and action. Indigenous knowledge systems understand that climate and health are interconnected and that historic and ongoing colonialism are fundamental causes of both climate change and disease [9].

The theory of fundamental causes of disease, first published by Link and Phelan in 1995, explains how “upstream” determinants of health contribute to health disparities that persist in the face of biomedical advancements and improvements in social equity and the standard of living [9]. Link and Phelan identify 4 criteria that a phenomenon must meet in order to be considered a fundamental cause of disease: (1) the cause influences multiple disease outcomes; (2) it affects disease outcomes through multiple risk factors and pathways; (3) it mediates access to health-supporting resources that prevent or minimize disease; and (4) the association between the cause and the disease persists even if some intervening mechanisms are modified. Socioeconomic status (SES), the fundamental cause that Link and Phelan identified in their 1995 article, is a fitting example because (1) SES is known to influence multiple disease outcomes (ie, dementia [10] and cytomegalovirus [11]); (2) SES affects disease outcomes through multiple risk factors, for instance diet (by stratifying access to food) or exposure to toxins (by stratifying access to environmentally safe living and working conditions); (3) SES mediates access to resources that prevent or minimize disease, such as healthy food or the resources to navigate insurance and healthcare systems; and (4) SES has been persistently associated with poorer health outcomes for lower-SES communities, despite efforts to intervene upon risk pathways. For example, lower-SES communities in wealthier countries no longer face the same burden from diseases such as cholera and polio as they did in the 19th and 20th centuries due to improved sanitation and vaccine infrastructure, but they still face disproportionate exposure to and mortality from other diseases such as coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) due to persistent underlying risk factors: likelihood of working in higher-risk jobs, lower nutrition, chronic stress, and other results of comparatively lower access to resources. Building on Link and Phelan's original analysis of SES, many researchers have identified other social processes, such as structural racism [12] and colonialism [13, 14], as fundamental causes of disease. In this perspective, we invoke fundamental cause theory to underscore how colonialism affects myriad human and ecological health outcomes through numerous pathways that are, in Indigenous epistemologies, understood to be interdependent.

Although highly diverse and locally specific, Indigenous epistemologies (theories of knowledge and understanding [15]) often share an emphasis on what Opaskwayak Cree scholar Shawn Wilson has identified as relationality. Relationality, according to Wilson, is a concept, method, and ontology (a shared system and theory of essence and being [16]) that emphasizes relationships and the reciprocal responsibilities that come with them [17]. For Indigenous peoples, relationality comprises the ways that relationships between relatives—including land, animals, plants, humans, ancestors, celestial features, spiritual entities, and more—shape and are reality and identity [17, 18]. In many Indigenous cosmologies (particular accounts or systems of the universe and its laws [19]), relationships between people and place mutually and simultaneously bring both people and their places into being; individual, familial, and political identity cannot exist separately from a network of relationships with the land, with community members, and within oneself. In this way, relationality is not a philosophical metaphor or simply a method; it constitutes Indigenous worlds. Relationality also encompasses ways of knowing that are common among, but not necessarily always exclusive to, Indigenous communities. In relational epistemologies, knowledge comes from belonging and participation within a broader network of ecological and spiritual relationships. These epistemologies emphasize reciprocity, community, and thinking through connections and entanglements (ie, relationships) rather than within the confines of disciplinary, politico-geographic, or other types of borders [17, 20, 21]. The disciplinary siloing emblematic of Western knowledge production often obscures connections between processes and ideas whose linkages are already obvious within many Indigenous knowledge systems. Indigenous communities who create and employ relational methods contribute integrated understandings of and interventions to address climate and health. Climate change and infectious disease are clearly 2 interconnected nodes of the same ecosystem when the relationships that concurrently support human and ecological health are fully considered. These linkages will be discussed throughout this essay, as will relational ways of addressing the problems posed by both climate change and infectious disease.

In contrast to relationality, colonialism—an ideology that often guides the actions of governments, researchers, individuals, and other agents—can be understood as a reorientation of relationships toward domination and extraction and away from reciprocity and connection [20, 22, 23]. By pulling the “threads” (including people, plants, animals, landscapes, and ideas) that weave integrated, place-based Indigenous knowledge systems, colonialism attempts to unravel the ecosystem in which Indigenous knowledge is created and transmitted. It thereby attempts to create the very states of intellectual and cultural poverty that it claims defined Indigenous life a priori. The myth that Indigenous people do not and never did hold or create meaningful knowledge continues to justify the replacement or outright historical erasure of Indigenous epistemes. It is in this ideological landscape that Indigenous communities experience extreme epistemic violence as colonial institutions seek to fill a vacuum of their own design and replace Indigenous knowledge with knowledge they claim is more rigorous, more evidence-based, more modern, and more universal. However, these settler attempts at replacement rely upon continued assaults against the relationships that constitute Indigenous reality and that produce Indigenous knowledge. The target, and the opposite, of colonialism is always relationality; colonialism ruptures relationality and relational knowledge systems and then claims that they do not exist. The colonial, anti-relational paradigm of knowledge production is inadequate to address the full scope of the linkages between climate crises and infectious disease. Not only does colonialism drive climate change and infectious disease outbreaks; it also covers its tracks and perpetuates epistemological systems that obscure its responsibility for climate and disease crises, as well as the connections between the two. We posit that relationality offers climate and infectious disease scientists an alternative way forward by naming colonialism as a fundamental cause of both infectious disease and climate change, and as an ideology that obfuscates the connections between them [9].

COLONIALISM AS A FUNDAMENTAL CAUSE OF INFECTIOUS DISEASE AND CLIMATE CHANGE

Historically and presently, colonizers directly spread disease through interaction with Native populations, sexual violence, biological warfare, and other mechanisms. Historical examples of this include the distribution of smallpox-infected blankets to North American Indigenous communities and the spread of cholera by the British East India Company from the Indian subcontinent to Africa and the Americas [24, 25]. Colonial forces also spread and exacerbate disease through less direct, though no less insidious methods, such as military occupation, resource and wealth extraction, engineering of substandard living conditions, withholding of medical care and supplies, and destruction of Indigenous foodways, medicines, mobility, and relationships [24]. Colonial institutions harm the biological, cultural, spiritual, and epistemological health of entire ecosystems through their insistence on division rather than relationality. They seek to separate people from their places (ie, through Indian removal policy, the transatlantic slave trade, or the destruction of ecosystems that support biological and cultural life), people from their families (ie, through chattel slavery, or the US and Canadian Indian boarding school systems), and people from themselves (ie, through forced Christianization and assimilation, and the pervasive infliction of trauma). Because these relationships are an integral part of Indigenous ontology and interdependent socioecological health, their denigration by colonial forces never affects only one species, one location, one point in time, or one health outcome; it is an outright intergenerational attack on material and metaphysical Indigenous existence and worldview. By targeting health-supporting relational ontologies, colonial regimes entrap colonized populations in states of illness as a tactic of maintaining power and control [18, 24, 26].

Colonialism is not just an unfortunate historical event; it is ongoing today, as are its disease-causing capacities. For example, Israel's decimation of housing, healthcare, and sanitation facilities and blockade of medicine, food, water, and vaccines represents an immediate cause of the reemergence of polio and the proliferation of other diseases in Gaza [27–29]. Additionally, ongoing treaty violations, underresourcing, and settler colonial occupation represent upstream, structural causes of current susceptibility to COVID-19, tuberculosis, and other infectious diseases in North American Indigenous communities [24, 30, 31].

Colonialism is also a primary cause of anthropogenic climate change [26]. Colonialism appropriates land (which we take to include plants, animals, minerals, water, and the land itself) and reinscribes it as a resource rather than a relative, as it is understood in many Indigenous epistemologies (where plants, animals, landscapes, celestial features, and more can all be relatives of humans and of each other) [20]. Colonial governments and neocolonial actors (such as oil, mining, and logging industries) unsustainably exploit the ecosystems they occupy, driving climate change and reproducing Indigenous dispossession. In a particularly relevant example, deforestation and mining in the Amazon rainforest not only contribute to local ecological collapse and global warming, but also encroach on Indigenous lands and expose workers to mosquito-borne and other infectious diseases [4]. Another example of colonial-capitalist devastation of Indigenous ecosystems is investigated in detail by anthropologist Sophie Chao in her book In the Shadow of the Palms: More-Than-Human Becomings in West Papua. Chao writes about the oil palm, a common agribusiness monocrop with particularly notorious climate impacts, and its arrival and conceptualization in the Indonesian province of West Papua [32]. Beyond the oil palm's well-evidenced use by colonial and neoliberal industrial actors to aid in wealth extraction and land occupation, Chao explores how Indigenous Marind communities in West Papua also make sense of the oil palm as a salient foreign actor that is reconfiguring the biological, ecological, social, and spiritual relationships that constitute Marind worlds. This relational perspective highlights just how deep and diverse the oil palm's impacts are on Marind and global ecosystems, while also emphasizing that the oil palm itself is a being with needs, desires, and motives that align with the extractive ethics of colonialism.

Anthropogenic climate change resulting from logging, mining, oil palm culture, and many other colonial and extractive industries has been widely demonstrated to facilitate the emergence and spread of zoonotic viruses [4, 33]. For example, research on Nipah virus has demonstrated that climate-related factors, including deforestation, have resulted in migration pattern changes of the natural reservoir, Pteropus giganteus bats [33]. This has increased bat contact with human food sources, and thus the frequency of zoonotic transmission events, thereby ensuring the continuation of Nipah outbreaks [33]. Furthermore, the global increase of climate events, such as floods and wildfires, has also contributed to displacement, poor air and water quality, food insecurity, infrastructure failure, and other processes that exacerbate disease symptoms and spread and limit access to care, especially for colonized and otherwise oppressed populations.

Relational frameworks clearly illustrate that colonialism—the violent disruption of relational ontologies and epistemologies—is a fundamental cause of the interrelated socioecological processes of climate crises and infectious disease. Climate and health justice are inextricably linked, and they cannot be achieved without Indigenous communities, who experience and address these issues as deeply interconnected. Relational frameworks are crucial for researchers who are interested in the linkages between climate change and infectious disease as these problems continue to intensify in tandem. Without appropriating culturally specific relationalities from communities of which we are not part, researchers can learn broadly from relational methods and interventions that have been publicly shared, and apply them to their own research and community contexts. We provide more applied examples of relational climate and disease interventions in the next section. Although inapplicable to every community context, they all illustrate how different communities may operationalize relational models in a way that challenges dominant research paradigms while advocating for specific community health goals.

RELATIONAL APPROACHES TO INFECTIOUS DISEASE MANAGEMENT

A relational methodology in climate and infectious disease research requires consideration of pathogens and vectors as dynamic mediators in a determinative web of colonial abuses that stretch back in time and link distant continents [34]. Researchers interested in the intersection of climate change and infectious disease may enrich their work by incorporating social and historical analysis that reveals pathogens and vectors not only as biomedical causes of specific diseases, but as dynamic actors that signal social and ecological processes (such as colonialism and resultant climate change) that affect health. Pathogens and vectors are not arbitrary and universally bad actors, nor are they mere foot soldiers co-opted into colonial systems of power; they can also be important and agentic members of their own ecosystems. Bats, for example, who take the blame for diseases like rabies and Nipah virus, are critical pollinators and insect population mediators; bacteria from the Clostridia family (including Clostridium botulinum, which causes botulism) are vital decomposers in many ecosystems and are not typically toxic to scavengers like they are to humans. Even the oil palm, which has devastated ecological, physical, and spiritual health for Marind communities in West Papua, is conceptualized by those communities as an empathy-deserving being with its own mysterious needs and desires [32]. The belief in eradication—or, where eradication is impossible, total isolation—of disease threat as the gold standard for disease control assumes that some parts of our ecosystem should not exist, or should not exist in any kind of relationship with humans, because they do not always benefit human health. This anthropocentric model, as opposed to relational models in which all members of ecosystems can be relatives, truncates the relational capacity of nonhuman beings and ignores the interrelated needs and responsibilities of animals and environments that harbor pathogens, as well as of the pathogens themselves.

Infectious disease interventions that suggest distancing from humans, animals, and the land as a disease prevention method can be harmful to Indigenous communities and unfeasible for the global population. For instance, avoidance of vector habitats (ie, mosquitoes in rainforests) may not be acceptable to communities who understand those landscapes as relatives. In a similar vein, the harvesting and consumption of wild animals and preserved meats has been identified as a facilitator of spillover events, but is also a necessity in many Indigenous communities [4, 24]. The traditional harvesting and consumption of animal relatives is an aspect of food sovereignty, a treaty right, and a culturally important, holistic health-supporting activity [18, 22, 26]. Further, bushmeat consumption is often crucial for food security in communities that are denied access to quality grocery stores by colonial geographic and economic structures [24, 26]. We posit that relational interventions can counter the eradication/isolation paradigm by promoting all interdependent aspects of ecological health and maintaining healthy relationships under changing environmental conditions.

One excellent example of a relational climate and health intervention related to food harvesting comes from British Columbia, Canada, where shellfish are important relatives to and food sources for many Indigenous communities. Shellfish are also widely known to be susceptible to contamination with a variety of pathogens that harm human health, including viruses, bacteria, parasites, and marine biotoxins [35]. These illnesses disproportionately impact Indigenous communities that rely heavily on the fish and shellfish harvest as a nutritionally, economically, and culturally vital source of food [36]. In British Columbia, the federal Canadian Shellfish Sanitation Program routinely closes or limits access to coastal fisheries based on environmental quality testing [35]. This approach, which seeks to protect humans by curbing their access to the environment, has trespassed upon First Nations' ability to maintain their relationships with the shellfish and the landscape [35]. First Nations in British Columbia have protected their food sovereignty and relational health goals by developing alternative safety procedures, such as the W̱SÁNEĆ Clam Garden Restoration Project and We All Take Care of the Harvest (WATCH). These Indigenous-led projects both focus on holistic community and ecological health to address the interrelated threats of climate change, ongoing settler colonialism, and contamination of shellfish beds with biological pathogens and chemical pollutants. Both projects have helped restore shellfish beds that have been harmed by colonial environmental degradation [37, 38]. They have also created community-based guidelines for testing and safe harvesting to expand Indigenous sovereignty in shellfish maintenance, and they have revitalized community members' traditional knowledge and relationships with the shellfish and the land [37, 38]. For Indigenous communities, strengthening relationships with a changing landscape delivers on biological, cultural, human, and ecosystem health goals, whereas preventing ecological engagement under the guise of health management only perpetuates the coloniality that has been so detrimental to holistic Indigenous health. Recognizing pathogens and vectors as members of ecosystems and therefore as relatives opens up new ways of conceptualizing responsibility. Beyond the isolation/eradication paradigm, communities can think creatively about what the environment needs, so that it in turn has the capacity to meet their needs.

The isolation/eradication paradigm of infectious disease management is anti-relational, both in its relatively siloed emphasis on human health and in its conceptualization of infected beings as threats. In the recent examples of the Ebola virus epidemic and the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)/AIDS and COVID-19 pandemics, scientists and clinicians, and subsequently news outlets and the general public, applied terms like “vector” and “superspreader” to infected persons [39]. Framing disease in this way assigns guilt, blame, and stigma to individuals who are often already marginalized and thus more susceptible to infection and reduced access to structural and interpersonal care. For example, the ideas that the Chinese government leaked severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) from a laboratory in an act of biological warfare [40], that the LGBTQIAA+ community wanted to spread HIV/AIDS to the general public, that individuals should practice abstinence to avoid sexually transmitted infections, or that those who refuse vaccination deserve to get sick and die, all imply personal choice in the disease process by suggesting that individual behavior alone can determine disease outcomes. We posit that these words give rise to stigma by implying—either explicitly or not—that individuals are responsible for spreading a disease by virtue of having it. In other words, conceptualizing disease as a “choice” is not relational both because it denies empathy and care to the sick, and because it fails to capture the interconnected social, historical, and environmental determinants of disease such as colonialism, which should be identified and intervened upon in order to improve Indigenous and global health.

There are many contemporary Indigenous-led initiatives to learn from that operationalize relationality to reduce stigma, expand access to care, identify structural determinants of health, and center Indigenous values in care. Methodologically, these initiatives emphasize community-based and asset-based research, as well as traditional pedagogy [26, 41]. Community-based research models, such as community-based participatory research [42], allow the community to decide what questions to ask, what methods to use, and what results should be, ensuring that they control their data and decide upon research that will be generally beneficial. In a similar vein, asset-based research works to counter deficit-based and damage-centered research that has long been the status quo and portrayed Indigenous communities as perpetually lacking, broken, and problems to be fixed [43, 44]. Traditional pedagogy may encompass a variety of teaching, thinking, and learning methods that are community-specific and integral to meaningful knowledge production and problem solving in a local context. Taken together, these methodological interventions can protect against the extractive, damage-centered, and coercively Western research paradigms that marginalize Indigenous people and systems of knowledge [23, 43]. Indigenous practitioners also incorporate aspects of relationality in managing infectious disease. They emphasize land-based learning, language revitalization, interdependent community health, amelioration of shame and stigma, and cultural values of care. Examples of such efforts include Chee Mamuk, a British Columbia–based sexual health initiative; the Navajo & Hopi Families COVID Relief fund, a fundraising, education, and resource allocation organization that serves the Navajo and Hopi Nations; the CheckUp Project, an Inuit-focused blood-borne infection prevention initiative; and the Tribal Vaccine Equity Project, a vaccine access initiative administered by the National Native Network and the Inter-tribal Council of Michigan. These projects are all part of a broader constellation of diverse Indigenous-led efforts to reclaim and revitalize relationships with land, language, community, and culture—factors that coalesce to support interdependent human and more-than-human health.

CONCLUSIONS

Indigenous knowledge systems and relational methodologies understand that land, humans, animals, plants, microbes, social processes, culture, and knowledge are all interconnected. Colonialism is both a fundamental cause of climate change and climate-related infectious disease, and an epistemology that actively oppresses relational ways of knowing. Relational methodologies elucidate the deep connections between colonialism, climate change, and infectious disease. Such methods can also begin to counter the scientific establishment's legacy as an extractive colonial agent [17, 20]. Furthermore, the return of land to Indigenous communities is a critical, reparative intervention in the joint fight against climate change and infectious disease. This is not strictly a policy intervention; climate and health researchers can use their positions to advocate for Indigenous sovereignty, too. The revitalization of sovereign Indigenous relationships to territory and the consideration of relationality as a valid methodological and ontological framework are structural-level interventions that will undoubtedly improve health for the communities most directly harmed by colonialism as well as population health for the collective. Relationality has the power to challenge the coloniality that oppresses Indigenous communities, drives climate change and infectious disease, and upholds myopic and inequitable visions of global health.
