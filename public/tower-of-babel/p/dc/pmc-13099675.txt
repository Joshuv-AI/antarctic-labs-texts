Metabolomics-guided Mechanisms of Mesenchymal Stromal Cell and Exosome Therapies in Chronic Diseases
Authors: Asma Ismail Mahmod, Kayatri Govindaraju, Yogeswaran Lokanathan, Nur Akmarina, B. M. Said, Baharudin Ibrahim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) and their extracellular vesicles (EVs) are promising therapies across cardiovascular, inflammatory, metabolic, and neurodegenerative diseases. Metabolomics has revealed how MSCs/EVs reshape host metabolism, but current studies remain fragmented, small, and largely descriptive. Synthesizing evidence across disease models, we identify conserved metabolic checkpoints, succinate and α-ketoglutarate in the TCA cycle, acylcarnitines in fatty acid β-oxidation, and glutathione in redox balance, as recurrent targets linked to therapeutic benefit. Distinct signatures, including polyamine metabolism, bile acid–FXR/TGR5 signaling, and neurotransmitter regulation, provide disease-specific insights. Yet, most studies lack validation and reproducibility, limiting clinical translation. Progress requires larger datasets, targeted assays, integration into randomized trials, and GMP-aligned pipelines. We propose a Metabolomics-Derived MSC Potency Index, a standardized metabolite panel benchmarked against functional assays, as a framework for therapeutic readiness. This approach positions metabolomics not as a descriptive tool but as a translational benchmark guiding MSC/EV therapy development.Graphical abstract

Introduction

Metabolic dysregulation lies at the heart of a broad spectrum of chronic and degenerative diseases, driving inflammation, oxidative stress, and tissue degeneration across diverse systems, from cardiovascular and neurodegenerative conditions to liver disease, inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), diabetes, lung disorders, and osteoarthritis [1–8]. Despite advances in conventional therapies, these conditions often remain resistant, underscoring the need for interventions that can re-establish systemic metabolic balance rather than merely alleviating symptoms. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) and their extracellular vesicles (EVs) have gained significant attention as multifunctional therapeutic agents owing to their regenerative capacity, immunomodulatory properties, and secreted bioactive cargo [9]. Increasing evidence now suggests that their therapeutic benefits extend beyond immune regulation, as they directly influence fundamental metabolic pathways such as glycolysis, fatty acid oxidation, amino acid metabolism, and the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle [10–13]. Importantly, recent preclinical studies demonstrate that MSC- and EV-based therapies can normalize disease-specific metabolic alterations, for instance, MSC-EVs rebalancing systemic metabolites in metabolic syndrome [14], adipose-derived MSCs promoting lipid catabolism through AMPK activation [15], and umbilical cord MSCs restoring ovarian metabolic profiles linked to fertility [16].

At the mechanistic level, metabolomics provides a unifying analytical framework to interrogate the distinct yet interconnected metabolic actions of MSCs and MSC- EVs. MSC-based therapies influence host metabolism through cell–cell interactions, paracrine signaling, and dynamic adaptation to the injured microenvironment, including mitochondrial transfer and modulation of local metabolic fluxes in target tissues such as heart, liver, and immune organs [17, 18]. In contrast, EV-based therapies act primarily through the delivery of metabolically active cargo, including enzymes, lipids, amino acids, metabolites, and regulatory RNAs, which can reprogram recipient cell metabolism independently of the presence of viable cells [19, 20]. For example, metabolomic profiling has been used to characterize metabolic reprogramming induced by viable MSCs, including normalization of dysregulated amino acid and lipid metabolites in tissue injury models and restoration of TCA cycle intermediates, while EV metabolomics reveals characteristic cargo compositions that reflect specific lipid, amino acid, and small metabolite signatures associated with therapeutic activities or disease states in recipient cells [21]. Importantly, despite these biological distinctions, convergent metabolic outcomes have been observed across both platforms, such as correction of tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle dysregulation, restoration of redox balance via glutathione metabolism, and normalization of lipid and amino acid perturbations in chronic disease models, supporting the concept that metabolomic profiling can serve not only as a mechanistic discovery tool but also as a standardized translational readout to compare and benchmark MSC- and EV-based therapeutic strategies [22, 23].

However, while the metabolic effects of MSCs and EVs are increasingly recognized, the metabolomic dimension of their therapeutic activity remains fragmented and largely descriptive. Most studies focus on single disease contexts without identifying recurring metabolic checkpoints or establishing validated biomarkers that can support translation. Accordingly, this review focuses on disease areas in which sufficient preclinical metabolomics evidence is available to enable meaningful mechanistic synthesis, while conditions with limited or highly heterogeneous data are discussed more selectively. By synthesizing metabolomic evidence across preclinical models, contrasting conserved versus disease-specific pathways, and proposing a framework for advancing MSC/EV metabolomics from exploratory profiling to a benchmark for clinical readiness, this review aims to address these current gaps.

Core Metabolic Pathways and their Dysregulation in Chronic Disease

Metabolism encompasses a network of interconnected biochemical pathways that supply cellular energy, generate biosynthetic precursors, and maintain redox balance. Among these, glycolysis, the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) are central hubs regulating cell function and survival. Under physiological conditions, these pathways operate in a tightly coordinated manner, allowing cells to switch between energy sources depending on nutrient availability and functional demands [24, 25].

In chronic inflammatory and degenerative diseases, this metabolic balance is disrupted, a phenomenon termed metabolic reprogramming. Such reprogramming is not merely a bystander effect but an active driver of disease progression, influencing immune cell activation, tissue remodeling, and fibrosis [26, 27]. Below, we summarize the major metabolic pathways and the characteristic dysregulations observed in chronic pathologies.

Glycolysis & Oxidative Phosphorylation (OXPHOS)

Glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) are the primary pathways for cellular energy production. Glycolysis, an oxygen-independent process, converts glucose into pyruvate, generating ATP less efficiently than OXPHOS. However, glycolysis is rapidly upregulated in pro-inflammatory immune cells to meet the high energy demand and provide biosynthetic intermediates required for activation and growth [28, 29]. This metabolic reprogramming, a hallmark of immune activation, enables a swift response but can also drive excessive inflammation in conditions such as autoimmune diseases and severe viral infections. By sustaining glycolytic flux, immune cells amplify cytokine production, contributing to hyperinflammatory states, including “cytokine storms.” Modulating glycolysis, either by enhancing or suppressing it, therefore represents a promising strategy to adjust immune responses in both physiological and pathological contexts [30–32]. Under normal conditions, glycolysis-derived pyruvate fuels the TCA cycle, generating succinate, which donates electrons to complex II for oxidative phosphorylation. In chronic disease or hypoxia, mitochondrial dysfunction leads to succinate accumulation, driving inflammation, ROS production, and HIF-1α activation. In M1 macrophages, elevated succinate further stabilizes HIF-1α, enhancing IL-1β production and sustaining the inflammatory response [33]. In addition, metabolic enzymes such as 6-phosphofructo-2-kinase/fructose-2,6-bisphosphatase 3 (PFKFB3) and pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2) are upregulated during immune activation. These enzymes not only regulate glycolytic flux but also participate in pro-inflammatory signalling, with PKM2 functioning as a coactivator of HIF-1α to drive cytokine expression [34, 35].

Amino Acid Metabolism

Amino acid metabolism is fundamental to numerous physiological processes, including energy production, redox balance, cellular signaling, immune regulation, and tissue repair [36]. Key amino acids such as arginine, proline, tryptophan, and glutamine serve as metabolic substrates and signaling molecules that influence cell function across various systems. In this context, altered arginine metabolism changes nitric oxide (NO) availability and polyamine synthesis, affecting vascular tone, fibrosis and immune activation. Alternatively, arginine can be metabolized by arginase to produce ornithine and urea, a pathway often upregulated in anti-inflammatory (M2) macrophages [37]. Proline is closely linked to collagen synthesis and redox balance, making it essential in wound healing and extracellular matrix remodelling. Meanwhile, tryptophan is catabolized primarily via the kynurenine pathway, where the enzyme indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO) converts tryptophan into kynurenine. This pathway has significant immunosuppressive effects, as elevated kynurenine levels can inhibit T cell proliferation and promote regulatory T cell (Treg) development [38, 39]. Finally, glutamine, a key nitrogen and carbon donor for anaplerosis (replenishing TCA cycle intermediates), is modulated by MSCs to fuel mitochondrial metabolism and antioxidant defense during immune activation [36, 37, 40]. It plays a central role in maintaining nitrogen balance, generating energy through conversion to α-ketoglutarate (α-KG), and synthesizing glutathione (GSH), the cell’s major antioxidant. Under pathological conditions such as osteoarthritis or ischemic injury, impaired glutaminolysis, elevated ROS, and depleted GSH levels contribute to chronic inflammation and cell death [41, 42]. Glutamine also regulates immune responses by modulating cytokine production, including Tumor Necrosis Factor-alpha (TNF-α) and Interleukin-6 (IL-6), and supporting lymphocyte proliferation partly through the activation of extracellular signal-Regulated Kinase (ERK) and c-Jun N-terminal Kinase (JNK) pathway [42, 43]. In neutrophils, it is primarily converted to glutamate, enhancing superoxide production and antimicrobial activity, while in macrophages, its availability shapes cytokine output and promotes anti-inflammatory M2 polarization via α-KG [43–45]. Due to its diverse regulatory functions, dysregulated glutamine metabolism has been implicated in cancer, cardiovascular diseases, neurodegeneration, and chronic inflammatory disorders, highlighting it as a potential target for MSC- or EV-based therapies [36].

Lipid Metabolism & Fatty Acid Oxidation (FAO)

Lipid metabolism refers to the complex processes by which lipids are synthesized, stored, and broken down to meet the body’s energy and structural needs. Among its key components, the balance between lipid synthesis and fatty acid oxidation (FAO) plays an important role in maintaining energy homeostasis and regulating immune responses [46]. FAO occurs in mitochondria and peroxisomes, where enzymes like carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 A (CPT1A) and acyl-CoA dehydrogenases oxidize long-chain fatty acids to produce ATP. Conversely, lipid synthesis involves enzymes such as acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC) and fatty acid synthase (FAS), which drive the production of fatty acids for storage or membrane formation [46, 47]. A central regulator of this balance is the AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), which, upon activation in response to cellular energy stress, shifts metabolism toward energy production by enhancing FAO and suppressing lipogenesis [48, 49]. AMPK achieves this by phosphorylating and inhibiting ACC, thereby reducing malonyl-CoA levels and relieving the inhibition on CPT1A, a rate-limiting enzyme in mitochondrial FAO [46, 47, 50]. Additionally, AMPK activation promotes the transcriptional activity of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha (PPAR-α), which upregulates genes involved in FAO, such as CPT1A and acyl-CoA oxidases. At the same time, AMPK downregulates lipogenic signaling by inhibiting sterol regulatory element-binding protein-1c (SREBP-1c) and fatty acid synthase (FAS), effectively reducing fatty acid synthesis [48, 51, 52]. Impaired FAO causes lipid accumulation (steatosis), lipotoxicity and mitochondrial stress; excessive FAO or dysregulated FAO in certain cells (e.g., tumor cells, some immune subsets) can support pathological proliferation or alter immune effector function [53]. This coordinated regulation maintains lipid homeostasis while also contributing to anti-inflammatory and tissue-protective outcomes.

TCA Cycle Intermediates & Redox Homeostasis

The tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, also known as the Krebs or citric acid cycle, is a central metabolic pathway responsible for generating energy through the oxidation of acetyl-CoA [54]. In addition to ATP production, the TCA cycle generates key intermediates such as citrate, α-KG, succinate, fumarate, and malate, which are critical not only for biosynthesis but also for regulating redox homeostasis [55]. These intermediates influence the cellular redox environment by modulating levels of NADH/NAD⁺ and FADH₂/FAD, essential cofactors for mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and reactive oxygen species (ROS) balance [55, 56]. Disruptions in the TCA cycle can lead to mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and aberrant inflammatory responses. One key mechanism involves succinate, which accumulates in activated macrophages due to blocked activity of enzymes like isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) and succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) when cells shift toward glycolysis [57, 58]. Increased levels of succinate can compete with α-KG and inhibit prolyl hydroxylase domain (PHD) enzymes, which are responsible for hydroxylating HIF-1α. This inhibition prevents the degradation of HIF-1α, resulting in its accumulation even in the presence of normal oxygen levels, a condition known as pseudohypoxia [57, 59]. Succinate also promotes reverse electron transport (RET) in mitochondria, generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) that further inhibit PHDs and amplify HIF-1α activity and IL-1β transcription [57, 60, 61]. In contrast, α-KG, another TCA intermediate, exerts anti-inflammatory effects. It supports M2 macrophage polarization by inhibiting the mTORC1/p70S6K pathway, activating PPARγ, and promoting transcription of genes involved in fatty acid oxidation including CPT1A, thereby driving immune homeostasis [62]. α-KG also enhances activity of α-KG-dependent dioxygenases (such as PHDs and histone demethylases), which helps maintain redox balance, supports epigenetic regulation, and represses pro-inflammatory gene expression [57]. Importantly, elevated ROS resulting from TCA dysfunction activates the Nrf2 (nuclear factor erythroid 2–related factor 2) pathway, a master regulator of antioxidant responses. Under oxidative stress, Nrf2 translocates to the nucleus and upregulates antioxidant genes including heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), NAD(P)H quinone oxidoreductase 1 (NQO1), and glutathione biosynthesis enzymes. This Nrf2/HO-1 axis plays a vital role in counteracting ROS-induced damage and restoring redox homeostasis, particularly under metabolic stress conditions [63, 64].

Choline Metabolism

The choline metabolic pathway plays a central role in regulating membrane biosynthesis, methylation reactions, and cellular signaling [65, 66]. Choline is metabolized into phosphocholine, glycerophosphocholine, and betaine, with phosphatidylcholine (PC) synthesized via the CDP-choline pathway, catalyzed by choline kinase (CHKA) and other enzymes. PC is essential for cellular membrane integrity, while betaine functions as a methyl donor in the BHMT (betaine-homocysteine methyltransferase) reaction, lowering homocysteine levels and supporting redox homeostasis [67–69]. Choline deficiency or altered choline flux impairs membrane repair, dysregulates lipoprotein secretion (promoting hepatic steatosis), and disturbs methylation reactions with downstream epigenetic effects [65]. Microbiome-mediated choline metabolism (e.g., conversion to TMA/TMAO) also links choline to cardiovascular risk and systemic inflammation. Disruption of choline–PC pathways therefore impact membrane signaling, lipid homeostasis and epigenetic control [70, 71]. Metabolomics Tool and Standardization for MSC and EV Research

Understanding how MSCs and EVs reprogram metabolism requires analytical tools capable of capturing complex biochemical shifts with precision. Metabolomics addresses this need by systematically identifying and quantifying small endogenous metabolites to reveal disease-related changes. These metabolites including lipids, amino acids, and sugars are structurally diverse, making their analysis challenging. Advanced platforms such as nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), mass spectrometry (MS), capillary electrophoresis (CE), and Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) provide the sensitivity and resolution necessary to track these dynamic alterations (A. Zhang et al., 2012). Additionally, both targeted and untargeted strategies are used, targeted approaches quantify predefined metabolites with high specificity, whereas untargeted approaches profile a wide range of metabolites to enable novel biomarker discovery [72, 73]. NMR spectroscopy is widely valued for its reproducibility and ability to quantify metabolites without extensive sample preparation [74, 75]. Major metabolomics platforms with their advantages, limitations, and applications in stem cell research

While each metabolomics platform brings unique strengths to MSC and EV research, their use in preclinical studies also introduces differences in sensitivity, coverage, and reproducibility that directly affect the reliability of reported findings [109–111]. Importantly, metabolomic profiling of MSCs and MSC-derived EVs reflects distinct biological processes and therefore requires independent analytical consideration: MSC metabolomics primarily captures intracellular metabolic states and adaptive responses to differentiation or stress, whereas MSC-EV metabolomics interrogates selectively packaged metabolites linked to EV biogenesis, cargo sorting, and paracrine signaling, often at lower abundance and with greater susceptibility to contamination [112–114]. In this context, extracellular vesicles represent a heterogeneous population encompassing exosomes, microvesicles, and apoptotic bodies, each differing in biogenesis, size, membrane lipid composition, and molecular cargo. This intrinsic heterogeneity has direct implications for EV metabolomics, as vesicle subtype, density, and membrane composition can influence metabolite abundance, detection sensitivity, and susceptibility to co-isolated contaminants [115]. As a result, variability in EV isolation, enrichment, and purification strategies remains a major contributor to inter-study inconsistency and must be carefully considered when interpreting EV-associated metabolic signatures [116, 117]. Moreover, MSC-derived EVs retain tissue-of-origin molecular signatures reflective of their parent cells, which can influence downstream metabolic reprogramming in recipient tissues and contribute to disease-specific therapeutic responses. Importantly, metabolomic changes observed following EV treatment may not exclusively reflect direct metabolite transfer. In several contexts, EV-delivered enzymes, regulatory RNAs, or signaling molecules may instead indirectly reprogram host metabolic pathways. Distinguishing between these mechanisms is essential for accurate interpretation of metabolomics data and for clarifying the relative contributions of MSCs versus MSC-derived EVs [118–120].

For example, NMR offers high reproducibility and quantitative consistency across laboratories but often misses low-abundance metabolites that may be critical in early disease stages. In contrast, LC-MS provides broad coverage and sensitivity, yet variability in ionization efficiency and sample preparation can make cross-study comparisons challenging, particularly in Ev metabolomics where standardized isolation protocols are lacking. GC-MS remains robust for fatty acid and volatile metabolite profiling but suffers from limited derivatization standards, complicating reproducibility between disease models. CE-MS shows promise in rare-sample and single-cell metabolomics but requires improved technical standardization to reduce inter-lab variability, while FT-IR, although rapid and high-throughput, still struggles with specificity and environmental sensitivity (Table 1). These methodological differences explain, at least in part, why preclinical MSC/EV studies sometimes report divergent metabolic signatures, even within similar disease models. Addressing this variability will require harmonized protocols, cross-platform validation, and broader adoption of underused but promising approaches such as single-cell metabolomics and stable-isotope tracing [111, 121]. As summarized in Table 1, future progress depends not only on selecting the most appropriate platform for a given question but also on establishing reproducible pipelines that enable validated biomarker discovery and facilitate translation from preclinical models into clinically relevant settings.

MSC and EV Therapies in Disease Contexts: Metabolomic Evidence and Mechanisms

Building on the overview of metabolic dysregulation and analytical platforms, this section examines preclinical evidence on how MSCs and EVs reshape metabolic networks in disease models. Metabolomics studies using LC–MS, NMR, and related approaches reveal disease-specific shifts linked to reduced inflammation, restored homeostasis, and improved functional outcomes [111, 122]. Where possible, metabolomic signatures arising from MSC-based interventions and MSC-derived EV therapies are discussed independently, and mechanistic interpretations explicitly distinguish direct cellular metabolic effects from those plausibly mediated by EV-driven paracrine signaling. Collectively, findings across cardiovascular, metabolic, inflammatory, and degenerative disorders highlight mechanistic insights supporting the translational promise of MSC- and EV-based therapies.

Energetic and Vascular Remodeling Disorders

Cardiovascular Diseases (CVD)

Across cardiovascular disease models, metabolomics studies consistently identify convergent modulation of amino acid metabolism, mitochondrial energy pathways, lipid remodeling, and redox balance following MSC- and MSC-EV–based interventions, despite differences in disease etiology. Mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) have emerged as promising candidates for treating cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), including heart failure and myocardial infarction. Their therapeutic potential largely stems from the secretion of bioactive factors that enhance angiogenesis, reduce cell death, and influence immune pathways. Increasing evidence highlights the critical role of MSC-derived extracellular vesicles (EVs) in mediating these effects [123–125]. These EVs, rich in proteins, lipids, RNAs, and metabolites, contribute significantly to cardiac repair, with microRNAs within them particularly noted for promoting angiogenesis in ischemic heart conditions, offering a novel therapeutic strategy for CVD [126].

Myocardial Infarction

Several key metabolic and molecular biomarkers implicated in CVD pathogenesis are influenced by MSCs and their EVs. Among these pathways, ornithine metabolism has been highlighted as a key contributor to the angiogenic and reparative effects of MSCs in CVD. Decidual MSCs (DMSCs), compared to bone marrow-derived MSCs (BMSCs), demonstrated superior promotion of vascular regeneration and cardiac remodeling following myocardial infarction (MI), largely due to enhanced activation of ornithine decarboxylase (ODC)-dependent metabolic processes. Inhibition of ODC in DMSCs diminished these benefits, underscoring the importance of ornithine metabolism in mediating their therapeutic efficacy [123]. Ornithine decarboxylase (ODC) is a key enzyme in the polyamine biosynthesis pathway, converting ornithine into putrescine, which is subsequently metabolized into spermidine and spermine. These polyamines are essential for cell proliferation, angiogenesis, and tissue repair. In the context of myocardial infarction (MI), enhanced ODC activity supports neovascularization in the damaged heart tissue, improves cardiomyocyte survival, and facilitates extracellular matrix remodeling. Conversely, impaired ornithine metabolism can hinder vascular repair and promote adverse remodeling, contributing to the progression of heart failure post-MI [127]. In parallel to the role of ornithine metabolism, bile acid signaling has emerged as another metabolic pathway influencing cardiac repair mechanisms post-MI. A recent study demonstrated that overexpression of the bile acid receptor farnesoid X receptor (FXR) in adipose-derived MSCs (ADSCs) significantly enhanced their retention and cardioprotective effects after intramyocardial injection in MI models. FXR activation promoted paracrine angiogenesis via Angptl4 secretion and reduced apoptosis through Nqo-1 upregulation, particularly in the presence of endogenous FXR ligands such as cholic acid and deoxycholic acid. Bile acid-targeted metabolomics of ischemic heart tissue identified 19 bioactive bile acids in the myocardial microenvironment, underscoring their regulatory role in ADSC function [128]. In addition, other strategies have leveraged the metabolic crosstalk between the heart and peripheral systems. In a myocardial infarction model, exosomes derived from human placental mesenchymal stem cells (PMSC-Exos) improved cardiac function and reduced fibrosis, partly by reshaping gut microbiota composition and increasing levels of beneficial short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs). These changes were linked to reduced pro-inflammatory cytokines and improved lipid metabolism, suggesting that PMSC-Exos mediate their therapeutic effects through gut-derived metabolic and immune modulation [129]. Expanding on this, a metabolomics study demonstrated that melatonin-pretreated MSC-derived EVs (MT-EVs) enhanced protection against hypoxia/reoxygenation injury in endothelial cells. UHPLC-QE-MS/MS revealed 932 differential metabolites, mainly involved in ABC transporters, purine metabolism, and glycerophospholipid metabolism. MT-EVs notably increased palmitoylcarnitine and gabapentin levels, indicating improved mitochondrial fatty acid utilization and metabolic resilience, further supporting the role of EVs in restoring cardiac metabolic homeostasis [130].

Cardiac Fibrosis

Complementing these findings, another study demonstrated that intramyocardial injection of MSC-derived extracellular vesicles (MSC-EVs) markedly improved cardiac function and reduced myocardial fibrosis in a mouse model of transverse aortic constriction (TAC). Targeted metabolomics and pathway analysis revealed that MSC-EVs modulated glutamate metabolism in cardiac fibroblasts by suppressing glutamic pyruvate transaminase (GPT2), thereby lowering α-ketoglutarate levels and triggering AMPK activation, a key regulator of cellular energy homeostasis. This metabolic shift resulted in decreased ATP production in fibroblasts, contributing to reduced fibrotic activity. Mechanistically, the effect was mediated by miR-30c-5p carried within the EVs, as overexpression of this microRNA enhanced therapeutic outcomes, while its inhibition attenuated the cardioprotective effects [131].

Cardiac Aging

Extending this line of evidence, a recent metabolomics investigation revealed that cardiac aging is characterized by the accumulation of acetyl-CoA, nucleotides, and short-chain acylcarnitines, alongside reduced levels of carnosine, anserine, and long-chain acylcarnitines, hallmarks of impaired mitochondrial and fatty acid metabolism. Notably, treatment with adipose-derived stem cell small extracellular vesicles (ADSC-sEVs) partially reversed these alterations by restoring metabolites associated with β-oxidation and antioxidant defense [132].

Ischemia-reperfusion Injury

A separate investigation using a human chambered cardiac organoid (CCO) model demonstrated that mesenchymal stem cell-derived extracellular vesicles (MSC-EVs) mitigate myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury (IRI) through regulation of lipid metabolism. Metabolomics analysis showed that MSC-EVs reduced the accumulation of unsaturated very long-chain fatty acids (VLCFAs), which are associated with increased cardiomyocyte apoptosis and impaired contractile function. Inhibition of fatty acid synthase, an enzyme responsible for VLCFA synthesis, produced similar protective effects, indicating that MSC-EVs exert their cardioprotective actions, at least in part, by restoring lipid metabolic balance during IRI [133].

Stroke

MSC-based therapies also show metabolic benefits in stroke, an ischemic cardiovascular condition. A metabolomics study reported that bone marrow-derived MSCs modulated brain metabolite profiles in a stroke model, marked by increased levels of 5-oxoproline and glutathione (GSH). This effect was linked to the upregulation of GSH synthesis enzymes, suggesting enhanced antioxidant defense. The elevation of 5-oxoproline also points to its potential as a biomarker for acute ischemic stroke, underscoring the role of MSCs in mitigating oxidative stress and supporting neuroprotection [134].

Pulmonary Arterial Hypertension

Pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH), a form of cardiovascular disease, shows distinct metabolic alterations linked to vascular remodeling. Metabolomic profiling of PAH lung tissue revealed disrupted glycolysis, increased TCA cycle activity, and altered fatty acid oxidation. These changes support elevated ATP production and include metabolites such as lactate, citrate, succinate, and various fatty acid intermediates [135]. Further metabolomic investigations in a flow-associated PAH rat model identified significant dysregulation in linoleic acid and arachidonic acid metabolism in both pulmonary arteries and the right ventricle, implicating lipid signaling pathways in disease progression [136]. Complementing these findings, studies have shown that mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes (EVs) can restore metabolic balance in PAH. Targeted metabolomics and transcriptomic analyses demonstrated that EVs enhance mitochondrial activity in pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells (PASMCs) by increasing oxygen consumption and upregulating pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) and glutamate dehydrogenase 1 (GLUD1), while suppressing sirtuin 4, a negative regulator of both enzymes [137].

Preeclampsia

Similarly, in preeclampsia (PE), EVs derived from human umbilical mesenchymal stem cells were shown to alleviate endothelial injury by promoting angiogenesis, reducing inflammation, and activating the arginine metabolic pathway. This was supported by increased arginine levels, nitric oxide production, and nitric oxide synthase activity [138]. Collectively, these preclinical metabolomics-based studies highlight the therapeutic potential of stem cells and their exosomes in correcting metabolic and vascular dysfunctions in cardiovascular-related diseases.

Diabetes

Diabetes mellitus is a chronic metabolic disorder characterized by impaired glucose homeostasis, insulin resistance, and progressive β-cell dysfunction. In diabetes, major metabolic disruptions involve pathways such as glycolysis, the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, gluconeogenesis, fatty acid β-oxidation, and branched-chain amino acid metabolism. Metabolomics studies in diabetic rats have shown that levels of metabolites like pyruvate, lactate, citrate, and glycerol fluctuate over time, reflecting dynamic metabolic alterations [139, 140]. Notably, these metabolic disturbances overlap with pathways reported in cardiovascular and liver disease models, particularly those involving central carbon metabolism, amino acid turnover, and oxidative stress, suggesting shared metabolic checkpoints targeted by MSC-based therapies. Given the complexity of these metabolic disturbances, there is growing interest in therapeutic strategies that can restore metabolic balance while addressing underlying inflammation and tissue damage. In this context, MSC therapy and its EVs represent promising approaches, combining potent immunomodulatory properties with the capacity to modulate metabolic pathways relevant to diabetes progression [141, 142]. As highlighted in earlier sections, MSCs can reshape gut microbiota composition and function, thereby altering metabolic outputs and immune responses in a coordinated manner. Zheng et al. (2024) showed that hAMSC treatment modulated gut microbiota, enriched Bifidobacterium, Prevotella, and Alcaligenes, increased taurine levels, and shifted immune balance toward Th2/Treg while reducing Th1/Th17 cells, supporting a microbiota–metabolite–immune axis in therapy [143]. Similarly, in a db/db mouse model of diabetic cardiovascular complications, hUCMSC-derived exosomes restored 15 of 74 significantly altered aortic metabolites, notably normalizing pathways such as central carbon metabolism and galactose metabolism, which are critical for energy balance and redox homeostasis. These metabolic modulations were accompanied by proteomic shifts implicating anti-inflammatory and antioxidative mechanisms, underscoring the capacity of MSC-based interventions to target systemic metabolic dysregulation in diabetes [144].

Degenerative and Ageing-Linked Disorders

Neurodegenerative Disorders (NDDs)

In neurodegenerative disorders such as Alzheimer’s, ALS, Huntington’s, and Parkinson’s, MSCs exert therapeutic effects by differentiating into neurons and glial cells while also modulating the diseased microenvironment. Preclinical studies using MSCs from sources such as bone marrow, adipose tissue, and umbilical cord have demonstrated beneficial effects, leading to an increase in clinical trials, which are primarily focused on safety. MSCs exert their therapeutic potential mainly through paracrine mechanisms, promoting neurogenesis, angiogenesis, and anti-inflammatory responses [145–147]. Similar to cardiovascular and metabolic disorders, metabolomic studies in neurodegenerative disease models point to recurrent alterations in mitochondrial function, lipid metabolism, and antioxidant pathways following MSC or EV treatment, indicating conserved mechanisms of metabolic support across tissues. While the mechanisms are not yet fully understood, early results are encouraging for the future development of more effective cell-based therapies [148].

Parkinson’s Disease

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a progressively debilitating neurodegenerative disorder characterized by motor and cognitive impairments, primarily resulting from the loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra. Existing clinical therapies provide symptomatic relief but fail to modify the underlying disease course. Mesenchymal stem cells, especially those derived from the human umbilical cord (hUC-MSCs), have demonstrated significant neuroprotective potential in preclinical models, offering a promising avenue for disease-modifying interventions in PD [149]. A recent study showed that hUC-MSCs-derived exosomes enhance neuronal survival in a PD model by promoting autophagy, reducing apoptosis, and restoring dopamine levels, effects linked to improved cellular metabolism [149].

Alzheimer’s Disease

Similarly, in Alzheimer’s disease (AD), early metabolic disturbances play a crucial role in disease progression, often occurring decades before clinical symptoms appear. Metabolomic studies have identified disruptions in energy metabolism, mitochondrial function, and lipid and amino acid pathways, all of which reflect the underlying neurodegenerative processes [150]. MSCs have shown promise in AD by targeting metabolic dysfunction, particularly cholesterol dysregulation. In an AD-like rat model, intraventricular transplantation of adipose-derived MSCs improved neurological outcomes and reduced Aβ plaque formation. Metabolomic and molecular analyses revealed restored cholesterol homeostasis, with decreased brain cholesterol levels, downregulation of HMGCR, ApoE, and ABCA1, and upregulation of CYP46A1. These effects were also linked to changes in cholesterol-regulating microRNAs, highlighting the ability of MSCs to modulate lipid metabolism at both gene and miRNA levels [151]. Despite promising findings, studies exploring the metabolic effects of MSC therapy in neurodegenerative diseases remain limited, particularly those employing metabolomics approaches. Most existing research is confined to animal models and focuses primarily on neurological outcomes rather than comprehensive metabolic profiling.

Osteoarthritis (OA)

Osteoarthritis (OA) is the most prevalent degenerative joint disease, characterized by progressive cartilage degradation, subchondral bone remodeling, synovial inflammation, and chronic pain [152]. While traditionally considered a biomechanical disease, growing evidence indicates that OA is also driven by complex metabolic and molecular alterations that impair cartilage homeostasis and regeneration [153]. An integrative study analyzing chondrogenesis in bone marrow-derived MSCs from OA patients revealed significant metabolic disruptions using SILAC-based proteomics and MALDI-MSI. The analysis identified 43 proteins linked to altered metabolic pathways and distinct early metabolomic signatures compared to healthy controls. In particular, reduced UDP-glucuronic acid synthesis and amino sugar metabolism were associated with impaired production of hyaluronic acid and extracellular matrix components, potentially contributing to deficient cartilage repair in OA [154]. In a multi-omics study by Ge et al. (2025), metabolomic profiling of synovial fluid and synovium from OA patients revealed distinct metabolic alterations linked to disease progression. The tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle was markedly elevated, indicating enhanced energy demand possibly associated with cartilage repair processes. Disruptions in arginine metabolism were also evident, with increased levels of ornithine, proline, and hydroxyproline pointing to active collagen turnover and matrix degradation. Furthermore, elevated phenylalanine and tyrosine metabolites suggested a role for altered phenylalanine metabolism in promoting cartilage damage and OA pathogenesis [155]. Building on the metabolic disturbances observed in osteoarthritis, a preclinical study demonstrated that MSC therapy reprograms lipid metabolism by reducing pro-inflammatory ω-6 PUFAs while enhancing anti-inflammatory ω-3 PUFAs in synovial fluid. This shift was accompanied by a marked reduction in inflammatory lipid mediators, including 5(S)-HETE and PGA2, indicating that MSCs may attenuate OA progression through metabolic rebalancing and suppression of inflammation [156]. MSC therapy shows promise in modulating metabolic pathways in osteoarthritis however, the evidence remains limited, and further studies are necessary to elucidate its mechanisms and therapeutic impact.

Inflammatory and Barrier-Disruption Diseases

Liver Diseases

Liver diseases, including fibrosis, cholangitis, autoimmune hepatitis, metabolic dysfunction–associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD), and acute liver failure, are characterized by profound metabolic disturbances that reflect disruptions in lipid handling, bile acid homeostasis, amino acid turnover, mitochondrial function, and redox balance. Metabolomics provides a powerful framework for capturing these alterations with high sensitivity and for elucidating how therapeutic interventions, such as MSCs and their EVs, modulate dysfunctional pathways. Across preclinical models, metabolomic profiling reveals consistent signatures of lipid remodeling, oxidative stress, and bile acid dysregulation, alongside disease-specific perturbations linked to inflammation and immune activation [157, 158]. These insights position metabolomics as an essential tool for understanding MSC- and MSC-EV–mediated hepatoprotection and for identifying metabolic biomarkers that may guide early diagnosis, therapeutic monitoring, and mechanistic assessment.

Liver Fibrosis

Among the various liver disorders, liver fibrosis stands out as a representative condition in which metabolic dysregulation plays a central role in disease progression. It arises in response to chronic hepatic injury and is defined by the excessive accumulation of extracellular matrix proteins, primarily driven by the activation of hepatic stellate cells (HSCs). This fibrotic transformation is closely associated with widespread metabolic alterations, including disrupted lipid metabolism, mitochondrial dysfunction, increased oxidative stress, and imbalances in amino acid and bile acid pathways [159, 160]. Bile acid accumulation due to impaired transport causes cholestatic injury [161], while inflammatory metabolites like succinate further activate immune cells and fibrogenic pathways [162]. Given the central role of metabolic dysregulation in driving fibrotic progression, therapeutic strategies that can restore metabolic balance hold significant potential. MSCs and their EVs have appeared as promising candidates due to their multifaceted ability to modulate inflammation, oxidative stress, and fibrogenic signaling [163]. Supporting this potential, a metabolomic study in a CCl₄-induced liver fibrosis model demonstrated that ADMSC treatment restored hepatic metabolic balance, particularly within bile acid and retinol metabolism pathways. Key metabolites such as taurochenodeoxycholic acid (TCDCA), glycochenodeoxycholic acid (GCDCA), glycocholic acid (GCA), and taurocholic acid (TCA) were elevated after therapy, reflecting improved bile acid homeostasis [164]. Similarly, in Wilson’s disease (WD)-related liver fibrosis, metabolic disruptions included reduced glutamate, choline, glycerol, glutamine, and several amino acids, alongside elevated glycogen, α-glucose, and lactate. Treatment with traditional Chinese medicine (TCM), bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs), or their combination (TCM+BMSC) reversed these alterations, with TCM+BMSC increasing glutathione, β-glucose, alanine, glycine, valine, and lactate while reducing glutamine, indicating improved redox balance, amino acid metabolism, and energy regulation [165]. Additionally, Ji et al. (2024) showed that human adipose-derived stem cells (hADSCs) alleviate liver fibrosis in mice by modulating hepatic metabolism. hADSC treatment significantly altered lipid-related metabolites and impacted key pathways such as the mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR), peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR), and choline metabolism. Integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analysis showed downregulation of genes including Elovl3, Scd1, Cyp4a12a, and Cyp4a12b, corresponding with reduced levels of palmitic acid and docosapentaenoic acid. These findings suggest that the anti-fibrotic effects of hADSCs are mediated through regulation of lipid metabolism [166]. Complementing these findings, Yang et al. (2021) further highlighted the metabolic mechanisms underlying MSC therapy in liver fibrosis by employing human umbilical cord-derived MSCs (hUC-MSCs) delivered via open-flow microperfusion (OFM). This approach not only enhanced MSC engraftment and therapeutic efficacy but also enabled real-time sampling of liver interstitial fluid for in vivo metabolomic profiling. Their analysis revealed distinct metabolic signatures across treatment groups, with hUC-MSCs modulating key metabolites, such as 9-cis-retinoic acid and dehydroretinal dehyde, and regulating eight metabolic pathways associated with fibrosis progression [167].

Sclerosing Cholangitis

Another significant form of chronic liver injury is sclerosing cholangitis, a progressive and fibroinflammatory disease primarily affecting the bile ducts. The primary form, primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC), has no known cause but is linked to immune dysfunction and inflammatory bowel disease [168]. PSC is marked by profound alterations in bile acid metabolism, which contribute to ongoing hepatobiliary injury. The Regulatory Elements of Metabolite and Enzyme Dynamics in Injury (REMEDI) framework highlights dynamic shifts in bile acid regulation and the host’s adaptive mechanisms throughout PSC progression [169]. Clinically, increased levels of alkaline phosphatase (ALP) are commonly observed in PSC patients and correlate with disease progression, serving as a biomarker for biliary injury [170]. Metabolomic analyses further reveal that bile from PSC patients exhibits a distinct profile, notably enriched in dipeptides and polyamines, reflecting aberrant bile composition and impaired enterohepatic circulation [171]. These findings underscore the metabolic complexity of PSC and its potential for biomarker-driven insights into disease progression and therapeutic targets. In a PSC mouse model, Yu et al. (2024) showed that hP-MSC treatment modulates bile acid, lipid, and hydroxyproline metabolism, enhancing bile acid transport, promoting unsaturated fatty acid production via FADS2 and elongase, and stimulating fatty acid β-oxidation. Downregulation of proline 4-hydroxylase lowered hydroxyproline levels, indicating reduced fibrotic activity, with eight metabolic biomarkers linked to therapeutic response [86]. In a related study, hP-MSCs were shown to exert therapeutic effects through activation of the Takeda G protein-coupled receptor 5 (TGR5) signaling pathway. Using liver tissues and cholangiocyte organoids derived from PSC patients, and validating in multiple mouse models, hP-MSCs significantly reduced inflammation, fibrosis, and cholestasis. Mechanistically, hP-MSCs activated the PI3K/ERK pathway, enhanced cholangiocyte proliferation, and suppressed inflammation by modulating TGR5–Pellino3 interactions and downstream NF-κB transcriptional activity [172].

Liver Trauma

In addition, recent studies underscore the bidirectional relationship between liver disease and gut microbiota, often referred to as the gut–liver axis. The liver receives approximately 70% of its blood supply from the portal vein, which drains the gut, making it highly susceptible to microbiota-derived metabolites, toxins, and inflammatory signals [173]. Dysbiosis, a disruption in the composition and function of gut microbiota, has been implicated in various liver conditions, including non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), cirrhosis, and liver injury, where it contributes to systemic inflammation, immune dysregulation, and progression of hepatic pathology [174, 175]. A recent study on liver trauma in rats demonstrated that MSCs and their derived exosomes (MSC-exos) influenced key changes in systemic metabolism. Untargeted metabolomic profiling showed significant disruptions in serum metabolites following liver injury, including altered levels of oxidized glutathione, eicosapentaenoate, 6-methylnicotinamide, and ascorbate-related compounds. Administration of MSCs or MSC-exos partially normalized these metabolic disturbances, with MSC-exos yielding more pronounced effects. Notably, certain metabolites, particularly those related to ascorbate, were strongly associated with specific gut microbial taxa such as Intestinimonas and Faecalibaculum, as well as with inflammatory cytokine levels, highlighting the interconnected roles of metabolism, microbiota, and immune modulation in the healing process [176].

Metabolic Dysfunction-associated Fatty Liver Disease

Following the discussion on the gut–liver axis, it is also important to consider the metabolic crosstalk between diabetes and liver disease, particularly in the development of metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD). Diabetes mellitus (DM) is strongly associated with MAFLD through shared mechanisms that include systemic insulin resistance, chronic inflammation, and impaired lipid metabolism. The liver plays a central role in glucose and lipid homeostasis, and in the context of DM, persistent hyperglycemia and altered insulin signaling exacerbate hepatic fat accumulation, oxidative stress, and inflammation, accelerating liver injury and fibrosis [177, 178]. Consequently, DM significantly increases the risk and severity of MAFLD, making metabolic regulation a key therapeutic target. Zhou et al. (2025) used a streptozotocin-induced rat model of DM with MAFLD to assess the therapeutic impact of hUC-MSCs. Metabolomic analysis showed that hUC-MSCs significantly improved liver metabolic profiles by modulating key pathways related to carbohydrate, amino acid, and lipid metabolism. Seventy differential metabolites were identified with strong diagnostic value and predictive potential for therapeutic response. Additionally, several of these metabolites were significantly correlated with fasting blood glucose, triglycerides, and liver enzyme levels, highlighting the metabolic basis of hUC-MSC-induced liver protection [179].

Autoimmune Hepatitis (AIH)

is an immune-mediated liver disorder characterized by chronic hepatocellular inflammation, interface hepatitis, autoantibody production, and progression toward fibrosis. Metabolomics studies show that AIH is associated with substantial metabolic reprogramming, including elevated bile acids, increased lactate, reduced glutathione, and perturbations in fatty acid, phospholipid, and tryptophan–kynurenine pathways. These alterations, observed in both patient samples and Con A models, reflect mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and heightened inflammatory metabolism [180–182]. Building on this metabolic vulnerability, metabolomics-guided evaluation of MSC-derived therapies has shown promising results. In preclinical models of autoimmune hepatitis induced by concanavalin A (Con A), MSC-derived EV therapy has been directly evaluated using untargeted metabolomics. Untargeted LC–MS profiling identified over 2,000 liver metabolites with significant differences between EV-treated and control groups, including changes in lysophosphatidylcholines, eicosapentaenoic acid, and acetyl-DL-valine, implicating glycerophospholipid metabolism and unsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis as key pathways modulated by treatment. These metabolite shifts correlated with improvements in liver histopathology and gut microbiota associations, illustrating how MSC-EVs can affect systemic metabolic reprogramming in inflammatory liver injury models and highlighting the utility of metabolomics in mechanistic evaluation [183].

To conclude this section on liver diseases, liver failure represents the most advanced and life-threatening stage of hepatic dysfunction, marked by a collapse in the liver’s metabolic, synthetic, and detoxification capacities [184]. In a D-galactosamine-induced acute liver failure model in miniature pigs, human placental MSC (hPMSC) transplantation significantly restored disrupted metabolic profiles. UPLC/TOF-MS-based metabolomics revealed normalization of bile acids (e.g., glycoursodeoxycholic acid, glycochenodeoxycholic acid), phosphatidylcholine, lysophosphatidylcholine, sphingomyelin, and aromatic amino acids [185].

Across diverse liver disease models, metabolomics reveals that MSCs and MSC-EVs consistently restore key metabolic functions, including bile acid regulation, lipid metabolism, redox balance, and amino acid pathways, while also modulating disease-specific metabolic circuits. These convergent and context-dependent effects highlight the central role of metabolic reprogramming in MSC-based hepatoprotection. Although evidence remains uneven across different liver conditions, the available studies collectively demonstrate that integrating metabolomics with MSC and EV therapies provides mechanistic clarity and offers a promising direction for future translational development.

IBD: Ulcerative Colitis and Crohn’s Disease

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), including Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC), is a chronic gastrointestinal disorder characterized by immune dysregulation, intestinal barrier dysfunction, and gut microbiota imbalance. Despite growing knowledge, the molecular mechanisms underlying IBD remain unclear, complicating diagnosis and treatment. Metabolomics has emerged as a promising tool to uncover metabolic alterations linked to disease activity, including changes in the TCA cycle, amino acid metabolism, and oxidative pathways [186]. Specifically, metabolites related to amino acid, purine, and bile acid metabolism have been linked to IBD severity. For example, elevated aspartate metabolism has been associated with increased disease progression risk, while enhanced linoleic acid metabolism appears protective [187]. Given these distinct metabolic disruptions in IBD, MSCs and their EVs have gained attention for their potential to modulate disease pathology through metabolic reprogramming [188, 189]. Despite distinct intestinal pathologies, metabolomic studies across IBD models reveal recurrent disruptions in amino acid metabolism, bile acid signaling, redox balance, and microbiota-derived metabolites, several of which mirror metabolic alterations observed in liver and metabolic diseases.

Ulcerative Colitis

Recent evidence highlights the therapeutic potential of mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes (MSC-Ex) in modulating the metabolic landscape of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). In a murine colitis model, MSC-Ex treatment restored gut metabolic balance and altered microbiota composition. Metabolomics profiling revealed increased colonic expression of the bile acid receptor FXR, a central regulator of intestinal barrier integrity and immune homeostasis. This metabolic reprogramming was accompanied by enhanced microbial diversity, enrichment of beneficial bacterial taxa, and attenuation of pro-inflammatory microbial pathways [190]. Complementing these findings, untargeted metabolomic analysis in another MSC-treated colitis model revealed significant enrichment of metabolites involved in ferroptosis and glutathione metabolism pathways, including increased levels of gamma-glu-glu, Glu-Ser, alpha-L-Glu-Gly, and glutaric acid. These metabolic changes were linked to reduced oxidative stress and enhanced epithelial protection, underscoring the metabolic mechanisms by which MSCs restore intestinal homeostasis in IBD [191]. Recent findings using hUC-MSCs in a DSS-induced colitis model highlight the therapeutic role of gut microbiota and metabolites. HUMSC treatment alleviated inflammation while enriching SCFA-producing bacteria like Akkermansia and Faecalibaculum. Targeted metabolomics confirmed increased SCFA levels, which were linked to restored Treg/Th2/Th17 balance. Functional studies with fecal transplants and sterile filtrates confirmed that microbiota-derived SCFAs are key mediators of the MSC-induced immunomodulatory effects in IBD [192]. These results align with broader evidence that gut dysbiosis plays a central role in colitis pathogenesis, where reduced microbial diversity, loss of beneficial species, and diminished SCFA production contribute to epithelial barrier disruption and chronic immune activation [193]. In this context, Thandar et al. (2024) have shown that adipose-derived stem cell (ADSC) therapy promotes intestinal repair by reprogramming host and microbial metabolism. Their metabolomic analysis revealed significant alterations in bile acid biosynthesis, amino acid metabolism, and the TCA cycle. Additionally, ADSCs influenced the metabolic activity of gut Bacteroides, supporting coordinated microbiota–metabolic recovery in radiation-induced intestinal injury [194].

Crohn’s Disease

is associated with characteristic metabolic alterations reflecting disrupted amino acid, lipid, and energy metabolism. Serum metabolomics studies have consistently reported changes in amino acid pathways, including phenylalanine-, tyrosine-, and tryptophan-related metabolites, alongside disturbances in pyruvate metabolism, branched-chain amino acids, and lipid-associated metabolites, indicating altered host–microbiome metabolic interactions and impaired energy homeostasis in active disease [195, 196]. Similarly, in Crohn’s disease, MSC therapy normalized elevated metabolites, 4-hydroxyphenylpyruvate, 4-hydroxyphenylacetaldehyde, caffeate, and N-acetyltryptamine, linked to dysregulated tyrosine metabolism, indicating restored metabolic balance and therapeutic efficacy [197]. In conclusion, MSCs and their EVs show promise in treating IBD by modulating gut microbiota and restoring metabolic balance. Metabolomic data highlight their role in reducing inflammation and promoting gut homeostasis.

Lung Diseases

Lung diseases, including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), acute lung injury (ALI), and pulmonary fibrosis, contribute substantially to global morbidity and mortality. These conditions are characterized by persistent inflammation, oxidative stress, and metabolic dysregulation, which together drive disease progression and complicate therapeutic management [198, 199]. In lung diseases, inflammation often induces a metabolic shift toward increased glycolysis and activation of the pentose phosphate pathway, promoting immune cell activation and proliferation. In contrast, anti-inflammatory macrophages predominantly rely on fatty acid oxidation and oxidative phosphorylation to sustain energy production and maintain redox homeostasis [198]. Exploring the potential of MSCs in modulating metabolic pathways in lung disease, a few key studies have highlighted their influence on both tissue metabolism and the gut–lung axis.

Acute Lung Injury

In an LPS-induced acute lung injury (ALI) model, treatment with human umbilical cord-derived MSCs (hUC-MSCs) significantly improved pulmonary and gut barrier integrity, reduced inflammation, and rebalanced microbial composition in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) and feces. Metabolomic analysis of lung tissue revealed differential expression of metabolites involved in purine metabolism and taste transduction pathways, linking metabolic reprogramming to microbial restoration in the BALF [200].

Pulmonary Fibrosis

Similarly, in a bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis model, UC-MSCs enhanced survival, reversed alveolar damage, and suppressed inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, TGF-β1). LC–MS/MS metabolomic profiling showed significant alterations in lipid metabolism and related pathways, while 16 S rDNA sequencing demonstrated a reduction in microbial diversity and abundance, further supporting MSCs’ role in gut–lung axis modulation [201]. Consistent with these findings, a recent study combining untargeted metabolomics with 16 S rRNA sequencing in an idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) model demonstrated that hUC-MSCs partially restored lung microbiota composition and corrected associated metabolic disturbances. Key metabolic pathways affected included amino acid metabolism, glycerophospholipid metabolism, and SCFA-related pathways, with improvements corresponding to reduced fibrosis and enhanced tissue repair [202]. Collectively, available studies emphasize that MSCs modulate lung pathology through metabolite-level adjustments in lipid and purine metabolism and through microbiota-mediated mechanisms spanning the gut–lung axis. However, despite increasing interest in MSC and MSC-EV therapies across pulmonary conditions, metabolomics-based evidence remains limited reflecting the broader dominance of other omics approaches and leaving metabolic response signatures insufficiently defined.

Taken together, despite heterogeneity in disease models and analytical platforms, MSC- and EV-based therapies consistently reshape host metabolism by converging on a limited set of conserved pathways, including the TCA cycle (e.g., succinate, α-ketoglutarate), fatty acid utilization, and glutathione-mediated redox balance, while simultaneously modulating disease-specific metabolic circuits such as arginine–nitric oxide signaling, tryptophan–kynurenine metabolism, and lipid remodeling. Conclusion and Future Perspectives

Metabolomics is no longer just a descriptive readout but an emerging guide for understanding and optimizing MSC and EV therapies. Preclinical evidence demonstrates that these therapies modulate conserved pathways such as the TCA cycle, fatty acid β-oxidation, and glutathione-dependent redox balance, while also engaging disease-specific circuits including polyamine synthesis, bile acid receptor signaling, and neurotransmitter metabolism. These findings underscore that metabolomic profiles can serve both as biomarkers of therapeutic potency and as mechanistic targets for intervention. Table 3 highlights recurring metabolites that consistently appear across cardiovascular, inflammatory, hepatic, and neurodegenerative models, pointing to a restricted set of conserved metabolic checkpoints through which MSCs and EVs exert their effects.

Nonetheless, several limitations remain. Current metabolomics studies are often underpowered, exploratory, and largely descriptive, relying on small sample sizes and lacking independent replication or targeted validation. This constrains their clinical applicability. To progress, future work must include larger, rigorously designed preclinical studies, coupled with targeted validation using isotope-labelled standards and functional perturbation assays. Clinical translation will require randomized trials that incorporate metabolomic endpoints, enabling the field to link metabolic shifts directly to therapeutic outcomes. In parallel, embedding metabolomics within GMP-compliant analytical pipelines will be critical to ensure reproducibility, quality control, and regulatory acceptance.

Looking forward, integration of artificial intelligence and machine-learning approaches offers a powerful strategy to disentangle robust metabolomic signatures from complex datasets, thereby improving reproducibility and biomarker prioritization. Within this framework, we propose the concept of a Metabolomics-Derived MSC Potency Index, envisioned as a validated panel of conserved metabolites (e.g., succinate, acylcarnitines, glutathione, short-chain fatty acids, bile acids) benchmarked against standardized functional assays of angiogenesis, oxidative stress regulation, and immunomodulatory capacity. Importantly, this index is not intended as a fixed or uniformly weighted scoring system at the current stage, but rather as a translational framework in which individual metabolites or metabolite classes would be differentially weighted based on reproducibility, effect size, and correlation with functional outcomes, potentially through multivariate or machine-learning–based modeling. Practical implementation would require explicit consideration of MSC tissue source, EV subtype, and isolation methodology, all of which are known to influence metabolomic profiles. By evolving from exploratory profiling toward validated, harmonized benchmarking strategies, such an approach could enable metabolomics to serve as a regulatory and clinical reference for product standardization, potency testing, and therapeutic readiness across cell sources and disease contexts, thereby supporting the development of reproducible and effective MSC and MSC-EV therapies.
