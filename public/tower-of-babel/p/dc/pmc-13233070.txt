RAC1 regulates Sonic Hedgehog-medulloblastoma growth via GLI-mediated transcription
Authors: Nitish Jangde, Mi-Hye Lee, Luz Ruiz, Isabelle Egan, Anna M Jermakowicz, Rishika Chowdary, Jonathan Chu, Daniel T Wynn, Erik Goka, Marc Lippman, David J Robbins, Nagi G Ayad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMedulloblastoma (MB) is the most common malignant brain tumor of childhood, and current treatments cannot sufficiently inhibit tumoral growth, leptomeningeal dissemination, and metastasis. Ras-related C3 botulinum toxin substrate 1 (RAC1), a low molecular weight GTPase involved in cytoskeletal regulation and cell migration, is established to facilitate tumorigenesis in other neoplasia, but RAC1 is unexplored in MB and has no targeted therapies.MethodsWe examined RAC1 activity in Sonic Hedgehog (SHH)-subtype MB using human and mouse Ptch1−/− cell models, RNAi knockdown, pharmacologic inhibition with GYS32661, transcript profiling, chromatin immunoprecipitation, and cytotoxicity assays in primary human astrocytes. We also determined the efficacy, brain penetration, and toxicity of GYS32661 in orthotopic spheroid Ptch1−/− mouse and rat models.ResultsRAC1 activity was markedly increased in the MB tissue compared to the normal cerebellum. RAC1 depletion suppressed the proliferation and migration of SHH-MB cells. Mechanistically, RAC1 regulated GLI1 and GLI2 expression and bound the upstream loci of GLI1 and DNMT1, revealing a novel mechanism of SHH transcriptional regulation. GYS32661, a brain-permeable RAC1 inhibitor, was not toxic to normal astrocytes, suppressed SHH-MB tumor growth, and improved survival in vivo without toxicity in rats.ConclusionRAC1 functions as a critical transcriptional regulator of SHH signaling and epigenetic mediators in medulloblastoma, highlighting its potential as a druggable target in SHH-dependent MB. Our findings also characterize the RAC1 inhibitor GYS32661 as a promising drug candidate for the treatment of SHH-MB.Keywords: cell migration, cytoskeleton, medulloblastoma, proliferation, RAC1 GTPase, targeted therapy

Importance of the Study

Current treatments of medulloblastoma (MB), especially in children, are ineffective at regulating tumor growth, leptomeningeal dissemination, and metastasis. RAC1 emerges as an oncogenic driver in SHH-subtype MB with significantly higher expression levels in tumors compared to normal cerebellum. Suppression of tumor growth by RAC1 through genetic knockdown or pharmacologic inhibition supports its potential as a therapeutic target. Mechanistically, RAC1 regulates actin cytoskeletal dynamics and expression of the SHH effectors GLI1 and GLI2 to enhance tumor aggressiveness. We report herein that GYS32661 is a brain-penetrant RAC1 inhibitor with a favorable safety profile that effectively inhibits SHH-MB growth in vivo. These findings provide strong evidence for the preclinical efficacy of RAC1 targeting and suggest that GYS32661 monotherapy or combination regimens are a promising therapy for improving outcomes and reducing toxicity in children with high-risk SHH-MB.

Medulloblastoma (MB) is the most prevalent malignant primary brain tumor in children and a leading cause of childhood cancer-related illness and death.1 Current treatment strategies involve surgery, radiation (for children older than 3 years), and chemotherapy.2 MB is categorized into 4 distinct molecular subgroups: Wingless (WNT), Sonic Hedgehog (SHH), Group 3, and Group 4. The SHH subgroup is complex, affecting patients across a wide age range—from infants to adults—and is further subdivided into 4 subtypes: SHH-α (adolescents), SHH-β (infants with a poor prognosis), SHH-γ (infants with a favorable prognosis), and SHH-δ (adults).3 It accounts for approximately 30% of all medulloblastomas, and the 5-year survival is about 70% for SHH-MB patients.4 SHH-MB most frequently occurs in children under 3 years of age and above 17 years of age, with fewer cases reported in childhood and adolescence.5 It originates in the cerebellum from cerebellar granule progenitor cells with activation of the SHH pathway, where SHH dysregulation triggers SHH-MB tumor growth during embryonic brain development.6,7 For example, mutations of receptors in the SHH pathway, PTCH1 and SMO (Smoothened), are often associated with SHH-MB development.8 Previous therapeutic approaches have focused on SMO antagonists as well as inhibitors of the downstream transcriptional regulators of the pathway, GLI1 and GLI2 (refs9–12). However, resistance has been observed in both preclinical and clinical settings, suggesting the need for novel therapeutic strategies.13–16

Ras-related C3 botulinum toxin substrate 1 (RAC1) overexpression has been implicated in several cancers, such as breast cancer, non–small cell lung cancer, and head and neck squamous cell carcinomas.17–19 Emerging evidence suggests that RAC1 may be a therapeutic target in cancer due to its involvement in various cellular processes.20–24 Our study uncovers a new mechanism in which RAC1 controls the levels of several mediators of the SHH pathway, including GLI1. RAC1 depletion or inhibition reduces the transcription of GLI1, GLI2, and various transcriptional and epigenetic regulators involved in SHH signaling. Consistent with this finding, RAC1 inhibition with the brain-penetrant molecule GYS32661 reduces medulloblastoma growth in vivo. Our study identifies the RAC1 pathway as a point of therapeutic intervention for pediatric and adult SHH-MB.

Methods

Detailed methods for standard techniques and assays, such as immunofluorescence and cell culture, are in the Supplementary Methods File.

Gene Silencing

Cells were transfected with Rac1 targeting siRNA (SantaCruz Biotechnology, Catalog no.: sc-36351; Dharmacon, ON-TARGET plus Human RAC1(5879) siRNA—SMART pool Catalog no. L-003560-00-0010; ON-TARGET plus Mouse RAC1(19353) siRNA—SMART pool, Catalog no. L-041170-00-0010) and non-targeting siRNAs (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Catalog no.: sc-370071; Dharmacon Accell Green non-targeting siRNA D-001950-01-20), using the appropriate transfection reagent and transfection media per the manufacturer’s protocol.

GTP-bound RAC1 Detection

Cells were treated with varying concentrations of GYS32661 and harvested under non-denaturing conditions by rinsing them with PBS and lysing them with an ice-cold lysis solution (25 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.2, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 1% NP-40, and 5% glycerol containing PMSF), followed by incubation on ice. Cell lysates were mixed with EDTA, GTPγS (positive control), or GDP (negative control) and incubated at 30°C. The reaction was terminated with 1 M MgCl₂ (final working concentration 60 mM), followed by cooling on ice for GTPγS or GDP treatment. Lysates were subjected to affinity precipitation using glutathione resin and GST-PAK1-PBD. GTP-bound RAC1 protein was eluted using a reducing sample buffer containing DTT. The samples were then electrophoresed and immunoblotted for RAC1.

Proximity Ligation Assay

Proximity ligation assay (PLA) was performed on cells using a Duolink Proximity Ligation Assay (Millipore Sigma) as described by the manufacturer. The following antibodies were used for PLA: UHRF1 (catalog no. sc-373750, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, RRID: AB_10947236), DNMT1 (catalog no. 5032, Cell Signaling Technology, RRID: AB_10548197), GLI1 (catalog no. AF3455, R&D Systems, RRID: AB_2247710), GLI2 (catalog no. AF3635, R&D Systems, RRID: AB_2111902), and rabbit IgG (catalog no. 12370, Millipore Sigma). DAPI staining was performed using ProLong Gold Antifade Mountant with DAPI (Invitrogen).

In Vivo Drug Dosing

MSC4 spheroid cells were dissociated by passing them through an 8 μm cell strainer followed by Accutase (Gibco) treatment. Viable cells were counted using a Cellometer followed by acridine orange/propidium iodide staining. A total of 1 × 105 cells/mouse were injected orthotopically into the cerebellum as previously described.25 The mice were injected intraperitoneally every other day with GYS32661 (35 mg/kg of body weight) in 0.9% normal saline pH 5.5 and vehicle (0.9% normal saline pH 5.5). MRI imaging was performed to monitor tumor development every week.

Rotarod Test

Wild-type C57BL/6 mice were treated with either GYS32661 or vehicle, and motor coordination was assessed using a rotarod test. Animals were given a brief training session to familiarize them with the rotating rod. For the test, each animal was placed individually on the rod set to an accelerating speed ranging from 5 to 80 rpm, and the latency to fall was recorded for up to 180 seconds. Animals underwent 3 consecutive trials, with 15-minute rest intervals to prevent fatigue, and the average latency to fall was calculated over 3 days.

Results

RAC1 Is Overexpressed in Medulloblastoma Tumors

As RAC1 has not been extensively characterized in MB, we wanted to determine its levels in tumors relative to the normal cerebellum. Microarray analysis from de Bont et al26 revealed that RAC1 mRNA was significantly elevated in MB compared to the normal cerebellum (Figure 1A). Consistent with these findings, analysis of single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets revealed significantly elevated RAC1 mRNA expression in neoplastic MB cells (Hovestadt et al27) compared to non-neoplastic cells (Darmanis et al28) (Figure 1B). To determine whether the elevated expression was specific to tumor cells, we compared RAC1 levels across various non-neoplastic cell populations, including oligodendrocytes, microglia, neurons, endothelial cells, and astrocytes. Even when accounting for variable expression in non-neoplastic cell populations, RAC1 expression was still most pronounced in neoplastic cells (Figure 1C). As shown in Figure 1D, RAC1 protein levels are higher in human MB tissue relative to the cerebellum as judged by immunohistochemistry. In addition, RAC1 protein expression was assayed in several human cancer cells, including MB. Among these, the highest expression was observed in ONS76, an adherent SHH-MB cell line (Supplementary Figure S1C and D; see Supplementary Methods).

To further characterize RAC1 expression in human medulloblastoma tumors relative to normal cerebellum, we analyzed the Vo et al (2023) dataset, which contains spatial transcriptomics data of SHH-MB PDOX cells implanted into the cerebella of NSG mice (Supplementary Figure S1A and B).29 We applied our recently reported OrthologAL application that allows for the conversion of mouse genes to human genes.30 This allows integration of mouse transcripts from the mouse cerebella and the human PDOX transcripts and direct comparison of transcripts across the sections. As shown in Supplementary Figure S1A and B, RAC1 expression is highest in the tumor. The pattern of expression for RAC1 was similar to that of GLI1 and GLI2. Collectively, these studies indicate that RAC1 expression is higher in MB relative to normal cerebellum and that ONS76 cells may be a good model system to probe RAC1 activity in vitro (see Supplementary Methods).

RAC1 Regulates the Expression of GLI1 and GLI2 and Associated Proteins

Since ONS76 cells showed the highest RAC1 expression among all tested cells, we selected them for transfection with siRNAs targeting either RAC1 or a non-targeting control. As shown in Figure 2A and E, we achieved greater than 80% knockdown of RAC1 as measured by real-time qPCR and Western blot analysis. Importantly, we observed reduced GLI1 mRNA and GLI1 protein when RAC1 was depleted (approximately 70%, Figure 2A, B, and E). As GLI1 is a major transcription factor in the SHH pathway, we sought to determine whether other components of the SHH pathway were decreased. Indeed, when we analyzed another transcriptional activator in the SHH pathway, GLI2, it was similarly decreased in RAC1 siRNA-transfected cells relative to control cells (Figure 2C and E). Our prior studies described a novel complex of GLI1, GLI2, and the epigenetic regulators DNMT1 and UHRF1.4 Both DNMT1 and UHRF1 are required for SHH signaling and therefore we hypothesized that RAC1 may also control their transcription and protein levels. Indeed, RAC1 siRNA-transfected ONS76 cells had reduced levels of DNMT1 and UHRF1 mRNA and protein (Figure 2D and E) (see Supplementary Methods).

We also transfected ONS76 cells with different RAC1 siRNAs from Dharmacon Discovery and immunoblotted the cells lysates. GLI1 was reduced after RAC1 knockdown while SUFU levels were not affected (Supplementary Figure S9A). Further, we also knocked down RAC1 in MSC4 cells by siRNA-mediated transfection and cell lysates were immunoblotted for GLI1, DNMT1, and RAC1. GLI1 and DNMT1 were significantly decreased after RAC1 knockdown (Supplementary Figure S9C). These studies suggest that RAC1 regulates the SHH pathway in MB cells by controlling the expression levels of GLI1, GLI2, DNMT1, and UHRF1 (see Supplementary Methods).

The RAC1 Inhibitor GYS32661 Reduces Levels of GLI1 and GLI2 In Vitro

To complement RAC1 knockdown studies in SHH-MB, we assessed the effect of pharmacological inhibition of RAC1 in ONS76 cells. We selected the recently reported RAC1 inhibitor GYS32661, as our studies demonstrated that it is safe, non-toxic, and brain penetrant in animals (Supplementary Figures S2 and S3).31–34 We also tested GYS32661 for in vitro toxicity on normal and immortalized human astrocytes, and it was not toxic (Supplementary Figure S5C). We treated ONS76 cells with varying concentrations of GYS32661 and assessed RAC1 activity (measured as GTP-bound RAC1) using GTP-RAC1 affinity-based purification followed by RAC1 immunoblotting. GYS32661 reduced levels of GTP-RAC1 in ONS76 cells, suggesting that it effectively inhibits RAC1 activity in SHH-MB cells (Figure 3A) (see Supplementary Methods).

We next evaluated the downstream effects of RAC1 inhibition on SHH signaling. GYS32661 treatment led to a reduction in GLI1 and GLI2 levels in ONS76 cells (Figure 3B and C). DNMT1 and UHRF1 levels were also inhibited with time after GY32661-mediated RAC1 inhibition (Figure 3C and D). Further, GYS32661 reduced GLI1 and GLI2 levels in a model of SHH-MB derived from Ptch1 knockout mouse tumors (Ptch1−/−; MSC4 cells), suggesting that this compound can broadly affect SHH-MB cells (Figure 3E and F). At the transcriptional level, GYS32661 significantly reduced GLI1 mRNA expression in ONS76 cells (Figure 3G). Consistent with this, GYS32661 treatment decreased mRNA levels of GLI1, GLI2, DNMT1, and UHRF1 in MSC4 cells (Supplementary Figure S4A; see Supplementary Methods).

Prior studies in other cancers suggested that RAC1 can function within a transcriptional complex, and therefore we wanted to determine whether RAC1 controls GLI1 levels by binding to chromatin in SHH-MB.35–38 To assess this, we performed a Chromatin Immunoprecipitation assay (ChIP) in ONS76 cells to determine whether RAC1 is localized to the GLI1 upstream locus. We found that RAC1 binds to previously characterized GLI1 and DNMT1 upstream loci in ONS76 cells (−4230/−4424 GLI1 upstream, 1951-2463 bp and 2440-2843 upstream DNMT1 (Supplementary Figure S10A and B). Among 3 different regions, the upstream −4230/−4424 showed significantly higher fold enrichment as compared to the IgG control, which suggests that RAC1 binds to this region. Importantly, treatment with GYS32661 for 24 hours reduced RAC1 binding to this region as compared to the DMSO control (Figure 3H and I)(see Supplementary Methods).

To determine whether gain-of-function studies can help delineate the role of RAC1 in MB cell proliferation, transcription, and epigenetics, we overexpressed either wild-type RAC1 (RAC1-WT) or a constitutively active version of RAC1 (RAC1-V12) in ONS76 cells. We measured proliferation and chromatin recruitment to the GLI1 and DNMT1 upstream loci. As shown in Supplementary Figure S6F and G, overexpression of RAC1-V12 increased proliferation in the presence of the RAC1 inhibitor GYS32661. Similarly, RAC1-V12 overexpression increased RAC1 binding to the GLI1 and DNMT1 upstream loci (Supplementary Figure S10C-E). Collectively, these studies suggest that GYS32661 targets RAC1 since a constitutively active version of RAC1 overcomes its effect on proliferation, and that RAC1 activity is required for recruitment to chromatin. Further, these studies suggest that RAC1 directly controls GLI1 and DNMT1 transcription by binding to their upstream loci (see Supplementary Methods).

GYS32661 Treatment Reduces Levels of GLI1-UHRF1-DNMT1 Complex In Vitro

Our prior studies demonstrated that the FDA-approved DNMT1 inhibitor 5-azacytidine disrupts the GLI-UHRF1-DNMT1 complex, and dramatically reduces SHH-MB growth in vivo.4 Therefore, pharmacological targeting of this complex is feasible, which motivated us to test whether GYS32661 would similarly affect the GLI-UHRF1-DNMT1 complex. We treated ONS76 or MSC4 cells with GYS32661 and measured the steady-state levels of the mRNA and protein of all the components of the GLI-UHRF1-DNMT1 complex. As shown in Figure 3A-G, GYS32661 treatment reduced the levels of all components of this complex (see Supplementary Methods).

As an independent test, we also checked the interactions of the GLI-UHRF1-DNMT1 complex using an immunofluorescence-based PLA (Figure 4A-D). The GLI1-DNMT1 interaction was significantly inhibited by GYS32661 treatment, as evidenced by a significant decrease in the number of PLA puncta per nucleus in both ONS76 and MSC4 cells (Figure 4A and B). Similarly, GYS32661 treatment reduced GLI1-UHRF1 PLA puncta per nuclei in both ONS76 and MSC4 cells (Figure 4C and D). Importantly, siRNA-mediated depletion of RAC1 in ONS76 cells also reduced the GLI1-GLI2 interaction by PLA in ONS76 cells (Supplementary Figure S4B). Altogether, these studies suggest that RAC1 inhibition or depletion reduces the steady-state levels of the components of the GLI-UHRF1-DNMT1 complex, thereby diminishing their interaction in ONS76 and MSC4 cells (see Supplementary Methods).

To determine where in the SHH pathway RAC1 acts, we knocked down the negative downstream regulator SUFU in GLI reporter cells (Light2) and measured the effect of GYS32661 treatment on GLI reporter activity. Consistent with the model that RAC1 affects the SHH pathway downstream of SUFU, we found that GYS32661 reduced GLI reporter activity in SUFU knockdown cells (Supplementary Figure S5D; see Supplementary Methods).

GYS32661 Treatment Inhibits the Cellular Motility of ONS76 Cells In Vitro

RAC1 has been shown to have a myriad of roles in the proliferation and migration of multiple cell types,21,22,32 and therefore, we wanted to first test whether RAC1 inhibition would affect SHH-MB cellular migration. We first assessed the efficacy of GYS32661 treatment in a wound healing assay in ONS76 cells. GYS32661 treatment resulted in a marked decrease in cellular migration across the wound, thereby impairing wound healing (Supplementary Figure S5A). A chemotaxis experiment was also performed to assess the efficacy of the GYS32661 compound on cellular movement. ONS76 cells were seeded in very low numbers, and movement was tracked by time-lapse imaging in the presence of GYS32661 or vehicle control. Importantly, the path length in GYS32661-treated cells was shorter (approximately 280 μm) than in control (approximately 380 μm), suggesting that this compound inhibited cellular migration in a chemotaxis assay (Supplementary Figure S5B; see Supplementary Methods).

We next determined whether disrupting actin dynamics can be part of the reason for reduced migration in GYS32661 cells. Actin polymerization and depolymerization are essential for stress fiber formation, lamellipodia, filopodia, and membrane ruffling, which facilitate cellular motility or migration. The Rho GTPases RhoA, RAC1, and CDC42 are well recognized as critical regulators of actin dynamics and membrane structures.23,24,39,40 To determine the effect of GYS32661 on actin polymerization, ONS76 cells were treated with GYS32661 or DMSO control, then stained with phalloidin (Alexa Fluor 488; green) or DAPI (blue). The results showed a significant decrease in filamentous actin levels after GYS32661 treatment (Supplementary Figure S6A). Interestingly, treated cells had a distinct morphology compared to control cells with less stress fibers and filopodia formation (Supplementary Figure S6A). Therefore, GYS32661 disrupted actin cytoskeleton organization, consistent with the established roles of RAC1 in these processes (see Supplementary Methods).

We next evaluated the potential cytotoxicity of GYS32661 in untransformed cells. We performed crystal violet viability assays in normal human astrocytes or immortalized human astrocytes treated with varying micromolar concentrations of GYS32661. As shown in Supplementary Figure S5C, there was no difference between untreated and treated cells, indicating that GYS32661 is non-toxic to normal cells in vitro (see Supplementary Methods).

GYS32661 Treatment Inhibits MB Cellular Proliferation In Vitro

Multiple studies have shown that proliferation and migration are coupled processes,41 and therefore, we wanted to determine whether the effects we observe on the migration of ONS76 cells can also be related to proliferation inhibition following GYS32661 treatment. To test this, we treated ONS76 cells with GYS32661 and performed PI-FACS analysis. As seen in Supplementary Figure S6B, GYS32661 treatment arrests ONS76 cells at the G1 phase of the cell cycle after 6 hours, 18 hours, and 24 hours of treatment. This is consistent with the observed reduction in Cyclin D1 levels after treatment (Supplementary Figure S6C), as Cyclin D1 has an established role in G1 progression and RAC1 has previously been implicated in controlling its levels in other cancers.42 These studies suggest that RAC1 controls cell cycle progression of ONS76 cells (see Supplementary Methods).

Primary cilia formation is the hallmark of SHH-MB, and its disassembly facilitates mitotic division or cellular proliferation.39,40,43 We stained GYS32661-treated ONS76 cells for acetylated tubulin and pericentrin, and we found a dramatic increase in acetylated tubulin and pericentrin-positive primary cilia number in treated cells after 24 hours. However, primary cilia length was not affected (Supplementary Figure S8A). Intraciliary transport downstream signaling was also assessed by immunoblotting upon GYS32661 treatment of MSC4 cells. Vav2 and Ift88 phosphorylation was inhibited after GYS32661 treatment of MSC4 cells via dephosphorylation of Pak1 (Supplementary Figure S8B and C). Gsk3β phosphorylation was also inhibited with increasing concentrations of GYS32661 treatment (Supplementary Figure S8C). In addition, acetylated tubulin (AcTub) levels showed a marked increase after 18 and 24 hours of 8 μM of GYS32661 treatment, while Arl13b (ADP-ribosylation factor-like protein 13B) was not affected by the treatment (Supplementary Figure S8D). Arl13b is a small GTPase involved in migration and intra-flagellar transport (IFT) signaling,44,45 while acetylated tubulin is an axoneme marker for primary cilia, also involved in IFT. Importantly, tubulin acetylation is associated with reduced proliferation.46 Collectively, these studies suggest that GYS32661’s effects on ONS76 and MSC4 cells may be related to cell cycle progression and signaling (see Supplementary Methods).

GYS32661 Reduces SHH-MB Growth and Is Well-Tolerated and Brain-Penetrant In Vivo

Given that GYS32661 treatment reduced the proliferation of SHH-MB cells in vitro, we wanted to determine whether it could inhibit tumor growth in vivo. To test this, we performed allograft experiments in mice using MSC4 cells (Figure 5A-D). SHH-MB tumors started developing after 2 weeks, which we confirmed by MRI. We treated mice in 2 groups: one with vehicle (sterile normal saline; pH 9.0) and another with GYS32661 (35 mg/kg body weight in sterile normal saline). The mice were treated for 3 weeks, and samples were collected after perfusion when the mice showed neurological symptoms. Once mice started showing classical MB neurological symptoms, such as head tilting, difficulty in walking, lethargy, or ataxia, their brains were scanned by MRI to detect MB tumors. GYS32661-treated mice showed a marked reduction in SHH-MB tumor development compared to vehicle-treated mice (Figure 5A-C). Notably, mice showed a statistically significant increase in survival upon GYS32661 treatment (Figure 5D) (see Supplementary Methods).

Tumor tissues were collected at 3 weeks, fixed in 4% formalin, and subsequently embedded in paraffin. These paraffin-embedded tumor samples were sectioned and stained for histological markers for proliferation (Ki67) and cell death or apoptosis markers (cleaved caspase-3). GYS32661-treated tumors showed markedly reduced Ki67 staining compared to vehicle-treated mice MB tumors and had higher apoptosis as judged by cleaved caspase-3 (Figure 5E). GYS32661 treatment also reduced levels of on-target biomarkers in the SHH pathway, such as GLI1 and GLI2 in vivo, suggesting that the compound reaches the tumor site (Figure 5F). DNMT1 was also inhibited in vivo after treatment, suggesting disruption of the GLI epigenetic complex in vivo (Figure 5F). Collectively, these studies suggest that GYS32661 increases apoptosis while reducing proliferation and SHH signaling in vivo (see Supplementary Methods).

We posit that part of the reason GYS32661 was effective in SHH-MB is due to higher expression of the guanine-nucleotide-exchange factor for RAC1, TIAM1 in SHH-MB relative to other MB subtypes (Supplementary Figure S12A-C). Indeed, RAC1 activity was highest in SHH-MB relative to G3-MB cells as judged by a GST-PAK-PBD pull-down assay (Supplementary Figure S12A). Consistent with this finding, GYS32661 reduced cell viability of SHH-MB cells to a greater extent relative to Group 3 MB cells (Supplementary Figure S12B). Overall, these studies suggest that RAC1 activity is higher in SHH-driven MB relative to other MB types.

In addition to effectively reducing SHH-MB tumor burden in vivo, GYS32661 was well-tolerated in animals. Tumor-bearing mice treated with GYS32661 did not show any reduction in body weight compared to untreated mice (Supplementary Figure S2A). Similarly, toxicological studies in rats suggest that GYS32661 has no negative effect on the body weight of either male or female rats (Supplementary Figure S2B and C). Further, the dose used in our efficacy studies (35 mg/kg) is well below the maximum tolerated dose tested in rats (45 mg/kg; Supplementary Figure S2B and C). To assess the effect of treatment on the visceral organs, liver and brain weights were also measured in the same groups of male and female rats, and no observable toxicity was found (0-45 mg/kg/day for 28 days; Supplementary Figure S3A-D and Supplementary Tables S3 and S4). We also assessed the effect of GYS32661 on motor function by a rotarod test of C57BL/6 mice treated (35 mg/kg) for 3 weeks. We found no difference in normalized latency after 3 days of rotarod learning (Supplementary Figure S2D), suggesting the compound is not toxic at this dose. Importantly, GYS32661 is 50% brain penetrant as determined by HPLC analysis of brain and plasma levels (Supplementary Figure S3E and F). Collectively, these studies suggest that GYS32661 is brain penetrant and safe in animal models (see Supplementary Methods).

RAC1 Knockdown Reduces In Vivo SHH-MB Development and Improves Survival

To determine whether RAC1 deletion in human SHH-MB cells reduces proliferation, we generated doxycycline-inducible knockdown ONS76 cells. We induced RAC1 knockdown with doxycycline for 72 hours in vitro and measured proliferation via a crystal violet assay. As shown in (Supplementary Figure S11A and B), doxycycline addition to cells expressing inducible RAC1 shRNA constructs reduced proliferation in vitro. In addition, we implanted these cells in the brains of NSG mice as previously described40 and found that relative to control shRNA, mice bearing ONS76 cells expressing RAC1 shRNA had better overall survival after doxycycline administration in the drinking water (Supplementary Figure S11C and D). Collectively, these studies suggest that RAC1 deletion can reduce tumor growth and improve survival (see Supplementary Methods).

Discussion

Our present study provides compelling evidence that RAC1 controls SHH-MB growth by regulating GLI1 and GLI2 levels. Genetic or pharmacological inhibition of RAC1 reduced GLI1 and GLI2 levels in vitro. In addition, GYS32661 reduced tumor growth and increased survival in a mouse model of SHH-MB. It is also safe in multiple animal models. Collectively, our findings demonstrate that GYS32661 is a promising treatment for SHH-MB.

Microarray and scRNA-seq analysis of neoplastic relative to non-neoplastic cells showed high RAC1 expression in neoplastic cells, with low expression across various non-neoplastic cell types (Figure 1A-C). Further, RAC1 protein levels were higher in medulloblastoma patient samples relative to the normal cerebellum. Notably, RAC1 levels are further increased in metastatic MB compared to non-metastatic MB, suggesting a potential association with tumorigenesis and dissemination (Figure 1D). Collectively, our results suggest RAC1 mRNA and protein levels are higher in neoplastic cells within medulloblastoma tumors relative to normal cerebellum. These findings support the notion that RAC1 plays a role in MB tumorigenesis with limited effects on non-tumor brain tissue, and therefore, it may represent a viable therapeutic target for SHH-MB. However, direct assessment of RAC1 activity in tumor cells and different subtypes of MB relative to the surrounding cerebellum is needed to support the conclusion that it can be selectively targeted in SHH-MB.

Although RAC1 has been described within the context of SHH signaling, we present a new model of how it affects this important pathway in SHH-MB. Prior studies focused on a possible role for RAC1 in GLI1 shuttling from the cytoplasm to the nucleus.20 However, we did not observe any concentration or time point where GYS32661 treatment affected GLI1 shuttling. Rather, under all conditions, we observed a marked reduction in the total mRNA and protein levels of GLI1, GLI2, DNMT1, and UHRF1. This is consistent with a role for RAC1 in the transcription of these genes, supported by our findings that RAC1 localizes to the GLI1 and DNMT1 upstream loci. Therefore, RAC1 may have a similar role in other cancers where it acts in the nucleus to affect transcription of essential genes involved in proliferation, migration, and metastasis.35,36,38,47

Indeed, nuclear roles for RAC1 have been previously reported. RAC1 nuclear localization is associated with the G2 phase of the cell cycle, actin dynamics regulation, and cell shape.47–50 In breast cancer cells, nuclear RAC1 interacts with the ER (estrogen receptor) chromatin region and regulates RNA Pol II activity and ER transcription.35 In prostate cancer cells, nuclear active RAC1 is involved in actin-dependent nuclear membrane organization. RAC1 export from the cytoplasm to the nucleus is controlled by 2 NES motifs and the chaperone B23. Further, RAC1 nuclear shuttling establishes the RAC1-RHO balance, activates RhoA signaling in the cytoplasm, and enhances the highly invasive phenotype of cancer cells.47

Given that RAC1 plays multiple roles in different cells it is not surprising that it affects both proliferation and migration of SHH-MB cells. Although it is not possible to separate the effects on migration from proliferation in dividing cells as we observed migration differences after only one hour of GYS32661, we favor a model where RAC1 does plays a role in the proliferation of SHH-MB cells. As shown in our FACS analysis, RAC1 inhibition via GYS32661 treatment increased the percentage of cells in the G1 phase of the cell cycle, reduced G2 and S phase cells, and reduced Cyclin D1 levels (Supplementary Figures S5C and S6B and C). This suggests that RAC1 inhibition regulates cell cycle progression, which is consistent with its role in other cancers.36,49 Our data suggest that RAC1’s effects on the cell cycle are at least in part due to RAC1 binding to the GLI1 upstream loci and affecting GLI1 transcription, although we acknowledge it is difficult to separate its multiple roles in the cell cycle. We also posit that RAC1 inhibition can be partially overcome by GLI1 overexpression as shown in Supplementary Figure 12E, although this may require simultaneous GLI1, GLI2, DNMT1, and UHRF1 expression to fully overcome the effects of GYS32661.

One recent study showed that Netrin-1 is essential for SHH-MB development, and Netrin-1 is blocked by a monoclonal antibody, and its inhibitor reduces tumor growth and improves survival.51 Trio binds to Netrin-1 and activates RAC1 in axon outgrowth and guidance.50 Importantly, RAC1 and Netrin are both essential for SHH-MB development. Therefore, there could be potential crosstalk of the Trio-Netrin-1 and RAC1-CDC42-Vav2 cascades in SHH-signaling. Consistent with this model, we find that GYS32661 reduces Tiam1 levels (Supplementary Figure S6E; see Supplementary Methods).

The effects of RAC1 on proliferation can also be related to primary cilia dysfunction, as suggested by other studies.20,52–54 However, our analysis of GYS32661-treated cells did not indicate a change in the length of primary cilia (Supplementary Figure S6A). We further noted that treatment with GYS32661 increased α-tubulin acetylation substantially, which is associated with changes in proliferation based on prior studies. By contrast, GYS32661 did not affect Arl13b accumulation, which is associated with longer cilia in other studies.55 Therefore, the effect of GYS32661 on primary cilia number is likely related to its ability to inhibit the cell cycle in ONS76 cells.

Our studies suggest that targeting SHH-MB via inhibiting RAC1 with GYS32661 is a novel means of downregulating GLI1, GLI2, UHRF1, and DNMT1 activity. This indicates GLI proteins as potential druggable targets to treat medulloblastoma patients. Recently, an epigenetic regulatory complex, GLI-UHRF1-DNMT1, has been found to be a key regulator and druggable target of SHH-MB.4 In a genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 knockout screen of human (DAOY) and murine (SMB21) SHH-MB cells, DNMT1 has been identified as an epigenetic regulator and a potential target for monotherapy for SHH-MB.56 Normal cerebellar development and SHH-MB expansion depend on DNMT1. Targeting SHH signaling downstream of SMO, either through its pharmacological inhibition alone or in conjunction with SMO inhibitors, effectively restricts tumor development and increases survival in SHH-MB models.57–59

UHRF1 is a key regulator of DNA methylation in mammalian cells. Research has also indicated that UHRF1 contributes to the development of tumors.60–62 In a recent study, immunostaining and immunoblotting of 68 MB patients showed that UHRF1 expression is present in MB tissues but absent in the normal cerebellum.62 In the same study, RNAi-mediated UHRF1 downregulation inhibited MB cell proliferation and colony formation, induced G1/G2 cell cycle arrest, elevated p16 expression, and altered CDK4 localization. Altogether these results suggest that UHRF1 may be a useful biomarker and treatment target for MB and therefore GYS32661’s ability to decrease UHRF1 levels in SHH-MB tumors is therapeutically advantageous.

Our present study identifies crosstalk between RAC1-mediated GLI1 activity and the GLI epigenetic complex-mediated GLI1 activity in SHH-MB development. This is the first report of RAC1 inhibition targeting every component of the GLI-UHRF1-DNMT1 epigenetic complex. Collectively, our study suggests a novel role for RAC1 in targeting the druggable GLI epigenetic complex by regulating the transcription of the components of the complex, which can restrict SHH-MB development (Graphical abstract).

GYS32661’s ability to reduce on-target biomarkers in the SHH pathway in orthotopic models is likely related to its considerable brain penetrance (Supplementary Figure S3E and F). In the present study, the brain and plasma level assessment showed that brain levels of GYS32661 reach ∼1.5 µM and stay above 1 µM for ∼1 hour. The half-life of GYS32661 is substantially longer in the brain compared to plasma, supporting meaningful target engagement (Figure 5 and Supplementary Figure S3).

Since RAC1 requires only partial inhibition to reduce RAC1-GTP levels, transient exposure can produce prolonged transcriptional repression in prostate cancer cells even after the drug is washed out.63 Similarly, a very short duration of GYS32661 treatment was enough to suppress estrogen receptor-driven transcription for more than 8 hours in breast cancer cells.35 Together, these studies suggest that the kinetics of RAC1 signaling may differ substantially between canonical cytoskeletal rearrangement pathways and non-canonical transcriptional regulatory functions.

Although other RAC1 inhibitors have been described33,64 to our knowledge, this is the first time one has been demonstrated to be brain penetrant and effective in animal models of SHH-MB. Although we cannot formally exclude the possibility that GYS32661’s effects in reducing MB proliferation in vitro and in vivo are RAC1 or GLI-independent, we provide several lines of evidence suggesting that RAC1 activity is required. First, RAC1-GTP and not RhoA-GTP levels are affected by GYS32661 treatment. Second, overexpression of a constitutively active version of RAC1, RAC1-V12, overcame the proliferation inhibition of GYS32661 in MB cells, and restored binding to GLI1 and DNMT1 upstream loci. Further, the observed in vivo effects for reducing GLI1 and GLI2 levels are completely aligned with a dose-dependent pharmacodynamic (PD) response observed in the present study.

Importantly, GYS32661 showed no toxicity in body weight, health, or motor coordination by the rotarod test (Supplementary Figures S2 and S3), suggesting it can be used safely to inhibit RAC1 and reduce SHH-MB tumor burden. These studies and other drug-like characteristics of GYS32661 suggest that a clinical trial should be considered for patients suffering from SHH-MB.
