Ligand Engineering of Dithiolate-Protected Au24Pt Nanoclusters for Improved Thermocatalytic Activity
Authors: Riki Nakatani, Tokuhisa Kawawaki, Sara Yoshikawa, Chaoqi Chen, Taishi Suzuki, Soichi Kikkawa, Satoshi Anada, Seiji Yamazoe, Yuichi Negishi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Thiolate-protected gold (Au) nanoclusters (NCs) are a well-established class of model NCs that have been extensively studied as catalysts for various reactions. However, many Au NCs feature surfaces completely passivated by ligands, leaving no exposed metal sites to serve as active centers. To address this issue, researchers have searched for methods to remove the ligands. However, simply introducing weakly bound ligands often compromises the structural stability of the NCs. In this study, we identified a novel Au NC that remains stable even with relatively weakly bound thiolate ligands by reinforcing the staple motifs through the introduction of dithiolate ligands. This Au NC exhibits excellent CO oxidation activity at relatively low temperatures, because the ligands can dissociate under relatively mild conditions. These findings show that advanced ligand engineering, beyond the metal core structure, significantly affects catalytic activity, providing a robust strategy for the design of diverse metal NC catalysts.

Atomically precise metal nanoclusters (NCs) have emerged as highly active catalysts for various applications. − The geometric and electronic structures of these NCs can be tailored through atomic-level control of the constituent atom count, crystal structure, and alloying. − Among these NCs, monothiolate (SR)-protected gold NCs (Au n (SR) m ) have been extensively investigated owing to their exceptional thermal and chemical stability. However, the surfaces of many Au n (SR) m systems are extensively passivated by organic ligands, which, while ensuring structural integrity, hinders the exposure of the active metal sites. Consequently, the direct application of Au n (SR) m as catalysts often results in suboptimal activity. To overcome this limitation, several groups have attempted to create active sites by removing the ligands via thermal, − chemical, − or electrochemical − treatments. For instance, Jin et al. , reported that Au25(SR)18/CeO2, pretreated at relatively low temperatures under an oxygen atmosphere, functions as a highly active CO oxidation catalyst, with the metal–support interface serving as the active site. Tsukuda et al. , reported the size-dependent activity of Au n (SR) m in cyclohexane oxidation and the effects of heterometallic doping in alcohol oxidation after pretreatment at 300 °C under reduced pressure. Furthermore, studies by Noelia, Rupprechter, and Bürgi − revealed that the SR ligands desorbed during thermal treatment can remain on the support as sulfur oxides. Crucially, ligand desorption under harsh thermal conditions often triggers the aggregation of Au n (SR) m owing to thermally induced atomic diffusion. These challenges underscore the necessity for developing methods to desorb ligands from Au n (SR) m and their alloy NCs under mild conditions to effectively expose reactive sites while maintaining their atomic precision.

Motivated by these considerations, the aim of this study is to develop an alloy NC (Au24Pt NC) that achieves both high stability and high catalytic reactivity through the strategic modulation of the ligand binding strength. Conventional Au24Pt [Au24Pt­(SR)18] NCs, featuring relatively strongly bound SR ligands, exhibit high structural stability but suffer from low reactivity (Figure a). Conversely, although the introduction of more weakly bound ligands can lower the threshold temperature for ligand removal, it typically compromises the intrinsic stability of the NC (Figure b). To overcome this trade-off, we designed a stable yet easily activatable Au24Pt NC by bridging weakly bonded SR ligands with dithiolate (SR′S) groups. This approach is intended to compensate for the ligand-induced instability by reinforcing the staple motifs, thereby creating a robust structure that remains susceptible to low-temperature ligand desorption (Figure c). The efficacy of this ligand engineering strategy was evaluated through a detailed analysis of the ligand detachment process and an assessment of the thermal catalytic activity.

Ligand Desorption Mechanism of Au24Pt NCs

[Au24Pt­(TBBT)12(TDT)3]0 (TBBT = 4-tert-butylbenzenethiolate, TDT = thiodithiolate) (protected by both SR and SR′S) was synthesized according to our previously reported protocols (Figures S1–S3). For comparison, SR-protected clusters, [Au24Pt­(PET)18]0 (PET = 2-phenylethanethiolate) and [Au24Pt­(TBBT)18]0, were also prepared following established methods (Figures S1–S3). In this case, the enhanced core robustness resulting from Pt doping serves a pivotal role in suppressing the ligand-exchange-induced structure transformation (LEIST) associated with the introduction of TBBT. While all three NCs possess nearly identical Au12Pt core frameworks, they exhibit distinct staple motifs. [Au24Pt­(PET)18]0 and [Au24Pt­(TBBT)18]0 feature six Au2(SR)3 staples (SR–Au–SR–Au–SR) encircling the core. − In contrast, [Au24Pt­(TBBT)12(TDT)3]0 is characterized by three elongated staples, each composed of (SR)2Au2–SR′S–Au2(SR)2 units, where two Au2(SR)2 staples are bridged by a SR′S ligand. This structural transition is driven by the replacement of the core-site S atom (originally from the TBBT ligand) with the terminal S atom of the TDT; consequently, the incoming S atom of TDT anchors to the core site, forming cooperative bonds with the central Au12Pt core framework. Therefore, these elongated (SR)2Au2–SR′S–Au2(SR)2 motifs can be described as four-coordinated to the Au12Pt core.

Before studying the ligand desorption behavior, the structural stability of these NCs in solution was examined (Figures S4). The results revealed that [Au24Pt­(TBBT)18]0 possessed poor stability due to the weak coordinating ability of the TBBT ligands. This inherent instability was further evidenced by the gradual mass loss observed in the low-temperature regime (from ∼110 °C) of the TG curve (Figure S5). This fragility caused the cluster framework to collapse before its deposition onto the CeO2 support, making catalyst synthesis unfeasible. Therefore, we focused our further experiments solely on [Au24Pt­(TBBT)12(TDT)3]0 and [Au24Pt­(PET)18]0.

The ligand desorption behavior was investigated by our established methodology (Figures , S6 and S7, Tables S1 and S2). The desorbed species were identified by direct insertion probe mass spectrometry (DIP-MS), which allows for real-time monitoring of the species evolved during the heating process. In the DIP-MS spectrum of [Au24Pt­(PET)18]0, major signals were observed at mass-to-charge (m/z) ratios of 105 and 274 during heating from 80 to 500 °C (Figure a and Tables S1). Notably, the peak corresponding to the intact PET ligand (m/z = 137) was not detected with significant intensity. The signals at m/z = 105 and 274 were assigned to phenylethane (PE) and the disulfide dimer ((PET)2), respectively. These species originate from the cleavage of S–C and Au–S bonds within the PET ligand. This desorption pattern is qualitatively consistent with our previous findings for [Au25(PET)18]−. The temperature profiles of these desorbed species indicate a two-step desorption process, occurring at 140–245 °C and 250–265 °C (Figures c and S6).

In contrast, the DIP-MS spectrum of [Au24Pt­(TBBT)12(TDT)3]0 exhibited signals at m/z = 165, 179, and 330 (Figure b and Table S2). These peaks are attributed to the dissociation products resulting from Au–S bond cleavage of the TBBT and TDT ligands (Figure S8). Unlike [Au24Pt­(PET)18]0, species originating from S–C bond cleavage were scarcely detected, suggesting that ligand desorption in [Au24Pt­(TBBT)12(TDT)3]0 mainly proceeded via Au–S bond dissociation. Furthermore, the temperature profiles of these species revealed that desorption occurred in a well-defined, single step within the 160–195 °C range (Figure d). While thermal desorption typically triggers S–C bond cleavage due to the robust Au–SR interaction, the weakly coordinating nature of the TBBT ligands in [Au24Pt­(TBBT)12(TDT)3]0 alters this pathway. The resulting tendency toward Au–S bond cleavage serves as an effective strategy to mitigate sulfur poisoning at the catalyst surface.

The differences in the desorption temperatures and peak profiles were further investigated by correlating the NC structures with their interatomic bond distances. Single-crystal X-ray diffraction analysis was performed to compare the Au–S and C–S bond lengths across the NCs (Figures and S9, Table S3). Specifically, we categorized and evaluated the bonds between the core and staple atoms, focusing on the distances between the core/apex gold atoms (Aucore and Austaple), the corresponding sulfur atoms (Score and Sapex), and the carbon atoms of SR or SR′S (CSR and CSR′S). For [Au24Pt­(PET)18]0 and [Au24Pt­(TBBT)12(TDT)3]0, the total Au–S bond lengths were 2.32 ± 0.04 and 2.34 ± 0.04 Å, respectively. Although the bond difference was small, [Au24Pt­(TBBT)12(TDT)3]0 exhibited slightly longer, and thus relatively weaker, Au–S bonds (Figure c–e, Table S3). Conversely, the total C–S bond length of [Au24Pt­(TBBT)12(TDT)3]0 (1.80 ± 0.02 Å) was shorter than that of [Au24Pt­(PET)18]0 (1.84 ± 0.05 Å), indicating a stronger C–S interaction in the former (Figure f–h, Table S3). These structural variations are mainly attributed to the different electron-donating abilities and bulkiness of the TBBT and PET ligands; the aromatic TBBT ligand is less electron donating and more bulky than the aliphatic PET ligand. ,, Consequently, the staple motifs in [Au24Pt­(TBBT)12(TDT)3]0 are more loosely bound to the metal core. Indeed, the Aucore–Score distance in [Au24Pt­(TBBT)12(TDT)3]0 (2.39 ± 0.03 Å) was longer than that in [Au24Pt­(PET)18]0 (2.37 ± 0.03 Å), corroborating the presence of relatively weaker Aucore–Score bonds in [Au24Pt­(TBBT)12(TDT)3]0 (Table S3). As shown in Figure c, [Au24Pt­(TBBT)12(TDT)3]0 exhibit longer bond lengths across almost all corresponding Aucore–Score bonds compared to the [Au24Pt­(PET)18]0. These observations suggest that structural dissociation is more prone to occur at the weakened, elongated Au–S bonds. This trend is consistent with [Au24Pt­(TBBT)18]0, which also features a Aucore–Score distance of 2.39 ± 0.03 Å and a total C–S distance of 1.78 ± 0.02 Å. Furthermore, whether in solution or on a support, the steric bulkiness and rigidity of the TBBT ligand likely promote its dissociation without accommodating or alleviating the overall structural distortion of the metal NC induced by solvent and support interactions.

DIP-MS analysis revealed that ligand desorption from [Au24Pt­(TBBT)12(TDT)3]0 was completed at a significantly lower temperature than that for [Au24Pt­(PET)18]0 (195 °C vs 270 °C). This reduction in the desorption termination temperature is mainly ascribed to the relatively weak Au–S bonds inherent in the [Au24Pt­(TBBT)12(TDT)3]0 structure. These results indicate that the specific ligand environment in this NC facilitates the total removal of organic shells under relatively mild thermal conditions.

Overall, these findings suggest that, even for NCs possessing identical metal cores, the desorption process is highly sensitive to the nature of the protective ligands. The magnitude and distribution of the Au–S and C–S bond distances serve as critical descriptors for predicting and controlling the low-temperature exposure of the active metal sites. Such insights into ligand engineering provide a rational basis for designing highly active NC catalysts while minimizing thermally induced structural degradation.

Thus, far, we have focused on the ligand desorption processes of unsupported NCs. Next, the DIP-MS spectra (Figures S10–12) of the NCs supported on CeO2 (Figure S13) were examined. [Au24Pt­(TBBT)12(TDT)3]0 and [Au24Pt­(PET)18]0 were loaded onto the CeO2 support by a standard impregnation method. CeO2 is one of the most widely utilized supports for CO oxidation, owing to its exceptional oxygen storage capacity and redox flexibility. ,, For both catalysts, the identified dissociation products were consistent with those observed for their unsupported counterparts. Furthermore, the temperature profiles of the ionized species at each m/z exhibited trends similar to those of the precursor NCs, despite slight shifts in the temperature ranges following deposition onto the support.

Our previous study using p-mercaptobenzoic acid, a ligand featuring a carboxylic acid group capable of chemically bonding with metal oxide supports, suggested that the interaction strength between the support and ligand significantly modulates the desorption process. , In the present system, for [Au24Pt­(TBBT)12(TDT)3]0/CeO2, ligand removal was completed at a temperature approximately 50 °C lower than that for [Au24Pt­(PET)18]0/CeO2 (210 °C vs 260 °C). This confirmed that the inherent desorption characteristics of the NCs were preserved for the supported NCs. These findings indicate that [Au24Pt­(TBBT)12(TDT)3]0/CeO2 can be activated under significantly milder pretreatment conditions than [Au24Pt­(PET)18]0/CeO2, which is expected to enhance the catalytic performance by minimizing core aggregation.

CO Oxidation Ability of the Au24Pt NCs

Following the investigation of the ligand desorption processes, we evaluated the thermal catalytic activity of the NCs using CO oxidation, the conversion of toxic CO to CO2, as a model reaction. ,, Before catalytic testing, multiple analytical techniques confirmed that no significant structural changes, such as aggregation, occurred upon loading the NCs onto CeO2 (Figures and S14). Specifically, transmission electron microscopy (TEM), high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM), and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping confirmed that the NCs remained highly dispersed on the CeO2 support, maintaining their original size distribution. Furthermore, X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS) measurements were performed to verify the stability of the electronic and geometric structures (Figures c and S14). The Au L3-edge X-ray absorption near-edge structure spectra revealed that the absorption edges for both the precursor NCs and supported catalysts corresponded to a near-zerovalent state, indicating electronic consistency and the absence of significant charge-state alterations (Figure S14). Additionally, Fourier-transform extended X-ray absorption fine structure analysis showed peaks corresponding to Au–S (∼1.8 Å) and Au–Au (∼2.8 Å) coordination in both samples, with no significant deviations in the bond distances or intensities (Figure c). These results collectively confirmed that the structural integrity of the NCs was preserved without aggregation during the supporting process.

Next, the catalytic performance of [Au24Pt­(TBBT)12(TDT)3]0/CeO2 and [Au24Pt­(PET)18]0/CeO2 was evaluated by monitoring CO conversion during a temperature-programmed reaction to investigate the effect of pretreatment on the catalytic activity (Figures S15 and S16). The CO oxidation profiles for the untreated catalysts are shown in Figure S16a. The activation onset temperature (defined as the temperature at which CO conversion reaches 0.5%) was approximately 236 °C for [Au24Pt­(PET)18]0/CeO2. In contrast, [Au24Pt­(TBBT)12(TDT)3]0/CeO2 exhibited an activation onset temperature of 213 °C, representing a 23 °C reduction in the reaction initiation temperature. In these untreated states, the active metal sites remain passivated by the protective ligands, resulting in negligible activity until the temperature reaches a threshold sufficient for in situ ligand desorption, at which point the CO conversion sharply increases. Subsequently, we evaluated the activity of the catalysts after specific pretreatment based on the DIP-MS results. The CO oxidation activity following pretreatment at 250 °C for 30 min under an oxygen atmosphere is shown in Figure S16b. For [Au24Pt­(PET)18]0/CeO2, the onset temperature significantly shifted to 128 °C (from 236 °C for the untreated sample). Remarkably, [Au24Pt­(TBBT)12(TDT)3]0/CeO2 initiated CO oxidation at an even lower temperature of approximately 110 °C. Furthermore, the temperature required for 50% conversion was 301 °C for [Au24Pt­(PET)18]0/CeO2, whereas it was 262 °C for [Au24Pt­(TBBT)12(TDT)3]0/CeO2, a substantial difference of 39 °C. These results suggest that subtle variations in the ligand desorption process not only dictate the activation temperature, but they also contribute to the effective structural control of the resulting active sites. By precisely modulating the surface chemistry of NCs through ligand engineering, we have successfully developed metal NC catalysts that exhibit superior reactivity to the untreated catalysts at significantly lower temperatures.

In this study, we elucidated the effect of ligand engineering on the thermal catalytic activity of Au24Pt NCs protected by SR and SR′S ligands. Our findings reveal that ligand desorption in [Au24Pt­(TBBT)12(TDT)3]0 proceeds via preferential Au–S bond cleavage, which is facilitated by the inherently weaker Au–S interactions of the TBBT ligand. Importantly, the introduction of SR′S bridges confers superior structural stability to the staple motifs, allowing [Au24Pt­(TBBT)12(TDT)3]0 to maintain its geometric integrity despite the presence of these labile Au–S bonds. This synergistic combination of weak Au–S bonding and staple reinforcement enables low-temperature activation, achieving complete ligand removal at significantly lower temperatures than conventional NCs. Consequently, [Au24Pt­(TBBT)12(TDT)3]0 exhibits markedly higher CO oxidation activity at lower temperatures than [Au24Pt­(PET)18]0. These results show that the strategic introduction of SR′S stabilizes the NC framework while simultaneously lowering the energy barrier for ligand desorption. This dual functionality enables the preparation of robust yet highly active metal NC catalysts. The findings of this study provide a rational design strategy for creating metal NC-supported catalysts with exceptional activity, selectivity, and durability. Future research will focus on elucidating the precise correlation between the ligand desorption mechanisms and postactivation surface structures of the supported NCs to further refine the catalyst design. −

Methods

Synthesis of [Au24Pt­(PET)18]0

AuCl­(PPh3) was prepared by mixing HAuCl4 (4.80 mmol) and PPh3 (10.6 mmol) in acetone at 0 °C, followed by conversion to Au­(NO3)­(PPh3) using AgNO3 in dichloromethane. Concurrently, Pt­(PPh3)3 was obtained by refluxing Pt­(PPh3)4 in ethanol under argon. These intermediates were reacted in THF under H2 for 3 h, and subsequently treated with triethylamine and additional Au­(NO3)­(PPh3) overnight. The product was crystallized from methanol/diethyl ether to yield [PtAu8(PPh3)8]­(NO3)2. Next, this precursor was dissolved in ethanol and reduced with NaBH4 (0.1 M, 500 μL). A solution of AuCl­(S­(CH3)2) and PETH (900 μmol) in THF was then added, and the mixture was stirred for 30 min. After adding triethylamine (1.20 mmol) and stirring for another 30 min, the crude product was purified via an alumina column (dichloromethane/hexane = 1:1) to afford [Au24Pt­(PET)18]0.

Synthesis of [Au24Pt­(TBBT)18]0

[Au24Pt­(PET)18]0 (5.0 mg) was dissolved in toluene (3.4 mL), mixed with TBBTH (500 μL, 98%, FUJIFILM), and stirred at 60 °C for 1.5 h. The mixture was washed with water/methanol and extracted with dichloromethane. Purification via TLC (dichloromethane/hexane = 7:13) yielded [Au24Pt­(TBBT)18]0. ,

Synthesis of [Au24Pt­(TBBT)12(TDT)3]0

[Au24Pt­(PET)18]0 (5.0 mg) and TBBTH (500 μL, 97%, Tokyo Chemical Industry) were reacted in toluene (3.4 mL) at 60 °C for 1.5 h. Following water/methanol washing and dichloromethane extraction, the final product was isolated by TLC using a dichloromethane/hexane (7:13) eluent.

Preparation of [Au24Pt­(TBBT)12(TDT)3]0/CeO2 and [Au24Pt­(PET)18]0/CeO2

The presynthesized Au24Pt NCs were dissolved in acetone and supported on CeO2 via an impregnation method. Based on the concentration measured by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry, a solution was prepared to achieve a metal (Au and Pt) loading of 0.5 wt % on the CeO2 support. The solution was added dropwise onto the CeO2 support in a watch glass, and the sample was then dried.

DIP-MS Measurements

The DIP-MS measurements were performed with a JMS-Q1500GC analyzer (JEOL, Tokyo, Japan). The sample was introduced into a glass tube with a diameter of approximately 1.6 mm and heated from 80 to 500 °C at a rate of 1 °C min–1 under reduced pressure (∼1.0 × 10–4 Pa). The desorbed ligand was directly inserted into the mass spectrometer, where each compound was ionized by electron impact. Peaks with m/z = 18 (H2O), 28 (N2), 207 and 281 (derived from the column or grease) were observed even when the sample was not loaded.

Thermocatalytic Measurements

All of the thermocatalytic measurements of the CO oxidation reaction were performed with a BELCAT II catalyst analyzer (MicrotracBEL, Osaka, Japan). First, 30 mg of the catalyst was introduced into a sample tube with an inner diameter of 13 mm. As a pretreatment, the sample was heated from 50 to 250 °C at a rate of 10 °C min–1 under a flow of 95 mL min–1 of He and 5 mL min–1 of O2, and then held at 250 °C for 30 min. The sample was then cooled to 50 °C. Subsequentially, the sample was heated from 50 to 350 °C at a rate of 5 °C min–1 and then held at 350 °C for 5 min, in a gas mixture consisting of 96.5 mL min–1 of He, 1.5 mL min–1 of CO, and 2.0 mL min–1 of O2. CO and produced CO2 detection was performed using an infrared absorption gas detector (PG-300, HORIBA, Kyoto, Japan). Before the measurements, calibration was performed using 4608.0 ppm of CO, 9.502 vol % O2, and 4.9860 vol % CO2.
