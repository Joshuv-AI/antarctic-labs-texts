Association between common mental disorder and glycemic control in women with gestational diabetes: A mixed-method study
Authors: Bhanushree Soni, Venkatachalam Jayaseelan, Shivanand Kattimani, Sasirekha Rengaraj, Kalaiselvy Arikrishnan, Naveenkumar Veerasetty
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is associated with an increased risk of mental health disorders among pregnant women. Poor mental health can negatively impact glycemic control in women with GDM, leading to adverse outcomes for both the mother and the baby.Aim:To determine the prevalence of common mental disorder (CMD) in women with GDM and its association with poor glycemic control. Additionally, to explore the reasons and coping strategies for CMD in women with GDM.Methods:A sequential explanatory mixed-method study was conducted among 351 women with GDM visiting a tertiary care hospital. The prevalence of CMD was assessed using Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7), Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), and Perceived Stress Scale (PSS). Glycemic control was determined based on two-hour postprandial blood glucose levels. In-depth interviews were conducted with six women who screened positive for CMD and had poor glycemic control. Data were analyzed using SPSS v23 and stats v12. Chi-square test and Poisson regression were performed, and adjusted prevalence ratios (aPRs) were reported.Results:The prevalence of CMD was found to be 19.08% (95% CI: 15.32%-23.52%), with 18.2% (95% CI: 14.5%-22.6%) anxiety symptoms 8.3% (95% CI: 5.8%-11.6%) depressive symptoms and stress each. CMD was significantly related to poor glycemic control (aPR: 1.58; 95% CI: 1.23-2.03; P value <0.001). The qualitative analysis revealed individual, family, health, and facility factors influencing mental health and glycemic control.Conclusion:Common mental health disorders are prevalent in women with GDM. It has a negative association with glycemic control. Implementing a routine screening program in the ANC clinic can aid in early identification and prompt management of the CMD and its associated complications.

INTRODUCTION

In India, mental health problems are increasing, with one in six individuals needing medical attention.[1] Pregnancy and motherhood can be overwhelming, and a diagnosis of diabetes during pregnancy can exacerbate the risk of mental health disorders. Approximately 15% of pregnant women experience mental health disorders, and those with GDM are at a two to fourfold higher risk.[23] In women with GDM, the link between negative mental states like anxiety, depression, and stress with diabetes is recognized as bidirectional.[34] The awareness of the risk associated with both the pregnancy outcome and the baby’s health makes them vulnerable. Alternatively, a negative mental state can cause immune dysfunction leading to GDM.[5]

In women with GDM, poor glycemic control is influenced by clinical, social, and psychological factors. In Puducherry, 38.5% of pregnant women have poor glycemic control.[6] The prevalence of CMDs is 23.6%, with 18.8% anxiety, 6.9% depression, and 4.2% stress.[7] CMDs can lead to poor glycemic control by reducing motivation toward treatment compliance, diet adherence, and physical activity, increasing the risk of serious outcomes.[8]

Our study focuses on the co-occurrence of GDM and CMD. We followed a mixed-methods design, combining quantitative surveys and qualitative in-depth interviews. The primary objective was to estimate the prevalence of CMD and to determine its association with glycemic control using a quantitative approach in women with GDM. Additionally, the study aimed to explore the reasons and coping strategies for CMD as lived experiences of women with GDM using a qualitative approach to gain a more comprehensive understanding.

METHODOLOGY

Study design and setting

Our study was a sequential explanatory mixed-method study conducted from March 2022 to April 2023. The study was done in two phases: quantitative phase (cross-sectional analytical study) followed by the qualitative phase (in-depth interviews). Ethical clearance was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee (No. JIP/IEC/2022/131). The study was based on pregnant women attending in-patient and out-patient departments at a tertiary care hospital in South India. The facility caters to high-risk pregnancies from nearby districts, with common medical disorders like hypothyroidism, anemia, GDM, and hypertension. Maternal diabetes screening and management are provided free of cost, representing 10–15% of total deliveries, with approximately 30% requiring medications for blood glucose control during pregnancy.

Pregnant women diagnosed with GDM, attending the antenatal clinic or admitted to the ward in their second or third trimester while being on treatment for at least 2 weeks were included in the quantitative part of the study. These women were screened for anxiety, depression, and stress levels using standard screening tools. These women received appropriate clinical management along with dietary counseling. Women less than 18 years of age, having any cognitive impairment, or were taking medication for pre-existing psychiatric illness were excluded from the study.

We did qualitative part through face-to-face in-depth interviews, to explore the reasons and coping strategies. Those screened positive for CMDs, while having poor glycemic control, and willing to converse, were included.

Sample size and sampling technique

The sample size was calculated using OpenEpi v3.0. The prevalence of anxiety symptoms among GDM mothers was anticipated to be 15%. At 5% level of significance and 25% relative precision, the estimated sample size was 349. All the consecutive eligible women with GDM who reported during the study period were included.

For the qualitative part, six in-depth interviews were considered as a minimum sample technique to reach data saturation based on expert opinion. Participants were selected based on the purposive (maximum variation) sampling technique.

Study tools and operational definitions

We collected sociodemographic, obstetric, and clinical data from each participant through pre-piloted proforma through semi-structured interview. Anxiety symptoms, depressive symptoms, and stress were assessed using Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7), Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), and Perceived Stress Scale (PSS). The scales have seven, nine, and ten questions, respectively. All three are freely available, validated in Tamil language, and self-report measures.

GAD-7 and PHQ-9 have statements related to the past two weeks. Each statement is scored on a Likert scale from 0 to 3, with 0 indicating “not at all,” 1 indicating “several days,” 2 indicating “more than half the days” and 3 indicating “nearly every day.” Higher the total score, higher the severity of the symptoms. For GAD-7, a score of 10 or higher was classified as having anxiety symptoms.[9] For PHQ-9, a score of 10 or more was indicative of the presence of depressive symptoms.[10]

PSS: Each item is evaluated as 0 (almost never), 1 (almost never), 2 (sometimes), 3 (fairly often), and 4 (very often) during the last month. The total score can be anything between 0 and 40. For our study, a total score of 20 or higher was considered as having stress.[11]

CMDs: Any participant having either screened positive for anxiety symptoms, depressive symptoms or stress was considered as having CMD.[12]

Poor glycemic control: After receiving regular treatment for at least two weeks, if the values of a person’s PPBS taken two hours after a meal are above 120 mg/dL, it indicates poor glycemic control.[13]

The participants were explained the purpose of the study and upon agreement, written informed consent was obtained. The most recent glucose level was taken from the medical records of the participant.

For qualitative, in-depth interviews (IDI) were conducted among six mothers who screened positive for depressive symptoms, anxiety symptoms, and/or stress along with having poor glycemic control. An interview guide was prepared by consulting specialists and a literature review. The guide was pilot tested among peers to improve the understandability of questions. The aim of IDI was explained to the study participants, consent was taken for participation, and recording of the interview was done. Privacy and confidentiality while interviewing were provided.

Data analysis

The data was entered into EpiCollect 5 and analyzed using SPSS v23 and Stata v12. All the categorical variables were summarized as frequency and percentage, and continuous variables as either mean (SD) or median (IQR), depending on their distribution. The proportion of anxiety, depression, and stress was presented as frequency and percentage with 95% confidence interval. The association of categorical variables with glycemic control was carried out using Chi-square, and the prevalence ratio was estimated. Anxiety, depression, and stress were combined as a dichotomous variable called CMD to avoid multicollinearity. The significant variables (P value <0.2) were considered in a generalized linear regression model with Poisson as family and log as link function and adjusted prevalence ratio (aPR) with 95% confidence interval was estimated. All the analysis was carried out at 5% level of significance, and a P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

For qualitative data, audio recordings were transcribed in verbatim format. Manual content analysis using deductive coding approach was used. Statements were the unit of analysis, and similar codes were clubbed into categories under themes. Thematic analysis of individual-, family-, health-, and facility-related aspects was obtained through in-depth interviews, and a conceptual framework was developed.

RESULTS

A total of 351 eligible participants with GDM were included. The mean (SD) age of participants was 28.8 (5.4) years. Most participants belonged to the below-poverty line (BPL) category, lived in rural areas, and traveled more than 10 km to avail services. Graduate or higher educated constituted 53% (186), whereas 18.5% (65) were employed [Table 1]. Of the total, 46.4% (163) women with co-morbidities, thyroid disorder was the most prevalent. Out of 351, 42.5% (149) were pregnant for the first time. For 70.9% (249) participants, GDM was diagnosed in the second trimester. 50.4% were on Medical Nutrition Therapy (MNT) exclusively and 88.9% adhered to the prescribed treatment. Out of the total participants, 40.5% (142) had poor glycemic control [Table 2].

DISCUSSION

The prevalence of anxiety symptoms, depressive symptoms, and stress among women with GDM was 18.2%, 8.3%, and 8.3%, respectively. A study conducted in Malaysia found a lower prevalence of anxiety symptoms but higher for depressive symptoms and stress, at 27.9%, 7.4%, and 3.8%, respectively.[7] Differences in study tools, participant demographics, and country-specific factors may account for the variation in the prevalence rates. There is a lack of research evidence in India. The prevalence of depression among women with GDM varies depending on the measurement tool used. In Kolkata, by Saha et al.,[14] the prevalence was found to be 8% using the Clinically Useful Depression Outcome Scale (CUDOS). However, in a study from South India by Sandhya et al.,[15] it was much higher at 32%, measured using the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS). The Sandhya et al. study considered mild, moderate, and severe as having depressive symptoms, whereas in our study only those with moderate or severe depressive symptoms were classified as having depressive symptoms. The absence of specific criteria for classification when using scales creates a hurdle in concluding an individual’s mental state, thereby adding to the difficulty in accurate assessments.

Our study showed a significant association between CMDs and poor glycemic control which was similarly reflected in a study by Tasnim S et al.[16] (r = 0.239; P value = 0.014) in Bangladesh. Saha et al.[14] in their study concluded higher mean OGTT values were observed in those screened positive for depression (P < 0.001). The evidence is abundant in type II diabetics. A study by Mansori et al.[17] in Iran contrasts our findings with nil association (OR: 1.11; 95% CI: 0.87-1.57) between glycemic control and depression. But it reported a significant (2.03 times) higher odds of depression in women as compared to men. Brown et al.[18] from Utah revealed a negative correlation that diabetics with mental health conditions had significantly better glycemic control. However, this may be confounded by factors, such as multiple physician appointments, regular monitoring, and integrated care. Several systematic reviews and meta-analyses are in line with our study findings indicating a significant association between mental health disorders and diabetes.[351920] CDC also recognizes depression as a major co-morbidity in diabetics which affects their motivation to follow the prescribed treatment and lifestyle modification.[21] Additionally, biological plausibility provides a sound background.[32022]

The biological pathways either accelerate gluconeogenesis or trouble the insulin receptors, increasing the glucose level beyond the metabolizing capacity of the human body. Our study also shows a significant association between multi-approach treatment and poor glycemic control. This throws light on unattended physiological processes which are persistently tempering the glucose levels, attenuated by the findings of Scifres et al.[23]

The prevalence of poor glycemic control in our study is similar to a study by Meena et al.[6] at 38.5%. The other factors found associated with glycemic control are type of family, past history of adverse pregnancy outcome, time of GDM diagnosis, treatment adherence, and planned pregnancy.

The nuclear family has a lack of social support. In a qualitative study, one of the women quoted “I’m with my mother. My father is away from us for his job. It’s difficult to manage.” Stating the importance of supportive joint living. This finding is also established by Sunny et al.[24] in type II diabetes patients where higher odds of depression (OR: 3.21; P value: 0.002) were in those living in smaller families in Nepal. Our finding states those without any history of complication have a higher prevalence of poor glycemic control. This finding may be attributed to the heightened vigilance and awareness among women with a history of complications. The timing of GDM diagnosis is another factor that affects glycemic control, with women diagnosed in the third trimester having poorer control. This may be due to the shorter duration available for intervention and management, and the need for more intensive interventions.[25] In addition, women who did not adhere to the treatment showed poor glycemic control. And women who did not plan their pregnancy may experience apprehension and concerns about the impact of GDM on their mental health, leading to poor glucose control. Cyganek et al.[26] in their study found mean HbA1c was significantly higher in the group with unplanned pregnancy.

Mental health counseling can be combined with diet counseling for comprehensive care in women with GDM. It was also highlighted in qualitative results stating “Mental health counselling for pregnant women will reduce their anxiety and confusion” and “It is very important, because if family is unsupportive and illiterate… it is hard to get mental health counselling… Provide counselling along with diabetes diagnosis as a routine practice.” Similar finding of confusion in the interpretation of treatment regimen, and management post-diagnosis due to improper counseling has been focused on a qualitative study by Arifin et al.[27] to study diabetes distress. WHO also advocates in support of our qualitative findings for the adoption of family and community-centered interventions to foster a conducive environment that promotes health literacy and minimizes misconceptions. Sensitization of relevant health workers toward providing sound and empathetic mental health counseling may improve the glycemic status in women with GDM further leading to a reduction in severity of adverse pregnancy outcomes among them.

Limitations

The cross-sectional design lacks the ability to determine temporality and establish causal relationships between CMD and glycemic control. Recruitment of patients from a single tertiary care hospital may limit the generalizability of the findings. The interpretation of glycemic control was based on the last available reading before the involvement of the study participant which is subjected to misclassification bias. The assessment of anxiety, depression, and stress are subject to recall and social desirability bias. The women with severe psychiatric symptoms were referred to mental health professionals but were not followed up to assess the persistence of symptoms.

Recommendations and future scope

Women with GDM are at risk of having mental health disorders which can be screened and managed as part of integrated holistic care to achieve better glycemic control. Supportive measures, including counseling, family inclusion, health system strengthening, and improved health literacy, can help establish a foundation for better coping with CMDs in women with GDM. This study lays the foundation for exploring the effects of poor mental health on glucose control among women with GDM using higher study designs. Prospective studies tracking from first trimester to third and including maternal and child outcomes during peripartum and postpartum periods need to be included in the future studies, for better validity of the results and a randomized-controlled trial intervening training of healthcare workers in providing mental health counseling could build a base on evidence-based practice. A deeper perspective on this association can be ascertained by strictly monitoring dose and adherence to treatment as confounders.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, our study reveals that anxiety, depression, and stress were prevalent among women with GDM and underscores their negative association with glycemic control. The qualitative analysis highlights the pivotal role of self, family, healthcare system, and overall well-being as crucial contributors in coping with these mental health issues. Consequently, our findings strongly advocate for the implementation of mental health counseling and a holistic approach that encompasses self, family, and healthcare support to effectively address the mental health challenges faced by women with GDM.
