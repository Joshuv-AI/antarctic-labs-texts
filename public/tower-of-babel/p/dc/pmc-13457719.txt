The role of glymphatic-related structural and functional imaging biomarkers in the pathological progression of Alzheimer’s disease: a multimodal imaging-based study
Authors: Mingyu Tan, Xiaosong Lan, Xiereniguli Anayiti, Peiying Chen, Yingying Liu, Bin Lin, Peijun Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe comparative utility of choroid plexus volume (CPV) and the diffusion tensor imaging-based perivascular space (DTI-ALPS) index as glymphatic biomarkers across the Alzheimer’s disease (AD) continuum is unclear. This study aimed to perform a head-to-head comparison of their relationships with AD pathology, cognition, and diagnostic performance.MethodsThis study analyzed data from 848 AD Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) participants [426 cognitively normal (CN), 309 with mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and 113 with AD dementia]. Group differences in CPV and the ALPS index were assessed using generalized linear models (GLM), and their associations with AD biomarkers and cognition were examined via partial correlation. Diagnostic performance using logistic regression, and longitudinal predictive value was assessed with linear mixed models.ResultsCompared with the CN and MCI groups, patients with AD exhibited significantly increased CPV and reduced ALPS indices (all P<0.001). Amyloid-β-positive (Aβ+) participants also showed significantly higher CPV and lower ALPS indices than Aβ-negative (Aβ−) individuals (all P<0.001). Elevated CPV was associated with lower cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) Aβ42 (r=−0.15, P<0.001), poorer Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE; r=−0.12, P<0.001), Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA; r=−0.17, P<0.001), and reduced hippocampal volume (r=−0.21, P<0.001). Conversely, higher ALPS indices were associated with increased CSF Aβ42 (r=0.12, P<0.001), better MMSE (r=0.13, P<0.001), and preserved hippocampal volume (r=0.15, P<0.001). CPV achieved superior diagnostic performance for differentiating AD from MCI [area under the curve (AUC) =0.774] and CN (AUC =0.912). Moreover, integrating CPV, ALPS, and hippocampal volume further improved classification performance across diagnostic and Aβ stratification tasks. Longitudinal analyses demonstrated that higher baseline CPV was associated with better baseline cognitive performance and slower decline in executive, language, and memory functions, whereas lower baseline ALPS indices predicted accelerated memory decline over time.ConclusionsOur findings identify CPV and the ALPS index as a pair of promising, complementary neuroimaging biomarkers for AD. Their synergistic integration into diagnostic models improves the precision of early detection and intervention strategies.Keywords: Glymphatic dysfunction, choroid plexus volume (CPV), diffusion tensor imaging-based perivascular space (DTI-ALPS), Alzheimer’s disease (AD)

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a neurodegenerative disorder and the most common form of dementia, causing significant economic and social burdens worldwide (1,2). Its neuropathological features include the deposition of amyloid-β (Aβ) plaques and tau neurofibrillary tangles (3). These two pathological changes synergistically promote neurodegeneration and cognitive decline. Increasing evidence suggests that the impaired clearance of Aβ and tau aggregates, rather than their excessive production, may be a key mechanism driving disease progression (4). Studies have shown that the brain’s glymphatic system may contribute to the clearance of metabolic waste products, including Aβ and tau proteins (5,6).

The glymphatic system refers to the brain-wide perivascular fluid transport system, where cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) penetrates the brain parenchyma through the perivascular spaces around arteries and subsequently clears interstitial fluid waste from the brain (7). To assess this, Taoka et al. (8) developed diffusion tensor imaging-based perivascular space (DTI-ALPS) analysis, which evaluates the diffusion rate along the deep medullary veins and indirectly reflects the functional state of the glymphatic system. A recent study proposed the ALPS index as a noninvasive imaging measure related to glymphatic function (9). However, the ALPS index represents an indirect imaging surrogate and may be influenced by white matter microstructural changes and brain atrophy (10). Therefore, changes in the ALPS index should be interpreted cautiously. The choroid plexus (ChP) is a highly vascularized structure that plays a crucial role in the secretion of CSF and the filtration of substances and cytokines within the brain (11,12). Despite its clinical importance, the ChP remains one of the least studied structures in the central nervous system. Preliminary studies suggest that choroid plexus volume (CPV) is closely linked to the brain’s glymphatic circulation (13), indicating that the ChP may represent an important, yet largely overlooked, candidate neuroimaging biomarker. However, the dynamic changes, diagnostic value, and longitudinal relationship of CPV and the ALPS index within the AD continuum, as well as their association with cognitive decline, remain unclear.

This study aimed to investigate and compare the relationships between CPV, the ALPS index, pathological CSF biomarkers, neuropsychological test scores, and neuroimaging measures. Additionally, we aimed to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of CPV and ALPS in identifying brain pathology, distinguishing between AD dementia, mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and cognitively normal (CN) controls, as well as assessing their predictive ability for AD progression. The study further explores the longitudinal association between baseline CPV and ALPS with clinical progression across the AD continuum. The primary objective is to elucidate the potential role of CPV and ALPS in clinical progression and to identify early biomarkers for AD and its precursor stages. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2025-1-2664/rc).

Methods

Participants

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The data used in this study were sourced from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) database (http://adni.loni.usc.edu/). ADNI is a large, multi-center initiative, with ethical approval obtained at each participating site, and all participants having provided informed consent. This study included only patients for whom complete diffusion-weighted (DW), T1-weighted (T1w), and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) imaging data were available. The data was sourced from ADNI-Grand Opportunity (GO), ADNI-2, and ADNI-3. The participant selection process is detailed in Figure 1. The final sample consisted of 113 patients with AD dementia, 309 with MCI, and 426 CN controls. Detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria for CN, MCI, and AD participants, as well as comprehensive diagnostic procedures, are provided in Appendix 1.

Additionally, a subgroup analysis was performed based on Aβ status. Aβ-positivity (Aβ+) was defined by CSF Aβ42 levels <976 pg/mL or amyloid positron emission tomography (PET) [18F-florbetapir ≥1.11 or 18F-florbetaben (FBB) ≥1.08] (14,15). In total, 398 Aβ+ participants and 450 Aβ-negative (Aβ−) participants were included in the analysis (Table S1). To assess whether baseline ALPS index and CPV could predict the risk of progression to AD dementia or MCI from the clinical baseline, participants were divided into conversion and non-conversion groups based on their conversion status during follow-up. Follow-up time was defined as the time from the first imaging assessment to the earliest occurrence of the following endpoint events: first diagnosis of MCI or AD dementia, death, or the last recorded date (September 2024). Ultimately, 89 participants progressed to MCI or AD during clinical follow-up, whereas 527 participants remained clinically stable after at least 2 years of follow-up (Table S2).

Clinical measures and neuropsychological tests

This study collected basic demographic information from all participants, including age, sex, and years of education. Laboratory assessments comprised CSF biomarkers such as Aβ42, tau proteins [including phosphorylated tau (p-tau) and total tau (t-tau)], as well as apolipoprotein E ε4 allele (APOEε4) status. Each participant underwent comprehensive neuropsychological testing, including the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) (16), Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) (17), and the Clinical Dementia Rating Sum of Boxes (CDR-SB) (18), as well as assessments of memory, executive function, language, and visuospatial ability. Specifically, composite scores for memory (ADNI-MEM), executive function (ADNI-EF), language (ADNI-LAN), and visuospatial ability (ADNI-VS) were used as outcome measures for these cognitive domains. All composite scores were standardized to a mean of 0 and a standard deviation (SD) of 1 (19).

MRI imaging

In the ADNI study, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data were acquired using multiple 3T scanners across different sites to obtain T1w imaging, FLAIR imaging, and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) data for each participant. Detailed imaging protocols are available in the open-source documentation (https://adni.loni.usc.edu/methods/documents/mri-protocols/). Brain structural measurements, including hippocampal, entorhinal cortex, and ventricular volumes, were obtained from the preprocessed datasets provided by the ADNI database. These volumetric data were generated using standardized ADNI processing pipelines based on FreeSurfer segmentation with the 2010 Desikan-Killiany atlas. The volumes of the hippocampus, entorhinal cortex, and ventricles were included in our analysis. The ADNI core laboratory utilized the ADNI pipeline (https://files.alz.washington.edu/documentation/adni-proto.pdf) for automatic segmentation of white matter hyperintensity (WMH) volumes.

ALPS index calculation

The DTI-ALPS index was computed according to established methodology (8). In brief, DW images were preprocessed using FSL [FMFSL (FMRIB Software Library, Oxford, UK] and MRtrix3, followed by tensor fitting to generate fractional anisotropy and directional diffusivity maps. Regions of interest (ROIs) were automatically placed in projection and association fiber pathways based on the JHU-ICBM-DTI-81 atlas (Johns Hopkins University, Baltimore, MD, USA). The ALPS index was then derived from specific diffusivity measures as described in the original reference. Complete details regarding preprocessing parameters, ROI coordinates, and the calculation formula are provided in Appendix 2. The ALPS indexes of the left and right hemispheres were calculated separately, and then we used the average ALPS index (Mean-ALPS) of the bilateral sides in the main analysis.

Quantification of CPV

The ChP ROI was automatically segmented from baseline three-dimensional (3D) T1w images using the FreeSurfer 7.4.1 platform (https://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu) (20). The brain volume segmentation technique was implemented based on a probabilistic anatomical atlas. Preprocessing steps included motion correction of 3D-T1 images, brain tissue extraction, segmentation of white matter and deep gray matter structures, intensity normalization, white matter surface smoothing, topological correction, and optimization of the cortical and white matter surfaces. Using this process, total intracranial volume (TIV) and CPV were quantified, and cortical thickness was calculated using the distance between white matter and the pia mater. All automated segmentation results were manually reviewed for quality by an experienced neurologist blinded to clinical and other imaging data. CPV was expressed as a ratio relative to TIV (21).

PET imaging

For the 18F-florbetapir (AV45) PET data, we utilized the average standard uptake value ratio (SUVR) across the frontal, anterior cingulate, precuneus, and parietal cortical regions, normalized to the cerebellum, as provided by the ADNI (https://ida.loni.usc.edu/). Participants were classified as Aβ+ or Aβ− based on a threshold of SUVR ≥1.11 (22,23). For the 18F-FBB PET data, we employed the mean SUVR of the frontal, anterior cingulate, precuneus, and parietal cortices, normalized to the cerebellum, as provided by the ADNI database, with an SUVR ≥1.08 used to define amyloid positivity (24).

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were presented as numbers (percentages) and compared using the χ2 test. Normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as mean ± SD and compared by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or independent t-tests, whereas skewed variables were summarized as median (interquartile range, IQR) and compared using the Kruskal-Wallis or Mann-Whitney U tests. Group differences in CPV and the DTI-ALPS index were examined using generalized linear models (GLM), with post-hoc pairwise comparisons performed using Tukey’s method for multiple testing correction. Correlations of CPV and the mean ALPS index with multidimensional biological, cognitive, and structural measures were assessed using partial Pearson or Spearman correlation analyses, as appropriate, adjusting for age, sex, years of education, APOE ε4 carrier status, and diagnosis (CN/MCI/AD).

Binary logistic regression models were developed for CPV, mean ALPS index, hippocampal volume, and their combination. To improve model robustness and reduce overfitting, 10-fold cross-validation was applied during model development. The diagnostic performance of each model was evaluated by the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC), sensitivity, and specificity in identifying CSF Aβ deposition status, differentiating among AD dementia, MCI, and CN individuals, and discriminating AD progression status. Differences in AUCs between models were compared using DeLong’s test.

To investigate longitudinal associations between glymphatic function and cognitive decline, linear mixed-effects models were applied separately for CPV and Mean-ALPS, with the glymphatic metric, time, and their interaction included as fixed effects. The MMSE score served as the primary outcome, and additional exploratory analyses were conducted across multiple cognitive domains, including memory, executive function, language, and visuospatial ability. Age, sex, years of education, and APOE ε4 carrier status were included as covariates. Case-specific random intercepts and slopes for time were included to account for repeated measurements within individuals. Within-case correlations were modeled using a compound symmetry covariance structure, selected for its suitability for longitudinal data with a limited number of repeated measurements per case.

All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.2.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and SPSS 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). All models were adjusted for age, sex, years of education, and APOE ε4 carrier status. Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed P value <0.05. Where applicable, multiple comparisons were corrected for a false discovery rate (FDR) of 0.05 using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure.

Neuropsychological and neuroimaging characteristics of the participants

After adjusting for age, sex, education, and APOE ε4 carrier status, significant differences in glymphatic and structural measures were observed across the diagnostic groups (Figure 2). Patients with AD demonstrated significantly lower left-ALPS, right-ALPS, and Mean-ALPS indices compared to those with MCI and CN groups (P<0.001 for all, Figure 2A-2C). Conversely, CPV was significantly elevated in the AD group relative to both the MCI and CN groups (P<0.001 for all, Figure 2C). Furthermore, compared with the CN group, the MCI group exhibited a significant increase in CPV (P<0.001).

When assessing amyloid status (Figure 2D), participants classified as Aβ+ exhibited significantly lower ALPS indices (left, right, and mean; all P<0.001) and higher CPV (P<0.001, Figure 2E) compared to Aβ− individuals. Further subgroup analysis within CN participants revealed no significant difference in CPV between Aβ− and Aβ+ individuals (1.001±0.283 vs. 1.035±0.282, P=0.394), whereas the mean ALPS index was significantly lower in Aβ+ CN participants than in Aβ− CN participants (1.169±0.138 vs. 1.216±0.134, P=0.001). Longitudinally, however, no significant differences in baseline mean ALPS indices or CPV were observed between participants who experienced clinical progression and those who remained stable (all P>0.05).

Baseline correlations of CPV and ALPS with CSF biomarkers, neuropsychological tests, and neuroimaging measures across the AD continuum

In individuals across the AD continuum, CPV exhibited significant associations with core AD biomarkers and clinical measures (Figure 3). Elevated CPV was significantly correlated with reduced CSF Aβ42 levels (r=−0.15, P<0.001), yet not with CSF t-tau or p-tau181. Clinically, higher CPV was associated with poorer global cognitive performance, as reflected by lower scores on the MMSE (r=−0.12, P<0.001), MoCA (r=−0.17, P<0.001), and CDR-SB (r=0.07, P=0.047). Furthermore, increased CPV correlated with worse performance across multiple cognitive domains, including memory (r=−0.22, P<0.001), executive function (r=−0.19, P<0.001), language (r=−0.21, P<0.001), and visuospatial ability (r=−0.08, P=0.022). Neuroimaging analyses indicated that larger CPV was related to structural brain changes, including smaller hippocampal volume (r=−0.21, P<0.001) and enlarged lateral ventricles (r=0.36, P<0.001).

Similarly, the Mean-ALPS index, a proxy reflecting glymphatic-related diffusion characteristics, demonstrated distinct correlational patterns along the AD spectrum. Higher Mean-ALPS values were significantly associated with increased CSF Aβ42 (r=0.12, P<0.001). These values, reflecting more preserved glymphatic-related diffusion properties, were also correlated with better global cognitive performance on the MMSE (r=0.13, P<0.001), MoCA (r=0.14, P<0.001), and CDR-SB (r=−0.13, P<0.001). The Mean-ALPS index also showed positive correlations with composite scores in specific cognitive domains: memory (r=0.18, P<0.001), executive function (r=0.13, P<0.001), language (r=0.09, P=0.008). Structurally, higher Mean-ALPS values were associated with preserved hippocampal volume (r=0.15, P<0.001), smaller lateral ventricular volume (r=−0.48, P<0.001), and lower total WMH (r=−0.16, P<0.001).

Notably, a strong inverse correlation was observed between the Mean-ALPS index and CPV (r=−0.38, P<0.001), suggesting a potential link between altered glymphatic-related transport and ChP structural alteration in AD.

Biomarker utility of CPV and ALPS in the stratification of AD

Figure 4 summarizes the performance of three neuroimaging biomarkers—CPV, Mean-ALPS index, and hippocampal volume—across a spectrum of AD-related classification tasks.

ROC curves depicting the discriminatory performance of three neuroimaging measures for stratifying disease stages (A: MCI vs. CN; B: AD vs. CN; C: AD vs. MCI), determining cerebral Aβ status (D), and identifying clinical conversion status (E) in the Alzheimer’s disease continuum. AD, Alzheimer’s disease; ALPS, analysis along the perivascular space; AUC, area under the curve; Aβ, amyloid-β; CN, cognitively normal; CPV, choroid plexus volume; HIP, hippocampus; MCI, mild cognitive impairment; ROC, receiver operating characteristic.

In discriminating MCI from CN individuals (Figure 4A), both CPV (AUC =0.720) and the Mean-ALPS index (AUC =0.700) demonstrated superior diagnostic performance to hippocampal volume (AUC =0.690), though these differences were not statistically significant. Integration of all three modalities into a combined model yielded a significantly higher AUC of 0.726 (P<0.05 vs. CPV alone).

In the CN vs. AD classification task (Figure 4B), both CPV (AUC =0.912) and the Mean-ALPS index (AUC =0.846) showed higher accuracy than hippocampal volume (AUC =0.838), with a statistically significant difference observed only between the CPV and hippocampal models (P<0.05). The trimodal integrated model provided a further significant boost in performance (AUC =0.922), outperforming both the Mean-ALPS and hippocampal volume models (all P<0.05).

For the MCI vs. AD distinction (Figure 4C), the CPV model (AUC =0.774) significantly outperformed both the Mean-ALPS (AUC =0.684) and hippocampal volume (AUC =0.686) models (all P<0.05). The combined model achieved the highest diagnostic accuracy (AUC =0.793), which was statistically superior to all single-metric models (all P<0.05).

For the task of identifying cerebral Aβ deposition status (Figure 4D), both CPV and hippocampal volume models (AUC =0.757 for both) outperformed the Mean-ALPS index (AUC =0.746), though no significant differences were found between the single models. The combined model again showed enhanced accuracy (AUC =0.763) and was statistically superior to the Mean-ALPS model (P<0.05).

Finally, in predicting AD clinical progression (Figure 4E), CPV (AUC =0.570) and the Mean-ALPS index (AUC =0.571) showed marginally better performance than hippocampal volume (AUC =0.559), though these differences were not significant. The combined model achieved the highest AUC (0.583), although the overall predictive performance remained limited; however, it was still significantly better than both the CPV and hippocampal volume models (all P<0.05).

Predictive value of baseline CPV and ALPS for longitudinal cognitive decline

A total of 828 participants (MCI: 308; AD: 113) completed follow-up assessments with a mean interval of 11.2 ± 6.0 months. Baseline CPV and mean ALPS index were examined as predictors of longitudinal cognitive decline (Figure 5).

Longitudinal associations of baseline neuroimaging measures with cognitive decline. The bar plot illustrates the relationship between baseline CPV and Mean-ALPS index and subsequent changes in ADNI cognitive composite scores across all participants and within specific diagnostic groups (MCI, AD dementia). AD, Alzheimer’s disease; ADNI-EF, Alzheimer’s disease neuroimaging initiative-executive function; ADNI-LAN, Alzheimer’s disease neuroimaging initiative-language composite scores; ADNI-MEM, Alzheimer’s disease neuroimaging initiative-memory; ALPS, analysis along the perivascular spaces; CPV, choroid plexus volume; MCI, mild cognitive impairment.

Linear mixed-effects analyses across all participants revealed that higher baseline CPV was significantly associated with better baseline cognitive performance across executive (β=0.310, P<0.001), language (β=0.429, P<0.001), and memory (β=0.265, P=0.001) domains (Figure 5A-5C). Significant CPV × time interactions in executive (β=−0.005, P=0.049), language (β=−0.008, P=0.003), and AD memory (β=−0.057, P=0.006) models indicated that higher baseline CPV predicted a slower rate of cognitive decline over time (Table S3). Conversely, lower Mean-ALPS was associated with poorer executive (β=−0.400, P=0.008), language (β=−0.478, P=0.001) and memory (β=−0.521, P=0.001) performance (Figure 5D-5E), whereas a significant Mean-ALPS × time interaction in the memory model (β=0.011, P=0.033) suggested that reduced ALPS index was linked to accelerated longitudinal memory deterioration (Table S4).

Across diagnostic groups, these associations were more pronounced in AD than in MCI. In AD participants, CPV showed robust positive correlations with all cognitive domains and significant CPV × time interactions, whereas in MCI, these effects were weak or nonsignificant. Similarly, Mean-ALPS demonstrated strong negative associations and a trend-level time interaction in AD, but minimal effects in MCI. Collectively, CPV and ALPS appear to be sensitive neuroimaging markers of longitudinal cognitive trajectories, particularly in AD. Additionally, neither CPV nor Mean-ALPS showed significant associations with MMSE or ADNI-VS (Figure S1).

Discussion

This study systematically evaluated CPV and the ALPS index across the AD continuum, revealing their potential role as structural and functional imaging markers associated with glymphatic-related processes. We observed that CPV enlargement and ALPS reduction progressed with cognitive decline, correlated with greater AD pathology severity, and were associated with progressive cerebral neurodegeneration. A trimodal model comprising CPV, the mean ALPS index, and hippocampal volume achieved superior diagnostic accuracy for disease staging and Aβ pathology detection. Furthermore, baseline CPV and ALPS values predicted domain-specific cognitive decline, although the ability to predict clinical progression was limited. These findings suggest that glymphatic-related alterations may contribute to AD progression and support the potential utility of CPV and ALPS as complementary neuroimaging markers associated with glymphatic-related processes.

Consistent with prior findings, CPV was increased in patients with MCI and AD (21), aligning with the emerging view that the ChP plays a central role in neuroinflammation and brain clearance mechanisms (25,26). This supports CPV as a promising therapeutic target in neurodegenerative protein-misfolding disorders such as Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s diseases. In parallel, we observed a significant reduction in the DTI-ALPS index in AD patients, corroborating earlier reports (27) and confirming substantial impairment of glymphatic clearance in AD. Notably, we found a significant negative correlation between enlarged CPV and decreased ALPS index (r=−0.43, P<0.001), a result consistent with previous research (28). These findings may indicate coordinated alterations between the ChP and perivascular spaces, both of which are considered important components of CSF dynamics and brain waste clearance pathways (29,30). However, this association may also be influenced by concurrent brain atrophy or structural remodeling, such as ventricular enlargement or medial temporal lobe degeneration (8,31), which may affect both CPV and diffusion properties.

Our findings demonstrate that CPV and the ALPS index are significantly associated not only with CSF Aβ42 levels but also with neuropsychological performance and structural degeneration in cognition-related brain regions, supporting their potential as early biomarkers for cognitive decline in initial AD stages—a conclusion consistent with prior reports (32-34). Notably, neither CPV nor ALPS showed significant correlations with CSF t-tau or p-tau181, a finding echoed in other studies (32) and potentially explained by the distinct spatiotemporal dynamics of Aβ and tau in Alzheimer’s continuum: Aβ deposition occurs early and is closely tied to glymphatic and blood-brain barrier impairment, whereas tau pathology generally emerges later, correlating more directly with neuronal loss and cognitive deterioration (3,35,36). In addition, emerging evidence suggests that CSF Aβ dynamics are influenced not only by clearance processes but also by physiological fluctuations related to sleep-wake and metabolic states, including hypocretin- and lactate-mediated regulation (37). Such state-dependent variability may further contribute to the observed selective association between CPV/ALPS and Aβ rather than tau. Notably, among CN participants, Aβ+ individuals showed significantly lower mean ALPS indices, whereas CPV did not differ significantly between groups. These findings suggest that ALPS-related alterations may emerge earlier than detectable CPV changes during the preclinical stage of AD. This early disease stage of our cohort may account for the absent tau associations, though independent regulatory mechanisms of tau cannot be excluded.

Notably, the model based on CPV demonstrated greater robustness than those representing glymphatic function (Mean-ALPS index) or medial temporal lobe atrophy (hippocampal volume) in distinguishing AD stages. Furthermore, it exhibited superior capability in detecting cerebral Aβ deposition, achieving an AUC of 0.757. This superior performance likely originates from the ChP’s central role in early AD mechanisms, including Aβ clearance impairment (38). The consistent diagnostic utility of CPV across different tasks further suggests that ChP dysfunction is a core pathological feature spanning the entire AD continuum. Importantly, no significant baseline differences in CPV or ALPS were observed between converters and non-converters. This negative finding suggests that glymphatic-related alterations may not be sufficiently pronounced at baseline to distinguish individuals who will subsequently convert. It is possible that glymphatic dysfunction becomes more evident at later disease stages or is more closely associated with overall disease severity rather than short-term conversion risk. These findings highlight the complexity of glymphatic involvement in AD progression and suggest that longitudinal changes may be more informative than single baseline measurements.

In contrast, this longitudinal study demonstrated that higher baseline CPV was associated with better cognitive performance and slower decline, particularly in executive, language, and memory domains, whereas lower mean ALPS was linked to poorer cognition and faster memory deterioration. These associations were stronger in AD than they were in MCI, suggesting that vascular and glymphatic dysfunctions become increasingly critical as neurodegeneration advances. Importantly, these findings suggest that CPV may not represent a unidirectional pathological marker, but rather a stage-dependent and potentially non-linear indicator across the AD continuum. Although our cross-sectional analyses showed CPV enlargement in AD, likely reflecting pathological structural remodeling and neuroinflammatory changes (39,40), relatively lower baseline CPV in longitudinal analyses may indicate reduced physiological reserve of the ChP, including impaired CSF production, immune regulation, and molecular clearance capacity (41). These observations support a stage-dependent and potentially non-linear relationship between CPV and disease progression, which may help to explain the apparent differences between the cross-sectional and longitudinal findings. Impaired ChP function may facilitate Aβ and tau accumulation and contribute to accelerated cognitive decline (40,41). Although CPV reflects structural characteristics of the ChP rather than direct glymphatic flow, its close association with CSF dynamics and waste clearance processes may explain its parallel relationship with cognition (26,42). Together, CPV and ALPS serve as complementary imaging biomarkers reflecting vascular and glymphatic health, offering potential tools for early detection and monitoring of AD progression.

Our study leveraged the large-scale longitudinal ADNI dataset to systematically integrate neuroimaging biomarkers of the brain’s clearance system—ChP structure and glymphatic function—revealing their dynamic, interrelated yet distinct trajectories across the AD continuum. Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, as indicated by Li et al. (43) in postmortem human brain studies, the DTI-ALPS index can be confounded by asymmetric white matter fiber microstructure and may not fully reflect glymphatic function. This limitation suggests that changes in ALPS values may partly reflect structural white matter alterations rather than glymphatic-specific processes. Therefore, ALPS findings in this study should be interpreted cautiously as reflecting glymphatic-related diffusion characteristics rather than direct measures of glymphatic activity. Second, CPV was derived using an automated FreeSurfer pipeline. Although this method shows good agreement with manual segmentation (33,44), ChP delineation may be affected by ventricular enlargement and brain atrophy in MCI and AD, potentially introducing variability in atrophic brains. Third, the relatively limited sample size, particularly the AD subgroup (n=113), may affect the statistical power and generalizability of AD-specific findings. Therefore, caution is warranted when interpreting results pertaining specifically to the AD cohort. In addition, longitudinal analyses were restricted to participants with MCI or AD at baseline, and the absence of CN individuals limits interpretation of glymphatic-related alterations in the earliest disease stages. Another limitation is the lack of information on obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), a common condition in elderly populations that may affect glymphatic function and ChP physiology. OSA-related fluctuations in intracranial and venous pressures may disrupt CSF dynamics and glymphatic clearance, as supported by studies demonstrating sleep apnea-related intracranial pressure elevations and venous-intracranial pressure coupling (45-47). In addition, the potential effects of medications influencing CSF dynamics, glymphatic activity, or ChP function were not systematically assessed. As these data were not comprehensively available in the ADNI dataset, their potential confounding effects could not be accounted for and may contribute to inter-individual variability in the observed associations. Future studies with larger sample sizes, more comprehensive phenotyping (including sleep-related assessments and medication profiling), refined longitudinal designs, and higher-resolution imaging or advanced segmentation methods are warranted to further elucidate the causal relationship between glymphatic system dysfunction and AD progression.

Conclusions

This study systematically demonstrates significant interrelationships among CPV enlargement, reduced DTI-ALPS index, cognitive decline, and brain atrophy across the AD continuum. The CPV model exhibited excellent diagnostic performance for identifying Aβ pathology and distinguishing clinical stages of AD. Importantly, CPV alone shows significant potential as an early diagnostic biomarker for AD, highlighting its clinical translational value.
