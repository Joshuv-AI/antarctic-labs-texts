Pain severity over duration: a new paradigm for hydrodilatation in frozen shoulder—pain, not timing, matters
Authors: Chris Hyunchul Jo
Published: 2026

Ultrasound-guided hydrodilatation has become a pragmatic “middle path” for adhesive capsulitis—more proactive than watchful waiting yet far less invasive than manipulation under anesthesia or arthroscopic release. What remains unsettled is not whether the procedure can help or not but instead when it should be offered and also which patients are most likely to benefit. Seeking to help answer this question, in a recent prospective observational study of 72 patients, Trakulkajornsak et al. [1] prescribed posterior, ultrasound‑guided hydrodilatation (total 30 mL, including 40 mg of triamcinolone) followed by home exercises, then compared outcomes between an “early” treatment group (<6 months of symptoms) and a “late” treatment group (≥6 months of symptoms) at 6 weeks [1].

Symptom duration, whether analyzed dichotomously (<6 vs. ≥6 months) or as a continuous measure, was not associated with short‑term functional change. Both groups experienced improvements in Oxford Shoulder Score (OSS), pain (visual analog scale score), and range of motion, with similar “success” rates when the OSS change exceeded the minimal clinically important difference (approximately 89% vs. 86%) [1]. Baseline pain severity remained significantly associated with OSS improvement in multivariable analysis. Safety was reassuring, with no serious adverse events and only transient postprocedure pain/swelling in a minority of patients [1].

These findings could be read as permission to worry less about the calendar and more about pain intensity at presentation. However, the apparent “baseline pain predicts improvement” message should be interpreted cautiously. In nonrandomized studies, patients who present with extreme baseline symptoms often improve on repeat measurement partly because of regression to the mean [2]. When outcomes are framed as change scores, baseline severity and change are also mathematically coupled, which can exaggerate the appearance of a responder signal [3]. The association between higher baseline visual analog scale scores and greater OSS changes is therefore best viewed as hypothesis‑generating, not as definitive evidence that more painful shoulders are intrinsically more treatment‑responsive.

Within the broader evidence landscape, hydrodilatation is increasingly viewed as a short‑term “window-opener” that enables movement and rehabilitation. A 2023 systematic review and meta‑analysis found that hydrodilatation can provide at least transient improvements in disability and passive external rotation versus intra‑articular steroid therapy alone, while noting that intensive mobilization after the procedure may be an important co‑intervention [4]. In a single‑blind randomized controlled trial, Huang et al. reported that a combination approach (hydrodilatation plus corticosteroid bursal injection, mobilization, and structured physical therapy) outperformed physical therapy alone across pain, function, and range of motion, with benefits persisting through at least 6 months [5]. Still, “early intervention” should not translate into indiscriminate steroid exposure: dose‑ and frequency‑dependent concerns have been raised regarding corticosteroid effects on tendon integrity and articular cartilage, reinforcing a “lowest‑effective‑dose” mindset and the value of adopting steroid‑sparing options when appropriate [6,7].

Two recent trends strengthen the clinical relevance of the paper by Pimenta et al. [8] and its implications for earlier decision‑making in high‑pain patients. First, capsule‑preserving hydrodilatation appears to outperform capsule‑rupturing techniques in pain and functional outcomes, supporting the approach used by Pimenta et al. [8]. Second, injectate choice is evolving: Liu et al. [9] reported noninferior 12‑week outcomes with 5% dextrose in water versus triamcinolone in capsule‑preserving hydrodilatation, positioning the former as a steroid‑sparing alternative when glucocorticoids are contraindicated or undesirable. Together, capsule preservation and steroid‑sparing injectates may improve the risk-benefit profile for offering hydrodilatation earlier to patients whose pain otherwise blocks rehabilitation.

Where should the field go next? These studies argue for reframing “timing” away from a calendar threshold and toward patient phenotype, especially baseline pain, while still validating that meaningful short-term gains can occur even in later presentations. Longer‑horizon trials stratified by, e.g., stage, symptom profile, or rehabilitation adherence comparing injectates and distension endpoints head‑to‑head are needed. Long‑term observational data remain useful; for example, a 200‑patient retrospective cohort reported improvements in pain/function alongside reductions in capsular and ligament thickness at 1 year after ultrasound‑guided hydrodilatation combined with corticosteroid injection [10]. Ultimately, the most actionable message is not that “more pain means more injections” but that hydrodilatation should be used judiciously as an adjunct to structured rehabilitation, translating pain relief into restored motion rather than serving as a quick fix.
