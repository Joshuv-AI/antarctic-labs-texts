Sleep and nocturnal breathing of individuals working over 1-week periods at high altitude: a cross-sectional polysomnographic study
Authors: Matiram Pun, Francisco Sanzana, Manuel Faundez, Mauricio Mancilla, Marcelo Jara, Gerardo Gomez, Fernando A Moraga, Claudio A Barrientos, Bradley Hansen, Jean M Rawling, Ivan Lopez, Marc Poulin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudy ObjectivesTo investigate objective sleep architectures, sleep fragmentation, and sleep-disordered breathing (SDB) indices in high-altitude workers rotating a week of altitude followed by a week of rest schedules from the Atacama Large Millimeter Array (ALMA) Observatory, Atacama, Chile.MethodsA total of 122 high-altitude workers (males/females: 70/52) completed a polysomnographic sleep study at the Operation Support Facility (OSF, 2950 m). The majority of the participants (78 [63.9%]) were low-altitude residents. Most of the workers (74 [60.7%]) worked at the Array Operation Site (AOS) at 5050 m (work high) during the day and returned to the OSF to sleep (sleep lower).ResultsThe ALMA workers had a sleep efficiency of 83.2 ± 10.3%, with a total sleep time of 352.2 ± 84.8 minutes. Sleep latency was 16.5 ± 19.3 minutes, and wake after sleep onset was 62.6 ± 56.5 minutes. The odds ratio product (ORP) showed that a quarter of altitude workers (n = 31) were in the severe sleep fragmentation category (ORP type 1,1 and 1,2), with deep sleep of 15.6 ± 11.4% of the total recorded time. The total Sleep Fragmentation Index (SFI) was 34.6 ± 13.5 numbers per hour, with the number of awakenings overnight being 31.3 ± 15.9. The Apnea–Hypopnea Index (AHI) was 27.4 ± 25.5 events per hour (central apneas: 2.4 ± 6.7 events per hour; obstructive apneas: 7.7 ± 14.6 events per hour). The average blood oxygen saturation (SpO2) was 88.6 ± 2.2%, with the nadir of 77.3 ± 11.8%, and the Oxygen Desaturation Index (ODI) was 17.2 ± 20.8 events per hour. Males had a higher number of arousals (26.5 ± 13.3 vs 20.4 ± 9.1 events per hour, p = .005), lighter sleep (15.4 ± 9.9 vs 9.7 ± 4.6% total sleep time, p < .001), and higher AHI (36.6 ± 28.7 vs 15.1 ± 12.9 events per hour, p < .001) and ODI (23.8 ± 24.2 vs 8.5 ± 10.0 events per hour, p < .001) compared to females. The number of sleep nights spent within the work week at altitude was negatively associated with sleep fragmentation (p = .0119) and arousal (p = .0092) indices and positively associated with ORP deep sleep (p = .0422), controlling for age. However, the number of years working at altitude was positively associated with the Respiratory Disturbance Index (p = .0014), AHI (p = .0026), and ODI (p = .0328), controlling for age. Older age, male sex, and higher body mass index played contributory roles in increasing AHI and ODI (p < .05).ConclusionsHigh-altitude workers had less deep sleep and high fragmented sleep. Further, they had high sleep apnea and oxygen desaturation indices. The workers who had sleep studies in the latter part of the work week had better sleep indices compared to the workers who had sleep studies on the first and second nights at altitude. The SDB indices were positively associated with the years of working at altitude. Statement of SignificanceTo our knowledge, this is the first study investigating sleep quality in high-altitude workers who have been following “work at very high altitude (5050 m), sleep at lower altitude (2950 m)” over the years. High-altitude workers who completed overnight sleep studies in the later part of their ~7-day work week at altitude had better sleep indices (traditional sleep architecture, odds ratio product, and sleep fragmentation index), suggesting that sleep acclimatization unfolds over nights spent within the work week after arrival to high altitude. Furthermore, a greater number of years working at high altitudes was associated with increased sleep apnea indices.

Introduction

Exposure to high altitude (>2500 m) impairs sleep in individuals who are born and raised at a lower altitude [1]. Impaired sleep is associated with excessive daytime sleepiness, increased reaction times, and impaired neurocognitive functions. The hypoxemia during sleep at altitude contributes to central sleep apnea, sleep fragmentation (SF), and impaired restorative sleep [1]. The acclimatization of physiological responses, such as ventilation, to high altitude starts within hours and full acclimatization may take days to weeks [2, 3]. High-altitude physiological acclimatization wears off gradually over 2–3 weeks after descending to a lower altitude. Hence, to mitigate the deleterious effects of high altitude exposure and retain acclimatization, workers employed by different industries located at high altitudes follow specialized work schedules [4], such as a week of work at high altitude followed by a week of rest at low altitude (~7 × 7 days schedule). The ~7 × 7 days schedule has been further modified by sleeping at a lower altitude and working at a higher elevation [5], which we refer to as “work at very high altitude, sleep at lower altitude” (WHSL). The extent of benefit in terms of objective sleep quality parameters and sleep-disordered breathing (SDB) indices (both central and obstructive) with the WHSL over months to years of rotating an ~7 × 7 days schedule is unknown.

About 15% of the Chilean workforce is involved in various types of work schedules [6], including over 200 000 workers employed in occupations that are exposed to high-altitude work schedules [7]. Many of these high-altitude workers work for mining companies in the copper, gold, and lithium mining industries and the workers are involved in physically demanding tasks. At high altitude, restorative sleep and mitigation of altitude illness among these workers are essential to improve their health and safety and to increase productivity. Previous studies have explored the acclimatization of the sleep macrostructure among altitude-naïve individuals [8–10] as well as self-reported measures of sleep quality in high-altitude workers [11, 12]. Objective sleep studies are limited among altitude naïve individuals with acute exposure and small sample sizes for the evaluation of periodic breathing and central sleep apnea [13–17]. Past studies of high-altitude workers suggest that the sleep patterns are not completely acclimatized even after a couple of years of high-altitude work schedules [18, 19]. The short- and long-term consequences of a high-altitude work schedule on objective sleep architectures, the sleep fragmentation index (SFI), and SDB and hypoxemia indices remain unexplored. Furthermore, the role of sex differences with a high-altitude work schedule on the sleep architecture, SFI, and SDB indices is not known. Therefore, in this study, we investigated both macro and micro sleep architectures, the SFI, and SDB and hypoxemia indices in a large cohort of high-altitude-acclimatized workers who follow work schedules of ~7 days of work at high altitude and ~7 days of rest at low altitude. First, we hypothesized that the high-altitude workers who had sleep studies at the beginning of the work week at high altitude would have poor sleep quality and higher SF, SDB, and hypoxemia indices compared to workers who had sleep studies later in the work week at high altitude, due in part to sleep acclimatization that unfolds over the first few nights of sleep at high altitude. Secondly, longer years of working at high altitude may be negatively associated with higher SDB indices.

Methods

Participants: high-altitude workers at the ALMA Observatory

In this cross-sectional descriptive study, the participants were high-altitude workers at the Atacama Large Millimeter Array (ALMA) Astronomical Observatory (5050 m), Atacama Desert, in Northern Chile. These individuals work in rotations that consist of one week at high altitude (i.e. for work) and another week at low altitude (i.e. for rest at their place of residence) [5].

High-altitude-ascent profile, work schedule, and work week pattern

One work cycle is about two weeks long. Every two-week cycle includes one week of work (at high altitude) followed by one week of rest (at lower altitude). The work week starts very early in the morning to travel to the Santiago Airport (Arturo Merino Benítez International Airport) to board a flight to Calama (flight duration is ~2 hours). The workers usually wake up around 04:00–05:00 am, depending on where they stay (overnight) in Santiago. The flight departures are usually between 07:00 and 08:00 am. After the workers land in Calama (Atacama), they embark on a bus to reach the Operation Support Facility (OSF, 2950 m) ~2 hours later. They arrive at the OSF between 10:00 and 11:30 am. Then, the workers go through a medical check-up with the polyclinic staff, and this is followed by lunch. After lunch, they complete a half day of work. No workers are allowed to go to the Array Operation Site (AOS, 5050 m) to work on the first day (i.e. arrival day).

The workers are allowed to go to work at the high site (i.e. AOS) for 4 hours on the second day. On the third day, they can work 6 hours at the AOS and from the fourth day onward, they are allowed to work 8 hours per day at the AOS. The commute time between the OSF and the AOS is about 45 minutes by a motorized vehicle. Every individual who works at the high site (AOS) undergoes a medical and security check-up before they depart for the AOS. Workers must have a full oxygen cylinder (~416 L capacity with 2–2.5 psi), a fitted nasal cannula, and safety gear.

At the end of the ~7-day work period, the ALMA workers leave the OSF at ~01:45 pm (regular schedule) on a bus to board a flight from Calama to Santiago later in the day. The flight usually leaves around 05:00–06:00 pm. The workers often arrive late in Santiago (later than 08:00–09:00 pm). The workers then travel to their home, which may take 30 minutes to 1 hour if they live in Santiago. For many individuals, Santiago is just a transit stop.

The daily work schedule for most ALMA workers, especially the contractors, is 11–12 hours per day in a schedule. The polyclinic staff start working at 07:00 am and they finish at 07:00 pm (12-hour schedule). The other workers’ schedule starts at 08:00 am and ends at 08:00 pm. The support workers from one of the companies that provide services to the kitchen and dormitories have different start times; for instance, some start at 05:00 am, others at 08:00 am, and some work on a night schedule. ALMA Observatory staff have an 11-hour schedule that runs from 08:00 am to 07:00 pm.

The study was approved by the Conjoint Health Research Ethics Board (CHREB) of the University of Calgary (REB 15-2709) and prior to initiating any process of evaluation, volunteers read and signed a consent letter.

Definitions of the terminologies in the study

Participants—low-altitude residents

Most of the ALMA workers are either from sea level or near sea level all around the country. Irrespective of their home location, all the workers come to Santiago to board a flight from Santiago to Calama. The participants living below 1000 m altitude were classified as “low-altitude residents” for the analysis.

Participants—higher-altitude residents

The higher-altitude residents were either from Calama (~2260 m) or San Pedro de Atacama (~2450 m). All the study participants were of Hispanic origin, and none of them were genetically adapted high-altitude residents of Andean origin [20]. Further, the high-altitude workers in our study were not seasonal; they work through all the seasons and have worked over many years.

Enrollment for the overnight polysomnography sleep study at the OSF (2950 m)

All the overnight sleep monitoring was conducted at the OSF. High-altitude workers were recruited for this study during their ~7-day high-altitude rotation work week and sleep at high altitude (OSF, 2950 m). The OSF (2950 m) is the sleeping altitude for all the high-altitude ALMA workers. All the ALMA workers (~290) were invited to participate in the overnight sleep study. A total of 133 ALMA workers were recruited for the sleep studies, and 122 completed the study. Eleven individuals could not participate in the sleep study due to scheduling conflicts. PSG night within the work week at ALMA

The participants completed a sleep study on a night of their convenience during the work week at altitude. Therefore, some of the workers had the sleep study earlier in their work week, while others had it later in their work week. The sleep night (polysomnography [PSG] night) is the night on which participants had overnight PSG recordings. This represents the number of nights spent by the worker at high altitude in that particular work week.

Years of working at ALMA/altitude

This represents the number of years an ALMA worker has been working as a high-altitude worker. Many of these workers have been working at high altitude for over a decade, while some of them have been working for less than a year. Therefore, the number of years working at ALMA represents the total number of years a person has been working at high altitude. All the ALMA participants were included in the study irrespective of the number of years of working at high altitude.

Work and facilities at high altitude

All the ALMA workers are based at the OSF (2950 m). The ALMA workers have polyclinic staff, support staff (dormitories, kitchen, and cleaning), and scientists who are not involved in heavy physical exertion and a lot of outdoor work. The antenna engineers, technicians, mechanics, and construction workers are involved in heavy physical exertion, outdoor work, and operating machines. The OSF is equipped with multiple blocks of buildings that have residences (individual dormitories for sleep/rest), a kitchen/cafeteria building, and a building equipped with recreational and gymnasium facilities. Further, there are designated buildings for scientific research offices, a medical clinic, work offices, and a building to assemble and repair the antennas.

Sleep assessment

Prodigy 2: level II PSG

A single night of overnight sleep monitoring in the field at high altitude (ALMA Observatory, Atacama, Chile) was carried out using the Prodigy 2 Sleep System (Cerebra Health Inc., Manitoba, Canada). The device consists of three units (i.e. a head unit, a chest unit, and a wrist oximeter [WristOx2, Nonin Medical Inc., Plymouth, MN, USA]), which are connected to a digital tablet via Bluetooth. The electroencephalogram (EEG) was recorded via the head unit, which includes two frontal electrodes (left and right), eye movements (left and right), an electromyogram (EMG, chin electrode), and a reference electrode (mastoid). Two frontal electrodes (F3 and F4) were placed according to the standard 10–20 EEG configuration system by placing the head unit to align the electrode placement. All the electrodes (EEG, electrooculograms (EOGs), mastoid, and chin) were connected to the head unit. The chest unit relates to a nasal cannula (airflow), chest and abdominal belts (breathing efforts), left and right leg electrodes (leg movements), and an electrocardiogram (ECG). The leg EMG was measured with two electrodes placed 2–4 cm over the anterior tibialis muscle of each leg. Finally, a wrist oximeter measured the blood oxygen saturation (SpO2) and pulse rate (PR) continuously. The three devices were connected wirelessly to an Android Tablet (Samsung Electronics Inc., South Korea) via Bluetooth, and data were collected continuously. The sleep data were uploaded wirelessly to the secured Cerebra Portal Cloud once sleep was completed.

PSG instrumentation and sleep data collection

The sleep study was conducted at the altitude residence of ALMA (OSF, 2950 m), where workers stay every alternate week during the altitude work week rotation at high altitude. The sleep study participants were recruited via flyers and word of mouth. Once the participants agreed to participate in the study, the ALMA Polyclinic Staff (F.S., M.F., or M.M.) visited the worker at night for the PSG instrumentation following the manufacturer’s (Cerebra Health Inc., Canada) guidelines. Participants went to sleep (lights off) after the PSG instrumentation was completed. The participants were instructed to make notes if there were any activities such as reading books, watching movies, or watching television before, during, or after the sleep recording started. Participants completed the sleep study following the instructions after they had finished sleeping in the morning. Then, the participants returned the PSG kit to the ALMA Polyclinic staff, who uploaded the sleep data to the cloud. The sleep data were verified (M.P. and Cerebra Team, when needed) and scored using the validated sleep autoscoring software Cerebra Viewer (Cerebra Health Inc., Manitoba, Canada).

Sleep data analyses and scoring

The sleep data were analyzed using the proprietary software Cerebra Viewer (Cerebra Health Inc., Manitoba, Canada) for all the traditional sleep variables as well as for the odds ratio product (ORP). The PSG data were analyzed for a 30-second epoch following the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) guideline [21] as described previously [22]. The apneas were defined as a nasal airflow decrease of ≥90% for ≥10 seconds. Obstructive apneas were defined as a persistent respiratory effort to breathe, while central apneas were defined when there was no respiratory effort. The hypopneas were classified as ≥30% airflow reduction for ≥10 seconds associated with ≥3% desaturation or an arousal [23]. The autoscoring was generated after the sleep data were uploaded to the portal for all the sleep variables [24]. The sleep data and other physiological signals, such as SpO2, were also reviewed by the study investigators for quality checks. The sleep microstructure, including the ORP, was reevaluated with the MY Sleep Scoring (MY SS) Application Programming Interface (API) software, which is a proprietary and patented metric algorithm. Then, the sleep variable output was reviewed by a registered polysomnography technologist (RPGST) and returned to the study investigators.

Odds ratio product

The ORP metric is the continuous measurement of sleep depth overnight with the analysis of EEG in 3-second epochs, according to the rules originally suggested [25, 26]. The 3-second epochs were averaged over each 30-second epoch. Hence, the ORP metric was calculated for the entire night of EEG on an epoch-by-epoch (30 seconds) basis. The ORP is superior in characterizing sleep depth across various sleep stages and robust in predicting arousability [27]. The scale for the ORP ranges from 0 (very deep sleep) to 2.5 (full wakefulness). The epochs of sleep were further divided into 10 bins with an interval of 0.25 ORP values. Then, the total time of each bin was converted into the percentage of total sleep recording time (TRT). This calculation provided 10 ORP bins as deciles of TRT. The 10 different bins (deciles) range from very deep sleep (decile 1) to full wakefulness (decile 10) as a percentage of the TRT. The deciles of the ORP are usually presented in histograms for individuals to characterize individualized sleep patterns such as OSA, insomnia, or sleep deprivation. Next, the results for the ORP decile histogram were averaged by combining sleep depth levels such as ORP deep sleep (deciles 1 [very deep sleep] and 2 [deep sleep]), transitional sleep (deciles 5–and 7), and drowsy awake (deciles 8 and 9).

Obesity, daytime blood pressure, and oxygen saturation measurement

The obesity of the ALMA workers was categorized based on the BMI [28, 29]: underweight as BMI < 18.5 kg/m2, normal weight as BMI of 18.5–24.9 kg/m2, overweight as BMI of 25–29.9 kg/m2, and obese as BMI > 30 kg/m2 based on World Health Organization (WHO) [28] and Health Canada [29] guidelines. Blood pressure (BP), blood oxygen saturation (SpO2), and heart rate (HR) were measured using a vital signs monitor (Comen NC5, Lotus NL BV, The Hague, Netherlands) in the ALMA polyclinic.

Sleep fragmentation index

The SFI is the crude estimation of sleep disturbance introduced to quantify sleep disruption in people with sleep-disordered breathing [30]. SFI is a simple, accurate, reliable, and practical metric, which is highly correlated with the gold-standard microarousal index (MAI) [31]. Primary outcome variables

The primary sleep outcomes were selected from the four blocks of sleep parameters: macro/micro sleep architecture, sleep disturbance, sleep apnea, and hypoxemia indices. Hence, sleep efficiency (SE), from the traditional macro sleep architecture, and ORP deep sleep, from the ORP deciles (micro sleep architecture) were chosen. The SFI and Total Arousal Index (TAI) were chosen as measures of sleep fragmentation. The Respiratory Disturbance Index (RDI) and Apnea–Hypopnea Index (AHI) were chosen as parameters for sleep disturbance. Finally, the Oxygen Desaturation Index (ODI) and total sleep time under 90% SpO2 (TST90) were selected for the hypoxemia profile. The ODI has been used as a 4% cutoff criterion for this analysis.

Self-reported sleep questionnaire and altitude sickness score

A subsample (~35%) of our study participants (n = 43) completed the self-reported sleep questionnaires: Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) [33], Insomnia Severity Index (ISI) [34], STOP-Bang questionnaire [35], Epworth Sleepiness Score (ESS) [36], and the Lake Louise Score (LLS) [37] for Acute Mountain Sickness (AMS). The older version of the LLS for AMS was used for the ALMA workers because of the sleep component, which was removed in the newer version [38]. All the questionnaires were translated into a Spanish version (FS) and the ISI was adopted from the previously translated and validated Spanish version [39]. The results for the self-reported outcome variables have been reported as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs).

Data analysis

Statistical analyses

Exploratory data analyses were carried out to characterize sleep macro- and microstructure. The associations of sleep with the number of days/nights spent at altitude were analyzed using both Pearson correlation and Spearman rank correlation. Then, the partial correlations were also conducted by controlling for age, sex, altitude of residence, and exposure to AOS. Within-group analyses of the macro sleep architecture and ORP were conducted comparing between males and females to tease out the potential role of biological sex. The sex differences on sleep-disordered breathing indices and sleep acclimatization within the work week (sleep nights 1 and 2 vs sleep nights 5 and more) were analyzed using an unpaired t-test.

Backward multiple regressions were conducted for the primary outcome variables (SE, ORP deep sleep, SFI, RDI, TAI, AHI, ODI, and TST90) initially with the independent predictors: age, sex, BMI, day at OSF (within work week), number of years employed (working) at altitude, and altitude of residence and daytime work at high site (AOS). The backward regression was chosen to refine the best-fitting regression model for sleep architecture, sleep fragmentation, as well as SDB indices among high-altitude workers. The backward elimination regression analysis was set with the probability of F-to-entry as p ≤ .15 and F-to-removal as p ≥ .20. The final (best-fitting) model from the backward regression analysis has been reported. In the best-fitting model, sex (0 = male and 1 = female), daytime work at the high site (0 = yes and 1 = no exposure to AOS), and altitude of residence (0 = low-altitude residents and 1 = high-altitude [Calama and San Pedro] residents) were coded categorically as specified for the analysis.

The multiplicity for the number of analyses was adjusted with family-wise p-value correction by using the false discovery rate (FDR) method (Benjamini–Hochberg procedure). The sleep and physiological data have been presented as means and standard deviations (mean ± SDs), and self-reported sleep variables as medians with IQRs. Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 29.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

General results

A total of 122 ALMA Observatory workers (52 females; 42.6%) completed the overnight polysomnographic sleep studies. Most of the sleep study participants (n = 78 [63.9%]) were low-altitude residents (either sea level or near sea level), while the others (n = 39 [32.0%]) were higher-altitude residents (i.e. from Calama and San Pedro of Atacama, 2260 m). Most of the workers (i.e. 74 [60.7%]) worked at the high site (AOS, 5050 m) during the day and came down to sleep at a lower altitude at the OSF (2950 m) overnight. The remainder of the workers (i.e. 48 [39.3%]) were employed and slept at the OSF. The ALMA workers had been in high-altitude work schedules for an average of 4.97 ± 5.02 years (males: 6.42 ± 5.55 years; females: 3.04 ± 3.39 years), with the overall range of 17.75 (0.25–18) years. Most of the ALMA workers were either overweight (n = 54 [44.3%]) or obese (n = 27 [22.1%]). Similarly, about a third of the workers were either past (n = 13 [10.7%]) or current (n = 27 [22.1%]) smokers. The prevalence of mild, moderate, and severe OSA was 35.2% (n = 43), 27.0% (n = 33), and 30.3% (n = 37), respectively. Only eight participants had an AHI < 5 (6.6%). Sleep architecture characteristics and sex differences of high-altitude workers

The ALMA workers had a sleep efficiency of 83.2 ± 10.3% with a TST of 352.2 ± 84.8 minutes. The wake after sleep onset (WASO) was 62.6 ± 56.5 minutes, while sleep onset latency (SOL) was 16.5 ± 19.3 minutes. The ALMA workers spent 13.0 ± 8.5 % of the TST in non-rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep stage 1 (N1), 55.3 ± 12.6 % of the TST in N2, and 15.8 ± 11.7% of the TST in N3. The total NREM sleep was 84.4 ± 7.7% of the TST, while their rapid eye movement (REM) sleep was 15.5 ± 7.9% of the TST. The SFI was 34.6 ± 13.5 numbers per hour, with the mean total stage shifts and number of awakenings of 162.6 ± 49.6 overnight and 31.3 ± 15.9 overnight, respectively. The overall AHI was 27.4 ± 25.5 events per hour, and the ODI was 17.2 ± 20.8 events per hour. The AHI was primarily contributed by obstructive events, 7.7 ± 14.6 events/hour, while there were fewer central events, 2.4 ± 6.7 events per hour. There was a negative association of SFI (r = −0.231, p = .0118), TAI (r = −0.182, p = .0462), and AHI (r = −0.189, p = .0398) with the number of nights spent at altitude within the work week. The sleep acclimatization within the work week was further teased out comparing individuals who completed sleep on the first two nights (PSG on nights 1 and 2, n = 47) with those who completed sleep in the later part of the work week (PSG on night 5+, n = 42) at high altitude. The ORP deep sleep was lower (13.1 ± 10.4% of the TRT vs 18.1 ± 11% of the TRT, t(86) = −2.18, p = .0323) and full wakefulness was higher (8.7 ± 8.3% of the TRT vs 4.7 ± 3.8% of the TRT, t(86) = 2.79, p=.0065) among the workers who had sleep on the first/second night of the work week. Best-fitting models for the sleep variables using backward regression

Backward elimination regression was run for the best-fitting models for the primary outcome variables with predictors that include age, sex, BMI, night of the PSG (within work week), years of working on a high-altitude work schedule, altitude of residence (high-altitude residence vs low-altitude residence) and whether they work at the high site (AOS, 5050 m) or not during the day. The best final model for sleep efficiency (F(5,109) = 5.1625, p < .001, adjusted R2 = 0.1544) included age (β = −0.38, t = −3.6926, p < .001), sex (β = 0.19, t = 2.04, p = .0433), and sleep night within the work week (β = 0.18, t = 1.9835, p = .498) as significant predictors. The ORP Deep Sleep had sleep night within the work week (β = 0.21, t = 2.2531, p = .0262) as a significant predictor in its best-fitting model (F(3,112) = 2.9786, p = .035, adjusted R2 = 0.0491). For SFI, age, sex, and sleep nights within the work week were significant predictors in its best-fitting model (F(4,110) = 5.6612, p < .001, adjusted R2 = 0.1707). Age and sex had significant contributions to TAI in its best-fitting model (F(4,112) = 4.7814, p = .001, adjusted R2 = 0.1154). Both RDI and AHI had sex, BMI, and years of working at altitude as significant predictors in their best-fitting models (F(3,112) = 18.2258, p < .001, adjusted R2 = 0.3100) and (F(4,111) = 13.9033, p < .001, adjusted R2 = 0.3098), respectively. ODI had age, sex, and BMI as significant predictors (Table 4) in its best-fitting model (F(5,110) = 10.8676, p < .001, adjusted R2 = 0.3002). Similarly, TST90 had age and BMI as significant contributors in its best-fitting model (F(4,111) = 5.8066, p < .001, adjusted R2 = 0.1432), while sex had a trend (p = .0704). Here, sex had played a significant role in all sleep disturbances (TAI and RDI), sleep apnea (AHI), and hypoxemia indices (ODI) (Table 4). The altitude of residence and daytime work at a higher altitude did not play a role in predicting the severity of sleep disturbances.

Self-reported sleep outcomes and altitude symptoms

The median of PSQI of our subsample (n = 43) was 7 (IQR: 5–9). Out of 43, 74.4% (n = 32) had poor sleep (>5 PSQI global). Similarly, 44.2% (n = 19) of our subsample population were in the subthreshold category of insomnia (ISI: 8–14). The median score of ISI was 9 (IQR: 6–12). Based on the STOP-Bang OSA screening questionnaire, only three ALMA workers were in the high-risk category of OSA, while seven were in intermediate (median: 1, IQR: 1–2). According to the daytime sleepiness score (ESS median: 7, IQR: 4–11), over 60% of the ALMA workers had daytime sleepiness (ESS > 6). Only eight of them were in the moderate-to-severe-sleepiness category (ESS: 13–24). The median score for LLS AMS was 2 (IQR: 1–3), with 11 individuals having mild AMS (LLS ≥ 3 with headache). The total AMS score was primarily driven by the sleep disturbance score. None of the ALMA workers had severe altitude sickness.

Discussion

Major findings

The high-altitude workers show high sleep-disordered breathing indices (moderate-to-severe OSA category). Sleep quality, sleep fragmentation, sleep apnea, and hypoxemia indices show improvements within the work week, suggesting that sleep is worse at the beginning of the work week and acclimatizes over the week, improving toward the later part of the work week. However, the number of years working at high altitude is negatively associated with sleep quality and positively associated with sleep apnea and hypoxemia indices. Age, sex, and BMI play consistently significant contributory roles in sleep fragmentation, SDB, and hypoxemia indices.

SDB indices and costs of sleep apnea

The estimated OSA prevalence in the Chilean population is much lower [40] compared to what we have observed in our study. Further, high-altitude workers seem to fall in the higher BMI range. Although our study population consists of healthy young adults, the prevalence and severity of OSA are very high based on AHI criteria. The males have higher light sleep, sleep apnea, and hypoxemia indices compared to females. However, our findings suggest that high altitude impairs sleep in both sexes, abolishing various sex differences in many sleep parameters, although males are predominantly affected. The significantly higher AHI and lower SpO2 in males compared to females suggest that the sex differences in sleep and nocturnal SpO2 may be differentially modulated from those previously observed daytime sex differences at high altitude [41]. In our study, all the females (except one) were in the premenopausal age group, and it is possible that progesterone may have played a role in contributing to a higher saturation and lower OSA prevalence at altitude [42].

Sex differences have been observed in physiological responses to rapid ascent, and acclimatization among altitude naïves [41, 43] and also among high-altitude residents [42, 44]. Acute exposure to very high to extreme altitude reduces obstructive apneas while increasing central apneas [45, 46]. However, our findings suggest that long-term exposure to a high-altitude work schedule increases the risk and severity of OSA. In our study population, the prevalence of central apneas is not high, which is not unexpected considering the modest altitude of sleep (<3000 m).

ORP sleep depth and ORP phenotypes

To our knowledge, this is the first study to characterize ORP sleep and its phenotypes at high altitude. The ORP phenotype histogram categorizes sleep architecture in nine categories that provide a visual description of normal to abnormal sleep patterns [47]. Over a third of the ALMA workers (37.7%) show the sleep patterns of sleep disorders (ORP types 1,1, 1,2, and 1,3). There is higher sleep fragmentation, which is also reflected in our SFI, and a higher prevalence of OSA. Similarly, our data also show that workers have restricted sleep due to insufficient bedtime (ORP type 2,1). ORP deciles show that the ALMA workers have less deep sleep (deciles 1 and 2) and more light sleep (decile 4).

Sleep changes within the work week and over the years

The association of sleep parameters (sleep efficiency and deep sleep) and sleep disturbance indices with the number of nights spent at high altitude within a work week suggests that sleep does not reach a complete acclimatization to the normal sleep patterns among these workers. Some physiological functions, such as sleep, might take longer to acclimatize or may never acclimatize among high-altitude workers, as observed in the previous literature [12, 18, 19]. Furthermore, the sleep indices during the first and second nights of the work week were worse than the sleep indices from the workers who had sleep studies on the fifth night and later in the work week. This finding suggests that within the work week, sleep acclimatization occurs at altitude among these high-altitude workers. The sleep architecture may even get worse over the years with a high-altitude work schedule, as we observed that the number of years working at high altitude has a rather strong association with a higher incidence of OSA and greater hypoxemia. The combination of this type of work schedule, remote location, and high altitude exposure may have an accumulative contribution to the negative association with the sleep indices of high-altitude workers. The sleep apnea indices associated with the number of years working at high altitudes are independent of age and sex. The work schedule outside the settings of conventional daylight hours (07:00 or 08:00 am to 05:00 or 06:00 pm) is associated with poor sleep [48], impaired neurocognitive [49] and mental health [50], an increase in BMI [51], and adverse cardiovascular health [52, 53]. Therefore, the work schedule at the altitude of ALMA workers may have adverse consequences on their sleep architecture and contribute to the increased SDB indices.

Role of sex, altitude of residence, and WHSL strategy

Increasing age and many years of working at high altitude are associated with poor sleep quality, although in every work week at altitude, there is an effort towards sleep acclimatization. The high-site workers (AOS) use mandatory oxygen supplementation throughout the day when they are at 5050 m (AOS). Overnight oxygen supplementation improves AHI in people with OSA [54]. A previous study showed that the enrichment of room oxygen at AOS (5050 m) increases SpO2, reduces blood pressure, and improves neurocognitive function among ALMA workers [55]. It is possible that ALMA workers with high AHI may have benefited from the use of oxygen supplementation during the day’s work at the AOS. Second, exposure to higher altitudes may be associated with better acclimatization for the AOS workers. Trekkers and climbers commonly engage in the practice of going on acclimatization hikes to a higher elevation than their sleeping elevation [56]. Hence, the use of oxygen and acclimatization exposure during the day-time work at AOS (5050 m) may have played a positive role in improving sleep in this finding. Sleep disturbances (SFI and TAI) are exacerbated with increasing age and male sex. There was a significant contribution of being male, increasing BMI, and longer years of working at high altitudes to both the RDI and AHI. Although the OSA incidence and prevalence increase among older adults [57], the ALMA population in our study was younger. Therefore, long-term work at high altitude may have played a contributory role in increased sleep apnea indices. Males have worse outcomes than females, and these findings are consistent with the literature that OSA is more common in males [58]. Similarly, the apparent sex differences observed may also have been influenced by the shorter number of years of working at altitude for females.

High-altitude work schedules, the nature of work, and their association with sleep

ALMA has adopted the model of working at a very high altitude with supplemental oxygen, sleeping at a lower altitude, and rotating on a weekly basis (~7 × 7). There are other different models of high-altitude mining work schedules, such as ~7 days at high altitude and ~7 days at low altitude or 4 days at altitude and 4 days at low altitude, predominantly in the copper mines of Chile located between 900 and 4400 m elevations [12]. A previous study reported that 72% of high-altitude copper mining workers (3900 – 4400 m) had sleep disturbances based on the PSQI [12]. Munoz et al. compared high-altitude copper mining workers from three different altitude ranges (3900–4400, 3000–3900, and <2400 m [low altitude]). There are other high-altitude work schedules, such as 20 days at altitude and 3 days at low altitude, and 4 days at altitude and 3 days at low altitude [18]. Similarly, a gold mining company in the Kyrgyz mountains (located at 3600–4500 m) uses 2 weeks of work at high altitude and 2 weeks of work at low altitude, with a 12-hour workday schedule while at high altitude [59]. Finally, the Government of Chile published a Technical Guidelines report (2013) for working at high altitude, which recommends sleeping at an altitude lower than 3000 m [60]. If it is not possible to come down to sleep at a lower altitude, supplemental oxygen should be used to increase the SpO2 to the equivalent altitude of 3000 m or lower. Our findings highlight the potential long-term consequences on the sleep architecture and SDB indices for workers who follow ~7 × 7-day work schedules according to the WHSL model. Our results align with the findings from a small group of heavy-machinery-operating mining workers (1–14 years of work), with a schedule of ~7 × 7 days at 4200 m, who had high sleep disturbance indices that improved with oxygen supplementation [19]. Taken together, within–work week acclimatization and long-term association with SDB suggest future avenues of exploring other optimal models, such as further lowering the sleeping altitude or oxygen supplementation of sleep dormitories at altitude.

Self-reported sleep and altitude sickness score

The sleep apnea screening questionnaires administered to our subsample suggest that questionnaire-based screening of sleep-disordered breathing can underestimate the burden of OSA. However, the findings from the PSQI and ISI are consistent with the daytime sleepiness score, suggesting poor sleep among ALMA workers. Similarly, the total AMS score is also driven by the sleep disturbance component. The findings from self-reported sleep questionnaires and the prevalence of AMS are consistent with a previous study [12]. Therefore, our findings highlight the importance of the self-reported sleep questionnaires for the daytime functional outcome and PSG studies for the SDB screening and macro/micro sleep architecture characterization. Furthermore, the Government of Chile recommends the PSQI as a screening tool for sleep problems in high-altitude workers, which seems unable to identify SDB based on our findings.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of the study include a large number of high-altitude workers who have been following the routine of “work at very high altitude (5050 m), sleep at lower altitude (2950 m)” for the PSG sleep studies. We recruited some participants who are sea-level residents and others who are high-altitude residents (2200 m). Similarly, some of the participants were high-site workers (daytime work at 5050 m) while others worked only at the OSF (2950 m). Both characteristics enabled us to attempt to unravel the role of altitude residence and AOS exposure on sleep patterns. Similarly, we have studied novel indices of sleep metrics among high-altitude workers, such as continuous sleep depth metrics (ORP) [26, 61] and the sleep fragmentation index [30–32]. Similarly, we have characterized ORP phenotypes, characterizing the sleep patterns in this cohort [47].

Our study has some limitations, which should be considered. In our correlation analysis, the associations are small, despite being statistically significant. Despite the small associations, it is important to note that the associations are in opposite directions (i.e. improvement in sleep within the work week, but a negative association over the years). Similarly, the best-fitting models only explain small amounts of the variability, which was at most one-third (as shown in the R2 in Table 4), despite being the best selected models. These small to moderate associations in our findings should be taken into consideration for the generalizability of our results. Similarly, future studies should consider increasing the sample size and include workers from different high-altitude work locations.

Our study population is diverse in terms of work schedules, the types of duties performed by the workers, comorbidities (e.g. obesity), and risk factors (e.g. smoking status). Another limitation is the absence of baseline PSG or home sleep apnea testing (HSAT) of the ALMA workers before starting work at ALMA. The sleep characteristics of these workers prior to working at high altitude or at the time of recruitment at their altitude of residence remain unknown. The lack of low-altitude PSG data for these workers makes it difficult to generalize our findings as a definite association of the altitude work schedule. Although our participants from high altitude are not a genetically adapted Andean population, they may have developed adaptations in their sleep patterns over time spent at high altitude. The different patterns of work schedules for different workers may also have played a role in sleep acclimatization at altitude. Similarly, only a subset of ALMA workers participated in completing the self-reported sleep questionnaires. We also cannot rule out the self-selection bias that workers with either better or worse sleep tended to participate more. Next, the high-site workers used mandatory supplementary oxygen when they were at the site (AOS, 5050 m), and the oxygen use may have conflated sleep among the AOS cohort.

In terms of the PSG assessment, we utilized overnight PSG with only two frontal electrodes (nonstandard EEG derivations). We also acknowledge the limitation that our sleep PSG evaluations were from an automatic sleep scoring software, although this software has been validated, and every assessment was reviewed by study investigators. The acclimatization of sleep within a work week may have been influenced by interindividual variability. The sleep acclimatization within the work week at altitude could be better addressed by measuring sleep in the same participants at the beginning and at the end of their work week. Finally, future studies should explore the functional outcomes of the workers during the day, such as neurocognitive function.

Conclusion

In this large cohort of high-altitude-acclimatized workers, there is a high apnea–hypopnea index, primarily obstructive, suggesting the presence of OSA. During their work week, the number of consecutive nights spent at high altitude had a negative association with sleep fragmentation and sleep disorder indices. However, a longer duration of having worked at high altitude was associated with a higher possibility of developing sleep apnea and worsening hypoxemia.
