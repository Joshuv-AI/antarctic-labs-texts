Demographics of paediatric trauma at a zonal referral hospital for northwestern Tanzania: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Arthi Kozhumam, Shazmah Suleman, Vihar Kotecha, Doug Lorenz, Charles Uttoh, Rebecca Yalimo, Colleen Fant
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionDespite a high burden of paediatric injury (25 % of all presenting injuries) in Tanzanian studies, literature remains limited, especially in recent years, regarding detail of care provided for the injured child across different healthcare settings in the country. This study aims to understand and map the pediatric trauma burden and factors descriptively associated with pediatric injury at the zonal referral hospital, Bugando Medical Center (BMC), for northwestern Tanzania.MethodsThis study was a subgroup cross-sectional analysis of paediatric (ages 0 through 17) data collected from trauma patients who received care at BMC from March - August 2023, focused on paediatric-specific injury occurrence, injury characteristics, and location. Descriptive analyses of paediatric patient demographics, past medical history, prehospital characteristics, and injury characteristics across body system were conducted to understand the burden of serious injury in this setting. Geospatial analyses across region were conducted to understand patient density, triage level, prehospital care, and Euclidean distance from injury region to BMC.Results146 children were included in analysis. Almost all children were injured at home (42.5 %) or in a traffic crash (33.6 %), with more than half (56.8 %) in the highest (red) triage category. Nearly 70 % had received prehospital care from a healthcare professional. Head and maxillo-facial injuries were most the most common. Distance from Bugando Medical Center was associated with higher triage level but not with prehospital care.DiscussionPaediatric injury in this referral hospital in northwestern Tanzania has distinct variations in referral patterns, injury locations and injury types. Efforts are particularly needed for head and maxillo-facial emergency support due to the predominance of injuries to these body systems. Regional variations in paediatric trauma incidence may represent a future target for outreach from this referral institution.

Introduction

There are 5 million annual deaths from trauma-related injury with 95 % of injuries occurring in low- and middle- income countries (LMICs), 1 million of which are paediatric [1,2]. Setting-adapted trauma care protocols, systems, and resource allocation for child injury prevention and treatment are key to high quality paediatric trauma care in LMICs. In Tanzania, paediatric injuries account for nearly 25 % of all injuries seen in multilevel health facilities [3,4]. Despite having a high trauma burden, there is limited healthcare system preparation which can increase risk of death and disability when injury occurs [5] and there are specific injury mechanisms, patterns, and treatments that are unique to children [4].

In Tanzanian studies, paediatric injury comprises approximately 25 % of all presenting injuries and most injuries occur at home, are due to falls, or are from road traffic injuries [4]. Despite this high burden of disease, literature remains limited regarding detail of care needed and provided for the injured child across different healthcare settings in Tanzania [6] particularly since recent urbanization of the lake zone in the country. No updated descriptive studies have provided information specific to paediatric injury in the northwestern region for understanding at-risk children based on demographic and physical examination characteristics. Additionally, to our knowledge no geospatial studies have been conducted in the northwest region, limiting understanding of disparities in injury outcomes based on distance travelled to care. Timely evaluation and treatment, especially for children, is essential to optimal health outcomes with several delays to care identified in this setting [7,8].

To understand the trauma burden for patients seen at the zonal referral hospital for northwest Tanzania, our team developed a trauma registry at Bugando Medical Center (BMC) in 2023 in order to standardize the process for data collection and evaluation for trauma patients, the methods for which have been previously described [9]. Previous work at Bugando Medical Center (BMC) and other settings in Tanzania have identified being referred to the hospital and having a high triage level as associated with higher likelihood of serious injury, with injuries to children predominantly being open wounds and fractures to the head, neck, and musculoskeletal regions [4,10].

This study aims to secondarily analyze paediatric trauma from our registry using descriptive and geospatial techniques to understand the injury burden for children in this setting, as well as identify factors and locations associated with more severe injury.

Methods

Bugando Medical Center (BMC) is a zonal and tertiary referral hospital in Mwanza, Tanzania. This hospital serves as the only specialty referral hospital for the public sector of North and Western Tanzania. The emergency department at BMC is equipped with patient monitoring facilities, ventilators, and radiologic studies as well as the performance bedside diagnostics, point of care ultrasound and ECG as well as access to specialty services [9,11]. Trauma patients presenting between March-August 2023 were enrolled into our registry, and information including demographics and data was collected using standard World Health Organization (WHO) Trauma Care Checklist questions [12]. Research assistants from the nursing cohort in the Emergency Department recruited patients and obtained written informed consent from adult caregivers and assent from children for data collection. Data collection occurred using the WHO form via REDCap [13] with transcription and cleaning performed by project leadership. All patients presenting to the Emergency Department for trauma were approached, and those who could consent or assent were eligible for inclusion in this study. Those with injuries > 5 days old (to ensure capturing the initial injury and not delayed injury complications), inability to consent, or failure of nearest relative or next of kin to provide consent or failure to assent for children, were excluded from enrolment. Of the total 760 patients seen in the resuscitation area and classified as trauma patients, 34 were excluded due to inability to consent/assent or were duplicate entries. Data collection was overseen by the study Co-PI, with intermittent review of data collection forms for completeness and accuracy.

This study is a subgroup analysis of data collected via prospective cross sectional study to describe trauma patients presenting to BMC from March - August 2023 and for this subgroup analysis of paediatric patients, the analyzed data was limited to those <18. Data analyzed from the paediatric trauma patients addressed specific injury occurrence, injury characteristics, and location as well as paediatric patient demographics, past medical history, prehospital characteristics (including mechanism and place of injury, prehospital care), and injury characteristics across body system. This data was analyzed to understand the burden of serious injury in this setting. Tables providing descriptive information were constructed using RStudio version 4.3.1 [14]. Frequencies of missingness for each variable analyzed are reported. Geospatial analyses across region were conducted to understand patient density, triage level (as a marker for injury severity), prehospital care, and distance from injury region to BMC. The triage system includes components of the South African Triage Scale [15] modified to our setting, which is applicable to patients at different stages of treatment. Maps were created using ArcGIS Pro [16], with country and regional boundaries for Tanzania downloaded from the World Bank Data Catalog [17]. Euclidean distances between region of injury and BMC were calculated using region centroid and BMC latitude/longitude location using the ArcGIS Pro spatial statistics toolbox.

Results are presented with regards to the STROBE checklist for reporting observational studies [18]. The original study was approved by the Ethics Committee at the National Institute for Medical Research in Tanzania, the Catholic University of Health and Allied Sciences CUHAS/BMC Research Ethics and Review Committee (CREC), and the Northwestern University Institutional Review Board in Chicago, IL, USA.

Results

During the study period, 6423 patients were seen in the emergency department, of whom 760 were seen in the resuscitation area and classified as trauma patients. Out of 726 included enrolled patients in the overall study, 146 (20.11 %) were paediatric (under age 18) and included in this analysis. Children were mostly male (70.5 %) between the ages of 4–13, with history obtained from a parent (87 %). Discussion

This study describes characteristics associated with paediatric injury in a primarily referral population in northwestern Tanzania. Findings suggest disparities across various demographic, clinical, and geographic factors. As no studies have examined paediatric trauma burden in northwestern Tanzania using a geospatial approach, and none have updated descriptive statistics within the last ten years, our analysis is a novel, recent contribution to understanding trauma needs and distribution in the area.

In this study population, we found that children were more likely to sustain injury to the head or HEENT systems which would implicate a need for preparedness at this facility to treat these disease presentations. Distribution of injury mechanisms, settings, and bodily locations were similar in our studies to those of pediatric trauma registries in other sub-Saharan African countries [[19], [20], [21]].

This was a primarily referral population. Most children had received prehospital care from a health professional and were in red and yellow triage categories. It is difficult to ascertain from this data whether lower acuity (i.e. triage level green) pediatric patients were more likely to be kept at lower-level institutions, or those who were more ill bypassed lower level facilities instead presenting directly to this specialist hospital, so further studies will need to be done to understand the pediatric trauma burden in referring institutions and help explain the differential injury severity seen across regions. While it is possible prehospital (i.e. pre-BMC) care by healthcare providers was conducted less in Mwanza due to closeness to BMC, more focus is needed on understanding prehospital care in this region. One should also note that the prehospital care system in Tanzania is limited in its availability and the prehospital care provided for these patients was from a prior institution, and not via a well-established pre-hospital system.

Regional disparities seen in our findings highlight the importance of geographic targeting for prehospital care access in the country. Geographic information systems work in Tanzania as of now predominates in Moshi [22], generally with regards to specific injuries such as burns and road traffic injuries, and none that are pediatric-specific to date. More efforts are needed for prehospital care in areas further from a medical center where children, as found in our registry, may present with higher severity.

Additionally, we found that a majority of paediatric injuries, especially for children under 5, occurred in the home setting, so this may be a future area of advocacy and safety work that can target home environments. Given the high prevalence of road injury and injuries at home for children in our sample and other work [[23], [24], [25]], studies specific to road traffic interventions and home safety in Tanzania are needed with the home and high-injury road areas as key locations to target for injury prevention.

There are several limitations of our study. Due to small sample sizes in the paediatric population, missing data with regards to body systems affected by injury, and the very small (n = 14) number of children with complete data for Kampala Trauma Score calculation, we did not conduct any statistical tests of significance or models, focusing on descriptive trends to provide impetus for future directions. Additionally, due to limited road network data in the country and region, Euclidean distance was calculated and likely underestimate distance as well as limit the ability to assess time-to-care. Euclidean distances do not directly correlate with real-world transport time based on terrain and road distribution. Despite this, we believe the results of our study to provide utility in identifying locations of focus for injury prevention (road, home) and treatment (primarily Mwanza, compared to other regions) for children at BMC.

Conclusion

Paediatric injury in this referral hospital in northwestern Tanzania has distinct variations in referral patterns, injury locations and injury types that necessitate a different approach to training and preparation to the general trauma patient being cared for in this zonal referral hospital. Further work is needed on children who are not referred to this speciality hospital, how referral relates to trauma severity, and how distance relates to prehospital care and severity. Efforts are particularly needed for head and HEENT emergency support due to the predominance of injuries to these body systems in children. Focus is needed on home-based injury prevention for younger children and road traffic injury interventions for older children in the northwest region. Regional variations in paediatric trauma incidence may represent a future target for outreach from this referral institution.
