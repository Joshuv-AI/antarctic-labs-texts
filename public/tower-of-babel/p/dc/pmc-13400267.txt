Declining effectiveness of major anthelmintic groups against gastrointestinal nematodes of farmed ruminants in Africa based on faecal egg count reduction: systematic review and first meta-analysis
Authors: EFX Guinda, SMS Afonso, J Krücken, G von Samson-Himmelstjerna, ER Morgan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesAnthelmintic resistance (AR) in gastrointestinal nematode (GIN) parasites of ruminant livestock has been widely reported in Africa but analysis of variation between regions and over time is lacking. The objective of this paper was to collate data on AR and, through meta-analyses, identify patterns in its occurrence in order to guide future research, practice and policy.Research design and methodsA systematic literature review following PRISMA guidelines retrieved publications on AR, from which comparable data on treatment effectiveness using the faecal egg count reduction (FECR) test were extracted for meta-analysis using logistic and linear regression.ResultsData were skewed towards East Africa and small ruminants and spanned 1995-2025. Of 646 articles identified, 60 were retained for analysis. There was evidence of failure across all drug groups: percent FECR (FECR%) was below 95% in sheep and goats in 48% of tests using benzimidazoles (BZ), and 57% for imidazothiazoles (IMTZ) and macrocyclic lactones (ML); in cattle, similar levels of treatment failure were reported but sample size was very low. FECR<95% was more commonly reported in sheep in southern and North Africa than in other regions, and in goats failure rates were higher for BZ and IMTZ than other drug groups and increased over time. FECR% declined in small ruminants over the reporting period for all drug groups and frequently involved survival of Haemonchus after treatment.ConclusionsTreatment failure due to AR appears to be widespread in ruminant livestock in Africa. This first meta-analysis shows increasing levels of AR over time for the three major anthelmintic drug groups in small ruminants, and similar levels of AR in GIN in cattle. These findings reinforce the need to adapt parasite control strategies to preserve anthelmintic efficacy. They also provide a baseline for future studies, which would benefit from clear and comparable methods for measuring AR and new in vitro technologies.Keywords: Parasites, Cattle, Sheep, Goats, Drug resistance, Risk factors, Haemonchus

Introduction

Ruminants have substantial socioeconomic importance across Africa, serving as a major source of protein and income (FAOFood Agriculture Organization of the United Nations, 2015). Their growth and productive performance are often constrained by health challenges, primarily infections caused by gastrointestinal nematodes (GIN), which often result in considerable economic losses and reduced productivity (Molento et al., 2004; Palkumbura et al., 2024). Globally, including in Africa, control of GIN in livestock has primarily relied on the use of broad spectrum anthelmintic (AH) drug classes such as benzimidazoles (e.g. albendazole), imidazothiazoles (e.g. levamisole) and macrocyclic lactones (e.g. ivermectin) (Coles et al., 2006). However, these drugs have often been used indiscriminately and excessively, without considering epidemiological factors or the risk of developing anthelmintic resistance (AR) (Molento et al., 2004; van Wyk et al., 1999).

Many studies have already reported resistance to AH in Africa, among GIN infecting small ruminants (sheep and goats) (e.g., Emsley et al., 2023; Tsotetsi et al., 2013; van Wyk, 2001b; van Wyk et al., 1999) and cattle (e.g., Keyyu et al., 2009; Mohammedsalih et al., 2021). Across Africa, however, knowledge is patchy. A recent systematic review of AR in ruminants on the African continent (Gatitu et al., 2025) highlighted the paucity of data, concentration of studies in southern and eastern Africa, and wide variation in study design and analysis. This makes it difficult to establish the current situation with respect to AR or to assess trends using longitudinal analysis, as has been attempted in Europe (Rose et al., 2015; Rose Vineer et al., 2020).

Susceptibility to AH varies among parasite populations depending on host animal species, geographical location, climate, drug type, management strategies and parasite epidemiology (Strydom et al., 2023). In Africa, another critical issue related to pharmaceuticals is the presence of low quality veterinary medicines, which can compromise the effectiveness of treatment (Getahun et al., 2023). Poor quality AH, or those administered incorrectly or to animals in poor condition, also confound measurement of AR (Morgan et al., 2022) and could promote its development due to underdosing. Definitive methods for assessing AH effectiveness in living animals across drug groups are not currently available, but the faecal egg count reduction test (FECRT) provides the best approximation and has been in use for some time, allowing comparisons across drug groups, regions, host and parasite species and other factors (Kaplan et al., 2023). A comparative review of the current state of knowledge on AR among GIN in ruminants in Africa would be valuable in guiding effective strategies for parasite prevention and control, and to focus future work on the key knowledge gaps. Such an analysis could assist producers and veterinarians in minimizing economic losses while ensuring sustainable parasite management.

The recent review by Gatitu et al. (2025) collates valuable information on the current status of AR in ruminants in Africa. Here, we aim to extend that qualitative overview, using published data on faecal egg count reduction (FECR) for the major drug groups to assess spatial and temporal patterns in anthelmintic effectiveness. This analysis will inform evidence-based parasite control programs and promote the prolonged efficacy of anthelmintic treatments in support of sustainable livestock production. It begins with an independent systematic review and proceeds to a meta-analysis of data extracted from eligible publications.

Methods

Information sources and identification

The systematic review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines (Moher et al., 2009). Search terms were agreed among authors and aimed to maximise the chances of finding primary data on the effectiveness of anthelmintics against GIN in ruminant livestock species.

The following databases were searched: Google Scholar, MEDLINE/PubMed, Web of Science, Hinary-research4life and African journals Online (AJOL) with no start date and an end date of December 2025, without language restrictions. The key words for the search used the following combination: (anthelmintic* OR wormer OR (treat* AND (helmint* OR nematod*))) AND (resistan* OR efficacy* OR effectiv* OR suscept*) AND (ruminant* OR livestock OR sheep OR goat* OR cattle OR cow OR zebu* OR ovin* OR caprin* OR bovin*) AND (Africa OR Algeria OR Angola OR Benin OR Botswana OR “Burkina Faso” OR Burundi OR Verde OR Cameroon OR “Central African Republic” OR Chad OR Comoros OR Congo OR Zaire OR “Cote d'Ivoire” OR “Ivory Coast” OR Djibouti OR Egypt OR “Equatorial Guinea” OR Eritrea OR Eswatini OR Swaziland OR Ethiopia OR Gabon OR Gambia OR Ghana OR Guinea OR Guinea-Bissau OR Guiné Or Kenya OR Lesotho OR Liberia OR Libya OR Madagascar OR Malawi OR Nyasaland OR Mali OR Mauritania OR Mauritius OR Morocco OR Mozambique OR Mozambique OR Namibia OR Niger OR Nigeria OR Rwanda OR “Sao Tome” OR Senegal OR Seychelles OR “Sierra Leone” OR Somalia OR “South Africa” OR Sudan OR Tanzania Tanganyika OR Zanzibar OR Togo OR Tunisia OR Uganda OR Zambia OR Zimbabwe OR Rhodesia).

An additional search substituted the names of individual African countries with Africa, thus: (anthelmintic* OR wormer OR treat*) AND (helmint* OR nematod*) AND (resistan* OR efficacy* OR effectiv* OR suscept*) AND (ruminant* OR livestock OR sheep OR goat* OR cattle OR cow OR zebu* OR ovin* OR caprin* OR bovin*) AND Africa.

Both search strings were additionally run substituting anthelmintic* OR wormer OR treat* terms with ‘dewormer’.

Selection criteria for screening

A study was considered eligible for inclusion in the review if: (i) it was performed on natural infection with GIN in any African country; (ii) the study population consisted of any group of ruminant animals from Africa which had been tested for GIN; (iii) quantitative measurements of anthelmintic efficacy were presented using FECR; (iv) it was a cross sectional study; (v) the method of diagnosis was stated. A study was considered excluded if: (i) it did not involve animals located in any African country; (ii) results did not present quantitative data; (iii) the study consisted of a thesis manuscript or review article; iv) articles related to animal species other than ruminant livestock; and (v) the full text was not accessible. Additional eligibility steps were imposed for meta-analysis, see below.

Rayyan software (rayyan.ai, MA, USA) was used for study selection and screening. The title and abstract of each article were viewed by three independent evaluators drawn from the five co-authors and indicated for inclusion or exclusion, with the reason for exclusion given. Where the decision was not unanimous, a fourth evaluator made the final decision.

Database for meta-analysis

Study metadata were entered into an Excel database (Microsoft, USA), where bibliographic information was extracted for each study, including the first author; year of publication; country and region of Africa. Data on the detailed assessment of anthelmintic efficacy were then extracted from the studies to compile statistics on AR prevalence and extent according to host species, farm and drug used. Animal data included the animal species, number of animals treated per group; design of the study; the percentage reduction in faecal nematode egg counts (FEC); classification of the GIN population as resistant or susceptible; the anthelmintic drug and group used; method of drug application; and the laboratory technique used for the FEC.

The quality assessment of the included papers was carried out independently using the ROBINS-II Cochrane for risk of bias in non-randomised studies of interventions (Sterne et al., 2016). Each study was evaluated into 7 domains (D1-Classification of intervention; D2-Selection of participants; D3-Classification of intervention; D4-Bias of intervention; D5-Loss of data; D6-Outcome measures; D7-Report of outcome) and classified into moderate, serious, low and critical. If the article had a critical classification in any domain, it was not included in the meta-analyses.

The articles provisionally retained for meta-analysis were carefully assessed according to their quality and importance to the objectives. In order to be included in the quantitative analyses, studies must have conducted a FECRT whose results were calculated according to Coles et al. (1992, 2006), providing a quality threshold for inclusion and enhancing comparability. Original data were rarely reported so it was not possible to recalculate FECR% and classify resistance according to the updated criteria of Kaplan et al. (2023). The older criteria were therefore used to provide the largest possible number of studies and temporal and geographical range. Each FECRT was assigned a working classification of resistant if FEC reduction (FECR) was lower than 95%, and susceptible if FECR was 95% or higher. The same set of articles was used to provide FECR% data for trends analysis, except Mwamachi et al. (1995), which was excluded as an outlier on the grounds that it studied a single intensively managed breeding stock farm with known AR problems and its position at the start of the time series risked disproportionately skewing temporal trend analysis.

Statistical analysis

Primary analyses and descriptive statistics were carried out using the pivot table function in Excel. Descriptive statistics and non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis and Mann Whitney tests (including for post hoc comparisons) were used in DATAtab.net to compare FECR% between host, geographical and drug categories.

To investigate risk factors for a classification of AR at farm level, binary logistic regression was used, after checking for collinearity. The dependent variable was the status of efficacy (pass-fail), classified as susceptible (0) if the FECR% was equal to or greater than 95% and resistant (1) if the FECR was less than 95%, used as the reference category. In the final regression model, three explanatory variables were included. The first was the anthelmintic group (AH GROUP), categorized into four levels: benzimidazoles (BZ), macrocyclic lactones (ML), imidazothiazoles (IMTZ), and the reference category others (OTH), which encompass treatments combining different AH groups (COMBI) and/or salicylanilides (SLN). These latter treatments were few and more recently available to farmers, and associated with the lowest levels of resistance, so were used as the reference group. The second variable was year, included as an explanatory variable. The third explanatory variable was region, which included four geographic zones: East Africa (EA), Southern Africa (SA), North Africa (NA), and West Africa (WA) serving as the reference category. For analytical purposes, WA also included countries from Central Africa (CA) to increase group size, since few studies were available from WA or CA alone. Logistic regression was conducted per FECRT, thus for studies reporting results for both sheep and goats, results were allocated to the relevant species-specific analysis. Logistic regression was conducted by including all variables to begin with, followed by stepwise reduction of the least significant until the final model was attained, which included only significant variables. In cattle, none of variables AH group, year and region variables were found to be associated with AR status (P > 0.10) and a final logistic regression model is not reported.

To further assess trends in extent of AR over time, correlation analysis was conducted between FECR% and the year of the study in small ruminants, with studies of goats and sheep merged on the grounds that they share most GIN species and often also grazing lands. To improve the distributional properties of the data, the FECR% values were transformed using the arcsine square root formula (asin(sqrt[FECR%/100])). Following Pearson correlation, linear regression was applied to transformed FECR% against year and the distribution of residuals assessed for normality. Change in AR over time was also assessed by splitting raw FECR% values per farm and anthelmintic group, into the first 10 years of eligible articles (1998-2007) and the last 10 years (2016-2025). The Kruskal-Wallis test was applied to determine whether rank distribution of FECR% differed between these groups, with pairwise Mann-Whitney comparisons between time periods (baseline and recent) for each drug group (BZ, IMTZ, ML). Salicylanilides (rafoxanide and closantel) and combination products were not included in temporal analyses of FECR% as too few eligible records were returned.

Results

Articles included and anthelmintic treatments assessed

The number of articles returned during the search and retained at each step is shown in Fig. 1. A total of 60 scientific articles published from 1995 to 2025 was taken forward to the meta-analysis. For small ruminants, there were 14 articles with unique data for sheep, 17 for goats and 24 common articles that provided data for both sheep and goats. In cattle, 5 scientific articles were retained. Most of the studies (59/60, 98.3%) were published in English with one (1.7%) in French. Substituting treatment terms with ‘dewormer’ yielded two additional studies which considered plant-based deworming only and were not included.

Flow diagram following PRISMA guidelines (Moher et al., 2010), showing the total number of studies identified, screened and assessed for eligibility at each stage of the selection process. Final numbers for sheep and goats divide studies considering both – see text.

Data from 12 African countries were analysed (Fig. 2). Most studies (68%) were conducted in East Africa, and goats and sheep were similarly represented with few studies in cattle. South Africa returned a higher number of studies initially, but many were excluded due to FECRT methodology being incompatible with meta-analysis.

Number of eligible publications investigating anthelmintic resistance in gastrointestinal nematodes, by host species, country and region.

A total of 6641 ruminants were sampled and treated with anthelmintics in the studies included, with goats and sheep the most numerous and few cattle assessed (Fig. 3). Anthelmintic groups evaluated included benzimidazoles (BZ), macrocyclic lactones (ML), imidiazothiazoles (IMTZ), salicylanilides (SLN) and combinations of different anthelmintic groups (COMBI). The number of reported FECRT at group level was 528: 230 in sheep, 271 in goats and 27 in cattle. Among drug groups, BZ were frequently assessed, with albendazole (ALBZ) the most commonly administered BZ, accounting for 209/528 (39.6%) of FECRT. In addition to ALBZ, both sheep and goats were also treated with fenbendazole (FBZ) or a combination of ALBZ and FBZ, although this occurred in only 8/528 (1.5%) cases; assessed cattle BZ treatments used only ALBZ. The ML ivermectin (IVM) was used in 122/528 (23.1%) cases, and the imidazothiazole (IMTZ) levamisole (LEV) was reported in 92/528 (17.4%) cases, with tetramisole also a commonly used IMTZ. Closantel was the least frequently used anthelmintic in the efficacy studies, being reported in only 5/528 (0.9%) cases.

Total number of animals treated with different types of anthelmintic in the studies included: BZ – Benzimidazoles; ML – Macrocyclic lactones; IMTZ – Imidazothiazoles; SLN – Salicylanilides; COMBI – Combinations of multiple anthelmintics.

Reduction in faecal egg counts by host species, drug group and region

Univariate comparisons in FECR% were first conducted for all AH groups together. The mean FECR% was 88.6 in cattle, 88.4 in sheep and 84.8 in goats. The Kruskal-Wallis test indicated that the difference in FECR% across species was statistically significant (X2 = 8.57, df = 2, p = 0.01) and accounted for by higher FECR% in sheep than goats (p = 0.01), in which the proportion of groups failing the FECRT was also lower. Approximately half of all reported treatments across all host species and drug groups failed to reduce FEC by more than 95% (Fig. 4).

Inset figures are the %FECR among FECRT classified as resistant, with 95% confidence intervals. Results by drug group are given in Table 1.

Geographic regions also differed in average assessed efficacy across drug groups (Kruskal-Wallis X2 = 52.12; df = 4; p < 0.001) with FECR% significantly lower in the SA region than all other regions (adjusted p < 0.05). The highest mean FECR% across drug groups was reported in WA at 95.8% (95% CI: 93.4 - 98.2) and in CA at 92.7% (95% CI: 88.6 - 96.7), and the lowest in SA at 70.4% (95% CI: 62.5 -78.4). Corresponding mean FECR% in NA was 84.9% (95% CI: 81.2- 88.6), and in EA 87.8% (95% CI: 85.9-89.7).

Results for the three main drug groups are shown in Table 1, with data for sheep and goats combined, given the overlap in GIN species between these hosts. Median FECR% was close to 95% for sheep and goats but around half the results fell under this threshold, and more for cattle. Confidence intervals for prevalence of treatment failure were wide for cattle due to small sample size.

Risk factors associated with treatment failure

Bivariate logistic regression models were applied for each host species to assess the association between the variables AH group (chemical class), year, and region, with AR status (susceptible/resistant) as the dependent variable, resistance being defined as FECR <95%. In the final model for sheep (Table 2), only region was retained. Studies in Southern Africa (SA) and North Africa (NA) were more likely to report AR than other regions. In goats, AH group and year were significantly related to the occurrence of AR, as determined by FECR% (Table 3). Treatment with BZ and IMTZ were associated with higher odds of AR, when compared to the reference group OTH (COMBI and SLN), with which ML did not differ. The model for goats also estimated that each additional year from 1995 to 2025 was associated with a 1.13-fold increase in the odds of detecting AR. Model fit was good for sheep and poor for goats, although pseudo r-square values nevertheless indicated some explanatory power. The finding on change in risk of AR over time was supplemented by AH group specific trend analysis, see below.

Change in faecal egg count reduction over time

Trends in treatment effectiveness over time were assessed for each of the major AH groups by correlation and linear regression of FECR% against year, using arcsin(square root) transformation to stabilise the variance in FECR%. Moreover, median FECR% (using raw data) was compared between the periods 1998-2007 and 2016-2025, i.e. the first and last decade of the data series. For these analyses, sheep and goat data were combined to increase power. Results are shown in Fig. 5. For all groups, FECR% correlated negatively with year, indicating a decline in mean treatment effectiveness over time. For BZ and ML, but not IMTZ, median FECR% was significantly lower in the last decade of the time series than the first decade. Variability in FECR% was higher for BZ (coefficient of variation 0.25) than for IMTZ (0.16) or ML (0.19).

Parasite identification before and after anthelmintic treatment

Some studies identified the GIN species involved in the FECRT using coproculture, which consistently showed the presence of multiple nematode species before and after anthelmintic treatment. In sheep, of the 26 studies screened, only 14 studies identified parasite genera before and after treatment. Of the remaining studies, 7 did not identify parasite genera at all and another 5 identified parasite genera only after treatment. In goats, of the 29 studies screened, 19 identified the parasite genera before and after treatment. Of the remaining studies, 4 identified the parasite genera after treatment but did not specify the drug group, and 5 studies did not identify parasite genera at all. One study, in goats, identified GIN by molecular methods. In cattle, of the 5 studies, 2 identified parasite genera pre- and post-treatment. Of the others, 3 did not provide genera after treatment and one did not identify parasite genera at all. Morphological analysis of L3 (third stage) larvae recovered from coproculture revealed the presence of eight main GIN genera whose distribution varied by host species and the AH group used (Table 4).

Haemonchus was the most frequently reported genus across the 60 studies reviewed, and was detected in 31 (51.7%) studies before treatment and 29 (48.3%) after treatment, mostly in small ruminants. After treatment, Haemonchus and Trichostrongylus were the most prevalent genera across host species; Haemonchus became more common relative to other species after treatment with all three AH groups.

Studies excluded from meta-analysis

Several studies offered quantitative data on AH efficacy but were excluded from the meta-analysis because they used methods that were not comparable between studies, for example deviating from Coles et al. (1992, 2006), calculating FECR% using a control group or geometric means, or classifying resistance without reference to FECR%. These articles are summarised in Table 5; three were not returned in the current systematic review but appeared in the review by Gatitu et al. (2025).

Discussion

Anthelmintic resistance has been investigated on African ruminant livestock farms since the 1990s across all regions, although with a bias towards eastern and southern Africa. While relevant studies were collated systematically by Gatitu et al. (2025), the present study goes further by including additional scientific articles (60 in the current study versus 28) and applying meta-analysis to eligible studies, using comparable data from faecal egg count reduction tests (FECRT).

Goats were subject to the highest number of reported FECRT, followed closely by sheep, while few studies on cattle were published. This reflects the fact that small ruminants play a fundamental role in the livelihoods of rural populations in most African countries and are strongly affected by parasites such as GIN. Goats, especially, are able to survive and reproduce under extreme temperatures and low humidity, even with minimal food availability, and there is growing interest in preserving the health and well-being of this species to increase its role in food security and climate change adaptation (FAOFood Agriculture Organization of the United Nations, 2015). In this context, the control of GIN in goats has become a priority (Mohammedsalih et al., 2019; Airs et al., 2023; Guinda et al., 2025), and emergence of AR could compromise the ability to tap into the advantages of this species.

Resistance in cattle has been reported only in a limited number of countries, primarily in Tanzania and Sudan and involving the BZ class. The lack of reports of AR cases in cattle in some regions may reflect historically lower intensity of AH use in this species, as suggested by Maurizio et al. (2024). However, where reported, levels of treatment failure in cattle were similar to small ruminants. Confidence intervals around prevalence are very broad at low sample sizes (see Table 1) so very little can be inferred on the true resistance situation in cattle in Africa, especially for less studied regions and drug groups. The high levels of AR detected in certain localities raise the possibility of an emerging, yet largely undetected, resistance trend in cattle populations, emphasising the need for proactive monitoring and targeted control strategies. The low number of published studies of AR in cattle might also be explained by lower priority given to GIN control than to other cattle diseases, and complacency on the effectiveness of AH in this species due to limited performance monitoring and lack of clinical consequences of marginal treatment failure. In both cattle and small ruminants, perceptions that lower treatment intensity in many African settings makes AR less of a concern than in other regions of the world seem misplaced, and complacency should be avoided when advising on strategies for sustainable parasite control.

The most commonly used AH in the papers included in this study was ALBZ from the BZ class, followed by IVM, a ML, and LEV, an IMTZ. The prominence of ALBZ is likely due to its low cost, broad spectrum and availability in suspension, paste or tablet form, making it easy to use (Batista et al., 2022). Studies provided evidence on the effectiveness of drugs across AH classes, with enough studies using BZ, IMTZ and ML to enable meta-analysis of factors influencing treatment outcomes.

Overall, FECR% values across the ruminant species and AH groups included in this study revealed treatment failure in approximately half the groups tested. The FECRT is an imperfect test for AR and failure (FECR<95%) can be due to other reasons (Morgan et al., 2022). Nevertheless, FECRT-based studies in Africa have confirmed AR using in vitro and molecular tests (e.g. Guinda et al., 2025), and it is likely that trends in %FECR are largely driven by AR, as elsewhere (Rose Vineer et al., 2020). The prevalence of AR can therefore be considered high in the current study and is consistent with findings from several African studies that have reported widespread and increasing AR, particularly among small ruminants (Wanyangu et al., 1996; Keyyu et al., 2002; Maurizio et al., 2024; Mohammedsalih et al., 2024).

Several factors might contribute to this trend. In many African contexts, livestock is raised under extensive subsistence-oriented systems, which sometimes provide animals with inadequate nutrition due to reliance on natural pastures and seasonal feed availability (Hawley and Ezenwa, 2022). This nutritional stress increases susceptibility to parasite infections and their effects. Furthermore, the lack of farmer awareness regarding the economic impact of parasite infections, a deficiency partly attributed to weak extension services (Belina et al., 2017), has led to the widespread adoption of ineffective treatment practices. These include repeated use of the same AH classes without rotation (Martin et al., 1989; Martínez-Valladares et al., 2013), and incorrect estimation of animal body weight based on visual assessment, resulting in underdosing or overdosing (Kumsa et al., 2010; Emsley et al., 2023). Underdosing is particularly relevant in goats, as drug data sheets and labels often recommend dosages equivalent to those for sheep, disregarding the fact that goats require 1.5 to 2 times higher dose rates due to their faster metabolism (Hoste et al., 2011). Additional compounding factors include the informal and unregulated sale of AH, poor storage conditions, and the use of expired or substandard products (Jaime et al., 2022). These practices reduce the cost and increase accessibility of drugs yet promote improper usage and favour underdosing (Mohammedsalih et al., 2024). The lack of widespread adoption of recommended practices, such as effective quarantine for newly acquired animals and selective treatment of the most affected animals, further contributes to the spread of resistance between and within herds (Maingi et al., 2002; Belina et al., 2017; Guinda et al., 2025). Despite general awareness of AR, measures to combat it are impeded by poor education, especially in older farmers in communal grazing systems (Ndwandwe et al., 2025). Factors driving AR are likely to differ between and within regions and sectors and further studies are needed to elucidate them and guide appropriate measures to preserve anthelmintic efficacy.

The simplicity of the FECRT allowed comparison across a large number of studies in the current meta-analysis. Nevertheless, some studies were excluded due to divergent methodology that made direct comparisons difficult. The most recent studies quite reasonably followed the latest recommendations for FECRT design and reporting (Kaplan et al., 2023) but this precluded fair comparison with older studies, while other excluded studies used methods such as geometric means or integration of a control group. It is recommended that raw FECR% data be made available in future studies, regardless of method used for analysis, to support cross-study comparisons.

Regarding risk factors associated with the detection of AR, sheep from Southern Africa (SA) and North Africa (NA) were found to be associated with a higher risk of AR compared to other regions. This result may be related to differences in GIN epidemiology, production systems, and/or AH use. The high incidence of resistance observed in sheep in southern Africa can be explained, in part, by the region's subtropical climatic conditions, characterised by hot and humid summers that favour the life cycle of gastrointestinal parasites (Zvinorova et al., 2016), requiring frequent AH treatments and favouring the selection of AR (Hodgkinson et al., 2019). Furthermore, the predominance of extensive production systems in the region and the difficulty of implementing sustainable parasite management practices could contribute to risks of AR (van Wyk and Malan, 1988; van Wyk et al., 1999; Tsotetsi et al., 2013; Emsley et al., 2023). In South Africa, the presence of long-standing large commercial sheep flocks has quite likely predisposed to a long history of intensive AH treatment, driving emergence of AR before other regions, and greater monitoring effort. This is reflected in early studies of AR in South Africa and neighbouring countries, most of which were excluded on the grounds of incomparable methodology. These studies nevertheless demonstrate the early emergence of AR on the continent, and the potential role of livestock improvement programmes that exported breeding stock from southern Africa in spreading AR across the continent (e.g., Chaka and Gizaw, 2009).

North Africa, with its predominantly arid and semi-arid climate, was also associated with a higher risk of AR. Sudan is one of the countries in this region, where some inappropriate practices in the use of AH have already been reported. The use of the three classes of AH (BZ, ML, IMTZ) has been reported in Sudan to control GIN infections in ruminants for more than two decades, and they have been distributed under various trade names by different companies without strategic plans for their use (Abakar et al., 2017; Abbakar et al., 2003). The development of AR in H. contortus, related to frequent underdosing due to incorrect prior estimation of body weight and not weighing the animals, high frequencies of treatment with BZ for more than two decades with an average of three treatments per year, also including treatments in dry seasons (Abakar et al., 2017). Treatment in dry seasons when on-pasture refugia are low would be expected to select strongly for AR (van Wyk, 2001b). In sheep, the highest rates of resistance to BZ were observed in Algeria and South Africa, consistent with findings of Djemai et al. (2025) and Mavundela et al. (2025), who also reported elevated resistance levels in these countries. Among goats, ML resistance was particularly marked in Sudan and Ethiopia, confirming patterns described by Maurizio et al. (2024). These authors noted frequent and sometimes improper use of IVM within communal grazing systems as a key contributing factor to AR. Furthermore, the inherently lower oral bioavailability of ML in goats may exacerbate therapeutic failure, facilitating the selection and persistence of resistant GIN populations.

With respect to IMTZ, high resistance levels were reported in sheep from South Africa and Kenya, and in goats from Uganda and Ethiopia. These results are corroborated by Byaruhanga and Okwee-Acai. (2013), who documented a progressive decline in LEV efficacy across several African herds, particularly in regions where drug administration occurs irregularly and without adequate control over dosing and frequency.

The meta-analysis indicated a progressive decline in AH effectiveness over time. In goats, both the AH group (higher risk for BZ and IMTZ) and the year of treatment were significantly associated with the occurrence of AR, although model fit was poor. Stronger evidence for declining AH efficacy comes from the negative correlation observed between FECR% and year, which applied to all three main drug groups when sheep and goat data were combined. This trend was stronger (higher slope) for ML than for BZ and IMTZ, despite later availability, as has also been observed for AR prevalence in Europe (Rose Vineer et al., 2020). Median FECR% was significantly lower for BZ and ML in small ruminants in 2016-25 than in 1998-2007, by approximately 5%, with increasing frequency of treatment failure at the 95% FECR level. While failure to reduce FECR by 95% does not prove AR, especially given widespread confounding factors (Morgan et al., 2022), this is the most likely explanation for the declining FECR% over time. High variability in FECR%, however, could indicate issues with drug quality, administration and metabolism, as well as underpinning genetic variation in GIN populations: variation was especially high for BZ, whose bioavailability is known to be strongly affected by formulation and gut transit time (Morgan et al., 2022).

The current findings highlight an urgent need for accessible and cost-effective parasite control strategies, including community-based interventions, that also address the risks of AR. These should include farmer education on GIN control that promotes awareness of the risks of poor control practices for the development of AR. Moreover, stronger governmental regulation is necessary to address the informal drug trade and assure the quality, efficacy, and appropriate distribution of AH. Targeted selective treatment (TST), in which animals are identified for treatment to control parasites and promote the sustainable use of anthelmintics, has been proposed as a practical strategy to generate refugia (van Wyk, 2001a), especially in small ruminants (Kenyon and Jackson, 2012). The use of the FAMACHA and Five Point Check systems allows for the selective treatment of only the most affected animals, thereby minimizing selection pressure on parasite populations (Nabukenya et al., 2014; Sargison et al., 2021; van Wyk et al., 2006). Studies conducted in various countries, including South Africa (van Wyk et al., 2006), Brazil (Molento et al., 2009), Uganda (Nabukenya et al., 2014), Botswana (Walker et al., 2015; Machekano et al., 2026) Mozambique (Guinda et al., 2025) and Malawi (Airs et al., 2026) have demonstrated that TST is a practical strategy in Africa for both smallholder and commercial farmers.

When analysing the genera of parasites present in sheep, goats and cattle, it was found that Haemonchus and Trichostrongylus were the most prevalent before and after treatment with BZ, ML and IMTZ. Other genera including Oesophagostomum were less commonly recorded after treatment. While drug groups were analysed independently in the present study, the frequent occurrence of Haemonchus after treatment across AH groups strongly suggests the presence of multiple resistance, corroborating patterns observed in regions of Africa (Atanásio et al., 2002; Kumsa and Abebe, 2009; Kabaka et al., 2012; Adediran et al., 2014; Mphahlele et al., 2021; Wondimu and Bayu, 2022). This result is in line with several studies that confirm the high frequency of this genus in humid tropical and subtropical regions, where it represents a significant threat to animal health and productivity (Besier et al., 2016; Tinsley and Morgan, 2025; Waller and Chandrawathani, 2005). Adult females of Haemonchus spp. have a high reproductive capacity and can produce thousands of eggs a day. This factor contributes to the rapid contamination of pastures by infective larvae and the consequent increase in the incidence of haemonchosis outbreaks (Roeber et al., 2013), driving high treatment needs. In the face of high infection pressure and frequent treatment, AR is especially likely to evolve. A molecular study in Sudanese goats detected polymorphisms in the codons of the β tubulin gene of H: contortus related to resistance to BZ in post-treatment samples (Mohammedsalih et al., 2021).

Considering the high prevalence of Haemonchus spp. in African countries and the strong evidence for widespread AR presented in the current results, the adoption of integrated parasite control strategies is urgently required. Such strategies should include the judicious and rotational use of anthelmintic drugs to prevent the rapid development of resistance, and the routine application of efficacy tests such as the FECRT to verify drug effectiveness before treatment. Rotational grazing and the segregation of young and adult animals are also helpful, as these practices help to interrupt the parasite life cycle and reduce pasture contamination. Finally, the implementation of genetic selection for natural resistance, particularly in smallholder herds, represents a sustainable long-term approach to controlling gastrointestinal nematodes (Sargison et al., 2021; Walker et al., 2015).

Limitations of the review

Out of the 54 African countries, data were available for only 12, highlighting major gaps in surveillance and research strategies on AR in ruminant livestock across the continent. The study consistently followed the original WAAVP guidelines (Coles et al., 1992, 2006) for the implementation of the FECRT. However, the recent classification proposed by Kaplan et al. (2023) suggests that a greater number of parasite populations would now be considered resistant compared with the original criteria (Krücken et al., 2024; Mohammedsalih et al., 2024; Ehnert et al., 2025; Guinda et al., 2025). The choice of diagnostic method for determining AR is crucial, as it allows quantification of resistance levels and supports the implementation of appropriate nematode control and management measures (Baiak et al., 2019). The use of in vitro assays such as the egg hatch test (EHT), larval development test (LDT), and larval migration inhibition test (LMIT) represents a viable alternative for implementation in African laboratories. For instance, the EHT has shown a good correlation with FECRT results and can serve as a reliable method for quantifying in vitro resistance to BZ (Álvarez-Sánchez et al., 2006; von Samson-Himmelstjerna et al., 2009; Calvete et al., 2014). The LDT, in comparison with the EHT, offers the advantage of simultaneously assessing resistance to multiple drug classes, including BZ, IMTZ, and ML, within a single assay plate (Fortes and Molento, 2013). The LMIT has proven useful for detecting resistance to ML and LEV (Rothwell and Sangster, 1993) and can be applied in most diagnostic laboratories (Demeler et al., 2010). Strengthening diagnostic capacity, standardising methodologies, and fostering collaboration among African laboratories are essential steps to improve AR detection and ensure effective parasite control programs across the continent.

Conclusion

This review highlights that AR constitutes a growing and widespread threat to livestock production systems across Africa. Small ruminants, particularly sheep and goats, exhibit the highest frequency, level and diversity of resistance profiles across BZ, IMTZ and ML. Although less frequently reported in cattle, resistance identified in countries such as Cameroon and Sudan raises important concerns and merits further epidemiological investigation. These patterns reinforce the urgent need for species-specific control strategies, supported by the rational and evidence-based use of anthelmintics. The emergence of AR was found to be associated with geographic location, drug class, and treatment year, factors that reflect broader regional and management dynamics. The most prevalent gastrointestinal nematode genera were Haemonchus and Trichostrongylus with a notable dominance of Haemonchus, known for its high biotic potential and pathogenicity and rapid development of AR. Evidence for declining treatment effectiveness as assessed by FECRT was strong across drug groups. In response, the implementation of integrated parasite management, the use of TST strategies, rotational use of AH classes, and routine monitoring of drug quality and efficacy are essential. Strengthening farmer awareness, improving veterinary extension services, and regulation of drug use and distribution will be critical components of long-term, sustainable control of GIN in African livestock systems.
