Real-world single-center experience with pixantrone in DLBCL prior to its withdrawal: clinical outcomes and exploratory immunologic observations
Authors: Andrea Duminuco, Nunziatina Laura Parrinello, Laura Anastasia Caruso, Sara Marino, Anna Triolo, Agnese Blando, Annalisa Santisi, Arianna Sbriglione, Giuseppe Alberto Palumbo, Francesco Di Raimondo, Annalisa Chiarenza
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

Pixantrone is a synthetic aza-antracenedione originally developed to provide anthracycline-like cytotoxic activity with reduced cardiotoxicity [1]. In fact, in the management of diffuse large B-cell lymphomas (DLBCL), cumulative exposure to conventional anthracyclines represents a well-recognized clinical constraint, particularly in older or heavily pretreated patients, for whom liposomal doxorubicin-based regimens have been explored as frontline strategies in selected subtypes, demonstrating improved tolerability and encouraging efficacy while mitigating cardiotoxicity [2]. Beyond its historical clinical positioning, pixantrone differs structurally and mechanistically from classical anthracyclines. As an aza-antracenedione, pixantrone lacks the sugar moiety typical of conventional anthracyclines and exhibits reduced iron-mediated reactive oxygen species generation, contributing to its lower cardiotoxic potential [3]. Moreover, pixantrone interacts with topoisomerase II in a manner distinct from that of doxorubicin, inducing DNA damage and chromatin stress through mechanisms beyond classical intercalation alone [4, 5].

In DLBCL, in the pivotal trial PIX 301, pixantrone demonstrated a 40% of improvement in progression-free survival (PFS) compared with the control group, extending median PFS by 2.7 months (HR 0.60; p = 0.005), and its historical role in Europe was confined to relapsed or refractory (R/R) DLBCL in patients who had exhausted standard chemotherapeutic options or reached the cumulative anthracycline dose threshold [6–8]. Although its approval initially filled a therapeutic niche for heavily pretreated patients, real-world experience demonstrated modest clinical activity, and the rapid emergence of far more effective immunotherapies progressively rendered it obsolete [9–13]. The modern therapeutic landscape of R/R DLBCL contextualizes our findings and underscores their historical nature. While identifying patients with poor prognostic features at the earliest possible stage is crucial, as it allows timely therapeutic decisions and may meaningfully influence clinical outcomes [14], the years since pixantrone’s initial approval have seen dramatic changes in the DLBCL therapeutic landscape. CAR-T-cell therapies have transformed outcomes for primary refractory or early-relapsed patients, providing durable remissions in a subset and shifting the standard of care in second-line settings [15]. CD20×CD3 bispecific antibodies (epcoritamab, glofitamab, and odronextamab) have expanded treatment options for patients ineligible for or relapsing after CAR-T therapy, delivering response rates far superior to historical chemotherapy benchmarks [16]. Even frontline treatment has evolved, with polatuzumab-R-CHP improving PFS in higher-risk patients [17]. Compared with these therapies, pixantrone’s efficacy profile appears clearly inadequate, and, ultimately, it was withdrawn from the Italian market, eliminating its availability in routine clinical practice. This regulatory withdrawal, while not clinically disruptive in the current therapeutic era, risked causing the loss of real-world and biological observations accumulated over years of clinical use.

In fact, in recent years, pixantrone has paradoxically garnered renewed attention for its immunobiological footprint. Preclinical studies have further expanded the understanding of pixantrone’s molecular activity, implicating noncanonical pathways beyond direct cytotoxicity. In particular, interactions with the linear ubiquitin assembly complex (LUBAC), including HOIL-1–mediated ubiquitination, have been shown to influence chromatin organization, DNA topology, and cellular stress-response signaling. These findings suggest that pixantrone may exert pleiotropic effects on transcriptional regulation and genome stability, thereby modulating cellular responses to stress and immune signaling pathways [18–20]. Parallel observations in multiple sclerosis suggested that pixantrone exerts selective pressure on B-cell compartments, producing sustained depletion with relative sparing or modulation of T-cell subsets [18]. These findings, coupled with the molecule’s re-emergence in experimental models of various solid tumors, highlight the broader biological activity of pixantrone beyond direct cytotoxicity [21–25].

In this context, re-examining real-world data is relevant, and combining it with an exploratory analysis of immune-cell kinetics performed before and during treatment with pixantrone could be helpful. Although pixantrone no longer holds a role in lymphoma therapy, the patterns of immune remodeling observed during treatment may contribute to our understanding of how older chemotherapeutic agents shape T-cell biology and influence downstream treatment responsiveness. In addition, in non-Hodgkin lymphomas, accurate flow cytometry assessment is a cornerstone not only for diagnostic classification but also for clinical decision-making, enabling the assessment of immune-cell composition, functional states, and treatment-induced modulation of lymphocyte subsets [26–29].

Between 2021 and 2024, we treated 19 consecutive patients with R/R DLBCL at our Center with pixantrone. The baseline characteristics reflected the typical late-line population historically considered for pixantrone. We treated 12 (63%) males and 7 (37%) females, with a median age of 71 years (range 34–85), 13 (68%) with stage IV disease (bone marrow, lung, gastrointestinal tract), and 16 (84%) with a high R-IPI score (≥ 3). 5 patients (26%) had transformed disease from an indolent histology.

Patients had received a median of 3 prior systemic therapy lines (range 2–6), with previous anthracyclines in all cases (R-CHOP/COMP as first-line for all patients, followed by R-GEMOX, R-DHAP, Pola-BR). Pixantrone was administered due to relapse in 1 case (5%, occurred after 16 months from autologous stem-cell-transplant) and refractory disease (95%), for a median of 2 cycles (range 1–6), after a median of 14 (range 7–114) months from DLBCL diagnosis. Treatment discontinuation was observed in 16 cases (84%), primarily driven by disease progression/clinical deterioration (13, 68%) and toxicity in 3 (16%, heart failure, CMV reactivation, atrial fibrillation). Overall, only 3 patients (16%) completed the scheduled 6 cycles. Among those who underwent re-evaluation, stable disease was observed in 6 out of 10 (60%) patients, and 3 (30%) patients had progressive disease. In comparison, the best response assessment showed an overall response rate of 10% (only 1 partial response, overall 5% of the entire cohort).

As part of the translational component of this study, we evaluated peripheral immune-cell dynamics in patients receiving pixantrone. Flow-cytometric immunophenotyping was performed routinely before treatment initiation and again after 3 to 4 cycles of therapy. They were performed on peripheral blood samples and processed within 2 h of collection. Red blood cell lysis was performed using standard ammonium chloride–based lysis buffer, followed by washing and surface staining. No peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) isolation was performed. Data were acquired on Beckman Coulter Navios cytometer using standard acquisition settings and compensation matrices according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. A minimum of 30,000 events were collected per sample.

The gating strategy followed a sequential approach based on forward- and side-scatter properties to exclude debris and doublets, then identified CD3 + T-cells and subsequently subdivided them into CD4 + and CD8 + subsets. Differentiation and functional subsets were defined by CD45RA, CCR7, CD57, and PD-1 expression, evaluated on total T-cells and within the CD4 and CD8 compartments.

These markers allow real-world assessment of how pixantrone may modulate immune contexture beyond cytotoxic depletion, offering insight into differentiation status, exhaustion tone, and potential implications for responsiveness to therapies such as lenalidomide that rely on preserved or primed T-cell and NK-cell function.

Paired analyses were available for 8 patients who completed at least 4 cycles. Paired changes between baseline and post-treatment assessments were described using within-patient deltas to account for inter-individual variability and non-normal data distribution. When inferential testing was applied, non-parametric paired tests were used, as appropriate. Overall, pixantrone produced modest but directionally coherent shifts across T-cell subsets. The CD3 + compartment demonstrated a small median contraction, with values decreasing from a median of 84.1% at baseline to 72.6% after treatment. The distributional spread was wide, reflecting substantial inter-individual variability, yet the central tendency suggested a mild reduction. CD4 + T-cells displayed an intriguing divergence between paired and unpaired summaries: while the overall post-treatment median reached 42.4%, the paired median delta was − 2.9, indicating that modest decreases were more common at the individual level, whereas a small number of high post-treatment values pulled the group median upward. CD8 + T-cells showed a slight median expansion, with a paired delta of + 1.8 and a percent change of + 7.64%. Meanwhile, the CD8 + CD57+ subset increased (median delta + 24.7%), suggesting enrichment of terminally differentiated effector cells. PD-1 metrics showed small but consistent downward shifts, with median deltas of − 0.49 for CD3/PD-1, − 0.57 for CD4/PD-1, and − 0.16 for CD8/PD-1. These reductions, while subtle, occurred across all compartments studied.

The decrease in PD-1 intensity (−4.44 in responders vs. −1.37 in refractory, p = 0.133) and the expansion of CD8 + CD57+ subsets (24.7 vs. 2.7, p = 0.133) observed after pixantrone suggest a transient shift in T-cell state, possibly influencing the effectiveness of these subsequent immunomodulatory or immune-reliant therapies.

The modest contraction in CD3 + and CD4 + compartments is compatible with cytotoxic pressure in a heavily pretreated population but does not fully account for the specific directional changes in differentiation and exhaustion markers. The increase in CD8 + CD57+ cells could be biologically meaningful. CD57 marks a population of highly differentiated effector T-cells with reduced proliferative capacity and persistent cytotoxic potential [30]. Expansion of this subset can result from selective depletion of proliferative or PD-1–high cells during chemotherapy, leaving behind more terminally differentiated clones. Cytotoxic regimens often exert disproportionate pressure on rapidly dividing lymphocytes. CD57 + cells, which cycle infrequently, may be relatively spared, leading to a subtle shift in the overall CD8 + landscape [31]. In this context, pixantrone may preferentially deplete proliferating T-cell populations, leading to apparent enrichment of CD8 + CD57+ cells rather than true clonal expansion.

Secondarily, PD-1 is a dynamic marker influenced by antigenic exposure, cellular stress, cytokine signals, and exhaustion pathways [32]. A downward shift could reflect decreased antigenic stimulation as tumor burden changes, preferential depletion of PD-1–high cells, or altered inflammatory signaling [33]. While the magnitude of change is modest, in this framework, the decline in PD-1 intensity observed after pixantrone likely reflects a selection process driven by cytotoxic vulnerability, resulting in a residual T-cell pool with lower baseline expression of exhaustion markers. This interpretation aligns with existing evidence indicating that chemotherapy can reshape immune-cell composition through selective pressure rather than immune activation per se. These T-cell shifts could theoretically influence responsiveness to immunomodulatory agents, especially those such as lenalidomide that rely heavily on T-cell activation, co-stimulation, and NK-cell cooperation [34]. In our cohort, 6 patients received lenalidomide-based therapy after pixantrone (with rituximab or tafasitamab). Among them, 4 achieved disease stabilization with improvement of clinical symptoms or partial/complete response (67%), although the sample is too small to draw conclusions. Nonetheless, the biological plausibility of pixantrone priming the immune microenvironment for subsequent immunomodulation resonates with these observations.

Finally, given the central role of ubiquitination pathways in regulating T-cell activation, survival, and exhaustion programs, modulation of LUBAC-related signaling may contribute to the immune remodeling observed in our cohort.

Even though pixantrone is no longer available, documenting these patterns provides insight into therapy-induced immune remodeling, which has sparked growing interest across hematologic malignancies.

This study has intrinsic limitations due to the retrospective nature, small sample size, heterogeneity of prior treatments, limited immunophenotypic panel, and the absence of direct B-cell quantification restrict the depth of mechanistic interpretation. Another relevant limitation is the absence of direct quantification of B-cell compartments. This is particularly important in light of prior evidence demonstrating pixantrone’s selective B-cell depletion in non-oncologic settings. The lack of CD19 + B-cell measurements in our cohort prevents correlation between B-cell dynamics and the observed T-cell remodeling, and future studies incorporating parallel B- and T-cell profiling would be necessary to fully elucidate the immunologic impact of pixantrone.

In conclusion, our single-center experience captures the final chapter of pixantrone use in Italy. As expected, clinical activity was modest and predominantly cytostatic in nature, with hematologic toxicity consistent with prior reports. The exploratory immune-cell kinetics reveal subtle but coherent shifts such as the reductions in PD-1 expression on CD3 + and CD4 + T-cells, enrichment of CD8 + CD57+ effector-type cells, and small realignments across CD3/CD4/CD8 compartments. These findings suggest that pixantrone influences immune differentiation and exhaustion states beyond simple lymphodepletion. Although clinically obsolete, the drug’s immunologic footprint offers insight into therapy-induced remodeling of lymphocyte populations and may be relevant to understanding how older cytotoxic agents interact with or precede modern immunotherapies.
