Plant-bacteria hybrid nanovesicles for targeted sonodynamic therapy: A microbiome-friendly precision strike against H. pylori infections
Authors: Li Yang, Qianlei Ye, Xinran Peng, Jiayin Yu, Xueyi Xiao, Lei Wang, Yi Yan Yang, Peiyan Yuan, Guo-Bao Tian, Xin Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRising antibiotic resistance and adverse effects on commensal gut microbiota severely compromise conventional antibiotic therapies for Helicobacter pylori infection. Sonodynamic therapy (SDT), which employs low-intensity ultrasound to activate sonosensitizers for localized generation of cytotoxic reactive oxygen species (ROS), presents a promising non-antibiotic alternative with minimal resistance development risk. However, the efficacy of SDT is inherently constrained by the short diffusion radius and lifetime of ROS. Herein, we developed a homologous-targeting biomimetic sonosensitizer platform to overcome this limitation: hybrid membrane nanovesicles (TNVs-DMVs) engineered from turmeric plant-derived exosome-like nanovesicles (TNVs) and H. pylori-derived double membrane vesicles (DMVs). TNVs contain sonosensitizer curcumin and improve its solubility, yet SDT efficacy against H. pylori is limited. The DMVs endow TNVs-DMVs with intrinsic homologous targeting capability towards H. pylori, significantly enhancing the intracellular delivery of curcumin and subsequent ROS generation within bacterial cells. As a result, TNVs-DMVs achieved potent eradication of H. pylori in both acidic and neutral conditions without inducing detectable resistance. Moreover, TNVs-DMVs exhibited superior mucus penetration compared to TNVs alone, enabling effective elimination of H. pylori and its biofilms residing within the protective gastric mucus layer. In an H. pylori-infected mouse model, TNVs-DMVs mediated SDT demonstrated efficacy surpassing free TNVs and comparable to standard triple antibiotic therapy. Importantly, unlike triple therapy which depletes commensal flora, TNVs-DMVs treatment not only preserves intestinal microbiota homeostasis but also significantly increases populations of beneficial bacteria. This rationally designed TNVs-DMVs platform represents a transformative therapeutic modality, offering resistance-free eradication of H. pylori while maintaining microbiome health, distinct from conventional antibiotics.Keywords: Antibacterial, Sonodynamic therapy, Drug delivery, Bacterial membrane vesicle, Turmeric-derived vesicles, Helicobacter pylori

Introduction

Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori), a spiral-shaped bacterium infecting nearly half the global population, is the primary etiological agent of gastritis, gastric ulcers, and gastric cancer [[1], [2], [3]]. In particular, it is implicated in 75%-90% of gastric adenocarcinoma cases [4]. Current treatment strategies rely predominantly on triple or quadruple antibiotic therapy [5,6]. However, escalating prevalence of antibiotic resistance significantly impedes effective treatment. Moreover, orally administered antibiotics cause collateral damage to commensal gut microbiota, further limiting their efficacy. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop novel antimicrobial strategies that circumvent traditional antibiotics and minimize harm to the gut microbiome [7,8].

Sonodynamic Therapy (SDT) offers a promising alternative by leveraging low-intensity ultrasound to activate sensitizing agents (sonosensitizers) within target tissues. This process generates cytotoxic reactive oxygen species (ROS), capable of eradicating bacterial infections [9]. Recent studies have demonstrated the efficacy of SDT against H. pylori through localized generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), offering a noninvasive alternative to antibiotics [[10], [11], [12]]. However, current sonosensitizers predominantly rely on inorganic or synthetic compounds (e.g., porphyrin derivatives, metal oxides), which face challenges in biocompatibility and potential long-term toxicity [[12], [13], [14]]. These limitations hinder the clinical translation of SDT for gastrointestinal infections. Curcumin, the principal bioactive compound in turmeric [15], functions as a natural sonosensitizer, producing antibacterial ROS under ultrasound irradiation [16]. However, its therapeutic potential is constrained by poor aqueous solubility and limited bioavailability [17]. Turmeric-derived nanovesicles (TNVs) which naturally encapsulate curcumin, present a viable strategy to enhance bioavailability and therapeutic delivery. These nanovesicles transport bioactive compounds efficiently, potentially improving efficacy while mitigating inflammatory responses in vivo [18,19]. We hypothesize that TNVs can significantly enhance the sonodynamic anti-H. pylori activity compared to conventional curcumin formulations.

Despite their potency, ROS exhibit limited stability in vivo due to susceptibility to being quenched and restricted biodistribution, hindering the effective antimicrobial action of SDT [20]. To address these challenges, targeted delivery approaches are critical to enhance the accumulation and retention of sonosensitizers at the H. pylori infection sites. Bacterial outer membrane vesicles (OMVs), leveraging their structural homology to bacterial membranes, offer a promising strategy for homologous targeting and site-specific drug delivery [21,22]. Notably, OMVs derived from H. pylori have been extensively explored as vaccine platforms to induce host immune responses against infection [[23], [24], [25], [26], [27], [28]]. While OMV vaccines show promise for prophylaxis, their utility in treating active infections is constrained by complex purification protocols and low yield [[29], [30], [31], [32]]. Double-membrane vesicles (DMVs), derived from Gram-negative bacteria and incorporating both inner and outer membrane components [33], offer distinct advantages for sonosensitizer delivery. When produced via low-temperature ultrahigh-pressure continuous flow cell disruption, DMVs exhibit greater yield and retain compositional similarity to OMVs [34]. Thus, DMVs derived from H. pylori likely retain analogous homologous targeting capabilities to OMVs, enabling selective fusion with bacterial membranes. Consequently, structural integration of DMVs with therapeutic nanovesicles TNVs may enhance targeted delivery of curcumin encapsulated in TNVs to infection sites, thereby maximizing sonodynamic antibacterial efficacy. In contrast, simple mixing or sequential administration of DMVs and TNVs could not modulate the biodistribution of curcumin from TNVs.

Herein, we engineer a novel sonodynamic nanotherapeutic: a hybrid membrane vesicle (TNVs-DMVs) integrating TNVs and H. pylori-derived DMVs (Fig. 1). Within the construct, the curcumin-loaded TNVs act as a sonosensitizer, generating bactericidal ROS upon ultrasound exposure. Concurrently, the DMVs’ component confers precise homologous targeting toward H. pylori, thus enhancing the localized sonodynamic antibacterial activity in the gastric region while preventing nonspecific sonosensitizer delivery to the intestinal tract. TNVs-DMVs hybrid platform is designed to overcome antibiotic resistance and reduce side effects associated with conventional therapies. Furthermore, this approach holds potential for preserving gut microbiota homeostasis and preventing antibiotic-induced dysbiosis.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of TNVs-DMVs

Hybrid membrane vesicles (TNVs-DMVs) were prepared as schematically depicted in Fig. 2A. H. pylori-derived double-membrane vesicles (DMVs) were generated using low-temperature cavitation force-induced cell disruption, followed by collection of self-assembled nanovesicles [34]. Consequently, DMVs exhibited significantly higher yield (approximately 6-fold) than that of OMVs, while requiring substantially less bacterial culture volume (10 mL) for extraction (Fig. 2B). Structurally, DMVs' higher yield originates from their production via controlled physical disruption of bacterial cells (i.e., ultrahigh-pressure homogenization). This process forcibly fragments both inner and outer membranes into bilayer fragments that spontaneously reassemble into vesicles, bypassing the energy-dependent budding machinery required for OMV biogenesis [35]. This enhanced production efficiency addresses a key limitation of OMVs as delivery vehicles. Moreover, DMVs form spherical particles with particle size and surface potential comparable to OMVs (Fig. 2C and S1), while possessing a characteristically thicker membrane compared to OMVs, consistent with prior findings [34]. Turmeric-derived nanovesicles (TNVs), obtained through juicing, fiber filtering, and differential centrifugation, were subsequently fused with DMVs through probe ultrasonication to form TNVs-DMVs. The resulting TNVs-DMVs showed a hydrodynamic diameter of 200.7 ± 1.7 nm and a zeta potential of −17.67 mV (Fig. 2D). Both parameters demonstrated excellent stability over time (Fig. S2).

Successful membrane fusion between TNVs and DMVs was confirmed using confocal fluorescence microscope and Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET) analysis. Co-localization of red fluorescent dye DiI-labeled TNVs and green dye DiO-labeled DMVs provided visual evidence of fusion (Fig. 2E). In contrast, a non-sonicated physical mixture of TNVs and DMVs showed distinct, separated fluorescent signals, confirming fusion required the ultrasonication step. To further validate membrane component integration, TNVs were dual-labeled with the FRET pair DiO (donor) and DiI (acceptor). Increasing the proportion of unlabeled DMVs within the TNVs-DMVs hybrid led to a significant decrease in DiI emission (568 nm) accompanied by a corresponding increase in DiO emission (527 nm) (Fig. 2F). This attenuation of FRET efficiency indicates DMV components intercalated between the dye pair, increasing their spatial separation and confirming membrane fusion.

TNVs are known carriers of curcumin [36], a natural sonosensitizer capable of generating bactericidal reactive oxygen species (ROS) upon ultrasound irradiation [37]. Therefore, both TNVs and TNVs-DMVs preserved sonodynamic functionality, generating ROS under ultrasound irradiation, as detected by the significantly increased fluorescence of the singlet oxygen probe Singlet Oxygen Sensor Green (SOSG) (Fig. 2G). TNVs-DMVs showed a slightly lower yield than TNVs alone at equivalent protein concentrations. ROS production, specifically singlet oxygen, was further verified using the probe 1,3-diphenylisobenzofuran (DPBF), as demonstrated by the time-dependent decrease in its UV-vis absorption peak (Fig. 2H).

In vitro sonodynamic antibacterial activity of TNVs-DMVs against H. pylori

Given H. pylori's survival in acidic gastric fluid and near-neutral condition under stomach mucus layer [12,38,39], we evaluated the antibacterial efficacy of TNVs-DMVs under both conditions (Fig. 3A). Since the sonosensitizer curcumin within TNVs generates ROS under ultrasound, TNVs exhibited decent antibacterial activity against H. pylori at pH 2.2. Upon hybridization with DMVs, TNVs-DMVs exhibited markedly enhanced efficacy, effectively eliminating bacteria after just 4 min of ultrasound exposure in acidic conditions (Fig. 3B and S3). Meanwhile, no antibacterial activity was observed without ultrasound. TNVs-OMVs hybrid vesicles showed similar trends (Fig. S4). As H. pylori also colonizes gastric epithelial surfaces and mucus layers under near-neutral conditions [1], effective mucus penetration is crucial. Transwell assays demonstrated significantly greater penetration of dye-labeled TNVs-DMVs through a simulated mucus layer compared to TNVs alone (Fig. 3C and D and Fig. S5). This enhanced penetration is likely attributable to DMV components, which confer a more negative surface charge to TNVs-DMVs (Fig. 2D), and thereby facilitate movement through the mucus [40,41].

Beyond efficacy in acidic fluid and mucus penetration, TNVs-DMVs also potently eradicated H. pylori at neutral pH (pH 7.4) without inducing resistance. Live/dead staining and colony counting confirmed TNVs-DMVs, under ultrasound, achieved over 3-log bacterial reduction (Fig. 3E and F). TNVs alone showed no significant bactericidal activity under identical conditions. Critically, repetitive SDT treatments with TNVs-DMVs maintained ∼99.9% antibacterial efficacy (Fig. 3G), whereas H. pylori rapidly developed high-level resistance (63-fold increase in minimal bactericidal concentration MBC) to amoxicillin after only 9 exposures (Fig. S6). This minimal resistance risk, stemming from ROS's multifaceted antibacterial action, represents a key advantage over antibiotics.

TNVs-DMVs also demonstrated excellent efficacy against antibiotic-resistant strains and biofilms, which is an important advantage over conventional antibiotic treatment. Activity against clinical multidrug-resistant strains (resistant to clarithromycin, amoxicillin, and levofloxacin) mirrored that against susceptible strains (Fig. S7 and Fig. 3E), achieving at least 99% bacterial reduction for all the tested strains. Importantly, eradicating biofilms is particularly challenging due to enhanced resistance. It has been reported that the minimum biofilm eradication concentration of first-line antibiotic could be up to 511-fold higher than MIC against planktonic H. pylori [42]. TNVs-DMVs significantly reduced H. pylori biofilm biomass by 85.87% after a single 4-min ultrasound treatment (Fig. 3H and I), with live/dead staining confirming predominant bacterial killing within the biofilm (Fig. 3H). Taken together, TNVs-DMVs are a potent sonodynamic agent effective against both planktonic (susceptible and resistant) H. pylori and their biofilms.

Antibacterial mechanism of TNVs-DMVs

Given that bacterial membrane vesicles can be internalized by homologous bacteria [34], we propose that the enhanced sonodynamic antibacterial efficacy of TNVs-DMVs compared to TNVs stems from increased cellular uptake facilitated by their fusion with DMVs (Fig. 4A). Both fluorescence imaging and flow cytometry confirmed higher internalization of TNVs-DMVs compared to TNVs in H. pylori (Fig. 4B and C). While DiO-stained TNVs were detected within bacteria, significantly greater quantities of TNVs-DMVs were observed, likely mediated by homologous membrane fusion. This enhanced uptake of TNVs-DMVs leads to higher intracellular concentrations of the sonosensitizer curcumin. Upon ultrasound irradiation, this leads to the generation of elevated levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS). Fluorescence imaging using the intracellular ROS indicator DCFH-DA revealed pronounced ROS production in H. pylori treated with either TNVs or TNVs-DMVs combined with ultrasound (Fig. 4D). In particular, the DCFH-DA fluorescence signal was significantly stronger for TNVs-DMVs than for TNVs, indicating higher intracellular ROS levels. Flow cytometry further quantified this difference, confirming that ROS levels in TNVs-DMVs-treated samples were approximately twice those observed with TNVs (Fig. 4E). The bactericidal ROS generated within the cytosol or near the cell membrane damaged the bacterial cell wall, resulting in evident deformation and destruction visualized by SEM after TNVs-DMVs plus ultrasound treatment (Fig. 4F). The quantified PI uptake (Fig. S8) correlated with SEM-observed bacterial deformation, confirming that ROS generated during sonodynamic treatment directly induce severe membrane disintegration. In contrast, even when TNVs-DMVs were present on the bacterial surface, the cellular structure of H. pylori remained intact without ultrasound application. Collectively, these results demonstrate that TNVs-DMVs eradicate H. pylori by first invading the bacteria via homologous membrane fusion and subsequently inducing lethal damage to the cell wall/membrane through ultrasound-triggered ROS generation.

Regarding superior anti-biofilm activity of TNVs-DMVs over TNVs alone, it likely stems from the enhanced biofilm infiltration and subsequent sonodynamic antibacterial activity. Natural H. pylori membrane vesicles are intrinsic components of biofilms and distribute throughout the biofilm matrix via diffusion and bacterial secretion, as documented in prior studies [[43], [44], [45]]. Given that TNVs-DMVs hybrids retain the core structural properties of native vesicles (e.g., membrane composition, surface charge), they likely exploit similar pathways to achieve uniform penetration within biofilms. Subsequently, the homologous membrane fusion (Fig. 4B and C) and ROS induced cell wall/membrane damage (Fig. 4D–F) collectively lead to biofilm removal and eradication of biofilm-embedded bacteria.

In vivo biodistribution and therapeutic efficacy of TNVs-DMVs

A BALB/c mouse model of H. pylori infection was established to evaluate TNVs-DMVs biodistribution and efficacy (Fig. 5A). CLSM imaging of stomach tissues harvested after oral gavage of DiO-labeled TNVs or TNVs-DMVs revealed significantly stronger fluorescence signals on gastric epithelial surfaces from TNVs-DMVs compared to TNVs (Fig. 5B). The enhanced accumulation of TNVs-DMVs was attributable to their enhanced mucus-penetrating capability (Fig. 3D and S5). This accumulation facilitates targeted interaction with epithelium-colonized H. pylori.

Biodistribution of TNVs-DMVs in the H. pylori-infected mouse was further evaluated using 1,1-dioctadecyl-3,3,3,3-tetramethylindotricarbocyanine iodide (DiR)-labeled vesicles. Fluorescence signals for both TNVs and TNVs-DMVs were confined primarily to the gastrointestinal tract, mainly in the stomach, while no significant fluorescence was found in other major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) up to 24 h (Fig. 5C and Fig. S9). This minimal systemic distribution likely reduces off-target effects. While both formulations exhibited peak stomach fluorescence at 4 h post-administration (Fig. 5D), TNVs signals diminished during gastric emptying, whereas TNVs-DMVs maintained sustained levels from 4 h to 12 h (significantly stronger than TNVs at 12 h). This prolonged retention of TNVs-DMVs in the H. pylori-infected mouse stomach was probably attributed to their homologous targeting function towards bacteria colonized in the stomach.

The synergistic effects of mucus penetration and gastric retention enabled potent therapeutic efficacy. Gram-stained sections showed TNVs-DMVs + ultrasound eliminated bacteria with efficacy comparable to standard triple therapy OAC (omeprazole, amoxicillin and clarithromycin), while substantial bacterial colonization persisted in other groups (Fig. 5E and Fig. S10). Bacterial quantification confirmed TNVs-DMVs + ultrasound reduced H. pylori burden equivalently to OAC and significantly surpassed TNVs + ultrasound (Fig. 5F), demonstrating the essential role of DMV fusion. Due to the antibacterial effects, the inflammation caused by the infections was also alleviated, as evidenced by the reduction of inflammatory cytokines IL-6 and TNF-α (Fig. 5G and H). TNVs-DMVs + ultrasound and OAC groups displayed the lowest cytokine levels among other treatment groups. Notably, TNF-α suppression was superior to OAC in the TNVs-DMVs + ultrasound group. Importantly, histological analysis further showed that gastric mucosal morphology in the TNVs-DMVs + US group closely resembled that of uninfected mice, whereas the OAC group still exhibited inflammatory cell infiltration and epithelial disruption (Fig. S11). The results suggest that TNVs-DMVs + US can effectively treat H. pylori infections without damaging stomach tissues.

Influence of TNVs-DMVs therapy on the homeostasis of intestinal flora and in vivo biocompatibility of TNVs-DMVs

TNVs-DMVs-mediated sonodynamic therapy demonstrated enhanced biocompatibility relative to standard triple antibiotic therapy OAC, particularly regarding intestinal flora preservation. Microbiota analysis revealed no significant differences in community richness (Chao1 index, Fig. 6A) or diversity metrics (Shannon, Simpson, and PD whole-tree) (Fig. 6B–D) between TNVs-DMVs-treated and uninfected mice. In contrast, OAC treatment significantly reduced the Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson indices, reflecting collateral damage to commensal gut bacteria. The localized antibacterial activity of TNVs-DMVs, which were activated only by ultrasound, prevented intestinal microbiota disruption. Notably, microbiota analysis showed no significant reductions in diversity indices in the TNVs-DMVs + US group compared with untreated controls, suggesting that TNVs-DMVs combined with ultrasound exposure under the present therapeutic conditions did not induce detectable perturbations in gut microbial composition. Consistently, beta diversity analysis based on NMDS showed that the OAC group was clearly separated from the other groups, suggesting pronounced disruption of microbial community structure after antibiotic treatment (Fig. S12). In contrast, TNVs-DMVs + US-treated group partially overlapped with the infected group, suggesting moderate modulation of microbial community structure without drastic disruption. These results further support that TNVs-DMVs-mediated sonodynamic therapy exerts antibacterial effects while preserving intestinal microbiota homeostasis.

Recent vesicle-based antibacterial strategies have leveraged bacterial membrane vesicles (OMVs) or plant-derived vesicles primarily as carriers for conventional antibiotics [46,47], including gentamicin, ciprofloxacin, clarithromycin, and β-lactams, to enhance therapeutic efficacy [[48], [49], [50]]. While these systems improve drug bioavailability and targeting, their efficacy inherently depends on antibiotic action, which poses risks for microbiota dysbiosis and resistance development, especially in H. pylori infection requiring repeated dosing [51,52]. In contrast, the TNVs–DMVs hybrid system combines homologous targeting (DMVs) and ultrasound-triggered ROS generation (TNVs) for self-contained bactericidal activity, eliminating antibiotic use, circumventing resistance, preserving intestinal microbial diversity.

Taxonomic profiling showed TNVs-DMVs maintained a greater microbial diversity across seven classification levels compared than other groups (Fig. 6E), suggesting a potentially beneficial role in microbiota richness. This effect may be attributed to curcumin within the hybrid membrane vesicles [53]. Critically, further analysis at the family (Fig. 6F) and genus level (Fig. 6G) revealed a significant rise in the relative abundance of Enterobacteriaceae in the OAC group, known for its association with enteritis and zoonotic infections [54]. Additionally, there was an increase in the relative abundance of Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1, which positively correlated with inflammatory cytokines [55]. Conversely, TNVs-DMVs + ultrasound enriched beneficial genera like anti-inflammatory Unclassified_Lachnospiraceae (Fig. 6J) and intestinal mucosal repair-associated Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136 (Fig. 6K) [53,56]. Notably, this regulatory effect is likely attributable to the well-documented prebiotic properties of curcumin. Curcumin has been shown to reconstruct gut microbiota composition, increasing beneficial taxa including members of the Lachnospiraceae family and associated short-chain fatty acid producers, accompanied by improvements in intestinal barrier integrity and inflammation markers [[57], [58], [59], [60]]. Furthermore, curcumin's modulation of gut microbiota metabolism and host-microbe interactions may further reinforce a beneficial ecological niche for probiotics [[61], [62], [63]]. Overall, sonodynamic therapy with TNVs-DMVs avoided the dysbiosis induced by triple antibiotics, enhanced probiotic representation, and supported recovery from microbiota disturbances.

In addition to microbiome analysis, blood biochemistry and histological analysis confirmed systemic safety. Following therapeutic TNVs-DMVs dosing with ultrasound, hepatic markers (alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST)) and renal markers (blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and creatinine (CREA)) remained within normal ranges (Fig. S13A), indicating absence of hepatotoxicity or nephrotoxicity. Histological examination of major organs including gastric tissue showed no abnormalities versus untreated controls (Fig. S13B), further validating the biocompatibility of sonodynamic therapy using TNVs-DMVs.

Conclusion

TNVs-DMVs, hybrid membrane vesicles that integrate turmeric-derived nanovesicles and H. pylori-derived double membrane vesicles, were engineered for sonodynamic therapy against H. pylori infections. These vesicles generate ROS under ultrasound conditions, demonstrating superior bactericidal efficacy against both drug-susceptible and resistant H. pylori across varying gastric pH compared to turmeric nanovesicles alone. Bacterial membrane-derived targeting and enhanced mucus penetration promoted significant retention at infection sites. Combined with potent antibacterial activity and site-specific accumulation, TNVs-DMVs achieved therapeutic efficacy comparable to standard triple antibiotic therapy in an H. pylori-infected murine model. Critically, unlike antibiotics, TNVs-DMVs preserved intestinal flora abundance and biocompatibility. This bioinspired sonodynamic platform represents a paradigm shift toward ecology-preserving antimicrobial interventions by simultaneously eradicating drug-resistant H. pylori and preserving gut ecological balance. Its scalable production and murine biosafety profile support imminent clinical translation for infections treatment.

Preparation of TNVs

Turmeric was peeled, weighed, and mixed with PBS (1 mL/g). The mixture was juiced and filtered through sterile gauze to remove crude plant fibers. The homogenized extract underwent sequential centrifugation: first at low speed (3000 g, 4 °C, 30 min), then at high speed (10000 g, 4 °C, 60 min). The supernatant was carefully collected and subjected to ultracentrifugation (100000 g, 4 °C, 60 min). The resulting pellets, containing exosome-like vesicles derived from turmeric plants (TNVs), were collected, re-suspended in PBS, and stored at −80 °C.

Bacteria culture and preparation of outer membrane vesicles (OMVs)

H. pylori ATCC 43504 was cryopreserved in BHI liquid medium containing 10% FBS and 50% of glycerol at −80 °C. H. pylori were cultured on a Columbia agar plate containing 5% sterile defibrinated sheep blood. The plates were incubated at 37 °C under microaerobic conditions (5% O2, 10% CO2, 85% N2, and appropriate humidity) for 36-72 h. Colonies were transferred to a 10 mL of BHI broth supplemented with 10% FBS and incubated under microaerobic condition for 72 h (120 rpm, 37 °C). The obtained suspension was added to fresh 200 mL BHI with 10% FBS and incubated for an additional 72 h (120 rpm, 37 °C). Subsequently, the suspension was centrifuged to remove the H. pylori (6500 g, 4 °C, 10 min). The supernatant was then subjected to ultracentrifugation (100000 g, 4 °C, 90 min) to collect the pellets (OMVs). OMVs were resuspended in PBS and preserved at −80 °C.

Preparation of DMVs

Harvested H. pylori cells were washed with PBS and adjusted to an OD600 value of 0.1. The bacterial suspension (10 - 15 mL) was placed in a low-temperature ultrahigh-pressure continuous flow cell disruptor (1300 bar, 4 °C), with rapid pressure release. The process was repeated three times for complete cell disruption. The lysate was centrifuged (6500 g, 4 °C, 30 min) to collect the supernatant. The resulting supernatant was centrifuged (100000 g, 4 °C, 60 min) to obtain DMVs. The DMVs protein concentration was determined via a BCA assay.

Preparation and characterization of TNVs-DMVs

TNVs and DMVs were dispersed in PBS at a protein ratio of 1:1. The dispersion was subjected to probe ultrasonication (50 W, 2s on/3s off, 10 min) in an ice bath. Subsequently, ultracentrifugation was performed (120000 g, 4 °C, 60 min) to collect TNVs-DMVs. To confirm membrane fusion, Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET) was employed. DiI dye (λex = 551 nm, λem = 569 nm) and DiO dye (λex = 483 nm, λem = 501 nm) were selected as the donor-acceptor pair for FRET. TNVs were labeled with DiI and DiO, then mixed with DMVs at protein ratios of 1:1, 1:2, and 1:3 to prepare hybrid vesicles. After excess dye was removed excess dye by washing with PBS, fluorescence intensity was measured using a spectrofluorometer (excitation: 488 nm; emission scan: 500-650 nm). An increase in DiI emission at 569 nm and a decrease in DiO emission at 501 nm, upon excitation of DiO at 488 nm, indicated FRET and thus membrane fusion. Fluorescence colocalization was used to visualize membrane fusion. TNVs were labeled with DiI, while OMVs or DMVs were labeled with DiO. The samples were then washed with PBS to remove excess dye. The hybrid membrane vesicles were generated by a probe ultrasonication as aforementioned, and two types of vesicles were physically mixed as a control group. TNVs-DMVs suspension (10 μL, 1 mg/mL) was placed on a glass slide, followed by image acquisition on an Olympus FV3000 confocal laser microscope equipped with a 60× oil-immersion objective.

Detection of ROS

To assess the sonodynamic therapy (SDT) activity of TNVs and TNVs-DMVs, the presence of reactive oxygen species (ROS) was detected using DPBF. DPBF (10 μM) was added to TNVs or TNVs-DMVs suspended in PBS (200 μg/mL, 1 mL). Subsequently, the mixtures were subjected to ultrasonication (1.0 MHz, 50% duty cycle, 1.5 W/cm2) for 0-10 min. The ultrasound parameter was selected based on previous antibacterial SDT studies, which demonstrated efficient activation of sonosensitizers and ROS generation under low-frequency and low-intensity ultrasound conditions [[11], [12], [13],64]. The absorbance (350-550 nm) was measured using a UV-vis spectrophotometer. DPBF degradation (decrease in absorbance at 418 nm) indicates ROS generation.

To quantify 1O2 generation, Singlet Oxygen Sensor Green (SOSG) reagent was utilized. In brief, SOSG reagent (10 μM) was added to the suspension of TNVs or TNVs-DMVs (200 μg/mL, 1 mL). PBS was used as a control. Then, the mixture was subjected to ultrasound treatment (1.0 MHz, 50% duty cycle, 1.5 W/cm2) for 0-10 min. Fluorescence intensity (λex = 488 nm, λem = 528 nm) was measured using a spectrofluorometer. Increased SOSG fluorescence indicates singlet oxygen (1O2) generation.

In vitro antibacterial tests

Three strains of H. pylori (ATCC 43504, 26695 and LQ2#) were used to assess the antibacterial activity of TNVs-DMVs using the plate count method. TNVs-OMVs and TNVs-DMVs (protein ratio 1:1) were prepared. Harvested H. pylori (OD600 = 0.1 in PBS or SGF pH 2.2 + 10 mM urea) were incubated with TNVs, TNVs-OMVs, or TNVs-DMVs (200 μg/mL) at 37 °C with shaking (120 rpm) for 10 min. The acidic condition (pH 2.2) was used to simulate the highly acidic gastric environment [1,12,65,66]. Mixtures were then exposed to ultrasound (1.0 MHz, 50% duty cycle, 1.5 W/cm2, 4 min). Treated suspensions were serially diluted, plated on Columbia blood agar plates, incubated microaerobically for 72 h, and colonies were counted.

TNVs or TNVs-DMVs (200 μg/mL) were incubated with H. pylori (OD600 = 0.1) at 37 °C for 10 min. Following ultrasound treatment (1.0 MHz, 50% duty cycle, 1.5 W/cm2, 4 min), bacteria were stained with SYTO 9 (1.67 μM) and propidium iodide (PI, 20 μM) in the dark for 20 min. Fluorescence images were acquired using CLSM (SYTO9: λex/em = 488/501 nm; PI: λex/em = 535/617 nm). The mean fluorescence intensity of PI was quantified using ImageJ.

In vitro membrane vesicle penetration through simulated gastric mucus

A Transwell system was employed to simulate gastric mucus penetration. Simulated gastric mucus (2.5% hyaluronic acid, 0.5% mucin in PBS) was placed in the upper chamber. DiO-labeled TNVs or TNVs-DMVs (500 μg/mL) were added to the upper chamber. PBS (500 μL) was added to the lower chamber. The setup was incubated on a shaker (90 rpm, 37 °C) for 4 h. Fluorescence intensity in the lower chamber was quantified using a fluorometer. The penetration speed of TNVs-DMVs through simulated gastric mucus was visualized in glass bottles. Coomassie Brilliant Blue-stained TNVs or TNVs-DMVs (500 μg/mL) were layered onto the surface of the mucus gel. Penetration depth was monitored and recorded over time.

Anti-biofilm activity

H. pylori suspension (OD600 = 0.1 in BHI + 10% FBS) was aliquoted (200 μL/well) into a 96-well plate. Plates were incubated for 96 h to establish mature biofilms. Biofilms were incubated with TNVs or TNVs-DMVs (200 μg/mL) for 10 min at 37 °C. Samples were exposed to ultrasound (1.0 MHz, 50% duty cycle, 1.5 W/cm2, 4 min). Wells were gently washed twice with sterile PBS (pH 7.4). Biofilm biomass was stained with 0.02% (w/v) crystal violet (200 μL/well) for 30 min. Wells were washed three times with sterile PBS to remove unbound stain. Bound crystal violet was solubilized by adding 200 μL of 95% ethanol per well. Absorbance of the solubilized crystal violet solution was measured at 570 nm.

Uptake of hybrid membrane vesicles by bacterial cells

TNVs and TNVs-DMVs were separately incubated with DiO solution (10 μM) for 30 min. Afterward, the excess dye was removed by washing the samples with PBS. The DiO-labeled TNVs and DiO-labeled TNVs-DMVs (50 μg/mL) were then cultured with H. pylori (∼107 CFU/mL) for 3 h, followed by centrifugation (4500 g, 10 min) to remove the excess membrane vesicles. Subsequently, the bacteria were labeled with Hoechst 33342 nucleic acid stain (1 μg/mL). To visualize the samples, 10 μL of the stained samples were placed on a glass slide and covered with coverslips. Fluorescence images were acquired using an Olympus FV3000 confocal laser microscope (Hoechst 33342: λex = 364 nm, DiO: λex = 488 nm). Additionally, flow cytometry analysis was performed to investigate the uptake of various nanovesicles by H. pylori. FlowJo_V10 was used to quantify and normalize the mean fluorescence intensities.

Detection of ROS in bacterial cells

After collecting the planktonic H. pylori from Columbia blood plates, bacteria were dispersed in PBS (∼107 CFU/mL). Subsequently, the suspension was incubated with TNVs or TNVs-DMVs for 10 min at 37 °C, followed by ultrasonication (1.0 MHz, 50% duty cycle, 1.5 W/cm2, 4 min). Meanwhile, the bacteria without membrane vesicles were used as a control. The sample was centrifuged to remove the supernatant (4500 g, 10 min). The residue was re-suspended in 1 mL of PBS and incubated with the carboxy-2′,7′-dichloro-dihydro-fluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) probe (10 μM) and Hoechst 33342 nucleic acid stain (1 μg/mL) for 30 min. Subsequently, free dye was removed by centrifugation (4500 g, 10 min). Then 10 μL of each sample was used for CLSM observation through an Olympus FV3000 confocal laser microscope (Hoechst 33342: λex = 364 nm, DCFH-DA: λex = 485 nm). Additionally, flow cytometry analysis was performed to assess the uptake of various nanovesicles by H. pylori. FlowJo_V10 was used to quantify and normalize the mean fluorescence intensity.

Bacteria morphology characterized by SEM

H. pylori were dispersed in PBS (∼107 CFU/mL), and incubated with TNVs or TNVs-DMVs for 10 min at 37 °C, followed by ultrasonication (1.0 MHz, 50% duty cycle, 1.5 W/cm2, 4 min). The bacteria were centrifuged to remove the supernatant (4500 g, 10 min). The resulting bacteria were then treated with a 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution and incubated for 12 h at 4 °C. The obtained bacterial suspension was then centrifuged (4500 g, 10 min) and dehydrated sequentially using 50%, 70%, 90%, and 100% concentrations of ethanol, each for 10 min. Finally, the samples were dried in a vacuum and coated with a gilded film using a sputter-coating technique. The morphology of H. pylori was observed under a JSM-6330F SEM.

In vivo antibacterial experiments

All in vivo experiments were approved by the Guangzhou Institutes of Biomedicine and Health (GIBH), Chinese Academy of Science (Approval number: N2022135). Female BALB/c mice (6–8 weeks old) were infected by oral gavage with H. pylori (1 × 108 CFU/mL, 200 μL) once daily for 4 consecutive days (days 4 - 7). Infection was allowed to establish for 2 weeks. Infected mice were randomly divided into 7 groups (PBS, US, TNVs, TNVs + US, TNVs-DMVs, TNVs-DMVs + US, and triple therapy OAC) (n = 5/group). Mice were treated with PBS, TNVs or TNVs-DMVs (12.5 mg/kg) once daily for 3 days (days 21 - 23). The OAC group received omeprazole (400 μmol/kg) 30 min prior to antibiotics (amoxicillin 28.5 mg/kg + clarithromycin 14.3 mg/kg). For US groups, the stomach was externally exposed to ultrasound (1.0 MHz, 50% duty cycle, 1.5 W/cm2, 6 min) immediately after gavage (ultrasound coupling gel applied). Mice were sacrificed 24 h after the final treatment (day 24). Stomachs were excised. Stomachs were homogenized in PBS. Homogenates were serially diluted in PBS and plated onto selective Columbia blood agar (containing 10 μg/mL vancomycin, 5 μg/mL cefsulodin, 5 μg/mL trimethoprim, 5 μg/mL amphotericin B). Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 4 days. CFU were counted and normalized per gram of stomach tissue. Gastric tissue sections were Gram-stained to visualize bacteria. H&E staining was used to assess gastric inflammation and injury. The specific scoring criteria were based on previously reported methods [67,68]. Stomach homogenates were centrifuged (35,000 g, 4 °C, 10 min). IL-6 and TNF-α levels in the supernatant were quantified using commercial ELISA kits according to the manufacturer's instructions.

In vivo hybridized membrane vesicles penetration through gastric mucus

Female BALB/c mice were orally administered 200 μL of H. pylori suspension (1 × 108 CFU/mL) once daily for 4 consecutive days. Two weeks post-infection, mice were orally gavaged with DiO-labeled TNVs or TNVs-DMVs (1.5 mg/kg). After 4 h, the stomachs were dissected and embedded. Tissue sections were prepared and stained with Hoechst 33342 for 10 min, followed by imaging using CLSM.

In vivo biodistribution of TNVs-DMVs

The H. pylori gastric infection model was established as described in previous section. Two weeks post-infection, DiR-labeled TNVs or DiR-labeled TNVs-DMVs (12.5 mg/kg) were administered via oral gavage. At 2, 4, 8, 12, and 24 h post-administration (n = 3 per time point), major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, stomach) were harvested. Ex vivo fluorescence imaging of organs was performed using an IVIS Lumina III system (λex/em = 754/778 nm). The relative radiant efficiency of TNVs and TNVs-DMVs within the stomach was calculated by normalizing the fluorescence intensity at each time point to the intensity measured at 2 h.

16S rRNA gene sequencing analysis of gut microbiota

Fecal pellets were collected from mice across treatment groups (Uninfected, PBS control, TNVs-DMVs + US, Triple Therapy OAC). Three representative pellets per group were randomly selected for microbiome analysis. Genomic DNA was extracted from feces using a commercial kit (e.g., QIAamp PowerFecal Pro DNA Kit, QIAGEN). DNA concentration and purity were assessed using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). DNA integrity was confirmed by agarose gel electrophoresis (1% w/v). The V3–V4 regions of the 16S rRNA genes were amplified, and the quality was verified; the amplicons were then purified and subjected to a second round of amplification. Sequencing of the V3–V4 gene amplicons was obtained using the Illumina NovaSeq platform. The abundance, diversity, and structure of gut microbiota in the feces were analyzed.

In vivo biocompatibility assessment of TNVs-DMVs

Mice received daily oral gavage of PBS (control) or TNVs-DMVs (12.5 mg/kg) for 3 consecutive days. For the TNVs-DMVs + US group, immediately after each gavage, the stomach region was externally sonicated (1.0 MHz, 50% duty cycle, 1.5 W/cm2) for 6 min total (2 min/each, 3 times). On day 4, blood was collected. Samples were kept at room temperature for 2 h, then centrifuged (3500 g, 4 °C, 10 min) to obtain serum. Standard blood biochemical parameters (e.g., ALT, AST, BUN, Creatinine) were analyzed using an automated clinical chemistry analyzer. The major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, and stomach) were collected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and histological observation.

Statistical analysis

The data were obtained through at least three independent biological replicates, and expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical comparisons between multiple groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (One-way ANOVA). Post-hoc testing for multiple comparisons was conducted using Tukey's test. Significance levels were defined as follows: ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001; “ns” represents non-significance. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (Version 9.0).
