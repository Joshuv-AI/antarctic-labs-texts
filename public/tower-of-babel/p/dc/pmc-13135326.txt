Investigation of the effect of using cavity disinfectant on microleakage in composite restorations by micro-CT analysis
Authors: Burcu Kuş, Numan Aydın, Serpil Karaoğlanoğlu, Bilge Ersöz, Elif Aybala Oktay, Mert Ocak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe aim of this study is to evaluate the effect of the use of cavity disinfectant on the amount of microleakage seen in composite restorations.Materials and MethodsForty caries-free permanent molars extracted due to periodontal or orthodontic problems were used in the study. Class II box cavities were prepared in the mesial and distal of teeth. Then, the teeth were divided into subgroups (n:10) according to the use of universal adhesive and cavity disinfectant. Chlorhexidine digluconate (2%) was used as a cavity disinfectant. In the prepared cavities, universal adhesives were used with total and self-etch modes and restored with posterior composite resin. After the restoration of the teeth was completed, 1000 thermal cycles were applied. Microleakage analysis was performed with a micro-computed tomography device after the teeth were soaked in silver nitrate solution for 24 hours. Two-way analysis of variance test was used in the analysis of microleakage data and Tukey test was used to show the differences between groups in multiple comparisons (p<0.05).ResultsApplication of universal adhesives to Class II cavities in self-etch mode caused more microleakage than total-etch mode (p<0.05). There was no statistically significant difference between the microleakage values of the universal adhesive (p>0.05). When universal adhesives are used with cavity disinfectants decreased the microleakage value in the self-etch mode (p<0.05), but it did not show a statistically significant decrease in the total etch mode.ConclusionWhile the use of cavity disinfectant in teeth restored with composite resin reduces the amount of microleakage in the self-etch mode, the total etch mode does not affect the amount of microleakage.

Introduction

The adaptation of the material to the cavity walls and the integrity of the adhesive bond between the composite resin and dental tissues are important for the long-term clinical success of composite restorations (1). The smear layer created during cavity preparation must be modified or removed using adhesive systems to achieve adequate bonding. In the total-etch mode, the bonding mechanism relies on micromechanical retention and the formation of a hybrid layer. In addition to micromechanical adhesion, the diffusion and infiltration of resin into the demineralized collagen fibrils contribute to effective bonding to dentin. In the self-etch mode, the smear layer is partially dissolved and acidic monomers penetrate the dentin to form a hybrid layer, which constitutes the bonding mechanism (2, 3).

Adequate marginal integrity and the durability of the restoration depend on controlling the polymerization shrinkage stress of composite resins (4). The well-known clinical consequences of polymerization shrinkage include cuspal deflection, debonding, enamel microcracks, postoperative sensitivity, secondary caries, and microleakage (5).

Microleakage, defined as the penetration of oral fluids and bacteria between the cavity wall and the restoration, has been associated with marginal discoloration, restoration fracture, postoperative sensitivity, secondary caries, and pulpal pathology (6). In addition, microorganisms remaining in the cavity after preparation may lead to secondary caries (5). Studies have shown that bacteria left in the prepared cavity can survive for extended periods (7, 8). Therefore, disinfection of the prepared cavity prior to restoration has been recommended (9).

Chlorhexidine, benzalkonium chloride, sodium hypochlorite (5.25%), hydrogen peroxide (3%), iodine, potassium iodide, and copper sulfate have been used as cavity disinfectants. Chlorhexidine gluconate (CHX) is a broad-spectrum antimicrobial agent widely used for disinfection. It has been shown to disinfect dentinal tubules and adsorb onto dentin surfaces. Furthermore, chlorhexidine may enhance bonding to dentin by inhibiting dentin matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) activation, even at low concentrations. CHX is available in various forms, including solutions, gels, and sprays (10, 11, 12).

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM), light microscopy, energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy, and micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) are commonly used analytical methods in in vivo and in vitro microleakage studies (13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18). Recently, the micro-CT method has been proposed for the evaluation of marginal leakage in adhesive restorations. One advantage of micro-CT is that it enables non-destructive, continuous evaluation of the tooth–restoration interface and allows three-dimensional reconstruction (13, 14, 15, 16, 17). Micro-CT analysis has been shown to be a reliable alternative to conventional sectioning methods for assessing marginal leakage (18, 19).

A tight bond between restorative materials and dental hard tissues positively influences the clinical success of restorations. Although many studies have investigated the effect of CHX on microleakage in Class II composite restorations, this effect has not been extensively evaluated using the micro-CT method. Josic et al. (9) concluded that, despite promising in vitro findings, there is currently insufficient evidence to support the use of CHX to improve the prognosis of adhesively bonded composite restorations. Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate, in vitro, the effect of cavity disinfectant use on microleakage in composite restorations using micro-CT analysis. The null hypothesis of the study was that the amount of microleakage occurring in Class II cavities would not be affected by the application of CHX prior to the use of a universal adhesive in either total-etch or self-etch modes.

Materials and Methods

Ethical statement and storage conditions

This study was initiated with the approval of the ethics committee (Project No:2021/353). In the study, 40 human permanent molar teeth that had no caries or restoration were extracted for orthodontic or periodontal reasons were used. After the calculus and soft tissues in the extracted teeth were cleaned with an ultrasonic scaler, they were stored in a 0.1% thymol solution at +4⁰C for a maximum of 3 months until the work was started.

Sample size estimation

The sample size in the study was calculated using the G Power 3.1 analysis program (α = 0.05, 1−β = 0.80 and f = 0.4).

Preparation of samples

A total of 80 class II box cavities were prepared for the teeth planned for the study, 10 in each group (n=10). Class II box cavities were standardized by preparing the mesial and distal surfaces of each tooth, as Garcia et al. (20) did, with a buccolingual width of 4 mm, the distance of the axial wall from the mesial and distal tooth surfaces to be 2 mm, and the cavity floor to be 1 mm above the cemento-enamel border. Each of the five cavities was made using a new cylindrical diamond fissure bur (Diamond FG Burs, Adia, Turkey) that rotated at a high speed while being cooled by water.

To recreate the missing proximal walls, matrix bands (Adap SuperCap Matrix, Kerr, Italy) were applied to the samples once the preparations were finished. Clearfil Tri-S Bond (Kuraray Noritake, Niigata, Japan), Bond Force II (Tokuyama Dental, Tokyo, Japan) as universal adhesive, and Cavity Cleanser (Bisco, Schaumburg, IL, ABD) containing 2% chlorhexidine digluconate as cavity disinfectant were used to complete restorations in compliance with the manufacturer's instructions. Groups with and without cavity disinfectant were created in both the self-etch and total-etch modes of Clearfil Tri-S Bond and Bond Force II. Before the adhesive in the self-etch mode and the glue in the entire etch mode following acid, Cavity Cleanser was applied to the cavity for 60 seconds. (Table 1)

Clearfil Majesty Posterior (A2, Kuraray, Japan), a nanohybrid composite, was used as the restorative material. Each composite layer was applied with the incremental technique to be 2 mm thick and polymerized by irradiation for 20 seconds with the help of an LED light device (Woodpecker Led-E Plus) with a light power of 1000 mW/cm². After using a polishing kit (OptiDisc, Kerr, Switzerland) to complete and polish each restoration, they were left in distilled water for a full day to allow for post-polymerization.

The samples were applied 1000 cycles using a standardized thermal cycle device (Thermocycler the 1100, SD Mechatronik, Germany) with a bath temperature of 5-55°C (+/- 2°C), a 10-second transfer period and a 30-second pause between cycles.

Evaluation of microleakage with micro-CT

Following the thermal cycle procedure, boxing wax was applied to the specimens' apex, and two layers of nail polish (Flormar, Turkey) were applied to the portions beyond the 1 mm radius surrounding the restorations to stop silver nitrate (AgNO3) from passing through. After the preparation of the samples was completed, the samples were kept in 50 wt% AgNO3 (Merckb 7761-88-8 Silver Nitrate, ACS, ISO, Reag. Ph Eur) in the dark at room temperature for 24 hours. To reduce the silver or diamine silver ions to metallic silver particles, the silver-impregnated teeth were placed in the first bath solution (Dental X-Ray Developer, Medley, MDC, Turkey) under fluorescent light for eight hours before being thoroughly rinsed with distilled water.

The samples were then scanned using a SkyScan 1275 micro-CT system (SkyScan, Kontich, Belgium) to calculate the AgNO₃ penetration volume. Each sample was securely fixed to the original scanning table using radiolucent orthodontic wax. During scanning, a full 360° rotation was performed with a rotation step of 0.2°, and each scan lasted approximately 1 hour. This protocol was preferred to ensure stable imaging and to minimize potential motion artifacts.

The region of interest was first selected using CTAn micro-CT analysis software. After determining the regions to be analyzed in three dimensions, each sample was scanned for approximately 40 minutes, and about 500–600 images were obtained per sample. The images were reconstructed using NRecon reconstruction software (SkyScan, Antwerp, Belgium). Because AgNO₃ penetration exhibits high radiopacity, artifact formation is inevitable. However, these artifacts were largely minimized using the original NRecon software. In addition, correction parameters—including 40% beam-hardening correction, 1% smoothing, and 6% ring-artifact reduction—were applied to further reduce artifacts during reconstruction.

The reconstructed axial images were then imported into DataViewer software (SkyScan, Antwerp, Belgium), and three-dimensional images were generated to visualize the penetration depth of each restoration in axial, coronal, and sagittal planes. To calculate the AgNO₃ penetration volume at the gingival floor of the restorations, a volume of interest (VOI) encompassing the entire relevant region was selected for each slice. Grayscale threshold values were defined to differentiate dentin, restorative material, and penetrated AgNO₃. Subsequently, the silver penetration volume was calculated automatically using 3D analysis. For the evaluation of AgNO₃ penetration, grayscale values of 235 and above were accepted as representing AgNO₃.

Statistical analysis

The results of this investigation were statistically assessed using the SPSS 22 software program (SPSS 22 for Windows/SPSS Inc., Armonk, NY, USA). The data was statistically analyzed using two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and the Tukey test was employed to display group differences in multiple comparisons (p<0.05).

Results

In the present study, the effect of chlorhexidine (CHX) use in composite restorations on microleakage was evaluated using the micro-CT method, and the mean AgNO₃ penetration volumes were compared among all groups. The microleakage values, calculated by measuring the volume of AgNO₃ penetrating the tooth–restoration interface in a total of 80 prepared cavities, are presented in Figure 1.

Application of universal adhesives in Class II cavities in the self-etch mode (Clearfil Tri-S Bond: 0.333 ± 0.12; Bond Force II: 0.389 ± 0.17) resulted in significantly higher microleakage than in the total-etch mode (Clearfil Tri-S Bond: 0.059 ± 0.03; Bond Force II: 0.042 ± 0.03) (p < 0.001). The combined application of a cavity disinfectant and a universal adhesive led to a decrease in microleakage values in the self-etch mode (Clearfil Tri-S Bond: 0.176 ± 0.09; Bond Force II: 0.184 ± 0.09) (p < 0.001). However, no statistically significant reduction was observed in the total-etch mode (Clearfil Tri-S Bond: 0.032 ± 0.02; Bond Force II: 0.010 ± 0.02) (Table 2).

According to the two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) of the microleakage data, there was no statistically significant difference between the universal adhesive systems (Clearfil Tri-S Bond vs. Bond Force II) (p = 0.754). However, when analyzed according to the application mode (self-etch, total-etch, self-etch + CHX, and total-etch + CHX), the differences were statistically significant (p < 0.001) (Table 3). Discussion

In recent years, improvements in adhesive systems have strengthened the bond between tooth structure and restorative materials and have contributed to the reduction of microleakage (21). When studies evaluating the effectiveness of adhesive systems are reviewed, it has been reported that the total-etch approach should be preferred when bonding to enamel is required (22, 23).

When enamel and dentin bonding were evaluated in total-etch and self-etch modes, acid application significantly increased bond strength to enamel, whereas dentin etching increased bond strength but not to a statistically significant degree (24). Chen et al. (25) reported that when universal adhesives were applied in both self-etch and total-etch modes, microtensile bond strength to dentin did not differ between the modes, although the hybrid layer was thicker in the total-etch mode. In the present study, when Clearfil Tri-S Bond and Bond Force II adhesive systems were applied in total-etch mode, they exhibited lower microleakage values than in the self-etch mode. This may be explained by the stronger micromechanical bonding achieved in total-etch mode, which limited the penetration of AgNO₃ beyond the enamel and reduced its diffusion toward dentin.

Adhesive systems are classified according to their pH as strongly acidic (pH ≤ 1), moderately acidic (pH ≈ 1.5), mildly acidic (pH ≥ 2), and ultra-mild (pH ≈ 2.5). Differences in microleakage observed with self-etch adhesives are thought to be related to the application protocol, pH level, type of monomer and solvent, and sensitivity to dentin moisture (26).

The universal adhesives used in this study were evaluated according to their acidity: Clearfil Tri-S Bond (pH = 2.3) is a mild self-etch adhesive, whereas Bond Force II (pH = 2.8) is an ultra-mild self-etch adhesive. This difference may explain the slightly lower microleakage values observed with Clearfil Tri-S Bond in self-etch mode, although the difference was not statistically significant.

Chlorhexidine is widely used in clinical practice because it is a broad-spectrum antimicrobial agent with low toxicity and a high affinity for negatively charged surfaces such as cell walls and extracellular polysaccharides (27). Chlorhexidine may also improve dentin bonding by inhibiting dentin matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) activation, even at low concentrations; however, its effect on the bonding performance of adhesive systems remains controversial (28).

Matrix metalloproteinases, which are endopeptidases present in the dentin matrix, play an important role in dentin caries and tooth development. During dentin bonding procedures, MMPs may be released and activated, leading to degradation of collagen fibers that are not fully encapsulated within the hybrid layer. Both total-etch and self-etch adhesive strategies can activate dentin MMPs (29). Nishitani et al. (30) reported that self-etch adhesives may activate latent MMPs in dentin and contribute to deterioration of the resin–dentin bond over time. Similarly, another study demonstrated that two different self-etch adhesive applications activated MMPs in dentin powder (29). Because acidic monomers in self-etch adhesive systems may activate MMPs and negatively affect long-term bond durability, the use of chlorhexidine for MMP inhibition has been suggested as beneficial.

It has been reported that applying chlorhexidine to the tooth substrate prior to the use of a universal adhesive, in either self-etch or total-etch mode, does not adversely affect the adhesive interface under cariogenic challenge. Although the thickness of the adhesive resin layer may be less uniform when chlorhexidine is used, variations at the adhesive interface do not appear to compromise the sealing ability of universal adhesive systems regardless of the etching strategy employed (31).

Lokhande et al. (32), in a scanning electron microscopy study evaluating the effect of disinfectants containing chlorhexidine, benzalkonium chloride, and iodine–potassium iodide on microleakage of self-etch adhesives (Clearfil SE Bond and Prompt L-Pop) in Class V cavities, reported that CHX had no effect on microleakage in either one-step or two-step self-etch systems. Similarly, Güneş et al. (33) reported that chlorhexidine did not affect microleakage values when used with a self-etch adhesive system (Clearfil Protect Bond) in Class V cavities. Çelik et al. (34) evaluated a cavity disinfectant containing 2% chlorhexidine (Cavity Cleanser) in Class V cavities with two different bonding agents and found that the use of the disinfectant did not affect microleakage and that there was no difference between enamel and dentin margin leakage. Şenol et al. (35) also reported that the use of CHX as a cavity disinfectant did not affect microleakage in Class V cavities restored with giomer, glass ionomer, or resin composite. In contrast, Jing and Wang (36) reported that pretreatment with 2% CHX could increase bond strength and slow degradation of the adhesive interface.

Loguercio et al. (37) evaluated the effect of 2% CHX pretreatment on microleakage of two total-etch adhesive systems (Prime & Bond NT and Adper Single Bond 2) and reported increased microleakage in untreated groups after 24 months of aging. Bin-Shuwaish et al. (38) found that CHX improved the cervical marginal seal of bulk-fill composite restorations. Qamar et al. (39) reported that CHX, when used as a cavity disinfectant with both self-etch and total-etch adhesives, resulted in higher bond strength and lower microleakage values.

In the present study, when universal adhesives (Clearfil Tri-S Bond and Bond Force II) were applied in total-etch mode, no statistically significant difference was found between the groups treated with 2% chlorhexidine after etching and those without chlorhexidine. However, in the self-etch mode, the use of a cavity disinfectant resulted in significantly lower microleakage. Therefore, the null hypothesis was rejected. The lack of a significant effect of chlorhexidine in the total-etch groups may be explained by the already improved enamel sealing achieved with the total-etch technique, resulting in lower baseline microleakage.

The application time of chlorhexidine gluconate prior to composite restoration varies among studies. Dallı et al. (40) applied 2% chlorhexidine gluconate, 1% chlorhexidine gel, and 0.2% chlorhexidine gluconate solutions for 20 seconds before applying self-etch adhesives in Class V cavities and reported that cavity disinfectants did not adversely affect microleakage. Chaharom et al. (41), who examined the interaction between universal adhesives and 2% chlorhexidine applied for 30 seconds, reported no effect of chlorhexidine on Clearfil S3 Bond. Differences between these findings and the results of the present study may be related to variations in chlorhexidine concentration and application time. In this study, 2% chlorhexidine was applied for 60 seconds.

The literature indicates that the application of cavity disinfectants before or after the etching procedure may influence dentin bonding. Applying disinfectants before acid etching may render the dentin surface more resistant to demineralization and reduce the effectiveness of acid conditioning (42). Settembrini et al. (43) reported that most microorganisms are eliminated during smear-layer removal by acid etching and that subsequent disinfectant application primarily affects microorganisms remaining within dentinal tubules. Therefore, it is recommended to apply cavity disinfectants after acid etching in total-etch systems and before adhesive application in self-etch systems, since no separate acid-etching step is present. In the present study, the cavity disinfectant was applied after etching in the total-etch groups and before adhesive application in the self-etch groups.

This study was conducted in vitro using extracted teeth. In the oral environment, restorative materials are exposed to various physical and chemical factors that may influence the integrity of the tooth–restoration interface. Therefore, clinical studies are needed to further evaluate the effect of cavity disinfectants on microleakage under clinical conditions.

Conclusion

Within the limits of this in vitro study, the use of universal adhesives in the total-etch mode resulted in lower microleakage in Class II cavities. The application of a cavity disinfectant (CHX) significantly reduced the microleakage values of dental adhesives when applied using the self-etch technique. However, when dental adhesives were applied using the total-etch technique, the use of a cavity disinfectant (CHX) did not produce a statistically significant difference in microleakage values.
