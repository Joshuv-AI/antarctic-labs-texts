Deep learning-based approach for accurate detection of fetal QRS complexes in abdominal ECG signals
Authors: Kimia Esmaeili Alidash, Hamed Danandeh Hesar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCongenital heart defects are the leading cause of mortality related to birth defects, making early monitoring of fetal electrocardiogram (FECG) vital for the detection of abnormal fetal heart rate (FHR) patterns. Accurate fetal QRS complex detection in FECG is crucial for assessing fetal health, including heart rate and the early identification of congenital conditions. This study presents a novel automated framework using a one-dimensional Convolutional Neural Network (1D-CNN) to detect fetal QRS complexes from abdominal electrocardiogram (AECG) signals, sourced from the PhysioNet Non-Invasive FECG Database (NI-FECGDB). The proposed CNN architecture comprises five convolutional layers, seven batch normalization layers, three dropout layers, and three dense layers. In the initial phase to generate annotations tailored for the proposed network, each AECG signal was partitioned into overlapping 1-second windows, facilitating data augmentation. Binary annotations were generated for each 100-millisecond segment based on the presence or absence of fetal QRS complexes. Subsequently, after performing preprocessing steps, the developed algorithm was applied, and its performance was evaluated using metrics such as accuracy, mean squared error, F1-score, sensitivity, specificity, and precision. The results were compared with those from previous studies utilizing the NI-FECGDB database. The proposed lightweight 1D-CNN architecture demonstrated exceptional performance, achieving 96.79% accuracy, 97.91% sensitivity, 92.79% specificity and 97.88% precision while requiring only 20 AECG signals for training - a significant improvement over existing methods that typically demanded larger datasets. This framework’s innovative design eliminated the need for maternal ECG component extraction, thereby reducing computational complexity and potential signal decomposition artifacts. The combination of a simplified five-layer architecture with our novel 100 ms resolution labeling strategy enabled high-precision fetal QRS detection while maintaining minimal preprocessing requirements. These advances position our method as a robust and efficient solution for clinical fetal monitoring applications.Keywords: Detection of fetal QRS complex, Fetal heart rate, Deep learning methodsSubject terms: Biological techniques, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Cardiology, Health care, Medical research, Engineering

Introduction

Cardiac defects are among the most prevalent congenital anomalies and represent the leading cause of mortality associated with birth defects1. Numerous cardiac defects and complications initiate during the prenatal period of development2. It is estimated that each year approximately 1 in 125 babies are affected by congenital heart defects before their birth1,2. Fetal heart rate (FHR), as a one of most important vital signs, provides valuable information for assessing fetal health and physiological status. Consequently, FHR monitoring has become a widely utilized tool for both antepartum and intrapartum fetal surveillance. Some abnormal FHR patterns, including rapid accelerations and decelerations, may indicate fetal distress3. Throughout pregnancy adequate fetal oxygenation is essential to support the metabolic demands of the fetus. Inadequate oxygen levels during labor could potentially lead to irreversible damage to vital organs, such as the brain, or even fetal death. Oxygen deficiency can be detected through the assessment of FHR variability4. Moreover, variations in the width of the fetal QRS complexes provide additional insights into the specific physiological condition of the fetal heart5. Furthermore, the analysis of the fetal electrocardiogram (FECG) waveform provides valuable information for detecting congenital heart defects in the fetus6. By analyzing the FECG signal, it is possible to identify fetal heart defects that may lead to mortality during birth, enabling timely intervention to prevent such outcomes5. Therefore, reliable monitoring techniques are essential for assessing fetal health throughout pregnancy and during labor3. However, analyzing noninvasive FECG signals presents significant challenges due to their severe contamination by various sources of interference and noise, including maternal ECG (MECG), powerline interference, muscle electrical activity, baseline drifts, and motion artifacts etc4. Identifying the fetal QRS complex within the AECG signal remains a critical challenge.

Recently, deep learning techniques have gained widespread application in the field of time series analysis. In recent years, numerous studies have explored the application of deep learning techniques in the domain of processing of FECG signals. In study3, a deep learning approach utilizing CNN Networks is proposed for the identification of fetal QRS complexes from AECG signals. Unlike conventional methods, this approach does not require the removal of the MECG signals from AECG signals. The dataset utilized in this study is the Non-Invasive FECG Database (NI-FECGDB)7. In this study, signal quality was assessed using sample entropy, and channels with lower quality were excluded from further analysis. The CNN model presented in this study comprises three convolutional layers and one fully connected layer, taking a single-channel AECG signal as input. It is important to highlight that synthetic data was employed in the training process of the proposed network in3. In a separate study, the proposed CNN model incorporated a more advanced architecture aimed at optimizing the extraction of fetal QRS complex features from the AECG signals, ultimately leading to improved overall performance5. The proposed architecture consists of seven convolutional layers and two fully connected layers, applied to multi-channel AECG signals. To further improve the accuracy of fetal QRS complex detection, the model’s output undergoes postprocessing techniques. The dataset used in this study is the NI-FECGDB. The authors reported a precision of 92.77% and a sensitivity of 89.06%. Despite the relatively deep architecture of the proposed network in5, the sensitivity achieved is lower compared to that of previous studies. The study in8 introduced a residual convolutional encoder-decoder network (RCED-Net) designed to extract the FECG signals from single-channel AECG signals. This technique requires of presence of target FECG during the training which is derived from synthetic data and this approach may not be reliable for real time scenario where complex events affect the recorded signals. The algorithm proposed in8, was tested on the NI-FECGDB database, and achieved an average sensitivity, precision and F1 score of 96.06%, 92.25% and 94.10%, respectively. In the study9, a 1-D octave convolution approach utilizing the ResNet model was employed to capture the temporal dynamics in AECG signals. This method effectively reduces both memory requirements and computational cost. The NI-FECGDB database was used for evaluation in this study. The study in4 combines convolutional neural network (CNN) with a long short-term memory (LSTM) network to capture both short-term and long-term temporal relations, thereby enhancing the efficiency and accuracy of FHR prediction. The proposed algorithm was trained on a private dataset collected during childbirth, and its performance was evaluated on the NI-FECGDB database. The algorithm achieved a positive percentage agreement (PPA) of 92.1% and 98.1% on the two datasets, respectively. The performance of this method is constrained in low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) situations, where it has been unable to accurately estimate the FHR. The study in10 also aims to directly estimate FHR from AECG signals using deep learning techniques. In this approach, FECG extraction is achieved by decomposing the AECG signals into their fetal and maternal components. The method presented in10 combines dilated inception convolutional neural networks with an LSTM network. This approach achieved a PPA of 97.3% on a private database from Maxima Medical Center and 99.6% on the NI-FECGDB database. The study in11 proposed a deep learning-based method for identifying FECG signals from AECG signals, utilizing multi-channel AECG signals as input. The proposed technique involves several preprocessing steps, followed by the application of the short-time Fourier transform to convert the signals into spectrograms. These spectrograms are then fed into two layers of two-dimensional CNN, followed by a fully connected layer with the aim of classification. After postprocessing, an accuracy of 95.20% was achieved. In another study12, with the aim of extracting FECG signals from AECG signals, single-channel signals from three different databases were utilized. The study employed a dual attention model within a CNN-LSTM network architecture, with the prediction window set to 200 milliseconds. The results achieved were an accuracy of 96.26%, an F1 score of 98.02%, sensitivity of 97.38%, and precision of 98.68%. In13, a method for FECG extraction from AECG signals is proposed, which utilizes an end-to-end W-net architecture to decompose the AECG signals to the maternal and fetal components. This approach estimates the maternal and fetal ECG signals independently at the output of each decoder. After the separation of FECG signals, the fetal R peaks are subsequently identified. The method proposed in this study demonstrates an F1 score of 96.91%, precision of 97.45%, and sensitivity of 95.74% in accurately identifying the R peaks. The study in14 presents an innovative method leveraging the Internet of Medical Things (IoMT), which employs deep learning techniques to identify fetal QRS complexes from AECG signals without the need to remove the maternal components. In this study, AECG signals are first transformed into time-frequency images (TFI), which are then provided as input to a deep neural network. The Hilbert-Huang transform (HHT) and Stockwell transform (ST) are the time-frequency techniques employed to convert the AECG signals into two-dimensional image representations. The NI-FECGDB database is utilized for performance evaluation in this study. Enhanced features derived from TFI representations are fed as input into a transfer learning architecture for classification, allowing pre-trained models to boost accuracy. The evaluation metrics, including accuracy, sensitivity, precision, and F1 score, are reported as 91.32%, 89.52%, 94.37%, and 90.12%, respectively.

A review of existing studies reveals that researchers have explored a variety of approaches for fetal QRS detection, often combining CNNs with complex architectures such as LSTM networks, RCED-Net, OctConv, and dual attention mechanisms. While these methods have demonstrated promising results, they typically involve extensive preprocessing, such as MECG removal, and rely on large annotated datasets. These factors increase computational complexity, introduce potential signal decomposition errors, and risk the loss of fetal QRS components. Such limitations significantly hinder their practical deployment in real-world clinical settings, particularly in low-resource environments. Therefore, there is a critical need for a robust, data-efficient, and computationally lightweight solution that maintains high detection accuracy without depending on elaborate model architectures or preprocessing steps. This study addresses this need by proposing an efficient deep learning framework for accurate fetal QRS detection from abdominal ECG signals, requiring minimal data and reduced processing complexity.

It is important to emphasize the performance differences between the proposed 1D-CNN architecture and previously developed hybrid architectures, as this contrast highlights the rationale behind selecting a simpler design over the more commonly used complex models; in the following, we provide a detailed comparison between prior studies and our proposed method to further underscore the motivation for this work. The foremost advantages of our method lie in its architectural simplicity, computational efficiency, and reduced data and preprocessing requirements. Our method employs a 1D-CNN architecture and this configuration significantly reduces computational complexity and memory usage, making it more practical for real-time and low-resource applications. In comparison, hybrid CNN + LSTM models, such as those used in4,10, and12, combine convolutional networks with recurrent layers to capture temporal dependencies. While effective, these designs introduce considerable complexity due to the recurrent nature of LSTMs, which require a larger number of parameters and computational resources. For instance, the dual attention model in12 and the dilated CNN + LSTM model in10 involve intricate architectures that can limit their efficiency and practicality for real-time deployment. Another key distinction is in data efficiency. In Approach 1, which allocated 20 of the 25 total available signals for training and 5 for testing, our model demonstrated high performance despite the minimal training data. This is enabled by our novel annotation strategy based on 100-ms prediction windows, along with data augmentation techniques that improve generalizability even with limited samples. In contrast, hybrid methods typically require larger datasets and longer training times due to their increased architectural complexity. For example, studies4 and 10 utilize private datasets and require extensive preprocessing steps, which may not be feasible or replicable in resource-constrained environments. Our approach also differs significantly in its preprocessing steps. We intentionally avoid explicit removal of MECG components, relying instead on adaptive filtering methods to handle the noise. This decision simplifies the processing steps and avoids potential errors introduced by imperfect MECG suppression. Prior studies (e.g10,12. , often include explicit MECG removal or signal decomposition, which can introduce artifacts and reduce model reliability, especially in low-SNR conditions. Our method demonstrates robustness to noise without requiring these additional steps, offering a notable practical advantage. Finally, the clinical applicability of our approach must be emphasized. The lightweight nature of the 1D-CNN makes it suitable for real-time deployment, aligning with the growing demand for portable fetal monitoring solutions. In contrast, the computational burden of hybrid architectures may hinder their use in real-time systems and low-resource settings, despite their theoretical strengths in long-term dependency modeling. Furthermore, while advanced architectures such as RCED-Net8 and dual-attention CNNs12 demonstrate compelling theoretical performance in fetal QRS detection, their practical deployment faces significant limitations. These models incur substantial computational costs due to their structural complexity. RCED-Net’s encoder-decoder framework requires iterative signal reconstruction, while dual-attention mechanisms introduce parameter-heavy spatial-temporal feature weighting. Such designs not only increase memory usage and inference latency but also demand high-end hardware, rendering them less practical for real-time monitoring in resource-constrained settings. Also, their reliance on large datasets and sensitivity to noise compromise generalizability in low-SNR environments. In contrast, our 1D-CNN architecture achieves comparable accuracy with only five convolutional layers and minimal training data (20 signals), prioritizing computational efficiency without sacrificing performance. This balance addresses critical clinical needs, enabling reliable deployment in portable or low-resource prenatal care systems where complexity and latency are prohibitive factors.

Recent advances in deep learning for biomedical signal processing have demonstrated the versatility of 1D-Convolutional Neural Networks (1D-CNNs) across a range of cardiac monitoring tasks, including both fetal ECG and pediatric phonocardiogram (PCG) analysis. For instance, Alkahtani et al.17 utilized a 1D-CNN with pitch-shifting augmentation to classify congenital heart diseases from PCG signals, illustrating the effectiveness of time-series data augmentation in enhancing model robustness under noisy and data-limited conditions. Similarly, Haq et al.18 achieved 98.56% accuracy in congenital disease detection using PCG signals and a custom 1D-CNN architecture, further highlighting the diagnostic capabilities of CNNs in pediatric populations. These efforts, although differing in clinical focus and signal modality, collectively reinforce the growing utility of deep learning frameworks for automated cardiac anomaly detection.

Our work builds upon this paradigm by introducing a lightweight and optimized 1D-CNN tailored for fetal QRS complex detection from abdominal ECG signals. Together, these developments underscore the transformative potential of 1D-CNNs in enabling robust, efficient, and scalable solutions for cardiac health monitoring across diverse clinical settings and signal modalities.

The objective of this study is to utilize a CNN solely, without incorporating additional complex architectures, to identify the fetal QRS complex and diagnose FHR, thereby presenting an efficient, low-complexity approach that relies exclusively on convolutional layers without the need for auxiliary architectures. The overall block diagram of the proposed method is shown in Fig. 1. Although the network may initially appear to have considerable depth, it comprises only five convolutional layers, underscoring the effectiveness and efficiency of the proposed architecture. Batch normalization and dropout layers are incorporated to mitigate overfitting, vanishing and exploding gradients, and internal covariate shift. These layers serve exclusively as regularization mechanisms, enhancing the network’s stability and generalization rather than contributing directly to data processing.

Proposed methodology overview: this block diagram outlines the proposed methodology, detailing the five preprocessing methods, each corresponding to one of the five introduced approaches applied to the PhysioNet data before it is fed into the deep learning network, along with the steps taken for data augmentation and the creation of tailored annotations.

One of the key innovations of this study is the discovery, through a series of experiments, that the network can be effectively trained using a minimal dataset of only 20 signals (from a total of 25 available signals, with 20 allocated for training and 5 for testing), demonstrating its robustness under limited data availability. The proposed CNN was trained using this limited number of AECG signals obtained from the PhysioNet/Computing in Cardiology Challenge database19 without requiring the removal of MECG components. Another significant contribution of this work is the implementation of a tailored annotation technique, in which each 100-ms segment of the AECG signal was labeled to indicate the presence or absence of a fetal QRS complex. This approach substantially enhanced the accuracy of the proposed deep learning model. Furthermore, the study leverages the comprehensive nature of the dataset and applies data augmentation techniques, enabling the achievement of strong results without reliance on pre-trained networks.

In this study, five experimental approaches were designed to systematically examine the influence of dataset size, preprocessing strategies, and training–testing splits on the performance of the proposed deep learning network. The performance of these approaches was rigorously evaluated using multiple metrics, as reported in the following sections. The results consistently validated the efficiency and adaptability of the proposed framework for fetal QRS detection.

The first approach introduced in this study is based on aforementioned finding of the capability of our network to be trained with minimal data (20 signals). This approach has demonstrated significant performance with minimal preprocessing requirements. It is important to mention, experiments of training the proposed network with datasets containing fewer than 20 signals were performed; however, the optimal results were obtained when utilizing 20 signals. In the second approach, the impact of applying window functions during the preprocessing stage was examined utilizing a dataset of 60 signals, and the corresponding results were presented. The claim that the network can be effectively trained using only 20 signals was further explored in the third approach. In this approach, the proposed network was challenged by altering the training-to-testing data split ratio to 33%, using 20 signals for training and 40 signals for testing. According to the attained results in the third approach, it can be concluded that the performance of the proposed network, even under challenging conditions, is comparable to that reported in other studies, and in some cases, surpasses those results. These results support the claim of this study that the network can be effectively trained using only 20 training signals. In the fourth approach, the impact of applying the discrete wavelet transform on the performance of the proposed network was examined and presented. Finally, in the fifth approach, the results obtained from applying a three-stage preprocessing procedure using Butterworth, median, and notch filters were assessed and presented.

This paper is organized as follows. The Results and Discussion section presents the performance of the five experimental approaches, comparing accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, and other evaluation metrics under varying preprocessing strategies and training conditions, with results benchmarked against previously published studies. The Materials and Methods section provide a detailed description of the dataset (NI-FECGDB), the preprocessing techniques employed (including Butterworth filtering and discrete wavelet transform), the tailored labeling strategy for fetal QRS complexes, and the architecture of the proposed CNN model. It also outlines the training protocols, cost functions, and evaluation metrics used in this study. The Conclusion section summarizes the key findings and highlights the main contributions, such as the minimal data requirement and the retention of maternal ECG components without explicit removal. Finally, references to relevant prior work are provided.

The fourth approach (applying discrete wavelet transform)

Conclusion

In this article, a deep learning-based method utilizing convolutional neural networks was proposed to detect fetal QRS complexes from AECG signals. Several distinct approaches were explored to implement this method, each varying in terms of preprocessing steps, cost functions, and training strategies, to optimize the performance of the network. The comparative analysis demonstrates that the proposed method exhibits strong performance in accurately detecting the location of fetal QRS complexes. In the optimal approach, the proposed network achieved an accuracy of 96.79%, sensitivity of 97.91%, specificity of 92.79%, and precision of 97.88%. The innovations of the proposed network include its ability to retain the mother’s electrocardiogram signal, rather than removing it, and its capacity to train effectively using only a small subset of the AECG signals. A key factor contributing to the success of the network is the data segmentation and labeling strategy, where each 100-millisecond interval of the AECG signal is labeled. This approach enabled the network to achieve effective training with fewer layers, improving performance while minimizing computational complexity. In this study, five distinct approaches were introduced to explore the network’s training capability with varying amounts of data. The findings demonstrate that the network can be effectively trained using as few as 20 AECG signals (25 signals in total, 20 used for training and 5 for testing), highlighting its efficiency in learning from limited data. The first approach, which involved minimal preprocessing, yielded impressive results. In the second approach, the effect of window functions during the preprocessing stage was evaluated using 60 signals. The third approach challenged the claim of the network’s ability to train effectively with only 20 signals by adjusting the data split ratio to 33% (20 training signals and 40 test signals). The results from this approach demonstrated that even under challenging conditions, the network’s performance was comparable to the results of other studies, and in some cases superior to, the results reported in other studies. In the fourth approach, the impact of discrete wavelet transform on the network’s performance was examined and reported. Finally, in the fifth approach, the results obtained using 60 signals and incorporating three preprocessing steps—Butterworth filters, median filtering, and notch filtering—were analyzed. Based on the positive outcomes across all five approaches, it can be concluded that the proposed deep learning model has demonstrated strong performance. The highest results were achieved in the first and fifth approaches, using 25 and 60 signals, respectively, and these results were selected for comparison with findings from other studies.

Materials and methods

Dataset

The dataset utilized in this study was acquired from set A of the PhysioNet/computing in cardiology challenge database (NI-FECGDB). The dataset for the challenge comprises a collection of 1-minute AECG recordings, each consisting of four channels of AECG signals sampled at a frequency of 1 kHz. Consequently, each channel contains 60,000 samples, equivalent to 60 s. Seven records namely a54, a33, a38, a47, a52, a54, a71, a74 due to inaccurate reference annotations were excluded in this study similar to the work in19. In the dataset, certain records contained missing data at specific timestamps, either in a single channel or across all four channels, resulting in incomplete entries. Since our network was trained using 1-second-long inputs, these incomplete records could not be utilized. To ensure the quality and integrity of the neural network’s training, these records—specifically a01, a02, a07, a09, a11, a16, a18, and a43—were excluded after being identified through visual inspection. Eventually, in this study, a total of 60 records were used from the 75 available in Set A of the NI-FECGDB.

Preprocessing

Previous studies have explored a variety of preprocessing methods to optimize the performance of their proposed network architectures. In studies3,8, the researchers selected high-quality signals manually; however, it is important to acknowledge that, in real-world scenarios, a range of complex factors impact the quality of recorded AECG signals. In references10,11, preprocessing techniques are specifically designed for the detection and elimination of maternal QRS complexes. Also, in references4,12, and13 preprocessing procedures incorporate methods such as Butterworth filtering and data normalization. Reference9 employs the extended Kalman filter for elimination of MECG, while reference14 utilizes the Hilbert-Huang transform (HHT) and Stockwell transform (ST).

In this work, preprocessing methods are applied exclusively to mitigate noise present the dataset, while data of lower quality are not excluded. Given the diversity of preprocessing techniques employed in prior researches, through the five approaches presented in this study, the impact of various pre-processing methods on the outcomes of the proposed network has been systematically examined. Additionally, the influence of different levels of preprocessing on the performance of the proposed algorithm has been compared and reported. Subsequently, the preprocessing methods applied in each approach will be presented.

In this study, the approach proposed in20, was employed to eliminate baseline wander. To prevent phase distortion, baseline wander was removed using a high-pass second order Butterworth filter (with a cutoff frequency of 5 Hz) applied in both forward and backward directions. Finding outliers

In this article, inspired by the preprocessing method introduced in20, outlier data are eliminated using a median filter. In this approach, for each ECG channel a median filtering with a 60ms window was applied and the absolute difference between the original and the median filtered signal was obtained. If the absolute difference was over an estimated threshold, the corrupted interval was replaced with the estimated values. Discrete wavelet transform

In one of the proposed approaches in this study, the Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT) was employed for data preprocessing. In this approach, the AECG signals from each channel are decomposed using a Biorthogonal wavelet (Bior 4.4) up to a decomposition level of 10. The fetal and maternal QRS complexes contain higher frequency components, and experiments indicate that the information associated with these complexes is predominantly found within decomposition levels 1 to 4. Consequently, only the detail coefficients from levels 1 to 4 were utilized in the signal reconstruction process. Architecture of the proposed CNN network

In this study, an innovative CNN architecture has been proposed for detecting fetal QRS complexes and, ultimately FHR, aiming to enhance accuracy while reducing computational complexity. The architecture of proposed CNN network is illustrated in Fig. 10. The input signal, after undergoing preprocessing and data preparation, is fed into the proposed network with dimensions of 4 (channels) × 1000 (samples). The output of the proposed network consists of a vector of 10 samples for each 1000-millisecond segment of input the AECG signal, representing 10 binary predictions (1 or 0) for that interval. In this vector, a value of 1 indicates the detection of a fetal QRS complex by the network, while a value of 0 signifies detection of no fetal QRS complexes. Figure 11 has been incorporated to enhance clarity and provide a more comprehensive visual representation of the proposed network architecture and its operational workflow. In other words, for each 100-millisecond section of the input AECG signal, the proposed network generates a prediction of either 1 or 0. If the network’s predictions align with the annotations, the network’s prediction is considered correct.

Modular block diagram of the proposed CNN network architecture.

In the context of FECG extraction, a detected fetal QRS is classically considered a true positive if it is within 50ms of the reference annotation3. Therefore, in this study the frame size is set to 100ms. The proposed model comprises five convolutional layers, seven batch normalization layers, three dropout layers, and three fully connected (dense) layers.

During different executions of the proposed network, the ability to train the network with a smaller number of signals was revealed. The proposed method in this article was processed in two distinct modes, each utilizing different number of records. In the first mode, the proposed network was evaluated using a total of 25 signals (20 for training and 5 for testing) from the dataset, whereas in the second mode, it was assessed using 60 records. The objective of evaluating the proposed network in these two modes is to assess its ability to be effectively trained using a smaller number of signals (20 signals). It is important to note that this ability was also explored with fewer than 20 signals; however, the best results and optimal network performance were achieved using 20 signals. Additionally, the network’s performance was evaluated using the maximum number of available signals (60 signals).

As it is known, higher stride effectively reduces the size of the output feature map22. Therefore, in the first and second convolutional layers, the stride is set to 2 to decrease the size of the output feature map. The Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) function is used as the activation function following each convolutional layer, which is the most widely used activation function in CNNs. The ReLU activation function converts all input values to positive values. The primary advantage of ReLU over other activation functions is its reduced computational load22. A potential issue associated with the use of the ReLU activation function is the “dying ReLU” problem. The dying ReLU problem is a form of vanishing gradient, occurring when ReLU neurons become inactive and consistently output 0 for all inputs. This condition is recognized as one of the challenges in training deep feed-forward neural networks with ReLU activation. One of the most effective techniques to overcome dying ReLU problem is the batch normalization23,24. Training deep neural network models is often challenging, not only due to issues like vanishing or exploding gradients but also because of the change in data distribution across different layers, a phenomenon known as “internal covariate shift”. Batch normalization is a technique that can mitigate this phenomenon25. Additionally, increasing the number of layers in CNN architectures can induce the vanishing gradient problem, which subsequently impairs the network’s performance26. Given the high depth of the proposed network, batch normalization has been incorporated to prevent the vanishing gradient problem and improve training efficiency. To address the overfitting problem in the model, dropout layers has been employed. Dropout has become widely adopted due to its simplicity, as it requires minimal alterations to the network architecture, primarily involving the addition of dropout layers27. After applying the convolutional layers, batch normalization, and dropout layers, the two-dimensional output of the deep network is flattened into a vector, which is then passed through three fully connected (dense) layers. To further mitigate overfitting beyond dropout and batch normalization, several complementary strategies were employed. All results were averaged over five independent runs to ensure robustness across different initializations. The model was evaluated under varying training/testing splits (e.g., 80–20% and 33–67%), serving a cross-validation-like role. These measures collectively enhanced the model’s generalization and reduced overfitting risk.
