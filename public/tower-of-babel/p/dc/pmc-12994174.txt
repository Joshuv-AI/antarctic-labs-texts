Atomistic simulations reveal the photoactivation mechanism of a carotenoid-binding photoreceptor
Authors: Amanda Arcidiacono, Mattia Bondanza, Lorenzo Cupellini, Benedetta Mennucci
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificancePhotoprotection is essential for cyanobacteria to prevent light-induced damage, and the orange carotenoid protein (OCP) plays a central role in this process. Although the structural changes associated with OCP photoactivation have been characterized, the underlying molecular mechanisms have remained elusive. Using atomistic simulations that integrate excited-state dynamics and enhanced sampling techniques, we reveal that a trans-to-cis photoisomerization of the bound carotenoid is the key photochemical trigger which promotes the large-scale conformational motions leading to the active state. Our simulations achieve an atomic-level understanding of OCP function and illustrate the critical role of photoisomerization in biological light sensing.

Orange carotenoid protein (OCP) is a photoreceptor that plays a key role in the photoprotection of the photosynthetic apparatus of cyanobacteria (1–4). In its light-activated form, OCP binds to the antenna megacomplexes of these photosynthetic organisms, the phycobilisomes (PBS), and promotes the dissipation of excess excitation energy in the photosynthetic pigments (2, 3, 5, 6). OCP uses a specific chromophore, a keto-carotenoid such as echinenone (ECN), 3′-hydroxyechinenone (hECN), or canthaxanthin (CAN), as light sensor and quencher (6, 7). In its resting “orange” form (OCPO) the carotenoid is embedded within a tunnel, bridging the two domains of the protein, N-terminal domain (NTD) and a C-terminal domain (CTD) (Fig. 1A). The chromophore is stabilized via hydrogen bonds with conserved residues, notably Tyr201 and Trp288, located in the CTD (3, 4). These H-bonds are critical for the stability of the protein in its resting state (8, 9). Upon absorption of blue-green light, OCP undergoes a photoinduced conformational change: The carotenoid loses the H-bond interactions with the CTD and translocates by as much as 12 Å within the protein, finding a different binding site within the NTD (7, 10, 11). Upon carotenoid translocation, the two domains can dissociate (7, 10, 12–14), resulting in the formation of the active “red” form (OCPR). In this form, the protein can bind to the PBS and quench the excitation energy of the phycobilin pigments by energy transfer to the bound carotenoid (15–17).

Although the OCPR active structure has confirmed the overall translocation-dissociation mechanism (15, 16), the details of this mechanism and especially the primary photochemistry are still heavily debated (18–23). Time-resolved spectroscopy has detected a multistep photoactivation pathway with intermediates occurring on time scales from picoseconds to microseconds (10, 11, 18, 24), revealing a general picture of the OCP photocycle. However, spectroscopic investigations are hindered by the extremely low quantum yield of the primary photochemistry and of the overall photoactivation (<1%) (18). As such, considerable uncertainty remains on the molecular events linking photon absorption to the formation of the OCPR active state. A key unresolved question is how carotenoid photoexcitation initiates such profound structural changes in the protein. Several hypotheses have been proposed in the literature regarding the molecular mechanisms following carotenoid excitation.

One proposed mechanism involves isomerization of the single bond connecting the β1 ring (located on the CTD side) to the conjugated chain (24). This conformational rearrangement was suggested to disrupt hydrogen bonding through ring flipping. However, this hypothesis has been challenged by the pump–probe visible–infrared absorption anisotropy measurements of Konold et al. (18), who excluded the rotation of the β1 ring. An alternative mechanism, proposed by Bandara et al. (11) involves keto–enol tautomerization of the carbonyl group, which potentially leads to hydrogen bond weakening. However, this pathway seems unlikely due to the substantial energy gap between the two tautomeric forms. Yaroshevich et al. (20) proposed another mechanism involving proton transfer from a hydrogen-bonding residue to the carbonyl oxygen of the keto-carotenoid. Still, this pathway is also energetically unfavorable, primarily due to the low proton affinity of the keto-carotenoid. Time-resolved crystallography experiments suggest that conformational changes near the β2 ring (on the NTD side) may occur following carotenoid excitation and subsequent relaxation (21). However, such remote structural rearrangements are unlikely to directly perturb the strong hydrogen bonds that the β1 ring makes with the CTD. More recently, Chrupková et al. (25) proposed that the photoactivation of OCPO may be driven by elevated vibrational excitation in the longitudinal stretching modes of the carotenoid polyene backbone, resulting from a “hot” ground state formed upon photoexcitation and decay. Recent evidence suggested that the photoactivation mechanism of OCP involves absorption of two photons (26–28). This “two-photon activation” does not refer to nonlinear multiphoton absorption, but rather to a sequential process in which multiple, discrete photoexcitation events are necessary to fully convert the protein to its active OCPR state. A recent study employing a two-pulse excitation scheme with variable interpulse delays demonstrated that the photoactivation behavior is strongly dependent on the identity of the bound carotenoid (29). Specifically, while OCPO functionalized with CAN undergoes efficient photoconversion to the OCPR state upon absorption of a single photon, OCPO complexed with hECN or ECN requires absorption of two photons to fully convert to the active OCPR form. Some of the present authors used quantum-chemical nonadiabatic dynamics to study the excited-state decay of canthaxanthin (CAN) in OCP (30). We identified the involvement of additional excited states (SX and S∗) in the excited-state dynamics and characterized their nature with respect to the experimental evidence (20, 26, 31–33). Our results revealed the fundamental role of the OCP binding pocket in tuning the excited-state decay of CAN, and linked the multiphasic dynamics observed in experiments to the ground-state conformational heterogeneity, with the long-lived component assigned to the S∗ state. Importantly, our simulations revealed that the distortion imposed by hydrogen bonds on the β1 ring (within the CTD) promotes selective photoisomerization at the C7=C8 double bond (Fig. 1B).

Building upon these findings, here we integrate quantum mechanics/molecular mechanics (QM/MM) excited state nonadiabatic dynamics and enhanced sampling approaches to clarify the complete photocycle of OCP, beginning with the characterization of the photoproduct and extending to the carotenoid translocation and the final recovery of OCPR. Our results indicate that photoisomerization at the C7=C8 double bond is essential for enabling the translocation of the carotenoid within the NTD. Furthermore, we show that multiphoton absorption facilitate the recovery of the all-trans canthaxanthin, which is the isomer found in the structure of OCPR bound to the phycobilisome (16).

Results

The Photoproduct.

Previous excited-state dynamics simulations (30) revealed that upon photoexcitation of CAN in OCPO, approximately 10% of photon absorption events lead to the formation of a 7-cis carotenoid isomer, which we denote here as PPcis (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). In contrast, the remaining ∼90% of photons result in a photoproduct that retains the original all-trans configuration, referred to as PPtrans. Representative structures of the two photoproducts are shown in Fig. 1C and SI Appendix, Fig. S2. The photoisomerization mechanism involves a concerted rotation around two adjacent dihedral angles, resulting in a volume-conserving isomerization that preserves the spatial position of the β1 ring. This mechanism is often indicated as hula-twist (34). Accordingly, in PPcis, this ring adopts the same orientation as in the nonisomerized PPtrans state (Fig. 1C). This finding is consistent with the experimental observations, which showed minimal rotation of the β1 ring during the early phase of the photocycle (18). Notably, this volume-conserving isomerization allows the β1 carbonyl to retain its hydrogen bonds with Tyr201 and Trp288 in the CTD. In contrast, the conjugated polyene chain of CAN undergoes significant distortion to accommodate the cis configuration (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Overall, the carotenoid occupies essentially the same space within the protein pocket in both photoproducts (SI Appendix, Fig. S2).

To characterize the trans and cis photoproducts, we propagated the final structures of the corresponding surface hopping trajectories (7 for the cis isomer and 27 for the trans isomer) on the ground state for 30 ps. Within these trajectories, we did not observe significant changes in the structure of PPcis or PPtrans. Specifically, the position of CAN in the binding pocket and the orientation of the β1 ring remain stable (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). In comparison with resting-state OCPO, the PPtrans trajectories show a slightly weakened H-bond with Tyr201, whereas the H-bond with Trp288 remains always stable. Conversely, in PPcis both H-bonds show some significant perturbations (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A and B). Nevertheless, this higher H-bond flexibility does not lead to their dissociation within these ground state trajectories, and the carotenoid does not leave the original binding site. This suggests that despite a reduced H-bond stability, an energy barrier must still be overcome to initiate translocation.

Structures sampled from the photoproduct trajectories were employed to simulate the absorption spectra. We report in Fig. 1D the calculated QM/MM absorption spectra of both photoproducts, compared to the spectrum of OCPO calculated with the same method. While PPtrans shows a negligible difference with respect to OCPO, we observe a clear shift for the spectrum of PPcis. This different behavior is amplified when considering the difference spectra (photoproduct-minus-OCPO, dashed lines). For PPtrans the difference spectrum is null, showing that PPtrans and OCPO are spectroscopically indistinguishable. Conversely, the difference spectrum of PPcis shows a differential shape with a maximum ∼25 nm to the red of the OCPO absorption. If we compare these results with those from the UV-vis transient absorption spectroscopy experiments of ref. 18, we clearly see that only the isomerization of the carotenoid can explain the red-shifted signal measured for the primary photoproduct in the OCP photocycle.

According to experiments, this photoproduct has a relatively long lifetime of 50 ns, which is consistent with the stability of H-bonds observed in our QM/MM simulations. We note that the experiments of ref. 18 were conducted on echinenone (ECN) instead of CAN. Although the carotenoid species has a significant influence on various stages of OCP activation, we can assume that the early photochemistry and the nature of the photoproduct would be the same for different carotenoids. As such, we conclude that the primary photoproduct observed in transient absorption represents a CAN molecule still H-bonded to the protein, but with a 7-cis configuration.

To better understand the spectroscopic differences between PPcis and PPtrans, we recalculated their absorption spectra in the absence of the protein environment (i.e. in gas phase), while retaining the geometries of CAN obtained within the protein context. Comparison with the spectrum of OCPO reveals that the primary spectroscopic differences between PPcis and PPtrans cannot be attributed to their conformational differences alone (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). Indeed, in the gas-phase (VAC) calculations, the cis and trans isomers exhibit nearly indistinguishable spectra. This indicates that the observed spectral differences in OCP are induced by the protein environment—more specifically, by the differential response of the two isomers to the same electrostatics effects. In fact, the electrostatic potential generated by the protein on the atoms of both photoproducts is nearly identical (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). This distinct sensitivity to the same external electric fields is not unexpected for conjugated systems like CAN, where electronic and nuclear degrees of freedom are strongly coupled. Here, in particular, the cis isomer experiences binding constraints, which enhance its sensitivity to the protein’s electric field, ultimately resulting in a red-shifted absorption spectrum. This does not occur in PPtrans, whose response is unchanged with respect to OCPO.

From the Photoproduct to the Translocation.

Building on the insights into the photoisomerization mechanism presented in the previous section, we next turned our attention to longer timescales (nanoseconds to milliseconds) to investigate how trans/cis isomerization influences the translocation process. To this end, we combined umbrella sampling (US) with replica-exchange molecular dynamics (REMD). The simulations were carried out using a simple yet effective collective variable (CV) dNTD, defined as the distance between the center of mass of the carotenoid and that of the N-terminal domain (NTD) (see Materials and Methods for details). This approach has previously been demonstrated to yield a well-converged free energy surface (FES) along the translocation coordinate, while accounting for protein conformational rearrangements (14).

In Fig. 2A, we compare the FESs associated with the translocation of the all-trans (red) and 7-cis (blue) isomers of CAN. To maintain consistency and clarity, we have labeled each basin in the FESs following the nomenclature established in our previous study (14). The initial position of the carotenoid, corresponding to dNTD=11.5, represents the photoproducts in a fully equilibrated state. In the case of the trans isomer, OCPtransO indeed corresponds to the dark resting state of OCP, as there are no significant structural differences between PPtrans and OCPO. In contrast, for the cis isomer, the OCPcisO state represents a relaxed version of PPcis, which is likely not fully attained during the activation process. Following the collective variable, at dNTD ≃ 7/8 we find the first local minimum corresponding to the translocation state T1, while at dNTD ≃ 2/3 we have the second translocation state T2. In Fig. 2B, representative structures of the free-energy minima are also reported, where we show the CAN position within the protein and the most strongly interacting residues.

It is evident that the conformation of CAN has a substantial impact on the translocation energetics. While the three-minima profile already observed with the all-trans CAN is conserved, the relative energies are severely affected: The barrier leading from the photoproduct to the first intermediate is almost halved in the cis isomer, T1cis and T2cis states are almost iso-energetic as for the trans analogs, but now the barrier between them is reduced from more than 7 kcal/mol to less than 3 kcal/mol. Moreover, the barrier that prevents the carotenoid from reverting to the initial state is double (5 kcal/mol) the corresponding one calculated with the all-trans CAN (about 2.5 kcal/mol). Those two facts suggest that, if the T1cis state is populated after the photochemistry, it can easily reach an equilibrium with T2cis without fast back-conversion. The opposite applies to T1trans for which we expect a relatively fast back conversion to OCPO. This was further verified by performing plain MD simulations of the different states; while T1cis is stable for more than 1.5 μs in two different MD simulations, when we simulated T1trans, we observed a back-translocation event in less than 500 ns (SI Appendix, Fig. S9).

To investigate the reason for such a radical change in the free-energy profile, we studied the protein–carotenoid interactions along the translocation pathways. Both the orientation of CAN inside the protein and the positions of interacting residues are generally similar for 7-cis and all-trans CAN in all intermediates (Fig. 2). However, in the T1cis structure 7-cis CAN is H-bonded to the backbone nitrogen of Met284, located within a conserved region of the protein CTD (Fig. 2B). This H-bond appears to be stable, and is observed in the majority of representative structures sampled in this basin. It is clear that only the 7-cis isomer of CAN can make this interaction, as in the all-trans isomer the carbonyl shows a completely different orientation.

The new interactions lead to a differential stabilization of the 7-cis isomer. This is shown in Fig. 3, where we plot the difference in CAN–residue interaction energy for all amino acids of OCP. The T1cis state is stabilized by strong electrostatic interactions with Met284, as expected by the H-bond, as well as Gly283 on the same loop. Another stabilizing interaction occurs between the carotenoid and the salt-bridge residues Arg155 and Glu244 at the T1cis–T2cis transition barrier (dNTD ≃ 4.5). In contrast to the previously discussed interactions, this one appears to be predominantly nonelectrostatic in nature (SI Appendix, Fig. S8), and can be attributed to a reduced steric clash of the cis isomer within that region of the protein. It is worth noting that, in the equilibrated cis photoproduct, hydrogen bonding is significantly weaker compared to the all-trans configuration. Other minor interactions stabilizing the trans state, with a dominant nonelectrostatic character, are observed between NTD residues and the carotenoid, but they are more spread across residues, and therefore less strong and specific. In the same region a cis-stabilizing electrostatic interaction is found with Ala54 and corresponds to another H-bond.

We have investigated the internal structure of CAN, and specifically the dihedral angles along the conjugated chain, which are the main degrees of freedom in the carotenoid. These angles are all stable along the translocation, with the notable exception of the terminal single bonds C6–C7 and C6′–C7′, which are known to experience a certain rotational freedom (35). SI Appendix, Fig. S6 shows the distribution of those two angles for both cis and trans CAN. As expected, only minor differences are found for C6′–C7′, as it is far away from the isomerization site and does not experience any significant change in its environment. A different situation can be seen for the C6–C7 single-bond torsion: In the simulations of all-trans CAN, this dihedral remains in its initial value for all the first part of the translocation process, and then at dNTD<7.0 it acquires a greater conformational freedom, spanning all the possible conformations. This is a consequence of both the lack of specific interactions and the low steric constraint. In the cis pathway, instead, the angle is always blocked in a single value, which shifts from an initial value—determined by the photoisomerization—to a conformation roughly rotated by 90°. Here, the head of the carotenoid is experiencing a much reduced conformational freedom. This is a consequence of the specific interactions (H-bonds) formed by the cis isomer which are not present in the all-trans. When the same analysis is performed on plain MD simulations (SI Appendix, Fig. S10) a smaller conformational freedom is observed, suggesting that the different conformations are separated by quite high barriers which cannot be crossed in the short time scales used for those simulations, while they are easily crossed in REMD simulations.

Recovery of All-Trans Carotenoid.

Our results support the involvement of 7-cis CAN in the photoactivation of OCP. However, the presence of a cis-isomerized CAN in the active form of OCP was never observed in high-resolution structures of OCPR (15, 16). This led us to investigate the potential back-isomerization of 7-cis CAN to the all-trans configuration. Isomerization of xanthophylls and other carotenoids can occur thermally or photochemically, and cis isomers of carotenoids are found naturally (36–39). Since our simulations do not show any spontaneous conversion to all-trans in the time window explored, we considered the possibility of a second photon being absorbed in the activation process, that could lead to back-isomerization and recovery of the all-trans form. The hypothesis that the activation process of OCP could be a multiphoton process has been put forward in recent years (27–29). In those investigations a continuous irradiation is used with intensities corresponding to at most one photon per second (27). This suggests that a second photon is unlikely to interact with the chromophore during the activation photocycle which is completed in much smaller time windows. Instead, the second photon could be absorbed when the active form has already been formed.

To model this scenario, it would be necessary to simulate the final domain separation step. However, in our previous study, we demonstrated that dissociation of the NTD and CTD becomes feasible only upon reaching the T2 state (14). Furthermore, the structures of NTD and CTD remain largely unchanged upon dissociation. Based on these findings, we did not repeat the full dissociation process for the 7-cis isomer. Instead, we reconstructed the active form of OCP by artificially separating the two domains in the T2 state: The CTD was removed, and the CAN-embedding NTD was relaxed via plain MD simulations. The resulting complex, from now on CAN@NTD, is expected to properly resemble the NTD of OCPR.

To simulate the effects of a second photon absorbed by cis-CAN@NTD, we performed excited-state QM/MM surface hopping dynamics. These simulations allow us to investigate whether the cis-to-trans isomerization can proceed via a photochemical pathway. Naturally, the cis-CAN@NTD →trans-CAN@NTD conversion would result in a fully all-trans population only if the reverse process occurs at a negligibly low rate. Therefore, we also performed excited-state dynamics on the trans-CAN@NTD complex.

The results of the surface hopping simulations are shown in Fig. 4, where we report the initial and final values of the dihedrals Φ describing the double bond C7=C8 torsion (x axis) and the adjacent single-bond (C6–C7) torsion (y axis). The concerted change of these two bonds reveals a hula-twist photoisomerization, akin to the one observed in OCPO within our previous work (30) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). Starting from cis-CAN@NTD (Fig. 4A), most of the surface hopping trajectories remain in the 7-cis configuration, but 13% of them end up in all-trans. Although only a small portion of the trajectories exhibits a cis-to-trans photochemical conversion, none of the trajectories starting from trans-CAN@NTD forms the 7-cis isomer (Fig. 4B). Therefore, absorption of multiple photons after OCP activation can only lead to the accumulation of all-trans and the disappearance of the 7-cis isomer. A closer analysis of the dihedral angles involved in this photoisomerization shows that CAN explores different conformational regions compared to the photochemical event in OCPO (SI Appendix, Fig. S1), particularly along the ΦC6−C7 dihedral. The barrier for single-bond rotation is about 3 kcal/mol (35), but the exact potential energy curve of the C6−C7 torsion depends on the conformation of the ring and on the environmental constraints on the carotenoid. The difference in the ΦC6−C7 distribution (SI Appendix, Fig. S11) between OCPO and CAN@NTD is expected, as the protein pocket of OCPO imposes geometric constraints on the carotenoid that are absent in OCPR (or CAN@NTD). As a result, PPcis has a more twisted geometry (40) and considerably less conformational freedom than the cis-CAN@NTD isomer. Specifically, conformations with 270<ΦC6−C7<360 and −90<ΦC7=C8<0 (blue in Fig. 4A) resemble the s-cis form. These states are not populated immediately after PPcis formation—which initially retains the s-trans geometry—but are only accessed later along the translocation pathway. Return to the all-trans geometry from these conformations proceeds via a clockwise rotation. By contrast, the other set of initial geometries (yellow in Fig. 4A) more closely resemble PPcis itself. Their back-isomerization follows the reverse of the OCPO mechanism, involving a counterclockwise rotation and leading to s-cis conformers. Consequently, the two conformational motifs of the 7-cis isomer are retained upon re-isomerization to all-trans, yielding both s-cis and s-trans forms. Finally, as also indicated by classical MD simulations (SI Appendix, Fig. S10), changes in the orientation of the β1 ring remain possible in CAN@NTD because the ring is solvent-exposed. This, combined with the inherently higher flexibility of the terminal conjugated single bonds, permits thermal interconversion between s-cis and s-trans.

Discussion.

By integrating excited-state nonadiabatic dynamics with ground-state enhanced sampling techniques, we have reconstructed the complete photoactivation pathway of the Orange Carotenoid Protein. Our simulations demonstrate that the active state can be accessed only if the embedded carotenoid (here, canthaxanthin) undergoes photoisomerization from the all-trans to the 7-cis conformation. In addition, only the 7-cis photoproduct exhibits red-shifted absorption features, consistent with those observed in transient absorption experiments (18).

Our simulations indicate that the translocation of CAN is unlikely to occur in its all-trans form. This is due to the high barriers involved in the forward process, and the low barrier for the back-conversion to the dark resting form. According to our data the only stable translocated state for trans carotenoid is T2trans: For this state to be populated directly after the photochemistry, a very fast displacement of the carotenoid from its initial position to the final one [which is almost 12 Å away along the binding site (7)] would be required. This is both in contrast with chemical intuition and with the spectroscopical data reported by Konold et al. (18) and other authors. The translocation for the cis-isomerized CAN instead, only requires a small initial displacement (roughly 3 Å, to reach the barrier) after the photochemistry to populate T1cis; then, on a longer timescale, the translocation can be completed without any significant back-conversion due to the high barrier involved in the backward process and to the low barrier needed to reach T2cis state.

We recall that the FESs obtained here correspond to a fully equilibrated process, in which both the protein and the carotenoid—as well as the solvent—are fully relaxed and mutually adapted along the entire translocation pathway. As such, these surfaces are expected to capture the true thermodynamic landscape governing the long-lived, stable states of the system. In contrast, short-lived, nonequilibrium species, such as the photoproducts, do not occupy the global minima of the FESs. Therefore, the initial barrier of 6 kcal/mol associated with the transition toward T1cis should be considered an upper bound to the actual barrier experienced by PPcis. This limitation prevents us from determining the exact kinetics of translocation for the two species. Nevertheless, the large difference between them—and the higher energetic propensity of 7-cis to translocate—strongly supports the isomerization as the trigger for carotenoid translocation.

The quantum yield calculated in our simulations for photoisomerization is higher than the experimentally reported for the whole photoactivation of OCP, which is typically estimated to be below 1%. This is not unexpected as it is likely that not all the formed cis photoproducts lead to the complete activation of the complex. This can be attributed to the presence of competing processes, such as cis-to-trans back-isomerization of the PPcisor backward translocation of the T1cis. Although these processes have relatively high barriers, they may still occur and thus progressively reduce the overall OCP activation quantum yield.

Our results indicate that CAN remains in the 7-cis configuration throughout the entire photoactivation process. Following the formation of the final active state, the incidence of multiple photons can progressively drive the system toward a fully all-trans population, with no regeneration of the 7-cis isomer observed once the system reaches this state. Specifically, achieving a 7-cis population below 1% requires the absorption of approximately 34 photons. This level of excitation can be reached in less than a minute at 200 μmol m−2 s−1 continuous illumination, or in about five minutes at 30 μmol m−2 s−1. The latter condition corresponds to the experimental setup used in ref. 15 for the cryo-EM reconstruction of OCPR bound to PBS, where the complex was kept at room temperature and exposed to continuous illumination for 10 min. Based on our estimation, this timescale would be sufficient to photochemically convert the carotenoid entirely to the all-trans configuration.

The possibility of photochemical cis-to-trans isomerization does not rule out thermal conversion. Keto-carotenoid cis isomers can form thermally at a very slow rate, and this process is fully reversible (38). The 7-cis-canthaxanthin form has been detected in several studies on carotenoid isomers, (36, 38, 39) although it is less prevalent than others such as 9-cis and 11-cis, suggesting a lower stability. On the other hand, our results show that 7-cis is stabilized with respect to the other isomers when CAN is within the OCPO binding pocket. In the OCPR state, however, CAN occupies a very different local environment, with both rings fully exposed to water. These conditions may destabilize the 7-cis form and accelerate its spontaneous conversion to the all-trans configuration.

These results provide a comprehensive mechanistic framework for the light-driven activation of OCP and highlight the essential role of carotenoid photoisomerization in governing its functional dynamics. The robustness of our conclusions is reinforced by the agreement between our computational predictions and available experimental observations on the photoproduct, providing reasonable validation of the proposed activation pathway.

Materials and Methods

Umbrella Sampling-Replica Exchange Simulations.

The starting points of the US-REMD simulations were sampled from the final structure of the surface-hopping trajectories from a previous work of some of the present authors (30). The trajectories were selected randomly between the ones undergone to the trans-to-cis isomerization. This choice is not relevant, as the US-REMD simulation protocol is designed to achieve a complete equilibration and therefore small differences in the starting structure should not have any influence on the overall result. We selected the collective variable (CV) already used in our previous paper (14), to be an approximate and “mechanism-agnostic” reaction coordinate. This variable, here referred to as dNTD, is defined as distance between the center of mass of the carotenoid and the one of the NTD. The latter is defined as residues from ASP-19 (sequence fragment LAADVVP) to ILE-151 (sequence fragment GQQITVL) in the reference sequence PDB:4XB5, including hydrogen atoms. The results have been analyzed using the mBAR (41) method and compared with the previous one obtained in all-trans CAN in OCP (14). To guarantee consistency of the results, the analysis was also repeated on raw data from the previous simulation using the updated software toolchain used in the present paper. All results proved to be perfectly consistent with the one reported in our previous paper. All properties reported along the reaction coordinate are computed as weighted averages (using mBAR weights) of the properties computed on each individual configuration extracted from the simulation, if not specified otherwise. More details on the simulation protocol and analysis are reported in SI Appendix.

Absorption Spectra.

The absorption spectra of CAN in OCPO, PPtrans, and PPcis were computed starting from ground state QM/MM molecular dynamics trajectories, where CAN is treated at the QM level with the semiempirical FOMO-CI method (42–44) and the rest of the system is treated at the classical level. More details on the QM/MM approach are reported in SI Appendix. The spectra were computed from transition probability based on the transition dipole moments of the first 5 singlet excited states of CAN in OCP. The spectrum of OCPO was obtained from the thermal equilibration trajectories of ref. 30, consisting in 10 trajectories of 10 ps each, starting from structures sampled from a classical MD of OCPO (35). The thermalization trajectories were performed at 300 K using the Bussi–Parrinello stochastic thermostat, (45) with a time step of 0.5 fs. The spectra were computed on the second half of the trajectories, in a range of 5 ps. The spectra of PPcis and PPtrans were computed from 30 ps ground state trajectories (7 for PPcis and 27 for PPtrans) that were propagated from the final conditions (positions and velocities) of some of the OCPO surface hopping trajectories (ref. 30). These simulations were performed in energy conserving conditions, again with a time step of 0.5 fs.

Surface-Hopping Simulations.

The nonadiabatic excited-state dynamics was simulated using Tully’s “fewest switches” surface-hopping (SH), (46) coupled with the QM/MM scheme described in SI Appendix. The description of the electronic states of CAN within the SH simulations is provided by the semiempirical FOMO-CI method, specifically parameterized for carotenoids (43). Such methodology has been used for the excited state dynamics of carotenoids in different protein systems (44, 47), but also for photoinduced isomerization in general (48–52). The starting conditions (positions, velocities, and initial electronic state) of the SH simulations were sampled from the last 5 ps of thermalized trajectories of cis and trans CAN@NTD. For each isomer, we obtained 20 structures from plain MD simulations, and equilibrated them on the ground state for 10 ps at 300 K at the QM/MM level using the Bussi-Parrinello stochastic thermostat (45). The SH simulations were started using 783 trajectories for cis-CAN@NTD and 648 trajectories for trans-CAN@NTD. The total number of SH trajectories that reached the ground state in 20 ps is 707 for cis-CAN@NTD (9% starting from S1, 88% starting from S2, and 3% starting from S3) and 519 for trans-CAN@NTD (15% starting from S1, 83% starting from S2, and 2% starting from S3). More details on the SH simulations are provided in SI Appendix.
