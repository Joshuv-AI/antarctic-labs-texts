Effects of semaglutide on mortality, cardiovascular, and kidney outcomes across the cardio-kidney-metabolic continuum: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Federica Marzano, Stefania Paolillo, Paola Gargiulo, Ciro Cotticelli, Mariafrancesca Di Santo, Ermanno Nardi, Giuseppe Maria Abbellito, Laura Liccardi, Fabrizio Perrone Filardi, Dario Bruzzese, Pasquale Perrone Filardi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSemaglutide, a glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonist, has demonstrated cardiometabolic benefits in randomized controlled trials (RCTs). However, its overall effects on mortality, cardiovascular (CV) and kidney outcomes have not been comprehensively synthesized. This meta-analysis aimed to assess the prognostic impact of semaglutide across the cardio-kidney-metabolic continuum.MethodsA systematic literature search was conducted to identify all eligible RCTs comparing the prognostic effects of semaglutide with placebo across diverse patient populations. Primary outcomes were all-cause and CV mortality. Secondary outcomes included major CV and kidney events, while major adverse limb events (MALE) were analyzed as an exploratory endpoint. A random-effects model was used to pool hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsEight trials encompassing 39,204 patients were included. In patients treated with semaglutide a significant reduction of all-cause (HR, 0.84; 95% CI, 0.77–0.92; p = 0.0001) and CV mortality (HR, 0.83; 95% CI, 0.72–0.95; p = 0.0078), major adverse CV events (MACE, HR, 0.82; 95% CI, 0.77–0.87; p < 0.0001), nonfatal myocardial infarction (MI, HR, 0.75; 95% CI, 0.68–0.84; p < 0.0001), worsening heart failure (HF, HR, 0.84; 95% CI, 0.73–0.98; p = 0.0245), and kidney outcomes (HR, 0.83; 95% CI, 0.73–0.95; p = 0.0080) was observed compared to placebo. No significant effects were observed for nonfatal stroke.ConclusionsTreatment with semaglutide, compared to placebo, is associated with significant lower incidence of all-cause and CV mortality, as well major CV and kidney events across the continuum of cardio-kidney-metabolic syndrome.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12933-026-03215-y.Keywords: Semaglutide, Placebo, Randomized controlled trials, Mortality, Cardio-kidney-metabolic syndrome

Introduction

Cardiovascular (CV) disease remains the leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, particularly among individuals with type 2 diabetes (T2DM), obesity, chronic kidney disease, and heart failure (HF) [1, 2]. These conditions frequently overlap or represent different stages of the cardio-kidney-metabolic syndrome and are associated with an increased risk of major adverse CV events (MACE), premature death, and recurrent hospitalizations, underscoring the need for therapies that provide both metabolic, renal, and CV protection [1, 2]. Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have demonstrated to provide CV benefit in patients with T2DM [3, 4] and, more recently, in obese patients with atherosclerotic CV disease without T2DM [5, 6]. However, GLP-1 receptor agonists were usually tested in CV outcomes trials enrolling T2DM patients aiming at demonstrating CV safety as primary endpoint but none of them was powered to assess CV or all-cause mortality. Among GLP-1 receptor agonists, semaglutide was the only agent studied in multiple clinical settings, including diabetic patients with atherosclerotic CV disease [7], chronic kidney disease [8], peripheral artery disease [9], non-diabetic patients with obesity [5] and HF patients with and without T2DM [10, 11]. In all CV outcomes trials semaglutide significantly met the primary endpoint, demonstrating CV and renal benefit in patients with and without T2DM. Yet, none of these trials was adequately powered to assess effects on CV and all-cause mortality. Thus, a meta-analysis of semaglutide clinical trials appears of relevance to explore the effects on CV and all-cause mortality and to validate the robustness of evidence on endpoints of clinical relevance. By integrating data from all available trials, including either oral or subcutaneous formulations of semaglutide, this study seeks to provide a comprehensive and drug-specific appraisal of the CV and renal effects of semaglutide.

Methods

Search strategy and selection criteria

The meta-analysis was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) reporting guidelines [12]. A systematic literature search to identify all eligible randomized, placebo-controlled trials assessing efficacy and safety of semaglutide across diverse patient populations was conducted (Fig. 1). The search strategy is detailed in Supplementary Table S1. Study inclusion criteria were phase 3 randomized, placebo-controlled, large clinical trials of semaglutide, assessing the following outcomes: CV mortality, all-cause mortality, MACE, nonfatal myocardial infarction (MI), nonfatal stroke, worsening HF, major kidney disease events, and major adverse limb events (MALE). No language, background medication therapy, background CV risk or disease restrictions were applied. We excluded observational non-randomized studies, registries, trial rationales and designs, duplicate series, reviews and meta-analysis, and secondary or pooled analyses. Articles were screened for fulfillment of inclusion criteria by two independent reviewers (F.M., S.P.). Reviewers compared selected trials and discrepancies were resolved by consensus discussion. Corresponding author was asked to provide full-text articles if not available. Neither ethics approval nor patient consent was required for this analysis. This meta-analysis was previously registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251063371).

Risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias for the included trials was assessed using the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomized trials (RoB 2). The evaluation focused on five domains: bias arising from the randomization process (D1); bias due to deviations from intended intervention (D2); bias due to missing outcome data (D3); bias in measurement of the outcome (D4); bias in selection of the reported result (D5). Each domain was rated as low risk, some concerns, or high risk of bias, as reported in Supplementary Table S2.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were all-cause mortality and CV mortality. Secondary outcomes included a three-point MACE (composite of nonfatal MI, nonfatal stroke, or death from CV causes), assessed in a time-to-first-event analysis, the individual components of MACE (nonfatal MI and nonfatal stroke), and worsening HF (defined as either hospitalization for HF or a composite endpoint of hospitalization or urgent medical visit for HF). Additional secondary outcome included major kidney disease events, a four-point composite of death from kidney-related causes, a persistent reduction from baseline in the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) of 50% or more, a persistent eGFR of less than 15 ml per minute per 1.73 m2, or the initiation of long-term kidney-replacement therapy with either dialysis or transplantation. Along with data from original trials, data were extracted from additional sources [6, 13]. In addition to prespecified CV and kidney outcomes, we also evaluated MALE (a composite outcome consisting of acute limb ischemia hospitalization or chronic limb ischemia hospitalization) as an exploratory endpoint.

The definition of HF outcomes differed across trials included in this meta-analysis; endpoints available across included trials are defined in Supplementary Table S3. For each endpoint of interest, the quality of evidence was assessed according to the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) criteria (Supplementary Table S4).

Data analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted with R version 4.5.1 (http://www.r-project.org). Package meta, Version 8.1—0 [14] was used for all the meta-analysis elaborations. The random-effects model was a priori selected to obtain pooled estimates of treatment effect (Hazard Ratio, HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). The meta-analysis was conducted using the inverse-variance method, applying log-transformed HRs and their corresponding standard errors derived from the reported confidence intervals. Between-study variance (τ2) was estimated using the Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML) estimator. Heterogeneity was quantified through Cochran’s Q, the I2 statistic, and τ2, with values of 25%, 26–50%, and > 50% indicating low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively. Because fewer than ten studies were available for each outcome, funnel plots and formal tests for small-study effects were not performed, as these methods are unreliable with limited numbers of studies.

Meta-regression analyses including both categorical (semaglutide formulation: oral vs. subcutaneous; dose: high vs. low; disease type: diabetes vs. no diabetes) and continuous (median follow-up duration and discontinuation rate in the semaglutide arm) study-level moderators were conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. All moderators were entered into a random-effects meta-regression model fitted using the REML estimator. Categorical moderators with only one study in a given level were excluded from meta-regression, as such contrasts are not informative.

For the MACE outcome, the presence of a trial-level effect modifier for sex was evaluated using the meta-analytical approach described by Fisher et al. [15], which provides a framework for assessing differential treatment effects across patient subgroups.

A cumulative meta-analysis was performed by ordering the trials according to their year of publication. For each step in the cumulative sequence, a pooled hazard ratio was computed using a random-effects model with REML estimation. Cumulative estimates were plotted to visually evaluate the stability and potential saturation of the evidence base.

Results

Baseline characteristics across included trials

A total of 39,204 patients enrolled in eight randomized controlled trials (RCT) [3–5, 7–11] comparing semaglutide versus placebo were included in this meta-analysis. Of included patients, 19,603 were randomized to semaglutide and 19,601 to placebo arm. The median follow-up ranged from 13.2 to 49.5 months. Key characteristics of the included studies are summarized in Table 1, including population characteristics, intervention details, and follow-up duration. Background medication use was evaluated in all trials and was generally well balanced between treatment arms, as expected in randomized designs.

All-cause and CV mortality

All-cause mortality was reported in six trials, including 19,030 patients treated with semaglutide and 19,029 treated with placebo. A total of 1219 patients treated with semaglutide died from any cause, compared to 1428 patients in the placebo arm, corresponding to 16% lower death rate (HR, 0.84; 95% CI, 0.77–0.92; p = 0.0001) (Fig. 2A). Excluding the SELECT trial [5], which enrolled patients with obesity but without T2DM, did not alter the overall results, confirming the lower incidence of all-cause mortality in diabetic patients treated with semaglutide (HR, 0.84; 95% CI, 0.72–0.98; p = 0.0288).

CV mortality data were available from six trials, including 18,944 patients treated with semaglutide and 18,939 with placebo. Since there was only one CV death reported in STEP-HFpEF trial, the trial was not included for this endpoint. For CV mortality, a total of 708 events were reported among semaglutide treated patients compared to 831 in the placebo group, corresponding to a 17% lower CV mortality compared to placebo (HR, 0.83; 95% CI, 0.72–0.95; p = 0.0078) (Fig. 2B). Exclusion of the SELECT trial [5], only modestly weakened the statistical significance of the results (HR, 0.80; 95% CI, 0.64–1.00; p = 0.0478).

In the random-effects model, a moderate but not significant heterogeneity was observed for all-cause (I2 = 40.7%, τ2 = 0.0017, p = 0.13) and CV mortality (I2 = 34.5%, τ2 = 0.0082, p = 0.18) across all trial populations.

Effects of semaglutide on CV outcomes

Among CV outcomes, MACE, nonfatal MI, and nonfatal stroke were reported in five trials, including 18,634 patients treated with semaglutide and 18,633 treated with placebo.

In the overall population, a total of 1529 patients treated with semaglutide experienced MACE during follow-up, compared to 1845 patients in the placebo group with semaglutide significantly lowering the rate of MACE by 18% (HR, 0.82; 95% CI, 0.77–0.87; p < 0.0001), with a consistent effect observed across all trial populations (I2 = 0%; Fig. 3).

Overall effect of semaglutide on MACE. The forest plot depicts the treatment effect of semaglutide on MACE composite at the level of the individual trials and in the overall study population. HR, hazard ratio; CI, confidence interval; MACE, major adverse cardiovascular events

The sex by treatment interaction test revealed no statistically significant effect modification (female vs male ratio of HRs, 1.06; 95% CI, 0.89–1.26), indicating that the treatment effect was consistent in males and females, although the pooled HR was numerically lower in men (Supplementary Fig. 1).

A total of 561 and 400 semaglutide-treated patients experienced nonfatal MI and nonfatal stroke, respectively, during follow-up, compared to 734 and 437 patients in the placebo arms. Semaglutide significantly reduced the risk of nonfatal MI by 25% (HR, 0.75; 95% CI, 0.68–0.84; p < 0.0001) (Supplementary Fig. 2A) with no heterogeneity among studies (I2 = 0%), but had no significant effect on stroke (HR, 0.91; 95% CI, 0.79–1.04; p = 0.16; I2 = 27.5%) in the overall population (Supplementary Fig. 2B).

Worsening HF events were available from six trials, including 17,440 patients treated with semaglutide and 17,439 treated with placebo. A total of 331 events were reported among semaglutide treated patients compared to 397 in the placebo group; thus, semaglutide was associated with lower risk of worsening HF by 16% compared to placebo (HR, 0.84; 95% CI, 0.73–0.98; p = 0.0245) (Supplementary Fig. 3). However, in the random-effects model, a significant heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 59.6%, τ2 < 0.0001, p = 0.0301) across all trial populations.

Effects of semaglutide on kidney outcomes

A four-point composite of major kidney disease events was reported in four of included trials, encompassing 17,043 patients treated with semaglutide and 17,041 treated with placebo. A total of 405 patients treated with semaglutide experienced major kidney disease events, compared to 473 patients in the placebo arm. Thus, semaglutide was associated with lower incidence of the four-point composite of major kidney disease events by 17% compared to placebo (HR, 0.83; 95% CI, 0.73–0.95; p = 0.0080), with low heterogeneity observed across all four trial populations (I2 = 6.8%; Fig. 4).

MALE, intended as acute or chronic limb ischaemia requiring hospitalization, were only investigated as secondary endpoint in three trials [4, 8, 9], including 6988 patients treated with semaglutide and 6987 treated with placebo. In the overall population, a total of 93 patients treated with semaglutide experienced MALE during follow-up, compared to 132 patients in placebo (HR, 0.70; 95% CI, 0.53–0.91; p = 0.0092), with a consistent effect observed across all four trial populations (I2 = 0%; Fig. 5).

Meta-regression

Meta-regression analyses did not identify any statistically significant moderator for all-cause mortality, cardiovascular mortality, or worsening heart failure (Supplementary Table S5). Estimates for administration route, dosage, median follow-up, treatment duration, and discontinuation rate were all non-significant, and the residual τ2 remained close to the unadjusted value. As the number of included trials per outcome was small, these analyses should be interpreted with caution because meta-regression is inherently underpowered in this context and cannot exclude clinically relevant heterogeneity across populations or treatment strategies.

Cumulative meta-analyses

Across all outcomes, the cumulative meta-analyses showed early variability in effect estimates driven by the first two, relatively small, trials (SUSTAIN-6, 2016; PIONEER-6, 2019). A marked stabilization of the cumulative HRs occurred with the inclusion of SELECT in 2023 (n = 17,604). Subsequent studies, including STEP-HFpEF (2023), FLOW (2024), and STEP-HFpEF DM (2024), introduced only modest refinements to the pooled estimates. The addition of the recent but large SOUL trial (2025, n = 9650) further confirmed the established trend, while the small STRIDE trial (2025, n = 792) contributed minimally. Overall, the cumulative estimates remained stable after the major trials were incorporated, indicating temporal robustness and saturation of the evidence base (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Discussion

Semaglutide, originally introduced as an antihyperglycemic therapy for T2DM, has progressively demonstrated broader favorable effects on several clinical scenarios across the continuum of the cardio-kidney-metabolic syndrome. Clinical benefits likely derive from multiple mechanisms of action, encompassing improvements in glycemic control, body weight, blood pressure, lipid profile, and systemic inflammation, as well potentially direct vascular and renal protective effects [16].

The populations enrolled in the trials included in this meta-analysis can be contextualized within the cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome framework recently defined by the American Heart Association, with most participants falling within advanced stages (CKM stages 3–4) based on enrollment criteria and baseline characteristics [17, 18]. The results of this meta-analysis, which included 39,204 patients, 19,603 of whom were treated with semaglutide, demonstrated that semaglutide use is associated with a significant 16% and 17% reduction of all-cause and CV mortality, respectively, with no significant heterogeneity across trials. In addition, patients treated with semaglutide showed a significant 18% lower incidence of MACE, primarily driven by 25% reduction of nonfatal MI, while no significant effect was observed on nonfatal stroke.

Major kidney disease events were reported in four studies [4, 5, 7, 8] that used a consistent definition of a four-point composite endpoint. Meta-analysis of these studies showed a significantly 17% lower incidence of renal function deterioration in patients treated with semaglutide, without heterogeneity.

In patients treated with semaglutide a 16% reduced risk of worsening HF events was observed but this effect was associated with significant heterogeneity across the six studies that reported an HF outcome. In four of these studies [4, 5, 10, 11] the HF outcome consisted of the composite of hospitalization or urgent medical visit for HF, and, in the remaining two [3, 7], was hospitalization for HF only. Thus, the results for HF outcome should be interpreted as hypothesis generating in the absence of a trial specifically designed to assess HF hard endpoints.

Finally, in three trials [4, 8, 9], patients treated with semaglutide showed a 30% lower incidence of MALE, consistently defined in all three studies. Yet, in none of these studies, including the STRIDE trial [9], MALE events represented the primary endpoint, and their incidence was quite low. Thus, although no heterogeneity was observed, these findings should be interpreted with caution and as hypothesis generating.

Previous meta-analyses mainly assessed GLP-1 receptor agonists as a class and restricted analyses to patients with T2DM [6, 19, 20]. In this context, Sattar et al. demonstrated that GLP-1 receptor agonists reduced all-cause mortality by 12% [19] and a more recent meta-analysis by Lee et al., including results from the SOUL [4] and FLOW [8] trials, confirmed a reduction in all-cause mortality by 12% with long-acting GLP-1 receptor agonists compared to placebo, along with lower risk of CV mortality (HR 0.86; 95% CI, 0.79–0.93) [6]. Similarly, Kalayci et al. [20] confirmed that GLP-1 receptor agonists significantly reduce the risk of MACE, CV death, and all-cause mortality in patients with T2DM. Taken together, these data support CV protection as a class effect of GLP-1 receptor agonists. Within this class-level context, the magnitude of risk reduction in MACE, CV death, and all-cause mortality observed in our semaglutide-focused meta-analysis appears broadly comparable to that reported for other GLP-1 receptor agonists. In particular, the PIONEER 6 study [3], using oral semaglutide in high risk diabetic patients, and the FLOW trial [8], in diabetic patients with kidney diseases, reported a significant reduction of all-cause and CV mortality, and all-cause mortality was significantly reduced in the SELECT trial [5], whereas in the remaining studies a non-significant trend for reduction of all-cause (with the exclusion of SUSTAIN 6 [7]) and CV mortality was observed.

Emerging evidence helps clarify the mechanisms underlying the CV benefits observed in our meta-analysis. Pre-specified analyses of the SELECT trial indicated that only a modest proportion of the effect of semaglutide on CV outcomes can be explained by weight loss [21] or improvement in glycemic control [22]. CV risk reduction appeared largely independent of baseline adiposity, or changes in glycated hemoglobin, suggesting additional mechanisms beyond adiposity reduction and glycemic changes. These findings support the concept that semaglutide may exert disease-modifying actions that extend beyond metabolic pathways. Moreover, recent evidence from a systematic review and meta-regression analysis [23], showed that semaglutide significantly reduces the incidence of new-onset atrial fibrillation, particularly among individuals with metabolic and CV comorbidities. This observation further supports the broader cardioprotective profile of semaglutide and aligns with the cardio-metabolic benefits highlighted in our meta-analysis.

In real-world clinical practice, the adoption of semaglutide may be influenced by factors such as accessibility, cost, and guideline recommendations. While semaglutide use is recommended in guidelines [24, 25], its relatively high cost and limited availability in certain healthcare settings may restrict widespread use. Although adverse events and tolerability were not formally meta-analyzed as prespecified outcomes in this study, the safety profile of semaglutide observed in the included trials was consistent with previous GLP-1 receptor agonist studies, with gastrointestinal events representing the most common adverse effects and the main reason for treatment discontinuation, yet generally manageable and in line with the known class profile [6, 26]. Nonetheless, current data strengthen the evidence of substantial cardiometabolic and renal favorable effects of semaglutide, across advanced stages of cardio-kidney-metabolic syndrome. Furthermore, although quantitative CV risk prediction tools incorporating kidney function parameters are available [27], their application requires individual patient-level data, which were not accessible in this aggregate-level meta-analysis. Evaluating the impact of semaglutide on integrated cardio-kidney-metabolic risk scores represents an important area for future investigation, which may further elucidate the mechanisms by which semaglutide modifies cardio-kidney-metabolic risk and impacts prognosis.

Limitations

The current meta-analysis presents limitations that must be acknowledged. First, the included trials differed for baseline characteristics of patients, including populations with different comorbidities and risk profiles, as well as dosing regimens and formulations of semaglutide (oral versus injectable, which may differ pharmacokinetically), all of which may have contributed to heterogeneity in the pooled results. However, although differences in study populations may have influenced baseline risks and effect estimates, such diversity captures the complexity of the cardio-kidney-metabolic syndrome, characterized by interrelated metabolic, CV, and renal pathways, and no heterogeneity was observed for MACE, renal and mortality outcomes analysis. Second, the effects on all-cause and CV mortality should be interpreted with caution from a trial level meta-analysis and do not infer a causal relationship, despite the statistical significance of the incidence rate and the lack of significant heterogeneity among studies.

Third, follow-up durations varied significantly across the included studies, ranging from 13.2 to 49.5 months. Several included trials had relatively short follow-up durations, which may have limited the assessment of long-term effects on mortality and renal outcomes. Fourth, this meta-analysis was based on trial-level rather than individual patient-level data, which limited the ability to conduct detailed subgroup analyses or adjust for patient-specific characteristics. Consequently, residual confounding and heterogeneity across trials cannot be excluded and may have influenced the precision of pooled estimates. Fifth, the definition of HF outcomes differed across trials, as some studies reported only hospitalizations, while others included either hospitalizations or urgent visits. This may have influenced the pooled estimate of worsening HF events and should be considered when interpreting the results. Sixth, although random-effects models partially attenuate the influence of very large trials by incorporating between-study variance into the weighting scheme, studies contributing a large number of events—such as SELECT and SOUL—remain highly influential. Consequently, the pooled estimates should be interpreted in light of the substantial contribution of the largest trials. Finally, all included trials were industry-sponsored; however, the use of prespecified protocols, publicly available analysis plans, and blinded endpoint adjudication likely minimized the risk of funding-related bias.

Conclusions

In this meta-analysis of eight RCTs, use of semaglutide, compared to placebo, was associated with reduced all-cause and CV mortality, as well MACE and kidney events across the continuum of the cardio-kidney-metabolic syndrome.
