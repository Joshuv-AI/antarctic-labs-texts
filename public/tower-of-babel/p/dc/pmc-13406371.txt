Causal association between hip osteoarthritis and the risk of pancreatic cancer: A bidirectional two-sample Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Wei Li, Bo Hu, Hongyu Zhuang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The potential causal relationship between hip osteoarthritis (HOA) and pancreatic cancer (PC) is not well understood. To investigate this, our study utilized a two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) method to examine a possible causal link between HOA and an increased risk of PC. Genome-wide association study summary data for both HOA and PC were sourced from the Integrative Epidemiology Unit OpenGWAS database (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/datasets/). The single-nucleotide polymorphisms associated with these conditions at a statistically significant level (P value <5 × 10−8) were identified as instrumental variables for subsequent analysis. We conducted bidirectional two-sample MR analyses employing inverse-variance weighting (IVW), weighted median, MR-Egger regression, and weighted mode methods. The robustness of the findings was assessed through sensitivity testing. The IVW analysis indicated that HOA elevated the risk of PC (odds ratio = 1.788; 95% confidence interval: 1.166–2.742; P = .008). Conversely, in the reverse MR analysis, the IVW analysis provided no evidence that PC increased the risk of HOA (odds ratio = 1.016; 95% confidence interval: 0.973–1.060; P = .475). In addition, the weighted mode, weighted median, and MR-Egger regression methods did not uncover a causal relationship. There was no heterogeneity or horizontal pleiotropy among the instrumental variables. The “leave-one-out” analysis revealed that no single-nucleotide polymorphism significantly influenced the overall results. Genetically predicted HOA is significantly associated with an increased risk of PC. However, we found no evidence that PC leads to an increased risk of HOA.

1. Introduction

Pancreatic cancer (PC) is a highly malignant and rapidly progressive tumor, leading to a significantly poor prognosis.[1] Recent epidemiological surveys have shown that its incidence is increasing worldwide, accompanied by a relative survival rate of <10% at 5 years, underscoring the ongoing challenge of effective treatment.[2] Surgical treatment is crucial in managing PC. However, because of its stealthy onset, rapid progression, and tendency to metastasize, most cases are diagnosed at an advanced stage, leading to the loss of surgical opportunities.[3] Eventually, PC has emerged as one of the major life-threatening health diseases.[4] Furthermore, follow-up results revealed that the majority of treated patients developed metastases or symptomatic residuals.[5] Thus, early diagnosis and preventive interventions for PC are particularly important, and an in-depth exploration of its risk factors presents a significant potential clinical benefit.

Hip osteoarthritis (HOA) is a chronic disease characterized by degenerative changes in the articular cartilage and the formation of secondary osteophytes.[6] The initial manifestation of the disease is subtle, with progression occurring gradually.[7] Patients often overlook hip problems until they experience noticeable symptoms, such as acute pain, stiffness, and restricted joint mobility, which significantly impair their quality of life.[8] A few studies have delved into the association between HOA and various forms of cancer. As early as 1999, Olsen’s research revealed a heightened risk of cutaneous melanoma among individuals with prolonged HOA conditions.[9] A nationwide retrospective cohort analysis has revealed an increased likelihood of bladder cancer among individuals diagnosed with HOA.[10] To our knowledge, the causal link between genetically predicted HOA and the risk of PC remains unclear.

Mendelian randomization (MR) is a novel approach in epidemiology for evaluating causal associations using genetic variation. By employing genetic variants as instrumental variables (IVs) to investigate causal links between exposures and outcomes, this method mitigates the influence of potential confounders and reverse causation, thereby enhancing the reliability of study findings.[11] In this study, we examined the causal relationship between genetically predicted HOA and PC using MR analysis. This approach may offer a novel hypothesis regarding the pathogenesis of PC and suggest new avenues for its prevention and treatment.

2. Materials and Methods

2.1. Data sources and selection of genetic variants

Figure 1 provides an overview of the study design. The OpenGWAS archive was thoroughly searched for comprehensive genome-wide association study (GWAS) dataset compilations. Since all the information used was publicly available, no additional ethical approval was required. To reduce ethnic-related biases, the genetic lineage of the study cohort was limited to individuals of European descent. A two-sample MR study was conducted using publicly available data, with genetic variations associated with HOA and PC serving as IVs, applying a P value threshold of 1 × 10−8 to ensure a sufficient number of single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). IVs were pruned based on linkage disequilibrium (LD) criteria (r2 ≥ 0.01 and physical distance > 10,000 base pairs).[12] LD clumping (r2 < 0.01) ensured instrument independence, and pleiotropy was evaluated via MR-Egger, Cochran’s Q, and funnel plots. In addition, palindromic SNPs were excluded by employing minor allele frequencies to prevent uncertainties related to strand orientation.[13] The R2 statistic, which indicates the variance explained by IVs, and the F statistic were computed for each IV, ensuring that all exhibited an F statistic of 10 or higher, indicating robust instruments. A low weak instrument bias was anticipated, as an F statistic below 10 is generally considered “weak.” Data specifics for HOA (n = 393,873 Europeans) and PC (n = 476,245 Europeans) are available at the website (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/). To examine potential confounders for the effect of HOA on PC, PhenoScanner (http://www.phenoscanner.medschl.cam.ac.uk/) was utilized.

Design for a two-sample Mendelian randomization study to investigate the causal relationship between HOA and pancreatic cancer. HOA = hip osteoarthritis, MR = Mendelian randomization.

2.2. MR analysis

This study employed different MR methods to scrutinize the causal association between SNP exposure and outcome manifestations. The primary technique utilized was the inverse-variance weighting (IVW) method, complemented by the weighted median (WME), weighted mode, and MR-Egger methods. Causal significance required IVW P < .05, consistent directional estimates across methodologies, and nonsignificant outcomes from both the MR-Egger intercept assessment and the Mendelian Randomization Pleiotropy Residual Sum and Outlier global test. By considering these criteria, the study aimed to ensure a comprehensive evaluation of the causal link between SNP exposure and outcome effects.

2.3. Heterogeneity and sensitivity analysis

Causal inferences were derived from odds ratio (OR) calculations accompanied by 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Heterogeneity among SNPs was examined through Cochran’s Q-statistics[14]and the I2 statistic.[15] A thorough “leave-one-out” examination was also carried out to evaluate the possible influence of individual SNPs on the causal association under scrutiny.[15] All MR procedures were executed using R Studio Software (Version: 2023.06.0 Build 421; Posit [formerly R Studio, Inc.]) and R Software (Version: 4.3.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing). The reporting of findings adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology – Mendelian Randomization guidelines.[16]

3. Results

3.1. Influence of HOA on pancreatic cancer

A total of 20 distinct genome-wide significant SNPs were revealed in the study on HOA. Each SNP employed for the MR analysis was classified as a “robust” instrument, with all having an F statistic value exceeding 10. This F statistic considers the influence magnitude and accuracy of the SNP on the condition of HOA. The association effect estimates for every SNP concerning HOA and PC are presented in Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content 1. The IVW analysis revealed an OR of 1.788 (95% CI: 1.166–2.742; P = .008; Table 1), suggesting a statistically significant correlation between genetic variations in HOA and PC susceptibility. Although the P values for the other 3 methods, MR-Egger (OR = 2.948; 95% CI: 0.427–20.326; P = 1.081), WME (OR = 1.370; 95% CI: 1.007–2.461; P = .292), and weighted mode (OR = 1.107; 95% CI: 0.477–2.565; P = .816), were not statistically significant, the results still suggested that HOA increases the risk of PC (Table 1). Despite variations in significance across methodologies, our outcomes pointed to a high concordance in the estimated causal effects of HOA on PC through IVW, MR-Egger, WME, and weighted mode. The forest plot and the scatter plot illustrate the effect estimates derived from the two-sample Mendelian Randomization method and their corresponding 95% CIs (Figs. 2A and 3A).

3.2. Influence of pancreatic cancer on HOA

In total, we obtained 5 LD-independent (r2 < 0.001) IVs with P < 1 × 10−5 from GWAS for PC (Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content 2). The value of F statistics for selected IVs is >10, demonstrating that the IVs we selected in this study were powerful enough. Through IVW analyses, we did not detect any evidence for a causal effect of PC on HOA (OR = 1.016; 95% CI: 0.973–1.060; P = .475), which was supported by the MR-Egger (OR = 1.004, 95% CI: 0.911–1.107, P = .940), the WME (OR = 1.022; 95% CI: 0.969–1.078; P = .431), and MR-Egger estimate (OR = 1.005; 95% CI: 0.949–1.064; P = .874) (Table 1). The forest plot illustrates there was no link between PC and the risk toward HOA (Fig. 2B), and the scatter plot for effect sizes of SNPs for PC is shown in Figure 3B. In summary, combined with the results from different MR methods, we concluded that there is no causal effect of PC on HOA.

3.3. Heterogeneity and sensitivity analysis

The Cochran’s Q test was employed to assess variability among IV estimates derived from distinct genetic variants. The results showed no significant heterogeneity, which enhances the reliability of MR estimates (Table 2). Furthermore, the I2 statistics corroborated this, suggesting minimal variation, thereby enhancing the validity of the MR results (I2 = 12.18%). A “leave-one-out” assessment was carried out to assess the effect of each SNP on the IVW point estimate upon its exclusion. The outcome indicated that no solitary SNP significantly influenced the overall outcome, reflecting the stability of the MR estimates (Fig. 4). In addition, both the funnel plot visualization (Fig. 5) and the MR-Egger regression analysis (Table 2) exposed no considerable asymmetry, implying negligible publication bias and nondirectional horizontal pleiotropy, which underscores the robustness of the findings. In summary, the evidence of low heterogeneity, consistent I2 values, the “leave-one-out” analysis outcomes, and the symmetry observed in the funnel plot and MR-Egger regression collectively validate the dependability of the MR estimates and alleviate concerns regarding potential bias.

4. Discussion

The present study analyzed the causal relationship between HOA and PC using a two-sample bidirectional MR method based on a large sample of GWAS public data. The results indicated that HOA is genetically associated with an increased risk of PC. Consequently, the significance of this study lies in emphasizing the necessity for enhanced screening of PC in patients with HOA, with the aim of early prevention and treatment strategies.

The HOA potentially plays a role in the pathogenesis of PC through specific mechanisms. While these mechanisms have not been definitively identified, numerous studies have attempted to explain this link. SMAD family member 3 (SMAD3) has been found to be significantly elevated in patients with HOA compared with normal subjects.[17] Functionally, SMAD3 acts as a central transcriptional regulator in the transforming growth factor-beta pathway, directly promoting matrix metalloproteinase (MMP)-2/9 expression – key proteases facilitating tumor invasion and metastasis in PC. Similarly, epidemiological and molecular evidence suggests SMAD3 promotes pancreatic carcinogenesis by regulating MMP activation through transforming growth factor-beta signaling,[18] thereby positioning the SMAD3-MMP axis as a potential hypothesis between HOA and PC. Future studies should evaluate the expression of SMAD3, TGF‑β, and related signaling molecules in pancreatic tissues from patients with concurrent HOA and PC and explore whether these pathways are indeed activated in this patient subgroup. Such investigations could provide essential validation for the causal link suggested by our MR findings. In addition, several signaling pathways contribute to the development of HOA. Wnt,[19] receptor activator of nuclear factor κB ligand (RANKL),[20] TGF-beta,[21] and AKT serine/threonine kinase[22]are particularly associated with stimulating joint inflammation in HOA. Notably, these pathways are not only active in HOA but also drive key oncogenic processes in PC: Wnt/β-catenin signaling enhances epithelial-mesenchymal transition and chemoresistance, RANKL overexpression correlates with poor survival in PC patients by promoting tumor cell survival, AKT serine/threonine kinase activation mediates gemcitabine resistance via metabolic reprogramming.[18,23–25] On the other hand, this systemic dysregulation of lipid metabolism may extend to pancreatic tissue, where ectopic fat deposition (fatty pancreas) impairs autophagic flux. Mechanistically, impaired autophagy leads to p62/SQSTM1 accumulation, which stabilizes nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor and upregulates NQO1 – creating an oxidative microenvironment conducive to DNA damage and KRAS proto-oncogene-driven tumorigenesis in the pancreas.[26] However, fatty infiltration into the pancreatic acinus may lead to the dysfunction of autophagy, resulting in the overexpression of p62 and NQO1, thereby increasing the incidence of PC.[27] Based on the aforementioned perspective, we hypothesize that HOA can contribute to the development and progression of PC through systemic metabolic dysfunction and fat infiltration-mediated stress responses. While these pathways offer biological hypotheses, they remain speculative and require rigorous testing in human tissues, patient cohorts, and appropriate preclinical models before any mechanistic conclusions can be drawn.

Biologically, the connection between HOA and PC may be rooted in systemic inflammatory processes. In the context of osteoarthritis, an upsurge of proinflammatory mediators such as interleukin-1β/15/17, tumor necrosis factor-α, nitric oxide, and MMPs is observed, suggesting concurrent local and systemic inflammatory reactions that contribute to joint cartilage degradation and tissue restructuring.[28,29] Total hip replacement is widely recognized as the primary intervention for symptomatic HOA.[30] Furthermore, this surgical procedure may suggest a broader dissemination of inflammatory responses and an increased expression of inflammatory cytokines.[31] The chronic inflammatory environment of HOA is persistent and relatively mild, primarily mediated by the innate immune system,[32] which contributes to neoplastic transformation and the progression of PC.[33,34] Therefore, inflammatory factors may underpin the causal relationship between HOA and PC. While the biological plausibility of this causal link exists, further studies with rigorous designs are necessary to thoroughly investigate and confirm the mechanistic basis of the causal effect of HOA on PC. These findings emphasize the importance of not only concentrating on traditional risk factors for PC but also highlighting the significance of individuals with hereditary pancreatitis. Understanding the impact of hereditary pancreatitis on PC will aid in implementing effective prevention and control measures, which are crucial to alleviate the burden of the disease. While the reverse MR analysis utilized fewer IVs (n = 5 SNPs) compared with the forward direction (n = 20 SNPs), power calculations indicated 72% power to detect an OR ≥ 1.3. The observed null association (OR = 1.016) was consistent across all sensitivity methods and suggests that insufficient IVs are unlikely to explain this result. Nevertheless, we cannot rule out the possibility that a very small effect (OR < 1.1) might be undetected. Future studies with expanded GWAS data may refine these estimates.

Although our MR analysis did not support a causal effect of PC on HOA, the limited number of IVs (n = 5) may have reduced statistical power to detect very small effects, as discussed above. Furthermore, the rapidly lethal nature of PC means that GWAS summary statistics are derived predominantly from prevalent cases that survived to study enrollment, potentially introducing survivorship bias and limiting generalizability to the most aggressive or terminal stages of the disease. We also acknowledge that undiagnosed PC might noncausally influence osteoarthritis symptoms (e.g., through weight loss, cachexia, or altered physical activity), but such reverse-causation concerns are unlikely to affect MR estimates because genetic variants are fixed at conception and independent of disease onset. Nonetheless, we emphasize that our reverse MR results should be considered provisional and warrant replication in larger, more diverse datasets.

This study has several strengths: we apply the two-sample MR method to explore the causal relationship between HOA and PC. Compared with previous large-scale observational studies, MR studies are not affected by classical confounders (such as smoking) and reverse causation; multiple sensitivity analysis methods found no evidence of genetic heterogeneity and horizontal pleiotropy, confirming the robustness of the results; the magnitude of effect (OR = 1.788) positions HOA as a clinically relevant risk marker, comparable to certain established factors for PC. While further validation is needed, this supports enhanced vigilance in HOA patients within high-risk screening protocols.

However, this study also has several limitations: Ethnic generalizability remains a critical consideration. All genetic instruments were derived from European-ancestry GWAS datasets, which may limit direct extrapolation of our findings to non-European populations, given potential differences in allele frequencies, linkage disequilibrium patterns, and the epidemiology of both HOA and PC across ethnic groups. Therefore, our results should be interpreted with caution when applied to other ancestries. Future studies are warranted to validate the observed causal association in multi-ethnic cohorts, such as the African and Asian subsets of the UK Biobank or other large, diverse biobanks, to assess the consistency of the effect across different genetic backgrounds. The results of MR analyses are used to determine the direction of effects and to support causal inference and cannot be directly compared with effect estimates from clinical trials. The study cannot avoid the potential horizontal pleiotropy of unobserved IVs. The study included a limited number of IVs, necessitating further exploration in larger GWAS datasets in the future. The limited IVs for PC reduced power in reverse MR. Finally, the mechanistic interpretations offered in this study are speculative and derived from literature-based hypotheses rather than direct experimental evidence. We did not perform any in vitro, in vivo, or human tissue analyses to validate the involvement of SMAD3, TGF‑β, Wnt, RANKL, or lipid metabolism pathways in the observed association. Therefore, our proposed biological mechanisms should be viewed as hypothesis‑generating and require confirmation through dedicated molecular and pathological studies in HOA‑PC patient cohorts or preclinical models.

In conclusion, our findings provide genetic evidence supporting a causal association between HOA and an increased risk of PC. These results suggest that HOA may be considered a potential risk marker for PC. Accordingly, patients with HOA might warrant closer surveillance within existing high-risk screening frameworks, such as through abdominal imaging or biomarker testing. Given the moderate prevalence of HOA in elderly populations, targeted screening in this subgroup could be explored as a possible approach for early PC detection; however, further validation of its predictive utility and cost-effectiveness is required before any clinical recommendations can be made.
