Cholinergic basal forebrain degeneration in isolated REM sleep behaviour disorder
Authors: Ryul Kim, Bora Jin, Heejung Kim, Kyung Ah Woo, Eun Jin Yoon, Seoyeon Kim, Jung Hwan Shin, Hyunwoo Nam, Yu Kyeong Kim, Beomseok Jeon, Jee-Young Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough growing evidence suggests that cholinergic basal forebrain degeneration is linked to cognitive impairment and axial motor symptoms in Lewy body disorders, the cholinergic contribution to their prodromal phase remains largely unknown. Herein, we aimed to address three important yet unresolved problems focusing on prodromal Lewy body disorders: (i) to examine whether and where basal forebrain degeneration begins; (ii) to determine how such alterations are related to other brain morphometric changes and monoaminergic deficits; and (iii) to investigate the extent to which basal forebrain atrophy contributes to the clinical picture.We included 93 patients with polysomnography-confirmed isolated REM sleep behaviour disorder (iRBD), 33 with de novo Parkinson’s disease (PD) with a premorbid history of RBD (dnPDRBD) and 36 healthy controls. Participants underwent baseline assessments including volumetric MRI, 18F-N-3-fluoropropyl-2β-carboxymethoxy-3β-(4-iodophenyl)-nortropane PET scan, the Movement Disorders Society-Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale and neuropsychological evaluations. Regional volumes of cholinergic nuclei 1, 2 and 3 (Ch1–3) and cholinergic nucleus 4 (Ch4) were extracted using probabilistic maps, and voxel-based and surface-based morphometric analyses were applied to identify basal forebrain atrophy-associated cortical and subcortical regions. Subgroups of patients with iRBD underwent repeated motor and cognitive assessments (38 and 34 patients for 2 and 4 years, respectively).Among the basal forebrain complex, Ch4 volumes, but not Ch1–3 volumes, were significantly reduced in patients with iRBD. This reduction was positively correlated with limbic regions, including the amygdala and cingulate cortex, and, to a lesser extent, with the neocortical regions, particularly the frontal and temporal cortices. With respect to clinical symptoms, both Ch1–3 and Ch4 volume reductions were modestly associated with severe axial motor symptoms. Additionally, Ch1–3 volume reduction was associated with higher incidence of dementia and faster progression of memory impairment, whereas Ch4 volume reduction was associated with faster progression of limb bradykinesia. Using a multimodal imaging approach, we found that iRBD patients who later converted to PD showed predominant monoaminergic deficits but variable cholinergic involvement, and these patterns were similar to those observed in the dnPDRBD group. Conversely, iRBD patients who later converted to dementia with Lewy bodies showed predominant cholinergic deficits but variable monoaminergic involvement.This comprehensive analysis provides important implications for understanding how cholinergic basal forebrain degeneration is associated with brain morphometric changes, clinical outcomes and monoaminergic degeneration during the prodromal phase of Lewy body disorders.Keywords: REM sleep, Lewy body, cholinergic, basal forebrain, prodromal

Introduction

Lewy body disorders (LBDs) include Parkinson’s disease (PD) and dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB), in which the presence of α-synuclein-positive Lewy bodies and Lewy neurites in nigrostriatal dopaminergic neurons is considered their pathological hallmarks.1,2 However, autopsy and imaging studies have revealed significant concomitant degeneration not only in other monoaminergic systems, such as those involving serotonin and noradrenaline, but also in cholinergic systems.3-5 Accordingly, comprehensive investigation of these neurotransmitter system changes is crucial for advancing our insight into the pathomechanisms underlying LBDs.

The basal forebrain complex is the major source of cholinergic innervation for the human cerebral cortex, comprising four groups: the medial septum (Ch1) and the vertical limb of the diagonal band (Ch2) project to the hippocampus and hypothalamus, the horizontal limb of the diagonal band (Ch3) to the olfactory cortex and bulb, and the nucleus basalis of Meynert (Ch4) to limbic regions and most areas of the neocortex.5 Degeneration of basal forebrain cholinergic neurons and associated cortical and subcortical projections is a well-established pathological feature of the LBD spectrum, which is regarded as playing an important role in cognitive function in both the non-demented and demented stages of LBD.6,7 Furthermore, these cholinergic system changes are related to dopa-resistant motor features, including postural instability and gait difficulties (PIGD), in patients with PD.7,8 However, it remains unclear when and where basal forebrain degeneration begins in LBDs, how such alterations are linked to other brain structural changes and clinical evolution in their prodromal stages, and whether cholinergic and monoaminergic degeneration progress interdependently or independently over the disease course.

Isolated REM sleep behaviour disorder (iRBD) is a parasomnia characterized by loss of muscle atonia during REM sleep, which is regarded as the most robust clinical prodromal marker of LBDs identified to date.9 In this context, iRBD can represent a critical opportunity to clarify the above-mentioned uncertainties; however, clear evidence is still lacking. In previous studies using data from the Parkinson’s Progression Markers Initiative cohort, iRBD patients showed a significant decrease in the volume of Ch4, but not in the combined volume of Ch1, Ch2 and Ch3 (Ch1–3), in comparison to controls,10,11 and only Ch4 volume reduction was associated with poorer working memory/executive function10 and faster global cognitive decline.11 However, these findings were based on a small patient sample size (n = 35) and have not been validated in other datasets. Another study using voxel-based morphometry showed that there were no significant differences in the Ch1–3 and Ch4 regions between iRBD patients with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) (n = 17), those without MCI (n = 35) and controls.12 Although the impact of basal forebrain cholinergic degeneration on motor symptoms, especially axial symptoms, is well established in PD, whether this degeneration contributes to motor progression in the prodromal phase of the disease is unknown. Of note, the association of basal forebrain atrophy with brain morphometric changes and monoaminergic degeneration in patients with iRBD has not been explored.

In this study, we aimed to address important yet unresolved aspects regarding the association between cholinergic basal forebrain degeneration and prodromal LBDs through the following approaches. First, we compared the basal forebrain volumes among patients with iRBD, patients with de novo PD with a premorbid history of RBD (dnPDRBD) and healthy controls using volumetric MRI. We then performed voxel-based and surface-based morphometry analyses to explore structural changes in cortical and subcortical regions relevant to the basal forebrain atrophy. Second, we assessed the associations of the basal forebrain volumes with motor and cognitive function in patients with iRBD, using both cross-sectional and longitudinal data analyses. Lastly, we investigated the associations between the basal forebrain volumes and striatal and extrastriatal monoaminergic innervation integrity as measured by 18F-N-3-fluoropropyl-2β-carboxymethoxy-3β-(4-iodophenyl)-nortropane (18F-FP-CIT) PET scans.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

We generated two prospective cohorts of iRBD, in which all patients were confirmed to have RBD by video polysomnography and were free of parkinsonism and dementia at the time of enrolment. In the first cohort, we enrolled patients with iRBD and age-matched groups of dnPDRBD and healthy controls from the Seoul Metropolitan Government–Seoul National University Boramae Medical Center between 2013 and 2015.13,14 The second cohort has been enrolling patients with iRBD and age-matched healthy controls from the same hospital since 2018.15 For both cohorts, the same inclusion/exclusion criteria were applied to rule out secondary causes for RBD, as described previously.16 Patients with dnPDRBD were newly diagnosed according to the UK Brain Bank criteria and had probable RBD confirmed by a RBD screening questionnaire score of six or higher. Healthy controls were recruited from individuals who visited our centre for brain imaging as part of health check-ups. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrolment, and the study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board at Boramae Medical Center.

All participants in these cohorts were scheduled to undergo detailed clinical evaluations and MRI and 18F-FP-CIT scans at enrolment. Additionally, patients with iRBD also received follow-up clinical assessments biennially as part of the cohort protocol.15 Initial and follow-up assessments performed until January 2024 were included in the analysis.

Clinical evaluation

Overall parkinsonian severity was assessed using the Movement Disorder Society Unified PD Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS). To assess motor severity, we used the MDS-UPDRS Part 3 total score and its subscores, including bradykinesia total (items 3.4–3.8 and 3.14), limb bradykinesia (items 3.4–3.8), axial bradykinesia (item 3.14), rigidity total (item 3.3), axial (items 3.9–3.13), PIGD (items 3.10–3.12) and resting tremor (items 3.17 and 3.18) scores. The MDS-UPDRS Part 3 was performed either in a drug-naive state or after ≥12 h of dopaminergic medication discontinuation. Cognitive function tests applied were the Seoul Neuropsychological Screening Battery,17 including the Digit Span Test Forward–Backward and Trail-Making Test (TMT) Part A to assess attention function, the TMT Part B, Colour–Word Stroop Test (CWST), and phonemic and semantic items of the Controlled Oral Word Association Test to assess executive function, the immediate-recall, 20-min delayed recall, and recognition items of the Seoul Verbal Learning Test (SVLT) to assess verbal memory function, the copying item of the Rey–Osterrieth Complex Figure Test to assess visuospatial function, and the Korean version of the Boston Naming Test to assess language function. Raw neuropsychological test scores were converted to standardized z-scores adjusted for age, sex and educational levels using available normative data.17 MCI was defined according to the Level I category of MDS Task Force diagnostic criteria.18 Impairment on each neuropsychological test was defined as a z-score < −1.5.

In addition to baseline assessment data, we collected motor and cognitive function test results at the 2- and 4-year follow-up visits in patients with iRBD. Moreover, we followed the patients through in-person evaluations every 3–4 months for ≤10 years to identify phenoconversion to PD,19 DLB20 or multiple system atrophy.21

Brain MRI acquisition and analysis

Brain MRI was performed using a Philips Ingenia 3 T MRI scanner (Philips Medical Systems, The Netherlands). Volumetric MRI data were collected using a high-resolution 3D T1-weighted MRI sequence, with the following parameters: repetition time = 8.3 ms, echo time = 4.6 ms, slice thickness = 1 mm, flip angle = 8°, field of view = 110 mm2 × 110 mm2 and voxel size = 0.5 mm3 × 0.5 mm3 × 0.5 mm3. Preprocessing was conducted using the Statistical Parametric Mapping software (SPM12, Wellcome Department of Imaging Neuroscience, London, UK) running on MATLAB v.9.14 (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA). The images were automatically segmented into grey matter, white matter and CSF. Subsequently, affine registration to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) template space was performed, followed by non-linear normalization to standard stereotactic space using the DARTEL algorithm. The spatially normalized images were then smoothed using an 8 mm full-width half-maximum Gaussian kernel. To eliminate artefacts, an absolute threshold of 0.1 was applied to the grey matter segmentation.

Basal forebrain volume measurements were performed using the Computational Anatomy Toolbox (CAT12, Jena University Hospital) running under the SPM12. The basal forebrain can be divided functionally into an anterior part (Ch1–3) and a posterior part (Ch4).5 Accordingly, we extracted separately the combined volume of Ch1–3 and the volume of Ch4 using an established automated volumetry approach in combination with stereotactic probabilistic maps of Ch1–3 and Ch4 in the MNI space. These probabilistic maps have been derived from combined histology and MRI of post-mortem brains.22 We used total intracranial volume-normalized Ch1–3 and Ch4 volumes for each participant, which were calculated by dividing the sum of the left and right volumes by the total intracranial volume and multiplying by 1000.

18F-FP-CIT PET image acquisition and analysis

The 18F-FP-CIT PET was performed using a PET/CT scanner (Gemini TF64, Philips Healthcare, Best, The Netherlands) to measure the integrity of monoaminergic innervation. Participants received an intravenous injection of 185 MBq 18F-FP-CIT and subsequently underwent 10-min-emission scans after 2 h. 18F-FP-CIT PET images were reconstructed by applying a 3D row action maximum-likelihood algorithm set to 90 2-mm-thick slices in a 128 × 128 matrix, with corrections for attenuation and scatter. In this analysis, the anatomical regions of interest (ROIs) were the nucleus accumbens, amygdala, globus pallidus, caudate nucleus, anterior and posterior putamen, substantia nigra, subthalamic nucleus and cerebellum (as a reference region). These regions were segmented on individual T1-weighted MR images of each participant using the automated tool FIRST in the FSL v.5.0.2 package (https://fsl.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl/docs/#/). The putamen was separated into the anterior and posterior regions using the midpoint of its longitudinal axis. Monoaminergic innervation integrity was assessed as the 18F-FP-CIT specific uptake value ratio (SUVR), which was extracted from volumes of interest in the native space of each individual. The 18F-FP-CIT SUVR for each region was calculated as follows: (18F-FP-CIT SUV in target region/18F-FP-CIT SUV in the cerebellum) − 1. These data for all participants were collected in a drug-naive state or after ≥12 h of medication withdrawal. In this study, we used the average 18F-FP-CIT SUVR of the left and right sides for participants to align with the MRI data analysis.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using R v.4.2.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The data are presented as the means, standard deviations and frequencies. The Shapiro–Wilk test was performed to test the normality of the data. Demographic and clinical characteristics between the dnPDRBD, iRBD and healthy control groups were compared using one-way ANOVA, the Kruskal–Wallis test, Mann–Whitney U-test or the χ2 test, as appropriate. Comparisons of basal forebrain volumes and monoaminergic innervation integrity between the groups were performed using non-parametric analysis of covariance adjusted for age and sex, followed by Bonferroni post hoc corrections.

In analyses within the iRBD group, we used standardized z-scores adjusted for age and sex for MRI and 18F-FP-CIT PET imaging markers. The z-scoring procedure was performed using healthy controls as the reference population. Bivariate correlations between basal forebrain volume z-scores and outcome measures at baseline were investigated using Spearman’s rank-correlation coefficient. The comparison of basal forebrain volume z-scores according to the presence of MCI was performed using an analysis of covariance adjusted for the MDS-UPDRS Part 3 total score to minimize the influence of motor severity. Linear mixed-effects models with participants as a random effect of intercept were applied to investigate the association of basal forebrain volume z-scores with longitudinal changes in outcome measures. These models included baseline basal forebrain volume z-scores, follow-up time, their interaction, baseline age, sex, use of cholinesterase inhibitors at follow-up (for cognitive outcomes only) and educational levels (for cognitive outcomes only). Although the correlation and linear mixed-effects analyses included multiple clinical variables, adjustments for multiple comparisons were not primarily applied to minimize the risk of type II error given their exploratory nature. However, Bonferroni post hoc corrections (9 variables for motor function and 11 for cognitive function) were conducted as needed to evaluate the robustness of the findings. We used the Kaplan–Meier method with the log-rank statistic to assess differences in the cumulative risk of dementia and phenoconversion between groups with reduced basal forebrain volumes (z-scores < 0) and normal volumes (z-scores ≥ 0). If appropriate, the Cox proportional hazards model was used to adjust for age, sex and educational levels (for dementia only) next. We used the Mann–Whitney U-test or Fisher’s exact test to compare clinical and imaging variables between subgroups based on the patterns of cholinergic and monoaminergic deficits. Two-sided P-values < 0.05 were regarded as statistically significant.

Voxel-based and surface-based analyses

We conducted two separate regression analyses using SPM12, with basal forebrain volume z-scores as an independent variable and voxel-based and surface-based morphometry data as a dependent variable in each analysis, respectively. The covariates included age, sex and total intracranial volume. For the voxel-based morphometry analysis, we applied an explicit mask excluding the basal forebrain, given that this region was used as the independent variable. To evaluate associations of varying statistical strength, we performed these analyses using both corrected and uncorrected thresholds. Family-wise error rate correction was applied to control multiple comparisons at the voxel level and surface vertex level, with a corrected threshold of P < 0.05. For the uncorrected analyses, a threshold of P < 0.001 was used. The cluster extent thresholds (k) were set to 100 for voxel-based analysis and 25 for surface-based analysis. Significant regions were anatomically localized using the automated anatomical labelling atlas 323 for voxel-based analysis and the Desikan–Killiany cortical atlas24 for surface-based analysis.

Results

Study population characteristics

A total of 164 participants (95 patients with iRBD, 33 with dnPDRBD and 36 healthy controls) were considered in this study. During a median follow-up of 3.2 years (mean, 3.7 years; range, 0.6–9.8 years), 19 patients with iRBD phenoconverted to PD, 8 to DLB and 2 to multiple system atrophy. Given that the present study targeted the prodromal phase of LBDs, we excluded the two patients who converted to multiple system atrophy, hence 93 patients with iRBD were finally included in this analysis. Among the PD converters, five developed dementia 2–4 years after their PD diagnosis. At baseline, volumetric MRI data were available for all participants, whereas 18F-FP-CIT PET data were missing for 5 patients with iRBD, 2 with dnPDRBD and 17 healthy controls. The average time interval between volumetric MRI and 18F-FP-CIT PET scans was 2.1 ± 2.0 months. Among the patients with iRBD, we obtained follow-up motor and cognition data at 2 and 4 years for 38 and 34 patients, respectively.

The iRBD patients had a mean age of 70.7 ± 6.1 years, with a mean RBD symptom duration of 5.0 ± 4.1 years. Of these, 48 (51.6%) were male (Table 1). The prevalence rate of MCI was 44.1%. Demographic and clinical characteristics of iRBD patients in the first and second cohorts are presented in Supplementary Table 1, demonstrating the comparability between the two cohorts. There was no significant difference in age and sex among the three groups. In the comparison between the dnPDRBD and iRBD groups, the MDS-UPDRS Parts 2 and 3 scores were significantly higher in the dnPDRBD group than in the iRBD group (P = 0.001 and P < 0.001, respectively), whereas the scores for MDS-UPDRS Part 1 and neuropsychological tests did not differ between the two groups. Among patients with iRBD, three received acetylcholinesterase inhibitors at the 2-year follow-up and two at the 4-year follow-up.

Basal forebrain volumes and striatal and extrastriatal monoaminergic integrity

Post hoc comparisons showed that both the dnPDRBD and iRBD groups had significantly decreased Ch4 volumes in comparison to the control group (P = 0.008 and P = 0.031, respectively) (Fig. 1A). No significant difference in Ch4 volumes was observed between the dnPDRBD and iRBD groups (P = 0.890). Regarding Ch1–3, the dnPDRBD group showed a trend towards reduced volumes compared with the iRBD and control groups (P = 0.085 and P = 0.096, respectively), although not statistically significant (Fig. 1A). There was no significant difference in Ch1–3 volumes between the iRBD and control groups (P = 1.000).

The 18F-FP-CIT SVUR results are shown in Fig. 1B. Post hoc comparisons showed that the dnPDRBD group had significantly decreased SUVRs in the globus pallidus, caudate nucleus, anterior and posterior putamen, substantia nigra and subthalamic nucleus compared with the iRBD and control groups. Overall, the iRBD group had intermediate SUVRs between those in the dnPDRBD and control groups in all striatal and extrastriatal ROIs. We found significant differences in globus pallidus and substantia nigra SUVRs between the iRBD and control groups (P = 0.009 and P = 0.002, respectively).

In the iRBD group, Ch4 volume z-scores were positively correlated with SUVR z-scores in the globus pallidus (r = 0.28, P < 0.001), caudate nucleus (r = 0.24, P = 0.022), anterior putamen (r = 0.24, P = 0.025) and posterior putamen (r = 0.23, P = 0.029) (Fig. 1C and Supplementary Table 2). However, no significant correlation was observed between Ch1–3 volume z-scores and SUVR z-scores in both striatal and extrastriatal ROIs.

Basal forebrain and other brain region changes: voxel-based and surface-based morphometry in iRBD

After identifying significant Ch4 atrophy in patients with iRBD, we performed further analyses to investigate the correlations between Ch4 volumes and other brain morphometric changes in these patients. The regression models using voxel-based morphometry with family-wise error rate correction revealed Ch4 volume z-scores positively correlated with bilateral amygdala (Fig. 2A and Supplementary Table 3). When an uncorrected threshold of P < 0.001 was applied, the extent of the associated areas was increased, and positive correlations were newly observed in the bilateral inferior temporal gyrus, left superior frontal gyrus, right precuneus, left middle temporal gyrus, bilateral precentral gyrus, bilateral lingual gyrus and left supplementary motor area (Fig. 2B and Supplementary Table 3).

In the regression models using surface-based morphometry, we found significant positive correlations between Ch4 volume z-scores and the anterior portion of the cingulate gyrus at a corrected threshold of P < 0.05 (Fig. 2C and Supplementary Table 4). At the uncorrected threshold of P < 0.001, additional positive correlations were identified in the left precentral gyrus, left superior frontal gyrus and right insula (Fig. 2D and Supplementary Table 4).

Basal forebrain volumes and cross-sectional and longitudinal clinical outcomes in iRBD

In baseline data analyses, significant correlations included lower Ch1–3 volume z-scores with higher body bradykinesia scores (r = −0.22, P = 0.043) and lower Ch4 volume z-scores with higher axial (r = −0.24, P = 0.026) and PIGD (r = −0.24, P = 0.027) scores and lower CWST z-scores (r = 0.22, P = 0.041) (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Table 5). However, these associations did not remain significant after adjustment for multiple comparisons using Bonferroni correction. In contrast, posterior putamen SUVR z-scores were significantly correlated with all motor function variables excluding limb bradykinesia and resting tremor scores (Fig. 3A). The presence of MCI was associated with a trend towards lower Ch1–3 volume z-scores (P = 0.093) but was not associated with Ch4 volume z-scores (P = 0.870) (Fig. 3B).

In longitudinal data analyses, lower Ch1–3 volume z-scores were associated with a greater increase in PIGD scores [estimate: −0.09, 95% confidence interval (CI): −0.16 to −0.02, t = −2.55, P = 0.012], whereas lower Ch4 volume z-scores were associated with greater increases in MDS-UPDRS Part 3 total (estimate: −0.34, 95% CI: −0.62 to −0.05, t = −2.34, P = 0.021), bradykinesia total (estimate: −0.46, 95% CI: −0.70 to −0.21, t = −3.72, P < 0.001), limb bradykinesia (estimate: −0.41, 95% CI: −0.63 to −0.18, t = −3.57, P < 0.001), body bradykinesia (estimate: −0.05, 95% CI: −0.09 to −0.01, t = −2.57, P = 0.011) and axial (estimate: −0.094, 95% CI: −0.178 to −0.009, t = −2.19, P = 0.030) scores over time (Table 2). Following adjustment for multiple comparisons, the associations of lower Ch4 volume z-scores with longitudinal changes in bradykinesia total (Bonferroni-corrected P = 0.003) and limb bradykinesia (Bonferroni-corrected P = 0.004) remained significant. With respect to cognitive function, lower Ch1–3 volume z-scores were associated with a steeper decline in SVLT-delayed recall (estimate: 0.084, 95% CI: 0.004 to 0.163, t = 2.09, P = 0.039) and SVLT-recognition (estimate: 0.11, 95% CI: 0.03 to 0.18, t = 2.99, P = 0.004) z-scores (Table 3). The association between lower Ch1–3 volume z-scores and longitudinal changes in SVLT-recognition showed persistent significance even after post hoc adjustment (Bonferroni-corrected P = 0.044). We did not find any significant effects of Ch4 volume z-scores on longitudinal changes in cognitive assessments.

Distinct patterns of cholinergic and monoaminergic degeneration in iRBD

For this analysis, we divided iRBD patients based on Ch4 volume z-scores and posterior putamen SUVR z-scores (Fig. 5A). As a result, there were three patterns of cholinergic and monoaminergic degenerations in this group. We found 13 patients with decreased Ch4 volumes/normal posterior putamen SUVR (isolated cholinergic deficits), 21 with normal Ch4 volumes/decreased posterior putamen SUVR (isolated monoaminergic deficits) and 40 with decreased Ch4 volumes/decreased posterior putamen SUVR (both deficits). Patients with isolated monoaminergic deficits also had significantly lower SUVRs in all other ROIs, except for the subthalamic nucleus, in comparison to those with isolated cholinergic deficits (Supplementary Table 6). The isolated cholinergic deficits group showed lower PIGD scores (P = 0.014) but poorer performance on the TMT Part B (P = 0.040) and CWST (P = 0.016) tests compared with the isolated monoaminergic deficits group (Supplementary Table 7). The dnPDRBD group showed heterogeneous Ch4 volume z-scores, while posterior putamen SUVR z-scores were consistently decreased (Fig. 5A). A similar pattern was observed in PD converters, although the decrease in posterior putamen SUVR z-scores was less marked than in the dnPDRBD group. Conversely, all DLB converters, except for one, had decreased Ch4 volume z-scores but heterogeneous posterior putamen SUVR z-scores (Fig. 5B).

Discussion

This is the first comprehensive analysis to examine the associations of cholinergic basal forebrain degeneration with brain morphometric changes, clinical outcomes and monoaminergic degeneration in patients with iRBD. Our results have several important implications for understanding how cholinergic basal forebrain degeneration contributes to the prodromal phase of LBDs, as detailed below. The major strengths of this study include a relatively large iRBD sample size from a well-designed cohort and the availability of longitudinal clinical data.

We found that Ch4 volumes, but not Ch1–3 volumes, were significantly reduced in patients with iRBD, which is consistent with recent results from the Parkinson’s Progression Markers Initiative cohort data.10,11 These observations indicate that Ch4 is preferentially affected among the basal forebrain complex during the progression of LBDs. Supporting this hypothesis, a prior cholinesterase PET study showed lower neocortical binding in patients with iRBD compared with controls,25 suggesting neocortical denervation originating from Ch4 cholinergic neurons. Furthermore, a previous longitudinal study on PD showed that Ch4 atrophy was followed by longitudinal Ch1/Ch2 atrophy.26 A combined cholinergic PET and structural MRI analysis revealed marked atrophy in Ch4 and less pronounced atrophy in Ch1–3 in patients with DLB, whereas there was non-significant atrophy in Ch4 and no atrophy in Ch1–3 in patients with PD.6 These findings are in line with ours and suggest that Lewy pathology contributes to the cholinergic basal forebrain degeneration in a posterior-to-anterior gradient, given that Ch4 is located posterior to Ch1–3.

We observed robust associations between Ch4 volumes and the amygdala and cingulate cortex in patients with iRBD. In addition, associations with neocortical regions, especially within the frontal and temporal cortices, were also present but less pronounced. From these results, we can postulate that the cortical and subcortical degenerative changes in iRBD stages are driven, in part, by cholinergic basal forebrain degeneration. Moreover, the weaker relationships with neocortical areas might be attributed to the relatively mild degree of cortical atrophy typically observed during prodromal phase of LBDs, where neurodegeneration is thought to progress in a subcortical-to-cortical manner over time.27 Alternatively, Ch4 can be subdivided topographically into anterior (Ch4a), intermediate (Ch4i) and posterior (Ch4p) regions. Cholinergic neurons in the Ch4a project primarily to limbic structures, such as the amygdala and cingulate cortex; those in the Ch4i innervate much of the neocortex; and those in the Ch4p project to the superior temporal gyrus and temporal poles.28 Thus, the stronger associations with limbic regions might reflect a pattern in which Ch4 degeneration begins in the anterior portion rather than in the intermediate and posterior regions. The confirmation of this possibility requires precise volumetric measurements of Ch4 subregions, which are currently limited by the lack of reliable imaging methods.

Cross-sectional data analyses showed that basal forebrain atrophy was associated with more severe axial motor symptoms in patients with iRBD, although there was some heterogeneity between Ch1–3 and Ch4. However, these associations were statistically significant only in exploratory analyses, suggesting modest effects that were probably attributable to the heterogeneity of iRBD with respect to phenoconversion risk and the relatively mild motor severity compared with clinically established LBDs. In contrast, longitudinal data analyses showed that Ch4 atrophy was consistently associated with more rapid progression of bradykinesia, particularly limb bradykinesia, even after adjustment for multiple comparisons. Bradykinesia is known to be most strongly linked to nigrostriatal dopaminergic loss among the cardinal motor symptoms of PD.29 However, although dopaminergic medications significantly improve bradykinesia, they usually do not restore it to premorbid levels,30-32 which might suggest the co-involvement of non-dopaminergic pathways in the pathomechanism of bradykinesia. A previous cholinergic PET study on PD showed that distal limb bradykinesia was associated with cholinergic terminal deficits in globus pallidus pars externa and the paracentral lobule.33 Interestingly, a role for the cholinergic system in the primary motor cortex in relationship to bradykinesia has also been suggested in patients with Alzheimer’s disease.34 In the present study, we found that Ch4, but not Ch1–3, was associated with longitudinal progression of distal limb bradykinesia in patients with iRBD. This discrepancy might be explained by the fact that cholinergic neurons corresponding to Ch4 are connected to the neocortex, including the frontal regions,5 as also observed in this analysis. Taken together, our observations suggest that the cholinergic basal forebrain might worsen or contribute to some motor impairments even during the premotor stage before the onset of parkinsonism, potentially extending the current concepts on the association between central cholinergic deficits and motor symptoms in PD.7 These findings might also provide an explanation for why some patients with DLB who present with parkinsonism have normal dopamine transporter imaging.35 In such cases, motor symptoms might primarily reflect cholinergic rather than dopaminergic degeneration.

We did not find clear evidence of an association between the basal forebrain and cognition in patients with iRBD in the baseline data analyses, which might be explained, in part, by the exclusion of patients with significant cognitive impairment in the study design. However, the presence of MCI in these patients had a tendency towards lower Ch1–3 volumes and, more importantly, longitudinal data analyses revealed that lower Ch1–3 volumes were associated with an increased risk of developing dementia, but not with overall LBD phenoconversion. These findings suggest that Ch1–3, rather than Ch4, is more strongly connected to the progression of cognitive impairment during the prodromal phase of LBDs and that such changes might precede and potentially predict dementia regardless of disease subtype. The posterior-to-anterior pattern of basal forebrain degeneration indicates that reductions in Ch1–3 volumes might reflect more advanced cholinergic degeneration. Alternatively, among the Ch1–3, Ch1 and Ch2 are known to project predominantly to the hippocampus,5 which might account for the significant association between Ch1–3 and the longitudinal progression of memory impairment observed in our study. In addition, considering that memory decline best differentiates between dementia-first and parkinsonism-first phenoconversion in patients with iRBD,36 faster memory decline associated with Ch1–3 might suggest progression towards the DLB phenotype. In a previous cross-sectional study, reduced Ch4 volumes were associated with poorer performance on letter–number sequencing in patients with iRBD, indicating impairment in working memory/executive dysfunction.10 This finding is consistent, in part, with our baseline data showing that Ch4 was correlated only with CWST among various working memory/executive function tests.

Using a multimodal imaging approach, we observed that a major proportion of patients with iRBD exhibited deficits in both the cholinergic and monoaminergic systems. The correlations between Ch4 and some monoaminergic deficits were significant but weak, probably owing to the presence of isolated cholinergic or monoaminergic deficits in some patients. This variability resulted in two different subtypes: one characterized by predominant cholinergic deficits with heterogeneous monoaminergic involvement and the other by predominant monoaminergic deficits with heterogeneous cholinergic involvement. Of note, the latter pattern was observed in dnPDRBD patients and iRBD patients who later progressed to PD, whereas the former pattern was observed in patients with iRBD who ultimately developed DLB. Additionally, iRBD patients with isolated cholinergic deficits showed more severe impairment in executive function in comparison to those with isolated monoaminergic deficits. Executive dysfunction is regarded as one of the strong risk factors for the development of DLB.36 These observations suggest that PD and DLB are distinct entities, particularly with respect to their divergent trajectories of cholinergic and monoaminergic degeneration, despite significant overlap in the core neuropathophysiological changes underlying the two conditions. Such differences arise primarily from the distinct anatomical distribution of pathological deposition; however, variations in compensatory mechanisms between the dopaminergic and cholinergic systems also play a role, potentially leading to different thresholds for the onset of clinical symptoms. As noted in a previous paper,27 the pathology underlying DLB progresses along a time line similar to that of PD, whereas the clinical diagnosis of PD typically occurs earlier than that of DLB. In line with this, the phenoconversion rate to DLB was relatively low compared with that to PD, although central cholinergic degeneration was evident on volumetric MRI. This suggests that the cholinergic system might have a greater capacity to compensate for neurodegeneration than the dopaminergic system. Given the recent advancements in the diagnostic and prognostic utility of biofluid biomarkers in iRBD,37,38 future studies integrating dopaminergic and cholinergic imaging with biofluid biomarkers, such as α-synuclein seed amplification assays, amyloid and tau pathology markers and neurofilament light chain, hold promise for establishing a comprehensive framework for the classification of prodromal LBD and developing predictive models for progression to diverse neurodegenerative conditions.

The present study has several limitations. First, as with other volumetric measurements, basal forebrain volumes may not directly mirror cholinergic function. Furthermore, in addition to the Ch1–3 and Ch4 complex, the Ch5 and Ch6 complex of the upper brainstem are key components of the central cholinergic system, with evidence suggesting a potential role in LBDs.5 However, given the limitations of structural brain MRI in delineating these brainstem regions precisely, our analysis focused on the basal forebrain cholinergic system. Future studies should explore these aspects using molecular imaging of the cholinergic system in combination with structural brain MRI. Second, the number of phenoconverters in patients with iRBD was insufficient for subgroup analyses. Consequently, we were unable to explore whether cholinergic basal forebrain changes and their clinical implications differ between prodromal PD and DLB. Third, amyloid pathologies contribute to cholinergic basal forebrain atrophy and cognitive impairment in Alzheimer’s disease39 and are also commonly observed within the LBD continuum.40-42 In this context, it might mediate the relationship between cholinergic basal forebrain and iRBD observed in our findings, but we could not examine the potential impact of amyloid pathology in the present study design. Further longitudinal studies using amyloid imaging will be necessary to clarify this issue. Lastly, the diagnosis of RBD in the dnPDRBD group was based on a questionnaire, whereas all iRBD participants were confirmed by polysomnography. Although the RBD screening questionnaire used in this study is widely used for the screening of RBD in patients with clinically established PD, its diagnostic accuracy has certain constraints.43-45 However, a previous meta-analysis reported that a cut-off score of six on the RBD screening questionnaire yields a sensitivity of 77% and specificity of 88% in patients with PD.46 Given that the prevalence of polysomnography-confirmed RBD in PD is 40%–50%,47 the estimated positive predictive value is 81.1%–86.5%, suggesting that the majority of positive results from the RBD screening questionnaire are true positives in our dnPDRBD patients. Thus, this limitation is less likely to be significant in our results.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides three important aspects regarding the cholinergic basal forebrain degeneration and its associations with the other brain morphometric and monoaminergic changes and clinical progression during the prodromal phase of LBDs. First, our results suggest that Ch4 is preferentially affected within the basal forebrain complex and that its degeneration impacts the limbic regions and, to a lesser extent, the neocortical regions, particularly the frontal and temporal cortices. Second, basal forebrain degeneration in patients with iRBD was modestly associated with axial motor symptoms. However, the clinical significance of Ch1–3 and Ch4 might vary during the prodromal phase of LBDs. Specifically, Ch1–3 was more closely associated with cognitive impairment, particularly memory dysfunction, whereas Ch4 was more closely associated with limb bradykinesia. Third, the pattern of cholinergic and monoaminergic deficits might differ between prodromal PD and prodromal DLB. The iRBD patients who later converted to PD showed predominant monoaminergic deficits but variable cholinergic involvement, whereas those who later converted to DLB showed predominant cholinergic deficits but variable monoaminergic involvement. Further research using combined structural MRI and cholinergic PET imaging could provide valuable insights into the role of central cholinergic degeneration in the development and progression of LBDs.
