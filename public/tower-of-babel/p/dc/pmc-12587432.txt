Gaussian Mixture Modeling of MRI‐Derived Torus Tubarius and Inferior Turbinate Mucosal Morphometrics: Implications for Nasal Cycling and Eustachian Tube Interventions
Authors: Layth R Alkhani, Yusef Qazi, Siddhant Suri Dhawan, Paraag R Bhatt, Christopher J Shin, Tarik F Massoud
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveThickness of torus tubarius (TT) surface mucosa overhanging the Eustachian tube (ET) ostium, and mucosa covering the posterior aspects of bilateral inferior turbinates (ITs) are potentially pertinent to ET balloon tuboplasty. Whether TT and IT mucosal thickness is fixed or varies cyclically is potentially important in therapeutic planning but remains unknown. We establish normative MRI morphometrics for mucosa of TTs, posterior ITs (post‐ITs), and anterior ITs (ant‐ITs) in healthy subjects, and test the hypothesis that post‐IT and TT mucosal thickness may vary during the nasal cycle akin to known changes in ant‐ITs.MethodsWe retrospectively selected 60 subjects with normal nasopharynx MRIs, and measured maximal contrast‐enhancing mucosal thickness for bilateral TTs, post‐ITs, and ant‐ITs. We conceived a novel indirect test for nasal cycle variations by computing likelihood ratio (LR) statistics comparing unimodal versus bimodal Gaussian distributions of left–right mucosal thickness differences, with significance assessed using bootstrapped p values. We validated this test by confirming detection of known nasal cycling in ant‐ITs, and then applied it to assess potential cycling in TTs and post‐ITs. Significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultsMean maximal mucosal thicknesses for TTs, post‐ITs, and ant‐ITs were 3.1 ± 0.8, 7.6 ± 1.9, and 4.5 ± 1.3 mm, respectively. LR testing revealed significant cyclical variations in ant‐ITs (LR = 4.599; p = 0.021), thus validating our methodology for inferring nasal cycling. There was no mucosal cycling for TTs (LR = −0.801; p = 0.728) or post‐ITs (LR = −1.208; p = 0.799).ConclusionTT and post‐IT mucosa unlikely exhibit nasal cycle variations. Single MRI views of TTs and post‐ITs would suffice for assessment during pre‐interventional planning of ET procedures.Level of Evidence4.Keywords: middle ear, mucous membrane, nasal cavity, nasopharynx, turbinates

Introduction

The cartilaginous Eustachian tube (ET) can be difficult to characterize on imaging. This structure has a medial lamina extending anteriorly in a J‐shaped manner over the ET ostium to form a smaller lateral lamina of cartilage [1]. In doing so, the anterior end of the medial lamina bulges into the nasopharynx as the torus tubarius (TT), which also contains the levator veli palatini muscle more posteriorly. The cartilage of the lateral lamina forms the roof of the ET and is rich in elastin to aid as a hinge during ET opening [1]. Endoscopic and imaging morphologies of the adjacent TT (as it sits anterior to the fossa of Rosenmϋller and posteromedial to the ET) are used to assist in judging the presence of any abnormality in the ventral cartilaginous ET and its ostium. However, the TT is covered with a layer of mucosa composed of pseudostratified ciliated columnar epithelium, which cannot be adequately defined on computed tomography imaging [1] but is visible on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) as an enhancing mucosal stripe after administration of contrast medium [2]. This is best outlined with additional suppression of fat signal on axial T1‐weighted images.

Other than the potential for the ET to facilitate rare direct spread of nasopharyngeal carcinoma toward the middle ear, the main ET disorders include ET dysfunction (ETD) [3], where there is failure of the cartilaginous ET to open and ventilate the middle ear (occurring in 1% of adults), and the less common patulous ET [4], which is failure of closure of the ET [5]. Both conditions lead to middle ear infection and cholesteatoma, and their treatment can be challenging. ET balloon tuboplasty plus stenting for ETD [6, 7, 8], and transnasal endoscopic [9] or CT‐guided percutaneous [10] injection of bulking agents into the TT for patulous ET are minimally invasive interventional options. However, to guide these therapeutic procedures, there are no studies to date evaluating the relative contribution of the surface mucosa covering the TT to the overall measured size of the TT. There has only been one prior literature account of TT mucosal dimensions, where a tangential comparison was made between normal individuals and those with chronic suppurative otitis media [2].

The goals of our study are twofold. First, to establish comprehensive normative age‐related MRI morphometrics of TT mucosa in healthy subjects to inform therapeutic planning of procedures on the adjacent ET ostium and the cartilaginous ET. Second, to test the novel hypothesis that mucosal thickness of each TT may vary cyclically with its contralateral counterpart, akin to nasal cycle changes in the nasal turbinates [11]. We base this hypothesis on knowledge that the autonomic nervous supply in both regions is similar, with the nasopharynx and nasal cavity being innervated by sympathetic nerve fibers (causing vascular constriction and relative mucosal thinning) originating in the superior cervical ganglia, and parasympathetic ones (causing vasodilation and relative mucosal thickening) originating in the pterygopalatine ganglia [12, 13]. This question of potential TT involvement in cyclical changes is clinically pertinent because, if present, the overall sizes of the TTs may not be fixed as they follow these physiological alterations. Consequently, this could be a potentially relevant consideration when assessing the size of the ET ostium and the TT prior to any therapeutic interventions. To test this hypothesis, instead of performing multiple but impractical repeated static imaging in the same individuals to detect the presence of temporal alterations in their TT mucosal thickness, we conceived the novel idea that the use of single timepoint images from numerous subjects and subsequent statistical analysis would represent an alternative surrogate methodology to indirectly assess the extent of potential maximal and minimal mucosal swelling for right and left TTs. This, in turn, would help infer the presence of any potential cyclical variations that may be present in bilateral TT mucosa.

Materials and Methods

We obtained Institutional Review Board ethical approval, including waiver of individual authorization for recruitment and informed consent pursuant to Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act information in the protocol application. In this case–control study on adult patients accrued retrospectively over 9 months, we randomly selected and then reviewed 60 consecutive adult patients based on availability of normal axial and coronal fat‐suppressed post‐contrast MR images clearly showing the nasopharyngeal structures, and that were all reported by multiple clinical neuroradiologists as being normal. These images were obtained from different dedicated MRI studies of the head and neck, including those of the oral cavity (31 studies), parotid (16 studies), paranasal sinuses (11 studies), nasopharynx (1 study), and larynx (1 study), all using the “hard palate line” as the anatomical reference landmark for determining axial plane of scanning. Patients were excluded if their imaging revealed structural abnormalities owing to trauma, infection, inflammation, neoplasms, oronasal intubation devices, prior surgery, chemotherapy or radiotherapy, and if the axial imaging plane deviated from the hard palate line.

We obtained all MRI scans on 3 T MR units (Discovery MR450, Optima MR450, or Signa HDx from GE Medical Systems, Milwaukee, MN), using standard 21‐channel or 48‐ channel coils (GE Medical Systems, Milwaukee, MN). Intravenous gadolinium was administered to patients for MRI contrast enhancement at the standard dose of 0.1 mmol/kg of body weight. Imaging parameters for the selected T1‐weighted MR images from these various MRI studies included: echo time (TE) = 10–15 ms; repetition time (TR) = 500–700 ms; matrix size = 256 × 256 or 320 × 320; slice thickness: 3 mm with a 1 mm gap; field of view = 18–20 cm; flip angle = 70–90 degrees; bandwidth = 100–200 Hz/pixel; and number of averages (NEX) = 2–3.

We first recorded patient demographics, and then a senior neuroradiologist and a senior trainee (34 and 5 years' experience, respectively) concurrently and collaboratively analyzed together all anonymized retrospectively obtained images on a Sectra PACS review workstation (Linköping, Sweden). We used click‐and‐drag electronic calipers in the PACS built‐in image measurement tools to obtain all morphometric measurements on the digital images that optimally showed the enhancing mucosal stripes of bilateral TTs on axial images (Figure 1A,B), and of the anterior and posterior aspects of bilateral inferior turbinates (ITs) on coronal images (Figure 1C–E). We obtained bilateral measurements for lengths of ETs, widths of the TTs, as well as the maximum mucosal thickness of TTs, and that of the anterior and posterior aspects of the ITs. Any minor dissimilarities arising between raters (notably, there was an excellent reliability coefficient of > 0.90 for our first‐time agreements) as we jointly performed the quantitative, objective/unambiguous measurements, were discussed and settled by additional consensus agreement.

Using Microsoft Excel, 2021, v16.0 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond WA), we statistically tested: (1) differences between measured characteristics in females and males using a Welch's t test (two‐sample t test assuming unequal variances); (2) the effect of patient sex as an independent variable on measured dependent variables using ANOVA; (3) the effect of age as an independent variable for these measured dependent variables using a linear regression test; (4) linear relationships between the continuous variables of right and left TT mucosal thickness, and ipsilateral and contralateral IT mucosal thicknesses using a pairwise Pearson correlation test; and (5) detection of cyclical variations in mucosal thickness using Gaussian mixture modeling and likelihood ratio (LR) testing.

For analysis of cyclical variations using LR testing we computed the difference between right and left mucosal thickness values (D = R − L) for each structure and tested whether these differences followed a unimodal normal distribution centered around 0 (indicating no cycling) versus a bimodal mixture of normal distributions centered around +μ and −μ (indicating cyclical behavior). We formulated our null hypothesis (H 0) as the absence of cycling, and the alternative hypothesis (H 1) as the presence of cycling.

A low LR would indicate that the mixture distribution does not provide superior explanatory power compared to the unimodal distribution, whereas a high LR would suggest a substantially better fit with the mixture distribution. Statistical significance was assessed using bootstrapped p values (n = 1000 resamples). We validated this methodology by first confirming detection of nasal cycling in anterior ITs, where its existence is already established based on prior accounts of nasal mucosal physiology [11], before applying it to TTs and posterior ITs. For all tests, statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Patient age and sex distributions, and the means, standard deviations, and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for all measured characteristics are shown in Table 1. There were 31 female and 29 male patients, with an overall mean age of 56.6 ± 15.1 years, 95% CI [52.7–60.5]. Mean length for all cartilaginous ETs was 17.0 ± 2.9 mm, 95% CI [16.5–17.5]; and mean width for all TTs was 5.0 ± 1.1 mm, 95% CI [4.8–5.2]. Mean maximal mucosal thicknesses for bilateral TTs, posterior ITs, and anterior ITs were 3.1 ± 0.8 mm, 95% CI [3.0–3.3]; 7.6 ± 1.9 mm, 95% CI [7.2–7.9]; and 4.5 ± 1.3 mm, 95% CI [4.7–4.3], respectively. Of note, the mean mucosal thickness of posterior ITs was larger than that of the anterior ITs. This is because the more posterior IT mucosa was consistently found to protrude off the inferior concha as it projected posteriorly toward the nasopharynx (Figure 2), whereas the more anterior IT mucosa (as it encroaches on the space of the respiratory nasal cavity) was always located parallel to the bony margin of the inferior concha.

Males had significantly longer left ETs (17.9 ± 3.3 mm, 95% CI [16.7–19.2]) vs. females (16.4 ± 2.2 mm, 95% CI [15.6–17.2]) (p = 0.046) (Table 1). The overall difference between the means for all (longer) ETs in males and (shorter) ETs in females was also significant (p = 0.006). Similarly, the differences between the thicker mucosa of the posterior ITs in males than in females were also significant (p = 0.011). The maximal mucosal thickness for bilateral TTs was positively correlated with age in females (p = 0.010). Otherwise, there were no other statistically significant correlations between age and each measured characteristic for either males or females.

On both sides, TT maximal mucosal thickness did not significantly correlate with ipsilateral TT width or ET length, for both sexes combined (not shown in Table 1). There was a significant correlation between bilateral values for TT maximal mucosal thickness (R 2 = 0.41; p = 0.000) (Figure 3A,B). Additionally, there were significant correlations between values for maximal mucosal thickness for right and left posterior ITs (p = 0.003), right posterior and anterior ITs (p = 0.000), and left posterior and anterior ITs (p = 0.000) (Table 1).

Gaussian mixture modeling and likelihood ratio testing revealed distinct patterns of cyclical behavior across the measured structures. As expected, anterior ITs demonstrated significant cyclical variations (LR = 4.599; p = 0.021) (Figure 4A). In contrast, both TTs (LR = −0.801; p = 0.728) (Figure 4B) and posterior ITs (LR = −1.208; p = 0.799) (Figure 4C) showed no evidence of cyclical thickness variations. The negative LR values for TTs and posterior ITs indicate that a simple unimodal normal distribution provided a better fit to the left–right difference data than a bimodal mixture model, supporting the absence of cycling behavior.

The models demonstrate how the anterior IT mucosa exhibits significant nasal cycle variations whereas the TT and posterior IT mucosa are unlikely to do so.

Discussion

There are limited effective therapeutic options for patients with ETD [7, 14]. Other than previously performed surgical myringotomy and tympanostomy tube placement, now considered to be a suboptimal strategy for ETD, some other prior therapies of historical note included transtubal insertion of gold threads, transmastoid shunting, and laser tuboplasty [7]. In some patients there is redundant mucosa at the ET orifice preventing its proper opening, and the use of laser [15] or microdebridement [16] to ablate this tissue has shown promise for a few patients in small studies. However, since 2010, therapies have focused on tuboplasty of the cartilaginous ET by cannulation with a balloon catheter via the nasopharynx [6], and a number of studies have reported the efficacy of this minimally invasive treatment in relieving ETD symptoms [8].

A further conceptual and practical elaboration on the use of ET balloon dilatation tuboplasty for ETD has been offered by Di Rienzo Businco et al. [7]. They put forward the notion that the underlying tubal dysfunction results not only from inflammation in ET mucosa requiring balloon dilatation, but also from mucosal inflammation and thickening of the TT at the ET ostium, as well as in the posterior part of the IT. They advocate that the latter two components of this triad also require additional mucosal decongestion and shrinkage, and in their practice, they have used quantic molecular resonance to apply high‐frequency electromagnetic waves to the surface mucosa of the TT and IT after ET tuboplasty [7].

Hence, there is a clear need to better understand the characteristics and behavior of the TT and IT mucosa in relevance to these described therapeutic techniques for ETD. This would also be applicable to procedures for patulous ET and in the rare instances of performing TT surgical resection for mucosal hypertrophy [17]. Our results for TT maximal mucosal thickness (3.1 ± 0.8 mm) are similar to those recorded by Terzi et al. [2]. We also show that TT maximal mucosal thickness is positively correlated with age in females, which is an unexpected and possibly counterintuitive finding but may be related to perimenopausal hormonal surges in the age group of our female patients (57.2 ± 14.4 years).

Three other important sets of correlated findings also emerge from this study. First, we used a novel statistical approach of LR testing to indirectly assess whether TT mucosal thickness exhibits cyclical variations by comparing unimodal versus bimodal distributions of left–right thickness differences. The negative LR value with a nonsignificant p value indicates that a simple normal distribution provided a better fit than a bimodal mixture model, thus providing statistical evidence against cyclical changes in TT mucosal thickness. Second, posterior ITs similarly showed no evidence of cyclical behavior, contrasting with the significant nasal cycle variations detected in anterior ITs, which are well‐established in the literature [11]. Third, we observed positive correlations between values for right and left TT maximal mucosal thickness, and between each unilateral anterior and posterior IT maximal mucosal thicknesses.

The nasal cycle is a poorly understood physiological phenomenon under autonomic control resulting in a natural change in airflow between nostrils; its functional purpose is still being debated but may be related to air warming and humidification, mucociliary clearance, and possibly aiding in olfaction. Our finding that anterior ITs exhibit significant cyclical variations while posterior ITs do not behave in a similar manner supports the notion suggested by others that the nasal cycle involves the anterior aspects of the nasal septum and ITs [11]. We here provide evidence from morphometric data and statistical analysis showing specifically that nasal cycle variations may not extend throughout the entire IT structure.

The factors involved in this disparity in mucosal cycling between anterior ITs, posterior ITs, and TTs remain unclear. We speculate that this might possibly be related in part to: (1) documented inherent asymmetry in autonomic supply of the nasopharynx—the sympathetic nerve fibers originate in the ipsilateral superior cervical ganglion, whereas parasympathetic ones do so in bilateral pterygopalatine ganglia [12]; (2) some 80% of individuals exhibit an irregular nasal cycle while the remaining 20% have irregular fluctuations of ipsilateral turbinates without corresponding alternating changes in the contralateral turbinate [18]; and (3) variations in nasal airflow during the nasal cycle can alter the concentration of inhaled nitric oxide originating in the paranasal sinuses, a molecule involved in the regulation of nasal blood flow and modulation of epithelial function, with these changes possibly extending to the nasopharynx and influencing its physiological environment [19]. Future additional research is required to examine the precise mechanisms that may be involved in the interplay of these various mechanisms with variations in mucosal thickness of the TTs and ITs as they possibly impact therapeutic procedures on the ETs.

We note several limitations of our study that have been alluded to earlier. These include: (1) Its retrospective nature. (2) Reliance on indirect evidence using statistical analysis of static images for TT and IT mucosal thickness. However, we point out that this is an alternative to repeated imaging aimed at direct assessment of dynamic morphometric mucosal changes, which would be laborious and unfeasible (as nasal cycles can vary in duration from 25 min to 8 h, reviewed in Kahana‐Zweig et al. [11]). (3) Lack of functional verification for some of the mechanisms we have considered in explaining our correlative results. (4) The spatial resolution on our MR images was slightly limited by their slice thickness of 3 mm. However, as the mean maximal mucosal thickness values we obtained were all above 3 mm, we believe that reduced measurement precision and inaccuracies owing to partial volume averaging would not be a significant limitation for most measurements. (5) We also do not provide histological verification of mucosal thickness, although we used contrast‐enhanced T1‐weighted images for morphometrics and avoided using T2‐weighted images that tend to exaggerate thickness compared to histology [20]. (6) Other factors such as smoking history, allergic rhinitis, reflux and history of ETD, and prior ear surgery are also important factors that may influence mucosal thickness and should be considered and investigated in future studies. Nonetheless, we believe our findings will be useful to stimulate further research and are important and practically pertinent to ET therapeutic procedures.

Conclusion

We report the normative MRI dimensions for TT and IT surface mucosa. The novel application of Gaussian mixture modeling as a statistical technique to evaluate these dimensions reveals that the surface mucosa of TTs and posterior ITs is unlikely to participate in the cyclical variations of the nasal cycle that affect the anterior ITs. Therefore, the mucosal thickness at and near the ET ostium likely remains constant. This is reassuring during the planning of ET procedures because single timepoint imaging views of this region would be sufficient for pre‐interventional assessment. The reported morphometrics for mucosal thickness of TTs and ITs will be useful in pre‐procedural MRI planning for therapeutic interventions on the ETs.
