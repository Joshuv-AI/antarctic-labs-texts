Importance of early prehospital intervention in crush injury and compartment syndrome: a case report
Authors: Zeyen Seah, Julie Wulandari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCrush injuries are commonly associated with natural disasters or human-made mass casualty events, affecting millions annually. Reports on non-disaster-related cases are limited.Case presentationWe present the case of a non-disaster-induced crush injury in which a Chinese patient’s arm was trapped in a toilet drain for 15 hours. Emergency medical services and a hospital medical team were activated to extricate the patient and demonstrated preparedness to address potential complications before transfer to the emergency department for further management.ConclusionThis case highlights the importance of adapting emergency department management protocols for prehospital settings to optimize patient care and outcomes.

Introduction

Crush injuries are frequently reported in natural disasters or human-made mass casualty events, and they affect millions of people every year [1]. They are caused by prolonged direct pressure on muscle tissue [2], resulting in ischemia and the release of intracellular contents into the bloodstream upon reperfusion. The most commonly affected sites are the lower limbs, followed by the upper limbs and trunk, with the degree of damage directly proportional to the bulk of muscle damaged. The mainstay of treatment is to emphasize the prevention of acute renal failure by initiating intervention early, even before extrication.

In large-scale civil emergencies, appropriate equipment is typically available, and the environment is often already compromised, reducing concerns about causing further destruction. Non-disaster-related crush injuries, particularly those occurring in residential areas, present unique challenges. To date, there is relatively limited literature on non-disaster-related crush injuries [3–5]. This case report highlights the obstacles faced by the field team and how they successfully implemented an ED level of care in the prehospital setting.

Case presentation

The emergency medical support team (EMST) was called to provide assistance at a home on the 9th floor in a 49-year-old Chinese man’s flat due to prolonged arm entrapment. His tenant discovered him on the bathroom floor with his right arm trapped in the toilet drain. He had been attempting to retrieve some cash he had dropped inside and had been stuck in that position for 15 hours. Upon arrival at the scene, the bottom half of the toilet pipe (on the 8th floor) had already been removed by the disaster assistance and rescue team (DART). Fluids were being administered through an 18G intravenous (IV) cannula inserted into the unaffected left arm by paramedics.

Assessment of the right arm revealed extensive desquamation distal to the obstructing drain. There were multiple blisters on the right forearm, hand, and fingers, as shown in Fig. 1. The patient was alert and conscious, reporting a loss of power and sensation distal to the obstruction. The compartments of the arm and forearm remained supple, and the radial pulse was strong. A total of 10 mg of IV morphine was administered for pain relief, along with three doses of IV calcium gluconate 10 mg. Drilling was performed to loosen the drain from the surrounding tiles. Upon the patient’s arrival in the ED, the initial point-of-care blood gas analysis showed a potassium level of 4.6 mmol/L. He was started on a ketamine infusion for pain control. The obstructing pipe was subsequently removed using a drill by the DART. His right axillary tourniquet was gradually released over 5 minutes. Continuous telemetry monitoring showed no widening of the QRS complex during the procedure. When the level increased from 4.8 mmol/L to 5.1 mmol/L, the patient promptly received a 40 ml bolus of dextrose 50% along with 6 units of short-acting insulin. His initial calcium levels were 2.07 mmol/L [2.1–2.6 mmol/L]. On the second day of admission, he reported worsening swelling of his entire arm, and compartments were noted to be firm and tense. He underwent a right upper limb fasciotomy, followed by multiple wound debridements. He eventually had VAC dressings applied to three of his fasciotomy wounds (two on the upper arm and one on the forearm) owing to persistent purulent discharge and poor healing. His condition was further complicated by rhabdomyolysis, which was treated aggressively with hydration and sodium bicarbonate. His urine appeared dark brown and concentrated, and the dipstick test was positive for blood (5+) with only 5 red blood cells (RBCs)/HPF on microscopy—findings consistent with myoglobinuria. His creatine kinase (CK) levels, as shown in Table 2, subsequently trended downward. He also underwent superficial skin grafting performed by the plastic surgery team. As the fasciotomy wounds were deep and involved a large surface area with exposed tendons, split-thickness skin grafts were harvested from his right thigh and applied to all three wound sites. His total length of hospital stay was 53 days, followed by 62 days of rehabilitation. At the time this manuscript was drafted, he had not regained power or sensation in his affected limb. After his discharge, he was referred to the hand surgery team for consideration of free muscle transfer as a reconstructive option to restore his upper limb function. Discussion

The stages of crush injury can be categorized into three pathophysiological phases, describing the cascade of cellular events that, if unaddressed, may ultimately lead to acute renal failure (ARF). The clinical spectrum includes local complications such as compartment syndrome, biochemical disturbances from rhabdomyolysis, and systemic effects characteristic of crush syndrome. This case illustrates how targeted prehospital intervention can alter this trajectory. Here, we outline the stages and their corresponding management principles.Compression phase: Prolonged and continuous compression of the muscle tissue leads to ischemia, necrosis, and increased compartmental pressures. In this patient, 15 hours of entrapment resulted in extensive soft tissue injury and signs consistent with compartment syndrome. Elevation of intracompartmental pressure impairs venous drainage, creating a viscous cycle that can further exacerbate ischemia. Compartment syndrome is identified clinically using the “6 Ps” (pain, pallor, paraesthesia, paralysis, pulselessness, and poikilothermia). While fasciotomy is the definitive treatment, current literature does not recommend prophylactic fasciotomy without objective evidence of elevated compartmental pressures owing to its high risks of infection, bleeding, and limb loss [1]. Therefore, the focus during the prehospital phase should remain on early fluid resuscitation and effective analgesia.Reperfusion phase: Upon the release of the crushed extremity, reperfusion of the ischemic limb may result in the sudden release of intracellular toxins into the circulatory system, including potassium, phosphate, and myoglobin. This can lead to hyperkalemia, metabolic acidosis, myoglobinuria, and life-threatening cardiac arrhythmias. This effect can be temporized by applying a tourniquet proximal to the site of compression, to delay the onset of reperfusion injury [7, 8]. The use of tourniquets remains controversial, with other sources suggesting that a tourniquet should only be used as a last resort to control life-threatening hemorrhage when direct pressure is ineffective [9]. In this case, it can be postulated that, despite 15 hours of compression, the serum potassium levels measured were never significantly elevated owing to the prophylactic administration of three doses of IV calcium gluconate at the scene as well as gradual release of the tourniquet, both of which proved to be a fortunate intervention.Systemic manifestations: Without timely intervention, the cascade may progress to crush syndrome, a systemic condition characterized by rhabdomyolysis, metabolic acidemia, hypovolemic shock, and systemic inflammatory response syndrome leading to multi-organ failure [10]. In severe cases, renal replacement therapy may be necessary and life-saving. In this patient, rhabdomyolysis developed as evidenced by elevated CK levels, but early aggressive fluid therapy likely prevented further renal complications. Timely prehospital intervention beginning with fluid resuscitation followed by close biochemical monitoring in the ED played a critical role in preventing the development of full-blown crush syndrome.

Conclusion

This case illustrates the collaboration between the prehospital team, DART, and the EMST team in safely extricating the patient, resulting in a seamless transition from prehospital to ED care. By implementing interventions typically reserved for the ED, particularly the administration of IV calcium gluconate and early fluid resuscitation, the prehospital team was able to address potential severe metabolic derangements before they reached critical levels. Such coordinated efforts ensured timely and effective interventions, bridged resource gaps between field care and hospital management, and improved patient care and outcomes.
