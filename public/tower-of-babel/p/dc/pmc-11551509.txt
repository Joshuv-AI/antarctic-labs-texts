Direct transpapillary resection of a left hepatic duct polyp

Published: 2024

Introduction

Bile duct polyps (BDPs) are elevated growths belonging to common bile duct (CBD) mucosa1 that can in rare instances cause obstructive jaundice.2 Noninvasive diagnostic methods such as MRCP rarely provide a definitive diagnosis. EUS with fine-needle biopsy is emerging as a reliable diagnostic tool to establish a diagnosis of BDP.1 The latter step is crucial to plan patient management correctly and avoid unnecessary surgical interventions. To date, no indications are available regarding treatment of BDP; hence, endoscopic resection may be considered the primary option for minimally invasive treatment3, 4, 5 (Fig. 1).

Direct cholangioscopy using a front-view endoscope; approaching the lesion with a hot snare can be seen.

Case

A 78-year-old woman with severe chronic heart disease and end-stage renal disease was admitted to our hospital for abdominal pain and new-onset jaundice. Laboratory test results revealed direct hyperbilirubinemia with increased cholestatic enzymes. CT and MRCP showed dilation of the left intrahepatic ducts (IHDs) attributable to a 55- × 30-mm inhomogeneous polypoidal mass without liver infiltration or pathologic lymphadenopathies (Fig. 2). EUS with fine-needle biopsy confirmed the BDP arising from the left IHDs and extending over the proximal portion of the CBD (Fig. 3), and histology findings revealed a intraductal papillary neoplasm with high-grade dysplasia. ERCP with a biliary plastic stent was performed to decompress the biliary system. Considering the localized stage of BDP and after multidisciplinary discussion incorporating shared decision-making with the patient, who refused surgery, an endoscopic mini-invasive therapeutic approach was scheduled (Video 1, available online at www.videogie.org).

Procedure and outcomes

The procedure was started using a side-view duodenoscope under CO2, and endoscopic resection of the lesion was attempted by peroral cholangioscope. Only partial resection was achieved using the dedicated 9-mm retrieval snare, given its lower yield and the cold technique (Fig. 4). After up to 18-mm endoscopic balloon dilation of the major papilla, the lumen of the CBD was easily accessed by a slim gastroscope (distal end diameter 5.9 mm, working channel 2.4 mm), which reached the hepatic hilum through pulling and rotating movements. Afterward, direct vision of the lesion was obtained (Fig. 1), and the use of multiloop and monofilament hot-polypectomy (Endocut Q effect 2.0, interval cutting 6, cutting duration 2; forced coagulation effect 3.0) snares guaranteed a more efficient resection. However, the scope stability was poor. Hence, a diagnostic gastroscope (distal end diameter 9.9 mm, working channel 2.8 mm) was used to obtain a complete piecemeal resection (Fig. 5). No adverse events occurred during the resection. The pathologist reported intraductal papillary neoplasm with high-grade dysplasia and focal areas of adenocarcinoma.

Peroral cholangioscopy with cold endoscopic resection using a 9-mm dedicated retrieval snare (arrow): direct endoscopic (A) and fluoroscopic (B) views.

Endoscopic evaluation of the resection base at the end of the procedure.

After 3 months, a direct cholangioscopy showed a large fibrotic scar at the left IHD close to the confluence without clear evidence of local recurrence (Fig. 6). Given the presence of adenocarcinoma on the pathologic report and considering the piecemeal resection, gemcitabine-based chemotherapy was performed in the patient in 2 cycles with close oncologic follow-up taken.

A, Resection base after 3 months of follow-up. B, Schematic view of resection base.

Discussion

Although rare, BDPs and lesions have been reported1,5,6 in the literature. Regarding their management, no clear indications are available in literature, mainly because of their low incidence. Sturgis et al4 first reported a case of endoscopic resection of BDP, which they recommend is advisable if the diameter is ≥2 cm.7 Radical surgery carries increased risks of adverse events and greater morbidity.8 In our case, after precise prediagnostic work-up and staging, endoscopic resection was attempted, which achieved successful short-term outcomes despite the unknown defined radicality of the technique resulting from the piecemeal resection.

The use of different types of endoscopic instruments may facilitate the achievement of endoscopic resection, allowing lesions to be approached under direct view with good scope stability and using different standard polypectomy devices, reducing related adverse events. Considering the small operative field and the location inside the CBD, the possibility of temporary biliary metallic stenting was considered a valuable hypothetical tool to manage possible bleeding or perforation.9 Direct endoscopic resection for BDP is technically challenging but, in expert hands, may be feasible and safe, especially for lesions located at the proximal site of the biliary system and IHDs.

Our BDP was located at the left IHD and, to our knowledge, this is the first case of resection in the proximal biliary tract reported in the literature. In conclusion, BDP is a rare cause of obstructive jaundice, and a complete preoperative staging with radiologic images and high-quality EUS is a fundamental step to clear the patient for a mini-invasive approach.
