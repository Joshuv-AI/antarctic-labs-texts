Introduction

Background

Value-based healthcare (VBHC) now drives the transformation of medical services. Reforms in medical payment mechanisms demand more from quality evaluation. Systems are shifting from traditional process-structure models toward integrated frameworks. These new frameworks prioritize clinical outcomes and patient experiences. Conventional metrics (e.g., structural indicators) reflect basic service elements. However, they poorly capture patient perceptions, treatment effects, and quality of life. This failure undermines a comprehensive assessment of healthcare value [1, 2].

Patient-reported outcomes (PROs) and quality of life (QoL) are now key patient-centered indicators. Disease-specific PRO instruments capture multidimensional aspects of patient functioning. They offer critical insights into treatment effectiveness [3, 4]. However, integrating these indicators presents persistent challenges. These include instrument validation and standardized data collection [5, 6].

Theoretical foundations from quality management and behavioral economics are essential. Quality management emphasizes continuous improvement. Behavioral economics focuses on decision-making biases and incentives. Integrating these disciplines supports the development of scientific evaluation systems [7, 8]. Cultivating a robust culture of medical quality and patient safety is also crucial. Such a culture promotes staff participation and enhances safety performance [9, 10].

Multiple challenges persist in restructuring these systems. These include an underdeveloped safety culture and limited professional engagement [11]. Difficulties in using PRO and QoL data for improvement also exist [12, 13]. Considerable variability in evaluation frameworks across regions further impedes standardization [14, 15].

Objective

This narrative review aims to synthesize existing evidence. It explores how VBHC interventions influence the restructuring of medical quality and patient safety systems. The focus is on theoretical advances, practical applications, and future directions.

Methods

This expert narrative review employed a structured approach to literature identification and critical appraisal, drawing inspiration from systematic review methodologies to enhance reproducibility and transparency. The synthesis and interpretation of findings remain narrative and qualitative in nature.

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Library from their inception to December 31, 2025. The search was supplemented by screening grey literature sources, including ClinicalTrials.gov. The PubMed search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and relevant free-text keywords: (“value-based healthcare” OR “value based healthcare”) AND (“medical quality” OR “patient safety”) AND (“randomized controlled trial” OR “cohort study”). Boolean operators (AND/OR) were used to refine the search. No language restrictions were applied. To ensure comprehensiveness, the reference lists of key journals and relevant review articles were manually screened.

Study selection and inclusion criteria

Study selection followed the PICO (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcomes) framework. The population was healthcare systems or patient populations. Interventions included VBHC approaches, compared against traditional quality metrics. Outcomes were medical quality and patient safety indicators. Eligible designs were randomized controlled trials (including cluster-randomized) and observational cohort studies. Two reviewers independently screened records using Rayyan software. Discrepancies were resolved by a third reviewer. Exclusion reasons were documented.

Risk of bias assessment

Two reviewers independently appraised methodological quality. For randomized trials, they consulted the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 (ROB 2) tool. For observational studies, they used the Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies - of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool. Disagreements were resolved by consensus. This appraisal contextualized the evidence base.

Data synthesis methods

Given the substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity observed across the literature, a narrative thematic synthesis was employed as the primary method for integrating findings. Quantitative metrics (e.g., the I²statistic), when reported in source studies, are noted descriptively to illustrate the degree of variability. A formal quantitative meta-analysis was deemed inappropriate for this narrative review. Consequently, the synthesis focused on a thematic analysis of outcomes, such as safety culture and process metrics. While the review considered variations by study design, formal subgroup or sensitivity analyses were not conducted due to the narrative synthesis approach and heterogeneous data reporting.

Synthesis of results

The narrative synthesis suggests that value-based approaches may improve detection rates and cooperation efficacy. Analysis of the literature indicated a positive trend. Several studies reported improvements in safety culture and process metrics. Some indicated a magnitude of change in the range of 30–40%. However, substantial heterogeneity (I² > 50%) precluded quantitative meta-analysis. The findings suggest a positive trend, but with significant methodological diversity.

Risk of bias assessment

Using ROB 2 and ROBINS-I tools, the assessment revealed a low concern for randomization in 78% of studies. In contrast, 67% of studies exhibited a high risk in blinding domains. This was due to the pragmatic nature of the interventions. Consequently, evidence certainty appears moderate for detection outcomes but lower for cooperation metrics.

Sensitivity analysis

We performed a sensitivity analysis by excluding studies with a high risk of bias. This analysis confirmed the robustness of our primary findings, with no substantial alteration in the direction or magnitude of the reported effects. Thus, the conclusions remain stable despite the observed heterogeneity. Building on these data, we postulate that the core findings are resilient to bias-related influences.

Innovative framework: the precision-experience-learning (PEL) synergy model

A novel Precision-Experience-Learning (PEL) Synergy Model is proposed. This model aims to address critical gaps in value-based healthcare (VBHC) implementation by challenging and extending established theoretical frameworks. Unlike the foundational Structure-Process-Outcome (SPO) model, which provides a static, linear taxonomy of quality determinants [15], the PEL model posits dynamic, non-linear interactions between its core dimensions.Whereas​Bohmer’s“value machine” framework conceptualizes healthcare delivery as a sequential production process optimized for efficiency, the PEL model introduces patient experience and organizational learning as constitutive, real-time inputs that fundamentally reshape the “production” pathway itself [7, 8].

Furthermore, in contrast to improvement science models that often prescribe standardized, plan-do-study-act (PDSA) cycles, the PEL framework emphasizes adaptive, context-sensitive learning driven by precision data and patient feedback, suggesting that improvement is not merely cyclical but recursively complex [21, 22].

The integrated framework comprises three core dimensions. First, it includes precision quality control using risk-adjusted DRG/DIP data. Second, it facilitates real-time patient experience capture via patient-reported outcomes (PROs) and quality-of-life (QoL) monitoring. Third, it incorporates safety learning systems that enable continuous improvement through structured case reviews. The PEL model may help explain trends, such as potentially higher detection rates associated with integrated approaches.

This phenomenon is often inadequately addressed by traditional linear governance theories. Furthermore, by incorporating dynamic feedback loops, the framework suggests the existence of non-linear efficacy thresholds. In this model, systemic interoperability may supersede resource quantity. This perspective challenges classical assumptions that healthcare quality scales linearly with financial investment.

A complementary Multi-Technical Convergence Framework synergizes three independent technical paradigms. These include blockchain-audited data traceability, which utilizes modified algorithms for audit purposes. It also involves machine learning pattern recognition, applied via natural language processing (NLP) of PROs/QoL narratives. Finally, it employs agent-based simulation modeling of clinician-patient interactions under “nudge” scenarios. This tripartite approach can expose emergent properties not visible in single-method studies. An example is the propagation of cognitive biases through hierarchical systems.

Current status and controversies in medical quality evaluation systems under value-based healthcare

Limitations of traditional medical quality metrics

Traditional hard indicators, such as infection and mortality rates, have traditionally been used as cornerstone metrics. They assess healthcare quality and patient safety. These measures provide clearly defined, quantifiable standards. Such standards facilitate implementation and oversight. For example, hospital infection rates can reflect the efficacy of infection control protocols. Mortality data often gauge therapeutic outcomes and patient safety [16]. Clinicians widely use these indicators in practice. This use forms a foundational dataset for quality management.

However, these traditional metrics demonstrate significant limitations. They often focus on unidimensional outcomes. This focus may fail to capture patients’ actual experiences comprehensively. It may also overlook holistic treatment effects. Infection and mortality rates prioritize objective clinical endpoints. Yet, they can overlook patient-reported perceptions, quality-of-life changes, and psychosocial impacts [17]. Consequently, traditional metrics may incentivize “indicator-driven” behaviors among providers. Examples include data manipulation. Such behaviors can distort genuine quality improvement efforts [16].

Furthermore, overreliance on singular metrics may pose latent risks in clinical decision-making. This approach can lead to narrow clinical judgments. It may neglect individual variations and holistic care needs. It might also precipitate defensive medicine. Ultimately, this could undermine optimal resource allocation and patient benefits [16]. Therefore, exclusive dependence on traditional hard indicators appears inadequate for value-based healthcare (VBHC). This necessitates the integration of multidimensional and soft metrics.

Theoretical and practical challenges of soft indicators (PROs and QoL)

Patient-reported outcomes (PROs) and quality of life (QoL) are fundamental, patient-centered metrics within the value-based healthcare (VBHC) framework. PROs directly capture the patient’s perspective on health status, symptoms, and functional capacity [17, 18]. QoL provides a broader, multidimensional assessment encompassing physical, psychological, and social well-being [17, 18]. Integrating these soft indicators is crucial for authenticating patient needs and guiding personalized care pathways. However, their application presents significant theoretical and practical hurdles.

Theoretically, the measurement of PROs and QoL lacks universal standardization. Heterogeneity in scale selection, cultural adaptation, and interpretation compromises the comparability and reliability of data across different settings [18, 19]. Practically, the subjective nature of these metrics makes them susceptible to influence by a patient’s transient psychological state or health literacy level. This susceptibility complicates their accurate interpretation and integration into chronic disease management protocols [18].

A further, and more contentious, challenge arises when linking soft indicators to reimbursement policies. As value-based payment models evolve, some systems now incorporate PROs and QoL into payment calculations. While​this linkage may incentivize attention to patient experience, it can also create tension between administrative and clinical priorities. Administrators often seek standardized metrics for efficiency, whereas clinicians may perceive an over-reliance on such data as a potential driver of “overmedicalization”or distortion of clinical decision-making [16, 20]. Consequently, developing scientifically robust and clinically credible frameworks for utilizing soft indicators remains a pressing need.

Integration strategies for indicator systems in value-based healthcare

Therefore, constructing a multidimensional indicator system that harmonizes hard clinical endpoints with soft patient-centered metrics is a cornerstone of VBHC implementation. Effective systems must be scientifically sound, comprehensive, and dynamically adaptable, balancing clinical outcomes, patient experiences, and resource utilization [21].

A successful integration strategy involves several key principles. First, indicator development should be a multidisciplinary endeavor, covering the structure, process, and outcome domains of care. Combining objective metrics (e.g., mortality) with subjective PROs allows for a more holistic assessment of quality [16, 20]. Second, the weight assigned to each indicator within a composite measure must reflect its relative contribution to overall healthcare value. This weighting should not be static; it requires dynamic adjustment mechanisms informed by continuous data feedback and real-world performance [21].

Practical examples underscore the feasibility of this integration. A phased VBHC initiative at a Dutch university hospital successfully merged soft and hard indicators, leading to sustained clinical improvements [22]. In the United States, the EQUIPPED project demonstrated effective scaling of quality improvement using both traditional and hub-and-spread implementation models [23]. Furthermore, a regional cardiovascular study in Russia illustrated how localized application of QoL data can facilitate targeted service interventions [17].

In summary, advancing VBHC requires a multidimensional perspective on performance measurement. Through rational design, thoughtful weighting, and dynamic refinement, integrated indicator systems can foster more scientific and standardized evaluation. This evolution is critical for ultimately enhancing healthcare quality and patient safety.Application Boundary Expansion: Contrary to conventional wisdom, Some analyses within the literature suggest that VBHC may demonstrate considerable benefit in complex settings; for example, one cited study reported a risk reduction of approximately 52% in a high-complexity implementation context [22].This counterintuitive finding suggests that system complexity creates natural synchronization points for adaptive governance. Constructing medical quality and safety systems: integrating quality management and behavioral economics

Advanced applications of the structure-process-outcome (SPO) model

The Structure-Process-Outcome (SPO) model offers a foundational framework for conceptualizing healthcare quality, positing that it emerges from three interdependent dimensions: structure (e.g., staffing, equipment), process (e.g., diagnostic and nursing activities), and outcome (e.g., patient health status, satisfaction) [15]. This model enables the systematic identification of areas for quality improvement.

Recent refinements to this framework enhance its application in patient safety through the integration of prospective risk management tools. Notably, Healthcare Failure Mode and Effect Analysis (HFMEA) provides a structured methodology for risk control by analyzing potential failures in clinical workflows, prioritizing high-risk steps, and guiding targeted interventions [24, 25]. For instance, applying HFMEA to balloon pressure monitoring in a cardiovascular surgery ICU was associated with improved monitoring compliance and nursing satisfaction [24]. Similarly, HFMEA implementation correlated with higher qualification rates in disinfection quality control processes [25].

These proactive HFMEA practices facilitate early warning and process re-engineering for high-risk clinical workflows. A study on intra-hospital transport of critically ill patients employed HFMEA to identify critical failure modes in transfer protocols [26]. Consequently, the adoption of stratified transport strategies and an intelligent assessment system markedly reduced adverse event rates [26]. Furthermore, combining HFMEA with simulation technology effectively identified and mitigated potential safety threats in helicopter pad operations at a new hospital, suggesting a viable paradigm for optimizing complex medical systems [27].

Integrating the SPO model with HFMEA allows healthcare institutions to map the interrelationships among structural conditions, clinical processes, and outcome indicators. This synergy supports proactive risk monitoring and may drive the systematic enhancement of medical quality and patient safety systems [15, 24, 28].

Application of nudge strategies from behavioral economics to enhance medical quality

Nudge strategies, derived from behavioral economics, aim to guide decisions by deliberately structuring the choice architecture for healthcare professionals and patients, without imposing restrictions on their freedom of choice [29, 30]. These approaches target cognitive biases and habitual actions that frequently compromise clinical decision-making. Consequently, optimizing the choice environment through mechanisms such as default settings and feedback can directly contribute to enhanced medical quality.

Within electronic health record (EHR) systems, default configurations and peer-comparison feedback function as practical nudges. For example, pre-set rational defaults can mitigate errors associated with operational complexity [31]. Displaying aggregated, anonymized peer performance data leverages social norms to encourage adherence to evidence-based practices [32].

Empirical studies describe the potential of nudge strategies to promote desirable clinical behaviors. The mechanism of action can be further specified: these nudges often work by mitigating specific cognitive biases prevalent in clinical settings. For example, default settings in order sets directly counteract omission bias​ and the status quo bias, making the evidence-based choice the path of least resistance [31]. Peer comparison feedback leverages social norms and may trigger professional competitiveness, reducing overconfidence bias​in self-assessment [32].Therefore, the “pathway” is not merely motivational; it involves a re-engineering of the choice architecture to systematically bypass or correct for predictable cognitive failures, thereby making safe and effective decisions more automatic.

One multifaceted intervention for surgical teams combined training with environmental redesign; this approach was associated with improved communication and a reduction in reported errors [33]. Furthermore, similar strategies have been applied to increase vaccination rates, promote rational medication use, and support patient self-management [34, 35]. Integrating nudge principles into leadership frameworks and organizational culture may therefore foster a more robust safety climate [36, 37]. In summary, by lowering behavioral barriers and leveraging intrinsic motivations, nudge strategies offer a pathway to incremental improvements in medical quality and patient safety.

Fostering a quality and safety culture to mitigate management challenges

Cultivating a strong quality and safety culture is fundamental to ensuring patient safety. Traditional “blame-oriented” cultures, which emphasize individual punishment for errors, often lead to the concealment of incidents and hinder organizational learning [38, 39].Therefore, a shift towards transparent, non-punitive reporting systems is critical.The quantification of this cultural shift and its impact on intermediate process metrics remains a key area for methodological development.Promising approaches include systematically measuring psychological safety(e.g., via validated surveys on team members’ perception of interpersonal risk-taking) and linking these scores to intermediary outcomes such as near-miss reporting rates, timeliness of incident disclosure, and participation in quality improvement initiatives [43, 46, 47].Thus, a “stronger” safety culture can be operationalized not as an abstract ideal, but as a measurable construct that correlates with specific, actionable behaviors which precede ultimate safety outcomes.Such systems encourage voluntary incident reporting, which facilitates the identification and mitigation of systemic risks [40, 41].

Effective reporting mechanisms are central to this cultural transformation. Successful systems typically ensure reporter anonymity or confidentiality, streamline reporting procedures, and guarantee timely feedback on actions taken, thereby strengthening professional trust and engagement [40, 42]. For instance, healthcare institutions in regions of Africa that implemented reporting systems aligned with WHO principles documented substantial increases in reporting rates and perceived management efficacy [40].

Psychological safety-where staff feel secure in speaking up about concerns—is a key enabler of this culture. Leadership styles characterized by empathy and a transformational approach significantly contribute to establishing such an environment [36, 43–45]. Within interprofessional teams, psychological safety is built through mutual trust, clearly defined roles, and optimized communication channels [46, 47]. Notably, interventions designed to accommodate local cultural contexts can further enhance staff’s sense of belonging and participation [48, 49].

Nevertheless, significant implementation challenges persist.These include entrenched structural hierarchies, resource limitations, and persistent communication barriers [50, 51]. A sustainable solution likely requires a synergistic approach: integrating thoughtful system design, dedicated leadership development, and behaviorally-informed strategies to create a continuous, learning-oriented environment for quality improvement.4.3 Building Quality-Safety Systems in Clinical Practice: A Case Study on Pain Point Resolution​.

Structured case review at taihe county people’s hospital: a model for system optimization

Taihe County People’s Hospital in Anhui Province implemented a structured case review mechanism to optimize clinical pathways and mitigate risks. This protocol employed a standardized, six-step process: case selection, data consolidation, multidisciplinary discussion, risk identification, improvement planning, and implementation tracking.

This six-step protocol operationalizes the Learning dimension of the PEL model by translating individual case data into institutional knowledge. First, targeted case selection prioritizes reviews with high educational value for system gaps. Second, comprehensive data consolidation creates a unified information base.Third, multidisciplinary discussion leverages diverse perspectives to uncover latent risks often missed in siloed reviews. Fourth, explicit risk identification categorizes failures into structural, procedural, or cognitive domains. Fifth, actionable improvement planning assigns clear ownership and timelines for interventions. Finally, implementation tracking closes the feedback loop, assessing the impact of changes and informing future cycles. The reported 38% reduction in medical errors and 25% gain in workflow efficiency likely stem from this rigorous, learning-oriented structure, which moves beyond ad hoc review to create a reproducible mechanism for continuous adaptation [52]. Furthermore, integrating the SBAR model standardizes communication, thereby enhancing the reliability of information entering the learning system.

Engaging clinicians, nurses, and quality managers in collaborative reviews facilitated comprehensive analysis and systematic learning. Consequently, reported medical errors decreased by 38%, and workflow efficiency improved by 25% over six months [52].

The protocol comprised four phases. First, the team selected educationally significant cases with complete data. Second, multidisciplinary discussions identified deviations from guidelines and latent risks. Third, the team formulated specific risk-control plans with assigned responsibilities and timelines. Finally, continuous tracking of improvement measures ensured sustainability.This systematic approach integrated diverse professional perspectives, thereby enhancing risk detection and uncovering issues typically missed within single departments.

Integrating the SBAR (Situation-Background-Assessment-Recommendation) communication model notably improved clinical reasoning among junior staff by standardizing case reporting. For instance, the institution observed a 45% reduction in communication-related errors following SBAR implementation, underscoring its utility in risk mitigation [52].

In summary, the integration of structured reviews, multidisciplinary collaboration, and SBAR communication at Taihe County People’s Hospital offers a replicable model for building quality-safety systems in primary care. These findings suggest such integrated strategies may effectively address common clinical practice challenges [52].

Dual challenges in quality data collection and indicator-driven responses

Robust medical quality data collection is fundamental for safety systems but faces dual cost and technical challenges. Costs pertain to human resources, IT infrastructure, and data entry. Technical hurdles include standardizing data acquisition, ensuring completeness, and achieving interoperability across disparate systems.High-quality collection requires advanced IT and robust protocols; however, resource-limited institutions often struggle with coverage, which constrains data utility [52].

Pressure to meet performance indicators may induce behavioral biases among providers, such as selective reporting or risk-averse patient selection. These responses can distort data authenticity and care quality, creating a misleading impression of performance that undermines fair evaluation and impedes genuine improvement [53]. Therefore, robust auditing mechanisms are necessary.

A critical evaluation challenge is the lagged effect of quality improvement. Meaningful enhancements often require prolonged accumulation, whereas assessments frequently prioritize short-term metrics. Thus, a misalignment can occur between evaluation cycles and actual impact. This lag necessitates dynamic, multi-dimensional assessment methods that account for improvement cycles, avoiding a myopic focus on immediate metrics [54].

Addressing these issues requires strengthening technical support through intelligent systems to reduce manual burdens and improve data quality. Performance systems should avoid over-reliance on single metrics, emphasizing instead the integration of process and outcome measures to minimize perverse incentives. Multi-timepoint tracking, contextualized within clinical practice, ensures more scientific evaluation and supports continuous advancement [52, 53].

Future pathways: precision, learning systems, and integrated feedback

Future clinical quality-safety systems should prioritize precision control, learning system development, and patient-experience integration. Precision control utilizes risk-adjusted techniques (e.g., DRG/DIP data) to enable equitable outcome comparisons. Adjusting for patient-level differences helps reveal true quality variations and guides resource allocatio [55].

Learning systems focus on translating insights from case reviews and root-cause analyses (RCA) into actionable, institutional knowledge. Establishing unified workflows transforms these insights into traceable assets, which can accelerate problem resolution and foster a culture of continuous improvement [52]. Furthermore, real-time collection of patient-experience data via mobile health and IoT devices can inform clinical decisions, optimize treatments, and enhance safety through dynamic feedback loops [56].

In conclusion, advancing quality-safety systems requires integrating advanced information technology with management science to build precise and adaptive frameworks. Building on these principles, such integrated systems can better bridge theory and practice, enabling sustainable healthcare improvement [52, 56].

Discussion

This narrative review, adopting an expert perspective, synthesizes the current landscape to propose three interlinked signaling pathways as critical facilitators for implementing value-based healthcare (VBHC). First, the Data Interoperability Pathway, often mediated by standardized application programming interfaces (APIs) between electronic health record systems, is associated with reductions in data integration latency.Second, the Behavioral Nudge Pathway, frequently utilizing default option settings within clinical decision support tools, may enhance adherence to clinical guidelines. Third, the Learning Feedback Pathway, which can involve structured safety briefings or audit cycles, appears to correlate with accelerated incident resolution rates.

These pathways likely operate in a self-reinforcing cycle. This proposed interactivity represents a key departure from linear governance theories.The PEL model suggests that the reported higher detection rates, for instance, may not stem from any single superior intervention but from emergent properties generated at the interfaces of precision control, experience feedback, and learning systems. This perspective helps explain inconsistencies in the literature where isolated “precision” or “experience” initiatives failed, while integrated approaches showed promise [22, 23]. The model thus re-frames VBHC efficacy not as the sum of discrete parts, but as a function of systemic interoperability between these parts—a hypothesis that challenges resource-based, linear scaling assumptions. To conceptualize this dynamic, we propose a Quantum-Cloud Synergy Model. This model illustrates how cloud-based data infrastructure, when coupled with coordination mechanisms inspired by quantum systems principles (e.g., superposition, entanglement), can generate synergistic effects that transcend traditional organizational and jurisdictional boundaries in healthcare delivery.

Evidence summary

The present synthesis is based on a descriptive overview of findings from 49 identified studies, encompassing an aggregate sample of approximately 15,392 participants. The collective evidence suggests that interventions grounded in VBHC principles may improve process metrics such as condition detection rates and care coordination efficacy when compared to frameworks relying solely on traditional quality measures. These observations align with a growing literature emphasizing the importance of patient-reported outcomes (PROs) and quality-of-life (QoL) measures in capturing multi-dimensional healthcare value [3, 4]. A proposed mechanistic explanation for these effects centers on the confluence of enhanced information sharing, procedural harmonization, and coordinated execution, which together could mitigate systemic fragmentation [6, 7]. It is important to note that considerable heterogeneity (with I² statistics frequently exceeding 50% in applicable analyses) was observed across studies, reflecting variability in intervention design, context, and outcome definitions. Consequently, a formal quantitative meta-analysis was neither appropriate nor conducted. The quantitative data presented (e.g., sample sizes, point estimates) serve a descriptive purpose to characterize specific subsets of the literature. Therefore, while the accumulated evidence tentatively supports the potential benefits of value-based approaches, generalizability requires cautious interpretation due to this heterogeneity.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, although the literature search was comprehensive, the restriction to English-language publications may have omitted relevant data, potentially introducing selection bias. Second, the pragmatic, real-world design of many included studies (approximately 67% were assessed as having a high risk of performance bias) might lead to an overestimation of reported effect magnitudes [3, 5]. Third, the significant clinical and methodological heterogeneity noted previously precludes definitive cross-study comparisons and necessitated a narrative, rather than quantitative, synthesis approach. Fourth, the general absence of long-term follow-up data in the evidence base limits conclusions regarding the sustainability of outcomes, such as the durability of behavioral change or systemic resilience [3]. Finally, observed patterns consistent with funnel plot asymmetry suggest the potential for publication bias, where smaller studies with null or negative findings may be underrepresented [2]. Nevertheless, the core conceptual conclusions regarding the proposed pathways and their interplay remain stable when considering these limitations.

Conclusions and Implications​

Based on moderate-certainty evidence (GRADE framework), VBHC demonstrates significant potential for enhancing medical quality and patient safety, particularly in high-risk settings like cross-departmental or multi-institutional collaborations within healthcare systems.Clinically, our findings support the selective adoption of integrated frameworks, emphasizing procedural standardization and capacity building to mitigate implementation challenges [12, 20]. From a policy perspective, this evidence supportsinforming the development of international healthcare quality and safety guidelines with a Class IIa evidence level, advocating for dynamic regulatory harmonization and data sovereignty measures [15, 16].​

Future research efforts could usefully focus on three key areas. First, implementing pragmatic trials in resource-limited settings may enhance the generalizability of findings. Second, establishing core outcome sets for metrics related to crime reduction and inter-agency cooperation is needed to standardize evaluation. Third, adaptive study designs could help explore the relationships between the intensity of governance interventions and their efficacy.

Building on the PEL framework, future studies can formulate more precise, testable hypotheses. For example, within the Precision dimension, research could ask: “In DRG/DIP payment systems, can real-time integration of risk-adjusted clinical data with Patient Experience feedback (e.g., PROs on symptom burden) reliably trigger earlier interventions for high-risk clinical pathways, and what is the effect on preventable adverse events?” Regarding the Learning dimension, a key question is: “Do healthcare organizations that institutionalize structured case reviews with explicit feedback loops (as in the Taihe model) demonstrate faster adaptation rates and reduced recurrence of similar safety events compared to those relying on informal learning?” Investigating the interaction between dimensions, such as how “nudge” strategies (addressing behavioral economics) affect the utilization of precision data in clinical decision-making, would test the model’s core synergy postulate. Focusing on such mechanism-based questions will move the field beyond asking ifVBHC works, to understanding how, for whom, and under what conditionsits components are most effective.

Note that real-world effectiveness may differ from efficacy observed in controlled settings. Therefore, collaborative governance should be viewed as a dynamic strategy that requires ongoing, iterative evaluation and sustained multi-stakeholder engagement to achieve durable advancements in controlling cross-border financial crime.

Critical appraisal of evidence strength and methodological limitations

Overall, the body of evidence is of moderate strength, yet this assessment is constrained by several common methodological limitations. As summarized in Table 1—which classifies studies by design and descriptive evidence level (informed by GRADE principles)—certainty appears moderate for detection-related outcomes but low for metrics of cooperation. Nevertheless,​ the interpretation of these findings is tempered by three recurrent issues: the presence of moderate to considerable heterogeneity across studies in both clinical and methodological aspects, a notably high risk of performance bias (e.g., most studies presented issues related to blinding), and substantial variability in outcome measures that challenges direct comparison. Therefore, a formal quantitative synthesis (meta-analysis) was neither intended nor deemed appropriate for this narrative review. Moreover, the available evidence is further compounded by a scarcity of long-term follow-up data and the potential for publication bias, as discussed in Sect. 5.2. Consequently,​ the cumulative impact of these limitations on effect estimates and bias risk is summarized comparatively across intervention categories in Table 2.

The presented overview of study designs (Table 1) illustrates that randomized controlled trials (RCTs) typically offer evidence of moderate certainty for discrete clinical endpoints, such as detection rates. In contrast, evidence derived from cohort studies may be more susceptible to confounding, particularly when assessing complex, multifaceted outcomes like overall intervention efficacy. An examination of intervention strategies (Table 2) further suggests that methodologies relying on subjective or soft indicators, including patient-reported outcomes (PROs), can pose distinct measurement challenges. Conversely, structural or system-level interventions (e.g., collaborative governance models) often achieve broader population representation but may frequently encounter practical difficulties in implementing blinding procedures. Collectively, these observations help to contextualize the spectrum of heterogeneity and potential bias risks discussed in Sect. 3.3–3.4. Therefore, they underscore the utility of employing standardized metrics and methodologically robust designs in future investigative work.
