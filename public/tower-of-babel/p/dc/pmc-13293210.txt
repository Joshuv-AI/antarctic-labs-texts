Colorectal Squamous Cell Carcinoma; A Comparative Look into Its Molecular Profile
Authors: Tadiwa Chiedza Chirima, Benjing Cheng, Jin-jian Xiang, Ming-Duo Mei, Mohamed Lamin Bangura, Sy-Trung Tran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Colorectal cancer is the third leading cause of cancer-related deaths in the world, while colorectal adenocarcinoma is more common, colorectal squamous cell carcinoma is exceptionally rare making it difficult to study the molecular profile, signaling pathways and pathogenesis of the disease which limits the viable treatment modalities. By analyzing and comparing the similarities between colorectal adenocarcinoma and colorectal squamous cell carcinoma we are able to extrapolate data that assists in assigning molecular profiles, signaling pathways, and possible anticancer therapy targets.Case Presentation:We present the case of an Asian woman in her fifties who had no previous history of predisposing factors and presented with a chief concern of fatigue, later found to have severe anemia requiring blood transfusion and an ascending colon tumor causing a significant narrowing of the lumen and a suspicious liver mass. Endoscopic biopsy of the specimen revealed that the obstructed mass was a squamous cell tumor. The patient underwent right hemicolectomy with excision of the liver mass, and histopathological examination confirmed the biopsy results. The patient has completed adjuvant chemotherapy and is still alive at the time of submission.Conclusion:This uncommon presentation, molecular profile and the histopathological data provides new insight into the possible presentations of colorectal squamous cell carcinoma in different patients.Keywords: Ascending colon, Case report, Chemotherapy, Colorectal cancer, Hemicolectomy, Immune therapy, Squamous cell carcinoma

INTRODUCTION

Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) of the colon is exceedingly rare, affecting less than 1% of the population. It exhibits a heightened rate of progression and a dismal prognosis compared to adenocarcinoma of the colon.1 Although only 64 cases have been documented in the English literature, over 150 cases have been reported globally since 1919.2,3 Due to the infrequency of this malignancy, the associated risk factors remain unconfirmed; however, a tenuous correlation with certain conditions, such as inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), the presence of adenomas or adenocarcinomas, and the existence of an abdomino-cutaneous fistula, has been previously noted.4 Chronic infections, including human papillomavirus (HPV), schistosomiasis, and entamoeba histolytica infections, have also been linked to the onset of SCC; however, HPV is predominantly associated with SCC of the anus and anal verge.4–6

While it was formerly posited that SCC primarily affected the right hemicolon, a review of 34 reported cases from the past decade indicates that the sigmoid colon has emerged as the most frequently implicated anatomical region in recent years (S3, S4, S5, S7, S16, S24, S25, S28, S29; Supplement A).6 The descending colon, sigmoid colon, and rectum are now affected more often than the ascending and transverse colon. The disease was previously observed to be more prevalent in women than in men; however, with the increasing number of documented cases, a more equitable distribution between the sexes has become increasingly evident. The histopathological features typically encountered in colorectal squamous cell tumors include biomarkers such as p40, p16, p63, and p53 (Supplement A). The implicated genetic markers include multiple mutations of homologs, cytokeratin (CK)5, CK7, and CK56. v-raf murine sarcoma viral oncogene homolog B1 (BRAF) and Kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog (K-RAS) are also present but are less common than other mutations. While SCC infrequently expresses common tumor antigens, when it does, the antigen is typically carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) (Supplement A). In this instance, we present a case that lacks any of the correlated factors and presents without abdominal pain or discomfort.

Case Presentation

In June 2024, a 58-year-old Asian female visited our hospital outpatient department with unexplained, near-constant fatigue for 1 month, during which she experienced 2 dizzy spells alleviated by rest. She also reported her bowel habits had changed from normal firm to watery stools with small blood amounts visible on toilet paper. The frequency remained one bowel movement per day. She had stage 1 hypertension for 3 years, managed with Irbesartan and Nifedipine, with good blood pressure control and no family history of colorectal disease or cancer. Her vital signs were normal, and her physical examination was unremarkable. A complete blood count showed abnormal values: white blood count 6.61 × 109/L, red blood cell count 2.45 × 1012/L, platelets 381 × 109/L, hemoglobin 47.00 g/L, mean corpuscular volume 66.5 fL, mean hemoglobin content 19.2 pg, and neutrophils 3.95 × 109/L, indicating severe anemia and mild inflammation. She was postmenopausal, with a low malnutrition risk. She was admitted to hematology for further investigation.

Further blood tests showed abnormalities: ferritin 3.34 ng/ml, iron 0.74 umol/L, reticulocytes 16.90109/L, albumin 34.0 g/L, and calcium 1.98 mmol/L. Fecal analysis was (+++) for occult bleeding, prompting gastrointestinal investigation. Abdominal computed tomography (CT) showed thickening of the ascending colon at the hepatic flexure and 71 × 54 mm low-attenuating liver lesions suspected to be benign cysts (see Figure 1 and Figure 2). Upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopy revealed atrophic gastritis in the pyloric antrum and an ulcerating mass lesion obstructing the ascending colon at the hepatic flexure, suspected to be malignant. Biopsies were taken; pathology was positive for immune markers: P63 (+), P40 (+), CK5/6 (+), with Ki-67 expression of 30%, negative for CK20, villin, SABT2, p16, and little CK7 expression (Figure 3 and Figure 4).

After completing preoperative gastrointestinal preparation and transfusion to rectify severe anemia, the patient underwent 3D laparoscopic right hemicolectomy with en bloc resection of the liver mass for biopsy. A 6 × 5 × 3 cm ulcerating colonic lesion was resected along with one small intestinal lymph node approximately 0.3 cm and 32 colonic lymph nodes, the largest about 2 cm. Pathological analysis was positive for immune markers: P63 (+), P40 (+), CK5/6 (+), and had Ki-67 expression of 80% and TNM staging of pT3N1M0 stage IIIb. The patient with squamous cell carcinoma satisfied all four Williams Criteria, confirming primary colorectal squamous cell carcinoma. The patient had an uneventful postoperative recovery. A liver biopsy pathology report revealed the mass was a benign cyst.

The patient received six cycles of adjuvant chemotherapy. The capecitabine and oxaliplatin regimen, standard for colorectal adenocarcinoma, was chosen. The patient reported multiple side effects from chemotherapy, ranging in severity. Common side effects included nausea, vomiting, and cold sensitivity. The most concerning symptoms were neutropenia and liver function abnormalities, often delaying intravenous oxaliplatin by a day or two. In March 2025, the patient returned to the outpatient department for a follow-up after adjuvant chemotherapy. Physical examination showed the wound had healed completely with no abnormalities. A computed tomography of the abdomen showed no recurrence or new lesions, and serum tumor markers were normal. The patient remained in remission 10 months after the initial diagnosis (see Figure 5 for complete timeline).

DISCUSSION

In 2022, colorectal cancer was the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths and the third most prevalent cancer worldwide.7 Over 90% of patients are diagnosed with adenocarcinoma, while colorectal SCC (CSCC) is rare.8,9 The epidemiology of CSCC is poorly characterized compared to colorectal adenocarcinoma (CRC) due to limited data from few case reports. Previously, case reports indicated CRC was more common in men, but CSCC was more common in women. However, recent reports show an almost equal sex ratio of about 1:110 (Supplement A). CRC is linked to well-researched unmodifiable risk factors, including age (>50 years), family history of CRC, inherited syndromes, and IBD. Modifiable risk factors include obesity, diet, type 2 diabetes, smoking, and alcohol.11–13 Studies suggest a weak link between CSCC and risk factors like smoking, chronic inflammation (such as schistosomiasis, fistulas, and IBD), HPV infection, immunosuppression, and prior pelvic radiation.14,15 Only smoking and IBD correlate with both CRC and CSCC, suggesting a common link in tumorigenesis and risk factors.16,17

The adenoma-carcinoma progression model is a widely accepted framework for understanding CRC development, characterized by a series of mutations and epigenetic alterations that culminate in the metaplasia of the colorectal epithelium.12 The most prevalent mutations occur in the adenomatous polyposis coli (APC), K-RAS, and TP53 genes, along with a deletion of the locus on chromosome 18q, affecting the remaining allele.18 CRC is typically associated with three predominant pathways: chromosomal instability (CIN), microsatellite instability (MSI), and CpG island methylator phenotype (CIMP), as well as a less common inflammatory pathway, each characterized by distinct genetic alterations, histopathological features, and clinical manifestations that facilitate the transition from normal colonic epithelium to CRC.19

The CIN pathway is frequently activated by the APC tumor suppressor gene, leading to alterations in K-RAS, the SMAD family, and phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K), ultimately resulting in the loss of the TP53 gene and hyperactivation of the Wnt signaling pathway.20 The MSI pathway is commonly observed in individuals with Lynch syndrome and is linked to mutations in transforming growth factor β receptor 2 (TGF-β). Sporadic cases of the MSI pathway tend to exhibit mutations in the BRAF, which are associated with recurrence and unfavorable outcomes.21 The CIMP pathway diverges from the adenoma-carcinogenesis pathway as precancerous lesions progress into serrated polyps and exhibit significant DNA methylation. These polyps often demonstrate high expression of BRAF mutations and mutL homolog 1 (MLH1).22 The chronic inflammatory pathway, although the least common, is notable; persistent inflammation transforms the epithelium from normal to atypical dysplasia, progressing from low-grade heterogeneous dysplasia to high-grade heterogeneous dysplasia, ultimately leading to tumorigenesis. This pathway does not require external mutagens to initiate tumorigenesis and promote tumor proliferation, with p53 expression frequently elevated in affected tissue samples.20 This process is theorized to be upregulated by the generation of reactive oxygen species and reactive nitrogen intermediates during chronic inflammation, utilizing the NF-κB, PI3K, and Akt pathways to facilitate the accumulation of β-catenin, thereby impacting the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway.23

The pathways associated with CSCC remain ambiguous; however, immunohistochemical profiles resemble those in CRC and some other gastrointestinal SCCs24,25 (Supplement A). CSCC typically shows high levels of CK5/6, MLH1, MLH2, MLH6, P40, P63, and Ki-67, while also showing elevated K-RAS, BRAF, CK7, CK20, p53, and CAM5.2 (Supplement A). The pronounced expression of P40, P63, and Ki-67 in CSCC parallels findings in esophageal SCC (ESCC), often arising from chronic inflammation, further supporting the chronic inflammation etiology theory of CSCC26 (Supplement A). This theory is bolstered by observing that the pathways governing ESCC development mirror those in the chronic inflammation pathway of CRC, namely the PI3K, Akt, and Wnt/β-catenin pathways.23,26,27 Currently, three hypotheses regarding CSCC etiology exist: chronic inflammation, papillomavirus involvement, and basal stem cell metaplasia, with the chronic inflammation theory most accepted.28 The presence of CK20 suggests a pathogenesis similar to CRC, as CK20 expression correlates with elevated CEA levels in CRC tumors.29

Our patient showed positive expression of P63, P40, CK5/6, and Ki-67, yet had no history of gastrointestinal discomfort. Serum CEA levels were normal, consistent with the negative CK20 expression in the immunohistochemical panel. While patients with elevated CEA levels typically lack gastrointestinal bleeding signs, P40 and P63 expression correlates with rectal bleeding, hematochezia, or melena (Supplement A). Although gastrointestinal bleeding is common, it is unusual for a patient to present mainly with fatigue and dizziness due to anemia (Supplement A). Notably, over ten patients had profuse or visible rectal bleeding; however, bleeding presence or absence did not correlate with outcomes (S8, S10, S12, S13, S15, S20, S22, S30, S32; Supplement A). In the past decade, only 2 cases involved patients with significant anemia upon admission; however, anemia was found during investigations, with abdominal pain as the chief concern in both cases. Immunohistochemical markers in the Kussaibati et al case included P40, CK5/6, MSH2, and MSH6, while the Linardoutsos et al case noted no markers (S14, S18; Supplement A). Abdominal pain is associated with advanced disease and poor outcomes, and while our patient’s tumor was pT3N1M0 stage IIIb, the prognosis postadjuvant therapy is promising, correlating with the lack of abdominal pain (Supplement A).

The high rates of metastasis, recurrence, and drug resistance necessitate ongoing surveillance, yet even with monitoring, survival outcomes remain poor.30 By elucidating the similarities between the signaling pathways and molecular profiles of CSCC and ESCC and CRC, clinicians and researchers can infer potentially viable treatment options for aggressive CSCC tumors. CSCC is divided into 2 types: colon SCC and rectal SCC. The current standard of care for SCC of the colon is based on CRC treatment, encompassing cyto-reductive surgery with either platinum-based neoadjuvant or adjuvant chemotherapy.31 Rectal CSCC has shown comparable sensitivity to a combination of neoadjuvant chemoradiation, cytoreductive surgery, and adjuvant chemoradiation (usually the Nigro regimen), akin to that observed in anal SCC or in cases where platinum therapy is contraindicated in ESCC.31,32 Although there is no official recommended therapy for rectal SCC, the Nigro regimen (radiation with 5-FU and mitomycin C) has been highly effective at inducing stable disease, resulting in it being used as a defacto first-line therapy pending higher-level evidence.32 Furthermore, certain molecular types of rectal CSCC have exhibited sensitivity to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), similar to CRC; however, this sensitivity, observed in dMMR, has not been linked to other specific biomarkers exclusive to CSCC, aside from the immune checkpoint targets.31

In the case reported by Kussaibati et al, the patient was treated with the ICI drug pembrolizumab, primarily due to a diagnosis of Lynch Syndrome and previous treatment for CRC unrelated to the CSCC tumor. Clinicians suspected a high likelihood of recurrence or drug resistance, prompting an aggressive treatment regimen of Pembrolizumab, Mitomycin C, and hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy (S18; Supplement A). Given the anatomical location of our patient’s tumors, specifically in the ascending colon at the hepatic flexure, the Nigro regimen was briefly considered during multidisciplinary discussions. However, it was swiftly dismissed because the hypothetical risks outweighed the negligible benefits. The patient was evaluated for ICI therapy; however, the tumors were surgically excised with clear margins, and there was no evidence of metastasis. Furthermore, the patient's serum tumor markers were not elevated, and radiological assessments indicated no new lesions following complete surgical recovery. These factors collectively informed the decision to implement the CAPOX regimen, which successfully achieved disease control in this case.

CONCLUSION

Colon cancer is a leading cause of cancer mortality. Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the most common variant, while colonic SCC (CSCC) is rare, with high mortality and metastasis rates. CRC's prevalence has led to extensive studies on its epidemiology, risk factors, molecular profile, pathogenesis, and immunohistochemical markers; in contrast, data on CSCC is limited. CRC has four tumorigenesis pathways: CIN, MSI, CIMP, and a less common inflammatory pathway. CSCC is linked to chronic inflammation, papillomavirus infection, and basal stem cell metaplasia, sharing the chronic inflammation pathway with CRC and ESCC. CSCC often shows markers like MLH1, MLH2, MLH6, Ki67, p40, CK5/6, and p63, and may have additional CRC or ESCC markers. Our patient displayed typical CSCC markers but had an atypical presentation, both associated with positive outcomes. Similarities in molecular profiles among ESCC, CRC, and CSCC provide insights for understanding tumor behavior, signaling pathways, symptom prediction, and therapeutic targets. CSCC treatment, depending on tumor location, typically combines chemotherapeutic agents, cytoreductive surgery, and radiation. However, CSCC often leads to metastasis, recurrence, and drug resistance post-treatment.
