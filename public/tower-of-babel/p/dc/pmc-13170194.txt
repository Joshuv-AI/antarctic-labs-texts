Copper-rhein nanozymes induce bacterial activation-exhaustion death for the treatment of bacterial pneumonia and infected wounds
Authors: Yuyu Cui, Qiang Niu, Zhaojia Li, Guoqiang Jiang, Daoyuan Zhao, Chengcheng Luo, Jie Wei, Dongsheng Cao, Kelong Fan, Yang Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bacterial pneumonia and chronic wounds are life-threatening conditions affecting the elderly that are often refractory to preventive measures because of drug-resistance infections and biofilm formation. In order to overcome these clinical challenges, Cu2+ with the natural product rhein were coordinated to achieve a multifunctional nanozyme for antibacterial therapy and tissue regeneration. The tactic augmented the catalytic stability and biocompatibility of the nanozyme and at the same time improved the solubility and bioavailability of rhein. Cu-Rhein (Cu-Rh) prepared through a hydrothermal process exhibited a homogenous rod-shaped morphology and both the peroxidase-like and glutathione peroxidase-like catalytic activity. These characteristics facilitated effective production of reactive oxygen species under infection-like conditions, leading to broad-spectrum antibacterial activity against MRSA and PA. Mechanistic experiments had shown that Cu-Rh activated bacterial ribosomal function, caused futile protein synthesis, and accelerated intracellular energy depletion, resulting in an “Activation–Exhaustion” antibacterial mechanism. Besides, rhein enhanced antioxidative and anti-inflammatory effects, promoting collagen deposition, angiogenesis, and tissue repair. Dual-delivery approach involving topical application of sprays to infected wounds and aerosol delivery of treatment to cases with acute pulmonary infections, achieved highly efficient bacterial clearance and tissue protection in vivo. Biosafety studies confirmed negligible toxicity. This study found out that Cu-Rh are safe and effective nonantibiotic therapeutic platforms capable of treating multidrug-resistant bacterial infections while promoting tissue regeneration.Graphical Abstract The Cu-Rh with multi-enzyme-like activities generates ROS and consumes GSH to disrupt bacterial membranes and biofilms. It activates bacterial ribosomes, induces futile protein synthesis, and accelerates energy depletion, forming an “Activation–Exhaustion” antibacterial mechanism. Cu-Rh effectively eliminates MRSA and PA infections through dual administration of topical spraying and aerosol inhalation, while promoting angiogenesis, collagen deposition, and repair of infected wound and lung tissues. Introduction

Bacterial infections especially wound and pulmonary infections are one of the most threatening infections to the general health of the world and cause most deaths globally [1, 2]. Patients with chronic illnesses including bone fractures, pressure ulcer, or diabetic foot frequently need have a long period of bed rest and because of this their vulnerability to bacterial pneumonia and unhealing wound infection is also significantly elevated, thus leading to a clinical mortality [3–6]. Among various pathogenic bacteria, MRSA and PA represent two of the commonest and hard-to-treat strains [7, 8]. MRSA is a gram-positive bacterium that often leads to chronic wound infections, pneumonia, osteomyelitis and bacteremia, but PA is a gram-negative opportunistic pathogen, which causes bacteria pneumonia in bedridden or immunocompromised patients and leads to about 10% of all hospital-acquired infections [9–11].

The biofilm in which these bacteria grow is a physical and biochemical barrier made up of an extracellular polymeric substance (EPS), impeding antibiotic penetration and enabling bacterial evasion from immune clearance, thereby resulting in inappropriate treatment outcomes and poor tissue repair [12, 13]. Moreover, prolonged and improper prescribing of antibiotics has enhanced the development of multidrug-resistant (MDR) strains of bacteria, thus undermining the effectiveness of their administration by large margins [14, 15]. Development of new antibiotics is a time consuming and costly process that does not keep up with the dire clinical need to treat bacterial infections, which have developed resistance. Antibacterial drug resistance has become a major global challenge and creates an urgent need to develop safe, effective, and nonantibiotic treatment methods [16].

In recent years, nanozyme-based antibacterial have emerged as promising alternatives to conventional antibiotics [17–19]. Nanozymes are artificial nanomaterials with enzyme-like catalytic activities that can mimic various natural enzymes, including peroxidase (POD), catalase (CAT), and superoxide dismutase (SOD) [20–22]. Nanozymes catalytically produce ROS effectively disrupting bacterial membranes, biofilms, and causing oxidative damage, leading to strong antibacterial effects [23–28]. Compared with natural enzymes, nanozymes offer several advantages, such as high catalytic stability, low cost, robustness, and facile tunability, making them suitable for biomedical applications [23, 29–31]. Among them, copper-based nanozymes have demonstrated outstanding antibacterial properties because of their multiple catalytic activities and ability to release Cu²⁺, which damage bacterial membranes and DNA [18, 32, 33]. Additionally, Cu²⁺ also has inherent biological roles that include the promotion of angiogenesis, the regulation of inflammation and the enhancement of tissue regeneration [34–37].

However, copper nanozymes are prone to oxidation and aggregation under physiological conditions, which limits their in vivo stability and therapeutic performance [20]. As a solution to these challenges, the combination of bioactive organic molecules and metal nanoparticles has been suggested [38–41]. Rhein, a natural anthraquinone compound extracted from Rheum palmatum, exhibits broad-spectrum antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant activities [42, 43]. The coordination of Cu²⁺ with rhein not only increases the catalytic stability and biocompatibility of the nanozyme but also increases bioavailability and solubility of rhein in vivo. Thus, Cu-Rhein (Cu-Rh)-coordinated nanozyme is a potential source of a safe, efficient, and antibiotic-free therapeutic platform with the ability to treat MRSA/PA infection and promote wound healing and tissue regeneration.

Based on this, we developed a multifunctional Cu-Rh nanozyme for the synergistic treatment of MDR bacterial infections and tissue regeneration. Uniform rod-shaped Cu-Rh were synthesized via a hydrothermal method and exhibited robust POD-like and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px)-like activities that enable efficient catalytic ROS generation in infectious microenvironments, thereby achieving broad-spectrum antibacterial activity against MRSA and PA [44]. Mechanistic experiments found that Cu-Rh activated bacterial ribosomal function, induced futile protein synthesis, and caused intracellular energy depletion, leading to an “Activation-Exhaustion” antibacterial effect. Furthermore, the incorporation of rhein significantly enhances the antioxidative and anti-inflammatory capabilities of the nanozyme, which effectively modulates the wound microenvironment and facilitates tissue repair (Fig. 1)

To enhance the treatment effects further, a dual-administration strategy was developed that combines topical spray application for infected wounds with aerosol inhalation for the treatment of lung infections. In vivo studies demonstrated that Cu-Rh effectively reduced the bacterial burden, promoted collagen deposition and angiogenesis, and accelerated wound closure. In a murine model of bacterial pneumonia, aerosolized Cu-Rh exhibited potent antibacterial and tissue-protective effects. Biosafety assessments confirmed that Cu-Rh displayed negligible toxicity to major organs. Cu-Rh served as a safe, efficient, and multifunctional therapeutic platform with great potential for the treatment of MRSA/PA infections and for promoting tissue regeneration.

Materials and methods

Materials

Copper(II) chloride dihydrate (CuCl2·2H2O), polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP), rhein solution, O-phenylenediamine (OPD), 3,3’,5,5’-tetramethylodihydrofluorescein diacetate (TMB), glutathione (GSH), dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO), nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH), 5,5’-dithiobis (2-itrobenzoic acid) (DTNB), agar powder (C12H18O9)n, 2’,7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA), 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI), and tryptone soy broth (TSB) were used.

Preparation of Cu-Rh

First, 300 mg of PVP was dissolved in 50 mL of deionized water under vigorous stirring. After this, the solution was stirred with 100 mg of CuCl2·2H2O until it became clear. The pH was adjusted to 13 by adding NaOH aqueous solution (6 mg/mL) dropwise. The Rhein solution (6 mL, 60 mg) was slowly added dropwise while vigorously stirring. After the addition was complete, stirring was continued for 24 h to obtain the coordination polymer Cu-Rh. The Cu-Rh solution was dialyzed with deionized water via a dialysis membrane with a molecular weight cutoff of 8,000–14,000 Da. The dialysis solution was replaced periodically over 48 h. Eventually, the Cu-Rh were obtained via freeze-drying via a freeze dryer and stored at 4 °C for further analysis.

Characterization of the Cu-Rh

The morphology of the Cu-Rh was observed via transmission electron microscopy (TEM; Hitachi HT7800, Japan) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM; Hitachi S4800). The composition and structure of the Cu-Rh were characterized via Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy. The chemical composition of the Cu-Rh was determined via X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS).

Drug release investigation of the Cu-Rh

The Cu-Rh were added to PBS solutions at different pH values (6.5 and 7.4). PBS was added to dilute the Cu-Rh to 200 µg/mL. The ultraviolet absorption wavelength at 432 nm was tested at certain time intervals (10 min, 20 min, 30 min, 40 min, 50 min, and 60 min).

POD-like activity of the Cu-Rh

OPD can be oxidized by oxidants to form yellow ox-OPD. Using OPD as the probe, six experimental groups were established: H2O2, OPD, H2O2+OPD, Cu-Rh + OPD, Cu-Rh+H2O2, and Cu-Rh+H2O2+OPD. Reactions were conducted at 25 °C for 40 min, followed by centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant was analyzed via a UV-vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Science Genesys 50, China). On the basis of these experimental results, three sets of gradient experiments were further designed: a Cu-Rh nanozyme concentration gradient (0, 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 µg/mL) with 1 mM H2O2. H2O2 concentration gradient (0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, and 1.0 mM) with 5 µg/mL Cu-Rh.

TMB can be oxidized by oxidants to form blue ox-TMB. Six experimental groups were set up with TMB as a probe via the same method as OPD: H2O2, TMB, H2O2+TMB, Cu-Rh + TMB, Cu-Rh+H2O2, and Cu-Rh+H2O2+TMB. On the basis of these results, three sets of gradient experiments were further designed: a Cu-Rh nanozyme concentration gradient (0, 10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 µg/mL) with 1 mM H2O2. H2O2 concentration gradient (0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, and 1.0 mM) with 50 µg/mL Cu-Rh.

NADH consumption capacity of the Cu-Rh

NADH can be oxidized by ROS into NAD+. 100 µl of NADH (2.1 mmol/L), 50 µl of H2O2 (0.1 M), and a Cu-Rh concentration gradient (0, 5, 10, 15, 20, and 25 µg/mL) were mixed together, and then PBS was added until the system reached 1 mL. Reactions were conducted at 25 °C for 2 h in the dark, followed by centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 3 min. The absorbance was measured via a UV-vis spectrophotometer to analyze the NADH consumption capacity of the Cu-Rh.

GSH-Px-like activity of the Cu-Rh

DTNB reacts with GSH to form yellow 5-thio-2-nitrobenzoic acid (DSNB). A GSH concentration of 10 mM was mixed with a Cu-Rh concentration gradient (50, 100, 150, and 200 µg/mL). Then, 50 µL of the mixture was added to 450 µL of PBS (pH 7.4) at certain time intervals (0 min, 1 min, 5 min, 10 min, 20 min, 30 min, 45 min, 1 h, and 1.5 h), and DTNB (0.1 mM) was added to the solution. Reactions were conducted at 25 °C for 2 min, followed by centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 3 min. The absorbance was measured via a UV-vis spectrophotometer to analyze the GSH depletion capability of the Cu-Rh.

In vitro antibacterial ability

In this study, the antibacterial capacity of MRSA and PA was evaluated in vitro. Prior to the experiments, the target strains were first resuscitated at 37 °C for 12 h in LB medium. The strains were then divided into four treatment groups according to the treatment measures: (ⅰ) PA/MRSA, (ⅱ) PA/MRSA+H2O2, (ⅲ) PA/MRSA + Cu-Rh, and (ⅳ) PA/MRSA + Cu-Rh+H2O2. The concentrations of bacteria were quantified by measuring the absorbance at 600 nm via a UV-vis spectrophotometer. The final concentrations of the components in the whole system were 1 × 109 CFU/mL bacteria, 100 µM H2O2 and 200 µg/mL Cu-Rh. Each treatment group was incubated at 37 °C for 2 h, and the bacterial culture was diluted 1000 times. The growth curves of the Cu-Rh group were recorded by measuring the absorbance at 590 nm at 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 24, and 36 h. Simultaneously, 40 µL of the suspension from each treatment group was evenly coated on an agar medium plate and incubated at 37 °C for 15 h. The colonies were photographed and counted via ImageJ. The relative survival of the bacteria was calculated according to the following formula:

Relative bacterial survival (%) = Nt/Nc* 100%, where Nt represents the number of colonies in the experimental group and Nc represents the number of colonies in the blank control group.

Live/dead staining of bacteria

MRSA/PA was divided into four treatment groups according to the treatment measures: (1) PBS, (2) PBS+H2O2 (100 µM), (3) PBS + Cu-Rh (200 µg/mL), and (4) PBS+H2O2+Cu-Rh, and each group was treated for 2 h. Then, SYTO-9 and PI were used for staining, and the samples were cultured at 37 °C for 20 min. The stained bacteria were visualized via confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM), and the ratio of green/red fluorescence intensity in the stained samples was calculated via ImageJ software.

ROS staining test

The bacterial mixture of each treatment group was treated with DCFH-DA and incubated in an oven at 37 °C for 20 min. Subsequently, the incubated treatment groups were washed with PBS three times, and the green fluorescence level of the staining results was observed via CLSM.

DNA damage in bacteria

MRSA/PA from the four treatment groups were stained with DAPI to trace bacterial nuclear DNA (blue fluorescence; DAPI, λex = 360 nm, λem = 460 nm). After 20 min of incubation in the dark, the extent of DNA damage to the stained bacteria was examined via CLSM produced by Leica, Germany.

Observation of bacterial and biofilm morphology

After the abovementioned treatments, the bacteria were centrifuged (4000 rpm, 3 min) and dispersed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4 °C overnight to fix the bacteria. The fixed samples were dehydrated gradually with increasing ethanol concentrations (10%, 30%, 50%, 70%, and 90%) for 10 min each. Finally, the bacterial samples were dispersed in 200 µL anhydrous ethanol and dropped onto a silicon wafer for SEM observation. In addition, the fixed cells were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series and infiltrated with Spurr’s epoxy resin. The resin was polymerized at 70 °C, and ultrathin Sects. (70–90 nm) were cut via an ultramicrotome. The sections, which were collected on copper grids, were stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate to enhance contrast. These samples were observed via TEM.

In vitro antibiofilm ability

One hundred microliters of MRSA/PA suspension (1 × 10⁸ CFU/mL) was mixed with 900 µL of LB and incorporated into a 24-well plate. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 48 h to form biofilm. The biofilms were treated in four groups: PBS, PBS+H2O2, PBS + Cu-Rh, or PBS+H2O2+Cu-Rh, and the mixed solutions were incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. Removed the supernatant and washed the biofilm for 3 times using PBS (pH 7.4). The biofilms were stained with crystal violet and the absorbance of the stain measured at 590 nm through a UV-vis spectrum photometer. Moreover, live/dead fluorescence staining was done, and following 15 min of staining, the biofilms were seen through CLSM.

In Vivo wound healing assay

To evaluate the ability of Cu-Rh to promote wound healing in infected wounds, a BALB/c mouse model of MRSA-induced wounds was established. Healthy BALB/c male mice (6–8 weeks) were randomly divided into the following four groups: (A) Control (PBS), (B) H2O2, (C) Cu-Rh, and (D) Cu-Rh+H2O2. The experiment started one week later, when the vital signs of the mice were stable due to one week of feeding. Initially the hair of the backs of mouse was shaved and they were anesthetized using pentobarbital sodium (40 mg/kg). Then, the back of the skin was carefully disinfected with alcohol soaked cotton balls. An aseptic scalpel was used to make a circular wound on the back of the wound with a diameter of around 9 mm. The MRSA suspension (20 µL, 1 × 109 CFU/ml) was subsequently applied evenly to the wound surface and allowed to dry naturally to construct the MRSA-infected wound model. Each group was administered the corresponding pharmaceutical treatment on a daily basis. Their body weights were recorded on days 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10.

Wound healing analysis

The wound areas of all the mice were analyzed via ImageJ software. The wound healing rate was calculated at each designated point in time via the following calculation: Wound healing rate (%) = 1 - [(the remaining area of the initial wound at the recording time)/the initial wound area] * 100%.

Histological analysis

All the mice were humanely sacrificed on day 10, and their wound tissues and primary organs were collected. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Masson staining were applied for histological analysis. The organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung and kidney) were subjected to H&E staining, and whole blood was used for biochemical analysis.

Bacterial pneumonia model and evaluation of the therapeutic effects

Fifteen healthy BALB/c mice (6 ~ 8 weeks) were randomly assigned to three groups: (A) Control, (B) PA, and (C) PA + Cu-Rh. On Day 0, the mice in the control group received nebulized inhalation of PBS (20 µL), whereas those in the other two groups received nebulized inhalation of a PA-containing aerosol (20 µL, 1 × 10⁸ CFU/mL) to establish a bacterial pneumonia model. The PBS group, PA group and PA + Cu-Rh group received PBS, PBS, or Cu-Rh nebulization, respectively, on days 1, 3, 5 and 7. The mice were weighed daily on an electronic scale, and the results were recorded until day 8.

Lung tissue analysis

At the end of the experiment, the lungs of all the mice were extracted, weighed, and photographed to document their morphology. The PBS was injected slowly into the lungs through the trachea via a syringe, and then the solution was withdrawn to collect the alveolar lavage fluid for subsequent bacterial plate counting. All bacterial counts were performed via ImageJ software. The lung tissues were harvested for H&E staining to observe morphological changes and the extent of inflammatory cell infiltration in the lungs.

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining was used to detect the expression of myeloperoxidase (MPO) and NF-κB P65 to comprehensively evaluate the inflammatory state in the lungs. Finally, the wet-to-dry ratio of the lung tissue was calculated by comparing the weight of each lung after drying at 80 °C for 72 h with its predrying weight to evaluate the degree of pulmonary edema.

Results & discussions

Synthesis and characterization of the Cu-Rh

The Cu-Rh were conveniently prepared by adding copper ions and PVP to the rhein solution and dialyzing to remove unbound Cu2+ and PVP (Fig. 2a). SEM (Fig. 2b) and TEM (Fig. 2c) images showed that the Cu-Rh had a rod-like morphology with dimensions ranging from 120 to 220 nm. According to the mapping analysis of the scanning transmission electron microscope (STEM), the element of Cu was evenly distributed in Cu-Rh (Fig. 2d). Dynamic Light Scanning (DLS) was used to determine the particle size and the zeta potential of the Cu-Rh. The results showed that the Cu-Rh had a uniform particle size distribution with an average particle size of 173.5 ± 10.01 nm (Fig. 2e) and a zeta potential of -14.2 ± 0.42 mV (Fig. 2f). Measurement of the UV-vis spectra of the rhein solution and the synthesized Cu-Rh was next to be measured. A distinct absorption peak of the rhein solution was observed at 432 nm, whereas the absorption peak for the Cu-Rh shifted to 520 nm. This outcome was an indication of the successful synthesis of the Cu-Rh (Figure S1).

The pH-responsive release capability of the Cu-Rh was subsequently investigated. Cu-Rh were added to PBS solutions at different pH values (6.5 and 7.4). At pH 6.5, the rate of rhein release from the Cu-Rh was much higher than that at pH 7.4, and the TEM images showed a significant decomposition compared to pH 7.4 (Fig. 2g-h). It is important to note that Cu-Rh were disintegrated when subjected to modest acidic conditions thus prompting their quick release of drugs.

FTIR spectroscopy was used to determine the composition and structure of the CuRh. The FTIR spectra exhibited characteristic bands, confirming the successful coordination of Cu2+ with rhein, and also indicating that Cu-Rh was modified with PVP (Fig. 2i). Further evidence for the chemical composition of the Cu-Rh was provided by XRD and XPS. The X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis of Cu-Rh did not show sharp and high-intensity crystalline diffraction peaks (Fig. 2j). XPS spectra revealed peaks associated with Cu, C, and O (Fig. 2k). The Cu 2p XPS spectrum exhibited two main peaks at 934.1 and 954.1 eV, with satellite peaks appearing at 943.2 and 962.2 eV, indicating that the valence of Cu in the Cu-Rh was in the form of Cu2+ (Fig. 2l). The XPS spectra of C and O exhibited peaks at 284.3 eV and 530.7 eV, corresponding, respectively, to the C4+ valence of C and the O2− valence of O (Fig. 2m-n). Experimental evidence has shown that Cu-Rh with a rod-like morphology and an average size of 173.5 nm were successfully synthesized.

Evaluation of the enzyme-like activity of the Cu-Rh

In recent years, copper-based nanomaterials have gained increased attention for their applications in wound healing and antibacterial therapy because of their POD-like and GSH-Px-like nanozyme activities. To evaluate the POD-like activity of the Cu-Rh, OPD and TMB were used as chromogenic substrates. This figure illustrates the catalytic mechanisms of the various enzyme-mimicking activities of the Cu-Rh (Fig. 3a). The Cu-Rh were able to oxidize OPD to yellow oxOPD in the presence of H2O2. These findings suggested that the Cu-Rh may have POD-like activity. With a fixed concentration of H2O2 (1 mM), the color of the solution intensified as the concentration of the Cu-Rh increased (Fig. 3b). At a constant concentration of Cu-Rh, H2O2 at concentrations as low as 0.2 mM induced a color change in the solution (Fig. 3c). This observation implied that the Cu-Rh were very sensitive to H2O2. This property allowed them to catalyze reactions of substrates effectively and efficiently even when low amounts of H2O2 were present.

Interestingly, the Cu-Rh released rhein under acidic conditions and the OPD system became yellow (Figures S2). The specific absorption peak was significantly high in the H2O2 experimental group. This important observation confirmed that the change in colour in the OPD system was mainly due to oxidation of OPD by •OH radicals catalysed by Cu-Rh (Fig. 3d). The POD-like activity of the Cu-Rh was further measured by TMB. Similar to OPD, TMB was also oxidized by a •OH radical to give the blue product oxTMB, the absorption peak of which was at 650 nm. As the concentration of Cu-Rh or H2O2 increased, the absorbance at 650 nm simultaneously increased (Fig. 3e and f). The OD value of the characteristic absorption peak in the H₂O₂-containing group was 4.4 times greater than that in the H2O2-free control group (Fig. 3g). This experiment further confirmed the POD-like activity of the Cu-Rh.

Electron transport chain uses NADH as the first donor of electron. Imbalances in intracellular NADH levels within bacterial cells directly disrupt oxidative phosphorylation, thereby inhibiting the tricarboxylic acid cycle. This prevents bacterial metabolic activity, triggering a “bacterial starvation” phenomenon which finally realizes the objective of bacterial eradication [45, 46]. Hence, an effective NADH depletion plan may lead to a substantial improvement of the bactericidal effect. Only 25 µg/mL of the Cu-Rh completely oxidized 2.1 mmol/L NADH within 2 h (Fig. 3h). The elimination of NADH was increased with the concentration of Cu-Rh. This observation suggested that the Cu-Rh would be able to efficiently consume NADH to inhibit the energy supply.

Being one of the major antioxidants of bacteria, GSH can convert ROS into non-toxic molecules. Additionally, GSH is essential in the binding of the toxic substances in cells to facilitate their excretion and in the control of bacterial growth and metabolism. Therefore, an efficient GSH depletion strategy could significantly increase the bactericidal effect of ROS. As little as 50 µg/mL of the Cu-Rh completely depleted the GSH within 6.5 h (Fig. 3i). At a concentration of 200 µg/mL, the Cu-Rh significantly reduced the concentration of GSH within 5 min and completely removed the GSH within 1.5 h (Fig. 3k). The consumption rate of glutathione increased with increasing concentrations of Cu-Rh (Fig. 3m). These findings confirmed that the Cu-Rh had strong GSH-Px-like activity. These results indicated that Cu-Rh with POD-like and GSH-Px-like dual enzymatic activities enhanced antibacterial effects by accelerating reactive oxygen species generation and disrupting antioxidant barriers.

In vitro antibacterial activity

Cu-Rh served as potential antibacterial agents to kill bacteria through multiple mechanisms, exhibiting POD-like and GSH-Px activities. Therefore, a series of in vitro antibacterial experiments were performed to elucidate its antibacterial mechanism. MRSA/PA cultures received four different treatments for 12 h (pH = 6.5). The number of colonies in the Cu-Rh+H2O2 group was less than that in the other groups. Additionally, the OD600 values of MRSA and PA were only 5.5% and 3.2%, respectively, in the Cu-Rh+H2O2 group. The bacterial viabilities of MRSA and PA were 1.7% and 1.2%, respectively, in the Cu-Rh+H2O2 group (Fig. 4a-c). This may be because the Cu-Rh locally convert minute amounts of H2O2 into •OH, which results in stronger antibacterial effects.

To detect the survival of MRSA/PA, SYTO-9 (red fluorescence) and PI (green fluorescence) were used for live/dead staining. The green-stained bacteria were still alive, whereas the red-stained bacteria were dead. The control group and H2O2 group exhibited mostly green fluorescence. In contrast, the Cu-Rh+H2O2 group presented no green fluorescence but displayed intense red fluorescence (Fig. 4d), revealing nearly complete bacterial death. The findings revealed that Cu-Rh had POD-like activity leading to the death of bacteria.

The extent of production of ROS was determined using DCFH-DA. ROS oxidation transforms DCFH-DA into DCF, which fluoresces in the colors of bright green. Cu-Rh group and Cu-Rh+H2O2 group exhibited distinct green fluorescence. In addition, the Cu-Rh + H2O2group exhibited the maximum green fluorescence (Fig. 4e). These results indicated that Cu-Rh could aid in increasing the oxidative stress in bacteria, which led to the accumulation of ROS.

As promoted by the abovementioned methods, ROS can increase bacterial NADH consumption [47]. Based on this, it is speculated that Cu-Rh generates ROS through its POD-like activity, disrupting the bacterial electron transport chain. This prevented the respiration and metabolic processes of bacteria, ultimately inducing “bacterial starvation” and damaging the bacteria.

Notably, Wu et al. proposed a “respiratory chain disruption-oxidative stress amplification” antibacterial mechanism [47]. To verify whether Cu-Rh induced bacterial DNA damage through this mechanism, DAPI staining was employed to evaluate the extent of DNA damage [48]. DAPI is able to react to bacterial DNA, which causes the blue-flueness. The control-group and H2O2 group agglutinated and the fluorescence was strong. The blue fluorescence of the Cu-Rh group and the Cu-Rh+H2O2 group was faint and broken (Fig. 4g), confirming that the Cu-Rh effectively induced bacterial DNA damage. It is speculated that Cu-Rh can continuously capture protons and electrons involved in the bacterial respiratory chain, which disrupts energy metabolism. Long-term exposure to this energy-starved condition inhibited a wide range of biosynthetic activities and initiated extreme oxidative stress, ultimately leading to DNA damage.

Besides this, the morphology of the bacteria was observed using SEM. The bacteria in the control group and H2O2 groups appeared intense with intact, smooth cell membranes. However, the bacterial count in the Cu-Rh+H2O2 group decreased significantly, and the cell membranes were damaged (Fig. 4f). This phenomenon may be attributed to Cu-Rh eliminating bacteria by inhibiting their proliferation and disrupting the integrity of their cellular structure.

In vitro antibiofilm activity

Bacterial biofilms are rigid structures composed of a bacterial community and an EPS matrix. This structure provides physical stability to bacteria and acts as a barrier against the diffusion of antibacterial agents. Therefore, the effective elimination of biofilms is a top priority in antibacterial therapy. First, the biofilms formed by MRSA/PA received four different treatments. Crystal violet staining was subsequently used to evaluate the degree of biofilm destruction. Compared with the control and H2O2 groups, the Cu-Rh group presented a significant decrease in crystal violet staining. Surprisingly, the purple color of the plates was nearly eradicated in the Cu-Rh+H2O2 group. Moreover, both the OD600 value and the growth curve demonstrated that the Cu-Rh+H2O2 group exhibited superior antibiofilm activity. The OD600 values of MRSA and PA were 21.05% and 30.5% of those of the control group, respectively, and the growth curve was markedly lower (Fig. 5a-c). Therefore, the combined effect of Cu-Rh and H₂O₂ was confirmed to have an outstanding effect on the clearance of biofilms.

The biofilms were subsequently stained with SYTO-9 (red fluorescence) and PI (green fluorescence). The green-stained biofilms revealed that the bacteria were still alive, and the red-stained biofilms revealed that the bacteria were dead. The live/dead staining of the control group and H2O2 group revealed almost green fluorescence. In contrast, the Cu-Rh+H2O2 group presented intense red fluorescence, indicating that the Cu-Rh eliminated bacteria and biofilms (Fig. 5f, i).

To further confirm the antibiofilm ability of the Cu-Rh, morphological changes in the MRSA/PA biofilms were observed. Bacteria in the Cu-Rh+H2O2 group were virtually eliminated in comparison to the other groups and the structures of the bacterial membranes were disrupted. This observation revealed that H2O2 enhanced the antibiofilm efficacy of the Cu-Rh (Fig. 5d, g).

TEM images showed that the biofilm bacteria in the control group and H2O2 groups remained intact with smooth cell membranes. In contrast, the Cu-Rh group exhibited marked cellular deformation (Fig. 5e, h). Based on these observations, Cu-Rh had their antibiofilm effect through disrupting structural integrity. In the Cu-Rh+H2O2 group, this effect was significantly amplified, manifesting as severe membrane damage and intracellular material leakage. This was due to the interaction of H₂O₂ and Cu-Rh, which disrupted the integrity of bacteria and thereby inhibited biofilm formation.

Transcriptomic analysis of the antibacterial effect of the Cu-Rh

To further explore the antibacterial mechanism of the Cu-Rh, transcriptional analysis was carried out on MRSA treated with the Cu-Rh. A volcano plot was drawn, revealing that a total of 1163 significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were obtained of which 563 genes were downregulated (blue) and 600 genes were upregulated (red) following the treatment of the Cu-Rh (Fig. 6a).

Analysis of Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway information and heatmaps revealed that, after treatment with Cu-Rh, the significantly differentially expressed genes were enriched in pathways such as microbial metabolism in diverse environments and ribosome and purine metabolism (Fig. 6b, c).

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis further categorized the DEGs by function. The DEGs were involved primarily in activities related to translation, transcription, and ribosomal function and structure. Proteins are fundamental to all bacterial life activities, and ribosomes represent the sole sites where mRNA information is decoded to synthesize proteins. Ribosomes are composed primarily of ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and ribosomal proteins [49]. This intricate molecular machinery orchestrates the sequential assembly of amino acids into polypeptide chains, which are precisely guided by the codon sequence on mRNA, ultimately producing proteins with specific biological functions. Subsequently, heatmap analysis of relevant genes (Fig. 6e) and protein interaction analysis of specific genes (Figures S3-S6) were conducted to elucidate the antibacterial mechanism of the Cu-Rh.

Significant difference analysis indicated that the expression of transcription-related genes (such as those regulating DNA template transcription) was downregulated. Genes associated with translation and ribosomal structure and function were upregulated. These findings suggest that Cu-Rh interfere with bacterial gene expression, thereby effectively suppressing bacterial growth and proliferation. In addition, GSEA revealed significant downregulation of transcription-related genes but prominent upregulation of translation- and ribosome-related genes after Cu-Rh treatment (Fig. 6f).

Accordingly, the ribosome “Activation-Exhaustion” mechanism of the Cu-Rh was proposed (Fig. 6g). Initially, Cu-Rh effectively curtailed the “raw material supply” (mRNA) for protein synthesis by inhibiting transcription processes. Concurrently, the Cu-Rh promoted the activation of translation and ribosomal processes, leading to the prolific synthesis of nonfunctional, misfolded proteins. This process results in a substantial depletion of intracellular ATP and GTP, precipitating severe energy exhaustion. Furthermore, the aggregation of these misfolded proteins elicited profound stress responses, ultimately causing bacterial death. Moreover, the Cu-Rh contributed to bacterial eradication through the release of metal ions and the generation of ROS, as well as by damaging DNA and proteins. In summary, these findings elucidated a highly complex and multifunctional composite antibacterial strategy. Cu-Rh exert potent bactericidal activity by releasing copper ions, generating ROS, and disrupting DNA and proteins, thereby killing bacteria.

Invivo infectious wound healing

In order to further assess the antibacterial efficiency of the Cu-Rh in vivo, a model of MRSA-infected wounds was set up. Figure 7a illustrates all the experimental steps. The mice were first randomly grouped into four sets: (A) PBS, (B) H2O2, (C) Cu-Rh and (D) Cu-Rh+H2O2. Then, the MRSA suspension (20 µL, 1 × 109 CFU/ml) was evenly applied to the wounds. Each group was treated with PBS, H2O2, Cu-Rh, or Cu-Rh+H2O2.The findings indicated that the Cu-Rh+H2O2 group had the highest wound healing rate among the Cu-Rh (78.72%), H2O2 (64.81%), and PBS (65.47%) groups whose wound healing rate was 93.14% on day 10 (Fig. 7b-d). The wound tissues were then collected on day 10 for bacterial plate culture and counting. It was found that the relative percent of viable bacteria in the Cu-Rh+H2O2 group was 1.58%, which was significantly lower than in other groups (Fig. 7e). These findings revealed that Cu-Rh + H₂O₂ group had the most promising antibacterial and healing characteristics in vivo.

The normal wound healing process is generally accompanied by an increase in granulation tissue, migration of fibroblasts, re-epithelialization, collagen deposition, and infiltration of inflammatory cells [50]. The effect of Cu-Rh healing was determined by using H&E staining after various treatments. The H&E staining images showed that the dermal tissue of the Cu-Rh + H2O2 group contained more skin appendages, including the sweat glands, hair follicles, and hair compared to other groups (Fig. 7f). Moreover, re-epithelialization and granulation tissue are important indicators of wound healing. Cu-Rh + H2O2 group exhibited more complete epithelial coverage and thicker granulation tissue (Fig. 7g).

Normal wound healing is usually divided into three phases namely the inflammatory phase, the proliferation phase, and remodeling phase [51]. The extracellular matrix has an element called collagen, which is significant in the wound tissue remodeling [52]. Masson’s trichrome staining revealed a significantly greater level of collagen deposition in the Cu-Rh + H2O2 group than in the other groups, with the collagen fibers appearing well aligned and orderly (Fig. 7h). These results also established that Cu-Rh are useful in the healing of MRSA-infected wounds.

In addition, it was found that the presence of TNF-α and IL-6 indicated the inflammatory condition of wounds. These are some of the proinflammatory cytokines released in the initial inflammation that is vital in the triggering of immune response. As shown in the immunofluorescence image, the intensity of signal positive expression and the amount of TNF-a and IL-6-positive cells were significantly less in the Cu-Rh + H2O2 group than the rest (Fig. 8a-c). Collectively, these data above indicated Cu-Rh + H₂O₂ effectively mitigated excessive inflammatory responses, thereby promoting wound healing and facilitating the transition to the proliferation phase.

Impressively, Li J et al. reported that VEGF could be activated by Cu2+, which boosted the development of new blood vessels [53]. The expression of VEGF in the wound site neovascularization process was evaluated using immunofluorescence analysis. The Cu-Rh + H2O2 group expressed significantly more VEGF and it remained mainly in the endothelial cells of new blood vessels and surrounding stromal cells. VEGF signal intensity and spatial distribution of the groups was significantly better than in the rest (Fig. 8a, d). All the evidence in vivo proved that Cu-Rh + H2O2 was an effective substance, which could hasten the process of collagen deposition, control the inflammatory reaction, and angiogenesis, which would largely shorten the healing of infected wounds.

Therapeutic effects against bacterial pneumonia in vivo

Considering the healing of infected wounds in the astonishing case of Cu-Rh, an acute bacterial pneumonia model was developed to determine their therapeutic outcome in vivo. Tracheal intubation or intraperitoneal injection is the common way of establishing conventional acute pneumonia models. However, the above approaches do have the following limitations: (a) they are quite complex; (b) the distribution of bacteria in the lungs is not even; and (c) they may cause uncontrollable injury to the mice. This study innovatively employed ultrasonic nebulization technology to establish an acute pneumonia model, successfully removing the shortcomings of the conventional techniques.

All the experimental procedures are shown in Fig. 9a. The PBS, PA and PA + Cu-Rh groups were first nebulized with PBS, PBS, or Cu-Rh respectively. At the end of the experiment, a digital camera was used to take the lungs which were removed and recorded. Photography evidence indicated that the control group as well as the PA + Cu-Rh had well-perfused lungs. Conversely, in the PA group, the lungs were congested, swollen, and dark red in color (Fig. 9b). This result indicated that the presence of Cu-Rh can help in the alleviation of pulmonary edema caused by PA infection. The alveolar lavage fluid was then taken of each group and plated to assess the bacterial load in the lungs (Fig. 9d). The PA + Cu-Rh treatment group presented the lowest colony count, indicating its excellent antibacterial performance in vivo (Fig. 9c, i). Morphological alterations in the lung tissue were observed via H&E staining. The analysis revealed alveolar edema and congestion, alveolar collapse and closure, extensive inflammatory cell infiltration, and interstitial edema in the PA group (Fig. 9e). Notably, only some of the lung tissue in the PA + Cu-Rh group presented mild alveolar edema and focal inflammatory cell infiltration.

As demonstrated in previous studies, bacterial infection can induce excessive accumulation of inflammatory secretions in the lungs, resulting in pulmonary edema [54]. Consequently, the lung wet-dry ratio has emerged as a pivotal indicator in the assessment of pulmonary edema. The findings revealed no statistically significant difference in the wet-dry ratio of the lung tissue between the control group and the PA + Cu-Rh group (Fig. 9h). These findings suggested that the Cu-Rh may effectively alleviate pulmonary edema, which is consistent with the condition of the lungs observed in the digital photographs.

To further evaluate the inflammatory status of the lungs from each group, an appropriate amount of lung tissue was harvested for IHC analysis. MPO, a specific marker of neutrophil activation, is primarily localized within the azurophilic granules of neutrophils [55]. Its expression is frequently associated with inflammatory responses, tissue injury, and oxidative stress. Consequently, the number or staining intensity of MPO-positive cells in IHC images may be used to quantify the degree of neutrophil infiltration and the severity of acute inflammation within tissues. The results demonstrated that the PA + Cu-Rh group presented reduced MPO expression levels, suggesting that the Cu-Rh effectively mitigated neutrophil infiltration (Fig. 9f, S7).

Furthermore, NF-κB is a transcription factor involved in processes such as inflammatory and immune responses [56]. Fluctuations in NF-κB levels can reflect the intensity of inflammatory reactions in lung tissue. NF-κB p65 is a subunit of NF-κB. IHC analysis revealed that NF-κB p65 expression levels decreased in the PA + Cu-Rh group, suggesting that Cu-Rh significantly reduced the inflammatory response induced by PA infection (Fig. 9g, S8).

Conclusions

This study reports multifunctional Cu-Rh that offer a novel non-antibiotic strategy to concurrently address MDR infections and impaired tissue regeneration. The rod-shaped nanozymes exhibit both POD-like and GSH-Px-like activities, enabling efficient catalytic generation of ROS within the infection microenvironment and exerting broad-spectrum antibacterial effects against MRSA and PA. Mechanistically, most nanozyme antibacterial effects are achieved by interfering with ribosome translation, Cu-Rh operate through a unique “Activation-Exhaustion” pathway, wherein bacterial ribosomal function is hijacked to drive futile protein synthesis, leading to rapid depletion of intracellular energy reserves and effectively circumventing conventional antibiotic resistance mechanisms. In vivo experiments have shown that this dual delivery strategy not only effectively eliminates bacteria, but also promotes collagen deposition, angiogenesis and tissue remodeling. Collectively, this work establishes a promising multifunctional nanozyme platform that integrates antibacterial action with regenerative therapy.
