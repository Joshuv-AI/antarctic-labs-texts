Sodium‐Containing Pharmaceutical Products as Contributors to Cardiovascular Risk
Authors: William B. White, Richard J. Kovacs
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Restricting sodium intake has been identified as one of the most cost‐effective measures to improve public health. The substantial improvements in blood pressure and cardiovascular outcomes observed after dietary sodium restriction in clinical trials and observational studies provide evidence‐based support for national strategies that recommend limits on daily sodium consumption. Although dietary sources of sodium account for most of an individual's intake, the sodium content of pharmaceutical products can also contribute to excess sodium intake, thereby increasing cardiovascular risk. Sodium is included in oral medications as an active ingredient (or as a cation for an active ingredient) or to enhance solubility. The sodium content of certain medications can exceed the daily recommended maximal sodium intake if higher doses are administered. In this contemporary review, we describe the basis of cardiovascular risk with increased sodium intake as it pertains to the clinical relevance of medications with a high‐sodium content that are associated with increases in blood pressure, development of hypertension, and adverse cardiovascular outcomes. Improvements in approaches to sodium content labeling and prescriber education are warranted to ensure that patients receive appropriate therapeutic options.

Sodium is the major cation in extracellular fluid. It regulates electrolyte balance, supports muscle and nerve function, and facilitates the transport of potassium, phosphates, glucose, and amino acids across cell membranes. 1 Approximately half of the body's sodium content is in the extracellular fluid at a homeostatic concentration of 135 to 145 mmol/L. However, chronic excess sodium intake increases fluid retention, leading to sustained elevations in blood pressure (BP) and related cardiovascular complications. Data from the Global Burden of Disease study (1990–2021) reported that 7 cardiovascular or kidney diseases (including stroke, hypertensive heart disease, and chronic kidney disease) may be attributed to high‐sodium diets, and estimated that a high sodium intake accounted for 1.86 million deaths and 41.3 million disability‐adjusted life‐years in 2021. 2

In the United States, it has been estimated that dietary sources of sodium account for ~80% of total sodium intake, with drinking water, dietary supplements, and medications accounting for the remainder. 3 , 4 , 5 Reducing dietary sodium intake has been shown to benefit cardiovascular health. 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 Many countries have issued public health guidelines that set maximum recommended daily sodium intake levels, and these have been endorsed by organizations such as the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA), the World Health Organization, and the American Heart Association. 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 Although the health implications of dietary sodium intake are well recognized, the contribution of sodium from pharmaceutical sources and the potential effect on cardiovascular risk remain largely overlooked and form the basis for this review.

SODIUM INTAKE AND BLOOD PRESSURE

A direct association between sodium intake and BP levels has been established in large‐scale, global observational studies. 16 , 17 , 18 INTERSALT (International Cooperative Study on Salt, Other Factors, and Blood Pressure), which included 10 079 people (20–59 years) from 52 centers in 32 countries, demonstrated significant linear associations between 24‐hour urinary sodium excretion (a surrogate indicator of sodium intake) and systolic BP (P<0.001 for the pooled regression after adjustment for age and sex) as well as for diastolic BP (P<0.001). 17 Other large epidemiologic analyses support these data. An assessment of 322 624 individuals in the UK Biobank identified a positive linear relationship between urinary sodium excretion and BP (Figure 1). 16 Additionally, the PURE (Prospective Urban Rural Epidemiology) study, which analyzed data from 95 767 participants, found that systolic BP increased by 2.9 mm Hg for every 1000 mg increase in daily sodium intake (P<0.0001). 18

GLOBAL AND NATIONAL HEALTH STRATEGIES TO REDUCE SODIUM INTAKE

Given the association between sodium intake and BP, 6 , 7 , 8 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 96 national salt reduction initiatives have been implemented as of 2021, with several others in the planning stages. 12 The World Health Organization considers restricting sodium intake to be one of the most cost‐effective measures to improve public health, 23 and a Cochrane review concluded that there were no adverse effects associated with initiatives to reduce sodium intake. 24 Recommendations for maximal sodium intake vary among national and international guidelines. 3 , 14 , 23 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 However, a maximum sodium intake of 2000 to 2400 mg/day is consistent with most societies and regulatory agencies in the United States (Figure S1). A sodium intake <2300 mg/day is considered a healthy eating pattern for adults by the US Department of Agriculture, as outlined in the US 2025 to 2030 nutrition guidelines, though the American Heart Association 2025 guidelines note that a limit of <1500 mg/day is ideal for most adults, and particularly those with hypertension. 14 , 31 Guidance from the FDA acknowledges that the current average sodium intake for adults in the United States exceeds the recommended 2300 mg/day maximum, and the FDA is pursuing a multiphased plan to reduce sodium intake in the US population. 13 , 32 In this plan, the goal is to balance the need for reductions in sodium with the technical and market constraints on the reduction of sodium content in food. Evaluations of population sodium intake will be reported by the FDA, 13 and future recommendations are anticipated to introduce lower target levels.

Several ongoing initiatives in the United States have incorporated sodium‐restriction targets and dietary sodium content information into public health programs aimed at improving cardiovascular health. The US Department of Health and Human Services' Million Hearts is a national initiative that aimed at preventing 1 million acute myocardial infarctions, strokes, and other cardiovascular events within 5 years, and it includes sodium restriction as a key public health priority. 33 During the initial 2‐year period (2012–2013), it was estimated that up to 115 000 cardiovascular events may have been prevented by the Million Hearts campaign, with 500 000 estimated to have been prevented by 2016. 33 , 34 In addition to sodium restriction, the initiative supports the use of aspirin for secondary cardiovascular disease prevention (where appropriate), hypertension control, cholesterol management, and smoking cessation.

Clear product labeling has been shown to effectively influence the sodium content of food at the manufacturing level and consumer purchasing habits. 35 , 36 For example, countries in Latin America that have adopted such regulations have shown that including front‐of‐package warning labels regarding sodium content has led to a decline in purchases of sodium‐rich foods. 36 Consistent with this observation, in 2025, the FDA proposed to standardize front‐of‐package food labeling in the United States. 36 The proposed labeling system would display the percentage of sodium per serving (as well as saturated fat and added sugars) and indicate whether this corresponds to low, medium, or high levels of sodium intake, providing greater understanding of dietary sodium intake at the individual and population levels. 36

IMPACT OF REDUCED DIETARY SODIUM INTAKE ON CLINICAL OUTCOMES

Data from both interventional trials and population‐based studies support an association between reduced sodium intake levels and improved cardiovascular health. These findings provide evidence‐based support for the global and national recommended thresholds for sodium intake. BP is assessed either by ambulatory BP monitoring or office and home BP measurement in these studies. Ambulatory BP monitoring has the advantage of being able to assess BP variability over 24 hours, including assessments at night. In contrast, office‐ or home‐based assessment provides BP values at a specific time point and is subject to less reproducibility compared with ambulatory BP monitoring values. Both methods have advantages and disadvantages; for example, office BP is subject to white‐coat effect and masked hypertension but is easy to perform in practice whereas ambulatory BP monitoring provides more information compared with office or home BPs but is cumbersome to perform repeatedly in individual patients and is poorly covered by medical insurance. 37

Clinical Trials

The TOHP (Trials of Hypertension Prevention), initiated in the 1980s and 1990s, assessed whether nonpharmacologic lifestyle changes, including reductions in sodium intake and weight loss alone and in combination, could reduce BP (TOHP I, TOHP II) or prevent incident hypertension (TOHP II) in individuals with high‐normal diastolic BP (prehypertension). 9 , 20 , 21 Prehypertension was defined as a cumulative average diastolic BP falling within the following ranges at 3 screening visits in TOHP I: 75 to 97 mm Hg at screening visit 1, 77 to 94 mm Hg at screening visit 2, and 80 to 89 mm Hg at screening visit 3 and an office systolic BP<140 mm Hg and diastolic BP within the following ranges in TOHP II: 81 to 97 mm Hg at screening visit 1, 82 to 92 mm Hg at screening visit 2, and 83 to 89 mm Hg at screening visit 3. The sodium intervention in both studies comprised dietary and behavioral counseling to educate participants on identifying dietary sources of sodium, monitoring intake, and selecting and preparing foods with reduced sodium content. 38

After 18 months of follow‐up in TOHP I (n=2182), significant reductions in systolic (1.7 mm Hg; P<0.01) and diastolic (0.9 mm Hg; P<0.05) BP were observed. 20 In TOHP II, 2382 participants with prehypertension were followed for at least 36 months with secondary follow‐ups at 42 and 48 months. 21 Compared with the standard care group, the intervention resulted in a reduction in sodium excretion (average decrease of 929 mg/day; P<0.001) at Month 36, a significant reduction in systolic BP (1.2 mm Hg; P=0.02), and lower, nonsignificant rates of developing incident hypertension (relative risk reduction of 10%; P=0.09); diastolic BP declined by 0.7 mm Hg but did not reach statistical significance (P=0.10). 21 Although the average reductions in BP were small in the initial phase of the TOHP studies, the impact of the intervention on individuals without hypertension is likely to be smaller than that of a similar intervention in patients with hypertension. 39 In individuals without hypertension, even a small change in BP is likely to be of relevance, as supported in the longer‐term outcome data from the TOHP studies. 9 At Month 48 of TOHP II, the incidence of new‐onset hypertension was significantly lower in the sodium intervention group (n=595) than in the standard care group (n=596), with a risk reduction of 0.82 (P<0.05). 9

Long‐term observational follow‐up studies of TOHP I and TOHP II have also provided useful data on the impact of sodium reduction on cardiovascular outcomes. 9 The average relative risk for incident hypertension across the entire trial duration (up to 48 months) for each active intervention group, including sodium reduction, was 0. 78 to 0.82 compared with the control group (18%–22% reduction). 21 Since these pivotal data from the TOHP trials were published, most subsequent randomized controlled trials have consistently demonstrated a linear association between reductions in sodium intake (1000–7000 mg/day exposure) and reduced BP in adults, with the most pronounced effect observed in individuals with hypertension at the time that sodium intake restriction was initiated. 40 For example, a dose–response meta‐analysis of 11 studies published between 1990 and 2017 confirmed the association between sodium intake and hypertension risk, with the model reporting an almost linear relationship between the 2 across a wide range of sodium exposures in individuals with and without hypertension. 41

Study participants who originally participated in the TOHP I and TOHP II studies were also included in a follow‐up analysis 5 to 10 years later to assess the impact of sodium intake on cardiovascular outcomes. 9 The relative risk of prespecified cardiovascular outcomes (composite of myocardial infarction, stroke, coronary revascularization, or cardiovascular death) was reduced by 25% in the sodium reduction group when adjusted for baseline characteristics (P=0.04) (Figure 2). Another TOHP follow‐up study conducted ~25 years after trial initiation identified a direct, albeit nonsignificant, linear relationship between high sodium intake and total mortality. 42 The hazard ratio (HR) for total mortality in the sodium intervention group compared with the control group was 0.85 (95% CI, 0.66–1.09; P=0.19) after adjustment for study site, age, sex, and ethnicity. Similar findings were reported in an observational analysis of participants from the TOHP study based on urinary sodium excretion (low defined as <2300 mg/24 hours, intermediate defined as 2300 to <3600 mg/24 hours, and high defined as ≥4800 mg/24 hours). In this analysis, adjusted HRs for mortality were 0.74, 0.90, and 1.13, respectively, compared with a reference group that had sodium excretions of 3600 to <4800 mg/24 hours (P value for the trend=0.092). 42

The DASH (Dietary Approach to Stop Hypertension)‐Sodium study evaluated the impact of sodium reduction, alone and in combination with the DASH diet—which prioritized fruit, vegetables, and low‐fat dairy products—on BP in individuals with or without hypertension. 11 During the run‐in period, all participants consumed a typical US diet (non‐DASH) with high sodium intake (mean urinary sodium excretion: 3330 mg/day) before being randomized to either the DASH or control (non‐DASH) diet (Figure 3A). Office BP reductions were both statistically and clinically significant when sodium intake decreased from high to low levels (Figure 3B). Among study participants randomized to the DASH diet, systolic BP fell by 3.0 mm Hg (95% CI, 1.7–4.3; P<0.001) and diastolic BP by 1.6 mm Hg (95% CI, 0.8–2.5; P<0.001) (Figure 3C). 11 Significant decreases in systolic BP with each stepwise reduction in sodium, from high to intermediate and intermediate to low, were observed (Figure 3C). Similarly, diastolic BP was significantly reduced in participants when transitioning from an intermediate‐to‐low sodium intake. Of note, changes from baseline in diastolic BP did not achieve statistical significance when patients switched from high to intermediate sodium intake. Among participants randomized to the control diet where sodium intake was then switched from high to low amounts, systolic BP fell by 6.7 mm Hg (95% CI, 5.4–8.0; P<0.001) and diastolic BP by 3.5 mm Hg (95% CI, 2.6–4.3; P<0.001) (Figure 3D).

Reducing sodium intake from high to low levels and adopting the DASH diet lowered systolic BP in participants regardless of their hypertension status or racial and ethnic background. 11 However, the magnitude of systolic BP reductions was greater in participants with hypertension at study initiation compared with those who were normotensive (−11.5 versus −7.1 mm Hg). In addition, among individuals with hypertension, Black participants had the greatest reductions in systolic BP, achieving decreases of 12.6 mm Hg compared with 9.5 mm Hg in non‐Black participants. A secondary analysis of the DASH‐Sodium study showed that the DASH diet combined with sodium reduction independently reduced 10‐year atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease risk score (American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association pooled cohort equation based on age, sex, race, systolic BP, diabetes, smoking status, treatment for hypertension, total cholesterol and high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol) by 5.3% compared with participants consuming a normal US diet and by 9.4% compared with individuals consuming a high sodium intake diet (as defined by daily sodium excretion of 3300 mg). 11 , 43 , 44 Furthermore, there was an additive effect of a 14.1% reduction in atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease risk score (when combining the DASH diet with decreased sodium intake). 43 Similar to the primary analysis, these lifestyle changes had the greatest impact among individuals with hypertension (P<0.008 versus normotensive), Black participants (P<0.001 versus non‐Black), and women (P<0.001 versus men).

Observational Studies

Population‐level data from both the United States and United Kingdom have demonstrated that the benefits observed in the clinical studies noted above are also evident in broader epidemiological analyses. A US model of cardiovascular disease (coronary heart disease policy model) projected that reducing total daily dietary sodium by 1200 mg would result in reductions in systolic BP that could prevent an estimated 60 000 to 120 000 new cases of coronary heart disease, 32 000 to 66 000 strokes, 54 000 to 99 000 new or recurrent myocardial infarction events, and 44 000 to 92 000 all‐cause deaths annually. 45 Consistent with results from the DASH‐Sodium study, the coronary heart disease model by Bibbins‐Domingo and colleagues demonstrated that all racial and sex groups studied would benefit from reduced sodium intake. Still, the relative benefits would be greater among Black individuals than among other racial groups and among women than among men (Figure S2). 45 Similarly, in the United Kingdom, a model published in 2011 estimated that reducing sodium intake by 1200 mg/day would prevent 32 200 cardiovascular events and 44 300 deaths from cardiovascular disease over a 10‐year period, resulting in 96 000 life‐years gained. 46 Although the 1200 mg/day reduction has not yet been achieved, between 2001 and 2014, the average population‐level sodium intake in England did decrease by 19.2% (720 mg/day). Despite the trend in sodium intake stalling between 2014 and 2018, the Preventable Risk Integrated Modeltime Cost Effectiveness model estimated that 27 630 premature ischemic heart disease events and 39 250 premature strokes were averted between 2000 and 2018. 47 A recent public health statement for England described the need to reduce sodium intake to 2400 mg/day by 2024. 30

Relationship Between Sodium Intake, BP, and Cardiovascular Outcomes

Whereas most large‐scale observational studies have described a linear relationship between sodium intake and BP, 16 a few have suggested a nonlinear association, based on observed increases in cardiovascular disease and mortality at very low sodium intake levels. 48 , 49 For example, a 2011 prospective cohort study involving 3681 participants enrolled in either the FLEMENGHO (Flemish Study on Genes, Environment, and Health Outcomes) or the EPOGH (European Project on Genes in Hypertension) and followed for nearly 8 years reported a “J‐shaped” relationship between urinary sodium excretion and some cardiovascular outcomes (P=0.02 for cardiovascular mortality but P=0.10 for all‐cause mortality and P=0.93 for incident hypertension). 49 This population model implied that both low (defined as 1150–2898 mg) and high (4094–9200 mg) levels of daily sodium excretion may be associated with adverse outcomes, including cardiovascular mortality.

However, methodological concerns have been raised regarding reported J‐shaped relationships between sodium intake and cardiovascular outcomes. A report from the Institute of Medicine concluded that associations between low sodium intake and adverse cardiovascular outcomes were both inconsistent and inconclusive. 50 One key concern was that assessments of sodium excretion did not reflect interindividual variability and day‐to‐day fluctuations in sodium intake. 51 For example, in the FLEMINGHO/EPOGH studies, 49 24‐hour sodium levels were extrapolated from a single spot urine sample, rather than assessing sodium excretion at multiple time points. In contrast, the TOHP studies based estimates of sodium intake on the average of several 24‐hour sodium excretions collected over up to 3 years, reaffirming the linear relationship between lower sodium intake (as low as 1500 mg/day) and improved cardiovascular outcomes. 10 , 21 , 52 The linear relationship was also confirmed in a pooled study of healthy adults from 6 prospective cohorts that assessed average sodium excretions from multiple 24‐hour urine samples during a median follow‐up period of 8.8 years. 53 There were 571 cardiovascular events that occurred in 10 709 participants followed prospectively. High sodium excretion was associated with increased cardiovascular risk in the model that excluded confounding factors such as age, sex, and race or ethnicity. Risk increased in a dose‐dependent manner with daily sodium intake ranging from 2000 to 6000 mg. Each daily increment of 1000 mg in sodium excretion was associated with an 18% increase in cardiovascular risk (HR, 1.18 [95% CI, 1.08–1.29], P<0.001).

Some analyses describing nonlinear relationships extrapolated sodium excretion using formulas applied to a single sample or a limited number of samples 17 , 54 , 55 ; a methodology that has since been shown to influence the shape of the relationship. For instance, a robust analysis of the 24‐year follow‐up TOHP data set (n=2974) clearly demonstrated that the use of different formulas affected the shape of the relationship between sodium intake and health outcomes. The average measured sodium intake was 3766±1290 mg/day. There was a significant linear association between the average measured sodium intake and mortality (P=0.029). This relationship was altered by the use of estimated sodium intakes. In fact, the 24‐hour urinary sodium excretions estimated from sodium concentration using the 3 commonly used formulas (Kawasaki, Tanaka, and INTERSALT) were all systematically biased with overestimation at lower levels and underestimation at higher levels of sodium intake. Furthermore, when sodium concentration was kept constant, the estimated values showed a similar systematic bias for all formulas. Application of the Kawasaki, Tanaka, or INTERSALT formulas yielded a range of J‐ or U‐shaped curves, demonstrating that the methodology employed can influence conclusions (Figure S3). 56 , 57 These results suggest that other variables in the formulas, independent of sodium, were contributing to the biased estimates.

The interpretation of observational data also requires caution due to the possibility of reverse causality, 51 as has been demonstrated by studies in people with chronic and debilitating diseases and elevated mortality rates. Individuals with chronic heart or kidney disease may reduce their sodium intake either on medical advice or when severe morbidity necessitates a change in diet. This is also likely to apply to individuals with hypertension, where there is generally more recognition of the link between salt intake and BP. 42 Additionally, individuals identified as being at risk of cardiovascular disease may take medications that affect kidney function, impacting urinary sodium levels, and further weakening the observed association between sodium excretion and outcomes. 58 Reverse causality may also have contributed to the poor outcomes reported at low sodium levels in observational analyses describing a J‐shaped relationship—particularly in patients with chronic and debilitating cardiovascular disorders. 10 , 58 In contrast, the linear relationships between sodium intake and cardiovascular outcomes described in the TOHP analyses and in the prospective 24‐hour sodium excretion study are based on data from healthier individuals who are prehypertensive, thereby reducing the risk of reverse causality bias. 42 , 53

The debate linking low sodium intake to adverse health outcomes has the potential to undermine public health messaging and place individuals at risk of poor cardiorenal outcomes. 59 However, given robust evidence supporting the benefits of reduced sodium intake, global and national guidelines continue to recommend sodium restriction to improve health outcomes, and the ongoing US Department of Health and Human Services' Million Hearts cardiovascular disease prevention initiative actively promotes population‐level sodium reduction. 14 , 23 , 33 , 60

Implications of Reducing Dietary Sodium Intake in Patients With Hypertension

The benefits of restricting sodium intake in patients with hypertension are substantial, 11 , 40 , 43 , 45 and lifestyle changes to reduce sodium levels in patients with hypertension, including in those treated with antihypertensive medications, are strongly recommended by clinical practice recommendations, including the recent 2025 American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology hypertension guidelines. 14 These recommendations are based on decades of research showing the benefit of salt restriction/sodium reduction in patients with hypertension, whether untreated or on chronic antihypertensive therapies. A recent meta‐analysis by Song and colleagues that included 35 studies and around 2880 patients showed that for every 2300 mg reduction in 24‐hour urinary sodium excretion, systolic BP (office or ambulatory) decreased by 6.8 mm Hg (95% CI, 5.0–8.7) and diastolic BP (office or ambulatory) decreased by 3.9 mm Hg (95% CI, 2.3–5.4). 61 The authors reported that dose–response effects varied across classes of BP‐lowering medications, with greater effects observed in patients taking β‐blockers and renin‐angiotensin‐aldosterone system inhibitors. There were no subgroup differences in BP reduction by age, baseline 24‐hour urinary sodium excretion, BP levels, or study duration. 61 These data strongly support sodium restriction in patients with hypertension on antihypertensive therapy.

Restricting sodium intake is particularly relevant in patients with treatment‐resistant hypertension (defined as uncontrolled BP despite treatment with three or more antihypertensive drug classes, including a diuretic). 14 Patients with resistant hypertension are at high risk of cardiovascular disease, and effective pharmacologic management remains an important clinical issue. In a study of 15 patients with resistant hypertension in Denmark, a reduction in urinary sodium excretion from 4276 to 2092 mg/day resulted in substantial and statistically significant reductions in mean 24‐hour ambulatory BP (systolic BP by 10 mm Hg, P<0.001, and diastolic BP by 5 mm Hg, P<0.05) after 2 weeks on the low‐sodium diet. 62 Of note, nocturnal systolic and diastolic BP were reduced significantly on the low‐sodium diet (8 and 5 mm Hg, respectively, both P<0.05). These data support the clinical contribution of high sodium levels in treatment‐resistant hypertension and highlight how lowering sodium intake could reduce BP beyond what is achieved with antihypertensive agents. Other studies have also shown the impact of sodium's influence in patients with stage 2 (nonresistant) hypertension treated with antihypertensive medication. 63 , 64 Using 24‐hour BP monitoring, Weir and colleagues 64 studied 16 patients with salt sensitive hypertension and found a significant reduction in mean BP (P<0.01) on a low versus high sodium diet.

SODIUM IN PHARMACEUTICAL PRODUCTS

Although current public health policies focus on dietary sodium intake, pharmaceutical products may also contribute to an individual's daily sodium intake and risk of adverse cardiovascular outcomes (Table). Sodium is included in oral medications either as an active ingredient when physiological sodium needs to be replaced, as a cation for the active ingredient, or as an excipient to enhance solubility or disintegration. 65 Analgesics are the most frequently prescribed sodium‐containing medications, and calcium supplements, gastrointestinal motility agents, oral corticosteroids, and sodium oxybate formulations also contain substantial amounts of sodium. 66 , 67 , 68 Analgesic medications such as acetaminophen (paracetamol) are available in several formulations; effervescent or soluble forms contain >400 mg of sodium/dose, whereas standard oral tablets are sodium free (Table). 67 , 69

In a UK population‐based nested case‐controlled study that included 1 292 337 individuals with a mean follow‐up time of 7.2 years, the median daily sodium intake in individuals consuming high‐sodium effervescent, dispersible, and soluble medications was ∼2455 mg/day, exceeding the current US recommended dietary intake limit of 2300 mg/day. 67 Notably, many of the high‐sodium pharmaceutical products are available over the counter. 69 Together with effervescent nutritional supplements (eg, vitamins, minerals), these agents can significantly increase daily sodium intake without individuals realizing they may be contributing to an increased risk of cardiovascular events. For example, an analysis in Germany estimated that each tablet of over‐the‐counter medication contained 52 to 575 mg of sodium, 70 representing ~2% to 25% of the recommended US daily intake. 14

Sodium Information in Pharmaceutical Product Labeling

Although regulatory agencies have acknowledged the importance of reporting sodium content in medications, labeling requirements remain inconsistent across regions. In the United States, over‐the‐counter drug labeling must warn patients if the maximum recommended dose contains >140 mg of sodium and must list the drug's sodium content if it exceeds 5 mg in the maximum recommended dose. 70 , 71 Additionally, regulations require the product information sheet to report the amount of sodium in each product (in mg) regardless of whether sodium is present as an active or inactive ingredient. If a single dose contains <5 mg of sodium, manufacturers may elect not to include information on sodium content. Dietary supplement labels must also disclose sodium content. 71

For prescription medicines in the United States, reporting sodium content in the prescribing information is recommended for products containing ≥5 mg of sodium per single dose; however, this is not a mandatory recommendation. 70 In Europe, pharmaceutical products containing ≤23 mg of sodium per dose are considered to be “sodium free.” 65 Medications exceeding this threshold must include a warning that individuals on a low‐sodium diet should consider the sodium content, along with a comparison to the World Health Organization's recommended daily maximum intake (indicated as a percentage). 65 Where sodium content exceeds 391 mg, the medication is considered to be a high‐sodium pharmaceutical product.

THE INFLUENCE OF HIGH‐SODIUM‐CONTAINING MEDICATIONS ON BP

Few studies have assessed the impact of sodium intake from pharmaceutical products compared with dietary sources of sodium on health outcomes. 68 , 72 , 73 , 74 A systematic analysis of 8 published studies by Perrin and coworkers 72 evaluated the cardiovascular impact of medication‐derived sodium intake of >390 mg/day using BP as a surrogate marker for cardiovascular outcomes. 72 Although the authors concluded that the quality of information provided by their analysis was moderate, there was evidence of cardiovascular complications of high‐sodium containing medications with daily sodium intake of ≥1500 mg/day and cardiovascular risk was increased with long‐term exposure to high sodium containing medications, particularly if hypertension developed. Other studies also demonstrated comparable increases in BP, 73 , 74 which were reversible upon discontinuation of the high‐sodium‐containing pharmaceutical agent.

Sodium‐containing analgesics. Effervescent, high‐sodium formulations of analgesic medications are relatively common. In a study of 34 patients with uncontrolled hypertension receiving acetaminophen (paracetamol) for osteoarthritis, switching from an effervescent formulation (1700 mg sodium/day) to sodium‐free tablets for 4 weeks reduced office systolic BP by 13.1 mm Hg (95% CI, 11.9–14.3, P<0.0001) and diastolic BP by 2.5 mm Hg (95% CI, 2.1–2.9, P<0.0001). 73 These findings were subsequently supported by a multicenter, randomized, crossover clinical trial in which 24‐hour ambulatory BP monitoring was used to evaluate BP changes in 46 patients with hypertension on a high sodium effervescent acetaminophen (paracetamol) versus a standard low sodium paracetamol formulation. 74 Ambulatory systolic BP was found to be significantly higher in the effervescent group compared with the sodium‐free group (3.99 mm Hg [95% CI, 1.35–6.63]; P=0.04) (Figure 4).

High‐sodium therapies for narcolepsy. Patients with narcolepsy, a chronic sleep disorder characterized by excessive daytime sleepiness, cataplexy, disturbed nocturnal sleep, and sleep‐related hallucinations, are at increased risk for cardiovascular disease. 75 , 76 , 77 Sodium is a component of some oxybate formulations used to treat patients with narcolepsy. 68 An observational study by Ben‐Joseph and colleagues demonstrated an association between new‐onset hypertension and sodium oxybate use (1100–1640 mg sodium, depending on dose, 48%–71% of the US maximum recommended daily sodium intake). 68 Compared with a control cohort of patients (n=1908) with narcolepsy who did not initiate sodium oxybate therapy, the sodium oxybate‐treated patients (n=954) had a nonsignificant increased risk of a hypertension diagnosis within 6 months of treatment initiation (0.94 versus 0.52 per 100 patients) (odds ratio [OR], 1.81 [95% CI, 0.73–4.46]). The composite end point of either a hypertension diagnosis or initiation of antihypertensive medication was higher among sodium oxybate users (6.60 per 100 patients) than among the control cohort (4.20 per 100 patients) (OR, 1.61 [95% CI, 1.15–2.27]). This difference in outcome was sustained in a sensitivity analysis that excluded patients with cardiovascular disease.

In a multicenter, single‐arm, open‐label trial, a low‐sodium oxybate formulation with the same active oxybate moiety was studied for BP effects in 43 patients with narcolepsy. 78 The average doses of both high‐ and low‐sodium oxybate were ~8 grams per night in an oral suspension. In the low‐sodium formulation, sodium is replaced by a calcium‐, magnesium‐, sodium‐, and potassium‐based salt, which reduces sodium content to 87 to 131 mg when used at recommended doses (4%–6% of the US maximum recommended daily sodium intake). Patients switching from the high‐sodium oxybate to the low‐sodium formulation (median change in sodium excretion of 1339 mg/day; Figure 5A) had significant reductions in mean 24‐hour ambulatory systolic BP at the end of the 6‐week treatment period (4.1 mm Hg; P<0.0019). 78 Seated office systolic BP was also significantly reduced in this period (9.1 mm Hg; P<0.0001). Furthermore, the proportion of patients with normal BP (systolic BP<130 mm Hg) increased from 34.9% at the start of the study while on the high‐sodium oxybate to 55.8% after switching to the low‐sodium formulation (Figure 5B).

Impact of Sodium‐Containing Pharmaceuticals on Cardiovascular Outcomes

The large number of individuals using effervescent or soluble forms of acetaminophen (paracetamol) has provided an opportunity to assess the influence of sodium from these analgesics on cardiovascular outcomes. A population‐based nested case‐controlled study using the UK Clinical Practice Research Datalink database followed 1 292 337 individuals who received at least 2 prescriptions for sodium‐containing formulations or matched standard formulations of the same drug, for an average of 7.23 years. 67 The primary end point of the study was a composite cardiovascular outcome of nonfatal myocardial infarction, nonfatal stroke, or vascular death, which occurred in 61 072 subjects. Individuals who were prescribed the high‐sodium containing formulation (median sodium consumption of 2456 mg/day; 107% of the US maximum recommended daily intake) were more likely to have sustained a cardiovascular event than those receiving standard formulations (OR, 1.14 [95% CI, 1.09–1.18]) when adjusted for body mass index and smoking. Among individuals on high‐sodium formulations, there was also an increased chance of developing hypertension (OR, 7.18 [95% CI, 6.74–7.65]), incident nonfatal stroke (OR, 1.22 [95% CI, 1.16–1.29]), and all‐cause mortality (OR, 1.28 [95% CI, 1.23–1.33]). Increases in incident heart failure were not observed with the high‐sodium formulations (OR, 0.98 [95% CI, 0.93–1.04]).

Analysis of The Health Improvement Network, an electronic database of primary care records in the United Kingdom that includes 17 million individuals, evaluated the risks of incident cardiovascular disease and all‐cause mortality in individuals with or without hypertension who initiated sodium‐containing acetaminophen (paracetamol) formulations between January 2000 and December 2017. 79 Individuals without hypertension who initiated effervescent acetaminophen (paracetamol) had an increased risk for new‐onset cardiovascular disease (HR, 1.45 [95% CI, 1.18–1.79]) and all‐cause mortality (HR, 1.87 [95% CI, 1.74–2.00]) compared with those receiving sodium‐free formulations (Figure 6). Individuals with hypertension taking the high‐sodium formulations had a higher risk of developing cardiovascular disease (HR, 1.59 [95% CI, 1.32–1.92]) and all‐cause mortality (HR, 2.05 [95% CI, 1.92–2.19]) compared with those without hypertension, although the 2 groups were not compared directly. The risks for individual components of the cardiovascular disease composite end point—myocardial infarction, stroke, and heart failure—were also significantly increased in individuals without and with hypertension in the effervescent acetaminophen (paracetamol) group.

The Health Improvement Network analysis also evaluated the influence of sodium‐containing ranitidine and ibuprofen on the risk of cardiovascular disease. 79 Although the number of patients included was small, an increased risk for sodium‐containing formulations compared with sodium‐free formulations was demonstrated in patients with hypertension, where cardiovascular outcomes were increased by 34% with high‐sodium ibuprofen and by 66% with high‐sodium ranitidine. The association between number of prescriptions for sodium‐containing medication and the risk of cardiovascular disease (P=0.030 and P=0.034 in participants without and with hypertension, respectively) and all‐cause mortality (P<0.001 for both) increased linearly. The results from this large‐scale analysis demonstrate the substantial contribution of sodium‐containing pharmaceuticals to overall sodium intake and highlight their impact on cardiovascular outcomes.

Most evidence demonstrates a correlation between the sodium content of analgesic medications and cardiovascular risk, but this has not been demonstrated in all analyses. A machine learning study assessing electronic health record data in the United Kingdom on individuals 60 to 90 years of age with at least 1 prescription for acetaminophen (paracetamol) found no difference in BP with short‐term use of normal‐ and effervescent‐formulations and no association with major cardiovascular events among high‐sodium‐containing products. 80 However, all‐cause mortality was increased in individuals taking high‐sodium‐containing analgesics compared with those not taking sodium‐containing analgesics. These findings led the authors to question whether previous studies accounted for all potential confounders and highlighted the need for more robust studies to investigate the influence of sodium in analgesic medications on long‐term health. 80 , 81

Factors That May Influence the BP Response to High‐Sodium Pharmaceutical Products

The BP response to sodium is modulated by intake of potassium and calcium. 82 , 83 , 84 Both epidemiologic and clinical data suggest that sodium chloride‐induced increases in BP are exacerbated in individuals with diets low in potassium or calcium and that the ratio of urinary sodium to potassium may be a stronger correlate of BP and cardiovascular risk than either sodium or potassium alone. 53 , 82 Consequently, BP changes in patients taking high‐sodium pharmaceutical products could be influenced by dietary intake of potassium or calcium.

Kotchen and Kotchen reported that the anion ingested with sodium (ie, chloride) may also influence the BP response to pharmaceutical and dietary sodium. 82 These data are exclusively from early animal studies and indicated that sodium linked to anions other than chloride (eg, bicarbonate, phosphate, and amino acids) had little effect on mean arterial BP compared with control intake. However, effervescent forms of acetaminophen (paracetamol), as described earlier can increase BP and usually contain sodium bicarbonate or sodium hydrogen carbonate. Clinical data evaluating whether different anions provided with sodium have an impact of BP or cardiovascular outcomes have not been reported. 67 , 69 , 74

CONCLUSIONS

Widespread global public health initiatives supporting the reduction in daily dietary sodium intake have been developed due to the established relationships between sodium, hypertension, and adverse cardiovascular outcomes. The contribution of sodium in pharmaceutical products is often overlooked in public health and society guidelines, but as shown in this review, pharmaceutical sodium intake is associated with increased BP, development of hypertension, and related adverse cardiovascular effects.

Evidence suggests that increased data availability and prescriber knowledge regarding sodium content from medication sources can be influential. In the United Kingdom, using a longitudinal cross‐sectional analysis, investigators have demonstrated a substantial decline in the prescription of high‐sodium‐containing medicines between 2008 and 2019 following the publication of research reporting the cardiovascular risk associated with these formulations and guideline revisions related to excipient labeling. 66 However, it is important to acknowledge that many high‐sodium effervescent analgesic formulations are available over the counter, rather than by prescription, underscoring the need for effective education, not only for health care providers, but also for the general population. 85 Prescribers should remain vigilant about the cardiovascular risks associated with high‐sodium medications and prioritize prescribing low‐sodium alternatives when available, particularly for patients at increased risk of cardiovascular complications. Regulatory inconsistencies persist in sodium content labeling across global regions and between over‐the‐counter and prescription medications, creating additional barriers to informed decision‐making. There is a clear need to establish standardized labeling requirements to ensure that prescription and over‐the‐counter medications with a high sodium content are clearly identifiable. Given the weight of evidence demonstrating the increased cardiovascular risk with sodium‐containing medications, continued inaction on labeling of sodium‐containing medications is untenable. 85 Improving product labeling and increasing education regarding the contribution of pharmaceutical sodium content in medications on cardiovascular outcomes provides an opportunity to build on existing dietary sodium restriction initiatives and further improve public health.
