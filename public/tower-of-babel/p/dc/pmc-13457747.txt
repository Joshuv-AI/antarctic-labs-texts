Prognostic impact of epicardial fat volume, anatomical and functional imaging across the cardiovascular risk spectrum in patients with suspected coronary artery disease: a single-center retrospective cohort study
Authors: Chunbo Wang, Fan Sun, Yu Tian, Wenji Yu, Yue Yue, Xiaoyu Yang, Feifei Zhang, Xiaoliang Shao, Yunmei Shi, Bao Liu, Jianfeng Wang, Yuetao Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEpicardial adipose tissue (EAT) is associated with coronary artery disease (CAD) and adverse cardiovascular outcomes; however, its prognostic relevance across different cardiovascular risk scores remains uncertain. This study evaluated the long-term prognostic significance of EAT, coronary anatomical findings, and functional ischemia in patients with suspected CAD stratified by Framingham risk score (FRS) categories.MethodsA consecutive retrospective cohort of 361 symptomatic patients who underwent both single-photon emission computed tomography/computed tomography myocardial perfusion imaging (SPECT/CT MPI) and coronary angiography (CAG) or coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) at baseline was analyzed. Epicardial fat volume (EFV) and coronary artery calcium score (CACS) were quantified from integrated computed tomography (CT) with SPECT/CT. Ischemia burden was defined as ≥5% ischemic myocardium on MPI, and obstructive CAD as ≥50% stenosis. Major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) were recorded during follow-up.ResultsOver a median follow-up of 4.6 years, 54 patients (15%) experienced MACE. In the multivariable analysis, EFV [adjusted hazard ratio (aHR) =2.09; 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.08–4.05; P=0.029], CACS (aHR =2.58; 95% CI: 1.39–4.80; P=0.003), and obstructive CAD (aHR =3.07; 95% CI: 1.59–5.90; P<0.001) were independently associated with MACE, while ischemia burden showed borderline significance (aHR =2.27; 95% CI: 0.94–5.49; P=0.069). EFV improved risk discrimination over FRS, CACS, obstructive CAD, and ischemia burden (all P<0.05). In the stratified analyses, CACS (aHR =2.88; P=0.017) and obstructive CAD (aHR =3.69; P=0.005) predicted MACE in low-to-intermediate FRS patients, whereas EFV (aHR =3.13; P=0.020) and ischemia burden (aHR =4.67; P=0.005) were only associated with MACE in high-risk FRS patients. EFV was significantly associated with MACE in patients with diabetes (aHR =6.61; P=0.010), but not in those without diabetes (aHR =1.45; P=0.359), with ischemia showing a similar pattern.ConclusionsEFV independently predicted long-term MACE and improved prognostic discrimination beyond clinical risk, anatomical imaging, and functional ischemia. Prognostic determinants varied by cardiovascular risk level, with anatomical markers being more informative in lower-risk patients, while EFV and functional ischemia were more informative in high-risk and diabetic patients. These findings support a risk-adapted strategy for the use of quantitative imaging biomarkers in patients with suspected CAD.Keywords: Epicardial adipose tissue (EAT), coronary stenosis, myocardial ischemia, prognosis, Framingham risk score (FRS)

Introduction

Coronary artery disease (CAD) is the leading cause of death worldwide (1-3). The 2024 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines recognize CAD as a continuum of structural and functional abnormalities in the epicardial and microvascular circulation (4). Myocardial ischemia represents a relatively late manifestation in the process of CAD, while coronary atherosclerosis represents an early stage of the disease process (5). Although the prognostic value of myocardial ischemia has been well established (6-8), recent studies have shown inconsistent results when comparing anatomical and functional imaging in predicting cardiovascular outcomes (9-13). Studies have reported heterogeneous prognostic performance of anatomical and functional imaging across different cardiovascular risk states. Evidence suggests that functional ischemia may be most prognostic in higher-risk symptomatic patients, whereas anatomical markers such as obstructive stenosis may provide greater value in lower-risk patients (14,15).

Epicardial adipose tissue (EAT), a metabolically active visceral fat depot surrounding the myocardium and coronary arteries, has emerged as a potential contributor to CAD through local inflammatory, oxidative, paracrine, and vasocrine mechanisms. There is no distinct fascial barrier between EAT and the coronary arterial wall. Dysfunctional EAT may directly affect the adjacent vasculature by secreting pro-inflammatory adipokines and cytokines while reducing protective adipokines, thereby promoting endothelial dysfunction, coronary atherosclerosis, plaque progression, and plaque vulnerability (16-18).

Consistent with these mechanisms, previous studies and meta-analyses have reported that increased EAT is associated with CAD presence, coronary plaque burden, coronary calcification, and adverse cardiovascular outcomes (19-24). The association between EAT and adverse cardiovascular outcomes appears particularly relevant in cardiometabolic high-risk populations, such as patients with type 2 diabetes, in whom EAT has been linked to incident cardiovascular disease (CVD) and mortality (25,26). However, the clinical and prognostic significance of EAT remains inconsistent.

The CORE320 multicenter study found no significant association between epicardial fat volume (EFV) and coronary artery calcification, CAD severity, or myocardial perfusion abnormalities in symptomatic patients (27). Another longitudinal study in patients with established CAD showed that echocardiographic EAT was not a strong predictor of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) (28). Recent research found that EAT was correlated with the progression of coronary plaque quantified by coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA); however, EAT was not associated with MACE (29). These conflicting findings suggest that the prognostic value of EAT may depend on patient characteristics, underlying cardiometabolic risk and disease stage.

Although the Framingham risk score (FRS) was originally designed for primary prevention (30), it may still reflect a cumulative risk profile in symptomatic patients and could potentially modify the prognostic value of anatomical, functional, and metabolic imaging biomarkers. However, to date, these combined indices have not systematically been examined in a real-world suspected CAD population. Therefore, this study evaluated the long-term prognostic impact of EFV, coronary anatomy, and functional ischemia across FRS-defined risk categories in patients with suspected CAD. We hypothesize that the prognostic impact of EAT or functional ischemia may be more prominent in higher-risk patients, while anatomical coronary imaging may be more significant in lower-risk patients. In this study, we aimed to determine whether EFV adds incremental prognostic value and whether different imaging biomarkers hold distinct prognostic roles depending on baseline cardiovascular risk. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2025-1-2684/rc).

Methods

Study design and populations

This study was a post-hoc analysis of a previous retrospective cohort population (31). Patients presenting with chest discomfort and suspected CAD at The Third Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University were consecutively enrolled from January 2014 to December 2024 in the study. All included individuals underwent either coronary angiography (CAG) or CCTA and stress/rest single-photon emission computed tomography/computed tomography myocardial perfusion imaging (SPECT/CT MPI), with the interval between the two examinations limited to no more than 3 months. All participants were hospitalized during the period in which stress/rest SPECT/CT MPI was performed.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) acute coronary syndrome, including ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), and unstable angina (UA); (II) prior myocardial infarction or coronary revascularization, including percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) or coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG); (III) valvular heart disease, heart failure, or cardiomyopathy; (IV) active malignancy; (V) acute or chronic inflammatory disorders; or (VI) incomplete clinical or imaging data, suboptimal image quality precluding diagnosis, or loss to follow up.

The study was designed as a long-term follow-up cohort with follow-up ending on May 1, 2025. Patients enrolled within the most recent two years (January 2023 to December 2024) were subsequently excluded. The study flowchart is shown in Figure 1.

Information on discharge medications prescribed by the attending physician was recorded. At the patient level, individuals were classified as having obstructive CAD if CAG/CCTA demonstrated ≥50% diameter stenosis in any native epicardial coronary artery. Those without such findings were categorized as having non-obstructive CAD. The FRS was calculated based on patients’ clinical risk factors, including age, total cholesterol (TC), smoking status, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, systolic blood pressure (SBP), and hypertensive treatment status, stratified by sex according to the Adult Treatment Panel III hard coronary heart disease framework (30). Based on the continuous FRS, participants were stratified into 10-year risk categories: low risk (<10%), intermediate risk (10–19%), and high risk (≥20%) (30,32).

Stress test, SPECT/CT MPI image acquisition, and myocardial ischemia

The SPECT/CT MPI acquisition was performed using a Siemens Symbia T16 dual-head SPECT/CT system from Germany. 99mTc-methoxyisobutylisonitrile (MIBI) was provided by Shanghai Xinke Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., with radiochemical purity > 95% and an injection dose of 740–925 MBq. Patients discontinued betablockers, nitrates, and other medications affecting heart rate or coronary artery dilation prior to the test for 24 h. All patients underwent a 2-day protocol and rest plus exercise or Adenosine/Regadenoson pharmacologic stress MPI, following specific guidelines (33). Patients who were unable to perform exercise stress testing underwent pharmacologic stress MPI instead. The acquisition parameters for both the rest and stress studies were as follows: a parallel-hole, low-energy, high-resolution collimator; a 128×128 matrix; and a magnification factor of 1.45. The two detector heads were arranged at a 90° angle, each rotating 90° to complete a 180° arc, with data collected every 6° and 35 seconds per frame. Electrocardiogram (ECG) R-wave-gated acquisition was applied, yielding eight frames per cardiac cycle. Image reconstruction was performed using the three-dimensional Flash iterative algorithm (16 iterations, two subsets) to generate three-dimensional axial images of the left ventricle, including short-axis, horizontal long-axis, and vertical long-axis views.

The left ventricle was segmented into the standard 17-segment model, and the perfusion territories of the left anterior descending artery, left circumflex artery, and right coronary artery were assigned according to the American Heart Association guidelines (34). Two nuclear medicine physicians independently evaluated the images. Reversible myocardial perfusion defects were defined as defects present in at least three consecutive frames and two adjacent slices within the coronary perfusion territories on stress/rest MPI, characterized by decreased uptake on stress images with partial or complete fill on rest images. When diaphragmatic attenuation influenced the inferior wall or breast attenuation affected the anterior wall, attenuation-corrected SPECT/CT images were reviewed alongside wall-motion data generated by Cedars-Quantitative Gated SPECT (Cedars-QGS) software. Short-axis images were converted into polar maps using Cedars-Sinai–Quantitative Perfusion SPECT (Cedars-QPS) software, which automatically assigned a perfusion score to each myocardial segment on a 5-point scale (0 = normal, 1 = mild reduction, 2 = moderate reduction, 3 = severe reduction, 4 = absent perfusion). The summed stress score (SSS), summed rest score (SRS), and summed difference score (SDS = SSS − SRS) were calculated. Myocardial ischemia was defined as the presence of reversible perfusion defects and/or an SDS ≥2 (35,36). An SDS ≥2 was used as an auxiliary criterion for identifying myocardial ischemia. If the visual interpretation and the SDS classification were inconsistent, the visual assessment was considered the definitive judgment.

The percentage of ischemic areas on the left ventricular (%ischemia) was automatically detected by Cedars-QPS software as follows: %Ischemia = Stress extent on left ventricular % − Rest extent on left ventricular %. The %ischemia was then categorized into the following cut-off groups: no or very mild ischemia (<5%), mild ischemia (5–10%), and moderate-to-severe ischemia (>10%) (37,38). Ischemia burden was defined as the percentage of myocardial ischemia area ≥5% detected by SPECT MPI (Figure 2).

Measurement of EFV, epicardial fat volume index (EFVi), and coronary artery calcium score (CACS)

Following the acquisition of the rest SPECT/CT MPI, a low-dose cardiac CT scan was performed to assess the EFV and CACS. The CT protocol included the following parameters: cardiac cycle phase, 60–80% of the R–R interval; tube voltage, 130 kV; tube current, 100 mA; and slice thickness, 3 mm. The scan range extended from the level of the tracheal bifurcation to approximately 1–2 cm below the diaphragm. EAT was defined as adipose tissue located between the visceral pericardium and the epicardial surface, with attenuation values between −190 and −30 Hounsfield units (HU). The upper boundary was determined by the axial slice where the pulmonary artery bifurcation was visualized within the aorta-pulmonary window, while the lowest slice containing the cardiac apex served as the inferior limit. The pericardial contour was manually delineated across all slices within this range.

EFV and epicardial fat mean density (MEAN density) were then automatically quantified using dedicated software (Syngo Volume, Siemens Medical Solutions, Erlangen, Germany; Figure 2). The EFVi was calculated by normalizing the EFV to the body surface area (BSA), defined as follows: EFVi = EFV (cm3) ÷ BSA (m2) (39). Coronary artery calcium (CAC) was identified as any high attenuation focus within the coronary arteries with a density greater than 130 HU. The CACS was calculated as the sum of the calcium scores for all coronary arteries, derived semi-automatically using Agatston analysis software (Figure 2) (40).

Follow-up and outcomes

Patients were followed up through electronic medical records and/or telephone contact. MACE was defined as a composite of all-cause mortality, non-fatal myocardial infarction, stroke, heart failure, and late revascularization. The secondary endpoint was defined as a composite of all-cause mortality, non-fatal myocardial infarction, and stroke. The follow-up period began on the date the patients visited the nuclear medicine department for the first day of SPECT/CT. Early revascularization was defined as PCI or CABG performed within 3 months of MPI, while late revascularization was defined as PCI or CABG performed more than 3 months after MPI. If patients experienced multiple instances of the defined endpoints, only the first occurrence was included in the analyses of MACE and the secondary endpoints. For each patient, the time to MACE and the secondary endpoint was calculated from the first date of SPECT/CT to the date of endpoint occurrence; otherwise, the data were censored at the last contact in the medical system or May 1, 2025.

Statistical analysis

Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were compared using independent t-tests or analysis of variance (ANOVA) with false discovery rate (FDR) adjusted post-hoc pairwise t-tests. Non-normally distributed variables are reported as median [interquartile range (IQR)] and were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test or Kruskal-Wallis test with FDR-corrected pairwise Wilcoxon tests. Categorical variables are expressed as counts (%) and were compared using chi-square tests, with continuity correction or Fisher’s exact tests applied as appropriate. Cox proportional hazards models were used to evaluate associations between variables and clinical outcomes, expressed as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated and compared using the log-rank test. Restricted cubic splines were applied to assess potential nonlinear relationships between EFV, EFVi, and CACS (as continuous variables) and outcomes. Optimal cut-off values for the EFV, EFVi, and CACS were identified using the “pROC” package based on the maximum Youden index. These thresholds were subsequently incorporated into multivariable Cox models and subgroup analyses stratified by the FRS. Harrell’s Concordance index (C-index) values from the Cox models were compared using the DeLong test with the “CompareC” package. To examine potential effect modification by the FRS, Poisson regression was used to calculate adjusted annualized event rates for the EFV/EFVi, CACS, obstructive CAD, and myocardial ischemia in the overall cohort and within the FRS subgroups, adjusting for low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, fasting blood glucose (FBG), left ventricular end-systolic diameter (LVESd), and creatinine. All analyses were performed in R version 4.5.1 (http://www.R-project.org). Two-tailed P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics across MACE

After excluding 38 participants who were lost to follow-up, the data of 361 patients were ultimately analyzed over a median follow-up of 4.6 years (IQR, 3.07–6.31 years). MACE occurred in 54 (15.0%) patients, and secondary endpoints occurred in 35 (9.7%) patients (Table S1). Exercise stress was performed in 76% of patients and pharmacologic stress in 24% of patients; coronary anatomy was evaluated by CAG (77%) or CCTA (23%). The median age of the patients was 63 years (IQR, 57–69 years), and 57% were male. Table 1 presents the clinical characteristics of the patients stratified by outcome. The patients with MACE were older, more often male, smokers, hypertensive, and had larger BSA than those without MACE (all P<0.01). The patients with MACE were treated more frequently with Angiotensin II Receptor Blocker (ARB) than those without MACE (46.3% vs. 30.9%, P=0.04). The patients with MACE had a higher CACS [46.40 (IQR, 0–227.63) vs. 0.20 (IQR, 0–83.70), P=0.002] and more obstructive CAD (72.2% vs. 48.9%, P<0.001) than those without MACE. The FRS high-risk category was more common in the MACE group than in the non-MACE group (55.56% vs. 23.45%, P<0.001, Table 1). FBG, hemoglobin glycated (HbA1c), creatinine, B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP), and left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVEDd) were also higher in the MACE group than in the non-MACE group (all P<0.05; Table S2).

Baseline characteristics across the FRS

The low-, intermediate-, and high-risk FRS categories included 169 (46.8%), 90 (24.9%), and 102 (28.3%) patients, respectively (Table S3). Age, the proportion of males, BSA, smoking, SBP, and the prevalence of hypertension increased across the FRS categories (all P<0.05). The incidence of MACE increased from 8.88% to 10.00% to 29.41% across the low-, intermediate-, and high-risk groups (P<0.001). Coronary obstruction, creatinine, LVEDd, and LVESd increased, while HDL decreased across groups (Table S3).

Comparison of EAT by MACE and FRS stratification

EFV and EFVi were higher in the patients with MACE than those without MACE [EFV: 174.33 (IQR, 130.38–202.09) vs. 146.32 (IQR, 119.46–184.25) cm3, P=0.008; EFVi: 96.21 (IQR, 76.17–115.79) vs. 84.79 (IQR, 71.43–103.15) cm3/m2, P=0.023, Table 1]. Epicardial fat mean density (MEAN density) did not differ between the with and without MACE groups. EFV and EFVi increased progressively from the low to high FRS categories (both P<0.001; Table S3 and Figure 3).

Association of EAT, coronary stenosis, CACS, or ischemia with MACE

The optimal cut-off values for predicting MACE were 170.15 cm3, 94.22 cm3/m2, and 3.15 AU for EFV, EFVi and CACS, respectively (Figure S1). Univariate Cox regression revealed significant associations for EFV (HR =2.79, 95% CI: 1.63–4.80, P<0.001), EFVi (HR =2.46, 95% CI: 1.43–4.23, P=0.001), CACS (HR =3.31, 95% CI: 1.83–5.98, P<0.001), obstructive CAD (HR =3.70, 95% CI: 1.98–6.91, P<0.001), and traditional factors, including age, sex, hypertension, and smoking (all P<0.05; Table 2) with MACE. After adjustment for age, sex, body mass index (BMI), and traditional cardiovascular risk factors, EFV [adjusted hazard ratio (aHR) =2.09, 95% CI: 1.08–4.05, P=0.029], CACS (aHR =2.58, 95% CI: 1.39–4.80, P=0.003), and obstructive CAD (aHR =3.07, 95% CI: 1.59–5.90, P<0.001) remained independent predictors. EFVi (aHR =1.80, 95% CI: 0.94–3.45, P=0.074) and ischemia burden (aHR =2.27, 95% CI: 0.94–5.49, P=0.069) showed borderline associations (Table 3). Spline analyses confirmed linear dose-response relationships between EFV, EFVi and CACS with MACE (Figure S2). The secondary endpoint results are shown in Tables S4,S5.

Subgroup analysis by FRS

The low- and intermediate-risk FRS groups were combined due to similar survival (P=0.704; Figure 4). In the high-risk subgroup, EFV (aHR =3.13, 95% CI: 1.20–8.17, P=0.020) and EFVi (aHR =2.78, 95% CI: 1.13–6.84, P=0.025) were independent predictors of MACE, while CACS and obstructive CAD were not (both P>0.10, Figure 5). In the low-intermediate subgroup, CACS (aHR =2.88, 95% CI: 1.21–6.89, P=0.017) and obstructive CAD (aHR =3.69, 95% CI: 1.50–9.07, P=0.005) were independent predictors of MACE, while EFV/EFVi was not (P>0.39). Ischemia burden only predicted MACE in the high-risk subgroup (aHR =4.67, 95% CI: 1.60–13.63, P=0.005, Figure 5). Annualized MACE rates showed consistent patterns: high EFV was associated with higher overall risk (4.2% vs. 1.7%, P=0.001), significant only in the high-risk FRS group (9.2% vs. 3.6%, P=0.02; Figure 6). The EFVi results were similar. CACS and obstructive CAD significantly stratified risk in low-intermediate FRS but not in high-risk FRS (Figure S4). Ischemia burden was only significantly associated with MACE rates in the high-risk FRS group (22.9% vs. 5.4%, P=0.011, Figure S4).

Sensitivity analysis

After removing patients with early revascularization (N=29), the results remained stable. In the high-risk FRS group, EFV (aHR =4.00, 95% CI: 1.36–11.78, P=0.012), EFVi (aHR =3.59, 95% CI: 1.31–9.84, P=0.013), and ischemia burden (aHR =5.31, 95% CI: 1.29–21.78, P=0.020) were identified as predictors of MACE, while CACS and obstructive CAD were not (P>0.09). In the low-to-intermediate-risk FRS group, CACS (aHR =2.58, 95% CI: 1.003–6.61, P=0.049) and obstructive CAD (aHR =3.23, 95% CI: 1.26–8.26, P=0.014) remained significantly associated with MACE (Figure S5).

Subgroup analysis by diabetes

In the patients with diabetes, EFV (aHR =6.61, 95% CI: 1.56–28.0, P=0.010), EFVi (aHR =6.33, 95% CI: 1.46–27.3, P=0.014), obstructive CAD (aHR =7.15, 95% CI: 1.39–36.9, P=0.019), and ischemia burden (aHR =5.36, 95% CI: 1.77–16.2, P=0.003) were independent predictors of MACE, while CACS was not (P=0.112). In the non-diabetic patients, only CACS (aHR =2.64, 95% CI: 1.29–5.41, P=0.008) and obstructive CAD (aHR =2.86, 95% CI: 1.35–6.06, P=0.006) were independent predictors of MACE (Table S6).

Discussion

In this real-world study with a median follow-up period of 4.6 years, several key findings emerged. First, EFV and EFVi increased progressively from the low- to intermediate- to high-risk FRS categories, indicating that epicardial fat accumulation increases in parallel with global cardiovascular risk. Second, in the overall cohort, EFV, CACS, and obstructive CAD were independently associated with MACE, while ischemia burden showed only a borderline association. Third, EFV provided incremental prognostic value beyond FRS, CACS, obstructive CAD, and ischemia burden as reflected by higher C-index values. Fourth, when stratified by the FRS, EFV/EFVi and ischemia burden only predicted MACE in the high-risk group, whereas CACS and obstructive CAD were independent predictors only in the low-to-intermediate-risk group. Finally, the prognostic impact of EFV was evident in diabetic but not in non-diabetic patients, suggesting that the clinical utility of EFV may be greatest in cardiometabolic high-risk subsets.

Prognostic impact of EAT across FRS and diabetes

Previous studies have confirmed the prognostic value of EAT for MACE (22,41-43). Our results, which showed that EAT was associated with MACE in diabetic patients but not in non-diabetic patients, were consistent with those of Tesche et al., who enrolled patients with suspected and known CAD and found that EFV and obstructive CAD increased the risk of adverse cardiovascular events in diabetic patients, while obstructive CAD and CACS increased the risk of MACE in non-diabetic patients (25). Similar results were also reported in other studies, including SCOT-HEART trial cohorts, which showed that EFV increased the risk of future myocardial infarction in cardiometabolic patients but not in non-cardiometabolic subgroups (44). A recent cross-sectional study by Wang et al. (31) found that the association between EFVi and CAD phenotype was strongest among diabetic patients. Our study extends previous work by exploring the relationship between EAT and MACE across FRS-based risk categories and diabetic stratification in patients with suspected CAD. We found that both EFV and EFVi were significantly associated with MACE, particularly in the FRS high-risk group and diabetic subgroup, indicating that quantifying and potentially targeting EAT may improve prognostic stratification and management in FRS high-risk and diabetic individuals. These results align with previous findings suggesting a shared mechanism: as clinical risk factors accumulate, EAT expands and secretes inflammatory adipokines that promote endothelial dysfunction, plaque formation, and atherosclerosis progression (16). In diabetic patients, hyperglycemia may exacerbate oxidative stress originating from EAT, leading to coronary endothelial damage. This mechanism may explain the enhanced prognostic impact of EAT and ischemia burden observed in our study among diabetic patients (16).

Several randomized clinical trials have demonstrated that targeting EAT with sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT-2) inhibitors or glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) agonists can reduce EAT volume or thickness in high-risk patients, including those with diabetes or heart failure (45-49). A recent meta-analysis of 21 randomized controlled trials, including 99,599 patients, demonstrated that GLP-1 agonists improved clinical outcomes in high-risk patients, including those with diabetes, obesity, heart failure, and chronic kidney disease (50), suggesting that in these patients, these benefits may be partly mediated by EAT reduction. EAT can predict MACE, especially in high-risk patients or diabetic patients, providing a theoretical basis for targeting EAT in these populations. Further clinical trials are needed to determine whether interventions aimed at reducing EAT in FRS high-risk or diabetic patients can provide maximum benefit in those with suspected CAD.

Prognostic impact of anatomical and functional imaging across FRS

Our study also found that anatomical imaging parameters such as CACS and obstructive CAD were independently associated with MACE in the low-to-intermediate FRS group. Our results are consistent with findings from the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA), which included population-based asymptomatic women with low FRS risk, and showed that CACS could predict future CAD and cardiovascular events (51). In the Prospective Multicenter Imaging Study for Evaluation of Chest Pain (PROMISE) trial, patients with stable chest pain (N=4,356) were stratified using a pooled cohort equation (PCE) cut-off value of 7.5% (<7.5% as low-to-borderline risk and >7.5% as intermediate-to-high risk). The study found that in patients with low-to-borderline PCE risk, obstructive CAD was associated with a higher hazard for adverse outcomes than in intermediate-to-high-risk patients, which aligns with our findings on the prognostic impact of obstructive CAD (15). However, their results showed that CACS was useful for risk stratification in the intermediate-to-high risk subgroup. The different results may be due to the overlapping intermediate-risk patients between our study and theirs, or the use of different CACS cut-off values (15). A review of 10-year atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) risk score stratification and CACS reported that CACS was not recommended for risk stratification in low-risk (<5%) or high-risk (>20%) groups; it was only recommended for patients with borderline-to-intermediate risk (5–20%) (52). In summary, initial assessment of coronary anatomy for detecting obstructive CAD and CACS was necessary in low-to-intermediate FRS risk patients.

Conversely, we found that myocardial ischemia burden was independently associated with MACE only in the high-risk FRS group rather than in the low-to-intermediate-risk group. This finding aligns with a recent large Canadian cohort study, which found that stress functional ischemia detected by SPECT MPI was associated with death and myocardial infarction only in moderate-to-high risk subgroups pre-stratified by PCE (14). Our study further showed that ischemia burden increased the risk of MACE in diabetic patients but not in non-diabetic patients, which contrasts with the findings of Tao et al., who enrolled 331 patients with suspected CAD and reported that ischemic perfusion defects >4 based on stress CT-derived MPI increased the risk of MACE in both diabetic and non-diabetic patients (53). This discrepancy may be due to the different hardware used between our study and theirs; CT-derived MPI has the ability to calculate the myocardial blood flow, while SPECT MPI is not absolutely quantitative, potentially making CT-derived MPI more sensitive in the detection of coronary microvascular dysfunction or ischemia in non-diabetic patients. Conversely, the relatively small number of events in non-diabetic patients in our cohort may have limited the statistical power to detect a significant ischemia burden in our study.

Possible mechanisms

The stronger prognostic impact of EFV and ischemia burden is biologically plausible in patients with diabetes or a high FRS. These individuals exhibit an adverse cardiometabolic milieu characterized by chronic low-grade inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, microvascular disease, and ectopic fat accumulation. In this context, EAT behaves as a metabolically active, pro-inflammatory visceral fat depot rather than a neutral healthy buffer fat and can secrete cytokines and adipokines that promote plaque inflammation, endothelial injury, and atherosclerotic progression (16,54). Systematic reviews and meta-analyses have consistently shown that increased epicardial fat, quantified by CT volume or echocardiographic thickness, is associated with higher rates of cardiovascular events, and an original cohort study showed that this impact is particularly pronounced in high-risk cardiometabolic subgroups (22,44). At the same time, patients with diabetes or a high FRS often exhibit diffuse atherosclerosis, severe complex coronary anatomy, and microvascular dysfunction, making them closer to the threshold at which ischemia translates into adverse outcomes (55,56).

Conversely, in patients with low-to-intermediate FRS, prognosis is more strongly driven by the presence or absence of manifest atherosclerosis than by subtle differences in metabolic or functional markers. Many individuals in this risk range are at an early stage of disease, or even free of overt coronary calcification or obstructive stenosis. In this context, the key prognostic “step” is the transition from no apparent coronary disease to detectable subclinical or clinical atherosclerosis, so that simple anatomical measures such as CACS (with a low cut-off value) and ≥50% stenosis provide the largest risk discrimination. This concept is supported by population-based data from MESA and other cohorts showing that coronary calcium can reclassify a subset of apparently “low-risk” individuals into substantially higher-risk categories and is particularly useful for refining risk in borderline- or intermediate-risk patients (51,52). However, the evidence based on symptomatic patients with suspected CAD across risk scores is limited. Our study addressed this gap, showing that initial CACS assessment in low-to-intermediate FRS risk patients with suspected CAD for MACE stratification was useful. In contrast, the absolute event rate in low-to-intermediate-risk groups is low. Further, the prevalence of inducible ischemia burden is limited, and the impact of EAT expansion over 4 to 5 years may be modest. Due to these factors, it is statistically more difficult for ischemia burden or EFV to emerge as independent predictors in low-to-intermediate FRS patients.

Clinical implications

To date, FRS has largely been used and developed for primary prevention in population-based cohorts. The present study extended the use of FRS to symptomatic patients with suspected CAD, showing that FRS categories can personalize prognostic assessment by identifying which imaging findings are most relevant (i.e., anatomical stenosis in lower-to-intermediate-risk patients versus functional ischemia and expanded EAT in higher-risk patients), thereby transitioning FRS from a primary prevention tool to a clinical decision aid for integrated imaging interpretation.

Recent evidence from the PROMISE trial of symptomatic patients found no significant difference in the 10-year mortality rate between initial anatomical imaging (CCTA) and stress functional imaging in low-to-intermediate-risk patients with suspected CAD (57). However, it is still unknown whether this conclusion varies across different risk strata defined by PCEs or FRS. Our results showed that in low-to-intermediate FRS risk patients with suspected CAD, anatomical imaging better predicts MACE, while in high-risk FRS patients with suspected CAD, stress functional imaging better predicts MACE. This implies that in routine clinical practice, cardiovascular risk scores may initially be used in patients with suspected CAD to guide the choice of downstream imaging modality for MACE risk stratification. For patients with myocardial ischemia in high-risk FRS groups, more intensive surveillance and clinical care may be warranted, as these patients exhibited extremely high annualized MACE rates in our study. Conversely, in low-to-intermediate-risk patients, more attention should be paid to the initial anatomical assessment of the coronary arteries, as early intervention in coronary atherosclerosis and initial statin usage may improve patient outcomes. However, these findings require confirmation in clinical trials.

Strengths and limitations

Our study had several strengths. First, we simultaneously evaluated EFV, coronary anatomic imaging (CACS and obstructive CAD), and functional ischemia within the same real-world cohort of patients with suspected CAD, allowing a direct comparison of their prognostic performance. Second, we applied the FRS to stratify baseline cardiovascular risk and showed that the prognostic importance of different imaging markers varies across risk categories, which supports a risk-guided interpretation of multimodality imaging. Third, we included long-term follow-up with clinical endpoints and performed dedicated subgroup analyses in patients with diabetes, thereby enhancing the robustness and clinical relevance of our findings.

However, this study had several limitations. First, it was a retrospective single-center analysis, which may introduce selection and information bias and limit generalizability. The retrospective nature of the observational design does not allow causal inferences to be drawn between epicardial fat, imaging findings, and clinical outcomes. Second, the sample size and number of events were relatively modest, preventing more granular modeling of complex coronary anatomical features and moderate-to-severe ischemia, and precluding separate analyses of low- and intermediate-risk FRS categories. Thus, subgroup analyses by FRS or diabetes should be interpreted with caution in our study. However, our findings are consistent with those of larger studies, including the PROMISE trial (N=4,356) (15) and a Canadian cohort (N=48,845) (14), which supports the robustness of our results. In the future, larger prospective studies should validate the prognostic impact of EAT across risk score strata and diabetic status. Third, clinical risk was assessed only once at baseline using the FRS in our study; we did not account for longitudinal changes in risk factors or medical therapy. Future prospective studies should incorporate the dynamic reassessment of clinical risk to determine whether the prognostic impact of anatomical and functional imaging varies across different risk clusters and to better define the evolving prognostic role of EAT over time. Finally, the cohort consisted of patients with suspected CAD and preserved left ventricular function, limiting the generalizability of our findings to populations with established CAD, ischemic cardiomyopathy, or other high-risk patient groups.

Conclusions

EFV gradually accumulates from low- to intermediate- to high-risk categories stratified by the FRS. EFV provides incremental prognostic value beyond the FRS, anatomical imaging and functional imaging. EFV and functional imaging are useful for MACE stratification in high-risk populations, while coronary anatomical imaging biomarkers, such as CACS and obstructive CAD, are useful for MACE stratification in low- to intermediate-risk populations with suspected CAD. EFV was associated with MACE in diabetic patients but not in non-diabetic patients. Future large-scale, multicenter, prospective studies and clinical trials are needed to validate whether early targeting of EFV provides maximum benefit in high-risk FRS or diabetic patients and whether the selective use of initial anatomical and functional imaging can improve clinical outcomes across different FRS risk categories in patients with suspected CAD.
