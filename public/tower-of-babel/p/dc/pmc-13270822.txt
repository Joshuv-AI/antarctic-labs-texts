Determinants of immune recovery and survival in HIV late presenters: a retrospective cohort study from Southwest China

Background

Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection remains a major global public health challenge. Although the widespread use of antiretroviral therapy (ART) has significantly improved the survival of people living with HIV (PLWH), a substantial proportion of patients are diagnosed and treated at an advanced stage of the disease, commonly referred to as late presenters (LPs). LPs are typically defined as individuals with a CD4⁺ T cell count < 350 cells/µL at diagnosis or those presenting with an AIDS-defining illness, regardless of the CD4⁺ T cell count [1]. Their prevalence remains high in various regions and populations worldwide, particularly in low- and middle-income countries [2].

Late presentation in PLWH significantly worsens clinical outcomes, particularly increasing mortality risk due to severe immune system impairment. The European COHERE study reported that the first-year mortality rate among LPs was 6–13 times higher than that of non-late presenters (NLPs) [3]. An African cohort study demonstrated that untreated individuals with CD4⁺ T cell count of 200–349, 100–199, 50–99, and 0–49 cells/µL had mortality or AIDS progression rates of 10.4, 30.9, 60.8, and 99.9 per 100 person-years, respectively [4]. Furthermore, in LPs, even with successful viral suppression, complete immune recovery remains elusive owing to synergistic effects of CD4+ lymphopenia, cellular dysfunction, and persistent immune activation, all contributing to adverse clinical outcomes [5]. Studies have shown that only about 16.2% of patients achieved a CD4⁺ T cell count of ≥ 500 cells/µL, while approximately 20.8% remained below 200 cells/µL after 5 years of treatment [6]. Compared to those with robust CD4+ T cell recovery, individuals with incomplete immune reconstitution face a higher risk of clinical events, including death, AIDS-defining illnesses, malignancies, cardiovascular events, and severe infections [7].

Moreover, studies have shown that compared to individuals who receive timely HIV treatment, those who present late or very late incur nearly 2 times and 7 times higher short-term non-ART costs, respectively, and 56% and 246% higher total medical costs over a five-year period [8], imposing a heavier financial burden on individuals and society. Additionally, the median time from HIV infection to late presenters is estimated at 3–5 years [9]. Late presenters contribute disproportionately to community viral load, with undiagnosed individuals transmitting HIV at approximately 3.5 times the rate of early presenters [10]. Thus, LPs not only worsens individual disease outcomes but also increases the likelihood of community transmission.

To date, research has linked late presentation to survival and immune recovery outcomes, but variations in mortality patterns and complex influencing factors require further investigation. Based on large-sample long-term follow-up data, this study innovatively assesses long-term survival and immune recovery in late presenters by examining interactive effects and stage-specific characteristics of influencing factors, providing new evidence to optimize clinical management and improve long-term outcomes.

Methods

Data source

The data for this study were obtained from the Yunnan Provincial Infectious Disease Hospital database, with the enrollment period from January 1, 2004, to December 31, 2024. All included patients had key data available at baseline and at the last follow-up (up to December 31, 2024).

Clinical follow-up and laboratory tests follow the standard diagnosis and treatment procedures, that is, CD4⁺T cell count and other indicators are monitored at 3, 6, and 12 months after the start of ART and every 6–12 months thereafter. Due to the differences in the time from the initiation of ART to the last visit among individual patients, in this study, the total follow-up time was calculated based on the time interval from the initiation date of ART to the last valid visit before December 31, 2024.

Study population

This study included HIV-infected adults (≥ 18 years) who initiated antiretroviral therapy (ART) between January 1, 2004, and December 31, 2024, with complete baseline and last-follow-up data. Patients with missing baseline CD4⁺ or CD8⁺ T cell counts were excluded. Of 10,136 patients initially screened, 2,587 were excluded (2,515 for missing baseline data and 72 for not meeting inclusion criteria), resulting in a final cohort of 7,549 patients.

Data collected included demographics (age, sex, marital status), laboratory measurements at baseline and last follow-up (CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cell counts [cells/µL], white blood cell count [WBC, ×10⁹/L], platelet count [PLT, ×10⁹/L], creatinine [CREA, µmol/L], glucose [GLU, mmol/L], aspartate aminotransferase [AST, U/L]), HBsAg and anti-HCV status, ART regimen, and ART initiation period.

ART regimens were categorized by core drug components into 2NRTI+NNRTI, protease inhibitor-based (PI), integrase strand transfer inhibitor-based (INSTI), other. ART initiation periods were grouped as 2004–2009, 2010–2014, 2015–2019, and 2020–2024. Clinical outcomes occurring during follow-up were also recorded.

Based on the European Late Presentation Consensus [11], LPs are conceptually defined as individuals with a CD4⁺ T-cell count < 350 cells/µL at diagnosis or the presence of an AIDS-defining illness, regardless of the CD4⁺ T cell count. In this study, the operational definition for statistical analyses was based solely on baseline CD4⁺ T cell count, classifying patients into a Late presentation group (LP group, CD4⁺ T cell count < 350 cells/µL) and a non-Late presentation group (NLP group, CD4⁺ T cell count ≥ 350 cells/µL), with the LP group further stratified into < 100, 100–199, and 200–349 cells/µL subgroups. Patients with documented AIDS-defining illnesses were not included in the analysis due to the absence of complete clinical records in the present dataset.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of the study was all-cause mortality, with the baseline time being the day of ART initiation. The secondary outcome was the immune recovery status of LPs at the end of follow-up. Complete immune recovery was defined as a CD4⁺ T cell count ≥ 500 cells/µL and a CD4⁺/CD8⁺ ratio ≥ 0.8; otherwise, patients were categorized as having incomplete immune recovery [12].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R (4.4.1). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), and categorical variables as frequency (percentage). Comparisons were made using the t-test, Mann-Whitney U test, chi-square test, or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. For baseline laboratory parameters other than the core indicators (CD4⁺ T cell count and CD8⁺ T cell count), missing values were imputed using the “mice” package (version 3.17.0) in R, with the optimal imputation dataset selected based on the minimum Akaike Information Criterion value.

Survival analysis was conducted using the “survival (3.8.3)” and “survminer (0.5.0)” packages to generate Kaplan-Meier curves and perform Log-rank tests. Cox proportional hazards models were used for univariate and multivariate analyses, and forest plots were created with the “forestplot (3.1.3)” package. In the multivariable Cox regression model, the antiretroviral treatment regimen was treated as a stratification variable because it violated the proportional hazards assumption based on Schoenfeld residual testing. ART era was not included in the final model due to potential collinearity with the treatment regimen. Age and baseline viral load were categorized to improve interpretability and account for potential non-linear associations. Interaction terms between age group and marital status were examined to explore potential heterogeneity in mortality risk across age groups.

For immune recovery analysis in the LP group, the Kruskal-Wallis and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were used to compare subgroup differences in the most recent CD4+ and CD8+ T cell counts, CD4⁺/ CD8+ ratio, and immune recovery rate ([(last follow-up CD4⁺ T cell count− baseline CD4⁺ T cell count)/follow-up years]). A linear mixed-effects model with random intercepts was used to evaluate longitudinal CD4 cell count changes. Time was modeled using natural cubic splines with three degrees of freedom to capture nonlinear trends. Interaction terms between time and LP subgroup were included to assess differences in CD4 recovery trajectories across baseline CD4 categories. Chi-square tests assessed complete immune recovery rates, and logistic regression was used to calculate odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). All tests were two-sided, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Bonferroni correction was applied for multiple comparisons.

Results

Study participants

A total of 10,136 PLWH who initiated ART at Yunnan Provincial Infectious Disease Hospital from 2004 to 2024 were initially screened. Based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 7,549 PLWH were ultimately included in the study, with a mean follow-up time of 10.0 (IQR 5.7–13.6) years (Fig. 1). Among them, 5,533 individuals (73.3%) were classified as LPs, and 2,016 individuals (26.7%) as NLPs.

Significant differences were observed between the LPs and NLPs in demographic characteristics, initial antiretroviral therapy regimen, treatment era, co-infections (hepatitis A, hepatitis B), and baseline biochemical indicators (except for creatinine), with all p-values < 0.001. Long-term survival analysis

We conducted a survival analysis to compare survival outcomes among groups with different baseline CD4⁺ T-cell counts. The results showed a significant difference in survival probabilities across the groups (log-rank p < 0.0001) (Fig. 2). The NLP group consistently exhibited the highest survival probability and showed markedly better survival than the LP subgroups. Within the LP subgroups, patients with CD4⁺ T-cell counts ≤ 100 cells/µL had the lowest survival probability and experienced the most rapid decline. The survival curves of the 100–199 and 200–349 cells/µL groups were intermediate, with the 200–349 cells/µL group showing relatively better survival, indicating a gradient trend with increasing baseline CD4⁺ T-cell counts.

The difference in survival rates among the groups was statistically significant (p < 0.001)

In the Cox regression analysis, LP status, HBsAg positivity, anti-HCV positivity, and higher baseline levels of glucose and creatinine were identified as significant predictors of mortality among people living with HIV (Fig. 3). Compared with the NLP group, individuals in the LP group had a significantly higher risk of death, and viral hepatitis coinfection was also associated with increased mortality risk.

The model included interaction terms between age group and marital status, with age 31–50 × divorced and age > 50 × divorced reaching statistical significance. Due to sparse events in some strata, the estimated hazard ratios for these groups were unstable. Importantly, the main effects of LP status, viral hepatitis coinfection, and baseline glucose and creatinine remained robust after accounting for these interactions.

The ART era variable was excluded from the model due to potential collinearity with the antiretroviral treatment regimen. Schoenfeld residual tests indicated that the treatment regimen violated the proportional hazards assumption (p < 0.05), while other covariates met the assumption (Table S1). Therefore, the treatment regimen was incorporated as a stratification variable in a stratified Cox proportional hazards model, which confirmed that the study conclusions remained stable.

Sensitivity analyses yielded results consistent with the main analysis (Table S2), further supporting the reliability of the findings.

Subgroup analysis showed that, compared with the NLP group, the LP group had a significantly increased risk of mortality (HR = 2.29, 95% CI: 1.83–2.88, P < 0.001)(Fig. 4). Across most subgroups, including sex, marital status, antiretroviral treatment regimen, and treatment era, LP was consistently associated with a higher risk of death. Age-stratified analysis indicated that this association was more pronounced among individuals aged 18–30 and 31–50 years, whereas no statistically significant difference was observed in those aged ≥ 50 years. In addition, LP was also associated with an increased risk of death among individuals who were HBsAg-positive and anti-HCV-positive.

Immune recovery status relationship between baseline CD4⁺ T cell count and immunological indicators

To assess the impact of baseline CD4⁺ T cell count on immune recovery among the LPs, this study conducted Kruskal–Wallis tests to compare immunological indicators across three LP subgroups during the follow-up period. These indicators included the most recent CD4⁺ T cell count, most recent CD8+ T cell count, most recent CD4/CD8 ratio, and the rate of immune recovery. Results showed that the most recent CD4⁺ T cell count increased significantly with higher baseline CD4⁺ cell count categories (P < 0.0001; Fig. 5A). The immune recovery rate differed across groups, with the < 100 cells/µL group exhibiting a significantly higher annual growth rate than the other two groups (P < 0.0001; Fig. 5B). In contrast, the recent CD8⁺ T cell count showed no statistically significant difference among the baseline subgroups (P = 0.17; Fig. 5C). Regarding immune homeostasis, the recovery of the CD4⁺/CD8⁺ ratio was markedly superior in the group with higher baseline CD4⁺ levels compared to the group with lower levels (P < 0.0001; Fig. 5D).

Relationship between baseline CD4⁺ T cell count and immune recovery status

The linear mixed-effects model showed a significant interaction between time and LP subgroup, indicating that CD4 recovery trajectories over time differed among LP subgroups (P < 0.05). At the initiation of ART, compared with the baseline CD4⁺ <100 cells/µL group, the 100–199 cells/µL group (β = 109.88, P < 0.001) and the 200–349 cells/µL group (β = 230.17, P < 0.001) had significantly higher CD4 counts. Based on model-predicted results, CD4 counts in all subgroups increased markedly after ART initiation, but the long-term trends differed (Fig. 6A). In addition, the chi-square test indicated that the rates of complete immune recovery differed significantly among the baseline CD4⁺ groups (χ² = 139.76, P < 0.001). As baseline CD4⁺ levels increased, the proportion of patients achieving complete immune recovery gradually rose, from 21.4% to 39.1% (Fig. 6B).

Long-term immune recovery trajectories and rates of complete recovery by baseline LP subgroup. A Model-predicted trajectories of CD4⁺ counts after the initiation of ART; B Proportion of patients achieving complete immune recovery

Multivariable logistic regression analysis demonstrated that, after adjusting for confounding factors such as age and treatment period, baseline CD4⁺ T-cell count was the strongest predictor of complete immune recovery. Compared to patients with a CD4⁺ count < 100 cells/µL, those with counts of 100–199 cells/µL and 200–349 cells/µL had odds of immune recovery that were increased by 1.88-fold (OR = 1.88, 95% CI: 1.55–2.29) and 4.38-fold (OR = 4.38, 95% CI: 3.60–5.35), respectively (both P < 0.001). Among demographic factors, female patients had significantly higher odds of immune recovery than males (OR = 1.76, 95% CI: 1.36–2.26, P < 0.001), while both divorced and married statuses were weakly positively associated with recovery odds. Regarding clinical indicators, higher baseline viral load became an independent protective factor after adjusting for confounders (OR = 1.12, 95% CI: 1.06–1.19, P < 0.001), whereas higher baseline blood glucose was an independent risk factor (OR = 0.93, 95% CI: 0.87–0.99, P = 0.03). Additionally, initiating treatment after 2010 was associated with reduced odds of immune recovery. In summary, good baseline immune status, female sex, and higher baseline viral load promote immune recovery, while higher baseline blood glucose and later treatment initiation impede it (Fig. 7).

Discussion

This study, based on a long-term follow-up cohort of HIV-infected individuals from Yunnan Provincial Infectious Disease Hospital, showed that LPs had a significantly higher risk of mortality and incomplete immune recovery compared to NLPs. Sex, baseline CD4⁺ T cell count, and baseline viral load were identified as the main independent factors influencing complete immune recovery in LPs, with baseline CD4⁺ T cell count being the key predictor of long-term immune restoration.

Compared with previous reports from the Swiss HIV Cohort Study (49.8%) and the European COHERE cohort (53.8%), our study cohort demonstrated a substantially higher proportion of LPs (73.3%). This likely reflects delayed HIV testing due to low risk perception and inadequate awareness of HIV transmission, symptoms, and treatment among infected individuals [13], suggesting the need for strengthened early screening and intervention to increase early diagnosis rates, reduce transmission risks, and improve outcomes. Consistent with previous research findings, LPs demonstrated significantly lower long-term survival probabilities compared to NLPs [3, 13, 14]. This survival disparity primarily stems from the fact that LPs are typically diagnosed at advanced disease stages, often presenting with markedly depleted CD4⁺ T cell count and frequently having already developed opportunistic infections or AIDS-defining illnesses [15] .The late HIV diagnosis and immune dysfunction in LPs limit the timeliness and effectiveness of ART, impairing the immune system’s ability to rebuild, thus adversely affecting long-term prognosis.

This study identified LP status, HBsAg positivity, anti-HCV positivity, and higher baseline levels of glucose and creatinine as key determinants of survival outcomes in HIV patients, a finding supported by numerous existing studies [16, 17].Higher baseline CD4⁺ T cell levels are associated with better immune recovery and better prognosis [18]. Additionally, there is a significant interaction between marital status and age, suggesting that the effect of marital status on survival may differ across age groups.

HBsAg positivity and anti-HCV positivity indicate the presence of HBV or HCV co-infection in HIV-infected individuals. Such co-infections have been shown to accelerate liver disease progression, increase mortality risk, and potentially contribute to renal complications [19]. In addition, elevated baseline glucose may reflect metabolic disturbances and chronic inflammation, which are associated with higher cardiovascular risk [20], while elevated baseline creatinine suggests potential renal impairment that could affect ART drug metabolism and treatment efficacy [21]. Unlike previous studies, this study identified through stratified analyses that the potential high-risk populations for LPs include patients of different sexes, those aged 18–30 and 31–50 years, individuals with various marital statuses, patients with different antiretroviral therapy regimens and treatment eras, as well as those who are HBsAg- or anti-HCV-positive. These findings underscore the clinical value of routine laboratory markers in assessing survival outcomes and risk stratification among HIV patients, although larger multicenter studies are still needed to establish specific clinically actionable thresholds.

The baseline CD4⁺ T cell count at the initiation of ART is a key factor in predicting long-term immune recovery in the LPs. The study found that the high CD4 group (200–349) demonstrated higher CD4⁺ T cell count, a more favorable CD4/CD8 ratio, and a faster rate of immune recovery by the end of the follow-up period, consistent with findings from studies conducted in other regions [12, 22, 23]. Linear mixed-effects analysis showed that CD4⁺ recovery trajectories differed significantly among baseline CD4⁺ subgroups, suggesting that higher baseline CD4 levels can markedly accelerate immune recovery, a finding that is also supported by previous studies [24].

In addition, this study systematically analyzed multiple factors influencing immune recovery, using complete immune restoration as the primary endpoint. The results showed that female patients had a higher likelihood of immune recovery than males, suggesting that sex differences may affect immune response and drug metabolism. Patients with baseline CD4⁺ T-cell counts of 100–349 cells/µL demonstrated better recovery than those with counts below 100 cells/µL, indicating that higher baseline levels can accelerate immune restoration and reduce infection risk. Patients with higher baseline viral loads exhibited greater potential for immune recovery after viral suppression, achieving higher recovery rates than those with less severe immune suppression. These findings are consistent with existing literature, indicating that sex, baseline CD4⁺ T-cell count, and baseline viral load are important independent factors influencing immune recovery [5, 25–27], further supporting the notion that higher baseline CD4 levels promote immune restoration.

The study still has some limitations. First, the data were primarily obtained from medical institutions in a single region, which may affect the generalizability of the findings. Although baseline viral load was included in this study, some other key indicators, such as transmission routes, were not incorporated, which may limit the comprehensiveness of the results. Compared with previous studies, the follow-up cohort was expanded, reducing selection bias, but the reliability of the findings still needs to be validated in larger, multicenter populations.

Conclusions

In summary, this study, based on real-world long-term follow-up data of PLWH in southwestern China, systematically evaluated the impact of LPs on survival outcomes and immune recovery. It identified key high-risk groups and confirmed the critical predictive value of baseline CD4⁺ T cell count in immune reconstitution. The findings are consistent with international research while also highlighting region-specific epidemiological features and challenges, providing valuable evidence for developing more targeted early screening strategies and individualized intervention approaches. However, limitations such as missing variables and the single-center study design may affect the generalizability of the results. Future studies should incorporate more comprehensive indicators and consider psychosocial factors and treatment adherence to enhance the overall management and long-term health outcomes of PLWH.
