Respiratory Support in Pediatric Critical and Near-Fatal Asthma: A Narrative Review
Authors: Long Xiang, Herng Lee Tan, Andrew Miller, Alexandre T. Rotta, Jan Hau Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pediatric asthma remains a leading cause of emergency department visits and hospitalizations worldwide. Patients with critical asthma represents a subset of patients requiring respiratory support beyond standard pharmacologic therapy. Management of respiratory support in these patients has evolved dramatically, with intubation rates decreasing from 6.9% to 3.4% between 2009 and 2019 while noninvasive ventilation use doubled and high-flow nasal cannula utilization increased from 11% to 52%. This narrative review examines current evidence and practical considerations for respiratory support strategies across the spectrum of pediatric critical asthma care. The pathophysiology of critical asthma creates unique challenges fundamentally different from other causes of respiratory failure. Dynamic hyperinflation from progressive air trapping and intrinsic positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) development represents the cornerstone derangement, requiring ventilation strategies that seems divergent from those utilized in acute respiratory distress syndrome. High-flow nasal cannula provides benefit primarily through dead space washout and gas conditioning, while noninvasive ventilation offers comprehensive support by augmenting ventilation and offsetting intrinsic PEEP effects. When mechanical ventilation becomes necessary, controlled hypoventilation with acceptance of hypercapnia while maintaining pH > 7.20 has dramatically reduced mortality compared with historical approaches targeting normocapnia. Pressure-controlled ventilation may offer physiologic advantages over volume-controlled modes by providing more uniform gas distribution in airways with varying degrees of obstruction. A structured three-phase ventilation approach emphasizes initial hyperinflation clearance, followed by acidosis resolution, then careful weaning with attention to dynamic hyperinflation monitoring. Current survival rates exceed 96% for children reaching medical care, representing substantial improvement from historical mortality rates of 20–30%. Optimal respiratory support in critical asthma requires understanding that mechanical ventilation strategies in asthma differs from other respiratory failure etiologies, with dynamic hyperinflation management taking precedence over traditional lung-protective strategies. Future research priorities include developing severity prediction tools, optimizing noninvasive protocols, and investigating personalized approaches on the basis of emerging understanding of asthma phenotypes and endotypes.

Introduction

Pediatric asthma remains a leading cause of emergency department visits and hospitalizations worldwide, with over 270,000 emergency department visits and 27,000 hospitalizations annually in the USA [1]. The global burden is even more striking, with an estimated 22 million children affected by asthma worldwide and nearly half experiencing severe symptoms [2–4]. While most children with asthma exacerbations respond to standard pharmacologic therapy (i.e., inhaled bronchodilators and systemic corticosteroids), and can be discharged home or treated in a regular inpatient or intermediate care unit, some may require noninvasive respiratory support (NRS) or invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV), a condition termed “critical asthma”[5]. Those who require endotracheal intubation and IMV are classified as having “near-fatal asthma” [5]. NRS encompasses high-flow nasal cannula (HFNC), continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) and noninvasive ventilation (NIV) [equal to biphasic positive airway pressure (BiPAP)] [6, 7].

According to the most recent clinical practice guidelines on pediatric critical asthma by the American Association for Respiratory Care (AARC) and Pediatric Acute Lung Injury and Sepsis Investigators (PALISI), pediatric critical asthma is defined as a severe asthma exacerbation in children ≥ 2 and < 18 years of age which requires hospital admission, refractory to intermittent inhaled short-acting beta agonists (SABA) and systemic corticosteroids, necessitates continued management with continuous inhaled SABA and/or adjunctive therapies. Adjunctive therapies include continued intravenous (IV) SABA (terbutaline, salbutamol, epinephrine), IV methylxanthines (aminophylline), IV magnesium, helium–oxygen mixture, NRS, IMV, inhaled anesthetics, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation [8].

The landscape of respiratory support for pediatric critical asthma has evolved dramatically over the past decade. Between 2009 and 2019, intubation rates decreased from 6.9% to 3.4%, while NRS use more than doubled from 8.9% to 20% [9]. This shift reflects both improved understanding of asthma pathophysiology and recognition of the significant risks associated with IMV in this population. NRS aid to reduce the work of breathing (WOB), deliver nebulized medications, improve gas exchange, and potentially reduce the need for IMV. HFNC use has increased even more drastically, from 11.0% in 2012 to 52.3% in 2021, though significant institutional variability exists [10].

Despite these advances, critical asthma continues to challenge clinicians. The pathophysiology of severe airway obstruction creates unique ventilatory demands that differ fundamentally from other causes of respiratory failure. Unlike acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) where lung-protective ventilation with low tidal volumes and sufficient positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) has become standard of care, asthma requires a different approach. This involves ventilation with higher tidal volumes, minimal PEEP, and prolonged expiratory times to combat dynamic hyperinflation.

We searched for publications reporting children and pediatric patients with status asthmaticus, acute severe asthma, fatal asthma, near-fatal asthma, and critical asthma. We searched Pubmed and Google Scholar for studies published before 1 April 2026.

This narrative review provides an overview of respiratory support strategies in pediatric critical asthma, from noninvasive approaches through IMV, and advanced rescue therapies. We emphasize the unique pathophysiologic considerations that distinguish asthma from other forms of respiratory failure and provide detailed, practical guidance for managing these challenging patients.

Pathophysiology of Critical Asthma

Airway Obstruction and Dynamic Hyperinflation

The pathophysiology of critical asthma creates distinct challenges that fundamentally influence respiratory support strategies. Asthma is characterized by variable airway obstruction resulting from bronchospasm, airway wall inflammation with mucosal edema, and mucus hypersecretion which can lead to mucus plugging [11]. Mucus plugging in asthma arises from a complex interplay of airway inflammation, altered mucin biology and immune cell activity. Neutrophils can also interact with eosinophil products, further amplifying plug persistence. Mucus plugs physically obstruct segmental and subsegmental airways, correlate with reduced forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1), air trapping, and frequent exacerbations, and often persist in the same bronchopulmonary segments for years. Histology and imaging studies confirm that plugs are tethered to remodeled epithelium and perpetuate inflammation, making them a distinct asthma phenotype associated with poor outcomes [12, 13].

Treatment strategies continue to evolve: traditional mucolytics and bronchodilators show little benefit outside cystic fibrosis, and guidelines do not recommend routine pharmacologic airway clearance in noncystic fibrosis asthma [14]. Instead, biologic therapies targeting type 2 inflammation have shown promise. Anti-interleukin (IL)-5 agents (mepolizumab, benralizumab) reduce eosinophils and can decrease mucus burden; anti-IL-4Rα therapy (dupilumab) attenuates IL-4/IL-13–driven mucin production and improves airflow; and tezepelumab (anti-TSLP) may reduce plugs by dampening upstream epithelial signals [13]. Imaging-based mucus scores demonstrate that reductions in plugs with these biologics parallel improvements in lung function and exacerbation rates [15, 16].

During an acute exacerbation, the processes of bronchospasm, airway wall inflammation with mucosal edema, and mucus hypersecretion combine to create severe airflow limitation, particularly affecting expiration. The airways narrow from smooth muscle contraction, wall thickening from inflammatory cell infiltration and edema, and luminal obstruction from mucus plugs containing eosinophil extracellular traps, Charcot–Leyden crystals, and cellular debris. The physiologic consequences of this obstruction are profound. Increased airway resistance leads to low inspiratory and expiratory flow rates despite large transpulmonary pressure gradients [17]. To maintain minute ventilation, patients increase their respiratory rate, but this shortens expiratory time. Combined with the prolonged time constant (resistance × compliance) for expiration, this leads to incomplete exhalation before the next breath begins. The result is progressive air trapping and dynamic hyperinflation.

Dynamic hyperinflation represents the cornerstone of pathophysiologic derangement in critical asthma. As air trapping progresses, end-expiratory lung volume rises above functional residual capacity (FRC), creating intrinsic PEEP (PEEPi or auto-PEEP) [18]. This PEEPi typically ranges from 5–20 cm water (H2O) in severe cases and must be overcome before inspiratory flow can begin, dramatically increasing the work of breathing. Patients breathe at lung volumes approaching total lung capacity, where the respiratory system is less compliant and the diaphragm is mechanically disadvantaged owing to flattening.

Static and Dynamic Lung Strain

In critical asthma, conditions capable of inducing high lung strain (tidal volume to end-expiratory lung volume ratio) and stress (transpulmonary pressure) are generated. Static strain occurs owing to overstretching of tissue at end-expiration caused by dynamic hyperinflation. The first goal of IMV is to minimize static strain. Given the high end-expiratory lung volume, end-inspiratory lung volume can be dangerously close to total lung capacity even at physiologic tidal volumes, increasing the risk of air leak [19].

Ventilation-Perfusion Relationships

The primary cause of hypoxemia in critical asthma is ventilation-perfusion (V/Q) mismatch. Mucus plugging creates areas of complete obstruction with continued perfusion (shunt), while hyperinflated lung regions may have reduced perfusion owing to alveolar capillary compression that results in increased dead space. The large negative intrapleural pressures during inspiration can cause transcapillary fluid movement into the pulmonary interstitium and alveolar spaces, creating a form of negative pressure pulmonary edema that further worsens V/Q matching and contributes to the development of hypoxemia and respiratory failure [17].

Cardiopulmonary Interactions

The cardiovascular effects of critical asthma are clinically significant and directly influence management decisions. The large negative intrathoracic pressures generated during inspiration against obstructed airways have multiple effects [20]. The negative intrathoracic pressure increases the transmural pressure gradient across the left ventricle, effectively increasing afterload. These pressures have variable effects on venous return, initially enhancing venous return to the right heart, but severe hyperinflation can compress the vena cava and impede venous return [21]. Additionally, increased right ventricular filling can cause leftward septal shift, impairing left ventricular filling, while hyperinflation increases pulmonary vascular resistance, increasing right ventricular afterload [21]. These interactions manifest clinically as pulsus paradoxus, which is an exaggeration of the normal inspiratory decrease in systolic blood pressure. When the difference exceeds 10 mmHg, it indicates clinically significant obstruction; differences greater than 20 mmHg may not be sustainable and suggest impending respiratory failure [22].

Metabolic Derangements

Children with critical asthma frequently develop mixed respiratory and metabolic acidosis. The respiratory component results from carbon dioxide (CO2) retention as airway obstruction worsens. The metabolic component typically manifests as lactic acidosis, which can be type A (tissue hypoperfusion) or more commonly type B (related to excessive β-agonist stimulation and steroid-induced hyperglycemia) [23]. The presence of a “normal” partial pressure of CO2 (PaCO2) of 40 mmHg in a tachypneic child with critical asthma should be interpreted as impending respiratory failure, not reassurance.

Noninvasive Respiratory Support

High-Flow Nasal Cannula

HFNC has rapidly become a first-line therapy for pediatric critical asthma, though its mechanisms of action differ from those in bronchiolitis or ARDS. The primary mechanisms by which HFNC reduces work of breathing in asthma include dead space washout, gas conditioning, and offsetting of the nasopharyngeal resistor [24, 25]. The continuous high flow washes CO2-rich gas from the nasopharynx and upper airway, effectively reducing anatomical dead space. This decreases the minute ventilation (and effort) required to maintain a given alveolar ventilation. Studies using electrical impedance tomography have demonstrated this effect, showing reduced breathing frequency without changes in tidal volume [26]. Proper heating and humidification of inspired gas is crucial in asthma, as dry, cool gas can trigger bronchospasm and increase airway inflammation. HFNC delivers gas at 37 °C with 100% relative humidity, optimizing mucociliary clearance and reducing the metabolic cost of gas conditioning. While HFNC generates some nasopharyngeal positive pressure, intrathoracic pressure measurements in children receiving HFNC show this rarely exceeds 1 cmH2O with the mouth open, far less than the PEEPi present in critical asthma. Therefore, the pressure effect of HFNC likely contributes insignificantly to its benefit in asthma.

The evidence for optimal flow settings in pediatric asthma remains limited. Extrapolating data from children with bronchiolitis, flows of 1.5–2.0 L/kg/min maximize dead space washout in infants [27]. However, this may be excessive in older children. In adults, benefits plateau around 30–40 L/min (approximately 0.5 L/kg/min), suggesting lower flows may suffice in larger children [28]. Clinical trials of HFNC in pediatric asthma show mixed results. Ballestero et al. randomized 62 children in the emergency department to HFNC (2 L/kg/min for first 10 kg, then 0.5 L/kg/min per kg above 10 kg) versus standard oxygen therapy [29]. While the HFNC group showed faster improvement in pulmonary scores at 2 h, there were no differences in admission rates or length of stay. Similarly, Gauto Benitez et al. found no significant differences in major outcomes despite physiologic improvements. No patients were admitted to pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) or had complications in this randomized controlled trial (RCT) [30].

On the other hand, Rogerson et al. evaluated 433 children with severe acute asthma receiving treatment with HFNC within the first 24 h of hospitalization versus matched controls and found that children treated with HFNC had slower improvement in clinical symptoms and longer PICU and hospital LOS compared with controls [31]. In the PICU setting, Gates et al. compared HFNC with aerosol mask in 171 children with moderate to severe asthma (Modified Pulmonary Index Score ≥ 8) [32]. HFNC was associated with reduced time on continuous albuterol (1.0 versus 1.5 days, p = 0.048) but no difference in hospital or PICU length of stay. A critical limitation of HFNC in asthma is its effect on aerosol delivery. When HFNC flow exceeds patient inspiratory flow, aerosol delivery to the lower airways decreases dramatically [33]. In vitro studies show that aerosol delivery is optimized when HFNC flow is set at 50% of patient inspiratory flow [34]. This creates a clinical dilemma: higher flows improve dead space washout and decrease work of breathing but compromise bronchodilator delivery. Practical solutions include temporarily reducing flow during intermittent nebulizer treatments, using vibrating mesh nebulizers positioned on the dry side of the humidifier, or considering brief removal from HFNC for critical bronchodilator doses [34].

Current RCTs and retrospective studies do not provide strong evidence for or against the use of HFNC in the pediatric emergency departments or PICUs. A recent review and meta-analysis by Mangkuliguna et al. concluded that HFNC did not demonstrate superior outcomes over conventional oxygen therapy and NIV [35]. The latest clinical practice guidelines on pediatric critical asthma by AARC and PALISI could neither recommend for or against the application of HFNC versus conventional oxygen therapy in children presenting with critical asthma with persistent hypoxia and/or respiratory distress [8]. Future studies are required to delineate and the role of and refine patient selection for HFNC in pediatric critical asthma. Clinical prudence dictates that as we increase HFNC flow and the patient’s symptoms do not improve, escalation to NIV or IMV should be considered [36].

Noninvasive Ventilation

NIV, defined as the application of alternating inspiratory and expiratory pressures to the mouth and/or nose via an occlusive interface, provides more comprehensive respiratory support than HFNC by actively augmenting ventilation and offsetting the adverse effects of dynamic hyperinflation. NIV offers multiple physiologic benefits in asthma: improved gas exchange, reduced work of breathing, respiratory muscle unloading, and counterbalancing of intrinsic PEEP [37, 38]. A meta-analysis by Dai et al. of 10 RCTs (558 children) found NIV associated with 1.8 fewer hospital days, though methodological limitations exist [39]. In the recent propensity-score-matched cohort study by Abbott et al., NIV use (n = 1622 pediatric patients with NIV versus n = 4.766 pediatric patients who did not receive NIV) was associated with a decreased risk of intubation but not significant for an improvement in in-hospital mortality [40]. The inspiratory positive airway pressure (IPAP) directly assists inspiration, reducing the work performed by respiratory muscles across the narrowed intrathoracic airways. This is particularly important in asthma where the work of breathing can increase tenfold above normal. By providing 8–15 cmH2O of inspiratory pressure support, NIV aid to reduce diaphragmatic pressure–time product [44]. One of the benefits of NIV in asthma is the application of expiratory positive airway pressure (EPAP) to counterbalance PEEPi. When PEEPi is 10 cmH2O, the inspiratory muscles must generate −10 cmH2O of pressure before inspiratory flow begins. Applying 5–8 cmH2O of EPAP reduces this threshold load, improving patient-NIV device synchrony and reducing work of breathing.

NIV appears to have some direct bronchodilator effects independent of medications. Studies in adults show improved forced expiratory volume and reduced airway resistance during NIV, possibly owing to pneumatic stenting of airways or activation of airway stretch receptors [45]. Unlike HFNC, NIV can enhance aerosol delivery when properly configured. Using vibrating mesh nebulizers positioned between the mask and leak port (in single-limb circuits) or on the dry side of the humidifier (in dual-limb circuits) can achieve two to three times greater lung deposition compared with standard nebulization [46].

Successful NIV requires careful attention to interface selection, pressure settings, and patient tolerance. Starting with nasal masks in cooperative children or oronasal masks for those with significant dyspnea or mouth breathing, initial settings typically include IPAP 10–14 cmH2O and EPAP 4–6 cmH2O. Titration involves increasing IPAP by 2 cmH2O increments for persistent work of breathing or tidal volumes below 6 mL/kg, and gradually increasing EPAP to facilitate triggering and synchrony. Monitoring should assess tidal volumes (target 6–8 mL/kg), respiratory rate, and patient comfort every 30 min initially. Light sedation with dexmedetomidine is often necessary for tolerance of the occlusive mask interface, particularly in younger children.

Pediatric critical asthma guidelines from AARC and PALISI suggest the use of NIV over conventional oxygen therapy but unable to recommend for or against the application of NIV over HFNC in children presenting with critical asthma with persistent hypoxemia and/or respiratory distress [8], The clinical practice of use NIV in pediatric critical asthma should also be considered with respect to different thresholds for NIV initiation, local practice variation, type of interfaces used, and criteria for weaning from NRS. Identifying patients likely to fail NIV is crucial for timely escalation. A reasonable trial of NIV duration should be no more than 2 h with reassessments every 30 min. Risk factors for failure include need for significant sedation to tolerate the mask, poor mask fit or excessive leak, presentation late in disease course, pH less than 7.25 despite NIV, rising PaCO2 after initial improvement, hypoxemia, increasing work of breathing, and altered mental status. The typical total duration of NIV use in pediatric critical asthma ranged from 1 to 3 days [8, 37, 39].

CPAP Versus NIV

While both CPAP and NIV can offset PEEPi, only NIV provides the inspiratory support needed to overcome the increased airway resistance in critical asthma. CPAP merely applies continuous pressure without assisting inspiration, a significant limitation given the high resistive work of breathing these patients face. Since both modalities require tolerance of an occlusive interface with its inherent discomfort and risks, the use of pure CPAP in critical asthma is difficult to justify. NIV delivers all the potential benefits of CPAP while adding crucial inspiratory assistance, making it the superior choice for children with critical asthma who require noninvasive support beyond HFNC [37, 38].

Invasive Mechanical Ventilation

The approach to IMV in asthma differs fundamentally from lung-protective ventilation strategies used in ARDS, and understanding these differences is crucial for safe management [47]. In ARDS, the primary pathology is alveolar damage with decreased lung compliance, heterogeneous lung injury, and propensity for ventilator-induced lung injury from overdistension of remaining healthy alveoli. The ARDSNet strategy of low tidal volumes (6 mL/kg), plateau pressure limitation (< 30 cmH2O), and adequate PEEP to prevent atelectrauma has reduced mortality in adults with ARDS and has been adopted by pediatric guidelines [48].

By contrast, asthma presents entirely different pathophysiology. The lung parenchyma is essentially healthy with near-normal compliance; the problem is airway obstruction causing markedly elevated resistance. Dynamic peak inspiratory pressures may exceed 50 cmH2O owing to elevated airway resistance, not alveolar overdistension. The product of resistance and compliance determines prolonged time constants needed for exhalation, and dynamic hyperinflation becomes the primary threat, unlike ARDS, a disease of short time constants and decreased compliance, where atelectasis is problematic. These differences mandate unique ventilation strategies. While ARDS benefits from low tidal volumes and elevated PEEP, asthma requires accepting larger tidal volumes (up to 10–12 mL/kg initially), minimal PEEP, and very low respiratory rates to allow complete exhalation.

The Decision to Intubate

The decision to intubate a child with critical asthma should never be taken lightly, as intubation itself carries significant risk and IMV in this population is challenging. Absolute indications are clear: cardiac arrest, respiratory arrest, profound obtundation, or inability to maintain airway protection. However, most decisions are more nuanced. Clinical indicators suggesting need for intubation include progressive exhaustion despite maximal therapy, declining mental status or severe agitation, persistent hypoxemia (SpO2 < 90%) despite high-flow oxygen, rising PaCO2 with acidemia (pH < 7.20) despite NIV, and hemodynamic instability. Importantly, blood gases alone should not drive the decision. The overall clinical trajectory matters more than isolated values [5].

Intubation Technique and Induction Medications

When intubation becomes necessary, meticulous preparation and technique are essential. The most experienced available practitioner should perform or closely supervise the procedure, preferably using video laryngoscopy which improves first-pass success rates [49]. Pre-intubation preparation requires ensuring adequate vascular access and fluid resuscitation, preparing for post-intubation hypotension (from decreased left ventricular preload) with fluid boluses and readily available vasopressors, having multiple backup plans for the difficult airway, and assembling a team familiar with asthma ventilation.

For medication selection, ketamine (1–2 mg/kg intravenous) is the preferred induction agent owing to its bronchodilator properties, preservation of respiratory drive (if not paralyzed), and hemodynamic stability [50, 51]. Ketamine increases airway secretions, which can be managed with glycopyrrolate. Propofol is an acceptable alternative, offering rapid onset and some bronchodilation, but requires careful attention to blood pressure. For muscle relaxation, rocuronium (1.2 mg/kg) is preferred owing to its rapid onset and favorable side effect profile over succinylcholine. Sugammadex availability allows rapid reversal if needed.

The moment of intubation represents a critical period. Rapid sequence intubation is preferred as bag-mask ventilation of these patients with abnormally high airway resistance is challenging or often not feasible. These patients have been generating enormous negative intrathoracic pressures to maintain ventilation; after neuromuscular blockade administration and rapid relaxation, post-obstructive pulmonary edema may develop. Passage of the endotracheal tube (ETT) through the larynx is often not the most significant challenge. Over-enthusiastic bagging with excessive rates will lead to rapid hyperinflation with dire hemodynamic consequences. Severe hyperinflation can also cause loss of end-tidal carbon dioxide readings owing to deadspace ventilation, which might be misinterpreted as ETT dislodgement. It is imperative that the person handling bag ventilation makes a conscious effort to expertly ventilate the patient. In this initial phase, this often requires application of very high inspiratory pressures, long inspiratory times, and very long expiratory phases, with a resultant low respiratory rate.

Ventilator Mode Selection: Pressure versus Volume Control

The choice between pressure-controlled ventilation (PCV) and volume-controlled ventilation (VCV) represents a critical decision in asthma management, with some evidence favoring pressure control for physiologic reasons specific to obstructive airway disease [52]. In traditional VCV, tidal volume is delivered with constant flow, causing relatively less obstructed airways with shorter time constants to receive disproportionately more volume early in inspiration compared with more obstructed airways with longer time constants. This uneven distribution results in higher peak inspiratory pressures and decreased dynamic compliance.

PCV offers several physiologic advantages in asthma. With constant inflation pressure, less obstructed lung units with shorter time constants achieve pressure equilibration earlier during inspiration, while more obstructed areas with longer time constants continue to receive additional volume throughout the inspiratory phase. This results in more uniform distribution of inspired gas, delivery of greater tidal volume for the same inflation pressure, and improved dynamic compliance compared with VCV. The decelerating flow pattern of PCV also provides higher initial gas flow with progressive deceleration, which may improve gas distribution in airways with varying degrees of obstruction.

A critical disadvantage of PCV is variable tidal volume delivery depending on respiratory system resistance. In asthma, where airway resistance can change rapidly, tidal volumes may fluctuate significantly for the same inflation pressure, necessitating close monitoring and frequent pressure adjustments. While controlled hypoventilation with deep sedation has become standard for mechanically ventilated children with severe asthma, pressure support ventilation (PSV) offers an alternative that preserves spontaneous breathing. Wetzel demonstrated successful management of severe pediatric asthma using PSV while avoiding risks associated with prolonged paralysis [53].

In PSV, each patient-triggered breath receives preset inspiratory pressure support, allowing patient control of respiratory rate, inspiratory time, and tidal volume. Benefits include reduced sedation requirements, lower risk of critical illness myopathy (important given the concurrent use of corticosteroids), improved patient-ventilator synchrony, and potentially shorter ventilation duration. Ideal candidates are children with preserved respiratory drive who have passed the acute obstruction phase. Initial settings typically include pressure support 10–20 cm H2O above PEEP (targeting tidal volumes 6–8 mL/kg), PEEP 4–8 cm H2O to offset intrinsic PEEP, and appropriate sedation to maintain comfort while preserving drive. The choice between controlled hypoventilation and PSV should be individualized. Some patients benefit from initial controlled ventilation during acute obstruction, transitioning to PSV as they improve.

Ventilator Settings: The Controlled Hypoventilation Strategy

The fundamental principle of IMV in asthma is controlled hypoventilation: accepting hypercapnia to avoid dynamic hyperinflation [54, 55]. This paradigm shift, introduced by Darioli and Perret in their landmark 1984 study, dramatically reduced mortality from status asthmaticus by prioritizing lung protection over normalization of blood gases [54]. Whether using PCV or VCV, initial ventilator settings should prioritize avoidance of worsening hyperinflation. For PCV: peak inspiratory pressure 25–35 cmH2O (starting conservatively), respiratory rate 6–12 breaths/min, inspiratory time 1.0–1.5 s targeting I:E ratio 1:3 to 1:4, minimal PEEP (0–4 cmH2O), and FIO2 as needed for SpO2 > 90%. For VCV: tidal volume 8–10 mL/kg (based on ideal body weight), respiratory rate 6–12 breaths/min, inspiratory time and I:E ratio as above, with plateau pressure (Pplat) monitoring to ensure < 28 cmH2O [56].

In asthma, the difference between peak inspiratory pressure (PIP) and Pplat reflects airway resistance. PIP may exceed 50 cmH2O owing to the high resistance, but if Pplat remains < 30 cmH2O, alveolar pressure is acceptable. The Pplat is obtained with an inspiratory hold and reflects true alveolar pressure. An increasing peak-to-plateau gradient suggests worsening airway resistance, while a decreasing gradient indicates improvement [5, 56].

Monitoring Dynamic Hyperinflation

Vigilant monitoring for dynamic hyperinflation is essential, as this represents the primary threat to the ventilated patient with asthma [57]. Plateau pressure should be maintained < 30 cmH2O, as elevations beyond this value suggest hyperinflation. Intrinsic PEEP, measured with expiratory hold, with values > 10 cmH2O indicates severe air trapping and must be addressed by optimizing the ventilation strategy. Driving pressure (Pplat—PEEPi) should be minimized, as high values increase barotrauma risk.

Flow-time curves provide real-time assessment of exhalation adequacy. Failure of expiratory flow to return to zero before the next breath confirms air trapping. The shape of the expiratory flow curve (scooped versus linear) indicates severity of obstruction. Clinical signs such as difficulty with manual ventilation, decreasing SpO2 despite adequate FIO2, hypotension, and subcutaneous emphysema all suggest worsening hyperinflation.

Use of PEEP in Asthma

The use of PEEP in mechanically ventilated patients with asthma remains controversial. Tuxen’s landmark study demonstrated that, in patients under neuromuscular blockade, applied PEEP uniformly increased lung volumes in patients with severe obstruction [18]. In other words, in paralyzed patients with near-fatal asthma, any PEEP will contribute to air trapping and hyperinflation. Current recommendations suggest using zero or minimal PEEP (≤ 4 cmH2O) during controlled ventilation in patients with paralysis. During assisted ventilation with spontaneous breathing effort, the application of PEEP up to approximately 80% of measured PEEPi can reduce triggering work and improve synchrony without additionally increasing lung volume. The practical approach is to start with zero PEEP immediately following intubation and during the initial phase of treatment. Once there is evidence of improved airway resistance and the neuromuscular blockade is discontinued, PEEP should be carefully titrated to facilitate ventilator synchrony while monitoring for worsening hyperinflation.

Permissive Hypercapnia

Permissive hypercapnia is not merely tolerated but actively pursued in critical asthma to minimize the effects of dynamic hyperinflation on lung volumes [58]. Evidence suggests remarkable tolerance to hypercapnia if developed gradually. PaCO2 values of 80–100 mmHg are commonly tolerated, and pH > 7.20 is generally safe in the absence of other complications. Much higher PaCO2 values encountered in clinical practice have been reported with intact recovery [59].

The physiologic effects of hypercapnia include cerebral vasodilation (usually well-tolerated unless intracranial pathology exists), pulmonary vasoconstriction (which may worsen V/Q matching), decreased myocardial contractility (usually offset by sympathetic stimulation), and rightward shift of the oxyhemoglobin curve (improving tissue oxygen delivery). Bicarbonate therapy is rarely indicated and potentially harmful by increasing CO2 production during an elimination-limited state. Consider buffering only if pH < 7.15 with hemodynamic instability, hyperkalemia in the setting of severe acidosis, or myocardial dysfunction is evident. If bicarbonate is given, use slow infusion (0.5–1 mEq/kg over 30–60 min) rather than bolus administration [60].

Sedation and Paralysis

Adequate sedation is crucial for the ventilated patient with asthma to prevent patient-ventilator asynchrony and minimize oxygen consumption. Ketamine infusion (1–2 mg/kg/hr) provides sedation and analgesia with continued bronchodilation [61]. Propofol is effective but requires monitoring for propofol infusion syndrome if used > 48 h. Dexmedetomidine serves as a useful adjunct with minimal respiratory depression, while benzodiazepines are effective but may prolong the ventilation course.

Neuromuscular blockade is often necessary initially to ensure ventilator synchrony and minimize oxygen consumption. However, prolonged use with concurrent corticosteroids risks critical illness myopathy. Recommendations include using paralysis for the shortest duration possible, preferring cisatracurium over aminosteroid neuromuscular blockers (e.g., vecuronium) owing to less myopathy risk, monitoring train-of-four to avoid excessive blockade, and performing daily paralysis holidays to assess readiness for weaning [5].

Weaning and Liberation

As airway obstruction improves, careful weaning prevents both premature extubation failure and unnecessary prolonged ventilation. Signs of improvement include decreasing peak-to-Pplat gradient, decreasing PEEPi, improved expiratory flow graphics, reduced bronchodilator requirements, and improving gas exchange at lower ventilator settings. The weaning strategy involves first reducing sedation and discontinuing neuromuscular blockade, then transitioning to pressure support ventilation. Pressure support should be gradually reduced as the patient assumes more work. Applying PEEP to match PEEPi improves triggering. It is important to monitor for excessive tidal volumes as compliance improves and consider daily spontaneous breathing trials once minimal support is achieved. Given the high risk of post-extubation failure, prophylactic HFNC or NIV should be considered, particularly in younger children (< 5 years), those with prolonged ventilation (> 72 h), multiple prior intubations, or persistent wheezing despite treatment.

Delivery of Aerosolized Medications During Noninvasive Respiratory Support

Historically, nebulizers were thought to be more effective than pressurized metered-dose inhalers (pMDIs); however, data suggest that for bronchodilators, the same clinical response is often achieved with the labeled dose from the pMDI or nebulizer despite the higher nominal dose for the nebulizer [62, 63]. Available evidence supports the delivery of aerosols during NIV and inhaled bronchodilator response might be improved with the use of NIV in acute asthma; however, there are limited in vivo data in children [64, 65]. Aerosol delivery with NIV is influenced by the NIV settings, the leak port position, the type of aerosol generator and the interface used during treatment [66]. Increasing inspiratory pressure during NIV increases aerosol deposition as opposed to increasing expiratory pressure, which decreases aerosol delivery [66].

In in-vitro studies using pediatric models, vibrating mesh nebulizers (VMN) has been shown to be more effective than jet nebulizers (JN) regardless of the position of the nebulizers in the delivery of albuterol during noninvasive ventilation via a single-limb as well as a double-limb circuit [67, 68]. In the single-limb circuit, VMN was twice as efficient in the delivery of aerosol than JN when the nebulizers were placed before the humidifier and in between the mask and exhalation port [67]. In single-limb circuit, both VMN and JN placed in between the mask and exhalation port were more efficient than when these two types of nebulizers were placed before the humidifier [67, 69]. Most of these pediatric in-vitro studies were done with nonvented masks with the use of a leak port distal to the mask.

Limited adult studies have found that with the leak port integrated into the mask, the efficiency of pMDI is better than that of nebulizers as aerosol loss with pMDI during expiration is less compared with the nebulizer [66]. However, the clinical efficacy of utilizing pMDI during NIV has not been studied in children with asthma [65]. In-vitro adult studies found that aerosol delivery in a single-limb circuit with a non-vented mask was higher than with a vented mask and that aerosol delivery was generally poor with the vented mask regardless of nebulizer position [70, 71]. In the dual limb circuit, VMN was 3–4 times more efficient in the delivery of aerosol compared with JN when the nebulizers were placed before the humidifier and before the Y-piece [68]. In the dual limb circuit, VMN placed before Y-piece delivered the most medication, while delivery efficiency of the JN was not affected by the position of the nebulizer [68].

During HFNC, aerosol delivery efficiency is affected by various factors such as configuration and type of HFNC system, nebulizer type and position, and set flow. The Optiflow (Fisher Paykel, Auckland, NZ) system results in higher aerosol delivery than the Vapotherm (Exeter, NH) system at similar flow rates, likely owing to the higher gas velocity of the Vapotherm system [65]. VMN are more effective and delivered 2–3 times more inhaled medications that JN [72, 73]. VMN placed before the humidifier delivered more inhaled medications than when placed closer to the patient [72, 73]. Higher HFNC flow results in decreased aerosol delivery. As the ratio of HFNC gas flow to patient’s inspiratory flow increased, the delivered dose decreased, with a steep decline in aerosol delivery when HFNC gas flow was more than twofold higher than patient’s inspiratory flow [65, 73]. There are limited studies evaluating the delivery of pMDI with HFNC. Limited data shows that the delivery of pMDI with spacer using mask/mouthpiece during HFNC decreased the inhaled dose to a level that was only 6–50% of the inhaled dose compared with when use with pMDI and spacer alone and without concurrent use of HFNC as the high velocity of HFNC gas flow disperse the aerosol away from the upper airway [73, 74]. When pMDI was placed in-line with HFNC, the use of a spacer increased the inhaled dose by 2–5 times in comparison with no spacer, regardless of MDI placement and HFNC flow settings. The inhaled dose was higher with the spacer placed close to the nasal cannula than close to the humidifier [46, 75].

As a gas lighter than oxygen and air, heliox has the theoretical potential to create less turbulent flow which can lead to increased aerosol delivery through improved penetration and distribution of aerosol particles in the respiratory tract [65, 76]. In vitro studies using pediatric models found VMN placed in line with the HFNC delivered higher inhaled dose of albuterol than JN [76, 77]. There are however limited studies reporting on the use of heliox during NIV in pediatric patients. To date there is paucity of data to suggest heliox applied noninvasively reduces mechanical ventilation rates [78]. Limited data suggest that continuous nebulization of albuterol during NIV and HFNC was safe and feasible [32, 37, 65].

Delivery of Aerosolized Medication During Invasive Mechanical Ventilation

During invasive MV, use of either the VMN or MDI with spacer for the aerosol delivery are recommended. Use of JN, which requires an external compressed gas to drive aerosol delivery, is not recommended owing to changes in tidal volumes, inspiratory flow patterns, trigger sensitivity and fraction of inspired oxygen. This issue can be circumvented with the use of ventilator-integrated JN with the limitation that aerosol delivery time is much longer. In addition, aerosol delivery efficiency for JN is low [46]. Regardless of nebulizer placement and ventilator settings, studies reported higher aerosol delivery efficiency with VM and MDI compared with JN [46, 63]. Optimal placement of VMN and JN is in the inspiratory limb between the ventilator and before the humidifier. MDI is best used with a spacer and placed in the inspiratory limb before the Y-piece and actuated at the beginning of the inspiratory flow from the ventilator [46, 63].

Despite studies reporting reduction of aerosol delivery efficiency by 50% during IMV with heated humidification compared with dry conditions, there is a need to balance the consideration of potential harms of delivering dry gas to a patient’s airways along with the time interval needed for a humidifier and circuits to cool down and dry. As such, turning off the humidifier is therefore not recommended for routine aerosol therapy. However, removal or bypassing the heat-moisture exchanger (HME) which is a barrier to aerosol drug delivery in ventilated patients is recommended during aerosol delivery [46]. To prevent de-recruitment from the removal of HME for aerosol delivery, use of HMEs designed to allow aerosol delivery by bypassing the HME during aerosol delivery can be utilized.

Adding heliox for the sole purpose of improving aerosol delivery efficiency is not recommended as the costs of heliox offset the limited benefits of improved aerosol delivery with heliox use [8, 46]. Additionally, the use of heliox with IMV can be challenging as some ventilators become non-functional with heliox whereas others have software to permit accurate measurement of exhaled tidal volume when using heliox [79]. To reduce fugitive aerosols and to protect ventilator expiratory sensors, use of a filter on the expiratory limb is recommended. As a result of contradictory reports and the potential adverse impact on patient-ventilator synchrony, changing ventilator mode and parameters for the sole purpose of improving aerosol delivery efficiency in patients receiving invasive ventilation is not recommended [46].

Advanced and Rescue Therapies

Inhaled Anesthetic Agents

When conventional ventilation strategies fail to deliver acceptable results, administration of an inhaled anesthetic offer potent bronchodilation through direct smooth muscle relaxation, independent of β-receptor pathways [80–82]. Both isoflurane and sevoflurane have proven effective, with most reports showing improvement in gas exchange within 2–4 h.

The mechanisms include direct smooth muscle relaxation, reduced vagal tone, decreased airway hyperresponsiveness, and anti-inflammatory effects. Practical implementation requires an anesthesia ventilator or special adapter (e.g., AnaConDa device), with close collaboration with anesthesiology essential. A scavenging system is mandatory to protect staff from exposure to exhaled gas. Monitoring for hypotension is important, particularly with isoflurane owing to its attendant vasodilation. Typical doses range from 0.5–2% for isoflurane and 1–4% for sevoflurane. While no randomized trials of inhaled anesthetics in pediatric near-fatal asthma exist, multiple case series report success in children failing conventional therapy.

Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation (ECMO)

ECMO represents the ultimate rescue therapy, for children refractory to maximal conventional therapy [83, 84]. Indications in asthma include severe air-leak syndrome (especially pneumoperitoneum), refractory respiratory acidosis (pH < 7.0) despite optimal ventilation, cardiac arrest or severe hemodynamic instability, and serving as a bridge to recovery while avoiding ventilator-induced lung injury.

Data from Extracorporeal Life Support Organization (ELSO) registry indicate an 84% survival rate for pediatric asthma rescued with ECMO [84]. Importantly, ECMO does not treat the underlying asthma, so bronchodilator and, principally, anti-inflammatory therapy must continue. VV-ECMO is usually adequate for respiratory failure alone, while VA-ECMO, though much less common, may be needed following cardiac arrest or severe hemodynamic instability. Complications and Outcomes

Barotrauma and Air-Leak Syndromes

Barotrauma remains a feared complication, occurring in approximately 6% of mechanically ventilated children with asthma [85]. The spectrum includes pneumothorax, which is most common and often presents with tension physiology due to positive pressure ventilation, requiring immediate decompression and chest tube placement. Pneumomediastinum is often benign and self-limited unless progressing to pneumothorax, requiring close monitoring but rarely intervention. Subcutaneous emphysema has a dramatic appearance but is usually clinically insignificant, indicating air leak and necessitating search for pneumothorax. Pneumoperitoneum is rare but life-threatening, as air dissects along vascular sheaths into the abdomen and may require ECMO as abdominal distension worsens ventilation. Risk factors for barotrauma include severe dynamic hyperinflation, plateau pressure > 30 cmH2O, high minute ventilation, patient-ventilator asynchrony, and prolonged ventilation (> 72 h).

Cardiovascular Complications

The cardiovascular stress of critical asthma and its treatment can precipitate various complications. Myocardial injury with elevated troponin occurs even without known cardiac disease, associated with diastolic hypotension from continuous β-agonist therapy [86]. Arrhythmias, usually supraventricular tachycardia or frequent ectopy, relate to β-agonists, hypokalemia, and acidosis, rarely requiring specific treatment beyond correcting underlying abnormalities. Hypotension is multifactorial, stemming from dynamic hyperinflation impeding venous return, β-agonist vasodilation, and sedation effects. Management requires careful balance of fluid resuscitation, reducing mean intrathoracic pressure, and occasionally vasopressor support.

Long-Term Outcomes

While immediate mortality rates are nearly zero for children reaching medical care without anteceding cardiorespiratory arrest, long-term outcomes deserve consideration. Data from Melbourne showed 5% mortality within 10 years of PICU admission for asthma, suggesting these children represent a high-risk phenotype requiring close follow-up [87]. Furthermore, up to 88% have persistent asthma symptoms requiring ongoing therapy. Concerns exist about prolonged hypercapnia, sedation, and potential neurotoxicity from inhaled anesthetics regarding neurodevelopmental outcomes, though specific data in asthma are lacking [59]. The combination of corticosteroids and neuromuscular blockade can cause critical illness myopathy with prolonged weakness. Most recover completely but rehabilitation may be needed.

Special Populations and Other Considerations

Obesity

Obese children have higher risk of PICU admission and may require modified ventilation strategies [88]. They have reduced functional residual capacity at baseline with higher work of breathing. They may need higher PEEP to maintain recruitment, and tidal volumes, along with many drug doses, should be calculated on ideal body weight.

Perception of Dyspnea

Some children have reduced perception of dyspnea and chemosensitivity to hypoxia, and may present to medical care late in extremis. These patients often require immediate intubation and have higher mortality risk.

Protocols and Pathways in Pediatric Critical Asthma

AARC and PALISI clinical practice guidelines on pediatric critical asthma suggest the use of a dedicated protocol or pathway for managing children treated for critical asthma [8]. A recently published systematic review and meta-analysis on protocols and pathways in pediatric critical asthma concur with this recommendation [8, 89]. However, even though pediatric asthma protocols were associated with reductions in hospital stay, PICU stay and decreased need for respiratory support escalation but there is no impact on intubation or NRS use [89].

Asthma Monitoring and Scores

The 2026 World Health Organization (WHO) Consolidated Guidelines for the Management of Common childhood Illness and the 2024 Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) Summary Guide recommend peak expiratory flow rate (PEFR) monitoring for outpatient pediatric asthma management for children > 5 years old but with limited use in patients < 5 years old as PEFR maneuvers are effort dependent and children < 5 years old may not be able to cooperate. Similarly, PEFR monitoring is of limited use in critical asthma and in the PICU setting as children with asthma exacerbation and in distress may be unable to perform peak flow reliably [90, 91]. Instead, in pediatric critical asthma, emphasis should be on continuous clinical assessment and monitoring [8, 90]. Likewise, asthma scores such as Pediatric Respiratory Assessment Measurement (PRAM), Pediatric Asthma Severity Score (PASS) and Hospital-wide Asthma Severity Score (HASS) were not designed nor validated for patients on NRS and IMV [92–95].

Conclusions and Future Directions

The management of respiratory support in pediatric critical asthma has evolved significantly, with increasing emphasis on noninvasive strategies and refined approaches to MV when required. The fundamental principle that MV strategies in asthma differs significantly from lung-protective strategies used in ARDS cannot be overemphasized. NRS is recommended as the first line of respiratory support for patients with critical asthma but when IMV is necessary, dynamic hyperinflation management takes precedence. With modern approaches, survival from asthma now exceeds 96%, underscoring the importance of evidence-based and physiology-guided care.

Future research priorities include developing validated severity scores for escalation decisions, optimal protocols for noninvasive support, strategies to enhance aerosol delivery during respiratory support, and long-term outcome studies of children requiring MV for asthma. As our understanding of asthma phenotypes and endotypes expands, personalized approaches to respiratory support may further improve outcomes in this vulnerable population.
