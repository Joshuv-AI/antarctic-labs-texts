Impact of context on nurse-led implementation of evidence-based pain management practices in the Sumamos Excelencia project: a qualitative study
Authors: Leticia Bernués-Caudillo, Azucena Pedraz-Marcos, Esther González-María, Mª Teresa Moreno-Casbas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundContext analysis is a key element in the implementation of evidence-based practice. It contributes to understanding why certain interventions succeed in some settings and fail in others, while also supporting the tailoring of implementation strategies to different populations and settings. This task becomes especially challenging when implementing complex interventions that involve multidisciplinary teams operating across multiple organisations. Current literature highlights the need for innovative methodological approaches capable of capturing complexity across settings and examining the interactions between contextual factors and the various levels of the healthcare system. The aim of this study is to understand how context influenced the implementation of evidence-based recommendations on pain assessment and management in adult hospital units participating in the Sumamos Excelencia implementation project.MethodsThis study is a qualitative process evaluation. Eleven team leaders and ten nurses from local implementation teams participating in the Sumamos Excelencia project were selected based on their audit implementation results, and unit and hospital characteristics. Semi-structured interviews with local implementation leaders and one focus group with non-leader members were conducted between February and June 2023. Thematic saturation was achieved. Data were analyzed using content analysis, guided by a coding frame. Barriers, facilitators, and strategies were identified and categorized. Heatmaps and social network analysis visualized contextual interactions and influencing factors.ResultsContextual factors were categorized into four levels: individual characteristics, internal context, external context, and project-related factors. Key barriers included time constraints, resistance to change, high workload, and record system limitations. Facilitators included coherence with care objectives, positive outcomes, leader commitment, teamwork, and alignment with institutional goals. Heatmaps and social network analysis emphasized motivation as central to implementation success.ConclusionSuccessful implementation depends on motivation, organizational support, staff stability, and registration systems. Context analysis is essential for tailored strategies. Innovative methods are needed to holistically identify barriers and facilitators. Effective evidence-based practice implementation requires addressing interconnected factors like motivation, support, resources, and knowledge. The Sumamos Excelencia strategy is effective but requires usability and feedback improvements.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12912-026-04956-7.

Background

Evidence-based practice (EBP) is a fundamental pillar of high-quality, safe, and cost-effective healthcare. Its integration into nursing care has been associated with improved patient outcomes, fewer adverse events, reduced errors, and more efficient use of resources [1–4]. Furthermore, EBP adoption contributes to nurse job satisfaction and supports workforce retention [5].

Despite these well-documented benefits, EBP remains underutilized in clinical practice [6, 7]. Pain management illustrates this persistent evidence-to-practice gap: although clinical guidelines are available, pain continues to be inadequately assessed and treated across healthcare settings [8, 9]. In Spain, acute pain affects between 36.7% and 76.61% of hospitalised patients [10–12], contributing to complications and prolonged hospital stays. Effective pain management enhances comfort and functional recovery [13], and nurses, due to their proximity to patients, play a key role in its implementation [8].

Successful EBP implementation requires proactive strategies that actively engage nurses and acknowledge context-specific barriers and facilitators, thereby ensuring sustainability and integration into healthcare settings. Context is a key element in the implementation of EBP, with some arguing that the success or failure of implementation projects cannot be understood or explained without considering the specific context in which they take place [14–16]. Implementation practice has often focused on isolated factors such as facilitators and barriers, without fully addressing the interaction between them and the different levels of the healthcare system [15, 17]. This fragmentation hinders our understanding of which implementation strategies work in real-world settings and the factors and mechanisms that make them effective [16, 18]. Rather than being a fixed and passive environment, context is a complex and dynamic entity comprising the characteristics, conditions, and relationships that shape the environment in which an intervention is implemented and is continuously influenced by interactions among components across different levels [14, 15, 18]. Therefore, context analysis is essential not only for understanding why certain interventions succeed in some settings and fail in others, but also for tailoring implementation projects to different populations and contexts [14, 19].

The present study was conducted within the Sumamos Excelencia project [20], a national initiative aimed at integrating evidence-based recommendations into direct care units in hospitals, primary care centres, and nursing homes within the Spanish National Health System. Participants selected one of four different packages of evidence-based recommendations: pain management, urinary incontinence management, childhood obesity prevention, or breastfeeding promotion, alongside a mandatory hand hygiene package.

This manuscript reports a qualitative study nested within the broader Sumamos Excelencia implementation project. Specifically, the study focused on units implementing the pain assessment and management intervention package. Pain management was the most frequently chosen, underscoring nurses’ interest in improving this area of care. The pain assessment and management intervention package comprised five recommendations, selected by an expert panel based on their feasibility for implementation with minimal resource requirements, their suitability to be led by clinical nurses within the units, their ability to be implemented without structural modifications, and their adaptability across diverse clinical contexts (Table 1).

Within the broader the Sumamos Excelencia project, context analysis was used to identify barriers and facilitators influencing implementation and to support the development of locally tailored implementation strategies. To support this process, the global coordination team developed a barriers questionnaire based on the Tailored Implementation for Chronic Diseases (TICD) checklist [21]. However, its application revealed limitations in fully capturing the complexity of unit contexts and the interactions among their elements. Consequently, a qualitative study was conducted in units implementing the pain management intervention package to gain a deeper understanding of the contextual dynamics involved.

Context analysis is particularly challenging when implementing complex interventions involving multidisciplinary teams across multiple organizations as the Sumamos Excelencia project. In such cases, to analyze the implementation complexity and facilitate comparisons between organizational units and stakeholders, data visualization methods such as heatmaps, data matrices, or social network analysis (SNA) may help identify patterns, interrelationships, and contextual influences affecting implementation processes [22, 23]. However, the adoption of this techniques remains limited in implementation science [22].

Methods

Aim

The primary aim of this study was to understand how contextual factors influenced the implementation of evidence-based recommendations on pain assessment and management in adult inpatient units participating in the Sumamos Excelencia project.

The specific objectives were:

Design

A qualitative process evaluation design through interviews and focus groups was conducted [24, 25].

Theoretical framework

Guided by implementation science principles, this study integrated multiple theories, models, and frameworks selected according to project phase and purpose [26, 27]. Specifically, TICD and CFIR were used to structure the identification of contextual barriers and facilitators, ERIC was used to classify implementation strategies, and EPIS was used to organise implementation phases and contextual dynamics over time.

To examine barriers and facilitators, we applied the Tailored Implementation for Chronic Disease (TICD) checklist [21] and the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) [28, 29], both used in data collection and analysis. The TICD checklist was developed through a systematic review and a consensus process involving implementation researchers. It comprises a comprehensive set of 57 determinants of practice grouped into seven domains and is designed to inform the tailoring of implementation strategies based on identified barriers and facilitators within the Tailored Implementation for Chronic Disease project [21]. The CFIR is a widely used meta-theoretical framework developed from a systematic review of theories, models, and frameworks in implementation science, primarily in healthcare. It integrates existing theoretical approaches into a single structure to guide implementation research. The updated version includes 48 constructs and 19 subconstructs across five domains and is broadly applied across settings and interventions, supporting the identification of determinants and the design of implementation strategies [28, 29].

The Exploration, Preparation, Implementation, Sustainment (EPIS) framework [30] structured the project’s phases and guided the analysis of contextual dynamics over time. The EPIS framework is a comprehensive implementation framework developed from a review of the literature on implementation in public sector social and allied health service systems in the United States. It conceptualises implementation as a process occurring across four phases: exploration, preparation, implementation, and sustainment [30]. This framework was adapted to align with the Sumamos Excelencia phases by operationalizing the EPIS phases according to the project structure.

Implementation strategies developed by local implementation teams were classified using the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC) taxonomy [31, 32], which offers standardised definitions to enhance reporting clarity and consistency in implementation research. The ERIC list is a taxonomy of discrete implementation strategies. It was developed through a review of health and mental health literature, followed by a modified Delphi process and concept mapping. It includes 73 strategies organised into nine thematic categories and is recommended to standardise the description and reporting of implementation strategies in research and practice [31, 32].

Study setting and recruitment

Over 15 months (February 2022–May 2023), the Sumamos Excelencia project employed a multicentre quasi-experimental before-and-after design, supported by a multifaceted global implementation strategy combining training, facilitation, context analysis, audits at four time points (baseline, 3, 6, and 12 months), and tailored strategies. This global strategy operated on two levels: the global implementation strategy coordinated by the global coordinating team, and local implementation strategies, designed and coordinated by local implementation teams within each unit. Participating units formed nurse-led local implementation teams and voluntarily selected one of four intervention packages.

Participants were selected from the Sumamos Excelencia participants using predefined inclusion criteria: adult hospital units implementing pain assessment and management recommendations. Intensive care and emergency units were excluded due to their distinct patient profiles and organisational contexts.

Sampling considered unit specialisation (medical/surgical), geographic location, hospital type and size (based on bed count), role and gender of the local implementation team leaders, and changes in adherence between baseline and six-month audits. Adherence was defined as the overall percentage of compliance with audited evidence-based recommendations within each participating unit. Adherence rates were calculated by averaging the compliance percentages across all assessed recommendations for each audit. Units were then ranked according to the magnitude of change in adherence between baseline and 6-month audits.

To capture diverse perspectives, local implementation team leaders were invited to participate in interviews, while other members of the local implementation teams (excluding leaders and unit managers) were invited to join the focus group. A purposive sample was drawn for both.

Of the 32 eligible units, 25 local implementation team members were contacted. Invitations were extended via telephone calls to local implementation team leaders, who were asked to participate in interview or nominate a colleague for the focus group. Interview logistics (date, time, and location) were arranged by participants; focus group details were agreed jointly with the research team.

Data collection

In-depth, semi-structured interviews were conducted in the second half of the project with local implementation team leaders implementing pain management recommendations. The first author, a female PhD student and project coordinator, conducted 11 individual interviews between February and June 2023. She had previous contact with the local implementation team members via email and project webinars. Interviews (30–120 min) were held in private hospital rooms, with no other individuals present.

To complement these data, a focus group was conducted in June 2023 with non-leader local implementation team members from the same units. Researcher AP, also female researcher, with no prior contact with participants, facilitated the 120-minute session at the research department. Travel expenses were covered. A purposive sample was used to ensure representation across units. Data were collected and analysed concurrently, and data saturation was considered achieved when no new codes emerged in the final three interviews and the focus group.

The CFIR framework guided both data collection activities. A thematic interview guide (additional file 1) was developed by the first author and finalised by consensus with the second and third authors, both experienced nurse researchers in qualitative and implementation science, respectively. The same guide was used for all sessions. Field notes were taken during and after the interviews and focus group to capture observations and insights. Techniques such as paraphrasing were used during the interviews to confirm understanding and ensure data accuracy.

Data analysis

All interviews and the focus group were audio-recorded and professionally transcribed verbatim. Data collection was conducted in Spanish, the native language of both participants and researchers. Quotations included in this manuscript were translated into English during manuscript preparation and reviewed against the original transcripts by researchers with advanced English proficiency to ensure accuracy and preservation of meaning.

Data were analysed using a qualitative content analysis approach [33]. A coding frame and a codebook, informed by multiple implementation frameworks, were developed to guide data organization and interpretation, including the development of the heatmaps and social network analysis (SNA) (Table 2).

Using the resulting coding framework and codebook, transcripts were examined to identify contextual factors (barriers and facilitators), and strategies used by local implementation teams, and coded accordingly. Transcripts and field notes were read iteratively to ensure familiarity with the data. Field notes were used to complement and contextualise the analysis. Two researchers independently coded the first three interviews and compared results to ensure consistency and shared understanding. The research team held regular meetings to refine codes, categories, and themes. Codes were analysed by theme and grouped according to unit type (surgical/medical), local implementation team leader role, and level of implementation success. In the final phase, categories were further abstracted to explore patterns and relationships. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached.

To explore similarities and differences across units, a heatmap was generated following the approach of Salvati et al. [22]. The heatmap was developed using the levels, categories, and subcategories derived from the coding framework. Rows represented participating units, while columns represented contextual factors identified during analysis. Factors highlighted in the heatmap were those identified by at least six participants or considered highly relevant by at least one unit. Green cells indicated factors perceived as facilitators, red cells indicated barriers, and yellow cells represented factors described as both barriers and facilitators within the unit, depending on the implementation phase or specific circumstances.

Additionally, drawing on the work of Conroy et al. [23], a frequency matrix of relationships between categories was developed and visualised as a network graph using SNA in Kumu© [34].

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Instituto de Salud Carlos III (Ref: CEI PI 18_2022_Amp 2023). Participants received oral and written information about the study, including assurances of voluntary participation, confidentiality, anonymity, and the right to withdraw or pause the recording at any time without justification. All participants provided written informed consent. Audio recordings were pseudonymised and stored on encrypted institutional servers accessible only to the research team.

Rigor and reflexivity

According to Lincoln and Guba [35], the approach to trustworthiness encompasses four essential elements: credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability. We ensured credibility through methodological and researcher triangulation. This was complemented by an iterative process of reaching consensus on codes and themes. Transferability was supported by a thick description of factors, setting, inclusion criteria and participant characteristics. Adherence to the COREQ checklist, an audit trail of analytic decisions, and secure document archiving reinforced dependability. Confirmability was achieved by linking interpretations to verbatim quotations and preserving raw data for external review. Throughout, researchers kept reflexive diaries, discussed and critically examined assumptions after each interview to attenuate bias and ensure that the results remained grounded in the participants’ perspectives.

Motivation versus resistance and the central role of coherence and perceived benefits

Most local implementation teams reported as factors related to the individuals involved in the implementation, that time constraints. This often requires work beyond rostered hours, limited their participation in the project. Despite this, team commitment emerged as a key facilitator, enabling members to compensate for these constraints through additional effort. Institutional networks with other units and departments, such as quality teams and nursing leadership, facilitated access to resources and expedited policy navigation.It has all been extra, either we’ve worked extra hours or some of them, maybe taking advantage of the night shift when it’s quieter, have used the time to make the audits, review the clinical records, we have meetings on Monday extra hours. Among frontline professionals, knowledge was both an enabler and a barrier. Two distinct types of knowledge were identified as influencing the process: knowledge of the specific recommendations being implemented and broader knowledge of EBP. Lack of familiarity with the recommendations was common but improved with training, In contrast, limited EBP literacy, particularly among nurses trained before EBP was included in curricula, was harder to address.Well, this ward has a lot of staff rotation, so there’s a lot of young staff. So, once they’ve completed their degree, they’ve learned more about scientific methodology and evidence-based practice, so they don’t find the changes difficult. (I7)

Over time, professionals perceived the recommendations as coherent with existing routines and reported benefits such as improved self-efficacy, smoother workflows, and greater patient satisfaction. One of the most frequently highlighted facilitators was the perception that patients received better care and experienced improved well-being. As implementation progressed, these perceived benefits also helped mitigate other barriers, particularly time constraints, as more effective early pain management reduced the need for more intensive care later.I think the patient will be better cared for, and that’s perhaps what comforts us the most, we’re happy with that aspect, (…) but it’s a very positive mess, I think we are learning a lot, and the ones who will benefit the most will be our patients, I have that very clear. (I10)

Low prioritisation and forgetfulness regarding the recommendations, particularly their documentation, also emerged as key barriers, especially under increased workload. When competing clinical demands required urgent attention, nurses tended to prioritise immediate patient care over the implementation of recommendations, which were consequently delayed or omitted. Visible support from institutional and unit leaders legitimised the initiative, unlocked resources, and shortened approval timelines, while limited engagement hindered the development of some implementation strategies.In our hospital, the nursing management initially told us to do whatever we wanted with what we had, which wasn’t bad. Less effort and more collaboration to overcome organisational barriers at unit level

In relation with the unit internal context, implementation was facilitated when recommendations required minimal effort, particularly regarding documentation. Local implementation teams actively sought to reduce the burden of documenting compliance as a key strategy.It’s true that anything new is really challenging, and getting people to participate is even harder, especially if it requires extra effort. There’s an initial resistance and convincing them or getting them on board is always difficult. Change is hard. (FG)

Management-related factors played a decisive role. Incentives, such as participation certificates, functioned as motivators, while their absence was perceived as a lack of recognition. However, many participants reported limited ability to implement such strategies, as they largely depended on the engagement and support of hospital leadership. Discussion

This study explored contextual factors influencing the implementation of evidence-based pain assessment and management recommendations in adult inpatient units within the Sumamos Excelencia project. Findings reveal a complex interplay of individual, organisational, and project-related factors acting as barriers, facilitators, or both. Most were linked to individual and internal unit dynamics. Common factors included limited time of local implementation teams, professional knowledge, perceived relevance, alignment with care provided and patient needs, resistance to change, leadership commitment, workload, incentives, team stability, resource availability, teamwork, institutional support, and project design. These findings are consistent with previous research on EBP implementation in healthcare, which consistently underscores the critical role of local context in determining implementation success [18, 36, 37, 41]. The distribution of these factors across the coding framework highlights the interconnected influence of individual, organisational, and implementation process factors, consistent with the multilevel perspective reflected in the TICD, CFIR and EPIS frameworks [21, 28–30] and reinforcing the value of integrated approaches to context assessment.Within the category of local implementation team members, the main barrier was the lack of time to initiate and sustain the project, largely due to workload and limited resources. Although this could be mitigated through the commitment of local implementation team members, sustained support and motivation from unit and institutional leaders is essential to avoid compromising implementation processes and outcomes [3].

Among professionals who have to comply with the recommendations, knowledge emerged as a key but not decisive factor, consistent with prior research showing that increasing EBP knowledge alone does not ensure greater implementation [3, 18, 38, 39]. In our study, two types of knowledge were identified: EBP knowledge and knowledge of the specific recommendations, with each type having different effects on the implementation process. Training on the innovation being implemented, in addition to EBP training, has been considered important before [4], indicating that different strategies may be needed to address the barriers related to each of these types of knowledge. Most of the facilitators at this sublevel related to professionals’ perceptions of the positive consequences associated with the recommendations, alignment with existing routines, and ability to meet patient needs, consistent with other studies [40]. These findings suggest that incorporating content on perceived benefits into training and feedback strategies could enhance professionals’ motivation and capacity to implement the recommendations, especially given the central role of motivation in addressing key barriers such as low prioritisation, resistance to change, and negative emotions. These issues have been identified in several studies [4, 19, 40, 41].

A key finding was the nuanced relationship between professional experience and resistance to change. While some participants and previous studies reported greater resistance among experienced nurses [5, 37, 42], others noted that stable teams with veteran staff facilitated implementation through effective teamwork and openness. This aligns with research showing no consistent differences in EBP attitudes by experience level [39, 43, 44]. Further studies should examine how team stability and teamwork influence experienced nurses’ engagement with change.

Institutional leadership commitment was an influencing factor, although not a decisive one. This support usually eases implementation facilitating changes in registration systems or allocating time to the project [4, 6, 45, 46]. However, in some units, implementation progressed despite limited institutional support, suggesting that other factors, such as teamwork or motivation, may compensate for this gap. Notably, unit-level leadership was not identified as relevant by participants, despite its prominence in the literature [6, 41, 43, 45, 46]. This may reflect the overlap between local implementation team leader roles and existing unit leadership, diluting its visibility as an independent factor.

Participants did not identify factors related to patients or their support networks as important barriers or facilitators, with only limited acknowledgment of their influence in some cases. However, professionals perceived improvements in patient care as a key facilitator for adherence. The absence of patient-related factors in participants’ accounts may indicate limited patient involvement or consideration of their perspectives during implementation. While nurses valued patient benefits, the findings suggest a persistent gap in integrating patient preferences into decision-making, an issue highlighted in previous studies [41, 43].

Excessive workload emerged as a universal barrier across units, intensifying resistance to change and negative emotions among professionals. Nurses often perceive new recommendations as adding to their already demanding workload [5], a barrier frequently associated with staffing shortages [4, 18, 36]. However, other studies suggest that staffing issues and inadequate resources can be mitigated in the presence of other facilitating factors [18]. Given the limited capacity of the local implementation team members to address these barriers within their units, enhancing professionals’ motivation and perceptions of the recommendations could help to offset these difficulties. Staff stability also influenced implementation: stable teams showed lower resistance and higher motivation, whereas high turnover increased resistance and required additional training efforts, placing added demands on local implementation teams, findings consistent with prior literature [19, 47, 48].

The electronic record system was a major barrier in most units, hindering both implementation and monitoring of recommendations. This aligns with prior studies reporting nurses’ dissatisfaction with systems poorly adapted to clinical practice and patients’ care needs [49]. Although modifying the system was a common strategy, organisational constraints often limited its feasibility, highlighting the importance of involving nurses in the design of efficient, workload-sensitive documentation tools [49].

Two external contextual factors influenced implementation: institutional regulations and organisational objectives. Administrative complexity and excessive bureaucracy were noted as key barriers, consistent with prior research [4, 19, 50]. Several leaders reported difficulties in adapting strategies due to institutional regulations. Conversely, alignment between project and institutional objectives could facilitate implementation and support compliance with recommendations [50].

Participants positively evaluated the multifaceted global strategy of Sumamos Excelencia, particularly the training and online seminars. While online training was useful, it was not sufficient alone, reinforcing the value of external facilitation and expert mentorship [18, 38, 41, 43]. Some participants suggested more frequent contact with the coordinating implementation team. Many of the strategies described by participants could be readily mapped to the ERIC taxonomy, reinforcing its usefulness for systematically documenting and standardising implementation strategies while facilitating comparisons across settings and studies. A major challenge was the online audit platform, which many found complex and time-consuming. Addressing this issue is essential for future editions. Participants appreciated that the recommendations required minimal material resources and were perceived as beneficial. However, more complex actions, such as comprehensive pain assessment and individualized pain management, were harder to implement and showed lower adherence, consistent with previous findings [45]. This highlights the need for tailored strategies according to recommendation complexity.

Despite contextual differences, several units identified recurring factors influencing the implementation of evidence-based recommendations. This suggests the potential to standardise implementation strategies across diverse settings. Identifying common barriers and facilitators can inform both the design and refinement of the global strategy and support local implementation teams in selecting the most effective approaches.

Such standardisation is particularly valuable in multi-site projects or in contexts requiring rapid implementation, such as during the COVID-19 pandemic [51], as it can reduce the time needed for in-depth context assessments.

To support this process, the use of complexity science approaches and visualisation methods, including heatmaps, matrices, and SNAs, offers a comprehensive understanding of context without oversimplifying it [22, 23, 52]. These tools facilitate the visual synthesis of large datasets, enable comparisons across units, and reveal patterns and relationships between factors at different levels, highlighting those with the greatest influence.

Understanding these interactions supports the selection of targeted strategies: either addressing modifiable barriers directly or compensating for persistent ones by strengthening related facilitators. This approach enhances the efficient use of limited resources and informs adjustments to the global strategy, allowing better adaptation to diverse contexts and improved implementation outcomes.

As a result of this study, future editions of Sumamos Excelencia will incorporate these findings by adapting the global strategy. For example, greater emphasis will be placed on the perceived benefits of the recommendations for both professionals and patients, and efforts will be made to improve the user-friendliness of the platform. The effectiveness of these adjustments will be evaluated in subsequent research.

Limitations

The transferability of the findings to other settings should be interpreted with caution, given the context-specific nature of the study, the characteristics of the participating units, and the sampling strategy. Some sampling-related limitations were identified. First, participants were nurses already engaged in the project, likely more motivated towards EBP; including less-engaged professionals might have yielded different perspectives. Second, some units declined participation due to time constraints, potentially affecting representativeness. Finally, the characteristics of the participating units varied considerably, for example, in terms of documentation systems, which may have influenced the results. Additionally, the interviewer’s dual role as project coordinator may have influenced participants’ responses. To mitigate this, her interactions during the project were limited, and precautions were taken to ensure interviewer neutrality and confidentiality throughout the data collection process.

Conclusion

This study identified a range of context-specific barriers and facilitators influencing the implementation of evidence-based recommendations on pain assessment and management. No single factor determined implementation success; rather, outcomes resulted from the dynamic interaction of multiple elements, such as individual motivation, organizational support, and documentation systems.

These findings highlight the importance of comprehensive context analysis to design implementation strategies tailored to specific settings. Bridging the research-practice gap requires integrative approaches to identify context-specific barriers and facilitators. In addition, identifying recurring patterns across diverse settings may inform the development of more standardized, scalable implementation strategies, particularly in multi-site projects or settings requiring rapid implementation responses. Future research should continue to explore how interactions among contextual factors shape the effectiveness of evidence-based practice implementation.
