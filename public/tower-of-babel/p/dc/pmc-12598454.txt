From Uncertainty to Performance Optimization: Longitudinal Insights Into Metacognitive Development
Authors: Qianqian Wan, Olivera Savic, Mengcun Gao, Robby Ralston, Allison P. O'Leary, Vladimir M. Sloutsky
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis longitudinal study investigates metacognitive development in children aged four to six (N = 148; 74 girls; 106 White, 21 multiracial, 17 Black, 3 Asian, 1 Latino; collected in 2017–2019) compared to adults (N = 26, 13 women; collected in 2022). We assessed metacognitive monitoring and control using experimenter‐elicited and self‐generated responses in decision‐making tasks. Children demonstrated reliable task monitoring by age five and performance monitoring by age six, only on self‐generated measures. Children's choice patterns were driven by uncertainty until age six, when performance‐optimizing patterns emerged. Cross‐lagged panel analysis showed early monitoring abilities predicted later control, supporting the monitoring‐drives‐control theory. These findings illuminate the early metacognitive development trajectory, notably the transition from uncertainty‐based to performance‐oriented decision‐making.

Metacognition, the ability to evaluate and regulate one's cognitive processes, is integral to learning and academic success. It consists of two core components: metacognitive monitoring and metacognitive control. Metacognitive monitoring involves assessing one's performance during tasks, whereas metacognitive control involves regulating cognitive processes to improve outcomes (Nelson and Narens 1990). For instance, metacognitive monitoring allows individuals to identify gaps in their knowledge, and metacognitive control enables them to adjust their study strategies to fill those gaps (Paris and Winograd 1990). These abilities are widely recognized as crucial for academic achievement, underscoring the importance of fostering metacognitive skills early in life (Geurten and Bastin 2019; Lockl and Schneider 2002; Perry et al. 2019; Schneider and Artelt 2010).

A growing body of research has documented the early development of some metacognitive skills. For example, studies have shown that when infants are allowed to seek help from caregivers, they request more help in challenging situations and subsequently perform better than infants without access to help (Goupil et al. 2016). Such findings provide compelling evidence of the early emergence of simple metacognitive abilities, which are further supported by behavioral, neural, and physiological evidence (Dautriche et al. 2022; Goupil and Kouider 2016). However, as children enter their preschool years, they face increasingly complex cognitive challenges that demand more sophisticated metacognitive processes. This developmental period is particularly critical as children must navigate tasks requiring higher‐order thinking, making the role of metacognition more crucial and theoretically significant.

Assessment of Metacognitive Monitoring and Control in Preschoolers

Research on metacognitive monitoring and control in preschoolers examined two types of participant responses: experimenter‐elicited and self‐generated. Experimenter‐elicited responses involve following specific instructions or answering explicit questions. For example, participants have been asked to (1) predict their own ability to recognize or recall studied information (judgments of learning), (2) estimate their confidence in recognizing pictures, including common objects and faces (Destan et al. 2014; Lyons and Ghetti 2011), or (3) provide verbal ratings of their performance (Dunlosky and Metcalfe 2008).

These tasks require children to not only evaluate their own performance but also categorize their responses into discrete units. Studies show that children as young as three demonstrate early signs of relative monitoring accuracy, providing lower confidence ratings for incorrect versus correct answers (Gardier and Geurten 2024; Lyons and Ghetti 2011). However, their absolute monitoring accuracy remains poor, with confidence levels typically exceeding actual performance. To address this complexity, researchers often simplify these measures, reducing them to binary ones—whether or not children show relative accuracy in their judgments, such as higher confidence for correct answers (Hembacher and Ghetti 2014; Vo et al. 2014). While this binary approach is straightforward, it may prevent detecting nuanced developmental changes. Crucially, it could obscure important relationships between monitoring and control that only fine‐grained assessments can capture.

An alternative to analyzing experimenter‐elicited responses is an approach focusing on self‐generated responses. Unlike experimenter‐elicited responses, this approach does not rely on explicit metacognitive instructions or the child's ability to understand them. Therefore, it may provide a more sensitive measure of metacognitive monitoring. These self‐generated responses include behavioral indicators such as decision‐making speed (choice latencies), which reflect how much information children accessed when making decisions, whether based on memory (e.g., recognition tasks) or perception (e.g., perceptual discrimination tasks) (Koriat 1994; Leckey et al. 2020; Ratcliff and Starns 2013). Another indicator is post‐error slowing—when children naturally slow down after making an error—which may offer a sensitive indication of performance monitoring (Mayr 2004). Because these self‐generated measures are not constrained by a child's ability to use rating scales and provide continuous rather than binary data, they may capture the nuanced developmental changes and monitoring‐control relationships that binary measures miss (Coughlin et al. 2015; Koriat and Ackerman 2010; Roebers et al. 2019, 2020).

In the case of metacognitive control, research often finds evidence for experimenter‐elicited control emerging earlier than self‐generated control. For example, when specific instructions are provided, children as young as three can follow them, such as skipping items they are unsure about (Balcomb and Gerken 2008), withdrawing answers they are uncertain about to change their initial response (Hembacher and Ghetti 2014), or requesting cues when uncertain (Geurten and Bastin 2019). In these paradigms, children are explicitly offered instruction as to what to do, such as being told, “If you don't know the answer, you can skip the question” (Balcomb and Gerken 2008) or “If you're not sure about your answer, you can change it” (Hembacher and Ghetti 2014). These prompts demonstrate that children, around 40 months of age, deploy metacognitive control when guided, thereby reducing errors and improving performance.

However, when children are not guided with specific instructions, they face the challenge of generating their own performance‐optimizing approaches (Dufresne and Kobasigawa 1989; Lockl and Schneider 2002; O'Leary and Sloutsky 2017, 2019). For example, in a study where children aged five and seven were asked to choose between two numerosity discrimination tasks of varying difficulty, with accuracy being rewarded, even seven‐year‐olds struggled to adopt the optimal strategy of selecting the easier task to maximize accuracy and conserve cognitive effort. This was true despite children's ability to discern the differences in task difficulty (O'Leary and Sloutsky 2017, 2019). Therefore, the development of self‐generated metacognitive control strategies lags behind the ability to apply experimenter‐provided strategies. This finding aligns with observations in children's problem‐solving research. Children often struggle to generate and apply strategies independently, despite being able to successfully use strategies provided by others (Bulley and Schacter 2020; Winsler and Naglieri 2003). However, the exact timeline and transition into self‐generated strategies in both metacognitive control and problem‐solving contexts remain unclear.

Longitudinal Evidence on Metacognitive Development

Although much progress has been made in understanding metacognition and its development, the reported research addresses several limitations. First, most research on metacognitive development has used cross‐sectional designs; these designs, while important for detecting age differences, cannot directly assess within‐individual developmental changes or identify directional effects (e.g., Balcomb and Gerken 2008; Bazhydai et al. 2020; Goupil and Kouider 2016; Lyons and Ghetti 2011). In contrast to the existing cross‐sectional designs, our longitudinal design allows us to directly examine how metacognitive monitoring and control develop within individuals and interact with each other. Specifically, by analyzing cross‐lagged relations between monitoring and control, our longitudinal study provides a more precise understanding of the developmental trajectories of these two metacognitive components and how one may shape the emergence of the other.

Our longitudinal approach can, therefore, address the somewhat contradictory findings previously reported. For example, there is evidence that monitoring can influence control behaviors, as demonstrated when infants ask for help in response to uncertainty (Goupil et al. 2016; Koriat et al. 2006; Nelson and Narens 1990). Similarly, control behaviors can affect monitoring—for instance, when the time or effort required for a decision reflects uncertainty and informs monitoring processes (Koriat and Ackerman 2010). There is also evidence suggesting that these components may operate independently, as scaffolding one process does not necessarily improve the other (O'Leary and Sloutsky 2019).

And second, our methodology overcomes significant measurement limitations found in prior longitudinal research. The few extant longitudinal studies on metacognitive development in preschoolers have primarily relied on experimenter‐elicited responses (Gardier and Geurten 2024; Gonzales et al. 2022). While valuable, these approaches may fail to capture subtle developmental changes and cannot reveal children's capacity to spontaneously devise control strategies without explicit prompting. Our research strategically employs both experimenter‐elicited and self‐generated measures, creating a more comprehensive assessment framework for early metacognitive abilities. By analyzing response time patterns and applying reinforcement learning models to classify children's choice patterns, we can detect developmental shifts that might otherwise remain invisible when relying solely on experimenter‐elicited responses, particularly in preschool‐aged children whose metacognitive capabilities may exceed their ability to articulate these experiences (Roebers et al. 2020).

Present Study

The current study addresses crucial gaps in our understanding of metacognitive development using a longitudinal design, tracking children's performance from ages four to six (N = 148), with a separate group of adults (N = 26) serving as the benchmark of mature metacognition. We tracked children from age four to six because this age range is characterized by rapid changes in executive functions (Diamond 2013; Roebers 2017) and memory systems (Bauer 2015; Gardier and Geurten 2024; Schneider and Ornstein 2019), making it particularly important for understanding the emergence of metacognitive abilities. By combining self‐generated and experimenter‐elicited measures with individual‐level analysis, we aim to provide more direct evidence on the relationship between metacognitive monitoring and control development.

We investigated metacognitive development using a decision‐making task developed by O'Leary and Sloutsky (2017, 2019). In this task, participants first choose between two colored dots (e.g., blue vs. red) representing two types of games, with each color (unbeknownst to the participants) associated with either an easy or difficult numerosity discrimination task. After choosing the color (and hence task difficulty), participants perform the numerosity discrimination task without feedback.

Our approach combines both self‐generated and experimenter‐elicited responses to provide comprehensive measures of metacognitive abilities. Self‐generated responses include participants' task choices, numerical discrimination task response times (Task‐RTs), and post‐error choice response times (Choice‐RTs)—the time taken to choose a task following a discrimination error. We included an experimenter‐elicited measure to assess metacognitive monitoring, which entails asking several questions after task completion (e.g., “Was one game easier than the other?”).

For metacognitive monitoring, we examine two distinct aspects: task monitoring and performance monitoring. Task monitoring is measured through discrimination Task‐RTs, which reflect participants' sensitivity to task difficulty. Slower responses indicate more difficult discriminations, potentially due to slower evidence accumulation or the processing of more ambiguous evidence (Ratcliff and Starns 2013). Conversely, easier discriminations typically result in faster responses as clearer evidence facilitates quicker decisions.

Performance monitoring assesses participants' ability to evaluate their performance and detect errors. We examine this through Choice‐RTs, specifically analyzing whether participants take longer to select a task after making an incorrect response. While this differs from traditional post‐error slowing measures that examine changes in task RTs across consecutive trials (Danielmeier and Ullsperger 2011), the concept of strategic adaptation following errors is similarly applicable to our decision‐making paradigm. If participants effectively monitor their errors, they should consider adjusting their subsequent task difficulty choices, with these considerations reflected in their decision times (Yeung and Summerfield 2012). Based on previous developmental research (Ghetti et al. 2013; Roebers et al. 2019), we expected to observe age‐related improvements in both types of monitoring, though the precise developmental timeline remained uncertain.

We also collected experimenter‐elicited monitoring measures at the end of the experiment. Based on previous research, we expected that (1) younger children would overestimate their performance (Bjorklund 1997; O'Leary and Sloutsky 2017, 2019; Shin et al. 2007; Xia et al. 2024), and (2) self‐generated monitoring responses would be detected earlier in development than experimenter‐elicited verbal reports (Roebers et al. 2019, 2020).

For metacognitive control, we analyzed participants' task choices across trials and their trial‐to‐trial choice patterns using reinforcement learning models. This modeling approach allows us to distinguish between different factors driving children's choices, such as performance optimization (consistently choosing easy tasks) versus uncertainty‐driven exploration (systematically alternating between tasks). Unlike paradigms that provide guided strategies, our task requires children to develop their own strategies aligned with task goals. Previous studies suggest that even seven‐year‐olds struggle with formulating performance‐optimizing strategies (O'Leary and Sloutsky 2017, 2019). Therefore, we expected younger children to exhibit more exploratory behavior driven by uncertainty, which may require minimal metacognitive monitoring and control, relying instead on basic signals such as novelty and recency (Blanco and Sloutsky 2024). In contrast, we anticipated that older children and adults would demonstrate performance‐optimizing strategies by consistently selecting easier tasks (Blanco and Sloutsky 2024; Gopnik 2020; Meder et al. 2021).

Method

Participants

We recruited 148 four‐year‐old children (74 girls; demographic breakdown based on parents' reports: 106 White, 21 multiracial, 17 Black, 3 Asian, 1 Latino) for this longitudinal study. Children were recruited from preschools, schools, and childcare centers in middle‐class, ethnically diverse suburban neighborhoods of Columbus, Ohio, USA, based on permission forms signed and returned by parents.

Participants were tested annually at ages four (M = 4.23 years, SD = 0.23), five (M = 5.23 years, SD = 0.48), and six (M = 6.25 years, SD = 0.47). Recruitment occurred through a larger ongoing project at the Cognitive Development lab, the Ohio State University. The reported experiments were part of a broader longitudinal study. Additionally, 26 undergraduate students (13 women; M = 19.4 years, SD = 2) participated for course credit. Child data collection spanned from 2017 to 2019, while adult data were collected in the fall of 2022.

The study was approved by the Ohio State University IRB. Before data collection, informed consent and assent were obtained from all participants and their caregivers.

The sample size for children was determined based on a power analysis (Type I error rate α = 0.05, Type II error rate β = 0.20) using G*Power 3.1, which indicated a target sample size of 140 children to detect a medium age effect (Cohen's d = 0.5), accounting for 20% attrition. We initially recruited 148 children; however, we experienced higher‐than‐expected attrition across the longitudinal timespan. This attrition was not related to the current task but rather reflected logistical difficulties (such as scheduling) within the larger longitudinal project.

Our final sample consisted of 136 children. From them, 79 children completed all three time points (ages 4, 5, and 6), while 136 children (92% of our original sample) participated in at least two waves. Specifically, 116 children (78% of our original sample) participated in Waves 1–2 (ages 4–5), 112 children (76% of our original sample) in Waves 1 and 3 (ages 4 and 6), and 114 (77% of our original sample) children in Waves 2–3 (ages 5–6). Given that the analyses comparing children who completed all waves with those who completed fewer waves revealed no systematic differences in task performance or demographic characteristics, we used all available data for all analyses, including children who may have been missing a single time point (see our description below).

The sample size for adults was determined through a separate power analysis (Type I error rate α = 0.05, Type II error rate β = 0.20) using G*Power 3.1, which indicated that 26 participants would provide adequate power (0.80) to detect a medium‐sized effect (d = 0.5) in our planned comparisons between task types within adults (i.e., choice between easy vs. hard task) and between adults and 6‐year‐old children (i.e., different measures of metacognitive monitoring and control). The adult sample served primarily as a benchmark of mature metacognition and was not included in longitudinal analyses.

Materials

Stimuli were pairs of sets of same‐sized dots designed for a numerical discrimination task (see Figure 1). They were presented via Open Sesame software (Mathôt et al. 2012). We designed two difficulty levels (Halberda and Feigenson 2008): easy (1:2 dot ratio, e.g., 4:8, 5:10, 10:20) and difficult ratios (9:10 or smaller, e.g., 9:10, 10:11, 13:14).

Trial structure. Each trial began with the question, “which game would you like to play?” after they made a choice, participants saw a fixation dot (500 ms), followed by two sets of dots. After 500 ms, the sets of dots disappeared, and participants had up to 7 s to answer the question, “which box had more dots in it?” No feedback was provided. Task monitoring is measured through the Task RT difference between hard and easy tasks. Performance monitoring is assessed through post‐discrimination choice RT.

Design and Procedure

Children completed the task on a 17‐in. touchscreen, with trained experimenters reading instructions aloud. Children responded to the task by directly tapping on the touchscreen. Adults completed the task alone, reading the instructions themselves on a 14‐in. monitor and using a mouse to respond. All participants were run in a quiet testing room on the university campus.

Trial Structure

On each trial, participants choose between two colored dots (see Figure 1), with positions randomized to prevent positional bias. The core of our experimental design involved linking these colors to different levels of task difficulty in the numerosity discrimination task. The association between dot color and task difficulty was not disclosed to participants, requiring them to detect this relationship through task experience. For each participant, the color‐difficulty mapping was randomly assigned but remained consistent throughout their session.

Task Incentives

To motivate accurate performance, participants were informed that correct responses would earn points, while incorrect responses or responses exceeding the 7‐s time limit would result in point deductions. Although no immediate feedback was provided regarding point accumulation during individual trials, participants were told that their accumulated points would determine their reward of stickers upon task completion. However, to maintain ethical research practices, all children ultimately received an identical number of stickers, regardless of their actual performance.

Measuring Discrimination Performance

To measure participants' discrimination performance, each trial followed a structured sequence. After the participant's color choice, a fixation cross was displayed in the center of the screen for 500 ms to aid in focusing attention. Subsequently, the test stimulus appeared for 500 ms, comprising two sets of dots presented within gray boxes. The dots' positions were randomized to prevent pattern recognition or memorization, and their color matched the chosen task (red or blue). The ratio of the number of dots in the two sets reflected the difficulty level associated with each color—one task was an easy discrimination problem (i.e., very different numerosities—high discrimination ratios, 1:2 dot ratio) and the other was a hard discrimination problem (i.e., close numerosities—low discrimination ratios, 9:10 or higher dot ratios). Once the stimulus disappeared, leaving only the empty boxes, participants indicated which box contained more dots within a 7‐s time limit.

Measuring Metacognitive Monitoring

Our study incorporated both self‐generated and experimenter‐elicited measures of metacognitive monitoring. For self‐generated monitoring, we measured (1) Task monitoring: Response time differences between easy vs. hard numerosity discrimination tasks (Task‐RT), reflecting greater decision uncertainty in hard tasks (Ratcliff and Starns 2013); (2) Performance monitoring: Post‐discrimination task choice response times (Choice‐RT), where longer times choosing the task, following incorrect trials, indicate error detection without performance feedback (Yeung and Summerfield 2012).

For experimenter‐elicited monitoring, participants answered six multiple‐choice questions after completing all 30 trials (see Table S1). To prevent these questions from influencing subsequent metacognitive control decisions, they were administered only after task completion. The questions assessed overall performance estimation (Question 1), relative performance monitoring between easy and hard tasks (Questions 2–3) and task difficulty awareness (Questions 4–6) (Table 1).

Measuring Metacognitive Control

Metacognitive control was assessed through participants' task choices throughout the experiment. The proportion of easy versus difficult trials experienced by each participant was determined by their choices between the red and blue games. To analyze trial‐to‐trial dynamics in choice patterns (e.g., effort‐saving versus challenge‐seeking), we employed a computational modeling approach, detailed in the Data Analysis Approach section.

Data Analysis Approach

Our investigation into metacognitive development utilized Bayesian hierarchical models implemented via the “rstan” and “brms” package in R (Stan Development Team 2021) to maximize the informational value of our dataset. This approach allowed us to incorporate all available data points while appropriately modeling both within‐participant and between‐participant variability. Importantly, we did not employ listwise deletion in any of our analyses, with the single exception of the individual growth curve analysis discussed below. For all other analyses examining age‐related changes in metacognitive abilities, we included all participants with relevant data at each time point rather than restricting analyses to only those with complete three‐wave data. This includes our cross‐lagged panel analyses (which included 136 children with data in at least two time points) and group‐level models (all 148 children).

The only analysis that required complete three‐wave data was the individual growth curve analysis, which specifically examined whether each child's developmental trajectory was linear or non‐linear. For this particular analysis, the sample of 79 children who completed all three waves was sufficient, as our primary interest was in characterizing the qualitative pattern of development (linear vs. quadratic) rather than precisely estimating population parameters. This focus on pattern identification rather than parameter estimation mitigates concerns about sample size for this specific analysis; though appropriate caution must be exercised when interpreting this finding.

Our analytical framework was selected for several key reasons: (1) the ability to incorporate all available data without requiring complete cases or imputation, (2) appropriate modeling of nested data structures with observations clustered within participants, (3) explicit quantification of uncertainty in parameter estimates, and (4) the capacity to evaluate evidence for both the presence and absence of effects. Analysis scripts and data are available on OSF (https://osf.io/2z8nk/?view_only=bcd104c6b7e348cb810e4f23d35025e1).

We employed weakly informative priors (e.g., a normal distribution with mean 0 and standard deviation of 5, for regression coefficients, and a positive‐restricted Cauchy distribution with mean 0 and standard deviation 2.5, for variance parameters) that allow the data to drive our conclusions while providing regularization similar to that used in frequentist approaches (Pillonetto et al. 2022). Sensitivity analyses with alternative prior specifications (see Table S2) confirmed that our key findings were robust to reasonable variations in prior choices.

Following Kruschke (2021)'s guidelines, we report parameter estimates with 95% credible intervals (95% CI) and Bayes factors (BF10) for all analyses. 95% CI directly quantifies uncertainty in our estimates, appropriately widening when data are sparse. CIs that exclude zero indicate substantial evidence for the parameters being different from zero, which were described as “significant” in our report. For hypothesis testing and model comparison, we employed Bayes factors (BF; Gronau et al. 2020), which quantify the relative evidence for competing models. Unlike traditional p‐values, BFs provide a continuous measure of evidentiary strength for both alternative (BF > 1) and null hypotheses (BF < 1).

To understand participants' trial‐to‐trial choice patterns, we implemented reinforcement learning models (Sutton and Barto 1998) that classified response patterns into three categories: effort‐saving (consistently selecting easier tasks), systematic exploration (reducing uncertainty by selecting recently unchosen options), and challenge‐seeking (deliberately choosing harder tasks). Patterns not matching these categories were classified as random. To analyze longitudinal transitions in response patterns, we constructed transition matrices capturing how children's choice strategies evolved across consecutive time points. These matrices quantified the probability of shifting from one pattern to another (e.g., from systematic exploring at age four to effort saving at age five) or maintaining the same pattern across ages. We used Bayesian multinomial logistic regression to estimate these transition probabilities while controlling for individual differences. Model selection was performed using the Leave‐One‐Out Information Criterion (LOOIC; Vehtari et al. 2023).

Furthermore, we employed cross‐lagged panel models following Schuurman et al. (2016) to examine temporal relationships between monitoring and control abilities. These models incorporated data from all available time points while accounting for the nested structure of observations within participants. Our growth curve analyses examined individual developmental trajectories using linear, quadratic, and exponential models to identify the patterns best characterizing metacognitive development.

We conducted posterior predictive checks to evaluate model fit and performed comprehensive robustness checks across all analyses (See Table S3). For transparency, all parameter estimates, credible intervals, and Bayes factors are provided in Table S1.

Exploratory analyses examining links between task monitoring, performance monitoring, and metacognitive control were conducted using Bayesian multivariate analysis. We developed a mixture model to estimate the relative contributions of cognitive effort and uncertainty in participants' decision‐making processes. These exploratory models helped us better understand how the two components of metacognition interact and influence each other throughout development. Additional analyses also examined potential practice effects to distinguish between developmental changes and improvements due to task familiarity. In the main text, we include only the most relevant findings, but our more extensive exploratory analyses can be found in the Supporting Information.

Numerosity Discrimination Task Validity

We used Bayesian hierarchical logistic regression to examine whether children and adults performed better on easy tasks than on hard ones. We controlled for individual differences by assigning each participant a random intercept.

In children, there was a significant interaction effect between task types and age, β = 0.62, 95% CI = [0.52, 0.73], BF = 1.00 × 1010, with a larger accuracy difference between easy and hard tasks as age increased. The predicted probability for higher accuracy in easy than hard tasks was 0.60, 95% CI = [0.57, 0.62] at age four, and 0.83, 95% CI = [0.82, 0.85] at age six. Notably, children, on average across ages, showed significant above‐chance differences in discrimination accuracy between the easy and difficult tasks, BFs > 1.00 × 1010, supporting the validity of the task design. If children could deploy metacognitive monitoring and control, they should notice the task differences and opt for easy tasks.

In adults, the predicted probability of higher accuracy in easy than hard tasks was 0.96, 95% CI = [0.90, 0.96] 1 , BF = 2.99 × 105. Compared to children at age six, there is a significant interaction between age group and task types, β = 1.03, 95% CI = [0.22, 1.88], BF = 10.32, with adults showing greater accuracy differences between the tasks.

Development of Metacognitive Control

As discussed above, we used only self‐generated measures to measure metacognitive control. We first assessed the proportion of easy task choices using Bayesian hierarchical logistic regression models, with age and block (10‐trial) as predictors; controlling for individual differences.

In children, we did not find significant effects of age, block, or their interaction; BFs < 0.064, supporting the null hypothesis of no preference for easy or hard tasks. The average proportion of easy task choices in the last block was M 4yo = 0.51, SD4yo = 0.18, M 5yo = 0.51, SD5yo = 0.22, M 6yo = 0.57, SD6yo = 0.26. In contrast, adults predominantly chose the easy task to optimize their performance in the last block: M = 0.85, SD = 0.18.

Given the limited sensitivity of the proportion measures, we next employed computational modeling to identify decision‐making patterns reflecting age differences in metacognitive control.

Simple Reinforcement Learning Models

In our investigation into children's patterns of choices, we identified the best‐fitting single‐process reinforcement learning model for each participant (see Methods and Supporting Information).

As illustrated in Figure 2, our findings highlighted a distinct trend: most adults were fit best by the effort‐saving model, whereas children's responses across ages were best explained by a pattern of systematic exploration, where responses were generated to reduce the uncertainty of each game. Additionally, there was a discernible age‐related increase in the proportion of children fitting the effort‐saving model, especially between ages five and six. However, even at age six, the proportion of effort‐saving patterns was numerically smaller than in adults.

To analyze how children's patterns of choices changed longitudinally, we first focused on transitions in response patterns across ages, presented as transition matrices (see Figure 2, panels B1–B3). Importantly, these matrices suggest substantial individual variability in transitions. For analyzing these transitions, we used a Bayesian multinomial logistic regression model. In this model, we predicted children's response patterns at each time point using both their age and their pattern from the previous time point as predictors. This approach allowed us to estimate a transition probability matrix that quantified the likelihood of children shifting from one response pattern to another between consecutive time points (e.g., the probability of moving from a risk‐seeking to a risk‐averse strategy). The transition probabilities provide insights into the developmental trajectory of decision‐making patterns, revealing how children's patterns evolved across the three time points.

For the transition between ages four and five, the highest probability was staying in the systematic‐exploring pattern (M = 0.21, 95% CI = [0.15, 0.29]), significantly above the chance level of 0.0625. For those who did change their pattern of choices, the most frequent transition was a shift from systematic‐exploring to effort‐saving (M = 0.11, 95% CI = [0.07, 0.16]). From age five to six, the most probable transitions were to remain systematic‐exploring (M = 0.41, 95% CI = [0.34, 0.49]) or shift from systematic‐exploring to effort‐saving (M = 0.20, 95% CI = [0.14, 0.27]). Between ages four and six, children either transitioned from systematic‐exploring to effort‐saving (M = 0.20, 95% CI = [0.13, 0.27]) or maintained systematic‐exploring behavior (M = 0.17, 95% CI = [0.11, 0.24]). All transitions were statistically distinct from uniform expectations with 95% posterior confidence, BFs > 10 × 103. Our analysis revealed an age‐related increase in transitions to the effort‐saving pattern, indicating a gradual improvement in the ability to develop a performance‐optimizing pattern.

Development of Metacognitive Monitoring

Self‐Generated Responses

We compared participants' discrimination response times (Task‐RT) between easy and hard discrimination tasks to assess non‐verbal, self‐generated task monitoring (how children tracked task difficulty), and their task choice response times (Choice‐RT) after incorrect and correct trials to assess non‐verbal, self‐generated performance monitoring (how children tracked their own accuracy).

We log‐transformed and z‐score normalized participants' RTs across age groups. To ensure data quality, we implemented two levels of RT filtering. At the trial level, we excluded individual trials where RT deviated more than 2.5 standard deviations from the participant's trial mean RT. At the participant level, we excluded participants who had more than 30% of their trials flagged by this criterion (five 4‐year‐olds, three 5‐year‐olds, and two 6‐year‐olds).

Experimenter‐Elicited Responses

Experimenter‐elicited monitoring was assessed by analyzing participants' responses to questions presented at the end of the task.

Performance Monitoring

To assess absolute performance monitoring (Questions 1–3 in Table S1), we compared participants' performance estimates to their actual performance by calculating the absolute difference between them. Adults underestimated their performance (β = −0.34, 95% CI = [−0.40, −0.29], BF = 3.10 × 107), while children overestimated theirs at age four and five (β 4yo = 0.25, 95% CI = [0.23, 0.28], BF = 8.85 × 1017; β 5yo = 0.25, 95% CI = [0.22, 0.28], BF = 2.76 × 1016), showing accurate estimation at age six (β 6yo = 0.04, 95% CI = [0.01, 0.07], BF = 0.104). No significant improvement was seen between ages four and five (β 5–4yo = −0.005, 95% CI = [−0.05, 0.04]), but six‐year‐olds showed higher accuracy compared to younger children and adults (β 6–5yo = −0.17, 95% CI = [−0.20, −0.14]; β 6yo–Adults = −0.25, 95% CI = [−0.29, −0.20]).

For relative performance monitoring, which reflects whether participants report relative performance correctly (i.e., reporting better performance in the easy tasks than hard tasks), only 24% of 4‐year‐olds, 26% of 5‐year‐olds, and 37% of 6‐year‐olds accurately estimated their relative performance; compared to 77% of adults.

Task Monitoring

Next, we examined task monitoring (Questions 4–6 in Table S1), which reflected whether participants knew one type of task was easier. A composite score of all three questions showed children performed at chance level (M = 1.74 out of 3, SD = 0.92, BF < 0.001); while adults scored significantly higher than children and the chance level (M = 2.57, SD = 0.44; β = 0.85, 95% CI = [0.41, 1.27], BF = 2.68).

Relations Between Experimenter‐Elicited and Self‐Generated Responses

Lastly, we employed multivariate Bayesian linear regression models to explore the relationships between experimenter‐elicited and self‐generated measures. In children, the model revealed a significant correlation between experimenter‐elicited performance monitoring and self‐generated performance monitoring (measured through choice RT) (r = 0.19, 95% CI = [0.05, 0.33], BF = 5.79); whereas the null hypothesis of no correlation was favored for the other relationships with BFs < 0.35. In adults, a significant correlation was found between experimenter‐elicited relative performance monitoring and experimenter‐elicited task monitoring (r = 0.38, 95% CI = [0.02, 0.68], BF = 3.97), while other relationships remained non‐significant with BFs ranging from 0.51 to 2.22.

Longitudinal Analysis: Relationship Between Metacognitive Monitoring and Control

Bayesian Cross‐Lagged Panel Analysis

We examined the longitudinal relations between metacognitive monitoring and control using a multivariate Bayesian model (see Table S1 for complete model specifications). Our analysis focused specifically on cross‐lagged relations between these processes across development. Through Bayesian model selection using Leave‐One‐Out Cross‐Validation (Vehtari et al. 2023), we found strong evidence that early monitoring abilities (measured through Task RT and Choice RT coefficients) predicted later control behaviors (as classified by our reinforcement learning models). Notably, the reverse relationship was not supporte; earlier control did not predict later monitoring. For interpretive clarity, we inverted the monitoring coefficients so that larger values indicate better monitoring performance. The “effort‐based control” category in our analysis combined both effort‐saving and challenge‐seeking patterns. This unidirectional monitoring‐to‐control model received substantially stronger evidence (Bayes Factors > 3.89 × 10262) compared to alternative models, including bidirectional influence models, control‐to‐monitoring models, and models proposing independence between these processes.

In addition, significant auto‐associative relations (e.g., control performance at earlier time points predicting control performance at later time points) and within‐time correlations (e.g., monitoring performance at earlier time points predicting control performance at later time points) were supported by BMS (BFs > 2.04 × 10155), indicating strong evidence for these links. These results suggest that is a strong within‐subject reliability of these measures.

In Figure 4, we present parameter estimates from the full model, which represent the most conservative estimates of cross‐lagged relationships. Among the statistically significant relations (indicated by solid arrows), task monitoring at earlier time points negatively predicted effort‐based control at later time points (β = −0.36, 95% CI = [−0.65, −0.08]), suggesting that children with stronger task monitoring were less likely to adopt effort‐based control strategies. Conversely, systematic exploring behavior at earlier time points positively predicted performance monitoring at later time points (β = 0.25, 95% CI = [0.001, 0.51]), indicating that children who engaged in systematic exploration subsequently showed enhanced performance monitoring abilities. These bidirectional pathways reveal a complex interplay between monitoring and control processes during early development. Nevertheless, model comparison statistics (BF = 3.89 × 10262) provide overwhelming evidence favoring the monitoring‐to‐control directional hypothesis over models testing control‐to‐monitoring pathways, or models with no or both directional relationships.

We also performed a cross‐lagged panel analysis to examine the relation between experimenter‐elicited monitoring (e.g., responses to monitoring questions) and self‐generated monitoring (e.g., Task RT and Choice RT). BMS provided only moderate evidence (BFs ranging from 3 to 10) that self‐generated monitoring at earlier time points predicted experimenter‐elicited monitoring at later times. No significant relations were found in the best‐fitting models; indicating limited support for the idea that self‐generated monitoring guides experimenter‐elicited monitoring over time.

Individual Growth Curve Analysis

To assess whether metacognitive monitoring and control follow a linear trajectory, individual growth curve analyses were conducted across three time points. Three variables were modeled: (a) task monitoring (effect of task type on RT), (b) performance monitoring (effect of previous trial accuracy on post‐discrimination choice RT), and (c) metacognitive control (the φ parameter from the reinforcement learning model). In our reinforcement learning models, the φ parameter represents the extent that choices are driven by reducing uncertainty about task difficulty (values near 1) versus maximizing performance (values near 0). See the Supporting Information for further details.

Each variable was analyzed using three growth models—linear, quadratic, and exponential—to determine the developmental trajectory. The quadratic model, which allows for non‐linear, non‐monotonic changes by incorporating a squared term for time, best fitted the data for 72 out of 79 children across all variables, supported by BMS. Importantly, 47 children's changes were non‐monotonic, having the vertex at age 5. This suggests that the development of metacognitive monitoring and control follows a parabolic trajectory with periods of acceleration and deceleration, indicating that improvements are non‐linear and dynamic over time.

Repeated Exposure Effect

To evaluate whether performance improvements were driven by task familiarity rather than development, we conducted two complementary analyses. First, the Individual growth curve analysis reported above revealed that over 92% of children showed quadratic (non‐linear) developmental trajectories, with over 60% displaying non‐monotonic changes in performance. This pattern of acceleration and deceleration is inconsistent with simple practice effects, which typically show linear and monotonic improvements. Second, we conducted a Bayesian hierarchical linear regression analyzing the effect of repeated task exposure while controlling for age and individual differences. Among the 6‐year‐olds, 79 children completed all three sessions, 36 completed only two sessions, and 4 completed only one session. The results showed no significant effect of repeated exposure on either accuracy (β = 0.14, 95% CI = [−0.07, 0.34], BF = 0.24) or choice patterns (φ: closer to 1 indicates systematic‐exploring, closer to 0 indicates effort‐saving; β = 0.06, 95% CI = [−0.23, 0.38], BF = 0.16). In contrast, age significantly predicted both accuracy (β = 0.33, 95% CI = [0.13, 0.53], BF = 23.44) and choice patterns (β = −0.31, 95% CI = [−0.61, −0.02], BF = 1.32). While the uneven distribution of participants across sessions (with most children completing all three sessions) limits our ability to definitively rule out practice effects, the combination of non‐monotonic developmental trajectories and null effects of repeated exposure strongly suggests that improvements are driven by developmental progression rather than task familiarity.

Discussion

The present longitudinal study examined the developmental trajectory of metacognitive abilities in children ages four to six, employing a combination of simple decision‐making and a numerical discrimination task, and compared the performance of children with that of adults. On each trial, participants chose between two color‐coded numerosity discrimination tasks, with one being more difficult than the other. This task design allowed participants to learn through experience that one task is easier and thus has the potential to more consistently result in better performance and greater rewards. Participants were incentivized to optimize their accuracy, encouraging them to choose the easier task more frequently. This decision‐making process relied on two core components of metacognition: monitoring and control.

We assessed monitoring through both experimenter‐elicited and self‐generated responses, focusing on two key aspects: task monitoring and performance monitoring. Task monitoring captured participants' awareness of the differences in difficulty between the tasks, measured through (self‐generated) response times and (experimenter‐elicited) task‐specific questions. Performance monitoring, on the other hand, evaluated participants' awareness of their own performance, using (self‐generated) post‐discrimination choice reaction times—where participants took longer to respond after giving incorrect answers—and (experimenter‐elicited) performance‐oriented questions. To evaluate metacognitive control, we applied computational reinforcement learning models that classified participants into three choice patterns based on their task choices: uncertainty‐based exploration, effort‐saving, and challenge‐seeking.

Our findings indicated a gradual development of both task and performance monitoring abilities. Notably, four‐year‐olds did not spend more time on harder tasks; nor were they slowing down after making errors, behaviors that suggest immature monitoring skills. The ability for task monitoring began to emerge around age five, as children began to respond more slowly to difficult problems, reflecting improved task monitoring. However, they only displayed longer post‐discrimination choice response times around age six, indicating limited post‐error adjustment, a strategic adaptation that also requires control. It is possible that five‐year‐olds could detect errors made in our task but lacked the necessary control skills to strategically adjust their decisions after mistakes; therefore, failing to slow down. By age six, children's monitoring abilities show improvement, although they remain significantly less pronounced than those of adults.

Interestingly, children did not demonstrate effective monitoring skills in their experimenter‐elicited responses, except for a gradual age‐related decrease in overconfidence as they grew older. The lag between self‐generated and experimenter‐elicited monitoring aligns with previous findings on the developmental lag in explicit metacognitive awareness (Ghetti et al. 2013; Koriat 1994; Mellor and Moore 2014). Self‐generated responses, such as response times, likely rely on more implicit processes, while experimenter‐elicited monitoring—especially the ability to map one's task performance to a scale or express it clearly—may require greater explicit metacognitive awareness, which tends to develop later and is reinforced by formal education (Koriat 1994; Mellor and Moore 2014; Schneider 1998). We found a positive correlation between experimenter‐elicited and self‐generated responses in performance monitoring, suggesting a common underlying mechanism, despite their different developmental trajectories. Children's ongoing overconfidence in their verbal reports might be linked to adaptive cognitive biases such as wishful thinking, which has been shown to be advantageous in early development (Bernard et al. 2016; Bjorklund 1997; Shin et al. 2007; Xia et al. 2024).

Our computational analyses provided further insights into the development of metacognitive control. Unlike adults, who primarily showed effort‐saving choice patterns—considered the optimal method for maximizing task performance and minimizing effort—children aged four and five exhibited a range of choice patterns. These patterns included systematic‐exploring, effort‐saving, and challenge‐seeking, with most children favoring systematic‐exploring, which involves choosing tasks based on how long a particular task has gone unchosen. A consistent shift toward effort‐saving patterns did not occur until around age six, reflecting a developmental progression in how children approached controlling their task choices in a task‐optimized manner.

These findings align with previous research on decision‐making development (Blanco and Sloutsky 2024), suggesting that children's systematic exploration may arise from a need to reduce uncertainty rather than from a lack of awareness regarding task difficulty. Children may monitor task difficulty or their own performance, even without fully understanding how to optimize their choices. Additionally, an uncertainty‐based choice pattern may not require fully developed metacognitive control. Behaviors such as novelty‐seeking or recency effects (Blanco and Sloutsky 2024) might correlate with uncertainty, potentially explaining the exploratory tendencies seen in younger children.

Children's systematic exploring pattern could be attributed to a slower learning rate that necessitates more trials to distinguish between tasks. However, research by O'Leary and Sloutsky (2019) demonstrates that when children receive explicit instructions to choose the easier task for maximizing correct responses—without being told which task is easier—they successfully increase their selection of the less demanding option. This suggests that children's failure to devise and adopt effort‐saving strategies stems from underdeveloped strategic thinking rather than an inability to perceive differences in task difficulty.

The observed developmental shift from uncertainty‐based exploration to effort‐saving patterns around age six aligns with the maturation of executive functions. Younger children, aged four and five, may have lower working memory capacity and less inhibitory control, which are crucial for formulating and executing strategies that optimize performance. This finding is consistent with previous research, such as the study by Baer and Odic (2019), which demonstrated that five‐year‐olds were more likely to choose the easier task when the difficulty levels of the options were visually apparent. In their study, no working memory was needed to associate difficulty levels with choice options, unlike the current study, which imposed greater demands on working memory, as the task difficulty was represented symbolically (i.e., by the dot color). The delay in adopting a performance‐optimal strategy may reflect the heightened demands on two executive functions: working memory, which maintains task goals; and inhibitory control, which suppresses exploratory impulses (Diamond 2002; Gonzales et al. 2022). As children's executive functions develop, they become better equipped to formulate and use strategies that optimize their performance, such as the effort‐saving approach observed in older children and adults.

Longitudinal Insights

Consistent with the overlapping wave theory, which characterizes cognitive development as a dynamic process marked by variability, diversity, and adaptability (Siegler 1996), our study revealed fluctuating patterns in metacognitive monitoring and control. Children's developmental trajectories for these abilities followed quadratic growth curves, underscoring non‐linear changes. At the group level, we observed a general transition from systematic exploration to effort‐saving response patterns. However, when examining individual children, this transition was not monotonic; some of them showed a reversed transition at later time points. This variability reflects the inherent complexity of cognitive development. Our findings suggest that children at any given age show a range of response patterns, likely influenced by the different maturation rates of cognitive abilities. As children develop, they become increasingly likely to adopt task‐optimized response patterns.

To explore the interaction between metacognitive monitoring and control over time, we employed a multivariate Bayesian model to examine cross‐lagged relations. Our analysis revealed two findings: earlier monitoring abilities significantly predicted later control behaviors; this model outperformed those proposing either reverse relations or independence between components. This finding supports the prevailing theory that effective metacognitive monitoring is a necessary precursor to control behaviors (Nelson and Narens 1990). The direction of this developmental relation is particularly important in the context of our task, where participants could only make metacognitively controlled decisions after recognizing differences in task difficulty through monitoring. This finding highlights the critical role of monitoring in shaping children's ability to devise and execute strategies.

Interestingly, our findings diverge from previous research that attempted to scaffold monitoring or control in isolation and found no cross‐domain improvements (O'Leary and Sloutsky 2019). While these earlier studies suggested that monitoring and control operate independently, our results paint a different picture: the natural development of monitoring appears to create a foundation for enhanced control capabilities at a later age. This distinction suggests that naturally developed monitoring skills become more thoroughly integrated with control behaviors over time, unlike the connections created through isolated scaffolding interventions. It is also worth noting that previous studies relied exclusively on experimenter‐elicited measures, whereas our self‐generated measures may be more sensitive in detecting these relationships.

Limitations and Future Directions

As the current study included a limited number of sessions, it may be sufficient to detect nonlinear developmental changes while not fully capturing the complexity of developmental trajectories. Additionally, the longitudinal models may have lacked the power to detect subtle changes and interactions between variables. For instance, we could not determine the directionality of certain relations, such as how task/performance monitoring relates to systematic exploring response patterns.

Additionally, although effective for assessing metacognition, the two‐choice decision‐making paradigm employed in this study may not fully capture the complexities of children's decision‐making processes. Children's general decision‐making tendencies, such as a preference for exploration over exploitation, could have influenced the outcomes. For instance, some children may switch between tasks even when they recognize one is easier. Future studies incorporating a wider range of choices could offer more nuanced insights into whether children's exploration is systematic or more random.

Conclusion

Our longitudinal investigation illuminates the developmental sequence of metacognitive abilities from ages four to six. We found that task monitoring appears first at age five, followed by performance monitoring and the ability to implement performance‐optimizing control at age six. However, children's implicit metacognitive capabilities consistently outpace their ability to articulate these processes through experimenter‐elicited verbal measures.

Our longitudinal analyses revealed that early monitoring abilities serve as a foundation for later control strategies, with stronger early monitoring predicting more sophisticated control behaviors over time. This finding supports theoretical models proposing that metacognitive monitoring is a prerequisite for developing effective metacognitive control. These insights into the dynamic relationship between monitoring and control during early childhood provide theoretical understanding and an empirical framework for future research investigating the mechanisms underlying metacognitive development.
