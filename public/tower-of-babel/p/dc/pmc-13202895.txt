From bench to bedside: relevant animal models across the migraine attack phases
Authors: Georgii Krivoshein, Mignon van der Watt, Teresa Jordà-Baleri, Katrin Põld, Viktor Gkotzamanis, Arn M.J.M. van den Maagdenberg, Eloisa Rubio-Beltran, Philip R. Holland
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Migraine is a debilitating brain disorder for which there remains a considerable therapeutic gap that needs to be addressed in order to develop novel effective treatment options for patients. Preclinical translational models have contributed significantly to migraine research by improving our understanding of migraine pathophysiology as well as identifying and validating new drug targets. In this review, we will summarise key animal models and discuss how commonly used experimental readouts used in these models can be integrated for the investigation of the various phases of a migraine attack. A major focus is to highlight the translational potential of the experimental models and how they can help bridge the gap between preclinical research and clinical application and how increased understanding of fundamental disease mechanisms can form the basis of improved migraine treatments. Providing such a resource may inform the broader migraine research community on which model and readout they might consider when studying a specific phase of the migraine attack, when evaluating the efficacy of new possible drugs and innovative therapeutic strategies.

Introduction

Migraine is a highly dynamic and disabling neurovascular disorder affecting over one billion people worldwide with an estimated global prevalence of 15–20% [1, 2]. It is a sensory processing disorder with complex, heterogeneous physiological and symptomatic features that occur in attacks [3]. Attacks are characterized by recurrent episodes of severe typically unilateral headache that can be preceded by an array of symptoms, including aura in up to one-third of cases. This range of diverse symptoms includes nausea, and multisensory hypersensitivity to, for instance, touch (allodynia), light (photophobia), and sound (phonophobia) [4]. The dynamic features and symptoms of a migraine attack are commonly separated into four phases: the premonitory phase, the aura phase, the headache phase, and the postdrome phase [5]. Despite clear advances in understanding the neurobiological mechanisms of migraine-related head pain, unanswered questions remain, resulting in substantial therapeutic gaps. Among the clinically relevant issues not yet resolved are the knowledge gaps in understanding why attacks start and stop, as well as why approximately 30–40% of individuals with migraine do not benefit from current medications. Together, these challenges underscore the need for increased research and novel therapeutic strategies [6, 7]. In the present review, we focus on the knowledge gained from, and the future potential of animal models to inform our understanding of the various phases of the migraine attack. Focusing on the underlying physiological mechanism explored at the bench, we have where possible attempted to align these within relevant clinically expressed attack phases, however, we acknowledge that this phase-specific association is not always direct. In doing so, we seek to highlight their potential utility to explore novel phase-dependent therapeutic strategies.

Experimental animal models of human disorders have been instrumental in gaining insights into the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms, but undisputedly translating that knowledge into therapeutic advances that benefit patients has been limited. For example, decades of research on animal models of stroke yielded hundreds of candidate neuroprotective drugs, many of which were thoroughly tested in animals, but failed to reach the clinic [8]. A key reason for this apparent failure comes from an improved understanding of fundamental species differences in brain composition, with rodents not only having significantly more grey matter as compared to humans that have ~ 50% white matter [9], but also exhibiting a more complex brain structure, and being more vulnerable to reduced blood flow [10]. In this respect, preclinical animal models of migraine [11] have done remarkably better and have served as valuable tools for both investigating the underlying disease mechanisms and evaluating potential therapies. Notably, preclinical models of migraine have yielded pivotal insight into successfully translated migraine-specific drugs, foremost those that target specific serotonin receptors (i.e., the 5-HT1B/D and 5-HT1F receptor agonist triptans and ditan drugs) and calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) or its receptor (i.e., small molecule antagonists and monoclonal antibodies) [12–17], and played a significant role in their approval for the acute or preventive treatment of migraine [18–20].

Despite obvious benefits to advancing migraine research, experimental animal models also have shortcomings that need to be considered [11, 21, 22]. Importantly, no single animal model fully captures the multifaceted complex nature of migraine. Hence, in preclinical research, a reductionist approach is often used that involves dissecting the complex biological mechanisms and investigating them in isolation, with the aim of providing valuable mechanistic information. When adopting this approach, it is critical to select a relevant preclinical model that successfully recapitulates a key disease-relevant clinical phenomenon that is notoriously difficult to study in humans (e.g., cortical spreading depolarization (CSD), trigeminovascular system (TVS) activation, or altered sensory processing networks). Thus, it is essential to grasp the strengths and weaknesses of existing experimental models and readouts while integrating advanced genetic approaches and emerging neurotechnologies that will help bridge the gap between bench and bedside, facilitating the discovery of more targeted and effective therapies.

In this review, we present a comprehensive overview of the current translational relevance and design considerations of the most used experimental animal models. When considering specific readouts, we further attempted to associate these with specific phases of the migraine attack, highlighting key developments and remaining challenges. In doing so, we intend to support the research community to further refine migraine modelling approaches.

Premonitory phase: models of non-pain symptoms

Clinical relevance

Migraine is far more complex than an isolated headache as it involves progressive sensory and homeostatic dysregulation, as evidenced by non-pain (premonitory) symptoms, which clinically can present hours to days before headache onset [23, 24]. These earliest symptoms, commonly including fatigue, mood changes and stiff neck, are often reported among the most bothersome aspects of a migraine attack [23, 25]. The array of symptoms reflects early dysfunction within hypothalamic, brainstem, limbic, and cortical networks, as demonstrated by patient-reported data and advanced neuroimaging studies [26–28]. The clinical relevance of premonitory-focused preclinical models lies in the ability to address the earliest phase of a migraine attack, thus providing unique opportunities for early therapeutic intervention. Recent clinical data suggests that early intervention may be beneficial during the premonitory phase as early treatment with the CGRP receptor antagonist ubrogepant can reduce both attack likelihood and severity, while further ameliorating some non-pain symptoms [29], though the extent of this benefit remains an area of active investigation [28]. Hence, advancing premonitory-targeted therapies could result in a paradigm shift in migraine management towards early interruption of attack progression [30, 31].

Preclinical models

Of the specific phases of a migraine attack, the premonitory phase is perhaps the most complex to model considering the subjective nature of the human experience and the lack of an objective biomarker for reverse translation (Fig. 1). Clinical research has yielded various compounds, among which nitroglycerin (NTG), CGRP, and pituitary adenylate cyclase-activating polypeptide (PACAP), that can reliably trigger a migraine attack in individuals with migraine, but not in healthy controls [26, 30–32]. Foremost, NTG, a nitric oxide donor, is one of the most potent clinical triggering agents, which has provided insights into the activation of specific brain regions during different phases of the migraine attack, including the premonitory phase [26, 30]. In comparison, CGRP, a neuromodulator that is deemed to play a central role in the generation of migraine headache and PACAP, an emerging therapeutic target [33], have both been shown to trigger delayed migraine-like headache in individuals with migraine [31, 32], albeit at lower rates when compared to NTG. Notably, levels of CGRP and PACAP are increased in migraine attacks [34–36], although the results are inconsistent between studies and the measurements themselves have been challenged due to methodological issues [37]. Clinically, NTG and PACAP administration have been demonstrated to successfully reproduce key features of the premonitory phase, including fatigue, yawning, mood swings, allodynia (sensory hypersensitivity), photophobia (light aversion) and fatigue in migraine [26, 30, 38–40], occurring over a more condensed premonitory phase in the hours between triggering and headache onset. Given their ability to trigger migraine attacks in humans, these compounds have provided translational opportunities for developing relevant preclinical models to study the mechanisms of action underlying migraine pathophysiology and to reveal novel therapeutic targets. Clinically relevant traits, including sensory hypersensitivity to touch (allodynia) and light (photophobia) can be modelled in rodents following administration of several of the triggering agents, including NTG, CGRP, and PACAP [41–44]. However, these readouts are not explicitly associated with the premonitory phase as allodynia, for example, can also be present in other phases of the migraine attack (see below), and even can occur interictally in some individuals with migraine. Despite the ability of NTG and PACAP to induce premonitory-like symptoms [26, 39, 40, 45], there remains very little preclinical research exploring potential early non-pain symptoms.

Considering the complexity of the premonitory symptoms, it is likely that preclinical approaches focusing on the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms may herald informative translational knowledge. For example, fatigue is one of the most commonly reported premonitory symptoms [23]. However the mechanisms that lead to the subjective experience of fatigue are complex and multifaceted [46], since they include sleep disruption, arousal, inflammation and, in the context of central fatigue, specific neural circuits and complex interplay of neurotransmitter pathways, resulting in a reduced ability to activate and maintain mental effort [47]. Thus, preclinical models exploring fatigue in migraine should focus on different potential contributing factors to assess the impact of altered brain circuits, including arousal promoting networks, making use of modern neurotechnologies that provide the ability to modulate neurons in a type- and region-specific manner [48], the impact of inflammation, and how activation of peripheral sensory pathways may contribute to fatigue-like phenotypes. As such, while preclinical modelling of premonitory-like symptoms remains in its infancy, the clinical importance of targeting these earliest identifiable biological symptoms underlines the potential translational benefits.

Experimental readouts

Animals, not unlike humans, change their behavioural responses based on internal and external environmental changes, and when subjected to pain or discomfort. These behavioural responses to experimental modulation provide insight not only into migraine pathophysiology, but also treatment efficacy. Experimental readouts for the premonitory phase can be categorised as sensory-discriminative, affective-emotional and cognitive aspects of migraine symptomology. The most used assays in a migraine context investigate sensory-discriminative behaviour. Here, we will only briefly discuss some key readouts of premonitory symptoms and behavioural measures in animal models of migraine, as they have been extensively discussed in previous reviews [49, 50].

Anxiety is a recognised premonitory symptom [23] and a feature that is readily assessable in preclinical models. Most commonly, anxiety-like behaviour is assessed using open field and elevated plus maze assays. Both assays rely on the conflict of the natural behaviour of a rodent, that is the innate drive to explore novel environments against their natural fear of open, brightly-lit spaces and, in the case of the elevated plus maze, elevated, brightly-lit spaces [51, 52]. Less anxious animals will explore the open spaces more freely, while more anxious animals exhibit thigmotaxis (wall seeking behaviour) or remain in the enclosed arms. In migraine research, the open-field and elevated-plus maze assays have demonstrated that NTG induces anxiety-like behaviour in rodents that could, for instance, be attenuated by orexin A, a hypothalamic neuropeptide responsible for modulating homeostasis [53–56].

The light-dark assay is another classical way to assess anxiety-like behaviour, again based on the innate aversion of rodents to brightly-lit spaces, although the assay has been more commonly adopted to model light-aversion (photophobia), where time spent in the light and dark zones, the number of transitions between zones, the latency to enter the dark zone, as well as rearing, are quantified readouts. The utility of the light-dark assay in migraine is evidenced by several studies demonstrating that CGRP and PACAP all induce light aversion in rodents [42, 43, 57]. Notably, behaviour in the light-dark assay was show to be attenuated by anti-migraine therapeutics, including sumatriptan and CGRP monoclonal antibodies [43, 58]. In contrast, NTG produces variable results, with chronic administration being more effective than acute administration at inducing light aversion [53, 59]. Even though in recent years the light-dark assay has been further refined to assess anxiety measures, it is advised to also use light-independent measures of anxiety, such as the open field assay [60].

As noted, one of the most commonly reported premonitory symptoms is fatigue [23], which is a nuanced phenotype as it has potential physical, cognitive, and emotional components. Foremost, locomotion is used as a readout of physical fatigue [61, 62]. However, other assays, including home cage monitoring, have been proposed as proxies of fatigue-like behaviours [63]. Notably, novel object recognition (to assess learning and memory) or novel object introduction (to assess motivation) hold promise for the exploration of fatigue-like phenotypes [64], relying on the innate drive of rodents to explore novel environments. Future research should carefully consider the nuanced nature of fatigue and set out to carefully characterise the utility of fatigue-related readouts in migraine.

Translational value and design considerations

Recent clinical studies highlight the potential importance of early intervention during the premonitory phase of a migraine attack [29], supporting earlier considerations to treat early when the pain is still mild [65]. Therefore, it stands to reason that a greater understanding of the underlying biology of the earliest attack symptoms could herald novel approaches to tackle migraine. The use of clinically relevant chemical migraine triggers, especially those known to trigger premonitory-like symptoms in individuals with migraine offers an opportunity to explore the earliest alterations. However, such agents are often used at supramaximal doses and this may inadvertently shorten the premonitory-like phase, blurring the boundary between modelling premonitory and headache-relevant symptoms. While not fully studied, recent “priming” models offer a potential to explore responses to normally sub-threshold doses, combined with an induced hyper-responsiveness that mimics the selective vulnerability observed in individuals with migraine as compared to healthy subjects [66]. Two key considerations need to be highlighted regarding the behavioural assays. Firstly, some symptoms, such as photophobia and fatigue are subjective and can only be approximated by observing behavioural changes in the animal. Other migraine-relevant symptoms, such as nausea and vomiting, are difficult to model in rodents as rodents do not have vomit reflex [67]. Secondly, the behavioural tests described in this review represent surrogate endpoints rather than migraine-specific outcomes and would need to be trialled in humans to reveal their translational relevance. However, a clear advantage of preclinical models of premonitory-like symptoms is their capability to shed light on the underlying biology of these symptoms. Their greatest potential lies here, particularly when informed by clinical studies, as they help determine the critical role of hypothalamic circuits during the earliest phases of a migraine attack.

Aura phase: models of cortical excitability and migraine aura

Clinical relevance

The aura phase of a migraine attack is considered to be the consequence of a neurophysiological phenomenon called CSD [68]. CSD represents a slowly self-propagating depolarizing wave of neuronal and glial cells, followed by a transient suppression of neuronal activity [69]. CSD has also been implicated in various other neurological conditions, including stroke, subarachnoid haemorrhage, and traumatic brain injury [70–73]. The disturbance of transmembrane ion homeostasis is considered a central event in the initiation of CSD that subsequently leads to the release of various neurotransmitters and neuropeptides [74]. Preclinical models of migraine aura have substantially advanced our understanding of CSD. In particular, studies in rats have shown activation of meningeal trigeminal afferents following CSD [75, 76]. While more recent approaches have identified that optogenetic-induced CSD events evoked migraine-like pain and anxiety behaviours in freely behaving mice [77]. Furthermore, research in mice revealed that CSD can induce significant alterations in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) proteome [78]. Notably, the authors showed that CSF obtained from CSD-exposed mice could directly sensitise trigeminal neurons from CSD-naïve mice, an effect that was partially attributed to the presence of CGRP.

CSD is additionally associated with dynamic changes in cerebral blood flow [79, 80]. Both animal and human studies have identified transient periods of vasoconstriction and vasodilation following CSD, likely reflecting suppressed neuronal activity secondary to ionic imbalance, increased metabolic demand during the recovery phase, and neurovascular uncoupling [81, 82]. However, the precise impact of these vascular and perfusion alterations remains to be determined. Taken together, the findings suggest that CSD may constitute a critical mechanistic link bridging the aura phase (and perhaps the premonitory phase) of a migraine attack with its subsequent full clinical manifestation in migraine with aura [83].

Given the role of CSD in migraine aura, there are ongoing explorations as to what extent CSD may represent a viable therapeutic target, most of them focussing on antagonising the N-Methyl-D-Aspartate receptor (NMDAR) [84, 85] even though formal evidence for efficacy still has to be obtained [86]. Hence, while the exact mechanisms underlying the efficacy of anti-CSD drugs are not fully elucidated, their influence on the generation or propagation of CSD in preclinical models is not questioned. Much of the research on preclinical models of migraine aura have focussed on testing the efficacy of anti-epileptic agents [86, 87], aiming to modulate ion channel activity that is central to the neuronal hyperexcitability that underlies CSD. Specifically, lacosamide, an anti-epileptic drug not traditionally prescribed for migraine, was shown to reduce the frequency of CSD events in rodents [87]. Also, propranolol has been reported to modulate CSD-associated cerebral blood flow alterations [88]. Finally, recent findings indicate that onabotulinumtoxinA may attenuate CSD-induced macrophage activation within the meninges [89]. Taking into consideration the clinical observation that acute treatment tends to be more effective during the premonitory and aura phase, in both of which CSD may play a role, at least in those individuals that experience an aura, it is conceivable that targeting the disease mechanisms of the migraine aura constitute a promising strategy for migraine management.

Preclinical models

Over the last decades, various preclinical animal models have been developed to investigate CSD events (reviewed in [11]). Experimental CSD models have proven invaluable for advancing our understanding of how CSD events can be evoked and propagate through brain tissue (Fig. 2). In vitro approaches, such as brain slices, isolated retina, and even resected human tissue, allow for good control over the experimental conditions. While slices lack full network integration, they have been vital for probing cellular processes in areas that are inaccessible in vivo [90]. In vivo models of CSD, despite technical challenges that are mainly linked to surgery and/or anaesthesia, involve the intact, well-perfused, and networked brain, which are essential for effectively studying the neurovascular interplay and allow the investigation of the link of CSD and the generation of a subsequent headache. The in vivo CSD models, predominantly in rats and mice, have been instrumental in mimicking clinical aspects of migraine aura and testing current and novel therapeutics [91–93]. Recently, CSD has been studied in awake freely behaving animals, thereby eradicating major confounders of anaesthesia and minimising invasive surgery. Especially, the feasibility to study optogenetic light-induced CSD events in awake animals [94, 95] has aided translational research.

Spreading depolarization (SD) events can be elicited by various experimental methods (extensively reviewed in [49, 90, 96]), i.e., by electrical stimulation, the application of chemical depolarizing agents onto the dura or injection into the brain (e.g., KCl, glutamate), mechanical stimuli [91], optogenetics [94, 95], or physiological triggers (e.g., sleep deprivation and hypoglycaemia [97, 98]). Of these, electrical stimulation is one of the more direct methods to assess SD susceptibility, with trigger threshold value, amplitude, and propagation speed of the SD events as commonly utilised readouts. For chemical depolarizing agents, escalating (from subthreshold to threshold) or fixed (submaximal threshold) concentrations of KCl applied is common, the latter correlating with assessing SD frequency. As noted, more recent advances using optogenetics offer a non-invasive alternative to trigger SD through precise stimulation of cortical neurons expressing blue light-sensitive channelrhodopsins [94, 95]. Notably, that technique can be used for repeatable and controlled induction of SD events through the intact skull in awake animals, enabling long-term, injury-free studies [95]. Together, the models provide a multi-scale framework that is essential for understanding SD mechanisms and advancing therapeutic development.

Experimental readouts

Once SD is triggered, typically studied upon application of the intense depolarizing trigger KCl, extracellular potassium (K+) rapidly rises, diffusing out to adjacent cells, and triggering the so-called “depolarization cycle” [99]. The minimal condition required to trigger an SD in rodents is raising the extracellular K⁺ concentration above a critical threshold of 12 mM in at least 1 mm³ of brain tissue [100]. The SD wave then propagates through brain tissue causing neuronal swelling, multiphasic blood flow changes, a drop in tissue oxygen tension, as well as a profound elevation of glutamate, but the spatiotemporal dynamics of ionic shifts favours interstitial diffusion of K+ and not glutamate as leading event in SD [101]. CSD also stimulates dural afferents and second-order neurons in the TVS [102], leading to increased CGRP release and changes in pain-related behaviour [103].

The profound ionic, vascular, metabolic, and cellular changes associated with SD have led to the development of multiple approaches for its detection and characterization. The electrophysiological gold standard is the recording of a large extracellular, direct-current (DC; <0.05 Hz) slow potential shifts with amplitudes of above 5 mV, accompanied by a sustained suppression of electrocorticographic activity lasting one to several minutes. Typically, a brief burst of neuronal firing precedes the DC potential shift, as detected in single-unit recordings [104, 105]. The SD-associated DC shift can be detected both in vivo and in ex vivo brain slice preparations [106]. SD can also be visualized using fluorescent voltage-sensitive dyes, imaged with either wide-field cameras or microscopy approaches [107, 108]. For instance, two-photon microscopy enables simultaneous monitoring of calcium and glutamate dynamics during SD, whereas the use of modern genetically encoded fluorophores allows to implement cell-type specific imaging [109, 110]. Several other in vivo techniques focus on SD-induced changes in cerebral perfusion. Such as Laser Doppler flowmetry enables point measurements of blood flow by detecting changes in laser light scatter [111], whereas laser speckle flowmetry extends this approach to two-dimensional imaging of cortical perfusion [112]. High-field blood oxygenation level-dependent (BOLD) and spin-lock functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) have also been used to examine neurovascular coupling during SD in rodent models [113]. Additionally, vascular responses to SD can be assessed using vessel-impermeant dyes, including fluorescein or rhodamine dextran, to visualize changes in vessel diameter and cerebral blood flow [114], whereas cellular swelling and dendritic beading associated with SD can be directly observed in fluorescently labelled cells [115, 116]. The massive extracellular ion fluxes that occur during SD can be quantified using ion-selective electrodes in both in vivo and in vitro preparations [117–119]. Finally, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) techniques, including diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) and magnetization transfer MRI, have been employed to investigate the spatiotemporal characteristics of SD in vivo using high-field MRI systems [120, 121]. Although SD detection with MRI is challenged by limited temporal resolution and spatial averaging, MRI offers important advantages as a non-invasive, three-dimensional modality and holds strong translational potential for both animal and human studies.

Translational value and design considerations

Over the past ten years, CSD has gained significant interest due to its relevance to translational research. It represents a robust and reliable experimental model of migraine with aura, supported by evidence linking CSD to aura manifestations, trigeminal pain activation, and downstream pathophysiological mechanisms relevant for therapeutic targeting [83, 106]. Evidence from fMRI of somatosensory auras, along with recent intracranial stereotactic EEG recordings in a patient with migraine aura made the argument stronger that the migraine aura indeed results from CSD in the human brain [104, 122]. This insight builds on earlier clinical evidence that imaged CSD in patients [82]. The combined insight led to a novel “Buildup Hypothesis” explaining that noxious chemicals released from cortical cells accumulate in brain tissue and cerebrospinal fluid and only when they reach high enough levels can they potentially sensitise pial afferents and cause headache [105]. Perhaps one of the more important and definitely understudied areas of aura is the association between women with migraine with aura and the increased risk of ischemic stroke, especially for those under 45 who smoke with further evidence of a role for those also taking oral contraceptives. As such, there is an untapped opportunity to explore the impact of CSD on cerebrovascular vulnerability in the context of sex and migraine [106].

Headache phase: models of migraine pain

Clinical relevance

The headache phase of migraine is often reported as the most salient feature of a migraine attack and is characterised by moderate-to-severe unilateral throbbing or pulsating pain typically lasting 4 to 72 h [107]. The pain often worsens with movement and can be accompanied by multisensory sensitivity to touch, light, smell and sound, as well as nausea [5]. Clinically, the headache phase responds best to early treatment, with acute therapies administered at the onset of symptoms. The current understanding of migraine pain highlights the role of the TVS, where both neuronal and vascular mechanisms contribute to its generation [3, 109]. The activation of trigeminal peripheral afferents, which innervate the cranial vasculature and meninges, results in the release of neuropeptides including CGRP and PACAP into the dura mater, and centrally at the level of the spinal trigeminal nucleus (STN). The peripheral inflammatory response, which may include mast cell degranulation, is considered to influence the sensitization of meningeal nociceptors, while the central release modulates ascending nociceptive transmission leading to the perception of pain [110]. Repeated activation of the TVS is considered to lead to a state of central sensitization contributing to disease chronification [111]. However, the exact disease mechanisms underlying the activation and sensitization of the peripheral TVS remains incompletely understood, and modelling aspects of these mechanisms in ex vivo and in vivo animal studies is therefore warranted.

Preclinical models

Given that the TVS contributes to the processing of migraine pain via activation of trigeminal sensory afferents [112], there has been significant focus on preclinical models to explore trigeminovascular activation and its modulation (Fig. 3). This is most commonly achieved via the direct activation or sensitisation of trigeminal sensory afferents at the level of meningeal vessels including the middle meningeal artery (MMA), superior sagittal sinus or the transverse sinus, with simultaneous recording of neuronal activity at the level of the trigeminal ganglion (TG), the STN or other migraine-relevant brain regions [41, 102, 113–116]. The relatively invasive nature of such studies, where the TG access requires stereotaxic insertion of electrodes, meningeal vessels being accessible via a craniotomy and the STN via a laminectomy, necessitates the use of general anaesthesia, which should always be considered a considerable confounder. Signals recorded include neuronal responses to electrically-evoked (divided into Aδ- or C-fibres based on their response latencies), or spontaneous neuronal activity and mechanical stimulation of the facial and meningeal receptive fields [15, 41, 113, 114]. In vivo preclinical electrophysiological recordings of trigeminal-related pain can be complemented with a variety of techniques from micro-iontophoresis (i.e., the direct application of compounds into discreet areas of the brain) [117] or systemic drug delivery [15], to more novel chemogenetic and optogenetic techniques, allowing the impact of circuit modulation to be determined. Importantly, this model has demonstrated significant success at predicting clinical translational efficacy [15, 16, 41, 118].

Alternatively, the hemiskull preparation has been developed to study peripheral nociceptive mechanisms, where one half of the skull is investigated, ensuring an intact connection of the meninges with the peripheral components of the trigeminal nerve in the preparation [63, 101, 123]. A key advantage of the hemiskull preparation is the preservation of the basic morphology of meningeal tissue, including the meningeal nerves and vasculature. The meningeal branches, specifically innervate the area around the MMA [119]. The hemiskull preparation allows for the direct examination of nociception mediators in the TVS, such as meningeal resident mast cell population [120, 121], CGRP release [124] and trigeminal nerve spiking activity [125–127], in response to chemical, mechanical, or inflammatory stimuli. The hemiskull model also allows assessment of trigeminal nerve spiking activity, for example, in response to PACAP, a peptide known to activate meningeal mast cells and contribute to TVS signalling [128]. An alternate ex vivo model that preserves partial anatomical continuity of the TVS involves targeted stimulation of either the trigeminal nucleus caudalis (TNC) or the TG, permitting parallel measurement of CGRP levels by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) [129]. For this purpose, tissues must be dissected as a single unit and transferred to dual-compartment chambers. The application of various pharmacological compounds allows the selective stimulation of either the central (TNC) or peripheral (TG) compartment independently, while simultaneously measuring CGRP release at both sites. This approach enables investigation of directional signaling along the TVS axis, including whether central activation can drive peripheral neuropeptide release, making it a valuable tool for studying the headache phase and trigeminal activation relevant to migraine. A limitation of these two ex vivo models is that both methods do not have the complex intact TVS that an in vivo animal model has.

Alternate models for assessing migraine-related pain include the application of chemical mediators directly on the meninges or via intracerebroventricular routes. Commonly used mediators in such experiments are histamine, serotonin, bradykinin, prostaglandin E2, low- or high-pH-buffered solutions, complete Freund’s adjuvant, and, of particular relevance to migraine research, CGRP and the transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1) agonist capsaicin [112, 114]. When using such methods, it is important to avoid supramaximal stimulation, as tissue damage can occur.

Experimental readouts

Migraine-related pain processing in preclinical models can be examined at the biochemical, functional and behavioural level. At the biochemical level, TVS activation can be studied by measuring the release of specific neuropeptides including CGRP [34, 35]. This can be achieved typically, via the stimulation of trigeminal afferents (electrically or chemically with e.g., KCl, capsaicin, or inflammatory mediators) in preparations such as the ex vivo hemiskull, isolated dura, TG cultures, or in an in vivo animal model, and subsequently quantifying CGRP in the appropriate fluid. In ex vivo preparations, the meninges (with the trigeminal fibres intact) are superfused with artificial CSF and after baseline collection of samples, a stimulus is applied and the superfusate samples are collected, typically at multiple time points. The samples are then analysed using an ELISA or a similar immunoassay to determine the concentration of CGRP [124, 130, 131]. In in vivo models, CGRP can be measured from jugular or peripheral (e.g. femoral) venous blood after trigeminal activation, or from microdialysate obtained near the meninges or trigeminal structures [132]. This versatile approach allows researchers to quantify how different triggers, drugs, or genetic modifications affect neuropeptide release, thus providing a biochemical readout of TVS activation that is directly relevant to migraine-related head pain and CGRP-targeted therapies. Again, it should be noted that there is considerable variability in measuring CGRP impacted by the collection, storage and assay used [133].

Single- and multi-unit electrophysiological recordings from the TG or the STN are powerful preclinical in vivo migraine-related head pain models to study the peripheral and central processing of trigeminal nociception. Using this approach, meningeal afferents are functionally identified by their constant-latency response to controlled electrical stimulation of the trigeminal afferents at the level of the dura mater via a bipolar electrode. This allows for a distinction between Aδ- and C-fibre neurons based on conduction latency and an assumed dural–ganglion conduction distance. Mechanosensitive dural afferents are further verified by mapping mechanoreceptive fields on the dura using blunt probes or von Frey filaments. The approach yields high-resolution functional data on dural nociceptors in a relatively intact preparation, with the advantage that the entirety of the TVS, including ascending thalamocortical projections can be monitored [116].

Electrophysiological recordings from a hemiskull preparation are an effective means to study the peripheral component of migraine-related head pain at the functional level. The recordings give insight in the level of activity of meningeal trigeminal afferents and thereby peripheral nociceptive mechanisms that are relevant to migraine. For the recordings, the nervus spinosus of the trigeminal nerve is carefully dissected within the hemiskull preparation and isolated from surrounding meninges before being placed into a glass microelectrode filled with the appropriate physiological solution. This set up allows for the recording of extracellular action potentials from C- and Aδ-fibres in a stable manner. Both spontaneous and stimulus-evoked activity can be assessed, for example following chemical stimulation with capsaicin or other migraine-relevant modulators [30, 125, 127, 134]. The recordings provide a direct functional readout of meningeal nociceptor excitability in a preparation in which the native meningeal architecture is essentially preserved, making the ex vivo hemiskull model a useful asset.

Preclinical models offer insights into vascular responses dependent and independent of trigeminal activation. Intravital microscopy, developed by Williamson et al. [135] provides an in vivo model for direct visualization and measurement of diameter changes in the cranial vasculature in response to trigeminal activation by electrical (neurogenic vasodilation) or chemical stimulation, thus offering insight into the mechanisms of neurovascular activation that is central to migraine pathophysiology. The intravital microscopy model uses a thinned, closed cranial window coupled with a stereomicroscope and a video capture system to visualize the dural and pial vasculature, with the middle meningeal artery being the most commonly imaged vessel. Studies have demonstrated that the neurogenic vasodilatory responses observed in this model are driven by CGRP release from dural sensory fibres, and subsequent activation of the CGRP receptors present on vascular smooth muscle cells [136]. A particular strength of the model is its high translational value. For example, while inhibitors of the CGRP pathway significantly attenuate neurogenic vasodilatory responses, neurokinin-1 receptor antagonists are ineffective in modulating them [137] which aligns with the clinical efficacy observed for these therapeutic targets. The intravital microscopy model also allows the evaluation of (presynaptic) modulation of CGRP release, another useful predictor of clinical efficacy [137, 138]. Vascular responses can also be evaluated in vitro, allowing separation of neuronal trigeminal activation from intrinsic vascular mechanisms [139, 140]. Vascular segments can be mounted in organ baths and changes in vascular diameter are measured to determine the potency (pEC50) and efficacy (Emax) of potential anti-migraine agents. Previous studies have evaluated vascular responses in genetic models of migraine, as well as in porcine, rat and rabbit vascular segments [141, 142]. In particular, contraction of the rabbit saphenous vein is an established model used to analyse potential vasoactive properties of anti-migraine drugs [142]. This is due to the expression pattern of 5-HT receptors on the vascular bed that mirrors expression in the human vasculature. Additionally, vascular studies can use human isolated blood vessels, obtained from surgical procedures or organ donors, which provides the opportunity to study cranial selectivity and the potential coronary side effects of prospective anti-migraine drugs [143, 144]. Previous studies with this model have evaluated the vasoactive properties of several current anti-migraine drugs, including the ditans, gepants and CGRP (receptor)-antibodies [13, 145, 146].

Complimentary to these approaches, more novel imaging methodologies have emerged that offer the opportunity to visualise TG neuronal activity and its interaction with meningeal afferents [78]. For instance, gradient-Index (GRIN) lens–based epifluorescence imaging, combined with genetically encoded calcium indicators, allows for chronic optical access to the TG through a lens stereotaxically implanted above the ganglion without penetrating it. This set up permits high‑resolution monitoring of TG neuronal activity during controlled manipulation. Complementary acute preparations, in which the forebrain is aspirated to expose the skull base and TG, enable the simultaneous epifluorescence-based imaging of large populations of neurons, while applying defined solutions directly onto the ganglion surface. The combined technique can provide a dynamic, cell-resolved view of trigeminal afferent function in vivo, bridging mechanistic links between trigeminal excitability and migraine-related nociceptive signalling. However, the invasive nature of the GRIN lens implantation necessitates the disruption of large areas of the brain to permit access to the TG.

Finally, there are various approaches to assess pain(-like) behaviour in preclinical migraine pain models. Pain-like behaviour can be assessed by non-evoked (i.e., spontaneous) and stimulus-evoked means [147, 148]. Non-evoked assessments of pain behaviour rely on observations of altered behaviour of the animal. Assessing non-evoked pain in rodents, however, can be challenging as rodents will behaviourally repress any sign of pain and discomfort. Nonetheless, decreases in exploration, rearing, food and water consumption may be indicative of a nociceptive state in the animal [147]. Increased grooming and freezing behaviour can also be perceived as spontaneous responses to pain [149]. For an objective assessment of pain-like behaviour the so-called “Mouse Grimace Scale” (MGS) score has been designed that can quantify pain (and other discomfort) behaviour in mice [150] and that later was automated as to provide an objective and rapid way to quantify spontaneous pain and pain relief in rodents [151]. The MGS relies on rating facial signs of discomfort that include squinting (orbital tightening), nose/cheek bulging, whisker displacement, and ear orientation. In migraine research, the MGS has been used to assess pain in response to chemical mediators relevant to migraine [152, 153]. MGS has been effective in detecting spontaneous pain in mice after administration of CGRP with subsequent relief after receiving CGRP antibodies [153]. Finally, an automated squint assay, nociceptive-associated squinting in mice that was interpreted as sign of discomfort in response to CGRP and amylin [154]. However, it should be noted that these pain-related readouts are not exclusively linked to trigeminal pain and may also represent extracephalic pain states.

As an alternative to observing spontaneous behavioural changes, stimuli can be applied to measure evoked behavioural responses in preclinical models of migraine-related pain. Cutaneous allodynia, defined as perceiving a non-painful sensory stimulus as painful, affects around two-third of migraine cases [155]. Allodynia is widely considered a reliable marker of sensory amplification and the result of central and peripheral sensitisation as a consequence of TVS activation [156]. Allodynia that occurs in response to mechanical and thermal stimuli can also be investigated in rodents, making it a clinically relevant readout [157]. Von Frey filament testing, which uses calibrated filaments to determine the mechanical withdrawal threshold value, is routinely used in preclinical migraine pain research to assess mechanical allodynia in cephalic (forehead) and extracephalic (paw) regions. The three widely used methods for calculating the withdrawal threshold are the “up-down”, the “ascending stimulus”, and the “percent response” method [147]. NTG, as migraine trigger that sensitises the TVS, can induce mechanical hypersensitivity (allodynia) in rodents and is ameliorated by triptans [157]. CGRP, PACAP, histamine and prostaglandin E2, and CSD also induce mechanical hypersensitivity via activation of the TVS [158, 159]. Additionally, thermal allodynia can be assessed either by using acetone evaporation as measure of cold allodynia, or the Hargreaves’ test as measure of thermal hyperalgesia. Nociceptive behaviours induced by acetone cooling are counted or timed, whereas heat allodynia is determined by measuring the withdrawal latency to a heat stimulus. Taken together, allodynia is a meaningful tool to investigate the underlying disease mechanisms or to assess the efficacy of anti-migraine drugs.

Translational value and design considerations

Despite the strong translational value of the preclinical migraine-related pain models, there are important limitations. First, none of the migraine pain models fully captures the complexity of migraine as a disorder with sensory, cognitive, and emotional symptoms [2]. For example, many models focus on peripheral activation of the TVS system, potentially overlooking central mechanisms involved in the regulation of migraine pain. Furthermore, electrophysiological recordings require invasive electrode placements that may alter normal physiological environments. Interspecies differences, such as the variable anatomy of the middle meningeal artery, can also limit translational relevance of findings from animal models to humans. Surgical approaches requiring anaesthetics, by definition, introduce pharmacological interactions that need to be taken into consideration when evaluating the experimental results. During surgery, strict physiological monitoring is crucial as factors such as temperature, CO2 level and surgical trauma can influence neuronal and vascular responses. As the majority of the described experimental approaches are short-lasting, any long-term changes relevant to migraine pain are not consistently explored. However, more modern approaches permit the recording of neuronal tissues over prolonged periods and at a scale that was previously not practical, allowing many of these limitations to be overcome [160]. Limitations around behavioural proxies of pain in rodents should also be considered. Rodents often hide signs of pain due to their prey species instinct and, crucially, cannot self-report experiences of pain. Here, an important distinction needs to be considered between the subjective experiences of pain and what is being measured in animal models. Pain in preclinical models is often inferred, for instance, by assessing withdrawal from noxious stimuli (nociception) or non-noxious stimuli (allodynia/hyperalgesia), or by observing subtle changes in spontaneous behaviour or facial expression. Due to this, combining multiple measures of pain-like behaviours in rodents can provide a more comprehensive picture of the animals’ state but pain cannot be directly measured. Importantly, preclinical models of migraine-related head pain have substantially aided in the development of novel therapeutic strategies, including novel and pipeline therapeutics [15, 16, 161] with a focus on reducing trigeminal sensory activation.

Postdrome phase: models of resolution of the attack

Clinical relevance

The postdrome, also referred to as the “migraine hangover”, is the final phase of the migraine attack. Reported postdrome symptoms include feelings of extreme fatigue, difficulty concentrating and stiff neck [162]. Clinical studies that have investigated this phase are limited, and there are currently no preclinical models specifically designed to investigate the postdrome phase, creating a dearth of research on this important phase of the migraine attack. Regardless, specific symptoms persist throughout the migraine attack [23, 162] and indeed it is unclear if these symptoms represent a continuous phenotype masked by the headache phase or are truly separate entities. Thus, ongoing research into premonitory-like symptoms is likely to also hold relevance for related postdromal symptoms. As with all preclinical models of migraine-related symptomatology, their value lies in the reverse translation of clinical features to generate an increased understanding of the underlying biology. As clinical interest in the postdromal phase increases it is likely that this will stimulate preclinical research.

Models of persistent neuronal hyperexcitability and altered sensory processing

In the previous sections the status of the preclinical models and readouts were tailored to the investigation of individual phases of a migraine attack. Typically, these were acute models in which a trigger is used to evoke a relevant migraine-related phenotype. While beneficial for understanding underlying mechanisms and testing novel therapeutics, these more acute models often lack the ability to explore long-term consequences or the episodic/chronic nature of attacks. In the following section, the focus is on alternate models that rely on persistent exposure to migraine-relevant triggering agents/environmental stimuli or genetic modification that can be used to help understand the recurrent nature of migraine as a threshold disorder.

Chronic migraine models

In individuals with episodic migraine the frequency and duration of the attacks can increase to transform into chronic migraine [163]. Chronic migraine, classified as 15 or more headache days with at least 8 headaches having typical migraine features over at least 3 months [107], is extremely disabling and often accompanied by psychiatric comorbidities [164]. A salient feature and predictor of chronic migraine is the presence of cutaneous allodynia [165]. Although the exact mechanisms leading to migraine chronification are not fully understood, central sensitisation of the TVS is suggested to underlie the condition [166]. Therefore, allodynia, as important clinical marker of central sensitisation, is a symptom of high relevance and a commonly adopted behavioural readout in preclinical models. The most established preclinical model of chronic migraine involves the repeated administration of NTG [44]. Intermittent NTG administration induces a state of persistent mechanical hypersensitivity and light sensitivity that mimics chronic migraine symptoms. This preclinical model is well-suited to better understand the mechanisms underlying chronification and to test the efficacy of preventive migraine therapies [44, 167]. Alternatively, others have utilised the “inflammatory soup model”, whereby an inflammatory mixture containing histamine, serotonin, bradykinin and prostaglandin E2 is applied repeatedly to the dura mater, evoking peripheral sensitization and subsequently central sensitization [168]. As a consequence, various neuropeptides, among which CGRP and substance P, are released from dural afferent nerves, causing vasodilatation and mast cell degranulation, enhancing sensitization. Chronic migraine models and their strengths and limitations have been discussed extensively in the literature [22, 169].

Medication overuse headache models

Individuals with migraine are selectively vulnerable to persistent exposure to several acute anti-migraine therapeutics. This exposure on more than 10–15 days per month for at least 3 months, depending on the therapeutic agent taken, increases the risk of attack chronification resulting in a sensitised state termed “medication overuse headache” (MOH) [170, 171]. MOH is closely related to chronic migraine, as individuals with chronic migraine are at higher risk of developing MOH due to an increased basal attack frequency and subsequent increased usage of acute medications [171]. Therefore, MOH is a serious and debilitating burden for those who already live with a primary headache disorder. Preclinical MOH models, that have demonstrated behavioural (cutaneous allodynia) and physiological changes (increased CGRP levels) have provided insights into the underlying pathophysiology of MOH [172]. Repeated triptan administration in rats results in a state of ‘latent sensitisation’ consisting of pronociceptive neural adaptations and subsequent susceptibility to migraine triggers such as stress and nitric oxide donors [173]. Prolonged exposure to triptans and opioids in rats results in lasting alterations in the descending pain modulation system [174]. Preclinical evidence also suggests that MOH alterations may be CGRP-dependant and thus may benefit from anti-CGRP-based therapies [175]. Animal models have also been used to assess the MOH risk profiles of novel anti-migraine therapies [176]. For example, triptan, ditan and gepant risk profiles have been investigated [14]. While both the gepants and triptans were effective in ameliorating stress and nitic oxide donor-evoked cephalic allodynia in mice and rats, only repeated exposure to gepants did not induce “latent sensitisation” [14, 177, 178]. In this way, although preclinical models are limited in recapitulating MOH, they are beneficial in understanding the pathogenesis of MOH and the MOH risk of novel acute anti-migraine treatments.

Priming models

Stress is intrinsically associated with migraine, representing the most commonly reported migraine trigger [179] that can prolong attack duration [180] and impact chronification [181, 182]. Stress, either acute or chronic, can significantly impact the nervous system, inducing changes in brain structure and function. How the body responds to stress depends on the allostatic load, leading to physiological dysregulation. As previously noted, individuals with migraine present with selective vulnerability to several chemical triggers (e.g.., NTG) that reliably induce delayed migraine-like attacks in approximately 90% of cases, compared to only a mild, acute, non-migrainous headache in healthy individuals [40, 41, 55].

A potential criticism of several animal models of migraine is that they fail to recapitulate this selective vulnerability. To address this in more detail, more recent preclinical studies have started utilizing models of “priming” [66], which have emerged from initial studies identifying opioid hyperalgesic priming [183], whereby repeated exposure to opioid drugs can induce a state of heightened pain sensitivity. The approach was initially adapted to explore mechanisms of triptan-induced latent sensitization as a preclinical model for MOH [173], identifying that persistent exposure of rodents to several anti-migraine therapeutics can induce a state of acute sensory hypersensitivity, followed by a more prolonged state of latent sensitization, combined with alterations in key migraine-related molecules, including nitric oxide and CGRP [173]. More recently, a similar approach has been adopted to explore the impact of repetitive exposure to restraint stress [66], demonstrating that three days of repetitive restraint stress is sufficient to induce acute sensory hypersensitivity. This hypersensitivity resolves in approximately two weeks and the “primed-mice” now demonstrate a selective vulnerability to low-dose NO donors that is responsive to blockade of CGRP signalling [66]. Follow up studies have identified a key role for the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis [184], confirming a critical role for stress in migraine vulnerability.

While several models of migraine-related priming have been developed, they share a key component being the ability to induce a selective vulnerability to several migraine triggering agents. This primed response is observed in response to persistent exposure to agents such as NTG [44] that trigger migraine attacks in individuals with migraine, acute anti-migraine therapies that have been shown to induce a state of MOH [173], and stress [66]. Importantly, these priming models have proven predictive of, for example, MOH risk, as agents including small molecule CGRP antagonists that can be used both as an acute or preventive therapy, and no MOH induction, similarly do not produce a primed state in mice [14]. While the specific mechanisms that lead to this primed state remain to be fully characterised, such models offer an opportunity to bridge the translational gap and utilise preclinical models to understand the potential mechanisms that lead to the selective vulnerability of individuals with migraine when compared to healthy controls.

Genetic animal models of migraine

Notably, most animal research is performed in a limited set of inbred mouse lines (e.g. C57Bl/6J and C3H) and consequently genetic variation, as it occurs among humans, is lacking. This is particularly relevant as certain individual inbred strains have specific traits and are differentially sensitive for experimental outcomes relevant to pain, including nociception [185] and mechanical allodynia [186]. Taking genetics into account is, important as migraine is considered a multifactorial genetic disorder. It is influenced by many DNA variants, each with a very small contribution to disease risk, in combination with multiple environmental factors [109]. Currently, some 150 genetic migraine susceptibility loci (based on so-called “single nucleotide polymorphisms”, SNPs) have been identified in the human genome [187]. Using a plethora of bioinformatical tools, many of these loci have been linked to changes in expression of specific genes by mining database that contain expression data of human and animal tissues, in an attempt to shed light on what molecular pathways are involved in migraine pathophysiology. One study revealed that the majority (88/107) of genes within 20 Kb of migraine susceptibility loci were annotated in a single-cell RNA sequencing mouse brain dataset with 54 of them being expressed in CNS, PNS and/or neurovascular cell types deemed relevant to migraine pathophysiology [188]. Therefore, human genome-wide association studies (GWAS) provide a rich source for further exploration in animal models of the contribution of the associated genes in migraine pathophysiology. One striking example is transient receptor potential melastatin 8 (TRPM8), a non-selective cation channel of the melastatin subfamily of transient receptor potential (TRP) channel superfamily, that was identified as one of the GWAS loci [189]. Given that TRPM8 is expressed on sensory afferents innervating the meninges and the cortex, it is considered an interesting candidate for subsequent investigation. The reduction in hind paw withdrawal threshold normally seen in response to the clinical migraine triggers (NTG and CGRP) is attenuated in Trpm8 knockout mice [190, 191], in line with earlier data that tested a TRPM8 agonist (icilin) and antagonist (AMG1161) on allodynia measures in rats [192]. These findings support a role for TRPM8 in migraine-like pain that is considered likely due to reduced CGRP release [193]. A possible role of TRPM8 in migraine pathophysiology is, however, complex since activation of cortical TRPM8 by icilin was shown to increase the susceptibility to KCl-induced CSD as well as subsequent cortical neuroinflammation in rats, whereas TRPM8 inhibition by M8-B had no effect [194]. Therefore, the recent successful announcement of a positive phase IIb clinical trial for a TRPM8 antagonist [195] offers an insight into how human genetic and experimental animal findings can be translated into potentially effective therapeutics.

Up to now, most preclinical genetic animal research has been conducted in mice expressing gene mutations that cause monogenic forms of migraine in humans, foremost familial hemiplegic migraine (FHM) [196, 197]. The true benefit of a genetic over other preclinical animal models is that the presence of a gene mutation should make the brain inherently susceptibility to migraine features, hence likely making the gap between preclinical models and the clinic smaller [198]. Following this premise, mutations - identified in individuals from families with hemiplegic migraine - in the CACNA1A (FHM1), ATP1A2 (FHM2) and SCN1A (FHM3) genes, which encode neuronal α1 subunit of CaV2.1 calcium channels, the glial α2 subunit of sodium-potassium ATPases, and the neuronal α1 subunit of NaV1.1 sodium channels, respectively, have been introduced by a gene targeting approach in the corresponding mouse genes. Among the functional consequences in FHM1 mutant animals are a hyperexcitable phenotype, such as increased cortical glutamatergic neurotransmission, an increased susceptibility to experimentally evoked CSD [197].

Optogenetic induction of CSD revealed migraine-relevant behavioural and neuroinflammatory phenotypes that were more pronounced and longer-lasting in FHM1 mutant than wildtype mice [196]. Notably, FHM1 mutant mice also experience (albeit very rarely) spontaneous CSD events that were associated with body stretching, one-direction slow head turning, and rotating movement of the body [199]. Importantly, the characteristics of the spontaneous CSD events resembled those of CSDs that were evoked by optogenetics [199], which allows for timing of the events that is needed to study, for instance, the effects of acute drug administration on CSD characteristics. In recent years also various FHM2 mutant mouse models have been generated [200] that also show an increased susceptibility to experimentally evoked CSD, but in these models this is due to impaired glial uptake of K+ and glutamate [201], leading to the spontaneous release of “plumes” of glutamate pointing at a possible mechanism of initiation of SD [202]. In one FHM2 model, homozygous mutant mice could be investigated that showed regular bidaily spontaneous SDs that typically originated from hippocampus and that could be prevented by a blocker of persistent sodium channels [203]. Finally, FHM3 mutant mouse models have been generated that also showed an increased susceptibility to experimentally evoked CSD and, interestingly, beneficial effects of blockade of persistent sodium channels [204, 205]. One FHM3 model revealed hyperactivity of fast-spiking inhibitory interneurons due to impaired Na+ channel inactivation and increased ramp Na+ currents resulting in a buildup of extracellular K+ [204] that, as was shown in parallel research in brain slices of non-FHM3 animals, was sufficient to ignite an SD in the cortex (but not other brain regions) [206]. The other FHM3 model revealed spontaneous CSD events that consistently propagated from visual to motor cortex [205]. Future research in preclinical genetic animal models will logically focus on how spontaneous SD events are generated, which gives unprecedented opportunities for the testing of novel anti-migraine medication. In line with the review, one can envisage that preventing or in time stopping of SD events may prevent the subsequent activation of headache mechanisms, foremost in individuals suffering from migraine with aura but perhaps also in those with migraine without aura.

Additionally, a specific family of individuals suffering from familial advanced sleep phase (FASP) as a result of a loss of function mutation of the casein kinase 1δ (CK1δ), were shown to have a high penetrance of migraine with aura [207]. CK1δ, in this context has several functions. Key to its circadian mechanisms, CK1δ phosphorylates PERIOD proteins, regulating their stability and setting the length of the circadian period [208]. Importantly, migraine attacks demonstrate a circadian pattern of onset and circadian disruption is known to increase attack susceptibility [206, 207], suggesting an important role. As previously noted, mice expressing the human CK1δ-T44A mutation demonstrated an advanced sleep phase, but normal sensory thresholds [204]. However, following exposure to low-dose NTG (5 mg/kg), CK1δ-T44A mice present with hypersensitivity to tactile stimuli that is not present in wild type littermates. Notably in earlier discussed non-genetic preclinical animal models a high-dose NTG (10 mg/kg) was needed to bring about migraine-relevant phenotypes. Subsequently, and in agreement with FHM mice, CK1δ-T44A mice demonstrated increased calcium signalling and increased CSD susceptibility, suggesting that alteration of normal circadian mechanisms impacts migraine phenotypes and as such, preclinical genetic models can be utilised to explore the potential mechanisms of circadian biology and its disruption in migraine [209].

Conclusions

Migraine is increasingly understood to be a complex state and not merely a headache disorder. Instead it is a dynamic and highly disabling neurovascular disorder that is characterised by a plethora of neurological, autonomic and sensory symptoms. These symptoms distribute across distinct, overlapping phases of a migraine attack, i.e., the premonitory, the aura, the headache, and the postdromal phase. Understanding the biology of the different phases is crucial to enhance both our understanding of the complexity of migraine pathophysiology and to uncover novel therapeutic targets that transcend the headache phase. While still in its infancy, there is an emerging approach to explore phase-independent preclinical models as they seem essential to investigate the complex, integrated, underlying mechanisms of migraine and potential novel therapeutic targets. In this review, we have sought to define some of the most commonly used preclinical models and highlight their utility to explore the biology of phase-dependent or dynamic multi-phase phenotypes. This approach is likely to herald a greater understanding of the earliest attack alterations and how attacks start during the premonitory phase and how they stop and cause postdromal symptoms.
