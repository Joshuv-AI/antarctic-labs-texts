Comparative machine learning models for early prediction of preterm birth from maternal serum biomarkers
Authors: Kaleem Maqsood, Javeria Malik, Mahnoor Fatima, Sundas Akram, Husna Ahmad, Nabila Roohi, Shahid Bashir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPreterm birth (PTB) is a major cause of neonatal morbidity and mortality. Inflammation and metabolic disruption are involved in its pathology. This study aimed to assess maternal serum inflammatory and lipid markers as predictors of PTB using various machine learning models.MethodsWomen who were pregnant and attending antenatal clinics were recruited for this study. A group of 186 females who had their births before 37 weeks was marked PTB. One hundred forty women who delivered at term and met the same eligibility criteria were selected from the same source population during the study period. T-tests were used to evaluate variations in baseline and clinical parameters. Pearson correlations were visualized via a heatmap. We built models for random forests (RF), logistic regression (LR), XGBoost, and support vector machine (SVM) using a 70/30 train/test split and 5-fold cross-validation. Models performance was measured using accuracy and AUC.ResultsCRP (r ≈ 0.45), IL-6 (r ≈ 0.40), C3 (r ≈ 0.31), BMI, and lipids correlated positively with PTB, whereas HDL correlated inversely (r ≈ − 0.13). Multivariable logistic regression identified age, BMI, IL-6, C3, and CRP as independent predictors. All ML models showed good discrimination (test AUC ≥ 0.819); LR performed best (accuracy 78.57%, AUC 0.849) with cross-validated AUCs around 0.86–0.87 across models. SHAP analysis confirmed that IL-6, BMI, CRP, age, and C3 were dominant contributors to PTB risk.ConclusionsMaternal inflammation and high BMI are important risk factors for PTB in this cohort. The LR model combining clinical and serum measures is as good a predictor as a complex ML algorithm. It is an interpretable model that can help with risk assessment at an early stage in similar settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-08784-0.

Introduction

Globally, preterm birth (PTB) is a main healthcare issue due to its strong link with increased neonatal morbidity and mortality. It is clinically defined as the delivery of an infant before 37 gestational weeks [1]. Around 15 million children are born preterm yearly worldwide, with a global 11% PTB rate [2]. Among these, about 1 million die due to PTB complications [3]. In terms of PTB rate, Pakistan (14.3%) is one of the worst-affected countries in the world, and in South Asia, it is only after Bangladesh (16.2%) [4]. Pakistan has the 3rd-highest rates of maternal and child mortalities [5], and PTB is among the major contributors to these incidents [6]. PTB inflicts a great burden on hospital economies, and in Pakistan, the health care systems are consistently in crisis [7]. Therefore, this study aims to investigate cost-effective, time-efficient, and reliable prediction models to identify which pregnancies will result in PTB.

The etiology of PTB is multifactorial, making it challenging to delineate its causative mechanisms [8, 9]. Almost half of all cases of PTB occur without a clearly identifiable cause, reflecting the characteristic complexity of the condition [10]. Over the last decades, several studies have tried to evaluate numerous serum/plasma components for predicting premature membrane rupture or spontaneous preterm labor [11]. The multifactorial nature of PTB presents a challenge to identifying individual predictive markers. Machine learning (ML) models show promise by integrating these variables.

The selected biomarkers were chosen based on their established roles in inflammatory, metabolic, and adiposity-driven pathways of PTB. In recent years, it has become clear that infection/inflammation and preterm delivery have a strong association [11]. During labour, there is an influx of immune cells which initiates the parturition process characterised by cervical ripening uterine contractility, and membrane rupturing, leads to inflammation [12–14] Immune molecules like IL-6 (high levels at labour onset ) [15, 16], C-reactive protein (CRP), C3, and C4 have been associated with PTB [17, 18]. Obesity, on the other hand, can lead to maternal morbidities like thromboembolic and hypertensive disorders, gestational diabetes, and infections [19]. Dyslipidemia is also associated with maternal hormonal changes and metabolic syndrome [20, 21]. These complications contribute to the increased risk of PTB. So, integrating biochemical and maternal risk factors allows ML models to capture complex interactions while maintaining clinical interpretability.

Although several predictive systems using proven risk factors, maternal demographics, and medical and obstetric history have been explored, their predictive performance has remained limited [22, 23]. This may be attributed to their reliance on simple linear statistical models, which are inadequate for capturing the complex and multifactorial nature of PTB. Consequently, traditional risk-factor-based approaches fail to identify more than half of PTB cases [24]. Compared to the LR algorithm, machine learning (ML) models can process higher-dimensional data and have self-learning capacity [25]. Therefore, in recent years, ML techniques have emerged as promising tools for enhancing individual risk prediction beyond traditional models [26].

In Pakistan, biochemical tests and fetal fibronectin testing to predict PTB remain costly. Similarly, uptake of cervical-length ultrasound is low due to resource and personnel shortages. There is also a lack of PTB prediction guidelines in Pakistan, resulting in delayed identification of women at risk [5]. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate maternal inflammatory and lipid biomarkers as predictors of PTB and to compare the predictive performance of these machine-learning models LR, Random Forest, XGBoost, and Support Vector Machine. These models were chosen because of their widespread use and interpretability in clinical settings, their ability to model non-linear relationships, handle interactions among heterogenous maternal and biochemical variables, and reduce overfitting of data [27]. In contrast, more complex models such as Neural Networks and LightGBM are suitable for massive datasets but can lead to overfitting in smaller clinical cohorts. We hypothesized that machine learning approaches would outperform traditional regression by better capturing nonlinear interactions among biomarkers. This work fills a major knowledge gap by providing the first biomarker-based ML stratification framework for PTB in Pakistan, where reliable screening tools remain limited.

Methodology

Study design and population

This prospective observational study had ethical approval from the University of the Punjab ethics review board in Lahore (Approval Letter No. F/112/UZ). Subjects for the study were recruited in March 2021 and December 2023 at Lady Wellington Hospital and Jinnah Hospital, Lahore, during their antenatal visits in the second and third trimesters, respectively. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants after they were informed of the objectives and procedures of the study. Participants were followed longitudinally until delivery, and pregnancy outcomes were recorded after birth. Gestational age was determined in accordance with usual obstetric practice, using the Last Menstrual Period (LMP) when no ultrasound measurements were available. The spontaneous PTB was identified as one below 37 gestational weeks. Out of these, 186 premature birth (PTB) women were included in the PTB group and remaining who delivered at term while 140 women who delivered at term were included as the term birth control group.

Maternal blood samples were collected during routine antenatal visits in the second and third trimesters, prior to the onset of labor. Detailed information was collected on their sociodemographic and clinical baseline characteristics. The study excluded women who had known risk factors or medical conditions that could affect inflammatory or metabolic biomarkers, or those who were found to smoke, have active infections, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, history of uterine fibroids or polycystic ovarian syndrome, carcinoma, hepatitis, or other critical pathological conditions at the time of study recruitment.

Sample collection and serum analysis

About five milliliters (5 mL) of blood was collected from each individual. Only one blood sample per participant was collected to reflect routine clinical practice and ensure feasibility in resource-limited settings. The blood samples were centrifuged for 10 min at 3000 × g to collect the serum for the analysis. The serum was aliquoted into labeled cryovials and stored at -80 °C until further biochemical or molecular analysis was carried out. Serum lipid analysis was done with commercially available kits of “Monlab” (Spain).

Serum concentrations of immune markers, including C-reactive protein (CRP), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and complement factor 3 (C3), were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits from Glory Bioscience (China). All reagents and materials were handled according to the manufacturer’s biosafety and storage recommendations.

Descriptive and inferential statistical analysis

Normally distributed variables confirmed by the Shapiro-Wilk test were compared between term and PTB groups using the independent samples t-test, while non-normally distributed variables were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. A p-value of < 0.05 is statistically significant. To give a suitable representation of data, all values are presented as Mean ± SEM. Also, a Pearson Correlation Matrix was computed to examine the interrelationships among the parameters. Variables studied included maternal age, BMI, triglycerides, HDL-C, total cholesterol, LDL-C, IL-6, Complement C3, C-reactive protein, and the outcome PTB. A heatmap visualization was generated using seaborn.

Data processing and preparation

The outcome variable was encoded into a binary form: 0 = term birth and 1 = PTB (including both very preterm and extremely preterm cases). Categorical predictors were converted to numerical values via one-hot encoding where necessary. Because some algorithms are sensitive to predictor scale, standardized scaling was applied selectively. For LR and SVM models, continuous predictors were standardized to zero mean and unit variance using StandardScaler after mean imputation. For RF and XGBoost models, only mean imputations were applied; tree-based methods were trained on unscaled features, as they are inherently scale-invariant.

Scaling and imputation were implemented using scikit-learn Pipelines, ensuring that all preprocessing operations were fitted only on the training sets and applied to the test sets.

Data partitioning and model development

For predictive modeling, the dataset was split into training (70%) and test (30%) sets using stratified sampling to preserve the proportion of preterm and term births and to mitigate the effects of class imbalance. We used traditional and advanced supervised machine learning algorithms to predict PTB outcomes. We used four algorithms for this task. The algorithms were LR [28], RF [29], XGBoost [30], and SVM [31]. In the case of Random Forest, XGBoost, and SVM models, they were not subjected to extreme over-fitting through large-scale hyperparameter optimization; rather, conservative, common hyperparameter settings were chosen in advance to limit model complexity and prevent overfitting. The most important hyperparameters were the number and depth of trees used in ensemble models, and the kernel parameters in SVMs. Specifically, Random Forest models were trained with 200 trees, XGBoost used 200 estimators with a maximum tree depth of 4 and a learning rate of 0.1, and SVM employed a radial basis function kernel with default regularization parameters. There was also no feature selection step; all the clinically significant variables were included, according to prior knowledge, and feature contributions were compared with model-based importance measures and SHAP analysis. LR was used as the baseline interpretable model. All maternal characteristics and serum biomarkers were included simultaneously in a single multivariable LR model. Multicollinearity among predictors was assessed prior to model fitting and was found to be within acceptable limits. We used Random Forest and XGBoost to capture nonlinear interactions and assess feature importance. We included the SVM model in this analysis to assess classification performance in high-dimensional data.

Model evaluation and validation

Model performance was evaluated on the independent test dataset. ROC-AUC was emphasized as a primary performance metric because it is robust to class imbalance in the outcome. Other evaluation metrics were, Precision, Sensitivity, and F1-Score. Each model was evaluated using its own ROC curve to assess discriminative performance. The best predictive accuracy was achieved among the classifiers tested with LR, with a mean cross-validated AUC of 0.86. To ensure model stability and reduce overfitting, five-fold cross-validation was invoked. Sensitivity (true positive rate) was defined as the proportion of PTBs correctly identified by the model TP/(TP + FN). Specificity (true negative rate) was defined as the proportion of term births correctly classified TN/(TN + FP).

Feature importance analysis

For the random forest and XGBoost models, global feature importance was obtained from the fitted models via the feature importances attribute, which reflects the average reduction in node impurity attributable to each variable’s splits. Importance scores were normalized and plotted as horizontal bar charts using descriptive feature names.

Because the RBF-kernel SVM does not yield direct coefficients, permutation feature importance was used to assess variable contributions. Using the trained SVM pipeline, baseline accuracy on the test set was recorded, and each predictor was permuted repeatedly while keeping all other features fixed. The mean decrease in classification accuracy across 30 permutations per feature, along with its standard deviation, was used as the importance score and visualized in a horizontal bar plot.

SHAP (Shapley additive Explanations) analysis

To quantify the relative importance and direction of influence of each predictor in the LR model, SHAP analysis was performed. SHAP values measure the marginal contribution of each feature to the model’s output using cooperative game theory, providing both local (individual-level) and global (model-level) interpretability. Summary plots and bar-type feature importance plots were generated using the shap Python library. These plots visually illustrate how increases or decrease in biomarkers contribute to the predicted risk of PTB. All SHAP visualizations were produced on the test set to avoid information leakage.

Five-fold cross-validation

Internal validation was achieved using 5-fold stratified cross-validation on the 70% training set with the complete preprocessing model pipeline retrained in each fold. The data were divided into five folds, preserving the proportions of term and PTBs. In each cycle, four folds were used to train the full preprocessing–model pipeline and the remaining fold served as the validation set. This process was repeated until every fold had been used once for validation. For each model and fold, accuracy, AUC, sensitivity, specificity, and the F1-score for PTB were calculated, and the mean and standard deviation across the five folds were taken as the cross-validated performance estimates.

Model evaluation

In addition to a measure of discrimination, the final LR was also assessed on the basis of clinical stratification. Agreeing with risks in the independent test set Model calibration on the independent test set was evaluated through a calibration plot and the Brier score. Probability thresholds that can give clinically interpretable results were examined (0.50 default threshold and ROC Youden J threshold), and the resulting sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value and negative predictive value were obtained. The further measure of clinical utility involved using decision-curve analysis to compare the model’s net benefit with a treat-all and treat-none strategy across a range of threshold probabilities.

Software and tools

All conventional statistical analyses were executed using IBM SPSS (Version 20). All computational analyses were performed using Python libraries including Pandas, Numpy, Scikit-learn, Matplotlib, Seaborn, and Xgboost. Graphical illustrations, confusion matrices, ROC curves, and summary tables were generated using Matplotlib and Seaborn. Data integrity and reproducibility were maintained by using the same train-test split across all algorithms, allowing for direct model comparison.

The development, validation, and reporting of the prediction models were guided by the TRIPOD-ML recommendations to enhance transparency and reproducibility [32].

Correlation among maternal serum markers and PTB

A Pearson correlation analysis was conducted in an exploratory manner to visualize the relationships between the PTB outcome and maternal serum biomarkers and demographics (Supplementary Figure S1). Inflammatory markers PTBwere found to be more significantly correlated with PTB, such as C-reactive protein (CRP; r ≈ 0.45), interleukin-6 (IL-6; r ≈ 0.40) and complement C3 (r ≈ 0.31).

Body mass index (BMI; r ≈ 0.32), triglycerides (r ≈ 0.25), total cholesterol (r ≈ 0.23), and maternal age (r ≈ 0.23) were the metabolic/maternal factors that exhibited moderate positive correlations with the PTB outcome. Conversely, HDL cholesterol showed a weak negative association. Since PTB is a dichotomous outcome, these correlations were presented solely to explain and describe, and were not inferred. The patterns observed have contextual meaning and are further compared using multivariable statistical models and machine learning.

Feature importance across models

Across all models, inflammatory biomarkers consistently emerged as the strongest predictors of PTB. Logistic regression identified CRP as the dominant factor, followed by BMI, maternal age, IL-6, and C3, while lipid measures contributed minimally after adjustment. Feature-importance rankings from the random forest and XGBoost models reinforced this pattern, with CRP, IL-6, and BMI showing the highest contributions, and triglycerides and C3 providing moderate additional value. The SVM model showed a slightly different pattern, placing C3 and maternal age highest, followed by BMI, CRP, and IL-6 (Fig. 2).

Model explainability using SHAP for logistic regression

SHAP analysis of the best-performing LR model showed that IL-6, BMI, and CRP were the dominant contributors to predicted PTB risk (Supplementary Figure S2). The SHAP summary plot further illustrated that higher values of IL-6, BMI, CRP, age, and C3 were associated with positive SHAP values, pushing predictions toward PTB. In contrast, lower values of these features tend to be protective. Lipid variables clustered around zero SHAP, and higher HDL levels were slightly associated with reduced predicted risk (Table 5; Fig. 3). These findings reflect the relative predictive importance of inflammatory markers and maternal characteristics within the model and should be interpreted as influences on model output rather than evidence of causal biological effects.

Clinical performance of logistic regression model

The calibration analysis of the final LR model showed good correspondence between predicted and actual PTB rates, as shown in the calibration plot (Fig. 4). The Brier score also indicated that the model can be calibrated acceptably.

Evaluation based on the threshold revealed balanced performance at the default probability threshold (0.50) and at the ROC-based Youden J threshold, with sensitivities of about 79% and specificities of about 77–81 (Supplementary Table 1).

Decision-curve analysis showed that the LR model had a greater net clinical benefit than either the treat-all or the treat-none approaches across a wide range of clinically relevant threshold probabilities (Supplementary Figure S3), suggesting its potential for risk stratification in clinical practice.

Discussion

In this study, women who delivered preterm had a less favorable inflammatory and metabolic profile. Older age and higher BMI, along with an atherogenic lipid pattern, were associated with PTB conditions, along with elevated inflammatory markers, including IL-6, CRP, and C3. In the altered models, maternal age, BMI, IL-6, C3, and especially CRP were significant predictors of PTB. All four machine-learning models performed reasonably well, with AUCs above 0.82 on the test set. The mean cross-validated AUCs were in the range of 0.86–0.87. The LR performed similarly and occasionally better than the other complicated models. SHAP analysis was also consistent with the regression results, indicating that IL-6, BMI, CRP, maternal age, and C3 were the strongest predictors.

The performance of all four machine learning models in our study is comparable to previous studies [1, 33, 34] that used these algorithms to predict PTB using maternal biomarkers.

In our study, the LR model performed somewhat better than machine learning models. Other studies that compared machine learning models to conventional LR to predict various clinical conditions also showed that, in general, no single method consistently provides the best prediction [35, 36]. The reason LR has shown superior performance might be due to our dataset characteristics, i.e., a smaller cohort size, a predominance of roughly linear relationships (e.g., high BMI and PTB), or fewer high-dimensional relationships [37, 38]. Although more complex machine-learning models were evaluated, LR demonstrated comparable performance with superior interpretability, suggesting that simpler models may be sufficient for practical clinical risk stratification.

According to earlier literature, inflammation is the primary cause of preterm labor and birth due to the infiltration of bacteria into the mother’s uterus [39] and the subsequent activation of maternal and fetal immune responses [40]. Chorioamnionitis, characterized by inflammation of the fetal membranes, is frequently linked to such infections and leads to elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6 and IL-1β. These mediators enhance uterine contractions and compromise the integrity of the membranes, thereby increasing the risk of premature delivery [41]. CRP, on the other hand, is generally known as an easy available marker of inflammatory stress. In early or mid-pregnancy, an increase in CRP has long been associated with a shorter duration of pregnancy (gestation) [42, 43].The complement system is increasingly recognized as a key contributor to PTB, as its activation has been linked to enhancing myometrium contractions, cervical collagen remodeling, and the recruitment of inflammatory immune cells [44, 45].

The complement cascade facilitates the recruitment and activation of neutrophils and macrophages [46, 47]. In PTB, activated macrophages promote the release of matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9), which degrades cervical collagen, contributing to tissue remodeling, distension, and eventual dilation [46, 48].

Women who had a preterm delivery showed greater levels of triglycerides, total cholesterol, and LDL cholesterol than normal women. Elevated levels of cholesterol and triglycerides during early pregnancy force beyond 2.8 times for pre-delivery in less than 34 weeks and two times in the case of 34–37 weeks of pre-delivery [49]. These findings suggest that pregnancy-related lipid alterations may reflect underlying inflammatory or infectious processes, as hypertriglyceridemia can function as an innate immune response and heightened inflammatory activity may contribute to hypercholesterolemia [50].

Nevertheless, most lipid components lost their high significance after controlling for age, BMI, and inflammatory markers. This interpretation is consistent with evidence that metabolic stress, adiposity, and inflammation are linked during pregnancy. Dyslipidemia is probably indicative of a higher-risk metabolic state, but our data demonstrate that inflammatory markers and BMI more directly predict PTB in this population.

If you are overweight or obese before pregnancy, you are at a higher risk for gestational hypertension and gestational diabetes [51, 52], both of which increase the likelihood of labor induction or planned cesarean delivery [53].

Given the rising trend in advanced maternal age and obesity globally, these findings reinforce the need for strategies that support maternal metabolic health before and during pregnancy. Interventions focused on weight optimization, improved nutrition, and management of chronic inflammation may help lower the risk of PTB, although further evidence from interventional research is needed. Although numerous machine learning models for PTB prediction exist, relatively few have been developed using this biomarker panel, especially in LMICs, where patient demographics and clinical characteristics differ from high-income countries. Thus, this study is particularly important for providing realistic, reproducible insights into the subcontinent population. Given their high AUC and accuracy, these machine learning models can be applied early in pregnancy, enabling timely identification of high-risk individuals and prompt interventions to reduce PTB, particularly in LMICs. Future research should increase sample sizes and incorporate multicenter data to enhance generalizability.

A better performance of LR model in our study highlights an important message: the choice of predictors and data quality often matter more than model complexity. Earlier research has shown that Linear Kernel SVM and LR are sensitive to variable scaling, and that appropriate normalization can improve their ability to detect meaningful patterns in the dataset [1].

The SHAP analysis was used to complement conventional regression, as it showed the direction and relative strength of each feature’s contribution to PTB risk stratification. Increased IL-6, BMI, CRP, maternal age, and complement C3 values consistently shifted the association toward high risk of PTB, and low values shifted the balance toward term birth. Interestingly, the importance of the predictors ranked by SHAP correlated well with odds ratios of LR and feature importance calculated by tree models, which makes the overall results of such models more credible.

Our study’s findings lend support to the routine use of a focused panel of easily measurable biomarkers namely CRP, IL-6 and C3, in conjunction with age and BMI, which may assist in early identification of women at higher risk of PTB. The machine learning models have shown good discriminatory performance in the current dataset, but this is achieved through internal validation. Model robustness and generalizability should be established through independent cohort validation in different clinical environments before implementation in clinical practice. Following external validation, the final LR model could be translated into a nomogram or simple risk-scoring tool to facilitate individualized PTB risk estimation in clinical practice.

The models showed similar sensitivity and specificity; the next step is external validation before clinical use. Even so, these results show how biomarker-informed risk stratification could be used to monitor closely, refer in a timely manner and deliver targeted preventive measures.

The primary contribution of this study is the application of advanced machine learning models for PTB risk discrimination while maintaining paired with strong SHAP-based interpretability. By measuring the contribution of each predictor, SHAP improves the model’s transparency and clinical relevance, providing clinicians with a clearer, more reliable decision-support tool [33]. Transparent interpretability is especially critical for clinicians in LMIC settings because they often work with limited resources, high patient loads, and variable data quality.

This study has several limitations. First, participants were recruited from only two tertiary care centers, which may limit the representativeness of the sample and the generalizability of the findings to the broader population. Although the sample size was adequate for the current analyses, it remains moderate for machine-learning applications. Biomarker measurements were obtained during the second and third trimesters; therefore, conclusions regarding earlier pregnancy screening cannot be drawn. In addition, some clinical and environmental factors associated with PTB were not fully controlled, and detailed information on gravidity and parity was not uniformly available. While internal validation demonstrated model stability, external validation in larger, multicenter cohorts is necessary to confirm generalizability and clinical applicability. For future studies, we should validate the findings in larger and more diverse populations. Additionally, we may explore early pregnancy or longitudinal trajectories of biomarkers. Finally, we should integrate biochemical, clinical, or ultrasound parameters into a joint model. PTB.

Conclusion

This study shows that PTB is closely linked to maternal inflammatory and metabolic status. Higher CRP, IL-6, C3, BMI, and maternal age were the strongest classifiers. All machine-learning models performed well, with LR matching more complex approaches and offering clearer interpretability. However, these findings should be considered exploratory, and further validation in external and multi-center populations is required before clinical application.
