Interplay between gut health, immunity, and bone development in broilers: role of feed additives
Authors: Ashen Epa Arachchi, Anh Dang Trieu Do, Adnan Alrubaye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The continuous growth of the global poultry industry is driven by high consumer demand and, in response, industrial improvements in poultry nutrition, bird health, and many other aspects of production. This comprehensive review presents and discusses various feed additives and supplements commonly used in the broiler industry (such as pro/prebiotics, phytogenic additives, organic acids, PUFA & SCFA-based additives, trace minerals and nano-minerals, miscellaneous feed additives and emerging biotechnological additives), their main functions as well as interactive nature with the broiler’s gut, bone and immune systems to keep the animals productive and healthy. Special emphasis has been placed on the gut-bone, gut-immune, and immune-bone axes in describing the role of feed additives, with the novel "trifecta concept" serving as the central and conceptual framework for explaining the interactions among these axes. This review will provide useful insights for different sectors of the poultry and livestock industries, with prospective applications across associated disciplines, and future directions within this paradigm, ultimately contributing to robust and sustainable broiler production to feed an ever-evolving world.

Introduction

The global broiler industry has expanded rapidly over the years as one of the fast-growing livestock sectors while ensuring global food security (Adaszyńska-Skwirzyńska et al., 2025; Neeteson et al., 2023; Wilcox et al., 2024). As a result of the rising consumer demand, intensive production systems now produce 95% of the global poultry meat and this trend is expected to expand in developing countries by 2030 (Dal Bosco et al., 2021). This continuous growth has largely been driven by advances in broiler breeding, nutrition, and precision management practices. For instance, a market age weight of 2.26 kg—which would have required 52 days of growth in 1992—now only takes 41 days (Maharjan et al., 2021; Zuidhof et al., 2014) thanks to significant improvements in production strategies and genetic advancements in the modern broiler (Korver, 2023). However, such advancements have also led to undesirable biological and physiological problems in the bird. Vigorous selection for quick growth and high feed efficiency has resulted in metabolic and physical abnormalities (Korver, 2023; Rauw et al., 1998) pulmonary hypertension syndrome (ascites), sudden death syndrome, cellulitis, and myopathies (woody breast syndrome, deep pectoral myopathy) (Julian, 2005; Kalmar et al., 2013). These conditions jeopardize the broiler industry’s bottom line and economics due to poor performance and increased mortality rates during their production cycle. For example, lameness is a major contributor to economic losses in broiler production, as it not only increases mortality but also reduces growth and overall performance, ultimately lowering productivity (Pearce et al., 2023). In the United States alone, where 9.17 billion broilers were produced in 2022 according to the USDA, bacterial chondronecrosis with osteomyelitis (BCO) has been associated with estimated losses ranging from $80 million to $120 million (Granquist et al., 2019; Yousaf et al., 2021). The development of BCO begins with bacterial translocation, followed by hematogenous spread to the bones, especially in the tibia and femur where infection and necrosis occur, eventually causing lameness in birds (Wideman, 2016). As another example of broiler bone deformities contributing to lameness, tibial dyschondroplasia (TD) is also mainly observed in tibial heads, though mostly differing from BCO in pathological mechanisms and development. Avian tibial dyschondroplasia occurs when growth plate chondrocytes in the tibiotarsus fail to mature properly, dying prematurely and producing insufficient extracellular proteins such as collagen X and fibroblast growth factor-β. This results in immature, highly cross-linked cartilage that resists resorption and vascularization by metaphyseal blood vessels, causing it to accumulate in the metaphyseal region (Orth and Cook, 1994). At the same time, health issues related to broiler gut health (Van Immerseel et al., 2009; Venkatas and Adeleke, 2019), immunity (Fathima and Selvaraj, 2022), and common infectious diseases (Swidan et al., 2020) also cause economic losses in the broiler industry. The supplementation of feed additives (such as pro/prebiotics, phytogenic additives, organic acids, and more) to a robust basal diet has thus become a popular strategy among producers seeking to improve flock health and productivity. In addition, broiler producers have also adopted alternative health-supporting strategies, vaccination programs and improved welfare practices to ensure proper flock health (Acheampong, 2024; Unveren and Luckstead, 2020).

Despite these recent insights, no multisectoral review has integrated gut health, bone development, and immune functions into a single framework to assess feed additives in broiler production. Thus, this comprehensive review aims to synthesize and present up-to-date information in poultry research regarding the gut-bone-immunity within a “trifecta concept”, focusing on the interplay between these systems. Most existing multisectoral reviews have focused on only one or two axes at a time, or have been limited to one or two feed additive categories. This review directly addresses this timely need by integrating all three axes across a broader range of additive categories. Additionally, it also aims to highlight the potential influence of feed additives on this overarching relationship from a holistic point of view—an aspect yet to be extensively investigated among the current body of works.

Literature search strategy

The literature search was primarily conducted using keywords such as gut-bone-immune interactions, feed additives/supplements, probiotics, prebiotics, phytogenic feed additives, organic acids, PUFA- and SCFA-based additives, trace minerals, nano-minerals, and broilers using PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar. However, this search resulted in very few papers covering all three systems simultaneously. Therefore, the same search terms were used again, replacing "broilers" with "poultry" and "layer chicken". This refined search notably returned papers addressing two of the three systems at once with respect to each feed additive category.

The same search was extended to rats and rabbits, as these are prominent species in most laboratory experiments. Therefore, a separate search was conducted using same keywords. The underlying pathways have been well studied in rat and mouse models, and this search returned a substantial amount of literature. In all these searches, we did not consider any specific time period of publication. However, priority was given to newly published information and knowledge.

In total, approximately 700 research and review papers were identified across all searches. The final paper selection was based on scientific quality and relevance to the scope of this review. The majority of papers related to feed additives were excluded due to repeated findings or overlapping information. Ultimately, approximately 320 papers were selected overall and examined in detail for this review.

Gut-immune axis

The digestive, bone, circulatory, and respiratory systems are very important and collectively act in the body to maintain vital functions. Gut health is a driving factor in the body that supports its normal functionality. Kogut and Arsenault (2016) defined gut health as “the absence/prevention/avoidance of disease so that the animal is able to perform its physiological functions in order to withstand exogenous and endogenous stressors”. Later, Celi et al. (2017) defined that gut health is “a steady-state, where the microbiome and the intestinal tract exist in symbiotic equilibrium, and where the welfare and performance of the animal are not constrained by intestinal dysfunction”. Regardless of author perspective, both definitions signify the importance of a harmonious intestinal environment capable of supporting animal welfare and productivity without stressors, emphasizing prominent interactions of gut components with the host animal (Wickramasuriya et al., 2022). Building on the critical role of gut health, it is important to recognize the gastrointestinal tract’s role as a central component of the immune system in broilers, directly influencing immune responses and overall disease resistance. The intestine is a vital organ that, in addition to digesting food and absorbing nutrients, supports the immune system in the fight against antigens (E lTawab et al., 2019). In particular, the gastrointestinal epithelium functions as a key physical barrier in innate (nonspecific) immunity, preventing the translocation of luminal antigens and bacteria into the bloodstream (Perera et al., 2024), thereby highlighting the close relationship between the intestinal tract and the immune system. It is a general fact that disruption of this barrier can compromise immune function and increase susceptibility to infections in broilers. Dinev (2012) reported that disruption of intestinal barrier function, accompanied by alterations in gut microbial composition, can significantly influence cytokine production in intestinal immune cells, thereby promoting intestinal inflammation. Poor gut health facilitates pathogen invasion and toxin accumulation, leading to a negative effect on overall broiler performance (Shojadoost et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2022). In contrast, a well-functioning intestinal mucosa, evenly weighted gut microbiota, and a sensitive, robust immune system are all indicators of proper gut health, contributing to better productive outcomes in the bird. For instance, the intestinal mucosa plays an important function in shaping immune responses (Hrnčíř, 2008) and early immune system development, which in turn is strongly affected by the composition of gut microbiota (Bach, 2002).

Immune-bone axis

The avian bone system is highly complicated, with various roles that support structural integrity and uphold mineral homeostasis in the body, especially regarding calcium (Ca) and phosphorus (P) (Kim et al., 2012; Lorenzo et al., 2008; Regmi et al., 2017; Whitehead, 2004). Bone metabolism is primarily regulated by hormonal signals and local factors within the bone microenvironment, which together maintain the dynamic balance between bone formation and resorption by osteoblasts and osteoclasts, respectively. Bone is a dynamic tissue that is continuously renewed, with the immune system playing a major role in this process. Both bone and immune cells originate from common progenitor cells in the bone marrow (Dar et al., 2018a) facilitating immune cell formation and residence (Sharma et al., 2023), which allows hematopoietic stem cells and immune cells (T cells, macrophages, neutrophils and B cells) to mature and fight against etiological pathogens (Dar et al., 2018a; Lorenzo et al., 2008; Tsukasaki and Takayanagi, 2019). Furthermore, Dar et al. (2018a) reported that bone homeostasis is largely regulated by various immune responses under both normal and pathogenic conditions along with aging. Pathogen-associated and immune-mediated bone diseases are characterized by the overproduction of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), which disrupt normal bone remodeling processes (Lu et al., 2025). In general, Eimeria and Clostridium perfringens infections interfere with several cytokines that are involved in Receptor Activator of Nuclear Factor Kappa-B Ligand (RANKL) expression and play an important role in osteoclast formation (Sharma et al., 2023). TNF-α strongly inhibits osteoblast differentiation by affecting multiple transcription factors and reduces the commitment of precursor cells toward the osteoblast lineage (Kotake and Nanke, 2014). This increasing body of evidence suggests that the immune system is a key contributor to the maintenance of bone health. With regards to origin, bone cells and immune cells also share strong interactions between one another, especially in osteoblast and immune cell formation such as dendritic cells and macrophages (Dar et al., 2018a; Lorenzo et al., 2008; Regmi et al., 2017). In avian species, immune cells, chemokines, and cytokines are involved in bone remodeling during infectious diseases (Sharma et al., 2023). Inflamatory mediators and cytokines are driven factors of bone formation and remodeling. Osteoclast genesis is driven by pro-inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α, interleukin-17 (IL-17), and macrophage colony-stimulating factors (M-CSF) (Kim et al., 2019), whereas Treg cells play a protective role by suppressing the differentiation of monocytes into osteoclasts (Mitra et al., 2018). Interestingly, some studies have found that these immunological shifts are closely linked to the mechanical properties of the tibia due to inflammation-mediated changes within (Prajwal et al., 2016). Similarly, Clarke et al. (2017) reported that osteoclast numbers and immune cell populations are strongly associated—particularly CD4+ T cells, as well as levels of IL-6, RANKL, and TNF-α—in local bones.

Nutritional interventions further help modulate immune-related compounds that are directly involved in maintaining bone health. For example, laying hens supplemented with calcium butyrate showed reduced circulating levels of IL-1 and TNF-α, along with increased TNF-β and immunoglobulin A (IgA), indicating improved immune balance (Sentíes-Cué et al., 2009). In current literature, TNF-α and IL-1 are known to be involved in osteoclastic activation and osteoclast genesis through the RANK signaling pathway (Adamopoulos et al., 2010; Boyce and Xing, 2008). Collectively, these findings emphasize that immunomodulation has a direct impact on bone health and can be targeted through dietary strategies and feed additives, further ensuring the close relationship between the bone and immune systems in maintaining overall physiological balance in the body.

Bone-gut axis

Recent studies show that gut microbiota (GM) plays an important role in maintaining bone quantity, quality and strength in poultry (De Sire et al., 2022). It supports bone health by interacting with the immune, nervous, endocrine, and gut barrier systems through the host’s circulatory system, and influencing bone signaling pathways through production of metabolites and ligands (Lu et al., 2025). For example, an imbalance in the gut microbiota (dysbiosis) can reduce Ca absorption in the intestine, resulting in lower bone strength and mass (Liu et al., 2021a). Bone health is also governed by gut health and many other various interactions within. Some studies have reported that disruption of the intestinal mucosal homeostasis-induced inflammatory response is closely related to bone formation and maturation (Li et al., 2016b; Sugiharto, 2016). Notably, the strength of the gastrointestinal barrier system also depends on functionality of tight junctions—intercellular adhesion sites between the epithelia and endothelia (Shehata et al., 2022) which regulates paracellular permeability (Perera et al., 2024; von Buchholz et al., 2022). These TJ proteins prevent passage of microorganisms, toxic substances and antigens from the gut lumen to the bloodstream (Ballard et al., 1995; Ulluwishewa et al., 2011), the disruption or destruction of which can lead to “leaky gut” syndrome (von Buchholz et al., 2022) which is a primary postulation for bacterial lameness in broilers. Degradation of tight junctions is a sequential process, mediated by bacterial factors (Perera et al., 2024) as shown in Fig. 2. Damage to the intestinal barrier can allow bacteria and toxins to leak into the bloodstream, causing systemic inflammation. This process is linked to inflammatory and immune-related bone disorders, such as osteoporosis and arthritis, and may reduce bone quality in poultry (Zhang et al., 2022). Notably, poor gut health reduces nutrient uptake and interrupts normal nutrient metabolism. Resultant complications such as TD, the mechanisms of which revolve around poor Ca and vitamin D absorption, can thus be partially attributed to such impairments caused by subpar gut health (Khan et al., 2010; Ledwaba and Roberson, 2003).Fig. 2Progression of tight junction protein destruction. As many signaling pathways share common metabolites within these systems, dysfunction in one component can cause negative effects on bones and intestinal immune cell functions (Fakharian et al., 2023). For example, alterations in intestinal integrity have been associated with changes in bone resorption markers and elevated levels of inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TGF-β in poultry (Wickramasuriya et al., 2022) which are closely related to bone formation and resorption (Adamopoulos and Bowman, 2008; Martin, 2005). Furthermore, when the gut does not function properly, the composition of the gut microbiota is often altered. As a result, gut microbiota-associated epithelial cytokine expression and activity of T and B lymphocytes, as well as macrophages, are also changed (Cianci et al., 2019; Kany et al., 2019). This disruption negatively affects gut homeostasis by disturbing the balance among epithelial barrier-protective, pro-inflammatory, and anti-inflammatory cytokines. More importantly, these cytokine imbalances also influence the overall function of the immune system (Van der Meide and Schellekens, 1996). In turn, immune dysfunction can further impair gut function and weaken the body’s overall defense against pathogens.

Given these findings, the relationship between the gut–bone axis is very prominent, with bone formation and resorption processes being influenced by many factors in the gut such as intestinal metabolites, short-chain fatty acids (Zaiss et al., 2019), vitamins, and bile acids (Du et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2025), tryptophan metabolites (Ouyang et al., 2022), and vitamin D3 metabolites (Kitay and Geibel, 2017). In chickens, the gut microbiota affects overall bone metabolism by producing tryptophan metabolites and short-chain fatty acids that enter the bloodstream and help regulate bone formation and resorption balance (Wang et al., 2023a). Given this background, the interconnected nature of bone and gut systems is well-explained and is highly important when considering dietary interventions to keep broilers health and productive.

The trifecta

As presented in Fig. 1, the gut-immune-bone trifecta shares an intimate, interconnected relationship among its components, with each part playing vital roles in the others’ functions. For example, the gut microbiota plays a crucial role in bone metabolism by modulating the immune-bone axis. It influences bone formation and remodeling through immune system regulation, modulation of inflammatory cytokines, and the absorption of essential nutrients such as Ca and vitamin D (Ahmad et al., 2009; Wickramasuriya et al., 2022). In addition, a growing body of evidence further supports the link between gut microbiota composition and immune cell populations, particularly helper T lymphocyte 17 (Th17) and regulatory T cells (Treg), which directly influence bone remodeling (Sharman et al., 2010). Under conditions of intestinal dysbiosis, Th17 cells become activated and migrate to peripheral tissues—including joints—promoting bone loss through enhanced RANKL expression and osteoclast activation (Wickramasuriya et al., 2022). Collectively, these findings highlight the intricate interrelationship among the gut, immune, and bone systems in broilers, emphasizing their coordinated role in maintaining overall health and physiological function.

For example, oxidative stress is one of many influencing factors that fuel this interrelation between the axes of the trifecta. Oxidative stress arises in broilers from multiple ways, such as mycotoxins in feed and pathogenic invasion of the intestinal mucosa. Mycotoxins trigger reactive oxygen species production, which disrupts cellular redox balance and weakens antioxidant defenses (Mishra and Jha, 2019). Heat stress, which can cause excessive immune stress (Rostagno, 2020) can activate the TLR4-mediated inflammatory signaling pathway, leading to increased production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and oxidative stress (Niu et al., 2022). Pathogen contact with the gut mucosa also elevates oxidative stress (Mishra and Jha, 2019). These redox changes affect several biological processes: they influence gut microbiota composition through redox signaling (Jones and Neish, 2017), support immune cell development and function (Lauridsen, 2019), and modulate NF-κB inflammatory pathways (Surai et al., 2021). Finally, oxidative stress also downregulates genes required for bone formation in broilers (Tompkins et al., 2023). These findings clearly indicate that the gut, immune system, and bone system are functionally linked. Redox disturbances originating in the intestine can impair immunity and simultaneously impair bone development. Thus, immune activation can influence gut barrier integrity and microbial balance. From a bird’s eye view, this evidence highlights the interdependence nature of the gut–immune–bone trifecta in broiler chickens, where disruption in any one component affects the performance of the other two.

To further illustrate this relationship within the trifecta and broiler health outcomes, let us consider a hypothetical scenario involving the modern broiler in an intensive environment. Having been exposed to subpar nutrition and mycotoxin contamination that predisposes “leaky gut” pathology, the broiler is subjected to increased oxidative stress, poor gut health, as well as immunosuppression and inflammation due to physiological stress from rapid biological growth and the immediate environment, which negatively impacts bone homeostasis via associated signaling pathways and metabolites/cytokines. At the same time, acquired pathogenic bacteria may translocate more easily through compromised epithelial barriers and reduced immune defense, causing hematogenous osteomyelitis, further impacting bone health and immune status in the animal. Finally, as the broiler becomes lame and/or paralyzed from osteomyelitis, feed and water intake is dramatically reduced, resulting in a downward spiral that ends in the bird’s death if not properly addressed. Aligning with the presented hypothetical scenario, leg disorders—such as muscle weakness, joint lesions, bone deformities, and femoral head necrosis—can lead to lameness, paralysis, and eventual mortality (Xu et al., 2022). These conditions have been documented to arise from multiple factors, including genetics, infections, nutrition, management practices, toxins, trauma, and incubation-related issues (Tullo et al., 2017; Waldenstedt, 2006).

As a worst-case outcome, such a scenario might not be as far-fetched as one may think. In fact, this exact scenario has been previously investigated by Alharbi et al. (2024b) where broilers exposed to mycotoxin contamination in the feed—under a high-stress wire flooring experimental model used to investigate BCO (Wideman, 2016)—experienced increased intestinal permeability and clinical lameness incidence, signifying a direct impact in all three axes of the proposed “trifecta concept”. Several other studies investigating BCO have also documented similar outcomes that may be rescued through inclusion of high-quality organic trace minerals (Zn, Mn, Cu) in the diet (Alrubaye et al., 2020) and/or other effective feed additives as mitigative and preventive measures (Alharbi et al., 2025a; Asnayanti et al., 2024; Perera et al., 2024). As discussed in subsequent sections, these additives can directly influence multiple axes within the trifecta, resulting in positive health outcomes for the broilers—such as reduced clinical BCO lameness incidence in these studies.

However, despite the importance of considering all mentioned systems in poultry health, there’s a marked lack of literature covering all three at once. Considering this intricate interplay between all systems in bird health outcomes, it behooves future research directions to investigate these parameters from a more holistic point of view to develop better, more effective solutions to poultry production. Given the strong interconnection between the gut, immune systems, targeted modulation through feed additives offers a promising strategy to improve bone integrity and overall health status in broiler production systems.

Categories of feed additives and supplements

Feed additives and dietary supplements are widely used in modern poultry production to improve growth performance, feed efficiency, gut health, immunity, and overall bird health and productivity. As a result, a wide range of feed additives—including probiotics, prebiotics, organic acids, phytogenic compounds, enzymes, minerals, and functional biomolecules—has been investigated for their ability to enhance intestinal integrity, nutrient utilization, immune responses, and bone health in poultry production systems. Ultimately, these health-promoting effects enhance broiler production performance by improving feed efficiency, growth performance, and overall productivity.

Probiotics

Probiotics are beneficial bacteria that can regulate host gut homeostasis by enhancing intestinal functions, protecting the intestinal mucosa, and maintaining the immune response as described in Table 1, Table 2. (Xu et al., 2022). Additionally, probiotics have been more extensively studied across all axes than other feed additives; therefore, they have been discussed in greater detail. Modification of intestinal microbiota by probiotic and symbiotic supplementation is a popular therapeutical approach to disease prevention in humans (Mars et al., 2021; Settanni et al., 2021; Shaik et al., 2020). Similarly, their usage is also common in modern poultry production. These additives can impart antibiotic-like effects (Nkosi et al., 2021) especially with supplemental use of Bacillus, lactic acid bacteria, bifidobacteria, Enterococcus, and yeast (Khalique et al., 2020; Neveling and Dicks, 2021; Tarradas et al., 2020). However, certain strains of probiotic Bacillus spp. are also used to achieve proper gut, bone health and immunity in broilers, as shown in Table 3. Therefore, probiotics can be considered as a promising solution to prevent the development of antibiotic resistance in broiler production, which has significant growing implications to human health (Castro-Vargas et al., 2020). In addition, probiotic use has shown strong potential as a therapeutic strategy to prevent and/or reduce BCO clinical lameness incidence (Alharbi et al., 2025b; Anthney et al., 2025; Do et al., 2024) potentially owing to their beneficial effects in mineral absorption, ability to regulate anti-inflammatory responses (Behera et al., 2020; Li et al., 2021) and improvement of gut integrity. Given these effects, probiotics can strengthen the connections within the gut-immune-bone trifecta by well supported findings such as enhancing gut barrier function, chemical barrier function, and immune responses. Notably, emphasizing the growing importance of probiotics, Bermúdez-Humarán et al. (2019) and Del Toro-Barbosa et al. (2020) reported that probiotics can help restore ecological stability of the gut microbiota by not only suppressing pathogenic organisms but also promoting the growth of beneficial bacteria through production of bioactive and immunomodulatory compounds. At the same time, probiotics play a vital role in maintaining normal physiological functions by alleviating stress in broilers through reducing detrimental intestinal bacteria abundance, decreasing inflammation, and enhancing gut integrity (Xu et al., 2022) through the expression of TJ proteins (Bilal et al., 2021; Li et al., 2025a). Probiotics can also improve immunity in terms of elevated level of IgA, IgG, IL-10 (Alqhtani et al., 2026; Hassanlou et al., 2026), increased antioxidant capacity and immune response (Aljumaa et al., 2025), and facilitated inflammatory regulation (Filidou et al., 2024). Specifically, immunity and inflammatory regulation directly connect gut health, immunity and bone health (Debnath et al., 2019; Sharma et al., 2023) within the trifecta. Prebiotics

Prebiotics were first defined as “a nondigestible food ingredient that beneficially affects the host by selectively stimulating the growth and/or activity of one or limited number of bacteria in the colon and thus improves host health” (Gibson and Roberfroid, 1995). This definition was further revised by Gibson et al. (2004) as ingredients which are selectively fermented in the gut, thereby enabling specific modifications in the composition and/or activity of the gut microbiota that benefit the host’s health and well-being. In general, prebiotics can be broadly classified into three main groups: carbohydrates, phenolics and phytochemicals, and additionally polyunsaturated fatty acids (Kocot et al., 2022). Administration of prebiotics has been shown to impart antibacterial effects (Bednarczyk et al., 2016) and raise the number of intestinal microorganisms and their composition (Pourabedin and Zhao, 2015), thereby boosting beneficial microorganisms and positive physiological outcomes (Limbu et al., 2024). This microbial boost connects the immune-bone axes and facilitates bone development by enhancing Ca absorption and regulating the production of gut-derived serotonin, a molecule that interacts with bone cells and has been suggested to act as a bone mass regulator. (D’Amelio and Sassi, 2018). In poultry production, common prebiotics include many types of oligosaccharides, inulin, lactulose, lactitol, and pyrometries (Gadde et al., 2017; Lin et al., 2022; Steiner, 2006) that can be effectively utilized in poultry nutrition to achieve desirable health attributes—including gut health modulation (Albala et al., 2004; Everard et al., 2011; Kyoung et al., 2023) and enhanced gut functions (Guo et al., 2023a; Hooge et al., 2003; Khan et al., 2026; Shao et al., 2013; Shehata et al., 2022). In terms of bone health, prebiotic supplementation has also shown promising results related to direct findings associated with bone parameters. Feeding inulin improved tibial length, growth plate thickness, strength, and weight (Li et al., 2025c). Similarly, Aspergillus meal increased the bone strength of broiler chickens (Reginatto et al., 2011) and improved tibia weight, diameter, breaking strength, as well as ash, Ca, and phosphorus levels in turkey poults (Reginatto et al., 2011). As a prominent immunomodulator, prebiotic supplementation also results in increased spleen and bursa weight (Al-Surrayai and Al-Khalaifah, 2022), improved innate immunity (Stefaniak et al., 2019), and elevated IgG levels in blood (Stefaniak et al., 2020). With such proven effects on immunity and gut health, prebiotics also regulate bone health through the gut-bone axis via shared mechanisms and pathways associated within such as TNFα (McCabe et al., 2015).

Phytogenic feed additives

Phytogenic feed additives are bioactive compounds obtained from plants and plant products (Gadde et al., 2017) such as essential oils, oleoresins, tannins, saponins, flavonoids, alkaloids and resin acids. Different combinations of naturally found chemicals are present in plant extracts—including berberine, phyto cannabinoids, isoflavones, isoquinoline alkaloids, phenolic derivatives, quercetin, thymol/carvacrol, resveratrol, and curcumin. Interestingly, several commercially available products have been formulated using a variety of herbal ingredients (Máthé, 2009). Such ingredients are very popular in the field of broiler nutrition due to their safe chemical profiles and health-promoting nature through antioxidant, pharmacological, antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory effects, in addition to enhanced feed digestibility (Salinas-Chavira and Barrios-García, 2024) and modulation of gut microbiota (Shehata et al., 2022). Moreover, phytogenic additives can improve gut microbiota and thereby improve immunity, showing the clear connection between the gut-immunity axis (Oni and Oke, 2025). This is achieved through mucosal immunity enhancement and gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT) regulation via activation of innate immune responses through toll-like receptor signaling and modulation of cytokine production—including interleukins and interferons (Ayalew et al., 2025). With respect to gut microbiota modulation, phytogenic additives promote the growth of beneficial bacterial groups, including Firmicutes (Li et al., 2018), as well as members of the families Ruminococcaceae, Lachnospiraceae, and the order Clostridiales (Díaz Carrasco et al., 2018). Furthermore, the antibacterial effects of phytogenic additives (Dai, 2013; Orhan et al., 2012; Vimal et al., 2013) reduce Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus spp. infections in the body that may contribute to bone-related diseases in poultry (Rodgers et al., 1999; Vlaicu et al., 2020). Organic acids, PUFA & SCFA-based additives

In poultry nutrition and health management, additives based on organic acids, polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), and short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) are widely utilized. While PUFAs serve as structural components of membrane phospholipids during embryogenesis and influence the fatty acid composition of immune cells and the gastrointestinal tract (Thanabalan and Kiarie, 2021), SCFAs act as major substrates for energy production in enterocytes and intestinal modulation, lowering the colonization of pathogens (Liu et al., 2021b). Furthermore, SCFAs contribute to broiler health and productivity by improving barrier function and extracellular matrix stability, as well as enhancing immune responses (Gavrylenko et al., 2026). It has been hypothesized that such dietary components participate in various biochemical pathways within the body, leading to multiple beneficial effects such as feed appetite regulation (Frost et al., 2014), inhibit the proliferation of Enterobacteriaceae to maintain overall intestinal homeostasis (Liu et al., 2021). Similarly, they contribute to immunomodulation (Rubio, 2019) and bone development through stimulating associated signaling pathways (Kruger et al., 2010), reducing the growth of harmful bacteria from antimicrobial activity (Tugnoli et al., 2020), supporting gut and bone health through shared common metabolites and pathways associated with the gut-bone axis (Kruger et al., 2010), and helping maintain production performance at optimal levels. As an emerging hypothesis, organic acids may also function as immunomodulators in broilers by influencing multiple physiological pathways that help maintain a favorable immune status for disease tolerance, as has been demonstrated in mice-related study (Du et al., 2023). Some organic acids can trigger the production of immune cells and signaling molecules such as cytokines, which enhance the body’s ability to fight against pathogens (Duan et al., 2023). Organic acids also improve gut microbiome and immunity (Peng et al., 2026; Samy et al., 2026), intestinal morphology and upregulated intestinal expression of TJ proteins in pigs (Yang et al., 2026), as well as increase tibial bone length, width, and ash content in broiler chicken (Zaman et al., 2026).

While individual organic acids may exhibit isolated standalone inhibitory effects, their efficacy can vary depending on the combinations used, with certain combinations demonstrating stronger synergistic activity as described by Liu et al. As has been demonstrated in mice-related studies, oleic acid influences the gut microbiota by promoting a balanced microbial community, strengthening intestinal barrier function, and regulating the production of microbiota-derived metabolites, including short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), bile acids, and indole derivatives. These metabolites in turn contribute to bone remodeling by activating signaling pathways mediated through free fatty acid receptors, bile acid receptors, and the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (Ayalew et al., 2025). Trace minerals and nano-minerals

Minerals are one of the key pillars in poultry nutrition due to their vital role in maintaining optimum growth and production in broilers (Park et al., 2004). Of the 26 elements which are involved for animal life (Underwood, 1971), Ca, phosphorus (P), magnesium (Mg), manganese (Mn), iron (Fe), zinc (Zn), and selenium (Se) can be considered essential in poultry nutrition and feed additives. As key elements in broiler mineral nutrition, Ca and P are critical for promoting bone mineralization and reducing the risk of bone disorders (Waldenstedt, 2006). Other elements—including Fe, Zn, Mn, and Cu—are essential for regulating physiological functions such as enzymatic activities, immune functions (Kawahara et al., 2023), bone formation via regulating signaling pathways (Kim and Choi, 2013), and bone integrity (Ciacciariello et al., 2021). As an indispensable component in broiler rations, many recent studies have reported that these minerals improve intestinal microbial composition and overall gut health in broiler chickens (Wang et al., 2023a).

Several trace minerals have been reported to play important roles in poultry gut health, immunity, and bone development. Zn has been shown to improve gut health (Li et al., 2006) and enhance immune responses through the stimulation of antibody production as well as B- and T-cell activity (Adli et al., 2026). In addition, Zn directly facilitates the invasion of osteoblast-related cells into the cartilage matrix (Dibner et al., 2007) and promotes endochondral ossification during bone development (Fraker, 2005). Apart from such effects, dietary Zn glycine supplementation showed improved tibia quality of meat ducks through regulating intestinal barrier and bone resorption (Wang et al., 2023b), indicating interplay between gut-bone axis. Copper (Cu) has also been identified as a potential intestinal modulator due to its role in improving intestinal structural integrity (Richards et al., 2010). Similarly, Mn regulates the expression of genes involved in several metabolic pathways, particularly those associated with immune functions (Bernardi et al., 2023), and chromium (Cr) has also been recognized for its immune-boosting properties in poultry (Kim et al., 2026).

Studies within the past several years have also closely investigated the effects of trace mineral elements on bone health, with particular attention to Zn, Mn, and Cu. Recently, Alharbi et al. (2024a) reported reduction of BCO lameness up to 20% to 25% in broilers with supplementation of organic Zn, Mn, and Cu (Availa® ZMC) in the broiler diet. This effect is hypothesized to have derived mainly from improved gut integrity and immunity in broilers rather than a direct impact on bones. Supplementation of methionine hydroxy analog bis-chelated zinc has also been shown to improve apparent mineral (Cu, Zn, Ca) retention, enhance tibial strength, and increase Zn and Ca deposition in the tibia of aged hens (Min et al., 2019). Similarly, feeding turkeys with Zn, Cu, and Mn chelated to methionine hydroxy analog improved tibial breaking strength and increased cortical bone thickness (Ferket et al., 2009). Chelated trace minerals enhance bone mineralization, antioxidant capacity, and immune function in heat-stressed broilers, emphasizing their impact within the gut-immune-bone trifecta in broilers (Mohammadizad et al., 2024).

In addition to improving bone health, several feed additives have also shown positive effects in reducing incidence rates of bone-related diseases, such as TD. For example, TD incidence did not increase with the addition of sodium chloride, copper sulfate, magnesium sulfate, and potassium sulfate (Veltmann and Jensen, 1981), while supplementation of 1,25‐dihydroxycholecalciferol and Ca was found to have reduced the incidence of TD in broilers (Rennie et al., 1995). Collectively, these trace minerals contribute positively to bone health, largely through their synergistic interactions and coordinated roles in bone development and mineral metabolism (Vieira, 2008). Miscellaneous feed additives

Miscellaneous feed additives play an important role in promoting gut health in broilers by supporting intestinal integrity, enhancing microbial balance, and improving overall digestive efficiency. For example, clay-based compounds—such as montmorillonite—have been shown to positively influence gut health in broilers. As a feed additive, montmorillonite can improve the gut barrier function and modulate the intestinal microbiota composition in broiler chickens (Biswas et al., 2024). As another example, frankincense (Boswellia serrata) is a natural aromatic resin that can be effectively used to improve the configuration of intestinal villi, modulate gastric microflora, and enhance overall gut health (Al-Yasiry et al., 2017).

In terms of impacts on immune functions, Lee et al. (2017) demonstrated through in vitro studies that Allium hookeri (Hooker chives) exerts immunomodulatory effects on lymphocytes, macrophages, and tumor cells. Similarly, frankincense resin oil upregulated the expression of CD3 and CD20 genes in the spleen, further emphasizing its immunomodulatory effect by increasing the activity of lysozyme, interleukin 10 and complement component 3. Palygorskite (PAL) was found to improve blood IgG and IgM levels in broilers under an avian-pathogenic Escherichia coli challenge (Tan et al., 2024). Furthermore, Karimi et al. (2020) reported that administration of PAL resulted in decreased concentrations of the inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IFN-γ, and TNF-α in both the liver and serum. Moreover, dietary supplementation of resveratrol has been shown to mitigate thiram–induced tibial dyschondroplasia in broiler chickens by regulating the expression of key genes, including BMP2, RUNX2, and HIF1A (Iqbal et al., 2025). Feed additives: immune responses, cytokines, immunoglobulins, and associated signaling pathways

Regular signaling pathways

As a biologically integrated system, the physiological functions of broilers are continuously modulated by complex immune responses and cytokine-associated regulatory pathways. In turn, the physiological and biochemical interrelationships between these pathways are strongly affected by dietary interventions—such as probiotics, prebiotics, organic acids, and more—that act as connectors of the gut-immune, immune-bone, and gut-bone axes. As a whole, these interconnected systems help maintain gut functions, support balanced immune responses, and promote proper bone development that are required for the high muscle mass of modern broilers.

Cytokines play a central role in the regulation and coordination of immune functions, forming a highly interconnected immunoregulatory network (Luo et al., 2013). Among immune cell populations, T helper 1 (Th1) cells contribute significantly to host defense mechanisms through the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines—including interleukins, IFN-γ, and TNF-α—which are involved in pathogen clearance and cellular immune responses (Abbas et al., 1996; Gao et al., 2007). In broilers, Th1-mediated cytokine responses are also essential for maintaining intestinal homeostasis and facilitating gut-immune system interactions, thereby supporting intestinal health and immune competence (Deng et al., 2020). Furthermore, cytokine signaling pathways are closely associated with the regulation of humoral immunity, including the stimulation and production of immunoglobulins (Coffman et al., 1989). Prebiotics and probiotics may represent a promising strategy to prevent bone loss by modulating host immune responses and inhibiting osteoclastogenesis during infections (Li et al., 2016a; Yousf et al., 2015). These modulations are mostly supported by pathways associated with RANKL and osteoprotegerin (OPG) produced by osteoblasts. RANKL activates immune cells (such as T-cells), binds to RANK on osteoclast precursors, and activates other signaling pathways like NF-κB and MAPK (mitogen-activated protein kinase), leading to osteoclast differentiation and bone resorption (Zhang et al., 2022). Moreover, the JAK/STAT signaling pathway is also crucial in bone health from its direct relationship with the RANKL signaling pathway, which regulates osteoclast/osteoblast activity (Damerau et al., 2020), and is therefore also directly involved with production of proinflammatory cytokines (Morris et al., 2018; Villarino et al., 2017). RANKL activates multiple intracellular pathways, like JNK (c-Jun N-terminal kinase) and PI3K /AKT (phosphoinositide 3-kinase), which promote osteoclast formation and bone resorption (Trouvin and Goëb, 2010). Additionally, OPG acts as a decoy receptor by binding to RANKL and preventing its interaction with RANK, thereby inhibiting osteoclastogenesis and reducing bone loss (Zhang et al., 2022).

Feed additives and metabiotic pathways

Different feed additives act on different metabolic pathways by bridging them to each other. For example, supplementation of L. acidophilus and L. plantarum improves interleukin (IL) production in broilers (Deng et al. 2020), which is involved in various signaling pathways such as RANKL–RANK and TNF-α and thus are directly related to gut-immune-bone trifecta. Furthermore, these Lactobacillus strains can possibly increase OPG levels, thereby helping to prevent the loss of cortical bone (Ohlsson et al., 2014) by interfering with production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β.

In current literature, Bifidobacterium longum has been shown to lower blood CTx levels and serum osteocalcins in ovariectomized rats (Parvaneh et al., 2015), while L. reuteri lowered TRACP (Tartrate-Resistant Acid Phosphatase) production by modulating immune response of host cells while suppressing bone mass loss through the RANKL pathway (Britton et al., 2014; McCabe et al., 2013). The MAPK signaling pathway is also regulated by some probiotics like Saccharomyces boulardii CNCM I-745, L. reuteri, and L. acidophilus, with observed reduction in intestinal inflammation (Justino et al., 2020). In addition, VSL#3®—a commercial probiotic mixture containing four Lactobacillus, three Bifidobacterium, and one Streptococcus species—has also been reported to improve gut and bone health through the regulation of TJ protein activity (Cheng et al., 2020) via the MAPK signaling pathway. Notably, NF-κB is also regulated by probiotic L. casei and activates innate immunity (Kim et al., 2006). Probiotics like Lactobacilli spp., Rhodopseudomonas sphaeroides and Bifidobacterium bifidum showed improved JAK2/STAT5 signaling pathways and thereby improved overall health and productivity in monogastric animals (Li et al., 2025b; Shi et al., 2021). However, the systemic effects of the aforementioned probiotics on gut, immune and bone-associated pathways remain poorly understood. Deeper insights of such pathways with regards to biotics and other additives could support their potential dietary applications for reducing inflammation and improving immune function in broilers.

Some organic acids can also regulate cytokine-immune related pathways. For example, butyric acid and oregano essential oil could significantly decrease the various pro-inflammatory cytokines in the blood such as TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8 (Samy et al., 2026). Notably, boswellic acid showed increased serum immunoglobulins and cytokines (Awaad et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2026) and thereby inhibitory action on S. aureus (Liu et al., 2022). These cytokines can directly interfere with bone formation and resorption (Adamopoulos et al., 2010; Srivastava et al., 2011; Wing et al., 2011) through the RANKL, JNK, PI3K pathways. PUFAs interact closely with immune functions through their bioactive metabolites, such as eicosanoids, prostaglandins (PGs), and lipoxins that are derived from dihomo-γ-linolenic acid (DGLA; 20:3n-6), arachidonic acid (AA; 20:4n-6), and eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA; 20:5n-3) (Calder, 2001). Lipoxins, primarily produced in the bone marrow, inhibit leukotriene activity (Stenke et al., 1991), thereby reducing pro-inflammatory cytokine-mediated bone resorption (Laufer, 2003) and osteoclast formation (Jiang et al., 2005). In addition, lipoxins exhibit anti-inflammatory effects and protect intestinal tissues from injury by stimulating nitric oxide production (Wallace et al., 2005).

In a similar vein, trace minerals have also been documented to exert regulatory effects like other classes of additives. For instance, Zn-MHA (Zn- Methionine Hydroxyl Analogue) improved the broiler chick’s immunity and NF-kB downregulation while mediating intestinal inflammation (Li et al., 2015). This downregulation interferes with bone homeostasis through the NF-κB-RANK signaling pathway (Abu-Amer, 2013). Chronic inflammation is largely regulated through the NF-κB signaling pathway (Neurath et al., 1998), which plays a key role in maintaining gut integrity, bone metabolism, and immune function in broilers. Regulation of this pathway may therefore contribute to improved gut health, enhanced bone development, and a stronger immune response. In this context, supplementation with trace mineral amino acid complexes (TMAAC; Zn, Cu, and Mn) has been reported to modulate NF-κB mRNA expression (de Arruda Roque et al., 2022), thereby potentially enhancing both innate and adaptive immune functions (Liu et al., 2017).

Furthermore, organic trace minerals such as Mn, Zn, Fe, Cu, iodine (I), and selenium (Se) have been shown to influence immune-related gene expression. Echeverry et al. (2016) reported that such minerals upregulated certain toll-like receptor (TLR) subgroups in the bursa of Fabricius and increased IL10 expression in the spleen, while downregulating TLR2B and TLR4 expression in the cecal tonsils. These findings are important because TLRs and interleukins are directly involved in immune regulation, maintenance of gut barrier integrity, and bone formation processes (Wing et al., 2011). Therefore, dietary supplementation with organic trace minerals may improve gut and bone health through modulation of the NF-κB signaling pathway and related immune mechanisms.

Several phytogenic feed additives have also been shown to regulate gut health, bone development, and overall broiler performance through immune and cytokine-related pathways. For example, capsaicin supplementation improves immune function by regulating immunoglobulin and cytokines specially by lowering IL-1β and TNF-α levels (Liu et al., 2021c; Su et al., 2023) which is beneficial for bone health since excessive TNF-α can stimulate osteoclastogenesis through the RANKL signaling pathway, leading to bone degradation (Boyce and Xing, 2008). Moreover, the reduced TNF-α levels keep lymphocytes and phagocytic activity of monocyte-derived cells activated, which is important for reacting antigens in the body (Adamopoulos and Bowman, 2008). Similarly, clove essential oil has been reported to regulate inflammatory and tissue remodeling pathways (Shehata et al., 2022). Genistein, hesperidin, and quercetin supplementation also enhanced antibody production and improved bone mineralization, highlighting the close relationship between immune regulation and bone integrity (Boskovic et al., 2020; Hager-Theodorides et al., 2014; Kamboh et al., 2018; Rasouli and Jahanian, 2015; Saied et al., 2026). In addition, cinnamon and silver nanoparticles reduced pro-inflammatory cytokines while increasing immunoglobulin levels, further supporting immune balance and bone health (Razagh Shazr et al., 2026). Moreover, a study has found that chestnut tannins can alter the JAK-STAT pathway and improve the host defenses against pathogens (Lee et al., 2021).

Collectively, these effects are mediated through important signaling pathways such as toll-like receptor, JAK-STAT, and NF-κB (Sisodiya et al., 2026). However, most studies in current literature are limited to the evaluation of one signaling pathway at a time. In general, though extensive research of feed additives and their effects on signaling pathways and immunity have been conducted, no single study exists in which these connected pathways are shown together—to the best of our knowledge. Therefore, there is a timely need for the evaluation of such interconnected pathways with respect to feed additives to understand their functional mechanisms and connection of the three systems in the gut-immune-bone trifecta.

Despite the critical role of immune and cytokine-mediated pathways in regulating physiological functions in broilers, many previous studies evaluating feed additives have primarily focused on production performance and phenotypic responses, with limited investigation into the underlying molecular and metabolic mechanisms. A more comprehensive understanding of how these additives modulate immune signaling pathways would provide stronger mechanistic evidence for their biological functions. Such insights are essential in future studies to develop targeted health-promoting nutritional strategies and facilitate efficient industrial application of feed additives in broiler production systems.

Feed additives: barrier functions and gut microbes

The maintenance of immunity, gut integrity, and overall health in broilers is closely associated with the activity of the gut microbiota and effectiveness of intestinal barrier functions. These gut barrier functions serve as the first line of defense against pathogenic invasion while supporting nutrient digestion, absorption, and immune regulation. Broadly, the intestinal barrier system consists of four interconnected components: the physical barrier, chemical barrier, microbiological barrier, and immunological barrier (Anderson et al., 2012; Bischoff et al., 2014).

Physical Gut Barrier & Chemical Barrier. The physical barrier, which mainly consists of intestinal epithelial cells and intercellular junctions, serves as the first line of defense in the gut—with TJ protein complexes playing a vital role (Odenwald and Turner, 2017; Otani and Furuse, 2020). These complexes are located between adjacent enterocytes, where they regulate intestinal permeability and prevent the translocation of microorganisms, toxins, and antigens into systemic circulation (Shehata et al., 2022; Ulluwishewa et al., 2011). On the other hand, the chemical barrier mainly consists of the mucus layer produced by goblet cells and intestinal epithelial cells, which acts as the first line of innate immune defense in the gut (Johansson and Hansson, 2016).

It is a widely held view that probiotics can maintain TJ functionality to prevent “leaky gut” syndrome in broilers. Perera et al. (2024) reported that probiotic supplementation increased TJ gene expression in the jejunum and ileum while reducing BCO-related clinical lameness. As a genus commonly used in probiotics, many Lactobacilli spp. are important probiotic bacteria that contribute to gut health by creating an acidic intestinal environment through the production of bacteriocins and SCFAs, thereby suppressing the growth of pathogenic bacteria (Li et al., 2018). Among them, L. reuteri has been shown to improve intestinal barrier integrity by enhancing the expression of TJ proteins such as CLDN, OCLN, and ZO1 (Nii et al., 2020; Xie et al., 2019). In addition, L. reuteri produces the antimicrobial compound reuterin, which plays an important role in reducing pathogenic microbial populations in the gut, emphasizing its chemical barrier functions (Jones and Versalovic, 2009). Several probiotic Bacillus strains, including B. pumilus and B. subtilis, have also been shown to upregulate key TJ proteins such as OCLN, ZO1, and JAM2 (Al-Abdullatif et al., 2026; Bilal et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2026b; Peng et al., 2019; Saikhwan et al., 2026). Similarly, Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron enhances OCLN expression in the ileum (Alizadeh et al., 2026). Taken into consideration, Bacillus-based probiotics may play an important role in reducing leaky gut in broilers, potentially preventing related complications—such as BCO lameness (Wideman, 2016).

Prebiotics also play a significant role in gut barrier functions. Yeast cell wall components have been shown to upregulate TJ proteins—such as CLDN1, CLDN2, ZO1, and MUC1 (Gu et al., 2022). In addition, multi-strain prebiotic supplementation such as L. acidophilus LAP5, L. fermentum P2, Pediococcus acidilactici LS, and L. casei L21 also enhances the mRNA expression of OCLN, ZO1, and MUC2 in the ileum (Chang et al., 2020). Other prebiotics, such as β-1,3-glucans and mannan-oligosaccharides, have also been reported to improve CLDN1 expression under pathogenic challenge conditions (Guo et al., 2023b; Kyoung et al., 2023; Lin et al., 2022; Shao et al., 2013).

Organic acids such as ursolic acid, caffeic and citric acid also play an important role in manipulating TJ proteins and preventing leaky gut syndrome in broilers (Chen et al., 2023a; Hu et al., 2024; Kalkan et al., 2025; Peng et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2019). Although TJ integrity and BCO-related lameness in bone disorders are directly linked, few studies have examined the effects of organic acids on BCO-like diseases. This highlights an urgent need for further research in this area. Such organic acids act as chemical barriers and facilitate establishment of a functional microbial barrier in broilers (El-Saadony et al., 2022; Kamal and Ragaa, 2014). Apart from organic acids, supplementation of polyphenolics like hydrolyzed gallotannin has also been shown to improve expression of ZO1 in the duodenum, and OCLN expression in both the duodenum and ileum (Guo et al., 2023a).

Insofar as the role of feed additives in modulation of the chemical barrier is discussed, increased mucus production—which strengthens the intestinal chemical barrier by providing antimicrobial protection through bioactive components such as β-defensins, lysozyme, avidin, and IgA (Duangnumsawang et al., 2021)—is rather well-investigated as a research topic. For instance, Patel et al. (2026) reported that humic acid supplementation promotes goblet cell differentiation and upregulates MUC2 gene expression, leading to increased mucin production, which makes up an important chemical barrier. Likewise, supplementation of probiotics containing a mixture of Streptococcus faecalis, Clostridium butyricum, and B. mesentericus has been reported to enhance mucin production through upregulation of the MUC2 gene (Mohammed et al., 2024). L. reuteri 22 has also demonstrated beneficial effects on intestinal mucus production in broilers, further supporting the maintenance of gut barrier integrity (Xie et al., 2019). In addition to probiotic-based approaches, organic acids also play an important role in enhancing intestinal chemical barrier functions. Among these, butyrate supplementation has been shown to stimulate mucin secretion and promote retinoic acid production, thereby contributing to improved intestinal protection and mucosal health (Jung et al., 2015; Schilderink et al., 2016; Sikandar et al., 2017).

Microbial Barrier and Immunological Barrier. The immunological barrier of the intestine consists of immune-related tissues, immune cells, and soluble immune mediators that collectively maintain intestinal immune homeostasis (Lecocq et al., 2013). Among these components, gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT) plays a central role in coordinating mucosal immune responses in poultry. Supplementation with prebiotics such as mannan-oligosaccharides and fructo-oligosaccharides has been reported to enhance immune cell activity within GALT in chickens (Janardhana et al., 2009).

In addition, certain organic acids may contribute to intestinal immune regulation. For example, benzoic acid has been shown to promote M2 macrophage polarization through activation of the mTOR/PPAR-γ/STAT3 signaling pathways (Jia et al., 2024). As M2 macrophages are associated with anti-inflammatory responses and tissue repair, such modulation may support intestinal immune homeostasis and contribute to improved gut health and resistance against pathogenic challenges. As the last component, the microbial barrier—which is referred to as the intestinal microbiota—is shaped by multiple factors, including host nutrition and overall health, and interacts closely with the immune system (Celi et al., 2017; Hayes et al., 2018). For example, L. plantarum and Paenibacillus polymyxa in the gut can suppress apoptosis and improve cell proliferation in the intestinal mucosa (Wu et al., 2019), resulting in decreased cell death rate and enhanced epithelial lining renewal, which is crucial for strong and intact gut barrier functions. It prevents pathogenic translocation events commonly seen in leaky gut syndrome (Wideman, 2016) and reduces inflammation events that may facilitate further bacterial translocation in the body and subsequent additional inflammation thereafter (Vancamelbeke and Vermeire, 2017).

Regarding microbial barrier function, a recent study showed that supplementation with heat-killed Limosilactobacillus ingluviei C37 promoted the growth of beneficial cecal bacteria, including Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium, while reducing pathogenic populations (such as Enterobacter and E. coli) and increasing production of SCFAs (Sirisopapong et al., 2026). In addition to this, Goldstein et al. (2015) found that certain Lactobacillus spp., Bifidobacterium spp., and Rhamnococcus spp. create an anaerobic and acidic environment in the gut, which inhibits the colonization of aerobic pathogens in the broiler chicken’s gastrointestinal tract. Evidently, such beneficial microbes provide robust support for proper gut health and functions—digestion, absorption, and barrier properties. Emerging biotechnological additives & gut health, bone health & immunity

With the advancement of science, various biotechnological interventions have been developed to identify effective solutions for gut health, immunity, and bone health–related problems in broiler chickens. As novel additives, paraprobiotics are found to be a novel entity. These consist of non-viable (inactivated) microbial cells or their structural components. Supplementation of 0.01 % paraprobiotic Lactiplantibacillus plantarum ABG0050 significantly increased the IFNG, IL6, and IL12 expression of Th1-type cytokines compared to basal diets (Kondo et al., 2025), highlighting its potential as an immunomodulatory additive. However, further in-vitro studies are needed to evaluate its broader effects on immune regulation, optimal inclusion levels, and long-term impacts on broiler health and performance. Equally novel in their application, several researchers have also investigated encapsulation techniques to further increase efficacy of existing feed additives. For example, microencapsulated organic acid mixture (citric acid and sorbic acids) has shown positive effect on intestinal barrier functions and expression of inflammatory cytokines (Bialkowski et al., 2023).

Bacteriophages (BP) also can be considered as a potential solution for gut health and immune related problems, the supplementation of which in broilers resulted in increased total serum antibodies (IgM and IgG), as well as reduced PGC1A and TLR4, and increased IL10 gene expression in the ileum (Sarrami et al., 2022). The use of high-molecular-weight polymers may also be a relevant approach to improve bone health through prevention of bone loss, preservation of bone structure, and increasing of cortical bone mineral density to rescue deleterious effects caused by Salmonella infection—which increases osteoclast activity, causing bone resorption and bone loss in broilers (Raehtz et al., 2018). Recently, Kamel et al. (2021) found that dietary nucleotides derived from Saccharomyces cerevisiae numerically improved immunological parameters in broilers under both high (0.050 m²/2.2 kg) and low (0.073 m²/2.2 kg) stocking densities, as evidenced by increased expression of IL2, IFNG, and higher lysozyme concentrations. In the same vein, supplementation of broiler diets with individual purine nucleosides, especially adenosine, has been shown to enhance growth performance, improve intestinal morphology, and strengthen immune function (Abdel Aziz et al., 2024a). Kamel et al. (2021) further determined that dietary nucleotide supplementation in pre- and post-challenge of lipopolysaccharides has improved serum IgG concentration in broilers. It is worth noting that an inclusion of 0.5% (w/w) nucleotide mixture showed higher expression of ZO1 and OCLN in ileum, whereas lower inclusion rates (0.1% and 0.3%) exhibited higher expression of MUC2 and TFF2 gene, with a 0.1% inclusion rate showing lower expression of IFNA1 (Wu et al., 2018). Finally, Abdel Aziz et al. (2024b) investigated the supplementation of an additive mixture consisting of nucleotides and butyrate, and found increased serum IgM on d7, as well as IgM and IgY on d14 in experimental broilers. While much work remains to determine their true practicality, it is evident that the use of these novel biotechnological feed additives holds significant potential in the goal of sustainable poultry production, owing to their contributions to gut health, bone development, and immune function in the broiler chicken.

Gaps, challenges & future research directions

Even though substantial literature and research findings are available regarding the role of feed additives in broiler gut, bone health and immunity, several aspects remain lacking and must be addressed before we can elucidate a holistic understanding of the interactions within these systems. One of the major challenges is the lack of commercially validated dose-response studies, which are fundamental to feed additive research as they enable the determination of optimal dietary inclusion levels required to induce the desired biological response while avoiding adverse effects and minimizing production costs. In broiler production, numerous feed additives exhibit dose-dependent effects, whereby various inclusion levels can result in markedly different physiological and performance outcomes. These studies should thus be validated under commercial production conditions, a critical step often overlooked or uninvestigated. Another major challenge is the lack of multi-omics approaches—such as metagenomics, transcriptomics, metabolomics, and proteomics—that provide insights into molecular and microbial mechanisms behind the effects of these additives. For example, during our literature search, only one relevant study was found related to BCO and proteomics, which was conducted by Cook et al. (2023) with clearly characterized proximal tibia proteome in BCO affected broilers. These advanced techniques may support identification of functional microbial communities in the broiler gut, metabolic and signaling pathways, host-protein responses and related gene expression, bone cell proliferation, and differentiation, as well as immune regulation. Ultimately, the integration of these analyses will augment the comprehensive understanding regarding how feed additives regulate these biologically integrated systems in broilers, beyond traditional growth and performance metrics. Additionally, several bone-related deformities—for example, TD, valgus-varus deformities, and spondylitis—have not been fully studied with respect to different classes of feed additives. As such, this gap in knowledge also warrants timely investigation under the same context of feed additives and their interrelation with the biological axes of the trifecta concept proposed in this review.

Another important consideration is the high degree of variability in parameter responses to additives based on broiler age, strain, sex, and production system. Most of the current research focuses on a single growth stage or a breed, which limits the generalizability of findings. The broiler’s gut microbiota composition, immune maturation, and bone mineralization rate are highly variable due to age, feed, and production conditions, which may result in different responses to the additive(s) being investigated. Moreover, potential synergistic effects of multiple combined additives such as biotics with different phytogenic additives, or enzymes with organic acids and mineral mixtures, remain insufficiently explored. For example, one recent study by Nopparatmaitree et al. (2026) entitled “Novel Double-Layer Microencapsulated Phytosynbiotic Derived from Probiotics and Tiliacora triandra Extract” has demonstrated positive effects on gut health in broilers. As reflected in the title, the study’s approach of combining multiple feed additives into a single mixture and enhancing them through microencapsulation represents a novel strategy for delivering feed additives (Nopparatmaitree et al., 2026). In addition, as different systems within the body are inherently connected due to shared metabolites and signaling pathways, investigated effects need to be evaluated representing those interactive systems as much as possible. For example, the digestive, immune, and bone systems are interconnected with each other in the framework illustrated within this review. Therefore, investigated parameters should not be isolated to a single system but rather extended to those that are closely related in order to accurately determine the real net impact of tested supplements and additives.

Conclusion

As the poultry industry continues to expand to meet the increasing global demand for broiler meat, achieving sustainable production while maintaining bird health has become a central priority. In this context, a comprehensive understanding of how feed additives influence the interconnected systems of gut health, bone development, and immune competence are essential. This manuscript highlights that strategic incorporation of functional feed additives into broiler diets can play a pivotal role in optimizing these physiological systems simultaneously. Conventional additives such as probiotics, prebiotics, minerals, organic acids, phytogenic compounds, along with emerging biotechnological interventions, demonstrate significant potential to modulate the intestinal microbiota, enhance nutrient utilization, and support bone mineralization and immune regulation.

More importantly, the reported evidence and synthesized knowledge in this review emphasizes that the gut, bone, and immune systems function as an integrated biological network rather than independent entities. Improvements in gut integrity and microbial balance can enhance mineral absorption and metabolic efficiency, thereby contributing to improved bone strength and immune resilience. Therefore, adoption of interconnected health interventions that fully consider a gut–bone–immune tri-axis provides a more holistic framework to evaluate the functional efficacy of feed additives in modern broiler production systems. This integrated approach is especially important in production systems with reduced antibiotic usage, where nutritional strategies should support both bird performance and animal health and welfare. Despite significant scientific and technological advancements, significant knowledge gaps remain regarding the mechanistic pathways and long-term impacts of several feed additives across multiple physiological systems, prompting much-needed timely investigation of these aspects. Collectively, the insights presented in this review provide a scientific foundation for the development of next-generation nutritional strategies aimed at improving broiler gut health, bone health, and immunity, thereby achieving production efficiency, and sustainability within the rapidly evolving poultry industry.

Overall, this review indicates that probiotics, prebiotics, phytogenic feed additives, and organic trace minerals currently provide the strongest evidence for simultaneously modulating gut health, immune function, and bone metabolism within the “trifecta concept”. The evidence suggests that these feed additives are capable of concurrently improving intestinal barrier integrity, modulating immune responses, and supporting bone metabolism while stabilizing overall physiological balance across these axes. Interestingly, probiotics were identified as the most well-studied additive category with solid findings over the other additive categories. Although the probiotic additive category has rarely been evaluated through the complete "trifecta concept," several studies have attempted to assess its effects through two of the three axes, namely the immune-gut, gut-bone, or immune-bone axis, while most of the other categories have only focused on one axis at a time. Therefore, to observe the real effect of those additives, their evaluation needs to focus on this proposed “trifecta concept” in future studies. In short, future studies should adopt integrated experimental approaches that evaluate gut microbial stability, immune responses, and bone health concurrently to better elucidate the mechanisms underlying these interactions.
