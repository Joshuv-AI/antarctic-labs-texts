Nonsurgical Correction of Brodie Bite in Mixed Dentition Using Quad‐Helix Expansion: A Case Report
Authors: Kazem Dalaei, Ali Shokraneh, Parsa Dayani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis case report presents a nonsurgical correction of a dentoalveolar Brodie bite in a mixed dentition patient using a quad‐helix appliance. Mandibular expansion resolved the transverse discrepancy within 3 months, improving occlusion, and eliminating a habitual mandibular shift that had contributed to facial asymmetry.

Introduction

Brodie bite is a severe malocclusion characterized by a bilateral buccal posterior crossbite and occurs when the mandibular dental arch is fully telescoped within the maxillary dental arch [1]. Its prevalence ranges from 0.5% to 6%, with an estimate of 1%–2% [2, 3, 4, 5]. Although relatively uncommon, early diagnosis is essential because untreated cases may progress and complicate future management. Many patients do not seek treatment in the early stages, as esthetic concerns are often minimal. Consequently, early‐phase management during mixed dentition is less frequently documented in the literature.

If left untreated, Brodie bite can adversely affect chewing function [6], muscle function [7], and normal growth and development of the mandible, potentially leading to facial asymmetry and mandibular retrognathism [7]. The etiology of Brodie bite can generally be classified into skeletal and dentoalveolar components, or a combination of both. Skeletal components include maxillary prognathism and mandibular retrognathism, while dentoalveolar components include transverse collapse of the dental arches or abnormal tooth position. Hypoglossia has also been identified as a contributing factor [8]. If the mandibular constriction is the primary factor, treatment can be challenging as expansion is limited to the alveolar bone and may primarily induce tooth inclination.

Early intervention in mixed dentition can prevent severe complications and reduce the need for surgical intervention. This case report presents a challenging unilateral Brodie bite with skeletal and dentoalveolar asymmetry and a mild Class II malocclusion, treated using an unusual nonsurgical approach of mandibular expansion with a quad‐helix appliance during mixed dentition over 3 months.

Case History/Examination

The patient was a 10‐year‐old Iranian girl who was referred to the orthodontic department of Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences because of facial asymmetry. Facial analysis showed a convex profile and mandibular retrognathism on the lateral view (Figure 1A). On the frontal view, the mandibular asymmetry, a functional shift to the left side, and a long upper lip were observed in maximum intercuspation (Figure 1B,C). No habits were recorded during examination and treatment.

Intraoral Examination and Dental Casts

Intraoral examination showed occlusion on the right side (Figure 1D), deviation of lower midline to the right side (Figure 1E), and dentoalveolar collapse with a severe left‐sided Brodie bite in centric relation (Figure 1F). The patient habitually shifted her mandible to the left to achieve occlusion in maximum intercuspation (Figure 1G–I) and avoid premature contact between the upper left lateral incisor and lower left canine in centric relationship (Figure 1F). This shift moved the lower midline to the left, causing the upper and lower midlines to coincide, unlike in centric relation. The patient's mandibular arch was narrow (Figure 1J,K).

Dental cast analysis revealed a Class II dental relationship, severe mandibular constriction (intermolar distance = 43.2 mm, intercanine = 23.6 mm), and cant resulting from overeruption of right‐sided maxillary dentition that had no stop. The upper left lateral incisor was lingually inclined. There was an asymmetry between the mandibular first molars and canines relative to the midsagittal line. The left canine and first molar were positioned 3 mm closer to the midsagittal line than those on the right side, showing the main mandibular deficiency was on the left side (Table 1). Diagnosis

Based on these findings, the patient was diagnosed with true mandibular asymmetry with both skeletal and dentoalveolar components, along with pseudo‐asymmetry due to a functional mandibular shift to the left side. The lower midline was also deviated to the right side in centric relation. Overall, the patient was diagnosed with a unilateral left‐sided Brodie bite.

The skeletal component was evident in the mild Class II sagittal relationship and the retrognathic mandible, while the dentoalveolar component included unilateral transverse mandibular collapse and excessive lingual inclination of the left posterior teeth.

An anterior crossbite involving the upper left lateral incisor was also observed and was associated with the premature contact caused by this discrepancy.

Investigations and Treatment

The primary treatment objectives were to increase the mandibular arch width by uprighting the posterior teeth, with greater emphasis on the left side to achieve arch harmony, aligning the upper left lateral incisor labially to correct the premature contact between upper left lateral and lower left canine to prevent the patient from positioning her anterior teeth in crossbite and shifting her mandible.

Subsequent re‐evaluation was planned; however, mandibular advancement was expected to improve the skeletal Class II relationship and improve the convex facial profile. This growth modification could also help the jaws' disharmony by positioning the wider part of the mandible across the narrower part of the maxilla. The occlusal cant and other discrepancies could be managed later with fixed appliances.

Given the mandibular arch constriction, we focused on expanding the mandible instead of limiting the maxillary arch width.

The excessive lingual inclination of the left posterior mandibular teeth suggested a transverse dentoalveolar deficiency rather than a skeletal problem. The torque of the lower left first molar was −19°, whereas that of the lower right first molar was −10°, further supporting the unilateral component of the primary Brodie bite diagnosis.

Uprighting the molars was considered a nonsurgical approach to expand the mandibular arch, potentially avoiding future surgical intervention. Since the patient was in mixed dentition and had remaining growth potential, mandibular dentoalveolar expansion with a quad‐helix appliance was considered a conservative and appropriate treatment choice.

The quad‐helix (Figure 4A,B) could be activated for both anterior and posterior expansion; in this case, both components were activated.

(A and B) Quad‐helix. (C) Removable appliance.

An interceptive treatment was planned with two objectives:

1‐ A quad‐helix appliance (Figure 4A,B) was used for transverse mandibular expansion. Both lower molars were banded, and the quad‐helix appliance was fabricated and cemented in position. The arms of the appliance contacted teeth numbered 6, E, and D.

2‐ A removable appliance (Figure 4C) with a Z‐spring for the upper arch to correct the upper left lateral position and remove the functional shift.

Further intervention was considered to accomplish the treatment: a twin‐block appliance for advancing the mandible and also accomplishing the harmony of the jaws. This phase of treatment was postponed for approximately 2 years because of the growth stage of the patient.

The upper removable appliance consisted of a posterior bite block to open the bite, two Adams clasps on the first molars for retention, and a Z‐spring behind the upper left lateral incisor to disengage it from premature contact and position it labially. The Z‐spring was activated twice during the intervention, with an interval of 3 weeks, and 3 mm of activation was applied each time. The appliance remained for the retention of the treatment.

During the intervention, a quad‐helix (0.7 mm) in the mandible was activated four times, with an interval of 3 weeks between activations. Each activation applied 5–6 mm of expansion, and the quad‐helix was retained for a month.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

After 3 months of treatment, with four follow‐up visits at 3‐week intervals, no significant changes were observed on the lateral view (Figure 5A). The mandibular dental midline was corrected without additional intervention, as elimination of the functional shift allowed the dentition to be positioned within a wider arch, in a more symmetrical appearance (Figure 5B,C). The upper lateral incisor was positioned correctly within the dental arch, preventing further mandibular shift. The mandibular intermolar distance increased by 7.5 mm (from 43.2 to 50.7 mm), and the intercanine distance increased by 5 mm (from 23.5 to 28.5 mm) by dentoalveolar expansion (Figure 5). The occlusal views demonstrated correction of the left‐sided Brodie bite and improved interdigitation in centric occlusion (Figure 5D–F). The posttreatment upper and lower occlusal views showed a wider mandibular arch and improved transverse relationship between the arches (Figure 5G,H).

The CBCT radiography showed a slight, nonsignificant bi‐gonial skeletal expansion (78.73 to 79.02 mm). The distance between the mental foramina (measured at the most inferior point) did not change significantly (40.59 to 40.65 mm) (Figure 6A,B). These findings suggest that the skeletal changes were minimal and that the main changes were dentoalveolar, as expected. In conclusion, a left‐sided unilateral Bordie bite with severe mandibular constriction and left‐sided dentoalveolar collapse, a Class II malocclusion, short lower facial height, and combined right midline deviation resulting from Bordie bite and mandibular asymmetry due to a leftward mandibular shift were successfully treated nonsurgically in the first stage, resolving the severe transverse discrepancy and asymmetry. In the next stage, further intervention with a hybrid functional appliance was considered to advance the mandible and achieve jaw harmony and cant correction. Discussion

Brodie bite, an uncommon form of malocclusion, is described as a severe posterior crossbite, which can be either unilateral or bilateral. Brodie first described it in 1952. This malocclusion “Brodie bite” or “Brodie syndrome” was defined as when the mandibular teeth are partially or wholly contained within the maxillary arch [10]. In this case report, we discussed a nonsurgical successful treatment of a unilateral left‐sided Brodie bite in a 10‐year‐old girl. This treatment addressed midline shifting, asymmetry, and a mild Class II malocclusion in 3 months during mixed dentition.

The etiology of Brodie bite can involve genetic factors, functional issues, muscular imbalances, or hypoglossia [8, 11]. In this specific case, the etiology appears to be muscular imbalance. It is crucial to address Brodie bite before correcting any other concurrent malocclusion [11]. When the mandibular arch is telescoped within the maxillary arch, lateral excursive movements may be limited or compromised, potentially leading to masticatory difficulties, an inability to chew on one side, and temporomandibular dysfunction. Early treatment is recommended as spontaneous correction is uncommon, and untreated cases can result in permanent asymmetry, often requiring surgical intervention [12, 13]. Once a buccal crossbite is established, it can lead to significant sequelae, such as deep bite, overeruption of the maxillary segment, lingually inclined mandibular posterior teeth, occlusal plane cant, facial asymmetry, limited lateral excursions, temporomandibular disorder, growth interference, and periodontal problems [14]. When associated with skeletal imbalances, Brodie bite can be accompanied by varying degrees of facial asymmetry [11]. Unilateral Brodie bite, as seen in our case, is particularly challenging [15]. It can be characterized by a flat and asymmetric palatal vault, often with an occlusal plane cant and lateral deviation. Bilateral Brodie bite, on the other hand, is associated with a flat but symmetric palatal vault and a functionally retruded mandible [16]. Brodie bite can also be associated with other malocclusions, including mandibular rotation or lateral shift, an inclined occlusal plane and facial asymmetry, deep anterior and posterior bite, and unilateral chewing contralateral to the scissor bite [12].

The mandible was asymmetric in two distinct ways. The first cause was the asymmetry resulting from a left‐sided mandibular shift to escape premature contact. The patient occluded her mouth in an anterior crossbite to avoid premature contact between the upper left lateral incisor and the lower left canine. Such premature contacts that cause shifting are common in unilateral Brodie bites like ours [16]. This component was resolved by positioning the lateral incisor facially with a Z‐spring, which eliminated the shift and harmonized centric relation with maximum intercuspation.

The second cause of this asymmetry was a rightward shift of the mandibular midline. This midline discrepancy resulted from a unilateral Brodie bite, a common condition among these patients [12, 13, 17, 18]. The condition was addressed early, potentially preventing progression to a severity requiring surgery. Mandibular dentoalveolar expansion using a quad‐helix positioned the dentition in a wider arch, which likely contributed to the correction of the midline shift.

Unlike the maxilla, the mandible lacks a midline suture. It begins as two separate halves that fuse early in fetal development. While removable appliances resembling mid‐palatal expanders can be used in the mandible, they are limited to tooth movement. A common method for effective maxillary expansion, especially in early mixed dentition, is the quad‐helix appliance. However, its effectiveness for mandibular expansion and the specific contributions of dental and skeletal changes require further investigation.

One of the main disadvantages of this appliance is the tendency to tip molars rather than move them bodily. In this specific case, the patient presented with dentoalveolar collapse on the left side, making the tipping movement beneficial. However, the inability to move teeth bodily is a limitation of the appliance. The use of CBCT in this study provided valuable insights into the extent of changes that occurred. Table 2 shows the values of various measurements. The intermolar distance was intentionally overcorrected to account for potential relapse associated with the amount of expansion. Subsequently, the torque of the lower right molar will be corrected toward a more normal value; pretreatment = −10° and posttreatment = +1.2°, and also true for lower left molar (pretreatment = −19° and posttreatment = −6.4°), slightly negative values. Another significant outcome was the correction of the mandibular dental midline without additional intervention. Before treatment, it was deviated 2 mm to the right side. After mandibular expansion, it aligned with the maxillary midline.

As mentioned, Brodie bite requires immediate intervention to prevent more problems [12, 13]. In mixed dentition, mandibular dentoalveolar expansion may be achieved, and if adequate overjet is present, this can be combined with functional appliances. In adults when this expansion could not compensate for the discrepancy, surgery is indicated. Mandibular expansion was performed using a quad‐helix which is usually used for maxillary expansion.

Some cases have been treated in early intervention with aligners or with fixed appliances combined with or without functional appliances [19, 20, 21]. Pinho et al. treated a 12‐year‐old girl with bilateral Brodie bite and Class ll div l with increased overjet and overbite by maxillary constriction with a clear aligner [19]. Xin et al. reported successful treatment with Invisalign aligners in a 3‐year‐old child who presented with a bilateral posterior scissor bite and anterior crossbite [20]. Song et al. treated a 12‐year‐old boy with a fixed appliance with one maxillary extraction combined with a functional appliance. The functional appliance was used to resolve sagittal discrepancy besides that, help transverse discrepancy with natural growth [21].

Chandorikar et al. used a lingual transformed appliance and modified twin block to treat a 9‐year‐old male with a unilateral scissor bite in mixed dentition and alveolar narrowing [22]. Their intervention was the same as that of the presented case, as both cases suffered from mandibular constriction more than the expanded maxilla.

These patients may also be treated later with miniscrews [23, 24]. In Yassir et al. they corrected a unilateral scissor bite nonsurgically using orthodontic mini‐implants and cross elastics [23]. If this condition is left untreated and the main problem is skeletal mandibular constriction, a surgical approach such as osteogenesis distraction or corticotomy may be indicated that was not true for the present case [25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30].

The patient was advised to undergo a second phase of treatment using a hybrid functional appliance. Given the short‐term follow‐up period of 3 months, the current outcomes should be interpreted cautiously. This phase aims to improve mandibular retrognathism and facial convexity and may help address the transverse dimension discrepancy by positioning the wide posterior part of the mandible across the narrower anterior part of the maxilla. Additionally, it may contribute to leveling the occlusal plane and correcting the cant.

Limitations

The primary limitation of this study is the short‐term follow‐up period of 3 months. A longer follow‐up period, preferably exceeding 1 year, would be necessary to properly assess the risk of relapse. Additionally, the treatment outcome may not represent fully controlled bodily movement, as molar tipping occurred alongside transverse dental expansion.
