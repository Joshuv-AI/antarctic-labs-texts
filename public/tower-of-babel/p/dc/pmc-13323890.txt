Ginsenoside Rb1 mitigates senescence-associated hepatic steatosis in mice through enhanced lysine degradation
Authors: Lin Wu, Qi Si, Bin Zhou, Yanyun Che, Yong Liu, Yingying Zhao, Ximei Zhang, Guangyao Shi, Baoshun Hao, Shujie Yu, Xiaoxian Qian, Jianrui Zheng, Dinghui Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe liver is the central organ for metabolism and detoxification, and its function gradually declines with age, often accompanied by pathological changes such as lipid metabolism disorders, inflammatory responses, and fibrosis, significantly increasing the risk of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH). Ginsenoside Rb1 (Rb1) is a major active constituent of ginseng. However, its regulatory mechanism on hepatic lipid metabolism and MASLD/MASH-related phenotypes during natural aging remains unclear.ObjectiveTo explore the effects of Rb1 on liver aging phenotypes, lipid deposition, inflammation, fibrosis, and related metabolic pathways in naturally aged mice, and to further elucidate the underlying regulatory mechanisms.MethodsNaturally aged male C57BL/6J mice (72-week-old) were used and divided into the Old group and the Rb1 treatment group (Old + Rb1). Young mice (6-week-old) served as controls. Therapeutic effects were evaluated through histopathology (H&E, Masson trichrome, Oil Red O staining), immunofluorescence (p21, FASN, FABP1, PPARα), immunohistochemistry (PCNA, F4/80), RT-qPCR analysis of lipid metabolism-related genes. Additionally, untargeted metabolomics analyses of both serum and liver tissues, as well as liver transcriptome sequencing, were performed to investigate the underlying mechanisms.ResultsRb1 alleviated hepatocellular senescence, as evidenced by reduced p21 expression and increased PCNA-positive cells. It ameliorated age-related hepatic pathological damage, as evidenced by reduced inflammatory infiltration (F4/80) and collagen deposition (Masson staining), along with improved hepatocellular vacuolation and lipid accumulation (Oil Red O staining). Rb1 regulated hepatic lipid metabolism through three mechanisms: inhibiting lipid synthesis (Fasn, Acaca, Srebf1), modulating lipid transport (Fabp1, Slc27a2), and promoting lipid oxidation (Pparα, Fgf21). Serum and hepatic metabolomics consistently revealed that Rb1 activated the lysine degradation pathway, accompanied by an increase in the key intermediate metabolite trimethyllysine (TML). Liver transcriptome sequencing further indicated an increasing trend in the numbers of genes associated with the biological process of metabolic process and the molecular functions of transporter activity and transcription regulator activity. Additionally, enrichment of the biological process of lipid export from the cell was observed in the comparison between the Old and Old+Rb1 groups.ConclusionRb1 ameliorates age-related hepatic lipid accumulation and pathological damage in naturally aged mice by promoting the lysine degradation pathway and comprehensively remodeling lipid metabolic homeostasis. These findings provide a potential target and theoretical basis for intervention in aging-related MASLD/MASH.

Introduction

As populations worldwide undergo progressive aging, hepatic senescence has emerged as a critical determinant of systemic organ aging [1]. As the central metabolic hub [2], the liver is susceptible to multiple pathological alterations during senescence, including dysregulated lipid metabolism [3], inflammation [4], and fibrosis [5], all of which critically compromise general health [6]. The prevalence of age-linked hepatic conditions, such as metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) [7] and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) [8], is increasing annually, positioning them as a significant public health challenge [9]. The interplay between lipid metabolic disturbances and cellular aging has emerged as a primary area of research. Consequently, identifying methods to slow liver aging and its associated metabolic disturbances is critically important for improving the health span. Additionally, metabolic reshaping in the senescent liver encompasses a sophisticated network involving numerous pathways [10], indicating that no single molecular mechanism can adequately account for its multifaceted phenotypes. Therefore, investigating natural bioactive compounds with multifaceted regulatory functions is of considerable scientific merit.

In conventional East Asian medicine, senescence-related conditions are viewed as a syndrome of “vital qi depletion” [11]. Ginseng has been traditionally used in East Asia to treat conditions that are now known as MASLD [12]. Contemporary ethnopharmacological studies further validate that ginseng is frequently recommended for “diseases of aging” [13]. Ginsenoside Rb1 (Rb1), the predominant bioactive saponin in ginseng root, is the principal agent responsible for the traditional hepatoprotective properties of ginseng. However, the correlation between the pharmacological effects of Rb1 and the traditional use of ginseng for age-associated hepatic lipid disorders has not been validated. This study addresses this knowledge gap by examining Rb1's influence on hepatic lipid management in naturally senescent mice, which aligns with the historical use of ginseng in alleviating age-related liver impairment. Rb1 is a primary active ingredient isolated from ginseng [14]. Prior research has demonstrated that it possesses a range of pharmacological effects, including anti-inflammatory [15], antioxidant [16], and anti-apoptotic properties [17], as well as metabolism-regulating capabilities [18]. In disease models of cardiovascular disorders [19], nervous system diseases [20], and metabolic ailments [18], Rb1 has demonstrated protective qualities. However, its specific regulatory function and associated mechanisms in hepatic lipid metabolism and MASLD/MASH-like phenotypes during physiological aging remain to be systematically elucidated.

This investigation utilized a model of naturally senescent male C57BL/6J mice, combining histopathological, molecular biological, liver transcriptome sequencing, and untargeted metabolomic approaches. This study aimed to determine the effect of Rb1 on hepatic lipid regulation by modulating the lysine degradation pathway, and to evaluate its therapeutic potential for age-related metabolic disorders, such as MASLD/MASH. The findings not only offer novel experimental support for Rb1's anti-aging capabilities but also provide crucial theoretical backing for creating intervention strategies targeting age-related liver diseases.

Materials and methods

Experimental reagents

Ginsenoside Rb1 (with a purity of over 98%, determined by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC); product number JOT-10001) was acquired from Chengdu Pufei De Biotech Co., Ltd (Chengdu, China). HPLC analysis was carried out using an acetonitrile-water (31:69, v/v) mobile phase, with a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min and a detection wavelength of 203 nm (Fig. S1).

Primary antibodies used in this study included anti-p21 (rabbit monoclonal, catalog number GB11153), anti-Fatty Acid Synthase (Fasn) (mouse monoclonal, catalog number GB15546), anti- Fatty Acid Binding Protein-1(Fabp1) (rabbit polyclonal, catalog number GB115587), anti-Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha (Pparα) (rabbit monoclonal, catalog number GB11163), anti-Proliferating Cell Nuclear Antigen (PCNA) (mouse monoclonal, catalog number GB15010), and anti-F4/80 (rabbit monoclonal, catalog number GB113373)—all purchased from Servicebio Technology (Wuhan, China). TRI Reagent (product number T9424) was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). RNA reverse-transcription reagents (product number RR047A) and reagents for real-time fluorescence quantitative polymerase chain reaction (product number RR820A) were purchased from TAKARA (Japan). All other reagents used were of analytical grade.

Animals and management

Male C57BL/6J mice were purchased from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China). All animal experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of The Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (Approval No. IACUC-F3-220830) and conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines. Mice were maintained in specific pathogen-free (SPF) enclosures featuring a 12-h light/dark rhythm, a regulated temperature of 24 ± 2 °C, and relative humidity of 50 ± 5%. The animals were provided with unrestricted access to food and water. The Young control group (6-week-old mice, n = 5) was administered intraperitoneal injections of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Senescent mice (72 weeks old) were randomly allocated to two cohorts: an Old control group (Old, n = 5), which received intraperitoneal PBS, and a Rb1 treatment group (Old + Rb1, n = 5), which was administered intraperitoneal Rb1 (20 mg/kg body weight, in PBS). The dosing regimen involved six sequential days of injections with a one-day pause each week, lasting a total of six weeks. Upon completion of the treatment course, all animals were euthanized under deep anesthesia induced by an intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (150 mg/kg).

Tissue collection

After a 12-h fast, mice were anesthetized, and liver tissues were promptly collected. The collected tissues were embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound to prepare frozen sections.

RNA isolation and real-time reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from liver tissues using TRI Reagent, and complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using reverse transcription reagents in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. RT-PCR reactions were performed using the LightCycler480 System. The sequences of the primers used are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Immunofluorescence staining

Frozen liver sections were air-dried and then fixed in pre-cooled methanol at −20 °C for 20 min, followed by three washes with PBS (pH 7.4). Antigen retrieval was performed using Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) antigen retrieval buffer (pH 9.0, G1203) by heating in a microwave oven (medium heat for 10 min, followed by a 5-min stop, then medium-low heat for 5 min), and the sections were allowed to cool to room temperature (RT). After washing, the sections were circled with a hydrophobic pen (G6100) and blocked with 3% Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA, GC305010) in PBS for 30 min at room temperature. Subsequently, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted in PBS containing 1% BSA. After washing the sections, they were incubated with appropriate fluorescently conjugated secondary antibodies (Cy3-labeled Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG, GB21303, dilution 1:300; or Cy3-labeled Goat Anti-Mouse IgG, GB21301, dilution 1:300) for 50 min in the dark at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (G1012) for 10 min, and autofluorescence was quenched using Autofluorescence Quencher (G1221). Finally, the sections were mounted with Anti-fade Fluorescence Mounting Medium (G1401), and images were captured using a 3DHISTECH (Pannoramic MIDI) scanner.

Immunohistochemical staining

Frozen tissue slices were fixed with methanol and then underwent antigen retrieval in a Tris-EDTA buffer (pH 8.0) at 92 °C for 48 min. Following antigen retrieval, sections were treated with 3% H2O2 in PBS for 25 min to block endogenous peroxidase activity. After washing, non-specific binding was blocked by incubating the sections with 3% Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibodies (anti-PCNA, dilution 1:500; anti-F4/80, dilution 1:500) were added to the sections, which were then incubated overnight at 4 °C. The next day, the samples were incubated with HRP-conjugated Goat Anti-Mouse IgG (GB23301, dilution 1:200) or HRP-conjugated Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (GB23303, dilution 1:200) for 50 min at ambient temperature. DAB (G1212) was used for chromogenic detection, followed by hematoxylin counterstaining, dehydration through an ethanol series, clearing with xylene, and mounting with coverslips. The prepared slides were imaged using a 3DHISTECH Pannoramic MIDI scanner, and the positive regions were quantified using ImageJ software.

Masson trichrome staining

Masson's trichrome staining was performed using a Masson's Trichrome Stain Kit (G1006, Servicebio), following the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, the sections were stained overnight using Masson A (hematoxylin), washed with water, and differentiated. Subsequently, the sections were stained with a blend of Masson B and Masson C for 1 min, washed, and differentiated again.

Subsequently, the sections were stained in Masson D for 6 min, rinsed, stained in Masson E for 1 min, and finally stained in Masson F for 2-30 s. After rinsing with 1% acetic acid and dehydration, the sections were cleared and mounted. Under the microscope, collagen fibers appeared blue, while muscle fibers and cytoplasm appeared red. The percentage of collagen area was quantified using ImageJ software.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

Liver slices were serially submerged in dewaxing liquid I for 20 min, dewaxing liquid II for 20 min, absolute ethanol I and II for 5 min each, and 75% ethanol for 5 min, followed by a rinse in distilled water. After a 1-min HD pre-treatment step, the slices were stained with hematoxylin for 3-5 min, differentiated, blued, and subsequently stained with eosin for 15 s. Following dehydration with graded ethanol, butanol, and xylene (2 min each), the sections were mounted with neutral gum, and images were captured for examination.

Oil Red O staining

Frozen liver sections (8 μm) were prepared and air-dried. Sections were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, rinsed with distilled water, and then incubated with filtered Oil Red O working solution at room temperature for 15-20 min. After incubation, the sections were rinsed with 60% isopropanol and distilled water. Nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin for 1-2 min. The sections were then rinsed, mounted with glycerin jelly or aqueous mounting medium, and examined under a microscope. Lipid droplets appeared red under the microscope, and the percentage of lipid area was quantified using ImageJ software.

Serum untargeted metabolomics study

Frozen serum samples from mice (n = 3 per cohort, grouped as Young, Old, and Rb1) were thawed on ice and mixed by vortexing for 10 s to ensure uniformity.

Metabolic profiling was executed on a UHPLC system (Vanquish, Thermo Scientific) connected to a Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific).

An unsupervised principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted using the prcomp function in R to observe overall separation trends and intra-group variability across the Young, Old, and Rb1 cohorts. Supervised orthogonal partial least-squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was performed using the MetaboAnalystR package to improve group distinction and identify discriminatory metabolites. Before OPLS-DA, the data underwent log2 transformation and mean centering. The quality of the OPLS-DA model was assessed using R2X, R2Y, and Q2 and validated through a 200-iteration permutation test to prevent overfitting. Differential metabolites were selected based on two criteria: a Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) > 1.0 from the OPLS-DA model and P < 0.05 from Student's t-test. Pathway enrichment analysis of differential metabolites was performed using the KEGG database, with significance determined by the hypergeometric test.

Hepatic untargeted metabolomics study

Liver tissue samples from mice (n = 5 per cohort, grouped as Young, Old, and Rb1) were subjected to untargeted metabolomics analysis. Briefly, 20 mg of tissue was weighed and homogenized using a ball mill (30 Hz, 20 s) after the addition of 400 μL of 70% methanol containing an internal standard. The homogenate was centrifuged, and the supernatant was collected, incubated on ice for 15 min, placed at −20 °C for 30 min, and then re-centrifuged. The resulting extract was analyzed on the same UHPLC system used for serum samples. Data processing and metabolite identification were performed using standard workflows, and differential metabolites were selected based on VIP > 1 and P < 0.05.

RNA sequencing of liver tissues

Total RNA was extracted from mouse liver tissue samples (n = 5 per cohort, grouped as Young, Old, and Rb1), and RNA quality was assessed using a Qsep400 bioanalyzer. mRNA was enriched, reverse-transcribed into cDNA, and libraries were constructed and sequenced on the MGI platform. Clean reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome (GRCm39) using HISAT2 software. Gene expression levels were quantified as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM). Differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 package, with genes meeting the criteria of |log2FoldChange| ≥ 1 and P < 0.05 considered as significantly differentially expressed genes.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were repeated at least three times. Experimental data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9 (San Diego, CA, USA) and expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). One-way ANOVA was used to analyze the data, and Tukey's test was employed for multiple comparisons. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

As a crucial cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor, p21 is a significant marker of cellular aging. Elevated p21 expression is a strong indicator of senescence. Immunofluorescence results revealed that the p21-positive signal within the liver tissues of the Old cohort was markedly stronger than that in the Young cohort, suggesting that hepatocytes in senescent mice undergo pronounced cell cycle arrest. Immunohistochemical examination of proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA) demonstrated that the Young cohort possessed a greater percentage of PCNA-positive cells (visible as brown nuclear staining), reflecting higher proliferative activity in young mouse hepatocytes. Conversely, the fraction of PCNA-positive cells in the Old cohort was significantly lower, confirming that the proliferative capability of hepatocytes in the senescent liver is markedly suppressed. After Rb1 administration, the proportion of PCNA-positive cells in the aged mouse livers was significantly recovered, showing that Rb1 treatment not only lessens cellular senescence but also boosts the proliferative capacity of the remaining hepatocytes (Fig. 1B).

Rb1 lessens age-associated hepatic pathological injury and lipid accumulation

The disparity between cellular senescence and suppressed proliferation forms the primary cytological foundation for structural damage and functional deterioration in the senescent liver. This fundamental imbalance fosters a detrimental hepatic microenvironment, which subsequently initiates characteristic pathological features, such as inflammatory infiltration and fibrosis.

Immunohistochemical studies indicated that, compared to the Young cohort, the positive area percentage for the macrophage marker F4/80 in the livers of the Old cohort was substantially greater, showing more pronounced inflammatory infiltration in the senescent liver. After Rb1 intervention, the F4/80-positive signal was markedly diminished, implying that Rb1 enhances the microenvironmental equilibrium disrupted by hepatic metabolic disturbances by curtailing macrophage recruitment and activation (Fig. 2A). Persistent inflammatory activity is a primary driver of hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) activation and the onset of fibrosis. To determine whether inflammatory conditions result in pathological remodeling of the liver structure, we assessed collagen accumulation using Masson's trichrome staining. The findings revealed only a minor quantity of regularly arranged blue collagen fibers in the Young cohort. In contrast, the Old cohort displayed widespread, diffuse accumulation of blue collagen fibers, clearly demonstrating that natural aging induces significant hepatic fibrosis. Remarkably, Rb1 treatment significantly reversed pathological development. Compared to the Old group, collagen accumulation in the Old + Rb1 group was significantly reduced, indicating that Rb1 can suppress the progression of fibrosis in the senescent liver and safeguard the liver's interstitial architecture (Fig. 2B). Histomorphological examination with H&E staining demonstrated that the Young cohort exhibited a distinct liver lobule structure, well-organized hepatocyte cords, and normal cellular morphology. In contrast, the Old group showed typical age-related damage, including disordered liver lobule structure, hepatocyte swelling, and severe vacuolar degeneration (suggesting significant lipid accumulation), accompanied by inflammatory cell infiltration and widened interstitium. The prominent vacuolation damage observed in the H&E staining may indicated considerable lipid buildup in the senescent liver. To further ascertain the degree of lipid accumulation, we assessed liver tissue using Oil Red O staining. The findings revealed only a few scattered small lipid droplets in the livers of the Young cohort. This finding directly corroborates that the vacuolation observed in H&E staining was primarily due to lipid buildup and shows that Rb1 can efficiently alleviate age-related hepatic lipid accumulation (Fig. 2D).

Regarding lipid synthesis, relative to the Young cohort, the mRNA levels of several vital lipid synthesis genes, such as Fasn, Acaca, and Srebf1, were substantially elevated in the livers of senescent mice. However, following Rb1 intervention, the expression of these genes was markedly reduced. The expression of Scd1 followed a similar trend across the three cohorts but did not achieve statistical significance (Fig. 3A–D). Rb1 enhances hepatic lipid metabolism in senescent mice by stimulating the lysine degradation pathway

To investigate the underlying mechanisms by which Rb1 restores lipid metabolic homeostasis in the aging liver, we performed an integrated analysis combining untargeted metabolomics with transcriptomics.

Initially, serum metabolomics analysis was performed. Orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was employed to evaluate the overall differences in the metabolic profiles across the three groups. The metabolic profiles of the Old and Young groups demonstrated clear separation, with a predicted principal component interpretation rate of 27.6%. Samples within each group showed tight clustering without significant outliers (Fig. 6A), indicating that natural aging substantially modifies the metabolic characteristics of mice. Following Rb1 intervention, the metabolic profile of aged mice was clearly distinct from that of the Old group, with a predicted principal component interpretation rate of 23.7% (Fig. 6B), suggesting that Rb1 treatment can partially mitigate aging-induced metabolic changes. Volcano plots were used to identify the differential metabolites. In the comparison between the Old and Young groups, 88 significantly different metabolites were found (21 upregulated and 67 downregulated). Among these, the L-lys in the Young group was significantly higher than that in the Old group (Fig. 6C). In the comparison between the Rb1 and Old groups, 88 differential metabolites were identified (57 upregulated, 31 downregulated). Notably, the level of TML, a crucial intermediate in the lysine degradation pathway, was significantly elevated after Rb1 treatment (Fig. 6D), implying that the lysine pathway may be involved in the metabolic regulatory action of Rb1. We then created metabolic network diagrams for the three pathways to visually represent the changes in key metabolites. In the Old vs. Young comparison, the level of the initial substrate L-lysine was markedly reduced (Fig. 6G), suggesting that the metabolic flux of this pathway is impeded during aging. After Rb1 intervention, the level of the key intermediate, TML, was significantly elevated (Fig. 6H), indicating that Rb1 effectively stimulated the middle and downstream stages of the lysine degradation pathway. These results suggest that the lysine degradation pathway is a primary target of Rb1 in modulating lipid metabolism in the senescent liver. KEGG pathway differential abundance (DA) analysis further quantitatively confirmed the changes in this pathway's activity: Compared with the Young group, the DA Score for the lysine degradation pathway in the Old group was negative, showing that its activity was significantly suppressed (Fig. 6I). After Rb1 intervention, compared with the Old group, the DA Score for this pathway in the Old + Rb1 group was positive (Fig. 6J), confirming the activating effect of Rb1. Finally, heatmap analysis of the key metabolites in the lysine degradation pathway across the samples validated these findings. Compared with the Old group, the L-lysine level in the Young group was significantly higher (Fig. 6K), whereas Rb1 treatment markedly increased TML levels in aged mice (Fig. 6L). The overall expression patterns of key metabolites identified in the untargeted metabolomics analysis across the different groups are also shown in the cluster heatmap (Fig. S2).

In the liver metabolomics analysis, consistent with the results of the serum metabolomics analysis, the KEGG pathways of lysine degradation and glycerophospholipid metabolism were also identified in the Old vs. Old+Rb1 comparison group. Additionally, the fatty acid degradation pathway was annotated (Fig. S3A). KEGG enrichment analysis of differential metabolites revealed significant enrichment in the lysine degradation and fatty acid degradation pathways (Fig. S3B). The DA score further confirmed that Rb1 intervention effectively activated the lysine degradation and fatty acid degradation pathways in the liver of aged mice (Fig. S3C). K-Means clustering analysis of the relative abundance trends of differential metabolites across groups revealed that the level of TML, was downregulated in the Old group compared with the Young group, and rebounded after Rb1 intervention (Fig. S3D). This finding was consistent with the trends observed for TML in the serum metabolome. Collectively, these results suggest that Rb1 may exerts its lipid metabolism-regulating effects by activating the hepatic lysine degradation pathway.

Furthermore, we examined the expression levels of genes related to the hepatic lysine degradation pathway, including Slc25a45, Tmlhe, Aldh9a1, and Bbox1. The results showed that the mRNA levels of these genes were significantly increased in the Old + Rb1 group compared with the Old group (P < 0.05), directly confirming the activation of the lysine degradation pathway by Rb1 treatment (Fig. S4).

Finally, we performed transcriptome sequencing of liver tissues and conducted Gene Ontology (GO) classification analysis of the upregulated and downregulated differentially expressed genes (DEGs). GO classification analysis revealed that the DEGs are involved in metabolic processes, transporter activity, and transcription regulator activity (Fig. S5A). GO enrichment analysis further revealed that the term "lipid export from cell" was significantly enriched in the comparison between the Old and Old + Rb1 groups, suggesting that Rb1 may regulate hepatic lipid efflux by modulating the transport activity during metabolic processes (Fig. S5B).

Discussion

Through systematic in vivo experiments combined with untargeted metabolomic analysis and transcriptome sequencing, this study first reveals that Rb1 can significantly improve hepatic lipid metabolism disorders and related pathological phenotypes in naturally aged mice through activation of the lysine degradation pathway. Rb1 treatment effectively alleviated aging-related cell cycle arrest (as evidenced by the downregulation of p21) [21] and promoted hepatocyte proliferation (as indicated by the upregulation of PCNA) [22]. Furthermore, it significantly reduced hepatic inflammatory infiltration (reflected by a decrease in the aggregation of F4/80-positive macrophages) [23] and alleviated fibrosis (as evidenced by reduced collagen deposition). Histological examination revealed that Rb1 administration tended to improve vacuolar degeneration and lipid accumulation in the senescent liver, and Oil Red O staining directly confirmed its role in reducing lipid deposition [24]. These results suggest that Rb1 provides multifaceted protective benefits at the cellular and tissue levels. On a mechanistic front, this investigation confirmed at both genetic and protein levels that Rb1 comprehensively reshapes lipid metabolism homeostasis via three mechanisms: inhibiting lipid synthesis, regulating transporter expression while enhancing functional nuclear translocation, and promoting lipid oxidation. We observed that the mRNA expression of lipid synthesis genes (Fasn, Acaca, Srebf1) [25] and lipid transport-associated genes (Fabp1, Slc27a2, Lpl) [26] in the liver of aged mice was substantially elevated, whereas Rb1 administration effectively suppressed the these elevations. Conversely, the expression of crucial lipid oxidation-regulating genes Fgf21 and Pparα [27] was diminished with aging, and Rb1 significantly increased their mRNA levels. Moreover, the changes in the protein abundance of FASN, FABP1, and PPARα, as measured by immunofluorescence, were consistent with the trends observed in their mRNA expression.

It is particularly significant that while the mRNA and protein levels of FABP1 in the Old group were markedly higher than those in the Young group, the protein was predominantly localized to the cytoplasm with minimal presence in the nucleus, indicating a loss of functional activity. This may be attributed to the disrupted interaction between FABP1 and PPARα. As a pivotal regulator of lipid metabolism, PPARα directly governs the transcriptional expression of its target proteins, including FABP1 [28]. The age-related decline in PPARα activity, particularly manifested by decreased in its functional nuclear localization, may be the root cause of the impaired nuclear translocation of FABP1 observed in the senescent liver. Following Rb1 intervention, although the overall expression of FABP1 was significantly reduced, its nuclear accumulation was markedly enhanced compared to the Old group, showing that Rb1 re-establishes the functional nuclear translocation of FABP1 [29].

Similarly, concerning lipid oxidation, Rb1 increased the mRNA expression of Pparα and Fgf21 and enhanced the protein level of PPARα. As a crucial hormone regulating energy metabolism, the transcription of FGF21 is directly regulated by PPARα [30], and FGF21 can modulate the browning of white adipose tissue and the expression of thermogenic genes [31]. Studies have shown that various traditional Chinese medicine formulas improve hepatic lipid metabolism by regulating the PPARα-FGF21 signaling pathway. For instance, Ma et al., through hepatic transcriptome analysis, found significant enrichment of the PPARα signaling pathway and that betulin significantly upregulated FGF21 expression. Combined with computational simulations and experimental validation, it was further confirmed that betulin directly binds to the ligand-binding domain of PPARα and acts as an agonist to activate PPARα, thereby inducing FGF21 transcription [32]. Similarly, Jia et al. reported that the traditional Chinese medicine formula Mailuoning alleviated high-fat diet-induced obesity by activating the PPARα-FGF21 axis to regulate thermogenesis [33]. In our study, the concurrent upregulation of Pparα and Fgf21 suggests that activation of the PPARα-FGF21 axis may be a key mechanism by which Rb1 promotes oxidative lipid catabolism. These findings are supported not only at the levels of gene expression and protein function but also explain the phenotype of Rb1-inhibited lipid accumulation observed by Oil Red O staining, thereby providing a complete mechanistic-to-phenotypic evidence chain for the action of Rb1 in improving hepatic lipid metabolism during aging.

Untargeted metabolomic analysis further revealed the central mechanism of Rb1 action at the metabolite level: stimulation of the lysine degradation pathway. Activation of this pathway mitigates lipid accumulation by enhancing fatty acid β-oxidation. This mechanism involves the biosynthesis of L-carnitine from lysine, which is a critical step in hepatic lipid metabolism. L-carnitine enables the transport of long-chain fatty acids into the mitochondria for oxidation, thereby efficiently reducing lipid accumulation. The level of L-lysine, the initial substrate of the pathway, is positively associated with the overall activity of the pathway [34]. The higher L-lysine level in young mice implies that this pathway has a robust basal activity. In contrast, the significantly reduced L-lysine levels in the senescent state imply that the metabolic flux at the entry point of this pathway is impaired, leading to an overall decrease in pathway activity. Following Rb1 intervention, the level of TML, a key intermediate in this pathway, was significantly elevated, indicating that Rb1 effectively stimulates the middle and downstream stages of lysine degradation. TML is a vital precursor for the endogenous synthesis of carnitine [35], which is an indispensable carrier for long-chain fatty acids to enter the mitochondria for β-oxidation [36]. Carnitine production capacity directly influences fatty acid oxidation efficiency. In recent years, several studies have indicated that plasma TML levels are characteristically lower in metabolic conditions, such as obesity, insulin resistance, and type 2 diabetes [37], further corroborating the crucial role of TML in regulating lipid metabolism.

At the gene expression level, compared with the Old group, the expression levels of key enzyme genes (Slc25a45, Tmlhe, Aldh9a1, and Bbox1) in lysine degradation and carnitine biosynthesis [38]. were significantly increased in the Old+Rb1 group. Concurrently, Rb1 intervention was also accompanied by the upregulation of genes involved in fatty acid degradation, suggesting that activation of the lysine degradation pathway may enhance fatty acid β-oxidation, thereby improving lipid metabolic homeostasis in the aging liver. Collectively, these findings indicate that the lysine degradation pathway plays a critical role in lipid metabolism in the aging liver, and that Rb1 exerts its core effect in ameliorating hepatic lipid metabolic disorders by activating this pathway.

It has been documented that Rb1 boosts mitophagy by activating the PINK1/Parkin pathway [39], and this process encourages the degradation of proteins containing TML residues (such as aged or damaged histones and mitochondrial proteins). This, in turn, enhances the release of free TML and propels the entire metabolic cascade of "lysine-carnitine-fatty acid oxidation". Consequently, L-lysine and TML are not merely protein constituents but also central hub molecules linking nitrogen and lipid metabolism. Their metabolic state directly influences energy metabolic activity and metabolic health in the body.

This study reveals a novel link between the anti-aging effect of Rb1 and the lysine degradation pathway, providing a potential target for intervening in age-related liver diseases such as MASLD/MASH. However, this research still has some limitations. There is a bidirectional regulatory relationship between liver aging and lipid metabolism disorders: aging leads to a decline in hepatic lipid metabolism function, increasing the risk of MASLD/MASH; conversely, hepatic lipid metabolism dysfunction accelerates hepatocyte senescence [40], forming a vicious cycle. Although this study hypothesizes that Rb1 alleviates pathological injury in the senescent liver by regulating lipid metabolism, the specific mechanism of the interaction between aging and lipid metabolism has not been directly verified. Future research could use gene knockout models, key enzyme activity assays, and multi-organ metabolomic analysis to further explore whether Rb1 acts directly through rate-limiting enzymes of lysine degradation, such as trimethyllysine dioxygenase (TMLD).

Another limitation is that this study was conducted primarily in male mice, and female mice were not included. Future studies should investigate the effects of Rb1 in female mice and in different organ systems to enhance the generalizability and clinical applicability of these findings.

Conclusion

This study clarifies that Rb1 enhances lipid oxidation by activating the lysine degradation pathway, thereby comprehensively improving lipid metabolic homeostasis and attenuating damage in the aging liver. These findings provide experimental evidence for the application of Rb1 as a potential therapeutic agent for aging and related metabolic liver diseases while revealing novel mechanistic targets.
