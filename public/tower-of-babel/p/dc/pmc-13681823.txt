Artifacts of orthodontic materials on cone beam computed tomography images
Authors: Mustafa Taha Guller, Yavuz Selim Genç, Muhammed Enes Naralan, Alper Ozdogan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to compare the artifact expression of various materials used in orthodontic treatment on cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) images. It was hypothesized that three-dimensional (3D) printing resins and polyetheretherketone (PEEK), owing to their relatively low density and low effective atomic number, would generate fewer artifacts than conventional materials.MethodsTen orthodontic materials and an empty phantom control (Temporary CB, Permanent Crown, Biomed Clear, Glass Ionomer Cement, Composite, Resin-Modified Glass Ionomer Cement, PEEK, Titanium Grade 2, Stainless Steel, and Monolithic Zirconia) were placed in a phantom model and scanned using the Planmeca ProMax 3D system. Each material was scanned three times, and grayscale standard deviation was measured in eight peripheral regions of interest (ROIs) per scan. Data were analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn-Bonferroni pairwise comparisons (α = 0.05), supplemented by scan-level and mixed-effects sensitivity analyses to account for the non-independence of ROIs within the same scan.ResultsA statistically significant difference in artifact expression was observed among the materials (P < 0.001). The lowest artifact expression was found in Biomed Clear (BC) and PEEK, whereas the highest artifact expression was observed in stainless steel (SS). Both PEEK and BC produced significantly fewer artifacts than many conventional materials. The scan-level and mixed-effects sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the primary findings.ConclusionsBC and PEEK materials produced significantly lower artifact expression than conventional metallic and ceramic materials in CBCT imaging, potentially contributing to improved image quality. However, the present study evaluated grayscale standard deviation only; further studies assessing diagnostic performance (e.g., sensitivity/specificity) are required to determine whether the observed reduction in artifact expression translates into improved clinical diagnostic accuracy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-09387-z.Keywords: Artifacts, Dental materials, Orthodontic appliances, Polymers, Printing, Three-dimensional, Cone-beam computed tomography

Background

Fixed orthodontic appliances are the preferred method for treating most malocclusions, and their most commonly used components include brackets, tubes, band materials, bonding agents, and archwires [1].

Orthodontic brackets can be fabricated from metals such as stainless steel and titanium, or from non-metallic materials such as plastic, ceramic, and polymer composites. More recently, brackets produced with three-dimensional (3D) printing resins have also begun to be applied in clinical practice [2, 3].

Cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) provides high-resolution three-dimensional images of hard tissues with low radiation exposure. Unlike conventional two-dimensional radiographs, CBCT overcomes several diagnostic limitations, such as distortion, magnification, superimposition, and restricted depth perception, thereby offering more accurate and detailed visualization. The ability to perform volumetric analysis, obtain multiplanar (axial, coronal, and sagittal) views, and examine complex anatomical structures three-dimensionally are among the major advantages of CBCT [4, 5]. Accordingly, CBCT has become highly valuable in many branches of dentistry, including orthodontics [6].

In orthodontics, CBCT is frequently used for diagnostic and treatment planning purposes, such as the localization of impacted or ectopic teeth, assessment of dental ankylosis and supernumerary teeth, evaluation of root morphology and resorption, airway analysis, examination of cleft lip and palate, measurement of alveolar bone thickness, planning and placement of temporary anchorage devices (TADs), analysis of craniofacial deformities, preoperative planning of orthognathic surgery, evaluation of maxillary transverse dimensions, and monitoring of maxillary expansion [7, 8]. However, artifacts that appear on CBCT images can adversely affect diagnostic accuracy in many of these applications.

Artifacts are defined as radiographic distortions not directly related to the area of interest [9, 10]. These distortions may result from patient-related factors, the scanning procedure, or the technical specifications of the imaging device. Common types of CBCT artifacts include beam hardening, metal artifacts, aliasing, cone beam effect, noise artifacts, ring artifacts, and motion artifacts [9, 11]. These imaging artifacts can compromise diagnostic evaluation in numerous clinical contexts, such as detection of interproximal caries, dentin defects, root fractures, root resorptions, perforations, internal resorptions, bone defects, assessment of osseointegration around implants, and determination of root proximity to TADs [9, 12–16]. Therefore, clinicians must recognize the presence of such artifacts and interpret CBCT data accordingly.

In addition to diagnostic limitations, artifacts may also affect the comparability of digital measurements and have been reported to challenge automated segmentation pipelines that operate on CBCT volumes [17]. While these effects on artificial intelligence (AI)-based applications have not been quantitatively addressed in the present study, the issue is increasingly relevant for digital workflows in dentistry.

Among CBCT-related artifacts associated with dense materials, beam hardening artifacts are particularly significant. These occur when lower-energy X-ray photons are absorbed by dense materials, allowing higher-energy photons to remain, thus “hardening” the beam [18, 19]. Materials with higher atomic numbers are more likely to absorb low-energy photons, contributing to this phenomenon [20]. The resulting artifacts appear as streaks or dark bands in the reconstructed 3D volume, which may obscure anatomical structures and hinder proper evaluation. Many dental materials contain high atomic number elements, making them prone to artifact generation in CBCT imaging [21]. Therefore, it is essential to examine the artifact expression of materials used in fixed orthodontic treatment. Since materials are composed of elements with varying atomic numbers, they do not produce artifacts to the same extent [22, 23]. Notably, material density has a greater influence on artifact severity than CBCT acquisition parameters [22]. Thus, selecting materials with low artifact expression may contribute to more accurate CBCT evaluations in the future.

The present study aims to compare the artifact expression produced by various materials commonly used in fixed orthodontic treatment on CBCT images. The research hypothesis is that 3D-printed resins and polyetheretherketone (PEEK) generate significantly fewer artifacts on CBCT images than conventional orthodontic materials. Additionally, it is hypothesized that artifact severity is positively and monotonically associated with material density.

Methods

The study followed the methodology outlined by Köse et al. [24] and Nejaim et al. [21]. A uniform phantom model, measuring 4.0 cm in both diameter and height, made of polymethylmethacrylate, and containing a central cylindrical cavity (0.5 cm diameter and height), was utilized. Cylindrical inserts (0.5 × 0.5 cm) produced using computer aided design-computer aided manufacturing (CAD-CAM) were inserted into the phantom’s cavity as part of the study. All three 3D printing resins (TE, PC and BC) were used to fabricate cylindrical specimens with relevant dimensions. The digital models were designed and exported in STL format, then printed using a Form 3B+ stereolithography printer (Formlabs, Inc., Somerville, MA, USA). Printing and post-processing procedures including support generation, cleaning with isopropyl alcohol, and UV post-curing were performed in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations. The GI, CO, and RG specimens were produced in the cylindrical molds and self-cured or light-cured (Valo Grand; Ultradent, USA). ZI and PEEK specimens were produced by CAD-CAM system (Ceramill Motion; Amann Girrbach) as stated by digital models of the cylindrical specimens. Monolithic zirconia specimen was sintered in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations. The SS and TI specimens were produced by computer numerical control (CNC) device. The final dimensions of all specimens were confirmed with a digital calliper, and all samples were stored in distilled water at 4 °C for 24 h. A single specimen was fabricated for each material and subsequently scanned three times.

Imaging was performed using a single device, the Planmeca ProMax 3D Classic (Planmeca Oy, Helsinki, Finland), with exposure parameters set to 8 × 8 cm field of view (FOV), 90 kV, 6.3 mA, 12 s, and a voxel size of 200 μm. The phantom was centered within the FOV, and each material, along with the empty phantom, was scanned three times. To assess artifacts, the standard deviation (SD) of grey values was calculated for the regions of interest (ROI) using Planmeca Romexis 4.6.2.R software (PLANMECA Romexis, Helsinki, Finland). No post-processing was applied to the reconstructed images.

For the analysis, the axial reconstruction corresponding to the centre of the cylindrical samples was utilized. An ROI large enough to encompass the entire material was defined, along with eight additional equally sized ROIs around it. (Fig. 1) The representative axial slices in Fig. 1 were selected as the central reconstruction slice corresponding to the mid-height of each cylindrical specimen, with all images displayed at identical window/level settings to ensure visual comparability across materials. Fig. 1 illustrates representative axial CBCT images from four selected materials (SS, BC, CO, and ZI) chosen to demonstrate the range of artifact expression observed across the study sample. Quantitative analysis of phantom homogeneity was performed using the software Romexis version: 4.6.2.R 18.10.2017 build 81 (Planmeca Romexis, Helsinki, Finland). A rectangular ROI tool was utilized to select the specific areas on the cross-sectional images. For each ROI, the mean gray value and the SD of the voxel values were calculated. The SD was specifically recorded to quantify the variation of grayscale values around each material insert; a higher SD indicates greater grayscale dispersion and, in this in vitro context, greater artifact expression around the material. For each material, three scans were obtained; in each scan, the SD of grayscale values was recorded individually for each of the eight peripheral ROIs (the central ROI containing the material was excluded). This procedure yielded 24 ROI-level SD measurements per material across the three scans, which were used as the primary descriptive statistics in the comparative analysis [21]. All measurements were performed by the same dentomaxillofacial radiologist.

Statistical analysis

For each orthodontic material, three separate CBCT images were obtained. The images were divided into a 3 × 3 grid system, creating a total of nine ROIs. Since the central ROI contained the material itself, it was excluded from the analysis, and the eight surrounding ROIs were used. Thus, a total of 24 measurements for each material were included in the statistical evaluation. This sampling design was based on the methodological precedent established by previous in vitro CBCT artifact studies that have employed three scans per material with eight peripheral ROIs, demonstrating sufficient sensitivity to detect material-related differences in artifact expression [21, 24].

The statistical analyses of the data were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics v20.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of the measurements was assessed with the Shapiro-Wilk test. For data that did not follow a normal distribution, the Kruskal–Wallis nonparametric test was applied. Where the Kruskal–Wallis test indicated a significant overall difference, pairwise comparisons were conducted using Dunn’s test with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. The level of significance was set at α = 0.05.

In addition, the median and mean rank values of the ROI measurements were calculated and presented in tables. This allowed for a more comprehensive demonstration of the distribution characteristics of the data and the differences among the groups.

Consistent with the sample-size rationale outlined above, the region of interest (ROI) served as the spatial sampling unit for artifact quantification. The eight peripheral ROIs, positioned at equidistant angular locations, captured the directional propagation of beam-hardening artifacts at distinct spatial positions rather than as repeated measurements of the same physical quantity.

The relationship between material density and median artifact expression was evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ). As a sensitivity analysis to address the within-scan correlation among ROIs, the eight peripheral SD values within each scan were first averaged to obtain a single scan-level summary value (three values per material, 33 in total), and the Kruskal–Wallis test and the Spearman correlation were re-computed on these scan-averaged data. To further address potential non-independence among the eight peripheral ROIs within each scan, a linear mixed-effects model was fitted to log-transformed grayscale SD values, with material as a fixed effect and a random intercept for scan nested within material. For materials whose density was reported as a range, the midpoint of the range was used in the Spearman correlation analysis, and a sensitivity analysis using the lower and upper bounds of the ranges was additionally performed. Intra-rater reliability was quantified by re-measurement after a two-week washout, using a two-way mixed-effects intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC; absolute agreement, single measurement) with 95% confidence intervals (CI).

Results

According to the Kruskal–Wallis test results, statistically significant differences were found among the artifact expression of the materials (H = 161.79, df = 10, P < 0.001). BC specimen showed the lowest median SD of grayscale values (9.03), whereas SS specimen showed the highest median SD of grayscale values (68.39). Mean rank and median SD values of the specimens are shown in Table 1. Pairwise comparisons revealed three principal patterns (Table 2; exact P-values in the lower triangle): (i) the low-density polymeric materials BC and PEEK produced significantly fewer artifacts than all higher-density materials (P ≤ 0.046 for all pairwise comparisons against CO, PC, TE, GI, RG, TI, SS and ZI), and were not significantly different from the empty phantom (PH); (ii) the high-density metallic and ceramic materials (TI, SS, ZI) clustered as the highest artifact group, with SS producing significantly higher artifact expression than CO (P = 0.001) and PC (P = 0.026); and (iii) the empty phantom (PH) differed significantly from TE (P = 0.019) and from all higher-density materials (GI, RG, TI, SS, ZI; P < 0.001). Detailed pairwise P-values are presented in Table 2. Median artifact values are visualized in Fig. 2, with materials sorted in ascending order to facilitate comparison across material categories. The relationship between material density and median artifact expression was strongly positive: Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was ρ = 0.93 (P < 0.001, n = 11), indicating that materials with higher density produced consistently higher artifact expression. Sensitivity analysis using scan-averaged values (one summary value per scan, three per material) yielded a consistent result (Kruskal–Wallis H = 29.83, df = 10, P < 0.001; Spearman ρ = 0.92, P < 0.001), supporting the robustness of the ROI-level analysis. Because the scan-averaged dataset comprised only three values per material, Dunn–Bonferroni pairwise comparisons on these data would be statistically underpowered and not meaningfully interpretable after multiple-comparison correction; the scan-averaged robustness check was therefore deliberately restricted to the omnibus Kruskal–Wallis test and the Spearman correlation, both of which were concordant with the ROI-level analysis. The full pairwise pattern is reported at the ROI level in Table 2. A linear mixed-effects model accounting for the nesting of ROIs within scans confirmed a highly significant overall difference among materials (likelihood-ratio chi-square (10) = 102.3, P < 0.001). Although ROIs sharing a scan were correlated (within-scan intraclass correlation = 0.51; design effect approximately 4.6), accounting for this clustering did not alter the overall significance of the material effect, which also remained significant in the more conservative scan-level analysis; the ROI-level pairwise comparisons should nonetheless be interpreted with appropriate caution in light of this within-scan correlation. A sensitivity analysis using the lower and upper bounds of the reported density ranges yielded consistent correlations (Spearman rho = 0.89–0.93 including the empty phantom and rho = 0.86–0.92 excluding it; all P < 0.01), confirming that the density–artifact association is robust to the density-value approximation. A further sensitivity check excluding the empty phantom yielded a similar Spearman correlation (ρ = 0.92, P < 0.001, n = 10), confirming the robustness of the density–artifact association. Intra-examiner reliability of the ROI measurements was excellent (ICC = 0.92; 95% CI: 0.89–0.95), based on 264 paired measurements obtained two weeks apart.

Discussion

This study aimed to comparatively evaluate the artifact expression produced by fixed orthodontic materials on CBCT images. To enable an optimal comparison of artifact values, all samples were fabricated in identical sizes and shapes using CAD-CAM units and stereolithographic 3D printing, and all CBCT scans were conducted under fixed imaging parameters.

The findings support the study hypothesis. The absence of a statistically significant difference between BC, PEEK, and PH should be interpreted with caution and does not constitute statistical equivalence; rather, it indicates that with the present sample size and analytical approach, no detectable difference was observed among these low-density groups. This is consistent with the overall pattern of lower artifact expression by low-density polymeric materials. However, the lack of a significant difference between the non-3D-printed Composite CO and PH also highlights the potential advantage of polymer-based materials in minimizing artifacts. On the other hand, although both TE and PEEK are polymer-based materials of relatively low density compared with metals and ceramics, TE differed significantly from PH, which limits the absolute validity of the hypothesis. TE is a photopolymerizable resin-based material, whereas PEEK is a thermoplastic polymer with a substantially different chemical structure; the two should not be regarded as compositionally similar. Thus, while the hypothesis is supported, it carries partial limitations depending on the material.

Artifact formation has also been studied across various dental applications, including restorations, prosthetic materials, dental implants, post systems, and root canal fillings [13, 14, 18, 25, 26]. These studies emphasize how the chemical composition, elemental atomic numbers, and physical properties of materials influence artifact generation and compromise diagnostic accuracy. Similarly, orthodontic materials have been reported to induce artifacts on CBCT images, a critical factor in diagnostic interpretation [8]. In this context, our study evaluated the artifact expression of various orthodontic materials, comparing the results with existing literature and offering clinical implications.

Although fixed orthodontic appliances are highly effective in clinical practice, they can promote plaque accumulation and compromise periodontal health. Brackets, bands, and ligature components hinder proper brushing and diminish the self-cleaning effect of saliva and tongue movement [27, 28]. Despite these drawbacks, fixed appliances remain among the most frequently preferred treatment options due to their predictable and successful outcomes [29].

Fixed orthodontic treatment relies on a variety of materials and components to guide tooth movement and execute the treatment plan, including brackets, buccal tubes, molar bands, crown materials, lingual buttons, ligature wires, and archwires [30]. In our study, commonly used clinical materials were evaluated for artifact production on CBCT images, with all samples standardized in size and shape. Ligature and archwires were excluded due to their easy removability and their classification as non-fixed components.

Artifact expression also depends on the technical specifications of the imaging system. Codari et al. [13] and Hirschinger et al. [31] compared artifact expression between different CBCT devices and found notable differences. These variations were attributed to differences in tube voltage and current, scan time, field of view (FOV), voxel size, and imaging protocols [13]. Therefore, to minimize parameter-related variability, our study used a single CBCT device with fixed settings.

Artifact expression was analyzed based on the median grey value deviations, in line with the non-parametric framework used in the present study; this choice was further supported by the fact that 10 of the 11 materials showed non-normal, right-skewed distributions (Shapiro–Wilk P < 0.05), for which the median is a more representative descriptor than the mean. The results demonstrated that different orthodontic materials produce varying degrees of artifact expression. When ordered by median, the lowest artifact expression was observed in BC, followed by PEEK, CO, PC, TE, GI, TI, RG, ZI, and SS. Notably, low-density polymeric materials such as BC and PEEK generated significantly fewer artifacts, while high-density metallic materials like SS caused the most severe artifact expression (Tables 1 and 3 and Additional file 1: Table S1).

Our findings confirm a strong positive monotonic relationship between material density and artifact expression (Spearman’s ρ = 0.93, P < 0.001), in agreement with the underlying physics of beam-hardening artifact formation in CBCT and with prior reports [23]. Higher-density materials such as SS and ZI generated the most pronounced artifacts, while low-density polymeric materials (BC and PEEK) generated the fewest, consistent with their lower X-ray attenuation and reduced beam-hardening interaction.

To our knowledge, there are no previous studies evaluating the artifact expression of BC, one of the materials with the lowest artifact expression in our study. BC is a transparent, biocompatible photopolymer resin composed of Bisphenol A dimethacrylate (50–70%), Urethane dimethacrylate (25–45%), and 2-hydroxyethyl methacrylate (HEMA, 7–10%). Both BC and PEEK are characterized by low density (1.09 g/cm³ and 1.32 g/cm³, respectively) and low effective atomic number, which together limit X-ray attenuation and beam-hardening interactions, accounting for their minimal artifact expression [24, 32].

Although studies on BC are limited, it has been reported to be compatible with common sterilization methods and has been used in the fabrication of orthognathic surgical splints and face masks for class III malocclusion treatment [33–35]. A study by Safi’ai et al. [36] found that BC exhibits acceptable mechanical strength and biocompatibility, though limitations in colour stability and cleanability have also been reported. Given its low artifact potential, BC may offer advantages in CBCT-intensive treatment workflows; however, broader recommendations for routine orthodontic use would require additional long-term evidence regarding wear resistance, colour stability, and mechanical durability under intraoral conditions.

TE and PC are polymer-based materials with densities between 1.4 and 1.5 g/cm³. Both contain Bisphenol A dimethacrylate (50–75%) and diphenyl(2,4,6-trimethylbenzoyl) phosphine oxide (2.5%) as primary components. Our findings revealed no statistically significant difference in artifact expression between them. However, minor differences may have arisen due to unreported ingredients present in trace amounts (< 1%), often protected as trade secrets by manufacturers. As noted by Diken Türksayar and Baytur [37], a lack of detailed compositional data for non-acrylic ester monomer materials complicates efforts to associate artifact expression with specific chemical properties in 3D printer resins.

GI and CO are commonly used to bond bands and brackets or to increase vertical dimension in fixed treatments [38]. Therefore, GI, RG, and CO were included in our artifact assessment. Although GI showed slightly lower artifact expression than RG, the difference was not statistically significant. GI contains silane-treated glass, 2-hydroxy-1,3-dimethacryloxypropane, and citric acid dimethacrylate oligomer. RG, in contrast, is a dual-paste material. Paste A includes 2-hydroxyethyl methacrylate, various silanized compounds, and urethane dimethacrylate, while Paste B contains ytterbium trifluoride, polyacrylic acid, tartaric acid, and quartz. Both materials are polymeric but differ in glass content, resin base, and density, which can influence artifact behaviour. Ytterbium fluoride in RG increases radiopacity and may indirectly contribute to higher artifact expression [39]. Thus, while not statistically significant, RG produced more artifacts than GI, consistent with similar findings in the literature [40].

CO contains silane-treated quartz, Bisphenol A diglycidyl ether dimethacrylate, Bisphenol A dimethacrylate, silane-treated silica, diphenyliodonium hexafluorophosphate, and triphenylantimony. In our study, CO produced lower artifact expression than GI and RG. However, artifact expression can vary across different formulations of CO and GI. Some studies have found higher artifacts in CO, while others reported more in GI [40, 41]. Kuusisto et al. [42] compared artifact expression in CO implants with varying radiopacities, finding that lower BaAlSiO₂ content (< 20%) resulted in significantly fewer artifacts. Accordingly, when using materials such as CO, GI, and RG in clinical CBCT, preference should be given to alternatives with lower artifact potential.

Among the metallic materials, SS showed numerically higher median artifact values than TI (68.39 vs. 39.80); however, the pairwise comparison did not reach statistical significance (Table 2). This trend is nonetheless consistent with the higher density (7.97 g/cm³) and the presence of higher atomic number elements (Fe-26, Cr-24, Ni-28, Mo-42) in SS compared with commercially pure Titanium Grade 2 (density 4.51 g/cm³, predominantly composed of Ti-22 with trace amounts of O, N, C, and H). The lack of statistical significance is likely related to the high inherent variability of artifact distribution around dense metallic specimens. These differences in elemental composition and density increase X-ray absorption and scattering in SS, leading to more prominent CBCT artifacts.

Literature comparing ZI with metal alloys reports inconsistent results. Some studies found ZI generated higher artifact expression [43–45], while others found no significant difference [24]. Our study similarly identified ZI as the second most artifact-prone material after SS when ordered by median. The lack of significant difference among the high-density materials (TI, RG, ZI, SS) supports the idea that ZI may behave similarly to metallic materials in CBCT imaging.

Different bracket systems produce different artifact expression based on material composition and density. Troca et al. [46] compared metallic, ceramic, self-ligating ceramic brackets with NiTi clips, and archwires made of NiTi and CrNi. They found that metal brackets with high Cr (24) and Ni (28) content produced the most artifacts, followed by NiTi-clipped ceramics, while non-metallic ceramics produced the least. Sanders et al. [15] evaluated stainless steel, titanium (Orthos2, Ormco), ceramic (Inspire Ice, Ormco), and plastic (Vogue, GAC) brackets and found stainless steel generated the most artifacts, followed by titanium, ceramic, and plastic. Our study mirrored this trend, with SS generating the highest artifacts due to its high Cr and Ni content. Although TI followed, the difference was not statistically significant. These results confirm that metallic orthodontic materials particularly those containing Cr (24), Ni (28), and Co (27) have higher artifact potential in CBCT.

The diagnostic impact of an artifact depends not only on its severity but also on its location relative to critical anatomical structures. A small artifact can markedly reduce the diagnostic value of images when it obscures essential regions, whereas larger artifacts arising in clinically insignificant areas may have only a limited effect. Moreover, streak artifacts can extend to distant anatomical sites, thereby degrading overall image quality.

In orthodontic CBCT imaging, diagnostic accuracy is contingent upon the precise delineation of osseous boundaries and adjacent anatomical structures, which are prerequisites for procedures such as temporary anchorage device (TAD) placement, root proximity and external root resorption assessment, impacted tooth localization, and orthognathic surgery planning [7, 8]. Beam hardening and streak artifacts arising from high-density materials such as SS and TI may obscure or distort these critical anatomical details, thereby introducing diagnostic ambiguity. It should be emphasized that these findings reflect artifact behaviour under standardized in vitro conditions, using grayscale variability as a surrogate measure; the study does not directly assess diagnostic accuracy, lesion detection, landmark identification, or segmentation accuracy, and the results should not be extrapolated to clinical diagnostic performance without further validation.

Specifically, increased artifact expression can lead to inaccurate visualization of cortical bone thickness and trabecular patterns, potentially compromising optimal TAD positioning and increasing the risk of cortical perforation or insufficient primary stability [12]. In the assessment of root proximity and external root resorption, streak artifacts may mask subtle resorptive defects and cause inaccurate measurement of interradicular distances [9, 16]. Similarly, in impacted tooth localization, artifacts may obscure the spatial relationship between the impacted tooth and adjacent roots or vital structures, affecting surgical planning. In orthognathic surgery planning, artifact-induced distortion of skeletal contours may reduce the reliability of three-dimensional measurements and virtual simulations [7].

Consequently, the selection of low-artifact materials in clinical scenarios where CBCT imaging is required (for example, planning and verification of TAD placement near root surfaces, evaluation of root proximity to anatomical landmarks, and post-treatment imaging following fixed appliance therapy) may help preserve image quality. Whether this translates into measurable improvements in diagnostic accuracy requires dedicated diagnostic-performance studies (sensitivity/specificity, area under ROC curve), which were beyond the scope of the present in vitro evaluation.

It should also be acknowledged that CBCT-related artifacts are inherently anisotropic in nature. Due to the Feldkamp–Davis–Kress (FDK) cone-beam back-projection geometry, beam-hardening and streak artifacts are not uniformly distributed in the reconstructed volume, but vary as a function of slice position and projection trajectory around the X-ray source [6, 9, 47]. In the present study, this geometric dependency was partially mitigated by analyzing eight peripheral ROIs distributed around the central material insert at the mid-height slice of each cylindrical specimen, thereby capturing artifact variation across multiple angular directions; however, the inherent anisotropy of cone-beam artifact propagation should still be considered when interpreting comparisons between high- and low-density materials.

This study has several limitations. First, no metal artifact reduction (MAR) algorithm was applied to the reconstructed volumes; the use of MAR algorithms can substantially modify the magnitude and spatial distribution of artifact expression and should be addressed in future investigations. Second, all images were acquired using a single CBCT device (Planmeca ProMax 3D Classic), and artifact behaviour may differ across devices employing different detector technologies, beam-shaping filters, and reconstruction kernels. In addition, only a single field-of-view (FOV) and voxel-size combination was used; both parameters are documented in the literature as influential factors in artifact expression and warrant evaluation in future studies. Third, the standardized cylindrical geometry of the specimens (0.5 × 0.5 cm) does not fully reproduce the heterogeneous shapes, surface curvatures, and assembly configurations encountered in clinical fixed orthodontic appliances; therefore, extrapolation of these in vitro findings to clinical CBCT acquisitions should be made with caution. Finally, artifact expression was quantified solely as the SD of grayscale values within peripheral ROIs; alternative quantitative metrics (e.g., contrast-to-noise ratio, frequency-domain analysis, observer-based detectability) and direct diagnostic-performance studies were not performed and represent important avenues for future research. In addition, a single specimen was used per material; repeated scanning of the same specimen captures acquisition-related variability but not specimen-level or fabrication-related variability, which may be particularly relevant for 3D-printed resins, composites, and glass-ionomer materials and cements, and the results should therefore not be interpreted as fully representing the intrinsic variability of each material class. Further limitations include the simplified PMMA phantom, which lacks soft-tissue simulation and does not reproduce real craniofacial anatomy; the possible non-independence of ROIs within the same scan; the limited number of scans per material; and the approximate, range-based density values used for some materials.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that BC and PEEK materials produced the lowest artifact expression on CBCT images. However, material selection in orthodontics should not rely solely on radiographic performance. Biomechanical compatibility, biocompatibility, aesthetic properties, colour stability, friction characteristics, and long-term durability must also be considered to ensure clinical success. In scenarios where CBCT imaging is frequently required or repeated, these low-artifact materials may be considered, provided that comparative evidence on their long-term mechanical, biological, and aesthetic performance under orthodontic conditions is also taken into account.

Artifact reduction is not solely dependent on material choice. Optimization of imaging techniques, adjustment of scan parameters, and the use of artifact-reduction software algorithms also play a crucial role in improving diagnostic accuracy. Such considerations may also be relevant for the future development of AI-based analytical tools, although the present study did not evaluate this aspect directly.
