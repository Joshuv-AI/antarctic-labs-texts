Nausea and vomiting of pregnancy in relation to fear of childbirth: Evidence from a Finnish cohort
Authors: Anna Murto, Linda Laitinen, Eeva Terävä‐Utti, Miina Nurmi, Mari Koivisto, Päivi Polo‐Kantola
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionNausea and vomiting of pregnancy (NVP) is a common disorder affecting most pregnant women. Previous studies have suggested associations between NVP and psychological pregnancy complications, whereas only a limited number of studies have explored a possible association with fear of childbirth (FOC). Shared biological underlying factors have been proposed, but the precise mechanisms remain poorly understood. The objective of our study was to explore the relationship between NVP and FOC.Material and MethodsPregnant women (n = 2411) were recruited from maternity health care clinics in the Turku area, Finland. The severity of NVP was evaluated with the Pregnancy‐Unique Quantification of Emesis (PUQE) questionnaire, and the women were categorized into two groups: the no/mild NVP group (PUQE points 3–6, n = 963) and the moderate/severe NVP group (PUQE points 7–15, n = 1366). Data on FOC (International Statistical Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision [ICD‐10] code O99.80) and maternal characteristics (age, body mass index, parity, marital status, employment status, and smoking) were drawn from the Finnish Medical Birth Register. The association between NVP and FOC was evaluated using logistic regression adjusted for maternal characteristics.ResultsThe data of 2329 women were eligible for the analysis. Compared with the women in the no/mild NVP group, those in the moderate/severe NVP group were more likely to suffer from FOC (OR 1.69, 95% CI 1.11–2.58, p = 0.015). The difference between the groups remained after adjustment for maternal background factors (aOR 1.72, 95% CI 1.09–2.71, p = 0.019).ConclusionsWe showed an association between more severe NVP and FOC. The evidence from our study implies that, beyond the physical manifestation, NVP may contribute to psychological distress and influence women's perception of childbirth. These findings highlight the importance of also addressing the psychological aspects of NVP in clinical care.Keywords: fear of childbirth, mothers, nausea and vomiting of pregnancy, pregnancy, women

Key message

Our findings suggest an association between nausea and vomiting of pregnancy and fear of childbirth, underscoring the need for a holistic clinical approach addressing both physical and psychological aspects.

INTRODUCTION

Up to 80% of pregnancies are complicated by some degree of nausea and vomiting of pregnancy (NVP). Symptoms are typically present in the first trimester and resolve by mid‐pregnancy. 1 Nevertheless, in some women, the symptoms last until the end of pregnancy 2 and thus, cause a considerable burden for both the physical and mental quality of life. 3

In general, pregnancy and fetal outcomes are deemed to be favorable in women suffering from NVP. 4 Pregnancies complicated with NVP have been associated, for instance, with a lower rate of miscarriages. 5 Nevertheless, if untreated, NVP and its severe form, hyperemesis gravidarum (HG), can even lead to potentially serious maternal complications. 6 Furthermore, previous studies have proposed associations between NVP/HG and pregnancy complications. 7 Associations between NVP/HG and psychiatric morbidity, such as anxiety and depression, have also been found. 8 , 9

Considering the associations between NVP/HG and psychiatric morbidity, it is also relevant to examine other aspects of maternal mental well‐being, including fear of childbirth (FOC). Independently, FOC is a well‐recognized phenomenon. Approximately one in 10 pregnant women experience severe FOC. 10 Beyond its detrimental effects on the quality of life during pregnancy, FOC is a significant factor in women's decisions to request an elective cesarean section. 11 It has also been linked to future family planning decisions, including avoidance of subsequent pregnancies, 12 as well as to anxiety and depression. 13 , 14

Given that NVP/HG and FOC likely share overlapping risk factors, including compromised maternal mental well‐being, such as depression and anxiety 8 , 13 , 15 as well as lack of social support, 16 , 17 an association between these two conditions can be hypothesized. Furthermore, NVP/HG may contribute to the development of FOC. Nevertheless, previous literature from the field is very limited. In a Turkish study, women with severe NVP had greater FOC. 18 A British case series supported this association, reporting overrepresentation of HG among women with FOC. 19 In addition, an Italian study evaluating the association between somatic symptoms during pregnancy and anxiety and depressive symptoms postpartum found that higher levels of vomiting during pregnancy were associated with greater FOC, and that FOC partly mediated the association between vomiting and postpartum depression. 20

The aim of our study was to investigate the association between NVP and FOC. We hypothesized that women experiencing more severe NVP are more likely to have FOC.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

The women were recruited from 33 maternity clinics in Turku city area (Finland) between 2011 and 2014. Nurses working in the clinics enrolled the women during their routine mid‐pregnancy visits. The data collection was supervised by the researchers. The original database contained information on 2411 women, but due to missing data or consent, 82 women were excluded, leaving the data of 2329 women for the analysis (Figure 1). During 2011 and 2014, the time of recruitment period for the participants, approximately 1800 children were born annually in Turku and 1200 in the surrounding municipalities, corresponding to an estimated participation rate of about 26%. To assess the presence of NVP in their current pregnancy, the Pregnancy‐Unique Quantification of Emesis (PUQE) questionnaire was used, referring to the worst 12‐h period of the current pregnancy, in accordance with guidelines: no NVP (PUQE points 3, n = 278), mild NVP (PUQE points 4–6, n = 685), moderate NVP (PUQE points 7–12, n = 1215), and severe NVP (PUQE points 13–15, n = 151). 21 The mean gestational age at the time of participation, as obtained from the study questionnaire, was 20.2 weeks (SD 4.5; range 7–40 weeks).

The flowchart of the study.

The diagnosis of FOC was drawn from the Finnish Medical Birth Register (FMBR). The code O99.80 from International Statistical Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD‐10) was used, as it is the standard diagnostic code for FOC also in Finland. FMBR was also used to collect the maternal basic characteristics: age (years, calculated by comparing the date of birth of the mother to the date of birth of the newborn), body mass index (BMI, kg/m2, at the beginning of the pregnancy), parity (0/≥1), marital status (cohabited/single), employment status (employed/unemployed), smoking (no/yes), as well as data on mode of delivery (vaginal/acute cesarean section/elective cesarean section).

Mild PUQE scores indicate mild NVP, characterized by restricted time and frequency of nausea, vomiting, or retching. In contrast, moderate and severe PUQE scores reflect a substantially more severe illness, which may be associated with clinically significant implications. To explore the feasibility of stratifying NVP by severity, baseline characteristics were compared across all four NVP groups. A difference was found only in parity (in all comparisons but mild vs. severe NVP and moderate vs. severe NVP) so that multiparous women tended to have higher PUQE scores. Accordingly, two study groups were formed: women with no NVP and mild NVP formed the no/mild NVP group (PUQE points 3–6, n = 963, 41.4%) and women with moderate NVP and severe NVP formed the moderate/severe NVP group (PUQE points 7–15, n = 1366, 58.6%).

Statistical analyses

Categorical variables were characterized using frequencies and percent. For normally distributed variables, means with standard deviations (SDs), and ranges (min–max), and for skewed distributed variables, medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs), and ranges (min–max) are presented. The comparisons between the NVP groups (no/mild vs. moderate/severe) for continuous variables were carried out using the non‐parametric Kruskal–Wallis test or with two‐sample t‐tests. With categorical variables, between‐group comparisons were assessed with the chi‐squared test. At first, the association between NVP and FOC was evaluated using logistic regression. Thereafter, all analyses were adjusted for age, BMI, parity, marital status, employment status, and smoking. The results are presented with odds ratios (ORs), adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and p values. Furthermore, the frequencies (percent) of elective cesarean sections due to FOC were evaluated in the NVP groups. All statistical tests were performed as 2‐sided, with a significance level set at 0.05. The analyses were carried out using SAS® System, version 9.4 for Windows (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

RESULTS

Overall, the mean age of the women was 30.7 years (SD 4.7, range 17.5–45.1 years) and the median BMI 23.5 kg/m2 (Q1: 21.3, Q3: 26.5, range 15.1–57.8 kg/m2). From the entire study cohort, 45.9% (n = 1070) were primiparas, 95.4% (n = 2222) cohabited, 72.2% (n = 1668) were working, and 12.9% (n = 301) were smokers. A total of 84.5% (n = 1967) of the women delivered vaginally. Compared with the no/mild NVP group, women in the moderate/severe NVP group were more often single (p = 0.021) and more often multiparas (p < 0.001). FOC was diagnosed in 4.6% (n = 107) of the women. Taken the four NVP groups separately, the frequencies of FOC were as follows: no NVP 2.5% (n = 7/278), mild NVP 3.7% (n = 25/685), moderate NVP 5.6% (n = 68/1215), and severe NVP 4.6% (n = 7/151). When comparing all these four groups, there was a tendency of a statistically significant association between NVP and FOC over the groups (p = 0.075).

Compared with the women with no/mild NVP, women with moderate/severe NVP were more likely to suffer from FOC (OR 1.69, 95% CI 1.11–2.58, p = 0.015). According to the multivariate analysis, after adjustments, the difference remained (aOR 1.72, 95% CI 1.09–2.71, p = 0.019). In addition, multiparous women had higher odds for FOC (aOR 1.78, 95% CI 1.10–2.86, p = 0.018). Instead, other maternal basic characteristics of age, BMI, marital status, employment status, and smoking were not associated with FOC (Figure 2). Among women with both moderate/severe NVP and FOC, 24.3% (n = 17) delivered by elective cesarean section, whereas 18.8% (n = 6) of women with no/mild NVP and FOC delivered by elective cesarean section.

DISCUSSION

Our results indicated that women suffering from more severe NVP were more likely to suffer from FOC. These associations remained after adjusting for maternal basic characteristics. Furthermore, women with more severe NVP and FOC were more likely to deliver with an elective cesarean section. Our findings suggest that NVP is not only a somatic pregnancy complication but may give rise to mental vulnerability with potential implications also for the mode of delivery.

It is challenging to compare our results with those of previous studies due to the limited literature in the field and substantial heterogeneity in study designs and diagnostic criteria. In particular, standardized and comprehensive diagnostic criteria for NVP/HG, such as the Windsor criteria, were introduced only recently in 2021. 22 In a retrospective observational cross‐sectional study involving 258 women, Tambelli et al. showed that FOC may partially mediate the association between somatic symptoms during pregnancy—especially vomiting—and postpartum mental health outcomes. 20 This finding suggests that physical symptoms are not merely a somatic burden but may also have consequences for women's mental health. Our findings align with this view, as FOC was more prevalent among women experiencing more severe NVP. Compared with our study, Tambelli et al. assessed nausea and vomiting without a validated instrument and included a smaller sample, which may limit direct comparability between the studies.

Our findings are broadly consistent with those of Karakayalı et al., who reported that greater severity of NVP was associated with higher levels of FOC, and that higher anxiety and stress levels, together with lower perceived psychosocial support, were related to more severe NVP. 18 In their cross‐sectional study of 659 women, the severity of NVP was assessed using the PUQE questionnaire, similar to our approach. FOC was measured using the Wijma Delivery Expectancy/Experience Questionnaire, version A (W‐DEQ‐A), which was developed to assess FOC during pregnancy, whereas in our study FOC was ascertained using ICD‐10 diagnostic codes. Previous research has highlighted FOC as a complex phenomenon. In a descriptive work by Hofberg and Brockington, women with FOC had high rates of HG (5 of 8 with primary FOC and 5 of 14 with secondary FOC), suggesting a possible psychological component to HG. 19 However, the study was based on clinical observations and included only 26 women.

In our study, a higher proportion of elective cesarean sections were observed among women with FOC and more severe NVP compared with women with FOC and milder NVP. Previously, FOC has been found to be strongly associated with a preference for cesarean section. 11 It can be hypothesized that co‐occurrence of NVP and FOC may further increase requests for cesarean section. Although, our sample size was insufficient to permit robust subgroup analyses, the descriptive findings suggest that the combined burden of pronounced physical symptoms and psychological distress may play a role in shaping delivery planning.

Psychological vulnerability appears to be important in the association between NVP and FOC. Women with FOC have been found to have higher levels of anxiety and depressive symptoms, lower self‐esteem, and reduced perceived social and partner support. 23 Furthermore, they are more likely to receive psychiatric care and use psychotropic medication. 24 Similarly, NVP has been associated with poorer mental well‐being. 3 , 8 , 15 , 25 We demonstrated in a large register‐based cohort a bidirectional association between HG and depression, with pre‐pregnancy depression being associated with an increased likelihood of HG. 8 Additionally, in a study including 1495 pregnant women, Andersson et al. found an association between depression and/or anxiety and increased nausea and vomiting. 26 In that study, they also showed a connection between depression and/or anxiety and visits related to FOC. Taken together, maternal mental symptoms may partly explain the observed association between NVP and FOC. Further, both NVP and FOC have been identified as potential predictors of postpartum depressive symptoms. 20 , 27

Several biological mechanisms may contribute to the psychological link between NVP and FOC. Anxiety during pregnancy and delivery has been associated with increased cortisol levels 28 with women suffering from HG similarly exhibiting higher cortisol concentrations compared with other pregnant women. 29 , 30 Dysregulation of stress hormone systems, particularly altered cortisol dynamics and hypothalamic‐pituitary‐adrenal axis function, has been found to be connected with anxiety‐related processes 31 providing a possible biopsychological framework for understanding FOC. Genetic variants related to anxiety traits, including polymorphism in serotonin transporter genes 32 may be relevant in the context of FOC. Neurotransmitter serotonin also plays a role in the regulation of nausea and vomiting, raising the possibility of potential overlap in serotonergic pathways. The clinical use of serotonin 5‐HT3 receptor antagonists, such as ondansetron, in the treatment of NVP further supports the involvement of serotonergic mechanisms in NVP. 33 In addition, elevated plasma levels of GDF15 have been associated with an increased risk of depression, 34 whereas GDF15 has also been identified as a genetic risk factor for HG, 35 and higher maternal plasma levels of GDF15 have been linked to the condition. 36

Our study has several strengths. We used the PUQE questionnaire, a validated and largely used questionnaire to distinguish the severity of NVP. 21 Though we used PUQE‐12, which assesses NVP during the worst 12 hours instead of PUQE‐24, which covers NVP symptoms during the past 24 h. At the time of our study design, PUQE‐12 was more commonly used, whereas PUQE‐24 had not yet been widely implemented, which guided our choice of questionnaire. Further, we did not include the general well‐being question. In some studies, an additional general well‐being question has been used alongside the PUQE questionnaire to assess the impact of NVP on quality of life. 37 We also included a considerably large number of women who represented the Finnish pregnant population well, since visits in maternity clinics in Finland are free of charge and thus almost all pregnant women utilize these services. 38 In addition, our data source, FMBR, has been shown to be accountable and accurate. 39 Furthermore, we adjusted the results for basic maternal characteristics, factors which have previously shown to be associated with NVP. 33

However, there are limitations in our study. The participants' history of NVP was self‐reported, and no medical records of NVP were available, which could lead to over‐ or underestimation of the severity of NVP. Further, we were unable to distinguish severe NVP cases requiring hospitalization or formally diagnosed HG from less severe NVP cases as diagnosis of hyperemesis (ICD‐10 code O21) is not systematically gathered in FMBR and we did not have access to the Finnish Care Register for Health care, the reliable source of ICD codes. The data collection was retrospective and, since the women answered the questionnaire mostly during mid‐pregnancy visits, their NVP symptoms had presumably already subsided. However, we have previously shown the reliability of retrospective reporting of PUQE. 40 Although the association between NVP and FOC is probably bidirectional, the cross‐sectional design and data structure of our study preclude conclusions about the direction of the association or the timing of FOC onset. Given that the Finnish population is predominantly Caucasian and that proficiency in the Finnish language was required for completion of the questionnaire, our results are not necessarily valid among all populations, especially with different ethnicities.

CONCLUSION

We showed an association between more severe NVP and FOC. Our findings support the notion that NVP not only constitutes a somatic burden but may also adversely affect women's mental health and attitudes toward childbirth. This was also reflected in a higher frequency of elective cesarean section among women with FOC whose pregnancies were complicated by NVP. Early recognition and management of NVP may help clinicians identify coexisting FOC and provide more comprehensive care, including antenatal support, appropriate mental health interventions, and informed delivery planning. Further research is needed to elucidate the causal direction of the association between NVP and FOC, as well as the underlying mechanisms.
