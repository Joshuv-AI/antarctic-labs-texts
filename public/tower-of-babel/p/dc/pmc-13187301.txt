Failure-avoidance bayesian optimization with failure-boundary search for automatic exploration of digging control parameters
Authors: Motoki Koyama, Masato Ishikawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bayesian optimization is an effective framework for identifying optimal control parameters of excavation machinery from a limited number of costly trials. While it can improve excavation performance by increasing soil yield and reducing time or fuel consumption, unsafe or off-target parameter combinations must be avoided in real operations. Such cases—e.g., cylinder overload, excessive tire slip, or unstable bucket–soil interaction—are regarded as “failures” in this study. To address this issue, we propose a two-stage safe optimization method designed for automatic excavation control. In the first stage, we explore the success / failure boundary in a simulation environment, where failures can be evaluated without risking damage to vehicles, and estimate the safety probability distribution across the parameter space. In the second stage, this prior safety information is incorporated into a safety-weighted acquisition function for Bayesian optimization, introducing safety as a soft constraint and enabling risk-aware exploration of the parameter space. By separating offline boundary learning and online optimization, the proposed approach aims to reduce failure trials. The effectiveness of the method is evaluated through comparisons with existing safety-aware optimization approaches on known objective functions, as well as through a physics-based excavation simulation.

Introduction

Automation of excavation operations has become increasingly important due to workforce shortages and limitations on consecutive working hours. Excavation is one of the most fuel-intensive and labor-demanding tasks in construction machinery1. Achieving fully automatic excavation remains challenging because the interaction between the bucket and soil is highly uncertain and nonlinear. Generally, computational costs for mechanical interaction models between soil and machinery tend to be high2. It has been demonstrated that using data-driven methods such as machine learning to control vehicle posture online during excavation is effective3. While data-driven approaches to tuning excavation control parameters also involve substantial risks. Unexpected soil reaction forces can overload hydraulic actuators or induce tire slip on soft or muddy terrain, potentially damaging equipment and compromising safety. Notably, the most productive excavation such as full-bucket loading often occurs near the boundary between success and failure, where excessive reaction forces are likely. Therefore, an optimizer must explore this boundary efficiently while minimizing the risk of unsafe trials.

Bayesian optimization (BO) provides a promising framework for automatically tuning black-box control parameters4, and has been successfully applied in robotics and control systems5,6. Conventional safe BO methods, however, face limitations in this context. Soft-constraint approaches, such as expected improvement weighted by failure probability7–9, tend to avoid the failure boundary excessively, reducing efficiency near the high-reward region. Hard-constraint methods, including SafeOpt5,10 or StageOpt11, maintain safety by propagating feasible sets, but they incur substantial computational cost and often explore overly conservatively. In particular, the method by Wakabayashi et al.7 heuristically enables exploration near the boundary but does not perform prior learning of the boundary shape, making direct application in real-machine environments potentially risky. We aim to build a practical method that accommodates the tradeoff between safety and exploratory search by directly incorporating success / failure probability into the acquisition function of Bayesian optimization.

To address these challenges, we propose a two-stage safe Bayesian optimization framework tailored for excavation tasks. In the first stage, a physics-based wheel-loader simulator is used to safely explore both successful and failed trials, allowing estimation of the failure probability distribution and physical insight into the geometry of the success / failure boundary. In the second stage, this boundary knowledge is incorporated into a safety-aware acquisition function, enabling active exploration of the high-reward region along the boundary while avoiding unsafe parameter combinations. By separating offline boundary learning from online optimization, the proposed framework allows efficient and safe identification of optimal excavation parameters, even in regions where conventional soft- or hard-constraint BO would be either too risky or too conservative. The present study validates the framework using a wheel-loader excavation simulator, providing comprehensive and risk-free evaluation prior to real-machine deployment. This two-stage approach conceptually resembles the adaptive sampling strategy proposed by Tian et al.12, where information learned in an initial stage is used to guide subsequent exploration. Similarly, our method first estimates the success / failure boundary offline and then incorporates this knowledge into a safety-aware acquisition function, enabling efficient exploration of the high-reward region while avoiding unsafe parameter combinations.

Proposed method

The proposed method consists of two sequential algorithms: boundary search and failure-avoidant Bayesian optimization (FA-BO). Boundary search estimates the failure probability across the control parameter space from binary success / failure labels, providing information about the geometry of the success / failure boundary. Since the most productive excavation parameters typically lie near this boundary, learning its shape in advance allows the optimizer to explore high-reward regions while avoiding unsafe areas.

Failure-avoidance bayesian optimization (FA-BO)

Bayesian optimization is performed after the boundary search for the success probability distribution in the previous section. The success probability p(ξ) is obtained from the GPC model learned during the boundary search stage. During FA-BO, the trained GPC model is kept frozen and only queried to evaluate the success probability. Updating the classifier during FA-BO is intentionally avoided to prevent feedback-induced bias in the estimated boundary. In this paper, we extend the usual acquisition function UCB by linearly combining the components of the safety probability distributions p. The extended UCB is defined as,10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\alpha\:}_{UCB,Safe}\left(\boldsymbol{\xi\:}\right)=\mu\:\left(\boldsymbol{\xi\:}\right)+\beta\:\sigma\:\left(\boldsymbol{\xi\:}\right)+\gamma\:p\left(\boldsymbol{\xi\:}\right),$$\end{document}

where γ is the safety offset weight. Here, γ is a safety weight that controls the exploration strategy: larger γ biases the acquisition function toward safer regions, while smaller γ allows more adventurous exploration near risky areas. This flexibility balances safety and efficiency near the estimated success / failure boundary. The acquisition function provides soft safety guidance without enforcing hard constraints. Importantly, simulated objective values obtained during boundary search are not used in FA-BO due to potential sim-to-real mismatch in soil–tool interaction and reward scaling. Only the pretrained success probability distribution is transferred as safety prior. Algorithm 2 summarizes the FA-BO procedure, which starts with 10 random evaluations and uses the prior success probability to guide safe exploration.

Application to digging control optimization

The proposed method FA-BO is applied to the optimization of automatic excavation control of a wheel loader, and it is verified by excavation simulation whether it is possible to find a combination of parameters with a high evaluation function while avoiding failures.

Digging simulation setting

A manipulator simulating a wheel loader’s work equipment was used for excavation experiments. The loader scoops soil by operating the boom, bucket, and accelerator: the boom controls bucket height, the bucket controls tilt angle, and the accelerator controls penetration depth. During actual excavation, operators adjust these controls based on ground load—for example, reducing bucket tilt or accelerator input when the boom feels difficult to raise to prevent overloading the hydraulic system.

A three-link rigid-body manipulator representing the boom, bucket, and acceleration DOFs is configured as shown in Fig. 5. The bucket, composed of a half-cylinder and flat blade, is attached to one horizontal linear joint for acceleration and two rotary joints for combined boom and bucket motions. The front and rear linear joints allow forward/backward vehicle motion. Excavation is simulated with 400 particles (10–100 mm diameter, 400 mm bucket width) representing a rock blast, where non-spherical particles and three-joint interactions create inter-particle snagging and a realistic ground slope. The physics engine Newton models rigid-body motion with collision and friction, ignoring adhesion and Coulomb forces. Each excavation trial, including digging, soil measurement, and one BO evaluation, requires approximately 200 s.

Design variables of automatic digging control

The excavation control problem was set up based on the following constraints with reference to the actual excavation behavior of a wheel loader [17–24].

(i)The bucket is operated rhythmically and repeatedly at the same distance between the straight penetration section and the scoop-up section.

(ii)The boom is raised slightly immediately after penetration by about 50 mm to increase the ground contact force of the tires to reduce slippage.

(iii)When the reaction force exceeds a threshold value, the horizontal velocity is set to zero to reproduce the tire slip phenomenon.

Based on the above, the control design parameter ξ and its design range were set as shown in Eqs. Figure 10 shows evaluation functions and their tentative maximum values in Bayesian optimization trials with FA-BO by exploration weight β = 0.1, safety weight γ = 3.0. The blue circles and red crosses represent success (Y = 1) or failure (Y = 0), respectively. The tentative maximum value is based on the evaluation function E(ξ)Y(ξ) multiplied by the success / failure label Y(ξ) in order to focus only on the cases of success. As shown in Fig. 10, the evaluation function converges after about 20 to 30 Bayesian optimization trials. In this case, the number of failed trials where the number of slips T exceeds 10 is 10 out of 50 optimization trials. Note that these slips do not immediately cause hazardous digging and mainly result in minor tire wear. Safety is treated as a soft constraint mediating the trade-off between performance and failure avoidance. The choice of the safety weight γ reflects an implicit risk preference, and defining it based on explicit risk constraints or allowable failure rates is left for future work.

Figure 11 shows contour plots of acquisition function of FA-BO αUCB, Safe(ξ) after 50 Bayesian optimization search to avoid failure, which is the number of slip T > 10 under (a) β = 0.1, γ = 0.0, (b) β = 0.1, γ = 2.0, (c) β = 0.1, γ = 3.0 and (d) β = 0.1, γ = 4.0. When the safety weight γ = 0.0 in (a), there are many failed trials. This is because the safety probability is hardly taken into account and the exploration approaches the one with the usual UCB. In comparison, when the safety weights are increased in (b)-(d), it can be seen that for γ = 2.0, a few failures also occur, but the exploration can be performed near the boundary. While for γ = 3.0 and 4.0, the parameters away from the boundary are explored and the number of failures is suppressed.

Figure 12 shows the maximum value of the evaluation function E(ξ) and E(ξ)Y(ξ), including successes and failures, with the failure rate Rf. The exploration weight β = 0.1 is fixed and the safety weight γ is varied from 0.0 to 4.0. It is obtained from 50 Bayesian optimization trials. Specifically, for each safety weight γ, optimization was repeated five times using different random number seeds, and both the mean and standard deviation were shown. From Fig. 12, it can be seen that as the safety factor γ increases, the failure rate decreases, and in the region where γ > 3.0, the variance is small and the failure rate is low, indicating stable exploration. It can be clearly noted that the maximum value of the evaluation function E(ξ)Y(ξ) converges to a value near 100 as the safety weight γ is increased. In the case of γ < 2.5, we are able to search for higher values than γ > 2.5 as a pure evaluation function E(ξ). However, in γ < 2.5, Rf is almost 1, and most are failed attempts., So the maximum value of E(ξ)Y(ξ) is more important from a practical standpoint.

These results highlight the flexibility and practical advantage of FA-BO: by adjusting , the user can smoothly trade-off between aggressive boundary exploration (accepting occasional failures) and conservative, safer exploration. In the current excavation scenario, setting ≥ 3.0 allows for efficient, safe parameter search, maintaining high excavation performance without excessive risk.

Conclusions

In this study, we proposed a two-stage safe Bayesian optimization method to reduce the failures during automatic excavation control parameter search. Benchmark validations demonstrated that the proposed method achieves convergence speeds comparable to other Bayesian optimization approaches [5,7] while maintaining a failure rate below 10%. Moreover, by adjusting the safety weight γ, it is possible on the excavation simulator to reduce failure trials (slip in excavation) by around 20% while exploring control parameters that maximize payload with minimal workload. These results highlight the main contribution of this study: the demonstration of a two-stage safe Bayesian optimization methodology, where offline boundary learning estimates the failure probability distribution and informs a safety-aware online optimization that actively explores high-reward regions while avoiding unsafe parameter combinations.

As a future prospect, the failure probability distribution obtained from excavation simulations can serve as an initial prior for safety-critical learning under domain shift, such as with real excavation robots [26] and vehicles [27]. The proposed methodology aims to adapt an offline-learned safety boundary online through interaction with the target system. In practical deployment, such adaptation may require gradual adjustment of the success probability distribution to account for domain mismatch between simulation and real environments. As a preliminary test, experiments using a simulated excavation robot suggest the feasibility of this approach. Importantly, the method is not tied to a specific soil constitutive model and can be applied to higher-fidelity geotechnical simulations or directly to real machines via HILS. Systematic evaluation on real machines remains a task for future work. The algorithm does not rely on explicit modeling of the boundary but inherits structural information from the success/failure probability. In this sense, the role of the boundary information is analogous to how adaptive RJMCMC leverages function-space posterior geometry to guide exploration without explicit parameterization [12]. Extending the current boundary search strategy to high-dimensional spaces is nontrivial, but alternative representations based on geometric or topological summaries of the boundary could provide a promising direction for future work.
