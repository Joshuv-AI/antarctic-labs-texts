Response to the letter RE: A prospective study for brain metastasis imaging screening in patients with advanced HER2-positive or triple-negative breast cancer
Authors: G.M. Kim, J. Sohn
Published: 2026

We would like to thank Nimura et al. for their careful reading of our study1 and for their two important comments2 regarding treatment decision-making and neurocognitive evaluation. We agree that the landscape of brain metastasis (BM) management is rapidly evolving, and their comments raise important points about how to apply our findings to current clinical practice.

Regarding their first point, we acknowledge the paradigm shift toward upfront central nervous system (CNS)-active systemic therapies, such as trastuzumab deruxtecan or tucatinib-based regimens, for asymptomatic human epidermal growth factor-2-positive BM. Our study was conducted during a period in which CNS-active drugs were not yet established as a viable alternative to local therapy for BM. Consequently, systemic therapy was not used as a way to keep these patients free from local treatment. Instead, this group consisted of patients with small, asymptomatic lesions who were managed with active surveillance, and patients whose poor general condition made any active treatment infeasible. However, we strongly agree with Nimura et al. that in the current era, the value of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) screening extends beyond merely expediting local therapy; it is crucial for identifying candidates who may benefit from prioritizing CNS-penetrating systemic regimens to safely defer radiation therapy.

Regarding the second point, we agree with the limitations of the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) in detecting subtle, radiation-induced neurocognitive decline. In our study, the MMSE was selected primarily for its feasibility and high completion rates in a busy, real-world clinical setting. Our findings should be interpreted with this limitation in mind, and we concur that future prospective studies should use more sensitive neurocognitive function tests—such as the Hopkins Verbal Learning Test-Revised, the Trail Making Test, and tests of verbal fluency.

In conclusion, we thank Nimura et al. for these valuable comments. We believe that early detection of BM through MRI screening remains highly relevant because it helps oncologists choose the best systemic and local treatments early, while also protecting patients’ quality of life and cognitive function. We also agree that its value should be reassessed in future studies in the context of modern CNS-active systemic therapy and sensitive neurocognitive assessment.
