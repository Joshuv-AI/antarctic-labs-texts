Biomimetic nanoimmunotherapy boosts spatiotemporal PANoptosis and reshapes desmoplastic tumor microenvironment
Authors: Hui Huang, Zebin Xiao, Wei Feng, Xinran Song, Liang Chen, Lili Huang, Li Ding, Yu Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryDendritic cell (DC)-based vaccines for solid tumors face major challenges, including limited tumor-specific antigens and immunosuppressive stroma. Here, we present a therapeutic nanovaccine (UCNP@MOF@MI@FM [UMMF]) composed of a DC/tumor fused cytomembrane-coated UCNP@MOF nanoparticle, co-loaded with a MutT homolog 1 (MTH1) inhibitor and combined with tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4). The fused membrane facilitates dual targeting to tumors and lymph nodes while enabling broad-spectrum tumor antigen presentation. Upon near-infrared (NIR) irradiation, upconversion-triggered reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation and MTH1 inhibition synergistically induce immunogenic PANoptosis, releasing antigens and promoting DCs maturation. Simultaneously, ROS remodels the tumor stroma by depleting collagen and cancer-associated fibroblasts, enhancing T cell infiltration. BH4 counteracts IDO-mediated kynurenine accumulation, reversing immune tolerance and restoring T cell function. This multifunctional platform integrates tumor cell killing, immune priming, and stromal reprogramming, resulting in robust antitumor immunity, reduced relapse, and metastasis suppression. UMMF offers a promising strategy for precision nanoimmunotherapy through controlled PANoptosis and microenvironment modulation.

Introduction

Cancer immunotherapy, which harnesses the immune system to combat tumors, has emerged as a transformative approach in cancer treatment.1,2,3 Among the various strategies, dendritic cell (DC)-based vaccines have shown promise for priming antigen-specific T cells and eliciting anti-tumor immunity by loading DCs with tumor antigens or mRNAs.4,5,6,7 However, conventional DC vaccines have faced limited clinical success due to challenges such as a lack of tumor-specific antigens, in vivo degradation of antigens, and immune tolerance. To address this, hybrid cells created by fusing DCs with tumor cells have been developed to combine tumor antigenicity with DC-mediated T cell activation.8,9,10 While these hybrid cells can home to lymphoid organs to improve antigen presentation,11,12 they also carry risks of carcinogenesis and logistical challenges related to preservation and transportation.13 Therefore, alternative strategies such as DC/tumor fused cytomembrane (FM) vaccines have been explored, showing strong immune responses but limited by chronic antigen exposure and the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME).14,15 Notably, the desmoplastic stroma in solid tumors presents a physical barrier to immune cell infiltration, further hindering vaccine efficacy.16,17 To overcome these barriers, an approach that enhances the potency of DC-based vaccines while addressing the challenges of the immunosuppressive TME is required.

One promising avenue is the induction of immunogenic cell death (ICD), specifically PANoptosis, which is an emerging inflammatory programmed cell death pathway combining the key characteristics of pyroptosis, apoptosis, and necroptosis.18 PANoptosis not only kills tumor cells19,20 but also promotes the release of tumor antigens, triggering robust anti-tumor immune responses.21,22 Leveraging PANoptosis with DC/tumor FM vaccines could synergize the immune-stimulating effects of the vaccine with the TME-altering effects of inflammatory cell death. This dual-action strategy could both enhance cytotoxic T cells (CTLs) activation and reprogram the TME to overcome immune suppression and physical barriers. Despite its potential, strategies for inducing PANoptosis in situ remain underdeveloped, particularly in the context of nanotechnology for cancer vaccines.

Photodynamic therapy (PDT), which generates reactive oxygen species (ROS) in a spatiotemporally controlled manner upon light activation,23,24,25,26 holds potential for inducing PANoptosis-associated ICD within tumors.27,28,29 Upconversion metal-organic frameworks (UCNP@MOFs) offer an advantage by converting low-energy near-infrared (NIR) light into high-energy visible light, enabling deeper tissue penetration and more effective ROS generation.30,31,32,33 Moreover, 2-hydroxy-dATP diphosphatase, known as MutT homolog 1 (MTH1), is an enzyme that can hydrolyze oxidized purine nucleoside triphosphates to monophosphates, thereby protecting cells, in particular tumor cells, from ROS-induced DNA damage. Therefore, ROS-defensing MTH1 inhibitor can serve as a potential adjuvant strategy to amplify ROS-induced DNA damage from PDT.34,35,36 In parallel, modulating L-tryptophan (Trp) metabolism, particularly by inhibiting kynurenine production, can enhance T cell proliferation and reverse immune suppression.37,38,39,40 Tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4) has been shown to rescue the inhibitory effect on T cell proliferation initiated by kynurenine.41,42,43 By combining these approaches, it is possible to create a synergistic treatment that not only enhances the immune response but also reprograms the immunosuppressive TME.

In this work, we present the strategic construction of a biomimetic UCNP@MOF-based DC/tumor FM vaccine enhancer and regulatory nanoplatform. This approach allows for efficient energy transfer from the upconversion nanoparticle (UCNP) core to the metal-organic framework (MOF) shell, enabling NIR light-triggered ROS production. The MTH1 inhibitor is encapsulated within the pores of the MOF shell, resulting in the UCNP@MOF@MI (UMM) nanoplatform. We then extracted the cytomembrane from DC/tumor fusion cells and coated the UMM nanoparticles with it, creating UCNP@MOF@MI@FM (UMMF). Unlike traditional DC/tumor FM vaccines, which may become physically trapped in the tumor stroma and inhibit T cell function due to antigen overexposure, our newly designed therapeutic nanovaccine has several key advantages as follows: (1) it directly targets tumor tissues and lymph nodes to present the full tumor antigen to CTLs, enhancing local anti-tumor immunity; (2) it spatiotemporally disrupts the tumor stroma via ROS-induced DNA damage, promoting T cell infiltration into the tumor core; and (3) it induces immunogenic PANoptosis in tumor cells, boosting host adaptive immunity by activating immature DCs, which then traffic to lymph nodes to prime and activate CTLs to eliminate the primary tumor. Furthermore, the inclusion of BH4 helps overcome immune resistance caused by excessive kynurenine accumulation, synergistically enhancing the anti-tumoral efficacy of this therapeutic nanovaccine. This nanoimmunotherapeutic strategy, based on the UMMF-potentiated light-controlled treatment and BH4 molecules, coordinately triggers robust immunity to suppress tumor growth, recurrence, and metastasis.

Results

Biosynthesis and characterization of UMMF

The synthesis of NaYF4:Yb,Er@NaYF4 core-shell nanocrystals (UCNPs) began with the preparation of highly uniform core of NaYF4:Yb,Er as observed by transmission electron microscope (TEM) with an average diameter of 37.7 ± 2.3 nm and hexagonal appearance in shape (Figures S1A–S1C). TEM image showed that the size of NaYF4:Yb,Er@NaYF4 increased to a mean diameter of 44.5 ± 2.7 nm (Figures 1A, S1D, and S1E), indicative of the growth of the NaYF4 shell around the core. Energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping images provided further evidence of the formation of NaYF4:Yb,Er@NaYF4 core-shell UCNPs (Figure 1A). The UCNPs were then modified with the carboxylic group to mediate the growth of ZrMOFs on their surfaces, resulting in the formation of UCNP@ZrMOF (UM) with a core-shell structure. TEM images further demonstrated the presence of a monolayer of UM nanostructure with a mean diameter of 87.8 ± 7.5 nm (Figure 1B). Elemental mapping and EDS linear scanning also confirmed that the metal components of this nanoparticle included Zr, Y, Yb, and Er (Figures 1B and 1C). Powder X-ray diffraction pattern showed peaks for both UCNPs and ZrMOF, indicating that a crystalline ZrMOF structure had been grafted on the surface of the UCNPs (Figure 1D).44 The upconversion luminescence (UCL) property of UM was then investigated under the NIR (980 nm) laser irradiation. UCNPs showed 3 UCL peaks centered at approximately 522, 542, and 655 nm, mostly overlapping with the absorption spectrum of ZrMOFs (Figure 1E). The fluorescence study further confirmed that ZrMOFs alone did not have any NIR (980 nm) reactive property; in contrast, UM demonstrated a dramatic decrease in emission due to energy transfer from UCNPs to ZrMOFs (Figure 1F). We then prepared the fused cells according to the established methods.4 Briefly, murine breast cancer cells (4T1) were adopted as the model cancer cells and treated with ethanol to induce the expression of “eat-me” signals. These ethanol-treated 4T1 cells were labeled with either carboxyfluorescein diacetate succinimidyl ester (CFSE) or fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-conjugated cell membrane dye, 3,3′-dioctadecyloxacarbocyanine perchlorates (DiO), and then mixed with DCs at a ratio of 1:2 to promote sufficient cell fusion (Figure S1F). Flow cytometry analysis of the harvested cells, cultured under normal conditions for 7 days, demonstrated over 50% of the cells co-expressed CFSE and CD11c (Figure 1G), indicating successful fusion of DCs and 4T1 cells. Additionally, more than 50% of the fused cells survived after the cell fusion (Figure S1G). Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) of the harvested cells showed the co-localization of DCs’ specific marker, CD11c, and 4T1 cells’ marker, FITC-DiO, in the 4T1/DC fused cells (Figure 1H), further confirming the successful cell membrane infusion of DCs and 4T1 cells. Furthermore, the flow cytometry results demonstrate that the fused cells exhibited enhanced expression of MHC-II (Figure S1H), suggesting DC maturation during the infusion with tumor cells. The cytomembrane protein components of 4T1 (CM), DCs (DM), and fused cells (FM) were characterized by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). Promisingly, almost all the membrane proteins derived from CM and DM can be detected in FM despite the occurrence of some new bands (Figure 1I), verifying the hybrid fusion of 4T1 and DCs with biological interactions. Western blot (WB) analysis further validated the expression of tumor cells (EpCAM) and DCs (CD11c) specific markers on FM (Figure 1J). Collectively, these data consistently indicated the successful construction of FM derived from the 4T1/DC fused cells.

Next, we cloaked the nanoparticles of UMM with FM under ultrasound in the ice bath to formulate the UMMF. SDS-PAGE and WB analysis confirmed that UMMF preserved all the membrane proteins of FM, including the tumor cell’s and DC’s specific cell surface markers (Figures 1K and 1L). TEM displayed the core-shell structure of UMMF with a uniform cell membrane shell (Figure 1M). The uncloaked UMM had a mean hydrodynamic diameter of about 212.2 nm with a narrow size distribution and a positive charge potential (ζ) at 0.82 mV (Figure 1N). Coating FM to the UMM surface led to a subtle increase in hydrodynamic diameter and a charge reversal of ζ potential. These findings manifest the successful coating of FM on UMM nanoparticles. As shown in Figure S1I, the average size of the biomimetic UMMF nanovaccine remained stable during a seven-day incubation in PBS or cell culture medium, indicating excellent stability. Additionally, in the presence of UM or UMMF, with the increase of NIR irradiation time, 1,3-diphenylisobenzofuran (DPBF) that reacted with the generated singlet oxygen (1O2), significantly decreased in the characteristic absorption at 425 nm (Figures 1O–1R), further supporting the generation of 1O2 by UM or UMMF under NIR irradiation. In addition, the accumulated released amount of MTH1 inhibitor after 24 h was approximately 80% (Figure S1J), which enabled effective drug release in vitro and in vivo to exert its function during the PDT.

FM-mediated cellular uptake and UMMF-based ROS generation

Subsequently, we explored in-depth the biological properties of UMMF in 4T1 mouse breast cancer cells. The cellular uptake and spatiotemporal distribution, crucial properties for a potential biological effect, of UCNP@MOF@FM (UMF) were investigated dynamically. With the prolongation of incubation time, an increasing amount of UMF was detected inside the cancer cells, with the peak of uptake at 8 h of co-incubation, and UMF inside the cancer cells was mainly accumulated in the lysosomes. After 8 h of co-incubation, the uptake of UMF did not evidently increase, and more UMF was secreted into the cytoplasm of tumor cells (Figures 2A and 2B), indicating the efficient and time-dependent cellular uptake behavior of UMF. Given the aforementioned ability of ROS generation triggered by UMMF-based PDT, a fluorogenic probe of 2,7-dichlorofluorescein-diacetate (DCFH-DA) was adopted to detect the ROS in vitro. Based on flow cytometric and CLSM analyses, we found that 4T1 cancer cells treated with NIR or UMF without NIR irradiation exhibited negligible intracellular ROS-induced DCFH-DA green fluorescence, comparable with the untreated control group. In contrast, treatment with UMF upon exposure to NIR irradiation significantly increased fluorescence of DCFH-DA (∼12-fold increase), and the strongest signal was detected in the cells incubated with UMMF followed by NIR irradiation (∼20-fold increase), suggestive of superiority of UMMF in triggering ROS generation (Figures 2E–2G and S2A). To directly verify that excessive ROS generation was associated with efficient DNA damage, Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated avidin, which has a special affinity to 8-oxo-dGTP, predominant in the process of DNA mispairing, was used. CLSM analyses demonstrated that in comparison to cells treated without treatments, with NIR, or with UMF alone, which showed almost no detectable avidin reactivity, cells treated with UMMF followed by NIR irradiation presented the highest 8-oxo-dGTP accumulation, which was 5 times of avidin signals than that treated by UMF with NIR irradiation (Figures 2F and 2G). This result suggests that, with the addition of MTH1 inhibitor, much more 8-oxo-dGTP can be retained in the cells to induce DNA damage when treated with UMMF followed by NIR irradiation. The biocompatibility of UMF was then evaluated in 4T1 mouse breast cancer cells and B16F10 mouse melanoma cells. The cell viability was greater than 90%, even at a high concentration of 1.25 mM, indicating that the UMF possesses negligible inherent cytotoxicity (Figures S2B and S2C).

UMMF-induced immunogenic PANoptosis in tumor cells

Then, we systematically evaluated the in vitro anti-tumor efficacy of UMMF under NIR irradiation and related mechanisms. After incubation with UMMF or UMF followed by NIR irradiation, the viability of the 4T1 and B16F10 cells substantially declined compared to the untreated control, NIR, or UMF without NIR irradiation (Figures S2D and S2E). The cytotoxicity effects were further qualitatively determined by co-staining of green/red fluorescence with calcein acetoxymethyl ester (calcein-AM)/propidium iodide (PI) assay. The fluorescence images showed a dramatic increase in dead cells accompanied by a decrease in live cells in the condition of UMMF upon NIR irradiation (Figure S2F), validating the robust anti-proliferation effect of UMMF upon NIR irradiation.

To explore the underlying anti-tumor mechanism, we performed bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) on untreated tumor cells and tumor cells co-incubated with UMMF following NIR irradiation. As shown in Figure 3A, UMMF treatment combined with NIR irradiation resulted in 498 upregulated and 1,803 downregulated genes compared to the untreated control (fold change ≥2, p < 0.05). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) confirmed the significant upregulation of transcripts involved in cell redox homeostasis (Figure S2G). Reactome enrichment analysis indicated significant upregulation of gene pathways associated with pyroptosis, programmed cell death, and apoptosis (Figures 3B and 3C). Similarly, KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) enrichment analysis revealed substantial upregulation of apoptosis- and necroptosis-related pathways and genes in UMMF-treated cells upon NIR irradiation compared to controls (Figures 3C and S2H). PANoptosis is an inflammatory programmed cell death pathway modulated by the PANoptosome complex, incorporating key features of apoptosis, necroptosis, and pyroptosis.18 This prompted us to explore whether PANoptosis occurs during UMMF-mediated antitumor therapy. To begin, we assessed the potential induction of apoptosis using the annexin V-PI apoptosis detection kit. Notably, UMMF treatment followed by NIR irradiation showed a significantly higher apoptotic rate compared to UMF with or without NIR irradiation (Figure 3D), indicating the initiation of apoptosis. Besides, the expression level of caspase 8 was downregulated, suggesting its activation in 4T1 cells treated with UMMF upon NIR irradiation (Figure 3G), providing further evidence of apoptosis. Next, we employed CLSM with YO-PRO-1/PI co-staining to investigate necroptosis, where necroptotic cells exhibit dual YO-PRO-1 (green) and PI (red) staining, appearing orange. The UMMF + NIR group exhibited a significantly stronger orange fluorescence signal compared to other treatment groups, indicating a substantial increase in necroptotic cells (Figure 3E). Given that mitochondrial ROS can trigger necroptosis via RIPK3-mediated mixed-lineage kinase domain-like pseudokinase (MLKL) phosphorylation,45 we assessed the expression of RIPK3, MLKL, and phosphorylated MLKL (p-MLKL) by WB assays. In the UMMF + NIR group, RIPK3 and p-MLKL were significantly upregulated, while MLKL expression was downregulated, confirming the occurrence of necroptosis (Figure 3G).

Additionally, bright field observation revealed that 4T1 cells treated with UMMF and subjected to NIR irradiation exhibited swelling and the presence of large bubbles (indicated by red arrows), characteristic of pyroptosis initiation (Figure 3F). Encouraged by this observation, we further assessed the expression levels of pyroptosis-related proteins. In the UMMF + NIR group, we observed upregulation of apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a CARD (ASC), NLRP3, cleaved caspase 1 (C-caspase 1), and N-GSDMD, along with downregulation of caspase 1 and Gasdermin-D (GSDMD), indicating activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, ASC, and caspase 1, followed by cleavage of GSDMD into its active form, N-GSDMD (Figure 3G). During pyroptosis, cells undergo swelling and form membrane pores, leading to the release of intracellular contents such as lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and the inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and IL-18.46 As shown in Figures 3H–3J, LDH, IL-1β, and IL-18 release significantly increased in 4T1 cells treated with UMMF upon NIR irradiation, further confirming pyroptosis. Interestingly, ZBP1 activation is a key upstream event that initiates the molecular machinery responsible for executing PANoptosis.19 As expected, the expression level of ZBP1 was notably upregulated in the UMMF + NIR group compared to other treatment groups (Figure 3G). Together, these findings indicate that UMMF combined with NIR irradiation induces substantial ROS generation, triggering three programmed cell death pathways—apoptosis, necroptosis, and pyroptosis. This leads to the assembly of PANoptosomes and the subsequent occurrence of PANoptosis, enhancing the antitumor efficacy of UMMF-mediated NIR light-programmed therapy.

The PANoptotic process attracts inflammatory cells, thereby enhancing the inflammatory response. To determine whether ICD occurs during the PANoptosis of tumor cells induced by UMMF under NIR irradiation, the release of key immunostimulatory damage-associated molecular patterns, including high-mobility group protein 1 (HMGB1), adenosine triphosphate (ATP), and calreticulin (CRT), was assessed. Immunofluorescence (IF) staining was employed to assess HMGB1 release and CRT exposure in cancer cells. Notably, the UMMF treatment combined with NIR irradiation resulted in significantly lower intracellular HMGB content, but higher CRT expression compared to other treatment groups, including untreated cells, NIR alone, and UMF with or without NIR (Figures 3K–3N). This indicates that UMMF with NIR irradiation enhances the ICD effect in PANoptotic tumor cells. Furthermore, elevated ATP secretion, measured using an enhanced ATP assay kit, was observed in UMMF-treated cells following NIR irradiation (Figure 3O), underscoring the capacity of this treatment to induce immunogenic PANoptosis in tumor cells.

Additionally, we assessed whether UMMF could directly activate T cells. Naive T cells were co-incubated with treatments including UM, UMF, and UMMF, with untreated naive T cells serving as the control (Figure 4E). In contrast to the negligible T cell increase observed in the untreated and UM groups, we found that naive T cells co-cultured with UMF or UMMF exhibited significantly higher proliferation, with greater numbers of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells and increased secretion of TNF-α, IFN-γ, and GZMB (Figures 4F, 4G, and S4A–S4F). To directly assess whether UMMF-activated T cells could mediate cytotoxic effects against tumor cells, we co-incubated naive T cells with UM, UMF, and UMMF, harvested the “conditioned” T cells, and used them to treat 4T1 cancer cells. Then, we explored the therapeutic capacity of UMMF under NIR irradiation to restrain tumor growth in vivo (Figure 5E). As shown in Figures 5F, 5G, S5C, and S5D, UMMF-enabled PDT treatment significantly promoted the ROS generation within the localized tumor and manifested the most pronounced tumor inhibition effect compared to all the other treatments, which was consistent with the previous results. However, mice treated by UMMF upon NIR irradiation exhibited uncontrolled and relatively rapid growth of tumor size after 8 days of PDT treatment (Figure 5G), indicating that immune activation triggered by UMMF was probably a short-duration response due to the immunologic tolerance of 4T1 tumors.

To fully uncover the contributing factors that profoundly diminished the long-term efficacy of UMMF upon NIR irradiation, we collected the tumor tissues after treatment at day 8 (early stage) and day 15 (late stage) to perform bulk RNA-seq. We found that 187 genes (i.e., Mmp12, Ido1, Ido2, etc) were upregulated and 196 genes (such as Itih5, Meg3, Ccl11, etc) were downregulated in the tumors at the late stage of treatment compared to the tumors at the early stage of treatment (Figure S5E). Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis indicated that the differentially expressed genes in late-stage tumors relative to early-stage tumors were dramatically enriched in the signaling pathways involving negative regulation of T cell activation, heme binding, tetrapyrrole binding, and negative regulation of T cell proliferation (Figure 5H). Moreover, GSEA analysis demonstrated that significant differences in signaling pathways included the negative feedback regulation of T cell activation and T cell proliferation (Figure 5J). Interestingly, we also found aromatic amino acid family metabolic process and indole-containing compound metabolic process associated signaling pathways were significantly upregulated. Further analysis on the differentially expressed genes indicated that Ido1 was involved in the negative regulation of T cell activation, negative regulation of T cell proliferation, tolerance induction, and T cell apoptotic process, which was significantly upregulated in the tumors at the late stage of treatment compared to the tumors at the early stage of treatment (Figures 5I, S5F, and S5G). Collectively, these findings suggest that tumors at the late stage of UMMF treatment under NIR irradiation exhibit upregulation of immune suppression and tolerance-related pathways, strongly associated with increased Ido1 expression. Indoleamine 2,3-Dioxygenase (IDO) is a heme-containing enzyme involved in tryptophan metabolism to catalyze the first and rate-limiting step in the kynurenine pathway.36,37,38 It has an immunosuppressive function to inhibit the functionality of T cells and engage mechanisms of immune tolerance.42 Given that the BH4 molecule has been shown to alleviate kynurenine-mediated suppression of T cell proliferation,40 we propose that BH4 can rescue T cell proliferation suppressed by IDO activity and sustain the immunostimulatory effects initiated by UMMF under NIR irradiation.

UMMF followed by NIR irradiation further improved the tumor inhibitory effects compared to UMF followed by NIR irradiation, suggesting that the MTH1 inhibitor can improve the therapeutic outcome of UMF. We further performed multiplex IHC/IF analysis to investigate the potential mechanisms involved. Consistent with our results in vitro, in contrast to other treatments, we found significantly lower expression of HMGB1 but higher levels of CRT in the tumors treated with UMMF following NIR irradiation combined with BH4, which strongly supported that our therapeutic nanovaccine can induce robust ICD in solid tumors (Figures S6C, S6G, and S6H). Notably, the ICD effects observed in tumors treated with UMF or UMMF upon NIR irradiation, as well as UMMF upon NIR irradiation combined with BH4, were associated with substantial depletion of cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) and degradation of the extracellular matrix (ECM). This was evidenced by the significantly lower expression of α-SMA (a CAF activation marker) and collagen type 1 (a major ECM component) compared with PBS, NIR, UMF, and BH4 treatment groups (Figures 6D–6F). These results suggest that the PDT provided by UMF or UMMF, upon NIR exposure, can also target and deplete immunosuppressive, immune-excluding CAFs and ECM. Subsequent multiplex IF and flow cytometry analyses revealed a marked increase in CD4+ and CD8+ tumor-infiltrating T cells, particularly PD-1+Ki67+ CD8+ T cells, as well as natural killer (NK) cells and CD11c+ DCs, in tumors treated with UMMF upon NIR irradiation combined with BH4 compared to other treatment conditions (Figures 6D, 6G–6M, S8, and S9). These findings indicate that this treatment combination effectively reprograms the tumor immune microenvironment. Additionally, flow cytometric analyses of tumor-draining lymph nodes demonstrated that UMMF upon NIR irradiation combined with BH4 significantly increased the numbers of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, as well as MHC-I+ and MHC-II+ DCs, suggesting that local immune responses were robustly stimulated and initiated (Figures S10 and S11A–S11G). Given the importance of chemokine-mediated T cell trafficking, we investigated the levels of CXCL9 and CXCL10, key TH1-type chemokines involved in recruiting effector T lymphocytes, within tumor tissues to further elucidate the enhanced T cell infiltration. As shown in Figures S11H and S11I, the expression levels of CXCL9 and CXCL10 were significantly elevated in tumors treated with UMMF upon NIR irradiation combined with BH4, compared to other treatment groups. This likely contributed to the enhanced recruitment and infiltration of T cells into the TME, thereby amplifying the overall antitumor immune response.

Hence, our data revealed that UMMF, upon NIR irradiation combined with BH4, can reprogram the tumor from immunologically “cold” to “hot” tumors. To assess the universality of our combination strategy, we also evaluated its effects on another subcutaneous B16F10 melanoma tumor model, which has been a widely used tumor model in preclinical investigation (Figure S12A). Promisingly, a similar pattern of the strongest tumor inhibition effects, smallest final tumor volume, lowest tumor weight, and the greatest apoptosis was also observed in the B16F10 tumor-bearing mice treated with BH4 combined with UMMF upon NIR irradiation (Figures S12B–S12G). Moreover, multiplex IF analysis indicated that BH4 in combination with UMMF upon NIR irradiation induced ICD of tumor cells, downregulated stromal cells, and depleted the ECM, which further promoted the infiltration and functionality of CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and DCs in the tumor site (Figures S12E and S12H–S12N). Together, the nanovaccine-enabled effective inhibition of both 4T1 breast cancer and B16F10 melanoma tumors strongly reinforces its robust immune activation and general applicability in various solid tumor indications.

The aforementioned data further trigger us to test if the therapeutic nanovaccines have any effects in inhibiting metastasis in distant organs. The lungs from the mice with the aforementioned treatments (Figure 6A) were collected, and the metastatic burden was calculated. Strikingly, compared with the mice treated with PBS, NIR, UMF, or BH4, which suffer from dramatic metastatic burden in the lungs, we found a significantly lower number of metastatic nodes and metastatic areas in the lungs from the mice treated with UMF or UMMF upon NIR irradiation, and the lowest metastatic burden occurred in the treatment group of BH4 combined with UMMF upon NIR irradiation (Figure S13A), suggesting that the combination of UMMF upon NIR irradiation with BH4 can not only eradicate the primary tumors but also inhibit the metastasis in distant organs. To further test our hypothesis that the combination of UMMF upon NIR irradiation with BH4 can enhance systemic adaptive anti-tumor immunity, we performed a more direct experiment, as depicted in Figure 7I. Promisingly, we observed a great inhibition of primary and re-challenging tumors in the mice treated with UMF or UMMF upon NIR irradiation, while the most profound inhibitory effects of primary and re-challenging tumors were found in the mice treated with the combination of BH4 with UMMF upon NIR irradiation (Figures 7J–7M and S13B). Our data strongly support that the combination of UMMF following NIR irradiation with BH4 can mediate immune-dependent tumor eradication in both primary and re-challenging tumors.

Then, we hypothesized that this phenotype can be extended to inhibit or even eliminate the formation and growth of metastatic tumors. To address this, we fully took advantage of this re-challenging experiment with some modifications (Figure 7N). Notably, metastatic lesions that were highly proliferating, evidenced by a higher number of Ki-67+ tumor cells, were extensively detected in the lungs treated with PBS, NIR, UMF, BH4, and UMF upon NIR irradiation, and UMMF upon NIR irradiation (Figures 7O–7Q). Collectively, these results suggest that the combination of UMMF with NIR irradiation and BH4 significantly enhances host systemic anti-tumor immunity, effectively suppressing both primary tumor growth and distant metastatic progression.

Discussion

Cancer vaccines, particularly conventional DC-based vaccines, have demonstrated their potential to induce antigen-specific anti-tumor responses. However, the clinical translation of these vaccines remains limited, largely due to challenges such as the scarcity of tumor-specific antigens, the immunosuppressive TME dominated by CAFs and matrix components, immune tolerance, and systemic toxicity. Advances in nanotechnology present new opportunities for enhancing cancer vaccines by employing various strategies, such as reversing the immunosuppressive TME and triggering ICD. In this study, we developed a multifunctional fusion membrane-encapsulated UCNP@MOF-based therapeutic nanovaccine (UMMF) that spatiotemporally modulates immunogenic PANoptosis in tumor cells. Specifically, NIR irradiation activates UMMF to induce ROS-mediated PANoptosis in tumor cells. The PANoptotic or dying tumor cells subsequently release tumor-specific or tumor-associated antigens, facilitating DC recruitment into the TME and promoting DC maturation in tumors or lymphoid tissues. These mature DCs, in turn, activate naive T cells and promote their trafficking to the tumor. Additionally, the tumor cell/DC FM can actively target the UMMF nanovaccine to the spleen and lymph nodes, directly priming antigen-specific T cells to engage with tumor cells. Moreover, upon irradiation, UMMF exhibits a strong capacity for stromal remodeling, leading to significant depletion of stromal cells and ECM, which are key contributors to immune suppression and exclusion in the TME. These unique attributes position the UMMF-based therapeutic nanovaccine as an excellent candidate for modulating and reshaping the tumor immune landscape.

Given the potential for immune tolerance to limit the efficacy of our nanovaccine platform, we incorporated BH4, a potent metabolic regulator that enhances T cell functionality, into our combination strategy. Notably, the combination of UMMF under NIR irradiation with BH4 synergistically augmented anti-tumor responses in primary tumors and inhibited metastasis in distant organs through multiple mechanisms. These include inducing extensive immunogenic PANoptosis, disrupting the physical barriers of the tumor stroma, facilitating effector T cell infiltration, and promoting the maturation of DCs. In addition to enhancing local anti-tumor immunity, UMMF combined with BH4 and NIR irradiation significantly boosted systemic adaptive immune responses. This was demonstrated by the inhibition of tumor growth upon re-challenge and the regression of metastatic lesions in distant organs, highlighting the potential of this strategy to achieve durable and comprehensive anti-tumor immunity.

The UMMF nanovaccine integrates antigen delivery, stromal modulation, and immune reprogramming into a single platform, addressing key limitations of existing immunotherapies. Unlike traditional DC vaccines, which suffer from inefficient antigen presentation and poor stromal penetration, UMMF employs DC/tumor hybrid membranes to deliver diverse tumor antigens directly in vivo while enhancing T cell infiltration via NIR-triggered ROS-mediated stromal disruption. Compared to immune checkpoint inhibitors, which often fail in “cold” tumors and cause systemic toxicity, UMMF actively primes CTLs through immunogenic PANoptosis and remodels the immunosuppressive TME, enabling effective treatment of immunologically resistant tumors with minimal off-target effects. CAR-T therapies face challenges such as antigen escape and complex personalized manufacturing; however, UMMF’s broad antigen spectrum and off-the-shelf scalability reduce immune evasion risks and production barriers. The inclusion of BH4 synergizes with UMMF by reversing IDO-mediated immune tolerance, amplifying anti-tumor efficacy against metastasis and recurrence.

While the UMMF platform integrates multiple functional components, each element addresses critical barriers in solid tumor immunotherapy—limited antigen diversity, immunosuppressive stroma, and metabolic immune tolerance—that simpler systems cannot overcome. The modularity of UMMF introduces manufacturing challenges; however, its components are grounded in scalable technologies already transitioning to clinical use. For example, MOF synthesis via solvent-free/microwave methods enables industrial-scale production,47 while cell membrane coating is advancing toward clinical use.48 Allogeneic DC/tumor membranes eliminate personalized customization, and NIR-controlled activation enhances precision while reducing dosing frequency.

In summary, this study demonstrates that the biomimetic FM-camouflaged UMMF nanovaccine effectively inhibits tumor growth, relapse, and metastasis by integrating PDT-induced immunogenic PANoptosis with FM-based anti-tumor responses. Most notably, the UMMF nanovaccine can extensively reprogram key components of the desmoplastic and immunosuppressive TME, converting an immunologically “cold” TME into a “hot” one, thereby sensitizing tumors to subsequent immunotherapies. Additionally, the incorporation of BH4 into the UMMF platform further enhances local and systemic anti-tumor immunity, presenting significant potential for personalized combination immunotherapy.

Limitations of the study

Despite its promising anti-tumor efficacy, the UMMF therapeutic nanovaccine faces several limitations related to its architectural complexity and unsatisfactory penetration of light. The integration of multiple sophisticated components, including UCNPs, MOFs, and hybrid cell membranes, introduces manufacturing intricacies that may impede large-scale reproducibility. To overcome this limitation, future iterations will prioritize nanoplatform simplification through substitution of UCNP@MOF with organic photosensitizers or other multifunctional nanosystems, thereby reducing synthetic burden while preserving functional synergy, aiming to bridge preclinical and clinical development. Additionally, the dependence on NIR light for spatiotemporal activation presents a second limitation due to its restricted tissue penetration depth, which compromises efficacy in deep-seated neoplasms. Future efforts will explore alternative exogenous stimuli such as focused ultrasound or alternating magnetic fields, which offer superior penetration capabilities and non-invasive spatiotemporal control. Such refinements aim to enhance clinical adaptability while maintaining the core therapeutic paradigm of targeted immunomodulation and TME remodeling.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell lines

The 4T1 mouse breast cancer cell (ATCC, CRL-2539) and B16F10 melanoma cell (ATCC, CRL-6475) were purchased from Shanghai Institutes for Biological Sciences (SIBS, Shanghai, China). The cells were cultured at 37°C and 5% CO2 in complete RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 100 μg/mL penicillin/streptomycin and 10% FBS. All cell lines underwent authentication via short tandem repeat profiling and were routinely screened to confirm the absence of mycoplasma contamination.

Mice models

6-week-old female BALB/c, BALB/c-nu, and C57BL/6J mice were purchased from Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc. (Shanghai, China) and maintained in a pathogen-free environment. All animal experiments were conducted with the approval of ethics by Ethics Committee of Shanghai University.

Method details

Synthesis of NaYF4:Yb/Er core nanoparticles

YCl3⋅6H20 (0.78 mmol), YbCl3⋅6H20 (0.2 mmol), and ErCl3⋅6H20 (0.02 mmol) were added into a three-necked flask containing 15 mL of 1-octadecene and 8 mL of OA. The mixed solution was then heated to 120°C and maintained for 60 min under vacuum. Next, 6 mL of methanol solution containing NaOH (2.5 mmol) and NH4F (4 mmol) was added after cooling to room temperature. The mixture was magnetically stirred for 30 min at 50°C, followed by heating to 100°C under vacuum to remove the methanol. Finally, the system was further heated to 300°C and kept for 120 min under argon flow. The obtained nanoparticles were precipitated and washed with cyclohexane and ethanol and dispersed in cyclohexane for further use.

Synthesis of core@shell structured NaYF4:Yb/Er@NaYF4 upconversion nanoparticles (UCNPs)

(1)Y-OA (0.10 M) shell precursor: 2.5 mmol of YCl3⋅6H20, 10 mL of OA, and 15 mL of 1-octadecene were added in a round-bottom flask and subsequently heated to 120°C under vacuum accompanied by stirring for 30 min to remove residual oxygen and water. The Y-OA (0.10 M) shell precursor solution was then obtained for future use.(2)Na-TFA-OA (0.40 M) shell precursor: 4.00 mmol of C2F3NaO2 (Na-TFA) and 10 mL of OA were mixed in a round-bottom flask at room temperature with stirring under vacuum to remove residual oxygen and water. Then the Na-TFA-OA (0.40 M) precursor solution was obtained for future use.(3)Core@shell structured nanoparticles: The core@shell upconversion nanoparticles were constructed based on a one-pot successive layer-by-layer protocol.49 Typically, 5 mL of the purified NaYF4:Yb/Er core nanoparticles solution (∼0.5 mmol), 12 mL of 1-octadecene, and 8 mL of oleic acid were mixed in a three-necked flask. The flask was first heated at 70°C for 30 min under vacuum to remove cyclohexane. Then the reaction mixture was heated to 300°C at a rate of about 20°C/min under argon flow. Next, the pairs of Y-OA (0.1M, 5.0 mL) and Na-TFA-OA (0.40 M, 2.5 mL) precursors were simultaneously introduced by dropwise addition at 300°C, and the dropping speed was approximately 2.5 mL/h. Finally, the as-prepared NaYF4:Yb/Er@NaYF4 core@shell upconversion nanoparticles were precipitated and washed with cyclohexane and ethanol, and dispersed in cyclohexane for subsequent carboxylic acid group anchoring.

Carboxylic acid group anchoring to UCNP

0.15 mmol of UCNP in 2 mL of tetrahydrofuran (THF) was mixed with 50 mg of 3,4- dihydroxyhydrocinnamic acid in 8 mL of THF. The reaction solution was first heated at 50°C for 3 h. After cooling to room temperature, 0.2 mL of aqueous NaOH (0.2 M) was added to precipitate UCNP. Finally, carboxylic acid-anchored UCNP was washed with ethanol and resuspended in N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF) for further use.

Synthesis of UCNP@MOF nanoparticles

Typically, solution A was obtained by introducing 12 mg of ZrCl4 into 12 mL of a mixture of DMF (9 mL) and ethanol (3 mL). Solution B was obtained by introducing 4.5 mg of TCPP to 4 mL of a mixture of DMF (3 mL) and ethanol (1 mL). Solution B was dropwise added into solution A and sonicated for 10 min. Subsequently, 2 mL of carboxylic acid-anchored UCNP (∼0.04 mmol) was added to the mixture of solution A and solution B. The resultant mixture was heated to 120°C and maintained for 5 h. Finally, the synthesized UCNP@MOF (denoted as UM) was washed with DMF.

Loading of (S)-crizotinib to UM

The inhibitor of the human mutT homologue MTH1, (S)-crizotinib, was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to form a solution with (S)-crizotinib concentration of 0.2 mg/mL. Then, the DMSO solutions containing (S)-crizotinib in designed amounts were introduced to a solution with UM (5 mM) under magnetic stirring to load (S)-crizotinib into the pores of UM. The UCNP@MOF loaded with (S)-crizotinib was denoted as UMM.

Generation of BMDCs and preparation of fused cells

Bone marrow-derived monocyte was isolated from mice femurs and washed with PBS. Using Red Blood Cell Lysis Buffer (ACK) to lyse red cells and then passing them through a 40 μm cell strainer. Bone marrow cells were suspended in RPMI-1640 containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Cells were seeded into the 6-well plate with a density of 1×106/mL, and cocultured with granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) (20 ng/mL) and Interleukin-4 (IL-4) (10 ng/mL). At day 4, the culture was discarded and supplemented with new complete RPMI-1640 medium with GM-CSF and IL-4. At day 7, cells were collected and washed twice with cold PBS for twice. 4T1 or B16F10 cells were inactivated using 40% alcohol on ice for 15 min. Tumor cells and collected DCs were mixed at a ratio of 2:1 and centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 10 min. Discarding the supernatant, 1 mL PEG (50% (w/v), average mol wt 1,450) prewarmed in 40°C was slowly added into the 50 mL centrifuging tube. After stewing in 40°C water bath for 5 min, prewarmed RPMI 1640 was added to the mixture to stop the fusion. Then the mixture was centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 10 min and resuspended in RPMI-1640 containing 10% FBS with 1% penicillin-streptomycin. After all, cells were seeded into a 6-well plate and cultured for another 7 days in a complete RPMI-1640 medium with IL-4 (10 ng/mL). Finally, fused cells were collected and detected by flow cytometry and confocal laser scanning microscope analysis.

Preparation of FM

When tumor/DCs fused cells were well prepared, cells were collected for cell membrane extraction according to the manufacturer’s instructions of Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology using the Membrane Protein Extraction Kit. Cell pellets were obtained and resuspended in a lysis buffer with phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) on ice, as well as vortexed and broken using a repeated freeze-thaw method three times. After 30 min incubation in the ice bath, the mixture was centrifuged at 700 g for 10 min, and the supernatant was centrifuged for another 30 min at 14000 g to collect the cracked cell membrane. The collected cell membrane was lyophilized and stored at −80°C for further use. By adding protein lysis buffer to the cell membrane, the cell membrane protein was obtained for the WB experiment.

Western blot analysis

The CM, DM, and FM protein lysates were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail. Protein samples were subjected to 4%–10% Tris-Glycine gels for electrophoresis for 90 min and then transferred to Polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes for 2 h. The membranes were incubated with primary antibodies (EpCAM, CD11c, and Na+/K+-ATPase) at a dilution (1:1000) overnight. The next day, HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were added for 1 h at room temperature. Protein expressions were analyzed with Pierce ECL Plus Substrate.

Preparation of FM-coated UMM

The UMM solution was introduced into the ultrapure water solution of FM with an equal weight of UMM and FM. Then the mixture was sonicated in an ice bath until the solution was transparent. The as-prepared UCNP@MOF@MI@FM (denoted as UMMF) was finally purified by centrifugation to remove the free FM.

Nanovaccine characterization

The morphology, elemental mapping, and EDS line scan were measured by a JEM-2100F electron microscope. The powder X-ray diffraction pattern was conducted on a D/MAX-TTRIII (CBO) series XRD. UV/vis spectra and UCL spectra were performed on a Hitachi 5300 spectrophotometer and a Hitachi F-4600 spectrometer with a 980 nm NIR laser as the excitation source, respectively. The hydrodynamic particle size and zeta potential were recorded on the Malvern Zetasizer Nanoseries (Nano ZS90).

To quantitatively analyze the 1O2 generation, UM or UMMF was mixed with DPBF (Sigma-Aldrich, 8 mM) and then irradiated by an NIR laser (980 nm, 1 W/cm2) at room temperature. The absorption of DPBF at 425 nm was recorded every 5 min from 0 to 30 min with a UV-vis spectrophotometer.

Cellular uptake

To effectively load fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC), the FITC solution was mixed with the UM solution under magnetic stirring for 12 h to obtain UM@FITC. Next, the UM@FITC was further coated with FM to gain UMF@FITC. Then, 4T1 cells were incubated with pre-stained UM or UMF at varying time points (2 h, 4 h, 8 h, 12 h) at 37°C. Cells were finally collected and measured with flow cytometry. Similarly, after the as-prepared UMF was co-cultured with 4T1 cells for different times (2 h, 4 h, 8 h, 12 h), the cells were fixed in glutaraldehyde (2.5%) and collected for further Bio-TEM imaging.

In vitro ROS detection

Five groups, including untreated, NIR, UMF, UMF+NIR, and UMMF+NIR, were set to measure the ability of UMMF nanovaccine in ROS production upon exposure to NIR irradiation. Briefly, 4T1 cells were treated with UMF or UMMF at 37°C for 12 h. Next, the culture media were replaced by DCFH-DA (Beyotime) and exposed to NIR or NIR-free irradiation (980 nm, 1.0 W/cm2, 5 min). After culturing for another 30 min, the cancer cells were observed by confocal laser scanning microscope or collected for flow cytometry analysis.

Fluorescence imaging of the DNA damage

Owing to the favorable binding affinity of avidin to 8-oxo-dG, Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated avidin was adopted to evaluate the incorporation of 8-oxo-dG in DNA. Firstly, the 4T1 cells were subjected to the same treatment in the ROS detection assay and then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at 37°C for 15 min, followed by incubating with 0.2% Triton X-100 at room temperature for 10 min. After blocking at room temperature for 30 min using QuickBlock Blocking Buffer for Immunol Staining (Beyotime), the cells were subsequently incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated avidin (10 μg/mL, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) for 1 h at 37°C. Finally, the cells were stained with DAPI and imaged with a confocal microscope.

In vitro cytotoxicity assay

The cytotoxicity against tumor cells was explored using Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Beyotime). In brief, 4T1 cells or B16F10 cells were seeded in 96-well plates with an intensity of 3000 cells per well and co-cultured with UMF or UMMF at varying concentrations for 12 h. Then the cells were exposed to NIR irradiation (980 nm, 1 W/cm2, 5 min) and incubated for another 24 h. Finally, the cell viability was measured by CCK-8 assay according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

The in vitro tumor inhibitory performance of UMMF upon NIR irradiation was further evaluated using the Calcein/PI Cell Viability/Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (Beyotime). 4T1 cells were treated by the same protocol as mentioned above and incubated for another 24 h. Then the cancer cells were stained with Calcein-AM/PI for further confocal laser scanning microscope analysis.

Cell apoptosis

4T1 cells were incubated with the UMF or UMMF, followed by NIR irradiation. To compare the apoptotic effect of UMMF, cells were cultured with PBS or not irradiated by an NIR laser. After another 24 h of incubation, cells were collected and stained with FITC-Annexin V/Propidium Iodide as apoptosis markers, as well as analyzed by flow cytometer.

PANopotosis evaluation

4T1 cells were seeded on 6-well plates at a density of 1×106 cells/well. After the same treatment as mentioned above, the YO-PRO-1/Propidium iodide (PI) (Beyotime Biotechnology) staining kit was used to detect the necroptotic cells. The cell morphology was observed in the bright field images to evaluate the cell pyroptosis.

Lactic dehydrogenase, interleukin-1β, and IL-18 evaluation

4T1 cells were seeded on 96-well plates at a density of 5×103 cells/well. After the same treatment as mentioned above, the release of LDH was measured according to the instructions of the LDH cytotoxicity test kit. After the same treatment as mentioned above, the supernatant was collected to detect the release of IL-1β and IL-18 through the Mouse IL-1β and IL-18 ELISA kit, respectively.

ICD detection

4T1 cells were seeded on 6-well plates at a density of 1×106 cells/well. After the same treatment as mentioned above, cells were washed with PBS twice and fixed with 4% PFA for 15 min, followed by permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 15 min (applicable to HMGB1 evaluation) and incubated with blocking buffer for 1 h. Cells were then incubated with anti-HMGB1 or anti-CRT antibody at 4°C overnight. Finally, cells were covered with an Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h in the dark. Images were taken by CLSM.

Bulk RNA sequencing

For bulk RNA-seq of 4T1 cells, total RNA was extracted from 4T1 cells treated with UMMF upon NIR irradiation. RNA isolation and library construction were performed by Shanghai Majorbio Bio-pharm Biotechnology Co., Ltd. RNA-seq transcriptome library was performed using Illumina Stranded mRNA Prep, Ligation from Illumina. Differentially expressed genes were identified using the transcripts per million reads method. Differential expression analysis was conducted using DESeq2. Differentially expressed genes with Log2Fold change ≥1 and FDR ≤0.05 were recognized to be significantly differentially expressed genes. Besides, KEGG pathway analysis was performed by KOBAS. GSEA was performed by the fgsea package based on differentially expressed genes with Log2Fold change >1.5 and p value <0.05.

T cell and DC cell function assay

To measure the antigen-specific T cell priming ability of UMMF nanovaccine under NIR irradiation, BMDCs were pre-incubated with differentially treated 4T1 cells (Untreated, NIR, UMF, UMF+NIR, UMMF+NIR) for 48 h. Then the BMDCs were co-cultured with Naive T cells from the spleen at a ratio of 1:3 for 2 days. To detect the cytokine production of T cells, cells were stimulated with 2.5 mg/mL Brefeldin A for 6 h at 37°C. Then cells were collected and resuspended in FACS buffer containing intracellular staining markers. Using Cytofix Fixation buffer to fix cells for 60 min at 4°C, followed by adding Permeabilization Wash buffer. Finally, cells were stained with intracellular markers in Stain buffer. T cell proliferation and DC function were analyzed by flow cytometry.

In vivo biodistribution of nanovaccine

BALB/c-nu mice were purchased from Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc. To detect the tumor and lymph node targeting ability and biodistribution of the nanovaccine in vivo, living images were performed at predetermined times. Mice were i.v. injected with UMF@Cy5.5 or UM@Cy5.5, and images were taken using the In Vivo Imaging System. After 24 h, mice were euthanized, and tumors, tumor-draining lymph nodes, and main tissues (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were collected for ex vivo imaging.

Tumor digestion

Tumor tissues were mechanically dissected and digested with 2.0 mg/mL Collagenase type IV (Worthington) and 0.1 mg/mL DNase I (Roche) in RPMI1640 for 30 min at 37°C. Digested medium was filtered with 70 μm cell strainers, followed by incubating with ACK lysis buffer for 10 min. For tumor single cell suspension, filtered cells were resuspended in staining buffer. Lymphoid nodes were mashed and passed through a 70 μm cell strainer and incubated with ACK lysis buffer. Filtered cells were washed with PBS and resuspended in stain buffer.

Flow cytometry

Single cell suspensions were incubated with Live/Dead fixable staining on ice for 15 min to exclude dead cells. Single cells were blocked with Fc receptor anti-CD16/32 for 10 min, followed by surface staining markers in FACS buffer for 30 min on ice. For extracellular staining, cells were fixed in Cytofix Fixation buffer for 60 min at 4°C followed by permeabilization with wash buffer. For transcription factor staining, cells were fixed in Mouse Foxp3 buffer set, then washed with permeabilization buffer. After that, cells were stained with an intracellular staining marker for 30 min on ice. Immune cell phenotypes were performed and analyzed by Cytoflex. Following anti-mouse antibodies are used: anti-CD45, anti-CD3, anti-CD4, anti-CD8, anti-FOXP3, anti-CD19, anti-CD44, anti-CD69 anti-PD-1, anti-CD11b, anti-CD11c, anti-CD206, anti-F4/80, anti-MHC-II, anti-MHC-I, anti-CD103, anti-Ly6G, anti-Ly6c, anti-CD49b.These antibodies were obtained from BioLegend, BD Biosciences, or eBiosciences.

Lung metastasis model

For the lung metastasis model, mice were injected with 5×105 4T1 cells into the fourth mammary fat pad of female mice. From day 0–2, mice were treated with PBS, NIR, UMF, BH4, UMF+NIR, UMMF+NIR, UMMF+NIR+BH4, respectively. On day 12, primary tumors were surgically resected. On day 19, mice were i.v. injected with 4T1 cells (5×105), and the lungs were harvested for H&E staining.

Rechallenge breast cancer model

For the rechallenge mouse model, mice were injected with 5×105 4T1 cells into the fourth mammary fat pad of female mice. From day 0–2, mice were treated with PBS, NIR, UMF, BH4, UMF+NIR, UMMF+NIR, UMMF+NIR+BH4, respectively. On day 12, primary tumors were surgically resected. On day 19, mice were injected with 4T1 cells (5×105) into the fourth mammary fat, and the tumor sizes were measured every two days.

Melanoma mouse model

C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc. To elucidate the in vivo antitumor efficacy of the combination of BH4 and UMMF upon NIR irradiation, mice were subcutaneously injected with B16F10 melanoma cells (5×105 cells). From day 0–2, mice were treated with PBS, NIR, UMF, BH4, UMF+NIR, UMMF+NIR, UMMF+NIR+BH4, respectively. The tumor sizes were measured every two days. On day 10, mice were euthanized, and the tumor was rapidly stored in 4% PFA for further studies.

Tissue immunofluorescence and immunohistochemistry

Tumor, lung, and main organ tissues were examined using H&E staining. Tumor apoptosis was detected using Terminal deoxynucle otidyltransferase-mediated dUTP nick-end Labeling (TUNEL) Assay Kit (Fluorescence, 488nm). For IF staining, tissues were blocked using blocking buffer (3% BSA, 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS) for 1 h. Sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C on a shaker. Secondary antibodies were incubated for 2 h at room temperature. Excess antibodies were removed and washed with TBST three times. Then, sections were labeled with DAPI. After labeling, tissue sections were mounted with mounting media. The following antibodies were used: HMGB1, CRT, COL1A, α-SMA, CD8, CD4, CD11c. ImageJ was used for image analysis and quantification. For IHC staining, 5-μm-thick tumor sections were blocked using Hydrogen Peroxide for 15 min. Sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C on a shaker. Secondary antibodies were incubated for 30 min at room temperature. Excess antibodies were removed and washed with TBST three times. Then, sections were labeled with DAB substrate for 10 min. After labeling, tissue sections were covered with Hematoxylin for 1 min and then mounted with mounting media. Images were analyzed with ImageJ software. The following antibodies were used for IHC staining: CD31, Ki-67, HMGB1, and CRT.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All data were presented as the mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism. Two-tailed, unpaired Student’s t test was used to analyze the two-group comparison. For more than two groups, data analysis was performed using One-way ANOVA or two-way ANOVA with multiple comparisons. Statistical significance is defined as ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001.
