Chemerin 15 enhances microglial phagocytosis to attenuate cerebral ischemia-reperfusion injury through the ChemR23/p38 MAPK pathway
Authors: Yuhan Yang, Yan Huang, Jun Li, Kunyi Li, Changhao Mao, Gang Liu, Shuang Li, Lan Wen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryMicroglia recover and maintain homeostasis in the central nervous system by phagocytosing dead cells and harmful substances under pathological conditions. Chemerin 15 (C15), a chemerin-derived peptide, is an endogenous phagocytic and inflammatory regulator that acts via the G protein-coupled receptor, ChemR23. However, the effect of C15 on microglial phagocytosis following ischemia-reperfusion injury remains unclear. Here, we found that microglial phagocytosis was activated and dynamically altered after ischemia-reperfusion injury. C15 administration promoted microglial phagocytosis of bioparticles and neuronal debris and upregulated the phagocytosis-related genes and pathways. Furthermore, C15 binding to ChemR23 decreased damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and ROS production and further alleviated the infarct volume and neurological deficits after ischemia-reperfusion injury. Thus, C15 is a potential therapeutic target for the recovery of neurological function after stroke.

Introduction

Ischemic stroke is the leading cause of mortality and disability in adults.1,2,3 According to the Global Burden of Disease study, the incidence and prevalence of strokes increased by 70% and 85%, respectively, from 1990 to 2019, with ischemic stroke accounting for approximately 76% of cases in 20193 Ischemic stroke imposes a heavy financial burden, with a global economic loss of up to $9,645.1 reported in 2019.4 Although endovascular therapy and intravenous thrombolysis reduce mortality and disability, the majority of patients experience permanent neuronal cell death due to missed time windows or reperfusion-injury. Dead neural cells release damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) such as high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), heat shock proteins (Hsp), and adenosine triphosphate (ATP).5 DAMPs activate immune cells, including microglia, astrocytes, and infiltrating macrophages and neutrophils, which further exacerbate neuronal loss, damage the blood-brain barrier, and eventually lead to neurological deficits by producing reactive oxygen species (ROS), pro-inflammatory and chemokine factors, and matrix metalloproteinases.6,7,8 Therefore, effectively removing dead cells and cell debris from the area of injury is beneficial for reducing the release of DAMPs and subsequent neuronal damage.9

Microglia, the innate immune and phagocytic cells of the central nervous system, are capable of being rapidly activated and recruited to the infarct area after ischemic stroke and play a crucial role in the degree of brain tissue injury and subsequent neurological function repair.10,11 After ischemia, DAMPs activate different receptors on microglia, such as CD36, P2X7, and TLRs, which mediate and amplify inflammatory responses and neurological impairment.6,12 Microglia phagocytize apoptotic cells and debris in a non-inflammatory process, due to the absence of inflammatory cytokines and the increase of anti-inflammatory mediators.13 Recent therapeutic studies have targeted microglial phagocytosis. Wen et al. demonstrated that the P2Y6 receptor inhibitor, MRS2578, reduced the phagocytic function of microglia via MLCK, which aggravated neurological recovery following cerebral ischemia in mice.14 Furthermore, upregulating TREM2 promotes microglial phagocytosis and exerts neuroprotective effects against ischemic stroke.15,16 Additionally, pseudostigmastatin-F11 attenuates cerebral ischemic injury by promoting microglial phagocytosis of myelin debris via complement receptor 3.17 Thus, targeting microglial phagocytic activity may alleviate damage to neighboring neurons, thereby protecting neurological function.

Chemerin is synthesized as a 163-amino acid precursor and forms pro-chemerin (chemerin 20–163), which is ubiquitously found in plasma at nanomolar concentrations. Pro-chemerin is converted into chemerin peptide isoforms with different bioactivity profiles via multiple cleavage sites in the C-terminal domain.18,19,20 ChemR23, the main receptor for chemerin, plays a role in inflammatory and metabolic diseases and is widely expressed in various immune cells, including microglia, macrophages, and neutrophils.21,22,23,24,25,26 Chemerin 15 (C15, A140–A154) is generated by calpain and cathepsin S from activated macrophages and binds to ChemR23 at pM concentrations.27 Its potent anti-inflammatory properties are significantly pronounced.24,27,28,29 Additionally, peripheral macrophages have demonstrated phagocytic activity. In a peritonitis model, C15 enhances the phagocytosis of microbial particles and apoptotic cells by macrophages, by facilitating phagocytic cup development through ChemR23.30 C15 promotes the engulfment of apoptotic cells during myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury, thereby inhibiting the production of pro-inflammatory mediators.28

Our previous study showed that C15 combined with ChemR23 attenuates cerebral ischemic injury by modulating the microglial phenotype and inhibiting pro-inflammatory factor secretion.31 However, the effects of C15 on microglial phagocytic function remain unclear. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the effects of C15 on the phagocytic ability of microglia following ischemia/reperfusion injury (I/R).

Results

Phagocytosis enhancement in microglia after cerebral ischemia/reperfusion injury

CD 68+ + IBA1+ cells represent phagocytic microglia. The results showed that microglial phagocytosis increased and changed dynamically following tMCAO. Next, we examined the phagocytic activity of microglia in vitro. BV2 cells were subjected to OGD to mimic the ischemic environment in vivo. To verify the optimal OGD time, we measured BV2 cell activity using the CCK- 8 assay. The results revealed a time-dependent decrease in BV2 cell viability after OGD, with approximately 50% cell viability observed after 3 h. Thus, 3 h was identified as the optimal duration for OGD (Figure S1). To determine the phagocytic capacity of microglia, BV2 microglia were incubated with pHrodo Red Zymosan Bioparticles, and the BV2 microglia containing zymosan were counted. As shown in Figures 1D–1F, the proportion of microglia containing Zymosan and the Zymosan+ area gradually increased within 24 h of reoxygenation. These results suggest that phagocytic microglia increase after I/R injury and show dynamic changes.

C15 improved microglial phagocytosis in vitro

Chemerin peptide C15 enhances macrophage phagocytosis of microbial particles and apoptotic cells.30 To explore the effect of C15 on microglial phagocytosis, microglia were pretreated with C15 and α-NETA (an inhibitor of the ChemR23 receptor). As shown in Figures 2A–2C, the proportion of microglia containing Zymosan and the Zymosan+ area increased after OGD and displayed a further increase after C15 intervention. C15 exerts its physiological effects primarily through the ChemR23 receptor.24 Thus, α-NETA, a competitive inhibitor of ChemR23, was used in subsequent experiments. Compared with the OGD+C15 group, the proportion of microglia containing Zymosan and the Zymosan+ area decreased in the α-NETA group, showing no statistical difference from the OGD group (Figures 2A–2C). The similar results were shown in the phagocytic ability of microglia on neurons (Figure 2D). HT-22 neurons were co-cultured with microglia constitutively expressing red fluorescent protein (RFP-microglia). Following OGD, the HT-22 neurons were immunostained with NeuN (a neuronal marker). C15 pretreatment yielded an increase in phagocytosed neurons, while α-NETA partially counteracted this ability. To investigate the molecular basis of the effect of C15/ChemR23 on microglial phagocytosis, we performed transcriptome sequencing and analyzed GO, KEGG pathway enrichment, and DEGs. We first compared the DEGs between the OGD and OGD+C15 groups. From a functional perspective, the GO enrichment analysis showed elevated phagocytosis and receptor internalization-related biological processes in the OGD+C15 group compared to the OGD group (Figure 3C). DEGs between the OGD+C15 and OGD groups included phagocytosis-related genes (Clec7a, H2-DMb2, Prg2) and internalization-related genes (csf3r, cd22, Amn) (Figure 3D). Phagocytosis-related genes (Clec7a, H2-DMb2, Prg2) were significantly upregulated in the OGD+C15 group. The above genes have been verified to by qPCR experiments, as consistent with RNA-seq results (Figures 3E–3G).

Transcriptome sequencing analysis indicated that C15/ChemR23 regulates microglial phagocytosis mainly via p38 MAPK

Transcriptome sequencing analysis of the OGD+C15 and OGD+C15+α-NETA groups was performed. The KEGG enrichment indicated that α-NETA administration facilitated the inhibition of the MAPK signaling pathway compared to the OGD+C15 group (Figure 4A). Along with the GO enrichment results, which revealed that biological processes such as phagocytosis and internalization were affected, we observed a significant down-regulation of the positive regulation of p38 MAPK with α-NETA treatment (Figure 4B). Several genes involved in the p38 MAPK signaling pathway, including Atf2, Map3k5, and Myc, were downregulated (Figure 4C), which was further confirmed by qPCR experiment (Figures 4D–4F). A previous study reported that ChemR23 activates the MAPK signaling pathway through the second messenger pathway, which produces multifunctional effects, such as a decreased immune response.35 Therefore, we subsequently measured the gene and protein expression levels of p38 MAPK and phosphorylated p38 MAPK (p-p38) in microglia. Compared with the OGD group, the C15 group showed a significant upregulation in both p-p38 mRNA and protein expression, whereas α-NETA inhibited this increase (Figures 4G–4I). To further evaluate the effect of C15 on microglial phagocytosis through the ChemR23/p38 MAPK pathway, we used the SB203580 to inhibit the activation of p38 MAPK. Before the experiments, we investigated the optimal dosage of SB203580 by WB assay and CCK-8 (Figure S2A–S2C). The WB results showed p-p38 expression was significantly reduced at a concentration of 50 μM. Meanwhile, no statistical difference was observed in cell viability among the different concentrations. Thus, 50 μM was chosen as the optimal concentration. We next explored the phagocytic ability of C15 on apoptotic neurons. The images displayed apoptotic neurons (RFP++NeuN+ cells) significantly reduced in the C15+SB203580 group compared to the C15 group (Figure 4J). Collectively, C15 regulates microglial phagocytosis after OGD through the ChemR23/p38 MAPK signaling pathway.

Discussion

Increasing evidence suggests that microglial phagocytosis is important for the protection of brain tissue following ischemic stroke. In the present study, we report that C15 enhances microglial phagocytosis, decreases DAMPs and ROS levels, and attenuates cerebral I/R injury and neurological impairment. These effects may be related to the ChemR23/p38 MAPK signaling pathway.

Ischemic stroke causes massive cell death in the brain, which releases a range of harmful substances, such as DAMPs and ROS, causing persistent inflammatory damage and impairing neurological recovery.10,37 Debris removal after neurological injury is a critical step in repairing damaged neural networks and effectively alleviating impaired neurological function.38,39 Microglia serve as the phagocytic and inflammatory cells. When ischemic stroke-related injury occurs, damaged brain cells release “find me” signaling substances, including ATP and chemokines, which attract neighboring microglia to rapidly migrate to the injury site.40,41 Simultaneously, the damaged brain cells also release “eat me” signaling substances, such as PS, calreticulin, and C3b, which are recognized by microglial phagocytosis receptors, including TREM2, LPR, and CR3, thereby triggering the polymerization of actin within the microglial cells to form phagocytosis cups.42,43 Microglia with phagocytic capacity phagocytose dead cells, cell debris, and myelin sheaths, and reduce the secretion of DAMPs, thereby alleviating inflammatory responses and neurological deficits in various central nervous system diseases.16,44,45 CD68, as a member of the scavenger receptor family, typically removes cellular debris, promotes phagocytosis, mediates macrophage recruitment, and is used as a marker of cellular phagocytosis.46 In our study, we observed dynamic changes in phagocytic microglia over time with reperfusion, both in vivo and in vitro. In in vivo experiments, microglial phagocytosis was activated after I/R injury, peaked on the first day, and gradually decreased thereafter.

Chemerin and chemerin-derived peptides, such as C15, are considered important chemokines and adipokines in inflammatory and metabolic diseases. C15 exerts anti-inflammatory and phagocytosis-promoting effects via ChemR23.24,27,28,30 In our previous study, C15 protected neurons and attenuated neurological deficits by polarizing microglia toward an anti-inflammatory protective phenotype and reducing the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines.31 Picomolar concentrations of C15 are highly potent in promoting macrophage phagocytosis of microbial particles and apoptotic cells in peritonitis by facilitating the development of phagocytic cups in a Syk-dependent manner.24,30 However, it remains unclear whether C15 regulates microglial phagocytosis. In this study, we demonstrated that C15 promotes microglial phagocytosis of dead neurons and debris in vivo and in vitro, and these effects could be attenuated by α-NETA, which suggested that the pro-phagocytosis effect of C15 is mainly mediated by ChemR23. In addition, transcriptomic analysis revealed phagocytic-related gene changes among the groups. Consistent with a previous study, ChemR23 played a key role in the macrophage-specific uptake and phagocytosis of oxLDL in atherosclerosis models.47 Ohira T et al. demonstrated that RvE1 enhances phagocytosis in human macrophages through ChemR23.48 In addition to phagocytosis, we tested the ability of C15 to remove DAMPs and protect brain tissue from ischemic injury in vivo. Our study showed that C15 treatment reduced the levels of DAMPs and the production of ROS, as well as improved brain tissue damage, neural cell damage, and neurological function impairment.

ChemR23 is a G protein-coupled receptor whose internalization is important for its physiological function. We verified that C15 triggered the ChemR23 internalization and α-NETA reduced this process. However, changes in the signaling pathway that occur after ChemR23 internalization, induced by C15, remain unknown. In this study, KEGG and GO enrichment analysis between the OGD+C15 and OGD+C15+α-NETA groups revealed an upregulation of the MAPK signaling pathway and p38 MAPK. The mRNA and protein levels of p38 MAPK and p-p38 were decreased after α-NETA treatment. p38 MAPK is a classical MAPK signaling pathway.49 Studies have reported that p38 MAPK is activated in microglia when co-cultured with damaged neurons and that SB 203580 (a p38 MAPK inhibitor) blocks the phagocytosis of cellular debris, suggesting that p38 MAPK contributes to the phagocytic activity of microglia.50 Our research also confirmed this result in vitro and in vivo.

In conclusion, the present study suggests that C15 regulates microglial phagocytosis after cerebral I/R injury through ChemR23, thereby reducing DAMPs and ROS production and attenuating brain tissue injury. These phagocytic effects are related to the ChemR23/p38 MAPK signaling pathway. This study demonstrates that C15 is a potential target for the reduction of cerebral I/R injury.

Limitations of the study

This study has some limitations. We focused on the effects of C15 on microglial phagocytic ability and brain tissue protection after cerebral I/R injury. However, after brain injury, peripheral macrophages expressing ChemR23 are recruited to the infarcted areas. The phagocytic activity of macrophages has not yet been investigated. This study uses the reliance on cell lines for in vitro experiments, rather than primary cells. Primary microglia might offer more physiologically relevant insights, thus we will use primary microglia to explore the effect of C15 in future. Additionally, microglia-specific ChemR23 knockout should be applied in the study, which may might be conducive to an in-depth exploration of the relationship between C15 and ChemR23. Moreover, this study only provides preliminary evidence of the therapeutic effects of C15. Therefore, future studies should explore the effects of C15 in animal models more closely related to humans, such as monkeys. Finally, the development of C15 delivery systems to achieve stable blood concentrations and determine the optimal therapeutic dose is also needed.

Experimental model and study participant details

Ethics approval

All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Research Ethics Committee of the Southwest Medical University (approval number: swmu20230042).

Animals

To minimize confounding effects of estrogen-mediated neuroprotection, we use male mice to establish stroke model.51,52 Adult male C57/BL6J mice (8–10-weeks-old, 22–25 g) were housed in a temperature- and humidity-controlled specific pathogen-free facility with a 12-h light-dark cycle. Apart from fasting before surgery, the mice had free access to food and water. Data analysis was conducted by two researchers who were blinded to the experimental groups. All procedures were performed to minimize animal suffering.

Method details

C15 peptide synthesis

C15 peptides were synthesized by Sicierbio Co., Ltd., dissolved in 1 mM phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) supplemented with 0.1% bovine serum albumin (BSA), and stored at -80°C for up to 6 months. Peptides were diluted with fresh reagents for each experiment.

Cell culture

HT-22 mouse hippocampal neurons (No. CL-0595, ProCell, Wuhan, Hubei, China), BV2 mouse microglia (No. CL-0493A, ProCell, Wuhan, Hubei, China) and RFP-BV2 mouse microglia (No.CC9007, cellcook, Guangzhou, Guangdong, China) were cultured in Dulbecco modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) high glucose (No. SH30243, Hyclone, Logan, Utah, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, No. 35-179-CV, Corning, New York, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin, at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. The medium was changed every two days. Upon reaching 80%–90% confluence, the cells were digested with 0.25% trypsin (No. SH30042, Hyclone, Logan, Utah, USA) and passaged. The cells from the third to fifth generations were used in subsequent experiments.

Oxygen-glucose deprivation reperfusion (OGD/R) model

An in vitro OGD/R model was used to simulate I/R injury. HT-22 and BV2 cells were transferred to glucose-free medium (No. 11966025, Gibco, Grand Island, New York, USA) and cultured in a hypoxic incubator (94% N2, 1% O2, 5% CO2) at 37°C. Following incubation for different times, cells were returned to normal complete medium at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. All experiments underwent 3 h of OGD.

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

The CCK-8 kit (No.AR1160, BOSTER, Wuhan, Hubei, China) was used to assess cell viability according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, the cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1×104 cells/mL/well. When the cells formed a monolayer, the medium was replaced with operating fluid that contained the CCK-8 reagent (10 μL) and complete medium (90 μL). After incubation for 1.5 h in the dark, the absorbance of different groups was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (XS2, BioTek, Vermont, USA).

Grouping and drug administration in vitro

The in vitro experiments were organized into four groups: CON, OGD, OGD+C15, OGD+C15+α-NETA, OGD+C15+SB203580. Before OGD, BV2 microglia were pretreated with 2-[α-naphthyl]ethyltrimethylammonium iodide (α-NETA), a ChemR23 inhibitor (100 nM, MCE, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) for 24 h, C15 (500 ng/mL)31 for 30 min.

Immunofluorescence staining

Paraffin brain sections (4 μm) were boiled in 0.01 M sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0, BOSTER) at 100°C for 15–20 min to perform antigen retrieval, blocked with 1% BSA for 1 h, and incubated with a primary antibody overnight at 4°C. Cells were washed in PBS, permeated with 0.1% tritonX-100 for 15min at RT, blocked with 1% BSA for 1 h, and incubated with a primary antibody overnight at 4°C. Following PBS washes, the sections were incubated with a secondary antibody for 3 h at RT in the dark. The nuclei were counterstained with 4,6-diamidino-2-phenyl-indole (DAPI, 1:2000) for 15 min. The sections or cells were imaged using a laser confocal scanning microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). The primary antibodies used included anti-Iba1 guinea pig antibody (No. OB-PGP049, Oasis Biofarm, Hangzhou, Zhejiang, China); anti-NeuN mouse monoclonal antibody (No. ab279296; Abcam, Cambridge, United Kingdom); anti-NeuN recombinant rabbit monoclonal antibody (No. ET1602, Huabio, Hangzhou, Zhejiang, China) and anti-CD68 rabbit antibody (No. GB113109, Servicebio, Wuhan, Hebei, China). The secondary antibodies used included goat anti-guinea pig IgG AF647 (No. OB-PGP049, Oasis Biofarm); FITC-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (H+L) (No. ab6785, Abcam); HRP peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (H+L) (No. GB23303, Servicebio); and iF555-Tyramide (No. G1233, Servicebio).

Phagocytosis test in vitro

The BV2 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1×104 cells/well and cultured for 24 h. Cells were incubated with pHrodo Red Zymosan bioparticles (5 ng/well, No. P35364, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) for 4 h and Hoechst (1:2000, No. 62249, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) for 30 min in cell incubator after different interventions. Images were captured via fluorescence microscopy (CLSM, Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). Phagocytic activity was quantified as the geometric mean fluorescence area ratio and the percentage of phagocytic cells were assessed using the ImageJ software (version 1.54i, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

To simulate microglial phagocytosis on neurons in vitro, HT-22 neurons were co-cultured with microglia constitutively expressing red fluorescent protein (RFP-microglia), then exposed to OGD. Subsequently, the HT-22 neurons were immunostained with an anti-NeuN antibody. Co-localization of RFP (microglia) and NeuN (neurons) was then assessed using a Leica confocal laser scanning microscope to evaluate the microglial phagocytic ability on neurons.

mRNA sequencing

RNA isolation and library preparation

Total ribonucleic acid (RNA) was extracted using the TRIzol reagent (No. 15596018CN, Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA purity and quantification were determined using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and integrity was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Transcriptome libraries were constructed using the VAHTS Universal V6 RNA-seq Library Preparation Kit (No. NR604, Vazyme, Nanjing, China) following the manufacturer’s guidelines. Transcriptome sequencing and analysis were performed by OE Biotech (Shanghai, China).

mRNA sequencing analysis process

RNA sequencing and differentially expressed genes (DEGs) analysis

Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform to generate 150-bp paired-end reads. Data were analyzed using HISAT2 for reference genome alignment and gene expression quantification (FPKM), and HTSeq-count was used to obtain the read counts per gene. Gene expression profiles were visualized by PCA using R (v. 3.2.0) to assess sample consistency.

DEGs were identified using DESeq2 software. Genes meeting the criteria of a q value < 0.05 and a fold change > 2 or < 0.5 were considered significant. Hierarchical clustering of DEGs was performed using R (v. 3.2.0) to illustrate the gene expression patterns across groups and samples. The top 30 genes were visualized using radar plots generated with the R package ‘ggradar.’

Subsequent functional enrichment analysis of the DEGs was performed using Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses with hypergeometric distribution algorithms. The enriched functional categories were visualized using bar graphs, chordal plots, or circle plots generated using R (v 3.2.0). Gene set enrichment analysis was performed to determine whether the predefined gene sets were significantly enriched at the top or bottom of the ranked list based on differential expression.

Real-time PCR (RT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from BV2 cells using the TRIzol reagent. The optical density (OD) at 260 nm (OD260) and 280 nm (OD280) was measured using a spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). A OD260/OD280 ratio greater than 1.8 was maintained to ensure the purity and quantity of RNA. Subsequently, 2 μg of the RNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA using the ExonScript RT SuperMix with dsDNase (No. A502, Exongen, Chengdu, China). RT-PCR analysis was performed using Fast SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix UDG (No. A402, Exongen), a Step One Plus Real-Time PCR Detection System (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and the corresponding primers (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China). Relative gene expression was calculated by comparing Ct (threshold cycle) normalized to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) information, and the 2-ΔΔCt method was used to reflect relative gene expression levels. Flow cytometry

BV2 cells were cultured in 6-well plates at the density of 2.5×105 cell/mL, and pretreated with C15 and α-NETA for 24 h. After subjecting the cells to OGD for 3 h, they were washed twice with PBS and blocked with 0.1% BSA. Subsequently, the cells were stained with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated monoclonal mouse ChemR23 antibody (No. FAB7610G, RD System, Minneapolis, USA) at room temperature (RT, 22-24°C) in the dark for 30 min and examined using flow cytometry (V8B7R4, Agilent, Santa Clara, California, USA). FlowJo software (V10.6.2, FlowJo, LLC, Ashland, OR, USA) was used for statistical analysis.

Transient middle cerebral artery occlusion/reperfusion (tMCAO/R) model

The tMCAO/R injury model was established using a rounded-tip monofilament nylon occlusion technique. Mice were fasted for 24 h prior to surgery and anesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital (50 mg/kg; TCI, Tokyo, Japan). The right common carotid artery (CCA), external carotid artery, and internal carotid artery (ICA) were exposed. A rounded-tip monofilament nylon (0.20 mm; Sinon Technology, Beijing, China) was inserted into the ICA until it reached a depth of approximately 9.5±0.05 mm from the CCA bifurcation, or until resistance was perceived, or the mouse exhibited twitching. After 1.5 h, the nylon monofilament was removed to allow reperfusion. The mice were euthanized by an intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital (150 mg/kg) at the designated time.

The drugs were prepared with normal saline in vivo. The usage of α-NETA (10 mg/kg),53 C15 (0.3 ng/kg)24,27,30 referred to the previous studies.31 SB203580( MCE, 5mg/kg,) was intraperitoneally injected at tMCAO according to the drug instructions.54

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

Brain tissues from different groups were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA, Wako, Osaka, Japan), embedded in paraffin, and cut into coronal sections. Paraffin sections (4 μm) were used for H&E, Nissl, and immunofluorescence staining. For H&E staining, the sections were baked, dewaxed, rehydrated, and stained with H&E (No. BL700B, Biosharp, Anhui, China). Images were captured using an optical microscope (DM500, Leica, Wetzlar, Germany): the nucleus and cytoplasm were stained blue and red, respectively.

Nissl staining

Nissl staining was performed using a Nissl Staining Kit (No. C0117, Beyotime Technology, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The paraffin sections (4 μm) were soaked in xylene for 5 min, stained with Nissl stain for 10 min at 37°C. The samples were then dehydrated with 95% ethanol for 4 min, clarified with xylene for 10 min, and sealed with neutral gum. The slices were observed under an optical microscope (DM500, Leica, Wetzlar, Germany).

Western blot analysis

BV2 cells and mouse brain tissue were lysed and homogenized in a radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (No. AR0102, BOSTER) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Equal amounts of protein (25 μg) were separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA). The membranes were incubated with primary antibody overnight at 4°C, followed by a secondary antibody incubation for 1 h at RT. Bands were imaged using a ChemiDoc system (XRS+, Bio-Rad, California, USA) and quantified using ImageJ software (version 1.54i, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). The following were the primary antibodies used: anti-phospho-p38 MAPK (Thr180/Tyr182) rabbit monoclonal antibody (No. 4511T, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA); anti-p38 MAPK rabbit monoclonal antibody (No. 8690T, Cell Signaling Technology); anti-HMGB1 rabbit monoclonal antibody (No. ab79823, Abcam); and anti-β-Actin rabbit monoclonal antibody (No. BM3873, BOSTER); anti-GAPDH rabbit monoclonal antibody (No. R380626, ZEN-BIOSCIENCE, Chengdu, Sichuan, China). The secondary antibodies used were HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG antibodies (No. BA1054, BOSTER).

ROS analysis

ROS production was measured using dihydroethidium (DHE; No. S0063, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). The mice were euthanized with excess glutaraldehyde (150 mg/kg), and their brain tissues were quickly removed and frozen in liquid nitrogen. The frozen brain tissues were OCT-embedded and cut into 8-μm frozen sections. The cryostat sections were incubated with 5 μM DHE at 37°C for 1.5 h and DAPI at RT for 15 min in the dark. A fluorescence scanner (Pannoramic MIDI II, 3D-HISTECH, Budapest, Hungary) was used to observe ROS.

Neurological functional assessment

Neurological scores, including the corner turn test and the modified neurological function severity score (mNSS), were measured pre-operation and 1d, 3d, 7d, 14d, 28d post-I/R injury.

Sensorimotor function in mice was tested using the corner turn test.55 Mice were placed between two 30 × 20 cm boards, with a 30° angle between the boards. The direction of the turn (left or right) was recorded as the mouse walked slowly into the deepest part of the angle. Each mouse was tested 20 times, and the percentage of right turns was calculated. The percentage of right turns in normal mice was approximately 50%, whereas that in ischemic mice was higher. The formula for calculating the result (%) of the corner turn test was (number of right turns/total number of turns) × 100.

The mNSS score was used to assess motor, sensory, balance, and reflex functions.56 Motor function was assessed by evaluating limb movement symmetry, presence of paresis, and coordinated ambulation on a flat surface. Sensory function included tactile stimuli and proprioception. Balance was assessed based on beam walking performance and postural stability. Normal and pathological reflexes were assessed, including the auricular (pinna), corneal, and startle reflexes. The test scores range from 0 to 14: mild injury, 1–4; moderate injury, 5–9; severe injury, 10–14.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data are presented as means ± standard deviations (SDs). GraphPad Prism version 10.0.0 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA) was used for all the statistical analyses. Normality was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test and appeared as quantile-quantile plots. Homogeneity of variance was examined using the Brown–Forsythe and Bartlett’s tests. The two-tailed t-test was applied to compare with two groups. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post hoc test or Welch’s ANOVA with Games–Howell’s post hoc multiple comparisons test was used to compare three or more groups. Two-way repeated measures ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test was used to compare the neurological function scores. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.
