Costs related to atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease in Brazil: analysis from the perspective of the society
Authors: Alessandro Bigoni, Lucas Fahham, Ana Carolina Padula Ribeiro Pereira, Ione Ayala Gualandi de Oliveira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAtherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD)—including ischemic heart disease (IHD), ischemic stroke (IS), and peripheral arterial disease (PAD)—is a leading cause of death and disability worldwide, creating a significant socioeconomic burden. In Brazil, ASCVD accounts for most cardiovascular-related hospitalizations and deaths; comprehensive national estimates of societal costs for IHD, IS, and PAD are still limited. This study aims to estimate the direct and indirect societal costs of IHD, IS, and PAD in Brazil from both public and private healthcare perspectives, and to project their economic burden for the 2025–2029 period.MethodsData were extracted from official national databases, including DATASUS (public sector), TISS (private sector), SIM (mortality), and InfoLogo (social security). Direct medical costs were derived from hospitalizations recorded between 2008 and 2023. Indirect costs included productivity losses due to absenteeism and premature mortality among individuals aged 14–64 years. Future costs were projected using the Exponential Smoothing with Error, Trend, and Seasonality (ETS) model.ResultsASCVD is projected to cause over 600,000 hospitalizations annually in Brazil, generating direct medical costs exceeding BRL 5.9 billion in 2029. Indirect costs related to premature mortality and absenteeism will reach approximately BRL 4.5 billion annually. The total projected 5-year societal burden (2025–2029) amounts to BRL 59.4 billion, with IHD representing the largest share of both direct and indirect costs.ConclusionsASCVD imposes a substantial and growing economic burden on Brazilian society, driven primarily by direct healthcare expenditures and productivity losses from premature deaths. These findings highlight the need for strengthened prevention, early detection, and risk-factor management strategies to mitigate the clinical and economic impact of atherosclerotic disease in Brazil.Keywords: Cardiovascular diseases, Costs and cost analysis, Ischemic heart disease, Ischemic stroke, Peripheral arterial disease

Introduction

Non-communicable chronic diseases—mainly represented by cardiovascular disease (CVD), cancer, and chronic respiratory disease—caused approximately 43 million deaths in 2021, being the leading cause of death worldwide [1]. The Brazilian Society of Cardiology (SBC) estimates that almost 400,000 Brazilian citizens die each year from heart and circulatory diseases. Among these pathologies, the vast majority (76%) is represented by atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) [2]. In Brazil, the most common form of ASCVD is acute myocardial infarction and heart failure [3].

ASCVD affects the carotid, coronary, aorta, and peripheral arteries [4]. The main symptoms are chest pain, edema, limb pain, palpitation, and syncope. There has been a high prevalence of risk factors for these diseases in the last two decades, such as obesity, sedentary lifestyle, smoking, diabetes, and poor diet [3].

CVDs are a public health concern that impacts the population’s health and economy [5]. Globally, the number of deaths due to CVD increased from 12.1 million (95% CI: 11.4 to 12.6 million) in 1990 to 18.6 million (95% CI: 17.1 to 19.7 million) in 2019 [5]. Considering the region of the Americas, ischemic heart disease and stroke were defined as the leading causes of death in 2021. However, despite a numerical increase in deaths, mortality rates seem to have decreased from 1990 to 2021 (from 260 to 139 per 100,000 persons in North America; from 288 to 157 per 100,000 persons in Latin America and the Caribbean) [6]. In addition to premature deaths, individuals at high cardiovascular risk incur more lost work hours and higher indirect costs compared to those without cardiovascular events [7]. Consistently, significant increases are observed in the global trends for disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) and years of life lost. The number of years lived with disability has doubled, from 17.7 million (95% CI: 12.9 to 22.5 million) in 1990 to 34.4 million (95% CI: 24.9 to 43.6 million) in 2019 [2].

In the specific context of South America, a heavy CVD burden is also observed. In 2019 alone, more than 750,000 deaths were caused by CVD, resulting in about 2,040,973 years of potential life lost (YPLL). Regarding productivity loss, this outcome resulted in an economic impact of 0.11% of the region’s gross domestic product (GDP), or approximately 4 billion USD in purchasing power parity terms [5].

Despite the decrease in clinical CVD hospitalizations from 2010 to 2015, a rise in surgical admissions and the use of advanced interventions, such as vascular surgeries and angioplasties, was observed, indicating increased severity of treated cases [8]. This scenario directly affects the health system’s direct costs, which exceeded 5 billion Brazilian reais (BRL) in 2015 [8]. In addition to hospital costs, indirect costs represent a significant burden, accounting for 76% of the total disease cost related to absenteeism and morbidity [8]. These data underscore CVD’s detrimental effects on workforce productivity and economic activity, which, on average, consumed 0.7% of national GDP between 2010 and 2015 [8].

In Brazil, the economic burden of four heart conditions—hypertension, heart failure, myocardial infarction, and atrial fibrillation—was estimated at 56 million BRL in 2015, covering both direct and indirect costs [9]. Additionally, the cost of CVD hospitalizations is regarded as the highest among the causes of hospital admissions in Brazil [9].

The total CVD estimated cost increased from 31 billion BRL in 2010 to 37 billion BRL in 2015 [8]. A continuous understanding of the burden of a specific disease is essential to develop effective public policies for disease management. Based on historical data from DATASUS and projected estimates for the following years, this study aims to estimate the societal costs of ASCVD in Brazil.

Methods

Data extraction

This investigation focused on three main groups of atherosclerotic cardiovascular diseases, for which information was extracted from the databases, considering the following ICD-10 codes as the primary diagnosis:

Ischemic heart disease (IHD): The complete I20-I25 codes were included, excluding I23 (Certain current complications following acute myocardial infarction), as it mainly refers to complications related to acute myocardial infarction.

Ischemic heart disease (IHD): The complete I20-I25 codes were included, excluding I23 (Certain current complications following acute myocardial infarction), as it mainly refers to complications related to acute myocardial infarction.

Ischemic stroke (IS): the complete I64 (Stroke, not specified as hemorrhage or infarction) was included. Hemorrhagic stroke (I60–I62), transient ischemic attacks (G45), and non-stroke-related (I65-I69) ICD-10 codes were excluded. I63 (Cerebral infarction) was not included because it is a less prevalent ICD-10 code for IS, accounting for fewer than 10% of all IS hospitalizations in the database.

Ischemic stroke (IS): the complete I64 (Stroke, not specified as hemorrhage or infarction) was included. Hemorrhagic stroke (I60–I62), transient ischemic attacks (G45), and non-stroke-related (I65-I69) ICD-10 codes were excluded. I63 (Cerebral infarction) was not included because it is a less prevalent ICD-10 code for IS, accounting for fewer than 10% of all IS hospitalizations in the database.

Peripheral arterial disease (PAD): The complete I73 (Other peripheral vascular diseases) code was used. Non-PAD codes, such as I73.0 (Raynaud syndrome) and I73.1 (Thromboangiitis obliterans), were observed in fewer than 5% of hospitalizations and aren’t expected to affect the results.

Peripheral arterial disease (PAD): The complete I73 (Other peripheral vascular diseases) code was used. Non-PAD codes, such as I73.0 (Raynaud syndrome) and I73.1 (Thromboangiitis obliterans), were observed in fewer than 5% of hospitalizations and aren’t expected to affect the results.

All databases are publicly accessible. For the historical set, the analysis used data from 2008 to 2023; however, the period varied across datasets. Historical and projected data information were included.

Direct costs

Direct costs from the Brazilian Unified Health System (SUS) were determined through the DATASUS hospital database. This public, anonymized database compiles data on all hospital procedures performed within SUS since 2008 for reimbursement purposes. DATASUS contains the primary diagnosis by ICD-10 code, medical procedures, healthcare provider, treatment costs, and patients’ demographic details. You can find more information about the database in the documentation available at the Brazilian Ministry of Health website [10]. Data were retrieved from 2010 to 2023.

The direct costs estimates for the Brazilian private health system (ANS) were based on the TISS database [11], an ANS information exchange standard. This database includes information on patient demographics and diagnostics, the types and quantities of procedures, and healthcare providers’ charges to health operators. Data were retrieved from 2018 to 2022.

Direct costs were segmented into total ASCVD costs by year, average cost per hospitalization, and ASCVD type (IHD, IS, or PAD).

Indirect costs

Two types of indirect costs related to ASCVD were examined in the working age range (14–64 years old): lost productivity due to morbidity (absenteeism) and premature death. This working age range has been defined by the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics (IBGE) in its Continuous National Household Sample Survey (PNAD) [12].

Productivity loss due to absenteeism was estimated by multiplying the hospitalizations per year for the specified ICD-10 codes, in both the SUS and ANS healthcare systems, by the average monthly wage in Brazil (BRL 3,113.00 in the second half of 2024), and by an average work absence period of 3.4 months [14, 16]. This period (3.4 months [< 1–11]) is based on the average time it takes for patients with acute coronary syndrome to return to work, serving as a proxy for ASCVD.

Mortality indirect costs were based on the 2022 projected life expectancy from the Brazilian Mortality Tables provided by the IBGE [13]. Deaths caused by cardiovascular disease were evaluated by the patient’s age, considering data from the Mortality Information System (SIM) death certificates. This centralized database has gathered information from all Brazilian territories since 1975.

The cost of productivity loss due to premature death was estimated by multiplying the productive years lost (64 minus the patient’s age at death) by the average annual salary in Brazil in the second quarter of 2024 (BRL 37,356.00), using a 5% discount rate [12]. This same methodology was used in other studies assessing Brazil’s cardiovascular disease burden [14].

The average monthly/annual wage and the work absence period were considered linear for all patients, regardless of sex, age group, or occupation.

Social security benefits

Social security benefits paid by the Brazilian government for temporary and permanent leave were extracted from InfoLogo [15], a public system administered by the Brazilian Ministry of Social Security that aggregates all government benefits paid to the Brazilian population. The benefits paid by each ASCVD disease group were analyzed by year, covering the period from 2017 to 2022, the latest year for which data were available.

Projections

Based on historical data from the various data sources used in this analysis, ASCVD costs were projected for the next five years (2025–2029). Costs were forecasted using historical data within the Error, Trend, and Seasonality (ETS) method, exponential smoothing, and Holt’s model. ETS/Holt is a state-space exponential smoothing family designed for non-stationary series with evolving levels and damped trends, and is recommended for medium-term projections when structural changes may have occurred (e.g., due to pandemics), making it suitable for series affected by one-off shocks [16]. The 5-year forecast presents nominal values, not inflation-adjusted values. Forecasting was performed in R version 4.4.1 using the forecast package [17, 18].

Discussion

Atherosclerotic cardiovascular diseases (IHD, IS, and PAD) represent a significant economic burden in Brazil, totaling a cost of approximately BRL 10 billion per year (approximately 4 billion international dollars, considering a purchasing parity power – PPP - factor of 2.52 in 2024) [19]. Indirect medical costs, including mortality-related and absenteeism-related costs, primarily drive the costs.

Projections from this analysis indicate that direct medical costs are substantial and growing, particularly within SUS, which covers much of the Brazilian population. The direct costs are higher in ANS than in SUS, with IHD incurring the highest projected costs and PAD the lowest. The breakdown by disease type shows a marked dominance of IHD in both direct medical costs and hospital admissions.

Stevens et al. (2018) conducted a similar study to assess the economic burden of heart conditions in Brazil from a societal perspective. Costs associated with the management of hypertension, heart failure, myocardial infarction, and atrial fibrillation were calculated. The total cost estimated for 2015 was BRL 56 billion (approximately 22 billion international dollars, using a PPP factor of 2.52), and health system costs accounted for 63% of this amount. Myocardial infarction was responsible for the highest amount [9]. Despite the different conditions assessed —IHD, IS, and PAD in our analysis, and hypertension, heart failure, myocardial infarction, and atrial fibrillation in the former study —it is possible to highlight an increase in costs.

Gheorghe et al. (2018) systematically reviewed the literature to understand the economic burden of cardiovascular diseases and hypertension in low- and middle-income countries. Stroke and coronary heart disease imposed the highest direct costs when compared to hypertension, heart failure, and other cardiovascular diseases. An important variation in estimated cost was observed. For example, the ratio of annual care costs to country-specific total health expenditure per capita ranges from 4.48 to 472.48 international dollars [20]. These results highlight a knowledge gap regarding the burden of cardiovascular disease in these countries, and further analysis is needed to identify initiatives to reduce it.

This analysis projected an increase in costs related to cardiovascular disease management for the 2025–2029 period. Despite substantial differences in values, a significant increase in the cost of cardiovascular disease is also expected in the United States. For cardiovascular conditions, the United States’ annual healthcare costs are projected to nearly quadruple, from 393 billion USD to 1,490 billion USD, between 2020 and 2050 [21]. In the United States, stroke is projected to account for the largest absolute cost increase, unlike in Brazil, where IHD was the most significant contributor. Further studies are needed to understand the reasons for the increase in burden in each disease.

Furthermore, social security benefits paid to individuals with atherosclerotic disease amount to approximately BRL 100 million annually. This represents another dimension of the indirect costs to the Brazilian economy. In 2022, the total amount paid by the Brazilian social security system was BRL 3,728,378,420.24. Diseases of the circulatory system accounted for 6% of this total, totaling BRL 222,170,309.50. In the European Union (EU), cardiovascular diseases cost an estimated €155 billion to the EU’s health and social care systems, representing 11% of total healthcare expenditure in 2021 [22].

The implications of high mortality rates among the economically active age group due to atherosclerotic diseases have a broader socioeconomic impact, including the loss of a productive workforce. Projected death-related indirect costs are significant, mainly because approximately 30% of deaths from atherosclerotic diseases occur in the economically active age group (14–64 years old), contributing to nearly BRL 4 billion annually in indirect costs. This is comparable to the projected direct medical costs.

Moreover, projected absenteeism-related costs are much lower than death-related costs, which are still notable (about BRL 180 million). It is expected to increase expenses by 54% [21].

The economic burden of atherosclerotic diseases in Brazil is expected to continue growing, driven by rising medical costs and the high number of hospital admissions. There is an ongoing trend in which IHD remains the most significant contributor to direct and indirect costs, underscoring the need for targeted interventions.

This study has some limitations. Our estimates rely on reimbursement-oriented administrative records that do not provide detailed clinical information about the hierarchy of diagnoses. While secondary-diagnosis fields are present, completion is sparse and heterogeneous, potentially introducing misclassification. We therefore used a conservative approach, relying only on the principal diagnosis. Also, as administrative databases, hospitalization data from DATASUS and TISS may be underreported, as some public institutions that don’t rely exclusively on government funding may not report every hospitalization to DATASUS. According to the TISS database, reporting is mandatory only for healthcare operators that meet criteria defined by ANS. Thus, the economic burden of ASCVD may be underreported. Another limitation is that the analysis relies on data from various sources with different periods of availability for each (SUS data from 2008 onwards and ANS data from 2015 to 2022), and the lack of consistent data across both systems and the absence of data from 2023 for ANS may introduce some bias or discrepancies in the analysis. Additionally, the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on healthcare utilization, particularly in SUS data from 2020 to 2021, may affect the accuracy and comparability of hospitalization and cost trends, which can be partially avoided by using an appropriate forecasting methodology. Indirect-cost estimates deliberately adopt a parsimonious, national-average specification. We valued productivity losses using Brazil’s average monthly wage (applied linearly, without stratification by age, sex, or occupation) and a constant absenteeism duration of 3.4 months across ASCVD conditions; given the predominance of coronary events in our dataset, evidence from acute coronary syndrome provides a reasonable proxy for time away from work. We also applied for a standard discount rate. Introducing age/sex/occupation-specific wages or labor-force participation, although possible, would substantially increase modeling assumptions with limited incremental insight for national totals. While the study focuses on direct and indirect costs, intangible costs, such as pain, suffering, and reduced quality of life, are omitted. These are essential components of the total economic burden of CVDs and could provide a more comprehensive understanding of the disease’s societal impact. The data on social security benefits covers only 2017 to 2022, which may only partially capture long-term trends or the impact of more recent policy changes. Additionally, categorizing benefits by disease may not capture all relevant aspects of the social costs associated with CVDs, such as disability benefits or long-term care costs.

Conclusion

In conclusion, atherosclerotic diseases represent a significant and growing economic burden in Brazil, with annual costs nearing BRL 10 billion and projections indicating they will reach BRL 60 billion over the next five years. Direct medical costs, particularly in the public healthcare system (SUS), dominate the financial impact, while indirect costs related to premature death and productivity losses are equally substantial. The high mortality among economically active individuals further exacerbates the societal and economic costs. The disparity in costs between public and private healthcare systems underscores the need for sustainable health policies. Data suggests the need to implement preventive measures to reduce the number of events and alleviate strain on both the healthcare system and patients. Addressing both direct and indirect financial burdens is critical to ensuring the sustainability of Brazil’s healthcare system.
