Multiple bursting patterns in lateral habenula neurons: Experiments and computational model
Authors: Dmitry Fedorov, Fabien Campillo, Mathieu Desroches, Edgar Soria‐Gómez, Serafim Rodrigues, Joaquin Piriz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe lateral habenula (LHb) is a small brain structure specialized in encoding aversive signals. Elevated bursting in the LHb has been linked to depressive‐like behaviours in animal models. Bursting is a complex dynamic process that has been extensively studied and modelled in neuronal contexts. However, in the LHb, bursting activity has typically been described only as transient periods of high‐frequency firing. Here, to provide a deeper understanding of LHb bursting, we analysed it from the perspective of dynamical systems. Ex vivo, LHb neurons display a variety of bursting patterns, characterized at one extreme by square wave‐like bursts and at the other by parabolic bursts, plus transitional forms referred to as triangular bursts. Notably, these bursting patterns, which reflect different LHb output modes, can occur within the same neuron, reflecting distinct dynamic states. In particular, membrane hyperpolarization selectively promotes square‐wave‐type bursts, rather than triangular or parabolic ones. To capture these complex behaviours, we propose an idealized multiple‐timescale dynamical model. This model successfully reproduces the three main bursting patterns observed in experimental data. Furthermore, we identify a special point in the parameter space, termed the saddle‐node homoclinic bifurcation, which acts as an organizing centre demarcating the boundary between the two primary bursting patterns and around which the third pattern appears. Our model suggests that LHb bursting activity is structured around distinct dynamic states with potentially diverse and unexplored impacts on mood regulation. By providing new insights into the principles underlying LHb bursting, this framework may advance our understanding of its biological significance. Key points Bursting activity of the brain nucleus of the lateral habenula has been linked to depressive states.Using brain slice experiments, we identified three key burst types: square wave‐like, parabolic and intermediate ‘triangular’ patterns.These patterns likely represent different functional modes of the same neuron rather than different neuron types.A mathematical model was developed that replicates these patterns and reveals a critical transition point that organizes their dynamics.This framework offers new insight into how LHb bursting activity is organized and could potentially guide future treatments for depression.

Introduction

The lateral habenula (LHb) is a highly conserved structure in the vertebrate brain that serves as a crucial communication hub between the limbic system and the midbrain. Hyperactivity of the LHb has been implicated in several psychiatric disorders characterized by heightened aversion, particularly depression (Hu et al., 2020). Indeed, the LHb has been proposed as a key brain target underlying the antidepressant effects of ketamine (Chen et al., 2024). Additionally, its direct modulation through deep‐brain stimulation is currently being explored as a potential treatment for depression (Abraham et al., 2023).

The LHb is predominantly composed of small glutamatergic neurons with high input resistance (>500 MΩ) that receive both glutamatergic and GABAergic inputs. Ex vivo, these neurons exhibit complex patterns of spontaneous spiking activity, which have been qualitatively categorized into four modes: silent, non‐regular tonic spiking, regular tonic spiking and bursting (Weiss & Veh, 2011). However, LHb neurons can transition between these patterns, suggesting that these modes are not associated with different neuronal classes but with specific dynamic states.

Among these activity modes, silent and tonic patterns are the most prominent, while only a minority of neurons display spontaneous bursting activity. However, the majority (≈90%) of LHb neurons exhibit rebound bursting after negative current injection, indicating that bursting is a general property, although largely latent, of LHb neurons (Weiss & Veh, 2011).

LHb bursting activity has attracted significant attention due to its association with depression‐like states in animal models, where the proportion of LHb neurons exhibiting bursting increases. Moreover, experimentally inducing bursting can reproduce depressive‐like behaviours, whereas pharmacological agents that suppress bursting, most notably ketamine, have been shown to counteract these effects (Cui et al., 2018; Seo et al., 2018; Cerniauskas et al., 2019; Chen et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2019; Ma et al., 2024; Mondoloni et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2018). However, the specific reasons why LHb bursting activity is linked to depression‐like states remain unclear.

Bursting activity is characterized by a temporal alternation between periods of high‐frequency firing and quiescence. This implies a temporal arrangement of firing, but not necessarily an increase in overall level. Thus, from a functional perspective, it is not immediately apparent why LHb bursting activity contributes to depressive states. Moreover, LHb bursting activity has been primarily described in broad terms as periods of heightened firing (Chen et al., 2024; Cui et al., 2018; Ma et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2018), contrasting with the detailed analyses and classifications that have been developed for this pattern of activity in other neural contexts (Desroches et al., 2022; Izhikevich, 2000). To fill this gap, in this article we studied and classified bursting activity in the LHb from the perspective of dynamical systems. Moreover, we present an initial phenomenological model to describe bursting activity of LHb neurons.

From a neurodynamics perspective, we observed a gradient of bursting patterns ranging from square wave‐like to parabolic bursts, as well as intermediate forms referred to as triangular. Neurons could transition between these bursting modes, with square wave‐like bursting being favoured by a hyperpolarized membrane potential. To account for this diversity of dynamical behaviours, we propose a minimal phenomenological model capable of capturing them. The model possesses an organizing centre in parameter space, enabling transitions between different behaviours driven by the two main slow processes at play in the system. We further conjecture that these bursting patterns arise from distinct sets of conductances, representing different modes of information transmission by LHb neurons, each with potentially unique implications on depressive‐like behaviours.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

All procedures were approved by the Ethics Committees of the University of the Basque Country EHU/UPV (Leioa, Spain; CEEA M20/2024/213) and comply with European Directive 2010/63/EU and the Journal of Physiology policies regarding animal experiments.

Animals

Experiments were conducted on seven female and 17 male (6–9 months old) C57BL/6JRj mice (Janvier Labs, France). Animals were kept at the animal facility of the University of the Basque Country, Bizkaia Campus, under controlled temperature and humidity conditions with a 12/12 h dark–light cycle. Food and drinking water were provided ad libitum.

Electrophysiology

Preparation of brain slices

Mice were placed in an induction chamber, anaesthetized with isoflurane (IsoFlo, Zoetis; 5% in 0.8 l/min oxygen) and quickly decapitated into chilled and gassed NMDG‐based artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF) of the following composition (in mM) (Ting et al., 2018): 2.5 KCl, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 30 NaHCO3, 25 glucose, 20 Hepes, 5 Na‐ascorbate, 2 thiourea, 3 Na‐pyruvate, 92 NMDG, 10 MgSO4, 0.5 CaCl2). The brain was extracted and sliced on a vibratome (VT1000S or VT1200S, Leica) in the same solution to a thickness of 250 µm. The slices were then recovered in 36°C NMDG‐ACSF solution for 40 min, with gradual introduction of Na+ into the solution, following the Na+ spike‐in procedures described by Ting et al. (2018). The slices were then transferred to room temperature holding‐ACSF (same as NMDG‐ACSF, with 92 mM NaCl instead of NMDG, 2 mM MgSO4, 2 mM CaCl2) storage solution for the day.

Patch‐clamp recordings

Patch‐clamp experiments were carried out with CleverExplore Neuroscience Workstation (MCI Neuroscience), including 10×air (CP‐Achromatic, Zeiss) and 40×water‐immersion (LUMPlanFL, Nikon) objectives, DIC optics, digital camera (MCI‐CXE‐B013‐U) and EPC‐10 USB Double amplifier (HEKA Electronics) using Patchmaster v. 2×92. The recording ACSF contained (in mM): 2.5 KCl, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 25 NaHCO3, 17 glucose, 125 NaCl, 2 CaCl2 and 1 MgCl2, feeding at a rate of 2.5 ml/min. The recordings were made at the average temperature of 29.5 ± 1.5°C. The internal solution of glass pipettes (GB150F‐10, Science Products, 5.5−9 MΩ) contained (in mM): 130 K‐gluconate, 5 KCl, 10 Hepes, 0.6 EGTA, 2.5 MgCl2, 10 phosphocreatine, 4 ATP–Mg and 0.4 GTP–Na3.

Electrophysiological recordings were performed in whole‐cell mode. After membrane breaking‐in, cells were left to stabilize without current injection for 3 min. After this period, the spontaneous activity was recorded for at least 1 min in the current‐clamp configuration. Following that, additional current‐clamp recordings were carried out, during which the neuron responses to injected current were evaluated. The current was injected in 11 steps, with the initial step bringing the cell to approximately −80 mV and the final step to approximately −40 mV. The protocol consisted of a 0.5 s pre‐step period, followed by a 1 s current step and a 10 s post‐step period, with no interval between successive steps. Thus, the start‐to‐start interval between current steps was 11.5 s. Rebound bursting and sustained bursting afterwards were analysed for each hyperpolarizing current step. In addition, in some cells in which bursting was observed within the hyperpolarizing step, an additional protocol with 10 s long hyperpolarizing steps was applied to increase yield. The analysis of in‐step bursts was restricted to bursts occurring during both standard and extended current steps that brought the membrane potential to approximately −80 mV. Cells with membrane potential under −40 mV were excluded from analysis. The collected data were converted to ABF format and analysed using pClamp 10.7 (Molecular Devices) and Matlab (MathWorks).

Analysis

Spike detection

Spike detection was carried out using the Clampfit software with threshold detection at 0 mV.

Burst detection and classification

Bursts were detected using a simplified version of the Poisson Surprise Method (Legendy & Salcman, 1985) that defines bursts as unexpectedly short inter‐spike intervals (ISIs) considering the average spiking frequency.

Assuming a Poisson distribution we calculated cumulative probability of observing one event for each ISI using the following formula: (1) P=e−λ1+λ, where λ is the product of the mean firing rate (in Hz) and the ISI (in seconds). Hence, the surprise index S was calculated as: (2) S=−logP.

To mark the beginning of the burst, we set a threshold of P ≤ 0.005 (S = 2.3). Since bursts are defined by an abrupt increase in spiking frequency that attenuates, we set a lower threshold to define the end of a burst at twice the basal frequency (S = 1.045). We limited our analysis to bursts with five or more spikes (four or more ISIs) in all cases.

Inspection of the data hints at the presence of square‐wave‐like (square), triangular and parabolic burst types. These burst types are differentiated by two factors: square bursts have a raised baseline in comparison to quiescent inter‐burst epochs (plateau), and feature a monotonic decrease in the spiking frequency (i.e. shortest ISI at the beginning and monotonically increasing along the burst; Fig. 1A, B ; Izhikevich, 2000). Parabolic bursts are characterized by the absence of a plateau and an increase in spiking frequency towards the middle of the burst (Fig. 1E, F ) (Izhikevich, 2000). Triangular bursts exhibit mixed features, lacking a plateau and displaying intra‐burst frequency patterns that span a continuum between monotonically decreasing and parabolic profiles (Fig. 1C, D ). Hence, to describe these burst features, we selected three parameters. First, we estimated the presence of a burst plateau, measuring the difference between the burst minimum and the baseline from 300 to 30 ms before the first spike (Δ from baseline, in mV, Fig. 1A, B ). Any action potentials found within that window were manually excluded. In addition, to evaluate the monotonic decrease in the spiking frequency, we computed two parameters. First, the relative position (in percentage) of the smallest ISI within the burst (Fig. 1) and second, the goodness of the fit (R 2) of the burst ISIs to a single exponential function (Fig. 1B, D, F ): (3) ISIduration=Y0eKx, where x is the index of the ISI within the burst.

Post hyperpolarization bursts were evaluated after a 1–20 s long hyperpolarizing current steps. After‐step bursts were separated into two categories. Immediately after‐step bursts were considered rebound bursts, while following bursts were considered oscillatory bursting activity. The first burst of this latter type was analysed. Membrane potential baseline for the rebound and oscillatory bursts was estimated from the 0.5 s pre‐step period.

Statistics

Statistical analysis was carried out using RStudio v.2023.12.0 Build 369. Each neuron was considered an individual observation depending on the analysis performed as explained below. Values are reported as means ± standard deviation.

The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to determine data normality, Levene's test was used to determine homogeneity of variance. For comparisons, in case of normally distributed samples with equal variance, a two‐sample unpaired t test was used. In the event of one or more variables being non‐normally distributed, a two‐sample Wilcoxon test was used. To test whether minimum burst values were significantly higher than zero in individual cells a one‐sample, one‐tail t test was implemented. To compare baseline burst values between burst groups (Fig. 6), given the unequal number of bursts per cell, we implemented a linear mixed‐effects approach. We fitted a linear mixed‐effects model using the lmer function (lme4 package, (Kuznetsova et al., 2017)). The model included R 2 category (High vs. Low) and Plateau status (Yes vs. No) as fixed effects, along with their interaction, and the cell from which each burst was recorded as a random intercept to account for repeated measurements within cells. Statistical significance of fixed effects was evaluated using Type III ANOVA with Satterthwaite's approximation for degrees of freedom (lmerTest package).

Computational model

Based on the experimental data, we developed a model reflecting the presence of two edge types of bursting patterns together with a third one that is intermediate between the other two. The right panels of Fig. 2 show the outputs of the model corresponding to each of these three bursting patterns, namely square (A2), parabolic (B2) and mixed‐type or triangular (C2); the left panels (A1, B1, C1) show recordings displaying the three different bursting behaviours. To identify these different bursting patterns in the computational model, we follow the standard approach introduced by J. Rinzel (Rinzel, 1986), termed slow‐fast dissection, and apply it to an extended version of a classical three‐dimensional phenomenological bursting model called the Hindmarsh‐Rose (HR) model (Hindmarsh & Rose, 1997). The initial model from (Hindmarsh & Rose, 1997) has two fast variables, which can be associated with membrane potential and fast ionic dynamics, as well as one slow variable associated with slow ionic dynamics. We extend the model by adding a second slow variable, which can be related to an additional slow current, in order to capture parabolic bursting (Desroches et al., 2016; Rinzel, 1986).

Experimental data versus mathematical model (1)

Left panels represent experimental data, right panels show model (1) output. Rows show three bursting patterns identified in lateral habenula neurons and captured by our idealized model. Namely: (A1–A2) square bursting; (B1–B2) parabolic bursting; (C1–C2) triangular (mixed) bursting, at the transition between the other two patterns (see also Fig. 8). Fixed parameter values are: a = 0.08, c = 3, d = 1.8, ε = 0.01, s = 0.01, x 1 = −3. Case‐specific parameter values are: (A2) b 0 = 0.52, z 0 = −0.7163, α = −0.1; (B2) b 0 = 1.5, z 0 = −1.75, α = −0.1; (C2) b 0 = 0.75, z 0 = −2, α = 0.003.

In the case of models with two slow variables, the slow‐fast dissection method consists of relating the bursting pattern produced by the complete model, projected onto a plane formed by the slow variables, with the two‐parameter bifurcation diagram of the so‐called fast subsystem obtained when freezing the dynamics of the slow variables and considering them as parameters in the fast differential equations. That particular bifurcation structure is the signature of the corresponding patterns; it allows its characterization and, hence, enables bursting patterns to be classified (Desroches et al., 2022; Izhikevich, 2000; Rinzel, 1986). More precisely, one only needs two bifurcations of the fast subsystem in order to fully characterize a given bursting pattern; namely, one bifurcation that organizes the transition between quiescence and bursting in the complete model, and another bifurcation organizing the reverse transition.

According to the classification system introduced by Rinzel, square bursting is associated with a saddle‐node bifurcation of equilibria, initiating the burst, and a saddle‐homoclinic bifurcation terminating it. Note that this bursting pattern requires only one slow variable, but it can also be obtained in a model with two slow variables. In contrast, parabolic bursting requires minimally two slow variables, and it corresponds to a bursting case in which both the initiation and termination of the burst in the full system are caused by the same bifurcation in the fast subsystem; namely, a saddle‐node on invariant circle (SNIC) bifurcation. To be more precise, parabolic bursting is associated with a family of SNIC bifurcations in the fast subsystem, depending on two parameters. Hence, the full system has minimally two fast and two slow variables, unlike minimal square bursting models, which have only one slow variable. Therefore, for the original three‐dimensional HR model (with one slow variable) to sustain parabolic bursting activity, we need to augment it by one differential equation, which will correspond to an additional slow ionic variable. As mentioned above, such a four‐dimensional model can also sustain square bursting patterns, single saddle‐node and homoclinic bifurcations of the fast subsystem being replaced by curves of such bifurcations.

Once we have a common computational model for both bursting patterns, the transition from one pattern to the other is organized in parameter space by a codimension‐2 bifurcation point; namely, a saddle‐node homoclinic (SNH) point, also referred to as saddle‐node separatrix loop bifurcation (Izhikevich, 2000), in the vicinity of which one can generate bursting patterns of parabolic and square types, respectively. At the boundary between these two bursting regimes, that is, in the vicinity of the SNH point in the plane of slow variables, one can obtain an intermediate bursting pattern, termed triangular or mixed‐type, whose output looks like square bursting for the initiation of the burst, and like parabolic bursting for the termination of the burst. The quiescent phase of mixed‐type bursting trajectories has intermediate characteristics between the other two patterns; see Section 3 and Fig. 8 for details.

Deterministic and stochastic versions of the model's equations

The extended HR model used in the present work is composed of a set of four nonlinear differential equations, and it is deterministic. Its equations read: (4) x˙=cx−x3/3−y+z+2, (5) y˙=x2+dx−by+a/c, (6) z˙=ε−sx−x1−b+b0, (7) b˙=εz−z0+αx, the last two equations corresponding to slow dynamics on (z,b) as a phenomenological way to include voltage‐gated slow channel dynamics. The parameter values are fixed in all three bursting cases, except for parameters b 0, z 0 and α, which allow us to switch between cases; see the caption of Fig. 2.

We also consider a stochastic version of System (1) by adding noise terms to the two slow equations, as an elementary way of mimicking channel noise. The resulting system of stochastic differential equations reads: (8) X˙t=cXt−Xt3/3−Yt+Zt+2, (9) Y˙t=Xt2+dXt−bYt+a/c, (10) dZt=ε−sXt−x1−Bt+b0dt+σ1dWt1, (11) dBt=εZt−z0+αXtdt+σ2dWt2, where Wt 1 and Wt 2 are two independent and standard Brownian motions, also independent from the initial conditions of the system.

Results

Experimental results

To characterize the bursting activity of LHb neurons we performed patch‐clamp recordings in current‐clamp configuration of 61 LHb neurons from a total of 24 animals (using two slices from each animal on average), of which 41 neurons showed spontaneous spiking activity. Following previous publications, we classified their activity according to the spiking patterns into regular, non‐regular, and bursting (Wagner et al., 2017). To identify and define bursts, we followed a statistical criterion based on the Poisson Surprise Method (see the section Burst Detection and Classification in Methods). Using this criterion and in agreement with previous reports (Cerniauskas et al., 2019; Cui et al., 2018; Mondoloni et al., 2024), only 15 cells spontaneously displayed bursting activity (Fig. 3A ).

In addition, the mean spiking frequency (excluding silent cells) was not significantly different between bursting and non‐bursting neurons (2.87 ± 2.19 Hz vs. 5.42 ± 4.39 Hz for bursting and non‐bursting cells, P = 0.067 Wilcox's test, Fig. 3F , N bursting = 15, N tonic/irregular = 26), supporting the notion that at the LHb, bursting activity does not represent an increase in the average output.

Visual inspection of spontaneous bursts revealed the presence of different types of bursts, including square, parabolic and triangular. Square and parabolic bursts are distinguished by their membrane potential dynamics and the evolution of spiking frequency within the burst. Square bursts are characterized by a sustained depolarized plateau and a monotonic decrease in spiking frequency (Fig. 1A ), whereas parabolic bursts lack a plateau and exhibit a peak in spiking frequency near the middle of the burst (Fig. 1C ). Triangular bursts represent a transitional form, lacking a clear plateau and displaying an intra‐burst frequency profile that bridges the properties of square and parabolic bursts.

To differentiate between those kinds of bursts, we calculated three parameters from the 809 bursts recorded in 15 spontaneously bursting neurons: the minimum voltage within the burst, the goodness of fit of the ISIs to an exponential decay (R 2), and the relative position of the minimal ISI within the burst (see Methods). Fig. 4 displays these results for all bursts and individualized by cell.

This population of events, characterized by features transitioning between parabolic and square‐type bursts, represents the triangular burst type (exemplified in Fig. 4B3 ).

Finally, bursts located in the left part of the upper half of Fig. 4 exhibited a plateau and had their minimal ISIs at the beginning of the burst, but did not fit well to a single exponential decay. These bursts represent more complex dynamics that do not clearly fit into any of the three defined categories (see Fig. 4B4 for an example). Included in this population, we observed a prominent presence of long bursts characterized by square‐like exponential decreases in spiking frequency at both burst beginning and end, and a prolonged period of tonic or irregular spiking in the middle (Fig. 4B4, B5 ). This unusual structure led to variable R 2 values, making them not visually identifiable in Fig. 4.

The majority of spontaneously bursting cells exhibited more than one burst type, indicating that different bursting patterns can be generated by the same LHb neuron. At the same time, bursts from individual cells tend to cluster preferentially on either the upper or lower half of the graph (Fig. 4, individual panels). This observation suggests that cell‐specific properties may influence the relative propensity of a cell to fire Plateau bursts or No‐Plateau bursts.

To examine that possibility, we classified bursting cells into two groups: those that, on average, produce bursts with a plateau (mean burst minimum > 0 mV), and those that do not (Fig. 5A ). We then compared membrane potential, membrane resistance, and capacitance between these groups (Fig. 5B ). No statistically significant differences were found for any of these parameters (membrane potential, P = 0.279; membrane resistance, P = 0.057; membrane capacitance P = 0.309; t test; N Plateau‐bursts cells = 7, N No‐Plateau bursts cells = 5).

This result is also evident in individual cells that display bursts both with and without a plateau (Fig. 6A bottom inset).

Remarkably, absolute burst minimum values display the opposite profile, being higher in Plateau bursts (High R 2 Plateau bursts: –59.91 ± 2.99 mV; High R2 No‐Plateau bursts: –66.17 ± 4.58 mV; Low R 2 Plateau bursts: –59.16 ± 2.51 mV; Low R 2 No‐Plateau bursts: –68.06 ± 4.53; Type III ANOVA: plateau factor, P < 0.001; R 2 factor, P < 0.001; Plateau × R 2 interaction, P = 0.013). This opposite trend rules out a potentially spurious mathematical artefact created by the variable used to estimate plateau magnitude (difference between the baseline and minimum voltage value in the burst), which would be expected to follow the same distribution if burst minima were constant across groups.

This observation suggests that Plateau bursts, but not other kinds of burst, are favoured by dynamic changes in membrane potential towards hyperpolarized values. Indeed, in cells showing both Plateau and No‐Plateau bursts, transitions between these classes follow a clear temporal trajectory; once a transition occurs from Plateau bursts to No‐Plateau bursts, or vice versa, subsequent bursts tend to retain that feature (Fig. 6B , Plateau bursting cells). Since most cells preferentially exhibited one type of burst, these transitions are represented by a few bursts in cells 9, 24 and 29, but this process is also clear in cell 8. In that cell, bursts transition from No‐Plateau (yellow dots) to square‐like Plateau bursts (green dots) and later to a final period of predominantly No‐Plateau bursts (blue dots). In contrast, in cells that exhibit No‐Plateau bursts distributed along the R 2 axis, there is no temporal clustering at specific R 2 values; instead, they present a tangled, disorganized sequence of bursts (Fig. 6B , Pure No‐Plateau bursting cells).

To further test whether Plateau bursting is favoured by hyperpolarization, we examined spontaneously occurring bursts while cells were hyperpolarized to ≈−80 mV. Twelve cells generated bursts under this condition. Supporting our hypothesis, in all but one cases the bursts during the hyperpolarizing step displayed a plateau, along, in most instances, with high R 2 (triangles in Fig. 7A1 ).

At the end of the current steps, most neurons (44/61) exhibited rebound spiking activity (crosses in Fig. 7A2 ). This activity represents the transition between an artificially imposed and an internally generated state, which may not be informative of intrinsic cellular dynamics. Nevertheless, 11 cells produced sustained bursting activity following rebound spiking, which is a purely internally generated activity that reflects recent hyperpolarization history. Consequently, we also analysed the second burst following the termination of the hyperpolarizing current pulse (circles in Fig. 7A3 ). Also in this case, a predominance of square‑wave bursts was observed, along with some triangular bursts, and, most notably, a clear absence of No‐Plateau, low R 2 bursts at the transition from square‑wave to triangular to parabolic bursting activity.

Overall, these observations suggest that distinct forms of bursting are associated with dynamic states. Hyperpolarized membrane potentials would favour square and other related Plateau bursts, but not parabolic or transitional triangular bursts.

Indeed, from the neurodynamics perspective, square and parabolic bursts represent different phenomena. Since individual LHb neurons could display those two patterns of activity and/or the intermediate triangular bursts, in the next section we develop a model that could account for that variety of behaviours.

Modelling results

System (1) has explicit timescale separation with two fast variables x and y, which mimic membrane potential and fast channel dynamics, respectively, and two slow variables z and b, which mimic slow channel dynamics. Standard slow‐fast analysis consists in setting ε = 0 in System (1), which yields the so‐called fast subsystem. In the fast subsystem, the slow variables become parameters, and the main interest in studying the fast subsystem comes through its bifurcation structure with respect to these distinguished parameters. Indeed, when reinstating ε = 0, one realizes through numerical simulations (and can prove mathematically) that the full system dynamics most of the time remain close to families of attractors (stationary or periodic) of the fast subsystem. When the system contains two slow variables, and hence the fast subsystem two distinguished parameters, the corresponding bifurcation diagram of interest is naturally a two‐parameter bifurcation diagram, onto which it is useful and customary to superimpose solutions from the full system, projected onto this diagram; see Fig. 8. This approach is particularly suited to studying bursting dynamics in the full system, in which case the projection onto the plane of slow variables typically shows a slow path flowing across bifurcation curves of the fast subsystem. In particular, bifurcation of equilibria, for example, saddle‐node (SN) bifurcations and Hopf (HB) bifurcations, and bifurcation of limit cycles, for example, saddle‐homoclinic (Hom) bifurcations and SNIC bifurcations.

Two‐parameter bifurcation diagram of the model's fast subsystems

The panel explains the state when slow variables’ dynamics are frozen and replaced by parameters, the two main bursting patterns found, as well as the intermediate one. The red curve corresponds to a family of saddle‐node bifurcations of equilibria labelled SN, the black curve corresponds to a family of homoclinic bifurcations, and the blue curve to a family of Hopf bifurcations. The first two curves meet at a codimension‐2 bifurcation point called saddle‐node homoclinic and labelled SNH. Above that point, in the two‐parameter plane, the two curves coincide and correspond to a family of saddle‐node on an invariant circle (labelled SNIC) bifurcation. Below SNH, the black curve corresponds to a family of saddle‐homoclinic bifurcations labelled Hom. Round‐shaped curves correspond to the slow evolution of parameters z and b. When the values of b stay below the SNH point, then the bursting pattern observed in the full system is of square type and corresponds to the profile A2 from Fig. 2. In contrast, if the value of b is located above the SNH point, then the observed bursting pattern in the full system is of parabolic type and it corresponds to the profile B2 of Fig. 2. At the transition from one pattern to the other, we have trajectories of the full system that revolve around the SNH point, giving triangular (or mixed) bursting orbits like the one shown here, which corresponds to the profile C2 of Fig. 2.

In Fig. 8, on top of the two‐parameter bifurcation diagram System (1)’s fast subsystem with respect to z and b, are superimposed the three bursting trajectories shown in Fig. 2A2, B2 and C2 , respectively, projected onto the (z, b) plane. As explained above, this plotting strategy allows us to shed important light onto the different bursting patterns depending on the region of parameter space visited by the slow dynamics. In the present case, the pattern corresponding to Panel A2 in Fig. 2 is of parabolic bursting type, the pattern corresponding to Panel B2 is of square type, and the intermediate pattern corresponding to Panel C2 is of mixed type, and indeed it revolves around the SNH point. All three patterns can be associated with the crossings of different bifurcation curves of the fast subsystem. Namely, parabolic bursting occurs when the slow variables drive the dynamics back and forth across a curve of SNIC bifurcations (black curve above the SNH point on Fig. 8), while square bursting is the result of a slow passage through a curve of saddle‐node bifurcations of equilibria (red curve) and a curve of saddle‐homoclinic bifurcations (black curve below the SNH point). The mixed‐type (triangular) bursting pattern corresponds to an initiation of the burst as the trajectory crosses the SN curve, and a termination of the burst as it crosses the SNIC curve, hence giving a mix of both main patterns. Square and parabolic bursting patterns dominate and, loosely speaking, they correspond to cases where the slow variables evolve below and above, respectively, the SNH point; see the dashed line in Fig. 8. In contrast, the mixed‐type bursting pattern is obtained from trajectories whose slow dynamics revolve around the SNH point.

The effect of noise on this two‐parameter structure allows the trajectories to visit different space points following a slow random path. This can produce time series where different bursting patterns are successively present, reproducing the behaviour observed in LHb neurons. An example of this is illustrated in Fig. 9, where a simulation of noise including trajectories displayed triangular, parabolic and square bursts (light blue, orange and green shaded areas, respectively).

Discussion

In recent decades, the LHb has been increasingly recognized as a central hub for the neural processing of aversive stimuli (Hu et al., 2020; Michel et al., 2024; Proulx et al., 2014). This conceptualization is supported by three observations consistently reported across models. First, the LHb exhibits systematic excitation in response to aversive stimuli. Second, its activity increases in states characterized by heightened aversiveness, such as stress or drug withdrawal. Third, its activation is sufficient to trigger aversion (Hu et al., 2020).

Physiologically, the LHb is characterized by low intraconnectivity and a predominance of glutamatergic neurons. Thus, its output is primarily shaped by the intrinsic properties of individual neurons and their synaptic interactions rather than by intra‐LHb interactions (Groos & Helmchen, 2024).

Both in vivo and ex vivo, LHb neurons exhibit high basal activity and a diverse spiking repertoire, which can be classified as silent, tonic, irregular or bursting (Weiss & Veh, 2011). Surprisingly, no consistent relationship has been found between these spiking patterns and anatomical localization, morphology or gene expression (Hashikawa et al., 2020; Weiss & Veh, 2011). In fact, most LHb neurons can express all four spiking modes depending on membrane potential (Cui et al., 2018; Weiss & Veh, 2011). Bursting activity, in particular, is promoted by membrane hyperpolarization (Cui et al., 2018; Weiss & Veh, 2011). Accordingly, we found spontaneously bursting LHb neurons to be more hyperpolarized than silent or tonically active neurons.

Ex vivo, only a minority of LHb neurons exhibit spontaneous bursting activity. However, animal exposure to chronic stressors persistently increases the proportion of bursting neurons. This enhancement in LHb bursting has been specifically linked to the emergence of depressive‐like phenotypes in mice (Cui et al., 2018; Seo et al., 2018, 2019; Cerniauskas et al., 2019; Chen et al., 2024; Cobb‐Lewis et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2019; Mondoloni et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2018). Remarkably, ketamine has been shown to reverse these behavioural alterations by suppressing LHb bursting activity (Chen et al., 2024; Ma et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2018).

Despite its importance, LHb bursting activity has been characterized only in broad terms, often described as periods of elevated activity defined by absolute frequency criteria, or in some cases left undefined (Cui et al., 2018; Seo et al., 2018, 2019; Chen et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2019; Mondoloni et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2018), but see Cerniauskas et al. (2019) and Cobb‐Lewis et al. (2024). This study represents a step towards addressing this gap by aligning LHb bursting activity with the systematic classification frameworks developed for bursting in other neuronal contexts (Desroches et al., 2022; Izhikevich, 2000).

We observed that LHb neurons spontaneously exhibited a wide variety of bursting patterns. These ranged from square bursts, characterized by a plateau and monotonic decrease in spiking frequency, to parabolic‐like bursts, which lack a plateau and display peak frequency in the middle of the burst. Between these distinct types, we also identified a substantial proportion of transitional triangular‐like bursts, that is, bursts without a plateau in which the intra‐burst frequency varies from pure monotonic to close to parabolic. Additionally, we detected other bursts that did not fit into these categories and could not be resolved by the three parameters we applied to quantify bursts features in Fig. 4. Among them, bursts mounted on a sustained depolarization that could last several seconds and display square‐like exponential decays in spiking frequency at the beginning and end of the burst, and maintain a tonic/irregular activity in the middle. This bursting pattern was described originally by Chang & Kim (2004) and has been recently linked to neuron‐to‐glia communication (Chang & Kim, 2004; Yamaoka et al., 2025).

Each cell could exhibit different bursting patterns yet showed a rather dichotomic preference for Plateau (square and complex long) or No‐Plateau (triangular and parabolic) bursts. However, this preference was not linked to average cell parameters. Instead, our results suggest that preferred bursting types reflect dynamical states of the neurons. In particular, more negative membrane potentials favoured Plateau bursts. Three observations support this idea. First, in cases where both Plateau and No‑Plateau bursts were observed, the former were associated with more hyperpolarized pre‑burst voltages. Second, bursts that appeared spontaneously during a hyperpolarizing current step were exclusively of the square type and were observed even in cells that did not spontaneously generate this type of burst. Third, sustained bursting following hyperpolarization prominently generated square‑wave bursts, with almost no occurrences in the triangular‑to‑parabolic transitional categories.

On the other hand, factors favouring parabolic and triangular No‐Plateau bursts were not identified. This type of bursting was dominant in some cells that were able to generate square bursts during hyperpolarization. This combination suggests triangular and parabolic bursts are favoured by a cell‐specific set of conductances that dominate at resting potential but not during hyperpolarized states. In summary, these findings define a scenario in which LHb cells can express different bursting modes, with hyperpolarization selectively associated with only the plateau possessing a subset of them.

In our experiments, LHb bursting did not correspond to an increase in mean neuronal spiking frequency, a finding recently supported in vivo by other authors (Zouridis et al., 2024). This suggests that the link between LHb bursting and negative motivational value may arise from the dynamics of bursting itself rather than by the average LHb output. In this context, square and parabolic bursts could represent different output modes with different impact at the presynaptic terminals governed by their own facilitating or depressing dynamics. The former, characterized by an initial high‐frequency spike followed by a decay, would be amplified by facilitating synapses and filtered by the depressing ones. The latter, with low initial frequencies that peak towards the middle of the spike train, would exhibit the opposite profile.

Interestingly, the fact that neurons could transition between bursting types and the lack of cell average parameters linked to bursting modes, indicate that these patterns are not limited to specific cell classes. To account for these complex dynamics, we developed an idealized model (System (1)) that is capable of displaying each of these patterns separately, based on slightly different values of two key parameters (b and z), the boundary between them being displayed in parameter space by the presence of a codimension‐2 bifurcation point, which we identify as SNH.

The model predicts that LHb neurons can transition between distinct bursting modes ranging from square to parabolic bursting, when shifted around the SNH in the b and z space. Hence, simulating the model with added stochastic components onto the differential equations where these two parameters appear (System (2)) produce simulations displaying more than one burst type.

We envisage that, in real neurons, the z and b parameters may relate to a combination of conductances rather than to a single one. Slow ionic conductances that set the resting membrane potential could contribute to the displacement of the system along b from the square plateau containing bursts to the No‐Plateau region of the b–z space. In this regard, recent work has identified astrocytic Kir4.1 as a conductance contributing to LHb neuronal hyperpolarization, a condition that favours square bursting (Cui et al., 2018). Consistent with this interpretation, in our experiments transitions between bursting modes occurred within the same cell, albeit on slow timescales.

On the other hand, burst‐generating trajectories would be linked to voltage‐dependent conductances of intermediate kinetics. For example, ionic conductances underlying square bursts at the LHb have been well described by Yang et al. (2018) and depend on low‐threshold voltage‐dependent calcium channels and, to a lesser extent, HCN channels. In addition, long Plateau bursts have also been shown to depend on cyclic nucleotide‐gated channels (Chang & Kim, 2004; Yamaoka et al., 2025). In contrast, ionic mechanisms underlying parabolic and triangular bursts in the LHb remain unknown and are less well characterized in other systems, where they may involve persistent sodium channels and M‐type potassium channels (Butera et al., 1999; Golomb et al., 2006).

Finally, excitatory and inhibitory synaptic inputs may add stochastic fluctuations, driving transitions across the bifurcation lines, triggering bursting behaviour and promoting mixed bursting trajectories. This would account for the great amount of evidence linking synaptic changes at the LHb with depressive‐like behaviours (Hu et al., 2020; Proulx et al., 2014).

Summarizing, our formalism predicts the bursting mode predominant on LHb neurons to be related to the balance of multiple conductances rather than to a single one. This highlights the multifactorial nature of neuronal dynamic behaviour and is particularly relevant for translational approaches aimed at controlling LHb bursting activity. Future experimental and modelling studies will be essential to determine how these different bursting modes influence mood and aversion, and how the interplay between intrinsic and synaptic conductances regulates their balance.
