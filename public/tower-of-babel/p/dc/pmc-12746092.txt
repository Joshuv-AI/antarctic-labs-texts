DiameTR: A cryo-EM tool for diameter sorting of tubular samples
Authors: Ruizhi Peng, Heidy Elkhaligy, Timothy Grant, Scott M Stagg
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

Tubular structures are ubiquitous in biological systems, exemplified by dynamin, microtubules, viral capsids, and flagella (Fujita-Fujiharu et al., 2022, Jimah et al., 2024, Kellogg et al., 2018, Kreutzberger et al., 2022). These structures are characterized by protein subunits arranged in helical lattices, with each particle image capturing multiple subunit views—an advantage for structural reconstruction. Indeed, the first cryo-EM structure determined was the helically arranged T4 phage tail (De Rosier and Klug, 1968). This inherent property of helical assemblies has enabled structural determination with relatively small datasets, contributing to many early successes in cryo-EM (Li et al., 2000, Toyoshima and Unwin, 1990, Woodhead et al., 2005).

As cryo-EM methodology advances, researchers have targeted increasingly challenging filamentous assemblies. Tubular samples can be particularly challenging due to heterogeneity in their diameter. For instance, microtubules formed in vitro are well established to form with varying numbers of microfilaments (Kent and Lele, 2017), resulting in discrete diameter differences. Other samples, such as membrane tubules, can display continuous variability in diameter. These heterogeneities significantly complicate cryo-EM structure determination, sometimes rendering it intractable. Achieving high-resolution reconstructions requires iterative particle classification to isolate homogeneous subsets, with diameter-based sorting serving as a critical first step.

Current approaches to diameter classification primarily rely on two methods. The first employs cross-correlation analysis, where particle images are compared against a series of reference images representing different diameters. The reference yielding the highest correlation peak indicates the particle's diameter (Kong et al., 2018). However, this approach requires prior knowledge to generate appropriate reference images. The second method uses iterative 2D classification to group particles based on shared features. Although diameter is one of the features being aligned, orientation and shifts often become the dominant sorting criteria, which decreases the effectiveness of diameter separation. In addition, when diameter variations span a broad continuous range, 2D classification, which sorts particles into an integer number of bins becomes suboptimal. This approach typically requires multiple rounds of classification and relies heavily on subjective visual inspection of class averages to interpret diameter differences (Nyenhuis et al., 2023, Wang et al., 2022). This subjectivity in diameter assessment introduces bias and inconsistency in the classification process, potentially compromising the reliability of subsequent structural analyses.

To address these limitations, we developed diameTR, a novel tool for determining tubular sample diameters. DiameTR analyzes 1D projections of individual particle images to generate characteristic profiles for diameter determination. This approach bypasses the need for pre-generated reference images and the prior knowledge inherent in cross-correlation technique. The algorithm determines diameters on a per-particle basis, eliminating the time-consuming alignment steps during 2D classification, and uses GPU acceleration for rapid computation. Here, we present the underlying principles of our diameter determination method and demonstrate how it can be used to overcome challenges with single particle analysis of heterogeneous tubular samples.

Results

Determine the psi angle using customized Radon transform

Our approach for measuring diameters of tubular particles involves first determining their tubular axis and aligning them horizontally. In cryo-EM reconstructions, particle orientations are defined by three Euler angles, psi (the in-plane rotation angle that aligns tubular axes), theta, and phi (Grigorieff, 2016). The refinement process in cryo-EM data processing aims to determine the most accurate values for these three angles. To orient a 2D particle image correctly in 3D space, three sequential rotations are applied: first, the particle is rotated by the in-plane psi angle around z axis; second, it is rotated by theta angle around y axis to transform the 2D image into the 3D space; finally, it is rotated by phi angle around z axis to achieve the desired orientation. In helical reconstructions, the symmetry axis typically coincides with the z axis. In cryo-EM preparations, filamentous specimens are much longer than the thickness of the ice they are embedded in, so their theta angle is usually ∼ 90°. Since the psi angle operates solely at the 2D image level, we bypass the conventional refinement process by determining psi angles directly from 2D images.

Tubular samples have repeating features along their tubular axis, including proteins and lipid membranes. These periodic elements generate distinctive high-intensity peaks on a line passing through the center in an autocorrelation image (Fig. 1 A). Similarly, the power spectrum of tubular particles displays high-intensity peaks along a central line perpendicular to the tubular axis (Fig. 1 B). In theory, projecting the autocorrelation image along the tubular axis should produce the maximum peak value. Thus, applying a Radon transform between 0–180 degrees to the autocorrelation image can readily identify the angle corresponding to the tubular axis (this approach also works with the power spectrum image, though with a 90-degree offset). However, conventional Radon transform computation requires images rotation across multiple angles, necessitating interpolation—a computationally intensive process. To enhance both speed and accuracy, we developed a customized Radon transform algorithm. Recognizing that the high-intensity line invariably intersects the image center, we implemented a simplified approach using a 2-pixel-wide rectangular mask (Fig. 1 B). The mask is positioned to share its center with the image center and pixels outside the mask are excluded from the calculation. Instead of rotating the entire image, which would require interpolation, we rotate the mask as a boolean array (Fig. 1 B & C). This optimized approach achieved a ∼ 200-fold reduction in computation time compared to the standard Radon transform, with further acceleration achieved through GPU implementation.

We validated our psi angle determination algorithm using both a simulated dataset and five public datasets yielding high-resolution helical structures. The simulated dataset provided an ideal test case, free from contamination that could interfere with angle determination. In this case, diameTR achieved a root mean square deviation (RMSD) of less than 0.5 degrees compared to preset values. When applied to real-world datasets, the initial angles determined by diameTR showed higher RMSD values relative to helical refinement results (Fig. 1 D, Table 1). Further analysis revealed that fewer than 1 % of particles in these datasets exhibited angular differences greater than 30 degrees between diameTR and helical refinement determinations. Visual inspection of 200 randomly selected particles from each dataset revealed that these discrepancies primarily occurred in low-quality particles affected by ice contamination, protein denaturation, or incorrect particle picking at filament intersections (Fig. S1). To better understand these outlier particles, we separated them using a 30-degree angular difference threshold and performed principal component analysis on their refinement parameters. The analysis revealed that log-likelihood scores correlated highest with the outlying particles, demonstrating that particles with large angular discrepancies correlated with poor refinement quality. After excluding these compromised particles (denoted as “clean” in Fig. 1D and Table 1), the RMSD values for all datasets except KpFtsZ-Mb decreased to below 2.5 degrees. Notably, the power spectrum strategy consistently yielded slightly better performance compared to the autocorrelation strategy (Fig. 1D). Given our implementation of a 2-degree step size, these low RMSD values validate the accuracy of diameTR's psi angle determination.

Diameter determination of homogeneous datasets

After determining the psi angles, diameter measurement becomes straightforward. Each particle is rotated to orient its tubular axis perpendicular to the Y axis (particles rotated by 90° for presentation in Fig. 2 A). Protein-containing regions generate higher signal intensity compared to the background, and projecting the particle image onto the Y axis produces a 1D intensity profile. Despite the increased SNR achieved by projecting onto a single axis, the 1D profiles are still noisy, and we discovered that applying a Gaussian filter to particle images generates clear peak features at tubular edges in these 1D profiles (Fig. 2 A). The edge regions consistently display a prominent positive peak adjacent to a negative peak. Since the highest positive peak often appears in the middle of tubules instead of at the edge, we therefore defined the edge position as the midpoint of adjacent negative and positive peaks whose intensity difference is the largest. To validate this diameter determination approach, we tested it on three homogeneous datasets: ESCRT-III (EMPIAR-10396), tobacco mosaic virus (EMPIAR-10022) and FljM flagellar (EMPIAR-12122). All three datasets yielded narrow diameter distributions with variations under 10 Å (Fig. 2 B). The diameters determined by diameTR showed excellent agreement with those measured from the published maps (Table 2).

Diameter determination of heterogeneous datasets

While homogeneous datasets demonstrated the accuracy of diameTR through their high quality and consistent diameters, we further evaluated its performance in analyzing heterogeneous samples by examining two datasets with reported diameter variations (Kefauver et al., 2024, von der Malsburg et al., 2023). The eisosome dataset (EMPIAR-12053) exhibited a broad diameter distribution spanning approximately 60 Å (Fig. 3 A). We partitioned this dataset into four subsets based on diameter measurements. Independent 2D classification of these subsets produced class averages that clearly displayed distinct diameters (Fig. 3 B). Similarly, the Opa1 dataset showed diameter variations of approximately 50 Å (Fig. 3 C), which were confirmed through 2D class averages (Fig. 3 D). We selected the central subset 2 for helical refinement, producing a map (Fig. 3 E) that matched the quality and structural features of the previously published reconstruction (von der Malsburg et al., 2023).

Initial analysis of the KpFtsZ-Monobody dataset (EMPIAR-11425) using diameTR revealed an unexpectedly broad diameter distribution spanning approximately 30 Å (Fig. 4 A). To investigate whether this represented an algorithmic limitation or genuine structural heterogeneity, we divided the dataset into two subsets. 2D class averages confirmed the presence of distinct diameters (Fig. 4 B). Notably, subset 1 failed to yield secondary structure features during refinement using the previously reported helical parameters (rise 7.70 Å, twist –23.40°) (Fujita et al., 2023), and the average power spectra from two subsets showed different frequency on zero order line (Fig. S2C). Hence, we used the layer lines from the averaged power spectra of subset 1 to estimate the helical parameters. After several trials of helical refinement, we identified unique helical parameters for subset 1 (rise 8.25 Å, twist −24.94°), different from what was previously published (Fig. S2A, B, C &D). The reconstruction easily reached near-atomic resolution (3.02 Å) and yielded features consistent with the polypeptide backbone and side chains (Fig. 4 D, E & F). The monomer atomic model could be easily fitted into the map yielding a two-start helical structure (Fig. 4 D, E & F). In contrast, subset 2 refined successfully using the reported symmetry parameters and the published model (PDB: 8H1O) fitted easily (Fig. 4 C & F). No significant differences could be observed between the maps when comparing the monomers (Fig. S2F). However, their helical lattices revealed significant structural differences (Fig. 4 D & E). Model 1 exhibited a smaller diameter and contained precisely one fewer monomer per turn compared to model 2. This novel structural insight from the KpFtsZ-Monobody analysis strongly validates diameTR's capability for accurate diameter determination in tubular specimens.

Bilayer thickness detection

Our approach's ability to determine both psi angles and tubular edges enables precise particle centering. We define the tubular particle's center as the midpoint between the computed left and right edges, with the shift value representing the distance between this tubular center and the image center. This computational framework allows for automated particle centering and subsequent generation of particle averages.

We demonstrated the utility of this centering capability using three galactosyl ceramide (GalCer) datasets. GalCer, a lipid known to induce membrane tubule formation, has been extensively used in cryo-EM structural studies (Dang et al., 2005, Kalia et al., 2018, von der Malsburg et al., 2023). Our analysis focused on three different lipid compositions, each sharing identical carbon chain lengths but varying in headgroup structure (Fig. 5 A). The averaged images revealed distinct bilayer thickness variations correlating with lipid composition (Fig. 5 B). DGS-NTA(Ni), featuring the longest lipid, exhibited the largest bilayer thickness at 44.6 Å. PI3P, with its intermediate headgroup size, showed a bilayer thickness of 43.1 Å, while PA, bearing the smallest headgroup, displayed a bilayer thickness of 40.6 Å. These measurements align precisely with the relative sizes of the respective lipid headgroups, validating diameTR's capability for accurate bilayer thickness quantification.

Average power spectrum

Helical symmetry determination typically begins with Fourier-Bessel indexing of particle image power spectra (Egelman, 2007, Estrozi et al., 2018, Zhang, 2022). Since an individual particle power spectrum is too noisy for optimal indexing, the conventional approach involves generating 2D class averages and performing helical indexing on their power spectra. However, poor particle alignment can result in blurred 2D class averages that impede indexing. An alternative method for improving signal-to-noise ratio involves averaging particles' power spectra directly. This approach is feasible because image shift does not affect power spectra, allowing tubular particles with psi angle rotations to be used directly for power spectrum averaging. While cryoSPARC includes this functionality (Punjani et al., 2017), its CPU-based implementation is computationally intensive.

We leveraged diameTR's ability to determine psi angles without 2D alignment and implemented GPU acceleration for power spectrum averaging. This resulted in significant performance improvements − processing 65,115 ESCRT-III particles with a box size of 352 required only 85 s to generate the average power spectrum. We applied this algorithm to analyze KpFtsZ-Monobody subsets. The layer line frequencies were consistent between subsets, reflecting identical rung spacing in both models (Fig. S2A & B). However, frequencies in the x-direction showed significant differences (Fig. S2C), consistent with the first subset’s more compact model structure.

Discussion

In this study, we developed a robust algorithm for determining tubular sample diameters on a per-particle basis that is both prior-knowledge free and computationally efficient. The algorithm employs a customized Radon transform to identify the tubular axis, followed by particle rotation, Gaussian filtering, and projection along the tubular axis to generate a 1D profile. A specialized peak-finding strategy identifies tubular sample edges, enabling diameter determination. We validated the algorithm's performance using several public datasets, confirming its accuracy by comparing angle determinations with refinement results and diameter measurements with 2D class averages. Notably, our analysis of the KpFtsZ-Mb dataset revealed a previously unreported narrower form of protein helices. These results establish diameTR as a reliable algorithm for diameter sorting of tubular samples.

Our approach represents a significant advancement in the image processing of tubular samples in cryo-EM by providing a fast automated method for analyzing individual tubular segments. Unlike traditional 2D classification or 3D, which requires the investigator to make subjective decisions about diameter groupings, our method enables direct and unbiased measurement of each segment, eliminating the need for manually identifying 2D classes with similar diameters. Moreover, as we showed with the KpFtsZ-Mb those approaches can miss minority subsets of particles which we catch with our approach.

We expect that diameTR will be especially powerful for examination of membrane thickness with different lipids. We were able to distinguish variations in membrane width due to different acyl chains and headgroups demonstrating that diameTR is both accurate and precise. These properties will allow users to extract subsets of particles with discrete diameter distributions, providing a more refined and quantitative assessment of structural variability. This opens new avenues for investigating how different lipids influence bilayer width and whether proteins of interest modulate membrane thickness, which can provide insights into the structural principles governing membrane-associated processes.

DiameTR can easily be inserted into the standard filament single particle processing workflow. We envision that users would employ diameTR immediately following particle extraction. Given that commonly used cryo-EM data processing software packages, such as cryoSPARC and RELION, employ different metadata formats, we have developed supplementary scripts to facilitate seamless particle data export, as detailed in our GitHub README documentation. Once the appropriate metadata file is obtained, diameTR first determines the psi angles, then uses these angles to measure particle diameters, recording the measurements as a new column in the metadata. The software automatically generates a histogram with an interactive dialog box, enabling users to define and select particle subsets. These selected subsets can subsequently be re-imported into data processing software for further analysis.

Currently, the selection of the number of classes requires manual user input, which represents a potential source of variation from user to user in the analysis. Adjacent diameter groups typically reflect a single monomer difference in the number of subunits per turn, with the absolute difference being related to monomer dimensions and therefore case specific. To address this limitation, we are actively developing a curve detection algorithm that will automatically identify groups in the diameter histogram, enabling automated diameter-based particle classification.

In developing the tool, we implemented two strategies: one utilizing autocorrelation images and another using power spectrum images. Across most tested datasets, the power spectrum strategy demonstrated superior performance, yielding psi angles with smaller RMSD compared to the autocorrelation approach (Table 1). While this difference likely stems from autocorrelation's higher sensitivity to contamination signals, we maintained both strategies in our implementation since non-tubular filaments, such as myosin and actin, may benefit from the autocorrelation approach for angle determination and subsequent average power spectrum analysis.

The specialized peak-finding algorithm we developed for diameter determination (Fig. 2A) processes Gaussian-filtered 1D projection curves to identify edge features. Rather than simply selecting the highest peak, which may appear in the middle of compact protein lattices, our algorithm defines edges based on the maximum intensity difference between adjacent positive and negative peaks. This approach proved effective for most datasets in Fig. 2, Fig. 3, except for Opa1 reconstituted in GalCer lipids, where GalCer tubules have stronger signals from lipids and a large empty lumen in the middle. Thus, we optimized the strategy to use the strong lipid signals, finding the top 2 highest positive peaks. This modification was validated by 2D class averages and image average, establishing this strategy reliable for GalCer lipids. Similarly, for datasets such as microtubules with sparsely decorated proteins, other specialized peak-finding strategies will be required.

Another important consideration for diameter sorting is the inherent variability introduced by defocus. When we applied diameTR to the three homogeneous datasets shown in Fig. 2, the resulting diameter distributions spanned approximately 10 Å. While some of this spread likely reflects natural variations in particle diameter, we also observed a general trend in which particles in higher-diameter bins tended to have higher average defocus values. We attempted to correct this effect by developing a defocus-based scaling factor, but applying it yielded inconsistent results. Therefore, users should exercise caution when sorting diameters with bin sizes approaching 10 Å. In such cases, we recommend limiting the defocus range during data collection.

The ability to directly determine diameters from unaligned particles, without prior knowledge, opens new possibilities for analyzing tubular samples in cryo-EM. By enabling automated, high-throughput diameter sorting, diameTR provides a powerful tool for identifying structurally distinct subpopulations. This capability is particularly valuable for studying dynamic biological assemblies, where structural heterogeneity can reveal underlying mechanisms of function. For instance, the identification of twelve subsets with discrete diameter variations in an archaeal virus dataset helped explain the formation mechanism of spindle-shaped architectures (Wang et al., 2022). DiameTR enables classification of such datasets in a systematic and unbiased way. Furthermore, the GPU-accelerated implementation ensures efficient processing of large datasets, making diameTR a broadly applicable method for researchers investigating membrane-associated processes, cytoskeletal filaments, and other tubular structures. Finally, aligning and centering tubular segments is a prerequisite for two other methodologies from our lab, RASTR, and SPOT-RASTR (Esfahani et al., 2024, Randolph and Stagg, 2020), and diameTR streamlines this preparatory step. Altogether, by providing precise diameter measurements, diameTR facilitates accurate segment alignment, improving the resolution and reliability of downstream structural analyses for all single particle analysis of tubular samples.

Methods

Data acquisition

The datasets were obtained from various sources: ESCRT-III particle stacks from EMPIAR-10396 (Nguyen et al., 2020), Tobacco mosaic virus movies from EMPIAR-10022 (Fromm et al., 2015), FljM flagellar movies from EMPIAR-12122 (Sanchez et al., 2023), Eisosome movies from EMPIAR-12053 (Kefauver et al., 2024). The Opa1 micrographs were provided by Prof. Halil Aydin (von der Malsburg et al., 2023), while the KpFtsZ-Monobody movies were accessed from EMPIAR-11425 (Fujita et al., 2023).

Data processing

The datasets underwent similar processing protocols unless otherwise specified. Movies were imported using dose and pixel size parameters from the original publications (Fromm et al., 2015, Fujita et al., 2023, Kefauver et al., 2024, Nguyen et al., 2020, Sanchez et al., 2023, von der Malsburg et al., 2023). Patch motion correction was performed using default knots calculated from image dimensions. CTFFIND4 was employed to estimate defocus values ranging from 0.1 to 10 μm, and micrographs with CTF fit resolution worse than 10 Å were excluded. Particle picking was conducted using cryoSPARC's filament tracer function, with diameters estimated from micrograph measurements (Punjani et al., 2017). Segments were configured with approximately 60 Å separation distances. Particles were extracted using box sizes specified in the original reports and downsampled by a factor of 2. A single round of 2D classification was performed with 50 classes over 20 iterations. Classes lacking distinct protein features or containing particles picked at tubule edges were removed. Selected particles were then re-extracted using aligned shifts and prepared for subsequent processing.

In cases where the filament tracer failed to identify tubular particles using estimated diameters, an alternative approach was implemented. A set of approximately 1000 particles were picked manually in random micrographs. This small particle stack underwent 2D classified into 10 classes over 20 iterations. Classes exhibiting helical features were selected and used as templates for a subsequent round of filament tracer particle picking.

The customized Radon transform was computed through several steps. First, the raw images were padded by 1 box size. Then, the autocorrelation (AC) image was generated from the raw image. In parallel, the power spectrum was obtained by taking the absolute value of the autocorrelation image's Fourier transform. The customized Radon transform was then calculated by sampling at angles from 0 to 360 degrees with 2-degree increments, producing a one-dimensional curve. The psi angle was determined by identifying the angle corresponding to the maximum peak in this curve using argmax function in cupy module. For angles obtained using the power spectrum approach, a 90-degree offset was added to the final reported value to account for the orthogonal relationship between Fourier space and real space.

The refinement results were exported from cryoSPARC and converted into readable star format using the csexport.py script from our repository. Principal component analysis

To statistically analyze the outlier particles, we performed principal component analysis (PCA) using the PCA_star.py script available in our GitHub repository (https://github.com/sstagg/RASTR). Briefly, the particles were divided into two groups: Group 1 consisted of particles with consistent psi angles (determined by either diameTR or helical refinement), while Group 2 contained particles exhibiting a psi angle difference greater than 30° between the two methods. The two groups were concatenated for PCA, with particle row numbers treated as observations and numerical columns as features. Following PCA transformation, the particles were re-split to identify which numerical features contributed most to the observed variance.

Diameter determination

Particle images were preprocessed using a Gaussian filter first. The images were then rotated according to previously determined psi angles. Tubular particles with axis rotated perpendicular to y axis were projected down to y axis. Peak detection was performed using scipy.signal.find_peaks to identify both positive and negative peaks. Peak differences were calculated by subtracting negative peak values from their adjacent positive peak values. The four largest peak differences were used to identify the appropriate edges. Diameters were calculated as the absolute difference between these two edges. In our implementation, diameter values were stored in star files under a new column labeled_rlnDiameterByRASTR, which remained compatible with relion operations.

For applications requiring shift determination, such as in SPOT-RASTR (Esfahani et al., 2024), the midpoint between the two edges was calculated. The distance between this midpoint and the y-axis center was determined. The X and Y shifts were then computed as distance * sin(psi) and distance * cos(psi), respectively. These shifts were scaled by the pixel size and recorded in the star file under the columns_rlnOriginXAngst and_rlnOriginYAngst.

We supplemented a function in the script to select particles based on_rlnDiameterByRASTR. For all heterogeneous datasets, particles were classified into subsets with a diameter range of 10 Å and spacing between subsets of 5 or 10 Å. Subsets were re-imported into cryoSPARC and underwent 2D classifications individually. Finally, class averages were used to validate diameters.

KpFtsZ-Monobody data processing

Unlike the other datasets which used previously determined symmetry parameters, for this dataset helical refinement was performed de novo. Specifically, two subsets of particles were created. Subset 1 contained 52,551 particles with diameters between 205 and 215 Å. Subset 2 contained 83,675 particles between 220 and 230 Å. Both subsets were re-imported into cryoSPARC, 2D classified again to validate diameter differences. Helical refinement was performed for both subsets with parameters rise: 7.70 Å and twisted: –23.40°. Subset 2 yielded a map at 3.59 Å resolution (Nyquist) with secondary structure features. Subset 1 on the other hand yield map at 3.59 Å resolution but without reasonable features. We computed the average power spectra of subset 1 and indexed the helical parameters. After several trial and error, we got the parameter set of rise: 8.25 Å and twist: −24.94°. Helical refinement with new parameters yielded a map at 3.59 Å resolution with secondary structure features. Atomic models of monomers were fitted into the new map, yielding a two-start model with ∼ 14.4 subunits per turn. We repeated the refinement using unbinned particles and get a final resolution of 3.02 Å for subset 1 (8.25 Å, −24.94°) and 2.85 Å for subset 2 (7.70 Å, –23.40°).

GalCer sample preparation, data acquisition and processing

GalCer tubes were prepared by mixing GalCer (2.5 mg/ml) with specified lipids (chloroform) at desired ratio in an amber glass vial. Mixtures were dried under a gentle nitrogen air flow. The glass vial was put under vacuum to remove residual chloroform for at least 2 h. Lipids were resuspended in 50 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl. Tubes were formed by gentle vortexing for 5 min and water bath sonication for 30 s. For the PA GalCer tube, membrane binding proteins were added and incubated for 30 min. All tubes were then plunge frozen on UltraAuFoil R2/2 300 mesh grids using Vitrobot Mark IV at 100 humidity (4 ℃). 4 μL samples were applied to the grid, waiting for 30 s before blotted for 3 s at 0 force.

Data were collected on a Titan Krios microscope (ThermoFisher Scientifici) operated at 300 kV. Movies were recorded on a DE Apollo direct detector using the Leginon software with a random defocus range of −0.5 μm to 3 μm. Apollo camera was set in 8 k x 8 k super-resolution mode. For all datasets, movies were collected at 37,000 X at 60 frames per second for a total dose of 40 e– / Å2.

For this study, 2000 movies for each dataset were imported into cryoSPARC. Frame alignment, CTF estimation and particle picking were done in the same way above. Particles were exported and processed by diameTR. Shifts were calculated using the −s option in diameTR. Detailed protocol was provided in GitHub.

Average power spectra

To calculate the average power spectra, each particle image was rotated by psi angle to have the helical axis horizontal. Then particles were padded by 2 (box size) to increase the sample rate in Fourier space. The power spectrum for every individual particle was computed and then averaged together. The averaged power spectrum was written as an mrc file and could then be used in downstream software for helical indexing (Estrozi et al., 2018, Jiang Lab, n.d, Zhang, 2022).

Speed benchmark

Speed tests were conducted on a workstation equipped with a single NVIDIA RTX A4000 GPU, Intel Xeon(R) Gold 5220R CPU, and conventional HDD storage. The test dataset comprised 65,115 ESCRT-III particles with diameter classification and a box size of 352 pixels. Psi angle determination completed in 1,898 s with a default pad factor of 1 (box size), while other psi angle parameters affected accuracy but not computational speed. Diameter determination completed in 86 s, with parameter adjustments showing no impact on processing time. Power spectrum averaging took 100 s using spline interpolation order 3, which could be reduced to 84.5 s using order 1 without compromising power spectrum quality. All three computational processes were repeated three times, with variations less than 2 s from the mean. As tested, computational time scaled linearly with particle number and quadratically with box size.
