Sympathetic Neural Overactivation, Vascular Dysfunction, and Exercise Intolerance in Long‐Term Survivors of Breast Cancer Treated With Doxorubicin and Trastuzumab‐Based Chemotherapy

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLong‐term survivors of breast cancer (BC) treated with doxorubicin and trastuzumab‐based chemotherapy are at increased risk of developing cardiovascular disease. However, the physiological mechanisms associated with increased cardiovascular disease risk are completely unknown. We hypothesized that long‐term survivors of BC, compared with controls, exhibit sympathetic neural hyperactivity, vascular dysfunction, cardiac morphofunctional changes, exercise intolerance, and alterations in the circulating milieu.MethodsTwenty‐three survivors of BC (age: 48.9±1.3 years; body mass index: 25.2±0.8 kg/m2) and 18 (age: 46.4±1.3 years; body mass index: 26.8±0.8 kg/m2) well‐matched controls were studied. Muscle sympathetic nerve activity (microneurography), brachial artery flow‐mediated dilation (ultrasound‐Doppler), carotid‐femoral pulse wave velocity (tonometry), left ventricular ejection fraction and global longitudinal strain (echocardiography), peak oxygen uptake (cardiopulmonary exercise testing), endothelial cell‐derived extracellular vesicles (flow cytometry), and plasma metabolome (mass spectrometry) were assessed. Complementary experiments were conducted on human umbilical vein endothelial cells cultured with plasma samples from subjects.ResultsSurvivors of breast cancer were tested ⁓8 years after cancer treatment completion. Muscle sympathetic nerve activity was higher and brachial artery flow‐mediated dilation and peak oxygen uptake were lower in survivors than controls. Survivors of breast cancer exhibited higher circulating endothelial cell‐derived extracellular vesicles, higher reactive oxygen species bioactivity, and lower acetylcholine‐evoked nitrics oxide production in plasma‐treated human umbilical vein endothelial cells. Twenty‐eight plasma metabolites differed in survivors versus controls. Peak oxygen uptake was inversely related to muscle sympathetic nerve activity or positively to brachial artery flow‐mediated dilation. No differences in carotid‐femoral pulse wave velocity, left ventricular ejection fraction, and left ventricular global longitudinal strain were observed.ConclusionsOur findings demonstrate that long‐term survivors of breast cancer exhibit sympathetic overdrive, vascular dysfunction, and exercise intolerance, which may contribute to increased cardiovascular disease risk in this population.

Doxorubicin and trastuzumab are highly effective drugs for treating breast cancer (BC) patients with amplification of HER2+ (human epidermal growth factor receptor type 2) tumors. 1 These treatments significantly improve the overall and disease‐free survival of this patient population. 1 However, survivors of BC treated with these drugs are at increased risk for cardiovascular events (eg, atrial fibrillation, heart failure, and cardiovascular‐related mortality) subsequent to completion of treatment. 1 , 2 The underlying mechanisms of this increased cardiovascular risk remain unclear, but a constellation of physiological changes may contribute.

Previous findings show that patients with early BC who received doxorubicin and trastuzumab exhibit increased cardiac adrenergic tone, as measured by iodine‐123‐labeled metaiodobenzylguanidine scintigraphy imaging, 3 increased resting heart rate (HR), 4 and reduced HR variability, 5 indicating cardiac autonomic dysfunction. More recently, we demonstrated that doxorubicin acutely increases peripheral muscle sympathetic nerve activity (MSNA) in these patients, 6 an independent predictor of mortality in certain disease states, including heart failure and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. 7 , 8 Subsequently, we found that those who developed heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (EF) due to anthracycline‐induced cardiotoxicity display increased resting MSNA. 9 However, whether sympathetic neural activity is increased in long‐term survivors of BC who received doxorubicin and trastuzumab is completely unknown.

In vitro/ex vivo findings from endothelial cell cultures and isolated vessels have shown these anticancer drugs significantly increase reactive oxygen species (ROS), decrease nitric oxide (NO) bioavailability, 10 , 11 and increase endothelial cell‐derived extracellular vesicles (endothelial cell‐derived EVs), 6 which contribute, in part, to vascular dysfunction. Notably, patients with BC during or in the short‐term period postchemotherapy adjuvant (<5 years) develop endothelial dysfunction 10 and greater aortic stiffness 12 compared with age‐matched controls without cancer. Although changes in circulating factors contribute to doxorubicin‐dependent vascular dysfunction in mice 11 these potential mechanisms of action have not been explored in long‐term patients with BC treated with doxorubicin and trastuzumab.

Exercise intolerance, objectively measured by reduced peak oxygen uptake (V̇O2peak) during whole‐body exercise, is present in survivors of BC and is linked to functional disability and decreased survival. 13 This reduced V̇O2peak is associated with impairments in the function and integration of pulmonary, circulatory, and musculoskeletal systems. 14 Because the adverse effects of doxorubicin and trastuzumab extend beyond the heart to affect other tissues 13 , attenuated V̇O2peak in survivors of BC may be related to sympathetic neural overdrive, vascular dysfunction, cardiac morphofunctional alterations, or changes in circulating molecular factor. However, these physiological responses have not been systemically investigated in long‐term survivors of BC.

Taken together, we aim to investigate whether long‐term survivors of BC treated with doxorubicin and trastuzumab exhibit autonomic‐cardiovascular abnormalities, including sympathetic neural overdrive, endothelial dysfunction (in vivo), aortic stiffening, cardiac morphofunctional changes, attenuated V̇O2peak, reduced NO production and increased ROS bioactivity (ex vivo), elevated circulating endothelial cell‐derived EVs levels, and alterations in plasma metabolites.

METHODS

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. All experimental procedures followed the Declaration of Helsinki and were approved by the Research Committee of the D'Or Institute for Research and Education (CAAE 52687421.0.0000.5249). Participants were informed about the nature, benefits, and risks of the study procedures, and their written consent was obtained before participation.

Participants

Women, long‐term survivors of BC, aged 30 to 60 years who received 4 cycles of doxorubicin (60 mg/m2) plus cyclophosphamide (600 mg/m2), being 1 cycle each 21 days, followed by 12 weeks of paclitaxel (80 mg/m2), and 1 year of trastuzumab (loading dose of 8 mg/kg, followed by maintenance doses of 6 mg/kg every 3 weeks) to treat stages I to III HER2+breast cancer, 15 at least 5 years ago, were included in the study. Patients were excluded in case of metastatic disease, severe lymphedema, chronic kidney disease (serum creatinine >1.5× upper limit of normal or CrCl <50), chronic heart failure (EF <40%), evidence of chronic liver disease (aspartate/alanine aminotransferase >3× upper limit of normal or total bilirubin >1.5× upper limit of normal), cardiovascular diseases, severe chronic pulmonary diseases, and treatment with cardioprotective agents such as beta blockers, 16 angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors, 17 hormonal replacement, 18 , 19 and antioxidants. 11 All patients were screened at the Cancer Institute of the State of São Paulo. Control subjects were recruited from the local community and enrolled after confirmation of no prior cancer diagnosis or oncologic treatment. They were matched 1:1 to cases for age, sex, body mass index, and comorbidities to ensure comparable baseline characteristics. The participants of both groups had a sedentary lifestyle for at least 3 months.

Muscle Sympathetic Nerve Activity

MSNA was measured for a period of 15 minutes using microneurography technique as described previously. 20 , 21 Neural activity was amplified (gain 2×104, bandpass 700–3000 Hz) using an isolated amplifier and headstage (NeuroAmpEX, ADInstruments, Sydney, Australia) and stored on computer (10 kHz sampling) using a computer‐based data acquisition and analysis system (PowerLab 16SP hardware and LabChart 8 software; ADInstruments). The subjects were tested at rest in a semisupine position with a pillow under the head and a support pillow under the right leg to maintain the stimulation site throughout the experiment. During the MSNA recording, all subjects were instructed to remain awake. The first part of the session consisted of identifying the peroneal nerve of the right leg by cutaneous electrical stimulation. 22 , 23 Then, an active microelectrode (UNP35FAT, FHC Neural MicroTargeting) was inserted into the peroneal nerve, which was preferably chosen to record MSNA due to its easy accessibility. The reference electrode was placed in the subcutaneous tissue 2 to 3 cm away from the active one. Electrode adjustments and audio monitoring were made until a clear MSNA signal was achieved. The establishment of the MSNA signal was assessed in real time using the following criteria: (1) a pulse‐synchronized burst pattern with a burst occurring 1.2 to 1.3 seconds after R wave in the ECG, (2) a typical sympathetic efferent nerve, and (3) good nerve quality. The nerve burst was confirmed by a voluntary expiratory apnea, which activates chemoreflex control of MSNA. Two investigators analyzed the recording. In a case of disagreement, a third investigator was asked to analyze the recording. MSNA was expressed as burst frequency (bursts per min) and burst incidence (bursts per 100 heart beats). 6 , 23

Vascular Measurements

Carotid Artery Stiffness

Blood pressure (BP) was measured while the patient was in supine position by an automated brachial artery cuff. Then, diameter and intima media thickness of the left common carotid artery were measured 2 cm proximal to the bulb, in accordance with current guidelines. 24 , 25 Perpendicular incidence of the B‐mode imaging ultrasound beam in relation to the orientation of the vessel provided clearly demarcated intima medial boundaries, which were optimized with the use of contrast control subjects. Based on the BP data, diameter and intima media thickness were calculated for the carotid parameters, wall:lumem ratio, distension, Young's modulus, distensibility, compliance, and stiffness.

Arterial Stiffness ‐ Pulse Wave Velocity

Central arterial stiffness. Central arterial stiffness was obtained by carotid‐femoral pulse wave velocity, using a validated noninvasive device (ComplioR, Artech Medical), which performs pressure wave recordings and calculates automatically the carotid‐femoral pulse wave velocity as described previously. 26 , 27 The intraobserver repeatability of carotid‐femoral pulse wave velocity measurements with the ComplioR device expressed as coefficient of variation was 2.8±1.3%.

Brachial Artery Flow‐Mediated Dilation

Endothelial function was evaluated by brachial artery flow‐mediated dilation (FMDBA), according to current guidelines. 28 , 29 FMDBA was measured using an appropriate‐sized rapid inflation/deflation pneumatic cuff (E‐20 Rapid Cuff Inflator, D. E. Hokanson) placed around the left forearm, immediately distal to the olecranon process, with the forearm supinated and the shoulder abducted at ∼80°. The brachial images (2–12 cm above the antecubital fossa) were obtained through duplex mode. The contrast resolution, depth, and gain were adjusted to optimize the longitudinal images of the lumen/arterial wall interface. The sample volume was located at the center of the brachial artery and then adjusted to cover the vessel width. Subsequently, baseline diameter and blood velocity waveforms were continuously recorded for >60 seconds. Reactive hyperemia was assessed immediately after the relief of a 5‐minute total ischemia, which was induced by external compression of the arm using an inflatable cuff at 220 mm Hg. After this period, the cuff was rapidly deflated and the change in arterial diameter was expressed as percent change from baseline. The probe location was marked on the skin to guarantee accuracy to the FMDBA.

Hemodynamic Measurements

The systolic BP (SBP), diastolic BP (DBP), and mean arterial pressure (MAP) were evaluated noninvasively, beat to beat, by the method of digital infrared photoplethysmography, through an appropriately sized cuff connected to the middle finger of the nondominant hand (FinometerPRO, Finapres Medical Systems, Amsterdam, The Netherlands). Finger BP values were adjusted according to brachial BP measurements with the oscillometric method, and height correction unit was zeroed and implemented according to the manufacturer's specifications (FinometerPRO, Finapres Medical Systems, Amsterdam, The Netherlands). 30 The HR was evaluated by the 3‐lead ECG. This signal is preamplified (General Purpose Amplifier Stemtech, Inc, GPA‐4, model 2), converted from analog to digital, and analyzed by the PowerLab software.

Cardiac Morphofunctional Evaluation

Cardiac morphological and functional evaluations of all patients were performed using transthoracic echocardiography (Vivid E9, General Electric, Horten, Norway), in accordance with current guidelines. 31 , 32 All parameters, including left ventricular (LVEF) by Simpson biplane, LV volumes, LV mass, regional or global wall motion abnormalities by strain, and myocardium work. Also, markers of LV diastolic function (mitral inflow with E/A ratio, tissue Doppler with lateral e', septal e', mean E/e') and left and right atrium volumes and surfaces were assessed. Finally, markers of right ventricle function (tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion, S′ wave, visual analysis, systolic pulmonary artery pressure estimated by tricuspid regurgitation peak velocity) were also assessed. All echocardiographic parameters were evaluated by a single experienced investigator blinded to all clinical aspects to avoid interobserver variability. 33 , 34 , 35

Cardiac Autonomic Function and Baroreflex Sensitivity

Cardiac autonomic modulation was assessed from spontaneous fluctuations in the time series of RR, using the customized freely available computer software PyBios. 36 All series were visually inspected, and artifacts or transients were manually removed. For time domain analysis we calculated the SD of RR values and the root mean square of successive differences between normal heart beats. For frequency domain analysis of HR variability, beat‐by‐beat series of RRs were converted to an evenly spaced series using cubic spline interpolation (10 Hz) and divided into half‐overlapping sequential sets of 512 data points (Welch periodogram). Segments with transients that could affect the calculation of power spectral density were excluded. A Hanning window was used to attend side effects, and the spectrum of each segment was calculated by fast Fourier transform. The spectral of RR were integrated and quantified in the very‐low frequency (<0.04 Hz), low frequency range (LF: 0.04 to <0.15 Hz), and in the high frequency range (HF: 0.15–0.4 Hz). The absolute values of the RR interval spectral densities are reported in absolute values (ms2) and are also reported in normalized units as previously recommended. 37 The normalization procedure was performed by dividing the power of the LF or HF component by the total spectral power from which the power of the very low frequency component had been subtracted. TheLF/HF ratio was also calculated. Respiratory rate was measured in breaths per minute to certify that respiratory frequency of each subject was into the HF range, between 0.15 and 0.4 Hz.

For the analysis of baroreflex function and HR variability, ECG, and arterial pressure recordings were processed by customized computer software to generate series of successive values of RR‐intervals and systolic arterial pressure. Spontaneous baroreflex control was analyzed by sequence method, as proposed by Bertinieri and coworkers. 38 This method assumes that spontaneous arterial pressure ramps elicit baroreflex‐mediated responses in RR length. 39 Series of systolic arterial pressure were searched for increasing or decreasing ramps with at least 3 values of arterial pressure. When a pressure ramp (up or down) shows linear correlation (r>0.8) with RR changes in the same direction, a baroreflex sequence has been found. The spontaneous baroreflex control is considered as the slope (ms/mm Hg) of the linear regression between systolic arterial pressure and RR. The baroreflex gain of each subject is taken as the average slope, calculated from all sequences.

Cardiopulmonary Exercise Testing

First, the maximal ventilatory capacity of participants was determined by maximal voluntary ventilation (MVV). 40 Participants are asked to breathe as hard, fast, and deeply as they can for 10 to 12 seconds through a spirometer (Ergostik; Geratherm Respiratory, Germany). By extrapolating the result to 1 minute, data are obtained in liters/minute and then corrected for body temperature and pressure, saturated (BTPS).

Subsequently, they performed a cardiopulmonary exercise test to determine maximal exercise capacity via a cycle ergometer (Micromed – Cardio PC 13) with an individualized ramp protocol that increased from 5 to 30 W per min, based on the clinical status of the subject. 41 , 42 Briefly, V̇O2 and carbon dioxide production were determined by measuring gas exchange on a breath‐by‐breath basis in a computerized system (Ergostik; Geratherm Respiratory, Germany). V̇O2Peak was defined as the maximum attained V̇O2 at the end of the exercise period. The minute ventilation to carbon dioxide production slope was determined by linear regression, incorporating all exercise data.

Plasma Exposure Experiments for Nitric Oxide Production and Reactive Oxygen Species Bioactivity

Glass bottom plates with 96 wells (Cellvis) were coated with attachment factor (cell systems) for 30 minutes. Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs, Promocell, used after 4–6 passages) were seeded for 24 hours under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2, 15% O2, humidified) in EGM‐2 (Promocell) media containing 4% FBS. After 24 hours, cells were washed with HBSS and exposed to basal media supplemented with 10% human plasma collected from participants (in triplicate). 31 , 43 Cells were stained with 10 μM DAR‐4M‐AM (Enzo Life 133 Sciences) for 30 minutes and imaged before and after addition of 100 μM acetylcholine to assess acetylcholine‐stimulated NO production. In addition, cells were stained with 2 μM CellROX Deep Red (ThermoFisher) to detect whole‐cell ROS production. Intensity was analyzed by quantitative fluorescence microscopy using Celleste 5.0 Image Analysis Software (Thermo Fisher). 32

Nitrite Concentration

Nitrite, a metabolite of NO, was determined in plasma using Griess reagent in which a chromophore with a strong absorbance at 540 nm is formed by the reaction of nitrite with a mixture of naphthyl ethylenediamine (0.1%) and sulfanilamide (1%). A standard curve was established with a set of serial dilutions (10−18 to 10−13 mol/L) of sodium nitrite as previously published. Results are expressed as nmol/L. 44

Systemic Oxidative Stress

Whole blood was sampled from the participants in EDTA tubes and then centrifuged at 448 relative centrifugal force for 10 minutes at 4 °C. Plasma was removed and kept aside for further analysis. The supernatant was stored at −80 °C for subsequent assessments. Proteins were quantified by the method described by Lowry et al. 45 , 46 Detailed descriptions are available in Data S1.

Endothelial Cell‐Derived Extracellular Vesicles

Blood samples were collected in EDTA‐containing vials and centrifuged at 2500 rpm (approximately ~ 770 g) for 15 minutes at room temperature (20 °C) using a refrigerated centrifuge (Model Z 326 K; Hermle Labortechnik, Wehingen, Germany). The endothelial EV population (CD41a‐/CD31+) was determined by flow cytometry (BD FACS Verse; BD Biosciences; Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). 47 Initially, 50 μL of platelet‐poor plasma samples were incubated in the dark with 4 μL of CD31 FITC (BD Biosciences; Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) and 4 μL of CD41a APC (BD Biosciences; Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) for 30 min at 4 °C. Samples were diluted with 425 μL of sterile PBS before flow cytometry analysis. Extracellular vesicles were determined as events smaller than 0.9 μm (0.9 μm NIST Traceable polystyrene particle beads, Polysciences Inc., Warrington, PA, USA). Flow rate was set low, and all samples were run for 90 seconds. CountBright Absolute Counting Beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, United Kingdom) were used to calculate the concentration of EVs by using the following formula according to manufacturer's instructions: [(number of events acquired/number of beads events)×(assigned bead count of the lot/volume of the sample)].

Mass Spectrometry‐Based Metabolomics Analysis

Plasma sample subjects were randomized before extraction. All samples were analyzed using an Acquity H‐Class liquid chromatography system (Waters, Manchester, UK) coupled to Xevo QTOF G2‐XS mass spectrometry (Waters, Manchester, UK), with an ACQUITY UPLC CSH C18 column (2.1×100 mm, 1.7 μm). Mass spectometry with elevated energy (MSE) analysis was conducted at 6 V for low collision energy and a 20 to 50 V ramp for high collision energy. Leucine enkephalin (200 pg/μL) served as a lock mass for accurate mass measurement. Liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry data were processed using Progenesis QI 2.4 software (Nonlinear Dynamics, Newcastle, UK) for raw data import, adduct selection, peak alignment, deconvolution, and compound identification based on MSE experiments. 48 Detailed descriptions are available in Data S1.

Data Analysis

MSNA, BP, and HR were calculated as mean values of the last 5 minutes registered. MSNA bursts were identified and confirmed through visual inspection by 2 independent experienced investigators (J.E.I. and A.R.K.S.) who were blinded to the study patients. Echocardiographic and vascular parameters were assessed by experienced investigators who were blinded to the study patients (R.M.B.). The Doppler ultrasound video signal was encoded in real time and captured at a frequency of 30 Hz. Video files from the brachial and carotid arteries were analyzed offline by experienced sonographers who were blinded to the study patients (J.E.I. and A.R.K.S.), using commercial automated edge detection and wall tracking software (Cardiovascular Suite, Quipu srl., Pisa, Italy) to minimize the evaluator bias. FMDBA was calculated according to the equation: FMDBA (%)=(peak diameter‐baseline diameter)/(baseline diameter)×100. FMDBA was adjusted to resting diameter via allometric scale. 32 After deflation of the cuff, the cumulative area under the curve of shear rate (s−1) was determined over a 3‐minute period. Shear rate was calculated as 4 times the ratio between mean blood velocity (in cm/s) and artery diameter (in cm) [ie, SR=4×(mean blood velocity/diameter)]. Carotid artery remodeling was calculated as ratio between intima media thickness and diameter. ∆SBP/∆Load was calculated as ratio between ∆SBP (peak response–resting response) and ∆Load (peak load–resting load).

Statistical Analysis

The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to verify data distribution, and the Mauchly test was used to verify sphericity. All data are presented as mean±SEM. The Fisher test was used to evaluate the proportion between the groups and the basal characteristics between the groups were tested by Student's t test or Mann–Whitney U test for independent data. Pearson's or Spearman's correlation was used to determine association between the variables. Probability value ≤0.05 was accepted as a significant difference. For metabolomics, the principal component analysis and partial least squares‐discriminant analysis were performed using MetaboAnalyst 5.0 software. The program used for the sample size calculation was G power. The data set was sum‐normalized, log‐transformed, and scaled by Pareto. Features were selected based on the t test P value (<0.05). The sample size was 17 subjects in each group, considering the main outcome as MSNA, based on a difference between the groups of 12 bursts/min. 6

RESULTS

Forty‐one participants (18 control subjects and 23 long‐term survivors of BC) were enrolled in the study. The participants' physical and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1. There were no significant differences between the groups in terms of age, body mass index, waist circumference, hip circumference, waist–hip ratio, menopausal status, medication use, or comorbidities (P>0.05 for all variables). Likewise, resting hemodynamic parameters (SBP, DBP, MAP, and HR) were similar between the groups (P>0.05). All survivors of BC had completed cancer treatment, which included doxorubicin and cyclophosphamide, followed by docetaxel and trastuzumab. The cumulative dose of doxorubicin was 240 mg/m2. Additionally, 76% of the patients received radiotherapy, and 48% had completed hormone therapy. The survivors were tested at an average of 9.6 years after diagnosis and 8.2 years after the completion of cancer treatment.

Muscle Sympathetic Nerve Activity

To determine long‐term effects of doxorubicin and trastuzumab on sympathetic nervous system, we evaluated MSNA directly via microneurography. MSNA could not be obtained in 2 control subjects and 3 survivors of BC. Representative examples of original nerve recordings per 20 seconds from 2 control subjects and 2 survivors are presented in Figure 1A. MSNA burst frequency was 31% greater in survivors than in control subjects (34.0±1.0 versus 26.0±1.0 bursts/min, P<0.0001, Figure 1B), whereas MSNA burst incidence was 28% greater in survivors (51.5±1.6 versus 40.3±2.0 bursts/100 hb, P<0.0002, Figure 1C).

Arterial Stiffness and Central Blood Pressure

Aortic arterial stiffness and central BP were measured by tonometry. Carotid‐femoral pulse wave velocity, central SBP, and diastolic BP were not different between groups (P>0.05, Figures 2A and Table S1, respectively). Augmentation index was higher in survivors than control subjects (P=0.03, Table S1), but pulse pressure was similar between groups (P>0.05 to all, Table S1).

Carotid Artery Vascular Parameters

Carotid artery vascular parameters were evaluated by ultrasonography. Survivors of BC and control subjects did not differ in intima media thickness, diameter, wall:lumen ratio, distension, Young's modulus, distensibility, compliance, or stiffness (P>0.05 for all variables, Table S2).

Vascular Endothelium Function (In Vivo)

To evaluate long‐term effects of doxorubicin and trastuzumab on vascular endothelial function, FMDBA was measured. FMDBA could not be obtained in 2 control subjects and 1 survivor. FMDBA (%) was 36% lower in survivors than control subjects (5.6±0.7 versus 8.7±1.0%, P=0.02, Figure 2B). FMDBA (%)/area under curve of shear rate, and FMDBA (cm) also were lower in survivors than control subjects (P<0.05 to both variables, Figure 2C and Table S3, respectively). Resting diameter, peak diameter, and time to peak were not different between groups (P>0.05 to all variables, Table S3). DISCUSSION

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to comprehensively demonstrate that long‐term survivors of BC treated with doxorubicin and trastuzumab‐based chemotherapy, despite normal cardiac systolic function (LVEF ≥50%), present several physiological, molecular, and cellular abnormalities, such as sympathetic neural hyperactivation, vascular dysfunction (in vivo), subclinical alterations in diastolic function parameters, attenuated aerobic exercise capacity, lower NO production and higher ROS bioactivity (ex vivo), reduced NO and increased oxidative stress (in vivo), elevated circulating endothelial cell‐derived EVs, and changes in 28 plasma metabolites. Furthermore, our findings revealed that exercise intolerance in long‐term survivors of BC was associated with excessive resting sympathetic neural outflow, vascular dysfunction, and cardiac diastolic function parameters.

It is well accepted that anthracycline‐containing chemotherapy, combined or not with trastuzumab, may lead to cardiac autonomic dysfunction in cancer patients and disease survivors. 49 , 50 In recent studies, we have demonstrated that a single cycle of doxorubicin chemotherapy increases MSNA, as directly measured in fibular nerve by microneurography, in patients with BC naïve to anticancer therapy and without a history of cardiovascular diseases. 6 Also, we observed that survivors of cancer who developed heart failure with reduced EF due to anthracycline‐induced cardiotoxicity have an increased resting sympathetic neural activity. 9 Here, we extended these findings and demonstrated that HER2+ survivors of BC treated with doxorubicin and trastuzumab, even without heart failure with reduced EF or heart failure with preserved EF, have sympathetic neural hyperactivity ~8 years after cancer treatment completion. This autonomic dysfunction may be critical for long‐term survivors because an elevated MSNA is an independent predictor of mortality in many disease states. 7 , 8

Given that survivors of BC exhibit sympathetic overactivity and endothelial dysfunction, an elevation in resting BP would be expected. However, this was not observed in the current study, as BP levels were similar between long‐term survivors and control subjects. Although the underlying mechanisms are beyond the scope of our study, several plausible explanations warrant consideration. It is possible that survivors of BC possess reduced vascular α1‐adrenergic receptor sensitivity to epinephrine, leading to attenuated peripheral vasoconstriction and a diminished impact on BP, indicative of decreased sympathetic‐BP transduction. In fact, attenuated sympathetic transduction has also been described in patients with chronic overactivation of the sympathetic nervous system, such as patients with heart failure 51 and chronic kidney disease. 52 Furthermore, the cardiac changes observed in survivors would offset the influence of higher MSNA and vascular resistance, given that BP is a function of cardiac output and total peripheral resistance (mean arterial pressure=cardiac output×total peripheral resistance). 53

The mechanisms by which survivors of BC exhibit sympathetic neural hyperactivation are complex and may be associated with alterations in the peripheral reflex sensitivity, including dysfunction in the baroreflex sensitivity. 41 , 42 , 43 In this regard, a study by Frye and colleagues observed attenuated spontaneous baroreflex control of BP in patients with cancer currently undergoing chemotherapy. 54 In contrast, our findings did not reveal differences in the baroreflex function in survivors versus controls. These divergent findings may be explained because the aforementioned study assessed patients with different types of cancer and chemotherapy, and they were tested 14 days after treatment completion. Our study tested survivors of VC in the long term (ie, ~8 years after receiving the same type of oncologic treatment). It is possible that baroreflex function changes were transient and returned to baseline values. Alternatively, alterations in the sensitivity of both the peripheral chemoreflex and the skeletal muscle ergoreflex may also contribute to the observed sympathetic overactivation. However, further investigations are required to evaluate these reflexes.

Circulating oxidative stress levels may have a role. Studies in animal models have shown that although the injection of superoxide dismutase into the rostral ventrolateral medulla or intravenously reduces the renal sympathetic nerve activity, the ROS increases this response. 55 In this line, our data showed that both systemic oxidative stress and ROS production in HUVEC are augmented in survivors of BC compared with control subjects, and systemic NADPH, an indirect measure of NADPH oxidase activity, was positively associated with MSNA (data not shown). Furthermore, angiotensin II also may contribute. Angiotensin II activates macrophages and other immune cells to produce an inflammatory cytokine storm, increasing centrally sympathetic outflow through the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis. 56 Similarly, angiotensin II also works in the peripheral sympathetic nerve terminals to elicit sympathetic neurotransmission. 57 Notably, studies have reported elevated levels of angiotensin II in response to doxorubicin and trastuzumab treatment. 58 , 59 Lastly, GABA may be another potential mechanism. The GABA act in the paraventricular nucleus of the hypothalamus (centrally), evoking a tonic inhibition of sympathetic outflow, 60 , 61 Also this neurotransmitter act in sympathetic ganglia (peripherally), inhibiting the release of norepinephrine. Preclinical and human (healthy subject) studies clearly show that GABA receptors modulate resting sympathetic neural outflow. 62 , 63 Interestingly, we observed reduced N‐docosahexaenoyl GABA levels, a metabolite derived from GABA, in survivors of BC but not in control subjects.

Our observations are consistent with the current knowledge, indicating that endothelium‐dependent dysfunction is present in patients with early BC. 64 , 65 , 66 Because FMDBA is a measure that is endothelium‐dependent and largely mediated by NO, we hypothesized that reduced FMDBA in survivors of BC is linked to decreases in NO bioavailability and increases in oxidative stress. Using an ex vivo cell culture model to determine the effects of circulating factors in subject plasma on endothelial cell function, we find a great tendency to decrease in endothelial NO production and an increase in the ROS bioactivity with exposure of plasma from survivors versus control subjects. Additionally, systemic nitrite levels, an indirect marker of NO, were found to be reduced in survivors. Furthermore, circulating levels of PI 4,5‐diphosphate, phosphatidylcholine, phosphatidylserine, lysophosphatidylinositol, and lysophosphatidylethanolamine are elevated and can alter the membrane and glycocalyx composition of endothelial cells. 67 , 68 These lipid alterations may compromise the integrity of the protective barrier, promoting endothelial dysfunction. Notably, circulating endothelial cell‐derived EV levels are associated with endothelial cell activation and apoptosis and thus may also contribute to vascular dysfunction. In fact, we have shown that 1 doxorubicin chemotherapy session significantly increases endothelial cell‐derived EV, reducing blood flow response. 6 Here, we revealed that circulating endothelial cell‐derived EVs are elevated in survivors of BC. Of course, there is the possibility that endothelial function change is also associated with sympathetic neural overdrive (assessed by MSNA). Our group 69 , 70 , 71 and others 72 , 73 have shown that FMDBA is inversely associated with MSNA in different populations. We also found an inverse association between FMDBA and MSNA.

We did not observe significant differences in both LVEF and LV global longitudinal strain, classical cardiotoxicity markers, between long‐term survivors of BC and control subjects. There is no unique explanation for these findings, but due to contemporary BC therapy based in low cumulative dose of anthracycline, the patients may not have manifested cardiotoxicity. Of note, the risk of cardiomyopathy is low (3%–5%) in cumulative doses of doxorubicin of up to 450 mg/m2 but may be high (48%) in cumulative doses of 700 mg/m2. 74 In present study, the cumulative dose of doxorubicin was 240 mg/m2. Another explanation is that changes in LVEF and LV global longitudinal strain may have occurred during treatment, but they may have recovered over time. In line with this, a seminal prospective work by Narayan et al. showed that doxorubicin and trastuzumab result in early modest declines in LVEF that are partially recovered at 3 years. 75 Interestingly, we found that survivors of BC had subclinical alterations in cardiac diastolic function parameters. A restrictive cardiomyopathy profile has been reported, especially in survivors of early BC 76 and childhood cancer, 77 with echocardiographic findings of an increased E/A ratio. Importantly, high MSNA and vascular dysfunction are associated with asymptomatic diastolic dysfunction. 78

Many reports have observed that exercise intolerance is present in patients with BC during or in the short‐term period (<5 years) post adjuvant therapy. Our study extends these findings by demonstrating that HER2+ survivors of BC, even ~8 years after receiving doxorubicin and trastuzumab‐based chemotherapy, exhibit a lower V̇O2peak on cardiopulmonary exercise test compared with controls, despite similar physical exertion. By the Fick equation, the V̇O2peak is determined by the product of cardiac output for the arteriovenous O2 difference. Because adverse consequences of cancer therapeutics may extend beyond the heart to affect all components of the oxygen cascade, such as lungs, blood, vasculature, and skeletal muscle, it is possible that the physiological mechanisms related to reduced V̇O2peak in long‐term survivors of BC are systemic. 13 , 14 Remarkably, we observed that survivors had lower O2 pulse and ventilation at peak exercise, indicating an impairment in the circulatory and ventilatory responses. Furthermore, attenuated V̇O2peak was associated with sympathetic neural outflow, endothelial dysfunction, and alterations in cardiac diastolic function parameters, which may lead to vasoconstriction and compromised muscle blood flow. These observations are consistent with the concept of a peripheral neurogenic, vasoconstrictor impairment of tissue oxygen delivery during exercise observed in different populations, such as those with heart failure 79 and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. 80

Although reduced habitual physical activity (PA) undoubtedly contributes to lower V̇O2peak in survivors of cancer, the reduced exercise intolerance in our cohort could be attributable to a combination of deconditioning and direct effects of the disease and its treatments. Oncologic therapies are associated with systemic toxicity that leads to cardiac injury, endothelial and mitochondrial dysfunction, and loss of skeletal muscle mass and oxidative capacity, all of which impair oxygen delivery and use independent of PA levels. 81 , 82 , 83 , 84 Importantly, studies that controlled for or directly measured PA have still reported treatment‐related reductions in V̇O2peak indicating an independent effect beyond activity‐related deconditioning. 81 , 84 , 85 , 86 We acknowledge the lack of objective, longitudinal PA data as a limitation of the present study and recommend future incorporation of accelerometry and questionnaires to assess PA levels in these patients; nevertheless, existing pathophysiologic and clinical evidence supports a substantive, potentially irreversible contribution of cancer and its therapies to the low V̇O2peak observed.

The current study has some limitations. This is a cross‐sectional study; therefore, causality between doxorubicin plus trastuzumab with physiological changes over time cannot be inferred. Thus, future longitudinal investigations are needed. We did not measure cardiac biomarkers, such as troponin I and pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide, 87 to best understand long‐term cardiotoxicity in survivors of BC. Because LVEF and global longitudinal strain were at normal levels, we do not expect alterations in these biomarkers. Our study did not evaluate the long‐term effects of doxorubicin or trastuzumab isolated treatment in survivors of BC. Therefore, it is not possible to say whether trastuzumab after doxorubicin‐chemotherapy has an additive impact on autonomic‐cardiovascular alterations. However, previous evidence has not observed additive effects in the short and medium term, at least, in the cardiac morphofunctional parameters. 75 , 76 Thus, further investigations are needed in this line. Our study did not test mechanisms related to sympathetic overactivation in long‐term survivors. Otherwise, this is the subject of an ongoing investigation by our group. Also, other vasodilatory and vasoconstrictor mediators, such as proinflammatory profile and endothelin‐1, were not assessed. Such information may have provided additional insight into the mechanisms involved in dependent‐endothelium dysfunction. Endothelium‐independent vasodilation was not measured, which limits specificity for endothelial versus smooth muscle dysfunction. 10 However, previous evidence indicates that endothelium‐independent dysfunction is preserved in this population. Although sympathoexcitation is associated with reduced peripheral blood flow during effort, contributing in part to exercise intolerance, 79 , 88 we did not measure the neurovascular response to exercise. Therefore, further studies in this population are warranted. Additionally, we did not evaluate microvascular function, which is a significantly better predictor of long‐term major adverse cardiovascular events in the general population. However, patients that receiving doxorubicin chemotherapy exhibit reduced microvascular function. 89 We also did not measure the PA levels among the study's participants, which could explain the attenuated V̇O2peak in survivors of BC. However, many studies have shown that survivors, compared with control subjects, have lower V̇O2peak despite similar physical activity levels. 81 , 84 , 86 Our in vitro experiments used HUVECs derived from neonatal umbilical veins, which may not fully capture the characteristics of endothelial cells from adult tissues. Additionally, we did not include controls to evaluate whether plasma components such as albumin or IgG influenced endothelial responses; because these factors were not isolated or depleted, they could have affected our results. We assessed NO production in response to plasma‐derived stimuli, which does not necessarily replicate in vivo vasodilator function that can be influenced by other pathways. 90 , 91 Therefore, future human studies using pharmacological blockade with L‐NMMA (a nitric oxide synthase inhibitor) 92 are needed to determine the contribution of NO to the observed effects. Finally, our metabolomics findings are exploratory, limiting the ability to identify definitive metabolites or directly link them to the physiological alterations observed. Future studies involving in vitro experiments with potential candidate metabolites could help establish more precise mechanistic connections, thereby deepening our understanding of the underlying biological processes.

Conclusions

In summary, the present findings indicate long‐term survivors of BC treated with doxorubicin and trastuzumab, despite normal cardiac systolic function (LVEF ≥50%), exhibit resting sympathetic neural overdrive, vascular dysfunction (in vivo), cardiac morphofunctional alterations, attenuated aerobic exercise capacity, reduced NO production and increased ROS bioactivity (ex vivo), reduced NO and elevated oxidative stress, elevated circulating endothelial cell‐derived EVs, and alterations in plasma metabolites. Additionally, our findings revealed that exercise intolerance in long‐term survivors was related to resting sympathetic neural outflow, vascular dysfunction, and cardiac diastolic function parameters. Collectively, these findings help to partially explain the increased risk of cardiovascular disease in long‐term survivors of BC.

PERSPECTIVES

Current study has demonstrated that long‐term survivors of BC treated with doxorubicin and trastuzumab exhibit several sympathetic‐cardiovascular abnormities, such as sympathetic neural overactivation, vascular dysfunction (in vivo and ex vivo), subclinical alterations in cardiac diastolic parameters, and attenuated exercise capacity. 7 , 93 , 94 These observations may have serious clinical implications for long‐term survivors given that all these abnormalities are independent predictors of cardiovascular disease‐related mortality. Thus, it is an urgent biomedical research priority to establish therapeutic strategies (eg, exercise rehabilitation, neuromodulation, and antioxidant supplementation) that can restore or alleviate these sympathetic‐cardiovascular abnormalities in long‐term survivors of BC.
