A mutation detection method of discontinuous structures in rock strata based on drilling parameters
Authors: Xin Sun, Jingyi Cheng, Zhijun Wan, Zhiqing Guo, Shuruo Zhang, Shuheng Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Discontinuous structures in coal mine roadway roofs, such as rock interfaces and joint fractures, are critical factors leading to surrounding rock instability. The use of Measurement While Drilling (MWD) technology to identify geological formations has become a growing trend. However, there is still a lack of rock structure recognition methods that offer high accuracy, efficiency, and strong generalizability. Therefore, this study acquired four drilling parameters including thrust, torque, modulation specific energy (SEM), and rock drillability assessment (RDA) through drilling experiments. By leveraging the Bayes algorithm, which has high precision, efficiency, and low cost, a change point detection model for drilling parameters was established, and a multi-parameter fusion criterion was proposed for identifying rock structures. The results show that for single rock interface identification, the errors of thrust, torque, SEM, and RDA were 13.3 mm, 4.6 mm, 4.4 mm, and 18.3 mm, respectively. For multiple rock interface identification, the recognition rates were 83.3%, 100.0%, 66.7%, and 83.3%, respectively. Moreover, the absolute value of the magnitude index (SLP) at the interface location was generally the highest among all change points. In multi-change-point detection, the SLP threshold should be set at ± 0.2. It is worth noting that the SLP value is correlated with data fluctuation intensity; greater fluctuation leads to higher SLP values at change points. This study contributes significantly to enabling intelligent perception of rock structures and improving the quality of rock mass control.

Introduction

Composite layered roofs are the predominant type of roadway roof in coal mines. Within these strata, there is a widespread presence of discontinuous structures, such as weak interlayers, joint fractures, and layer interfaces, which exhibit disordered distribution and varying sizes1. The beam-like structure within the composite roof is formed by multiple layers of strata with significant differences in their physical and mechanical properties. The overall strength of the roof is influenced by these discontinuous structures, leading to a reduction in the bonding strength and frictional resistance between the layers, thereby increasing the susceptibility to delamination and even roof collapse accidents. Research has shown that the failure of rock masses containing discontinuous structures is primarily caused by the opening, closure, and expansion of internal discontinuities, which lead to ongoing sliding displacements2,3. This indicates that the structural characteristics of the strata are key factors in the overall instability and failure of surrounding rocks in roadways4,5. Therefore, accurately and efficiently investigating the strength and interface characteristics of the strata, as well as effectively identifying the distribution of discontinuous structures, is of paramount importance for assessing the stability of roadway surrounding rock, optimizing support design parameters, and mitigating roadway instability and failure.

Traditional methods, such as borehole inspection, are predominantly employed at engineering sites to investigate the distribution of strata structures. While these techniques offer advantages such as strong visual intuitiveness and high accuracy, they are unable to simultaneously probe the mechanical properties of the rock mass. Furthermore, they are constrained by limited detection range, low efficiency, and significant subjectivity6,7. In response to these limitations, many researchers have proposed using drilling and anchoring equipment operational parameters to infer geological characteristics of the strata, giving rise to the application of MWD technology8,9. The MWD technology allows for the monitoring of various operational parameters of the drilling rig, such as thrust, torque, rotation speed, and penetration rate, without interfering with the normal construction of anchoring holes. Through the use of various monitoring sensors, these data are captured and analyzed to extract valuable geological information, including rock properties and stratum structures. This technique enables a highly refined geological survey of the surrounding rock throughout the entire roadway, offering real-time data collection, enhanced detection efficiency, and cost-effectiveness that far surpass traditional geological surveying methods.

Many researches have been conducted by numerous scholars in the field of while-drilling geological surveying. Rostami et al.10,11 discovered that when drilling strata with constant rotation speed and penetration rate, a thrust reduction exceeding 50% or a thrust curve resembling a “V” shape could indicate the development of fractures. Based on this observation, they developed a fracture detection program using thrust as a parameter, employing the CUSUM algorithm, which enabled the identification of fractures with widths greater than 2 mm. Furthermore, they demonstrated that utilizing sound and vibration signals could enhance the accuracy of fracture detection. Patel12 employed drilling parameters such as penetration rate, torque, and drilling pressure to characterize voids within the rock, and through analyzing the variation patterns of these parameters, he developed a method to predict the size, shape, and location of these voids. His approach established a comprehensive set of techniques for void feature detection and assessment. Tian et al.13 found that thrust, torque, and penetration rate exhibited significant responses to rock mass fractures, with thrust decreasing at fracture locations, while torque and penetration rate increased. They utilized drilling parameters to develop a Composite Fracture Index (CFI) to identify fractures within the rock mass. Liu14,15 and Kahrama16 observed substantial differences in the effectiveness of various while-drilling parameters for geological identification, with drilling pressure and torque providing better results for lithology and rock strength identification, while vibration signals were more suitable for detecting smaller structures such as fractures. He17,18 and Li et al.19 used drilling energy ratios to characterize discontinuous structural surfaces such as joints and fractures within rock masses, and proposed a rock mass quality assessment method based on drilling energy. Tan et al.20 found that parameters such as pressure and rotation speed during drilling responded significantly to strata interfaces, suggesting that the energy required for rock cutting primarily relies on rotational kinetic energy, which makes rotational kinetic energy a more effective means for identifying strata interfaces. However, Leung et al.21 argued that the drilling energy efficiency exhibits low specificity and high variance, leading them to employ a logistic function to process the drilling energy, resulting in a modulated energy efficiency. This approach enabled effective differentiation between coal and non-coal strata. Building on this, Liu et al.22 analyzed the influence of variations in different parameters of the modulated energy efficiency on the performance of the indicators and utilized the modulated energy to identify stratigraphic interfaces. Liu et al.23,24. applied the K-means algorithm and CPD algorithm to process borehole data from stratified grout samples, revealing that the drilling speed shows distinct response characteristics when crossing through stratigraphic interfaces. The CPD algorithm proved effective in identifying interface positions. Navarro et al.25 developed a structural factor (DI) index using drilling parameters and classified rock states into blocky, fissured, and heavily fissured categories based on the DI. Fernández et al.26 further employed the DI as an indicator for recognizing rock discontinuities, applying classical linear combinations and machine learning (ML) techniques in the prediction of discontinuous surfaces, achieving a recognition rate of discontinuous structures ranging from 89% to 96%. Chen et al.27 proposed a high-resolution rock type classification method based on deep convolutional neural networks, achieving high-accuracy detection and quantification of stratigraphic interfaces across different rock types through image recognition and deep learning techniques. Cemiloglu et al.28 attempted to apply an SVM-supervised classifier to classify and predict the compressive strength behavior of Maragheh limestone, according to the results, the model’s accuracy was 0.91, and its precision was 0.86. Nanehkaran et al.29 proposed a highly accurate deep learning-based predictive model to investigate the compressive strength of lightweight concrete with natural aggregates, their findings showed that the proposed model achieved the highest accuracy and the lowest error rate with a high learning rate.

The aforementioned studies demonstrate that the MWD technology is an effective means for identifying discontinuous structures. However, existing research primarily focuses on the prediction of lithological parameters using this technology, with relatively little attention given to the identification of discontinuous structures within the strata. Furthermore, most studies rely on a single index parameter for identifying strata structures, resulting in a significant issue where data fluctuations adversely affect the accuracy of the recognition model. Additionally, current research mainly explores the effectiveness of various drilling parameters and algorithm models for strata structure identification, without developing a systematic approach for automatic while-drilling recognition of strata structures. These factors have contributed to the difficulty in widely applying while-drilling recognition methods in field engineering practices.

This study established a detection model for sudden changes in drilling-while-drilling index parameters based on the Bayes algorithm, and proposed a multi-index parameter fusion criterion for identifying rock layer interfaces and structures. This method can achieve dynamic detection of rock layer structures during the construction of anchor holes in coal mine tunneling roadways, accurately identifying geological information such as rock layer interfaces, fractures, and weak interlayers existing within the rock layer. Furthermore, based on geological information, the stability of rock strata in different areas of the roadway is evaluated, and at the same time, the design of roadway support is guided. Therefore, this research is of great significance for achieving intelligent perception of discontinuous structures within rock masses, improving the quality control level of surrounding rocks, and ensuring the safe mining of coal mines.

Experimental method

Figure 2 shows the self-developed “ZKD-HIS-100 Surrounding Rock Geological While-Drilling Identification Experimental System” by the research team. The system mainly consists of an electrical control system, hydraulic power system, feed system, rotation system, and data monitoring system. The feeding system offers a thrust range of 0 ~ 100 kN, a penetration rate range of 0 ~ 50 mm/s, and a feed stroke range of 0 ~ 1000 mm, with the ability to control both the penetration rate and thrust during the drilling process. The rotation system provides a torque range from 0 ~ 400 N·m and a rotation speed range from 0 ~ 1000 r/min, enabling constant control of rotation speed. The drill rod used is a mining hexagonal anchor rod with a diameter of 19 mm, and the drill bit is a diamond composite bit with a diameter ranging from 28 ~ 32 mm.

Furthermore, the data acquisition system includes sensors for thrust, torque, displacement, rotational speed, and vibration. Each sensor outputs a 4 ~ 20 mA current signal, and the data from all sensors are collected and stored using a DH5983 data acquisition instrument. Experimental results

Response characteristics of drilling parameters

The data from the S4-B4 borehole were selected for analysis, with the penetration rate and rotation speed set at 5.0 mm/s and 400 rpm, respectively. The control parameters and drilling parameter curves are shown in Fig. 3. As depicted in Fig. 3(a), during the stable drilling phase, both the actual rotation speed and penetration rate of the rig fluctuate around the set values, indicating that the experimental system meets the requirements for this study. From Fig. 3(b), it can be observed that as the rock strength increases, both thrust and torque exhibit an upward trend. When transitioning from low-strength to high-strength rock, the torque shows a distinct “staircase” pattern, while the thrust increases gradually. This suggests that torque is more sensitive to changes in strength. Upon the drill bit passing through the strata interface at 150 mm, a sharp increase in both thrust and torque is observed. This phenomenon occurs because higher energy is required to fracture hard rock, leading to a corresponding increase in the thrust and torque output by the drilling rig. Additionally, as the drill bit transitions from low-strength to high-strength rock, the degree and rate of data fluctuation become significantly more pronounced. The interface transition phase lasts approximately 25 mm in length, with a duration of 6.2 s.

Construction of comprehensive index while drilling

(1) Modulation specific energy (SEM).

The process of rock cutting and fracturing is essentially an energy dissipation process in which the drill bit’s kinetic energy is “absorbed, accumulated, and released” by the rock. The energy required for rock fragmentation during this process is closely related to the mechanical properties of the rock. Teale proposed using the energy consumed during drilling-induced rock fragmentation as an indicator of rock properties, defining the drill bit’s kinetic energy required to break a unit volume of rock as the specific energy for drilling (SED)30. However, in practical applications, it was found that SED exhibits low specificity and high variance when identifying rock strength. To address this, Leung applied a logistic function and likelihood function to modulate the drilling energy ratio, thereby enhancing its sensitivity to changes in rock strength. Mutation detection of while-drilling index parameters based on Bayes algorithm

The principle of mutation detection

During the drilling process, when encountering discontinuous structures in different types of rock layers, while-drilling index parameters (including thrust, torque, SEM, and RDA) undergo various forms of abrupt changes. The key to identifying discontinuous structures using while-drilling index parameters lies in the precise detection of inflection points within these parameters that are associated with structural transitions. In rock layers of the same strength, drilling parameters typically fluctuate around a specific mean value. However, when the drill bit traverses a discontinuous structure, the while-drilling index parameters exhibit a sharp increase or decrease at the point of discontinuity. Upon re-entering a continuous rock layer, the while-drilling parameters will fluctuate around a new mean value. Thus, the abrupt changes in while-drilling index parameters at the location of the discontinuous structure can be characterized as mean-value shifts33.

The Bayes method treats model parameters and change points as random variables, assuming prior probability distributions for the parameters. By combining the prior distribution with the sample distribution, the posterior distribution of the change points can be determined, from which the number and potential magnitude of the abrupt changes can be inferred34,35. Single mutation point detection

The response sensitivity of different index parameters to variations in rock layer strength differs significantly. To identify the sensitive index parameters capable of effectively recognizing strata interfaces, this study analyzes the S1-B9 borehole, which corresponds to a lithological combination of M20 and M35, with penetration rate and rotation speed set at 5 mm/s and 600 rpm, respectively. Using the Bayes mutation detection algorithm, the data sequences for thrust, torque, RDA, and SEM were analyzed for abrupt changes. The location, number, and magnitude index (SLP) of the change points were determined for each index parameter, thereby allowing an assessment of the effectiveness of these parameters in recognizing the strata interfaces.

Figure 5 illustrates the mutation point detection results for the while-drilling parameters. As shown in Fig. 5(a), six distinct mutation points were detected using thrust. The sample numbers corresponding to these mutation points are sequentially 1167, 1318, 1889, 2012, 2124, and 2176, with the respective SLP values of −0.66, −0.51, 0.18, −0.19, 0.19, and − 1.43. Discontinuous structures identification method based on multi-indicator fusion

This study posits that the key to achieving while-drilling identification of discontinuous structures within rock layers lies in the accurate detection of discontinuity points in index parameters. As demonstrated in the previous research, the Bayes mutation detection algorithm is effective in detecting both individual and continuous mutation points. To accurately identify interfaces, fractures, weak interlayers, and other discontinuous structures within the rock layers based on the detected series of mutation points, this paper proposes a method for the regular identification of rock-layer discontinuous structures using multi-parameter fusion.

Criteria for discontinuous structures identification based on strength-interface fusion

In addition to strata interfaces, other discontinuous structures such as joints, fractures, and weak interlayers are commonly present within rock layers. These structures can be effectively modeled as variations in the distance and strength differences between two strata interfaces. Therefore, this study combines the dimensions and strength characteristics of different rock layer structures with interface distribution features to identify discontinuous structures. The aforementioned research has achieved strata interface detection based on multi-dimensional index parameters, and the use of these parameters for rock strength prediction has also gained widespread acceptance among scholars36,37. Based on this, this paper proposes the criteria for the regular identification of rock layer structures through strength-interface fusion, the basic principle of which is illustrated in Fig. 8.

The fundamental concept of this method is as follows: SEM and RDA are computed as composite indicators using while-drilling parameters. Thrust, torque, SEM, and RDA are then combined into a four-dimensional index parameter set, and the PSO-BP algorithm is applied to generate the rock strength prediction sequence during the drilling process. Subsequently, the multi-indicator fusion recognition method for multi-layer interfaces, described in Sect. 4.2, is employed to identify the sample numbers of valid mutation points associated with the rock layer structure and their corresponding drilling displacements. The displacements of adjacent mutation points are then sequentially calculated, and the type of rock layer structure is determined based on the displacement intervals and rock strength between adjacent mutation points. This study established a drilling parameter mutation detection model based on the Bayes algorithm and proposed a criterion for identifying rock strata interfaces and structures by fusing multiple parameter indicators. Compared with high-performance algorithms such as machine learning, this method is simpler to implement, requires less computing power, and has stronger on-site deployability.

Conclusion

This study addresses the issues in existing while-drilling identification methods for rock layer structures, such as the limitations of single algorithm models, low identification accuracy, and poor transferability. Based on indoor simulated rock drilling experiments, a mutation detection model for while-drilling index parameters based on the Bayes algorithm is established, and a multi-indicator fusion criterion for rock interface and structure identification is proposed. The main conclusions are as follows:

(1) During the drilling process, when transitioning from low-strength to high-strength rock, the torque exhibits a distinct “staircase” pattern, with torque being more sensitive to strength variations than thrust. The composite while-drilling indicators show a significant linear relationship with rock strength. The fitting equation for SEM and RDA with rock strength are expressed as SEM = 0.03Rc−0.23 and RDA=−0.02Rc + 3.87, respectively.

(2) The recognition errors for single mutation points detected using thrust, torque, SEM, and RDA are 13.3 mm, 4.6 mm, 4.4 mm, and 18.3 mm, respectively. The recognition rates for continuous mutation points are 83.3%, 100.0%, 66.7%, and 83.3% for each indicator, with torque providing the best mutation detection performance.

(3) The greater the fluctuation in the index parameters, the higher the average SLP value for the mutation points. The absolute SLP value of the mutation points at the interface location is generally the largest among all mutation points. In continuous mutation point identification, the SLP threshold should be set to ± 0.2. Additionally, the sign of the SLP is related to the direction of data change.

(4) Considering the high misclassification rate when using single index parameters to identify rock layer structures, a multi-indicator fusion method for rock interface and structure recognition is proposed. This method establishes criteria for identifying single-layer interfaces, multi-layer interfaces, joints and fractures, weak interlayers, and other discontinuous rock layer structures.
