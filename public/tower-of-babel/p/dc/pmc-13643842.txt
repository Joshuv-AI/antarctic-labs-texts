Self-Care Practices and Their Association with Glycemic Indices among Patients with Diabetes Mellitus Attending a rural Health Care Facility in Karnataka
Authors: P Monisha Mary, Ankeeta Menona Jacob, Avinash K Shetty
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Self-care plays a significant role in managing type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), particularly in rural settings. This cross-sectional study assessed the association between self-care behaviors and glycemic control among 142 T2DM patients at a rural healthcare facility in Karnataka. Data were collected using the diabetes self-care questionnaire (DSMQ) from March to April 2023. The mean age of participants was 55.3 ± 12.9 years, and the median duration of diabetes was 4 years. Chronic complications were reported in 74.6%, with foot and leg issues being the most common. The median blood sugar level was 208.5 mg/dl. Only 24.5% achieved optimal glucose management. Medication adherence was observed in 68.3%, with sulfonylureas (82.7%) and biguanides (56.3%) being the most commonly used drugs. Poor glycemic control was significantly associated with age >50 years and self-reported obesity. The findings highlight the need for targeted interventions to improve self-care, especially among older and overweight individuals, to achieve better glycemic outcomes in rural communities.

INTRODUCTION

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a leading cause of global morbidity and mortality. The prevalence of diabetes mellitus (DM) has nearly quadrupled over the past two decades and is projected to rise from 425 million in 2017 to 629 million by 2045, with over 90% of cases being T2DM.[1] Hyperglycemia contributes significantly to complications such as neuropathy, nephropathy, retinopathy, cardiovascular disease, and foot ulcers, necessitating intensive glycemic control. Yet, poor DM management remains widespread, underscoring the importance of practices such as blood sugar testing, medication adherence, and foot care.[2] Identifying the specific impact of self-care behaviors on glycemic control is significant for effective policy and intervention planning. Self-care, a complex yet natural decision-making process, plays a vital role in chronic disease management, with Riegel’s theory emphasizing the importance of self-confidence and self-efficacy in ensuring its effectiveness.[3] This study explores the association between self-care behaviors and glycemic control among T2DM patients in rural Karnataka.

METHODOLOGY

Study design and population

A cross-sectional analytical study was undertaken at the Rural Health Centre affiliated with the institution in Karnataka. The study was conducted over two months, from March to April 2023, among 142 consenting adults (≥18 years) with T2DM attending the center.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated as 142 based on an estimated 8% prevalence of self-care practices among T2DM patients in South Asia, derived from a 2022 systematic review by Paudel G, Vandelanotte C, Dahal P, et al.,[4] with 5% absolute precision and 95% confidence, using the Statulator tool.

Sampling method

The study adopted a nonprobability (consecutive) sampling approach until the desired sample size of 142 was achieved.

Study tool

Self-care practices were assessed using the diabetes self-management questionnaire (DSMQ), a validated 16-item tool covering four domains: glucose management, dietary control, physical activity, and healthcare use. The DSMQ has high internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha: 0.84–0.94) and strong construct validity.[5] A pilot test was conducted to ensure clarity and suitability of the questionnaire for the study population.

Method of data collection

After Scientific and Ethical Committee clearance, T2DM patients attending the health center during the study period were approached. The pre-tested semi-structured questionnaire contained three parts, which are attached as Supplementary Table 1.

Statistical analysis

The questionnaire was converted into a form using Epicollect V5.0 to capture the responses from interviews with DM patients, and the data were transferred to MS Excel. Quantitative data were presented as mean, standard deviation, median, or interquartile range. Qualitative variables were expressed as percentages and proportions. The Epicollect software (Epicollect5 is developed and maintained by the Oxford Big Data Institute at Oxford University) form, which contained the responses of the study’s participants, was analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (International Business Machines Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). The Chi-square test was employed to assess categorical variables and examine the relationship between DSMQ scores (Diabetes Self-Management Questionnaire (DSMQ), a validated 16 item tool, was used) and blood sugar levels.

RESULTS

Table 1 presents the demographic and clinical characteristics of the participants. A majority were aged ≥50 years, 87 (61.3%), and had education up to the primary level, 85 (59.8%). Most participants were married, 118 (82.5%). Chronic complications were reported by 106 (74.6%) patients, with foot and leg issues being the most common, 35 (25.4%). The duration of DM was more than 5 years in 102 (72.0%) participants. Sulfonylureas were the most commonly used medication, 115 (80.4%). Only 45 (31.7%) adhered to their prescribed regimens, and adequate self-care practices were observed in just 35 (24.5%) participants, reflecting a substantial gap in diabetes self-management.

DISCUSSION

This study highlights that only 24.5% of T2DM patients in a rural Karnataka setting demonstrated adequate self-care practices, with low medication adherence and physical activity being key concerns. Age over 50 years and self-reported obesity were significantly associated with suboptimal glycemic control.

Similar findings have been reported across India and other developing countries. A study conducted at a rural health training center in Lucknow showed high adherence rates; however, nearly one-third of patients still forgot their medications, suggesting forgetfulness as a common behavioral challenge even among those considered adherent.[6] In our study, medication adherence was similarly low, despite most participants being on treatment. Such behavioral gaps align with our findings, particularly the low adherence to prescribed regimens observed in 68.3% of participants. In southern India, a study in Mangalore[7] reported 82% medication adherence, especially among literate patients. This supports the known link between education and better adherence, although in our rural population, this association was not statistically significant.

Our results, showing poorer control among older and obese participants, reflect similar patterns. Several factors may explain these findings. Older adults may face age-related fatigue, cognitive decline, and financial limitations that hinder regular self-care. Obesity-related stigma or physical barriers could reduce physical activity. Health literacy, even among the educated, may remain low due to inadequate health education in rural settings. Cultural norms and gender roles may also limit time and autonomy for disease management. A geriatric study from India[8] supports these points, noting that comorbidities, disability, and social barriers significantly affect diabetes self-care in older individuals.

These findings have important implications for rural health policy and practice. Structured diabetes education catering to local language, culture, and literacy levels is essential. Primary healthcare centers should integrate pictorial tools, simplified drug instructions, and personalized counseling. Community health workers and family members can play an active role in monitoring and supporting daily self-care. Regular follow-ups, reminders via SMS or printed calendars, and access to affordable, healthy food and physical activity spaces can further reinforce good practices.

Our findings are supported by both global and Indian studies. In rural China, integrated self-care programs improved DM outcomes by strengthening government support, providing behavioral guidance, and offering regular follow-up services.[9] Similarly, an Indian randomized controlled trial showed that even a brief self-care intervention given during routine clinic visits led to significant improvements in self-care behaviors among T2DM patients.[10] These studies highlight that both large-scale programs and small, practical strategies can be effective in improving diabetes management, especially in rural settings.

Recent evidence from DM-focused behavioral studies shows that providing knowledge alone is often insufficient to change health behavior. Research on self-care among patients with T2DM indicates that awareness leads to meaningful action only when supported by personalized counseling and ongoing guidance. This suggests that similar behavioral mechanisms may influence how DM patients perceive their risk of TB.

For example, Wondm SA, Zeleke TK, Dagnew SB, Moges TA, Tarekegn GY, Belachew EA, et al.[11] in 2024 found that patients who actively engaged in recommended self-care practices had better health outcomes, highlighting the importance of behavioral reinforcement rather than passive knowledge acquisition. Similarly, Ubaidi BAAA, Abdulla KJ, Jenaidi NAA, Ali HA, Marhoon EM, Matar HM, et al.[12] in 2024 reported improved outcomes when patient education was supported by continuous counseling. Although these studies focused on DM management, their findings are relevant to TB–DM collaborative care, where sustained engagement and personalized risk communication may help improve TB risk perception among patients with DM.

Interventional studies also highlight the importance of family and community involvement. A randomized controlled trial by Radhakrishnan RV, Thirunavukkarasu P, Raghav P, Goel AD[13] in 2025 showed that family-centered training significantly improved self-care practices among DM patients. Incorporating family-based approaches into Tuberculosis (TB) education for individuals with T2DM may therefore enhance perceived susceptibility, reduce stigma, and encourage timely health-seeking behaviors in rural communities.

Strengths

A key strength of this study is its use of a validated, multidimensional tool (DSMQ) to assess self-care behaviors in a rural Indian population. However, its cross-sectional design limits causal inference. Self-reported data may be subject to recall and social desirability bias. The modest sample size may limit the generalizability of findings, although it still offers important insights into behavioral gaps in rural DM management.

Limitations

The study has another limitation that obesity was assessed based on self-reporting rather than objective body mass index calculation. Participants were asked whether they considered themselves obese without actual measurement of height and weight. This introduces the possibility of bias due to personal perception, stigma, or lack of awareness. Future studies should consider including anthropometric measurements to ensure more accurate classification of obesity.

CONCLUSION

The study found that only one-fourth of participants effectively managed their glucose levels and engaged in regular physical activity. Those aged over 50 years and self-reporting obesity faced challenges with glycemic control. These findings warrant the need for targeted interventions, especially for older individuals and those dealing with obesity, to improve DM management in rural communities. Addressing these concerns can enhance well-being and guide future healthcare interventions in similar settings
