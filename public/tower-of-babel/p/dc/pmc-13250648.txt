Pattern of height and weight development in a large cohort of underage Chinese pseudohypoparathyroidism patients from a single center
Authors: Yi Yang, An Song, Hanze Du, Yan Jiang, Mei Li, Weibo Xia, Xiaoping Xing, Ou Wang, Hui Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivePseudohypoparathyroidism (PHP) is a rare disorder characterized by hypocalcemia and elevated PTH. Although short stature is a key feature, especially in PHP type 1 (PHP1), growth data in Chinese patients remained limited.MethodsClinical data, including height, age, and biochemical indices, were retrospectively collected from PHP1 patients at Peking Union Medical College Hospital. Molecular diagnosis was performed using MS-MLPA, Sanger sequencing, and WES. Growth charts for height, weight, and BMI in underage patients were constructed.ResultsA total of 92 PHP1 patients (58 males, 34 females), including 32 pseudohypoparathyroidism type 1A (PHP-1A), 49 sporadic pseudohypoparathyroidism type 1B (PHP-S1B), and 11 autosomal dominant pseudohypoparathyroidism type 1B (PHP-AD1B), were recruited. Growth velocity peaked at 12 years in males and 5 years in females, with height plateau at 14 and 13 years, respectively. Adult height was 160.0 cm (SDS −1.04) in males and 155.0 cm (SDS −0.52) in females. PHP-1A patients had significantly shorter adult height than PHP-1B (male: 157.0 ± 6.9 vs 171.9 ± 8.9, P = 0.004; female: 146.8 ± 10.2 vs 156.2 ± 4.9, P = 0.007). PHP-1A showed early-onset weight gain from age 1, persisting into adulthood. Adult Wt-SDS was 1.67 vs 1.55 in males and 2.00 vs 3.10 in females (PHP-1A vs PHP-1B). Weight curves exceeded population P50 in most groups, with no significant differences in adult weight, Wt-SDS, BMI, or obesity prevalence between subtypes.ConclusionChinese PHP1 patients show early growth plateau and short stature, more severe in PHP-1A. Obesity varies by subtype and sex, with earlier and more persistent weight gain in PHP-1A. These growth charts may aid clinical management.

Introduction

Pseudohypoparathyroidism (PHP) is a group of heterogeneous disorders featuring hypocalcemia and elevated parathyroid hormone (PTH) level, mainly due to defected PTH receptor and resulting in resistance to PTH in the proximal renal tubule. PHP is traditionally divided into PHP type 1 (PHP1) and PHP type 2 (PHP2) based on the response to exogenous PTH in urine cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP). With the advancement of genetic molecular diagnostic technologies, it is generally believed that the malfunctioning in PTH receptor is mostly the result of (epi)genetic defects in GNAS gene or inactivated mutations in other genes in the Gsα/cAMP/PKA signaling pathway. PHP1 is further divided into PHP-1A and PHP-1B depending on varied defects on the GNAS gene (1). Inactivating mutation (PHP-1A) and methylation alteration of GNAS (PHP-1B) can lead to haploinsufficiency or malfunctioning of the α subunit of stimulatory G protein (Gsα). Importantly, the GNAS locus is subject to complex genomic imprinting. While Gsα is biallelically expressed in most tissues, paternal Gsα expression is selectively silenced in certain tissues, including the proximal renal tubule, thyroid, pituitary, and gonads. As a result, hormone responsiveness in these tissues predominantly depends on the maternal Gsα allele. This tissue-specific imprinting explains why maternally inherited GNAS mutations lead to hormone resistance (PHP-1A), whereas paternally inherited mutations typically result in pseudo-pseudohypoparathyroidism (PPHP) without endocrine abnormalities. PHP-1B can be further divided into autosomal dominant PHP-1B (PHP-AD1B), which carries both deletion in the gene STX16 and altered methylation of GNAS, and sporadic PHP-1B (PHP-S1B) with only epigenetic alteration in GNAS.

The abnormalities in calcium phosphorus metabolic features have been described thoroughly over the years ever since the first case of PHP was reported by Albright et al. (2). Apart from changes in biochemical indices and manifestations relating to hypocalcemia, other clinical manifestations include Albright’s hereditary osteodystrophy (AHO) and resistance to hormone, which interacts with Gsa-coupled receptors (e.g. thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) and growth hormone release hormone (GHRH)) Among these clinical manifestations, short stature and obesity received much less attention even though they have been included in AHO, which describes typical physical characteristics of PHP patients (1). Individuals affected by PHP, especially PHP-1A, PPHP, and acrodysostosis, have been reported in several studies to have severe short stature of around −2.5 standard deviation (SD) (3, 4, 5, 6), and a multi-center cohort of PHP patients showed that overweight in PHP patients could be early-onset (7). Clinical evidence on the prevailing of short adult height and obesity is accumulating, but there still lack direct description of the growth pattern of children affected by PHP in China.

In this study, we retrospectively described the growth pattern and built the growth curve of height, weight, and body mass index (BMI) of underage Chinese patients clinically diagnosed with PHP based on a rather large single-center cohort. For those with clear molecular subtyping with PHP-1A and PHP-1B, we provided a specified description and comparison.

Subjects and methods

Clinical data

From August 1996 to February 2023, patients diagnosed with PHP clinically in the department of endocrinology, Peking Union Medical College Hospital (PUMCH), were consecutively enrolled in this study. Diagnosis of PHP was established upon serum calcium (Ca) lower than 2.13 mmol/L combined with elevated serum PTH level in the absence of severe vitamin D deficiency (serum 25-hydroxy vitamin D lower than 20 ng/mL) and other conditions causing secondary hyperparathyroidism. Exclusion criteria were unclear molecular subtyping, idiopathic or secondary hypoparathyroidism, abnormal renal function, severe liver failure, history of using recombinant human growth hormone (rhGH) and/or gonadotrophin-releasing hormone analog (GnRHa) and/or aromatase inhibitor, and/or had used medications that could affect calcium/phosphorus metabolism except for calcium and vitamin D agents. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of PUMCH (Ethics approval registration number JS-3314). Consent has been obtained from each patient after full explanation of the purpose of obtaining relating clinical information.

Clinical data were collected retrospectively from the medical records maintained at PUMCH, including demographic characteristics, clinical manifestations (e.g. seizure, dumbness of face or limbs, brachydactyly, ectopic ossifications, round face), and family history. The age of onset was defined as the age when PHP-relating symptoms first showed up or abnormal biochemical indicators were first detected, and the year of duration was defined as the time lapse between the age of onset and the age of PHP diagnosis.

Biochemical indices and relating hormones evaluated at the patients’ first visit were recorded. Serum calcium (Ca), serum phosphorus (P), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), 24-hour urine calcium (24hUCa), 24-hour urine phosphate (24hUP), serum magnesium (Mg), serum potassium (K), serum natrium (Na), serum creatinine (Cr), luteinizing hormone (LH), and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) levels were measured with an automatic biochemical analyzer (AU5800, Beckman Coulter, USA). The serum insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF1) began to be tested in 2009 at our center (8) using an immunoassay system (Immulite 2000, Siemens, Germany). IGF1-standard deviation score (IGF1-SDS) was calculated by dividing the difference between the measured IGF1 and the medium value of each age group and gender. Serum PTH levels were detected by different methods over time: mid-molecule PTH radioimmunoassay (Los Angeles Diagnostic Reagent Co, USA; August 1996–July 1998), intact PTH (iPTH) chemiluminescence (ADVIA Centaur; Siemens, Germany; August 1998–November 2016), and iPTH electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (E170; Roche Diagnostics, Switzerland; since December 2016). Serum 25-hydroxy vitamin D (25OHD) was quantified using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA; pre-2014) and electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (E170; Roche Diagnostics, Switzerland; since 2014). Plasma adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) was measured by chemiluminescence (Cobas e601, Roche, Germany). Serum free triiodothyronine (FT3), free thyroxine (FT4), thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), and cortisol (F) were assayed by chemiluminescence (Siemens Centaur XP).

Growth parameter

The height and weight of each visit were obtained from the medical records. BMI was calculated according to the formula: weight (kg)/(height (m))^2. The age of every patient at each visit was calculated based on the date of birth extracted from the ID card and the date of each visit. The SDS of height/weight was calculated by dividing the difference between the patient’s and the average height/weight of the patient’s gender and age group by the standard deviation of height/weight of the same gender and age group (9, 10). The height, weight, and calculated BMI were plotted against the age to form the growth curve. Growth velocity (GV, cm/y) was calculated by height changes between two consecutive visits of each individual.

Molecular analysis

Collection of peripheral blood and subsequent genetic analysis was performed after acquiring the full consent of the patient or the guardians of underage patients. Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood leukocytes using a commercial kit (RelaxGene Blood DNA System, Tiangen, China). Methylation status and copy number analysis of GNAS and STX16 as well as methylation status test were conducted by methylation-specific multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification (MS-MLPA) using the ME031A kit (MRC-Holland, The Netherlands) following the instructions of the manufacturer’s protocol. Analysis and interpretation of the results were done as described previously (11). Genetic analysis for point mutation was then conducted with Sanger sequencing as previously described (12) or by whole exome sequencing (WES) at Berry Genomics, Beijing, China, as previously described (13).

Statistical analysis

Numerical results were analyzed by SPSS24 (SPSS Inc, USA). Growth charts were generated in R (version 4.3.1) using the ggplot2 package. Growth trajectories were estimated using locally weighted regression (LOESS) smoothing implemented through the geom_smooth() function. The shaded areas represent the 95% confidence intervals of the fitted curves. To avoid extrapolation bias, fitted curves and confidence intervals were displayed only within age ranges supported by observed data. All data that were normally distributed were described by the mean and standard deviation. Non-normally distributed data were described as the median (Q1, Q3). Patients enrolled were divided into groups according to gender and molecular subtype (PHP-1A and PHP-1B), while indices regarding body development were compared between groups and across age. Student t-test was used to examine the differences between groups of normally distributed data, and the non-parametric test was used to examine the differences between groups of non-normally distributed data. A P-value lower than 0.05 would be considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical characteristics and basic biochemical features of all subjects

A total of 92 PHP1 patients (58 males, 34 females) were included in this study (Table 1). Seizure, clamps of limbs, and hypothyroidism revealed by routine health check-up were the most often observed main complaints. The serum Ca, P, and iPTH were 1.90 ± 0.33 mmol/L, 2.26 ± 0.55 mmol/L, and 347.70 (236.00, 507.50) pg/mL, respectively. The number of patients in each molecular subgroup of PHP-1A, PHP-S1B, and PHP-AD1B was 32 (male 20, female 12), 49 (male 30, female 19), and 11 (male 8, female 3), respectively. None of our patients bore mutations of PRKAR1A or PDE4D that could result in acrodysostosis. The age of onset in patients of PHP-1A was significantly younger than PHP-AD1B and PHP-S1B patients (8.0 ± 7.8 vs 10.6 ± 4.2 and 10.4 ± 3.8 years; P = 0.002 and 0.049, respectively). The median duration of disease in the PHP-1A group was significantly longer than that in PHP-AD1B, though similar to the PHP-S1B group (4.00 vs 2.15 and vs 3.00; P = 0.046 and 0.258, respectively). The serum biomarkers, including serum Ca, P, and iPTH, did not differ significantly between the three molecular subgroups.

There were 34 patients who had IGF1 evaluated, 3 of whom had the IGF1 level lower than normal range (all female; two in the PHP-1A group and one in PHP-S1B). Two PHP-1A patients kept regular visit till age 18, and their adult height was both under −3.0 SDS. The serum IGF1 level of patients in PHP-1A group was significantly lower than that of patients in other two groups. The average IGF1-SDS of the general cohort was 0.06. Only PHP-1A group presented with IGF1-SDS that was lower than 0 (−0.60), but the differences between the three molecular subtype groups were not significant. One female patient of the 1A subgroup underwent L-dopa GH stimulation test at the age of 15 (height 147.0 cm), and the peak GH level was 7.65 ng/mL, supporting the presence of GH deficiency. Her adult height measured at the age of 18 was 149.0 cm (below −3.0 SDS of the general female Chinese population).

Thyroid (n = 69), gonadal (n = 26), and adrenal cortex functions (n = 25) were also evaluated. A total of 21 (30.4%) patients were euthyroid, while 48 (69.6%) patients had TSH resistance (12 were hypothyroidism and 36 were subclinical hypothyroidism). The proportion of TSH resistance in PHP-1A patients was significantly higher than that in PHP-S1B patients (74.2% vs 48.8%, P = 0.045), and a similar trend, although not significant difference, was also found between PHP-1A and all PHP-1B patients (P = 0.079). Levels of FSH and LH in the PHP-1A group were significantly higher than those in the PHP-S1B group (P = 0.024 and 0.031, respectively). Only one female patient in the PHP-1A group claimed amenorrhea at the age of 16 although the patient presented with Tanners’ breast stage 3 and pubic hair stage 4, while no other patients reported signs of pubertal delay/failure. None of the patients was found with elevated ACTH level or decreased cortisol level, and no difference had been found among groups.

Growth pattern of height in different age groups of PHP patients

Among the 92 patients, the number of height records of underage PHP patients was 153 and 144 for male and female, respectively. The height-for-age, Ht-SDS, and GV are displayed in Table 2. The average Ht-SDS went below zero since the age group of 11 (except age group 12) in males and 13 in females, respectively. As shown in Fig. 1A and B, the height for all of the male and female patients reached plateau at the ages of 14 and 13, and GV peaked at 12-year-old for male and at 5-year-old for female, respectively. The median adult height of male and female patients was 160.0 cm (SDS = −1.04) and 155.0 cm (SDS = −0.52), respectively (Table 3).

Growth pattern of height in patients of PHP-1A and PHP-1B

The growth pattern of height in PHP-1A and PHP-1B subgroups is depicted in Fig. 1C, D, E, F. The male PHP-1A patients had their height-for-age constantly below that of general population at each age groups, and their height eventually lagged below P3 of healthy children since age 13–14. On the other hand, female PHP-1A patient had an average height similar to healthy children before reaching the plateau at age of 11 and started to go below the P50 since age of 12 and even below P10 since 16.

The final height was available for 40 PHP1 patients of 23 male and 17 female (Table 3). The median adult height and Ht-SDS for male and female PHP1 patients was 168.0 and −1.10, 157.0 and −0.49, respectively. Among them, 14 (35.0%) had an adult Ht-SDS lower than −2.00 (3 female PHP-1A, 9 male PHP-1A, and 2 male PHP-S1B). The adult heights in both male and female PHP-1A patients were significantly lower than the patients from the PHP-1B group (P = 0.004 and 0.007, respectively).

Growth pattern of weight and BMI in patients of PHP-1A and PHP-1B

Growth curves of weight-for-age and BMI-for-age shown in Figs 2C, E and 3C, E indicated that the weight and BMI in both male and female PHP-1A patients were significantly higher than P50 of the general population. The weight and BMI in female PHP-1A patients gradually dropped to levels near P75 during growth, and the adult weight and BMI remained the similar level. However, male PHP-1A patients’ and female PHP-1B patients’ weight and BMI kept increasing and did not show the same trend as female PHP-1A patients did.

Adult weight data were available in 12 patients of PHP-1A and 29 of PHP-1B. The male PHP-1A patients tended to have a higher BMI than those in the PHP-1B group, although the difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.051). There were no significant differences in adult weight, Wt-SDS, and the number of overweight/obese individuals between the male PHP-1A and PHP-1B groups. Among females, no significant difference in adult weight, Wt-SDS, BMI, and the number of overweight/obese individuals was found between PHP-1A and PHP-1B groups.

Discussion

Based on a sizable cohort and molecular subtyping, this study detailed the growth pattern of Chinese PHP patients that regardless of gender, patients with PHP-1A presented with the most impaired adult height, whereas patients with PHP-1B had a relatively normal adult height. Calculated GV and SDS suggested that PHP patients’ growth rates peaked during prepubertal time, and signs of short stature symptoms may exhibit since puberty. On the other hand, underage PHP patients generally presented a higher-than-normal body weight which could exhibit as early as in their first year of life and persist during the patients’ childhood, and eventually result in overweight/obese in adult. Similar to height, PHP-1A patients’ weight was more affected than that of PHP-1B patients.

Our research showed that the Chinese PHP patients suffered from affected adult height, which was more obvious in PHP-1A patients. Hanna et al. conducted a multi-center study across the US and several European countries, noticing a non-negligible affected adult height in a total of 242 PHP-1A (final Ht-SDS −2.5 ± 1.1) and 64 PPHP (final Ht-SDS −1.9 ± 1.2) patients, although the final height of PHP-1B patients seemed to be unaffected (final Ht-SDS −0.4 ± 1.1) (7), which was also found in our study. However, the adult height and calculated SDS of each subtype in both genders between the current Chinese cohort and the work by Hanna et al. did not show a significant difference. Ludar et al.’s research on a small Israeli PHP-1A patients met with a similar result (14). The above results suggested that the disease cast similar adverse impact on patients from different ethnicities. It is reasonable to presume that differences in molecular defects to be the decisive reason of this remarkable disparity, since the inactivating mutation in PHP-1A patients casts a more adverse effect on transcription and translation than the methylation alteration in PHP-1B patients. These phenotypic differences may also be interpreted in the context of genomic imprinting at the GNAS locus. Because paternal Gsα expression is selectively silenced in specific endocrine tissues, hormone signaling in these organs largely depends on the maternal allele. Consequently, maternally inherited inactivating mutations (PHP-1A) result in broader endocrine resistance and more pronounced growth impairment, whereas epigenetic alterations in PHP-1B primarily affect renal PTH responsiveness without consistently disrupting Gsα expression in growth-related tissues. This tissue-specific imprinting may, therefore, contribute to the observed differences in growth patterns and metabolic phenotypes between subtypes. Previous research suggested that deletion/inactivation of Gnas greatly affected chondrocytes, which eventually resulted in brachydactyly and short stature (15, 16). Nevertheless, the impact of GNAS deficiency on the occurrence of growth hormone deficiency (GHD) (1, 7, 17) and TSH resistance (18, 19) has been found, but the differences in prevalence between subtypes have only been suggested in several clinical studies (12, 20, 21). Evidence is still needed to evaluate the power of factors other than genetic defects on the progression on short statue of PHP patients.

In this cohort of PHP patients, the growth velocity peaked at the ages of 12 and 5 for male and female, respectively, which both advanced the normal population (for male, 12 vs 14 years; for female, 5 vs 10 years) (10). Their growth spurt also ended earlier when compared to healthy children (for male, 14 vs 16; for female, 12 vs 15). The growth velocity of underage PHP patients had only been reported in the above-mentioned Hanna et al.’s research (7). Given that the excess weight gain has long been associated with early-onset puberty (22), the advanced age of growth acceleration could be a result of elevated estrogen promoted by weight gain (which was often observed in PHP population (1) due to elevated aromatase expression in adipose tissue (23)). Our findings may suggest that an early intervention for short stature should start as early as the PHP patients’ growth is still seemingly unaffected. An earlier termination of growth also adds necessity of early diagnosis and treatment upon height management of PHP patients.

Obesity/overweight is another clinical characteristic of PHP patients, which had received little attention during the treatment of PHP. This study showed that obesity could not only present as early as in the first year of life but also last throughout the childhood and result in adulthood obesity, especially in PHP-1A patients. There were two previous cohort studies, which systematically analyzed the features of weight gain in children with PHP. Long et al. reported that within 31 underage PHP patients from the US, the average BMI percentile was 95.4 ± 2.7, and the obesity/overweight prevalence was 83.9%, which both far exceeded the data presented in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Surveys (24, 25). Hanna et al.’s multi-centered study suggested that obesity in PHP-1A patients could appear as early as infancy with a 1-year-old Wt-SDS of 3.5 ± 2.3 (n = 169), which could persist until the patient reaches adulthood. With the 18-year-old BMI-SDS of 2.2 ± 2.0 (n = 67), obesity in PHP-1A patients was more severe than in PHP-1B patients (median BMI-SDS at age 18 was 2.6 and 1.1 for PHP-AD1B (n = 21) and PHP-S1B (n = 50)) (7). The occurrence of obesity in infant PHP1 patients, where feeding and lifestyle influences are minimal, suggests that the genetic defect could be influential. Defected GNAS has been hypothesized to impair pathways including the β2-adrenergic receptor pathway (26), melanocortin receptor 4 (MC4R) pathway (27), and corticotropin-releasing hormone receptor (CRHR) pathway (28). These pathways play roles in external cold stimulation response and regulation of feeding behavior. Thus, GNAS mutations might cause obesity by affecting thermogenesis and energy expenditure, as well as feeding behavior. Enlightened by a single-center observation of six obese female PHP-1A patients, which showed decreased resting energy expenditure compared to simple obesity male controls (29), it should be reasonable to presume that weight management in children with PHP should not be limited to controlling calorie intake through healthy diets, but should also involve interventions targeting the abnormalities in energy expenditure pathways.

In this study, the adult BMI and weight growth curves suggested that adult female PHP-1B patients’ weight was similar to that of female PHP-1A patients, and this was not observed in male PHP1 patients. The previous multi-center study also found that the mean adult BMI for female patients in PHP-1A, PHP-AD1B, and PHP-S1B groups was 29.2 ± 6.2, 28.8 ± 8.9, and 26.2 ± 4.7, respectively, which were all higher than their respective male counterparts (26.2 ± 4.7, 24.4 ± 3.7, and 25.0 ± 6.4) (7). These differences suggested the possible gender-specific metabolic pathways, warranting further research into the pathogenesis of obesity in PHP from this perspective.

The retrospective nature of this study resulted in certain limitations, although the cohort was relatively large when considering the rareness of PHP. First, previous growth records from patients whose initial symptoms came out late, especially after pubertal period were unavailable. The reasons above partly contributed to the rather small sample size in certain age and molecular subgroups and caused bias. Second, parental genetic testing was unavailable for most of the patients’ family members, thereby preventing us from determining whether the identified mutations in PHP-1A cases were of maternal origin or de novo. Due to similar reasons, another limitation of this study was that we did not include PPHP cases in our cohort. Only three patients had sufficiently complete longitudinal data for analysis. As PPHP is not associated with hormone resistance and generally presents with milder manifestations, parental concern and follow-up compliance are often lower than in PHP-1A. Consequently, several suspected PPHP cases were not genetically confirmed or were lost to follow-up, precluding reliable statistical analysis in this subgroup. In addition, GH and IGF1 were not evaluated routinely. Thus, it was difficult to assess the severity of GHD and its impact accurately. Moreover, bone age and the Tanner stage were seldom evaluated regularly in this cohort of PHP patients; thus, we were unable to systematically evaluate the difference between chronological age and bone age, which we presume may provide more information on skeletal maturation features on PHP patients.

Conclusion

This study described the growth pattern of Chinese patients affected with PHP based on a relatively large cohort. It observed that PHP patients suffered from early growth termination, which ultimately resulted in short stature. The adult height in PHP-1A patients was more affected than in PHP-1B patients. Differences also exist in the severity of obesity between the two molecular subtypes and between genders, with PHP-1A patients experiencing more pronounced obesity, which can be early-onset and persist over time. The difference in final height and weight gain between molecular subtypes should certainly call for the importance of genetic analysis once PHP is diagnosed. With the help of visualized and quantified growth chart, treatments for short stature and early-onset obesity could be better conducted and effectiveness of such medication could be monitored in the near future.
