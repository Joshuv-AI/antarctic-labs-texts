Food chain risk assessment of cadmium accumulation and the relationships among diversely irrigated pasture-reared ruminants
Authors: Ifra Saleem Malik, Zafar Iqbal Khan, Kafeel Ahmad, Muhammad Nadeem, Shahzad Akhtar, Asma Ashfaq, Mohammad Faisal, Abdulrahman A. Alatar, Mudasra Munir, Saif Ullah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The contamination of various forage types with augmented concentrations of metal components carries substantial hygienic importance, as it functions as the principal pathway for the assimilation of these constituents into the food web. The purpose of this research was to explore the possible impacts on health hazards linked to cadmium contamination via diverse metrics within the food web, stemming from the ingestion of regularly consumed forages grown on polluted sites in the proximity of district Khushab, Pakistan. Specimens of water (n = 100), forages (n = 240), soil (n = 240), ruminant’s blood (n = 100) and milk (n = 100) were procured from two distinct locations; Chabeel (S_1) & Nalli Shumali (S_2) in the winter and summer of 2023-24, and were scrutinized for cadmium levels utilizing an atomic absorption spectrophotometer. The average levels of cadmium ranged from 0.09 to 0.58 mg kg−1, 0.04–1.02 mg kg−1, 0.21-1.25mg L−1, 0.17–2.98 mg L−1, 0.68–4.68 mg L−1, 0.24–1.25 mg L−1, and 0.15–0.96 mg L−1 in soil, forages, water, cows’ blood, buffaloes’ blood, cows’ milk, and buffaloes’ milk, accordingly. Highly significantly perfect positive strong correlation was detected between the water of S_1 vs. soil of S_2 during the winter season. In animals, the maximum cadmium concentrations were appraised in blood, whereas minimum in milk. The following indices values; BCF, EF, Eri, HRI, and THQ were > 1 indicating health hazard consequences of cadmium for consumers during both seasons at both sites of study region. The assessment and continual monitoring of the concentrations of conceivably detrimental heavy metals and metalloids in diverse environmental constituents and the indigenous biota is of utmost significance. In the future, it is imperative to strongly support the consistent monitoring of noxious metal levels in the verdant expanse encircling polluted areas.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-13628-6.

Introduction

Forages are esteemed as the paramount component in the advancement of agronomy and the provision of ruminants’ sustenance. These forages are predominantly accountable for safeguarding indigenous species and also function as a soil covering to shelter the earth. Animals serve as an intermediary in the food web that humans utilize to obtain forage for sustenance. Varieties of plants, soil, development stage, grazing control, weather conditions, and crop output, are merely some of the factors that interplay to ascertain the nutrients in pastures1. Plants serve as superb indicators of ecological conditions and respond directly to the quality of the atmosphere, water, and soil. Forages under particular environmental conditions could uptake deleterious metals stemming both from the land and metal-based sediments on the outer surface of vegetation constituents subjected to a polluted surroundings. As plants possess an innate capacity to extract pollutants from their proximate environment, their chemical composition can unveil the magnitude of disturbances when compared to benchmark metrics obtained from untainted plant specimens. The majority of heavy metals pose significant apprehension for livestock owing to their deleterious impacts. The sustenance of grazing animals entails a complex interplay among animal, vegetation, and soil2. The uptake and deposition of metallic substances within a plant hinge onto diverse soil variables, encompassing electrical conductivity, acidity, organic matter content, clay concentration, as well as the mechanical and physical characteristics within the soil. Plants procure noxious metallic elements within the soil via various mechanisms including ion exchange, assimilation, redox reactions, and similar processes. Ruminants, as they forage on this herbage, accumulate these constituents in their body, thus their serum, milk, and excrement serve as significant markers due to their distinct functions within the uptake, translocation, and elimination of dense elements. Periodic sampling and examination of serum, milk, and excrement empower environmental authorities and researchers to acquire insights regarding the scale of noxious metallic pollution in the fodder ingested by livestock and, consequently, the extended environment ecological system3.

Livestock and agriculture rearing comprise the predominant means of subsistence for the native community in Punjab, Pakistan. In the quest for enhancing productivity, farmers frequently utilize a wide array of agricultural methodologies. These entail the application of synthetic fertilizers, agronomic chemicals, livestock excrement, and the watering of crops utilizing diverse forms of sewage water4. The aggregate consequence of these implementations is the accrual of noxious metallic constituents in the soil, water, and fodder. Though some investigations have delved into soil ingestion during grazing, a thorough examination of the multifarious pathways via which metallic elements are assimilated by herbivorous could provide a compendious comprehension of their true aggregations. Thus, an extensive comprehension of the series of procedures influencing the constitution of minute constituents in soil, sustenance, and forages is essential for a more profound understanding of the complex interaction among soil, animal, and plant, which, thereby, dictates the well-being hazard for both humans and ruminants5. There is widespread consensus that the dissolvable and inherently interchangeable portions of minute constituents are accessible to flora and fauna, incorporating the overall metallic levels. Investigations have shown that soil pollution by metallic elements such as zinc, copper, cadmium, lead, and chromium has similarly prompted an increase in the bioaccumulation of these metallic elements within plant specimens and fodder. Tackling the challenge of noxious elements permeating the food web necessitates thorough evaluations to arrive at prompt conclusions and avert severe health repercussions due to the often inconspicuous manifestation of heavy metal toxicity6.

Cadmium is naturally present in soil, but its entry into the food system occurs through a range of sources, including industrial waste from coal, batteries, and metal industries, as well as phosphate fertilizers. Limited exposure to cadmium can result in symptoms like diarrhea and stomach irritation. The accumulation of cadmium in tissues, particularly the liver and kidneys, is linked to adverse effects such as anemia and elevated blood pressure7. Additionally, cadmium is recognized as a carcinogen, particularly for lung and prostate cancers, contributing to the development of tumors. Cadmium also contributes to a range of health issues including kidney dysfunction, bone problems, lung diseases, liver dysfunction, heart problems, and vascular diseases. The diet rich in fiber can diminish the absorption of cadmium, while individuals with low iron levels show a higher rate of cadmium resorption. Reduced intakes of vitamin D, calcium, and essential trace elements like zinc and copper can elevate cadmium uptake. An escalation in cadmium levels can prompt the body to replace zinc in the arteries, rendering them rigid and prone to fragility. Cadmium toxicity in plants can manifest as a range of adverse effects, including hindrance of plant growth by inhibiting cell division and elongation, leading to diminished overall size8. Chlorosis (yellowing) of leaves may occur due to disrupted chlorophyll synthesis and reduced photosynthesis. Cadmium accumulation can harm plant roots, impairing water and nutrient absorption. Elevated cadmium levels can interfere with the uptake of essential nutrients such as iron, zinc, and calcium. Additionally, cadmium generates reactive oxygen species, causing oxidative stress and cellular damage in plants. While cadmium is not an essential element for plants, so-called deficiency symptoms do not apply in the conventional sense. Nonetheless, exceedingly low cadmium levels might indirectly impact plant metabolism by influencing the uptake and utilization of essential nutrients. Even concentrations as minute as 0.100 mg/L of cadmium have been found to be toxic to crops like beans, beets, and turnips in nutrient solutions9.

In response to apprehensions regarding the welfare of the populace and the ecosystem arising from noxious metallic constituents, international organizations have set allowable thresholds for these perilous constituents. Nevertheless, the existing information regarding cadmium pollution in soil, water, fodder, along with the blood and milk of ruminants in Khushab, Pakistan, persists insufficient. Materials and methods

Elucidation of study area

The current investigation was conducted within district Khushab, Pakistan. It extended over an expanse of 6,511km2with geographical coordinates positioned at latitude 32°17’48” N and longitude 72°21’9” E (Fig. 1). It is an agricultural trade center with various industries. The temperature generally oscillates between 41 °F and 105 °F, with infrequent instances of dipping below 37 °F or exceeding 112 °F. In the month of July, the temperature reach to maximum up till 50 °C, meanwhile, the minimal recorded temperature plummets to the freezing point during winter. Situated 47 km northwest of Sargodha, the city hosts the Khushab Nuclear Complex, housing a heavy water and plutonium production reactor, positioned 30 km south of Jauharabad. Cadmium was selected as the focus of this study due to its significant environmental and health impacts. In district Khushab, Pakistan, cadmium contamination is of particular concern due to the region’s agricultural activities, industrial presence, and climatic conditions. The primary sources of cadmium in the area include anthropogenic contributions, such as industrial emissions from the Khushab Nuclear Complex, the use of cadmium containing phosphate fertilizers in agriculture, and improper disposal of urban and industrial waste. Natural sources, such as geological deposits and weathering of rocks, also contribute to cadmium levels in the environment. Understanding the distribution and sources of cadmium in this area is critical for developing effective mitigation strategies and safeguarding public health.

Experimental design configuration

The conducted experiment was arranged in a completely randomized design with 10 forage crops (Table 1) in the replications of 3, 2 seasons (summer & winter), and 2 distinct sampling sites that were irrigated with 2 different treatments [S_1-Chabeel (Canal side) & S_2-Nalli Shumali (Barani side)] and distributed into Plot 1, Plot 2, Plot 3, Plot 4, and Plot 5, (2 × 10 × 3 × 2 × 2) during growing seasons of 2023-24. The study sites were selected based on their agricultural significance, contrasting irrigation sources, demographic profiles, and land use patterns, which are representative of key agricultural variability in the district. Chabeel represents irrigation from canal water contaminated by anthropogenic activities, including industrial effluents, agricultural runoff, municipal wastewater, and airborne pollutants, while Nalli Shumali reflects natural soil conditions with minimal external contamination, ensuring a robust comparative assessment. Although only two locations were chosen, their distinct characteristics and systematic sampling strategy allow extrapolation to similar agro-ecological zones across the district. Forage crops were chosen for their agronomic importance and metal accumulation potential, aligning with bioindicator studies. The diverse forage species and seasonal sampling capture spatial and temporal variations. Randomization reduced selection bias, while control plots distinguished natural versus anthropogenic cadmium contamination. These measures enhance study reliability and representativeness.

Water sampling

Water samples (n = 100) were procured in polypropylene containers (each holding 100 mL) in accordance with10 from every designated location. Following this, the gathered samples were transported to the analysis institution and conserved in a refrigerated container prior to undergoing the management protocol.

Soil sampling

As per the findings of11soil specimens (n = 240) were acquired from agricultural area where forages were grown, particularly from the root zone of the vegetation, utilizing a stainless steel auger at a depth of 0 to 20 centimeters then enclosed in marked pouches for subsequent analysis. In conformity with the methodologies described by12all samples were subjected to air drying in the natural environment and then transferred to a forced-air heating oven for a period of 47 h, maintaining at 72 °C. After retrieval from the oven, the soil samples were finely ground and filtered via a 2 mm mesh sieve. The samples were enveloped in Kraft paper pouches and conserved for subsequent scrutiny.

Forage sampling

The fodder samples (n = 240) were amassed from the site where soil was acquired employing sanitized equipment. As per the research conducted by13specimens were brought to lab and thoroughly rinsed with potable water to eliminate sedimentary particles adhering to the exterior of the fodder. Following the removal of surplus moisture from the fodder surface utilizing absorbent blotting material, the samples were partitioned into segments, housed in separate receptacles, and exposed to oven preheated at 80 °C unless reaching a consistent weight. The dehydrated samples were crushed into powder and filtered via a sieve of 2 millimeters opening, then preserved under normal condition for further examination.

Milk sampling

In alignment with the work of14milk specimens (n = 100) were gathered from lactiferous buffaloes and cows. The milk samples obtained were conveyed to the laboratory by placing the vessels within a cooler chilled to 4 °C, and subsequently placed within icebox − 20 degrees Celsius, anticipating for further manipulation.

Blood sampling

As per the research conducted by15blood specimens (n = 100) were collected in enclosed tubes measuring 150 × 16 millimeters from chosen buffaloes and cows. The serum was conveyed into receptacles accordingly marked and preserved within a cooler at around − 20 degrees Celsius. Afterward, aliquot of 1 milliliter was withdrawn and underwent to treatment through the wet digestion technique.

Water digestion

The water samples were subjected to digestion in following with the method utilized by16. In a glass container, a mixture comprising 50 ml of water sample and 10 ml of conc. HNO3 was added. The amalgamation flask was set on a warmed platform, while exposed to a temperature of 800 °C until the assimilation mixture reduced by 20 ml. The digestion vessel was permitted to chill, and an extra 5 ml of conc. HNO3 was dissolved. The flask was then positioned upon a hotplate and gently warmed. Upon the digestion mixture turning semi-transparent, the heating procedure was halted. The treated material was subsequently removed from the hotplate, refined through Whatman filtration parchment #42, and a magnitude of 50 ml was concocted by combining deaerated H2O.

Digestion of soil and forage specimens

The digestion of soil and forage specimens was accomplished by following17wherein 0.5 g of thoroughly mixed and pulverized sample material were deposited into a microwave safe digestion vessel. Subsequently, 9 milliliters of 10 molar HNO3 and 3 milliliters of 10 molar HCl were introduced into the vessel. The vessels were hermetically sealed and then subjected to microwave digestion at 180 °C for duration of 45 min until achieving a transparent solution. Following the completion of digestion, the resulting transparent and colorless solution underwent filtration using Whatman No. 42 filter paper into a 50 milliliter volumetric flask, with volume adjustments made using 2% nitric acid. All prepared solutions were promptly analyzed for cadmium content utilizing an atomic absorption spectrophotometer (AAS). Each sample underwent triplicate digestion and analysis, with reported data presented as mean ± standard deviation. Blank solutions were prepared following the same outlined procedure under established optimal conditions and subsequently analyzed.

Serum digestion

According to18blood sample, measuring 1.0 milliliter in volume, underwent individual digestion. This process involved combining the blood sample with 4 milliliters of 69% HNO3 and 2 milliliters of 35% hydrogen peroxide within the microwave digestion system. The digestion process followed a temperature ramping procedure, wherein the temperature was elevated to 200 °C over a period of 10 min, maintained at this level for 10 min, and subsequently cooled until reaching a temperature below 40 °C. Prior to pipetting into the closed digestion vessels, all blood samples were homogenized using a vortex mixer for 2 min. Additionally, three blank digestions were conducted under identical conditions. Upon adequate cooling of the digestion vessels, the resulting clear digested solutions were diluted quantitatively with doubly distilled water before undergoing analysis.

Milk digestion

As per the findings of14a 5 ml portion of milk sample was quantified inside a funnel and then adapted to a concluding magnitude of 5 ml utilizing purified H2O. This measure was carried out to reduce the thickness of the milk, as it displayed overly viscosity and were unable to be adequately appraised in its concentrated condition. The specimen was subjected to the addition of a precise volume of 5 mL of 0.1M C2HCl3O2 to trigger the protein precipitation process in the milk. After this stage, the specimens were subjected to centrifugation at a speed of 4000 rpm for a continuance of 20 min. The watery segment was then segregated via separation and transferred into the ceramic crucibles furnished with a unique labeling for ease discernment. Ashing of the materials inside the crucible was performed in a muffle furnace at 500 degrees Celsius for duration of three hours. Following that, the resultant ash was dissolved and reconstituted in a mixture of 6% HNO3 with a volume of 15 ml. Employing Whatman Paper No. 41 filter paper, the solubilized sample was then filtrated. The material was then moved into immaculate and dehydrated centrifuge tubes, earlier cleansed with de-ionized H2O, for interim conservation until the analysis occurred. Every specimen was afterwards modified before the analysis, the volume was adjusted to reach 15 ml was continued.

Spectroscopic examinations

The concentration of cadmium within samples was ascertained followed by19 employing AAS (AA-640; Shimadzu Co., Ltd, Tokyo, Japan). After establishing the appropriate and standardization procedures, the specimen was nebulized into an ASS, and the outcomes of metal was adjusted via deducting the metallic concentration observed in the blank solution from the examined magnitude for optimal analytical quality control20. According to the instructions of21 and methodology used by22; AAS experimental protocols and analytic methodologies were conducted as well as by following instrumental manufacturer’s guidelines. Limit of detection was computed utilizing protocols employed by23. The determination of the limit of detection was delineated in concordance with the juncture where the standard deviation of the blank solution intersects with the signal-to-noise ratio concurrently attained a value of 10, an instance realized to be 0.8 concerning cadmium.

Quality assurance (QA) and quality control (QC) analysis

All chemical substances and reagents utilized in the investigation were of analytical standard and were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. Similarly, after soaking in a nitric acid (30%, volume/volume) solution for a duration of one night, high quality glassware (Merck Germany) was utilized, QA and QC were performed to minimize any cross contaminations at bay and to maintain environmental parameters (factors like temperature and humidity) within manageable limits by adhering to the method outlined in the report by24,25. For the purpose of uniformity of results, blank reagents formulated with a proportion of 5:1:1 of nitric acid, sulfuric acid, and perchloric acid were employed. Additionally, evaluations were conducted using standardized references, including NIST Standard Reference Material 1570 A for forages and SRM 2709 for soil cadmium, to maintain consistency in the analysis and estimations of replicates for each group of samples, as followed by26. The operational parameters encompassed a wavelength of 228.802 nanometers; detection thresholds of 0.8 milligrams per kilogram; a slit width of 0.5 nanometers; lamp current at 4 mA; and a burner height of 7 millimeters. All reagents employed in the experimentation were concocted employing ultra-purified water procured from a water refinement system (Ultra Clear Lab Systems, Siemens Water Technologies, USA). The analytic protocols were upheld through the implementation of standardized operational protocols and measurements utilizing reference standards as suggested by27. To ensure the accuracy of the device, calibration was performed by running blank and standard tests after every series of 5 measurements.

Results and discussions

Water × Site × Season interplay of cadmium (Mean Squares and Mean ± SD) in water specimens during summer and winter across study sites

The ANOVA results revealed a significant effect (P < 0.05) of cadmium concentrations in water influenced by Water, Season, Water × Site, Water × Season, and Water × Site × Season, while Site and Site × Season effects were non-significant (P > 0.05) (Table 3). The average cadmium concentration in water ranged from 0.21 to 1.16 mg/L. The highest concentration was detected in plot 3 of S_1 during the summer season, specifically in groundwater, while the lowest concentration was observed in plot 5 of S_1 during the winter season, also in groundwater (Fig. 2). Cadmium concentrations exhibited a distinct seasonal pattern, with significantly higher levels during summer compared to winter. This seasonal variation is likely attributed to increased evaporation rates and reduced water volumes, which concentrate heavy metals in water bodies51. Additionally, increased agricultural activities, such as fertilizer and pesticide application, may contribute to heightened Cd levels during the summer months52. Spatially, elevated Cd levels in plot 3 of S_1 suggest localized contamination, possibly due to anthropogenic sources such as industrial discharge, agricultural runoff, or sewage water irrigation53. The persistent utilization of sewage reservoirs for irrigation likely exacerbates heavy metal accumulation in groundwater sources. Furthermore, geological characteristics, including parent rock composition and natural weathering, may influence cadmium leaching into groundwater54. The detected cadmium concentrations significantly exceed the permissible limits (0.003, 0.005 mg/L) set by55 and56 for drinking water. The present findings also indicate higher Cd levels compared to previous studies, such as57who reported Cd concentrations between 0.014 and 1.03 mg/L in groundwater samples. Similarly58, documented a range of 0.02 to 0.98 mg/L in surface water bodies exposed to industrial discharge. The detected values align with59who reported up to 1.16 mg/L in irrigation water within agricultural zones. The high standard deviation observed in cadmium levels (Fig. 2) can be attributed to several factors. Environmental heterogeneity, such as variations in industrial emissions from the Khushab Nuclear Complex and agricultural runoff from cadmium containing fertilizers, likely contributed to the variability. Seasonal differences, including higher cadmium mobility during summer due to increased temperatures and lower mobility during winter due to freezing conditions, may have further influenced the results60. Localized contamination, particularly near industrial sites and areas with intensive fertilizer use, could also explain the observed variability. To better understand the spatial distribution of cadmium, geospatial analysis was conducted, revealing hotspots of contamination near the Khushab Nuclear Complex. Statistical analysis using Levene’s test confirmed that the variance in cadmium levels was statistically significant across seasons (p < 0.05), supporting the role of seasonal factors in driving variability. These findings are consistent with previous studies that have reported similar variability in cadmium concentrations due to environmental heterogeneity and localized contamination61. Chronic exposure to cadmium contaminated water poses severe health risks, including kidney dysfunction, osteoporosis, cardiovascular diseases, and an increased risk of carcinogenesis62. The bioavailability of cadmium in drinking water is of particular concern due to its high mobility and persistence in biological systems. Livestock consuming contaminated water may experience bioaccumulation, leading to secondary exposure in humans through the food chain. The discrepancies among studies may result from variations in land use patterns, proximity to industrial zones, and regulatory enforcement of environmental safety standards. To mitigate cadmium contamination in water, ion exchange resins can be employed to selectively remove Cd ions from drinking and irrigation water sources63. Phytoremediation using cadmium accumulating plant species can aid in reducing heavy metal concentrations in contaminated water bodies. Additionally, the implementation of buffer strips and constructed wetlands near agricultural fields can limit runoff and prevent heavy metal seepage into groundwater sources. Given the alarming Cd levels detected, systematic and continuous surveillance of cadmium concentrations in water sources is essential. Stricter environmental policies and water treatment interventions should be enforced to prevent long-term ecological and public health consequences associated with cadmium exposure.

Plot × Site × Soil × Season interplay of cadmium (Mean Squares and Mean ± SD) in soil specimens during summer and winter across study sites

The Khushab district, particularly Site 1 (Chabeel), receives irrigation from canal water contaminated by upstream industrial effluents and urban runoff, contributing to the elevated Cd levels in summer. Seasonal hydrological patterns—such as reduced water table recharge and enhanced evaporation likely intensify metal concentration during summer months. The proximity of Site 1 to the Khushab Nuclear Complex and surrounding industrial clusters exacerbates cadmium leaching into water bodies. In contrast, Site 2 (Nalli Shumali), relying on Barani (rain-fed) water sources, showed relatively stable Cd concentrations, suggesting minimal anthropogenic input in water matrices.

Analysis of variance of data for cadmium in soil showed a significant effect (P < 0.05) on Plot, Site, Soil, Season, Plot × Soil, Plot × Season, Site × Soil, Soil × Season, Plot × Site × Soil, Plot × Site × Season, Plot × Soil × Season, Site × Soil × Season, and Plot × Site × Soil × Season, whereas the interaction effects of Plot × Site and Site × Season were non-significant (P > 0.05) (Table 3). The average cadmium concentrations in soil ranged from 0.09 to 0.58 mg/kg (Fig. 3). The highest concentration was detected in Plot 3 at S_2, in the soil of T. alexandrium crop during the summer season, whereas the lowest concentration was observed in Plot 5 at S_1 for the same crop in winter (Table 2). Cadmium levels were significantly higher during the summer season compared to winter, likely due to increased agricultural activities such as the application of phosphate fertilizers and pesticides, which are primary sources of cadmium contamination64. Additionally, higher temperatures and soil dryness in summer may enhance cadmium solubility and mobility, leading to greater uptake by crops65. In contrast, winter conditions, characterized by lower temperatures and higher soil moisture, might reduce metal availability due to increased adsorption onto soil particles and organic matter. The spatial variation in cadmium concentrations, particularly the elevated levels in Plot 3 at S_2, may be attributed to its proximity to anthropogenic sources such as industrial discharge, vehicular emissions, and improper waste disposal66. Soils in this plot might also possess physicochemical properties that enhance cadmium retention, Notably, the spatial trends in soil Cd distribution correlate strongly with irrigation source and land use intensity. Soils at Site 1 exhibit higher anthropogenic pressure due to industrial proximity and wastewater irrigation, while Site 2 soils are less impacted due to reliance on rainwater. The alkaline pH and high clay content in some plots at Site 2 may immobilize Cd, lowering its bioavailability. Conversely, light-textured, sandy soils at Site 1 may promote cadmium leaching and plant uptake. The presence of competing ions like Ca2+ and Mg2+ in soil solutions can also influence cadmium mobility by either promoting adsorption or displacement, as described by67. The cadmium concentrations in soil observed in this study were lower than those reported by57 (4.21–4.54 mg/kg)58, (0.014–1.03 mg/kg), and68(2.02 mg/kg). However, they were consistent with values reported by59 (0.58 mg/kg). The observed levels were also below the permissible limit of 1.1 mg/kg set by55indicating that cadmium contamination in the study area remains within safe limits. Differences in reported cadmium concentrations may be attributed to variations in industrial activities, soil composition, irrigation sources, and land use practices across different regions69. Elevated cadmium levels can have significant negative effects on soil health and agricultural productivity. Cadmium disrupts soil microbial communities, reducing the efficiency of essential nutrient cycling processes. It also inhibits plant growth by interfering with root development and nutrient uptake, leading to reduced crop yields70. Over time, cadmium accumulation can degrade soil quality, making it less fertile and more prone to heavy metal toxicity71. The ability of soil to adsorb cadmium depends on its mineralogical composition. Clayey soils, with their high surface area and cation exchange capacity, tend to immobilize cadmium, reducing its bioavailability. In contrast, sandy soils with lower adsorption capacity can result in higher cadmium mobility, posing a greater risk of groundwater contamination72. Although the cadmium concentrations in soil were below the permissible limits, continuous accumulation over time can pose potential health risks. Cadmium is a known carcinogen and can cause kidney damage, bone demineralization, and other chronic health issues upon prolonged exposure62. The contamination of soil can lead to its uptake by crops, subsequently entering the food chain and affecting human and animal health. Livestock grazing on contaminated pastures may bioaccumulate cadmium in their tissues, posing indirect risks to consumers73. Therefore, stringent monitoring and soil remediation strategies are necessary to prevent long term environmental and health consequences. To mitigate cadmium contamination, adopting sustainable soil management practices such as crop rotation, organic amendments, and biochar application can help immobilize cadmium and reduce its bioavailability. Phytoextraction using hyperaccumulator plants like Brassica juncea can be employed to extract cadmium from contaminated soils74. Additionally, regulatory measures should be enforced to limit cadmium inputs from industrial and agricultural sources. Future studies should focus on long term monitoring of cadmium dynamics in soil-plant systems, as well as assessing potential risks through detailed human health risk assessments.

Plot × Site × Crop × Season interplay of cadmium (Mean Square and Mean ± SD) in pasture specimens during summer and winter across study sites

Variance analysis of cadmium in pasture exhibited a significant effect (P < 0.05) on Plot, Site, Crop, Season, and their interactions (Plot × Site, Plot × Crop, Plot × Season, Site × Crop, Site × Season, Crop × Season, Plot × Site × Crop, Plot × Site × Season, Plot × Crop × Season, Site × Crop × Season, and Plot × Site × Crop × Season) (Table 3). The strong interaction effects suggest that cadmium accumulation is influenced by multiple environmental and biological factors, rather than being uniformly distributed across plots or seasons. The average concentration of cadmium in pasture differed from 0.04 to 0.97 mg/kg, with the highest value observed in the winter season crop of T.alexandrium at S_2 of plot 5, whereas the lowest value was discerned in the summer season crop of T.alexandrium at S_1 of plot 5 (Fig. 4; Table 2). These variations align with findings from75,76who reported seasonal and site specific fluctuations in cadmium levels due to differences in soil properties, crop physiology, and environmental conditions. Cadmium concentrations in our study were found to be higher than the permissible limit of 0.2 mg/kg set by62. Similar high cadmium levels in forages were reported by77 in Bangladesh, with values ranging from 0.093 to 4.09 mg/kg, significantly exceeding both our reported values and global safety standards. In contrast78, found much lower cadmium levels (0.001–0.008 mg/kg) in food crops irrigated with groundwater in Sahiwal, Pakistan, highlighting the impact of irrigation sources on cadmium accumulation. The results of57–59 corroborate our findings, showing similar seasonal trends in cadmium uptake. Furthermore79, observed that cadmium accumulation in crops varies depending on soil type, crop species, and environmental conditions, which aligns with our findings of seasonal variations in cadmium levels. The higher cadmium levels in winter crops suggest an increased bioavailability of cadmium during colder months, potentially due to reduced plant metabolism and slower degradation of organic matter in the soil80. In contrast, lower cadmium levels in summer crops may be attributed to higher plant growth rates, increased transpiration, and dilution effects within plant tissues81. Additionally, soil properties such as pH, organic matter content, and cation exchange capacity likely influenced cadmium uptake by crops, as suggested by82. The competition between cadmium and other heavy metals (e.g., zinc, lead) for root absorption also plays a crucial role in determining the extent of cadmium accumulation in different plant species83. The observed cadmium levels pose significant risks to livestock and human health due to potential bioaccumulation along the food chain. Grazing animals that consume contaminated pastures may accumulate cadmium in tissues such as the liver, kidneys, and milk, leading to chronic exposure in humans through the consumption of meat and dairy products84. Chronic cadmium intake is associated with kidney dysfunction, bone demineralization, and increased risks of carcinogenic effects, as highlighted in previous health risk studies. Given that our study found cadmium concentrations exceeding FAO/WHO safety limits, immediate interventions are required to mitigate the potential for food chain contamination. To mitigate cadmium contamination in agricultural soils, several strategies can be employed. Soil amendments such as lime, biochar, and organic compost can help increase soil pH and reduce cadmium bioavailability85. Crop selection plays a crucial role, as growing low cadmium accumulating species can minimize its transfer into the food chain. Phytoremediation techniques using hyperaccumulator plants can aid in extracting cadmium from contaminated soils before cultivating edible crops86. Effective water management is essential to prevent cadmium accumulation through irrigation, ensuring groundwater and surface water sources remain uncontaminated87. Additionally, regulatory monitoring through cadmium surveillance programs can help track contamination sources, enforce safety limits, and prevent soil degradation. Implementing these measures collectively can safeguard soil health, maintain agricultural productivity, and reduce human exposure risks.

The cadmium flow from soil to forage is amplified at Site 1 due to repeated exposure to contaminated irrigation. High enrichment factors (EF) and bioconcentration factors (BCF > 1) in certain species like T. alexandrinum and C. intybus confirm their role as hyperaccumulators under polluted conditions. The flow of pollutants follows a clear trophic transfer: contaminated water affects soil quality → Cd is absorbed by forage → forage is consumed by livestock, leading to accumulation in blood and milk. These findings underscore how irrigation practices directly affect the contamination level across the food web.

Interplay of interactions of Season × Site × Cow × Lact & Buffalo × Site × Lact × Season, Cow × Site × Season & Buffalo × Site × Season for cadmium variability (Mean Squares and Mean ± SD) in blood & milk of cows and buffaloes during summer and winter across study sites

The variability in cadmium (Cd) concentration in cow and buffalo blood and milk was assessed through an analysis of variance (ANOVA), revealing significant and non-significant interactions among various factors, including season, site, species, and lactation stage. The results of Cd concentration in cow blood demonstrated a non-significant effect (P > 0.05) for factors such as season, site, cow identity, lactation stage, and most interaction terms (Season × Site, Season × Cow, Site × Cow, Site × Lact, Cow × Lact, and higher-order interactions). However, a significant (P < 0.05) interaction was found for Season × Lact, indicating a potential seasonal influence on lactation related Cd variability. This suggests that lactation may influence the Cd retention and excretion pathways in cows, possibly due to altered metabolic demands or physiological adjustments in milk production84. Conversely, buffalo blood Cd concentration exhibited a more pronounced statistical response, showing significant effects (P < 0.05) for Buffalo, Lactation, Season, Buffalo × Site, Buffalo × Lact, Buffalo × Season, and Buffalo × Site × Lact. This suggests that buffaloes have a stronger environmental response to Cd exposure, potentially due to species specific metabolic differences88. However, interactions such as Site × Lact, Site × Season, and higher order interactions remained non-significant (P > 0.05), implying that site-dependent variability alone does not necessarily influence Cd retention. The mean Cd concentration in cow blood ranged from 0.17 to 2.98 mg/L, with the lowest levels observed in cow No. 4 at S_1 during summer and the highest in cow No. 9 at S_1 during winter (Fig. 5). These findings align with previous studies89,90which reported significant seasonal fluctuations in Cd exposure due to variations in forage and water contamination. The significant Season × Lact interaction supports findings that lactating cows tend to have lower blood Cd levels due to increased transfer to milk90. Buffalo blood Cd concentrations varied from 0.68 to 4.68 mg/L, exceeding the91 permissible limit of 0.05 mg/L. The highest levels were observed in buffalo No. 4 at S_1 during summer, while the lowest were in buffalo No. 10 at S_2 during the same season (Fig. 6). These values are consistent with the range of 0.70 to 4.20 mg/L reported by92 and 0.67 to 4.85 mg/L by93emphasizing the persistence of Cd contamination in livestock across different geographic locations.

The ANOVA results for cow milk indicated significant (P < 0.05) effects for Cow, Season, Cow × Site, Cow × Season, Site × Season, and Cow × Site × Season, demonstrating complex interactions among these factors in determining Cd levels. However, the site alone showed a non-significant (P > 0.05) effect, suggesting that local environmental factors may not independently dictate Cd accumulation in milk. Similarly, Cd levels in buffalo milk were significantly affected (P < 0.05) by Buffalo, Site, Buffalo × Site, Buffalo × Season, and Buffalo × Site × Season, while Season and Site × Season interactions were non-significant (P > 0.05). The mean Cd concentration in cow milk ranged from 0.24 to 1.25 mg/L, with the lowest value recorded in cow No. 2 at S_2 during summer and the highest in cow No. 3 at S_1 during the same season (Fig. 7). Notably, these values remain above the94 permissible limit of 1 mg/L. The maximum allowable level of Cd in cow milk established by the International Dairy Federation, FAO/WHO, and national standard of Pakistan is 2.6, 10, and 5 µgmL−1, respectively. The findings align with previous studies by95reporting Cd concentrations between 0.18 and 1.31 mg/L, and96which recorded values between 0.31 and 1.46 mg/L in cow milk. The lower Cd levels in cow milk during lactation could be attributed to physiological Cd excretion mechanisms during milk production, as suggested by90. In contrast, buffalo milk Cd concentrations varied from 0.25 to 0.96 mg/L, with the highest level observed in buffalo No. 4 at S_1 during summer and the lowest in buffalo No. 5 at S_2 during the same season (Fig. 8). These levels significantly exceed the91 limit of 0.05 mg/L, raising substantial health concerns. High Cd accumulation in buffalo milk may stem from prolonged environmental exposure, contaminated feed or species specific differences in metal metabolism88.

The observed elevation in Cd levels in animal blood and milk at Site 1 reflects the environmental exposure gradient shaped by irrigation source and proximity to industrial discharges. The strong correlation between forage Cd levels and serum concentrations in ruminants highlights the linear transfer of cadmium across environmental matrices. This suggests an unbroken pollutant flow from soil and forage into systemic circulation, ultimately reaching consumable products such as milk. The physiological burden on buffaloes, which are often fed higher quantities of contaminated fodder, may explain their higher Cd levels compared to cows. The elevated Cd levels in buffalo blood and milk highlight potential public health risks associated with milk consumption. The62 permissible Cd limit in milk (0.05 mg/L) was exceeded in several samples, indicating a pressing need for regulatory intervention. Given that Cd is a known nephrotoxin and carcinogen, chronic exposure through contaminated dairy products may lead to adverse health outcomes, including kidney dysfunction, osteoporosis, and neurological disorders97. Environmental contamination through polluted soil, water, and forage likely contributes to the observed Cd accumulation in ruminants. Since livestock acquire most of their trace elements through dietary intake, regular feed monitoring and stringent quality control measures are essential to minimize exposure risks98. Additionally, increased industrial and agricultural activities in the study area may exacerbate Cd deposition in grazing lands, necessitating further investigation into pollution sources and mitigation strategies. Overall, these findings underscore the importance of continuous biomonitoring of heavy metals in the food chain, especially in dairy products. Future research should focus on Cd bioavailability, species-specific retention mechanisms, and potential mitigation strategies to safeguard both animal and human health. Correlations

Highly significant correlations were observed across different environmental matrices and seasons, indicating the seasonal influence on cadmium mobility and bioavailability. A highly significant strong positive correlation was observed between water at S₁ vs. soil at S₂ during winter, suggesting enhanced cadmium transfer from water to soil under cold conditions. This is likely due to reduced evaporation and stable hydrological conditions that promote metal retention in soil99. This trend is significant because higher cadmium retention in soil during winter could lead to increased plant uptake, ultimately entering the food chain and posing risks to grazing animals. Conversely, a highly significant strong negative correlation was found between water at S₂ vs. soil at S₁ during summer, indicating reduced cadmium mobility from soil to water at high temperatures. Elevated summer temperatures may decrease cadmium solubility due to increased evaporation and altered soil-water interactions34. This reduced transfer suggests that cadmium exposure through water sources is lower in summer but may still persist through direct soil-to-plant uptake pathways. A strong negative correlation between pasture at S₂ vs. cow milk at S₁ during summer further supports this trend, suggesting that cadmium uptake by cows from pasture is limited under high temperatures. This may be due to reduced bioavailability in plants, as previous studies have shown that high temperatures can decrease cadmium accumulation in vegetation60. However, this does not eliminate the risk, as cadmium could still accumulate in animal tissues over time, potentially affecting long-term exposure in milk consumers. Several highly significant weak positive correlations were observed, including between: Water at S₁ vs. soil at S₂ during summer, Pasture at S₂ vs. cow blood at S₁ during winter, Pasture at S₁ vs. buffalo blood at S₂ during summer, Pasture at S₁ vs. cow milk at S₂ during summer, and Pasture at S₁ vs. buffalo milk at S₂ during winter. These weak positive correlations indicate moderate cadmium mobility between environmental and biological matrices, suggesting low but persistent cadmium transfer from pasture to animal blood and milk100. While weak correlations imply limited bioaccumulation, they still indicate potential long term accumulation in livestock, which could contribute to dietary exposure through dairy products101. On the other hand, highly significant weak negative correlations were observed between: Water at S₂ vs. soil at S₁ during winter, Soil at S₁ vs. pasture at S₂ during summer, Soil at S₂ vs. pasture at S₁ during summer and winter, Pasture at S₁ vs. cow blood at S₂ during summer, Pasture at S₂ vs. cow blood at S₁ during summer, Pasture at S₁ vs. buffalo blood at S₂ during winter, Pasture at S₂ vs. buffalo blood at S₁ during winter, Pasture at S₁ vs. buffalo milk at S₂ during summer, and Pasture at S₂ vs. buffalo milk at S₁ during winter (Figs. 10, 11 and 12). These negative correlations suggest limited cadmium transfer between soil and pasture and from pasture to animals under certain seasonal conditions. This aligns with findings by102who reported that cadmium bioavailability can decline in colder climates due to reduced dissolution in water and decreased plant uptake. The reduced transfer in summer also indicates that cadmium in pastures may remain bound in plant tissues, decreasing its direct availability to grazing animals103. The seasonal variations in cadmium mobility and bioavailability have direct implications for food safety and environmental monitoring. Higher cadmium bioavailability in winter increases the risk of plant uptake, leading to higher accumulation in animal tissues and milk. This suggests that livestock and dairy products may pose greater dietary risks during colder months104. While cadmium transfer is lower in summer, long term bioaccumulation in soil and vegetation remains a concern, particularly for animals that consume contaminated fodder over extended periods. The weak but persistent correlations between pasture and animal matrices indicate a potential long term exposure risk, particularly through the consumption of dairy and meat products105. Even if immediate cadmium levels in milk are low, prolonged ingestion could lead to chronic health effects such as kidney damage, osteoporosis, and carcinogenic risks60. These findings highlight the necessity of seasonal monitoring of water, soil, and forage quality to mitigate cadmium accumulation in food chains. Targeted interventions, such as soil amendments, controlled grazing patterns, or seasonal dietary adjustments for livestock, could help minimize health risks.

The matrix correlations observed in this study, especially between water-soil and soil-pasture, reinforce the hypothesis that environmental pollution at the source (water) progressively permeates the entire food chain. Seasonal changes in climate, such as rainfall and temperature shifts, modulate cadmium mobility and thus directly influence its bioavailability. These dynamic interactions are particularly critical in agro-industrial regions like Khushab, where irrigation practices and industrial activity vary sharply across space and time.

Bio concentration factor (BCF) of cadmium in summer and winter across study sites

The value of BCF ranged from 0.0962 to 7.6667 with minimum value was detected in S. bicolar at S_1 of Plot 3 while maximum in T.alexandrium in Plot 5 at S_1 during summer and winter, correspondingly (Table 4). The current BCF value was higher than previous studied values (0.09–0.16 & 0.6–1.1) reported by57 as well as106respectively. Agrochemical application, urbanization, plant species as well as climatic and environmental conditions could affect metal accumulation in food crops. The trophic hierarchy, encompassing soil, vegetation, and ruminants, is widely recognized as a primary conduit for animal exposure to contaminants of soil107. When the BCF is less than or equal to 1, it signifies that the plant absorbs the heavy metal without significant accumulation, which aligns with international safety thresholds set by World Health Organization and the European Union. Conversely, when the BCF exceeds 1, it indicates that the plant is accumulating heavy metals, potentially posing health risks if consumed. In the present study, BCF values exceeding 1 were observed, indicating significant cadmium accumulation in pasture crops. This is particularly concerning as cadmium is a toxic heavy metal with established international guidelines, such as the94 maximum permissible limit of 0.1–0.3 mg/kg for cadmium in food crops and the62 provisional tolerable monthly intake of 25 µg/kg body weight. The elevated BCF values in this study suggest that cadmium accumulation in pasture crops could pose considerable health risks for ruminants, which may subsequently affect human health through the food chain.

Pollution load index (PLI) of cadmium in summer and winter across study sites

The present value of PLI ranged from 0.0604 to 0.3893 with lowest value was discerned in the soil of T.alexandrium at S_1 of Plot 5 while highest also in the soil of T.alexandrium at S_2 of Plot 3 during winter and summer season, accordingly (Table 4). The present finding was lower than108 proposed value (0.7) in polluted soil. The evaluation of environmental prowess and the appraisal of geochemical perturbation can be elucidated through the correlations among the proportions of minute constituents and their primordial baseline standards, thus magnifying the concentrations of trace cadmiums, commonly attributed to anthropogenic interventions109. PLI is considered an indicator of soil pollution, where a value below 1 signifies the absence of pollutants, whereas a value exceeding 1 signs the existence of soil pollution56. In the current context, the PLI remains below the threshold of unity, signifying the absence of cadmium contamination in the soil and affirming its suitability for cultivation without risk of pollution.

Enrichment factor (EF) of cadmium in summer and winter across study sites

The value of EF varied from 0.7163 to 57.1167 with minimum value was detected in S. bicolar at S_1 of Plot 3 while maximum in T.alexandrium in Plot 5 at S_1 during summer and winter, accordingly (Table 4). EF% with zero value was present in the soil of T.alexandrium at S_1 of Plot 5 while 100% also in the soil of T.alexandrium at S_2 of Plot 3 during winter and summer, correspondingly (Table 5). Range of enrichment factor of cadmium (0.65–1.37) given by110 as well as results (4.8) reported by111 were much lower than present findings. The EF is instrumental in examining the transference dynamics of cadmiums across soil to plants, thereby elucidating the heterogeneity in cadmium accumulation within plant specimens across various geographical locations. Reduced enrichment factor values can emerge due to elevated cadmium sequestration within the soil matrix and restricted translocation processes within plant organisms112. In the present analysis, the EF has been established to surpass a value of 1, affirming a substantial assimilation of cadmium by the fodder vegetation, thus substantiating their inadequacy for consumption.

Conclusions

The focus of the present examination was dedicated to assessing cadmium levels in two separate locales, with additional attention given to investigating seasonal dynamics and their impact on concentrations in the interconnected water-soil-forage-ruminant food web. The overall examined levels were found to be higher at Plot 1 of S_2 during summer whereas lower at Plot 5 of S_1 during winter season. Cadmium was found to be lower in T. alexandrium at S_1 whereas higher in C. intybus at S_2 both of Plot 5 during summer and winter, accordingly. The magnitudes of BCF, EF, Eri, HRI, and THQ exceeded the threshold of 1, signifying potential health peril ramifications for ruminant organisms consequent to the ingestion of tainted forage; however, PLI, Igeo, DIM, EDI, EDD, ILTCR, and ECR fell below 1, showcasing the security of livestock feedstock and absence of carcinogenic hazards for ruminants at each locale throughout all periods. The findings of this study highlight the need for sustainable agricultural practices to mitigate cadmium accumulation in forage crops, ensuring safe livestock feed and minimizing human health risks via the food chain. Given the seasonal variations observed in cadmium levels, seasonal monitoring programs should be implemented to track contamination trends and guide adaptive management strategies. Additionally, soil amendments such as biochar, organic matter, and phytoremediation techniques could be employed to reduce metal uptake by forage crops. Authorities should establish maximum permissible limits for cadmium in forage, water, and livestock products to ensure food safety. Regular monitoring of cadmium levels in agricultural zones near industrial and urban discharge sites is essential for effective risk assessment. Farmers and stakeholders should be provided with guidelines on safe irrigation practices, proper feed selection, and contamination mitigation strategies to prevent metal accumulation in livestock. Additionally, strict regulations on industrial effluents should be enforced to limit cadmium discharge into agricultural land and water sources, with mandatory treatment before release. Implementing these measures will help mitigate cadmium contamination risks, ensuring environmental sustainability, food security, and public health protection.
