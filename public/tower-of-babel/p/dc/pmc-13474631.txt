Long Term Exposure to Ambient Particulate Matter 2.5 and Kidney Function in Urban Indian Adults
Authors: Siddhartha Mandal, Ram Jagannathan, Shuchi Anand, Mohammed K. Ali, Poornima Prabhakaran, Sailesh Mohan, Ruby Gupta, K.M. Venkat Narayan, Viswanathan Mohan, Joel D. Schwartz, Nikhil Tandon, Dorairaj Prabhakaran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite epidemiological evidence linking fine particulate matter (PM2.5) to cardiometabolic diseases, links between PM2.5 and kidney function or chronic kidney disease (CKD) remain inconclusive.MethodsIn a population representative cohort of 12,271 adults (age ≥ 20 years) in 2 Indian cities (Chennai and Delhi), we investigated time-varying associations between annual average ambient PM2.5 and estimated glomerular filtration rates (eGFR) calculated using CKD-Epidemiology Collaboration (EPI) 2009 equations, at 3 time points during 2010 to 2016. Ambient PM2.5 exposures were assessed at residential geocodes using a national high-resolution spatiotemporal model for daily PM2.5 at 1 km × 1 km. City-specific linear mixed effect models were employed, incorporating inverse probability-based weighting to account for loss-to-follow-up. Effect modification by sex, waist-to-hip ratio, hypertension, and diabetes status were assessed using stratified models.ResultsAnnual average PM2.5 at baseline and 2 follow-ups in Chennai were 33, 37, and 30 μg/m3 whereas those in Delhi were 118, 123, and 130 μg/m3. Median eGFRs at baseline were 112 ml/min per 1.73 m2 (interquartile range [IQR]: 101.9–121.0) in Chennai and 106.4 ml/min per 1.73 m2 (IQR: 94.4–116.6) in Delhi. Over time, a 5 ug/m3 increase in annual average PM2.5 was associated with a significant decline in eGFR in both cities as follows: −0.32 (95% confidence interval [CI]: −0.49 to −0.15) ml/min per 1.73 m2 in Chennai and −0.42 (95% CI: −0.57 to −0.27) ml/min per 1.73 m2 in Delhi.ConclusionAnnual average PM2.5 levels were associated with declines in eGFR. These data from a relatively young South Asian urban cohort underscore the need to further investigate the potential renal and cardiovascular impacts of sustained air pollution exposure.

CKD remains a significant global public health challenge, contributing to substantial morbidity and premature mortality. In 2022, an estimated 850 million individuals worldwide were living with CKD,1 including approximately 143 million in India.2 The World Health Organization identified kidney disease as 1 of the top 10 causes of death globally in 2019. These figures underscore the pressing need to identify modifiable risk factors to inform effective prevention strategies. Beyond established risk factors such as age, diabetes, hypertension, and obesity,3 emerging evidence implicates environmental exposures, notably PM2.5, in CKD pathogenesis. A recent meta-analysis of 14 studies reported that each 10 μg/m3 higher long-term PM2.5 exposure was associated with a 31% percent higher risk of incident CKD (relative risk 1.31 [1.24–1.40]).4 Furthermore, global exposure-response modeling estimated that over 120 million prevalent cases of CKD might be attributable to ambient PM2.5 exposure, with the largest burden observed in low- and middle-income countries.5 India has some of the highest levels of PM2.5 exposure globally and presents a unique opportunity to study these associations. The prevalence and risk factor profile for kidney dysfunction in India differs from that observed in Western populations,6 with higher albuminuria and GFRs. This distinct clinical profile raises the possibility that environmental exposures, through air, water, and soil, may have a stronger influence on disease onset and progression in the Indian population. Recent cross-sectional nationwide surveys have shown significant regional differences in impaired kidney function (defined as eGFR < 60 ml/min per 1.73 m2) in India, which could partially be driven by environmental factors.7 Previous work from our group, using high-resolution spatiotemporal modeling of PM2.5,8,9 has demonstrated robust associations between long-term ambient PM2.5 exposure and increased risks of type 2 diabetes,10 hypertension,11 and all-cause mortality.12 However, despite the growing body of evidence linking air pollution to cardiometabolic outcomes, data on the relationship between air pollution and kidney function in high-exposure settings remains scarce. Most studies to date have been conducted in populations with relatively low and narrow ranges of PM2.5 exposure, with only a limited number of studies examining concentrations exceeding 35 μg/m3. Consequently, the associations between high ambient PM2.5 levels and kidney health remains poorly understood in regions like India, where exposure levels are both markedly higher and sustained.

In this study, we assessed whether 1-year averaged, time-varying PM2.5 exposure was associated with kidney function and longitudinal changes in kidney function within the ongoing Center for Cardiometabolic Risk Reduction in South Asia cohort study13 over a 10-year period. These analyses leverage longitudinal samples with serum creatinine measurements taken at 3 time points, coupled with validated machine learning–based spatiotemporal models of PM2.5 exposure at a 1 km × 1 km resolution for India.

Methods

Cohort and Population

This study was based within the Center for Cardiometabolic Risk Reduction in South Asia cohort study in 2 Indian cities, Chennai (in the south) and Delhi (in the north). Details of this well-retained cohort with low bias because of loss of follow up are reported elsewhere.13,14 The Center for Cardiometabolic Risk Reduction in South Asia surveillance study followed a multistage random sampling scheme with appropriate sample weights to arrive at the sample population in 2010 to 2011 in Delhi and Chennai. Pregnant women and seriously ill bedridden individuals were excluded from the study because of difficulties in obtaining anthropometric measurements. Two individuals, 1 male and 1 female, from each household were recruited. This resulted in 12,271 (6906 in Chennai and 5365 in Delhi) adult participants (permanent members of the household). For this study, 12,064 individuals (6722 from Chennai and 5342 from Delhi), who had geocoded household information and questionnaire-based survey responses, were included.

The baseline in-person questionnaire-based survey (administered by trained field workers) and bio-specimen collection were performed during 2010 to 2012, with subsequent follow-ups (with both questionnaire interviews and bio-specimen collections) from 2013 to 2014 and 2016 to 2017. In addition, 2 intermediate follow-ups with only short questionnaire-based interviews were conducted between 2011 and 2013 and in 2014. Measures, methods, and validated instruments (adapted from existing studies) for recording demographic indicators, behavioral, physiological, and biochemical risk factors along with laboratory techniques are described in detail elsewhere.13 All participants in the study provided written informed consent for participation and the study was approved by the respective Institutional Ethics Committees.

Exposure Assessment

Time-varying annual average exposure to PM2.5 was assessed using a nationwide machine learning-based ensemble model, which provided daily average predictions of PM2.5 retrospectively at a spatial resolution of 1 km × 1 km across 2008 to 2020(9). The model was calibrated against ground monitoring-based observations of daily PM2.5 from 1056 stations and used predictors across a wide variety of sources, including meteorology, land use, satellite observations, and emissions inventories. Splitting the data into a training and test dataset based on leaving out monitors, we used 4 machine learning algorithms (deep learning, random forest, gradient boosting, and extreme gradient boosting) to model the relationships between the predictors within the training data and PM2.5. Predictions using these models were assembled to arrive at a final prediction for each grid-day combination. The cross-validated R2 in the validation dataset for the daily predictions was 0.86, whereas that for the annual average predictions was 0.94.

We aggregated daily PM2.5 estimates from the model at each grid to arrive at annual averages. We assigned exposure to participants based on the distance between geolocations of their household and grid centroids. For each participant, time-varying annual average PM2.5 was computed from the day before the date of assessment, at baseline, and each follow-up.

Outcomes, Confounders, and Effect Modifiers

eGFR was assessed for each participant at all time points where serum creatinine was available, using the CKD-EPI 2009 equations.15 We used the CKD-EPI 2009 equation based on previous work indicating this equation had the strongest explanatory power for the association with mortality among South Asians, compared with, for example, CKD EPI 2021 and CKD European Kidney Function Consortium equations.16 As a sensitivity analysis, we also computed eGFR using the CKD-EPI equations for South Asians.17 Given our main exposure of interest is annual average ambient PM2.5 exposures, the confounders we considered were neighborhood level socioeconomic status and calendar time, whereas individual level characteristics (such as age and sex) were considered effect modifiers. Since the annual average PM2.5 is assessed at 1 km ×1 km grids using spatiotemporal models, and individuals are assigned to these grids based on household locations and grid centroids, individual characteristics were not expected to inform the exposures and hence are not considered as confounders. Literature suggests that poorer neighborhoods are often exposed to higher levels of ambient air pollution and are often at higher risk of developing chronic diseases through nutritional or occupational pathways.18,19 Further, air pollution levels vary over time and because of the low correlation of calendar time with age at entry into the study (ρ = 0.08 in Delhi and −0.01 in Chennai), we included calendar time as a confounder in our models.20

We considered several effect modifiers, including age at entry into study, sex, hypertension, and diabetes at baseline, and waist-to-hip ratio at baseline, to assess differential associations across the levels of these effect modifiers. Hypertension was assessed at baseline and each follow-up based on measured systolic and diastolic blood pressure (systolic blood pressure ≥ 140mm Hg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90mm Hg), self-reported physician diagnosed hypertension and/or being on medication for hypertension. Type 2 diabetes mellitus was assessed at baseline and each follow-up based on fasting plasma glucose ≥ 126 mg/dl and/or hemoglobinA1c ≥ 6.5% and/or self-reported physician diagnosed diabetes mellitus and/or being on medication for diabetes.

Statistical Methods

Longitudinal trends in eGFR levels were analyzed using linear mixed effect models while accounting for loss-to-follow-up using an inverse probability-based weighting approach separately for each city (Figure 1). The main exposure of interest was annual average exposure to PM2.5 before each measurement, with calendar time and neighborhood socioeconomic status as confounders. We included an interaction term between the exposure and time to capture varying changes over time across different exposure levels. The annual average levels of PM2.5 decreased across time in Chennai by 5 ug/m3 whereas in Delhi the median levels increased by 29 ug/m3 over the study period. The analysis of annual averages of PM2.5 with longitudinal trends of eGFR showed significant associations in both cities, after adjusting for confounders. In Chennai, 5 ug/m3 change in annual PM2.5 (IQR in Chennai and Delhi at baseline were 6 ug/m3 and 17 ug/m3 respectively) was associated with 0.31 (95% CI: 0.15–0.49) ml/min per 1.73 m2 decrease in eGFR, whereas in Delhi we observed a decline of 0.40 (95% CI: 0.26–0.54) ml/min per 1.73 m2 lower eGFR. Across a 5-year period, the decline in eGFR in Delhi was 6.5 ml/min per 1.73 m2 (95% CI: 2.1–10.9), whereas this decline was nonsignificant in Chennai (estimate of 0.99 ml/min per 1.73 m2 [95% CI: −1.52 to 3.5]). We did not observe significant interaction effects between PM2.5 and calendar time.

Effect modification was not statistically significant, although there were different magnitudes of association in each city. In Chennai, stronger associations were observed among normotensives, males, and older participants. In Delhi, the associations were stronger in males, obese, and younger participants. These results are summarized in Supplementary Table S2. Temporal declines adjusted for PM2.5 exposure are provided in the Supplemental material (Supplementary Table S3), which showed different magnitudes of decline by subgroups. Further, interaction effects between PM2.5 and calendar time were not significant in these stratified analyses, except in participants without diabetes at baseline in Delhi.

In sensitivity analyses using alternate formulations of eGFR (using the CKD EPI-Pakistan (PK) equations), we observed similar magnitudes of association between annual average PM2.5 and eGFR in both cities (Supplementary Table S4).

Discussion

We observed significant associations between time-varying exposure to PM2.5 and decreases in eGFR in a cohort of adults in 2 cities of India, Chennai and Delhi. PM2.5 exposure in Delhi was higher than in Chennai, and the decreases in eGFR were greater in Delhi than in Chennai. The associations were stronger among younger participants and subgroups with higher central obesity in Delhi. Even though the associations were clinically small in magnitude, these significant findings in a relatively younger population could indicate increased vulnerability to PM2.5 in a large South Asian population and cumulative worsening over time. Temporal declines of varying magnitudes were observed in each city, with a dose-response relationship, and the largest 5-year decline observed in female participants from Delhi.

Previous studies on eGFR trajectories in the same population have indicated rapid decline in eGFR among individuals with risk factors such as diabetes and cardiovascular diseases.16 Faster decline has also been observed among South Asian population residing in United Kingdom, which could be attributed to the unique South Asian pathophysiology.22 In this study, we observed larger temporal decline, even after accounting for PM2.5, in Delhi specifically among females, diabetics, normotensive individuals, and individuals with low and medium central obesity, which might indicate city- and subgroup-specific differences even within South Asians. Potential reasons behind such differences could include dietary practices, and other environmental factors such as temperature and genetic susceptibility.

Evidence from studies linking exposure to PM2.5 with eGFR and CKD has been limited and inconclusive. In the USA-based Veterans Administration Normative Aging Study, a 2.1 μg/m3 (IQR) increase in annual average PM2.5 was associated with a 1.87 (0.76–2.99) ml/min per 1.73 m2 lower eGFR in older men.23 A study from Boston, USA, on participants hospitalized with confirmed acute ischemic stroke, reported closer proximity to major roadways was associated with a 3.9 ml/min per 1.73 m2 lower eGFR (95% CI: 1.0–6.7).24 In the multiethnic study of atherosclerosis, no significant results were reported for the association between PM2.5 and kidney function,25 whereas in the Jackson Heart Study, conflicting associations were reported for PM2.5 and ozone in relation to kidney function.26 In a Flemish population-based study, PM2.5 or black carbon were not associated with renal outcomes, either cross-sectionally or longitudinally.27

There are few longitudinal studies linking PM2.5 with kidney function and kidney disease from East and Southeast Asia, with significant associations being reported in China,28, 29, 30 Taiwan,31,32 Hong Kong,33 and South Korea,34 predominantly in elderly populations. Specifically, Li et al29,30 reported a 0.72% decline in eGFR for every 10 ug/m3 change in annual PM2.5, whereas we estimated a 0.99% decline for every IQR PM2.5 increase in both cities. However, large cross-sectional studies have reported no significant associations between PM2.5 and eGFR in Taipei (Yang et al35), emphasizing the need for population-representative and location-specific studies.

Most of the aforementioned studies were conducted in countries that reported much lower levels of PM2.5 than those in Delhi or Chennai, or in participants with existing conditions. For example, annual average PM2.5 concentrations in USA,23 Belgium27 and South Korea34 were 11.4, 13.1, and 25.0 μg/m3 respectively, which are 4 to 10 times less than those for Chennai and Delhi in our data. However, studies from China30 have reported annual average concentrations of 61 μg/m3, which is more than that reported for Chennai but less than that reported for Delhi. Further, CKD is mostly studied in older adults while observing decline in eGFR requires long-term follow-up starting from a young age. Although an annual reduction of 5 ml/min per 1.73 m2 is considered clinically significant, especially in individuals with CKD, temporal declines in eGFR were lower in this younger population. However, declines in kidney function usually follow other CVD risk factors such as hypertension, and treatment for such conditions could mask the actual effects of PM2.5 on declines in eGFR. Hence, our data provide crucial evidence that could be used to study the progression towards CKD and its environmental determinants in a large normal South Asian population.

Mechanisms linking PM2.5 with circulatory outcomes include imbalance in autonomic nervous system, endothelial dysfunction, direct translocation, inflammation, and oxidative stress.36 Specifically for the renal system, PM2.5 can cause vascular dysfunction and microvascular damage, affecting the ability of kidneys to filter blood effectively.37,38 PM2.5 is also known to affect the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone System, which regulates blood pressure and kidney function.39 Direct translocation of the chemical components of PM2.5 via blood is another potential pathway that introduces toxicity within the kidneys.37

The major strengths of this analysis include the use of a robust validated exposure model for PM2.5 assessment and leveraging a young population-based cohort in urban India with high retention and minimal bias because of loss of follow-up. A high-resolution spatiotemporal model that incorporates multiple sources of variation provides an accurate estimation of neighborhood level PM2.5 exposure, unlike monitoring stations. Further, these results provide early insights into the progression towards CKD in a South Asian population and thus provides an opportunity to develop early interventions and strategies to mitigate the detrimental effects of air pollution.

There are a few limitations to this study. There were not enough incident CKD cases in the cohort to study the association between PM2.5 and incident CKD (defined as eGFR < 60 ml/min per 1.73 m2), although this might be overcome as we gather data from more follow-ups. Secondly, we did not have validated models for other pollutants such as NO2, which limits our ability to assess multipollutant effects. Thirdly, the cohort comprising only an urban population limits the generalizability of the results to the entire country. Fourth, despite accounting for nonrandom missingness, the attrition in blood samples over time could impact inferences about causality of the associations. Finally, although the sample of participants from each city was representative of the urban Delhi and Chennai populations in 2010 and 2011, the estimates from these analyses should be interpreted as internally valid for the analytical sample rather than fully representative of the population. Further, we need to be cautious in extrapolating these results to more recent years because of the potential change in population composition in both cities.

Conclusion

Our analyses provide evidence linking long-term exposure to ambient PM2.5 with declines in eGFR in 2 urban locations in India with different magnitudes of association and declines across time. These findings are important in the context of the ambient PM2.5 exposure linked to high burdens of multiple chronic conditions and the unique pathophysiology of cardio-kidney-metabolic diseases.
