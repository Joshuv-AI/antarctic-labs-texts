Exome Sequencing Identifies a Novel Splicing Variant in COL9A3 Resulting in Multiple Epiphyseal Dysplasia: A Case Report
Authors: Bashayer Alnuaimi, Valancy Miranda, Anne Marie Sbrocchi, Philippe M Campeau, Sarah Campillo, Piya Lahiry
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundMultiple epiphyseal dysplasia (MED, OMIM #600969) is sometimes a mild skeletal dysplasia with diverse clinical findings, including early‐onset osteoarthritis and short stature. Radiographic surveys can identify delayed epiphyseal ossification and cartilaginous changes. Due to genetic heterogeneity in MED, with dominant or recessive inheritance, molecular testing is essential for its diagnosis.MethodsThe clinical manifestations, the results of laboratory examinations, and genetic analysis of a 14‐year‐old Pakistani male with MED are reported.Case PresentationHere we present a male patient with type‐1 diabetes and hypothyroidism with bilateral knee effusions, right knee flexion contracture, and chronic arthralgias in his elbows and wrists. Given his symptomatology, a diagnosis of juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA) was initially suspected. Radiographs revealed sclerotic changes and fragments in the femoral and tibial epiphyses, suggesting destructive arthropathy. Genetic testing identified a COL9A3 variant (c.148‐1G>C), as well as CTLA4 deficiency. The COL9A3 gene produces type IX collagen, and mutations in this gene can disrupt collagen folding or its interaction with other cartilage components. Complications include joint damage and early osteoarthritis, possibly requiring surgery.DiscussionTo date, only three COL9A3 splice‐site mutations have been linked to MED. Our patient's splicing variant (c.148‐1G>C) is novel and is likely causative, based on similar pathogenic mutations. Our patient presented with symptoms suggestive of JIA, but radiographic findings were inconsistent with this diagnosis. Genetic testing revealed a new pathogenic splicing variant in the COL9A3 gene, confirming MED.ConclusionThis case highlights the importance of early molecular testing if radiographic sclerotic changes are seen in the epiphyses due to the clinical and genetic heterogeneity of MED.

Introduction

Multiple epiphyseal dysplasia (MED, OMIM #600969) is a group of clinically and genetically heterogeneous diseases resulting in osteochondrodysplasia (Lohiniva et al. 2000). MED usually arises during childhood, between the ages of 2 and 14 years, with a prevalence ranging from 1 in 10,000 to 1 in 20,000 individuals (Briggs and Chapman 2002; Unger et al. 2008). This condition is characterized by rapid degeneration of healthy cartilage and the development of early‐onset osteoarthritis, mostly of the hips and knees. Other reported features may include short stature (Anthony et al. 2015) and proximal muscle myopathy (Bonnemann et al. 2000). Radiological findings include flattened, irregular epiphyses and joint deformities, often leading to early‐onset osteoarthritis (Anthony et al. 2015). Importantly, spinal abnormalities are typically absent in MED (Haga et al. 1998). The phenotypic spectrum of MED can vary widely, presenting challenges in both the diagnosis and elucidation of the underlying molecular mechanisms (Unger et al. 2008). Most of the identified cases exhibit an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern (Czarny‐Ratajczak et al. 2001; Dahlqvist et al. 2009); however, autosomal recessive forms and variability in disease expressivity make an accurate genetic diagnosis necessary (Unger et al. 2008).

Mutations in six distinct genes are responsible for MED. The autosomal recessive form of MED is linked to the diastrophic dysplasia sulfate transporter (DTDST, or SLC26A2) (Superti‐Furga et al. 1999), whereas the autosomal dominant forms of MED are linked to mutations in the genes encoding cartilage oligomeric matrix protein (COMP), matrilin‐3 (MATN3) protein, and the chains of type IX collagen proteins (COL9A1, COL9A2, COL9A3) (Anthony et al. 2015; Czarny‐Ratajczak et al. 2001; Dahlqvist et al. 2009; Jeong et al. 2014).

Herein, we report a patient with MED associated with a novel pathogenic splicing variant in COL9A3, presenting with knee effusions, joint contractures, and erosive arthropathy. This case expands the range of splice‐site‐associated phenotypes associated with MED, including its association with erosive joint pathology. The clinical and molecular analysis of this case advances our knowledge and understanding of the genetic factors and phenotypic expression of MED and may help guide further management.

Methods

Chromosome analysis was performed by a microarray CGH at the CHU Sainte‐Justine laboratory (using a PerkinElmer CGX‐HD 4 × 180 K array analyzed on an Agilent Bioanalyzer), which was normal. Genetic sequencing was then performed with peripheral blood samples from the patient and his parents. The samples were collected at Ste. Justine's and then sent to PreventionGenetics for PGxome sequencing, and an analysis for immune dysregulation panel genes was performed for the proband (assessing 59 genes). It allowed the identification of a CTLA4 c.125_126delinsC (p.Gln42Profs*30) heterozygous variant. Thereafter, the raw exome data was requested and reviewed by a medical geneticist and allowed the identification of the novel heterozygous COL9A3 c.148‐1G>C variant. The pathogenicity of candidate mutations was analyzed based on the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines (Richards et al. 2015). Sanger sequencing at PreventionGenetics was used to validate the variant and perform familial segregation.

Case Presentation

A 14‐year‐old Pakistani male from a non‐consanguineous family with a prior medical history of type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1D), diagnosed at age 12 and for which he receives insulin, and hypothyroidism, diagnosed at age 7 and for which he receives Levothyroxine. The study followed the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

The patient presented with bilateral knee effusions, a right knee flexion contracture, and chronic arthralgias affecting the elbows and wrists. These signs and symptoms raised the suspicion for an underlying chronic inflammatory condition such as juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA). Initial laboratory investigations including complete blood count (CBC), liver profile, renal function, and C‐reactive protein indicated an overall unremarkable profile with the exception of a slightly elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR). However, the plain radiographs showed abnormal distal femoral and proximal tibial epiphyses with sclerotic changes and fragmentation, pointing towards a destructive arthropathy with active participation of the epiphysis (Figure 1). These unusual findings were not typical for JIA and, as such, broadened our differential to consider septic arthritis, MED, and bone diseases associated with diabetes such as Wolcott‐Rallison syndrome and Charcot arthropathy.

Radiographic bilateral knee findings of patient. Demonstrates extensive epiphyseal remodeling with flattened articular surfaces, cortical irregularities, and joint space narrowing consistent with secondary erosive arthropathy.

To better understand the etiology, whole exome sequencing was done which identified a likely pathogenic variant in the COL9A3 gene (hg 38—NC_000020.11:g.62818517G>C, NM_001853.3:c.148‐1G>C). This variant was identified in the proband in a heterozygous state. Parental testing confirmed maternal inheritance of the variant. The proband's mother is in overall good health but has a reported history of joint pain and a height of 5 ft 1 in. The maternal family history is notable for a maternal aunt with unexplained knee pain and a maternal grandmother with arthritis and renal insufficiency requiring transplantation, both of unknown etiology. The proband's parents declined genetic testing of their other children, and additional extended family members were unavailable for genetic evaluation at the time of assessment.

Furthermore, a variation in the CTLA4 gene was detected (hg 38—NC_000002.12:g.203870601:GC>C, NM_005214.4:c.125_126delinsC, p.Gln42Profs*30), which was inherited paternally and likely contributed to the patient's autoimmune manifestations of T1D and hypothyroidism, given that it is a loss‐of‐function variant. Since his MED diagnosis, the patient has had regular follow‐up visits for symptom assessment and radiological imaging to monitor the progression of joint damage. Symptomatic management with nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) has been offered, with a plan to involve orthopedics for surgical intervention in the future, if required.

Discussion

MED is a phenotypically heterogeneous skeletal dysplasia with predominant features of delayed and/or irregular epiphyseal ossification and early onset of osteoarthritis. Clinical signs of MED frequently appear in the first 10 years of life with a gradual decrease in mobility and functional impairment, especially when the joints of the lower extremities are involved. Radiographs demonstrate epiphyseal abnormalities, including sclerotic changes and fragmentation.

Our patient demonstrated classic clinical and radiographical manifestations of MED, but he also had more severe features including bilateral knee effusions and a right knee flexion contracture. Upon genetic evaluation, he was found to have a heterozygous mutation in COL9A3 leading to the diagnosis of MED.

Type IX collagen is a structural component of the extracellular matrix, comprising a heterotrimer formed by one chain each from the COL9A1, COL9A2, and COL9A3 genes. The COL9A3 peptide chain has three collagenous (COL) domains separated by four non‐collagenous (NC) domains (Figure 2). COL9A3 is part of a structural protein essential for maintaining the integrity of various connective tissues, including bone, cartilage, and ligaments (Bonnemann et al. 2000). Mutations within COL9A3 impair the proper folding of type IX collagen or disrupt its interactions with other essential components of the cartilage matrix, potentially leading to structural abnormalities and functional deficiencies (Bonnemann et al. 2000). Thus far, 4 variants in the COL9A3 gene have been reported as causative of MED, including 1 missense variation and 3 splice‐site variants (such as c.148‐1G>A at the same nucleotide, or c.148‐2A>T at the adjacent one). These splice‐site variants are predicted to induce exon skipping, specifically within the COL3 domain of the type IX collagen proteins (Fresquet et al. 2007). The splice‐site variants cause mild MED that is characterized by knee pain and stiffness, osteochondritis dissecans (OCD) in the knees, osteoarthritis, and mild myopathy (Bonnemann et al. 2000; Nakashima et al. 2005; Paassilta et al. 1999).

Schematicre presentation of the COL9A3 c.148‐1G>C splice‐site variant and its predicted effect on gene, mRNA, and protein structure. The top panel shows the c.148‐1G>C substitution (red) at the intron 2/exon 3 splice acceptor site. This variant is predicted to cause exon 3 skipping, resulting in an aberrant mRNA transcript (middle panel in red). The lower panel illustrates the COL9A3 protein with 4 non‐collagenous (NC) and 3 collagenous (COL) domains; exon 3 loss is expected to affect the COL3 domain, leading to impaired assembly and stability of type IX collagen.

In our patient, the novel splicing variant in the COL9A3 gene (c.148‐1G>C) was classified as “likely pathogenic” for MED as per the ACMG criteria (Jeong et al. 2014) because the same nucleotide is affected by a previously established pathogenic variant (PS1), and the variant is absent from population databases such as gnomAD (PM2).

MED is typically marked by intrafamilial variability where relatives are found to have short stature and/or joint pain (Briggs et al. 2024), as we see with the mother who is a carrier for this novel mutation. It could be postulated that this variant leads to exon 3 skipping (most likely given experiments performed by Lohiniva et al. (2000) for the different G>A mutations at the same nucleotide), intron retention, or cryptic splice site activation, but this would need further evidence using RNA‐based experiments. Of note, while previous studies have identified pathogenic variants in COL9A3 associated with noninflammatory joint manifestations, our patient presents a unique scenario involving erosive arthropathy with joint effusions and contractures. Due to a heightened risk of progressive joint damage and early‐onset osteoarthritis, he may necessitate future surgical interventions, which highlights the importance of vigilant monitoring and management strategies.

In addition, this patient's CTLA4 mutation holds significance, as it likely contributes to the patient's autoimmune manifestations, including type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1D) and hypothyroidism. Indeed, the mutation induces haploinsufficiency for CTLA4, a known condition causing “Immune dysregulation with autoimmunity, immunodeficiency, and lymphoproliferation” (OMIM #616100) which can be treated with CTLA4‐Ig (abatacept), immunosuppressants such as systemic steroids, sirolimus, and rituximab, depending on the severity of the manifestations (Schwab et al. 2018). Continued monitoring of this patient will be imperative to track disease progression and adapt the management strategy accordingly.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this case underscores the importance of considering a broad differential diagnosis and incorporating genetic testing when clinical and radiological features are atypical. Although the patient initially presented with features suggestive of juvenile idiopathic arthritis, further evaluation revealed radiographic discrepancies that prompted genetic analysis. Exome sequencing identified a previously unreported splicing variant in the COL9A3 gene, supporting a diagnosis of MED. This case highlights the clinical and genetic heterogeneity of MED and demonstrates how genetic testing can facilitate timely and accurate diagnosis, guiding appropriate management. The novel COL9A3 variant also suggests a broader phenotypic spectrum of COL9‐MED than previously recognized.
