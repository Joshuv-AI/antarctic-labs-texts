Awareness of respiratory syncytial virus and other respiratory disease vaccines among healthcare professionals and their patients in Italy: Insights from a literature review and a web-based survey
Authors: Claudio Micheletto, Giancarlo Lorenzini, Sara De Grazia, Fabiano Di Marco, Rosa Di Matteo, Paola Faverio, Andrea Gramegna, Marta Vicentini, Francesco Blasi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRespiratory syncytial virus (RSV) causes respiratory infections across all ages, with an increasingly recognized burden in older adults, particularly those with comorbidities. Despite the recent licensure of RSV vaccines, awareness of RSV and other respiratory disease vaccinations remains limited. We conducted a literature review and a web-based survey to explore the awareness of RSV and respiratory disease vaccinations among Italian healthcare professionals (HCPs) and patients. This survey (N = 540) was conducted between April and May 2023 by the Italian Society of Pneumology and the Italian Association of Hospital Pulmonologists. HCPs consider RSV a major concern and understand the importance of vaccination. However, HCPs have limited familiarity with RSV, which may contribute to sub-optimal vaccination adherence. Patients are concerned about RSV, but their awareness is lower than for other respiratory infections. As trusted sources of information, HCPs could enhance education and support patient care with an integrated approach, from prevention to treatment.

Introduction

Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is a highly transmissible seasonal human ribonucleic acid (RNA) virus that causes respiratory tract infections.1 RSV is the most common cause of pediatric acute lower respiratory tract disease (LRTD), affecting almost every child by the age of 2 years.2,3 Natural infection only confers incomplete and short-lived immunity against RSV, with reinfections common.4,5 In addition to the pediatric burden, RSV poses an increasing clinical and economic burden in older adults,1,6–11 deemed at least equivalent to that of influenza.12–14 RSV symptoms in adults typically include upper respiratory infection signs, such as rhinorrhea, cough, fatigue and fever.2 Older adults (≥60 years of age) or high-risk patients are prone to severe LRTD-associated symptoms (e.g. pneumonia). Risk factors for severe disease include advanced age, frailty and comorbidities (e.g. immunodeficiency, pulmonary and cardiovascular conditions).2 RSV infections among older adults are widespread, although often underdiagnosed.15,16

RSV was responsible for approximately 5.2 million cases of acute respiratory infection in 2019, resulting in 470 000 hospitalizations and 33 000 deaths among older adults in industrialized countries.15 In Europe, the number of RSV-associated hospitalizations in 2016 was 158 229, of which 92% occurred in older adults.7 RSV infections can lead to severe complications, prolonged hospitalizations and high mortality rates in older adults and adults with comorbidities.17–20 Patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) or asthma are particularly susceptible to severe RSV disease,16,21–24 which poses considerable challenges in clinical practice.25,26

In Italy, recent estimates of RSV prevalence range from 4.5% to 13.1%,27–30 which aligns with the European estimate of 11% in 2023.31 RSV-related mortality in Italian older adults is high (6.6% to 7.2%), probably due to the underdiagnosis of RSV in this population.30,32–34

In the absence of antiviral therapy, supportive care has long been the standard approach for RSV disease in older adults.35 The first three RSV vaccines have recently been approved for adults aged ≥60 years: the adjuvanted prefusion F protein-based RSV vaccine (adjuvanted RSVPreF3, GSK), the bivalent prefusion F protein-based RSV vaccine (RSVpreF, Pfizer) and the messenger RNA-based RSV vaccine (mRNA-1345, Moderna).36–44

The adjuvanted RSVPreF3 vaccine prevents RSV-related LRTD (vaccine efficacy [VE] 82.6%, 96.95% confidence interval [CI] 57.9–94.1) and severe LRTD (VE 94.1%, 95% CI 62.4–99.9) in older adults.45 The adjuvanted RSVPreF3 vaccine is also efficacious in older adults with ≥ 1 cardiorespiratory condition (VE 92.1%, 95% CI 46.7–99.8) or any conditions of interest (VE 94.6%, 95% CI 65.9–99.9).46 One dose of the adjuvanted RSVPreF3 vaccine is efficacious for three RSV seasons in older adults, with a VE of 62.9% (97.5% CI 46.7–74.8) against RSV-related LRTD.47 The second licensed RSVpreF vaccine shows a VE of 66.7% (96.66% CI 28.8–85.8) and 85.7% (96.66% CI 32.0–98.7) against RSV-associated LRTD with ≥ 2 and ≥ 3 signs/symptoms, respectively.48 The mRNA-1345 vaccine is efficacious against RSV-related LRTD in older adults with a VE of 78.7% (95.04% CI 62.8–87.9) for LRTD with ≥ 2 symptoms and of 80.9% (95.10% CI 50.1–92.7%) for LRTD with ≥3 symptoms.39,49,50 All approved vaccines have an acceptable safety profile. Other RSV vaccines are in development for older adults.51,52

Given the high level of medical need, these encouraging results led to several directives for RSV vaccination, including the Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA),53 the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD),54 the American Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP)55 and the Italian Calendar for Life.56

Although the existing literature highlights knowledge gaps regarding respiratory vaccination in Italy, there are currently no data available regarding the awareness of RSV vaccination among pulmonologists.57–61 As RSV vaccines have only recently been developed and authorized, understanding how healthcare professionals (HCPs) and patients perceive RSV prevention is crucial for optimizing patient care in Italy.

To address this gap, in the present article we explore the awareness of RSV and other respiratory disease vaccinations among Italian HCPs and their patients, via a literature review complemented by a web-based survey. Materials and methods

For the literature review, the search was performed using the following keywords: ‘respiratory syncytial virus vaccines,’ ‘respiratory tract infections,’ ‘physician’s role,’ ‘patient compliance,’ ‘health literacy’ and ‘vaccination coverage’” until July 2024. The search was conducted with no formal language restrictions, focusing primarily on sources in English and Italian. References were identified through PubMed, Google Scholar and official institutional websites, including national public health authorities.

This review article is also supported by the results of an internal survey conducted between April and May 2023 by the Italian Society of Pneumology (SIP/IRS) and the Italian Association of Hospital Pulmonologists (AIPO – ITS). This web-based, self-administered survey was emailed to all 1426 members of the SIP/IRS and the AIPO – ITS, representing a convenience sample of Italian pulmonologists. The survey comprised 16 questions with multiple-choice items (see Supplemental Material) and, according to local regulations, did not require specific ethics approval or informed consent. Responses were anonymized, aggregated and subjected to descriptive analysis.

The survey assessed demographic and professional characteristics (i.e. framing variables), attitudes toward vaccine application and recommendations, perceived barriers to vaccination, and vaccination practices. Framing variables – including sex, medical specialization, years of experience, work region, work setting, operational setting and contribution to the active search for patients who have not been vaccinated – were assessed through open-ended questions. Agreement with statements regarding respiratory vaccine application and recommendations was measured on a 10-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (‘Absolutely disagree’) to 10 (‘Absolutely in agreement’), with 5 considered to mean ‘Uncertain.’ Barriers to vaccination perceived by HCPs and patients were assessed through multiple-choice questions, allowing one or more responses. Application, recommendations for, and awareness of each specific respiratory disease vaccine were evaluated as follows: Influenza vaccination: assessed through yes/no and multiple-choice questionsRSV and herpes zoster vaccination: assessed through multiple-choice questions and agreement scores based on a 10-point Likert scale, as detailed aboveSevere acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) vaccination: assessed through multiple-choice questions

Patient attitudes and perceptions toward vaccination

Despite the benefits of vaccination, misinformation can often change patients’ perceptions of new vaccines.62 This trend is evident for RSV vaccination among older adults, with the lack of public awareness affecting clinical practice.63 RSV remains mistakenly considered to be a pediatric virus, leading to major knowledge gaps (e.g. infection rates, severity risks and benefits of vaccination) among older adults.62 In a cross-sectional survey of older adults and adults with comorbidities (N = 1065) conducted in Germany and Italy, awareness of RSV (31%) was lower than that for other respiratory infections, such as pertussis (84%) and pneumococcal disease (73%).63 The mean score for knowledge of respiratory infections was 3.1 on a Likert scale ranging from 0 (‘No knowledge’) to 5 (‘Perfect knowledge’). Among older adults who were aware of RSV, the mean score for knowledge of RSV was 1.5 on this 5-point scale. Older adults were most concerned about contracting RSV (57% of patients who knew of RSV), followed by coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19; 53%), pneumococcal disease (51%), influenza (38%), pertussis (31%) and the common cold (28%). Vaccine hesitancy, associated with less concern about respiratory infections, was reported in 34% of patients. These results corroborate those of a recent survey conducted in the United States (US) (N = 827), in which just 43.3% of older adults had heard of RSV, 33.7% were concerned about it and 67.3% rarely considered it a cause of respiratory symptoms.64 Considerable knowledge gaps, such as the nature of the disease and its seasonality, symptoms and severity, were also identified.

This lack of knowledge among the public is also observed for other vaccine-preventable infectious diseases.59–61 An Italian survey (N = 414) showed that 71% of patients with chronic diseases knew none of the recommended vaccines.59 Only a third of them knew that vaccines were safe for patients with chronic conditions and did not risk exacerbating their disease. More than half of the respondents feared vaccine side effects and were less likely to have received the recommended vaccines. Despite positive attitudes toward vaccination, overall adherence was considerably below the 75% target set by the Italian National Immunization Plan. In an Italian survey conducted during the 2017–2018 influenza season (N = 700), 64.7% of adults with chronic diseases were aware of influenza vaccines and their higher risk of severe complications.60 Less than half of them (42.1%) had received the influenza vaccine during the previous season (2016–2017), while 46.9% declared their willingness to accept it during the next season. The level of awareness regarding the influenza vaccine was lowest among adults aged ≥65 years. Another Italian survey among older adults (N = 600) also reported a low coverage for pneumococcal vaccination (11.2%), with respondents found to be uninformed about vaccination opportunities.61 Overall, 48.1% of respondents considered their general practitioner to be their primary source of information. The main determinants of vaccine hesitancy were the perception of disease-related risks and the adverse effects of vaccination.

Together, these observations highlight a lack of public knowledge about RSV and other respiratory disease vaccinations in Italy. This limited awareness probably contributes to the low adherence to vaccination recommendations, despite the Italian National Immunization Plan 2023–2025 being considered one of the most advanced vaccination programs in Europe.65,66 Two of the 10 objectives of the current Italian National Immunization Plan are reinforcing communication about vaccination and promoting vaccination awareness and vaccinology education among HCPs.65 Indeed, it is imperative to ensure that patients receive comprehensive information through effective communication,67 enabling them to make informed decisions about their health.68 HCPs are uniquely positioned to address barriers to vaccination, as they are the most trusted sources of information. They play a key role in educating patients about vaccination,69–71 72% of whom follow their doctor’s advice to get vaccinated.72

Perspectives on vaccination among healthcare professionals

Most European HCPs view vaccination against respiratory disease to be an essential tool for self-protection and patient care.69,71,73 Findings from an Italian survey support this, with 88% of HCPs endorsing vaccinations, deeming them beneficial for their health (87.1%) and for their patients (86.6%).74 As recently demonstrated, understanding the characteristics of vaccines builds HCPs’ confidence and willingness to recommend them.69,71,75 However, just 14.1% of Italian HCPs are aware of all recommended vaccines, and only half of them believe that vaccination information is reliable.74

In this context, improving HCPs’ understanding has also become crucial to achieving optimal coverage of newly licensed RSV vaccines and addressing the burden of this disease. However, data show that HCPs’ knowledge of RSV remains limited in several countries, including Italy, Germany and the US.58,73,76 A 2021 Italian survey (N = 543) revealed unsatisfactory knowledge, with considerable gaps in relation to RSV epidemiology and prevention among HCPs.58 One-third of HCPs considered that nearly all RSV infections occurred in children. The perception of risk was higher for infants (77.6%) than for older adults (60.5%; p < .001), although most HCPs had a favorable attitude toward RSV vaccination. A recent cross-sectional survey (N = 273) conducted in Germany and Italy showed that most HCPs regarded RSV as a very important pathogen for patients aged ≥50 years with pulmonary (68%), cardiovascular (55%) or immunocompromising (74%) diseases. RSV was considered a somewhat (46%) or very (16%) important pathogen for adults aged 60–64 years.73 Most HCPs had high expectations of the RSV vaccine but exhibited a lower familiarity with RSV than with other respiratory viruses, with 71% expressing an interest in receiving more information about RSV.

Survey results

To complement the findings of our literature review and gain further insights into current practices and awareness in Italy, the SIP/IRS and AIPO – ITS societies conducted a web-based survey among their members. This survey aimed to explore the views of Italian pulmonologists regarding vaccination against RSV and other respiratory viruses, as well as their perceptions of patients’ attitudes.

Of the 1426 Italian pulmonologists invited to participate, 540 completed the survey, yielding a response rate of 37.9%. This response rate is more than twice that reported in an Italian survey recently conducted among AIPO pulmonologists (16.2%).77 HCPs from all 20 Italian regions responded to the survey, with the highest numbers being from Lombardy (n = 116), Veneto (n = 54) and Emilia-Romagna (n = 50). Just over half (58%) of the respondents were male, and most (93%) were pulmonologists specialized in respiratory diseases. Years of specialization varied among respondents, ranging from < 5 years (25%) to > 35 years (19%), with a mean of 20 years. Pulmonologists most often based at a hospital (39%), a healthcare service (26%) or a university hospital (26%). A majority (68%) reported working with both acute care and outpatient populations. These data reflect a specialist sample that is representative of the Italian context.

Interestingly, 81% of pulmonologists proactively assessed patient vaccination coverage by investigating their vaccination status, reflecting the importance of vaccination in clinical practice. The majority of HCPs agreed that vaccinations were safe and useful for respiratory and immunocompromised patients (Figure 2, Table 2). The most frequently recommended vaccinations for patients were the influenza and pneumococcal vaccines (99% for each), as aligned with the GINA/GOLD international guidelines for the prevention of respiratory infection-related exacerbations.53,54 Influenza vaccination has gained a prominent position among the annual recommendations, with 48% of Italian pulmonologists recommending vaccination for all patients over 65 years of age. Similarly, 93% of respondents acknowledged that they would recommend COVID-19 vaccination to their patients, and 71% of them recognized the need to include it among the annual recommended vaccines. Fewer respondents would recommend vaccination against pertussis (64%) and herpes zoster (73%) to their patients. Of note, approximately half of pulmonologists recommended herpes zoster vaccination for all patients who are immunocompromised and acknowledged a lack of information on the topic. This survey also identified barriers that prevent pulmonologists from recommending vaccination to their patients, particularly fear of adverse events (72%), lack of information (65%), personal conviction (44%), limited concern about the disease-related risks (39%) and, to a lesser extent, difficulty of access/availability (11%) (Figure 3A). HCPs reported the same barriers from the patient’s perspective, believing that their patients would mainly reject vaccination for fear of adverse events (Figure 3B). These findings suggest that fear of adverse events contributes to vaccine hesitancy, highlighting the need to better understand risk – benefit assessments in vaccination decisions. The introduction of new vaccines, such as those against RSV for adults, may encounter new obstacles to vaccination. In this survey, pulmonologists agreed about the usefulness of RSV vaccines, and 27% and 41% of them would recommend RSV vaccination to patients aged > 60 years and patients with chronic respiratory disease, respectively (Figure 4A). HCPs highlighted various barriers to RSV vaccination, such as a lack of information about the disease (61%) and the vaccine (55%), the absence of recommendations in the guidelines (45%), and limited concern about the risks associated with RSV (38%) (Figure 4B). Discussion

The survey findings are consistent with the literature and reinforce the fact that there is a persistent lack of awareness about vaccination for respiratory viruses, particularly for vaccination against RSV among older adults, in Italy and several other European countries.62,63 In line with these findings, previous studies have shown that fear of adverse events and limited knowledge remain key drivers of vaccine hesitancy, including in the context of RSV vaccination.61,64 The literature also indicates that, while most HCPs (88%) recognize the benefits of vaccination,74 important knowledge gaps persist among Italian HCPs,58,72,74 which is consistent with the survey findings. Altogether, these insights underscore the urgent need to strengthen HCPs’ knowledge of RSV and RSV vaccination to support uptake and reduce the associated disease burden. In parallel, enhancing their understanding of risk – benefit assessment is essential to address persistent concerns about adverse events following vaccination.

While the importance of vaccination against respiratory diseases is gaining recognition,6–11 there is a need for greater awareness among patients and their HCPs.63,73 This need is particularly urgent for new vaccinations against infections responsible for a heavy burden of disease, as illustrated by the new RSV vaccines for older adults. In recent years, Italy has witnessed an increase in the burden of RSV disease,78 with mortality levels reaching those of influenza worldwide.12–14 There are various possible factors responsible for this trend, including an aging population, improved diagnostic sensitivity, and reduced levels of immunity following the COVID-19 pandemic. In-hospital RSV-related mortality rates among Italian patients are particularly high (6.6–7.2%),30,33 with 1 death per 15.4 hospitalizations in adults > 70 years of age (versus < 1 death per 1000 hospitalizations in infants).32 This may be explained by an increased susceptibility to fatality, delayed hospitalizations and/or the underestimation of RSV incidence due to the lack of an adequate surveillance system in this population.15,16,30,32,76

Serology is generally ineffective for diagnosing acute respiratory infections, as 10–30% of infected patients are seronegative.79 Cell culture was historically considered the gold standard for diagnosis, but has now been replaced by faster and more sensitive methods.79 Although expensive, reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) assays testing nasopharyngeal swabs are the most sensitive type of routine test. However, additional approaches could improve RSV detection, such as the collection of alternative specimens and the use of multiplex respiratory pathogen panels.80 Triple combined rapid antigen tests also offer a rapid, low-cost and easy-to-perform alternative for detecting RSV, influenza A/B and SARS-CoV-2, although negative results should be confirmed with additional tests.81 The COVID-19 pandemic and the clinical need for rapid diagnosis of influenza have normalized large-scale respiratory virus testing. This has been driven by the widespread use of rapid antigen tests and multiplex RT-PCR panels, contributing to improved RSV detection.80,82 In addition to routine testing, careful record-keeping and automated reporting to healthcare authorities are essential for estimating RSV prevalence and raising public awareness.83

High awareness among HCPs remains essential to improving vaccination coverage. This is particularly the case in Italy, where HCPs have historically exhibited low awareness of RSV and other respiratory disease vaccinations.32,57,58 Raising awareness starts with identifying the at-risk population in clinical practice. As the global population ages, preventing RSV disease is becoming increasingly important for older adults and individuals with co-existing conditions. Patients with RSV tend to be older, frailer and more likely to have comorbidities than patients with influenza.33 The patients for whom RSV vaccination is most important include those who have cardiorespiratory diseases and immunocompromising conditions, even if they are aged <60 years.84

Additionally, it is now widely recognized that RSV also plays a crucial role in exacerbating COPD and asthma,16,21–23,25,26 conditions that contribute greatly to global mortality (with 455 000 and 3.23 million deaths, respectively, reported worldwide).85,86 As a result, the latest GINA/GOLD guidelines recommend RSV vaccination for older adults, which could help in raising awareness among HCPs. However, the literature indicates that Italian pulmonologists’ adherence to GINA/GOLD recommendations and national vaccination guidelines remains sub-optimal.74,75,87 This suggests that recommendations alone cannot guarantee high levels of adherence and that other factors contribute to vaccine hesitancy among HCPs.

To address knowledge gaps regarding respiratory vaccine awareness in Italy, we conducted a web-based survey and a literature review focusing on RSV and other vaccine-preventable diseases among HCPs and their patients. Strengths of this work included the use of a self-administered, web-based survey, which enabled the efficient collection of real-world data from a large sample (N = 540) within a relatively short timeframe. The online format facilitated accessibility, anonymity and geographical reach, potentially reducing social desirability bias and encouraging more accurate reporting. The survey instrument, composed of 16 targeted questions, was designed to ensure clarity and focus, supporting the collection of relevant data about respondents’ awareness and perceptions. The literature review complemented the survey by providing valuable insights that reinforced the interpretation of the results.

This study also had some limitations that must be acknowledged. The survey data were only analyzed descriptively, with no statistical methods performed. The self-selected nature of the respondents may have introduced selection bias, limiting the generalizability of the findings. Self-reported data are also subject to recall bias and subjective interpretation. Moreover, the use of a convenience sample further limited the representativeness of the surveyed population. The cross-sectional design precluded the assessment of causal relationships. Furthermore, the use of predominantly closed-ended questions may have limited the opportunity for respondents to provide more detailed or nuanced responses. Finally, the literature review was subject to inherent limitations, including potential selection bias and the lack of a systematic methodology.

Conclusions

In summary, our survey showed that HCPs consider RSV to be an important concern and understand the importance of vaccination. However, their limited familiarity with RSV may contribute to sub-optimal vaccination adherence and underscores the need for further education of HCPs about the disease and vaccination. Although patients are also concerned about RSV, their awareness of RSV is lower than that for other respiratory infections.

As trusted sources of information, HCPs could engage in transparent, patient-focused communication to raise public awareness on RSV disease and vaccination.66 HCPs should also prioritize continuing education to remain informed about the latest advances, such as the development of new vaccines. Public awareness may further be improved by incorporating patient-reported outcomes into vaccine research – an emerging trend that has been shown to enhance patient confidence in vaccination.88 Ultimately, an integrated medical approach that embeds prevention throughout the patient journey is essential for improved clinical outcomes.
