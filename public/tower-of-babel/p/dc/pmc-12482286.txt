Management of relapsed acute lymphoblastic leukemia in a patient with down syndrome: A case report
Authors: Eman Almatar, Sondus Alsharidah, Omnia A Hashem
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractChildren with Down syndrome (DS) have a 10–20-fold increased risk of acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) and heightened chemotherapy toxicity. Blinatumomab, a bispecific CD19 × CD3 T-cell engager, offers targeted immunotherapy with reduced myelotoxicity. We describe a 9-year-old girl with DS diagnosed with B-cell precursor ALL in early 2021 who relapsed during maintenance in September 2023. After reinduction, she received two 28-day blinatumomab cycles: the first resulted in <5 % blasts and undetectable minimal residual disease (MRD), and the second was well tolerated. She remains in remission pending allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, highlighting blinatumomab’s efficacy and safety as a bridge to transplantation.Keywords: Down syndrome, B-cell precursor acute lymphoblastic leukemia, Blinatumomab, Immunotherapy, Minimal residual disease

Introduction

Down syndrome (DS) is the most common chromosomal disorder worldwide, occurring in approximately 1 in 700 live births, and is associated with a 10–20-fold increased incidence of acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) compared to euploid children [1,2]. DS-ALL exhibits distinct molecular features, including overexpression of CRLF2 and JAK2 mutations, which contribute to leukemogenesis and sensitivity to cytotoxic agents [3]. Despite improved overall survival rates (85–90) % in standard-risk pediatric ALL), children with DS-ALL experience up to a 30 % higher rate of treatment-related toxicity and a 2-fold increased risk of relapse, primarily due to myelosuppression and organ dysfunction [4,5]. The management of DS-ALL remains complex. While conventional multi-agent chemotherapy regimens—comprising glucocorticoids, antimetabolites, vincristine, L-asparaginase, and anthracyclines—have contributed to improved survival outcomes in children with ALL, their application in DS patients must be approached with caution. Despite achieving remission in many cases, the risk of relapse remains significant, and treatment intensification is often limited by the heightened toxicity profile observed in this vulnerable population [2]. Conventional intensification protocols often necessitate dose reductions or delays, compromising disease control.

Blinatumomab, an FDA-approved bispecific T-cell engager, links CD3-positive T cells to CD19-positive leukemia blasts, yielding potent anti-leukemic activity with a favorable safety profile in high-risk pediatric patients. In phase II/III trials of relapsed or refractory B-ALL, blinatumomab achieved complete remission in 43 %–45 % of patients and MRD negativity in over 80 % of responders, with a lower incidence of grade ≥3 adverse events compared with standard chemotherapy [6]. However, data on blinatumomab in DS-ALL remain limited to small cohorts and case reports, and its role as a transplant bridge in this population has not been fully defined. Here, we present a case of a 9-year-old girl with DS-ALL who achieved sustained MRD-negative remission with blinatumomab, illustrating the agent’s therapeutic potential and manageable safety profile in high-risk pediatric patients.

Case presentation

A 9-year-old girl with a known history of Down syndrome (trisomy 21) presented in early 2021 with B-cell precursor acute lymphoblastic leukemia. Her past medical history included surgically corrected ventricular septal and atrial septal defects. She was treated per UK ALL 2011 Regimen A, encompassing induction (dexamethasone, PEG-asparaginase, vincristine, and intrathecal methotrexate), consolidation, interim maintenance, delayed intensification, and maintenance phases (Table 1). The patient achieved morphological remission and MRD negativity (<0.01 % by flow cytometry) after induction. She tolerated all therapy phases without significant complications.

In September 2023, while the patient was on maintenance therapy, she underwent a routine bone marrow evaluation.

Reinduction chemotherapy was administered per UK ALL 2011 Regimen A (dexamethasone, vincristine, PEG-asparaginase, intrathecal methotrexate) with achievement of a second morphological remission (MRD 0.06 % by flow cytometry), prompting sequential blinatumomab to attain MRD negativity (Table 2).

Subsequently, she received two consecutive 28-day continuous intravenous infusions of blinatumomab at 15 µg/m²/day with dexamethasone premedication. After the first cycle, marrow blasts were <5 % and MRD was undetectable (<0.01 %); cycle 2 was completed without dose delays or significant toxicity. Adverse events were limited to grade 1 fever managed supportively; no neurotoxicity or infectious complications occurred. Echocardiography showed stable function throughout therapy. The patient remains in sustained complete remission and is scheduled for allogeneic HSCT when human leukocyte antigen (HLA) match donor is received. Until then, blinatumomab is administered to the patient.

The planned conditioning regimen consists of fludarabine, treosulfan, and thiotepa, in alignment with reduced-intensity transplant protocols commonly used in children with Down syndrome to minimize toxicity while preserving engraftment efficacy.

Discussion

Management of ALL in children with Down syndrome is challenged by both intrinsic disease biology and increased treatment-related toxicity. Genetic alterations such as CRLF2 overexpression and JAK-STAT pathway mutations drive leukemogenesis and contribute to chemotherapy hypersensitivity, necessitating tailored treatment strategies [3,5].

While conventional salvage regimens can achieve remission, DS patients experience organ toxicity that delays therapy and increases relapse risk [5].

Blinatumomab’s mechanism of bridging CD3-positive T cells to CD19-positive blasts allows for potent clearance of minimal disease with reduced myelotoxicity compared to multi-agent chemotherapy [4]. In this patient, blinatumomab induced minimal residual disease negativity after one 28-day cycle and sustained it through a second cycle, facilitating timely transition to transplant. The only notable adverse event was mild cytokine release syndrome, aligning with safety profiles reported in broader pediatric and adult B-ALL populations [6].

Future research should focus on optimizing dosing regimens and integrating blinatumomab with targeted agents in the DS-ALL population to further improve outcomes.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates that blinatumomab can safely and effectively serve as a component of leukemia immunotherapy. Integration of precise MRD monitoring with bispecific immunotherapy offers a promising paradigm for high-risk pediatric subgroups and warrants validation in dedicated clinical trials. This case further reinforces the importance of personalized treatment approaches in pediatric oncology, especially for patients with underlying genetic syndromes, and supports the role of immunotherapy as an effective component of modern leukemia care.
