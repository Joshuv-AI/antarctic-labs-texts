Cardiac Tamponade, Pituitary Hyperplasia, and Macroorchidism in Severe Primary Hypothyroidism
Authors: Swagat Saha, Rajdeep Basu, Himanshu Singha, Sumit Kumar Chakrabarti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis case reports a rare and complex presentation of severe primary hypothyroidism (PH) in an 18-year-old male. The patient presented to the emergency department with dyspnea due to impending cardiac tamponade, requiring emergency pericardiocentesis. Further evaluation revealed features of long-standing PH, including short stature, cognitive delay, and pubertal discordance with macroorchidism. Laboratory tests showed extreme elevation of TSH and multiple anterior pituitary hormone deficiencies. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed a large sellar mass mimicking a pituitary adenoma. Treatment was initiated with levothyroxine and physiological hydrocortisone. Follow-up at 6 months showed improvement in clinical status and TSH level and significant regression of the pituitary mass on repeat MRI, confirming the diagnosis of pituitary hyperplasia in primary hypothyroidism (PHPH). This case highlights the potential for severe, life-threatening manifestations of PH and emphasizes that a therapeutic trial with levothyroxine is the definitive approach to differentiate PHPH from a TSH-secreting pituitary adenoma, thus avoiding unnecessary surgical intervention.Keywords: hypothyroidism, pericardial tamponade, macroorchidism, pituitary hyperplasia

Introduction

The precise regulation of thyroid hormone synthesis by the pituitary gland via secretion of TSH is fundamental to endocrine health. In primary hypothyroidism, the deficit in thyroid hormones removes crucial negative feedback, resulting in elevated hypothalamic thyrotropin-releasing hormone and subsequent pituitary TSH production [1]. This chronic stimulation can lead to thyrotroph hyperplasia of the pituitary gland known as pituitary hyperplasia in primary hypothyroidism (PHPH) [2, 3]. While rare, TSH-secreting pituitary adenomas also cause elevated TSH, but these typically manifest as secondary hyperthyroidism [1]. The exceedingly rare occurrence of an adenoma developing within a hyperplastic gland is often associated with specific genetic predispositions [2]. Accurate differentiation among these conditions is paramount, as their therapeutic approaches diverge significantly.

Here we describe some unusual clinical scenarios of primary hypothyroidism in a young male.

Case Presentation

An 18-year-old Indian male presented at the emergency department with progressively increasing shortness of breath, hypotension, raised jugular venous pressure, and muffled heart sounds. Chest radiographs and echocardiographic evaluation confirmed a pericardial effusion with signs of impending tamponade (Fig. 1). Emergency pericardiocentesis was performed, leading to hemodynamic stabilization. Repeat echocardiography did not show any evidence of recurrence.

Two-dimensional echocardiography in subxiphoid 4-chamber view showing significant circumferential pericardial effusion with impending tamponade (white arrow).

The patient also complained of lethargy, cold intolerance, and chronic constipation. His developmental history was notable for severe scholastic underperformance, leading to school dropout after 9 years of age. Physical examination revealed short stature, facial puffiness, bilateral pedal edema, a depressed nasal bridge, dry skin, coarse and lustreless hair, and poor dentition. Neurological assessment demonstrated a low intelligence quotient and delayed ankle jerks. His birth history was unremarkable.

On examination, his height was 135.2 cm and weight was 29.5 kg. It also indicated a height-for-age of 10 years, a weight-for-age of 9.5 years, and a height SD score of − 6.9 with a target height of 166 cm. His upper-to-lower segment ratio was 1.2, with an arm span of 130.4 cm.

There was absence of axillary hair with pubic hair assessment depicting Tanner stage 2. Stretched penile length was 6 cm with increased testicular volumes bilaterally (right: 25 mL, left: 15 mL).

Pericardial fluid evaluation showed no evidence of tuberculosis or malignancy.

Multiple hormonal abnormalities necessitated a comprehensive pituitary evaluation. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scan of the brain, performed with a pituitary protocol, revealed a sellar space occupying lesion measuring 16 mm supero-inferiorly, 12 mm antero-posteriorly, and 11 mm transversely (Fig. 3). Despite the absence of visual symptoms or headache, the presence of what looked like a pituitary mass, warranted a visual field assessment, which was found to be normal.

Sagittal section of magnetic resonance imaging of the brain demonstrating an enlarged anterior pituitary gland mimicking a sellar mass (white arrow).

Further investigations included a thyroid gland ultrasound, which showed a heterogeneous gland with right lobe measuring 1 × 0.6 cm and left lobe measuring 2.3 × 0.5 cm and a highly positive antithyroid peroxidase antibody titre of 301.1 IU/mL (SI: 301.10 kIU/L) (reference range, <35 IU/mL [SI: <35 kIU/L]).

The patient was diagnosed with primary hypothyroidism of autoimmune etiology, resulting in a pituitary enlargement inducing multiple hormone deficit secondary to glandular compression without evidence of external mass effect or elevated intracranial pressure.

Treatment

A medical management strategy was initiated. The patient was discharged on a regimen of oral levothyroxine 50 mcg once daily and oral hydrocortisone 10 mg at 6 Am and 5 mg at 4 Pm. Follow-up appointments were scheduled for 3 months and 6 months, with repeat TSH and free T4 measurements and a repeat pituitary MRI after 6 months.

Outcome and Follow-up

At the 3-month follow-up, TSH levels showed a decrease to 75 mIU/L (SI: 75 mIU/L), followed by a further reduction to 7.2 mIU/L (SI: 7.2 mIU/L) after 6 months (Table 2). After 6 months, a repeat morning serum cortisol was found as 6.1 μg/dL (SI: 168.29 nmol/L) after holding the hydrocortisone dose for 24 hours. Thus oral hydrocortisone was continued as a daily physiological dose of 10 mg divided into morning and afternoon hours, and oral levothyroxine was continued in the previous dose. A subsequent MRI scan performed at 6 months confirmed significant shrinkage of the pituitary mass to normal dimensions (Fig. 4), confirming the diagnosis of pituitary hyperplasia. Intelligence quotient testing and gonadal hormones assay were not reassessed at 6 months as further reassessment of all clinical and biochemical parameters was planned after 1 year from baseline.

Discussion

Primary hypothyroidism, a common endocrine disorder, results from diminished thyroid hormone synthesis by the thyroid gland with elevated levels of TSH [1]. The vast majority of cases are attributed to autoimmune thyroid diseases, with Hashimoto thyroiditis being the most frequent cause [4]. Other common etiologies include radioactive iodine ablation, iodine deficiency, postsurgical states, infiltrative disorders, congenital defects, and iatrogenic causes (eg, certain medications) [5, 6].

The clinical spectrum of primary hypothyroidism is notably broad and nonspecific. Common symptoms include fatigue, cold intolerance, weight gain, constipation, and bradycardia, while more severe manifestations may involve cognitive impairment, depression, and peripheral neuropathies [5]. In pediatric populations, the disorder can lead to growth retardation and compromised neurodevelopment. Adult patients may experience significant sexual and reproductive health issues [5, 7, 8]. The heterogeneous nature of its presentation, including atypical signs and symptoms, often contributes to diagnostic ambiguity [9].

The initial clinical manifestation in our patient was impending pericardial tamponade. Pericardial effusions, although often asymptomatic, are a recognized complication in severe, long-standing primary hypothyroidism and, in some cases, may be the presenting symptom [10]. The reported incidence of these effusions varies considerably across studies, ranging from 3% to 83% [11]. The underlying pathophysiological mechanisms are not fully understood, but proposed factors include increased capillary permeability, impaired lymphatic drainage, and myxedematous changes contributing to fluid accumulation [10]. Critically, these effusions can progress to severe, life-threatening cardiac failure tamponade [12].

Thyroid hormone plays a critical role in mediating somatic growth, skeletal development, and neurocognitive maturation [1]. In the present case, the patient's prolonged, untreated, or suboptimally managed primary hypothyroidism exerted a profound detrimental effect on growth. The mechanisms underlying this growth disturbance include direct inhibition of growth plate activity, resulting in delayed skeletal age, attenuation of the GH axis, and a generalized reduction in metabolic rate [13]. Consequently, a decelerated growth velocity frequently serves as the initial and most discernible clinical sign [14].

Alongside macroorchidism, discordant pubertal development was another notable finding in our patient. Thyroid hormones are recognized as crucial permissive hormones, influencing numerous endocrine axes, particularly the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis [15]. Reduced thyroid hormone levels disrupt the pulsatile secretion of GnRH from the hypothalamus and diminish the responsiveness of pituitary gonadotrophs [7, 8, 15]. Furthermore, hypothyroidism can directly impair gonadal function by compromising steroidogenesis [8]. In prepubertal males, this dysfunction can manifest as macroorchidism, characterized by a testicular volume substantially greater than the 95th percentile for age, or at least double the age-appropriate normal volume [8]. The primary mechanism for macroorchidism in this context involves excessively high TSH levels, which cross-activate FSH receptors on Sertoli cells, thereby inducing their proliferation and hypertrophy [8]. In rare instances, this constellation of symptoms, particularly when coupled with delayed bone age, may be indicative of Van Wyk-Grumbach syndrome [9, 16].

The occurrence of pituitary hyperplasia is a well-established observation in the background of primary hypothyroidism, as per previous studies reporting rates from 25% to 81% [2, 17, 18]. The hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid axis is fundamental to maintaining systemic thyroid hormone homeostasis [1, 2]. In patients with profound, longstanding primary hypothyroidism, sustained absence of negative feedback by thyroid hormones leads to compensatory hypertrophy and hyperplasia of pituitary thyrotrophs, driven by increased thyrotropin-releasing hormone secretion from the hypothalamus [2, 3]. This aligns with our case and also findings from an observational study by Khawaja et al, where pituitary enlargement on MRI was observed in 70% (37 out of 53) of patients with TSH levels > 50 mIU/L (SI: 50 mIU/L), with 84% (31 of these 37 patients) having TSH levels ≥100 mIU/L (SI: ≥100 mIU/L) [19]. As articulated by Melmed in his work on pituitary tumorigenesis [20], the complex interplay of central and peripheral trophic signals can induce reversible plastic alterations in cellular growth, culminating in hyperplasia, involution, or the development of benign adenomas originating from functionally active pituitary cells, exerting compressive effects on surrounding normal parenchyma, predisposing to multiple pituitary hormone deficiencies and neurological symptoms [21].

The coexistence of significant pituitary enlargement and elevated TSH levels necessitates differentiation from a TSH-secreting pituitary adenoma, as described in Table 3 [3, 22].

In our patient, initial MRI findings, mirroring observations in some earlier studies, were suggestive of a pituitary mass mimicking an adenoma [2, 19, 23]. Nevertheless, surgical intervention was judiciously avoided owing to the patient's positive response to thyroid hormone replacement therapy and the decision for close clinical monitoring [2, 23]. It is noteworthy that, albeit rarely, chronic stimulation and elevated proliferative rates in a hyperplastic pituitary gland can predispose to neoplastic transformation through an increased risk of genetic aberrations, potentially mandating surgical resection [20, 22]. These are sometimes termed “feedback adenomas” [24].

Most patients demonstrate a favorable response to thyroid hormone replacement therapy within 4 weeks to 3 months, with documented regression in nearly all cases [25]. Our patient exhibited a similar positive outcome, with a good response observed within 8 weeks and complete regression of hyperplastic gland by 6 months of replacement. Consistent with these findings, Shimono et al [3] reported a decrease in pituitary size just 5 weeks post-thyroid hormone replacement, and Khawaja et al [19] observed regression of pituitary hyperplasia in 85% of patients with follow-up MRI.

The primary limitation of this case report was that the patient was eventually lost to follow up. Thus, assessment of complete clinical parameters including cognition and all the baseline hormones could not be reassessed as per our plans. Furthermore, the case report lacks dynamic pituitary function testing due to limited resources.

Learning Points

Long-standing primary hypothyroidism can present with life-threatening critical manifestations, such as pericardial tamponade, requiring high clinical suspicion for diagnosis.
