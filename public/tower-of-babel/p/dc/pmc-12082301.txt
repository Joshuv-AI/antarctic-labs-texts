From sequence to mechanobiology? Promises and challenges for AlphaFold 3
Authors: Francesco Zonta, Sergio Pantano
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractInteractions between macromolecules orchestrate many mechanobiology processes. However, progress in the field has often been hindered by the monetary and time costs of obtaining reliable experimental structures. In recent years, deep-learning methods, such as AlphaFold, have democratized access to high-quality predictions of the structural properties of proteins and other macromolecules. The newest implementation, AlphaFold 3, significantly expands the applications of its predecessor, AlphaFold 2, by incorporating reliable models for small molecules and nucleic acids and enhancing the prediction of macromolecular complexes. While several limitations still exist, the continuous improvement of machine learning methods like AlphaFold is producing a significant revolution in the field. The possibility of easily accessing structural predictions of biomolecular complexes may create substantial impacts in mechanobiology. Indeed, structural studies are at the basis of several applications in the field, such as drug discovery for mechanosensing proteins, development of mechanotherapy, understanding the mechanotransduction mechanisms and the mechanistic basis of diseases, or designing biomaterials for tissue engineering.Keywords: AlphaFold, Protein folding, Biological complexes, Structure predictions, Drug design

The quest to predict protein structures from their amino acid sequences has been a central problem and a grand challenge in molecular biology since Anfinsen's groundbreaking experiments in the 1960s demonstrated that a protein's amino acid sequence determines its three-dimensional structure.

Besides its theoretical relevance in understanding the fundamental laws governing biological macromolecules, protein structure prediction has practical consequences as it opens the way to protein design for medical or industrial applications.1,2 This is particularly crucial in mechanobiology, where molecules' three-dimensional structures, interactions, and dynamic properties convey information that modulate cellular functions, tissue behavior, and physiological responses.

The Critical Assessment of Protein Structure Prediction (CASP) was introduced in 1994 to assess and drive progress in this field. While significant advancements were made over the years, with gradual improvements in accuracy, the problem of predicting protein structures remained largely unsolved for decades.3 Even the more accurate methodologies struggled to consistently predict the structure of proteins with limited homology to known structures, large proteins or protein complexes, and to achieve high accuracy across diverse protein families.

The introduction of AlphaFold by DeepMind in 2018 marked a turning point in the field. AlphaFold 1, which debuted at CASP13, outperformed other methods and hinted at the potential of deep learning in this domain. Its successor, AlphaFold 2, was unveiled at CASP14 in 2020 and truly revolutionized the field. With its remarkable accuracy, often rivaling experimental methods, AlphaFold 2 was hailed as a (technical) solution to the protein structure prediction.4 The third version of the AlphaFold software was presented on May 2024,5 representing a significant leap forward in the field. The improvement derives from a substantial evolution of network architecture and training procedure, which allows the representation of more general chemical structures and improves the efficiency of the learning process from available data. Furthermore, AlphaFold 3 applies a diffusion model to predict the coordinates of each atom, replacing the structure module of AlphaFold 2 that operated at the amino acid level.

These changes account for a qualitative improvement and open the possibility of learning structural patterns from smaller datasets more reliably. Even if the world of known structures is largely dominated by proteins (Fig. 1), AlphaFold 3 demonstrated an improved ability in predicting the structure of a larger array of biomolecules such as nucleic acids, small molecules, ions, modified residues, and, most importantly, complexes of these biomolecules.

Distribution of molecular species in the Protein Data Bank (PDB). Proteins-only structure accounts for more than three-quarters of all the data available in the PDB. The small amount of data existing for nucleic acids explains the difficulty in obtaining reliable predicting models using either traditional or machine learning methods.

This expanded capability allows researchers to study systems such as ribonucleoproteins and multi-protein complexes, surpassing the performance of previous models and specialized tools. For these reasons, AlphaFold 3 represents a groundbreaking work and will make it easier to understand how various cellular components interact, eventually providing a more comprehensive view of cellular machinery. This holistic approach to structural biology could accelerate research in mechanobiology, cell signaling, gene regulation, metabolic pathways, and drug design.

The ability of AlphaFold 3 to produce accurate protein-ligand models has been immediately recognized to be a crucial development for drug discovery. Computational design, indeed can reduce the time and monetary costs of the process, by reducing the necessity of initial experimental screenings.6 Similarly, AlphaFold 3's enhanced ability to model protein-antibody interactions holds significant promises for immunology research and therapeutic antibody development.7

AlphaFold 3 has also made substantial progress in the prediction of RNA structures. This field is becoming increasingly important with the advent of mRNA vaccine technologies, as intelligent RNA design can improve the stability and efficacy of mRNA fragments in producing antigenic proteins. Recent benchmarking studies have shown that AlphaFold 3 outperforms most existing solutions for RNA structure prediction and produces more physically plausible structures than ab initio methods.8 Nevertheless, we cannot consider the problem of RNA structure solved, as AlphaFold 3 still struggles with predicting non-Watson-Crick interactions and does not generalize well to orphan structures or long RNAs. This limitation is likely due to the lack of extensive training data for RNA structures compared to proteins, which constrains the model's performance in this area (Fig. 1).

Other limitations and persistent challenges faced by AlphaFold 3 are the accurate prediction of intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs) and the ability to obtain information on conformational dynamics. These two open problems are similar in nature, as they require the prediction of a whole ensemble of possible configurations and their relative probability. Currently, structural predictions are limited to finding the most likely or lowest energy conformer. Similarly to RNA structure prediction, the solution to these problems is hindered by the lack of experimental data and deficiencies in the force fields used to describe the macromolecular dynamics.

This is a clear limitation in Mechanobiology, as knowledge of the structure alone is only the first step in understanding the mechanical properties of proteins, tissues, or organs. Nevertheless, experimental techniques and simulations can help to overcome such limitations and thus remain crucial tools in the field. Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy and cryo-electron microscopy can help explore the dynamical configuration of macromolecules. At the same time, molecular dynamics simulations are routinely used to predict the time evolution of a biological system and to feed the Machine Learning models with synthetic data. Furthermore, the impressive ability of AlphaFold 3 to provide accurate structure prediction underlines the ability of Neural Networks to learn the physical properties of macromolecules from experimental data. The neural network could be trained to produce less probable states that correspond to higher energy configuration, and standard simulation techniques could be used to determine transition pathways between different states,9 providing important insight into molecular mechanics and dynamics. Finally, quantum effects, which are sometimes fundamental to correctly reproducing biological data,10,11 could be explicitly or implicitly taken into account by the neural network.

The success of AlphaFold has had far-reaching consequences for structural biology and related fields. It has led to a significant acceleration of research, allowing scientists to quickly obtain structural models for proteins that were previously challenging or impossible to characterize experimentally. This breakthrough has also contributed to the democratization of structural biology, expanding access to accurate protein structure predictions beyond specialized structural biology labs. Moreover, AlphaFold's success has triggered a shift in the field's focus, moving from the problem of structure prediction to more complex questions of protein function, dynamics, and design. Researchers can now devote more resources to understanding how proteins interact, how they change shape during their function, and how to design new proteins for specific purposes.

AlphaFold has spurred rapid development throughout the field of Computational Biology, inspiring the creation of competing and complementary tools. A notable example is RoseTTAFold, developed by the Baker Lab.12 This method employs principles similar to AlphaFold but utilizes a more lightweight architecture, making it more accessible to the broader scientific community. Another significant development is OpenFold,13 an open-source implementation of AlphaFold that fosters transparency and enables further innovations. These advancements have not only improved the accessibility of protein structure prediction tools but have also pushed the boundaries of what is possible in the field. These new tools and methods create a rich ecosystem of computational resources, further accelerating progress and opening new avenues for research and discovery. As happened with the first released versions, AlphaFold 3 will undoubtedly inspire new developments with similar architectures, improving the field of structural predictions at each step.

Structural models built from Artificial intelligence-based methods may set the initial step for various applications in Mechanobiology and Medicine (Fig. 2). The ability to screen and design small molecules or antibodies is a low-hanging fruit. On the one hand, as traditional computational methods become more reliable in predicting binding affinities between a drug and its target, structural models provide a stronger basis to reduce costs and time for producing new medicines by substituting experiments with modeling and simulation. On the other hand, the increasing complexity of what can be reliably predicted with computational methods enables a growing understanding of the biological processes relevant to medical applications, such as the mechanism of GPCR adhesion14 or tissue repair and regeneration.15 Structural insights are crucial for elucidating mechanotransduction pathways, designing biomaterials for tissue engineering, and understanding the mechanical basis of disease processes like cancer metastasis and cardiovascular disorders. Additionally, AlphaFold-derived models can aid in developing nanomedicine strategies, optimize gene delivery systems, and inform the design of mechanical sensors for diagnostic applications.

Potential application of Alphafold in mechanobiology and related fields.

In conclusion, AlphaFold 3 represents a significant leap forward in Computational Biology. While the technology still presents significant limitations, each iteration has pushed the boundary of the possible applications and opened new avenues for scientific discovery and therapeutic development. This trend will likely accelerate in the coming years as more scientists will work on applications of such methodology and produce meaningful real-world impact.
