Preclinical evaluation of rBCG-h12: an engineered Bacillus Calmette-Guérin with an enhanced activity against bladder and pancreatic cancers
Authors: Chang-Ching Yeh, Chi-Shuan Fan, Li-Li Chen, Kee Voon Chua, Chih-Hao Hsu, Chia-Chi Chen, Zhao-Lin Tan, Pei-Chu Tsai, Yung-Deng Song, Yong-Seng Low, Shiu-Ju Yang, Yih-Yuan Chen, Yi-Xiu Lin, Chia-Ming Wu, Horng-Yunn Dou, Tze-Sing Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntravesical Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) therapy has been the standard treatment for preventing recurrence of non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) for over four decades. Although maintenance BCG therapy improves recurrence-free survival in intermediate- and high-risk NMIBC patients, its long treatment duration, toxicity, and limited efficacy - particularly in carcinoma in situ -necessitate the development of improved therapeutic alternatives. rBCG-h12 is a recombinant BCG strain engineered to enhance immunogenicity. This study evaluated its ability to augment macrophage activation and antitumor responses.MethodsThe safety of intravesical rBCG-h12 was evaluated in NOD/SCID and C57BL/6 mice through four weekly instillations. Body weight, survival, urine hematuria, and organ histopathology were monitored. Antitumor efficacy was tested using an orthotopic NMIBC transplant model. Immune microenvironment changes were analyzed by detecting macrophage and T cell markers. The systemic antitumor potential of rBCG-h12 was further evaluated via intramuscular injection in pancreatic cancer transplant and K-RasG12D mouse models.ResultsIntravesical rBCG-h12 was well tolerated, with no observed mortality, weight loss, hematuria, or organ pathology. Compared with conventional BCG, rBCG-h12 more effectively suppressed bladder tumor growth, prolonged survival, and mitigated cachexia. Mechanistically, rBCG-h12 decreased CD206+ M2-macrophages while increasing CD4+TNF-α+ effector T cells and CD8+TNF-α+ cytotoxic T cells. In pancreatic cancer models, a single intramuscular injection of rBCG-h12 markedly reduced desmoplasia, suppressed tumor growth, and extended survival without inducing pathological lesion or granuloma formation in liver, lungs, and spleen.ConclusionsrBCG-h12 exhibited superior antitumor efficacy and safety compared with BCG. By suppressing M2-macrophages and promoting Th1 immune activation, rBCG-h12 shows promise as a next-generation BCG-based immunotherapy for both bladder and pancreatic cancers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00262-026-04441-9.Keywords: Recombinant BCG, Cancer bacterial therapy, Non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC), Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), Tumor microenvironment

Introduction

The development of cancer immunotherapy is flourishing worldwide. From the application of immune checkpoint inhibitors to the rapidly advancing cell therapies led by CAR-T (Chimeric Antigen Receptor T Cells) technology, research and clinical practices are both actively progressing. However, the complexity of immune-related adverse effects triggered by such treatments raises significant safety concerns, while the high costs of these therapies impose a heavy financial burden on patients. These factors inevitably limit both the adoption and accessibility of cancer immunotherapy, prompting renewed interest in bacterial therapy as a more cost-effective alternative [1].

Among the bacterial-based approaches, the attenuated Mycobacterium bovis strain has been developed as the bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) vaccine since 1921 to prevent pulmonary tuberculosis (TB) caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) infection [2]. Moreover, the administration of multiple high doses of BCG via intravesical instillation to prevent recurrence of superficial bladder cancer (BC) after transurethral resection has been practiced for more than 40 years [3]. In fact, as early as 1891, Dr. William Coley conducted the first trial of injecting live Streptococcus pyogenes into a patient with a tonsillar tumor [4]. Although the patient developed erysipelas within two weeks, the previously deemed incurable malignant tumor completely regressed. However, due to subsequent trials showing concerns regarding safety as well as limited and inconsistent efficacy, the approach of using bacteria to treat cancer did not gain wide acceptance or adoption in the medical community at that time. Nevertheless, because BCG was generally regarded safe in humans when used for TB prevention, research into its potential anti-tumor properties has continued for more than 65 years. BCG is known to stimulate the phagocytic activity of the reticuloendothelial system, also known as the mononuclear phagocyte system, and therefore BCG vaccination in mice significantly inhibited the growth of transplanted tumors [5]. Another investigation revealed that BCG injection increased both the number and activity of macrophages, as well as the circulating level of tumor necrosis factor (TNF), thereby contributing to suppression of implanted tumor [6]. In 1976, Morales et al. published the first successful study demonstrating that intravesical instillation of BCG could be used to treat BC, establishing it as the standard adjuvant therapy for high-risk non-muscle-invasive BC (NMIBC) following tumor resection [3].

At diagnosis, 75 ~ 80% of bladder cancer cases are classified as NMIBC, while the remaining 20 ~ 25% are muscle-invasive BC (MIBC). Following transurethral resection of bladder tumor (TURBT), about 50% of NMIBC patients experience recurrence within five years, with disease progression to MIBC [7]. Even after treatment, MIBC frequently recurs, and the five-year survival rate remains < 50%. NMIBC can be further classified into carcinoma in situ (CIS) and papillary NMIBC. CIS, accounting for approximately 13% of all NMIBC cases, is the subtype most prone to recurrence and progression to MIBC [8]. Clinically, patients with CIS or high-and intermediate-risk papillary NMIBC usually receive a complete course of intravesical BCG therapy 2 ~ 3 weeks after TURBT to reduce recurrence and progression [8]. According to the SWOG (Southwest Oncology Group) protocol, a complete BCG therapy consists of two phases: induction and maintenance [9]. The induction phase involves once-weekly intravesical BCG instillations for six consecutive weeks, followed immediately by the maintenance phase, which consists of three once-weekly instillations at months 3, 6, 12, 18, 24, 30, and 36. Reports have shown that for intermediate-risk papillary NMIBC patients, BCG maintenance therapy improves the five-year recurrence-free survival rate from 50 to 70% compared with no maintenance treatment [9–11]. For high-risk papillary NMIBC, this improvement is from 40 to 60%, and for CIS patients, from 30 ~ 40% to 50%. These data suggest that complete BCG therapy can improve outcomes in certain high- and intermediate-risk NMIBC patients; however, most CIS patients show no response. Moreover, the three-year treatment protocol is lengthy and toxic, and whether it is necessary for all patients remains debatable [12, 13]. To improve BCG therapy, more research efforts can focus on developing optimized recombinant BCG (rBCG), investigating alternative mycobacterial strains, or combining BCG with other therapeutic strategies.

We previously developed a rBCG strain, hereafter referred to as rBCG-h12, by introducing two plasmids into M. bovis (Tokyo 172) to express MTB Ag85B–CFP-10 fusion protein and human interleukin (IL)-12 [14]. Ag85B (antigen 85B) and CFP-10 (culture filtrate protein 10 kDa) are two highly immunogenic and virulent proteins expressed by MTB, whereas IL-12 is a potent immune-stimulatory cytokine. Our earlier studies showed that rBCG-h12 vaccination, compared with parental BCG, more effectively promoted immune T cell responses in the lungs and spleen of mice [14]. Moreover, rBCG-h12 induced stronger M1 macrophage polarization, more production of interferon (IFN)-γ and TNF-α, and conferred greater resistance to infection with the MTB strain H37Ra [15, 16]. In the present study, we further evaluated the safety of intravesical instillation of rBCG-h12 in mice, as well as its inhibitory activity against the growth of superficial bladder tumors. In addition, pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is a dismal disease exhibiting a marked desmoplastic and immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment that hinders the efficacy of both immunotherapy and chemotherapy. We also investigated and compared the effects of BCG and rBCG-h12 on the pancreatic tumor microenvironment.

Material and methods

Bacterial strains and cultures

The M. bovis Tokyo 172 (BCG) strain was cultured in Middlebrook 7H9 medium (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) supplemented with 0.5% glycerol, 0.05% Tween-80, and 10% oleic acid-albumin-dextrose-catalase (Creative life sciences, Taipei, Taiwan). The rBCG-h12 strain was grown in the same medium with the addition of 25 μg/mL kanamycin and 25 μg/mL hygromycin (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Both strains were subcultured weekly at 37 °C for 3 ~ 4 weeks, and log-phase bacteria (OD600 = 0.8 ~ 1.0) were used for experiments.

Cell cultures

The mouse urothelial carcinoma cell line MB49 (EMD Millipore, Temecula, CA, USA) and the mouse macrophage cell line RAW264.7 (Bioresource Collection and Research Center, Hsinchu City, Taiwan) were cultivated in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 μg/mL streptomycin, and 2 mM glutamine (1 × PSG). The mouse PDAC cell line Panc 02 [17], the mouse endothelial cell line 3B-11 (ATCC CRL-2160™, American Type Culture Collection, Manassas, VA, USA), and the human monocytic cell line THP-1 (Bioresource Collection and Research Center) were grown in RPMI-1640 medium containing 10% FBS and 1 × PSG. All cell cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. For induction of endothelial–mesenchymal transition (EndoMT), 3B-11 cells were preincubated for 16 h in RPMI-1640 containing 1% FBS, followed by treatment with 0.3 μg/mL osteopontin (OPN) for an additional 24 h [18]. For macrophage differentiation, THP-1 cells were treated with 150 ng/mL 12-O-tetradecanoyl-13-phorbol acetate for 24 h, after which adherent cells were harvested for further analyses.

RNA isolation and quantitative PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA from PBS-, BCG-, or rBCG-h12-treated macrophages was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). One μg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the RevertAid H Minus First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The resulting cDNA was used as the template for qPCR to quantify the mRNA levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, CD86, CD80, inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), and β-actin. qPCR was performed using the KAPA SYBR™ FAST qPCR Master Mix (KAPA Biosystems, Wilmington, MA, USA) on a QuantStudio 6 Flex system (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). The primer sequences and PCR condition are provided in Supplementary Material 1: Table S1. Melting curve analysis was conducted immediately after amplification by increasing temperature from 60 °C to 95 °C at a rate of + 1.6 °C per sec.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The secreted levels of TNF-α and IL-1β/IL-1F2 were quantified using commercial ELISA kits (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Briefly, 100 μL of cell culture supernatants, diluted serum samples, or cytokine protein standards were added to 96-well plates pre-coated with TNF-α or IL-1β/IL-1F2-specific capture antibody and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Following three washes with PBS containing 0.1% Tween 20 (PBST), wells were incubated with a biotin-conjugated anti-TNF-α or IL-1β/IL-1F2 detection antibody for 1 h at room temperature. After PBST washes, streptavidin-conjugated horseradish peroxidase was added and incubated for 30 min at room temperature. Plates were washed and developed with the 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine substrate for 15 min at room temperature. The reaction was stopped with 0.1 N of H2SO4, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using an Infinite M200 microplate reader (TECAN, Männedorf, Switzerland).

Cellular protein extraction and western blot analysis

Cellular proteins were extracted by resuspending treated macrophages in lysis buffer containing 25 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% SDS, 1% Nonidet P-40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, and 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride. Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Equal amounts of protein (40 μg per sample) were separated on 4 ~ 12% gradient SDS-polyacrylamide gels and subjected to standard western blotting procedures. Membranes were probed with primary antibodies specific for iNOS and β-actin, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Supplementary Material 1: Table S2). Immunoreactive protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (Luminata™ Crescendo Western HRP Substrate; EMD Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA).

Mouse cancer models

Mouse experiments were conducted with the approval of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the National Health Research Institutes (NHRI-IACUC-106031-A, 109,022-M2-S02, 109,153-A-S03, 110,112-A-S02, and 109,196-A). To evaluate the anti-BC efficacy of rBCG-h12, 7 ~ 12-week-old C57BL/6 mice were used to establish subcutaneous and orthotopic BC models. For the subcutaneous model, 5 × 105 MB49 cells were injected into the right dorsal flank of each mouse. On the following day, mice received intratumoral injections of PBS or 8 × 106 colony-forming units (CFUs) of BCG or rBCG-h12 once weekly for three weeks. Tumor size was measured superficially using Vernier calipers and calculated according to the formula ½ × length × width2. On day 22, mice were sacrificed and tumors were excised, weighed, and subjected to further analyses. For the orthotopic BC model, female C57BL/6 mice were water-restricted for 2 ~ 4 h to minimize urination during intravesical treatment. To disrupt the urothelial glycosaminoglycan (GAG) layer and create lesion sites, a catheter attached to a syringe was inserted into the bladder, and 0.3 mL of air was gently instilled and withdrawn six times within 12 s. Subsequently, 50 μL of poly-L-lysine (PLL; Sigma-Aldrich) was instilled into the bladder and incubated for 20 min. MB49 cells (6 × 104 in 50 μL PBS) were then instilled into the bladder and retained for 1 h. On the following day, PBS, BCG, or rBCG-h12 (at the indicated CFUs) was administered intravesically for 2 h and repeated once weekly for four weeks. Survival was monitored daily. At the experimental or humane endpoint, mice were weighed, sacrificed, and bladders were excised for weighing and further analyses. Body weight was corrected by subtracting the weight of the tumor-bearing bladder. On the other hand, three mouse models were used to evaluate the anti-PDAC efficacy of rBCG-h12. In the first, C57BL/6 mice were subcutaneously inoculated in the right dorsal flank with 1 × 106 Panc 02 cells mixed with Matrigel and 2.5 × 105 EndoMT cells (i.e. OPN-induced α-SMA-expressing CD31+ 3B-11 cells) to generate desmoplastic tumors [19]. One day after tumor implantation, mice received intramuscular injections of 2 × 107 CFUs of BCG or rBCG-h12 into the left hind limb. Tumor growth was monitored externally, and on day 31 mice were sacrificed and tumors were harvested for further analyses. In the second model, C57BL/6 mice were surgically implanted with Panc 02 plus EndoMT cell grafts into the pancreases. One day later, a single intramuscular injection of 2 × 107 CFU of BCG or rBCG-h12 was administered into the left hind limb. Mice were monitored for 25 days and then sacrificed for further analyses. In the last model, LSL-KrasG12D/Pdx1-Cre (KC) transgenic mice were injected intramuscularly with 2 × 107 CFUs of BCG or rBCG-h12 into the left hind limb at 6 months of age. Survival was monitored, and mice were sacrificed upon death, when reaching humane endpoints, or at 450 days of survival. At sacrifice, the pancreas, liver, spleen, and lungs were collected for histopathological analyses. KC mice were generated by crossing LSL-KrasG12D mice with Pdx1-Cre mice [20], both of which were obtained from the Mouse Models of Human Cancers Consortium Repository of the National Cancer Institute (Frederick, MD, USA).

Mouse safety assessment

For the safety evaluation of intravesical instillation of BCG or rBCG-h12, female C57BL/6 and non-obese diabetic/severe combined immunodeficiency (NOD/SCID) mice (National Center for Biomodels, Taipei, Taiwan) were water-restricted for 2 ~ 4 h prior to intravesical administration of 1 × 107 CFUs of BCG or rBCG-h12 once weekly for 4 weeks. Survival was monitored daily for up to 28 days. Body weight change (%) was calculated as: 100 × (body weight on Day-28 / body weight on Day-0). Urine samples were collected weekly in Eppendorf tubes for gross and microscopic hematuria assessments. At the end of the experiment, mice were euthanized, and blood and organ samples were collected. Blood samples were 100-fold diluted in PBS and plated on 7H10 agar plates to test for bacteremia. Bladder tissues were examined for lesions in the urothelial walls, and the lungs, kidneys, liver, spleen, and heart were subjected to histological analysis for granulomatous inflammation. Additionally, in PDAC-implanting C57BL/6 mice and KC mice that received a single intramuscular injection of BCG or rBCG-h12, the lungs, liver, and spleen collected at sacrifice were also examined for evidence of granuloma formation.

Histopathological examination

Tissue and organ samples were collected from experimental mice following saline and 10% formaldehyde perfusion, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, and embedded in paraffin. Paraffin-embedded tissue sections (4 μm thick) were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through graded ethanol dilutions. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) to assess histopathological alterations, including granuloma formation or detachment/denudation of the urothelial wall. In mouse PDAC models, tumor sections were additionally stained with Masson’s Trichrome using the Trichrome Stain Kit (ScyTek Laboratories, Utah, USA). Stained sections were dehydrated in graded ethanol and xylene and mounted at room temperature. Sections were examined with an Olympus BX50 microscope (Tokyo, Japan), and images were captured using the Nikon NIS-Elements system (Tokyo, Japan). Tumor desmoplasia levels were quantified as the percentage of collagen-positive (blue) area using the color cube-based model and eyedropper tool in Image-Pro Plus software (Media Cybernetics, Silver Spring, MD, USA).

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining

Paraffin-embedded tissue sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval in Trilogy™ Pretreatment Solution (Cell Marque Co., Rocklin, CA, USA). Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched by incubation with 0.3% H2O2 for 20 min, followed by blocking of nonspecific binding with BlockAid™ Blocking Solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 60 min. Sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against F4/80, CD206, CD163, or iNOS (Supplementary Material 1: Table S2). After three washes with PBST, sections were incubated for 60 min at room temperature with the corresponding HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Immunoreactivity was visualized using the REAL EnVision Detection System (DAKO, Produktionsvej 42, DK-2600 Glostrup, Denmark). Sections were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated through graded ethanol and xylene, mounted with mounting medium, and examined under an Axiovert S100/AxioCam HR microscope system (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). The positive staining area was quantified using the color cube-based model and eyedropper tool in Image-Pro Plus software.

Immunohistofluorescent (IHF) staining

Paraffin-embedded tissue sections (4 μm thick) were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through graded ethanol solutions. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating the sections under pressure in 10 mM citrate buffer (pH 8.0) for 15 min using a TintoRetriever Pressure Cooker (Bio SB Inc., Santa Barbara, CA, USA). Sections were then treated with 0.3% H2O2 for 20 min to quench endogenous peroxidase activity and blocked with PBS containing 3% bovine serum albumin at room temperature for 60 min in a humidified chamber. For detection of effector CD4+ T cells, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with a primary antibody set consisting of anti-CD4 and anti-TNF-α antibodies (Supplementary Material 1: Table S2). For analysis of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells, a primary antibody set of anti-CD8 and anti-TNF-α antibodies was used (Supplementary Material 1: Table S2). After washing with PBST, sections were incubated with the corresponding fluorescence-labeled secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6′-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; Sigma-Aldrich). Finally, sections were visualized, photographed, and analyzed using a Leica Stellaris 8 confocal microscope equipped with LAS X software (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany).

Genomic DNA isolation and qPCR

Genomic DNA was extracted from paraffin-embedded tissue sections (5 ~ 10 μm thick). Briefly, three sections per sample were deparaffinized in xylene, and DNA was isolated using the QIAamp DNA FFPE Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Germantown, MD, USA). The extracted DNA was further purified with QIAamp MinElute columns (Qiagen) and quantified using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). qPCR was performed using 2 × SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA). Relative DNA levels were determined by the comparative Ct method and normalized to glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) as an internal control. The primer sequences and qPCR conditions are provided in Supplementary Material 1: Table S3.

Statistical analyses

Statistical significance between control and treatment groups was assessed using two-tailed t-tests. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Fisher’s least significant difference or Dunnett’s post hoc test was applied. p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. Spearman’s correlation analysis was performed to examine the relationships between pancreatic Ag85B/CFP-10 DNA levels and desmoplasia, between pancreatic Ag85B/CFP-10 DNA levels and tumor growth, as well as between pancreatic Ag85B/CFP-10 DNA levels and mouse survival time. All statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism version 8 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

Pilot studies demonstrating rBCG-h12’s potential to activate macrophages and suppress tumor growth

The fundamental mechanism behind BCG’s clinical use as a TB vaccine and its efficacy in preventing recurrence of superficial BC lies in its ability to activate macrophage-mediated immunity. Whether our rBCG-h12 exhibits enhanced macrophage activation compared to conventional BCG is a critical criterion in determining its potential for development as an anti-cancer therapeutic. To preliminarily assess the immunostimulatory ability of rBCG-h12, we incubated human THP-1-derived macrophages and mouse RAW264.7 macrophage cells with either BCG or rBCG-h12 at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 5 or 10 for 24 h. Under identical MOI conditions, rBCG-h12 induced markedly higher gene expression of M1-associated markers—TNF-α, IL-1β, CD86, and CD80 — in THP-1-derived macrophages (Fig. 1a). Similarly, in RAW264.7 cells, rBCG-h12 triggered greater upregulation of M1-related genes including TNF-α, iNOS, and CD86 mRNA (Fig. 1b), as well as increased protein levels of TNF-α (Fig. 1c) and iNOS (Fig. 1d). To further explore whether this enhanced immune activation could translate into anti-tumor activity, we conducted a pilot mouse experiment. C57BL/6 mice were subcutaneously injected with 5 × 105 MB49 mouse bladder carcinoma cells. One day later, 8 × 106 CFU of rBCG-h12 were administered at the tumor injection site. This treatment was repeated on Day 8 and Day 15 (Fig. 1e). Tumor growth was monitored by external volume measurements, and the study was terminated on Day 22. At endpoint, tumors were excised and weighed. The data showed that rBCG-h12 treatment significantly suppressed tumor growth, as demonstrated by tumor volume kinetics (Fig. 1f) and final tumor weights (Fig. 1g). Moreover, while PBS-treated tumor-bearing mice showed a 9% reduction in body weight compared to healthy controls, rBCG-h12-treated mice maintained their body weights similar to healthy mice, indicating mitigation of tumor-induced cachexia (Fig. 1h). Further analysis of the tumor-infiltrating macrophages revealed that rBCG-h12 treatment increased the proportion of F4/80+ pan-macrophages (Fig. 1i), while reducing CD206+ M2-macrophages (Fig. 1j) and elevating iNOS+ cells (Fig. 1k), the latter serving as a marker for M1-macrophages and immune-activated T cells. Collectively, these results suggest that rBCG-h12 activates macrophage-mediated immunity more robustly than BCG, which reshapes the tumor microenvironment to favor anti-tumor immune responses, resulting in suppressed tumor growth and prevention of tumor-associated weight loss.

Safety assessment of intravesical instillation of rBCG-h12 in NOD/SCID and C57BL/6 mice

Before evaluating the therapeutic potential of rBCG-h12 in mouse orthotopic NMIBC model, we first assessed the safety of rBCG-h12 administration via intravesical instillation. NOD/SCID and C57BL/6 mice were water-restricted for 2 ~ 4 h prior to being intravesically instilled with 1 × 107 CFUs of BCG or rBCG-h12 once a week for 4 weeks (Fig. 2a). The mice were monitored daily for survival and potential adverse effects. Throughout the 28-day study period, neither BCG nor rBCG-h12 caused lethality (Fig. 2b) or significant body weight loss (Fig. 2c). Urine samples were collected weekly, and no incidents of gross and microscopic hematuria were observed in either the BCG- or rBCG-h12-treated groups (Fig. 2d and e). At the experimental endpoint, the mice were sacrificed, and their blood and organ samples were harvested for further analyses. Bacillary burden analysis of blood samples showed no evidence of mycobacterial infection (Fig. 2f and g). Bladder urothelial tissue sections were examined after H&E staining. No differences were detected among the NOD/SCID mice treated with intravesical instillations of PBS, BCG, or rBCG-h12 with respect to umbrella cell detachment and urothelial denudation (Fig. 2h and i). Additionally, H&E staining of tissue sections from the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys revealed no pathological lesion or granulomas caused by either BCG or rBCG-h12 (Fig. 2j). Collectively, these findings suggest that intravesical instillation of rBCG-h12 is safe in both NOD/SCID and C57BL/6 mice.

Establishment and optimization of a mouse orthotopic BC model

To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of rBCG-h12, we established a syngeneic and orthotopic BC model using MB49 cells in C57BL/6 mice. The luminal surface of the normal bladder urothelium is covered by a negatively charged, hydrophilic GAG layer, which serves as a protective barrier against toxins and microbial adhesion. Like many cancer cells, MB49 cells also carry a net negative surface charge, leading to electrostatic repulsion and poor adherence to the bladder wall upon intravesical instillation. As a result, these cells show a low implantation efficiency and are easily removed by subsequent instillations, making it difficult to accurately assess the therapeutic efficacy of rBCG-h12 in this setting. To overcome this challenge, we utilized a catheter and syringe to disrupt the GAG layer via gentle suction, followed by intravesical instillation of PLL, which provides a positively charged surface to facilitate MB49 cell adhesion (Fig. 3a). The day after tumor cell instillation, mice were subjected to weekly PBS flushes (four times in total) to mimic standard intravesical treatment schedules (Fig. 3b). The experiment concluded on Day 28, at which point mice were sacrificed and their bladders harvested. Bladders from the suction-treated group exhibited substantial enlargement with numerous tumor nodules, while PBS flushing did not significantly affect tumor growth (representative images in Fig. 3c). Data on bladder weights from all tumor-bearing mice confirmed significant tumor burden in the suction + PLL group (Fig. 3d). Additionally, the presence of tumors was reflected in reduced survival (Fig. 3e) and significant body weight loss (Fig. 3f), both of which were unaffected by PBS flushing. Histopathological analysis confirmed progressive tumor development in the suction + PLL group. On Day 4 post-implantation, tumor masses were observed on the bladder urothelium, accompanied by severe loss of epithelial folding. By Day 8, tumors expanded further into the lumen. By Day 11, tumor invasion into the submucosal layer was evident, and by Day 14, tumors had invaded the muscle layer, occupying most of the bladder lumen (Fig. 3g). These results confirm that our modified protocol successfully established a robust mouse orthotopic BC model. To test the therapeutic efficacy of intravesical BCG in this model, we performed instillations of 2 × 106, 5 × 106, or 1 × 107 CFUs of BCG once per week for four weeks, starting one day after MB49 cell instillation (Fig. 3h). At Day 28, bladders were excised and examined. Tumor growth was significantly suppressed in mice treated with 1 × 107 CFUs of BCG (Fig. 3i). Bladder weight measurements demonstrated significant tumor burden in MB49-implanted mice, which was effectively reduced by BCG treatment in a dose-dependent manner, with the highest dose showing statistically significant suppression (Fig. 3j). Regarding survival, 38% of tumor-bearing mice without treatment survived until Day 28, whereas BCG-treated groups showed improved survival rates of up to ~ 68%, although the differences among BCG doses were not statistically significant (Fig. 3k). Mice implanted with MB49 cells experienced a ~ 20% body weight loss, which was alleviated by BCG treatment; however, no significant differences were observed between different BCG doses (Fig. 3l).

Anti-cancer efficacy of rBCG-h12 in a mouse orthotopic BC model

We further evaluated the anti-cancer efficacy of rBCG-h12 using the optimized mouse orthotopic BC model. One day after MB49 cells were intravesically instilled with the aid of suction + PLL, mice received intravesical instillations of PBS or 1 × 107 CFUs of BCG or rBCG-h12 once every seven days for a total of four treatments (Fig. 4a). At the experimental endpoint on Day 28, mice were sacrificed and their bladders were harvested. The results showed that rBCG-h12 exhibited a stronger inhibitory effect on tumor growth compared to BCG (representative data shown in Fig. 4b). The increase in bladder weight caused by tumor burden was significantly reduced by either BCG or rBCG-h12 instillation, with rBCG-h12 showing a greater level of inhibition than BCG (Fig. 4c). Survival analysis revealed that while 36% of PBS-treated mice survived to Day 28, this rate increased to 50% in the BCG-treated group and to 70% in the rBCG-h12-treated group (Fig. 4d). In terms of body weight changes, mice inoculated with MB49 cells experienced an approximate 20% weight loss. While BCG treatment slightly alleviated this weight loss, rBCG-h12 treatment significantly improved body weight maintenance (Fig. 4e). We next performed IHC analysis on bladder tissues. An increased number of F4/80+ cells (pan-macrophages) was observed in MB49-inoculated bladders, and neither BCG nor rBCG-h12 treatment significantly altered the macrophage population (Fig. 4f). However, the number of CD206⁺ cells (M2-macrophages) was markedly elevated in tumor-bearing bladders, and this was significantly reduced by BCG treatment, with rBCG-h12 treatment resulting in an even more pronounced reduction (Fig. 4g). In contrast, BCG instillation induced an increase in iNOS+ cells (including M1-macrophages and activated immune T cells), and rBCG-h12 further enhanced this induction (Fig. 4h). Given the involvement of T cells in anti-tumor immunity, we also performed IHF staining to detect T cell subsets in bladder tissues. The results showed that BCG treatment increased the number of CD4+TNF-α+ (effector T cells) and CD8+TNF-α+ (cytotoxic T cells), and rBCG-h12 treatment further enhanced the recruitment of both T cell types (Fig. 4i and j). Collectively, these data demonstrate that rBCG-h12 possesses a stronger ability than BCG to modulate the tumor immune microenvironment, thereby exerting superior anti-tumor efficacy.

Our above data have demonstrated that rBCG-h12 instillation was more effective than BCG instillation at inhibiting the tumor formation of bladder cancer cells implanted on the bladder wall in experimental mice. We further evaluated whether intramuscular injection of rBCG-h12 could provide some benefit in the prevention or treatment of PDAC. Neither BCG nor rBCG-h12 injections affected the mice’s food intake or caused noticeable abnormalities in their spleens (Fig. 5d). To further validate the anti-cancer efficacy of rBCG-h12 in desmoplastic PDAC, we employed a mouse orthotopic transplantation model. C57BL/6 mice were inoculated with Panc 02 plus EndoMT cell grafts into the pancreases. One day later, a single intramuscular injection of BCG or rBCG-h12 (2 × 107 CFU) was administered into the left hind limb. Mice were sacrificed on day 25 for pancreatic analyses (Fig. 6a). Orthotopic inoculation of Panc 02 plus EndoMT cell grafts led to the formation of firm pancreatic tumors (Fig. 6b). While BCG treatment exerted only a minimal inhibitory effect on tumor growth, rBCG-h12 markedly suppressed tumor progression (Fig. 6c). Masson’s trichrome staining of tumor sections revealed that desmoplasia was significantly alleviated in mice treated with rBCG-h12 (Fig. 6d). In addition, compared with healthy pancreases, tumors derived from Panc 02 plus EndoMT cell inoculation exhibited increased macrophage infiltration, particularly CD163+ (M2) macrophages; this increase was attenuated by rBCG-h12 treatment (Fig. 6e). To investigate whether intramuscularly administered rBCG-h12 could reach the pancreas via the circulatory system and modulate the tumor immune microenvironment, we examined the presence of bacterial DNA in pancreatic tissues. We quantified DNA levels of Ag85B and CFP-10, two immunogenic components of the vaccine. Notably, pancreatic tissues from rBCG-h12-treated mice showed clearly detectable levels of Ag85B and CFP-10 DNA compared with those from PBS- or BCG-treated mice (Fig. 6g). Given the possibility that intramuscularly injected rBCG-h12 may disseminate systemically, we further evaluated the potential for mycobacterial infection or immune reactions in peripheral organs. Histopathological examination of the liver, lungs, and spleen using H&E staining revealed no evidence of granuloma formation in these organs following a single injection of rBCG-h12 (Fig. 6j).

Anti-cancer efficacy of rBCG-h12 in a mouse K-RasG12D-induced PDAC model

We next used KC transgenic mice to evaluate the inhibitory potential of rBCG-h12 on pancreatic cancer formation driven by K-RasG12D mutant. KC mice were first allowed to grow to 6 months of age, by which time the pancreas had developed extensive PanIN (pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia) lesions with a few PDAC lesions present. The experimental mice were then intramuscularly injected into the left hind leg with a single dose of 2 × 107 CFU of BCG or rBCG-h12. The mice were subsequently monitored until death, reaching humane endpoint criteria, or the experiment’s set endpoint of 450 days (Fig. 7a). The results showed that all KC mice injected with PBS or BCG died or reached humane endpoints before 425 days. In contrast, 4 out of 5 KC mice injected with a single dose of rBCG-h12 survived beyond 450 days (Fig. 7b). Some KC mice were found dead more than 5 h post-mortem; for these cases, tissue samples were not collected for further experiments. Pancreatic tissues collected from the remaining KC mice were subjected to histological analysis, including Masson’s Trichrome staining. The pancreatic tissues from PBS-injected control mice exhibited severe desmoplasia. A slight reduction in desmoplasia was observed in BCG-injected mice, while a significant reduction in desmoplasia was observed in rBCG-h12-injected mice (Fig. 7c). However, due to the small sample size, the results did not reach statistical significance. We further examined macrophage infiltration in the pancreatic tissues. While the overall macrophage content in the pancreatic tissues of BCG- or rBCG-h12-injected mice was not significantly different from that of PBS-injected control mice, the proportion of CD163+ M2-macrophages was significantly reduced (Fig. 7d and e). This finding suggests that intramuscular injection of BCG or rBCG-h12 can influence the immune microenvironment of the pancreas. To investigate whether Ag85B and CFP-10, the two key immunogenic proteins of the vaccine, were present in the pancreatic tissues, we analyzed the DNA from these tissues. Compared to PBS-injected control mice, pancreatic DNA samples from rBCG-h12-injected mice showed significantly higher levels of Ag85B and CFP-10 DNA (Fig. 7f). As expected, DNA from BCG-injected mice also contained detectable levels of Ag85B and CFP-10, albeit at much lower levels than in the rBCG-h12-injected group. Finally, we performed Spearman correlation tests to examine the associations of the levels of Ag85B and CFP-10 DNA in the pancreatic tissues with the degree of desmoplasia in the pancreatic tissues and the survival days of the KC mice. The results demonstrated a trend toward inverse associations between Ag85B/CFP-10 DNA levels and desmoplasia severity (Fig. 7g). Additionally, a trend toward positive associations were observed between Ag85B/CFP-10 DNA levels and the survival time of KC mice (Fig. 7h). These findings indicate that the enhanced vaccine efficacy of rBCG-h12 can also translate into its effectiveness in suppressing pancreatic cancer. However, since a single intramuscular injection of rBCG-h12 enabled the detection of Ag85B and CFP10 DNA in the pancreas, the potential risk of systemic TB infection was evaluated. Histopathological examination of the liver, lungs, and spleen revealed no pathological lesions or granuloma formation (Fig. 7i).

Discussion

BCG has been used to prevent pulmonary TB for over a century. It was derived from M. bovis through 230 serial passages between 1908 and 1921, resulting in an attenuated vaccine strain [2]. From the Pasteur Institute in France, BCG strains with different passage numbers were subsequently distributed worldwide. In 1997, Behr and Small reported that BCG strains progressively lost efficacy as they underwent further passages [21]. They initiated genomic comparisons of BCG strains from different regions and demonstrated that all BCG strains lack a genomic segment known as region of difference 1 (RD1), which is retained in both M. bovis and MTB [22, 23]. The deletion of RD1 is now recognized as the key factor underlying the attenuation of BCG. Furthermore, Behr and Small found that BCG strains distributed before 1926 (e.g., BCG Brazil, Russia, and Japan) exhibit RD1 deletion and contain two copies of the insertion sequence IS6110. However, strains distributed after 1931 (e.g., BCG Denmark, USA, and UK) not only carry the RD1 deletion but also lack RD2, while harboring only a single copy of IS6110 [22, 23]. Subsequent studies have identified additional genetic differences among BCG strains, which in turn affect gene expression, immunogenicity, virulence, and even bacterial viability—ultimately influencing their protective efficacy as vaccines [24]. Although BCG remains the only licensed vaccine against TB, the genetic variations among globally distributed strains contribute to the highly variable efficacy of BCG vaccination in preventing MTB infection and disease progression across different populations. To address this issue, one major research direction has been the development of rBCG strains aimed at restoring key genetic elements that were lost during attenuation.

Our rBCG-h12 strain was derived from M. bovis (Tokyo 172, passage No. 172), which had been transferred from the Pasteur Institute to Tokyo in 1925. Two plasmids were introduced into this strain to enable the expression of the MTB Ag85B–CFP-10 fusion protein and human IL-12 [14]. Ag85B and CFP-10 are two secreted proteins of MTB that are closely associated with both virulence and immunogenicity. Similar to Ag85A and Ag85C, Ag85B possesses mycolyl transferase activity, catalyzing the conversion of the cell wall glycolipid α,α′-trehalose monomycolate into α,α′-trehalose dimycolate [25]. Once engulfed by macrophages, α,α′-trehalose dimycolate blocks phagosomal maturation, thereby enabling bacterial survival [26, 27]. Among the Ag85 proteins secreted, Ag85B is the most abundant, accounting for approximately 25% of all secreted MTB proteins [28]. Extracellular Ag85B binds to the extracellular matrix protein fibronectin [29] and can stimulate CD4+ T cell proliferation and IFN-γ production [30, 31]. In guinea pigs, purified Ag85B has been shown to induce protective immunity against aerosol challenge [32], while Ag85B-overexpressing BCG provided stronger protective immunity compared to parental BCG [33]. Thus, Ag85B is not only associated with MTB virulence but also plays an important role in its immunogenicity. CFP-10 is another secreted protein of MTB associated with virulence [34]. Its gene, esxB, is located in the RD1 region, which was deleted during the serial passages that led to BCG attenuation [22, 23]. RD1 comprises nine genes, including esxB (encoding CFP-10) and esxA (encoding ESAT-6, early secretory antigenic target-6). Together, CFP-10 and ESAT-6 form essential components of the ESAT-6 secretion system (ESX-1), which regulates the secretion of certain virulence factors and directly contributes to T cell activation [35]. In addition to Ag85B and CFP-10, our rBCG-h12 strain carries a plasmid encoding human IL-12, a potent immune-stimulatory cytokine with strong anti-tumor effects. However, earlier clinical trials demonstrated that direct administration of recombinant IL-12 (rIL-12) in patients could trigger systemic immune toxicity resembling cytokine storm [36, 37]. In contrast, experimental studies showed that IL-12 expression through intermediate vehicles, such as viral vectors or plasmid DNA, markedly reduced toxicity [38, 39]. Accordingly, we engineered rBCG-h12 to express human IL-12 in combination with Ag85B–CFP-10. In our previous studies, rBCG-h12 was shown to induce significant activation of M1 macrophages and immune T cells in the lungs and spleens of mice following subcutaneous immunization, and to confer meaningful protection against infection with MTB strain H37Ra [14–16].

In our subsequent study, we aimed to evaluate whether rBCG-h12 provides superior efficacy compared with BCG as a cancer immunotherapeutic agent. We first assessed the safety of intravesical instillation of BCG and rBCG-h12 in NOD/SCID and C57BL/6 mice. Weekly instillations of 1 × 107 CFUs for four consecutive weeks showed no adverse effects on survival, food or water intake, or body weight. Neither BCG nor rBCG-h12 caused damage to the urothelial tissue, induced hematuria, nor resulted in bacterial dissemination, as blood cultures were negative and histopathological examinations of the heart, liver, spleen, kidney, and lung revealed no abnormalities. These results suggest that intravesical instillation of BCG or rBCG-h12 is a relatively safe therapeutic approach. We next compared the efficacy of rBCG-h12 versus BCG in suppressing NMIBC. rBCG-h12 demonstrated superior inhibition of tumor growth within the urothelium and obviously improved overall survival (OS) compared with BCG. Notably, in our mouse orthotopic NMIBC model established with MB49 cancer cells, tumor progression over 28 days led to a 20% reduction in body weight. Intravesical instillation of 1 × 107 CFUs of rBCG-h12 attenuated this tumor-induced weight loss more effectively than an equivalent dose of BCG. This weight loss can be attributable to cancer cachexia, a multifactorial syndrome characterized by progressive wasting of adipose tissue and skeletal muscle mass. Skeletal muscle wasting (sarcopenia) has been known as a poor prognostic factor in BC [40–42]. A review by Fukushima et al. of 29 international studies published between June 2014 and April 2018 highlighted that sarcopenia was significantly associated with inferior OS in patients with BC, particularly in those undergoing radical cystectomy for MIBC or receiving systemic chemotherapy for inoperable, locoregionally advanced, and/or metastatic disease [40]. Additionally, Soria et al. investigated 185 patients with T1 high-grade NMIBC treated with TURBT followed by BCG, and found that 130 (70.3%) patients exhibited sarcopenia [41]. They further demonstrated that CIS independently predicted progression from NMIBC to MIBC (HR = 2.82; 95% CI: 1.33–5.99; p = 0.007), while sarcopenia itself also served as an independent predictor of disease progression (HR = 3.41; 95% CI: 1.17–9.93; p = 0.02). In 2024, Mimura et al. adopted a cachexia index integrating serum albumin content, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, and skeletal muscle mass index [42]. In their analysis of 55 metastatic BC patients receiving first-line gemcitabine plus cisplatin, cachexia was identified as an independent risk factor for poorer OS (HR = 2.25; 95% CI: 1.12–4.52; p = 0.023). Together, these clinical findings support the superior potential of rBCG-h12 over BCG as an adjuvant therapy for NMIBC. The advantage of rBCG-h12 can be attributed to its stronger inhibition of tumor growth, which mitigates cancer recurrence and cachexia, thereby potentially reducing the risk of NMIBC progression to MIBC and consequently prolonging survival.

Cancer cachexia is an inflammatory response of the host to tumor growth, in which tumors release factors to create an immunocompromised microenvironment that facilitates their progression. As noted earlier, when applied as a vaccine against pulmonary TB, rBCG-h12 induced stronger activation of M1 macrophages and T cells than BCG. This raised the question of whether intravesical rBCG-h12 suppresses bladder tumor growth more effectively than BCG by more strongly modulating the immune microenvironment within the urothelium. Indeed, inoculation of MB49 cells into the mouse bladder increased macrophage infiltration, most of which were CD206+ M2-macrophages, indicating that tumor growth fostered an immunosuppressive microenvironment. Following intravesical instillation of either rBCG-h12 or BCG, total macrophage numbers did not significantly change; however, the proportion of M2 macrophages decreased, while CD4+TNF-α+ effector T cells and CD8+TNF-α+ cytotoxic T cells increased. This immunomodulatory shift was particularly pronounced with rBCG-h12 compared with BCG. Given its potent immune-modulating capacity, we further investigated whether rBCG-h12 could be applied beyond NMIBC therapy, such as for pancreatic cancer. PDAC is the most common and lethal pancreatic malignancy (accounting for over 85% of cases), and is characterized by marked desmoplasia, in which abundant activated mesenchymal cells produce large amounts of collagen I and hyaluronic acid [43–46]. This fibrotic remodeling progressively stiffens the tumor tissue, severely limiting surgical, chemotherapeutic, and immunotherapeutic treatments. Moreover, during pancreatic tumor development, infiltrating macrophages are driven toward M2 polarization under the influence of cancer-associated fibroblasts, resulting in a highly fibrotic and immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment [19].

We performed three pilot studies to assess the potential of rBCG-h12 against PDAC. In the first, Panc 02 pancreatic cancer cells mixed with EndoMT cells were subcutaneously implanted into the right dorsal flank of mice to grow desmoplastic tumor. One day later, mice were injected intramuscularly in the left hind leg with a single dose of rBCG-h12 or BCG. After 31 days, rBCG-h12 significantly suppressed tumor growth compared with BCG, with half of the grafts completely prevented from developing into tumors. Further analysis revealed that rBCG-h12 markedly reduced M2-macrophages in the tumor tissue. In the second study, we employed a mouse orthotopic PDAC model. C57BL/6 mice were surgically implanted with Panc 02 plus EndoMT cell grafts into the pancreases. One day later, mice were also injected intramuscularly with a single dose of rBCG-h12 or BCG. After 25 days, rBCG-h12 treatment markedly reduced pancreatic tumor growth and desmoplasia. Noteworthily, intramuscular administration of rBCG-h12 suppresses M2 macrophage accumulation in pancreatic tumors without inducing excessive systemic M1-associated cytokine production. In contrast, rBCG-h12 treatment reduces tumor-associated increase in serum TNF-α levels, which is known to contribute to cancer cachexia. PCR further confirmed the presence of Ag85B and CFP-10 DNA in the pancreas of rBCG-h12-treated mice, and the DNA levels were inversely associated with tumor desmoplasia and showed a clear trend toward inverse association with tumor growth. In the third study, we used KC transgenic mice carrying a pancreas-specific K-rasG12D mutation, which typically develop PanIN lesions by 6 months of age, with some progressing to PDAC. At 6 months, mice were injected intramuscularly with rBCG-h12, BCG, or PBS and monitored for survival. If not succumbed to disease or euthanized due to morbidity, mice were sacrificed at 450 days as the study endpoint. All PBS- and BCG-treated mice died before 425 days, whereas four of five rBCG-h12-treated mice survived beyond 450 days. Histological analysis showed that rBCG-h12 markedly reduced pancreatic desmoplasia and M2-macrophage levels. PCR results confirmed the presence of Ag85B and CFP-10 DNA in the pancreases of rBCG-h12-treated KC mice, and suggested that pancreatic Ag85B and CFP-10 DNA levels could be inversely associated with tumor desmoplasia and positively associated with mouse survival, although statistical significance was not reached due to the small sample size. Nevertheless, these pilot data strongly suggest that rBCG-h12 has potential as an adjuvant therapy to prevent pancreatic cancer progression or recurrence. It is known that bacteria can disseminate systemically and affect distant organs, as exemplified by periodontal pathogens, which not only cause periodontitis but are also linked to Alzheimer’s disease, cardiovascular disorders, pancreatic cancer, and colorectal cancer [47]. The mechanism may involve both chronic oral inflammation and hematogenous bacterial spread. Before further developing rBCG-h12 for the treatment of pancreatic cancer or other malignancies, it is essential to exclude whether intramuscular administration of rBCG-h12 induces granulomatous inflammation in the lungs, liver, spleen, or other organs. Accordingly, we examined histological sections of the liver, spleen, and lungs collected from our experimental mice. No granulomas were observed in any of these organs. Our findings therefore suggest that, similar to intravesical instillation, intramuscular administration of rBCG-h12 does not appear to carry a risk of inducing systemic TB in mice.

Although the above findings on rBCG-h12 are encouraging, this study has three important limitations. First, potential adverse effects associated with intravesical instillation of rBCG-h12 remain a concern. In our mouse model, weekly intravesical administration for four consecutive weeks did not result in hematuria, bacteremia, or granuloma formation. However, standard BCG therapy for bladder cancer typically spans up to 3 years with a total of 27 instillations, during which approximately 30 ~ 60% of patients develop lower urinary tract symptoms, including urinary urgency, frequency, nocturia, hematuria, and pelvic pain due to BCG-associated cystitis [48]. Notably, some patients remain asymptomatic during the first year of treatment but gradually develop adverse effects after prolonged exposure. In addition, postmenopausal women with urogenital atrophy are more susceptible to lower urinary tract symptoms and urinary tract infections during BCG therapy. Therefore, long-term safety evaluations—particularly regarding systemic cytokine release, autoimmune-like reactions, and late-onset granulomatous inflammation—are essential. Additional animal models, such as ovariectomized mice, may also help further assess the toxicity profile of rBCG-h12 in postmenopausal patients. Second, this study is limited to the immunomodulatory effects of rBCG-h12 in mice and their impact on tumor progression. Experience from immunotherapy development indicates that human immune responses are considerably more complex. Therefore, further evaluation of the anti-cancer efficacy of rBCG-h12 in more clinically relevant systems, such as humanized mouse models, will be necessary. Third, the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood. In the context of bladder cancer, our data suggest that intravesical rBCG-h12 activates both CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell responses. However, in pancreatic cancer, rBCG-h12 may elicit distinct immune responses at different sites, including the intramuscular injection site, the systemic circulation, and the pancreatic tumor microenvironment. In our previous study, rBCG-h12 vaccination in mice induced stronger M1 macrophage polarization, increased production of interferon IFN-γ and TNF-α, and enhanced resistance to infection with the MTB strain H37Ra [16]. Consistently, in the present study, co-culture of macrophages with rBCG-h12 promoted the production of the M1-associated cytokine TNF-α. In contrast, in the orthotopic PDAC model, rBCG-h12 suppressed M2 macrophage accumulation in pancreatic tumors without inducing excessive systemic TNF-α production, and even reduced tumor-associated increases in serum TNF-α levels. The mechanisms underlying these context-dependent immunological effects remain to be fully elucidated.

In summary, we engineered rBCG-h12 from the M. bovis Tokyo 172 strain to express the MTB Ag85B–CFP-10 fusion protein and human IL-12, thereby combining enhanced immunogenicity with immune stimulation but without increasing virulence. Building on our previous observation that rBCG-h12 more potently activates M1 macrophages and T cells than BCG and confers protection against MTB, we evaluated its potential as a cancer immunotherapeutic in NMIBC. Intravesical administration of rBCG-h12 was well tolerated, effectively suppressed NMIBC tumor growth, and mitigated cancer cachexia by shifting the urothelial immune microenvironment from M2 macrophages toward effector and cytotoxic T-cell responses. Furthermore, our pilot studies in PDAC models showed that intramuscular rBCG-h12 reduced pancreatic desmoplasia and M2-macrophage infiltration, improved survival, and did not induce granulomatous inflammation in distant organs. Taken together, these findings support the further development of rBCG-h12 as a safe and effective adjuvant therapy.

Conclusions

BCG has been used for over a century to prevent pulmonary TB. However, genomic analyses reveal that BCG strains distributed worldwide differ in key regions such as RD1 and RD2, leading to variations in immunogenicity and protective efficacy. To address these limitations, rBCG strains have been developed to restore or enhance lost functions. We engineered rBCG-h12 from the M. bovis Tokyo 172 strain by introducing plasmids encoding the MTB Ag85B–CFP-10 fusion protein and human IL-12. Ag85B–CFP-10 fusion protein can increase rBCG immunogenicity, while IL-12 is a potent immune-stimulatory cytokine. Previously, subcutaneous immunization with rBCG-h12 in mice enhanced M1 macrophage and T cell activation and conferred protection against MTB H37Ra. We next evaluated rBCG-h12 as a cancer immunotherapeutic. In a murine NMIBC model, intravesical rBCG-h12 was well tolerated, more effectively suppressed bladder tumor growth, and mitigated cachexia compared with BCG. Mechanistically, rBCG-h12 shifted the urothelial immune microenvironment from M2 macrophages toward effector and cytotoxic T cells. Pilot studies in pancreatic cancer models similarly showed that intramuscular rBCG-h12 reduced desmoplasia, M2 macrophages, and improved survival without inducing granuloma formation in distant organs, supporting its potential as a safe and effective adjuvant therapy.
