Introduction

Globally, many adolescents become sexually active without access to quality and gender-responsive information.1 Adolescents living in low and middle-income countries (LMICs) face significant challenges related to accessing sexual and reproductive health (SRH) education and services. The situation is more vulnerable and disproportionate to girls and young women living in hard-to-reach and underserved communities.2 3 In 2019, adolescents aged 15–19 in LMICs experienced an estimated 21 million pregnancies annually, with approximately 50% unintended, resulting in about 12 million births.4 Early pregnancy complications are the leading cause of death for girls aged 15–19 worldwide.1 Contributing factors include inadequate sexual education, early initiation of sexual activity, early marriage, low socioeconomic status, ineffective contraception use and low educational and career aspirations.5 6 Additionally, HIV-related mortality among young people also increased by 50% between 2005 and 2017, driven by greater exposure, biological susceptibility to infection and lack of SRH information and services.7

While Pakistan reflects global trends in adolescent reproductive health, it also faces unique sociocultural barriers that limit young people’s access to essential services and information. In Pakistan, adolescents make up around 23% of the population,8 yet they continue to face significant challenges, including early marriage, teenage pregnancy, gender-based violence (GBV), gender discrimination and low uptake of contraception. These issues are largely driven by the limited access to SRH information and services.9 As of 2019, about 617 000 pregnancies occur annually among adolescent girls aged 15–19 years in Pakistan, with around 36% of these pregnancies being unintended, either mistimed or unwanted. Alarmingly, over half (58%) of these unintended pregnancies end in abortion.10 Contraceptive use among this age group remains particularly low, at just 3.5%.11 Stark disparities exist between urban and rural areas, with knowledge and access to contraception and other SRH services significantly lower in rural settings. Human sexuality is often considered taboo in the context of Pakistan, which hinders open discussions. Additionally, there are prevalent false beliefs, particularly among rural populations, that unmarried individuals under the age of 18 do not require SRH and rights (SRHR) information and services, and there is a misconception that providing sex education will incite unwanted sexual activities.12 Furthermore, despite many adolescent girls marrying early, there is a lack of recognition that married adolescents need proactive preparation to address their SRH needs and promote their well-being.12

Despite International Conference on Population Development commitments, adolescent sexual reproductive health research (SRHR) is often not a health priority and rarely acknowledged.9 The lack of knowledge about sexual reproductive health research in Pakistan contributes to unplanned pregnancies, unsafe abortions and sexually transmitted infections (STIs). Therefore, it is imperative to raise young adults’ understanding of SRHR through comprehensive education, media campaigns, community outreach initiatives and accessible healthcare services. The current research highlights the significance of SRH knowledge, rights and needs and family planning advocacy among underserved populations. It actively engages adolescent boys and girls in the codesign of a toolkit, ensuring their voices are central throughout the process. This approach not only ensures the toolkit’s usability and effectiveness but also addresses gaps in existing toolkits that have not involved young people in their development and fail to meet their specific needs.13

Evidence on adolescent SRH initiatives

Globally, various interventions have been tested to improve SRH knowledge and access for young boys and girls, such as Comprehensive Sexuality Education, shown significant improvement among adolescents’ knowledge, attitudes and behaviours related to SRH in India. Adolescent Education Programme and the Life Skills Education Program reported up to 25%–40% increase in SRH knowledge among school-going adolescents and significant reductions in misconceptions about puberty and contraception. Similarly, programmes in Sri Lanka and Bangladesh noted a 20%–30% improvement in positive attitudes towards gender equality and safe sexual practices.1416

In addition to education, youth-friendly health services and spaces in Kenya, Ethiopia reported a significant increase in contraceptive uptake and SRH counselling. For example, youth centres linked to health facilities in Ethiopia led to a 30% increase in modern contraceptive uptake among adolescents, while Kenya reported a 35%–45% rise in SRH service utilisation, particularly counselling for contraception and STI prevention.17 18 Moreover, community engagement initiatives, such as Tostan’s programme in Senegal, demonstrate the importance of addressing harmful gender norms and reduced the stigma around SRH through dialogue and collaboration with local leaders.19 Adolescent Girls Initiative in Kenya has successfully combined life skills training with access to SRH services, improving contraceptive uptake among adolescents.20 These initiatives emphasise human rights, gender equality, self-care, contraception and STIs prevention, with content adapted for age and cultural relevance. A well-implemented intervention for adolescent health can contribute to reduced rates of teenage pregnancy and STIs.21 22

Pakistan faces significant challenges in promoting adolescent SRHR, like many other countries worldwide. Despite these challenges, some Non-Government Organization have developed effective strategies to support adolescents’ knowledge and understanding of SRHR as well as empowerment. Aahung and Rutgers Pakistan, for example, have implemented large-scale sexuality education programmes in Pakistan, reaching over 500 000 students. The Youth Champions Initiative empowers young leaders in SRHR, with champions making significant impacts in various countries, including Pakistan.23

Moreover, these organisations also piloted community support and youth engagement (a youth volunteers’ programme) in 12 cities of the country to empower community youth as champions and advocates for their own SRHR.24 These youth champions are volunteers from the same community and undergo a 1-month training on different topics such as puberty, early marriage, early pregnancy, GBV and self-awareness. They then pass on this information to other young people in their communities. However, one of the main challenges they face is obtaining permission from parents, as many consider such topics taboo or against religious views. Despite this, volunteers make efforts to convince parents of the importance of SRHR education for their children, often succeeding in gaining permission by emphasising its potential to positively impact their children’s lives.23 25

Pakistan had made a national commitment to achieving the sustainable development goal targets, particularly in improving contraceptives, reducing early marriages and expanding SRH services. But progress remains significantly behind the goals set for 2030.26 Adolescents continue to face substantial barriers in accessing information and services critical for their health and rights. Accelerated efforts, stronger policy implementation and youth-centred intervention are urgently needed to promote adolescents’ health and well-being.

Youth initiative in the current project

Engaging adolescents in the codesign of the SRHR and family planning advocacy toolkit is a promising initiative to better understand their needs and perspectives. In rural Sindh, local adolescents are actively involved in exchanging ideas, expertise and experiences with parents, siblings and friends living in the same community. We are working with young individuals on SRHR needs through a participatory process.

In collaboration with lady health workers (LHWs), identified active young male and female participants aged 15–24 to ensure inclusive and meaningful engagement with their peer group within the community. LHWs mapped out the adolescent population in their catchment areas and invited participants to join the Adolescent Advisory Group or Youth Champion group. A modest monthly stipend was allocated to each advisor to support travel expenses and sustain engagement. During the selection process, enthusiasm for the programme and valuable input during discussions were key considerations. Separate age-specific and gender-specific groups were established, and young advisors received comprehensive training that included research ethics, methodologies, SRH and social determinants of health. The inclusion of research ethics aimed to ensure that youth understood the principles of confidentiality, informed consent and respectful engagement when working with peers.

After comprehensive training, these advisors/champions initiated SRHR sessions within their communities among peer groups, targeting 20–25 girls and boys aged 10–19 years (separately) per group. These sessions cover various adolescent SRHR topics such as menstruation and hygiene, early marriage, early pregnancy, GBV, decision-making, effective communication, education and empowerment and family planning.

With 15 advisory members conducting sessions regularly for 6 months, this initiative has made considerable progress. In recent months, the frequency of group sessions has increased, and several key areas have been identified for focus. These include the integration of mental health topics, the provision of SRH information tailored for individuals with disabilities, specialised education for young adolescents who have experienced challenges related to floods and other humanitarian crises and the dissemination of knowledge on sexual abuse prevention for girls aged 10–14 years. Moving forward, these sessions will continue, gathering feedback on the SRHR toolkit usability and participating in knowledge mobilisation efforts. They will also address the perspectives and needs of vulnerable populations, including Lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender and queer + (LGBTQ+) youth, adolescents with disabilities and survivors of sexual violence groups often neglected in mainstream health programmes. Integrating psychosocial support with SRHR services has been shown to help address trauma, improve reporting rates and increase adherence to medical treatment, particularly for GBV survivors.27 There is also a critical need to raise awareness about STIs, preventive strategies and access to healthcare among these high-risk populations.28 The outcomes of these sessions will play a key role in codesigning a comprehensive SRHR and family planning toolkit that is inclusive and responsive to the specific needs of adolescents.

Experience of these youth advisors with community sessions

This narrative outlines the journey of the youth advisors, starting from their selection and initial experiences with the rural support network, to receiving education on critical issues such as physical violence and child marriage, which led to their interest and motivated them to educate others. They formed groups and conducted sessions within their communities, focusing on topics like puberty, which were often unfamiliar to many community members.

Initially, the youth advisors faced significant challenges in gaining permission from parents and guardians to interact with and register their children for sessions. Parents were concerned about the sensitivity of the topics discussed and the idea of sending their young girls to someone else’s house for an hour. However, through continuous efforts and determination to empower the community, the youth advisors successfully recruited participants by involving parents in the sessions and explaining the benefits.

Approaching young people within the same community proved easier, given the inherent cultural understanding and familiarity among community members. Despite initial challenges, the youth advisors achieved success in less than a year, even persuading families to delay or cancel child marriages in favour of education and health. Their success stories (with less than 1 year time) highlighted the impact of their efforts, from preventing child marriage to empowering young girls to prioritise education. However, they acknowledged the need for continuous guidance and support through training and capacity-building workshops.

Looking ahead, the youth champions expressed their unwavering commitment to continuing their efforts, emphasising the importance of ongoing education and community involvement. They also provided valuable feedback on improving the SRHR toolkit and programme, suggesting the inclusion of information on forced marriage and medical assistance resources to better address the needs of their communities.

Strategies

Despite these challenges, youth advisors have demonstrated strong commitment and resilience. Through peer counselling, community outreach and trust-building efforts, they have successfully overcome cultural resistance, fostered open discussions on sensitive SRHR topics and empowered peers with essential knowledge. Notably, within a year, youth advisors in rural Sindh successfully helped delay five child marriages and supported 10–15 young girls (aged 14–18) in initiating conversations with their parents about continuing their education, demonstrating the tangible impact of youth-led interventions. There has also been a marked improvement in adolescents’ knowledge of puberty-related physical and emotional changes as well as increased awareness of hygiene and bodily changes during puberty. Furthermore, there is growing recognition that teenage marriage and pregnancy are major contributors to school dropout rates in both urban and rural areas, which in turn limit adolescents’ future opportunities. Young adolescents have also expressed a need for stronger support systems to help teenagers continue their education.

Outcomes and sustainability

To ensure long-term impact, the youth advisory model must be institutionalised within Pakistan’s healthcare system. This includes embedding youth engagement within district health offices, training public health personnel on adolescent needs and formally involving youth representatives in the planning, implementation and review of SRHR programmes.

Policy recommendations

For scalability and sustainability, government support is essential. Concrete recommendations include:

Conclusion

Youth advisory groups have been crucial in providing SRHR counselling within their communities, creating safe spaces for open dialogue and peer-led support. Their involvement has not only increased awareness but also fostered trust, encouraging more adolescents to seek accurate information and services related to reproductive health.
