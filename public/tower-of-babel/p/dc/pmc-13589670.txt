Footwear Characteristics and Their Impact on Gait and Balance in Older Adults: A Systematic Review of Recent Evidence
Authors: Chi‐Hsien Chen, Jinfeng Li, Yoshiro Okubo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundImproper footwear has been identified as a modifiable risk factor for falls in older adults. Studies suggest that certain footwear characteristics can impair gait and balance, thereby increasing the risk of falls. However, the impact of footwear on gait and balance control may vary across research protocols. A systematic review focusing on these relationships is currently unavailable to date. This study aims to investigate the impact of footwear on gait and balance control in older adults, as well as the research protocols employed and the study outcomes.MethodA systematic review was conducted using PubMed, IEEE Xplore, Scopus, Web of Science, and Clinical Trials to identify relevant studies published between 2017 and 2024. Keywords were systematically searched, and data were manually extracted. Thirteen studies were included. The review synthesized findings on the influence of various footwear designs on gait and balance control in older adults.ResultFootwear featuring a minimal outsole, a textured insole design, and a high collar design was generally associated with improved balance outcomes. The evidence for toe‐beveled footwear designs was mixed. Studies incorporated both walking and balance assessments, with some including perception‐based questionnaires. A combination of walking, balance, and perceptual assessments provides a more comprehensive understanding of how footwear affects balance in older adults.ConclusionAmong the included studies, featuring a minimal outsole, a textured insole design, and a high collar design showed consistency to benefit older adults that provides textured feedback while offering foot protection, which may enhance balance control. However, some studies also implied that certain designs may have potential negative effects, including toe‐beveled, minimal outsole, less‐fixation, and barefoot condition. The lack of comprehensive descriptions of experimental footwear affects scientific transparency and reproducibility.

Introduction

Footwear plays a critical role in gait and balance control in older adults by facilitating somatosensory feedback to the foot [1]. A variety of footwear has been shown to impact balance, including: standard shoes with heel‐collar [2] provide extra sensitive feedback, five‐fingers design footwear increases somatosensory feedback from the plantar surface, and elevated collar with athletic outsole improves postural response in lower muscle activity. However, older adults often prefer looser‐fit footwear with an emphasis on aesthetic design, driven by comfort and a lack of knowledge about proper footwear choices [3]. Thus, wearing inappropriate footwear can increase the risk of falls in older adults.

Older adults often experience reduced sensitivity in their feet and footwear [4]. A wider forefoot and flatter medial longitudinal arch are key factors contributing to inappropriate footwear selections [5]. Lower‐extremity ulceration and pain may develop, which may reduce sensitivity if people wear inappropriate footwear. Several studies have demonstrated that wearing improper footwear can increase the risk of falls among older adults [6, 7]. Notably, many individuals who suffered fall‐related hip fractures were found to be wearing inappropriate footwear at the time of the fall [7]. Emerging evidence also supports the use of specific footwear designs—such as minimal footwear—which may enhance somatosensory feedback and postural control [1].

Given that the study participants in previous studies have varied in age and the mechanisms of gait and balance control may differ across different age groups, it is crucial to better understand how footwear influences gait and balance control in older adults. Additionally, to our knowledge, no systematic review has focused solely on the role of footwear in gait and balance control among older adults. However, previous studies did show that the footwear, including shoe insoles and foot orthoses, also show a consistent trend toward enhancing lateral postural stability in older adults [6, 8, 9]. While several reviews have investigated footwear interventions, not just footwear, they may combine orthoses and insoles as footwear interventions. The above‐mentioned reviews extracted and compared results from various studies, but they did not specifically address footwear designs or research protocols. As a result, inconsistent reports may have emerged due to variations in research methodologies. It is, therefore, essential to systematically report only the characteristics of footwear itself and the research protocols used to study its effects. Doing so will help integrate findings and clarify how different footwear features influence gait and balance control—either improving or impairing it among older adults.

This systematic review investigated the articles between 2017 and 2024 based on a systematic review reported that articles on footwear styles cause falls or increase fall risk among older adults between 1985 and 2016 [10]. Moreover, since 2018, research has increasingly focused on the minimal footwear for improving the plantar sensory input [1, 11, 12, 13]. Therefore, the present study aims to explore the impact of footwear on the gait and balance control in older adults, along with the employed research protocols and study outcomes to 2017–2024. This systematic review will provide guidance for older adults in selecting appropriate footwear and offer insights into future studies for selecting ideal research protocols.

Methods

Search Strategy

This systematic review used PubMed, IEEE Xplore, Scopus, Web of Science, and Clinical Trials as databases and searched the articles within 2017–2024: footwear, shoe, boot, gait, balance, postural, posture, older adults, elderly, aged as keywords to search for title and abstract to related studies. The references section of the queried article was surveyed to identify additional relevant studies.

Definition of Footwear and Older Adults

In the current study, the authors collectively defined all types of footwear features as footwear interventions. If the study only described “insole” or “foot orthoses” without other footwear designs (i.e., midsole, collar, lace, and upper material), that would be excluded from this study. The definitions of older adults were as references defined their participants as older adults or the older group, regardless of the average age.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

To be included in the present study, the studies must meet the following criteria: (1) original study (excluded: review article, secondary analyses, and abstract article); (2) the primary objective of the study should involve older adults and assess gait or balance outcomes from the study's title and abstract; (3) outcome measurements included quantitative analysis of gait investigation (walking test) or balance investigation (balance test in the same spot); (4) primarily used footwear as an intervention or experimental group compared to the control group or other foot conditions; (5) the study within 2017–2024.

Study Selection and Screening

The selection and screening process adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. Two authors (Chen and Li) removed the irrelevant studies based on reading their titles and abstract; in case of disagreement, a third researcher (Okubo) intervened. The authors filtered out the irrelevant articles based on three phases: (1) filtered out the studies beyond 2017–2024; (2) removed duplicate articles; (3) excluded the articles of not relevant, non‐English, conference, review study; (4) excluded ineligible articles. Authors screened all references manually to avoid key papers being inadvertently omitted. A modified quality assessment was conducted before the data extraction by using the Quality Assessment Tool for Observational Cohort and Cross‐Sectional Studies (Figure 1 and Table S1).

Study selection procedure.

Data Extraction

The author extracted the overview of studies, research information and principal outcomes from the selected studies into the EndNote X9 and Microsoft Excel worksheets. Data extracted from the studies included the following information: (1) reference basic information (year, author, journal, title); (2) participant information; (3) footwear and control group design; (4) research protocols (e.g., walking or balance assessments); (5) research tool; (6) investigation method and variable; (7) principal outcome; (8) conclusion. Since this systematic review only considered the participants of older adults, if the reference compared multiple population groups (older group * younger group), the present study would only extract the gait balance data from the older group. This study defined the insole, midsole, and outsole structure of footwear according to the footwear descriptions and diagrams from the included studies. If the included studies did not present footwear details with diagrams, the present study would follow the terminology used in the included studies.

Result

Literature Review Results

The initial search yielded 927 articles; 388 duplicate articles were removed. Based on titles and abstracts, 23 articles were identified as potentially relevant. After manual review by the author to ensure that the research specifically addressed footwear design in relation to gait balance among older adults, 13 articles were included in this systematic review. Some participant groups may be divided into younger adults, healthy adults, and elders, and the participant average age may be below 65 years if the studies defined the group as older adults. However, the current study only extracted the results of the older adult group. The study selection procedure of the included studies is listed in Figures 1. Footwear Interventions

A total of 17 experimental footwear types were evaluated along with various control conditions, including barefoot [1, 15, 16, 17, 19, 21, 22, 23], personal footwear [18, 24], control (standardized) footwear [1, 15, 16, 17, 20, 21, 23] and socks [3]. The experimental footwear designs included a wide range of features including: supportive footwear [14], Rocker footwear with varying angles (10°, 20°, 30°, and 40°) [15, 16, 17], Balance footwear [18], slippers [3, 19], Minimal footwear [1, 22, 23], Nodulous footwear [21], Boot‐like experimental footwear [24]. Reported footwear specifications varied widely, including sole hardness (Shore A: 35–85, Shore O: 30–75), midsole and outsole height (15–60 mm), toe beveled (10–40 degrees), and shoe weight (313–342 g).

Research Strategies

Straight‐path walking tests were the most common investigation for evaluating dynamic gait balance in older adults. These tests typically involved walking distances of 5–10 m at either preferred or maximum speed. Data were collected using tools such as force platforms, motion capture systems, and electronic walkways to capture variables. While electronic walkways are user‐friendly and effective for measuring spatiotemporal gait parameters, they are limited in their ability to measure minimum toe clearance and joint kinematics. Therefore, integrating motion capture systems is recommended for more comprehensive analyses.

Balance investigations have become standard in evaluating postural stability in older adults, including the simple sway test [3, 14, 24], bipedal and unipedal stance with eyes open and closed [1, 18]. Floor condition can vary depending on the study design, including solid floors and soft foam [14]. A 20‐ to 50‐s period was given for the balance investigations across the literature [3, 14, 24]. The limits of the stability test and Star Excursion Balance test [20] were also used to assess functional balance control.

Four studies assessed subjective perceptions of footwear using structured questionnaires. These questionnaires provided insights into users' preferences, which may influence long‐term footwear adherence in older adults.

Discussion

Toe Beveled Design

Rocker footwear typically incorporates a toe‐beveled anterior curvature that provides a fulcrum for transitioning from mid‐ to late‐stance during gait. This design can facilitate forward motion and reduce forefoot loading, which may benefit individuals with balance disorders or diabetic neuropathy (Figure 2B). Included references showed that it does not influence dynamic stability [17] and balance ability [20], but enhances ground reaction force and increases stance time that may facilitate forward movement in older adults [15, 16]. While barefoot walking was used as a reference condition, Rocker soles appeared to facilitate forward progression. A related toe beveled concept was observed in the “balance footwear” described by Amiez et al. [18], which included a widened midsole and heel bevel to enhance stability. However, details of the balance footwear design were not fully described in the article and were instead referenced via a commercial sales webpage. Balance footwear, for instance, improved stability, particularly when visual input was removed (eyes closed), suggesting enhanced somatosensory support [18]. Additionally, user feedback via a perception questionnaire indicated higher ratings for balance footwear compared to personal footwear.

(A) The control footwear and (B) the typical rocker footwear used in the study by Hemmati et al. [15].

Minimal Outsole Design

Minimal footwear demonstrated consistently positive outcomes across included studies. Specifically, minimal footwear improved walking and balance ability and contributed lower muscle demand [1, 22, 23], suggesting improved stability and efficiency of movement. The reduction of lower extremity muscle activity may be attributed to flatter foot contact, which could enhance stability during the weight acceptance phase of gait (Figure 3). Notably, Cudejko et al. [1] provided the most comprehensive analysis for minimal footwear design. They examined 11 minimal footwear configurations by systematically varying features such as textured insole, outsole width, collar height, toe beveled, hardness, and the presence of a split toe. This study represents a valuable contribution to footwear design research and shows a trend of positive effect when older adults wear minimal footwear in reduced sway velocity and range, implying better stability during quiet standing. Moreover, Franklin et al. [23] provided minimal detail about their footwear design, referencing only the manufacturer and product ID. The product is no longer commercially available, but its key features were inferred from figures in the publication.

The typical minimalist footwear [14].

As highlighted in the introduction, footwear plays a crucial role in facilitating somatosensory feedback by enhancing the tactile and proprioceptive input from the foot. Improvements in gait balance parameters suggest that certain footwear designs—particularly minimal footwear—may have indirect benefits on sensory integration and motor control. Minimal footwear designs appear to enhance proprioception and balance by amplifying somatosensory cues and muscle engagement. This is particularly relevant for older adults, as the central nervous system relies heavily on sensory input to regulate dynamic tasks such as walking and turning [1]. These benefits are likely attributable to the tactile interface of minimal footwear, which enhances the stimulation of plantar mechanoreceptors and facilitates proprioceptive feedback. However, while balance‐related variables such as center of mass and center of pressure were measured, none of the included studies directly assessed somatosensory system function. Therefore, although minimal footwear showed consistent benefits for balance, direct evidence of somatosensory enhancement is lacking.

Well‐ Versus Less‐Fixation Designs

Several studies have investigated the effects of fixation on gait and balance, showing that enclosed slippers—with a firm heel counter, rigid rubber sole, and robust fastening—improved static balance, dynamic gait balance, and perspective aspects [3, 23]. The enclosed slippers (firm heel counter, solid sole, and proper fixation) yielded superior gait performance outcomes (Figure 4), significantly improved directional control during the limits of stability test, reinforcing the importance of proper fixation in footwear for balance enhancement [3].

(A) A slipper, referred to as the less‐fixation condition; (B) an enclosed slipper, referred to as the well‐fixation condition, in the study by Menz et al. [3].

High Collar Design

Since the ankle plays a critical role in maintaining balance and postural control [25], providing external support through a high collar can offer significant advantages. Compared to low‐collar designs, high‐collar footwear may help reduce unnecessary ankle motion and improve balance performance, particularly during walking tasks [26]. Included references studies indicate that a well‐designed collar may support proprioception and ankle joint stability during both walking and standing balance assessments in older adults [1, 24].

High‐collar footwear provides additional ankle support in both minimal‐style [1] and boot‐style [24] footwear, with positive effects reported. The variability in outcomes may stem from differences in design features such as stiffness, material, or user familiarity. Despite some inconsistencies, ankle collar designs are generally considered beneficial, as they increase resistance to inversion and plantarflexion, potentially preventing ankle sprains while enhancing tactile feedback [27].

Menz et al. [24] found that experimental footwear with non‐uniform height soles and grooves—compared with barefoot, personal footwear, and standard controls—significantly reduced step width and end‐sway, indicating improved gait balance (Figure 5 and Figure 6A). Although Menz et al. [24] found no statistically significant differences between experimental and control footwear in postural outcomes, subjective ratings from perception questionnaires generally favored personal footwear over the experimental prototypes, indicating room for improvement in comfort or design aesthetics.

A boot‐like experimental footwear design with a high collar, heel bevel, and grooves [24].

(A) The schematic diagram of a textured insole [24] and (B) the nodulous footwear design [21].

Supportive and Textured Design

Previous review studies indicate that somatosensory feedback from the plantar surface of the foot is able to affect muscle engagement, and improve balance in healthy adults [6, 28]. Azhar et al. [14] improved the boot‐like experimental footwear to a sport‐like, supportive footwear, as described by Menz et al. [24], but the positive effects of gait and balance control did not transfer to the boot‐like experimental footwear. However, the participants did perceive the sport‐like supportive footwear to be more satisfied with their overall experience from the questionnaire. Interestingly, the Nodulous footwear improves postural stability and decreases muscle activity by stronger sensory stimulation to the mechanoreceptors [21] (Figure 6B). The threshold of somatosensory feedback may need to be determined in the future.

Comparison of Footwear and Control Group Setting

Across the included studies, common control conditions included barefoot, personal footwear, and standardized control footwear. Barefoot comparisons were used in several studies examining the effects of experimental footwear, especially compared to minimal footwear, because minimal footwear was designed to mimic the barefoot‐like feeling.

Personal footwear was often used as a comparator to evaluate the effects of experimental designs. Personal footwear was typically described as the shoes participants wore most frequently outdoors [24] or their outdoor shoes, which were usually worn during daily activities within the last 2 months [18]. Control footwear was defined as standardized footwear with similar specifications to the experimental condition, differing only in the feature being tested. These were widely used across studies [1, 17, 20, 21, 23]. From a research design standpoint, this approach helps isolate the effects of specific footwear modifications and enhances the reliability of comparisons. Additionally, Menz et al. [24] used flexible footwear with a uniform 18 mm rubber outsole (Shore A 35) and lace fixation as a comparator for their experimental prototype.

Research Protocol

Tandem walking tests were also used in several studies [3, 24]. These tests are particularly useful for detecting subtle balance impairments and have been recommended for clinical assessments in older adults with mild mobility limitations [29]. Additionally, the TUG test is widely recognized for its simplicity, low cost, and efficiency [30, 31]. It evaluates multiple components of mobility—standing up, walking, turning, and sitting down—and has demonstrated good predictive validity for fall risk in older adults [32, 33].

Standing balance tests have become standard in evaluating postural stability in older adults. Menz et al. [3, 24] employed a simple sway test, measuring angular velocity (in degrees per second) over 20‐s trials, repeated three times. The limits of the stability test were also used to assess functional balance control. This test measures how far a person can intentionally shift their center of gravity in eight directions (each separated by 45°) without losing balance. One study [18] conducted the LOS test with 8‐s trials, though others [3, 24] did not specify trial duration.

Finally, perception questionnaires were employed in several studies [1, 3, 18, 24], providing valuable insights into user experiences subjectively related to comfort, aesthetics, stability, and ease of use. However, interpretation of these results must be cautious, as some studies did not apply statistical analyses to the perception data [18]. Moreover, the lack of comprehensive descriptions of experimental footwear—sometimes limited to item codes or references to commercial websites—compromises scientific transparency and reproducibility. Future research should provide detailed specifications to maintain scientific rigor and facilitate the replication of findings.

Potential Problematic Design

Although all the included studies showed a positive trend in gait and balance control in older adults, some studies also implied that they may have negative effects. For the toe‐beveled design (Figure 2B), using Rocker footwear may prompt neuromuscular adaptations and increased gait variability, potentially due to altered motor‐control demands. Although Rocker footwear was associated with positive dynamic outcomes, such designs may also compromise postural stability; its effects on static balance were less clear [17]. Additionally, one study noted that a wearing period of 7–10 days might be necessary to optimize these effects [18], suggesting that it is not recommended to wear a directly toe‐beveled design before the adaptation period. For minimal outsole designs (Figure 3), it is also worth noting that a study excluded from this review reported potential soft‐tissue risks associated with wearing minimal footwear among runners aged 18–60 years, suggesting that the benefits may not generalize across populations or contexts [33], especially for older adults.

For the less‐fixation design (Figure 4A), footwear fixation emerged as another key factor influencing balance control. Older adults often prefer slippers at home due to comfort, but these designs typically offer limited support [3]. An excluded study has shown that footwear with poor dorsal fixation (e.g., slippers or loosely secured shoes) increases the likelihood of heel slippage and fall risk in older adults [34]. Regarding this, another study excluded from the literature also found that inadequate footwear fixation was associated with a higher incidence of hip fractures among adults aged over 95 years [7]. The footwear with less fixation, such as slippers and loosely secured shoes, was associated with larger knee and ankle joint angles during gait, particularly in the sagittal plane [19]. Slippers and loosely secured shoes caused wearers to adopt compensatory strategies (e.g., reduced knee flexion) to prevent the shoes from slipping off during movement [35]. Reinforcing the need for properly fitted, fixed designs to enhance stability and reduce fall risk. Moreover, while some studies have treated barefoot walking as a baseline or control group, it is essential to note that walking barefoot or in socks increases the risk of falls in older adults [36].

Limitations

This review has several limitations. First, the database search was restricted to studies published between 2017 and 2024. Although this ensured a focus on recent literature, additional relevant studies published prior to this period were identified during reference checks and may have been unintentionally excluded during the initial search. Second, studies that focused solely on insoles or foot orthoses, without discussing broader footwear design elements, were excluded. While this maintained a clear focus on overall footwear design, it may have omitted valuable insights from orthotic‐based interventions that influence gait and balance. Finally, the definition of “older adults” varied across the included studies. Some studies classified participants as older adults based on descriptors rather than strict age thresholds (e.g., 65 years or older). This variation may limit the generalizability of findings across different research fields, age groups, or clinical populations.

Conclusion

Footwear featuring a minimal outsole, a textured insole design, and a high collar design appears to enhance tactile sensory input and proprioception, offering foot protection while replicating a barefoot‐like condition. These characteristics are likely beneficial for improving gait and balance in older adults. However, some studies also suggest that certain design features—such as toe‐beveled, less‐fixation, and the barefoot condition—may exert negative effects on gait variability, soft‐tissue injury risk, fall risk, or the required adaptation period.

To comprehensively evaluate the effects of footwear on gait and balance in older adults, study designs should include both walking and balance investigations, complemented by perception‐based assessments. The use of appropriate statistical methods to analyze questionnaire data is strongly recommended to ensure rigorous interpretation of subjective outcomes. Moreover, the lack of comprehensive descriptions of experimental footwear—sometimes limited to item codes or references to commercial websites—compromises scientific transparency and reproducibility.
