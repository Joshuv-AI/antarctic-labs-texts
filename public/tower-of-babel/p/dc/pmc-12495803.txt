Determinants of anemia among children aged 6-23 months in Nepal: an alternative Bayesian modeling approach
Authors: Hari Prasad Upadhyay, Nirajan Budhathoki, Ram Prasad Khatiwada, Bijay Lal Pradhan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnemia remains a major public health concern among children under two years of age in low- and middle-income countries. Childhood anemia is associated with several adverse health outcomes, including delayed growth and impaired cognitive abilities. Although several studies in Nepal have examined the determinants of anemia among children aged 6-23 months using nationally representative data, alternative modeling approaches remain underutilized. This study applies a Bayesian analytical framework to identify key determinants of anemia among children aged 6-23 months in Nepal.MethodsThis cross-sectional study analyzed data from the 2022 Nepal Demographic and Health Survey (NDHS). The dependent variable was anemia in children (coded as 0 for non-anemic and 1 for anemic), while independent variables included characteristics of the child, mother, and household. Descriptive statistics including frequency, percentage and Chi-squared test of associations between the dependent variable and independent variables were presented. Bayesian binary logistic regression model was used to identify significant determinants of anemia. A noninformative normal prior with mean 0 and variance 1000 was assumed to derive the posterior distribution for the model coefficients. The Gibbs sampling method was executed to obtain posterior samples, and the results were summarized using posterior mean, odds ratio, standard error, Monte Carlo error, and 95% credible interval (CrI).ResultsThe analysis of data from 685 children aged 6-23 months revealed several determinants of anemia. Compared to children aged 19-23 months, children aged 6-12 months had 3.15 times higher odds of anemia (95% CrI: 2.02 - 4.91) and those aged 13-18 months had 2.35 times higher odds (95% CrI: 1.53 - 3.61). Second-born children had 1.49 times higher odds (95% CrI: 1.001 – 2.23) than first-born children. Mothers who did not take deworming medication during pregnancy had 1.57 times higher odds of having an anemic child (95% CrI: 1.14 – 2.51) compared to those who took such medication. Children living in the Terai region had 2.45 times higher odds (95% CrI: 1.53 – 3.98) than those living in the Hill region. Ethnicity also plays a role, as Dalit children had 2.29 times higher odds of anemia (95% CrI: 1.03 – 5.04) and Janajati children had 2.73 times higher odds (95% CrI: 1.24 – 5.92), compared to Muslim and other ethnic groups.ConclusionKey determinants of anemia among children aged 6-23 months in Nepal include child’s age, birth order, maternal intake of deworming medication during pregnancy, ecological region, and ethnicity. These findings highlight the necessity for targeted interventions that address demographic factors, maternal health, and geographic disparities to reduce childhood anemia in Nepal.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24581-4.

Introduction

Childhood anemia remains a critical public health challenge in low- and middle- income countries (LMICs) with significant implications for growth, development, and overall health of young children. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines anemia in children aged 6 to 23 months as a condition in which the hemoglobin level falls below 105 grams per liter (g/L), making it insufficient to meet physiological needs [1]. Anemia is a major cause of illness and death among children under the age of two years [2].

Globally, in 2019, approximately 39.8% of children under the age of five were anemic, with over 89% of these cases occurring in LMICs [3]. Specifically, among children aged 6-23 months, the prevalence of anemia was reported to be 47.9 % in Ethiopia [4], 66.6% in Iran [5], 64% in Bangladesh [6], and 29.7% in Hunan Province, China [7]. In Nepal, the 2016 Nepal Demographic and Health Survey (NDHS) reported that 68% of children aged 6–23 months were anemic [8]. The most recent estimate from the 2022 NDHS indicates a prevalence of 66% [9], reflecting only a modest 2% decline over a six-year period. This minimal reduction underscores the urgency to identify key determinants to inform targeted public health strategies. Although subgroup-specific data reveal more notable improvements within categories such as gender, residence, maternal education, and wealth quintile, anemia prevalence remains unacceptably high in many of these groups [8, 9]. The persistently high prevalence of anemia in the 6-23 months age group poses a significant barrier to Nepal’s progress toward achieving Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 2: Zero Hunger. This goal includes a specific target of reducing anemia among children and women to below 25% by the year 2030 [10].

Published literature investigating the determinants of childhood anemia in Nepal have largely focused on the 6-59 months age group and predominantly employed classical binary logistic regression models [11–14]. However, the burden of anemia is substantially higher during the first two years of life. According to the 2022 NDHS, the unweighted prevalence of anemia among children aged 6-23 months was 66%, compared to 34% among those aged 24-59 months [9]. This striking difference emphasizes the need to identify risk factors specifically during early childhood.

Although classical logistic regression model remains the most commonly used analytical method in the study of risk factors, Bayesian approaches have gained increasing recognition for their distinct methodological advantages [15–17]. To the best of our knowledge, no published study has applied a Bayesian framework to analyze childhood anemia in Nepal using NDHS data. Therefore, the current study seeks to fill that gap by applying this framework as a complementary alternative to classical approaches, with the goal of identifying important determinants rather than comparing statistical models.

This study aims to identify factors associated with anemia among children aged 6-23 months in Nepal. By utilizing data from a nationally representative survey and a Bayesian approach to analysis, it offers a unique perspective to understanding determinants of childhood anemia. The findings provide novel insights into a highly vulnerable age group and a stronger evidence base for designing informed public health interventions tailored to the Nepalese context. Furthermore, this study contributes to the existing body of knowledge on childhood anemia modeling in low-income settings.

Methodology

Data source

This study used data from the 2022 NDHS, a nationally representative, cross-sectional survey designed to provide up-to-date estimates on key demographic and health indicators. The 2022 NDHS employed a two-stage cluster sampling design to collect data from 14,280 households across the country. A detailed description of the sampling procedure and questionnaire is available in the official survey report [9]. Briefly, the survey used four questionnaires: household questionnaire, woman’s questionnaire, man’s questionnaire, and biomarker questionnaire. The woman’s questionnaire collected information from all women aged 15-49 years. The biomarker questionnaire recorded anthropometric measurements, anemia testing, and blood pressure measurements. Children aged 6-59 months and women aged 15-49 years were eligible for anemia testing.

For the present analysis, data from 685 children aged 6-23 months with complete information on anemia status and relevant covariates were included. The participant selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1, which presents a flow diagram detailing the inclusion criteria and sample derivation. Study variables

The outcome variable of interest was anemia in children aged 6-23 months, categorized as either non-anemic (coded as 0) or anemic (coded as 1). Following WHO guidelines in effect during the 2022 NDHS, children with hemoglobin level less than 11.0 grams per deciliter (g/dL) were classified as anemic [9]. A wide range of variables commonly identified as the determinants of childhood anemia in the literature [5, 7, 11, 18] were considered for potential independent variables. The final list of variables included in the study was constrained by the data availability in the 2022 NDHS. Table 1 presents the list of variables and their measurement type.

Here we describe few variables that need additional description. Within the 6-23 months age of children, further categorization was made into three categories: 6-12, 13-18, and 19-23 months. Children size at birth was assessed based on the mother’s recall. Children whose height-for-age z score was below −2 standard deviations (SD) from the median of the reference population were considered stunted. Children whose weight-for-height z score was below –2 SD from the median of the reference population were considered wasted. List of variables and their measurement

Age of mother was recorded as a continuous variable in the survey. However, we created three categories of age, namely less than 25, 25-35, and 35 or greater. Futhermore, mothers’ age at first birth was categorized as less than 20 and 20 or greater. Body mass index (BMI) of mothers was categorized to be in the underweight (<18.5 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$kg/m^2$$\end{document}), normal (18.5-24.9 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$kg/m^2$$\end{document}), overweight (25-29.9 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$kg/m^2$$\end{document}), and obese (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ge$$\end{document} 30 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$kg/m^2$$\end{document}) category [19]. Mothers were classified as anemic if their hemoglobin level was less than 12.0 g/dL [9]. Similarly, mothers were said to smoke if they smoked cigarettes/tobacco. Period of gestation was categorized as preterm, term, and post-term for pregnancies lasting less than 37 weeks, 37 to 42 weeks, and beyond 42 weeks, respectively [20, 21]. NDHS categorises households into poorest, poor, middle, rich, and richest category of wealth index as a composite indicator of the economic status [22].

Statistical analysis

Frequency (percentage) were used to describe the characteristics of the study population. This was followed by Chi-squared test as a bivariate analysis method. The bivariate analysis was carried out to identify potential variables for the multivariable Bayesian logistic regression model. Variables with a p-value \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$< 0.25$$\end{document} in the bivariate analysis were considered for inclusion in the final model. This threshold follows the purposeful selection strategy to avoid excluding important confounders at an early stage of model building [23]. Analyses were performed using R programming language [24].

Bayesian binary logistic regression approach was used to identify the determinants of anemia. Since the outcome is a binary variable, a logistic regression framework was appropriate. The Bayesian approach was selected not as a competing alternative to classical methods, but as an illustrative framework for studying risk factors. Its methodological advantages include, first, the ability to incorporate prior knowledge — although we used non-informative priors in this study, future research may build on this to improve precision. Second, Bayesian modeling combines prior information and observed data (likelihood) to generate full posterior distributions of the parameters [25]. This offers richer insights into parameter uncertainty compared to point estimates and standard errors produced by the classical methods. Moreover, Bayesian inference enables probability-based interpretation, such as the probability that an odds ratio lies within a specific range — typically represented by a credible interval (CrI) — providing more intuitive insights for policy making.

For a binary outcome variable Z (where Z can be either 0 or 1), the binary logistic regression model with a logit link function is expressed as:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text {logit}(P(Z=1)) &= \log \left( \frac{P(Z=1)}{1 - P(Z=1)} \right) \\&= \beta _0 + \beta _1 K_1 + \beta _2 K_2 + \dots + \beta _r K_r \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

In this model, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P(Y=1)$$\end{document} represents the probability that Z equals 1 (the occurrence of the event) while \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P(Z=0) = 1 - P(Z=1)$$\end{document} is the probability that Z equals 0 (the event does not occur). \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta _0$$\end{document} is the intercept (constant term); \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta _1, \beta _2, \dots , \beta _r$$\end{document} are the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r^{th}$$\end{document} regression coefficients corresponding to the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r^{th}$$\end{document} independent variables \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K_1, K_2, K_3, \dots , K_r$$\end{document}.

The logit function in Equation 1 is the natural logarithm of the odds ratio between \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P(Z=1)$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P(Z=0)$$\end{document}. The model estimates the coefficients \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta _1, \beta _2, \dots , \beta _r$$\end{document}, and the probability of success, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P(Z=1)$$\end{document}, is calculated by applying the inverse logit function:2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} P(Z=1) &= \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(\beta _0 + \beta _1 K_1 + \beta _2 K_2 + \dots + \beta _r K_r)}} \\&= \frac{e^{\beta _0 + \beta _1 K_1 + \beta _2 K_2 + \dots + \beta _r K_r}}{1 + e^{\beta _0 + \beta _1 K_1 + \beta _2 K_2 + \dots + \beta _r K_r}} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

The likelihood function for the data \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Z = (Z_1, Z_2, Z_3, \dots , Z_n)^T$$\end{document} is given by:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} P(Z \mid \beta ) = L(\beta \mid Z) = \prod _{i=1}^{n} \pi _i^{Z_i} (1 - \pi _i)^{(1 - Z_i)} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

Solving gives3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} L(\beta \mid Z) = &\prod _{i=1}^{n} \left( \frac{e^{\beta _0 + \beta _1 K_1 + \beta _2 K_2 + \dots + \beta _r K_r}}{1 + e^{\beta _0 + \beta _1 K_1 + \beta _2 K_2 + \dots + \beta _r K_r}} \right) ^{Z_i} \\&\left( 1 - \frac{e^{\beta _0 + \beta _1 K_1 + \beta _2 K_2 + \dots + \beta _r K_r}}{1 + e^{\beta _0 + \beta _1 K_1 + \beta _2 K_2 + \dots + \beta _r K_r}} \right) ^{(1 - Z_i)} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

Following [26], the common choice for the prior distribution of a binary logistic regression parameter is a normal distribution with mean \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu _m$$\end{document} and variance \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma _m^2$$\end{document}, i.e.,\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \beta _m \sim N(\mu _m, \sigma _m^2) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

Moreover, the prior distribution for the binary logistic regression parameter is given by:4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} P(\beta _m) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi \sigma _m^2}} \exp \left( -\frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{\beta _m - \mu _m}{\sigma _m} \right) ^2 \right) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

We assume a non-informative normal prior with mean 0 and variance 1000 in the absence of relevant knowledge about the regression parameters [25, 26]. Then, the posterior distribution is the updated probability distribution of a parameter after incorporating both prior beliefs and observed data, calculated using Bayes’ theorem:5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \pi (\beta \mid Z) = \prod _{m=1}^{r} P(\beta _m) \cdot \prod _{i=1}^{n} L(\beta \mid Z) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

Expanding the terms:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \pi (\beta \mid Z)= & \prod _{m=1}^{r} \left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi \sigma _m^2}} \exp \left( -\frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{\beta _m - \mu _m}{\sigma _m} \right) ^2 \right) \right) \nonumber \\ & \times \prod _{i=1}^{n} \left( \frac{e^{\beta _0 + \beta _1 K_1 + \beta _2 K_2 + \dots + \beta _r K_r}}{1 + e^{\beta _0 + \beta _1 K_1 + \beta _2 K_2 + \dots + \beta _r K_r}} \right) ^{Z_i} \nonumber \\ & \times \left( 1 - \frac{e^{\beta _0 + \beta _1 K_1 + \beta _2 K_2 + \dots + \beta _r K_r}}{1 + e^{\beta _0 + \beta _1 K_1 + \beta _2 K_2 + \dots + \beta _r K_r}} \right) ^{(1 - Z_i)} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

The posterior distribution in Equation 6 is a complex function. The most common approach to obtain the marginal posterior distribution is using Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods. We used Gibbs sampling to simulate samples from the posterior distribution, utilizing the rstanarm package [27] in R. The empirical results from an MCMC analysis are considered unreliable until the chain reaches its stationary distribution. The convergence of an MCMC algorithm refers to the point at which it has attained its target distribution. Therefore, monitoring the convergence is crucial for ensuring accurate results from the posterior distribution of interest. In this study, we used time series (history) plots, density plots, and the Gelman-Rubin statistic for convergence assessment. Summary statistics, such as the posterior mean, odds ratio (OR), standard error of the posterior mean (PSE), Monte Carlo error, and the 95% CrI for the OR, were computed for each parameter. The significance of each explanatory variable was evaluated by statistical tests of the odds ratio using a 95% CrI. The odds ratio are simply the exponentiated regression coefficients.

Results

Descriptive statistics

Results from Table 2 show that nearly two-thirds (65.5%) of the children aged 6-23 months were anemic. The gender distribution among children was nearly equal, with 52% male and 48% female. There were also similar proportions of children in the 6-12 and 13-18 months age group. Birth size data indicate that 74% of the children were reported as having an average size at birth, while 10% were larger than average, and 3.4% were very small. A majority of the children (75%) were either first- or second-born. Additionally, 16% of children experienced diarrhea in the two weeks preceding the survey, and 28% had a fever. Child nutrition and health remain major concerns, with high rates of malnutrition: 85% of children were stunted, 81% were underweight, and 66% suffered from wasting. A majority (71.7%) of the children had received micronutrient powder (MNP), locally branded as Baal Vita, in the 12 months prior to the survey.

More than half of the mothers (51%) were under 25 years of age, and nearly half (49.9%) gave their first birth before the age of 20. Regarding education, 42% of mothers had attained secondary-level education, while only 3.5% had completed higher education. Two-thirds of the mothers were employed. Maternal health indicators show that 96% received iron tablets or syrup during pregnancy, and 81% took deworming medication. However, only 18% reported receiving food or cash assistance during pregnancy. Two-thirds of the mothers had normal BMI. Anemia remained a major concern, affecting 38% of mothers. In terms of lifestyle behaviors, 5% of mothers self-reported smoking cigarettes or tobacco. Regarding birth outcomes, 91% of the births were classified as term, 7.2% preterm, and 1.9% were postterm. The majority of deliveries (80%) occurred in health centers.

The majority of participants were from the Terai region (48%), followed by the Hill (44%) and Mountain (8.6%) regions, reflecting the country’s geographic diversity. Provincial representation varied, with the highest participation from Karnali and Madhesh (19% each) and the lowest from Gandaki (7.6%). Participants belonged to diverse religious groups, with Hinduism being predominant (85%), followed by Muslim (4.7%), Buddhist (3.9%), Christian (3.6%), and Kirat (2.9%). In terms of ethnic composition, Brahmin/Chhetri (30%), Janajati (27%), Dalit (22%), and Terai caste (13%) groups were the most represented. Regarding household wealth, 34% of children lived in households categorized as the poorest, while only 10% lived in the richest. Results from Bayesian logistic regression

Table 4 presents the results from the Bayesian logistic regression model, which identified several statistically significant determinants childhood anemia. The posterior density plots and trace plots for the regression coefficients showed that the samples from the conditional distributions of each coefficient converged to the target distribution (see Figs. S2 and S3). Age of the child emerged as a significant determinant of anemia. Children aged 6-12 months had 3.15 times higher odds of anemia (95% CrI: 2.02 - 4.91) compared to those aged 19-23 months. Similarly, children aged 13-18 months had 2.35 times higher odds (95% CrI: 1.53 - 3.61) compared to the same reference group. These findings suggest that within the 6-23 months age range, younger children have an increased odds of being anemic.

Birth order was identified as another risk factor for childhood anemia. Second-born children had 1.49 times higher odds of anemia (95% CrI: 1.001–2.23) compared to first-born children. Although children with higher birth orders also showed elevated odds, these results were not statistically significant. Maternal deworming medication during pregnancy was another important factor. Mothers who did not receive such medication had 1.57 times higher odds of having an anemic children (95% CrI: 1.14–2.51) compared to those who took such medication, highlighting the importance of maternal care during pregnancy.

Geographic region also played a significant role. Children residing in Nepal’s Terai region had significantly higher odds of anemia (OR: 2.45, 95% CrI: 1.53–3.98) compared to those living in the Hill region. With regard to ethnicity, Dalit children had 2.29 times higher odds of anemia (95% CrI: 1.03–5.04), and Janajati children had 2.73 times higher odds (95% CrI: 1.24–5.92), compared to children from Muslim and other ethnic groups.

Discussion

This study aimed to identify the determinants of anemia among children aged 6-23 months in Nepal using a Bayesian logistic regression approach. Child age, birth order, maternal non-receipt of deworming medication during pregnancy, residence in the country’s Terai region, and belonging to Dalit and Janajati ethnic groups were found to be significantly associated with childhood anemia.

Our study revealed that within the 6-23 months age group, younger children had higher odds of anemia. This finding is consistent with studies conducted in Hunan Province, China [7], Northeast Ethiopia [5], and India [28]. Infants undergo rapid growth during the first year of life, substantially increasing their iron requirements that may be challenging to meet through diet alone. By 19-23 months, children consume a more diverse diet, including iron-rich foods, and have more developed digestive systems, which allow for improved iron absorption [29]. Moreover, the iron stores that newborns receive from their mother are known to begin depleting after six months of age, potentially contributing to the development of anemia [30]. Our finding emphasizes the importance of introducing iron-rich foods early in infancy to help prevent anemia.

Another possible explanation for the elevated prevalence of anemia among younger children may be due to challenges associated with the initiation of complementary feeding around six months of age. During this transition, infants are at increased risk of diarrhea due to immature digestive systems and suboptimal hygiene practices related to food preparation and feeding utensils, affecting iron absorption at a time of high physiological need [31]. Furthermore, inadequate iron content in commonly used complementary foods in Nepal — often dominated by rice-based gruels — fails to meet the rising iron needs of growing infants [32]. Therefore, in addition to promoting safe complementary feeding practices, there is a pressing need to emphasize the inclusion of iron-rich foods in the diets of both infants and their mothers, especially as breastfeeding continues up to two years or beyond.

We also found that race and ethnicity play a significant role in childhood anemia status. In particular, Dalit and Janajati children had higher odds of anemia compared to other ethnic groups. Studies conducted outside Nepal have similarly reported associations between ethnicity and childhood anemia [33, 34]. In Nepal, both Dalit and Janajati communities are recognized as relatively disadvantaged, facing social and economic marginalization. As a result, these groups may have limited access to maternal healthcare during pregnancy, which can contribute to adverse birth outcomes, including anemia in newborns. Several studies have previously documented maternal and child health disparities among ethnic groups in Nepal [35]. Our study further underscore the need for targeted interventions to improve childhood anemia outcomes among Dalit and Janajati populations.

Residence in the country’s Terai region was associated with higher odds of childhood anemia, indicating notable geographical differences. Consistent with our finding, several studies have highlighted geographic variations in childhood anemia prevalence, particularly in low- and middle-income countries [36, 37]. The infrequent feeding of iron-rich foods, such as green leafy vegetables and meat, to children in the Terai region may contribute to the higher prevalence of anemia among children [38]. This region also hosts a large proportions of socially and economically marginalized ethnic populations, who often lack access to essential healthcare resources [39]. Moreover, inadequate sanitation, such as lack of access to latrines, safe water, and hand-washing facilities, remains a widespread issue in many parts of the Terai. These conditions increase exposure to pathogens, leading to frequent diarrhea and impaired nutrient absorption, further exacerbating anemia risk [38]. These findings underscore the urgent need for integrated interventions focused on both nutrition and sanitation improvements to reach underserved populations in the Terai region of Nepal.

This study also revealed that higher birth order, especially being the second child, was associated with increased odds of childhood anemia. This finding is in agreement with several prior studies [18, 40, 41]. A likely explanation is that mothers may not fully recover nutritionally between pregnancies, especially when the birth interval is short [42]. Inadequate maternal recovery has been linked to low birth weight and increased risk of anemia in subsequent children [43]. In Nepal, regional studies have reported that a significant proportion of births occur with spacing of less than 24 months [44, 45]. These findings emphasize the need for interventions that promote optimal birth spacing as a strategy to improve childhood anemia outcomes.

The association of helminth infestation with maternal anemia and low birth weight has been previously reported [46]. Our findings further suggest that the maternal consumption of deworming medication during pregnancy is associated with reduced odds of anemia among children. Specifically, mothers who did not take deworming medication had more than 50% higher odds of having an anemic child. However, contrary to our findings, some studies have reported no beneficial effect of prenatal deworming medication on childhood anemia [47, 48]. It is important to note that the observed relationship between deworming medication and childhood anemia may be mediated by other factors, such as episodes of diarrhea in children. Frequent diarrhea, often associated with poor sanitation and hygiene, can impair iron absorption and potentially mask the benefits of maternal deworming interventions. Therefore, the relationship between deworming medication and childhood anemia may be indirect and influenced by other environmental or health-related factors. Future studies should consider exploring these mediating pathways to better understand the role of prenatal deworming medication in reducing anemia risk during early childhood.

Despite ongoing efforts by the Government of Nepal and its partners to combat childhood anemia — most notably through the nationwide distribution MNP, locally branded as Baal Vita [49] — the reduction in anemia prevalence remains minimal. The modest 2% reduction in anemia prevalence between 2016 and 2022 NDHS surveys among children aged 6-23 months imply a limited impact on population level. This stagnation suggests that existing nutrition programs may need to better target the most vulnerable groups identified in our analysis, such as children of marginalized ethnic groups, those residing in the Terai region, and children of mothers with limited access to prenatal care. However, we also urge that such intervention programs be culturally appropriate and economically feasible. Strategies must consider local dietary patterns, access to healthcare, and cultural beliefs around child feeding and maternal nutrition. Tailored approaches that engage community stakeholders and respect socio-cultural norms are more likely to be effective and sustainable in the long term.

This study has several strengths. First, it utilizes data from a nationally representative sample of children aged 6-23 months. Second, we employed a powerful and increasingly popular Bayesian approach to analysis. Unlike classical logistic regression models, Bayesian models provide direct probability-based interpretations [25]. For instance, based on our finding of higher odds of anemia among children in the Terai region, one can conclude that there is a 95% probability that the true odds ratio lies within the interval of 1.53 to 3.98. Future studies using Bayesian approach may benefit from our findings for their prior elicitation.

The limitations of this study stem from the use of cross-sectional survey data, which prevents us from establishing causal associations. Survey responses to questions such as the size of child at birth may be subject to recall bias. Additionally, mothers’ responses to question about smoking may be influenced by social desirability bias and therefore underreported. Although we incorporated a wide range of variables known to affect childhood anemia outcomes, any variables omitted could lead to residual confounding. Moreover, it is worth noting that in 2024, WHO released updated guidelines recommending a revised cutoff of 10.5 g/dL for defining anemia in children aged 6-23 months [1]. Since our study was based on the 2022 NDHS data, the earlier threshold of 11.0 g/dL was used. Future studies may consider adopting the updated cutoff to align with current recommendations. Lastly, it is important to clarify that the objective of this study was not to identify the optimal statistical model among alternative models, but rather to illustrate how Bayesian logistic regression can be applied in the study of childhood anemia. Exact logistic regression and modified Poisson regression are also valid approaches for analyzing binary outcomes, which may be considered in future research.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study identified several key determinants of anemia among children aged 6-23 months in Nepal using a Bayesian logistic regression approach. Younger age, second birth order, maternal non-receipt of deworming medication during pregnancy, residence in the Terai region, and belonging to Dalit and Janajati ethnic groups were all significantly associated with higher odds of childhood anemia. These findings emphasize the urgent need for interventions, with a particular focus on children under 18 months of age. Promoting optimal birth spacing and ensuring access to deworming medications during pregnancy may contribute to lowering anemia risk in early childhood. The geographic and ethnic disparities observed — especially in the Terai region and among Dalit and Janajati communities — underscore the importance of regionally tailored and equity-focused public health interventions. Without such interventions, achieving the SDG goal of reducing anemia prevalence among children and women will remain a significant challenge for Nepal. The use of a Bayesian framework in this study complements existing classical approach and contributes to methodological diversity in studying childhood anemia in Nepal.
