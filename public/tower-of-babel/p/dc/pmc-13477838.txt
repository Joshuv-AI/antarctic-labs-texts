Self‐Reported Periodontal Symptoms and Clinical Periodontal Status in Older Adults With and Without Dementia: A Population‐Based Diagnostic Accuracy Study
Authors: Tomoaki Mameno, Naoko Otsuki, Satoko Takeuchi, Sho Takeda, Tomoaki Iwayama, Masahide Takedachi, Ryohei Yamamoto, Kazunori Ikebe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of self‐reported periodontal symptoms compared to clinical periodontal status among older adults with and without dementia.Materials and MethodsThis cross‐sectional study included adults ≥ 75 years of age who underwent public dental checkups in Japan. Self‐reported gingival bleeding and tooth mobility were assessed as index tests against conceptually matched clinical reference standards: bleeding on probing (BOP) and clinically evaluated tooth mobility (Miller Grade ≥ II), respectively. Dementia status was obtained from health records. Sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative predictive values and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) were calculated overall and stratified by dementia status.ResultsOf the 158,990 participants (3.9% with dementia), 61.2% were BOP‐positive and 22.5% had Miller Grade ≥ II tooth mobility. Self‐reported gingival bleeding showed poor diagnostic accuracy for BOP‐positive status (AUC: 0.54; sensitivity: 0.24; specificity: 0.85). Self‐reported tooth mobility showed higher but limited accuracy for Miller Grade ≥ II (AUC: 0.66; sensitivity: 0.43; specificity: 0.90), with lower accuracy in participants with dementia (AUC: 0.61) than in those without dementia (AUC: 0.67).ConclusionsSelf‐reported periodontal symptoms showed limited diagnostic accuracy in adults aged ≥ 75 years, with the awareness gap most pronounced for tooth mobility in individuals with dementia.Keywords: dementia, epidemiology, older adults, periodontal disease, self‐reported symptoms

Introduction

Periodontal disease is one of the most prevalent oral conditions and a leading cause of tooth loss worldwide (Petersen and Ogawa 2012). In older adults, periodontal disease is a major public health concern, not only because of its direct impact on oral function and quality of life but also because of its association with systemic diseases such as diabetes (Stöhr et al. 2021), cerebrovascular disorders (El Masri et al. 2024), cardiovascular diseases (Leng et al. 2023) and other chronic illnesses (Hasan et al. 2025). Maintaining periodontal health in later life is essential for nutrition, overall health and longevity.

Periodontal disease is a chronic bacterial infection that typically progresses insidiously, presenting with mild clinical manifestations, such as occasional gingival bleeding during brushing and typically little to no pain. Accordingly, periodontal disease is often described as relatively silent in its earlier stages. By the time overt clinical signs become apparent, substantial periodontal attachment loss and alveolar bone resorption may have already occurred. This limited symptom awareness often contributes to delays in seeking professional care and preventive or early therapeutic interventions (Al‐Karadsheh et al. 2024). Furthermore, older adults often face other health issues, such as dementia or cognitive decline, which can further decrease their awareness of their oral health (Zuluaga et al. 2011). Consequently, the disconnect between subjective awareness and the actual periodontal condition is a significant obstacle to timely intervention and maintenance therapies.

Previous studies have explored the accuracy of self‐reported periodontal disease and oral health status in adults. In a cohort study of older postmenopausal women, simple self‐reported questions showed only moderate sensitivity in identifying clinical periodontitis (Lamonte et al. 2014). Notably, even among those with severe disease, only approximately 56% self‐reported a prior diagnosis of periodontal/gum disease. Generally, self‐perception of periodontal disease tends to underestimate the true prevalence of periodontal disease according to epidemiological surveys (Montero et al. 2019; Wiernik et al. 2024). However, most research has concentrated on middle‐aged or general adult populations. While some studies have included older adults, very few have specifically examined older adults or considered cognitive impairment in their analyses. To our knowledge, no large‐scale study has assessed the accuracy with which older adults recognise their own clinically verified periodontal status, nor has research determined whether cognitive impairment further impedes this recognition.

This population‐based cohort study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of self‐reported periodontal symptoms against clinically assessed periodontal status among older adults in Japan. The potential modifying effect of cognitive impairment on this diagnostic accuracy was also examined.

Material and Methods

Study Population

This cross‐sectional study was part of a larger ongoing cohort study called the ‘Oral Health Screening to Assess Keys of Well Aging (OHSAKA)’, which aimed to identify factors influencing healthy life expectancy in older adults. Additional details regarding the study can be found elsewhere (Mameno et al. 2024). Briefly, the present study used data from dental checkups conducted as part of public health care services. As of 1 April 2018, there were 1,100,572 adults aged 75 and older enrolled in Osaka Prefecture's late‐stage medical insurance system in Japan. Between April 2018 and March 2020, 256,772 of these individuals received a dental checkup. Participants with incomplete or invalid data and those who were edentulous were excluded from analysis. All insurance and medical claims data were obtained from the Kokuho database (KDB) of the National Health Insurance Database of Japan. This database includes monthly data on medical claims, featuring standardised codes for electronic claims processing, as outlined by the Ministry of Health, Labor and Welfare, and provides comprehensive details on prescriptions and medical procedures.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Review Board of The University of Osaka (approval nos 2022–2 and R4–M2). Participants were informed about the research study and their involvement and were allowed to opt out. The participants were also provided with comprehensive information detailing the study's purpose, procedures as well as potential risks and benefits on the research institution's website. The study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines (von Elm et al. 2007).

Data Collection

In this study, self‐reported periodontal symptoms were treated as the index tests, and clinical periodontal findings were treated as the reference standard. Self‐reported gingival bleeding was compared with bleeding on probing (BOP), and self‐reported tooth mobility was compared with clinically assessed tooth mobility, defined as Miller Grade II or higher. Periodontal pocket depth (PPD) was used in secondary descriptive analyses to summarise symptom reporting across levels of periodontal severity. Dementia status was used to stratify the analyses. Additionally, age, sex, body mass index (BMI), the number of remaining teeth, oral hygiene status, smoking history, diabetes and hypertension were summarised descriptively to characterise the study population.

The participants completed a brief oral health questionnaire and underwent clinical dental examination. Self‐reported periodontal symptoms were assessed using three questions: (1) Do your gums bleed? (2) Are your gums swollen? (3) Do your teeth feel loose? Response options were ‘usually’, ‘sometimes’ or ‘never’. Self‐reported gingival bleeding and tooth mobility were used in the primary diagnostic accuracy analyses, whereas self‐reported gingival swelling was evaluated only in supplementary exploratory analyses because no directly corresponding clinical reference standard had been prespecified.

The number of teeth and PPD were assessed by a dentist or dental hygienist while the participants were seated in a dental chair. A maximum of 28 teeth were examined, excluding third molars. The number of remaining teeth was recorded and categorised into three groups: 1–9, 10–19 and ≥ 20 teeth. Periodontal tissue was examined according to the modified Community Periodontal Index (CPI) of the World Health Organisation (Petersen and Baez 2013). Ten teeth were evaluated according to the modified CPI protocol (see Methods S1 for details). The PPD score was determined by the highest score given to one of these 10 teeth, where a score of 0 represented a PPD of ≤ 3 mm, 1 indicated a PPD of 4–5 mm and 2 signified a PPD of ≥ 6 mm. BOP was considered positive if it was observed in any of the teeth evaluated. Tooth mobility was measured according to the Miller classification (Miller 1950; Japanese Society of Periodontology 2022; see Methods S2 for grade definitions). The highest grade among all remaining teeth was recorded for each participant. Clinically significant tooth mobility was defined as Miller Grade II or higher. Oral hygiene status was assessed by evaluating plaque accumulation on the remaining teeth and categorised as mild (minimal or no visible plaque), moderate (plaque present but covering no more than one‐third of tooth surface) or severe (plaque covering more than one‐third of at least one tooth's surface).

Information on medications prescribed within 6 months before the dental checkup was obtained from the KDB system using the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical classification system developed by the World Health Organisation (World Health Organisation 2017). A history of dementia was defined as the prescription of anti‐dementia agents such as cholinesterase inhibitors or memantine. A history of hypertension was defined as the prescription of antihypertensive agents, including diuretics, beta‐blockers, calcium channel blockers, angiotensin receptor blockers or angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors. Participants who were prescribed antidiabetic medications were classified as having diabetes. Smoking status (current, former, or never) and BMI were determined using a self‐administered questionnaire.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics are expressed as the percentage for categorical variables and as mean and standard deviation for continuous variables. The frequencies of self‐reported symptoms were summarised by clinical reference standards and dementia status to illustrate crude trends.

The diagnostic accuracy of each self‐reported symptom was assessed using a conceptually matched clinical reference standard: self‐reported gingival bleeding was compared to BOP‐positive status, while self‐reported tooth mobility was evaluated against clinically determined tooth mobility of Miller Grade ≥ II. For these primary diagnostic accuracy analyses, responses were dichotomised as symptom present (‘usually’ or ‘sometimes’) versus symptom absent (‘never’). For each symptom–reference standard pairing, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV) and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) were determined both overall and stratified by dementia status. Full 2 × 2 contingency tables were also constructed for each symptom–reference standard pairing. In secondary diagnostic accuracy analyses, PPD score served as an alternative reference standard for both symptoms, facilitating assessment of diagnostic performance relative to a widely used periodontal screening index.

As additional model performance analyses, separate logistic regression models were fitted using the original ordinal self‐reported symptom responses (never = 0, sometimes = 1, usually = 2) as the sole predictor and the conceptually matched clinical reference standards as the outcomes. Overall performance was assessed using the Brier score; clinical utility was assessed using decision curve analysis; and calibration was assessed in supplementary analyses.

All analyses were performed using Python (version 3.13.9; Python Software Foundation, Beaverton, OR, USA) with α = 0.05, using the pandas, NumPy and statsmodels packages; figures were generated using matplotlib.

Results

Participant Characteristics

Among 256,772 older adults who received dental examinations between April 2018 and March 2020, 54,133 individuals were excluded because of insurance status, age, dental charting parameters or dentition‐related criteria. Of the 202,639 remaining dentate participants, an additional 43,649 were excluded for missing questionnaire data, incomplete dental examination data or the absence of teeth suitable for CPI assessment. Prevalence of self‐reported periodontal symptoms was examined according to the corresponding clinical reference standards and dementia status. For gingival bleeding and tooth mobility, higher symptoms reporting was observed among those with clinically verified disease compared to those without; however, a substantial proportion of affected participants remained unaware of their condition. Among those classified as Miller Grade ≥II, 56.8% did not perceive tooth mobility, increasing to 66.8% in participants with dementia compared to 56.3% in those without—a difference of 10.5 percentage points (Figure 3). This pattern persisted when self‐reported symptoms were stratified by PPD score (Figures S1 and S2), with gingival bleeding reported by 14.3% at a PPD score of 0 and rising to 26.6% at a PPD score of 2, while self‐reported tooth mobility increased from 11.2% to 26.1%. Notably, even among participants with the most severe PPD scores, approximately 75% did not report corresponding symptoms. Similarly, in an exploratory analysis of self‐reported gingival swelling, observed positivity for PPD score 2 increased across the ordinal response categories overall and within each dementia stratum (Table S1).

Diagnostic Accuracy and Additional Model Performance Analyses of Self‐Reported Symptoms

Primary Analysis

Self‐reported gingival bleeding showed poor diagnostic accuracy for identifying BOP‐positive status among all participants (sensitivity: 0.24; specificity: 0.85; PPV: 0.72; NPV: 0.41; AUC: 0.54; Table 2). When stratified by dementia status, the diagnostic accuracy was nearly identical in participants without dementia (sensitivity: 0.24; specificity: 0.85; AUC: 0.54) and only marginally lower in those with dementia (sensitivity: 0.21; specificity: 0.86; AUC: 0.53). Self‐reported tooth mobility yielded higher, yet still moderate, diagnostic accuracy for detecting clinically assessed mobility (Miller Grade ≥ II) overall (sensitivity: 0.43; specificity: 0.90; PPV: 0.54; NPV: 0.84; AUC: 0.66), indicating that 57% of participants with clinically significant tooth mobility were unaware of their condition. Upon stratification by dementia status, a more marked decrease in diagnostic accuracy was observed among participants with dementia: sensitivity declined from 0.44 (specificity: 0.90, AUC: 0.67) in those without dementia to 0.33 (specificity: 0.89, AUC: 0.61) in those with dementia, corresponding to a missed rate of 67% within the latter group. Full 2 × 2 contingency tables are provided in Table S2.

Secondary Analysis

When a PPD score of 2 served as the reference standard, diagnostic accuracy remained limited for both symptoms (Table S3). For self‐reported gingival bleeding, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV and AUC were 0.27, 0.82, 0.35, 0.76 and 0.54, respectively; the corresponding values for self‐reported tooth mobility were 0.26, 0.85, 0.39, 0.76 and 0.56. Differences by dementia status were small overall, although sensitivity for self‐reported gingival bleeding was somewhat lower among participants with dementia.

Additional Model Performance Analyses

Additional model performance results are summarised in Table 3. For the primary models, self‐reported gingival bleeding showed poor discrimination and limited overall performance for BOP‐positive status (AUC, 0.55; Brier score, 0.24), whereas self‐reported tooth mobility performed better for Miller Grade ≥ II tooth mobility (AUC, 0.67; Brier score, 0.15). When PPD score 2 was used as the reference standard, performance remained weak for both symptoms (AUCs, 0.54 and 0.56; Brier scores, 0.19 and 0.19, respectively). Decision curve analysis showed little or no net benefit of the gingival bleeding model beyond an examine‐all strategy, whereas the tooth mobility model showed modest net benefit over a limited threshold range (Figure S3). Apparent calibration was not grossly inconsistent with observed event rates across ordinal response categories in the primary models (Figure S4).

Discussion

This study evaluated the diagnostic accuracy of self‐reported periodontal symptoms compared with clinically assessed periodontal status in 158,990 Japanese adults aged ≥ 75 years. Self‐reported periodontal symptoms showed limited diagnostic accuracy for clinically assessed periodontal status, as most individuals with periodontal disease did not recognise their symptoms. This lack of awareness was particularly evident regarding tooth mobility among participants with dementia. These findings highlight a significant discrepancy between the objective periodontal status and subjective perception in the older population, which is understudied in periodontal epidemiology.

Our results were consistent with previous studies showing that self‐reported periodontal status partially reflects objective periodontal status. In a US cohort of postmenopausal females, only half of those with clinically confirmed severe periodontitis acknowledged having been diagnosed with gum disease (Lamonte et al. 2014). Similarly, although the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and the American Academy of Periodontology self‐report questionnaire has demonsreated reasonable performance in detecting advanced periodontitis when combined with demographic and behavioural characteristics, its sensitivity for early‐stage disease remains limited (Deng et al. 2021). A systematic review and meta‐analysis of self‐reported periodontal measures reported highly variable diagnostic accuracy, with sensitivities ranging from 17% to 82% and specificities ranging from 23% to 97% (Bi et al. 2025). These studies suggest that certain symptoms, particularly tooth mobility, may indicate advanced disease, although the diagnostic performance varies considerably. Conversely, studies among Japanese middle‐aged workers and Spanish adults reported higher sensitivity and AUC exceeding 0.80 (Iwasaki et al. 2021; Saka‐Herrán et al. 2020), likely reflecting younger populations without detailed information on comorbidities. By focusing exclusively on adults aged ≥ 75 years and incorporating clinical data on cognitive impairment, our study provides a comprehensive assessment of self‐reported periodontal symptoms in an ageing population. Given the advanced age of the cohort and the mobility standard reflecting moderate to severe periodontal breakdown, our findings likely reflect limited awareness of overt longstanding periodontitis in later life rather than failure to detect early subclinical disease.

The overall sensitivity of symptom self‐reporting was low, and an awareness gap persisted even across all levels of clinical severity. An AUC of 0.54 for self‐reported gingival bleeding versus BOP‐positive status indicates poor discrimination, demonstrating that symptom inquiry alone cannot discriminate between BOP‐positive and BOP‐negative individuals in this population. This awareness gap may partly reflect the advanced age of the study population. Previous research has consistently shown that older adults often demonstrate low self‐awareness of periodontal disease despite its higher clinical prevalence. A large US population–based study found that even among those with moderate to severe periodontitis, 72% were unaware of their condition, with awareness declining as age increased (Luo and Wu 2016). Similarly, a cross‐sectional Italian study reported that older participants were significantly more likely to underreport gingival bleeding and oral malodour (Romano et al. 2020). Age‐related reductions in interoceptive awareness and elevated sensory thresholds likely further reduce symptom perception (Dobrushina et al. 2024; Zhi et al. 2024). Importantly, the difference by dementia status was more pronounced for tooth mobility than for gingival bleeding. Self‐reported tooth mobility showed lower diagnostic accuracy in participants with dementia (AUC 0.61) than in those without dementia (AUC 0.67), whereas the corresponding difference for self‐reported gingival bleeding was minimal (AUC 0.53 vs. 0.54). This asymmetry likely reflects differing cognitive demands: detecting gingival bleeding during toothbrushing requires less cognitive effort than perceiving tooth mobility, which depends on sustained somatosensory integration during mastication and higher order processing. Consequently, cognitive impairment may selectively impair awareness of the latter.

Several additional neuropsychological mechanisms may contribute, including blunted perception of somatic sensations, difficulty in monitoring or recalling intermittent symptoms and impaired communication of symptoms to clinicians or caregivers (Alzola et al. 2024; Warren 2022). These factors can diminish the perception of discomfort and, consequently, reduce the likelihood of noticing and reporting oral symptoms. The additional model performance analyses support this interpretation: self‐reported gingival bleeding offered little or no net benefit beyond an examine‐all strategy, whereas self‐reported tooth mobility showed only modest utility over a restricted threshold range. Taken together, these findings suggest that relying solely on symptom inquiry will lead to substantial under‐detection of periodontal disease among older adults, particularly those with cognitive impairment. Although self‐reported tooth mobility may have some value as a triage aid, neither symptom can replace objective clinical examination in this population.

This study has some limitations. Although the primary analyses used matched reference standards, secondary analyses relied on PPD from the modified CPI and should be regarded as secondary rather than definitive estimates of severity. BOP prevalence (61.2%) should also be interpreted in the context of the partial‐mouth modified CPI protocol and compared cautiously with full‐mouth estimates. Second, the history of systemic diseases, including dementia, was inferred from medication records in the KDB system. As this medication‐based definition encompasses only treated cases, individuals with undetected or mild cognitive impairment may have been misclassified as non‐demented, potentially diminishing observed between‐group differences. Third, the prevalence of dementia identified in this study (~4%) was substantially lower than Japan's estimated prevalence (~20%) (Nakahori et al. 2021), likely reflecting the selection of participants who were able to attend dental checkups. Individuals with severe dementia or those residing in care facilities were not included, potentially underestimating the true impact of dementia on symptom–status discordance. Furthermore, the participation rate for the dental checkup programme was approximately 23%, aligning with previously reported low consultation rates for municipality‐led periodontal screening programmes in Japan (Tamada et al. 2024; Yatabe et al. 2018). As such, participants likely represent a health‐conscious, self‐selected group, which may result in a narrower awareness gap compared with the general older adult population. Fourth, it could not be confirmed from the available data whether clinical examiners were blinded to questionnaire responses. Finally, the cross‐sectional design precludes any causal inferences. Nevertheless, to our knowledge, no previous study has examined the relationship between self‐reported periodontal symptoms and clinically assessed periodontal status in a large sample of older adults. Overall, these findings support prioritising objective clinical examination over symptom‐based screening in older adults, while suggesting that self‐reported tooth mobility may have limited value as a triage signal when direct examination is not available.

Conclusion

This large‐scale population‐based study demonstrated that self‐reported periodontal symptoms have limited diagnostic accuracy compared with clinical assessments of periodontal status among adults aged 75 years and older. A considerable proportion of individuals with clinically diagnosed periodontitis did not recognise corresponding symptoms, with this awareness gap notably greater for tooth mobility in individuals with dementia. These results underscore the importance of objective clinical examination, particularly for older adults experiencing cognitive impairment.
