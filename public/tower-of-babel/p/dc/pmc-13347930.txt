Participatory research in mental health care services in low-income and middle-income countries: a scoping review
Authors: Naveen Krishnan, Poornima Sunder, Chitra Venkateswaran, Denny John
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveThis scoping review aims to identify and map the characteristics of participatory research approaches in mental healthcare services conducted in low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs).DesignThis review followed a scoping review methodology developed by Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI). This review searched various databases and screened studies that met inclusion criteria.Data sourcesPublished and unpublished studies were searched for in MEDLINE (PubMed), Embase (Ovid), PsycINFO (EBSCOhost), CINAHL (EBSCOhost) and Google Scholar (first 10 pages), as well as grey literature databases Shodhganga and ProQuest (Dissertations and Theses).EligibilityThis scoping review considered studies on participatory research in mental healthcare services in LMICs, without limitation to types of participants (ie, gender, children/adolescent/adults, urban/rural etc) and settings.Data extractionScreening of the articles was done using Nested Knowledge software. Two independent reviewers screened the titles and abstracts of the studies, followed by full-text screening. Data were extracted using a predefined form. The findings are descriptively presented with supporting tables and diagrams, accompanied by a narrative summary.ResultsWe included 31 studies with different aims and procedures. Significant proportion of studies were published from India followed by Ethiopia, Zimbabwe. Across the included studies, the most frequently reported participatory research method was workshops, primarily used during design and implementation phases to facilitate co-design, discussion and shared decision-making. In-depth interviews and focus group discussions were the second most common, followed by feedback mechanisms, which were mainly used in the evaluation phase. Stakeholder involvement most often included community members, health professionals and community health workers, with many studies integrating advisory groups to guide research priorities and methods. The most frequently reported outcomes were process outcomes, followed by health, community, empowerment and perspective outcomes.ConclusionsParticipatory research in mental healthcare services in LMICs mostly employs workshops and other interactive methods to engage diverse stakeholders meaningfully. While these approaches enhance collaboration, capacity building and empowerment, gaps remain in early-stage stakeholder involvement, critical reflection on power dynamics and sustainability planning. Strengthening participatory practices through deeper engagement from the research initiation stage, transparent reporting and long-term outcome tracking could improve the impact and sustainability of mental health interventions in LMIC contexts.Trial registrationhttps://osf.io/cn54r.Keywords: Community-Based Participatory Research, Community Participation, MENTAL HEALTH

Introduction

In 2016, over a billion individuals worldwide representing approximately 16% of the global population were affected by mental and addictive disorders; accounting for 7% of the global burden of disease in terms of disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), indicating their significant contribution to worldwide ill health and disability.1 Developing countries face a substantial treatment gap for mental health disorders, with estimates ranging between 76% and 85%. In comparison, the unmet treatment need in developed countries ranged from 35.5% to 50.3%. The reasons for this disparity include limited healthcare resources, a shortage of trained mental health professionals, pervasive societal stigma and insufficient implementation of effective interventions. Addressing this vast treatment gap is a pressing global health priority.2 Another concern is that isolated reliance on large psychiatric institutions without community linkages often results in treatment gaps, poor social outcomes and human rights concerns.3 In recognition of these limitations, many countries and the WHO promote deinstitutionalisation.4 This transition aims to retain the benefits of clinical oversight while embedding care in accessible, supportive community environments. Community-based models improve personal autonomy, decrease stigma and facilitate social integration, employment and vocational activities. They also tend to be more cost-effective for health systems and better at early identification and prevention, reducing reliance on emergency or inpatient care.4

Community involvement holds significant value in mental healthcare services for fostering deeper collaboration, mutual education and shared ownership between communities and health providers.5 It has the potential to play a critical role in bridging care gap.6 However, there remains a limited systematic understanding of mental healthcare needs and available resources from community perspectives. Participatory approaches that engage communities actively as equal partners in designing, governing and evaluating mental healthcare can be a valuable solution for integrating community perspectives, fostering better understanding and creating inclusive mental health strategies.5

Participatory research (PR) is an umbrella term for a ‘school of approaches that share a core philosophy of inclusivity and of recognizing the value of engaging in the research process (rather than including only as subjects of the research) those who are intended to be the beneficiaries, users, and stakeholders of the research.’7 (p.326) Community perspectives, when captured through PR, support the development of sustainable and contextually specific services.5 Different types of PR include community-based participatory research, participatory rural appraisal, empowerment evaluation, participatory action research and community-partnered participatory research, as well as qualitative methods like interviews, focus groups and capacity-building initiatives.8

PR aims to collaborate directly with communities, generating evidence that informs policy and secures funding.9 While historically criticised for lacking rigour due to its association with qualitative methods, PR has gained increasing recognition since the early 2000s for its benefits.9 These include strengthened partnerships, relevant research questions, capacity building and sustainable outcomes.9 Reflecting this shift, funding agencies now advocate for research partnerships that engage community organisations and stakeholders.9

In the past, mental healthcare service users were often excluded from meaningful roles in research and reform efforts.10 However, there is growing advocacy from researchers and funding bodies for consumer involvement in mental healthcare research to drive improvement.10 PR is especially relevant to mental health research, as community engagement ensures cultural appropriateness, relevance and acceptability of findings.1 11 Including traditionally excluded voices fosters empowerment and self-determination, reduces stigma and counters negative stereotypes through a more accurate understanding of mental health issues.12 13

An initial search of MEDLINE (PubMed) in June 2023 identified a realist review protocol on a related topic. This protocol focused on mental health interventions in low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs), aiming to explore the contexts triggering mechanisms of change and outcomes related to participatory mental health interventions.14 However, no other scoping or systematic reviews addressing our specific research question in LMICs were identified. Additional searches in MEDLINE, the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, Open Science Framework, Figshare and Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Evidence Synthesis found no ongoing reviews on this topic.

This scoping review aims to explore PR approaches in LMICs for three primary reasons: (1) to identify the methods used in the design, implementation or evaluation of PR in mental healthcare services, (2) to map the stakeholders involved in PR in mental healthcare services and (3) to identify outcome measures used in PR in mental healthcare services. Scoping reviews serve as a valuable tool to comprehensively explore and analyse a subject, providing insights into the phenomenon under study.15 This review focuses on types of PR and their associated outcome measures in the context of mental healthcare services within LMICs. By conducting this scoping review, the reviewers aim to identify factors that contribute to the provision of inclusive and community-sensitive mental healthcare services.

This scoping review seeks to gain an understanding of various methods used to achieve effective participation of the community in designing, implementing and evaluating a mental health service. The scope of this paper extends to enhancing the current information available regarding outcome measures in the context of LMIC, thereby looking at how these methods can be used meaningfully in engaging communities. This can inform practitioners and researchers on how to incorporate the knowledge gained to develop community driven, inclusive mental healthcare services.

Operational definitions

Mental health

Defined as ‘a state of mental well-being that enables people to cope with the stresses of life, realize their abilities, learn well and work well, and contribute to their community.’20

Mental healthcare services

Defined as ‘any interventions assessment, diagnosis, treatment, or counseling offered in private, public, inpatient, or outpatient settings for the maintenance or enhancement of mental health or the treatment of mental or behavioral disorders in individual and group contexts.’21

Treatment gap

Defined as ‘the proportion of people with disorder who require an intervention but do not receive one.’22(p.2)

Participatory research

Defined as ‘systematic inquiry, with the collaboration of those affected by the issue being studied, for purposes of education and taking action or effecting change.’7(p.327)

Low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs)

For the year 2024, ‘countries with a gross national income (GNI) per capita between $1,136 and $4,465.’23

Inclusion criteria

Participants

This review included a range of stakeholders, including facilitators, users, service providers, community volunteers, leaders and officials. There were no restrictions on population characteristics, such as demographic variables (eg, age, sex, ethnicity), or sampling techniques.

Concept

The review focused on studies employing participatory research (PR) methodologies within mental healthcare services. It considered studies involving participatory methods in the design, implementation or evaluation of such services. There were no limitations regarding the intervention or content of the studies. Outcome domains, adapted from an earlier systematic review, were as follows:

‘Process Outcomes: These include organizational processes, such as the formation, functionality, and accomplishments of community-based groups or coalitions, as well as community processes like participation, outreach, and service uptake.

Community Outcomes: Changes in community knowledge, attitudes, or behaviors concerning a health issue.

Health Outcomes: Improvements in the health status of the target population.

Stakeholder Perspectives: Levels of satisfaction or perceptions regarding community involvement processes or their outcomes.

Empowerment: Instances where communities unite to address self-identified problems, leading to sustainable, context-appropriate solutions and knowledge sharing.’24(p.8)

A relevant example is a study from India titled ‘Exploring community mental health systems: a participatory health needs and assets assessment in the Yamuna Valley, North India’, which used diverse methods such as interviews, focus groups and participatory rural appraisal.25 This study engaged a variety of stakeholders, including doctors, pharmacists, traditional healers, community health workers and local residents, resulting in the community identifying and prioritising their needs.

Context

The review explored PR methodologies used within mental healthcare services in low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs). Articles were included irrespective of the approach (individual, group, or organisational) or delivery format (eg, face-to-face or online). Research settings were not restricted and included non-profits, hospitals and other organisations.

Types of studies

The review encompassed all participatory study designs, including quantitative, qualitative and mixed methods. Experimental and quasi-experimental designs, descriptive research and qualitative approaches such as action research, in-depth interviews, focus groups and ethnographic studies were considered. Analytical methods included thematic, content and phenomenological analyses.

Exclusions applied to studies conducted outside LMICs, research not describing participatory methods or outcome measures and studies unrelated to mental health. Non-academic content, opinion pieces and conference abstracts were also excluded.

Search strategy

The search strategy aimed to capture both published and unpublished studies. Initially, a limited preliminary search was carried out of MEDLINE (PubMed) to locate relevant papers. The titles, abstracts and index terms identified from this search, along with MeSH terms provided in the Yale MeSH Analyzer,26 were used to create an extensive search strategy specifically for MEDLINE (see online supplemental appendix i). We used the Cochrane EPOC LMIC filter to filter studies published in LMICs.27 The search strategy, including all identified keywords and index terms, has been adapted for each database and information source to be searched.

No restrictions were imposed on publication date and sources published in any language were considered for inclusion. All the included articles were written in the English language and therefore required no translation.

The databases were searched from inception until 5 March 2025 and include PubMed (MEDLINE), Ovid Embase, PsycINFO (EBSCOhost), CINAHL and Google Scholar (first 10 pages, 20 results per page). Grey literatures were searched from government sources, websites of institutes and NGOs involved in mental healthcare services. We used ProQuest and Shodhganga to search PhD/Master’s dissertation/theses. Forward and backward citations of included studies were undertaken to identify any additional studies.

Study selection

After searching various databases, the identified studies were consolidated and imported into Nested Knowledge (Nested Knowledge Inc, Minnesota, USA), and duplicates were removed. No authors were contacted, as the team was able to retrieve full-text articles independently.

Once the studies were imported into Nested Knowledge, abstract screening was initiated. Initially, a pilot screening of 20 studies was conducted by two reviewers (NK & PS), and consensus was reached. We followed a two-pass screening system in Nested Knowledge, where the screened abstracts are advanced only if both reviewers have included the study. When disagreement arose during screening, the studies were redirected to the adjudicated screening option, and these issues were further resolved through discussions with the third reviewer (CV). At the next step, the included articles underwent a comprehensive full-text screening. An initial pilot screening of 10 studies was done, and consensus was achieved between two reviewers (NK & PS). Each reviewer screened all the included studies, and whenever there were disagreements during screening, an adjudicated screening option was employed. Discrepancies between the two reviewers were solved through discussion with the third reviewer (CV). Articles that did not meet the inclusion criteria were excluded. Reasons for excluding full-text papers that did not meet the inclusion criteria have been recorded and reported in the review (online supplemental appendix ii). The screening is outlined in the PRISMA-ScR flow diagram (figure 1) and presented in the results section of the scoping review.28

Data extraction

Two reviewers (NK & PS) independently extracted data from the included papers, capturing information as per data extraction tool. The information included authors, title, year of publication, location, objectives, study design, participatory methods used in design, implementation, and evaluation, types of stakeholders and their level of involvement, and reported outcome measures. The data extraction tool was pilot-tested by each reviewer individually, as recommended by the JBI Manual for Evidence Synthesis.29 Any disagreements that arose between the reviewers were resolved throughout data extraction, and an iterative process was followed. The reviewers agreed to remove the section on level of involvement from the final data extraction tool as it had considerable overlap with other sections.

Data presentation and analysis

The collected data and the selection process have been visually represented in figures, and tables, aligning with the review objectives. The tables have been explained using narrative summaries. The results have been categorised according to the following concepts: basic characteristics of the article, participatory approaches used, stakeholders involved and outcome measures.

Patient and public involvement

This study was not clinically focused; therefore, patients were not involved in the study.

Results

The search across all sources identified 21 989 articles. After this, 3668 duplicates were removed and 18 402 articles were included for abstract screening. At this stage, 17 164 articles were further excluded. Following this, a total of 1238 studies were eligible for full-text screening. 27 articles were obtained from websites and 53 were obtained from citation searches. After full text review, 1188 articles did not meet the inclusion criteria. Finally, 31 articles were included for data extraction.2530,59 Among the 31 included studies, three were identified through a citation search and one was found on websites.

The majority of studies have used participatory methods in either the design, implementation or evaluation phase. There were a few studies that followed participatory methods, although not in all phases, and have been included in the review due to their participatory nature and reported outcome measures.2536 39 43 44 48,54 56 Explanation of participatory methods in design, implementation, evaluation and types of stakeholders is given in online supplemental table 3.

Participatory methods in design

Co-design in participatory research refers to an approach where researchers, designers and stakeholders, including end users, community members or people affected by the research topic, actively collaborate throughout the design and development process.60 Among the included studies, 23 studies reported using participatory methods in the design phase of the research.2530,47 55 Among these, workshops were the most used method. All workshops were collaborative and were used in the context of training stakeholders in the development part of the intervention. These encompassed a variety of workshop types, reflecting diverse participatory approaches in intervention design. The most frequently reported were ToC workshops30,34 followed by co-design workshops35 37 38 and expert workshops.39 40 Other workshop formats were stakeholder meetings,41 nominal group technique,42 needs assessment workshops,43 participatory appraisal workshops25 and consultative workshops.44

ToC workshops were used for varied purposes, including building a mental healthcare plan,30 32 designing a mental health gap action plan (mhGAP) for priority disorder,31 developing an anti-stigma campaign33 and systematically developing a road map to detail steps to achieve the goal of effective psychological interventions delivered by village health workers.34

Co-design workshops were used to co-develop interventions. One of the studies focused on gathering their insights and recommendations to modify the prototype of a digital programme for detection and referral of schizophrenia.35 One of these studies focused on increasing mental health literacy and reducing stigma among adolescents in the community emphasised working with adolescents to co-design an intervention.37 Another study that focused on developing scalable intervention for common mental health problems among perinatal adolescents in Malawi highlighted inclusion of stakeholders’ priorities and preferences.38

Of the two expert workshops, one focused on co-production of a visual tool to support recovery for people affected by psycho-social disability39 and the other study focused on co-producing technology-assisted problem management plus (TA-PM+) a digital intervention for lady health workers.40

A variety of specified workshops were used in studies. Nominal group technique was used to identify top 10 concerns of service users and caregivers for prioritising action for strengthening mental health system in southern Ethiopia.42 Needs assessment workshop focused on identifying and prioritising problems and training related to mental health issues for law enforcement officers.43 Participatory appraisal workshop focused on health needs and assets assessment for mental health in Uttarkashi, India.25 Participatory consultation workshops were conducted with family advisers, with the goal of creating mental health promotion events for their communities.44

Other than workshops, most commonly used participatory methods were FGD2530 32 33 35 37 39 44,47 and IDI.25 30 32 33 35 37 40 43 46 IDI and FGD in the participatory design phase are generally done to gather insights and their priority in the research question. In some instances, IDIs and FGDs were also executed along with workshops.25 32 37 43 44 For example, one of the studies in Ghana involved young people, caregivers and community members through interviews and focus groups. Their inputs through the interviews were used to co-design the intervention, ensuring cultural and contextual relevance.45 Insights into the needs, concerns and preferences of community members were gathered through FGD; the information gained through these methods was used for developing a multicomponent positive psychology intervention for rural adults in Ghana.45 Other than above-mentioned participatory methods, stakeholder meeting36 41 general feedbacks45 and situation analysis32 were included.

In-depth interviews and focus group discussions in the evaluation phase mainly were conducted with stakeholders to understand their experiences as well as to understand the insights they gained from the intervention or the participation.

Level of involvement

Participation in research can range from one-way information sharing to fully collaborative decision-making; however, many studies do not explicitly define or quantify the stakeholder involvement. For example, in the development of a district mental healthcare plan (MHCP) in Nepal. Jordans et al51 (2016) employed mixed methods including qualitative interviews, focus group discussions, participatory workshops and routine service monitoring to engage policy makers, health service providers, community members and NGO partners in intervention design and pilot testing. Similarly, In Uganda, Kigozi et al32 (2016) reported a similar process, where national and district health authorities, primary healthcare providers, service users and community leaders contributed to the design and piloting of the MHCP. In Ethiopia, Fekadu et al30 (2016) described the formulation of an MHCP that incorporated perspectives from government health officials, service providers, community leaders and service users, employing workshops, situational analyses and expert consultation. Across these studies, although diverse stakeholders contributed at multiple stages—from needs assessment to implementation and evaluation—the value or specific contributions of each group were not explicitly described, nor was there any comparative assessment of whether one group had greater influence or participation than another. This absence of detail makes it challenging to determine which stakeholders had the most impact. Moreover, the functioning of each stakeholder group is integral to the intervention’s success, and even comparatively lower levels of participation may yield significant influence, meaning that participation cannot be simply categorised as ‘high’ or ‘low’ without risking misinterpretation.

Process outcomes

Process outcomes were achieved in most of the studies because of their participatory nature. Change in functioning while adhering to participatory research principles is invariably associated with process outcomes. From the included studies, 29 studies mentioned process outcomes.2530,39 41 For example, one of the studies has reported that community-based rehabilitation intervention led by lay workers where they were trained in home visits, family intervention and support returning to work and community mobilisation, in turn increased access to mental healthcare and antipsychotic medication adherence.53 Another study has reported that through training and ToC development, there was an increase in knowledge, skills and participation in both research advisory group and research participant group.42 Another study has reported that the photovoice training for participants, especially for people with lived experience, gave them an opportunity to share their experiences with the community, which had a positive effect on their mental health.49

Community outcomes

Community outcomes are referred to as an outcome in the community or a specific target group through the research or intervention. Community outcomes are achieved in a way that participants and stakeholders start seeing differences from the activities and interventions. About 26 of the included studies have reported community outcomes.2530,35 37 40 For example, in the study, development of a peer-delivered youth readiness intervention (YRI) youth community advisory board (YCAB) was involved in developing a context-specific intervention adaptation. Enhanced involvement of lay workers in workshops and training to deliver interventions was reported as a community outcome.55 Another study has reported that participants involved in ToC workshops for developing a mental healthcare plan have led to prioritising and increasing accessibility of services through integrating specialist services at villages.31

Health outcomes

Health outcomes are achieved when a participant group or a community have a positive change in their health status. 24 of the included studies reported health outcomes.2530,35 37 38 40 42 For example, in one of the studies implementing digital intervention systematic medical appraisal, referral and treatment (SMART), training was provided for ASHAs and doctors to implement screening and follow-up interventions, which in turn led to decreased anxiety and stress among community members.56 In another study reported the co-designed intervention for pregnant adolescents feasibility and acceptability of footpaths for adolescent maternal mental health (FOR MAMA), where intervention delivery has improved self-efficacy, interpersonal skills, daily functioning and improved psychological distress as health outcomes.38

Perspectives

Stakeholder perspectives are reported in 21 of the included studies.2530 31 33,35 39 40 42 Stakeholder perspectives are related to satisfaction or views with the processes and outputs of the research. In most of the included studies, researchers were able to gather feedback from stakeholders in all the phases of the research. One of the studies reported that service users and caregivers who were engaged in participatory action research described positive outcomes across multiple levels including personal, social, research and healthcare services.42 In another study, it has been reported that active involvement in the co-design and delivery process of the study enhanced confidence and leadership skills in adolescents; they even acted as peacemakers as they became more oriented to the community around them.55

Empowerment

Empowerment in a community is referred to as a change in self by co-developing and implementing activities, making them feel like they have power or from the knowledge obtained from participating in the research process. 26 of the studies mentioned empowerment as an outcome measure.2530,36 38 40 42 For example, in one study, youth facilitators used their shared experiences to foster trust and create a relatable intervention for peers.32 Another study reported enhanced leadership skills and confidence in youth facilitators and use of shared lived experiences to build rapport with participants.52 One of the included studies reported that engagement of youth and their families in the workshops has empowered them.44

Discussion

This scoping review provides an overview of participatory research methods used in mental healthcare services in LMICs. Our main objectives focused on understanding PR methods used in design, implementation and evaluation phases, types of stakeholders involved and to identify outcome measures of the research. Using the data extraction tool, we were able to gather information and understand how participation is achieved at different phases of the research and its impact on the stakeholders involved in the research, as well as the variations in the operationalisation of approaches within diverse backgrounds of LMICs. 31 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included for data extraction.2530,59

Among the identified PR methods in our scoping review, workshops2530,59 emerged as the prominently used method particularly in the design and implementation phase. In LMICs where socio-economic, cultural and religious factors determine various aspects of help-seeking behaviour and treatment, workshops were noted to enable not only meaningful engagement but also served as a mechanism for capacity building, co-development and implementation.62 Diverse formats of approaches used, including ToC, co-design and expert workshops, highlighted how workshops help in contextualisation of interventions to local needs, particularly where stigma, limited mental health literacy and workforce shortages shape service delivery. Normalising help-seeking in mental health settings in LMICs requires building a trust-based system, which was ensured through workshops that enabled researchers to take on the dual role of investigator as well as facilitator. In the implementation phase, workshops were used in most studies to equip stakeholders through training, highlighting the importance of workshops in task shifting in resource-constrained settings.

IDI and FGD were the second most reported PR methods, they were mostly used in design and evaluation phases.930,51 53 In some of the included studies, they were one of the important methods in involving participants in the research and fostering a sense of being heard among participants.39 49 However, the majority of FGD and IDI were primarily used to gather insights, inform intervention design and evaluate participant experiences, but there was no clarity whether they extended to shared decision making. Feedbacks25 30 33 39 41 44 45 51 52 55 were the third most reported participatory method; it is mostly used in the evaluation phase to gather information regarding experiences of participants, involvement in research activities, effectiveness of interventions and overall feedback.33 While it ensured that the data collected were valid and interventions were context specific, they were mostly retrospective in nature thereby limiting their real time influence on interventions.

Stakeholder involvement was broad in the included studies encompassing community members, health professionals, community health workers, NGOs, people with lived experience and caregivers, community leaders, religious leaders, LSG, police, district administration and others.2530,59 It was noted that community health workers2530,38 40 47 49 53 54 56 59 and health professionals3134 37 40 42 43 45,47 51 53 54 56 58 were more significantly involved, highlighting the importance of these intermediary actors in bridging gaps between formal health systems and communities. Involvement of community members consistently across studies reflects an emphasis in grounding mental health within local contexts. The involvement of stakeholders, especially as part of an advisory group that took on role of co-researcher also was noted to help in achieving participation and robust decision making.39 However, there was overall lack of transparency in articulating the specific roles and decision-making power of different stakeholders which made it difficult to reach conclusions regarding whose perspectives shaped the interventions. There was also a clear lack of critical reflection on power imbalances and boundaries between researchers and participants which questions the decision-making power among the stakeholders and ultimately the ownership of the project. This also raises another concern of system-affiliated actors being potentially accorded more privilege than people with lived experience.

The outcomes reported across studies reflect the layered impact of participatory approaches in LMIC mental health contexts. The majority of the included studies had reported process outcomes in contrast to a systematic review which reported that community outcomes were assessed more in high-income countries.24 In the included studies, process outcomes were reported when there were improvements in systems, group dynamics or service delivery processes.2530,39 41 These outcomes are significant in LMIC where strengthening system capacity is crucial to address treatment gaps. Community outcomes were reported in our review when there were positive shifts in community knowledge, attitudes and behaviour,2530,35 37 40 further demonstrating how participatory approaches can influence community structures and norms. Health outcomes were reported when there were measurable changes in mental health status at individual or group levels. They were also reported in studies that focused on implementing interventions and these were considered a by-product of the process of community engagement.2530,35 37 38 40 42 In addition, empowerment and perspectives emerged as key dimensions of impact. Empowerment outcomes were usually reported when participants experienced increased agency, confidence and ownership of change,2530,36 38 40 42 while perspective outcomes were reported when stakeholders expressed meaningful insights and perspective shift from their involvement.2530 31 33,35 39 40 42 These outcomes are especially relevant in mental health contexts of LMIC where stigma and marginalisation limit agency and social participation. Though consistent participation can empower stakeholders and may contribute to improved help-seeking behaviours, the sustainability of these effects remains unclear in the included studies.63

The strength of the participatory research lies within active meaningful involvement of stakeholders. Overall, the use of varied participatory methods reflects a strong emphasis on collaborative implementation and capacity enhancement in mental health services across LMICs. The findings also indicate that PR holds significant promise for strengthening mental health systems in LMIC, not only by improving intervention design and implementation but also by fostering capacity, empowerment and community engagement. However, when viewed through the lens of Arnstein’s Ladder of Participation,64 PR approaches across studies tended to cluster between consultation and collaboration, with limited evidence of fully shared decision making.65 This variability is reflected across methods, stakeholder involvement and outcomes, suggesting that the transformational potential of PR depends less on the methods themselves and more on how participation is ensured and sustained.

PR studies included in this review demonstrate clear value in enhancing relevance, acceptability and feasibility of mental healthcare services in LMICs, but important gaps remain including limited early involvement in decision making, being bounded within predetermined frameworks thereby limiting its potential to be truly community-led, insufficient attention to power dynamics and lack of longitudinal evaluation of outcomes.

Limitations of the review

The scoping review aided in gathering insights about various participatory methods used and the outcomes assessed in the context of LMIC. Only 31 studies reporting participatory research in mental health with defined outcome measures were identified across low-income and middle-income countries. However, we might have missed methods and strategies that have been used outside the LMIC. Inclusion criteria of having outcome measures were important in our review, this might have inadvertently excluded PR that does not have outcome measures. During the review, it was noted that several studies were published in phases which might have led to the exclusion of formative research as they lacked outcome measures. A huge variability in reporting of PR studies was observed which resulted in limitations in eliciting information regarding the level of involvement of participants and determining the exact outcome measure in the study.

Recommendations for practice and research

Future studies should involve stakeholders from the early stage of research priority setting to participation across all stages of implementation. Prioritising meaningful inclusion of people with lived experience and paying greater attention to power dynamics is also needed, especially in LMICs where structural hierarchies usually shape participation. Research should rigorously evaluate and transparently report stakeholder selection, their roles and influence to assess quality of participation. Strategically plan longitudinal and outcome-oriented evaluations to ensure sustained mental health and community-level impacts. Embedding participatory processes within existing healthcare systems and supporting community health workers and other stakeholders are critical for ensuring sustainability of mental health services in resource-constrained settings of LMIC.

Conclusion

Participatory research in mental health in LMICs shows significant promise for developing culturally appropriate, community-driven solutions. The majority of included studies demonstrate the feasibility and value of engaging diverse stakeholders across the research lifecycle. However, true co-production remains aspirational rather than achieved in most cases. Future research must go beyond participation to shared power in prioritising research questions, more equitable and sustained forms of participation, with greater transparency in levels of community involvement and focus on standardised reporting of long-term mental health and system level impacts to fully realise the transformative potential of participatory research.
