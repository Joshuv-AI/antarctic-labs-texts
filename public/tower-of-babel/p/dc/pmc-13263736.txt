Evaluation of the interference of 49 commonly used clinical drugs on 60 biochemical tests
Authors: Weiting Yu, Xiaochun Shu, Lian Wang, Daoyuan Yue, Wenge Xie, Huijun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to systematically evaluate the interference of 49 commonly used clinical drugs on 60 routine biochemical tests, providing a foundation for clinical laboratories to establish drug-interference alert systems and support more accurate result interpretation.MethodWe tested 49 drugs at three concentrations (baseline, therapeutic C1, supra-therapeutic C2) on 60 biochemical assays following CLSI EP07. For each drug-test combination, a single patient serum/plasma pool was split into three aliquots: baseline, C1-spiked, and C2-spiked. All samples were analyzed using a Mindray BS-2800M fully automated biochemical analyzer. Results at C1 and C2 were compared with baseline (zero concentration) to characterize drug-induced interference with each biochemical test.ResultsAt concentration C1, 23 drugs exhibited no interference across all 60 biochemical tests. Among the 2940 drug-test combinations evaluated, C1 generated 27 cases (0.9%) of strong interference, mainly within liver function assays, and 40 cases (1.4%) of weak interference. The majority (97.7%) exhibited no detectable effect. At concentration C2, a total of 2820 drug-test combinations were included in the analysis. Strong interference was detected in 103 cases (3.7%), primarily affecting liver and lipid profiles, alongside 117 cases (4.1%) of weak interference. Nonetheless, 92.2% of combinations still showed no interference.ConclusionThis study provides a comprehensive profile of drug-induced interference across 60 routinely performed biochemical tests. These findings could support the accurate interpretation and interference identification of laboratory results.

Introduction

Biochemical laboratory tests support approximately 60-70% of clinical decision-making across all levels of healthcare, playing a crucial role in disease diagnosis, risk prediction and stratification, therapeutic monitoring, and prognostic assessment [1,2]. However, the accuracy and reliability of these tests are significantly affected by pre-analytical variables. Common preanalytical factors such as hemolysis, icterus, lipemia, clotting, or insufficient sample volume are typically identified through stringent sample handling procedures, visual inspection, or serum index testing [3], enabling laboratory professionals to take appropriate corrective actions. Conversely, drug-laboratory test interactions (DLTIs) represent a more covert source of error. Medications taken within the 10 days preceding sample collection may interfere with biochemical measurements, and this may go undetected, particularly when the interference does not visibly alter sample color or turbidity [4]. Although visual changes may occasionally occur, for example, Rose Bengal (PV-10) may cause plasma to appear cherry red, affecting liver function tests based on colorimetric assays [5], or produce a green coloration in women taking oral contraceptives [6], most interferences remain visually undetectable. Consequently, such interference often goes unnoticed before analysis and may compromise the accuracy of the results.

DLTIs constitute one of several drug-induced factors affecting biochemical measurements in relevant patients, with reported prevalence reaching up to 43% of patients whose laboratory results are influenced by drugs [4]. Unrecognized interference can lead to erroneous results with potentially serious clinical consequences, such as misdiagnosis, delayed diagnosis, unnecessary follow-up testing, and/or increased healthcare costs [4,7,8]. DLTIs can arise from two distinct mechanisms: in vivo biological effects that alter actual analyte concentrations, and in vitro analytical interferences that affect the measurement process itself [9]. For example, proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) increase chromogranin A (CgA) levels through in vivo stimulation of enterochromaffin-like cell secretion, leading to falsely elevated results and potentially unnecessary repeat testing, as reported in a previous study [10]. This represents a biological effect rather than an analytical interference. In contrast, a metabolite of sulfasalazine has been reported to interfere with high-performance liquid chromatography with electrochemical detection measurements of urinary normetanephrine [11,12], raising the risk of misdiagnosing pheochromocytoma. The present study focuses specifically on the latter type—in vitro analytical interferences—where drugs directly impact the biochemical reactions used for measurement without altering the true analyte concentration in the sample.

The Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) developed the EP07 guideline. It standardizes the interference addition method and provides methodological support for interference research in laboratory medicine. Although increasing attention has been given to drug interference in recent years, documenting the effects of various classes of medications such as non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs [13,14], antibiotics [15], and anticoagulants [16], most studies remain narrow in scope. Existing research often focuses on a single drug (e.g., metamizole interfering with Roche creatinine assays [13,14]; baicalein interfering with Beckman uric acid assays [17]; calcium dobesilate interfering with creatinine assays from Roche, Beckman, Siemens, and other manufacturers [18], or with glycated albumin assays from Asahi Kasei [19]) or targets a few specific assays (e.g., cardiovascular drugs on cardiac marker assay [16]). Although many assay vendors conduct internal drug interference testing during assay development, these data are often proprietary and not published in the peer-reviewed literature. Consequently, large-scale, systematic investigations covering a broad range of drugs and biochemical assays remain scarce in the public domain. This has resulted in gaps in the accessible evidence base regarding interactions among drugs, analytes, and detection methods, with limited publicly available data concerning clinical implications.

In response, the present study conducted a comprehensive, large-scale experimental evaluation of drug interference in routine biochemical testing using the Mindray analytical platform. By assessing the impact of 49 frequently used drugs on 60 high-volume biochemical tests, this study aimed to generate a robust drug interference dataset specific to this platform. While interference effects may vary across different instruments and reagent formulations due to differences in assay design, the experimental approach—including drug concentration selection and systematic evaluation protocol—provides a methodological framework that can be adapted by other laboratories using different platforms. Ultimately, this evidence could support identification of drug interference with laboratory tests. Additionally, it may serve as foundational data for the future development of electronic clinical decision support systems (CDSS) for real-time DLTIs monitoring, should such systems become technically and operationally feasible in clinical settings.

Samples and methods

Drug selection and preparation

Drug selection in this study was based on multiple criteria, including frequency of clinical use, national Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) classification sales data, previously reported interference in the literature, proximity of the absorption spectra to biochemical assay wavelengths, and overall clinical relevance. A total of 49 drugs were selected for analysis (Table 1), including 12 anti-infective agents, 13 cardiovascular agents, 8 antipyretic, analgesic, and anti-inflammatory agents, 1 endocrine and metabolic agents, 3 respiratory agents, 3 central nervous system agents, 4 hematological system agents, 1 nutritional agents, 2 imaging and diagnostic agents, 1 immunosuppressant, and 1 digestive system medication. Stock drug solutions were prepared at approximately 20 times the toxic concentration to minimize matrix effects. Water-insoluble compounds were dissolved using dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), ethanol, or sodium hydroxide, depending on their physicochemical properties. All solutions were prepared under sterile conditions to minimize the potential confounding bias. In our experiments, all test samples (containing the drug) and control samples were prepared with identical final volumes by adding the same volume of drug solvent (e.g., DMSO, saline) to the base pool. Biochemical test selection

Biochemical analytes were selected according to their test-order frequency in clinical laboratories, inclusion in clinical practice guidelines, and relevance to common clinical scenarios. A total of 60 routine biochemical tests were evaluated (Table 2). These included liver function tests, renal function tests, lipid profile tests, glucose metabolism tests, and electrolyte and mineral tests. Creatine Kinase-MB isoenzyme (CK-MB) activity (U/L) was included in this interference evaluation because it remains in clinical use in various settings, despite no longer being recommended as a primary diagnostic marker for myocardial infarction [29]. For three analytes—glucose, total bilirubin, and direct bilirubin—two different methodological principles were evaluated to reflect their concurrent clinical use and to enable comparison of interference susceptibility across assay formats. Glucose was measured using both hexokinase and glucose oxidase-peroxidase methods; total and direct bilirubin were measured using both diazotized sulfanilic acid and vanadate oxidation methods. These method pairs represent fundamentally different detection principles and are known to potentially differ in their susceptibility to various interferences. Samples

Serum or plasma samples were collected in July 2024 from healthy adult volunteers undergoing routine physical examinations at Wuhan Tongji Hospital, who had not taken any medication in the two weeks prior to sample collection, as medications taken within this timeframe are more likely to interfere with laboratory test results. Pooled samples were prepared to serve as the base pool for further testing.

This study was approved by the institutional review boards of Wuhan Tongji Hospital (approval number: 2024260), which waived the requirement for informed consent due to the retrospective nature of the investigation.

Methods

For each biochemical test, a single base sample pool was prepared by mixing individual patient samples to achieve an analyte concentration within the normal reference interval or near clinically relevant decision cutoffs (specific concentrations provided in Table 2). The interference-addition procedure utilized in this study followed the CLSI EP07-A2 guideline. To simulate real-world clinical drug exposure in a clinical environment, drug solutions were added to the base pool to prepare three types of test pools: zero concentration serving as a drug-free control, concentration C1 of approximately a therapeutic dose, and concentration C2 of a toxic/supratherapeutic dose, which may cause adverse effects. To control for volume effects, drug-free control samples were prepared by adding an equal volume of solvent without the drug, matching the volume used in drug-spiked test samples.

Each test pool was analyzed using the Mindray BS-2800M fully automated biochemical analyzer (Mindray, China) with corresponding original manufacturer reagents, primarily relying on the principle of photoelectric colorimetric detection for testing. These colorimetric assays rely on various reaction mechanisms, which can be categorized into five major types relevant to this study: NAD+/NADH (Nicotinamide Adenine Dinucleotide) and NADP+/NADPH (Nicotinamide Adenine Dinucleotide Phosphate) systems, p-Nitrophenol (p-NP)/p-Nitroaniline (p-NA) systems, hydrogen peroxide (H2O2)-coupled indicator systems (Trinder reaction), antigen-antibody reaction-based indicator systems, and other colorimetric reaction systems. The concentrations of analytes such as glucose, cholesterol, and uric acid were determined spectrophotometrically using Trinder reaction-based enzymatic assays. Briefly, the analyte is oxidized by its specific oxidase to generate H2O2, which then reacts with 4-aminoantipyrine and a phenolic chromogen in the presence of peroxidase to form a stable quinoneimine dye. These H2O2-coupled indicator systems, first established by Trinder, are widely employed for measuring some clinically important analytes [30,31]. It should be noted that while the Mindray BS-2800M analyzer is capable of ion-selective electrode (ISE) measurements, this study focused solely on the colorimetric methods; ISE assays were not included within the scope of this investigation.

All assays were performed in strict accordance with reagent instructions, and each test was conducted in at least triplicate. Prior to the interference study, the within-run imprecision for all 60 biochemical tests was evaluated and found to be consistently below 3% (coefficient of variation, CV%), with the majority of assays exhibiting imprecision between 0.5% and 2.5%. According to CLSI EP07 guidelines, when the interference threshold (set at 10% in this study) substantially exceeds assay imprecision (<3%), triplicate measurements provide adequate statistical power to detect clinically significant interference. Each biochemical test has also undergone external quality assurance (proficiency testing) and is subject to daily internal quality control through Bio-Rad Liquichek™ controls, ensuring analytical accuracy and acceptable precision within the laboratory.

Using the zero-concentration pool as the baseline, both absolute and relative deviations in test results caused by concentrations C1 and C2 were calculated for each analyte. For each analyte studied, the upper limit of the normal reference interval served as the cutoff value to switch between absolute or percentage deviations compared to drug-free controls; when the analyte concentration was less than the cutoff, absolute deviation was used and when it was greater than or equal to the cutoff, relative deviation was used.

For relative deviations, interference was graded as follows: No interference if |Deviation| ≤ 10%; Weak interference if 10% < |Deviation| ≤ 30%; Strong interference if |Deviation| > 30%. The 10% threshold was chosen based on its common use in the literature as a clinically significant cutoff for drug interference studies. The 30% threshold was selected because it exceeds the maximum allowable total error specified for all biochemical analytes in the Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments 2025 criteria, ensuring that deviations classified as “strong” are likely to have clear clinical impact.

For absolute deviations (applicable when analyte concentration < cutoff), the same proportional grading was applied by multiplying the cutoff value by the relative thresholds. Thus, for a given analyte, absolute interference thresholds were: No interference if |Absolute deviation| ≤ (cutoff × 10%); Weak interference if (cutoff × 10%) < |Absolute deviation| ≤ (cutoff × 30%); Strong interference if |Absolute deviation| > (cutoff × 30%). This approach maintained consistency between absolute and relative deviation grading systems.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses and data visualizations were performed using Python 3.10.4. For each drug-biochemical test combination, relative deviation and absolute deviation were calculated to quantify the extent of interference compared to no-drug controls. A heat map of interference was generated, with the horizontal axis representing the biochemical tests, the vertical axis representing the 49 selected drugs, and the color gradient reflecting the degree of interference. Additionally, we calculated the absolute count of interference events exceeding the 10% threshold, as well as their relative proportion within each predefined category. To further elucidate the hierarchical patterns of drug interference, we constructed drug interference visualization diagrams (Sankey diagrams) using Python 3.10.4 to visualize the flow of these events. In these diagrams, nodes were stratified by three key dimensions: (1) drug classification, (2) interference intensity (weak vs. strong), and (3) the reaction principle of the affected biochemical assay. The size of each node was scaled proportionally to the total number of drugs or interference events within that category, while the width of the connecting links directly quantified the number of interference incidents transitioning between categorical levels.

Discussion

This study provides a systematic evaluation of the interference effects of 49 commonly used clinical drugs on 60 biochemical tests.

The results reveal that certain compounds, such as calcium dobesilate, etamsylate, and levodopa, exhibit strong reducing properties and cause significant negative interference in Trinder reactions for measuring creatinine, uric acid, cholesterol, or triglycerides. These results are consistent with previously reported results [18,32,33]. Strongly reducing drugs (e.g., calcium dobesilate, etamsylate, levodopa) contain phenolic hydroxyl groups that can directly consume the H2O2 generated in the first step of the Trinder reaction (see Methods), thereby reducing the amount of H2O2 available for the chromogenic reaction and leading to falsely decreased results [34,35]. Several analytical strategies have been developed to mitigate reducing drug interference, but each has limitations. For example, adding ascorbic acid oxidase to reagent formulations can eliminate interference from ascorbic acid by converting it to dehydroascorbic acid before the indicator reaction [36]; however, ascorbic acid oxidase does not affect other reducing drugs such as those tested in our study. Li et al. [17] reported that the stable radical Tempol (4-hydroxy- 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine-N-oxyl) could resist baicalein interference in uric acid measurement, but Tempol itself may interfere with peroxidase activity and cause spectral interference. Therefore, more robust and broadly applicable strategies are needed.

However, some discrepancies with earlier studies were also observed. For instance, Sonntag et al. [37] reported that doxycycline interfered with a serum complement C4 (C4) assay using a Roche turbidimetric inhibition immunoassay, describing this as a novel finding. However, we did not observe significant interference with C4 under identical drug concentrations using our Mindray turbidimetric system. This discrepancy likely reflects differences in assay design, including antibody specificity, reagent composition, and instrument settings, rather than drug concentration. These findings underscore that interference effects may be platform-dependent and require verification on the specific analytical system in use.

Moreover, a pronounced dose-dependent pattern of interference was identified. For example, verapamil at therapeutic concentrations (160 mg/L) exhibited only weak interference with urea, whereas at toxic concentrations (1600 mg/L), it interfered with 30 biochemical tests. Similarly, captopril at therapeutic levels (50 mg/L) showed strong interference only with ACE, yet at toxic concentrations (500 mg/L), it interfered with 15 biochemical assays. Captopril harbors a free thiol group (-SH) in its molecular structure, which confers potent reducing activity. Consequently, this drug can interfere with a wide range of biochemical assays based on the Trinder reaction, including measurements of uric acid, glucose, and triglycerides. These findings demonstrate a clear dose-dependent relationship for certain drugs, where interference is minimal or absent at therapeutic concentrations but becomes detectable at toxic levels. This pattern has important clinical implications: when patients present with suspected drug overdose, accumulation (e.g., due to renal or hepatic impairment), or supratherapeutic dosing, the potential for broad, multi-analyte interference should be considered. In such situations, clinicians and laboratories must exercise heightened vigilance in interpreting biochemical results, as multiple tests may be affected simultaneously, increasing the risk of misdiagnosis or inappropriate clinical decisions. In cases where drug interference is known or suspected, clinical laboratories should consider adopting more specific testing methods, modifying experimental protocols, or implementing automated warning systems to mitigate these errors.

The clinical significance of the observed interferences extends beyond statistical thresholds to potential impacts on patient care. For example, calcium dobesilate—a vasoprotective agent commonly prescribed for diabetic retinopathy—exhibited strong negative interference with creatinine measurement using sarcosine oxidase method in our study. This interference would likely result in falsely low creatinine values and consequent overestimation of glomerular filtration rate, potentially masking the true extent of diabetic nephropathy and delaying necessary interventions. Given that diabetic patients frequently undergo regular renal function monitoring and are also the primary recipients of calcium dobesilate, the overlap between the affected population and the drug's target population is substantial [18]. Similarly, the interference of levodopa with Trinder-based cholesterol assays—relevant for Parkinson's disease patients who often have cardiovascular comorbidities—could lead to mismanagement of lipid-lowering therapy. These examples underscore that drug interference is not merely an analytical nuisance but a patient safety concern when affected assays are commonly ordered for patients taking the interfering drugs. Awareness of such interactions among clinicians and laboratorians is essential to prevent release of incorrect results and ensure appropriate interpretation.

Over the past 5-10 years, rapid expansion of the pharmaceutical market and increasing medication use have posed significant challenges to clinical laboratory testing, particularly biochemical assays based on spectrophotometric detection. As early as the 1970s, Munzenberger [38] reported a quantifiable relationship between the number of concurrently used medications and the proportion of laboratory tests showing interference: approximately 7% with one drug, 16.7% with two drugs, 66.7% with three or four drugs, and 100% with five drugs. Although modern assay designs have incorporated protections against many interferences, this historical observation remains a striking illustration of how polypharmacy increases the risk of clinically relevant interference, as later confirmed by more recent reviews [4]. Similarly, Geerts et al. [39] conducted a comparative analysis of drug laboratory effect for 200 medications and identified 83 interference events associated with 23 drugs (11.5%), primarily among contraceptives and antibiotics. These effects predominantly impacted adrenal, liver, kidney function tests and urine tests.

The effects of drugs on laboratory test results can generally be divided into two categories: (1) in vivo biological effects, where drugs alter the actual physiological concentration of an analyte (e.g., PPIs increasing CgA secretion), and (2) in vitro analytical interferences, where drugs interfere with the measurement process itself without changing the true analyte concentration [9]. The present study addresses only the latter, but drugs may also affect laboratory results through in vivo mechanisms not captured by this in vitro interference evaluation. In vitro analytical interference can occur through several mechanisms. In some cases, drugs possess a chemical structure similar to the analyte, thereby interfering with its quantification. Others exhibit colorimetric properties similar to the target analyte, or may be inherently colored, turbid, or prone to forming precipitates, all of which can affect spectrophotometric measurements. In addition, many drugs or their metabolites possess redox activity that leads to significant interference in colorimetric assays; certain compounds may also interact with enzymes or proteins by blocking or modifying active sites and altering assay results.

With the significant advancement of testing technologies, manufacturers are actively working to mitigate drug interference during assay development. Several specific optimization strategies have been implemented. First, interference-eliminating agents have been incorporated into reagent formulations. For example, ascorbate oxidase is routinely added to Trinder reaction-based assays to eliminate ascorbic acid interference by converting it to dehydroascorbic acid prior to the indicator reaction [35]. Second, the optimization of reaction systems includes modifications to measurement protocols. For example, the adoption of kinetic (rate) measurement instead of the endpoint method in cholesterol enzymatic assays can reduce the impact of interfering substances [40]. Third, adoption of novel detection technologies has introduced fundamentally different approaches to interference mitigation. Bernstone et al. [41] established a simplified, rapid LC-MS/MS assay for serum and salivary creatinine quantification, demonstrating favorable anti-interference characteristics.

However, these improvements require lengthy development cycles, and the vast diversity of drugs and interference mechanisms make universal solutions difficult to achieve. While real-time DLTIs monitoring via electronic CDSS represents a promising long-term goal [[42], [43], [44]], its widespread implementation currently faces substantial practical hurdles, including limited electronic health record integration, lack of standardized interference databases, and the need for platform-specific validation. At present, more immediate strategies such as manual alert systems [45] or laboratory-developed interference lists may be more feasible for many institutions. As health information systems continue to evolve, the foundational data provided by studies such as this one may eventually enable automated CDSS alerts, but this remains an aspirational rather than currently achievable application. Furthermore, emerging technologies such as machine learning and artificial intelligence can be utilized to identify potential drug interferences and even facilitate the automatic optimization of test results, offering practical guidance and intervention strategies for both clinicians and laboratory personnel. The findings of this study contribute to filling existing data gaps by establishing a systematic, platform-specific drug interference dataset (available in full as Supplementary Table S1), which provides a foundation for hypothesis generation, comparison with other platforms, and integration into CDSS.

Nonetheless, this study has several limitations. First, only a single analyte concentration was tested, whereas CLSI recommends multiple concentrations to assess concentration-dependent interference. Future studies should include low and high analyte concentrations. Second, only parent drugs were tested in vitro, without considering in vivo metabolism. For drugs with short half-lives (e.g., cefoxitin, ampicillin-Na), rapid clearance may overestimate clinical interference risk; for long-half-life drugs (e.g., digoxin, phenylbutazone, allopurinol), the findings may be more relevant. Furthermore, metabolites, which were not evaluated here, could also cause interference. Third, polypharmacy scenarios were not simulated; clinical practice often involves multiple drugs that may produce synergistic interferences. Fourth, we did not systematically investigate the detailed mechanisms underlying each observed interference (e.g., reagent composition, reaction pH, or instrument-specific parameters). While we have provided the complete dataset and detailed assay characteristics to facilitate future mechanistic research, the precise reasons for susceptibility to specific drugs remain to be elucidated. Given these constraints, our findings should be regarded as preliminary reference data for clinical laboratory practice.

Conclusions

This study conducted a systematic evaluation of the interference effects of 49 commonly used clinical drugs on 60 biochemical tests. A comprehensive drug-biochemical test interference heatmap was generated, providing a visual overview of the distribution and magnitude of drug-induced interference across the tested conditions. The findings provide valuable data for the development of laboratory early warning systems and offer practical guidance for clinicians in interpreting biochemical results within the context of patients’ medication use. Nevertheless, follow-up in vivo studies are warranted to validate these interference effects under physiological conditions.
