Prediction of spinal cord injury after thoracoabdominal aortic repair by machine learning method
Authors: Jian Song, Kai Xu, Jingyu Wang, Tianxia Gu, Wenyuan Lu, Yumeng Ji, Yunpeng Ling, Juntao Qiu, Cuntao Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSpinal cord injury (SCI) is a severe complication after thoracoabdominal aortic repair (TAAR), substantially increasing postoperative mortality and disability. This study aimed to develop an improved machine learning model using comprehensive perioperative data to predict SCI risk.Methods270 patients were included in the study, and 66 potentially meaningful variables were selected from 84 original variables as input data to drive machine learning. Background

The optimal treatment of thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysm (TAAA) and dissecting aortic aneurysm remains a formidable challenge. Since its introduction in the 1950s, thoracoabdominal aortic repair (TAAR) has become one of the most effective surgical options for these complex pathologies [1]. However, extensive vascular replacement inevitably carries a high risk of early postoperative complications, which contribute to increased risk of death and disability [2, 3]. In recent years, the rapid development of endovascular and hybrid techniques has expanded treatment options and reduced perioperative mortality, yet the incidence of serious complications such as spinal cord injury (SCI) remains substantial [4].

In traditional open TAAR, SCI typically presents as paraplegia or paraparesis in the early postoperative period, with reported incidence ranging from 3% to 5% [3, 5, 6]. Clinicians employ strategies such as hypothermia, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) drainage, optimization of anastomosis, shortened operative time, and improved perfusion to mitigate the risk. Previous studies using conventional statistical approaches have examined both risk-associated factors for SCI, such as extent of repair and acute presentation, and protective strategies intended to reduce SCI risk, including cerebrospinal fluid drainage, systemic hypothermia, and epidural cooling [7, 8]. However, most of these studies focused on isolated variables rather than developing an integrated predictive tool.

Although numerous perioperative and anatomical factors influencing SCI have been reported, no comprehensive system currently exists to combine these variables for individualized risk assessment. Machine learning, increasingly applied in medical research, provides robust tools for analyzing complex datasets and predicting outcomes [9, 10]. Considering the complex and often nonlinear interactions among these factors, we applied an ensemble machine learning approach—Particle Swarm Optimization–enhanced Focal Loss XGBoost (PSO-FLXGBoost)—to construct an accurate and interpretable model for SCI prediction after TAAR, addressing the limitations of traditional statistical models.

Methods

Ethical statement

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fuwai Hospital (No. 2022 − 1745) on April 7, 2022. Given the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee of Fuwai Hospital. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (as revised in Edinburgh, 2000).

Study population and outcome definition

All consecutive patients who underwent TAAR in Fuwai Hospital (Beijing, China) from January 2015 to December 2023 were included in this study. All patients with TAAA or dissecting aneurysm conforming to Crawford classification (extent I ~ IV) and underwent surgical treatment were included in the study. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Patients with lesions that did not reach any extents of Crawford classification, especially localized lesion in the descending arch or the infrarenal abdominal aorta; (2) those who did not complete the operation as planned or died before operation; (3) those with preoperative signs of spinal cord injury; (4) those who were preoperatively intubated. A total of 270 TAAR patients were included into the final analysis.

SCI was defined as new-onset paraplegia or paraparesis before discharge and further categorized into: immediate (present at awakening) or delayed (appearing postoperatively), transient (resolved before discharge) or persistent (present at discharge), and paraplegia (complete motor loss) or paraparesis (partial motor deficit). For statistical analysis, all types of SCI were grouped together as the outcome event, but detailed subtypes were recorded and are presented in the supplementary material (Additional file 1). For laboratory variables with multiple preoperative measurements, the value obtained at the last preoperative assessment was used for analysis, including D-dimer, platelet count, and other routinely collected laboratory parameters. Postoperative blood test results were averaged from measurements within 48 h after surgery (usually every 8–12 h). The false lumen index was defined as the false lumen diameter divided by the total aortic diameter at the level of maximum aortic dilation. False lumen complete thrombosis (FLCT) was defined as ≥ 90% thrombus volume in the false lumen on computed tomographic angiography (CTA).

Operative technique

All patients underwent general anesthesia and were placed in a lateral twist position for a left thoracoabdominal incision. Repair was performed under a normothermic iliac perfusion strategy, with nasal temperature maintained at > 35 °C and warming blankets used to preserve normothermia. Cerebrospinal fluid drainage was routinely performed in patients with Crawford extent I, II, and III aneurysms. After systemic heparinization (200 U/kg), a 4-branched graft was used for thoracoabdominal aortic reconstruction. Distal perfusion was established via the left common iliac artery using a graft branch and cannulation system connected to a conventional heart-lung machine reservoir and pump, which also allowed reinfusion of blood aspirated from the surgical field. Blood products were supplemented when necessary to maintain lower-extremity systolic pressure at ≥ 60 mmHg. All affected intercostal and lumbar arteries were reconstructed to preserve spinal cord perfusion. All procedures were performed by four chief surgeons, each with more than 15 years of experience in our center. Further technical details have been reported previously [11].

Variable selection

Candidate clinical variables were screened using a two-stage process. First, 84 original variables, including baseline characteristics, operative details, and early postoperative outcomes, were collected from the institutional database. Preliminary screening was then performed based on clinical judgment, prior literature, and Pearson’s correlation analysis, while multicollinearity among variables was also evaluated. Through this process, 66 potentially meaningful variables were retained as candidate predictors (Table 1). Second, eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) was used to further rank the retained variables according to feature importance, and the top 10 variables were selected for final model construction and interpretation.

Handling of missing data

Several imputation strategies were tested, including mean substitution, zero imputation, and random forest (RF) imputation [12, 13]. For each imputed dataset, prediction models were trained and evaluated. RF imputation achieved the best performance in the validation cohort and was therefore adopted for subsequent analyses.

Model development and evaluation

The dataset was randomly divided into a training cohort (70%) and a validation cohort (30%) to develop and evaluate model performance. Stratified randomization was applied to ensure comparable distributions of key baseline variables between the two cohorts.

Multiple supervised machine learning algorithms were explored, including logistic regression, support vector machine (SVM), RF, and XGBoost. After comparative evaluation, XGBoost was chosen as the core framework. To enhance robustness, Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) [14] was used for global hyperparameter tuning, while Focal Loss (FL) [15] was incorporated to address class imbalance by adaptively up-weighting minority and misclassified samples. The optimized model (PSO-FLXGBoost) was then finalized for prediction. The entire process was illustrated in Fig. 1, while the detailed modeling and derivation procedures were provided in the supplementary material (Additional file 2).

Flowchart of the research. The calculation process and evaluation results of the improved machine learning algorithm are presented. PSO, Particle Swarm Optimization; FL, Focal Loss. AUC, the areas under receiver operating characteristic curve

Model interpretability was ensured using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) [16], which quantified the contribution of each variable to the predicted outcome. Discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) in the validation cohort.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses and data visualization were performed using SPSS Statistics version 24.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables following normal or near-normal distribution were reported as mean ± standard deviation, whereas skewed variables were summarized as median [interquartile range].

Results

Baseline characteristics

Between January 2015 and December 2023, a total of 270 patients underwent thoracoabdominal aortic repair at Fuwai Hospital and were included in the final analysis. Patients were randomly assigned into a training cohort (70%) and a test cohort (30%), with no significant differences observed between the two groups across all baseline variables (all P > 0.05). The overall mean age was 41.4 ± 11.6 years, and 73.1% were male. Hypertension was present in 50.4% of patients, and 28.9% had Marfan syndrome. Postoperatively, 22 patients (8.15%) developed spinal cord injury, manifesting as paraplegia or paraparesis of varying severity. These cases were classified as outcome events, with detailed subtype distributions provided in supplementary material (Additional file 1). Comprehensive demographic and perioperative characteristics of the cohort are summarized in Table 1.

Using XGBoost for preliminary feature evaluation, the top 10 most important variables were identified, and their pairwise correlations are illustrated in Fig. 2. This analysis confirmed the relative independence of these predictors and provided the foundation for subsequent model development.

Model performance

The predictive ability of different machine learning methods is summarized in Fig. 3. Baseline XGBoost achieved an AUC of 0.795, which was higher than that of logistic regression (0.758), support vector machine (0.715), and random forest (0.793) (Fig. 3A). After integrating PSO for hyperparameter tuning and FL for class imbalance adjustment, the optimized PSO-FLXGBoost model achieved the highest discrimination, with an AUC of 0.895 in the validation cohort (Fig. 3B).

Model interpretation

The variable importance matrix of the PSO-FLXGBoost model is shown in Fig. 4A, identifying intraoperative hemoglobin (Intra-HB), D-dimer, platelet count (PLT), operation time, creatinine, albumin, left ventricular end-diastolic dimension (LVEDD), direct bilirubin, false lumen index, and age as the top 10 predictors. SHAP summary plots demonstrated that lower Intra-HB, higher D-dimer, elevated PLT, and longer operation time were strongly associated with an increased probability of SCI (Fig. 4B).

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study involving 270 patients undergoing TAAR, we developed and validated an improved machine learning model—PSO-FLXGBoost—to predict the risk of SCI. The model outperformed traditional methods, achieving an AUC of 0.895, and identified four major perioperative predictors of SCI: Intra-HB, preoperative D-dimer level, PLT, and operation time. These findings not only improve risk stratification but also provide insight into the potential pathophysiological processes contributing to SCI.

In our cohort, the incidence of postoperative SCI was 8.15%, which appears higher than that reported in most contemporary literature, where rates generally range from 3% to 5% following open TAAR [17–19]. This discrepancy may be attributed to several factors. First, as a national referral center, our institution manages more complex and extensive aortic cases, including patients with connective tissue disorders or acute dissections, which inherently carry a higher risk of SCI. Second, our study spanned a 10-year period during which perioperative neuroprotective strategies evolved, and earlier cases may not have fully benefited from current protocols. Third, we adopted a broad definition of SCI, including both immediate and delayed-onset neurological deficits prior to discharge, which may have increased the reported incidence compared to studies using more restrictive criteria [17]. Notably, although the SCI event rate in our cohort was relatively higher than that reported in some previous studies, the absolute number of events remained limited. While this may have modestly improved the feasibility of model training, the risk of overfitting cannot be excluded.

The average age of patients in our cohort was 41.4 years (range: 16–65), which is substantially younger than the typical age reported in international series of thoracoabdominal aortic repair, often averaging around 67 years [6, 20]. This age difference may largely be explained by the underlying pathology. In our study, aortic dissection accounted for the majority of cases (82.6%), consistent with epidemiological trends in China where type A and type B aortic dissections tend to present at a younger age, often due to poorly controlled hypertension. Sun et al. [21] similarly reported a mean age of 45 years in patients with type A dissection in a large Chinese cohort. More specifically, among patients with dissection, chronic dissection accounted for the largest proportion (65.02%), followed by subacute (28.25%) and acute dissection (8.52%), while Crawford extent II was the most common anatomical extent in the overall cohort (62.59%) (Additional file 1). These characteristics likely reflect the complexity of the referral population treated at our center and may partly explain the relatively high SCI incidence observed in this series.

SCI following TAAR is multifactorial, involving hemodynamic insufficiency, coagulative abnormalities, and disruption of collateral blood supply [22, 23]. Our findings align with and extend prior literature. In particular, lower Intra-HB levels (< 70 g/L) were significantly associated with SCI. This observation supports the mechanistic hypothesis that anemia may reduce the oxygen delivery capacity of spinal cord microcirculation during vulnerable periods, especially when perfusion pressures are low. Several studies have emphasized that hemoglobin concentration is a critical determinant of tissue oxygenation and may act as a trigger for ischemia under stress conditions [24], and in aortic interventions, hemoglobin level has been identified as a modifiable risk factor for spinal cord injury in endovascular repair settings [25]. For spinal cord blood perfusion, the true lower limit of postoperative hemoglobin was still controversial, with studies reporting that ICU physicians recommend keeping it above 80 g/L, while surgeons recommend a higher standard [26]. Maintaining an adequate hemoglobin level intra- and postoperatively thus becomes a plausible protective strategy against SCI.

Elevated D-dimer and PLT levels likely reflect a hypercoagulable state and increased thrombotic activity, which may predispose to spinal microembolism during or after aortic procedures. In fact, a recent study by Pei et al. [27] in patients undergoing repair for acute type A aortic dissection found that higher preoperative D-dimer and altered platelet counts were independent predictors of postoperative SCI. Microthrombi formed in the false lumen or circulating in the arterial system can occlude spinal microvessels, leading to focal ischemia and eventual spinal cord injury. Furthermore, prolonged operation time (> 500 min) remains a strong predictor: extended surgery is associated with increased blood loss, longer ischemia duration, systemic inflammation, and greater disruption of microcirculation—all factors that may exacerbate spinal cord vulnerability and impair reperfusion recovery.

Interestingly, although Crawford type II repairs are historically linked with higher risk of spinal cord injury [17], our model did not rank anatomical classification among the top predictors. Likewise, surgical indication (TAAA, TAAD, or TBAD) and history of arch/descending aortic surgery, although included as candidate variables, were not identified as major contributors in the final model. Instead, the model prioritized laboratory and intraoperative physiological variables. One possible explanation is that while anatomical classification and indication reflect baseline disease characteristics, they may not fully capture the dynamic changes in perfusion, coagulation status, or intraoperative stress that more directly influence spinal cord vulnerability.

In designing our predictive framework, we opted for an improved XGBoost backbone augmented by PSO and FL, rather than relying solely on “off-the-shelf” methods. PSO acts as a powerful metaheuristic optimizer that explores the hyperparameter search space more globally and efficiently than traditional grid or random search, reducing the risk of local optima [14]. FL, on the other hand, directly addresses the challenge of class imbalance by up-weighting hard, misclassified minority samples while down-weighting easily classified majority samples [15]. This combination allowed our model to better discriminate SCI events, which in our cohort constitute a minority class, and to maintain stability in learning. Compared with logistic regression, SVM, or standard random forest, the PSO-FLXGBoost model achieved superior discrimination and greater robustness to imbalance. By ensuring jointly optimized hyperparameters and preserving sensitivity toward minority cases, our approach exemplifies how tailored optimization and loss-function design can substantially enhance predictive performance in medical datasets where imbalance is a critical challenge.

To make the resulting model clinically meaningful rather than a black box, we employed SHAP to interpret feature contributions. SHAP assigns each feature a consistent attribution value for each individual prediction, thereby allowing us to quantify how much each predictor pushes the risk estimate up or down [16].

Our findings carry important clinical implications. The identification of Intra-HB, D-dimer, platelet count, and operation time as dominant predictors of SCI highlights several modifiable perioperative factors. For instance, maintaining hemoglobin levels above critical thresholds through timely transfusion, correcting preoperative anemia, and optimizing perfusion pressure may reduce ischemic vulnerability. Similarly, elevated D-dimer and platelet counts suggest a hypercoagulable state; therefore, intensified perioperative anticoagulation strategies or closer coagulation monitoring may help minimize microembolic events. The strong impact of prolonged operation time underscores the importance of meticulous surgical planning, team coordination, and the use of adjunctive protective strategies to shorten ischemic duration. Beyond these clinical insights, our PSO-FLXGBoost model demonstrates the feasibility of integrating advanced machine learning into perioperative risk assessment. Future applications may also include prospective validation across multi-center cohorts, integration with imaging or genomic biomarkers, and deployment as a dynamic monitoring tool that continuously updates risk profiles during surgery. Such developments could ultimately move risk prediction from retrospective analysis toward proactive, individualized management, thereby improving outcomes for patients undergoing thoracoabdominal aortic repair.

The results of this study are consistent with the current recommendations of the Society for Vascular Surgery (SVS) [28] guidelines for thoracoabdominal aortic repair, which emphasize multimodal spinal cord protection through cerebrospinal fluid drainage, maintenance of distal perfusion, temperature management, and limitation of ischemic time. Our findings add a data-driven dimension to these recommendations by identifying specific perioperative physiological and hematologic factors that significantly influence spinal cord vulnerability. The derived thresholds for hemoglobin, operation time, and coagulation parameters could serve as quantitative complements to guideline-based protective strategies. By integrating machine learning–based individualized risk assessment with established neuroprotective protocols, this approach may help refine patient selection and intraoperative management within the framework of contemporary vascular surgery practice.

Limitation

This study has several limitations. First, this was a retrospective single-center study with a limited sample size and only 22 SCI events, which may increase the risk of overfitting. This study was designed as an exploratory prediction model rather than a formal etiologic analysis. Although XGBoost, Focal Loss, and PSO may improve rare-event prediction in an imbalanced dataset, the low event count remains a major limitation. Therefore, the model should be considered internally validated and hypothesis-generating, pending external validation in larger multicenter cohorts. Second, our dataset was derived from an institutional case reporting system, and some variables were manually entered and incomplete, lacking potentially relevant perioperative information such as spinal cord perfusion pressure monitoring, intraoperative temperature profiles, or detailed cerebrospinal fluid management. Third, the study population was younger than that reported in most international series, largely due to the high prevalence of dissection in China, which may limit generalizability of the model to older populations with atherosclerotic aneurysms. Finally, although preoperative D-dimer was identified as an important predictor in the model, this biomarker lacks specificity in the setting of acute aortic pathology. Elevated D-dimer levels may reflect thrombosis, false-lumen activity, and overall disease acuity rather than SCI-specific mechanisms alone. Therefore, its predictive contribution should be interpreted in the broader clinical context.

Conclusion

In this retrospective cohort, we established a PSO-FLXGBoost model that accurately predicted SCI after TAAR. Intraoperative hemoglobin, D-dimer, platelet count, and operative time were the strongest predictors. With advanced optimization, imbalance handling, and SHAP interpretability, the model combines accuracy with transparency, offering a practical tool to guide perioperative management and improve outcomes.
