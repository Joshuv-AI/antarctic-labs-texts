Dynamic biomarker-based machine learning model predicts short-term treatment response in multiple myeloma
Authors: Yaqin Xiong, Jiadai Xu, Bingjie Li, Panpan Li, Yawen Wang, Peng Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMultiple myeloma (MM) is a heterogeneous plasma cell malignancy with variable treatment responses. Conventional prognostic systems such as ISS and R-ISS rely on baseline parameters and fail to capture dynamic changes during therapy. There is an unmet need for models that can predict short-term treatment outcomes (STO) to guide timely clinical decisions.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 662 newly diagnosed MM patients treated between 2017 and 2021. Peripheral blood lymphocyte subsets, cytokine profiles, and bone marrow plasma cell phenotypes (by multiparametric flow cytometry) were assessed at baseline and every two treatment cycles up to Cycle 10. Predictive models were built using Random Under-Sampling Boosting (RUSBoost) and evaluated by cross-validation. Performance was compared with conventional staging systems using F1 score, precision, recall, and accuracy.ResultsCytogenetic abnormalities and ISS/R-ISS classifications did not consistently predict STO beyond Cycle 2. In contrast, dynamic biomarkers—including CD8+ T cells, CD56+ NK cells, lymphocyte counts, and plasma cell surface markers—showed significant associations with treatment responses. The biomarker-based model consistently outperformed conventional staging, reducing false predictions and improving accuracy. At Cycle 4, the model achieved an F1 score of 0.75 versus 0.32 for R-ISS. Both full and feature-selected biomarker sets maintained robust predictive performance across cycles.ConclusionsWe developed a dynamic, biomarker-driven machine learning model that accurately predicts short-term treatment response in MM. This approach outperforms conventional staging systems and, importantly, can identify high-risk patients as early as Cycle 2. Such early recognition allows timely therapy intensification or switching before irreversible disease progression, thereby supporting more personalized patient management and potentially improving long-term outcomes.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-07946-0.Keywords: Multiple myeloma, Short-term treatment response, Flow cytometry, Machine learning

Introduction

Multiple myeloma (MM) is a hematologic malignancy characterized by the proliferation of neoplastic monoclonal plasma cells residing within the bone marrow [1–3]. Despite considerable therapeutic advances, MM remains challenging to manage due to its profound heterogeneity in treatment response and clinical outcomes. This variability underscores the critical need for risk-adapted treatment strategies. Existing prognostic systems such as the International Staging System (ISS) [4] and the Revised International Staging System (R-ISS) [5] are widely used; however, they rely exclusively on baseline clinical and genetic features and do not account for dynamic changes during therapy. It is well recognized that risk profiles may evolve over the treatment course, necessitating tools that can offer real-time guidance to support clinical decision-making. Although models like the Individualized Risk in Newly Diagnosed Multiple Myeloma (IRMMa) [6] incorporate multi-omic data for personalized prediction, their dependence on complex genomic profiling limits broad clinical adoption due to technical and cost barriers. Thus, there remains an unmet need for a clinically tractable, dynamic biomarker model that can accurately predict short-term treatment outcomes in MM.

The immune microenvironment plays a pivotal role in MM pathogenesis and treatment response [7]. Peripheral blood lymphocyte subsets—including T, B, and natural killer (NK) cells—reflect systemic immune status and have emerged as potential prognostic indicators. While Liu et al. [8] demonstrated that examining lymphocyte subsets combined with plasma cell immunophenotyping could serve as a prognostic indicator for MM patients, their approach focused primarily on static baseline assessments rather than dynamic monitoring during therapy. Similarly, multi-parametric flow cytometry (MFC) of bone marrow plasma cells (PCs) provides detailed immunophenotypic information that is often underutilized in risk stratification. Mantas Radzevičius et al. [9] identified altered expression of markers like CD27, CD38, CD45, CD56, CD117, and CD138 in higher-risk MM groups, but this was limited to genetic risk correlations without integration into predictive models for treatment response. Additionally, cytokines such as IL-6 are known to support MM cell survival and proliferation, yet their dynamic changes during treatment have not been systematically incorporated into outcome prediction models.

Recent advances in machine learning (ML) have shown promise in refining prognostic accuracy in MM. Although recent progress, such as Zeshan et al. [10] and Guerrero et al. [11], has shown the promise of ML in myeloma outcome prediction, these models predominantly rely on genomic or trial-based data, which limits their real-world applicability due to high costs and complexity. To bridge this gap, the present study introduces a novel ML framework that integrates serial, readily measurable biomarkers—including peripheral immune subsets, cytokine profiles, and MFC-based PC phenotypes—for dynamic prediction of short-term treatment outcomes. Our study addresses this gap by pioneering the integration of dynamic, clinically accessible immune biomarkers with ML algorithms specifically for short-term outcome prediction, offering a more practical and timely tool for personalized MM management. Materials and methods

Patients

This retrospective study included 662 patients with newly diagnosed multiple myeloma (NDMM) who received treatment at Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University, a national medical center in China, between July 1, 2017, and December 31, 2021. Patients with amyloidosis were excluded. Clinical characteristics were collected from electronic medical records. R-ISS stage was determined according to the criteria published in 2015 [5]. Treatment response was evaluated according to the International Myeloma Working Group (IMWG) Response Criteria [12], which include stringent complete remission (sCR), complete remission (CR), very good partial response (VGPR), partial response (PR), minor response (MR), stable disease (SD), and progressive disease (PD). Treatment response was assessed per IMWG uniform response criteria using available clinical data at time points approximating every two treatment cycles (e.g., after cycle 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10, or the nearest documented evaluation), resulting in up to five assessment time points per patient. The data cutoff date for this analysis was February 2022.

MFC analysis of PCs was performed from diagnosis and after every two treatment cycles, using an eight-color antibody panel consisting of CD38, CD138, CD19, CD56, CD45, CD27, CD81, CD117, and cytoplasmic kappa (κ) and lambda (λ) immunoglobulin light chains, achieving a sensitivity of 10− 6. Malignant plasma cells typically exhibited aberrant immunophenotypes, including negative or dim expression of CD19, CD45, CD81, and CD27, together with aberrant expression of CD56 and/or CD117 [13]. Peripheral blood lymphocyte subsets were assessed by flow cytometry, including B cells (CD19+), T cells (CD3+), T helper cells (CD4+), Cytotoxic T cells (CD8+), the CD4/CD8 ratio, and NK cells (CD56+/CD16+). Serum cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor (TNF), Interleukin-1β (IL-1β), Interleukin-2 Receptor (IL-2 R), Interleukin-6 (IL-6), Interleukin-8 (IL-8), and Interleukin-10 (IL-10), were determined by flow cytometry (FC) or enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA).

Approval for this study was granted by the ethics committee of our hospital (B2023-185 R) and conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration of the World Medical Association. All patients provided consent for the use of their electronic medical record data for research purposes.

Data preprocessing and integrating

To integrate the cytokine data obtained by ELISA and FC, we first normalized the ELISA data by dividing each element by the square root of the sum of squared values in each row. Next, we constructed two separate matrices: one containing the first four rows of the normalized ELISA data and another containing rows five to nine, corresponding to the FC data. To integrate the data, we located the indices of the non-missing values in the FC data matrix and transferred those values to the corresponding positions in the ELISA data matrix. Finally, we constructed a new matrix by concatenating the updated ELISA data matrix, which now includes the integrated values from the FC data, with the remaining rows of the original ELISA data. This process resulted in a single integrated dataset that integrates the ELISA and FC measurements. We also retrieved the corresponding sample and feature identifiers for the integrated data, ensuring that the appropriate labels remained consistent throughout the merging process. The merging procedure enabled us to combine the ELISA and FC measurements into a unified dataset, facilitating downstream analysis and comparison of the combined data.

To address missing values in the integrated dataset, we utilized a forward-backward filling approach, considering the time series nature of the biomarker data and the clinical continuity of measurements in real-world MM cohorts [14].

This method involved propagating valid observations forward or backward to fill in the missing values, leveraging the correlation between measurements at adjacent time points. First, we applied forward filling to the integrated dataset iteratively over four cycles. In each iteration, missing values were replaced with the last valid observation encountered in the same row, assuming that biomarker levels are likely to remain similar to the previous time point. Next, we applied backward filling to the resulting dataset, also four times consecutively. In each iteration, missing values were replaced with the next valid observation encountered in the same row. This step addressed any remaining missing values by propagating the next available measurement backward, considering that biomarker levels are likely to exhibit continuity to subsequent time points. This approach helps preserve biomarker trajectories for dynamic modeling.

To evaluate the robustness of our imputation strategy and address potential attrition bias, we performed two additional analyses. First, a sensitivity analysis compared the primary FBF-imputed model with an alternative forward-fill-only (F-only) model (equivalent to last observation carried forward) to assess the impact of backward-propagated values [15]. Second, we conducted an attrition bias assessment by stratifying patients into three groups based on follow-up completeness: (1) complete follow-up, (2) intermittent gaps, and (3) lost to follow-up. These groups were compared for baseline clinical characteristics and early treatment responses (Cycles 2, 4).

Feature selection

The core of our feature selection method relied on a statistical analysis. For each biomarker, we examined its distribution in two patient groups: those who responded positively to treatment and those who did not. We employed a two-sample t-test to quantify the distinction between these distributions. This statistical test was chosen due to its ability to compare means between two independent groups, and to help identify individual biomarkers with the greatest discriminative power in the early stages of treatment, assuming normality of the data. To determine statistical significance, we adopted a threshold p-value of 0.05, which is widely accepted to strike a balance between sensitivity to potentially important biomarkers and the need to control for false positives in our selection process. Biomarkers meeting this criterion were considered to have meaningful discriminatory power in distinguishing between treatment response groups.

Random under-sampling boosting

Machine learning techniques, particularly ensemble learning methods like Random Under-Sampling Boosting (RUS Boost), have shown great promise in addressing the challenges of predictive modeling in biomedical research. These techniques are especially useful for handling imbalanced datasets, where the number of patients responding positively to treatment is often much smaller than those who do not. RusBoost, in particular, enhances predictive power by combining under-sampling of the majority class with boosting, which focuses on difficult-to-classify instances, thereby improving the accuracy of predictions for the minority class [16].

• Response Variable (Y). The primary outcome of interest is the treatment response, denoted as Y, which is recorded for each patient at five distinct stages of their myeloma treatment. This is a binary variable, where 1 indicates a positive response (e.g., ≥partial remission per IMWG criteria, including sCR, CR, VGPR, or PR), and 0 indicates non-response (e.g., MR, SD, or PD).

• Feature Matrix (X). The feature matrix X was constructed by integrating clinical scores such as R-ISS with a panel of longitudinal biomarkers, including serum markers, cytogenetic mutations, and protein expression levels. For each predictive cycle, only patients with evaluable treatment responses were included in the analysis. To address missingness in the biomarker time-series while preserving temporal continuity, we employed a sequential imputation strategy: initial values were estimated using a Forward-Backward (FBF) approach, followed by column mean imputation for any remaining missing entries. This ensured a complete and robust dataset for subsequent machine learning training and prediction.

•Handling Class Imbalance. Given the inherently imbalanced nature of the response variable—where positive treatment responses are less frequent than negative ones—a cost-sensitive learning approach was adopted. Acost matrix was employed to assign a higher penalty for misclassifying a positive response (i.e., treating a patient as non-responsive when they are, in fact, responsive). This approach helps improve the model’s sensitivity to detecting true positive responses, which is crucial for effective clinical decision-making.

• Model Training and Cross-Validation. The RUSBoost algorithm was employed to predict the treatment response at each stage of myeloma therapy. RUSBoost was chosen due to its effectiveness in addressing class imbalance by combining under-sampling of the majority class (non-responders) with the boosting technique, which focuses on misclassified instances in successive iterations.

For each assessment time point, the following steps were performed:

Model Training: The RUSBoost model was trained on the selected features X (data available up to the previous assessment time point) and the binary response variable Y (treatment response at the next assessment time point). The model was configured with a learning rate of 0.1 and 100 learning cycles, optimizing its classification accuracy for patients’ likelihood of responding to treatment.

Model Training: The RUSBoost model was trained on the selected features X (data available up to the previous assessment time point) and the binary response variable Y (treatment response at the next assessment time point). The model was configured with a learning rate of 0.1 and 100 learning cycles, optimizing its classification accuracy for patients’ likelihood of responding to treatment.

Cross-Validation: To ensure the robustness and generalizability of the model, a 5-fold cross-validation strategy was used. The dataset was partitioned into five subsets, with each subset serving as the validation set once, while the remaining subsets were used for training. This approach helps mitigate overfitting and provides a reliable estimate of the model’s predictive performance.

Cross-Validation: To ensure the robustness and generalizability of the model, a 5-fold cross-validation strategy was used. The dataset was partitioned into five subsets, with each subset serving as the validation set once, while the remaining subsets were used for training. This approach helps mitigate overfitting and provides a reliable estimate of the model’s predictive performance.

Performance Evaluation: The model’s performance was evaluated using several metrics: accuracy (overall correctness of predictions), precision (proportion of correctly predicted positive responses), recall (ability to identify all true positive responses), and the F1 score (a harmonic mean of precision and recall). These metrics were derived from the confusion matrix generated during cross-validation and were recorded for each stage of treatment prediction.

These metrics were derived from the confusion matrix generated during cross-validation and were recorded for each stage of treatment prediction.

• Visualization and Comparison. For each treatment stage, confusion matrices were plotted to visualize the distribution of true versus predicted treatment responses. Additionally, the performance metrics for each predictive model across the different stages were summarized and visualized using bar plots. This provided a clear comparison of the model’s effectiveness in predicting treatment response across the different stages of multiple myeloma.

To evaluate potential attrition bias, patients were classified according to data availability at Cycle 10 into Completers (n = 215), Intermittent gaps (n = 288), and Lost entirely (n = 159) groups (Supplementary Table S1). Patients with complete loss to follow-up were significantly older (p < 0.001) and had higher lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels (p = 0.015) compared with patients with complete or intermittent follow-up. In Lost entirely group, the most frequently documented reasons were loss to follow-up (40.3%) and not evaluable due to missing complete assessment data (28.3%), followed by death (20.8%) (Fig. 2D).

Given that patients in Lost entirely group lacked post-baseline response assessments, a direct comparison of treatment efficacy across all three groups was not feasible. Therefore, to evaluate the impact of treatment discontinuation on early outcomes, we compared the overall response rates (ORRs) at each assessment time point between the Completers group and the Intermittent gaps group. This analysis revealed a significantly higher response rate in the Completers group compared to the intermittent missing group from C2 to C8 (all p < 0.05，Fig. 3B). Furthermore, Kaplan-Meier analysis demonstrated that patients in the Intermittent gaps group had significantly shorter progression-free survival (PFS) (median PFS: NR vs 38.1 months, p < 0.001), as well as overall survival (OS) from the time of diagnosis compared to those with complete follow-up (median OS: NR vs NR, p < 0.001) (Fig. 3C, D).

Hence, to address this inherent bias and leverage the available information from all enrolled patients, we utilized imputation strategies to estimate missing values based on observed data, thereby aiming to reduce bias and enhance the robustness of our findings.

Cytogenetic markers are unable to timely indicate short-term treatment response

Initially, a multivariable Cox regression analysis was performed to assess the relationships between age, sex, cytogenetic abnormalities, and the risks of disease progression and overall survival. Chromosome 1q gain (p < 0.001), del (13q14) (p = 0.0025), del(17p) (p = 0.005), t(14;16) (p = 0.001), and t(4;14) (p < 0.001) were identified as having significantly adverse effects on disease progression (Fig. 4A). Chromosome 1q gain (p = 0.003), del (17p) (p = 0.005), and t(14;16) (p = 0.024) showed significant associations with OS (Fig. 4B). Interaction analyses suggested that the co-occurrence of chromosome 1q gain and del (17p) might significantly increase the risk of disease progression by 22% (p = 0.005, Fig. 4C) and mortality by 26% (p = 0.019, Fig. 4D).

Subsequently, to explore the predictive value of different factors on the therapeutic response of multiple myeloma, we first evaluated the impact of cytogenetic abnormalities. The multiple logistic regression analysis of the predictive value of cytogenetic abnormalities on short-term treatment response for MM patients demonstrated that chromosome 1q gain, t (14;16), and t(4;14) were not significant predictors of short-term treatment efficacy (Cycle 2, Fig. 4E; Cycle 4, Fig. 4F; Cycle 6, Fig. 4G). In Cycles 2 and 4, t(11;14) (C2, p = 0.004; C4, p < 0.001) and del(17p) (C2, p = 0.011; C4, p = 0.040) were significantly associated with a worse treatment response. Additionally, del(13q) appeared to have a markedly beneficial effect on treatment response in Cycle 2 (p = 0.005).

Further analysis of gender, age, ISS staging, R-ISS, and R2-ISS staging systems revealed that these factors similarly failed to demonstrate significant predictive value for treatment response during cycles 2 through 8. The predictive value of the R-ISS and R2-ISS systems only became apparent by cycle 10 (R-ISS, p = 0.008; R2-ISS, p = 0.005) (Supplementary Figure S1， Supplementary Table S1).

The aforementioned results indicate that therapeutic response prior to cycle 8 was largely independent of classical baseline risk factors. Based on this finding, the study defined therapeutic response achieved before cycle 8 as Short-term Treatment Outcome (STO) to distinguish it from therapeutic responses after cycle 10, which may better reflect long-term prognosis. This definition underscores the necessity of developing dynamic predictive tools to evaluate early efficacy.

Dynamic immune and flow cytometry biomarkers correlate with short-term outcomes

To improve model performance and reduce computational complexity, we first performed biomarker selection. Before data merging and preprocessing, we collected all the unconsolidated biomarkers from the five periods (Baseline, After Cycle 2, After Cycle 4, After Cycle 6, After Cycle 8). Upon completing the preliminary data processing, we categorized the therapeutic response status of the patients into three classes: positive response (1) (patients had sCR, CR, VGPR, and PR), negative response (0) (patients suffered from PD, SD, and MR), and missing response (−1). We calculated the mean and standard error for each biomarker across these three response states throughout five treatment cycles. Subsequently, we create a series of plots to visually represent the trend of each biomarker over time (Fig. 5A). We further investigated the statistical significance of each biomarker across different treatment cycles. Welch’s t-test, the unequal variances t-test, was employed to compare the differences in biomarker levels between the positive response group (Response = 1) and the negative response group (Response = 0) at each treatment cycle. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant and was marked with a colored dot on the heatmap-style scatter plot (Fig. 5B).

We further sought to integrate the distinctions using Welch’s t-test (the t-test for unequal variances) analysis across data from all cycles to achieve a more comprehensive perspective (Fig. 5C). In peripheral blood (PB) lymphocyte populations, the negative group exhibited a significantly lower lymphocyte count (p = 0.0066), a higher percentage of CD19+ B-lymphocytes (p = 0.0426), a higher percentage of CD3+ T-lymphocytes (p = 0.0081), a higher percentage of CD8+ T-lymphocytes (p = 0.0321), and a lower percentage of CD16+/56+ NK cells (p = 0.0002). In the flow cytometric (FC) analysis of plasma cells (PCs), the negative group exhibited a significantly higher percentage in the PCs gate (CD138+/CD38+) (p < 0.0001), a lower percentage of CD19 on PCs (p = 0.0007), a higher percentage of CD56 on PCs (p < 0.0001), a lower percentage of CD81 on PCs (p = 0.0267), and a lower percentage of CD27 on PCs (p = 0.0089).

Through feature selection, we identified subsets of the most relevant features for different target variables. In the subsequent modeling process, we employed both the selected biomarker subsets and the full feature set to optimize the performance of the predicted model.

Biomarker-based models outperform R-ISS for short-term prediction

To mirror clinical decision-making, we adopted a sequential prediction framework where variables from each assessment point were used to predict the subsequent response (e.g., baseline variables to predict Cycle 2). Within this framework, biomarker-based models—with and without feature selection—were compared against the R-ISS staging system.

Across all evaluated cycles, biomarker-based models consistently outperformed the R-ISS model (Fig. 6A). The R-ISS model exhibited high misclassification rates, particularly false negatives. For example, at Cycle 4, the false negative rate of the R-ISS model reached 80%, whereas biomarker-based models maintained false negative rates below 30%. This further demonstrates the limitations of R-ISS in predicting STO.

Model performance was further assessed using F1-score, recall, precision, and accuracy (Fig. 6B). At baseline, biomarker models with feature selection achieved the highest F1-score (0.71), outperforming both unselected biomarker models (0.67) and the R-ISS model (0.63). This advantage persisted at Cycle 2 (selected: 0.74; unselected: 0.73; R-ISS: 0.64). From Cycle 4 onward, however, models using unselected biomarkers demonstrated comparable or superior performance, achieving an F1-score of 0.75 at Cycle 4 (selected: 0.74), with this pattern continuing through Cycle 8. Similar trends were observed for recall, precision, and accuracy, with unselected biomarker models reaching an accuracy of 0.61 by Cycle 8, compared with 0.47 for models with feature selection.

To explore contributors to model performance, feature importance was examined for early-cycle predictions. At Cycle 2, immune- and tumor microenvironment–related markers dominated the top predictors (Table 3). The ten most influential features accounted for 78.02% of total importance and included IL2R (11.16%), IL10 (9.04%), CD19 (8.72%), as well as multiple T-cell, NK-cell, and plasma cell–associated markers.

Discussion

Multiple myeloma (MM) is a highly heterogeneous disease with diverse genetic and phenotypic features. Traditional prognostic models such as ISS [4] and R-ISS [5] provide lon-term risk stratification but fail to capture dynamic changes during therapy [17, 18]. Increasing evidence suggests that early on-treatment biomarkers may better reflect short-term treatment depth and response kinetics, complementing baseline risk stratification [19–21]. Given the limited reliability of baseline cytogenetic markers and static staging systems for predicting short-term outcomes (STO) [22, 23], tools capable of informing early treatment decisions are needed. To address this gap, we developed a dynamic prediction model that integrates serial measurements of peripheral immune cell subsets, cytokine profiles, and bone marrow plasma cell phenotypes.

Clinically, this dynamic model provides cycle-specific risk estimates early during induction, a period when treatment modification is still actionable. Methodologically, the model adopts a prospective, time-lagged framework, ensuring that predictions rely exclusively on contemporaneous clinical information and thereby reflecting real-world decision-making. By incorporating dynamic immune and tumor-related features during Cycles 2–4, the model achieved superior discrimination for short-term response compared to static R-ISS, particularly in early cycles with greater data completeness. Exploratory analyses suggested consistent performance across R-ISS risk strata, though predictive robustness declined in later cycles due to increasing data attrition. These results indicate that dynamic risk assessment based on early on-treatment biomarker trajectories may support individualized decision-making in multiple myeloma, providing continuous, cycle-specific risk estimates at clinically relevant timepoints.

Lymphocyte subsets play a central role in MM progression and response to therapy [24]. We observed that elevated peripheral CD8+ T-cell counts were associated with poorer short-term outcomes. This seemingly paradoxical finding aligns with prior reports linking high CD8+/Treg ratios to disease progression and early relapse [25, 26], suggesting that numerical expansion does not necessarily reflect functional competence. We hypothesize that these elevated CD8+ T-cell levels may represent compensatory expansion of dysfunctional or exhausted clones, indicative of an ineffective host immune response to aggressive disease biology [27]. Such dysfunctional immune expansion may therefore serve as an early biomarker of inadequate immune-mediated disease control during induction therapy. Conversely, increased NK cells (CD56+16+) and lymphocyte counts predicted favorable responses, underscoring the role of innate immune surveillance in controlling disease [28, 29].

Bone marrow plasma cell (PC) phenotypes also demonstrated prognostic relevance. High CD56 expression and low CD19/CD27/CD81 expression correlated with poor responses [30], supporting the concept that phenotypic plasticity of PCs may reflect treatment resistance and aggressive disease biology [31, 32]. These findings are consistent with prior studies suggesting that CD19+ subpopulations may represent stem-like myeloma cells [33], while aberrant CD56 expression marks more invasive disease [30, 34].

Notably, the prognostic implications of cytokines appeared to be time-dependent. Elevated IL-10 levels after Cycle 6 were associated with improved outcomes, suggesting a time- and context-dependent role that may reflect resolution of chronic inflammation or immune recalibration rather than immunosuppression per se [23, 35–37]. Separately, dynamic changes in cytokines like IL-2 R may reflect distinct aspects of ongoing immune–tumor interactions.

Importantly, these biomarkers are not merely passive reflections of tumor burden reduction but appear to capture therapy-induced remodeling of the immune microenvironment [38]. Prior studies have shown that early recovery of NK and CD8+ T-cell populations, together with rapid declines in pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α, are independently associated with deeper responses and improved survival [26, 29, 39, 40]. These patterns likely represent active immune reconstitution rather than epiphenomena of cytoreduction.

In real-world cohorts, missing biomarker data substantially increases in later treatment cycles (e.g., beyond Cycle 4) due to disease progression, adverse events, or death, leading to diminished sample size and heightened imputation noise. Under these conditions, models using the full set of biomarkers outperformed those relying on feature selection. This likely stems from the instability of feature selection when samples shrink and noise increases, whereas full-feature models with regularization may offer greater robustness in later cycles. Moreover, this pattern of missing data is often linked to poorer baseline prognosis, which may modestly overestimate model performance in later cycles. Nevertheless, the model demonstrates consistently strong and stable predictive performance during early cycles, supporting its role as an early-response monitor. Its applicability and accuracy in later cycles, however, require further evaluation in prospective studies with standardized assessment schedules to better control for attrition bias. Accordingly, predictions generated in later cycles should be interpreted with caution, and the primary clinical utility of the model lies in early on-treatment monitoring rather than late-stage prognostication.

In addition to the modeling framework, our use of the RUSBoost algorithm—optimized for imbalanced data—specifically addressed the critical need to reduce false-negative predictions, a key challenge in MM response assessment [41–43]. Nonetheless, whether earlier identification of poor responders translates into improved clinical outcomes requires prospective validation. Compared with prior AI-based models relying heavily on complex genomic data or clinical trial populations, our model emphasizes interpretability and real-world applicability.

To translate predictive models into clinical practice, the definition of clinically actionable thresholds is paramount. This necessitates the evaluation of net clinical benefit in independent cohorts, with careful balancing of the risks associated with false-positive and false-negative predictions. Presently, the principal utility of our model lies in its function as a high-resolution risk stratification tool. Its outputs must be interpreted in conjunction with established prognostic frameworks such as R-ISS, patient fitness, and preferences to inform—rather than dictate—clinical decision-making.

It is noteworthy that therapeutic heterogeneity may influence the interpretation of dynamic biomarker patterns. Our cohort reflects the real-world treatment landscape in China during the study period (2017–2021), characterized predominantly by proteasome inhibitor (PI)- and immunomodulatory drug (IMiD)-based regimens, with relatively low upfront utilization of anti-CD38 monoclonal antibodies and ASCT [44]. Consequently, the model should be interpreted as an early-warning tool optimized for these conventional therapeutic backbones. The absence of external validation constitutes the primary limitation of this work, and internal performance estimates may be optimistic. To address this, we have initiated a multi-center prospective study to validate these findings and to evaluate the model’s generalizability within contemporary treatment paradigms incorporating CD38-targeted therapies and emerging bispecific antibodies.

In summary, to address the unmet need for early, dynamic response assessment in MM, this study developed and initially validated a machine-learning framework that integrates serial biomarker measurements. It paves the way for future prospective studies to refine and clinically integrate this approach, potentially enabling adaptive treatment strategies.

Conclusion

This study pioneers the integration of dynamic, multi-omic biomarkers with machine learning to predict STO in multiple myeloma. Our RUSBoost model achieved superior predictive accuracy compared to traditional staging systems at critical early timepoints (Cycles 2, 4), providing a tool for the early identification of non-responders. The proposed clinical translation framework (Fig. 8) outlines a closed-loop system from dynamic biomarker monitoring to pre-emptive intervention via an EHR-integrated decision support tool. Future directions constitute multi-center validation, model enhancement with MRD status, and development of clinical calculators for adoption. This dynamic assessment strategy effectively complements static staging, advancing the goal of personalized myeloma therapy.
