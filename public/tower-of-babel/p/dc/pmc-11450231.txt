Piano performance evaluation dataset with multilevel perceptual features
Authors: Jisoo Park, Jongho Kim, Jeong Mi Park, Ahyeon Choi, Wen-Syan Li, Jonghwa Park, Seung-won Hwang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This study aims to build a comprehensive dataset that enables the automatic evaluation of piano performances. In real-world piano performance, especially within the realm of classical piano music, we encounter a vast spectrum of performance variations. The challenge lies in how to effectively evaluate these performances. We must consider three critical aspects: (1) It is essential to gauge how performers express the music and how listeners perceive the performance, rather than focusing on the compositional characteristics of the musical piece. (2) Beyond fundamental elements like pitch and duration, we must also embrace higher-level features such as interpretation. (3) Such evaluation should be done by experts to discern the nuanced performances. Regrettably, there exists no dataset that addresses these challenging evaluation tasks. Therefore, we introduce a pioneering dataset PercePiano, annotated by music experts, with more extensive features capable of representing these nuanced aspects effectively. It encapsulates piano performance with a wide range of perceptual features that are recognized by musicians. Our evaluation benchmark includes a novel metric designed to accommodate the inherent subjectivity of perception. Furthermore, we propose an enhanced baseline framework that grounds performance on score data, aligning model predictions with human perception. Harnessing the aligned features enhances the baseline performance and proves to be adaptable to various model structures. In conclusion, our research opens new possibilities for comprehensive piano performance evaluation.

Introduction

In the field of music performance, there exist various performances with the same piece since the same repertoire is constantly reproduced with reinterpretation. It is especially true in the context of classical music, as musicians have been playing e.g. Beethoven for centuries. While this enduring allure adds to classical music’s appeal, it also introduces complexity in evaluation since individual performance nuances must be considered in addition to the inherent compositional characteristics of the musical work1.

It is within this context that human perception has been considered a very important factor in music evaluation. Musicologists have traditionally emphasized perceptive elements in music criticism, which is inevitably subjective. The challenge lies in how to effectively evaluate these features in the various performances. To address this, we must consider two critical aspects of the features and one aspect of musical evaluation. (1) Perceptual features: Perceptual features refer to the aspects of music that can only be discerned through human perception. Such features are fundamental in music evaluation since music is inherently an experience shaped by how both performers and listeners interpret and express it. For instance, evaluating not just the superficial stability of the beat, but also how the pianists accept and convey the rhythm of the music and the way audiences recognize it hold great significance. We delve into the intricate aspects of how the musical features are perceived and expressed in the performance. (2) Multi-Level features: Musical features span from low to high level. Low-level features consists of fundamental elements captured within a short term, such as timing and articulation, while high-level features involve more complex and long-term aspects, including music making, emotional expression, and interpretation. (3) Evaluation by experts: Unlike image or language recognition, which often exhibits universality, musicality remains predominantly within the realm of experts2. However, existing music-related datasets often fall short of addressing these aspects comprehensively, as summarized in Table 1. Many of these datasets neglect the inclusion of perceptual features4. Even when they do incorporate perceptual aspects, they often do not encompass diverse performances and are inadequate for evaluating performances that inherently exhibit variability5. None of the datasets account for multiple levels of features5,6. While some annotations come from experts, their expertise levels can vary due to lower selection criteria determined by a musical test or limited numbers of experts.

To address this challenge, we introduce PercePiano, a novel dataset for expert-guided piano performance evaluation, considering both the perceptual and multi-leveled aspects of music. It encompasses a wide range of musical styles including variations within the same musical piece, thereby capturing the diverse expressiveness of piano performance. Each musical piece was interpreted by 6 to 13 different pianists. Each performance was labeled with comprehensive 19 perceptual features categorized by 8 distinct groups. These labels span aspects ranging from timing to interpretation, each emphasizing the perceptive dimension. Lastly, music experts assessed the performance. Each performance segment received evaluations from 5 to 12 annotators specialized in piano music to gauge consensus in music evaluation. In total, PercePiano comprises 1202 segments and 12,652 individual notations. Moreover, we have introduced a novel metric into our benchmark, which reflects annotator variance due to the inherent subjectivity in assessing perceptual features. This addition ensures a more reliable and nuanced evaluation process of perceptual elements in piano performances.

We benchmarked several baselines including MidiBERT7. However, information solely from performance may not be optimally encoded to capture the nuanced perceptual features inherent in piano performances. Inspired by the fact that when evaluating performance humans often refer to the score information that the musician plays, we propose an enhanced baseline by anchoring performance data to its corresponding original score data. Specifically, we capture features like dynamics and articulation, alongside hierarchical information such as beat and measure locations. The former serves as supplementary input, while the latter contributes to the hierarchical encoding of the performance. Our results demonstrate that our new baseline structure improves the performance evaluation, showcasing superior capabilities in capturing nuanced perceptual features when compared to relying solely on performance data. In conclusion, our work collectively introduces a new concept of piano performance evaluation.

Related works

Music performance evaluation dataset

In order to evaluate music performance, securing diverse performances including various interpretations and perceptual features of music should be guaranteed. However, not all datasets meet these criteria. MSD Music Genre Dataset (MAGD), for example, which provides genre labels for pop music, lacks both various performances for the same music and perceptual features8.

Some datasets do not cover perceptual features of each performance. In this case, datasets aim to capture various expressions of performances, including multiple performances of the same musical piece. For instance, the MAESTRO Dataset offers piano performance audio/MIDI data from the Yamaha International Piano-e-Competition, ensuring that multiple performances are available for the same piece of music9. Piano Concerto Dataset (PCD) comprises audio source data for piano concertos, featuring recordings from 5 pianists and orchestra10, and Groove MIDI Dataset (GMD) provides expressive drumming performance audio/MIDI data11.

Even datasets providing perceptual features of music, fall short of encompassing all the essential elements required for a comprehensive evaluation. These datasets primarily focus on higher-level perceptual aspects, neglecting the significance of low-level features, while leveled features were emphasized when analyzing classical piano music12–14. For example, the Mid-level Perceptual Musical Features Dataset includes seven mid-level features related to music concepts, whereas the EMOPIA Dataset, which centers on pop piano music, consists of four emotion classes5,15.

Leveled features in music

When it comes to the specific domain of classical piano music, musicologists particularly emphasize features with levels for music analysis12. They generally acknowledge that listeners perceive music with fundamental elements such as timbre, through leveled recognition13,14. However, in the field of Music Information Retrieval (MIR) or machine learning models related to music, the term “level” holds a distinct meaning. Typically, “low-level features” refer to spectral and cepstral aspects of the music audio signal, while mid-level or high-level representations are consistent with musicology: more music-oriented and perceptive such as genre or emotion6,15–17. These classifications do not include low-level perceptual features, such as tempo(timing). From this perspective, there is a demand for datasets that incorporate additional low-level perceptual features with mid-level or higher-level features from existing MIR studies.

Symbolic music understanding models

Recent advancements in MIDI encoding have paralleled those in the field of natural language processing (NLP) owing to its capacity to handle sequential data. Previous works applied Word2Vec to learn embeddings of chords or grouped musical notes from the MIDI corpus18,19. After the rise of the transformer20, pre-training approaches have emerged afterward7,21,22. However, the exclusive reliance on performance MIDI data in evaluating piano performances exposes inherent limitations. The deficiency lies in the absence of grounding provided by the original score, which encapsulates exact structural information crucial for human perception. While existing studies in symbolic music generation also studied leveraging the structure of music information23, they are constrained to measure-level information. Motivated by the score-MIDI alignment processing to generate expressive MIDI from the score file24, we leverage the alignment technique to ground the evaluation of MIDI.

Dataset

The main goal of our work is to gather diverse performances with leveled perceptual features that match the criteria for performance evaluation. We began by addressing the challenge of designing labels that effectively encompass the properties. In doing so, we meticulously curated leveled features drawing insights from previous research. Additionally, we provided clear guidelines to our annotators to ensure that the features are distinctly “perceptual” in nature. Next, we collected the performances that exhibit such variations and recruited only experts to label the data. This study adhered to ethical guidelines and received approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Seoul National University [IRB No. 2303/004-005] to ensure the protection of human subjects. All experiments were performed following relevant guidelines and regulations, and informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Label design

To compile the dataset, we employed terminology derived from diverse sources, including traditional musicologists, music information retrieval (MIR), and music AI. We selected a total of 19 features, which were then divided into 8 categories of 4 levels as shown in Table 2. First, we gathered descriptive terms used to portray piano performance. These terms were sourced from piano performance critics12,25–27, writings by pianists themselves28,29, and surveys conducted among Music School students. Next, we selected 19 features from these terms, each represented as polar extremes (Option 1, 2 from Table 2). Criteria for feature selection were based on three key considerations: (1) The words are widely used in the music field (2) The selected features avoid extremely positive or negative connotations, maintaining a balanced and neutral perspective (3) The features should be captured only through human perception, which cannot be extracted with simple computation.

Then the features were classified into nine categories, based on the musical elements they addressed (Category from Table 2). The selection of these categories was influenced by the works in piano education3,30, while excluding those that are not directly related to the expressive performance (e.g. sight reading, tuning). Lastly, we employed a leveled approach to categorize the musical features, which makes the dataset more explainable. This approach takes into account both the musical aspect13,25 and data science or MIR perspective6,15,16, but primarily following musical taxonomy. In our approach, the levels were divided by the length of music needed to judge each label, as levels for music representation features are often divided into length of analysis window6. Categories that could be detected in the very short term (in the extreme, even with a few notes) are considered as low-level (Timing, Articulation), while high-level category features (Emotion & Mood, Interpretation) should be considered in longer context, at least from musical phrase, sometimes for the whole song. In our criteria, mid-level features are defined as those that could be judged in 2-4 bars length. Data collection

To collect performances and labels, we ensure evaluation by experts and diverse performances with different interpretations. We stress that only music expert annotators participated, satisfying one or more of the following conditions: (1) Pianist. Graduate student or higher, or professional performer. (2) Music theory major. College graduates or higher. The music expert annotators were outsourced and evaluated each music segment on a 7-point scale according to each label, assigning 1 point for the strongest agreement with Option 1, 7 points for Option 2, and intermediate values for evaluations in between. While binary scales may sometimes better capture the content depending on the nature and format of the survey31,32, we opted to collect data using a 7-point Likert scale, as it was preferred by musicians in focus group interviews and is widely utilized for its utility in research through scales33–35. Additionally, we added an “I don’t know” label to allow annotators to express uncertainty or lack of knowledge about a particular segment.

For the choice of the performance, our study focuses on classical piano music. We specifically select pieces from the romantic repertoire, as the romantic era offers expressive, dramatic, and programmatic compositions36, providing ample opportunities for performers to shape their interpretations. Two types of musical forms were chosen: piano sonata and variations for solo piano performances. Piano sonata, whose form reached its peak until the romantic era, has to be performed with the individuality of divided movements and a long breath of the whole song37. Conversely, variations refer to the form of music(or technique) in which the theme is repeated with an altered form of various musical elements. Each variation length is generally shorter than the length of the sonata movements, so individual performances can show shorter breathing changes. In addition, model learning effectiveness was expected as variations include various techniques of performers38.

To gather the performance data, we took advantage of Yamaha’s release of performance MIDI files for the entire contest, and the MAESTRO dataset which organized its data9. Considering that Franz Schubert, a prominent composer of the Romantic era, had a round in the competition, we chose Piano Sonata No.21 in B-flat major, D.96039. For variations, 32 Variations in C minor (WoO.80)40, Four Impromptus D.935, No.3 in B-flat major41 were selected.

When determining the segment length, previous research used mid-level features of music typically employed segment length of 10 to 30 seconds per song16, and musical phrase, a minimum unit with complete musical sense, typically spans 4 to 8 bars42. For our evaluation purposes, we chose the length of 4 and 8 bars as the segment size, which corresponds to approximately 8 to 30 seconds of playing time. In addition, for comparison, some 16-bar-long segments were also included.

While e-competition and MAESTRO provide high-quality piano performance MIDI data, it does not include the corresponding score of the original piece. To address this, we collected the score files from MuseScore, a community-based web platform for musical scores. MusicXML format, an XML-based format aiming to represent Western music notation, was used, as it is widely used in MIR research involving musical scores due to its ability to accommodate a wide range of musical characters24,43,44. Indeed, it is important to note that score files obtained from crowdsourced platforms may exhibit inconsistencies in score transcription styles. To ensure accuracy and reliability, we manually reviewed and modified the score files to align with Henle’s edition, which is widely regarded as an “Urtext” (undistorted, reliable, and authoritative musical text) edition for classical music pieces45.

To allow annotators to listen to and annotate the performance, the original MIDI data was rendered through DAW (Logic Pro) using the ‘Yamaha Grand Piano’ virtual instrument. This basic sound font was chosen for its ease of reproducing the sound source. We consistently used the same virtual instrument to capture the characteristics of the performance itself, rather than those of the instrument or the recording space.

Data analysis

PercePiano consists of 12,652 annotations for 1,202 musical segments, including performances by 25 human pianists and two ‘score’ performances. These segments have lengths of 4 bars (624 annotations), 8 bars (10,219 annotations), and 16 bars (1809 annotations). The two ‘score’ performances, extracted directly from the original MusicXML score, serve as control groups for comparison with the human performances46,47. These are named ‘Score’ and ‘Score2’, with ‘Score’ including more musical notations (e.g. dynamics, legato) to mimic human-like performance, and ’Score2’ featuring fewer such elements, resulting in a more mechanical sound.

The annotations are ratings scored by 53 different annotators across 19 distinct labels. Specifically, there are 4076 annotations for Schubert D.960 mv2 (2nd movement), 6,708 annotations for Schubert D.960 mv3 (3rd movement), 624 annotations for Schubert D.935, and 1,244 annotations for Beethoven WoO.80. The average number of annotations per performance segment is 10.52, with a standard deviation of 3.62. Musical piece-named columns (D.950 mv2 to WoO.80) of Table 2 show the mean and standard deviation of the evaluations by the annotators for each performance, based on the 19 features. The visualization of the mean and standard deviation is in Supplementary Fig. S2. The total ratio of “I don’t know” annotations is 7.3% (921 out of 12,652), with the specific counts detailed in Table 2. Detailed statistics for each piece of music and data quality control are introduced in Supplementary Notes A.

Inter-annotator agreement is assessed using ICC(Intraclass Correlation Coefficient), which is a descriptive statistic widely used for quantitative measurements, describing how strongly units in the same group resemble each other48 (ICC(1,1) and ICC(1,k) of Table 2). ICC evaluation models are categorized based on the selection of annotators and data. For example, one-way random evaluation is used when each subject is measured by a different set of k randomly selected annotators, represented as ICC(1,k). Since each segment was annotated by a different set of random annotators, a one-way random evaluation was deemed suitable49. The reliability of both single ratings and averages was compared by calculating ICC(1,1) and ICC(1,k) for each label. The results in Table 2 show that ICC(1,1), single measure reliability, is “poor”, while ICC(1,k), average reliability, is “excellent”50,51. These findings suggest that individual perspective on music is subjective and may not fully capture the entirety of music, but when averaged, they converge towards a more widely agreed-upon perception of music.

Models and experiments

Benchmark models

We design experiments to establish benchmark performances and analyze current challenges to promote future research on PercePiano. We viewed the task as a regression task and aimed to predict 19 perceptual features from piano performance MIDI data. The conventional baseline for music performance understanding involves MidiBERT, processing a given MIDI input through a pre-trained network. In this section, our contribution is to propose score-aligned representation over conventional representation for performance evaluation and introduce a novel aligning framework.

Motivations

Our exploration into enhancing the baseline model began by pinpointing the inadequacies of conventional MIDI representations, particularly when relying solely on performances and lacking reference to the original score information. The limitations of conventional representations become apparent due to inherent noise in the information, such as timing extracted from performances. This makes it insufficient for capturing the richness of musical semantics, which is critical when aiming for a perceptual understanding of the performance. In contrast, the musical score provides the ground truth of precise and structured musical expression about the performance.

Therefore, we advocate for the adoption of score-aligned performance representation. When evaluating musical performances, a common practice is to refer to the musical score. This approach holds profound significance since the musical score provides an accurate foundation for the performance. Such a foundation can be divided into two categories. (1) The musical score provides precise expressive elements including timing and articulation. For instance, it encapsulates the specific timing when notes are played, their durations, and the composer’s intended velocities, offering a comprehensive and unambiguous representation of the musical expression. (2) The musical score embodies the hierarchical structure of the performance, where the perceptual boundaries for humans are largely determined52,53. In musical compositions, notes are arranged into voices, representing independent melodic lines or parts. These voices are further organized into beats, and measures are created by grouping a specific number of beats. Understanding this hierarchical structure is essential for perceiving music, as it allows us to interpret music beyond individual notes and comprehend the interplay between the different hierarchies. Our framework

Based on such motivation, we design an enhanced baseline, aligning the performance to score data to “ground” the performance for PercePiano. While previous works also explored the concept of alignment24,54, annotating alignments has been considered costly. In our target problem of piano performance evaluation, we have the advantage of access to rich and extensive score information. This allows us to effectively align the performance with the score and leverage the hierarchical structure encoded in the score data for improved music understanding.

We introduce our model based on hierarchical attention network (HAN)55 with the score-aligned features, to deal with the challenges involved in perceptual feature evaluation. Evaluation settings

Data

To derive an aggregated target value for model prediction, we averaged the annotation values from multiple annotators and normalized them between 0 and 1 for each label. Before aggregation, we removed any annotation values marked as “I don’t know.” Since no labels had all annotations marked as “I don’t know,” the size of the dataset remained unchanged.

For the evaluation, we employed both piece and performer splits. For the piece split, we grouped the performances at the piece level, where all performances of the same piece are considered as one group. We selected 14 music pieces as the test set, comprising 177 segments. We employed a 4-fold cross-validation strategy, ensuring that performances of the same piece do not appear in both the training and validation sets. For the performer split, the data of the two pianists for each music piece form the test set (180 segments), while the data of another two pianists serve as the validation set in a 4-fold cross-validation setting. We selected the best models from each fold regarding the validation set performance and reported the average performance on the test set.

Evaluation metrics

We utilized two commonly used metrics for the regression task: Mean Squared Error (MSE) and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2$$\end{document}R2. However, it is worth highlighting that there is relatively low agreement among annotators for individual segments, as evidenced by the low ICC(1,1) score presented in Table 2. In light of this, we introduce a novel metric, called range accuracy (RA), that takes into account the standard deviations of the annotation values for each label. Here’s how it works: if the model’s prediction falls within the range of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$value_{label}\pm \alpha \sigma _{label}$$\end{document}valuelabel±ασlabel, it receives a score of 1; otherwise, it receives a score of 0. The final metric is computed as the average of these scores. In this formulation, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma$$\end{document}σ represents the standard deviation, and we experiment with different values of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}α, including 0.1, 0.5, and 1. The significance test is conducted using a two-tailed paired t-test, where each fold in the cross-validation is treated as a separate sample.

Models

We evaluated the model’s performance using several baseline models. The first two baselines solely rely on performance data. The Bi-LSTM, a widely employed architecture for symbolic music understanding, serves as one of our baselines. Only the performance features are utilized as inputs and encoded at the note level. MidiBERT, a pre-trained transformer-based model known for surpassing recurrent neural networks, is another baseline in our evaluation. We incrementally integrate our framework with established baselines, to enhance their performance. SA denotes the score-aligned features and HAN denotes the hierarchical network. In the case of Bi-LSTM+SA, both aligned features and performance features are combined as inputs for note-level Bi-LSTM. Extending this, Bi-LSTM+SA+HAN incorporates Bi-LSTM units stacked to form HAN, also leveraging information on hierarchical positions. Lastly, to confirm the quality of our dataset, we evaluated a very simple model that only predicts the mean value (Mean Prediction) for each feature observed in the training set.

Configuration

In each hierarchical unit of our framework and Bi-LSTM baseline, the hidden size of Bi-LSTMs is selected from {64, 128, 256} and the number of layers from {1, 2}. The batch size is set to 8, and the learning rate is selected from {5e-5, 2.5e-5}. The output of HAN is selected from the measure level output. For MidiBERT, we set the batch size to 8 and selected the learning rate as 1e-5.

Code availability

The code for our framework is available at https://github.com/JonghoKimSNU/PercePiano57.

Discussion

Performances for each hierarchy

We delved into how perceptual features of piano performance, ranging from low to high, correlate with different hierarchy levels (note, voice, beat, measure). Each model implemented on the top of the Bi-LSTM baseline was trained and evaluated using outputs from varying hierarchical levels. To remove any potential bias from specific pieces or performers, we used a randomly split dataset. First, the line chart in Fig. 2 illustrates that the lower perceptual features such as timing, articulation or timbre are more sensitive to the inclusion of hierarchical information. This aligns with expectations, given the direct relationship of lower features with hierarchical information. Second, leveraging hierarchical information of note, voice, and beat level consistently enhances the performance across the whole level of perceptual features, indicated by the bar chart. The impact varies at the measure level, with mixed results. While low, mid-low, and high-level features demonstrate enhanced performance, mid-high features show no significant improvement. Necessity of RA metric

Upon scrutinizing the evaluation results, a discernible trend emerged: the model’s performance declined notably when applied to data characterized by high annotator disagreement. Figure 3 depicts the relation between performance (MSE) and standard deviation, using the Bi-LSTM+SA+HAN model. As illustrated in the Figure, this trend becomes evident and underscores the need for cautious interpretation of the model’s efficacy, particularly in contexts of perceptual performance evaluations where subjectivity is prevalent.

The rationale behind employing the RA metric becomes apparent in light of this observation. The RA metric is structured to penalize instances where the standard deviation is low, indicative of significant agreement among annotators. Essentially, the metric accentuates objectivity by penalizing instances where annotators largely converge, thereby highlighting potential inaccuracies in the model’s predictions. Conversely, it accommodates a higher level of subjectivity, as evidenced by cases with higher standard deviation. This nuanced metric aligns with the inherent challenges presented by subjective aspects in the annotated data, offering insights into the model’s performance across varying levels of annotator agreement.Fig. 3The relation between standard deviation and performance measured by MSE. Remaining errors

To better identify factors hindering the model’s predictive ability, we performed qualitative analysis by selecting performances with high prediction errors. We selected the performances where the Mean Squared Error (MSE) exceeded 0.1. Through attentive listening and examination, we categorized these performances into distinct scenarios that present challenges for computational models.

First, performances that exhibited disparity in articulation, dynamics, or pedaling degree between the right and left hands proved to be difficult for the model. The model’s focus on one hand over the other could lead to differential scoring, adding to the complexity of the learning task. Second, we identified instances where contrasting phrases within a performance complicated the scoring process. If a single piece contained phrases that required distinct articulation or pedal usage, assigning a unifying score presented an inherent difficulty, which the model might have struggled to navigate. Lastly, we observed that overlapping themes within 16-bar pieces, where the performance atmosphere and tempo could abruptly change, posed prediction challenges. To further elevate the precision of our system’s performance evaluations, future work should consider expanding the dataset to provide a richer context or refining the selection of musical excerpts for evaluation.

Conclusion

We present PercePiano, a pioneering dataset designed for machine learning-based piano performance evaluation. Annotated exclusively by music experts, PercePiano deals with various levels of perceptual features that are essential in performance evaluation. We suggest a new metric to address the subjectivity issue in evaluating perceptual features. We further study the comprehension of the features and present an enhanced baseline. The results highlight the significance of aligning the performance with its original score for improved comprehension. We anticipate that this line of research opens up exciting possibilities for future systems in the realm of piano performance.
