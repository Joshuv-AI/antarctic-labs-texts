Unrevealing the characteristics of low-alcohol citrus juice cofermented with different lactic acid bacteria and Hanseniaspora uvarum
Authors: Qin Li, Yuting Wang, Jianlong Li, Ning Zhao, Kaidi Hu, Xiaolin Ao, Shujuan Chen, Yong Yang, Shuliang Liu, Aiping Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study examined the effects of co-fermentation with different lactic acid bacteria (LAB) strains and Hanseniaspora uvarum on the physicochemical properties, bioactivity, and volatile flavor components of citrus juice. During fermentation, three LAB strains and H. uvarum Yr3 maintained high viable cell counts. Most co-fermented groups showed significantly higher DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging activities, reaching 538.10 μmol/L and 2685.41 μmol/L, respectively. Co-fermentation of Lactiplantibacillus plantarum M3 and H. uvarum Yr3 (YM3) for 6 d increased flavonoid release by 2.52-fold compared to the control, outperforming mono-fermentation. Co-fermentation reduced alcohol content, with YM3 achieving the lowest level (1.18 %) versus 1.40 % in mono-fermentation. Co-fermentation also increased the diversity and content of volatile flavor compounds, contributing to a more favorable aroma in the fermented citrus juice. Additionally, co-fermentation received higher sensory scores, with YM3 performing the best. These strains show promise for producing low-alcohol citrus juices with enhanced nutrition and sensory attributes.

Introduction

Citrus fruits constitute one of the most significant economic crops globally and the largest category of fruit production worldwide. China boasts a citrus cultivation history spanning over 4000 years, making it the country with the longest recorded tradition of citrus farming in the world. Fresh citrus fruits are rich in a variety of functional components, including vitamin C, carotenoids, flavonoids, and dietary fiber (Gao et al., 2017; Khalid et al., 2019; Padayatty & Levine, 2016; Saini et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2015). Compared with other fruits, fresh citrus fruits can be stored for a longer period. However, prolonged storage can lead to issues like mould growth, uneven peel colouration, and quality deterioration, thereby impacting the overall quality of fresh citrus fruits. Therefore, conducting deep processing of citrus fruits can not only expand the market for fresh fruits and reduce storage demands but also enhance the value of citrus products.

Citrus juice is an optimal medium for the fermentation of lactic acid bacteria (LAB) and yeast, offering synergistic effects that provide various nutritional advantages. These benefits include enhancing intestinal health, boosting human immunity, stimulating the synthesis of bioactive compounds, and reducing the risk of cancer and cardiovascular diseases (Garcia et al., 2020). LAB have been extensively utilized in food fermentation and positively influence human health. Researches indicated that the application of LAB in mulberry juice fermentation significantly increased total phenolic content, total flavonoid levels, and antioxidant capacity, as well as the conversion of anthocyanins (Wang et al., 2022). Furthermore, Omedi et al. (2019) demonstrated that dragon fruit fermented by LAB released a variety of antifungal phenolic acids. Yeast can metabolize sugars into alcohol and carbon dioxide under anaerobic conditions, a process that is commonly harnessed fruit wine production. However, due to the attention to healthy diets, the demand for low-alcohol fruit wines has increased. Non-Saccharomyces yeasts produce less alcohol and contribute to flavor formation by producing glycerol, higher alcohols, esters, and other compounds during fermentation, as well as releasing aroma components through glycosidases activity (Han et al., 2021; Sun et al., 2023). In addition, different non-Saccharomyces yeast strains demonstrate distinct acid-regulating effects during fermentation. Several non-Saccharomyces yeasts, such as Saccharomyces (S.) thermotolerans, Hanseniaspora (H.) uvarum, S. orientalis, S. mergens, Zygosaccharomyces bisporus, and Pichia fermentans, exhibit the potential to degrade organic acids (Boye et al., 2021). Sequential inoculation of H. uvarum HL-S-9 and S. cerevisiae CL2 can effectively decrease the concentrations of tartaric acid, lactic acid, and malic acid in wine (Wang, Chen, et al., 2023). Whereas, some non-Saccharomyces yeasts have been found to increase the total acid content during fermentation (Blanco et al., 2021).

Fruit juice fermentation has emerged as a significant research focus in recent years. Presently, most studies focus on fermentation substrates and strains selection, as well as refining fermentation processes. Additionally, numerous investigations have explored the effects of LAB fermentation on juice quality or the enhancement of wine flavor through non-Saccharomyces yeasts fermentation. Nevertheless, limited research has been conducted on the co-fermentation of LAB with non-Saccharomyces yeasts in fruit juice fermentation to improve product quality and sensory attributes. Therefore, in this study, different LAB strains were cocultured with H. uvarum Yr3 to ferment citrus juice, and their effects were investigated. These findings are anticipated to provide insights into the modern deep processing of citrus, thereby fostering diversified advancements in the citrus industry.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

Citrus reticulata was procured from the Fruit Wholesale Market in Ya'an City, Sichuan Province, with its origin traced to Meishan, Sichuan. Fresh citrus fruits that were unharmed, undamaged, and unspoiled were carefully selected. The orange peel and pith were removed and then blended into a pulp. The resulting fruit pulp was mixed uniformly and subsequently divided into portions for analysis (pH 3.73; total acid: 8.60 g/L; reducing sugar: 46.60 g/L; total sugar: 85.29 g/L). The samples were stored at −20 °C in a refrigerator for further use. Folinol, Trolox, and DNS reagents were procured from Beijing Soleibao Technology Co., Ltd. Gallic acid, rutin, succinic acid, tartaric acid, citric acid, and ascorbic acid standards were procured from Shanghai Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The acetic acid standard was procured from Putiantongchuang (Shenzhen) Technology Co., Ltd., and the lactic acid standard was obtained from Dr. Ehrenstorfer, Germany.

Strains and citrus juice fermentation

Lactiplantibacillus (Lp.) plantarum M3, Levilactobacillus (Lv.) brevis M11, Lacticaseibacillus paracasei (Ls.) J8, and H. uvarum Yr3 were used for the fermentation of citrus juice. These LAB strains were derived from spontaneously fermented fruit juices and were chosen based on their acid production and growth characteristics in citrus juice for 48 h. There was no antagonism between each LAB strain and H. uvarum Yr3 (data not shown).

Each activated strain was cultured to logarithmic phase, harvested by centrifugation at 4 °C (5000 g, 10 min) and washed twice with sterile physiological saline. Following the washing step, cells were inoculated into 150 mL of citrus juice. The fermented citrus juice samples were set as follows: (1) CK, blank control; (2) M3, Lp. plantarum M3; (3) M11, Lv. brevis M11; (4) J8, Ls. paracasei J8; (5) Yr3, H. uvarum Yr3; (6) YM3, Lp. plantarum M3 and H. uvarum Yr3; (7) YM11, Lv. brevis M11 and H. uvarum Yr3; (8) YJ8, Ls. paracasei J8 and H. uvarum Yr3. Each strain was adjusted to a final concentration of 2 × 106 CFU/mL. After inoculation, the thoroughly mixed citrus juice was incubated at 30 °C for 6 d without shaking.

Changes in pH, total acid content and viable cell counts during fermentation

The pH of the fermented citrus juice was measured using a pH meter (PHS-3CB, Shanghai Yueping Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd.). The total acidity was determined by titrating with 0.1 mol/L NaOH solution, and the result was expressed in terms of lactic acid equivalents. For microbial analysis, one milliliter of fermentation broth was gradiently diluted, and 100 μL of the diluted solution was spread onto MRS agar and PDA agar, respectively. The plates were incubated at 37 °C and 30 °C for 48 to 72 h, after which the colonies of LAB and yeast were enumerated.

Changes in total phenolic and total flavonoid contents during fermentation

The total phenolic content in the fermentation broth was determined using Folin–Ciocalteu's method with reference to the report by Cai et al. (2022). The total flavonoid content in the fermentation broth was determined using the aluminum nitrate colorimetric method, as reported by Liu et al. (2020).

Changes in superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity, DPPH and ABTS free radical scavenging rates during fermentation

The SOD activity was determined following the method described by Cai et al. (2022). Specifically, 2.35 mL of solution A (0.1 M Tris-HCl buffer with 1 mM EDTA-2Na), 1.80 mL of distilled water, 200 μL of fermentation broth, and 0.15 mL of solution B (4.5 mM pyrogallol-HCl solution) were combined in a 10 mL colorimetric tube. After thorough mixing, the mixture was transferred to a cuvette, and absorbance values at 325 nm were measured both initially and after 1 min.

Four milligrams of DPPH was accurately weighed and dissolved in ethanol. The solution was adjusted to 100 mL, and was used immediately. A mixture of 5 μL fermentation broth and 195 μL DPPH-ethanol solution was incubated in the dark for 30 min. The absorbance at 517 nm was measured with a microplate reader. The standard curve was constructed by plotting the Trolox concentration against the absorbance. The DPPH radical scavenging capacity of the sample was determined using the standard curve equation and expressed in μmol/L (Madjirebaye et al., 2022).

A total of 20 mL of 7 mmol/L ABTS solution was added to 352 μL of 140 mmol/L potassium persulfate to prepare the ABTS stock solution. The mixture was stored in the dark for 16 h. Prior to analysis, the solution was diluted with 0.2 mol/L phosphate buffer saline (pH 7.2) to achieve an absorbance of 0.70 ± 0.02 at 734 nm. Five microliters of fermentation broth was mixed with 195 μL of ABTS solution and incubated in the dark for 8 min, after which the absorbance at 734 nm was measured. The standard curve was constructed by plotting the Trolox concentration against the absorbance. The ABTS radical scavenging capacity of the sample was determined using the standard curve equation and expressed in μmol/L (Wu et al., 2021).

Characterization of citrus juice after fermentation

Alcohol content

The alcohol concentration in fermented citrus juice was quantified using the potassium dichromate oxidation method. First, the fermentation broth (100 mL) was distilled to remove potential sugar interference, with the distillate collected in a conical flask. When the volume reached 65–75 mL, the distillate was transferred to a 100 mL volumetric flask, diluted to the mark, and thoroughly mixed. The mixture was diluted by a factor of 4. One milliliter of each sample was added to a test tube, followed by sequential addition of 2 mL of 2.0 % potassium dichromate and 1 mL of 98 % concentrated sulfuric acid. The mixture was mixed well after each addition, sealed with aluminum foil, and allowed to react for 10 min. The mixture was then shaken well and allowed to cool to room temperature. Distilled water was used as the blank. The absorbance was measured at 610 nm, and the alcohol content was calculated using a standard curve.

Organic acid content

The citrus juice broth was centrifuged at 8000 g for 15 min. The supernatant was collected and diluted twofold. The solid-phase extraction adsorption column was activated with 10 mL of methanol and 10 mL of ultrapure water. The diluted fermentation broth was passed through the column to remove impurities, and the effluent from the rear section was collected and filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane filter. The filtrate was analyzed by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) using an HICHROM ALLTECH OA-1000 column (300 mm × 6.5 mm, 9 μm). The mobile phase was 9 mM sulfuric acid, with a flow rate of 0.6 mL/min. The column temperature was 75 °C; the injection volume was10 μL, and the detection wavelength was 210 nm.

Volatile flavor components

Volatile flavor components of citrus juice were initially analyzed using electronic nose. The juice was centrifuged at 8000 g for 15 min, and the supernatant was collected. Then, 4.00 g of the supernatant was transferred to a 20 mL headspace vial. After equilibration at 50 °C for 30 min and cooling to room temperature, the sample was analyzed. Electronic nose parameters were set as follows: cleaning time, 120 s; collection time, 60 s; and flow rate, 1 L/min. The 18 sensor response substances of the electronic nose are detailed in Supplementary data Table S1.

Volatile flavor compounds were further identified using HS-SPME-GC–MS (GC–MS system model 6890 N/5975C, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), as reported by Liu et al. (2024) with modifications. Four mililiter juice was placed into a 20 mL headspace vial, to which 20 μL of a 10 μg/mL methyl heptanoate in methanol and 1.5 g of NaCl were added. A divinylbenzene/carboxen/polydimethylsiloxane (DVB/CAR/PDMS) fiber was exposed to the headspace of a sealed vial for 40 min at 60 °C for analyte extraction. An HP-5MS chromatographic column (60 m × 0.32 mm, 1 μm; Agilent Technologies, USA) was employed. The column was held at 40 °C for 3 min before being increased to 60 °C at a rate of 2 °C/min and maintained for 1 min. Subsequently, the temperature was raised to 120 °C at a rate of 4 °C/min and maintained for 1 min, followed by a final increase to 240 °C at a rate of 6 °C/min, with this temperature being sustained for 3 min. Compounds were identified using the NIST 20 mass spectral library and relevant literature, with volatile flavor compounds analyzed if their match factor exceeded 80. Methyl heptanoate (10 μg/mL, 20 μL) served as the internal standard for semi-quantitative analysis, and semi-quantitative values were determined by comparing the peak area of each compound to that of the internal standard.

Flavonoid-targeted metabonomics

One hundred mg of sample was extracted using 2 mL of 70 % methanol-water solution through ultrasonic extraction for 30 min, repeated four times. The combined extract was concentrated to near dryness at 35 °C using a rotary evaporator. The residue was dissolved the residue in 200 μL of 50 % methanol-water solution and transferred to a detection vial. The analysis employed ultra-high performance liquid chromatography (Vanquish, UPLC, Thermo, USA) coupled with high-resolution mass spectrometry (Q Exactive, Thermo, USA). Chromatographic separation was conducted on a Waters HSS T3 column (50 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm) with a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min at 40 °C. mobile phase A was ultra-pure water containing 0.1 % formic acid; phase B was acetonitrile with 0.1 % formic acid. Injection volume was 2 μL. Elution gradient: 0.0 min – 90:10; 2.0 min – 90:10; 6.0 min – 40:60; 9.0 min – 40:60; 9.1 min – 90:10; 12.0 min – 90:10. ESI source conditions: sheath gas, 40 arb; auxiliary gas, 10 arb; ion spray voltage, −2800 V; source temperature, 350 °C; ion transfer tube temperature, 320 °C. Analysis was conducted in negative ion mode using SIM from 100 to 900 m/z. Process data with TraceFinder software.

Sensory analysis

To assess the sensory quality of citrus juices, thirteen panelists with relevant experience and expertise evaluated the color, posture, aroma, and taste of the juices, guided by the criteria outlined in Supplementary data Table S2 (Su et al., 2018; Wang, Han, et al., 2021).

Statistical analysis

All the tests were conducted thrice, and the results are expressed as the means ± standard deviations. The data were processed using SPSS Statistics 27 software. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed within a 95 % confidence interval (P < 0.05), with mean comparison done using the LSD test. Graphs were generated using Origin 2023, GraphPad Prism 8, MEGA11, and SIMCA 14.1.

Results and discussion

Changes in pH and total acid content

Unfermented citrus juice presented an initial pH of 3.88 and a total acidity of 4.28 g/L. Ls. Paracasei J8 exhibited a remarkably robust acid production capacity. By the 6th day, the total acidity in J8 peaked at 15.32 g/L (Fig. 1). This may be attributed to the ability of the strain to maintain a consistently high viable count during both mono-fermentation and co-fermentation processes. Lp. plantarum M3 exhibited moderate acid production, reaching 12.90 g/L on the 6th day. Among the three LAB strains, Lv. brevis M11 demonstrated the lowest acid production, with a concentration of 11.12 g/L on the 6th day (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Changes in pH and total acid content during citrus juice fermentation. H. uvarum Yr3 showed significant potential for acid regulation, as reported by Boye et al. (2021). The total acid content of Yr3 initially increased but then decreased as fermentation progressed. The maximum total acid content was recorded at 5.56 g/L on the first day, subsequently decreasing to 4.63 g/L by the end of fermentation. This reduction is likely attributable to the acid-regulating properties of Yr3.

Growth of LAB and H. uvarum

As shown in Fig. 2, LAB proliferated extensively within the first 24 h, with all fermentation groups reaching over 8.0 lg (CFU/mL) by day one. Notably, Ls. Paracasei J8 reached over 9.0 lg (CFU/mL) and maintained a relatively high viable bacterial count throughout the subsequent fermentation period. The number of viable LAB in both M3 and M11 groups peaked on the second day at 8.70 lg (CFU/mL) and 8.59 lg (CFU/mL), respectively, followed by a slight decline. Compared to mono-fermentation, co-fermentation groups presented a slightly higher number of viable LAB, in line with pH and total acid content variances, potentially due to yeast-produced metabolic byproducts, such as vitamins and soluble nitrogen compounds that likely facilitated LAB growth (Adesulu-Dahunsi et al., 2020). Sieuwerts et al. (2017) demonstrated that under identical culture conditions with glucose ranging from 5 to 20 g/L or fructose ranging from 1 to 20 g/L, the viable count of LAB in Lp. plantarum and S. cerevisiae cocultures was significantly higher than in monocultures. The viable cell count of H. uvarum Yr3 in citrus juice peaked on the first day, followed by a gradual decline, and stabilized from the third to the sixth day. The number of viable cells in Yr3 consistently exceeded those in the three co-fermentation groups, potentially due to organic acids generated by LAB metabolism in the co-fermentation system, which may have inhibited the growth of H. uvarum. From the second to the sixth day, the viable count of H. uvarum was consistently higher in YJ8 compared to YM3 and YM11, likely reflecting differences in metabolite profiles among LAB strains.Fig. 2Changes in the number of viable lactic acid bacteria (A) and yeast (B) during citrus juice fermentation.Fig. 2

Changes in total phenolic content

Phenolic substances are essential secondary metabolites primarily present in fruits. These compounds exhibit significant biological activities that enhance human health. Fermentation, a traditional bioprocessing method widely used in the food industry, has the potential to increase the concentration of phenolic compounds and their nutritional value (Leonard et al., 2021). The variation in total phenol content in citrus juice during fermentation was illustrated in Fig. 3A. The total phenolic content in all fermentation groups sharply decreased on the first day, followed by stabilization trend or slight fluctuations. The total phenolic content in CK continuously decreased (from 520.54 mg/L to 447.25 mg/L), which was likely attributable to the oxidation and degradation of polyphenolic substances during fermentation conditions at 30 °C. On the second day, the total phenol content in M11 peaked at 538.40 mg/L, decreasing to 480.51 mg/L on the third day, and remaining relatively stable thereafter. The total phenol content in J8 consistently decreased throughout the entire fermentation period. By the fourth day of fermentation, co-fermentation groups YM3, YM11, and YJ8 exhibited increases in total phenol content to 498.99 mg/L, 455.26 mg/L, and 480.51 mg/L, respectively. This increase may have been attributed to the interaction between LAB and yeast, which influenced the metabolism of phenolic compounds. Towards the later stages of fermentation, the total phenol content in the M11, YM3, and YJ8 was significantly higher than that in CK, suggesting that mono-fermentation or co-fermentation with specific LAB strains helps preserve phenolic compounds stability and mitigate oxidation and decomposition.Fig. 3Changes of total phenolic content (A), total flavonoid content (B), and SOD enzyme activity (C) during citrus juice fermentation.Fig. 3

The total phenolic content of plant-based materials may either increase or decrease after fermentation, depending on factors such as fermentation substrate, fermentation time, and microbial strains (Xu et al., 2023). Hashemi et al. (2017) observed a decrease of approximately 50 mg/L in total phenol content when fermenting sweet lemon juice with Lp. plantarum for 48 h. In contrast, Kwaw et al. (2018) reported that the fermentation of mulberry juice with Lp. plantarum, L. acidophilus, and L. paracasei significantly increased the total phenol content. The increase in total phenol content can be attributed to the release of phenolic compounds from the food matrix through microbial metabolism, and the conversion of bound phenols into free phenols (Huynh et al., 2014). Conversely, a notable decrease in total phenol content may result from enzymatic or nonenzymatic oxidation, and the condensation of phenolic compounds with proteins, ultimately reducing measurable total phenol content (Leonard et al., 2021). The changes in polyphenol composition were also observed in the study by Wang et al. (2024), which showed that H. uvarum strains, compared to S. cerevisiae, reduced glycolysis and TCA cycle intermediates, consumed more polypeptides, amino acids, and nucleotides, altered polyphenolic ratios, and decreased indole and its derivatives.

Changes in total flavonoid content

Flavonoids, the largest class of plant secondary metabolites, are commonly found in fruits and vegetables. Dietary flavonoids exert antibacterial effects through various mechanisms, including membrane disruption, biofilm inhibition, cell envelope suppression, nucleic acid synthesis inhibition, electron transport chain inhibition, ATP synthesis inhibition, flavonoid-metal complex activity, and toxin inhibition (Luo et al., 2025). The major subclasses of flavonoids commonly found in citrus fruits include flavanones, flavones, flavonols, anthocyanins, flavan-3-ols, and chalcones (Barreca et al., 2020). The variation in the total flavonoid content during citrus juice fermentation was presented in Fig. 3B. The total flavonoid content in CK gradually decreased from 179.62 to 125.31 mg/L, potentially due to the susceptibility of flavonoids to oxidation and degradation (Biesaga, 2011). The total flavonoid content in CK, J8, and YM11 groups initially declined slightly, then gradually recovered. Other groups exhibited a continuous increase during early and middle fermentation stages, with a decline occurring only in later stages. The total flavonoid content in M3 group peaked at 215.05 mg/L on day 4 before decreasing, whereas Yr3 group demonstrated a consistent increase throughout the entire fermentation. Notably, YM3 presented a significant increase in total flavonoid content, stabilizing at 322.31 mg/L by the second day. After fermentation, the total flavonoid content in YM3 was 2.52 times higher than CK and 1.38 times higher than Yr3. This substantial increase could be attributed to the synergistic effects of Lp. plantarum and H. uvarum in promoting flavonoid release. Polyphenolic compounds comprise phenolic acids and flavonoids. The weak correlation between changes in total flavonoid and phenolic contents may be attributed to enzymatic transformations during microbial metabolism including glycosylation, deglycosylation, ring cleavage, methylation, and glycolipidation (Huynh et al., 2014). During lactic acid and alcoholic fermentation, flavonoids may form protein conjugates, potentially reducing total flavonoid content. Additionally, the presence of ethanol may enhance flavonoid solubilization, potentially increasing their measurable content (Tian et al., 2017).

Changes in SOD activity

SOD enzymes serve as crucial defence mechanisms for cells to counteract the toxic effects of oxygen free radicals, demonstrating notable antioxidant properties, anti-aging effects, and immunomodulatory capabilities (Liu et al., 2023). The SOD enzyme in CK sharply decreased during the early stage of fermentation and became undetectable by day 2. In contrast, SOD activity in J8, M3, and Yr3 groups gradually decreased throughout fermentation, at 11.08–12.68 U/mL by the end. The M11, YM11, and YM3 groups tended to increase and then decrease throughout the fermentation. SOD activity in YJ8 peaked at 29.64 U/mL on the fourth day and remained stable through day 6 (Fig. 3C). This finding aligns with Liu et al. (2023), who reported similar SOD activity (29.90 U/mL) in a fermented juice of bananas, broccoli, and wolfberries using Lp. plantarum and Pichia cluvialis. Compared with the rapid inactivation of SOD enzymes observed in CK, inoculated fermentation effectively preserved or increased SOD activity in citrus juice. This could be attributed to either the production of SOD enzymes through microbial metabolism or the acidic environment generated during fermentation, which facilitates the stabilization of SOD activity (Wang, Lai, et al., 2023). Certain LAB and yeasts, including L. casei, S. cerevisiae and L. curvatus, have been reported to produce SOD enzymes (Casaburi et al., 2016; Wang, Lai, et al., 2023). Notably, coculturing LAB with specific yeasts significantly increases SOD activity. For instance, when L. casei F18 and S. cerevisiae J14 were cocultured, their respective SOD activity increased to 198.22 % and 66.35 %, surpassing individual culture levels.

Changes in antioxidant capacity

The changes in antioxidant capacity of citrus juice during fermentation were illustrated in Fig. 4. The DPPH radical scavenging ability of CK decreased steadily throughout fermentation. In contrast, the DPPH radical scavenging abilities of YM3, YJ8, and Yr3 groups increased during the initial fermentation stages. Notably, YM3 presented the highest DPPH radical scavenging ability, reaching 538.10 μmol/L on the first day and maintaining superiority throughout the subsequent fermentation period. The DPPH free radical scavenging capacity in YM11 decreased to its lowest value (168.36 μmol/L) on the second day and then increased to a peak (472.65 μmol/L) on the fourth day. In contrast, M3 and M11 exhibited minor fluctuations in DPPH free radical scavenging capacity, whereas J8 demonstrated a consistent decrease throughout fermentation. With the exception of CK and M3, most fermentation groups showed an increase in antioxidant capacity against ABTS free radicals as fermentation progressed. Notably, both YM3 and YJ8 consistently exhibited high ABTS radical scavenging abilities. On the 5th day, the ABTS radical scavenging capacities of YM3 and YJ8 reached their peak values at 2664.86 μmol/L and 2685.41 μmol/L, respectively. In summary, YM3, YJ8, and M11 groups exhibited superior scavenging abilities for DPPH and ABTS free radicals, likely due to the high total flavonoid content in YM3, increased SOD enzyme activity in YJ8, and substantial total phenolic content in M11.Fig. 4Changes in antioxidant capacity during citrus juice fermentation. Characterization of fermented citrus juice

Alcohol content

The alcohol content in fermented citrus juice can significantly influence its taste and aroma, thereby impacting consumers sensory experiences. The alcohol contents in mono-fermentation and the co-fermentation groups of H. uvarum Yr3 were presented in Supplementary data Fig. S1. Yr3 exhibited the highest alcohol content at 1.40 %, while YM3 presented the lowest at 1.18 %, which was below the low-alcohol fruit wine standard (0.5 % ∼ 7.0 %) specified in China's QB/T 5476–2020 and approached the alcoholic beverage threshold (1.2 %) defined in EU Regulation No.1169/2011 (Patra et al., 2023). Optimization of fermentation conditions, such as inoculation amount and fermentation time, can further reduce alcohol content. Notably, compared to Yr3, alcohol content decreased in all co-fermentation groups to varying degrees, consistent with the yeast count results: YM3 had low viable yeast counts, while Yr3 remained high. Co-fermentation of Torulaspora delbrueckii and H. uvarum also led to a reduction in alcohol content, although not significantly (Renault et al., 2015).

Organic acid contents

The organic acid contents in each citrus juice were presented in Supplementary data Table S3. The organic acids in CK primarily consisted of citric acid, succinic acid, and ascorbic acid, with citric acid being the most abundant. Following inoculated fermentation, lactic acid and acetic acid were detected. It was observed that higher levels of acetic acid were associated with lower yeast viability. This suggested that the acidic environment produced by LAB during co-fermentation inhibited yeast growth, leading to reduced alcohol production. Meanwhile, total content of organic acids was increased (P < 0.05), with J8 an YJ8 being the highest. The highest lactic acid yield was observed in J8, at 1221.15 mg/L, while the lowest was recorded in M11. In addition, H. uvarum also produced a small amount of lactic acid, although considerably less than LAB. M11 exhibited the highest acetic acid production at 178.14 mg/L, while J8 group showed the lowest at 37.63 mg/L. Previous studies indicated that non-Saccharomyces yeasts can promote malolactic fermentation by creating more favorable conditions, such as lower SO2 concentrations and reduced levels of medium-chain fatty acids during fermentation (Balmaseda et al., 2021).

With the exception of M11, citric acid content decreased in the other fermentation groups. Notably, Lp. plantarum M3 presented the greatest citric acid metabolic ability, consuming 97.75 % of the citric acid in M3, while consuming 94.85 % in YM3. These findings aligned with the research of Hashemi et al. (2017), which demonstrated that Lp. plantarum can utilize citric acid as a carbon source for growth and metabolism. LAB metabolize sugars, citric acid, and other nutrients in fruit juices via pathways like glycolysis and tricarboxylic acid cycle, producing lactic acid and acetic acid. These findings supported observations of reduced citric acid content and increased lactic acid and acetic acid levels in fermented citrus juice.

The succinic acid content was 226.74 mg/L in CK, 45.92 mg/L in M3 and 54.53 mg/L in YM3, indicating the role of Lp. plantarum M3 in succinic acid transformation. Additionally, the succinic acid content in M11 and Yr3 were similar to CK, but notably decreased in YM11, potentially due to interactions between Lv. brevis M11 and H. uvarum Yr3. YM11 exhibited the most significant decrease in ascorbic acid content (12.40 %) compared to CK, followed by M11 (11.20 %). Conversely, J8 presented an increase in ascorbic acid content (6.00 %). The reduction in ascorbic acid content in citrus juice may be attributed to the presence of ascorbic acid oxidase. Furthermore, the oxygen within the fermentation system played a role in oxidative degradation of ascorbic acid (Kaprasob et al., 2017). Overall, tartaric acid content showed minimal variation across each group.

Electronic nose analysis

The response values of electronic nose sensors in citrus juice (Supplementary data Table S4) were used to generate a characteristic odor radar map. As illustrated in Fig. 5A, significant variations were observed. Among these sensors, the VIP values of S1, S4, S5, S6, S9, S14, S15, S16, S17, and S18 exceeded 1, signifying the critical role of these odors reflected by the response values of the corresponding sensors. Notably, the sensor responses in the fermentation groups containing H. uvarum Yr3 surpassed those in mono-fermentation via LAB and CK. Volatile flavor components

The electronic nose mimics human olfaction, enabling accurate and rapid differentiation of samples based on their odor profiles. However, it lacks the capacity to quantitatively identify odor substances. In contrast, GC–MS enables precise quantitative analysis of volatile flavor compounds in food. The integration of GC–MS with electronic nose offer more comprehensive, reliable, and scientifically robust insights into food flavor (Jin et al., 2023). A total of 58 volatile flavor compounds, comprising alcohols, esters, phenols, aldehydes, ketones, acids, aromatics, terpenes, and alkanes, were identified via GC–MS (Supplementary data Table S5). As shown in Fig. 6, Yr3 contained the highest number, with 34 compounds, while CK had the fewest, with 20 compounds. Meanwhile, the co-fermentation of LAB and H. uvarum increased the diversity and content of volatile flavor compounds, contributing to a favorable aroma to the fermented citrus juice. Alcohols, esters and phenols were found to be the most abundant. Non-Saccharomyces yeasts produce enzymes such as lipase and protease to release esters, alcohols, acids, and phenylalanine derivatives. Additionally, β-glucosidase plays a role in releasing free terpenes by hydrolysing glycosidically bound terpenes, thus enhancing aroma complexity (Lin et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2016).Fig. 6The relative content (A) and number (B) of different volatile flavor compounds types in fermented citrus juice.Fig. 6

The relative content (A) and number (B) of different volatile flavor compounds types in fermented citrus juice.

Non-Saccharomyces yeasts are known for producing high levels of esters, particularly ethyl acetate (Lai et al., 2021), likely due to the reaction of acetyl-CoA with alcohols derived from the degradation of amino acids, carbohydrates, and lipids, catalyzed by alcohol acetyltransferase (Martin et al., 2018). Under the influence of H. uvarum Yr3 fermentation, ethyl acetate was the most abundant ester in fermented citrus juice, followed by phenyl ethyl acetate. Among the inoculated fermentation groups, YJ8 presented the highest ethyl acetate content, at 170.23 μg/L, followed by YM3 (114.97 μg/L) and YM11 (91.16 μg/L). Notably, Yr3 had the lowest at 26.72 μg/L. Ethyl acetate, a key ester found in fruit wine, contributes to its pleasant fruity aroma (Chen et al., 2023) with its levels being influenced by both the strains chosen and the grape varieties used (Hu et al., 2018; Martin et al., 2018). Phenyl ethyl acetate, with a rose floral scent and apple-like aroma, ranged from 7.43 to 44.72 μg/L in the fermentation group containing H. uvarum Yr3, significantly contributing to the flavor development of citrus juice. In terms of total ester content, YJ8 presented the highest level at 241.47 μg/L, followed by YM3 at 154.96 μg/L, YM11 at 117.57 μg/L, and Yr3 at 45.91 μg/L. Ester production primarily occurs through esterification reaction between acids and alcohols. Among the co-fermentation groups, Ls. Paracasei J8 presented the highest acid production, followed by Lp. plantarum M3, and Lv. brevis M11, aligning with the respective total ester content levels.

Higher alcohols are microbial metabolites produced via the Ehrlich and Shikimate pathways through amino acid conversion or carbohydrate breakdown, serving as precursors for ester formation (Martínez-Avila et al., 2020). When total higher alcohols concentration is below 300 mg/L, they enhance the flavor complexity of fruit wines (de-la-Fuente-Blanco, A., Sáenz-Navajas, M.-P., & Ferreira, V., 2016). Yr3 exhibited the highest alcohol content at 395.75 μg/L. Co-fermentation groups showed varied decreases in alcohol content: YM3 at 207.35 μg/L, YM11 at 209.48 μg/L, and YJ8 at 264.82 μg/L. Phenylethanol was the most abundant alcohol in co-fermentation groups, imparting a rose-like fragrance (Moreira et al., 2011). Notably, isobutanol and n-pentanol levels in YJ8 were significantly higher than in other groups. Isobutanol contributes to an alcoholic taste with a grassy aroma. Additionally, trans-vanilol content in CK was 0.17 μg/L, but its concentration increased by 2.82 to 9.76 times after inoculated fermentation.

Volatile phenols contribute distinct flavors to various foods. The most abundant volatile phenolic compound in citrus raw materials was 2,4-di-tert-butylphenol, with CK containing 51.79 μg/L. In the fermented citrus juice, its content was increased. Nevertheless, a precise characterization of the flavor profile and sensory threshold of 2,4-di-tert-butylphenol remained elusive. Eugenol exhibits characteristic clove and anise-like aromatic properties, and demonstrates antioxidant, antibacterial, and anti-inflammatory activities (Lenti et al., 2022). Lp. plantarum M3 effectively increased eugenol level in citrus juice, with no detection in other in other fermentation groups. Moreover, inoculated fermentation increased the concentrations of 2-methoxy-4-vinylphenol and thymol in citrus juice.

Model quality is evaluated using R2 and Q2, which indicate accuracy and predictive performance (D. Dhall et al., 2020). Targeted metabolomic analysis of flavonoids

Targeted quantitative analysis of flavonoid composition and levels was performed in the YM3, M3, and Yr3 groups. A total of 35 flavonoid compounds were identified and quantified, as shown in Supplementary data Table S6. Rutin, naringenin, protocatechuic acid, hesperetin, and vanillic acid were the main flavonoids in fermented citrus juice. Significant changes in flavonoid levels were observed after mono- or co-fermentation with Lp. plantarum and H. uvarum. The total of 35 identified flavonoids in Yr3 was significantly higher than in YM3 and M3. H. uvarum Yr3 had a more significant effect on flavonoid accumulation than Lp. plantarum M3. During mono-fermentation of H. uvarum Yr3, levels of vanillic acid, salicylic acid, trans-cinnamic acid, caffeic acid, and p-coumaric acid were significantly higher than in the YM3 and M3 groups. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis showed that this may be attributed to the trans-cinnamic acid 4-monooxygenase in H. uvarum, which promoted the synthesis of coumaric acid and triggered a cascade of downstream metabolic reactions, thereby increasing the levels of compounds such as caffeic acid, trans-ferulic acid, and sinapic acid (Fig. 8). The total flavonoid content of YM3 was 1.38 times that of Yr3, suggesting that the flavonoid content in the YM3 co-fermentation group was influenced by both Lp. plantarum and H. uvarum, and unknown flavonoids may have been released. Future research will focus on identifying these compounds, along with their structures, functions, and release mechanisms. Meanwhile, the characteristics of employed strains and their interactions also need further study. Similarly, La. plantarum bLp16 and S. cerevisiae ySc16 converted only part of the ferulic acid into 4-allyl cinnamic acid in mono-fermentation, but when co-cultured, nearly all ferulic acid was converted, with a large amount of dihydroferulic acid produced (Boudaoud et al., 2021). Sensory evaluation

As illustrated in Fig. 9, the sensory score of CK was 68.75, while all inoculated fermentation groups exhibited significantly higher sensory scores than CK, indicating that inoculated fermentation markedly enhanced the sensory quality of citrus juice. Compared to LAB mono-fermentation, fermentation groups involving H. uvarum achieved superior sensory scores, likely due to the production of volatile flavor compounds such as alcohols and esters by H. uvarum, which positively influenced the aroma profile of citrus juice. Co-fermentation groups exhibited lower total acidity than LAB mono-fermentation group, thereby reducing the juice's sourness and imparting a more balanced sweet and sour taste. Among the co-fermentation groups, YM3 achieved the highest sensory score of 87.00, followed by YM11 with 85.33, and YJ8 with 82.92. Conclusions

Three different LAB strains (Lp. plantarum M3, Lv. brevis M11, and Ls. Paracasei J8) were individually fermented and cofermented with H. uvarum Yr3 in cirtus juice. The investigation revealed that the inoculated fermentation significantly altered the physicochemical properties, bioactivity, and flavor profile of citrus juice, increasing its antioxidant activity and the composition and content of volatile flavor compounds. H. uvarum Yr3 played a key role in shaping the aroma profile of the fermented citrus juice by contributing rose and fruity-like aromas through the production of phenylethanol, ethyl acetate, and ethyl phenylacetate. Yr3 had the highest total of 34 volatile flavor compounds, followed by YM3 with 33. Furthermore, YM3 achieved the highest sensory score of 87.00, while CK scored 68.75. These findings provide a solid theoretical basis for the advancement of fermented fruit and vegetable juice products.
