Computed tomography as a solution to skeletal collection limitations: exploring cranial variation in the South African population
Authors: Gabriele Christa Krüger, Richard L. Jantz, Ericka N. L’Abbé
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Skeletal collections have provided the foundation for forensic anthropology in South Africa, offering invaluable reference material for developing and testing identification methods. However, these collections remain limited in both size and representativeness. Many are historically biased, restricted by donation practices, and unable to reflect the demographic and biological diversity of the country’s modern population. Indian South Africans, in particular, are notably absent due to cultural and religious beliefs that discourage body donation. This study explores the use of computed tomography (CT) as an alternative to overcome these limitations. A total of 408 high-resolution head CT scans representing Black, Coloured, Indian, and White South Africans were analysed using inter-landmark distances and geometric morphometrics. Measurement repeatability was high, with both intra- and inter-observer errors falling well within accepted forensic thresholds (< 2 mm; <3.5%). Significant population-level differences in cranial form were identified (p < 0.01). Black South Africans were consistently the most clearly differentiated group, while Coloured and Indian South Africans showed the greatest overlap, reflecting shared population affinity and historical admixture. Cross-validated classification accuracies ranged between 62% and 64%, indicating that both linear and shape-based analyses can capture relevant cranial variation. These results confirm that CT-derived data are reliable for examining cranial variation within contemporary South African samples and provide access to larger and more diverse datasets than traditional skeletal collections. This study establishes the first cranial reference data incorporating Indian South Africans and demonstrates how CT scanning can support the development of inclusive, population-specific standards for forensic anthropology in South Africa.

Introduction

Skeletal collections have long served as the foundation for forensic anthropology, providing the comparative material needed to develop and to validate methods for estimating biological profiles in unidentified individuals. In South Africa, collections such as the Raymond A. Dart, Pretoria Bone, and Kirsten Collections have been essential in establishing standards for sex and population affinity estimation [1–3]. However, these valuable resources are not without limitations. They remain historically biased toward certain demographic groups, are constrained in size by donation rates and storage capacity, and, being static by nature, fail to reflect the evolving diversity of the modern South African population. In particular, Indian South Africans are almost entirely absent. This has been linked to cultural and religious traditions that emphasise the respectful and timely handling of the deceased, often favouring cremation and discouraging post-mortem procedures or long-term retention of human remains for research purposes [4]. These limitations have direct implications for the accuracy and applicability of reference data used in population affinity estimation during forensic casework in the country.

Computed tomography (CT) scanning offers a practical alternative that can mitigate some of the ethical and logistical challenges associated with traditional skeletal collections. CT-derived three-dimensional (3D) cranial models are compatible with a range of analytical approaches including traditional osteometrics, inter-landmark distances (ILDs), and geometric morphometrics (GM), thereby allowing for comprehensive assessment of cranial size and shape variation [5–7]. Importantly, CT-based measurements have been shown to be accurate and repeatable within acceptable error margins when compared with data obtained from dry skeletal material [8–10]. As such, CT imaging may facilitate the development of larger and more contemporary population-relevant reference material.

Population affinity estimation remains an important component of the biological profile, yet existing South African standards do not adequately reflect the diversity of the population. Traditional morphoscopic approaches, such as Walker’s [11] cranial scoring system, are still widely used, but their performance declines when applied outside the populations from which they were originally derived, with classification rates dropping to as low as 31% in South African samples [12]. Osteometric approaches implemented through programs such as Fordisc 3.1 offer more statistically robust frameworks; however, they are similarly limited when applied to admixed or underrepresented groups [13–18].

Importantly, population affinity estimation in forensic anthropology is inherently probabilistic rather than deterministic. Human biological variation exists along continua, and morphological overlap between groups is expected, particularly in historically admixed populations such as those present in South Africa. Accordingly, the value of population affinity assessment does not lie in definitive classification, but in its ability to contribute probabilistic contextual information that may assist in narrowing the pool of possible matches during the identification process. The development of contemporary, population-relevant reference datasets is therefore essential to ensure that such assessments reflect present-day variation rather than historical skeletal collections that may no longer represent the individuals encountered in modern medico-legal contexts.

Within this context, methodological approaches that are able to model variation as continuous rather than discrete are particularly relevant. Integrating CT-derived 3D cranial data with a GM approach offers one possible way forward. By modelling variation as continuous rather than discrete, these methods allow for a more nuanced exploration of cranial diversity while reducing reliance on typological assumptions.

In South Africa, where identification is often complicated by incomplete documentation, migration, and individuals who may not be formally recorded in national systems, biological indicators derived from skeletal analysis may provide important contextual information when other avenues are limited. The country’s population structure reflects a complex history of migration, admixture, and social classification. Individuals who self-identify as Indian or Asian constitute a relatively small proportion of the national population (approximately 2.7%) [19], yet unidentified remains encountered in medico-legal contexts must still be assessed within frameworks that reflect the full diversity of the contemporary population. In practice, individuals may not always be readily assignable to socially recognised categories, further complicating identification efforts and highlighting the need for biological approaches that engage with present-day variation rather than historically derived reference samples.

Advances in medical imaging provide an opportunity to generate modern comparative samples using CT scans obtained from living or recently deceased individuals. Access to such material allows for the examination of cranial morphological variation within populations that more closely reflect those encountered in current medico-legal contexts. CT-derived cranial models offer a non-destructive means of capturing three-dimensional anatomical detail while enabling the inclusion of individuals who would otherwise be absent from traditional skeletal collections. In this way, imaging-based datasets may contribute to the development of updated reference frameworks grounded in present-day biological diversity.

The present study uses CT-derived cranial models to investigate population variation among contemporary South Africans, with particular emphasis on the underrepresented Indian South African population group. Cranial variation across Black, Coloured, Indian, and White South Africans was assessed using linear measurements, ILDs, and GM to evaluate their utility in modelling population-level cranial variation within a contemporary South African CT-derived sample. In doing so, this research highlights the potential of CT-based datasets to address longstanding limitations associated with skeletal collections and to provide contemporary comparative data that may inform future population-specific forensic research in South Africa.

Materials and methods

The study sample comprised 408 head CT scans representing Black, Coloured, White, and Indian South Africans, with equal numbers of males and females in each group (n = 51 per group). Only adults (≥18 years) were included to ensure complete cranial growth and development and individuals were selected according to the inclusion criteria and balanced to obtain equal representation of sex and population groups. Scans were retrospectively collected using convenience sampling from available hospital archives at three major South African hospitals: Steve Biko Academic Hospital (Pretoria, Gauteng), Groote Schuur Hospital (Cape Town, Western Cape), and Inkosi Albert Luthuli Central Hospital (Durban, KwaZulu-Natal). These sites were selected to reflect regional population distributions, with Coloured individuals sampled primarily from the Western Cape, and Indian individuals primarily from KwaZulu-Natal, while Black and White South Africans were sampled across all three regions. Ethical approval for the research was obtained through the University of Pretoria Research Ethics Committee (771/2018), and approval for access to the scans was approved through the National Health Research Database (KZ_201901_011 and WC_201901_014).

Only scans with a slice thickness below 0.625 mm were included to ensure isotropy and high-resolution reconstructions [20–22]. Scans showing pathology, trauma, or artefacts affecting cranial landmarks were excluded. Age distributions were not matched between groups, as the primary aim was to obtain sufficient numbers of adult individuals meeting the imaging quality and demographic inclusion criteria.

Hospitals did not consistently record population affinity in patient metadata. In cases where this information was unavailable, population affinity was cautiously inferred using recorded names and surnames. Only cases in which names were strongly and consistently associated with a particular population group were retained, and where any uncertainty existed, the scan was excluded from the sample. This approach has precedent in retrospective research where self-reported ethnicity is unavailable, with surname-based inference commonly used as a pragmatic proxy in epidemiological and population-based studies [23–25]. In the South African context, where historically structured population boundaries and relatively high levels of endogamy persist, this method may offer a cautious but workable means of approximating population affinity in the absence of recorded demographic data. Nevertheless, it does not constitute definitive classification and should be interpreted as an informed estimate, representing a limitation of the sample.

All data were anonymised, with only sex, age, and estimated population affinity retained for analysis.

Each scan was aligned to the Frankfurt Horizontal Plane using Avizo 9 (Thermo Scientific™; license: AVIZO.59228), with bilateral porion and inferior orbital margin landmarks used for alignment [26]. Cranial segmentation and volume rendering were performed using the Treatment and Increased Vision for Medical Imaging (TIVMI) software. This software applies a modified half-maximum height (HMH) thresholding protocol, which has been shown to reduce error rates compared to alternative segmentation approaches [27–30]. The resulting 3D cranial models formed the basis for all subsequent landmark placement and measurement.

Landmark collection and measurements

A total of 42 landmarks were digitised on each cranium, comprising 40 standard cranial landmarks [31] and an additional bilateral mid-zygomaticomaxillary suture landmark to capture malar projection. Three-dimensional landmark coordinates (x, y, z) were exported for analysis. Repeatability

Repeatability of landmark placement and derived measurements was evaluated using absolute and relative technical error of measurement (TEM and %TEM). Acceptable thresholds were set at <2 mm and <3.5%, respectively [17, 33, 34]. For intra-observer error assessment, landmarks were placed twice by the principal investigator on a subset of 15 crania, with a 14-day interval between landmarking sessions. For inter-observer error assessment, the same 15 crania were independently landmarked once by a second observer trained in cranial landmark identification using a standardised protocol. Bland-Altman plots were generated in R [35] to visualise intra- and inter-observer agreement [36].

For GM analyses, dispersion error was additionally calculated to assess landmark repeatability. Mean Euclidean distance was computed for each landmark across repeated placements by both observers.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics (means and standard deviations, and variance) were calculated for all variables. Differences between sex and population groups were assessed using analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s HSD post hoc tests [37, 38].

Linear discriminant analysis (LDA) with leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) was performed using FD3 software to evaluate classification accuracy, with forward stepwise variable selection employed to optimise model performance [14, 39].

For GM analyses, landmark coordinates underwent Generalised Procrustes Analysis (GPA) to remove variation related to size, orientation, and position [40, 41]. Shape variation was explored using principal component analysis (PCA) [42], Procrustes ANOVA with 1000 permutations and ordinary least squares [43], and 50-50 MANOVA (multivariate ANOVA) [44]. Canonical variate analysis (CVA) was conducted in MorphoJ and R to assess the discriminatory potential of shape variables [45].

Discussion and conclusion

Discussion

This study explored cranial variation among contemporary South African population groups using CT-derived data. The aim was not to assess the equivalence of CT and dry bone measurements, but rather to examine whether meaningful population-level patterns could be identified within a clinically derived sample using both inter-landmark distances (ILDs) and geometric morphometric (GM) approaches. In doing so, the study contributes to a growing body of work investigating cranial variation in contemporary populations represented in medico-legal contexts.

Measurement repeatability

Measurement repeatability was acceptably high for both ILDs and GM landmarks, with intra- and inter-observer error rates falling within accepted biological anthropology thresholds [17, 33, 34]. The low TEM and %TEM values, together with minimal landmark dispersion, indicate that the cranial models could be measured consistently within this study. The exclusion of a small number of less repeatable ILDs further strengthened the robustness of the analyses.

These findings demonstrate internal measurement consistency and support the use of CT-derived cranial models for examining morphological variation within the present dataset. While previous studies have reported comparability between CT and dry bone measurements, such similarity was not tested here. Accordingly, the results should be interpreted as reflecting variation within a CT-derived sample rather than direct correspondence with skeletal collections.

Population-level cranial variation

Both ILD and GM analyses revealed significant population-level differences in cranial form. Although classification accuracies were moderate (62–64%), they were well above chance and consistent with expectations given the inclusion of four biologically variable and historically admixed population groups. Patterns of misclassification were not random, but reflected structured overlap among particular groups. These structured patterns of overlap are consistent with the moderate classification rates observed and reinforce the expectation of biological continuity within historically admixed populations.

Across analyses, Black South Africans showed the greatest separation in analyses. Inter-landmark distance results indicated relatively longer cranial lengths and broader midfacial dimensions, while GM analyses highlighted differences concentrated in nasal height–related landmarks and posterior cranial vault morphology. In contrast, White South Africans tended to display larger cranial vault and base dimensions, with a relatively rounder vault shape.

Coloured and Indian South Africans demonstrated the greatest degree of overlap in both metric and shape-based analyses. Inter-landmark distance means placed these groups in an intermediate position between Black and White South Africans, and GM visualisations showed only subtle differences in anterior–superior facial and vault landmarks. The overlap observed between Coloured and Indian South Africans was especially pronounced in classification analyses, where misclassification frequently occurred between these two groups. White South Africans showed some overlap with both Coloured and Indian South Africans, although this was less extensive than the overlap observed between Coloured and Indian groups themselves.

These findings align with broader anthropological evidence that craniofacial morphology reflects a combination of genetic, environmental, and developmental influences [46–49]. The four population groups included in this study reflect different historical migration and admixture patterns within South Africa. These population groups also reflect historical demographic and sociopolitical processes, including migration, colonial settlement, and periods of legally enforced racial segregation that influenced patterns of admixture and gene flow. Black South Africans primarily derive their ancestry from Bantu-speaking populations, whereas White South Africans are largely of European descent. Coloured South Africans represent a highly admixed population with contributions from Khoesan, Bantu-speaking African, European and Asian populations, including documented Indian ancestry, while Indian South Africans largely descend from migrants from the Indian subcontinent and have remained comparatively genetically homogeneous. These differing population histories, together with varying degrees of admixture and gene flow, likely contribute to the observed pattern of greater overlap among Coloured, Indian and White South Africans and the relative distinctness of Black South Africans [50, 51]. These findings further support the population-specific nature of cranial variation and sexual dimorphism, as demonstrated in recent CT-based studies of modern South African populations [52, 53].

Rather than suggesting the existence of discrete biological groupings, these results highlight the importance of interpreting population affinity within a probabilistic framework. Contemporary South African populations exhibit considerable biological overlap, reflecting complex histories of migration and admixture. As such, the value of population affinity estimation lies not in definitive classification, but in its potential to contribute to a broader evidentiary framework that may assist in narrowing investigative possibilities.

Importantly, these interpretations relate to variation observed within the present CT-derived sample and should not be taken to imply direct equivalence with skeletal-based reference data.

Sex-specific population affinity

Significant sex differences were identified for the majority of ILDs and in GM shape analyses, supporting the decision to conduct sex-specific population affinity estimation. When analysed separately, females generally yielded higher classification accuracies than males. Despite these differences in performance, the same overall pattern of group separation and overlap was maintained across sexes: Black South Africans remained the most clearly differentiated group, while Coloured and Indian South Africans demonstrated the greatest degree of overlap.

The presence of significant sex-by-population interactions for multiple ILDs further addresses the importance of applying sex-specific standards in forensic analyses. Population affinity estimation that does not account for sexual dimorphism may obscure meaningful variation or reduce classification performance.

Implications for forensic anthropology in South Africa

This study highlights the value of examining contemporary cranial variation using clinically derived datasets. While traditional skeletal collections remain central to forensic anthropology, modern imaging archives offer an additional source of data reflecting present-day population structure. The findings here demonstrate that CT-derived cranial models capture patterned variation across South African groups.

Both ILD and GM approaches produced similar classification accuracies, demonstrating that traditional craniometric methods remain valuable alongside more advanced geometric techniques. Rather than replacing established metric approaches, GM analyses complement them by modelling shape variation in a continuous framework and visualising morphological trends. The comparable performance of the two methods suggests that forensic practitioners may employ either approach, depending on available resources and case-specific requirements.

The moderate classification rates observed here should be interpreted within context. With four population groups and documented admixture, perfect separation is neither expected nor biologically realistic. These findings should therefore be interpreted as reflecting patterns of likelihood rather than categorical assignment. Overlap between groups is expected in an admixed population, and misclassification is not indicative of methodological failure but of underlying biological continuity.

When used cautiously and in conjunction with other elements of the biological profile, probabilistic assessments of population affinity may still contribute meaningfully toward presumptive identification in cases where other demographic information is unavailable. In this context, population affinity estimation functions not as a tool for definitive classification, but as one component of a cumulative identification process.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. Variation in CT acquisition parameters across hospitals may introduce subtle differences in image resolution, although strict inclusion criteria were applied to minimise this effect. Population affinity was inferred in some cases using surname-based methods where hospital records were incomplete. While this approach has precedent in retrospective research and was applied cautiously, it does not constitute definitive classification and may introduce misclassification bias.

Furthermore, the population categories employed reflect socially and historically defined groupings used within the South African medico-legal system. As with all population affinity estimation research, these categories do not equate to discrete biological entities. Instead, they serve as pragmatic proxies for patterned biological variation within a specific sociocultural context.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that CT-derived cranial data can be used to investigate population-level cranial variation within a contemporary South African sample in a reliable and practical way. Both ILD and GM analyses identified significant differences among Black, Coloured, Indian, and White South Africans, with cross-validated classification accuracies between 62% and 64%. Black South Africans were consistently the most differentiated group, while Coloured and Indian South Africans showed the greatest morphological overlap, with White South Africans overlapping with both groups to a lesser extent. These moderate classification rates reflect the complexity of human biological variation and are consistent with expectations for historically admixed populations, reinforcing that population affinity is best understood in probabilistic rather than discrete terms.

The inclusion of Indian South Africans helps address a longstanding gap in South African forensic reference standards and contributes toward a more representative framework for interpreting population variation. Rather than offering definitive classifications, population affinity estimation should be viewed as providing contextual biological information that may assist in presumptive identification. The findings also highlight the importance of developing modern datasets that reflect individuals more likely to be encountered in current forensic casework, as reliance solely on historical skeletal collections may limit the relevance of anthropological assessments in populations shaped by recent demographic change. By utilising clinically derived CT datasets, this study demonstrates a practical approach to expanding forensic reference frameworks beyond the limitations of historical skeletal collections and toward data that more accurately represent contemporary South African population structure.
