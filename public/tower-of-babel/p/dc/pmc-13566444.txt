SEER sonorheometry enhances fibrinolysis detection during liver transplantation with heparin
Authors: Todd W Allen, Francesco Viola, Kenichi A Tanaka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhole blood viscoelastic tests (VETs) are key for detecting fibrinolysis during orthotopic liver transplantation (OLT). The Quantra System introduces a novel parameter, clot stability to lysis (CSL), for quantifying fibrinolysis. The objective was to assess CSL performance relative to established VET measures.MethodsData were drawn from a multicenter, prospective study of adults undergoing OLT, with Quantra and ROTEM delta testing performed in parallel. This sub-analysis focused on fibrinolysis-positive samples, emphasizing discordant findings between platforms.ResultsAmong 125 enrolled patients, 13 intraoperative samples involving the use of heparin showed discordant results. Calibrated automated thrombinography revealed that Quantra reagents produce slower, reduced thrombin generation compared to ROTEM, diminishing thrombin activatable fibrinolysis inhibitor (TAFI) activity. This enhances sensitivity of Quantra to detect fibrinolysis in these samples.ConclusionsQuantra's CSL parameter demonstrated improved detection of fibrinolysis in heparinized samples and may support individualized fibrinolysis management during OLT. Broader clinical validation is warranted.Keywords: clot stability to lysis, coagulation management, fibrinolysis, Quantra, viscoelastic testing

Introduction

Viscoelastic coagulation tests (VETs), such as thromboelastography and rotational thromboelastometry, are commonly used to diagnose systemic fibrinolysis during orthotopic liver transplantation (OLT). However, their sensitivity to fibrinolysis can vary, influenced by the specific reagents used and the presence of heparin and/or heparin neutralizers. The Quantra System (HemoSonics, LLC, Durham, NC) is a novel VET device that employs an ultrasound-based clot detection technology called sonic estimation of elasticity via resonance (SEER) [1]. The Quantra's QStat Cartridge outputs a parameter termed “clot stability to lysis” (CSL) designed to assess systemic fibrinolysis (Fig. 1a). The CSL parameter is calculated from two tissue factor (TF)-activated assays run concurrently in separate channels – one with tranexamic acid (CH2) and one without (CH3) (Fig. 1b). By comparing results from TXA-treated and untreated samples, the specificity for fibrinolysis detection is improved, for example, by excluding clot retraction [2].

In this short communication, we present a sub-analysis of OLT data previously published in [3] and aimed at demonstrating the sensitivity of the QStat CSL parameter.

Methods and results

A recent multicenter observational study [3] demonstrated a 90.3% clinical concordance for identifying hyperfibrinolysis (HF) between ROTEM delta (Werfen, Bedford, MA) EXTEM maximum lysis (ML, %) and QStat's CSL in samples collected during the anhepatic phase of OLT. The manufacturer provided guidelines were used for the classification of HF, that is, CSL <90% for QStat and EXTEM ML >15% for the ROTEM. A sub-analysis of the data revealed a discordance in the positive identification of HF when heparin was used during the procedure. The objective of this study was to evaluate the discordance between QStat CSL and EXTEM ML. The manufacturer-specific TF reagent concentrations are proprietary intellectual property; however, functional thrombin generation differences were characterized using calibrated automated thrombography (CAT).

As illustrated in Table 1, 13 samples were identified where heparin was used during the procedure. The CSL parameter on QStat identified HF in 100% (13/13) of the samples (CSL < 90%). However, EXTEM-ML measured >15% in 0% (0/13) of the samples, indicating no HF. FIBTEM-ML measured >15% in only one sample, likely due to an artifact in the test run. Interestingly, INTEM-ML was >15% in 77% (10/13) of the samples (Fig. 1c).

Discussion

To explain the possible reasons for the discordance, we considered several factors influencing the sensitivity and specificity of viscoelastic assays for measuring fibrinolysis. It has been demonstrated that TF activated assays are more sensitive to fibrinolysis than contact activated assays [4]. However, since both EXTEM and CSL are TF-based (and with heparin neutralization), TF activation alone does not fully account for the observed differences. Thus, we hypothesized that the observed differences were likely to be caused by variability in TF potency among these assays leading to different amounts of thrombin generated.

Our proposed mechanism is supported by converging experimental and clinical evidence. First, direct experimental data from calibrated automated thrombography (CAT) (Fig. 2) demonstrates that Quantra TF reagents generate significantly less thrombin with prolonged lag times compared to ROTEM reagents across multiple plasma conditions (normal, hemodiluted, and FVII-deficient) [5]. Second, established physiological principles support the pivotal role of thrombin in supporting endogenous antifibrinolytic activity. The activation of thrombin-activatable fibrinolysis inhibitor (TAFI), a transglutaminase, is diminished when thrombin generation is impaired. This may occur in conditions such as hemophilia [6], or with the use of anticoagulants like warfarin or low molecular weight heparin [7], potentially leading to enhanced fibrinolytic activity. Third, the clinical correlation in our data – that INTEM-ML (without heparin neutralization) showed fibrinolysis in 77% (10/13) of samples (Table 1) – supports the influence of anticoagulant effects in fibrinolysis detection.

Thrombin generation curves obtained by the CAT (calibrated automated thrombography) assay using the TF reagent from Quantra (solid lines) and ROTEM delta (dotted lines). Each experiment was performed in triplicate from normal (black), hemodiluted (blue), and FVII deficient (green) plasmas.

In OLT procedures, circulating heparinoids or the use of heparin decreases thrombin generation and subsequent TAFI activation to inhibit fibrinolysis. For this reason, viscoelastic assays that lack heparin neutralization, such as INTEM, can demonstrate the presence of fibrinolysis (Fig. 1c). Even after polybrene-mediated heparin neutralization in the Quantra QStat Cartridge, elevated circulating tissue factor pathway inhibitor (TFPI) may persist [8], suppressing TF mediated thrombin generation and increasing sensitivity to fibrinolysis. These findings collectively suggest that both TF potency and anticoagulant activity influence thrombin generation and fibrinolysis susceptibility in different viscoelastic platforms.

We acknowledge several important limitations of this analysis. First, the original multicenter study design did not include APTEM testing in the protocol, so direct comparison between Quantra CSL and the EXTEM-APTEM response is not available. While both Quantra CSL and EXTEM-APTEM assess fibrinolysis requiring TXA treatment, the key finding of our study – that Quantra CSL detected fibrinolysis in 100% (13/13) of heparinized samples while EXTEM-ML detected 0% (0/13) – demonstrates a clear discrepancy in sensitivity between these TF-activated assays performed under heparin-neutralized conditions. Future prospective studies should include APTEM data to provide a more complete comparison.

Second, while the CAT experiments shown in Fig. 2 establish the principle that Quantra TF generates less thrombin than ROTEM TF under standardized plasma conditions, these experiments do not perfectly replicate the clinical OLT scenario with heparinized blood and polybrene neutralization. The clinical samples presented in Table 1 serve as real-world evidence of the phenomenon, but additional CAT experiments using heparinized plasma with polybrene neutralization would more directly model clinical conditions and strengthen mechanistic understanding.

Third, an important limitation is that clinical management in the original study was guided by standard ROTEM results, and bleeding outcomes were not stratified by VET discordance patterns. Therefore, we cannot determine whether the fibrinolysis detected by Quantra CSL in these heparinized samples was clinically significant or whether antifibrinolytic therapy would have altered outcomes. Future prospective studies are needed to assess whether Quantra's enhanced fibrinolysis sensitivity translates into improved clinical decision-making and patient outcomes in the OLT setting.

In conclusion, the higher sensitivity to systemic fibrinolysis, combined with a confirmatory in vitro TXA response, positions the Quantra QStat system as a promising tool for individualized management of hyperfibrinolysis. Discordant results between TF-activated QStat and ROTEM delta assays are likely due to the different TF potencies. Further investigation is warranted to validate QStat's clinical utility across a broader population of OLT patients.
