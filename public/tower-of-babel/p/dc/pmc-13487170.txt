A macroscopic condensation theory for vibrational strong coupling effects
Authors: M. Elious Mondal, Sebastian Montillo Vega, Pengfei Huo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Recent experiments demonstrated the possibilities of modifying ground-state chemical reaction rates by placing an ensemble of molecules in an optical microcavity. This regime is commonly referred to as Vibrational Strong Coupling (VSC), and typically operates in the absence of any light source (in the dark). VSC causes reaction rate constant modifications, exhibits phase-transition type of behavior for equilibrium constant modifications, and the effect starts only when the collective Rabi splitting surpasses a threshold. Existing theoretical work often focuses on the single excitation pictures, centered around the idea of vibrational polaritons and dark states. We find that due to the many-body nature of VSC, most of the VSC effects can be potentially explained by forming a macroscopic condensation of vibrations. We theoretically demonstrate that by surpassing a critical Rabi splitting, the VSC system starts to macroscopically occupy one condensate state, which we believe could be the common explanation for VSC-induced effects.

Introduction

Collective coupling between matter excitations with photonic excitations inside an optical cavity forms polaritons1, a quasi-particle that is a superposition of both types of excitations. This field is often referred to as cavity quantum electrodynamics (QED)2–4, with the focus on investigating the fundamental properties of polaritons and how to control them. Historically, the field has focused on exciton-polaritons5–7 by coupling optical transitions of materials to cavity photonic excitations, including atoms4, molecules5–10, solid-state and nano materials11,12. Very recently (since 2015), Ebbesen13,14 and Simpkins15 have independently achieved the vibrational strong coupling (VSC) condition in cavity QED, by coupling the vibrational transition to an IR cavity mode, forming vibrational polaritons. Since then, there are a lot of intriguing phenomena reported under VSC, most of them operate without any optical pumping (under the dark16), and exhibit change of ground state chemical reaction rate constants, suppression17–21 or enhancement22–25, typically by 4–5 times. Note that these experiments are fundamentally different than those that use external lasers to probe the vibrational polaritons and single-excitation subspace26–28, which can be theoretically explained27,29–31. VSC effect under the dark has been used to selectively slow down one reaction out of two competing reactions 32, thus imposing the selectivities towards one of them33. VSC has been shown to change the equilibrium constant for charge transfer complexation reaction34, for conformational changes of chemical complex35, and exhibit a phase transition type of behavior for the equilibrium constant upon changing the molecular concentration 34,35. Similar phase transition-type behavior is also observed in the VSC-induced polymerization experiments25,36–38. It was speculated that the vibrational point group symmetry played a crucial role in the system to exhibit the VSC effect39. VSC has also been shown to significantly enhance the electrical conductance through originally non-conducting polymers40, and alter the glass transition temperature in polymer films41. Since there is no external pumping in these VSC experiments, it was speculated that the zero-point-vacuum field fluctuation must be responsible for the observed effects17.

While the experimental mysteries keep piling up, there is no well-accepted theoretical explanation42,43 for all of the above-mentioned VSC effects, despite progress on the VSC-induced rate constant modification theories for a few-molecules coupled to the cavity44–58, on VSC-influenced electron transfer reactions59,60, or for a special model of collective coupling between cavity mode, solvent, and reaction coordinate61–63. The most challenging effect to explain is indeed the collective coupling effect: how does weak light-matter coupling per vibration even modify chemical reactivities and properties? So far, neither classical Transition State Theory44,64–67 (or beyond classical rate theory68) nor quantum dynamics simulations54 (under the mean field limit69) could explain this effect, but there are several hypotheses to demonstrate the collectiveness in VSC21,29,31,46,54,59,61–63,70–81, typically for N ≈ 2 ~ 103 vibrations collectively coupled to the cavity.

The key challenge for any theory to explain the VSC effect (without any external laser pumping) is that the light-matter coupling strength gc is very weak, and only the collective light-matter coupling is large, which is often reported by the Rabi splitting \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\Omega }_{{{{\rm{R}}}}}=2\sqrt{N}{g}_{{{{\rm{c}}}}}$$\end{document}ΩR=2Ngc measured from linear optical spectra. Typically82, N ≈ 106–1012, and ΩR ≈ 100 cm−1, hence gc ≈ 5 × 10−5–5 × 10−2 cm−1. As a reference, room temperature thermal energy is kBT ≈ 208 cm−1 ≈ 26 meV. How can such a collective, delocalized light-matter coupling influence local chemical bond breaking, while the local light-matter coupling strength gc is so weak? Another theoretical challenge to understand the VSC effect is that if one uses the single excitation picture mentioned above, then the N–1 dense manifold of dark vibrational polariton states should dominate and diminish all of the VSC kinetics effects from the polariton states. The energy gap between \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left.| \pm \right\rangle$$\end{document}∣± and dark states is only ΩR/2 ≈ 50 cm−1, and the effective free energy for the dark states is lowered due to the entropic contribution from the dark state degeneracy, which is kBTln(N–1) ≈ 2760–5520 cm−1 (for N = 106–1012), making the dark states the most stable configuration in terms of free energy83. So one should expect that the dark state becomes the dominant manifold of states, and the chemical properties, including the reactivities and rate constant, should be identical to the outside cavity case.

The above-mentioned single excitation subspace pictures are largely borrowed from the excitonic/electronic strong coupling (ESC) case, and the mechanistic discussions are largely centered around the vibrational polaritons and dark states. However, we realize that ESC and VSC systems have very different energy scales compared to kBT. The key difference is the emitter frequency. For ESC, optical transition has a typical value of ℏω0 ~ 2 eV, and for VSC, ℏω0 ≈ 1000 cm−1 ≈ 124 meV. This means that under room temperature, the Boltzmann probability for the excitonic system to be in the excited state is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\bar{n}/N={e}^{-\beta \hslash {\omega }_{0}}\approx {e}^{-77}\approx 1{0}^{-34}$$\end{document}n¯/N=e−βℏω0≈e−77≈10−34, and for the range of N effectively coupled to each radiation mode in cavity QED, no molecules are in the excited states unless there is a laser excitation, and the single excitation/few excitation picture (polariton and dark states) is indeed a good mechanistic picture. On the other hand, for VSC, the Boltzmann probability for the vibrational DOF to be in the excited state is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\bar{n}/N={e}^{-\beta \hslash {\omega }_{0}}\approx {e}^{-5}\approx 6.7\times 1{0}^{-3}$$\end{document}n¯/N=e−βℏω0≈e−5≈6.7×10−3, so as large as 1/1000. For N = 106 molecules, that means \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\bar{n}\approx 1{0}^{3}$$\end{document}n¯≈103 of them are already in the excited states, due to the thermal distribution. As such, using the single/few excitation subspace picture in VSC is not appropriate. Upon coupling N = 1012 molecular vibrations to the cavity, one, in principle, needs to consider at least up to the billionth excitation subspace!

The above discussions made us realize that the key to unraveling the VSC mysteries lies in describing the many-body physics of it, not in the superposition of a one-excitation (one-body) picture described in Eq. (3). The condensed matter physics community has already provided well-established theories to understand intriguing phenomena, such as superfluidity and superconductivity. Weakly interacting bosonic particles can condense into one macroscopic state (bosonic Coherent state)84, which eventually explains superfluidity85. Weak interactions between pairs of electrons mediated through lattice phonon vibrations cause Cooper pairs to condense into a macroscopic state (Cooper pair Coherent state), through the Bardeen-Cooper-Schrieffer (BCS) mechanism86,87, which eventually explained superconductivity (the phonon-mediated kind). In the context of VSC, it is possible that weak light-matter couplings between the cavity mode and vibrations lead to effective interactions between two vibrations (phonons), and these many-body interactions lead to a new macroscopic state (just like the Coherent states in BCS or BEC), such that the VSC cavity QED system is dominated by that one macroscopic state at room temperature. We thus conjecture that in VSC, vibrations (described by hard-core bosons) condensed into one macroscopic state (under certain critical conditions) that dominates the behavior of the VSC systems and causes the mysterious effects observed experimentally17,35. As such, we believe that vibrational polaritons do not give the correct mechanistic picture to understand VSC-induced effects when there is no external laser pumping; rather, the macroscopic condensation into one state does.

In this work, we theoretically demonstrate that the weak interaction of vibrations and the cavity mode causes effective interactions among all vibrational DOFs that occur inside the cavity, using the Schrieffer-Wolff transform88. The resulting effective Hamiltonian (Eq. (14)) is mathematically isomorphic to the BCS-type effective Hamiltonian86,89. This suggests that we can use the standard BCS variational procedure to find the macroscopic ground state (for condensate), which we refer to as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left.| \widetilde{0}\right\rangle$$\end{document}∣0~ (Eq. (18)). This macroscopic state is a true many-body state, in stark contrast to the superposition of N-single-particle states in the single excitation subspace (Eq. (3)).

We further show that the macroscopic state \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left.| \widetilde{0}\right\rangle$$\end{document}∣0~ is stable compared to the normal thermodynamic state (which contains many microscopic states), in terms of free energy (when considering the dense manifold of vibrational excitations in the system). This \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left.| \widetilde{0}\right\rangle$$\end{document}∣0~ is also stable compared to the vacuum states of vibrations outside the cavity, in terms of both energy and free energy. In ref. 35, it was speculated that strong coupling of a significant fraction of the molecules at the onset of VSC appears to pull nearly all the molecules into one phase by dipolar interactions due to the zero-point field oscillations of the optical mode. Our theoretical analysis suggests that this phase transition corresponds to the normal phase to macroscopic coherent state transition. Our work thus provides a concrete theoretical foundation to support the early hypothesis in ref. 35. Despite the early theoretical work that hypothesizes a phase transition behavior in VSC74,76 or laser pumping-induced polariton condensation onto lower polariton state90 (similar to the exciton polariton condensation unpon laser driving), none of them suggest that the key mechanism of VSC is due to a macroscopic condensation due to the many-body interactions among vibrations mediated by cavity without laser pumping.

We further derived a simplified, analytic gap equation with approximations, giving rise to the condition (Eq. (40)) under which the condensation state is more stable in terms of free energy. Using detailed parameters from the Ebbesen NMR experiments, our theory provides a reasonable estimate of the critical Rabi splitting required to achieve the condensate phase, in agreement with the experimental values. Our theory predicts that the critical value of the Rabi splitting scales as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\Omega }_{{{{\rm{R}}}}}\propto \sqrt{\hslash {\omega }_{0}+{k}_{{{{\rm{B}}}}}T}$$\end{document}ΩR∝ℏω0+kBT, meaning that with lower vibrational frequency ω0 and lower T, it requires a smaller Rabi splitting to achieve the condensation. This prediction can be tested using the same Ebbesen NMR experimental setup, with isotope effects (replacing C–H vibrations to C–D vibrations) or substitutions (replacing C–H vibrations to C–F vibrations, which also have NMR signatures). Due to the macroscopic condensation, there is an additional energy penalty Δn (the gap equation) for each DOF to overcome it in order to get excited from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left.| \widetilde{0}\right\rangle$$\end{document}∣0~. We further use the gap equation to derive the reaction rate constant suppression factor k/k0, and found that it gives a quantitative agreement with the experimental data reported in refs. 17,18. We believe that macroscopic condensation could be the common origin of all VSC-induced rate constant and equilibrium constant modifications, phase transitions, and beyond.

Why cooperative VSC even works?

We should note that our above discussions on rate constant modifications are formulated in the context of a cavity directly coupled to vibrations of the reactant17,19,21, either the reaction coordinate-related normal modes17 or the non-reactive normal modes (spectator modes)45,55. There are other types of the cooperative VSC experiments22,23,118,119 that couple the cavity mode to the solvent vibrations (with frequency ω0), and the solvent frequency closely matches a reactive vibrational frequency ωR in reactant molecules. Due to the low concentrations of the reactant, the strong coupling and Rabi splitting are mainly caused by cavity coupling to solvent vibrations22.

In those cooperative coupling VSC experiments22,23,118,119, it was found that the cooperative resonant condition ωc ≈ ω0 ≈ ωR is required to achieve rate constant modifications. The isotope control experiments22,23 could set ωc ≈ ω0 ≠ ωR, and there will be no obvious VSC rate constant modifications, even though a strong coupling condition between cavity and solvent is still satisfied22,23. These cooperative couplings through solvent DOF have shown either rate constant suppression118 or enhancement22,23,119. In the past, we have developed a theoretical model61 and rate constant theory62 based on FGR to explain this phenomenon (through a mechanism of cavity-solvent strong coupling that removes the thermal vibrational excitations away from the reactant), but that theory62 is still in the effective single excitation subspace mechanistic picture (Eq. (3)). We should also note that the reaction reported in ref. 22 was under debate, as ref. 113 claims that there is no obvious VSC effect when trying to reproduce it, but later experimental works24,121 suggest that there are cooperative VSC modifications of the rate constant in different types of cavities.

With the current theory, we hypothesize that the new macroscopic state \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left.| \widetilde{0}\right\rangle$$\end{document}∣0~ now contains both solvent vibrations and the reactant vibrations. For example, we consider a total of N solvent DOF, and a total of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\mathcal{M}}}}$$\end{document}M reactant molecules (and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\mathcal{M}}}}\ll N$$\end{document}M≪N for these cooperative coupling experiments22–24,114,118,119,121). Through the same procedure, 53\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left.| \widetilde{0}\right\rangle={\prod }_{n=1}^{N}({u}_{n}+{v}_{n}{\hat{\sigma }}_{n}^{{{\dagger}} })\otimes {\prod }_{m=1}^{{{{\mathcal{M}}}}}({u}_{m}+{v}_{m}{\hat{\sigma }}_{m}^{{{\dagger}} })\left.| 0\right\rangle,$$\end{document}∣0~=∏n=1N(un+vnσ^n†)⊗∏m=1M(um+vmσ^m†)∣0,the macroscopic quantum state is now a many-body state that involves both solvent and reactant molecules, a co-condensate of their vibrational DOFs. The rate constant modification requires that the detuning is small, and thus the resonance condition ωc ≈ ω0 ≈ ωR. The current theory provides a possible explanation for this cooperative coupling effect as the co-condensation among the solvent and solute vibrational DOFs. Upon isotope substitution in the solvent, which makes ω0 ≠ ωR, even though the strong coupling condition is still achieved between cavity and solvent22,23 at ωc ≈ ω0, the vibrations in the reactant molecules no longer participate in forming the macroscopic state \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left.| \widetilde{0}\right\rangle$$\end{document}∣0~ (described by Eq. The current theory explains why the cooperative strong coupling is very sensitive to the frequency matching for the cavity, solvent, and reactant. Further numerical studies and detailed model investigations are still required.

In this work, we used a many-body physics approach, akin to the BCS theory, to analyze the many-body macroscopic ground state (Eq. (62)) in VSC Cavity QED. We derived the Gap equation (Eqs. (25), (27), and (68)) that characterizes energy stabilization of the condensate state compared to the normal thermodynamic state (Eq. (40)). We found that upon collective light-matter coupling, vibrations (described by hard-core bosons) could condense into one macroscopic state \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left.| \widetilde{0}\right\rangle$$\end{document}∣0~ (see Eq. (62)), as long as one can achieve a critical value of ΩR (see Eq. (40)). Our theory provides potential explanations for many recently observed VSC mysteries. The Moran-Ebbesen NMR experiment35 and charge-transfer complex experiment34 could provide direct evidence to see such a normal phase to the condensation phase. Our current theory provides a good agreement on the critical value of ΩR to achieve the observed new phase, which agrees with the experiment35.

The current theory has several predictions that could be experimentally verified. One of the most important predictions is the condition to achieve the condensation phase, which requires a critical ΩR (Eq. (40)). This phase transition type of behavior has already been observed in the NMR experiment35, the charge-transfer complexation reaction experiments34, and should also be observed in the VSC-modification of rate constants18,21. One possibility is to use the recently reported reactions in ref. 21, and gradually reduce the ΩR, one should observe a sudden jump of the k/k0 value back to 1, exhibiting a similar phase transition type behavior. Our theory provides quantitative predictions for the critical ΩR required to achieve the condensate and thus VSC-induced modifications. More generally, the critical condition is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\Omega }_{{{{\rm{R}}}}}\propto \sqrt{\hslash {\omega }_{0}+{k}_{{{{\rm{B}}}}}T}$$\end{document}ΩR∝ℏω0+kBT, which could be checked by multiple known VSC experiments17–19. Similarly, one can qualitatively understand the cooperative VSC rate constant effects and the change of equilibrium constant from the condensation picture, although detailed reaction models are required to construct a quantitative argument. Assuming that the VSC-induced effects originate from a single macroscopic state could also pave the way for understanding other mysterious effects. For example, why could vibrational symmetry also have a significant impact on the VSC effect39? This could be explained by the fact that the macroscopic state that dominates the VSC system’s behavior should be sensitive to the vibrational symmetry of each single-particle state, which are the building blocks of this macroscopic state. Why VSC can significantly enhance the conductivities40 could also be potentially explained by the macroscopic occupation of the condensation that impacts how electrons are transported through the phonon environments. The phase transition type of behavior in VSC-impacted polymerizations could also be explained by the macroscopic occupation of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left.| \widetilde{0}\right\rangle$$\end{document}∣0~.

Our theoretical analysis leads to the conclusion that the mystery of VSC modifications observed in experiments could arise from a many-body quantum mechanical effect: the condensation into a single macroscopic coherent state, or the macroscopic occupation of the state \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left.| \widetilde{0}\right\rangle$$\end{document}∣0~. This state is non-local and macroscopic. This is why the previous theoretical effects44,66, including our own56–58, cannot explain the collective effect, because this macroscopic state cannot be obtained perturbatively from a few-body solution86. A clear mechanistic understanding of VSC could elevate our knowledge of chemistry to a new era that goes beyond the traditional intuitive paradigm, which often involves only the local barrier crossing at the individual molecular scale. Similarly, this work brings the many-body physics picture into the cavity QED and quantum optics community, which traditionally focuses on few-body excitations (Eq. (3), due to the use of the exciton-polariton system).

We hope that we have provided the first promising candidate of a universal theory to explain nearly all VSC Cavity QED effects and offer valuable insights. If eventually verified by experiments (e.g., our theoretical predictions on critical \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\Omega }_{{{{\rm{R}}}}}\propto \sqrt{{\omega }_{0}}$$\end{document}ΩR∝ω0 through isotope/substitution experiments, or the rate constant scalings with different factors), our theory suggests that VSC Cavity QED is an experimental platform that actually generates yet another type of macroscopic condensation and macroscopic coherent state, in addition to the well-known Bose-Einstein (BEC) condensates, superfluidity, and the Cooper-Pairs condensation in the BCS-type superconductivity86,87. Unlike those other condensates and macroscopic states, which often require very low temperatures, VSC condensations occur at room temperature.
