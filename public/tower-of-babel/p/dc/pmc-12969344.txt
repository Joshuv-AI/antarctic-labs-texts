Metabolic modulation of TCA cycle by S-nitrosylation in Monascus spp.
Authors: Zitong Meng, Yingao Tan, Yali Duan, Mu Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Monascus spp. produces several beneficial secondary metabolites (SMs), such as Monacolin K and Monascus pigments, which have wide applications in the pharmaceutical and food industries. Various factors, including gene expression, regulate the metabolic processes of Monascus spp. Furthermore, S-nitrosylation also modulates the activity of its metabolic enzymes. However, the specific regulatory role and mechanisms of S-nitrosylation in the metabolic pathways of Monascus spp. remain unexplored. In this study, quantitative S-nitrosoproteomics of M. purpureus identified 352 modified proteins, representing 12% of the total protein content in M. purpureus HJ11. S-nitrosylated proteins were predominantly distributed in glycolysis, tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, amino acid metabolism and fatty acid metabolism. Notably, 87% of enzymes in the TCA cycle were S-nitrosylated. Further correlation analyses and in vitro enzymatic experiments suggest that protein S-nitrosylation inhibits the TCA cycle, thereby reducing the production of Monacolin K and Monascus pigments. To our knowledge, these findings demonstrate for the first time that S-nitrosylation serves as a key metabolic regulator in Monascus spp. This study elucidated how nitrogen-source-modulated protein S-nitrosylation inhibits the TCA cycle in Monascus spp., exerting differential effects on cell growth and the metabolic profile, including the biosynthesis of SMs, amino acids, and other key products. Our study also provides novel insights into enhancing high-value-added metabolite production in Monascus spp.

Introduction

Post-translational modifications (PTM) are crucial for regulating protein function in eukaryotic organisms, encompassing phosphorylation, glycosylation, ubiquitination, nitrosylation, methylation, and acetylation. Among these modifications, S-nitrosylation is recognized as one of the most prevalent forms of PTM. The interaction of nitric oxide (NO) with protein cysteine residues leads to structural changes in their three-dimensional conformations. This modification affects enzymatic activity, protein stability, protein-protein interactions, and subcellular localization [1,2]. Thus, it plays a crucial role in regulating key enzymes in various metabolic pathways essential for organismal growth and survival, including carbohydrate, lipid, amino acid, and nutrient metabolism [[3], [4], [5]]. For example, S-nitrosylation modulates glucose metabolism and ATP production by influencing the activities of acetyl-CoA kinase and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase in Citrobacter sp. S-77 carbohydrate metabolism. [6,7]. Additionally, it regulates the activities of aconitase and/or succinate dehydrogenase in the TCA cycle of Aspergillus flavus and Ganoderma lucidum [8,9].

To better understand the biological context of S-nitrosylation, it is essential to briefly outline its fundamental principles. NO, a core member of the reactive nitrogen species (RNS) family, is primarily generated in organisms through two main pathways. The first involves enzymatic synthesis of NO from l-arginine (Arg) by nitric oxide synthase (NOS) or NOS-like proteins (NOSL) [[10], [11], [12]]. The second, particularly relevant in fungi, is the reduction of nitrate (NO3−) or nitrite (NO2−) to NO by nitrate reductase (NarG) [11,13]. As a signaling molecule, NO and its derivatives play a dual physiological role. At elevated levels, RNS can cause nitrosative stress, leading to damage of DNA, lipids, and proteins [[14], [15], [16]]. Conversely, at physiological concentrations, NO acts as a crucial signal molecule, regulating diverse processes such as development, metabolism, and stress responses in various organisms, including fungi [[17], [18], [19]]. At the molecular level, NO exerts its extensive regulatory effects largely through S-nitrosylation, a reversible PTM where an NO group is covalently attached to the thiol group of a protein cysteine residue, forming an S-nitrosothiol [[20], [21], [22], [23]]. This modification can alter a protein's three-dimensional conformation, thereby affecting its enzymatic activity, stability, protein-protein interactions, and subcellular localization [[24], [25], [26]], which serves as a key mechanism linking environmental signals like nitrogen availability to metabolic reprogramming.

Fungi, such as Neurospora crassa, Phycomyces blakesleeanus, and Dictyostelium discoideum, convert Arg to produce NO by endogenous NOS or NOSL and/or reduced NO3− or NO2− by NarG [[11], [12], [13],27,28]. NO is redox-activated to react with protein thiol groups, forming S-nitrosothiols (RSNOs or Cys-SNO). Through this S-nitrosylation process, it exerts broad biological activities within the organism. An analysis of 474 S-nitrosylated proteins in Paracoccidioides brasiliensis revealed that most S-nitrosylated proteins are associated with catalytic activity, protein binding, and enzyme activity, and most are found in the cytoplasmic region [29].

The Monascus spp., a filamentous fungus with a thousand-year history in traditional Chinese medicine and food, is renowned for its diverse SMs, including cholesterol-lowering agents such as MK and food pigments. [[30], [31], [32], [33]]. The growth, metabolism, and biosynthesis of SMs in Monascus spp. are influenced by complex interactions among various factors, including nutrition, genetics, and the environment [30,32,34,35]. Previous studies have shown that nitrogen sources are critical for the growth, metabolism, and biosynthesis of SMs in Monascus spp. Inorganic nitrogen sources, such as ammonium sulfate (NH4SO4), reduce pigment production [36]. Low concentrations of sodium nitrate (NaNO3) enhance MK production, but excessively high concentrations are toxic to cells and reduce MK synthesis [37]. Nitrogen sources are not only critical nutrients for Monascus growth but also serve as substrates for protein S-nitrosylation. Thus, they affect Monascus growth and SMs synthesis through multiple pathways. However, the mechanisms by which nitrogen affects protein S-nitrosylation in Monascus and how this subsequently affects its growth and SM production remain poorly understood.

In this study, we used proteomics and metabolomics to elucidate the effects of various nitrogen sources on protein S-nitrosylation and their regulatory roles in the metabolism of M. purpureus. The novelty and significance of this study are threefold: first, it provides the first comprehensive, site-specific S-nitrosoproteomic dataset for Monascus; second, it demonstrates for the first time that S-nitrosylation acts as a key molecular switch, comprehensively inhibiting the TCA cycle by modifying 87% of its enzymes; and third, it reveals a novel mechanism by which nitrogen sources regulate primary metabolism and the biosynthesis of valuable secondary metabolites. These findings offer a new perspective on fungal metabolic regulation and provide novel strategies for metabolic engineering to enhance the production of high-value-added compounds in Monascus spp.

Materials and methods

Strains

The M. purpureus HJ11 strain was used in this study and is maintained in our laboratory [38]. For long-term preservation, the strain has been deposited at the China General Microbiological Culture Collection Center (CGMCC) under accession number 25474.

Culture media and growth conditions

To cultivate the M. purpureus HJ11 strain, a spore solution with a concentration of 200 μL of 105/mL was added to 50 mL of fermentation medium and incubated at 28 °C with shaking at 120 rpm for 7 days. The fermentation medium used in this study comprised glucose (30 g/L), KH2PO4 (4 g/L), MgSO4⋅7H2O (0.5 g/L), KCl (0.5 g/L), MnSO4⋅H2O (0.03 g/L), ZnSO4⋅7H2O (0.01 g/L), and FeSO4⋅7H2O (0.01 g/L). For experimental treatments, various nitrogen sources, NH4SO4 (5 g/L), NaNO3 (6.433 g/L), and L-Arg (3.295 g/L), were incorporated into the nutritional composition of the fermentation medium to achieve equivalent nitrogen content. The solid medium was supplemented with 1.8% agar and sterilized at 121 °C for 20 min. All reagents were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). The fermentation medium and culture conditions were based on our previous studies [39].

To ensure comparability, the concentration of NaNO3 (6.433 g/L) was initially calculated to provide an equivalent nitrogen content to the other nitrogen sources (NH4SO4 and L-Arg). Moreover, nitrate (NO3−) was chosen for focused study because it theoretically serves as a direct precursor for NO, which is intimately linked to S-nitrosylation. Accordingly, a high-concentration nitrate treatment (NaNO3_H, 12.866 g/L) was included to directly investigate the potential link between NO precursor availability and the extent of S-nitrosylation, and its subsequent impact on growth and metabolism. In the text, the treatment group with 6.433 g/L NaNO3 is abbreviated as NaNO3_L, and the group with 12.866 g/L NaNO3 is abbreviated as NaNO3_H.

Phenotypic and metabolomic analytics

For metabolomic analysis, each treatment group comprised six independent biological replicates (n = 6). Monascus mycelia were harvested, washed, freeze-dried, and weighed to quantify biomass. To extract pigments, dried mycelia were ground in an 80% ethanol solution, heated in a water bath, and centrifuged to collect the supernatant. The collected supernatant was subsequently diluted for optical density (OD) measurement. The color value (U/g) was calculated from OD values measured at wavelengths of 380, 470, and 520 nm. Monacolin K (MK) content was extracted from ground dried mycelia using a 75% ethanol solution, while citrinin was extracted from the fermentation broth utilizing a mixed solvent of toluene-ethyl acetate-formic acid (7:3:1 v/v/v) and quantified via UPLC. Metabolomic analysis included homogenization of mycelial samples, methanol extraction, centrifugation, and protein precipitation of the supernatant before LC-MS analysis. UPLC employed an ACQUITY BEH Amide column with gradient elution using water-acetonitrile. MS detection was performed on a QTRAP® 6500+ system equipped with an ESI source operating in both positive and negative ion modes; metabolites were quantified using MRM. The methods for phenotypic and metabolomic analysis were based on our previous studies [39].

Iodo-TMT labelling site-specific S-nitrosoproteomic

To investigate the S-nitrosylation sites in proteins, the analysis was performed on three independent biological replicates (n = 3) using iodo-TMT labeling. Samples were treated with a buffer to protect cysteine thiols, followed by lysis, centrifugation, and protein precipitation. The resulting protein pellet was washed, re-dissolved, and its concentration quantified. Proteins were labeled using the Iodo-TMT kit, and the reaction was subsequently quenched with DTT. After removing excess reagents, proteins were digested with ammonium bicarbonate to complete the S-nitrosoproteomics. The iodo-TMT labeling protocol was based on previous studies [40].

Tryptic digestion, HPLC fractionation and affinity purification

Protein samples underwent digestion with trypsin, reduced using dithiothreitol, alkylated with iodoacetamide, and subsequently desalted. Peptides were fractionated by high pH reverse-phase HPLC and combined into four distinct fractions. Peptide enrichment was performed using Anti-TMT resin, while non-enriched samples were reserved for direct analysis. Following enrichment, peptides were washed and eluted from the resin. The eluted samples were lyophilized and reconstituted in acetonitrile/formic acid for High-resolution liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis. The procedures for tryptic digestion, HPLC fractionation, and affinity purification were based on previous studies [40].

Database search and core gene screen

MS/MS data were processed with MaxQuant, which calculated false discovery rates (FDR) and removed contaminants. Trypsin/P specificity was employed, allowing up to two missed cleavages, and the minimum peptide length was set to seven residues. A maximum of five modifications per peptide was permitted, with mass tolerances of 20 ppm for the initial search and 4.5 ppm for the main search. Key modifications included methionine oxidation and N-terminal acetylation. Quantification was performed using iodoTMT-6plex, and a stringent FDR of 1% was set for both protein identification and PSM analysis.

Using Cytoscape v3.9.1 with CytoNCA plugin, we assessed six centrality metrics: Degree centrality (DC), Eigenvector centrality (EC), Local Average Connectivity-based method (LAC), Betweenness centrality (BC), Closeness centrality (CC), and Network centrality (NC). Hub target genes were identified as those ranking in the top 50% for all six centrality metrics. The top 20 genes were identified using MCC algorithm in CytoHubba, while MCODE analysis identified functional gene modules. Core target genes were defined as those identified by all methods.

In vitro S-nitrosylation and enzyme activity assay

S-nitrosoglutathione (GSNO) synthesis and quantification. GSNO was freshly prepared prior to use. Briefly, 0.4 mL of 400 mM reduced glutathione (GSH) was mixed with 0.2 mL of 0.2 M HCl, followed by the addition of 0.2 mL of 400 mM sodium nitrite (NaNO2). The mixture was allowed to react at room temperature for 15 min. The reaction was terminated by adjusting the pH to 7.2 with approximately 3-4 μL of 40% (w/v) NaOH. The concentration of the synthesized GSNO was determined spectrophotometrically. The crude product was diluted 100-fold, and then diluted an additional 10-fold. The absorbance of the final solution was measured at 334 nm against a water blank. The GSNO concentration was calculated using the molar extinction coefficient of 0.900 mM−1cm−1 and the total dilution factor. The final solution was then diluted with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to a working concentration of 100 nM. The synthesis and quantification of GSNO were performed as described previously [41].

Protein treatment and enzyme activity assay. To induce S-nitrosylation, purified Isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) and Pyruvate kinase (PK) protein samples were incubated with 100 nM GSNO for 30 min on ice, protected from light. Control samples were treated identically but without GSNO. The activities of both S-nitrosylated and control enzymes were measured using commercial IDH and PK Activity Assay Kits (Solarbio, Beijing, China), strictly following the manufacturer's instructions. To ensure comparability, the protein concentration of each sample was determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Enhanced, Solarbio, Beijing, China) prior to the activity assay. The effect of S-nitrosylation was evaluated by comparing the enzymatic activity of the GSNO-treated group to the untreated control.

Statistical analysis

The identified proteins were functionally annotated using Gene Ontology (GO), the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) metabolic pathways database, Clusters of Orthologous Groups of proteins (COG/KOG), and Subcellular localization. Data processing and statistical analysis were performed using Prism software (GraphPad 8.0). Experimental results are expressed as mean ± standard error (x ± SE) from at least three independent experiments. Differences between multiple groups were compared using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), with P < 0.05 as the criterion for significance.

Result

Identification of site-specific S-nitrosylated sites and proteins by S-nitrosoproteome in M. purpureus HJ11

The availability of various nitrogen sources can influence NO production in fungi [42], regulating S-nitrosylation. This modification can modulate the function of thousands of proteins, impacting a range of biological processes (Fig. 1A) [43]. Consequently, we conducted a study of S-nitrosylation in M. purpureus using iodo-TMT labeling to identify specific S-nitrosylated protein sites. This method involved irreversibly labeling S-nitrosothiols (SNO-Cys) with iodine-TMT, followed by the enrichment of S-nitrosylated peptides (SNO-peptides) through high-affinity anti-TMT chromatography and competitive elution. LC-MS/MS was then used to precisely localize and multiplex-quantify S-nitrosylation sites (Fig. 1B). Using this approach, we successfully identified 505 unique cysteine S-nitrosylation sites (SNS) on 352 SNO proteins, providing a comprehensive profile of the M. purpureus S-nitrosoproteome (Fig. 1C). Notably, our MS data exhibited a mass error distribution centered around zero, with most values below 5 ppm, ensuring high accuracy and quality (Fig. 1D). Moreover, the identified peptide length distribution met quality control standards, with most ranging from 7 to 20 amino acids, consistent with enzymatic digestion and mass spectrometric fragmentation rules (Fig. 1E). Among the identified 352 SNO proteins, approximately 70.5% (248 proteins) contained a single SNS, while 20.7% (73 proteins) possessed two SNS sites, two proteins displayed extensive modifications with six SNS sites each. In M. purpureus, these structural domains may regulate specific enzyme activities or participate in metabolic pathways via protein-protein interactions (Fig. 1F). Previous studies have shown that the amino acid composition of cysteine residues significantly influences sensitivity and specificity to S-nitrosylation [44,45]. Accordingly, we analyzed the sequence characteristics of 505 identifiable S-nitrosylation sites to identify potential amino acid motifs that enhance cysteine susceptibility to S-nitrosylation. Among these sites, 155 contained motifs with lysine residues, accounting for 30.7% (Fig. S1A). Distribution pattern of S-nitrosylated proteins in M. purpureus HJ11

To investigate the distribution patterns of S-nitrosylation modifications in M. purpureus under different nitrogen sources, we selected S-nitrosylated peptides with relative quantitation values greater than 1. We performed a Venn diagram analysis of S-nitrosylation sites induced by different nitrogen treatments. Interestingly, the S-nitrosylation modifications induced by NaNO3 and NH4SO4 exhibited the least overlap, while the sites induced by Arg nearly encompassed all sites from the other two treatments. Moreover, although increased NaNO3 concentration broadened the range of S-nitrosylation modifications in M. purpureus, the distribution of S-nitrosylation sites differed significantly between low and high doses (Fig. 2A). In this study, we determined the relative quantitative ratios for each modification site across different samples. Fig. 2B illustrates the number of proteins and modification sites that were significantly differentially modified among the nitrogen sources. To elucidate the biological roles of S-nitrosylation in M. purpureus, we functionally categorized the differentially S-nitrosylated proteins using Gene Ontology (GO) and Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG) analyses. Similarly, GO analysis classified these proteins into biological processes (cellular processes; metabolic processes; biological regulation; response to stimulus), cellular components (cell; intracellular; protein-containing complex), and molecular functions (catalytic activity; binding) (Fig. 2D, Fig. S2B). Considering that proteins in cells are localized to different cellular components, we used WolF Psort software for subcellular annotation. Subsequently, our findings indicated that S-nitrosylated proteins in M. purpureus are predominantly distributed in the cytoplasm, mitochondria, and nucleus (Fig. 2E, Fig. S2C).

Furthermore, we categorized the identified S-nitrosylated proteins into metabolic pathways based on KEGG annotations. Notably, differentially S-nitrosylated proteins in M. purpureus under various nitrogen conditions were significantly associated with pathways such as glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, TCA cycle, ribosome, amino acid metabolism, and fatty acid metabolism (Fig. 3). Specifically, the levels of S-nitrosylated proteins were significantly higher under Arg treatment than under the other nitrogen sources across nearly all annotated KEGG pathways. Similarly, the expression levels of S-nitrosylated proteins induced by NaNO3 and NH4SO4 also showed significant changes across different KEGG pathways. Moreover, various doses of NaNO3 affected the expression levels and distribution of S-nitrosylated proteins across different KEGG pathways (Fig. 3). Collectively, these findings suggest that nitrogen source variation significantly influences the S-nitrosylated proteome in M. purpureus, especially under Arg treatment. The observed changes encompass key metabolic pathways, including glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, the TCA cycle, ribosomal function, amino acid metabolism, and lipid metabolism. S-nitrosylation modulates amino acid and energy metabolism in Monascus spp.

To investigate the role of S-nitrosylation modification in the cellular processes of M. purpureus, we extracted proteins with a confidence score >0.7 (high confidence) to construct a protein-protein interaction network. Notably, these tightly connected interactions were predominantly involved in amino acid metabolism and energy metabolism. We employed CytoNCA, CytoHubba, and MCODE to filter the target gene sets from the protein-protein interaction network. The intersection of target gene sets identified by these three methods was defined as the core target genes (Table S1). KEGG pathways analysis of these core genes revealed the primary pathways where S-nitrosylated proteins play a critical role in cellular processes of M. purpureus. Interestingly, the key KEGG pathways associated with the core target genes were primarily enriched in glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, TCA cycle, and various amino acid metabolism pathways. These findings are consistent with those from protein-protein interaction network and KEGG pathway analyses. Overall, our results suggest that S-nitrosylation induced by nitrogen sources primarily modulates the growth and metabolic processes of Monascus spp. by affecting amino acid metabolism and energy transfer pathways (Fig. 4).Fig. 4Interaction network analysis and target protein selection of differentially S-nitrosylated modified proteins.Fig. 4

S-nitrosylation and metabolic characterization in Monascus spp.

To elucidate the complex connections between S-nitrosylated proteins and key metabolites in Monascus spp., we constructed heatmaps of the relative abundance distribution of SMs, amino acids, and key metabolic products under different nitrogen sources. The results indicated that different nitrogen sources significantly impacted the metabolic mode of Monascus spp. (Fig. 5A–C). Specifically, when NH4SO4 was used as a nitrogen source, Monascus spp. exhibited the highest biomass and pigment production, and NH4SO4 promoted the synthesis of metabolites in the TCA cycle. This suggests that modulation of the TCA cycle via S-nitrosylation can synergistically drive cell growth and secondary metabolite production under specific nitrogen conditions. In contrast, using Arg as a nitrogen source produced opposite results. Moreover, NaNO3 as a nitrogen source also triggered a distinct metabolite pattern, with different concentrations further altering the metabolite profile of Monascus spp. (Fig. 5A–C). S-nitrosylation and metabolic regulation in TCA cycle

Furthermore, we annotated the S-nitrosylation levels of key enzymes and metabolite content in the glycolysis/gluconeogenesis and TCA cycle pathways under different nitrogen source conditions. Notably, 87% of the enzymes in TCA cycle were extensively S-nitrosylated. Several enzymes had multiple S-nitrosylation sites, including KGD1 with five, PCK1 and PFK1 with three, and ACL1, FUM1, SDH1, IDP1, PYK1, and HXK1_2 each with two. Compared to other nitrogen sources, Arg induced the highest level of S-nitrosylation. In contrast, NH4SO4 treatment resulted in lower levels of S-nitrosylation. It promoted higher levels of most metabolites, including citrate, pyruvate, phosphoglycerate, malate, and other core intermediates of the TCA cycle, which are crucial for cellular energy production. These metabolites can influence the synthesis of SMs, such as MK, pigments, and acetic acid, by altering precursor availability, the cellular redox state and energy flow. Discussion

S-nitrosylation, a significant PTM, alter the structure and function of thousands of substrate proteins, thereby regulating intracellular signaling, enzyme activity, and metabolic pathways [46]. Indeed, its role as a master regulator of metabolism is increasingly recognized across various biological systems, particularly in pathological conditions. For instance, in neurodegenerative diseases like Alzheimer's and Parkinson's disease, aberrant S-nitrosylation directly inhibit key enzymes in the TCA cycle, such as α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase (αKGDH), leading to a block in mitochondrial metabolism and dysfunction [47,48]. Similarly, in the kidney, S-nitrosylation reprograms intermediary metabolism by targeting pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2), thereby balancing fuel utilization with redox protection [49]. This modification also plays an extensive regulatory role in microorganisms such as Monascus spp. In this study, we employed the iodo-TMT labeling technique to identify S-nitrosylated proteins in Monascus spp., successfully identifying 352 S-nitrosylated proteins with 505 unique SNS, providing the first comprehensive dataset for the S-nitrosylated proteome of Monascus spp. Notably, oxaloacetate dehydrogenase (with SNS at C241, C246, C518, C492, C933 and C832 in A0A507QIQ6) and the zinc finger FYVE/PHD type proteins (with SNS at C225, C236, C155, C158, C162 and C163 in A0A507QXR6), each exhibiting six SNS sites respectively, demonstrated the most extensive S-nitrosylation. Oxaloacetate dehydrogenase plays a crucial role in the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, catalyzing the oxidation of oxaloacetate to succinate, generating NADH and CO2, and providing essential energy for the growth and metabolism of Monascus spp. The zinc finger FYVE/PHD type domains are typically associated with DNA or RNA binding and signaling processes and may be involved in gene expression regulation or metabolic pathways. In Monascus spp., these domains may regulate the activity of specific enzymes or participate in metabolic pathways through protein-protein interactions.

In this study, we revealed that the distribution pattern of S-nitrosylation modifications in M. purpureus highlights its significance across various biological processes. Furthermore, target proteins identified from the protein-protein interaction network were predominantly involved in critical metabolic pathways in Monascus spp., including glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, the TCA cycle, and amino acid metabolism. Our data reveal a striking enrichment of S-nitrosylation on enzymes central to primary metabolism, particularly within the TCA cycle and glycolysis pathways. To move beyond correlation and establish causation, we conducted in vitro enzyme activity assays. Our results demonstrated that S-nitrosylation, induced by the NO donor GSNO, significantly inhibits the activity of two pivotal enzymes: IDH and PK. This direct inhibition establishes a clear mechanistic link between the observed PTM and the downstream metabolic phenotypes. The molecular mechanism by which S-nitrosylation impairs enzyme activity is likely multifaceted. The covalent attachment of an NO group to a cysteine thiol can sterically hinder substrate access to the active site, directly modify residues essential for catalysis, or induce allosteric changes that alter protein dynamics. For example, the inhibition of αKGDH involved S-nitrosylation of its subunits, disrupting its enzymatic function, while the activity of PKM2 was inhibited by S-nitrosylation at a novel regulatory locus [47,49]. The significant inhibition we observed for IDH and PK strongly suggests that at least one of the modified cysteines in these enzymes is functionally critical, likely located within the catalytic core, consistent with S-nitrosylation's role as a widespread negative regulator of metabolic enzymes in other systems [50]. The TCA cycle and glycolysis, catalyzed by a series of enzymes within the mitochondria, represent primary biochemical hubs within cells, generating energy and precursor metabolites through essential reactions vital for growth and metabolite production in Monascus spp. These S-nitrosylation modifications can alter enzyme conformation, thereby modulating catalytic efficiency and influencing metabolite production. Consequently, the biological significance of S-nitrosylation in Monascus spp. is reflected not only in its regulation of individual protein functions but also in its broader impact on the entire cellular metabolic network.

As a critical factor in the growth medium, nitrogen source type and concentration directly influence the growth of Monascus spp. and the yield of its metabolites [51]. In our study, as a NOS substrate, Arg induced elevated S-nitrosylation, yet reduced biomass and pigment production in Monascus spp. Conversely, with NH4SO4 as the nitrogen source, Monascus spp. exhibited lower levels of S-nitrosylation alongside maximal biomass and pigment production. Beyond the classical L-Arg-NOS pathway, inorganic anions such as nitrites (NO2−) and nitrates (NO3−) were also identified as alternative sources for NO production [11]. Consequently, our study further investigated the effects of NANO3 as a nitrogen source on S-nitrosylation, growth, and metabolism in Monascus spp. We observed that the susceptibility to S-nitrosylation with NANO3 was intermediate between NH4SO4 and Arg; moreover, varying concentrations of NaNO3 differentially affected growth, metabolism, and S-nitrosylation levels within Monascus spp. These results indicate that nitrogen sources can induce S-nitrosylation in Monascus spp., acting as a conserved signaling mechanism that affects a variety of proteins. Moreover, different nitrogen sources induce varying S-nitrosylation levels, impacting biological processes and metabolic pathways in Monascus spp., including the synthesis of SMs, such as pigments and MK precursors. A strong correlation exists between the expression levels of S-nitrosylated proteins and the production of key metabolites in Monascus spp. These findings underscore the general regulatory role of nitrogen-source-induced S-nitrosylation, offering a new perspective on how nitrogen sources regulate metabolism in Monascus spp. via this modification. Thus, S-nitrosylation functions as a critical molecular switch, transducing nitrogen source signals into a reprogramming of metabolic pathways, ultimately determining the physiological fate of Monascus spp. Therefore, the study of S-nitrosylation not only enhances our understanding of biological functions within Monascus spp. but also offers novel strategies for regulating metabolite synthesis.

Conclusion

In summary, our study identified S-nitrosylation as the key mechanism by which various nitrogen sources regulate the TCA cycle, thereby differentially impacting the growth and metabolism of Monascus spp. Site-specific proteomic analysis of S-nitrosylation revealed extensive variations induced by different nitrogen sources. Bioinformatics analysis underscored the broad impact of S-nitrosylation on various biological processes and pathways, particularly amino acid metabolism, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, and the TCA cycle. Correlation analysis established a strong link between S-nitrosylated protein expression and metabolite production. Moreover, S-nitrosylation emerged as a key mechanism mediating growth and metabolic processes in Monascus spp., and our research provided significant insights into the molecular mechanisms of nitrogen-regulated metabolism in Monascus spp., offering an excellent model for characterizing S-nitrosylation in fungi.
