Continuous vs. intermittent chronic vagus nerve stimulation: effects on T-cell dependent antibody response, heart rate variability and body weight in immunized mice
Authors: Michael Gerber, Ibrahim Mughrabi, Izumi Kurata-Sato, Ethan Paliwoda, The Anh Vu, Betty Diamond, Stavros Zanos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVagal-immune interactions are increasingly recognized to play a role in the pathogenesis of diseases associated with immune dysfunction. To study the direct effects of altered vagal activity on adaptive immune function and antibody production, we modeled a state of elevated vagal activation by delivering chronic vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) in immunized mice. We delivered VNS for 14 days before and 14 days after immunization with an antigen, using 2 schedules: Twice Daily VNS, to model intermittently elevated vagal activity, and Continuous Burst VNS, to model chronically elevated vagal activity. We found that Continuous Burst VNS is associated with increased heart rate variability (HRV) on the first week of treatment, remodeling of cellular splenic compartments, as well as weight loss. Such changes are not seen with Twice Daily VNS. High-affinity antibody production is reduced with both Continuous Burst and Twice Daily VNS, compared to no VNS. Total IgG antibody titers are reduced in Continuous Burst VNS, even prior to immunization, likely an effect of weight loss. In conclusion, both continuously and intermittently elevated vagal tone limits the antibody response. Nonselective, whole nerve VNS produces multiple effects that may confound interpretation of specific immune effects at higher stimulation doses.Keywords: Vagus nerve stimulation, Neuromodulation, Neuroimmune, Stimulation schedule, Adaptive immunity, Germinal center reaction

Background

The nervous system regulates immune function (Pavlov et al. 2018), with the vagus nerve serving as a conduit for neuroimmune communication (Wu et al. 2021). Bioelectronic stimulation of the vagus nerve suppresses acute inflammation via an anti-inflammatory pathway (Borovikova et al. 2000) which involves the splenic nerve (Rosas-Ballina et al. 2008) activating acetylcholine-producing T cells (Rosas-Ballina et al. 2011), which in turn engage alpha-7 nicotinic receptors on macrophages (Parrish et al. 2008). The vagus nerve also regulates the humoral immune response (Wu et al. 2024) through alpha-7 and alpha-9 nicotinic receptors, impairing germinal center formation (Kurata-Sato et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2020). Beyond its efferent role, the vagus nerve senses inflammatory mediators (Zanos et al. 2018; Hosoi et al. 2005) and relays these signals to the brain through dedicated afferent fibers (Jin et al. 2024), potentially contributing to inflammatory memory (Koren et al. 2021). These afferent and efferent functions are mediated by inflammatory reflexes that govern the sensing, integration, and output control of immune responses (Pavlov et al. 2018) These observations have raised the possibility that the vagus nerve may play a role in chronic immune dysfunction (Rana et al. 2018). For example, the vagus nerve is activated during sepsis (Goehler et al. 1999; Hosoi et al. 2005; Zanos et al. 2018; Katayama et al. 2022; Strohl et al. 2025), and while the initial anti-inflammatory effect is thought to be beneficial to organ function, persistent vagal activation may contribute to long-term adaptive immune dysfunction documented in sepsis survivors (Liu et al. 2022). However, it is unknown whether chronic activation of the motor vagus drives such immune dysfunction or if immune dysfunction acts on the sensory vagus to shape an abnormal autonomic tone (Strohl et al. 2025). The natural firing pattern of the vagus, especially of the subset of fibers responsible for controlling and sensing inflammation (Jin et al. 2024; Koren et al. 2021), has not been fully described during or after inflammatory disease (Strohl et al. 2025).

Understanding the role of the vagus nerve in immune function is critical for targeted therapy of sepsis and other diseases associated with dysfunction of neuroimmune mechanisms. To investigate the effects of different patterns of vagal activity on immune function, we compared immunization response in a model of chronic VNS (Mughrabi et al. 2021), under two distinct VNS schedules: twice daily stimulation trains during awake hours (Twice Daily) and continuous, frequent, short bursts over the 24 h cycle (Continuous Burst) (Fig. 1A). Twice Daily stimulation was previously found to influence innate (Mughrabi et al. 2021) and adaptive immunity (Kurata-Sato et al. 2024), while Continuous Burst may be eliciting vagal activation more similar to what occurs in sepsis (Strohl et al. 2025).

Methods

Animals and immunization

Male C57BL/6 were purchased from Charles River Laboratories and used at 8 to 15 weeks of age. All mice were housed under specific pathogen–free conditions. Mice were administered 100 µg of 4-hydroxy-3-nitrophenylacetyl chicken γ-globulin (NP-CGG) (Biosearch Technologies) emulsified in 100 µl of Imject alum (Thermo Fisher Scientific) by intraperitoneal injection, as previously described. (Kurata-Sato et al. 2024) All protocols including VNS were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee.

Chronic implant surgery

Mice were singly housed in custom-made cylindrical cages designed to administer VNS and monitor telemetry without impeding ad libitum access to food, water, and nesting material throughout the treatment period, as previously described (Kurata-Sato et al. 2024). Animals were acclimated to these cages for 1 week prior to chronic implant surgery and remained there for the duration of the experiment. On the day of chronic implant surgery, anesthesia was induced with 3–4% isoflurane and maintained at 1.5%. On a warmed surgical pad, the dorsal skull was exposed and a subcutaneous tunnel was blunt dissected to the ventral neck on the left side as previously described (Mughrabi et al. 2021). In treatment group animals, the left vagus nerve was then exposed from the carotid sheath and fastened to a commercial micro-cuff electrode (100-µm microsling, CorTec, Germany). In sham animals, the nerve was exposed but no electrode was fastened. ECG electrodes were sutured subcutaneously to the upper and lower chest. Stimulating and recording wires were passed through the subcutaneous tunnel from a strip connector that was cemented to the dorsal skull and sealed with tissue glue. Mice were supplemented with normal saline after the procedure and given 5 mg/kg of meloxicam postoperatively. Mice were allowed to recover for 1 week before beginning VNS treatment.

Vagus nerve stimulation schedule

Mice were stimulated on one of three schedules (Fig. 1, A): No stimulation, 10-second trains every 5 min (Continuous Burst), or 5-minute trains twice daily (Twice Daily). Stimulation trains were made up of 500-µs pulses delivered at 30 Hz, with current intensity adjusted to achieve ~ 20–30% reduction of baseline heart rate. The stimulation frequency of 30 Hz was chosen because it has previously been shown to activate the efferent inflammatory reflex (Mughrabi et al. 2021) and was shown to influence the antibody response (Kurata-Sato et al. 2024). ECG was recorded and reviewed daily by calculating a triggered average of all heart rate drops in VNS treated animals per 24-hours (144 total short stimuli in Continuous Burst animals and 2 total long stimuli in Twice Daily); current intensity was raised by 100uA intervals if target heart rate drop was not achieved. This current intensity tuning was done to ensure VNS was being delivered throughout the treatment period. Mice that were unresponsive even to maximum stimulus intensity (1.6 mA) were removed from further analyses; this criterion was met by one mouse from the Continuous Burst group, and implant dysfunction was confirmed on autopsy at the end of the treatment period. Typically, implants would achieve target heart rate drop at low stimulus intensities early during treatment but would require higher values towards the end of treatment, likely due to fibrosis around the implant (Ahmed et al. 2021; Mughrabi et al. 2021) (Fig. 1, B-C). Mice were monitored daily to observe indicators of well-being, including food and water consumption and nest building. VNS treatment was started on day − 14 relative to immunization, which was on day 0 (Fig. 1, B-C). On day 0 and day 7, blood was collected by tail-tip sampling under isoflurane anesthesia for serological assays. At the end of day 14, mice were euthanized, and spleen and blood were collected for analysis.

Heart rate variability

Heart rate variability (HRV) was calculated using a custom MATLAB script (MathWorks, Natick, MA), similar to standard methods (Thireau et al. 2008; Zafeiropoulos et al. 2024). We used a wavelet-based algorithm to detect ECG peaks corresponding to QRS complexes. ECG quality was assessed by calculating signal to noise ratio (SNR) (Eq. 1) of QRS complexes, in 30 s-long epochs. Epochs in which SNR was below 2.5, an empirically determined threshold, were excluded from analysis. ECG during stimulus trains were excluded from HRV analysis due to the presence of electrical artifacts that affect signal quality. Good quality ECG was then divided into 3-minute-long segments for frequency domain conversion. In each segment, heart rate was calculated from individual QRS time stamps, then resampled to a 20 Hz sampling rate, detrended and passed through a Hamming window. The power spectrum of the resulting time series was computed; the low-frequency (LF) and high-frequency (HF) components of the power spectrum were defined as the area under the curve between 0.15 and 1.5 Hz and 1.5–5 Hz, respectively (Thireau et al. 2008). The resulting HRV components from each 3-minute segment were averaged in 12-hour increments aligned with the day-night cycle (6AM-6PM). This resulted in approximately 240 averaged 3-minute segments per 12-hour increment. Data is reported as mean and standard error per 12-hour increment.

Equation 1: Signal to noise ratio function used to define good quality ECG. For a given epoch of ECG, represents the max value of the power spectrum between 7 and 15 Hz, represents the mean of the power spectrum between 0 and 15 Hz and represents the standard deviation of the power spectrum between 0 and 15 Hz.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

Serum titers of NP-specific antibodies, total IgG, and IgM were measured by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay as previously described (Kurata-Sato et al. 2024). Briefly, plates were coated with NP2 or NP25–bovine serum albumin (BSA) (10 µg/ml; Biosearch Technologies) or goat anti-mouse IgG (5 µg/ml; Southern Biotech) overnight at 4 °C then washed six times with 0.05% Tween/phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solution and blocked with 1% BSA/PBS at room temperature for an hour. Serum diluted with 0.2% BSA/PBS (1:2000 for anti-NP IgG, 1:20,000 for total IgG) was added to the wells. After 2 h of incubation, plates were washed, and alkaline phosphatase (AP)–conjugated goat polyclonal anti-mouse IgG (Southern Biotech) diluted with 0.2% BSA/PBS (1:1000) was added and incubated for an hour. After color development with phosphate substrate (1 mg/ml; Millipore Sigma), plates were read at 405 nm.

Flow cytometry

Splenocyte counts were obtained with flow cytometry using the same standard procedures, reagents and antibodies as previously described. (Kurata-Sato et al. 2024).

Immunohistochemistry

Fresh spleens were embedded in TissueTek OCT (Sakura), quickly frozen over liquid nitrogen and sectioned at 10 μm on a cryostat (Leica). Sectioned tissue was first fixed on the slide with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, then incubated in Triton-X for 30 min, then blocked with 5% donkey serum and 10% BSA. Primary antibody was applied overnight at 4 °C, then secondary was applied for two hours at room temperature. Primary antibodies and dilutions are listed in Table 1.

Analysis and statistics

For all figures except NP2 IgG, NP25 IgG, and total IgG antibody titers, data is representative of one cohort of animals that were treated simultaneously; for the NP2 IgG, NP25 IgG and total IgG antibody titers, an additional cohort of No Stimulation (n = 3) vs. Continuous Burst (n = 3) was included due to data availability. The combined cohorts were not statistically significant when compared to each other. ANCOVA was used to model the regression line for antibody titers over the 2-week post-immunization period between treatment groups and post-hoc pairwise comparisons were used to compare slopes and y-intercepts. This analysis was chosen to avoid multiple statistical comparisons at different time points on datasets with a relatively small sample size (6–7), which are susceptible to technical artifacts. This analysis compares the rate of increase of antibody titers (slope) between treatment groups as well as their starting values (y-intercept), but not exact antibody titer levels. One-way ANOVA with post-hoc pairwise comparisons was used to compare HRV averaged across each week of treatment, as well as spleen cytology and histology outcome measures, between treatment groups. For body weight data, a repeated measures ANOVA with multiple comparisons is used to make within-group and between-group comparisons. For immunohistochemistry (IHC), T cell, B cell, and red pulp areas were calculated by summing the total area from 4 separate sections spaced 100 μm apart. All calculations were done in MATLAB using the Statistics and Machine Learning toolbox.

Results & discussion

Heart rate variability is increased in Continuous Burst animals on the first week of stimulation

Mice were subjected to either no stimulation or one of two VNS schedules (Fig. 1, A). HRV on the day prior to VNS treatment is equal between groups (Fig. 1, D; Day − 15 to Day − 14). Compared to No Stimulation, Continuous Burst animals have elevated HRV during the first week of treatment with VNS (Fig. 1, D; Day − 14 to Day − 7). The LF component is significantly elevated in Continuous Burst animals while HF approach but do not reach significance (Fig. 1, E-F). HF is most representative of vagus nerve activity while LF is thought to represent a combination of sympathetic and parasympathetic control of the heart (“Heart rate variability. Standards of measurement, physiological interpretation, and clinical use. Task Force of the European Society of Cardiology and the North American Society of Pacing and Electrophysiology,” 1996). HRV returns to a level equal to No Stimulation animals during the 2nd week of treatment (Fig. 1, D; Day − 7 to Day 0) and all following weeks. Twice Daily animals are no different from No Stimulation at all time points, indicating that this stimulation may not be enough to cause measurable change in HRV compared to Continuous Burst. The transient rise in HRV in Continuous Burst animals may represent increased vagal activity due to repetitive stimulation from VNS (Lee et al. 2018). The return to No Stimulation levels in the following weeks may either represent decreased effectiveness of stimulus delivery or an adaptation response (Biggio et al. 2009; Egashira et al. 2010; Seewoo et al. 2019; Wang et al. 2012). Given the maintenance of heart rate responses to VNS in weeks 2 and 3, indicating engagement of vagal fibers by the stimuli, it is likely that the return of HRV to baseline may represent neural adaptations. This finding indicates that HRV may be a poor indicator of VNS response in repetitive stimulation, which may be clinically relevant if verified in humans (Anand et al. 2020; Nearing et al. 2021).

Continuous Burst stimulation reduces high affinity IgG, low affinity IgG and total IgG compared to No Stimulation

Compared to No Stimulation, the rising slope of high-affinity NP2 IgG antibody titers is significantly decreased in Continuous Burst stimulation animals, but not in Twice Daily stimulation animals (Fig. 2, A-B). We previously reported a reduction in NP2 IgG antibody production in Twice Daily stimulation animals (Kurata-Sato et al. 2024). In this study, we hypothesized that Continuous Burst stimulation would have a greater effect on antibody production and the study was not designed to detect subtle decreases in Twice Daily animals; nonetheless, there is an trend with Twice Daily animals having a smaller slope compared to No Stimulation, but greater than Continuous Burst. Similar to our prior report in Twice Daily animals, there is no effect of Continuous Burst on NP25 IgG titers (Fig. 2, C-D), however there is an emerging trend in total IgG with Continuous Burst having a lower y-intercept, suggesting lower levels of total IgG prior to immunization (Fig. 2, E-F). Total IgM and NP25 IgM are largely unchanged in Continuous Burst animals, similar to prior findings in Twice Daily animals (Fig. 2, G-H) (Kurata-Sato et al. 2024).

These results suggest that chronically elevated vagus activity (Continuous Burst VNS) has similar or greater effect on high-affinity antibody production as previously reported with intermittent activity (Twice Daily VNS) (Kurata-Sato et al. 2024). Importantly, Continuous Burst results in reduced total IgG titers prior to immunization that is not seen with Twice Daily stimulation. Total IgG titers are maintained through a balance between antibody production and clearance. Production is mostly dependent on secretion from long-lived plasma cells that depend on a bone marrow niche for survival and from short-lived plasma cells residing in secondary lymphoid organs (Manz et al. 2005). It is not known if changes to neural signaling associated with VNS can alter long- or short-lived plasma cell differentiation or survival; it is unlikely that VNS directly modulates plasma cell survival through efferent cholinergic signaling, as the vagus does not innervate the bone marrow or lymph nodes (Denes et al. 2005). However VNS may indirectly activate sympathetic reflex circuits via afferent fiber activation (Tanaka et al. 2021).

It is likely that this reflexive sympathetic activation is contributing to the reduction of total IgG titers in Continuous Burst animals. Noradrenergic signaling in the bone marrow has been shown to suppress CXCL12 expression (Mendez-Ferrer et al. 2008), which could affect plasma cell homing and differentiation (Hargreaves et al. 2001). Additionally, lymph nodes are sympathetically innervated and noradrenergic signaling may work through the β2-receptor to increase antibody secretion in primed B cells (Podojil and Sanders 2003), though the contribution of short-lived plasma cells to total IgG titers is small compared to long-lived plasma cells (Rozanski et al. 2011). However, aside from the effects of changing autonomic signaling, VNS has indirect effects on metabolism, especially when delivered using chronic schedules similar to our Continuous Burst parameters (Banni et al. 2012; Dai et al. 2020; de Lartigue 2016; Lyu et al. 2024; Vijgen et al. 2013). Metabolic effects of VNS may also influence antibody titers (Chandra 1975; Ksiazek and Konarzewski 2012; Nathan et al. 1977) thus overlapping with any direct effects of neurotransmitter release. We next sought to determine if metabolic effects are present in Continuous Burst animals.

Continuous Burst stimulation reduces total body weight, spleen size and total cellularity, compared to Twice Daily stimulation and No Stimulation

Continuous Burst stimulation causes a greater and more sustained reduction in body weight compared to Twice Daily (Fig. 3, A). Additionally, there are significant differences in body weight between No Stimulation and Continuous Burst from Day − 7 to Day 14. Twice Daily body weights are only different from No Stimulation on Day − 7. Continuous Burst animals have a significantly smaller spleen weight and fewer total splenocyte counts as well as fewer non-lymphocyte splenocytes compared to Twice Daily animals (Fig. 3, B-D).

Vagal activation is known to suppress appetite (de Lartigue 2016), reduce gut fat absorption (Lyu et al. 2024), and reduce body weight particularly with stimulation schedules resembling our Continuous Burst parameters (Fig. 1, A) (Banni et al. 2012; Dai et al. 2020; Vijgen et al. 2013; Mughrabi et al. 2023). Calorie restriction models similarly show weight loss alongside reduced spleen weight and lower splenocyte counts (Contreras et al. 2018). Weight loss alone can reduce antibody production (Nathan et al. 1977; Ksiazek and Konarzewski 2012) in calorie restriction models over a similar timeline to that in our study, which is the likely explanation for our findings with total IgG (Fig. 2, E-F). However, the fold change between body weight on Day − 7 [ 2.74 (CI: 1.19–4.23)] and total IgG titers on Day 0 [1.58 (CI: 0.46–2.65)] suggest a milder effect on antibody titers than expected. Also, body weight does not increase in unstimulated animals, which is atypical for 8–15 week mice; this is likely due to changes in activity and eating habits associated with single housing and the headcap connector (Pasquarelli et al. 2017).

We further characterized the spleens in stimulated animals by measuring absolute counts of splenic B and T cells. B cells are markedly reduced with Continuous Burst stimulation (Fig. 3, E), which may support our findings of reduced total IgG (Fig. 2, E-F) (Eckels et al. 1979). Total B cell counts are known to be reduced in calorie restriction models (Contreras et al. 2018), but there is limited evidence that direct effects of vagal activation, such as cholinergic or adrenergic receptor activation (Rosas-Ballina et al. 2011; Tanaka et al. 2021; Murray et al. 2021), can reduce B cell proliferation, production, or change egress from the spleen, and no such models have demonstrated reduced total B cell count in lymphoid organs. In contrast, nicotinic receptors have been shown to promote B cell survival in the spleen and increase production in the bone marrow (Skok et al. 2006, 2007). We previously found that the germinal center reaction was limited in Twice Daily stimulated animals, with lower counts of antigen-specific germinal center B (GCB) cells, but not total GCB cells (Kurata-Sato et al. 2024). In this study, Twice Daily stimulated animals are trending towards the findings from our prior study but do not reach significance. In contrast, Continuous Burst animals have reduced total GCB cells, as well as reduced NP + GCB cell subset (Fig. 3, F-G). This is likely due to a reduced total B cell population (Fig. 3, E) that can participate in germinal center formation (Victora and Nussenzweig 2022). This preliminary characterization of NP-antibody secreting cells suggests a dose-dependent effect of the level of vagal activation but more mechanistic study will be the subject of future work.

T cells were sub-divided into CD4 + and CD8 + sub-groups; only CD4 + are significantly reduced, while CD8 + totals are comparable to both other groups, indicating that the CD8 + population was less effected than CD4+ (Fig. 3, H-I). There are few reports in the literature showing differing effects on CD4 + vs. CD8 + T cell subsets of the factors likely present in our model, so our discussion is largely speculative. Adrenergic signaling has been shown to reduce proliferation of T cells in lymphoid organs (Feldman et al. 1987), while antagonism of alpha-1 receptors has been shown to increase lymphopoiesis in the thymus while altering lineage commitment to favor CD4 + cells over CD8 + through a mechanism suspected to involve reduced negative selection (Pesic et al. 2009). Thus, it is possible that the differential response of the CD4 + vs. CD8 + T cells may be related to increased thymic negative selection under influence of elevated adrenergic tone caused by reflex sympathetic activation from VNS (Tanaka et al. 2021; Murray et al. 2021). However, sympathetic signaling has also been shown to drive CD8 + T cell, but not CD4 + T cell, egress from the spleen in the context of a 3 day treatment with angiotensin II (Carnevale et al. 2020); our opposing findings may be explained by evolving dynamics of egress of lymphocytes in the progression of acute to chronic activation, or an indirect effect of other vagal circuits unrelated to the spleen. Weight loss is also a driving factor on the splenic T cell population, as calorie restriction models show increased apoptosis in both CD4 and CD8 subsets (Reddy Avula et al. 1999).

Normalizing non-lymphocyte splenocytes, B cells and T cells by the total population of splenocytes revealed that the reduction of cell populations in Continuous Burst animals is proportional such that there are no differences in the % composition of the spleen of these major populations (Fig. 3, J-M). Norepinephrine has been shown to cause splenic contracture by stimulating smooth muscle in the splenic capsule (Bakovic et al. 2013) and to promote leukocyte egress from the spleen (Ajmo et al. 2009; Rogausch et al. 1999); these mechanisms as well as the organ volume reduction seen with body weight loss (Weindruch and Sohal 1997) are possible explanations for the proportional reduction of splenocytes. Interestingly, the CD8+ %composition is not significantly higher in Continuous Burst animals compared to the other groups (Fig. 3, M) despite CD8 + cells being the only population comparable to the other groups in absolute counts (Fig. 3, I); this suggests that the CD8 + population was less affected but not entirely spared. IHC area measurements of B-cell, T-cell and red pulp zones show that Continuous Burst animals have lower means than the other groups, which aligns with flow cytometry data, however the statistical comparison fails to reach significance (Fig. 3, N-Q).

The lack of spleen size change in Twice Daily VNS may be related to the lack of weight loss or to differences in splenic nerve activity between the two stimulation schedules (Mughrabi et al. 2023). The changes in spleen size and cellularity alongside reduced antibody titers highlight the limitations of whole nerve VNS as a model for the study of neuroimmune interactions. Non-selective activation of autonomic reflexes by VNS, such as including those related to appetite and metabolism, can lead to confounding effects (e.g., weight loss) at higher doses, something that limits the applicability of such parametric studies. These limitations further highlight the continued need for parametric and controlled studies to optimize clinical benefits vs. off-target effects of neurostimulation (Chang et al. 2022). While these studies would be best done in human subjects, animal studies may reveal mechanisms limiting therapeutic effects at specific dosing schedules, like interactions with circadian rhythm or long-term adaptions in neural circuits.

Conclusions

In this report, we characterize a phenotype associated with a continuously elevated vagal tone to better understand its effect on antibody production. We find that chronically elevated vagal activity results in transiently increased HRV, reduced spleen size, reduced splenocyte count and antibody titers and weight loss, compared to intermittently elevated vagal activity. These outcomes are likely a result of direct and indirect effects of vagal fiber activation. These results underscore a need for more fiber-specific vagal activation models to study the effect of vagal-spleen signaling on the neuroimmune niche within the spleen.
