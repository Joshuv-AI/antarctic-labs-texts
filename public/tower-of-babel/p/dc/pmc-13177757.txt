Introduction

Imagine a familiar moment in an intensive care unit (ICU). A patient with septic shock deteriorates rapidly. Vasopressors are escalating, alarms are sounding, and the clinical team is balancing multiple priorities simultaneously. In such moments of cognitive overload, the promise of artificial intelligence (AI) is compelling. A digital assistant capable of summarizing evidence, drafting clinical notes, or suggesting differential diagnoses appears almost indispensable.

Yet, an uncomfortable reality sits beneath this promise: Fluency is not fidelity.

As AI—particularly large language models (LLMs)—begins to enter intensive care workflows, the profession must confront a fundamental truth. These systems can produce language that appears authoritative, coherent, and convincing, but they do not possess understanding. Their outputs are statistical predictions, not clinical reasoning.

Recent analyses of LLM safety vulnerabilities emphasize that hallucination—the generation of fabricated, distorted, or unverifiable information—is not a rare malfunction but a structural property of probabilistic language modeling.1 In critical care, where decisions carry immediate physiological and ethical consequences, this distinction matters profoundly. If inaccuracy is intrinsic rather than accidental, the safe adoption of AI requires not just enthusiasm but structured literacy and institutional oversight. This viewpoint aims to critically examine the implications of AI use in intensive care, with a focus on hallucination, automation bias, and accountability gaps, while proposing practical safeguards and an ICU-oriented framework for safe and responsible integration.

Structural Hallucination and ICU Vulnerability

Dong et al. recently synthesized the major safety vulnerabilities associated with LLMs, identifying hallucination, factual inconsistency, reasoning instability, and algorithmic opacity as recurring characteristics across architectures.1 In intensive care practice, these vulnerabilities manifest in ways that deserve particular attention.

First is fabricated authority. Artificial intelligence systems may generate references that appear academically credible yet do not exist. Fabricated biomedical citations have already been documented in real-world AI outputs.2 In a specialty built on evidence-based practice, such fabricated scholarship risks quietly contaminating academic discussions, clinical presentations, and even research manuscripts if not independently verified.

Second is reasoning instability. Critical care physiology rarely follows linear logic. The dynamic interplay between hemodynamics, ventilation, inflammation, and organ dysfunction requires contextual reasoning that evolves with each new data point. While systems such as GPT-4 have demonstrated impressive performance on structured medical examinations, success in exam-style questions does not translate into reliable bedside reasoning.3,4 These models recognize linguistic patterns; they do not internally model causal physiology.

Third is epistemic opacity. Artificial intelligence-generated outputs often appear confident regardless of their accuracy. Unless explicitly prompted, models rarely signal uncertainty. The result is an “illusion of certainty” that may subtly dampen clinicians’ instinct to question information presented with authority.

Artificial intelligence systems do not understand physiology; they approximate language.

A Clinical Vignette

Consider a common night-shift scenario. A fatigued resident managing a complex septic shock case turns to an AI tool to review evidence on early dual vasopressor therapy. Within seconds, the model produces a polished summary citing what appears to be a recent multicenter, randomized trial supporting the strategy.

The response sounds convincing. The citation appears legitimate. The recommendation seems reasonable.

Only later, during a morning case discussion, does someone attempt to locate the referenced study. It does not exist.

The patient's care fortunately remained unchanged, but the episode highlights an emerging challenge: Artificial intelligence can produce persuasive clinical narratives that feel authoritative while lacking factual grounding. The risk is not malicious fabrication—it is probability masquerading as knowledge.

Automation Bias and Cognitive Delegation

Critical care medicine has long recognized that cognitive biases contribute to clinical error. One such bias—automation bias—describes the human tendency to privilege machine-generated recommendations over independent judgment.5

Generative AI may amplify this tendency. Unlike traditional rule-based decision-support systems, LLMs produce narrative explanations that feel persuasive and intuitive. This narrative coherence can increase the perceived credibility of the output.

In high-acuity environments such as airway emergencies, vasopressor titration, or end-of-life discussions, persuasive coherence may gradually displace healthy clinical skepticism. The danger lies not in obvious errors but in plausible distortions that pass unchallenged.

Emerging Validation Evidence

Recent clinical evaluations illustrate both the promise and the limitations of generative AI in healthcare settings. Patel and Lam demonstrated that AI-assisted drafting of discharge summaries may reduce documentation burden and improve efficiency, but they emphasized that physician oversight remains essential.3 Similarly, Luo and colleagues reported strong performance of GPT-4o in the Chinese National Medical Licensing Examination–style questions while noting vulnerabilities to contextual reasoning traps and subtle misdirection.4

These studies suggest that generative AI may serve as a valuable tool for information retrieval and documentation support. However, the evidence does not support unsupervised clinical reasoning or decision-making.

The distinction between augmentation and delegation is therefore critical.

Governance, Not Prohibition

The appropriate response to these risks is not rejection but governance. Critical care has historically integrated complex technologies—from mechanical ventilation to extracorporeal life support—through structured training, competency frameworks, and institutional oversight. Generative AI represents a new category of cognitive technology that requires similar safeguards.

AI Literacy as a Professional Competency

Artificial intelligence literacy extends far beyond learning how to write prompts. It involves understanding how language models generate outputs, recognizing common hallucination patterns, maintaining disciplined verification practices, and appreciating the ethical boundaries of algorithmic assistance.

Most importantly, AI literacy requires recognizing the limits of these tools.

The ICU is not merely data-rich; it is morally complex. Families seek clarity during moments of profound uncertainty. Decisions about life-sustaining treatment carry emotional, ethical, and legal implications.

Algorithms can summarize information and recognize patterns, but they cannot shoulder responsibility for clinical judgment or empathetic communication. Even emerging discussions about AI-assisted informed consent emphasize that such tools should support—not replace—physician–patient dialog.6

In the age of generative AI, clinical vigilance must extend beyond physiology to epistemology.

A Word on Technological Solutionism

There is also a broader lesson. Artificial intelligence is often presented as a solution to systemic problems such as documentation burden, workforce shortages, and clinician burnout. While digital tools may help, they cannot repair structural inefficiencies in healthcare systems.

If residents are overwhelmed by administrative work, the answer may be better staffing, smarter workflows, or redesigned documentation processes—not simply another digital assistant that produces text requiring verification.

Technology should support clinicians, not obscure the deeper reforms healthcare systems require.

Conclusion

Generative AI offers genuine opportunities to improve efficiency, access to knowledge, and documentation workflows. Yet hallucination, reasoning instability, automation bias, and governance gaps represent real risks in high-acuity environments such as intensive care.

Safe integration, therefore, requires more than technical curiosity. It demands structured AI literacy, institutional oversight, and unwavering human accountability.

Algorithmic fluency must never be mistaken for clinical truth.

In the ICU, assistance may be digital. Responsibility remains human.
