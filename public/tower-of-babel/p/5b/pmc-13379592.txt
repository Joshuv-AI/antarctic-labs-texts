Fabrication and characterisation of hydroxypropyl methylcellulose/ethyl cellulose/polyvinylpyrrolidone based composite wafers as an anti-bacterial dressing: a promising approach for curcumin delivery
Authors: Samrat Chowdhury, Syed Mahmood, Mohd Aamir Mirza, Mohit Kumar, Eder Lilia Romero, Sabu Thomas, Wen-Cheng Chen, Kajal Ghosal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Wound healing is a complicated biological process primarily involving tissue regeneration and repair. However, conditions such as infection, poor blood circulation, or long-term illnesses/chronic diseases (like diabetes) can impede the healing process and cause delayed recovery. In order to address these challenges, authors developed wafers. The prepared wafers have emerged as a promising solution due to their ease of application, excellent biocompatibility, and ability to maintain a moist wound environment. These porous, sponge-like systems can also be loaded with bioactive agents, enabling sustained and controlled drug delivery. The wafer was fabricated using solvent casting method with polymeric mixture of hydroxypropyl methyl cellulose (HPMC), ethyl cellulose (EC), and polyvinyl pyrrolidone (PVP K-30) and loaded with curcumin to promote wound healing. The loaded curcumin was dispersed using a high-speed homogeniser and lyophilized in a controlled environment. The developed wafer formulation was further characterised using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), thermogravimetry–differential thermal analysis (TG–DTA), Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), X-ray diffraction analysis (XRD), tensile strength analysis, swelling index, and water vapour transmission rate. The in vitro drug release study was carried out mimicking USP 5 paddle-over-disc type dissolution apparatus and the results indicated that the created wafers have a sustained drug release while keeping the tissue moist. The anti-microbial potential of wafers was confirmed using the disc diffusion method. The developed wafer showed strong potential as an antibacterial wound dressing, providing controlled drug release along with fast-acting relief from bacterial infections.

Introduction

Wound healing is a multifaceted phenomenon encompassing various stages, including haemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodelling1,2. Wounds are typically classified as either acute or chronic, depending on their healing patterns: acute wounds heal normally through the four stages of healing haemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodelling, whereas chronic wounds show delayed or disrupted healing due to underlying factors, such as diabetes, persistent infections, prolonged inflammation, or poor circulation, which make them much more difficult to manage3.

The prevalence of chronic wounds in India is estimated to be 4.5 per 1,000 people. DFUs are more prevalent than other long-term diabetes complications and may cause death, accounting for 25% of hospitalizations and 50% of lower limb amputations that are attributable to diabetic complications. The number of people with diabetes is estimated at 77 million, of which 25% will have a DFU (https://woundsinternational.com/journal-articles/assessment-of-chronic-wound-patients-journey-in-india-a-survey-of-the-perspectives-of-healthcare-professionals-and-patients/). The India Wound Care Management Market is projected to grow at a CAGR of 6.2% from USD 277.1 million in 2022 to USD 374.44 million by 2029 (https://www.mordorintelligence.com/industry-reports/india-wound-care-management-devices-market.).

The presently available dressings for treating wound infections are available in the form of microemulsion, hydrocolloids, hydrogels, transparent films, electrospun fibres, and even the direct application of antibiotics to wounds4–8. Curcumin was tried to be delivered through different carrier due to their different therapeutic properties9–11,). The current study presents a novel polysaccharide-based wafer system for wound healing that incorporates curcumin in a dispersed form to circumvent its inherent solubility and bioavailability challenges, and unlike conventional dressings (hydrogels or films), the developed HPMC-based porous wafers simultaneously maintain moisture and absorb high levels of exudate to maintain an optimal wound environment.12. In this study, for the first time, sustained drug release with enhanced antimicrobial activity was combined into a single platform to control infection and promote tissue regeneration, and the wafer architecture was designed to promote effective vapor exchange to reduce the risk of maceration and secondary infections. Curcumin was incorporated into the biocompatible matrix in a dispersed form, which could be used as a novel strategy for localized and sustained drug delivery with potential advantages over antibiotic-based therapies, which may reduce resistance.

Materials and methods

Materials

Pure curcumin and hydroxypropyl methylcellulose K 15 M were obtained from Yarrow Pharma Ltd., Maharashtra, India. HPMC K15 M had a viscosity of approximately 15,000 cP when dissolved in 2% water. Molecular weight for HPMC K15 was approximately 86 kDa (kilodaltons). Ethyl Cellulose (EC) was obtained from CDH (P) Ltd., Delhi, India. Polyvinylpyrrolidone (K-30) was obtained from Sisco Research Laboratories Pvt Ltd., Maharashtra, India. The molecular weight of PVP was 40,000. The nitrogen content was 11.6%, and the pH of the 5% aqueous solution was 4.6. Denatured ethanol and chloroform were obtained from Merck Life Science Pvt. Ltd., Maharashtra, India.

Preformulation study

Physical characterization

The physical characterization of colour, texture, and odour of the drug was carried out by visual representation.

Determination of melting point

The melting point of curcumin was determined using a bunsen burner. The heat was applied to one end of the capillary tube to seal it. The capillary tube’s open end was then used to insert the drug curcumin. The filled capillary tube was inserted into the tube holder of the melting point apparatus (Monarch Scientific Industries) to measure the drug’s melting point. The temperature control knob was turned to the correct setting. Both the starting temperature at which the medication began to melt and the end temperature at which it melted completely were measured and recorded.

Determination of calibration curve

The calibration curve of curcumin was prepared using phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) in a 100 ml volumetric flask, with 10 mg of accurately weighed pure curcumin, thereby forming the stock solution. 1 ml of ethanol was used to dissolve curcumin initially. The absorbance of a curcumin solution in the concentration range of 2–20 µg/ml, which was generated from the stock solution, was measured in triplicate at 429 nm to obtain the standard calibration curve for curcumin. The curcumin calibration curve was then drawn on an x-axis representing curcumin concentration in ppm and a y-axis representing absorbance (Majumdar et al. 2020).

The desiccator’s relative humidity was stabilized with the use of silica beads at the base of the desiccator. Periodically, the cups were taken out and weighed. TG–DTA

TG–DTA used to determine the thermal stability, composition, and degradation behaviour of materials during controlled heating to identify melting and crystallization which detect potential drug-excipient interactions. In the current research, the 5 mg of the sample was taken in an aluminum crucible on a Perkin Elmer TG/DTA Diamond Thermal Analyzer. The wafer samples were analyzed in a dynamic-air environment (synthetic air 5.0 Linde Gas)19. The tests were conducted from 30 to 300 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min20. Exothermic and endothermic events were identified using calculated differential thermal analysis (c-DTA)21.

X-RAY diffraction (XRD)

XRD analysis is used to characterize whether materials are crystalline or amorphous and to identify their structural features, confirm the presence of certain crystalline phases, detect alterations in crystallinity after formulation. In order to identify the structural features, physical form (amorphous or crystalline) of the pure drug, blank wafer and drug-loaded wafers, 100 mg samples were examined using XRD (Bruker axs, D8 Advance). Using a glass slide, the wafers were compressed to cover the holder’s round tiles. Transparent plastic cling film was then used to safely attach the wafers to the sample cells. The D8 Advance X-ray diffractometer was used in transmission mode for the experiment. The equipment had a 0.6 mm exit slit, a primary solar slit of 4°, and a secondary solar slit of 2.5°. The tests were performed at 40 kV voltage and 40 mA current12.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

FTIR spectroscopy is used to identify functional groups and the chemical structure of a material. This step is crucial in order to authenticate the drug substance. To determine the drugs-polymers compatibility the developed wafers were characterized using an FTIR spectrophotometer (ITPrestige-21, Shimadzu) and a ZnSe attenuated total reflectance (ATR) accessory to identify the functional groups of curcumin and polymers used and analyze the chemical interactions between them. A potassium bromide (KBr) beam splitter and MCT detector were installed on the FTIR. Wafers (Blank, F1, F2, and F3) were positioned on a 45 °C ZnSe ATR crystal, and maximum pressure was applied using a pressure clamp accessory to provide close contact between the wafers and the crystal. Similarly, curcumin (drug) alone was examined as a control. OMNICR software was utilized to record spectra at a resolution of 4 cm−1 over a range of 650–4000 cm−1. Each sample’s true absorbance was calculated by removing the ATR crystal’s background spectrum information22.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

SEM is a high-resolution analytical technique used to examine the surface morphology and microstructure of materials, which provides information on particle shape, size, surface characteristics, uniformity, and aggregation. Structural properties that affect the performance of pharmaceutical systems are important to understand. To analyze the surface morphology and internal structure of the lyophilized wafer SEM analysis was carried out for blank, F1, F2, and F3 formulations. Double-sided adhesive carbon tape was applied to labelled stainless steel stubs to prepare the exterior surfaces of the wafers for SEM analysis. With caution not to harm the wafers’ surface topography, the samples were positioned on the exposed side of the carbon adhesive. Next, these were put inside the Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FESEM) (FEI QUANTA FEG650S, Class One Equipment) chamber after sputter coating with a thin layer of electrically conductive gold under a vacuum for charge neutralization.

In-vitro drug release study

The USP Paddle-over-disc Type dissolution apparatus was used for the in vitro study23. The setup was simulated by placing the wafers (F1, F2, and F3) in a Petri dish, and a muslin cloth was tied around it to act as a semipermeable membrane. Wafers were thus exposed to phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) across the muslin cloth. The dissolution study was conducted at 37 ± 0.5 °C at 50 rpm, with 900 mL of phosphate buffer inside every dissolution jar. Samples of 1 mL each were taken out at regular time intervals up to 8 h and diluted with 9 mL buffer solution and subjected to analysis at 429 nm (λmax of curcumin in ethanol) using a UV spectrophotometer against a standard buffer solution. The sink condition was maintained throughout the dissolution process.

Release kinetics

A kinetic model can help explain the pharmacokinetics of a drug delivery carrier. The volume, solubility, crystallinity, and size of the drug, together with the kind of carrier the drug is encapsulated in, all significantly influence how the drug releases from the formulation. The kinetics of drug release are described by a variety of kinetic models (Ghosal, K. and Nanda, A.24). This includes zero-order (Qt = K0t + Q0) (Möckel et al. 2009), first-order (Qt = Q0e−Kft)25, Higuchi (Qt = KH√t + Q0)26,Paul et al. 2011), Korsmeyer-Peppas (Qt/Q∞ = Kktn)27,28,, and Hixon-Crowell (Q01/3- Qt1/3 = Kst)26,29. The amounts of drug released at time t (Qt), the initial amount (Q0), and the release rate constants (K0, Kf, KH, Ks, Kk) for zero-order kinetics, first-order kinetics, the Higuchi model, the Hixon- Crowell model, and the Korsmeyer-Peppas model are all included in these equations. To determine the release kinetics, the data from in vitro release was plotted in the different kinetic models, and R2 value of various kinetic models was then calculated to determine the mechanism of release of the drug.

Anti-microbial study

The drug’s efficacy as an antibacterial was evaluated using the disc diffusion method. Gram-positive B. subtilis (ATCC 6633), and Gram-negative Escherichia coli (ATCC 10,536) were the two types of bacteria that were selected. Müller Hinton (MH) agar was utilized for the tests of antimicrobial effectiveness and inoculation, whereas nutrient agar was employed as the culture medium. The antimicrobial wafer was punched into discs with a 6 mm diameter.

The turbidity of the suspension suggested that there were 1 × 108–2 × 108 CFU/mL of B. subtilis or E. coli in the bacterial suspension. The bacteria multiply quickly; thus, the suspension was used within 15 min after preparation.

Using the disc diffusion method for antimicrobials, the dried surface of the MH agar plate was infected by streaking the sterile swab over the entire surface three times after dipping it into the inoculum tube. Approximately sixty rotations of the plate were performed to guarantee uniform dispersion of the inoculum. Before the disc was placed, the agar surface was left to dry for three to five mins at room temperature. Prior to the assay the disc was placed in a sterile petri dish inside a laminar airflow cabinet and exposed to UV light for 30–60 min per side to guarantee sterilization of the disc. To guarantee full contact between the wafer disc and the agar surface, the antimicrobial-containing wafer disc was placed onto the dried agar surface and carefully pushed with forceps.

Three identical wafer samples (selected randomly from Blank, F1, F2, and F3) were present in each petri dish. Following which, the plate was turned over and incubated for twenty-four hours at 37 ± 1 °C. After incubation, the diameter of the disc was used as a reference to measure the zone sizes with a ruler. The zone of inhibition diameter for both the bacteria was determined (Sharma et al.30).

Statistical analysis

The manuscript presents the data as mean values ± standard deviations (SD). The steady-state of release rate was calculated by analysing the slope of the linear relationship between the percentage of cumulative drug released vs time (t, hours). Microsoft Excel software was utilized for data analysis.

Swelling index (%) determination

Swelling is a crucial feature for hydrophilic polymer-based matrices, like the wafer developed in the present study, since it influences other functional attributes like mucoadhesion, disintegration rate, drug dissolution, and ultimately release from the swollen wafer. It is dependent upon a number of physical characteristics, such as mechanical strength, porosity, and matrix density, etc. Increased chain mobility resulting from moisture absorption during swelling causes polymer chains to change from a glassy to a rubbery state (Farias et al. 2018).

Figure 4 shows the swelling index of blank wafer, F1, F2, and F3 formulation. It depicts that higher curcumin (drug) content in wafer (F3) showed a lower swelling index, which may be due to the lower solubility of curcumin in aqueous media35. Moreover, with an increase in time, the swelling profile shows higher values. Thus, F1 formulation shows the highest swelling index and thus better swelling properties as compared to F2 and furthermore to F3. These data signify that curcumin wafer formulated with a hydrophilic polymeric mixture is suitable for potential gradual release of curcumin.Fig. 4Swelling index of blank, F1, F2 and F3 wafer formulation.

Swelling index of blank, F1, F2 and F3 wafer formulation.

Tensile strength

Texture analysis of the wafer (F1, F2 and F3) was done by comparing their tensile strength and extensibility. It is demonstrated that with an increment in drug (curcumin) content in the wafer, the tensile strength is found to decrease. This phenomenon can be due to the hydrophobicity of curcumin. Thus, F1 formulation having lower hydrophobic drug content have higher tensile strength than F2 or F3. The extensibility of the wafer formulation was shown to be negligible due to the porous and rigid nature of the wafer36. All three formulations are showing no extensibility at all.

Water vapour transmission rate

The constant flow of water vapour through a unit area of the object in a unit of time, through a certain parallel surface, under temperature and humidity conditions is known as the water vapour transmission rate.

To prevent exudate accumulation at the wound site, a wound dressing should be able deliver the ideal water vapour transmission rate. After eight days of loss of water vapour, the WVTR of Blank, F1, F2, and F3 were respectively found to be, 628.932, 574.305, 543.059, and 530.213 (g/m2/day). Study results (Fig. 5) showed that every wafer had the ability to permeate water vapour through them, optimally. Sustaining proper moisture levels is crucial for the healing process because it promotes migration of cell and aids in the epithelialization of the wound site.Fig. 5Comparison of WVTR of blank, F1, F2 and F3 formulation after 8 days of reading.

Comparison of WVTR of blank, F1, F2 and F3 formulation after 8 days of reading.

Research indicates that the WVT rate for uninjured skin is 204 g/m2/day, but for first-degree burns and granulating wounds, it varies from 279 to 5138 g/m2/day. The wafer formulations employed in the current study had WVT rates between 530.213 and 628.932 g/m2/day. It showed that the wafer formulations could keep low to medium-level exudative wounds moistened at the wound site without severe dehydration, by being sufficiently permeable to water vapour. Furthermore, the outcomes demonstrated that the WVTR of the different wafer compositions had differed significantly37. The presence of curcumin in the wafer enhanced hydrophobicity as a result WVTR decreased from F1 formulation to F3 formulation gradually.

This reduction in WVTR with increasing drug loading may indicate restricting vapour permeability while still maintaining adequate moisture balance. Such controlled moisture transmission, in combination with sustained drug release characteristics, is advantageous for wound healing applications as it helps create a stable and protective microenvironment at the wound site.

TG–DTA

Figure 6 shows the TG–DTA curve of the pure drug, blank wafer, and F2 formulation wafer. The DTA curve of pure curcumin (Fig. 6A) showed a sharp endothermic peak at 174ºC associated with the melting point of curcumin, whereas an exothermic peak at 260ºC shows the thermal degradation of the drug (Roy et al.38). On comparing the TGA data for the blank wafer (Fig. 6B), it can be depicted that the thermal degradation process of blank wafer can be divided into two stages; first stage being between 58ºC—85ºC which shows free water and bound water volatilization and the second stage degradation was between 235ºC—315ºC, which may be due to the degradation and depolymerisation process of the wafer formulation.Fig. 6TG–DTA graph of (A) pure drug, (B) blank wafer and (C) F2 formulation.

TG–DTA graph of (A) pure drug, (B) blank wafer and (C) F2 formulation.

Whereas, in drug loaded wafer (Fig. 6C), the first degradation stages due to the evaporation of volatile compounds occurred around 85ºC-110ºC. Between 200 ºC – 320 ºC, the second degradation stage signifies the degradation of the wafer. The drug-free and drug-loaded wafer showed identical thermal decomposition, which shows no change in the thermal stability of the wafer when curcumin is added to it (Roy et al.39). Moreover, the DTA curve of drug-loaded wafers (Fig. 6C) showed no endothermic or exothermic peak, confirming molecular dispersion of the drug inside the wafer matrix. DTA curves for both blank and drug-loaded wafer were the same, confirming that the addition of curcumin occurred in molecular dispersion form, and the presence of the drug didn’t change the stability of the wafer.

Disappearance of sharp peaks40,41 that appeared in case of curcumin, is mainly due molecular dispersion of drug inside the polymeric mixture of the wafer (Alzainy et al.42). This can be justified due to the homogenization step of the drug loaded polymeric mixture before vacuum drying.

It can be observed that with an increase in the concentration of the drug in F2 and F3 formulations, there has not been enhanced drug release but rather decreased drug release from wafer formulations. The curcumin loaded porous matrix thus prepared within the wafers can be used as a potential antibacterial wound dressing with slow drug release and quick relief from bacteria and inflammation due to these release patterns.

Release kinetics

The release of a drug from the patch-based formulation depends on various underlying factors, pH, temperature, drug solubility, polymer amount, drug diffusion through the polymeric matrix of the wafer, wafer’s porosity and swelling index, and the water vapour transmission rate. Wafers comprise a drug and a polymeric matrix where the drug is uniformly encapsulated inside the porous matrix structure, and the drug is released following some mechanism.

The Korsmeyer-Peppas model describes drug release from a polymeric system when the release mechanism is unknown. It is applied to the release profile to depict the diffusion type. After analysing the data obtained from Korsmeyer Peppas model, it was observed that n values in all formulations are from 0.3585–0.2277, which fall in the range of n ≤ 0.45, indicating Case I diffusion, also known as Fickian Diffusion. Here, drug release mainly occurs due to diffusion through the pores present in the wafer, and it complies with first order. From the data obtained by using First order equation, the R2 values of release profiles of all formulations are 0.9846 -0.9292, which shows acceptable linearity and show higher values than zero order.

The profiles obtained from the Higuchi model are linear, as supported by the R2 values (0.9919—0.9588). These values are higher than Hixson-Crowell, establishing diffusion-dominated release. Conclusion

The wound healing property of wafers are a major advance in the management of chronic wounds that are complicated by diabetes, chronic systemic inflammation, persistent infection, tissue destruction, poor primary treatment, and patient-related factors like poor nutrition. The developed could have potential to treat diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) and venous leg ulcers (VLUs) after exploring it’s ex-vivo potential. The pre-formulation investigations are required for the drug used in formulation before loading the drug in wafers, for targeted drug delivery can be prepared. To check for interactions, drug, blank, and drug-loaded wafers underwent various instrumental analyses. The wafer’s thermal stability and crystallinity were demonstrated by the TG–DTA and XRD graphs. In order to assess the wafer’s suitability for injured target sites, a number of physical characteristics were also measured, including folding endurance, WVTR, and swelling index; all of which displayed encouraging outcomes. Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) investigation has verified that the medication, curcumin, has been successfully incorporated into the wafer. SEM, or scanning electron microscopy, showed porous matrix of the prepared wafer with sized drug particles encapsulated amidst them. Because the wafer is porous, drugs can be released over time, improving wound healing by keeping the surrounding tissue moist and encouraging the growth of new cells. The release profile shows a consistent drug release over a long time, which is essential for treating chronic wounds like DFUs. By eliminating the need for frequent wafer changes and enhancing patient compliance, this optimized release profile guarantees a constant therapeutic impact.
