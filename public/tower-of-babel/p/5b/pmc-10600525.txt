Verification of different methods used for isolating Salmonella enterica serovar Dublin from cattle feces
Authors: Masatoshi FUJIHARA, Mika HAYASHI, Kiwako HARA, Noriko SAKAZUME, Takuma TSUKUDA, Yuuka TAGAINO
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractSalmonella enterica serovar Dublin is a cattle-adapted serovar, and some infected cattle can become asymptomatic carriers. Identification of carrier animals is important for preventing the spread of infection within a farm, but low diagnostic sensitivity of the fecal culture method is problematic. In this study, we investigated isolation methods of four S. enterica Dublin strains. Selective enrichment using the tetrathionate broth showed better performance than Rappaport-Vassiliadis R10 broth, but one of the strains was not detectable. Since isolation of such strains by selective enrichment can be difficult, we designed a method using immuno-plates that concentrates S. enterica Dublin by antigen-antibody reaction. Our method is able to detect approximately 200 clony-forming units of S. enterica Dublin in 0.1 g of cattle feces. If S. enterica Dublin was isolated from cattle with clinical signs, the method to identify carriers in the farm should be based on the growth kinetics of the target S. enterica Dublin strain.Keywords: cattle, isolation culture, Salmonella enterica serovar Dublin

Salmonella enterica subspecies enterica serovar Dublin is a cattle-adapted serovar which causes enteritis and/or systemic diseases, including septicemia and abortion, in bovine hosts [18]. The infectious dose of S. enterica Dublin may be strain-dependent, but more than 106 colony-forming units (CFU) usually lead to clinical signs in calves between 0 and 6 months of age [10, 17]. Not all infected cattle excrete bacteria in feces, but when they do, the average duration has been reported to be 15−17 days in calves with clinical signs and shorter in older animals as well as animals with subclinical infections [10, 12]. In a few cases, shedding in feces may continue for months or years, usually intermittently. Latent carriers only shed low amounts of bacteria (1−104 CFU/g), but active carriers may shed similar levels in acutely infected cows (up to 108 CFU/g) [10]. Carriers of salmonellosis and contaminated feedstuffs and environments are major problems. To limit the spread within a herd, infected animals should be identified and either culled or treated. Treated animals must be rechecked several times to confirm that they are no longer disease carriers. Asymptomatic animals identified as carriers through repeated positive fecal cultures over a course of several months should be removed from the herd.

There are multiple reports on selective enrichment for the isolation of S. enterica; however, low diagnostic sensitivity of the fecal culture method is problematic in the examination of S. enterica Dublin infections [11]. Peterz et al. [14] investigated Salmonella growth in Rappaport Vassiliadis broth at 40, 42, and 43°C and S. enterica Dublin showed lower proliferation than that of other serovars at high temperatures. Additionally, we previously examined the growth kinetics of S. enterica Dublin (two strains) and both of the strains did not grow in Hajna tetrathionate broth, Rappaport broth and modified semisolid Rappaport agar [3, 4]. In this study, we identified the appropriate selective enrichment required to isolate S. enterica Dublin strains.

Salmonella strains used in this study

Growth on different media

Four S. enterica Dublin strains were plated on xylose lysine deoxycholate (XLD) agar (Kanto Chemical, Tokyo, Japan), mannitol lysine crystal violet brilliant green (MLCB) agar (Nissui, Tokyo, Japan) and ES Salmonella agar II (ES2 agar) (Eiken, Tokyo, Japan), and incubated at 37°C.

The growth of these strains was examined in different broth media. Approximately 8.0 × 103 CFU of four S. enterica Dublin strains and S. enterica Typhimurium K13-R3-15 (as the control microorganism) were inoculated in 3 mL (2.7 × 103 CFU/mL) of Enterobacteriaceae enrichment mannitol (EEM) broth (Nissui), tetrathionate (TT) broth (Nissui) and Rappaport-Vassiliadis R10 (RV) broth (Becton, Dickson and Co., Sparks, MD, USA). Cultures were incubated at 37°C in EEM and 42°C in TT and RV broths, and CFUs were measured by plating on XLD agar.

Isolation of S. enterica Dublin from artificially contaminated cattle feces

Five samples of adult cattle feces (A−E) from five Salmonella-negative farms were used for this study. Four strains of S. enterica Dublin culture solutions, grown in EEM broth overnight, were diluted 1:1,000 to 1:1,000,000 with distilled water. Approximately 0.1 g of each fecal samples and 25 μL of S. enterica Dublin suspensions (approximately 20 to 2.0 × 104 CFU) were inoculated in 3 mL of TT and RV broth. After a 24 hr incubation period at 42°C, each culture was plated onto XLD agar for S. enterica Dublin isolation. According to the ratio of Salmonella to other bacterial colonies on XLD agar, we defined “dominant” as more than half of the colonies being Salmonella colonies, and “inferior” as Salmonella colonies being less than the other bacterial colonies.

Stress recovery in each broth

S. enterica Dublin K13-R1-3, K13-R3-14, K13-R3-30 and S. enterica Typhimurium K13-R3-15, grown in EEM broth overnight, were diluted to 1:1,000,000 with distilled water and 50 μL of the bacterial suspension was pipetted on cotton swabs (approximately 40 to 4.0 × 107 CFU). After drying for 6 and 24 hr at 25°C, each cotton swab was inoculated in three different tubes containing buffered peptone water (BPW) (Nissui), EEM, or TT broth. After 24 hr incubation at 37°C (BPW and EEM broth) and 42°C (TT broth), cell growth was determined by plating on XLD agar.

Isolation using immuno-plates

Salmonella O9 agglutinating antisera (Denka Seiken Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) was diluted 1:500 with carbonate buffer (pH 9.6) and used to coat Nunc MaxiSorpTM flat-bottom 96-well plates (Nunc, Roskilde, Denmark) at 4°C overnight. The plates were blocked with phosphate buffered saline (PBS) containing 1% skim milk and 0.5% Tween 80 for 1 hr at room temperature and then washed three times using PBS containing 1% Tween 80 (PBST).

Four strains of S. enterica Dublin grown in EEM broth overnight were diluted 1:1,000 to 1:1,000,000 with distilled water. Five samples of adult cattle feces (F−J) from five Salmonella-negative farms were used for these experiments. Approximately 0.1 g of each fecal samples and 25 μL of S. enterica Dublin suspensions (approximately 20 to 2.0 × 104 CFU) were inoculated in 900 μL of PBST containing 1 g/L peptone and 20 mg/L novobiocin (PBSTPn), and 900 μL of EEM broth containing 20 mg/L novobiocin (EEMn) in a 96-well deep-well plate. After 24 hr incubation at 37°C, 100 μL of each culture was added to immuno-plates and incubated for 90 min at 37°C. After washing the plates with PBST, the bottom of each well was scraped using a disposable needle and inoculated on XLD agar. To verify the effectiveness of the immuno-plates, incubated PBSTPn and EEMn was also directly plated on XLD agar.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we investigated the growth kinetics of four S. enterica Dublin strains in different media. All strains formed characteristic Salmonella colonies on XLD agar within 24 hr. Although S. enterica Typhimurium K13-R3-15 formed characteristic colonies on MLCB agar within 24 hr (data not shown), all of the S. enterica Dublin strains needed 48 hr incubation. ES2 agar which contains novobiocin, partially suppressed the growth of strain K13-R1-3. Therefore, we used XLD agar for further experiments. The growth kinetics in selective enrichment broth was shown to be strain-dependent. S. enterica Dublin exhibits a longer lag phase than S. enterica Typhimurium (control) in RV broth and on MLCB agar. Similarly, Harvey and Price reported that S. enterica Dublin forms smaller colonies than S. enterica Typhimurium on brilliant green MacConkey agar [6]. Growth of S. enterica Dublin is greatly affected by some dyes, such as brilliant green, malachite green and crystal violet.

Our results suggest that TT broth is suitable for S. enterica Dublin detection. Although S. enterica Dublin strains used in these experiments were intact, rectal swab samples collected on the farm will be dried before laboratory culture. Selective medium containing a bile acid component is known to suppress injured bacterial cell growth [1]. Therefore, we investigated the recovery of dry-stressed S. enterica in each medium. Although S. enterica Dublin is known to survive in the environment for a shorter period of time than S. enterica Typhimurium [9], our results suggest that the extent of damage the bacteria are able to endure as a result of drying can be strain-dependent but not serovar-dependent. Our results also show that BPW recovered dry-stressed S. enterica more effectively than EEM and TT broth. EEM broth is used as a pre-enrichment medium in the Japanese standard method and contains a bile acid component and brilliant green, which might be lethal to the injured cells [5]. Okamoto et al. [13] showed that BPW pre-enrichment was more effective than EEM broth for Salmonella detection in minced chicken. The case for the pre-enrichment of fecal samples is less clear. Rigby and Pettit [16] reported that pre-enrichment in BPW increases the Salmonella detection rate from chicken feces; however, Kim et al. [8] reported that the pre-enrichment step did not increase the detection rate in pig feces. The effect of BPW and EEM broth pre-enrichment requires further investigation.

S. enterica Dublin K13-R3-13 showed no growth in TT broth. Since isolation of this strain by selective enrichment is difficult, we tried to concentrate the bacteria by antigen-antibody reaction. There are some reports using immunomagnetic beads [5], but the use of commercially available immunomagnetic beads is unsuitable for multi-sample processing. Therefore, we prepared 96-well immuno-plates for improved isolation rates, but they were only slightly better. This result is in line with a previous report using immunomagnetic beads [5]. Latent carriers shed small amounts (1−104 CFU/g) of S. enterica Dublin [10]; therefore, it is important to improve detection sensitivity, even if only slightly. In our study, the fecal sample F was considered to contain the lowest number of enteric bacterial cells which were resistant to novobiocin and grew on XLD agar. While on contrary, other bacterial cells present in the fecal sample J proliferate more vigorously than S. enterica Dublin especially in EEMn enrichment. Microbial flora strongly affects isolation sensitivity and nutrient-poor broth is suitable for this method. In the cases of fecal sample G−J, detection limit for strain K13-R1-3 was higher than other strains. This strain had less CFU after 24 hr incubation in PBSTPn than other strains (Supplementary Table). Interestingly, addition of novobiocin suppressed the growth of K13-1–3 in EEMn but only had slight effect in PBSTPn. Though addition of novobiocin is known to improve S. enterica isolation [2, 7], as Restaino et al. [15] reported that the degree of growth inhibition was different in supplying media, novobiocin in PBSTPn may not suppress the sensitive bacteria. While isolating S. enterica Dublin, which does not grow in TT and other selective enrichment broth, for screening of epidemiologically related cattle, concentration of novobiocin should be examined after determining the sensitivity of the target strain.

In conclusion, S. enterica Dublin strains show lower proliferation in selective medium than S. enterica Typhimurium. All the investigated strains took 48 hr to grow on MLCB agar. The growth on ES2 as well as in TT and RV broths is strain-dependent. When S. enterica Dublin is isolated from some cattle with clinical signs, it is required to identify the carriers on the farm. The method used for inspection of the carriers should be decided based on the growth kinetics of the target S. enterica Dublin strains in the selective medium. Selective enrichment using the TT broth showed good performance in isolation test, however, dry-stressed S. enterica did not grow in TT broth. We propose that pre-enrichment using BPW can improve isolation sensitivity from dried samples. We have designed a method using immune-plates, since one strain did not grow in TT broth. Our method is much more suitable for multi-sample processing.
