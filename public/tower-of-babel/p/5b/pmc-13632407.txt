Epidemiological trends of non-small cell lung cancer and systemic treatment rates as part of initial therapeutic strategy from 2016 to 2020 in Greece: results of the epidemiological part of INFINITY study

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn view of the shifting standard of care for non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), we aimed to capture the evolution of the epidemiological and systemic treatment (ST) landscape in Greece.MethodsThis non-interventional, single-country, multicenter, retrospective study, collected high-level aggregate data for adult patients who were newly diagnosed with histologically/cytologically confirmed Stage I-IV NSCLC between 01-Jan-2016 and 31-Dec-2020 (index period) at leading oncology clinics across the country.ResultsOverall, 10,644 patients were newly diagnosed with NSCLC during the entire 5-year index period at the 18 participating sites (63.8% of the patients inside Attica; 51.0% at public academic institutions). An increasing trend in NSCLC diagnoses was observed throughout the index period, with the largest rise seen from n = 1765 in 2016 to n = 2013 in 2017 which was driven by an abrupt increase in Stage IV diagnoses, and the lowest from n = 2332 in 2019 to n = 2373 in 2020. Over the index period, 5.9%, 10.3%, 23.4% and 60.4% of all cases were Stage I, II, III, and IV, respectively. Of the Stage I and II patients, 41.3% and 73.3% were treated with ST, respectively, which mostly comprised of chemotherapy, with negligible variations through the years. Most of the Stage III and IV patients received ST (85.9% and 88.4%, respectively) with respective overall chemotherapy rates of 82.6% and 63.8% which remained relatively constant over the examined years, and immunotherapy utilisation rates of 29.6% and 31.3% which exhibited gradual and substantial increases (reaching 43.5% and 55.0% in 2020). Among Stage IV patients, 6.6% and 4.8% were treated with targeted and investigational therapies, respectively, accounting for the majority of NSCLC patients treated with such therapies (88.4% and 86.6%). Institutional variations were noticed in temporal trends of NSCLC diagnoses, stage distribution, and ST rates.ConclusionThe number of new NSCLC cases continues to rise with more than half still presenting with metastatic disease. Albeit certain medical practice variations, the observed remarkable increases in immunotherapy use are aligned with immune checkpoint inhibitor approvals and their incorporation into European and national clinical practice guidelines.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16441-0.

Introduction

According to 2020 estimates, lung cancer was the fourth most commonly diagnosed cancer and the leading cause of cancer-related deaths in EU-27 countries, accounting for 11.9% of all cancer diagnoses and 20.4% of all cancer-related deaths [1]. With an estimated 8,960 cases and 7,662 deaths in 2020, lung cancer had both the highest incidence and mortality among all cancer sites in Greece [2]. Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for 80–90% of lung cancer diagnoses [3–5].

Historically, NSCLC patients have had poor prognosis, especially those diagnosed at later disease stages; 5-year overall survival is estimated at ≤ 10% in metastatic patients and ranges from 92% in patients with Stage IA1 to 13% in those with Stage IIIC disease [5]. Surgical resection is considered the best option with curative intent for Stage I-IIIA patients depending on the health status of the patient, while radiotherapy is also a viable option for early and later-stage disease, either alone or in combination with platinum-based chemotherapy (PBC) [6, 7]. PBC has been an integral part of NSCLC treatment for several years, as follows: in the adjuvant setting for resected stage II and III NSCLC, but also for patients with resected stage IB disease and primary tumor size > 4 cm, and as main treatment for Stage IIIB-IV patients, with the exception of oncogene-addicted NSCLC patients [3, 6–9]. For the latter, targeted therapy (TT) has proven more effective, hence recommended options include tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) [7, 9, 10]. Nowadays, the advent of immune-oncology (IO) agents with improved treatment outcomes has brought new hope to lung cancer patients and has gradually become the standard of care, either as monotherapy or combination therapy (chemoimmunotherapy), for advanced NSCLC without actionable oncogenic driver mutations [8, 11].

After EMA approval of the first immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI), nivolumab, a programmed death protein 1 (PD-1) inhibitor, as second-line treatment in advanced squamous NSCLC in September 2015, numerous ICIs have received EMA approval for NSCLC treatment in different settings of the disease [7, 8, 11]. The major breakthrough came with the first-line opportunities for patients with advanced NSCLC, with first EMA approval in December 2016 for pembrolizumab, a PD-1 inhibitor, in NSCLC with programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1) tumor proportion score (TPS) ≥ 50%. Later, multiple ICIs proved to be superior [in combination with PBC or inhibitors of the cytotoxic T-lymphocyte associated protein 4 (CTLA-4)] over standard treatment, in advanced NSCLC patients, regardless of PD-L1 expression [7, 8, 11]. The therapeutic indications of ICIs have now extended to the early-stage setting, namely, as consolidation for chemoradiotherapy (CRT)-treated unresected Stage III NSCLC patients in 2018 [7, 12] and more recently as neoadjuvant, adjuvant and perioperative therapy of resected epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)/anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK)-negative NSCLC [7, 13]. TTs have also entered the early-stage setting as adjuvant therapy for EGFR-mutated stage IB-IIIA NSCLC [13]. More approvals for ICIs and TTs in the pre- and/or post-operative setting are anticipated shortly [10, 13–16].

As recent therapeutic advances rapidly expand the NSCLC armamentarium, streamlining the care pathway and changing the treatment paradigm, it is essential to gain a comprehensive understanding of the evolution of the local epidemiological and treatment landscape that can inform and guide national healthcare plans for lung cancer. Towards this direction and in view of the absence of a Greek lung cancer registry, we sought to generate real-world evidence on the epidemiology of NSCLC diagnosed in the second half of the last decade (i.e., 2016–2020) at diverse leading hospital institutions across the country. Furthermore, the study aimed to explore the temporal and institutional variations in the frequency of use of systemic treatment (ST) as part of initial management strategy, across the different disease stages.

Methods

Study design and population

For the purposes of this project, high-level aggregate data on NSCLC diagnoses and ST rate were collected for all adult patients newly diagnosed with histologically or cytologically confirmed Stage I-IV NSCLC between 01 January 2016 and 31 December 2020 (hereinafter referred to as “index period”) at 18 leading oncology clinics in geographically diverse locations throughout Greece.

Medical chart review and data collection was initiated on October 13th, 2021 and completed on May 23rd, 2022 (data cut-off date). During this period, the participating physicians were requested to provide information at an anonymous cumulative aggregate level on NSCLC diagnoses and ST rate by disease stage through a web-based Electronic Data Capture application, taking into consideration all patients who were diagnosed with NSCLC of any stage (i.e., Stages I-IV) at their site during the study index period. No restrictions were applied with respect to cancer staging system. No informed consent or consent waiver was required, as no data on a patient-level were abstracted nor recorded.

It is noted that this project initiative comprised the second part of the “INFINITY” study, which was a non-interventional, single-country, multicenter, retrospective observational 2-part study, the main part of which focused exclusively on the earlier stages of NSCLC and aimed to gain a deeper insight into individual patient profiles and management strategies. The results of the main part of INFINITY are outside the scope of this paper and will be considered for publication independently.

The study was designed, conducted and reported in accordance with the Guidelines for Good Pharmacoepidemiology Practice of the International Society for Pharmacoepidemiology [17], the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines [18], and where applicable, the European Union General Data Protection Regulation, and the local rules and regulations.

Study objectives and statistical analysis

The study objectives presented herein include the assessment of the number of all newly diagnosed NSCLC cases of any disease stage (I-IV) at the study sites during the 5-year index period in Greece, and the evaluation of the patients’ distribution across the different stages, overall, and per year of the index period. Other objectives were to estimate the ST utilisation rate, with ST referring to the initial therapeutic strategy for patients diagnosed with stages I-III and to first-line treatment for stage IV patients; to describe the frequency of different pharmacologic categories [chemotherapy (ChT), IO, targeted therapy (TT]] utilized by disease stage; and to map the temporal and institutional variation in the ST rate across the different NSCLC stages. ST refers to any systemic anticancer therapy including treatments administered in the context of clinical trials, but excluding best supportive care (BSC). The timing of ST as well as surgery or radiotherapy data were not collected for this part of INFINITY study, thus ST for stages I-III may refer to any setting, including neoadjuvant/adjuvant therapy prior to any recurrence or definitive therapy.

All analyses performed for addressing the study objectives are purely descriptive, and outcome data are summarized in frequency tables displaying the absolute and relative frequencies. Annual percent change (APC) was calculated by taking the difference between the total number of patients diagnosed in a particular calendar year and the previous calendar year, then divided by the latter and multiplied by 100. An average of the APCs (AAPC) was also estimated which is presented exclusively for narration purposes, in consideration of the limited number of APC timepoints examined (n = 4) which precludes the option of fitting an informative statistical regression model. Furthermore, in the absence of pre-planned inferential statistical tests, any reports on numerical differences in proportions for stage distribution and ST utilisation between years and institutions are purely narrative.

Statistical analysis was performed using the statistical software package SAS, version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) and standard Microsoft Excel® functions.

Results

Patient disposition

A total of 10,644 patients were newly diagnosed with NSCLC at any stage during the entire 5-year index period across the 18 participating sites. Of these patients, 16.6% (n = 1765), 18.9% (n = 2013), 20.3% (n = 2161), 21.9% (n = 2332), and 22.3% (n = 2373) were diagnosed in 2016, 2017, 2018, 2019 and 2020, respectively (Fig. 1A). With respect to geographic region, 63.8% (n = 6788) of the patients were diagnosed at the 11 study sites located in Attica, 5.4% (n = 577) at the 3 sites in Central Macedonia (Thessaloniki), while 9.7% (n = 1034), 9.7% (n = 1028), 6.9% (n = 730), and 4.6% (n = 487), at one site in Crete (Heraklion), Thessaly (Larissa), Epirus (Ioannina), and Western Greece (Patra), respectively. Approximately half of the patients (51.0%; 5426/10644) were diagnosed at the 6 public academic, 27.8% (2963/10644) of the patients at the 4 public non-academic sites, and the remaining 21.2% (2255/10644) at the 8 private hospital clinics (Supplementary Fig. S1).

NSCLC diagnoses and stage distribution

A 34.4% increase in the total number of new NSCLC diagnoses was observed from 2016 to 2020, with the AAPC being + 7.8% (Fig. 1A, B). The highest APC occurred in 2017 and the lowest in 2020 (Fig. 1B). The total number of Stage I, II, III, and IV cases increased by 32.2%, 3.1%, 15.4% and 52.4% from 2016 to 2020, respectively (Fig. 1A). The largest increase across stages and years was observed among Stage IV diagnoses from 2016 to 2017 (APC: +33.4%), whereas the largest decrease was noted for Stage II diagnoses from 2016 to 2017 (APC: -12.5%) (Fig. 1B). The temporal pattern of the overall population mirrored that of the Stage IV population, but a drop in Stage IV cases was observed in 2020 (APC of -3.8; Fig. 1B). While increases up until 2019 were mainly driven by patients diagnosed at non-academic institutions (public or private), an abrupt decrease in the number of diagnoses at public academic institutions accounted for the 2020 reduction in the overall Stage IV population (Table 1). The numbers of Stage I and III patients initially decreased in 2017, then started increasing in 2018 for Stage I and in 2019 for Stage III, for an AAPC of + 7.7 and + 3.9, respectively (Fig. 1B), reflecting the temporal patterns among patients diagnosed at private institutions (Table 1); the respective trends among those diagnosed at public institutions did not follow this pattern (Table 1). Lastly, Stage II diagnoses decreased substantially in 2017, which was consistent across the subpopulations by institution type (Fig. 1B; Table 1), whereas the direction of change thereafter was highly variable with an AAPC of + 1.2 (Fig. 1B).

Over the entire index period, 5.9% of all NSCLC diagnoses pertained to Stage I, 10.3% to Stage II, 23.4% to Stage III, and 60.4% to Stage IV disease. The frequency distribution of NSCLC stages by year of the index period is summarized in Fig. 2A. The proportion of non-metastatic NSCLC (i.e., Stage I-III) cases was the highest in 2016, representing nearly half of the population (47.2%), 59.3% of whom had Stage III NSCLC. Starting in 2017, there was an apparent shift in stage distribution which remained relatively constant throughout the subsequent years, with proportions of non-metastatic NSCLC ranging from 36.7% to 40.2%, and Stage I, II, and III representing 13.6–15.9%, 24.2–27.5%, and 57.3–60.9% of this population, respectively.

Temporal changes in stage distribution were even more pronounced at public non-academic institutions (Supplementary Fig. S2). Specifically, upon visual inspection, a numerically higher Stage IV proportion was noticed at public non-academic (73.0%) compared with public academic (58.3%) and private (48.7%) institutions (Fig. 2B). By focusing on non-metastatic NSCLC patients only, a numerically higher Stage I (22.2%) and lower Stage III (50.4%) proportion was observed among patients diagnosed at private institutions compared with public academic (I: 11.5%; III: 63.8%) and public non-academic institutions (I: 14.1%; III: 58.6%).

Over the entire index period, the ST rate in the Stage I and II subpopulations was 41.3% and 73.3%, respectively. ST mostly comprised of ChT, with the ChT rate being 39.1% and 71.5%, respectively, with negligible variations through the years (Fig. 3A; Supplementary Table S1), whereas the total rates (2016–2020) for the other pharmacologic categories did not exceed 2.1% (Supplementary Table S2). A small proportion of Stage I/II patients received TT (1.5%; n = 26), the majority of whom were diagnosed in 2020 (84.6%) (Supplementary Table S2). The number of Stage I or II patients receiving IO therapy or investigational (INV) agents did not exceed five in any of the years (Supplementary Table S2).

Throughout the index period, the ChT rate among Stage I patients treated at public institutions (academic 52.9% and non-academic 48.7%) appeared numerically higher than that observed among those cared for at private institutions (21.0%), whereas institutional differences were negligible among Stage II patients (Fig. 3B).

A relatively stable ST and ChT rate across the examined years was also noted for Stage III patients, with ranges of 84.6–86.8% (Supplementary Table S1) and 77.5–85.7% (Fig. 4A), respectively. Conversely, IO uptake steadily increased each year reaching 43.5% in 2020 from 13.6% in 2016, with the highest annual increase observed in 2018 (with the IO prescription rate nearly doubling compared to 2017; rising from 17.5% to 31.0%) (Fig. 4A). Throughout the 5-year period, ChT rates seemed higher among those diagnosed at public non-academic (97.9%) than private (87.5%) and public academic (75.6%) institutions (Fig. 4B). The lowest IO rate was noticed in private institutions (16.0%) which was negligible prior to 2018, whereas the highest was noticed in public non-academic institutions (39.4%), which was high from the beginning of the index period (43.8%) (Fig. 4B; Supplementary Table S2).

In Stage IV patients, ST rates slightly increased through the years reaching 93.2% in 2020 (Fig. 5A). This rising trend was mainly driven by IO therapy, the rate of which almost tripled by 2020 (from 15.8% in 2016 to 55.0% in 2020); on the contrary, ChT rates declined over the index period years dropping from 70.7% in 2016 to 60.6% in 2020. No apparent temporal trends in rates of TT and INV therapies were observed ranging from 5.7% to 8.2% and 4.2% to 5.8%, respectively (Fig. 5A).

Numerically higher TT rates were noticed in private (8.7%) and public academic (7.8%) institutions compared with public non-academic institutions (3.6%) (Fig. 5B). Variations in ChT rates among the different institution types were negligible, whereas the IO rate visually appeared much higher in public non-academic (42.1%) versus private (25.0%) and public academic (26.0%) institutions, reflecting an early sharp increase in 2017 in the former compared with the 2019 and 2020 sharp increase observed in the latter two institution types, respectively (Fig. 5B; Supplementary Table S2). Meanwhile, patients diagnosed in private or public academic institutions accounted for the majority of those treated with INV agents (95.5%) (Fig. 5B; Supplementary Table S2).

Considering that ST is the only viable option for Stage IV patients, the proportions of untreated Stage IV patients were expectedly the lowest compared to those with earlier disease stages in all examined years, reaching the lowest rate of 6.8% in 2020 among the overall Stage IV subpopulation (Fig. 5A). The respective rate of untreated patients was 14.9% at private, 9.1% at public academic and 0.4% at public non-academic institutions (Supplementary Table S2).

Discussion

The present study provides real-world evidence on the recent temporal dynamics of NSCLC epidemiology and ST rates in Greece, with preliminary insight into the disparities between institution types.

Overall, in this study, the number of new NSCLC cases increased by 34% from 2016 to 2020. The most notable increases were seen in 2017, which were driven by increases in Stage IV disease. Previous research has shown that population growth and aging are the main reasons for incidence increases [19], while improvements in lung cancer screening may also play a part especially in those diagnosed at an earlier stage [20]. Our data however cannot support the latter, given that an official national lung cancer screening program has yet to be established in Greece, though some efforts towards the implementation of such programs have already initiated on a private level in a few large hospitals [21].

Furthermore, the COVID-19 pandemic may have contributed to the observed decline in the increase rate in the annual total number of new NSCLC diagnoses for the year 2020 compared to the pre-pandemic period; this trend is in keeping with the published literature suggesting that COVID-19 outbreak has immensely disrupted the global health care system [22]. This may also justify the decrease in the absolute number of NSCLC cases in 2020 observed in public academic institutions considering that several of them may have been designated as COVID-reference hospitals. In particular, reduced access to healthcare and delayed lung cancer diagnoses due to pandemic-related imposed restrictions are proposed to have negatively impacted early cancer detection leading to higher tumor stages at diagnosis [23–25]. Nevertheless, this does not seem to be the case in INFINITY. The observed decreasing incidence trend for the year 2020 has been driven by a drop in the number of Stage IV diagnoses compared to the previous year and not by early non-metastatic stage diagnoses which continued to increase over this period. Speculatively, the overall higher frequency of chest CT scans to detect or monitor COVID-19 infection during the pandemic may have led to incidental lung cancer diagnosis, contributing to the detection of cases of early cancer [26, 27].

Though the present data do not reflect temporal changes in the per capita burden of NSCLC, to our knowledge, this is the first study to show recent trends in NSCLC diagnoses in Greece based on a real-world setting instead of projections from neighboring countries or data extrapolations [28].

Beyond the absolute numbers, this analysis also revealed a shift in the distribution of NSCLC stages between 2016 and 2020. In 2020, the proportion of patients diagnosed at Stage I, II, III, and IV was 6%, 10%, 24%, and 60%, respectively, compared to 7%, 13%, 28%, and 53% in 2016. This upward trend in late-stage diagnoses is further supported by the European EPICLIN-Lung study, which included Greek data and was published in 2009, reporting a stage distribution of 14%, 9%, 35%, and 43% [29]. When compared to NSCLC stage distributions across European countries over the previous decade—where reported ranges of 12-22% for Stage I [30–35], 6-10% for Stage II [30–35], 19-27% for Stage III [30–33, 35], and 43-55% for Stage IV [30–33, 35] our findings indicate a lower proportion of Stage I cases and a higher proportion of Stage IV cases in the five-year index period of this study. Though the impact of the new edition of the TNM (Tumor Node Metastasis) classification introduced in 2017 cannot be ruled out [5, 36] since different study periods are involved across studies, it is unlikely to have led to migration from a non-metastatic to a metastatic stage; rather, the aforementioned increase in Stage IV diagnoses possibly translated into a real change in the percentage of Stage IV patients in Greece, which remained relatively stable in the following years. This is important considering that patients with advanced NSCLC patients have poorer prognosis [5].

Stage IV NSCLC is more common in lower-income European countries due to: historically higher smoking prevalence; greater exposure to socioeconomic and occupational risk factors; lower health literacy delaying medical attention; lack of infrastructure and funding for nationwide low-dose CT screening programs; and, diagnostic delays in underfunded and overburdened healthcare systems [37–39]. Longer waiting times for imaging and specialist referrals often result in cancers being detected only after metastasis. The higher proportion of late-stage diagnoses observed in Greece compared to the published European literature can be used to promote public awareness, lung cancer screening programs, as well as faster diagnostic facilities [38] and thereby offer the opportunity of curative options to more patients in the future. Although nationwide data would be desirable, our data indicate that numbers continue to rise and NSCLC stage distribution can change drastically, highlighting the importance of country-specific and contemporary NSCLC epidemiology for reliable estimates of the pool of candidates for new treatments and optimal healthcare resources allocation.

In terms of intra-country institutional differences, a markedly high proportion of Stage IV disease (73%) was observed in public non-academic centers showing a clear difference compared with private (49%) and public academic (58%) institutions. The observed institutional variation in stage distribution cannot be attributed to differences in diagnostic and staging methods as the same algorithms are applied across institution types. However, an underlying referral bias may exist, since early-stage patients are preferentially referred to surgical and academic centers. This, along with the fact that emergency presenters are more likely to be diagnosed with advanced stage disease [40], may partly explain the higher percentage of patients diagnosed with Stage IV in public non-academic centers, the extent of which cannot be assessed since this level of detail is not available in our study data. Heterogeneity in stage distribution across institutions is thus mainly attributed to healthcare system factors rather than true epidemiological trends, confirming the importance of abstracting data from diverse institution types to yield generalizable findings.

Analysis of the ST rates showed that among Stage I/II patients, use of ChT which represented the majority of systemic therapies, remained stable through the years and a higher proportion was noted in Stage II over Stage I. This is in line with clinical recommendations on adjuvant ST [3, 6–9] and other real-world studies showing increasing neoadjuvant and adjuvant ChT use with increasing stage [41]. Although the (neo)adjuvant setting of ST cannot be confirmed since this level of detail is beyond the scope of this part of INFINITY, the overall ST rate of 62% in the Stage I/II subpopulation is aligned with the surgery rate in the Greek population of the 2009 EPICLIN-Lung study of 67% [29]. Up-to-date surgery rates and ST settings in Stage I/II patients in Greece are analyzed in the context of the first part of INFINITY that will be presented in a separate publication. When analyzed separately, the ST rate of 41% for Stage I patients appears higher than expected based on guideline recommendations for adjuvant ST in stage I disease at that time. Although staging method was not collected in this study, it is estimated that pathological staging accounts for the majority of patients treated in the participating centers, while clinical staging accounts for one in four patients. Speculatively, this may have partly contributed to the high ST rates observed among Stage I patients, who may have undergone pathological upstaging after surgery.

The rates of IO and TT therapy in Stage I/II patients in this study during 2016-2020 were negligible, providing a reference point for local treatment patterns in the pre-ICI/ TT era of early-stage NSCLC. This baseline will be valuable for future analyses assessing the integration of recent and upcoming ICI/TT approvals into clinical practice [7, 10, 13–15].

The overall ST rate in the period 2016-2020 observed herein for Stage III and IV patients was 86% and 88%, respectively, which is slightly higher than the previously reported respective rates of 83% and 81% in Greece in 2009, given that ChT was the only available ST option at that time [29]. As expected, given the higher clinical value that IO therapies have demonstrated, a marked increase in the use of IO was seen in both Stage III and IV subpopulations over the studied timeframe. Some disparities between institution types were found in ST patterns which is suggestive of medical practice variations.

The observed gradual and substantial uptake of IO therapies in INFINITY is consistent with the timeline of EMA approved indications for advanced NSCLC which extended from TPS ≥50% tumors in 2016 to patients with NSCLC regardless of PD-L1 expression, first in non-squamous tumors in 2018, subsequently squamous tumors in 2019, and an expansion of the pool of available ICI agents from 2019 onwards [11]. The fast rise in IO use seen as early as 2016 can be partly explained by participation in clinical trials, early access programs or off-label approvals [21], the details of which were not collected according to the INFINITY study design. The fast rise in IO use may also be the result of the national Electronic Pre-Approval System which was introduced in Greece to allow for access to innovative treatments prior to official approval [21].

In Stage IV patients, the increase in IO use coincided with a small decrease in ChT treatment, in line with indications of IO monotherapy [11]. This was less pronounced in Stage III patients, likely mirroring IO consolidation of CRT-treated unresectable Stage III patients [7]. Nevertheless, IO rate throughout the index period was 30% in Stage III patients and similar to the use in patients with Stage IV, which was 31%.

These rates compare favorably with the range of 11-26% reported in European retrospective cohort studies of similar periods [42–45] and are comparable to the pooled 33% reported in a review of US real-world studies [46]. Based on a comprehensive study of aggregate national data for systemic therapies in advanced NSCLC (stage IIIB-IV) in the period 2014-2020, IO use ranged from approximately 5% to 30% across 11 European countries in 2020; the ESMO-based benchmark for that year was estimated at approximately 40% (this was based on both ESMO treatment guidelines and timing of cancer drug approvals by EMA) [47], suggesting that the overall uptake of IO therapies in Greece has been substantial (reaching 44% in Stage III and 55% in Stage IV by 2020). Interestingly, 2020 overall ST rates in Greece (87% in Stage III and 93% in Stage IV by 2020) were also high compared with the ESMO-based benchmark of 75%, while estimates for other European countries were below 75% apart from Portugal which had similar rates to Greece (approximately 88%) [47].

The above analysis of how ST rates among Stage III and IV patients in Greece compare to other European populations should be cautiously interpreted given the slightly different study periods and differences in study population in terms of: disease stage (IV or IIIB/IV), histology (chemoimmunotherapy initially approved in non-squamous histology only), age (elderly patients are more likely to receive BSC), performance status (patients with poor performance status receive BSC), targetable genomic alterations (rendering certain patients eligible for TTs) and other factors influencing treatment decisions [3, 6–8]. Treatment course may also be influenced by refusal of physician-recommended ChT by patients, which has been correlated with certain sociodemograhic factors (e.g. age, income, comorbidity burden) [48, 49]. Treatment refusal cannot be accurately assessed and compared with the literature due to lack of information of patient-specific factors and treatment combinations, as per study design.

Our results indicated that nearly 7% of the Stage IV population were treated with TTs over the 2016-2020 period. At that time, TTs in the first line included TKIs such as those targeting EGFR or ALK, but also the angiogenesis inhibitor bevacizumab [3]. Hence, these rates seem lower than the expected based on the national EGFR positivity rates reported in the literature ranging from 11% to 16% [50–55]. Future studies may decipher whether this showcases the need for improvements in the numbers undergoing molecular diagnostics and standardization of methods, or if this reflects differential regional NSCLC epidemiology, and how this potentially relates to the NSCLC-related risk factors. As anticipated in light of the poor prognosis of this population, Stage IV patients accounted for most of INV therapies received in the context of NSCLC. Time trends were stable, in line with the number of oncology and lung cancer trials in Greece [21]. Lastly, 28% of the ST-treated Stage IV population still received ChT monotherapy in 2020, the explanation of which would be interesting to explore further in relation to patient’s age and performance status [46], or lack of information on PD-L1 expression [56].

Limitations of this analysis include its retrospective chart review design; as such, results could be influenced by misclassification bias or differential classification, since no specific staging (clinical or pathological), ST setting (neoadjuvant/adjuvant/definitive/consolidation/maintenance) or drug classification system was enforced. Additionally, the study was not designed to statistically analyze temporal trends due to the limited time period examined nor to statistically compare the findings between institution types, hence a narrative and purely descriptive approach was adopted. Limitations also include lack of information on important demographics (e.g. age), lifestyle factors (e.g. smoking, urban versus rural living conditions), diagnostic practices (e.g. mutation testing) and disease substage (e.g. Stage IB versus IA) which could provide explanations for the observed variations in clinical practice. Owing to the aggregate nature of the dataset and the absence of patient-level data, confounding could not be controlled, multivariable analyses were not feasible and potential biases cannot be adequately addressed.

Another limitation of this study is that it does not include a nationwide sample. Nevertheless, it is noted that the 18 sites are among the leading oncology clinics that treat NSCLC across the country and altogether represent more than one fourth (~28%; 10,644 cases of an estimated total of ~38,000 cases based on the estimated total of ~19,000 cases from an average of 9,000 newly diagnosed lung cancer cases annually [2] and considering 80%-90% are NSCLC [3, 4]) of the target NSCLC population (i.e., all patients diagnosed with NSCLC during the 5-year index period) in Greece.

Conclusions

This study provides real-world insights into the evolving epidemiology of NSCLC in Greece, highlighting significant increases in new cases between 2016 and 2020, along with notable shifts in stage distribution and ST patterns. The rising proportion of Stage IV diagnoses underscores the need for enhanced early detection strategies, including the implementation of national lung cancer screening programs and improved diagnostic accessibility. Additionally, the study reveals an increasing adoption of IO therapies in advanced NSCLC, aligning with European treatment trends and regulatory approvals, while also identifying disparities in treatment patterns across different institution types.

Despite limitations, including its retrospective design and lack of nationwide representation, the study offers valuable baseline data on NSCLC trends in Greece. These findings emphasize the importance of country-specific epidemiological research to inform healthcare resource allocation, optimize treatment strategies, and facilitate the integration of novel therapies. Future studies should further investigate molecular diagnostics, patient characteristics influencing treatment decisions, and the impact of emerging therapeutic advancements on NSCLC management in Greece.
