Exploring the polygenic landscape of wool traits in Turkish merinos through multi-locus GWAS approaches: Karacabey Merino
Authors: Yalçın Yaman, Sedat Behrem, Ramazan Aymaz, Murat Keleş, Yiğit Emir Ki̇şi̇
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study investigated the genetic basis of wool traits in Karacabey Merino sheep, a dual-purpose breed developed in Northwest Türkiye. Heritability estimates were moderate for fibre diameter (FD; h² = 0.360) and greasy fleece weight (GFW; h² = 0.443), but low for staple length (SL; h² = 0.119), suggesting that while some traits may respond well to selection, others may require larger datasets or complementary approaches to capture their underlying genetic variation. Genetic and phenotypic correlations with growth traits revealed complex interactions, indicating that selection for wool traits may have trade-offs with growth performance, a key consideration in the breeding of dual-purpose sheep. To identify genomic regions associated with these traits, we employed multiple multi-locus GWAS methods (mrMLM, FASTmrMLM, FASTmrEMMA, pLARmEB, and ISIS EM-BLASSO). To enhance the robustness of the findings and reduce the likelihood of false positives, a stringent significance threshold (LOD ≥ 5) was applied. Post hoc power analysis further demonstrated that a considerable proportion of the identified QTNs exceeded the critical statistical power threshold (≥ 0.8), providing greater confidence in their biological relevance. Importantly, several loci were consistently detected across different models, underscoring their stability and potential value as reliable markers. Together, these findings provide novel insights into the polygenic architecture of wool traits in Karacabey Merino sheep and identify promising targets for marker-assisted selection. Beyond their immediate relevance to this breed, the results also contribute to a broader understanding of the genetic regulation of wool traits in dual-purpose sheep. Future studies incorporating functional validation, gene expression analyses, and replication in independent populations will be essential to confirm the functional significance of the identified loci. Such efforts would not only improve the reliability of these associations but also enable their practical application in breeding programmes aimed at optimising both wool quality and growth performance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-025-05069-0.

Introduction

Sheep wool is one of the oldest and most valued natural fibres, with archaeological evidence tracing its use back over 10,000 years [1]. Its exceptional combination of comfort, durability, and processing performance has ensured its continued importance in the textile sector [2]. Today, approximately 56% of global wool production is used in apparel, 42% in home textiles, and 2% in industrial applications [3]. Over time, the wool trade has evolved from small-scale local production to a globally integrated commodity market, a transformation that began in the mid-19th century with the industrial expansion of the textile industry in the Northern Hemisphere [4]. Presently, Australia, New Zealand, South Africa, Argentina, and Uruguay dominate global exports, with fine wools and specialty fibres such as Merino and alpaca occupying premium niches due to their superior warmth, breathability, and environmental sustainability [5, 6].

Global production patterns are shaped by breed type, climate, and farming systems. For instance, Ethiopian and Kenyan breeds produce wools adapted to local needs [7, 8], whereas European breeding programmes have been driven by market demands for specific textile qualities [6, 9]. On the international stage, fine Merino wool remains the benchmark for high-value products [4–6].

Wool is a renewable, biodegradable fibre with carbon sequestration potential during sheep growth, giving it a lower cradle-to-farm-gate carbon footprint than many synthetics [10, 11]. Advances in closed-loop recycling further reduce waste and conserve resources [12]. Its thermal regulation, moisture control, and acoustic insulation also make wool a sustainable substitute for synthetic building materials, lowering energy use and CO₂ emissions [13]. Wool/polymer composites offer biocompatibility, hypoallergenicity, and insulation for medical uses such as bedsore prevention and tissue engineering [14]. In agriculture, washed wool and wool-soil mixtures serve as nitrogen-rich fertilisers, supporting zero-waste and organic farming [15, 16].

Nevertheless, the industry faces persistent challenges, particularly the low economic value of coarse fibres, which often leads to waste. To address this, dual-purpose production systems that integrate meat and wool have been adopted. Wool quality—determined by fibre diameter, staple length, and cleanliness—is influenced by genetic, environmental, and management factors [2]. Breeding programmes have therefore been pivotal in improving both yield and quality, with studies demonstrating the effects of regional environments and breed-specific traits [17–19]. However, trade-offs between meat and wool production, as seen in Spanish Merino sheep [20], highlight the need for balanced breeding goals. Crossbreeding has been applied globally to improve traits such as fibre fineness [8, 21], while preserving genetic diversity for long-term sustainability.

In recent years, genomic selection has emerged as a transformative approach to wool improvement. Candidate gene studies and genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified key genetic loci influencing wool traits in diverse breeds, including Turkish Akkaraman [22], Chinese Merino [23, 24], Rambouillet [25], and Chinese fine-wool sheep [26]. These genetic markers provide powerful tools for targeted breeding, with modelling suggesting that if genomic breeding value accuracy approaches the square root of heritability, genetic gain could increase by approximately 30% for terminal sire indices and 40% for fine-wool Merino indices [27]. Such integrative genetic and management strategies are essential for meeting the growing global demand for high-quality wool while maintaining the environmental and economic sustainability of the industry.

The Karacabey Merino, a composite breed of Türkiye’s northwestern Marmara region, originated from a 1940s breeding program at the Sheep Research and Breeding Institute (SBRI). The program aimed to improve both meat production and wool quality by crossing German Mutton Merino with the indigenous Kıvırcık sheep. The Kıvırcık, used as the maternal line, is an ancient Turkish breed prized for its flavourful meat but limited by low meat yield and poor-quality wool. It is characterised by a thin tail and has long been valued locally. For over three decades, the Karacabey Merino has remained genetically isolated, with no further crossbreeding. Its development involved more than 80 years of backcrossing and traditional selection, ultimately earning recognition as a native Turkish breed. This unique lineage, designed to overcome the low productivity of the Kıvırcık, produced a sheep well-suited to regional needs and conditions.

In this study, we examined the genetic basis of wool traits—fibre diameter (FD, µ), staple length (SL, mm), and greasy fleece weight (GFW, kg)—in Karacabey Merino sheep. To investigate these complex quantitative traits, we applied advanced GWAS approaches capable of detecting polygenic effects. Specifically, five multi-locus GWAS methods were used: Multi-Locus Random-SNP-Effect Mixed Linear Model (MrMLM) [28], Fast Multi-Locus Random-SNP-Effect Mixed Linear Model (FastMrMLM) [29], Iterative Sure Independence Screening EM-Bayesian LASSO (ISIS EM-BLASSO) [30], Fast Multi-Locus Random-SNP-Effect EMMA (FASTmrEMMA) [31], and polygenic-background-control-based least angle regression plus empirical Bayes (pLARmEB) [32]. These methods allow detection of polygenic effects and candidate genes underlying wool traits, which are typically controlled by multiple genes acting together. Their selection was motivated by superior SNP detection power, accuracy, and capacity to account for polygenic backgrounds compared with other methods such as EMMA, GEMMA, SUPER, CMLM, ECMLM, SCAD, and FarmCPU. A further advantage is their integration within a single software package, mrMLM, which provides an efficient platform for multi-locus GWAS [28, 30–33]. Multi-locus GWAS models have recently gained wide adoption in diploid organisms to study genotype–phenotype associations and disease resistance or susceptibility using high-throughput genotyping. They offer greater power to detect associations for complex traits than single-locus approaches. For example, they have been applied to investigate salt tolerance and yield traits in rice [34, 35], kernel moisture in maize [36], growth performance [37], wool traits [38], liver copper concentration [39], and resistance or susceptibility to toxoplasmosis in sheep [40].

Materials and methods

Animals

Karacabey Merino ewes served as the animal subjects for this study. The research included a total of 382 ewes, each approximately 18 months old. These ewes were reared at the Sheep Breeding Research Institute (SBRI) under identical climatic and management conditions. The age of the ewes (18 months) was chosen to represent mature animals that had completed their first wool growth cycle, providing a reliable basis for assessing wool traits. After blood and wool samples were collected from the animals, they continued their lives under standard care and feeding conditions as usual. No anesthesia or euthanasia procedures were applied to the animal subjects. A reported informed consent form was obtained from SBRI management for the interventions performed.

Wool quality and quantity assessment

The quantification of fiber diameter and staple length was conducted through a standardized sampling and analysis protocol. Fleece samples weighing approximately 50 g were collected from the caudal scapular region of each ewe using an electric shearing apparatus. To maintain sample integrity, each specimen was stored in a labeled and sealed plastic bag. Fiber analysis was performed utilizing the OFDA 2000 optical fiber diameter analyzer (BSC Electronics, Ardross, Australia), located at the laboratory facilities of the Ankara Sheep and Goat Breeders Association. The analytical procedure involved the meticulous alignment and straightening of a representative fiber subset from each sample prior to insertion into the device. Subsequently, the OFDA 2000 executed automated optical measurements, yielding quantitative data on fiber diameter (expressed in microns, µ) and staple length (expressed in millimeters, mm). The weight of greasy fleece was obtained by weighing the fleece obtained after shearing sheep using a scale accurate to 100 g. All sheep included in the study were of the same age, born in the same birthing season, and managed under the same conditions. A General Linear Model (GLM) was conducted to assess the effects of dam age and birth type (single, twin, or triplet). The analysis revealed that these factors had no statistically significant effects on FD, SL, or GFW parameters (p > 0.05; Supplementary material S1). Since the birthing season spans approximately a 2-month period, there is a maximum age difference of 2 months between the sheep. Fleece samples were taken when the sheep were an average of 18 months old. Prior to statistical analyses, the staple length and greasy fleece weight data for all sheep were adjusted to 18 months (540 days). Briefly, the staple length and greasy fleece weight data were divided by the sheep’s age in days and then multiplied by 540 day.

Descriptive statistics

SNP based heritability (h²) for each trait and genetic correlations between traits were calculated using the GCTA software [41]. Data on growth performance, such as birth weight (BW), weaning weight (WW), and average daily gain (ADG), of the studied population were also available. Since the Karacabey Merino is a dual-purpose breed, and classical breeding efforts continue for both meat production and wool quality, genetic and phenotypic correlations between traits related to wool quality and growth performance were also calculated to determine how selection for wool quality affects growth performance characteristics, or vice versa. To determine the coefficients of variation (CV%), we divided the standard deviation of each dataset by its mean and multiplied the result by 100. Phenotypic correlations between phenotypes were calculated in Minitab v21.4 software.

Genotyping and quality controls

Genomic DNA extracted from the whole blood of the study animals was genotyped using the OVINE 50 K BeadChip on the ILLUMINA platform. Prior to quality control (QC), we removed Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms (SNPs) located on sex chromosomes and mitochondrial DNA. We then applied the following QC criteria:

SNP Call rate ≥ 0.95.

Minor Allele Frequency (MAF) ≥ 0.05.

Animal missing genotype rates (mind) ≤ 0.1.

Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium threshold ≥ 10E-5.

These QC procedures were carried out using Plink v1.9 software [42]. After QC, 35.886 SNPs remained for subsequent analysis.

Association study

Before conducting the genotype-phenotype multi-locus association analyses, we tested the phenotype data for normality. The FD and GFW data conformed to a normal distribution, while the SL data was normalized using the Rank-based Inverse Normal Transformation. Consequently, 382 sheep meeting the QC criteria were available for association analyses for the FD and SL traits, while 288 sheep were available for GFW.

We conducted five multi-locus genome-wide association (GWAS) —mrMLM, FASTmrMLM, FASTmrEMMA, pLARmEB, and ISIS EM-BLASSO— using the mrMLM v4.0.2 package in R v4.3x environment [43], adhering to default parameters. To mitigate potential type 1 errors stemming from unaccounted cryptic relatedness, we constructed kinship matrices within the mrMLM package and incorporated them into the statistical model for each multi-locus method. In these multi-locus GWAS, the statistical significance of the markers is determined by Logarithm of Odds Ratio (LOD) scores. Traditionally, markers with LOD ≥ 3 are considered genome-wide significant, negating the need for multiple correction methods like Bonferroni correction or False Discovery Rate [28, 30, 31, 33, 34]. A LOD ≥ 3 threshold indicates that the likelihood of a marker-phenotype association is 1,000 times greater than the likelihood of no association [44]. To enhance result reliability, we implemented a more stringent significance criterion of LOD ≥ 5. This threshold suggests that the likelihood of a true association is 100,000 times greater than the likelihood of no association.

Post hoc power analysis is essential for evaluating a GWAS’s capacity to identify true associations based on the observed effect sizes and sample size. Using R v4.3.3 and the ‘pwr’ package, a statistical power analysis was performed, considering a threshold of 0.8 for sufficient power. For each associated QTN, the analysis incorporated the corresponding r2, minor allele frequency (MAF), and sample size values.

Genomic annotation, gene ontology, and pathway analysis

To identify and annotate genes within a ± 200 kb region surrounding the QTNs of interest, we employed the Ensembl REST API (https://rest.ensembl.org/) [45] in combination with a Python script utilising the Pandas library for data manipulation. This methodology established a robust and reproducible pipeline for gene annotation within targeted genomic regions, capitalising on the capabilities of the Ensembl REST API. Gene annotation was performed using the ARS-UI_Ramb_v2.0 assembly.

For gene enrichment and network analysis, we employed ShinyGO, a graphical tool that utilizes all available gene sets for the domestic sheep (Ovis aries) genome [46]. We set the false discovery rate threshold at 0.05 for determining significant gene enrichment. ShinyGO was also used to generate visual representations of the results.

Linkage disequilibrium (LD) analiyses

Linkage disequilibrium (LD) analysis is crucial for understanding the genetic architecture of complex traits. LD refers to the non-random association of alleles at different loci and is typically measured using two metrics: D’ and r² values. The D’ value quantifies the degree of association between alleles, while the r² value estimates the correlation between alleles at two loci. In this study, LD analyses were conducted to identify connections between trait-associated QTNs detected within a gene and other functional regions of the same gene, as well as to explore potential links between intergenic QTNs and nearby protein-coding genes or other functional gene regions. A D’ value greater than 0.7 is considered a strong indicator of LD, suggesting that alleles are inherited together more frequently than expected by chance [47, 48]. Therefore, a stringent threshold of D’ ≥ 0.7 was applied. To account for the dependence of r² on allele frequency and to ensure comprehensive identification of linked SNPs within a 500 kb window, a moderate threshold of r² ≥ 0.5 was adopted [49, 50]. All LD analyses were performed using the LD calculator available in the Ensembl online tool (https://www.ensembl.org).

Multi-locus GWAS analysis

Using five multi-locus methods, we identified 13 QTNs associated with the FD trait, all meeting or exceeding the significance threshold of LOD ≥ 5 (Table 3; Fig. 5a). Among the detected QTNs, several were co-detected by more than one method, enhancing their reliability and potential biological significance. Notably, oar3_OAR6_76493072 was identified by four different methods (mrMLM, FASTmrMLM, pLARmEB, and ISIS EM-BLASSO). Additionally, OAR1_295631193.1 and OAR10_10753846.1 were each detected by three methods, reflecting their consistent association across analyses. Beyond these, three other QTNs—oar3_OAR3_205317699, oar3_OAR5_20685591, and s67881.1—were identified by two methods. These co-detections highlight the robustness of these QTNs in their association with the FD trait.

The r² values, which represent the proportion of phenotypic variance explained by the QTNs, range from a minimum of 0.75% (Chr3:206852355) to a maximum of 4.068% (OAR10_10753846.1), this indicates that the detected QTNs explain a moderate proportion of the phenotypic variation in the FD trait. However, the cumulative r² of the 13 QTNs associated with the FD trait appears to account for 30% of the total variance. The QTN effect sizes, reflecting the magnitude of their influence on the trait, vary significantly, with a maximum negative effect of −1.130 (oar3_OAR25_720891) and a maximum positive effect of 1.041 (oar3_OAR5_20685591). These effect sizes demonstrate both positive and negative contributions of the QTNs to the FD trait.

For the SL trait, a total of 17 QTNs were identified across five multi-locus methods (Table 4; Fig. 5b). Among these, two QTNs were co-detected by multiple methods: Chr12:43669493 (detected by mrMLM and ISIS EM-BLASSO) and s32109.1 (detected by pLARmEB and ISIS EM-BLASSO). The proportion of phenotypic variance explained by significant QTNs ranges from a minimum of 0.761% (OAR22_9161453.1) to a maximum of 5.467% (s32109.1). Discussion

In the nucleus Karacabey Merino population, intensive conventional selection programmes have been implemented for many years to improve growth traits. In parallel, controlled mating strategies are employed to minimise the expected increase in the inbreeding coefficient. When the effects of these management practices on population genetic diversity, LD profile, and genetic drift are examined: The distribution of individual genotype heterozygosity rates is predominantly concentrated between approximately 38% and 44%, with the average heterozygosity rate observed to be 40% (Fig. 2a). At the marker level, heterozygosity is largely distributed between 10% and 50% (Fig. 2b). Most minor allele frequencies (MAF) fall within the range of 0.1 to 0.30, indicating that the majority of SNPs remain largely unfixed (Fig. 2c).

Similarly, the studied population was evaluated based on its linkage disequilibrium (LD) patterns. Examination of the decay curves of the correlation coefficient R (Fig. 3a) and the squared correlation coefficient R² (Fig. 3f) with respect to physical distance between SNPs reveals that LD is maintained at a relatively high level up to approximately 6–10 kb. The LD decay curve shown in Fig. 3c illustrates that while R values are high at short distances, they gradually decline as physical distance increases. However, this decline occurs relatively slowly, with average R values remaining at ameaningful level up to 6–10 kb. Consistently, the histogram presented in Fig. 3e, which depicts the distribution of LD distances (in kilobases), shows a higher frequency of LD blocks within the 0–1 kb range. The histogram of R correlation coefficients (Fig. 3b) shows a slight shift toward positive values, suggesting that certain alleles may be undergoing fixation within the population.

When all findings are considered collectively, it becomes evident that the Karacabey Merino population exhibits a relatively well-preserved level of heterozygosity—contrary to typical expectations for closed populations. Although some SNPs show reduced allelic diversity, overall genetic variation remains effectively maintained within the population. The observed linkage disequilibrium (LD) structure suggests a degree of genetic drift, characterised by a relatively slow decay of LD and moderately extended LD blocks. However, this genomic configuration appears to be kept in balance without leading to excessive homozygosity, as a result of the genetic structure being managed through controlled mating practices.

Descriptive statistics and heritability estimates for three essential wool characteristics in Karacabey Merino sheep are shown in Table 1. The data cover FD and SL (n = 382) as well as GFW (n = 288) traits. FD and GFW exhibit moderate heritability (h² = 0.360 and 0.443, respectively; p < 0.001), indicating strong genetic influence and potential for improvement through selective breeding. SL, however, shows considerably lower heritability (h² = 0.119, p = 0.068) compared to the other two traits.

The Coefficient of Variation (CV%) offers crucial information about trait variability. FD displays the lowest CV% (7.035%), suggesting relatively consistent fibre diameter within the population. This low variability implies that significant changes in FD through selection may require more intense selection pressure. GFW exhibits moderate variability (CV% = 14.988%), presenting a good balance between consistency and potential for improvement. Despite its lowest heritability, SL shows the highest CV% (26.034%), indicating substantial variability. This high CV% for SL, combined with its lower heritability, suggests that environmental factors have a significant influence on the expression of this trait. Additionally, one can speculate that this feature appears more susceptible to standardization errors in sampling.

The analysis of wool traits in sheep, including the interconnections among wool traits themselves and their interconnections with other desired traits, provides more insights for breeding strategies. Regarding genetic and phenotypic correlations within wool and growth traits, the moderate negative genetic correlation between FD and SL (rG = −0.390, p = 0.058), although not statistically significant at the 0.05 level, suggests that selecting for longer staples might result in finer fibres. However, weak and non-significant negative phenotypic correlations between these two traits emphasize the need to consider environmental factors. A strong positive genetic correlation (rG = 0.573, p < 0.001) and moderate phenotypic correlation (rP = 0.307, p < 0.001) exist between FD and GFW, presenting a challenge for breeders aiming to decrease fibre diameter while increasing fleece weight. SL and GFW demonstrate a moderate positive genetic correlation (rG = 0.342, p = 0.048), suggesting that selection for longer staples may lead to increased fleece weight. However, the absence of a significant phenotypic correlation between these traits (rP = 0.065, p > 0.05) indicates that breeding conditions and environmental factors should be considered, as this selection may not yield the expected phenotypic results.

Growth traits (birth weight, weaning weight, and average daily gain) demonstrate moderate to strong positive significant genetic correlations and consistently weak to moderate significant phenotypic correlations with GFW. This correlation is expected, as a larger body surface area typically leads to higher wool yield. Notably, there are no significant genetic or phenotypic correlations between FD, SL and growth traits.

These findings highlight the complex interplay between wool and growth traits in Karacabey Merino sheep. The discrepancies observed between genetic and phenotypic correlations for some trait pairs emphasize the importance of considering both parameters in breeding programs. The results suggest that simultaneous improvement of wool quality, quantity, and growth traits may be challenging due to some antagonistic relationships. On the other hand, the general lack of a strong direct correlation between wool characteristics and growth performance makes simultaneous selection for these two traits possible. Furthermore, the expected positive correlation between GFW and growth performance presents an advantage in breeding.

Five distinct multi-locus GWAS methods, FASTmrEMMA, mrMLM, ISIS EM-BLASSO, pLARmEB, and FASTmrMLM, were employed. This multi-method strategy aims to enhance the robustness and reliability of QTN identification associated with complex traits.

In the analysis of multiple traits using various multi-locus methods, 13 QTNs were associated with the FD trait, with all meeting the significance threshold. Several of these were detected by more than one method, strengthening the confidence in their findings. For instance, a specific QTN was identified by four methods, while others were consistently detected by three or two methods (Fig. 10a). Similarly, 17 QTNs were identified for the SL trait, with some being co-detected by multiple methods, further supporting their biological relevance (Fig. 10b). In the case of the GFW trait, 27 QTNs were identified, and 13 of them were detected by at least two methods, underscoring the consistency of the results (Fig. 10c). The high co-detection rate across methods in the GFW analysis enhances the validity of the associations, reinforcing the reliability of the identified QTNs. For all investigated traits, the detection of some QTNs by multiple multi-locus methods can enhance the reliability of the results, as it represents cross-validation between methods.

Gene annotation analysis identified numerous protein-coding genes either containing or located within ± 200 Kb of QTNs associated with all three wool traits. Several genes with QTNs in their intronic regions were particularly noteworthy: for FD, these included CTNNA2, CDC42SE2, and VWA8; for SL, they were ENSOARG00020027448, ENSOARG00020037521, RAB11FIP3, and AUTS2; and for GFW, the relevant genes were BCAR3, ENSOARG00020018830, ENSOARG00020026650, LRRC8A, GGT7, ENSOARG00020012610, ENSOARG00020026499, SHISA9, PCNX2, and ENSOARG00020020852. Additionally, an important discovery was that the QTN rs398487507 associated with GFW is linked to a missense mutation in the exon region of the MDM1 gene. The localization of trait-associated QTNs within functional gene regions suggests their potential significance in determining wool quality. Further functional investigations of these genes are crucial for gaining deeper insight into the genetic background of wool traits.

As a result of LD analyses, the identification of strong associations between the detected QTNs and coding genes, as well as regulatory regions potentially involved in gene expression mechanisms, appears to offer significant potential for advancing our understanding of the genetic basis of wool traits. For example, QTNs linked to missense variants or splice regions could influence protein function or mRNA processing, which might affect the structural or functional characteristics of wool fibers. Similarly, QTNs located in UTRs or other non-coding regions may influence gene expression, thereby modulating the production of proteins involved in wool formation. These results suggest that QTNs can interact with critical genomic elements—including exons, introns, UTRs, and non-coding transcripts—which collectively contribute to gene regulation, protein synthesis, and the resulting phenotypic traits.

Furthermore, the detection of these associations establishes a basis for additional functional studies, including gene editing or expression analyses, which could help clarify the contributions of specific genes and variants to the development of wool traits. Integrating LD analysis with functional genomic insights provides a robust framework for exploring the complex genetic networks underlying wool traits, potentially leading to advancements in both fundamental research and practical applications in livestock breeding strategies.

Previous GWA studies have reported over 250 candidate genes potentially associated with wool yield and characteristics in various sheep breeds [22, 25, 26, 51–55] (Supplementary materials S3). Among these candidate genes, consensus has been reached on only two, PTPN3 and TCF4, as highlighted in the studies by Wang et al. [51] and Bolormaa et al. [53]. Additionally, in another study conducted by our team on a different Turkish Merino breed, the Middle Anatolian Merino [38], PTPN3 and TCF4, reported by Wang et al. [51], along with ZBTB8A identified by Zhao et al. [55] were found to be associated with wool traits. Consequently, to the best of our knowledge, consensus has been established on five genes in genotype-phenotype association studies related to various wool traits. Notably, none of the potential candidate genes reported in this study have been previously identified.

With hundreds of recognized sheep breeds that have evolved to thrive in diverse pastoral environments across the globe since domestication [56], it is reasonable to suggest that the genetic basis of wool characteristics in different breeds has been uniquely shaped by thousands of years of adaptation to specific climatic and geographic conditions. This highlights a complex interplay between genetic factors and environmental influences, which has resulted in the wide array of wool-related traits observed in sheep populations today.

The pathway analysis of genes associated with FD, SL, and GFW in Karacabey Merino sheep reveals a multifaceted genetic architecture underlying these economically significant traits. Our findings highlight the involvement of a diverse range of pathways across all three traits, reflecting the intricate biological networks that likely contribute to the regulation of wool characteristics. When considered alongside previous studies, the results suggest that these traits are not governed by singular or isolated mechanisms but rather by a dynamic interplay of multiple biological processes, with interactions among numerous genes and pathways, as well as climatic and pastoral environments, collectively shaping the observed phenotypic outcomes.

Conventional Mixed Linear Model based single-locus GWAS on sheep wool traits have revealed valuable insights into the genetic basis of wool quality and production. These studies have examined various sheep breeds, including Akkaraman, Merino, Alpine Merino, Chinese Merino, Qinghai fine-wool, and Aohan fine-wool sheep. They have identified significant QTNs associated with important wool traits such as fibre diameter, staple length, greasy fleece weight, and clean fleece rate. These GWAS suggest some candidate genes associated with wool characteristics and have estimated genomic heritability, providing an understanding of the proportion of trait variance attributable to genetic factors [22, 25, 26, 38, 51–55]. The polygenic nature of wool traits has been highlighted through these studies, indicating that multiple genes contribute to the observed variation in wool characteristics.

Due to the polygenic determination of wool traits, the conducted multi-locus GWAS in the present study offers a new perspective for identifying markers that significantly affect these traits. By considering the power to detect multiple loci simultaneously affecting wool traits, multi-locus approaches provide a more comprehensive understanding of the genetic architecture underlying wool characteristics, potentially leading to more effective selection strategies in sheep breeding programs.

Limitations and considerations

In this study, we adopted a conservative significance threshold (LOD ≥ 5) to reduce the likelihood of false positives and enhance the robustness of the results. While this approach provides greater confidence in the detected associations, it inevitably reduced the co-detection rate across methods and may have excluded QTNs with small effects. Because different algorithms identify associations at varying significance levels, some QTNs detected at LOD ≥ 3 were not captured under the stricter threshold. Thus, the possibility remains that biologically relevant but minor-effect QTNs were overlooked.

Post hoc power analysis indicated that most identified QTNs possessed adequate statistical power, supporting the reliability of the findings. Nevertheless, associations falling below the 0.8 power threshold or not reaching the LOD ≥ 5 criterion should be interpreted with caution, as they may reflect constraints of sample size, allele frequency, or effect size rather than true biological signals.

Conclusion

This study elucidates the complex polygenic architecture of wool traits in Karacabey Merino sheep via multi-locus GWAS. We identified multiple QTNs associated with fibre diameter, staple length, and greasy fleece weight, implicating pathways linked to epigenetic regulation and other key molecular mechanisms. The variable heritability estimates suggest that selective breeding can effectively improve these traits, yet observed genetic correlations with growth performance highlight the need for integrated breeding strategies balancing wool quality and animal development. Functional validation of candidate loci and replication in larger populations will be essential to refine genomic selection. Overall, these findings provide critical insights into the genetic basis of wool traits and offer a foundation for precision breeding in dual-purpose sheep production systems.
