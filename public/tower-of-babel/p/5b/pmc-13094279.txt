Quadruplet daratumumab in combination with bortezomib, lenalidomide, and dexamethasone as front-line regimen improved clinical outcomes in high-risk myeloma patients in China: a multi-centered prospective real-world study
Authors: Lili Cheng, Dong Liang, Jing Jia, Runfeng Zhang, Yang Yang, Peng Liu, Hongli Sun, Xiaoqi Qin, Yanping Ma, Yin Wu, Hua Wang, Junling Zhuang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe addition of anti-CD38 monoclonal antibodies daratumumab to bortezomib, lenalidomide, and dexamethasone (D-VRd) has shown deeper responses in clinical trials. However, real-world data on D-VRd in patients with newly diagnosed MM (NDMM) remain limited in China. This study compared the efficacy and safety of D-VRd versus VRd focusing on high-risk subgroups.MethodsThis was a prospective, multicenter, observational study conducted across six Chinese hospitals. We prospectively enrolled NDMM patients receiving first-line D-VRd between August 2020 and August 2024. A historical control cohort of NDMM patients treated with VRd was retrospectively included. High-risk criteria included cytogenetic abnormalities such as del(17p), t(4;14), t(14;16), or 1q21+, while ultra-high-risk (UHiR) defined as ≥ 2 HRCAs. The primary endpoint was progression-free survival (PFS). A multivariable Cox proportional hazards model was fitted, adjusting for key baseline characteristics.ResultsA total of 100 NDMM patients were prospectively enrolled in D-VRd group, the other 221 patients treated with VRd as a control cohort.The median follow-up time was 16.1 months in the D-VRd group and 40.2 months in the VRd group. For the primary endpoint, the 1-year PFS rate was significantly higher in the D-VRd group compared to the VRd group (92.0% vs. 84.5%; HR 0.58, 95% CI 0.36–0.92). Regarding secondary endpoints, the 1-year OS rate also showed improvement (99.0% vs. 97.0%; HR 0.30, 95% CI 0.12–0.72). Among patients with HRCAs, both 1-year PFS (91.5% vs. 80%; HR 0.41, 95% CI 0.23–0.73) and OS (100.0% vs. 92.2%; HR 0.27, 95% CI 0.10–0.77) were significantly improved with D-VRd. In UHiR patients, D-VRd also provided a significant PFS benefit (95.0% vs. 72.0%; HR 0.31, 95% CI 0.14–0.68). In cytogenetic-specific subgroup analyses, patients with 1q21+ (1-year PFS: 91% vs. 81%; HR 0.45, 95% CI 0.24–0.86) or del(17p) (1-year PFS: 86% vs. 63%; HR 0.28, 95% CI 0.11–0.74) derived significant benefit from D-VRd. Among transplant-ineligible patients, 1-year PFS showed a trend favoring D-VRd over VRd (88% vs. 81%; HR 0.56, 95% CI 0.32–0.99). In terms of safety, leukopenia was more frequent in the D-VRd group (22.9% vs. 7.4%), whereas peripheral neuropathy was less common (16.9% vs. 32.5%), with an overall manageable safety profile.ConclusionThis real-world multicenter study in China demonstrates that D-VRd, compared to VRd, provides superior efficacy with a manageable safety profile as frontline therapy for NDMM, particularly in high-risk patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-15815-8.

Background

Multiple myeloma (MM), the second most common hematological malignancy, has demonstrated significant improvements in progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) over the past two decades. In patients with newly diagnosed multiple myeloma (NDMM), the median PFS has reached 67.5 months when treated with bortezomib, lenalidomide, and dexamethasone (VRd) induction therapy followed by autologous stem-cell transplantation (ASCT) [1]. Even in transplant-ineligible patients, VRd-lite regimen, i.e. a reduced-intensity regimen consisting of weekly bortezomib, lenalidomide and dexamethasone administered for 9 cycles, followed by 6 cycles of VR consolidation, could reach a 36-month PFS [2]. Therefore, VRd has been recommended as a preferred front-line treatment for NDMM [3]. The addition of monoclonal antibody targeting CD38, daratumumab in combination with VRd (D-VRd) demonstrated deeper response and maintained longer-lasting minimal residual disease (MRD)-negative status compared to VRd alone in transplant-eligible NDMM patients [4]. The other anti-CD38 antibody isatuximab with VRd was proved to be more effective than VRd in transplant-ineligible MM patients younger than 80 years old.

According to different risk stratification criteria, high-risk cytogenetic abnormalities (HRCAs) are generally defined as t(4;14), t(14;16), del(17p) or gain/amp(1q21) [5–7]. Patients harboring ≥ 2 HRCAs were classified as ultra-high-risk(UHiR) MM, a category also termed ‘double-hit’ or ‘triple-hit’ myeloma, which is associated with significantly poorer prognosis [8, 9]. International Myeloma Society (IMS)/International Myeloma Working Group (IMWG) recently updated the definition of high-risk multiple myeloma (HRMM), which incorporated del(1p32) [7]. A systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrated that adding daratumumab in front-line treatment was associated with superior PFS in patients with HRCAs [10]. Recent studies targeting high-risk populations have demonstrated that intensified quadruplet therapy holds promise for improving clinical outcomes in both high-risk and UHiR patient subgroups [9, 11]. Nevertheless, further validation of quadruplet regimen in broader routine practice remains an urgent need to substantially improve the long-term prognosis of this high-risk population.

In China, daratumumab is currently approved in both RRMM and transplant-ineligible NDMM patients. However, the application of quadruplet D-VRd as front-line therapy still lacks sufficient data in the real-world practice. This multi-centered prospective investigator initialized trial (IIT) is to explore the efficacy and safety of D-VRd vs. VRd as induction regimens, focusing on the population with high-risk disease and to explore outcomes stratified by transplant eligibility.

Methods

This prospective, multicenter, observational study was conducted in six hospitals in China. We prospectively enrolled NDMM patients who received D-VRd as first-line therapy between August 2020 and August 2024. Eligible patients were required to meet the following criteria: (1) a diagnosis of NDMM according to the IMWG criteria; (2) induction therapy with completion of at least two treatment cycles; and (3) availability of complete baseline clinical data and follow-up records for efficacy evaluation. Key exclusion criteria were missing essential baseline clinical data or having received fewer than two cycles of first-line therapy. An additional 221 NDMM patients who received VRd as first-line therapy at Peking Union Medical College Hospital (PUMCH) between September 2013 and July 2024 were retrospectively included as a historical control cohort.Baseline clinical data including age, gender, paraprotein subtype, revised international staging system (R-ISS), HRCA subtype, UHiR status and ASCT etc. were collected. After 4 cycle induction and ASCT, daratumumab plus lenalidomide (DR) were recommended as maintenance therapy, while non-transplant patients completed 8 cycles of induction then continued dual DR.ASCT relied on both physicians’ and patients’ discretion.HRCAs were defined according to the mSMART criteria as the presence of one or more abnormalities among 1q21+, del(17p), t(4;14), or t(14;16) [12]. Abnormalities such as t(14;20) and del(1p32) (newly classified in the latest IMWG criteria) were not routinely detected during the study period and thus not included in this definition.Patients harboring ≥2HRCAs were classified as UHiR MM. R-ISS, response assessment and progression were evaluated per IMWG Uniform Response Criteria.The primary endpoints were PFS, while secondary endpoints included depth of response, OS and safety profiles.PFS was calculated from the date of diagnosis to the date when disease progression or relapse was recognized or the date of the last follow-up. OS was measured from the date of diagnosis to the date of death or the date of the last follow-up.Adverse events were graded according to the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events, version 5.0.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Peking Union Medical College Hospital(1-23PJ504),and written informed consent was obtained from all participating patients.

Statistical analysis

As a real-world cohort study, we conducted power analysis a prior based on the maximum available sample size. We expected that about 100 patients would be eligible for D-VRd treatment during recruitment. Meanwhile, all the participating research centers could contribute about 200 patients as historical controls with VRd treatment. Based on previous clinical experience, it was expected to observe about 40 progression events in this population. Under a two-sided alpha of 0.05 and 80% statistical power, this sample size could provide enough power to detect a significant hazard ratio of 0.4.Survival functions were analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared by the log-rank test. A multivariable Cox proportional hazards model was constructed to estimate the effect of D-VRd on survival outcomes, adjusting for potential confounders. The baseline clinical characteristics collected above were selected in the model based on clinical experiences. Subgroup analyses were performed based on HRCA status and transplant eligibility, and interaction terms were tested to evaluate the consistency of the treatment effect. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 26.0) or GraphPad Prism (version 9.0). Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P value < 0.05.

Results

Patient characteristics at baseline

This study prospectively enrolled 100 NDMM patients receiving D-VRd as first-line therapy and retrospectively included 221 NDMM patients treated with VRd as a control group from September 2013 to August 2024.After initial identification of 337 patients in the both cohorts dataset, 16 cases (3 in D-VRd group and 13 in VRd group) were excluded due to incomplete baseline data (Fig. 1). Baseline characteristics in D-VRd and VRd groups were comparable in terms of median age (61 vs. 60 years), male gender (58.0% vs. 48.4%), percentage of HRCAs (58.0% vs. 52.5%), R-ISS stage III (34.0% vs. 20.3%) and the proportion of ASCT (35.0% vs. 38.9%). Differences were observed in cytogenetic profiles and disease presentation between the two groups: the D-VRd group had a higher incidence of del(17p) (25.0% vs. 10%) and 1q21+ (53.0% vs. 40.7%), as well as a greater proportion of patients with extramedullary disease(EMD) and plasma cell leukemia(PCL) (29.0% vs. 7.7%) (Table 1). The proportions of patients with elevated LDH and renal insufficiency were similar between groups.

Response

Efficacy was evaluated at three predefined timepoints — the end of induction (defined as the timepoint prior to high-dose chemotherapy followed by ASCT), post-ASCT, and final follow-up — for both ASCT and non-ASCT cohorts, with comparative data presented in Fig. 2. Among ASCT recipients, the D-VRd group demonstrated superior depth of response at the end of induction, with higher rates of ≥ CR (48.6% vs. 41.9%,P = 0.5) and ≥VGPR (86.7% vs. 74.5%,P = 0.176) compared to VRd, despite comparable ORRs (94.3% vs. 98.9%,P = 0.2).

In non-transplant patients, D-VRd significantly improved ≥VGPR rates (89.3% vs. 76.3%, P = 0.031), while ORR (98.5% vs. 93.3%, P = 0.171) and ≥ CR rates (61.6% vs. 50.4%, P = 0.138) showed numerical increases without statistical significance.In the D-VRd group, all patients achieved an objective response except one unevaluable case. Additionally, only two deaths were recorded in D-VRd group, i.e. one patient with plasma cell leukemia and the other suffering sudden death during ASCT.

In the D-VRd group, MRD was assessed upon first achieving CR before transplantation, and subsequently re-evaluated annually. Among 21 patients with serial monitoring, all 12 transplant-eligible patients achieved MRD negativity before transplantation, while non-transplant patients attained MRD negativity within cycles 4–9. By the end of follow-up, only one patient had clinical progressed, with all others maintaining sustained MRD negativity.In the VRd group, MRD was evaluated in 15 patients at a median of 16 months after treatment initiation, with 8 achieving MRD negativity and 7 remaining positive. Five of these patients experienced clinical progression during follow-up.

Survival analysis

The median follow-up time from diagnosis was 16.1 months in D-VRd group and 40.2 months in VRd group. The 1-year PFS rate was 92.0% in the D-VRd group versus 84.5% in the VRd group (P = 0.046; HR, 0.58; 95% CI, 0.36–0.92) (Fig. 3A). The 1-year OS rates were 99.0% and 97.0%, respectively (P = 0.066; HR, 0.30; 95% CI, 0.12–0.72) (Fig. 3B).

Subgroup outcomes by baseline risk status and ASCT status

Subgroup analyses were performed to assess the consistency of treatment benefit with D-VRd across patients with different baseline characteristics.First, among subgroups defined by disease risk status, in patients with HRCAs, the 1-year PFS rate was 91.5% in the D-VRd group, significantly higher than 80% in the VRd group (P = 0.017,HR 0.41,95%CI 0.23–0.73); the 1-year OS rate was also significantly better (100.0% vs. 92.2%, P = 0.014,HR 0.27,95%CI 0.10–0.77) (Fig. 4A and B).Similarly, In patients with UHiR MM, the D-VRd regimen significantly improved 1-year PFS (95.0% vs. 72.0%; P = 0.028; HR, 0.31; 95% CI, 0.14–0.68) and OS (100.0% vs. 89.0%; P = 0.014; HR, 0.25; 95% CI, 0.08–0.76) compared with VRd (Fig. 4C and D). For newly diagnosed patients with EMD or PCL, D-VRd also demonstrated superior PFS (88.0% vs. 59.0%, P = 0.04,HR 0.35,95%CI 0.12–1.027) with a numerically higher 1-year OS rate (100.0% vs. 82.0%; P = 0.129; HR, 0.27; 95% CI, 0.05–1.36) (Fig. 4E and F).

Further cytogenetic subgroup analyses indicated that among patients with 1q21+, the D-VRd group had significantly higher 1-year PFS (91.0% vs. 81.0%, P = 0.047,HR 0.45,95%CI 0.24–0.86) and OS (100.0% vs. 95.3%, P = 0.029,HR 0.26,95%CI 0.08–0.87) than the VRd group (Fig. 5A and B). Finally, we analyzed the treatment benefit in relation to ASCT status.Among non-transplanted patients, the D-VRd group showed a trend toward higher 1-year PFS (88.0% vs. 81.0%, P = 0.088,HR 0.56,95%CI 0.32–0.99) and OS (100.0% vs. 95.0%, P = 0.08,HR 0.22,95%CI 0.08–0.63) compared with the VRd group (Fig. 6A and B). Safety

Table 3 summarized the most common adverse events (AEs) of any grade. The overall incidence of AEs was 83% in the D-VRd group compared with 73.8% in the VRd group. Hematologic AEs predominated over nonhematologic events in both groups.Among hematologic AEs, leukopenia occurred most frequently, with significantly higher in the D-VRd group (22.9% vs. 7.4% in VRd). For nonhematologic AEs, the D-VRd group showed higher rates of infections (16.9%), peripheral neuropathy (PN, 16.9%), and rash (9.6%), whereas the VRd group exhibited different predominant toxicities: PN (32.0%), diarrhea (22.9%), and rash (13.5%). Grade 3–4 adverse events were uncommon. Leukopenia and infection each occurred in 7 patients (8.4%) with D-VRd versus 4 patients (2.5%) with VRd. Peripheral neuropathy was reported in 5 (6.0%) and 14 (8.6%) patients, and rash in 1 (1.2%) and 3 (1.8%) patients, in the D-VRd and VRd groups, respectively.

Discussion

Multiple clinical trials have demonstrated that adding daratumumab, either subcutaneously or intravenously, to induction therapy significantly enhances depth of response and prolongs PFS [4, 13, 14]. As the clinical superiority was verified, quadruplet combination incorporating anti-CD38 antibody with VRd has been recommended as a front-line regimen for both transplant-eligible and ineligible NDMM patients [15]. However, large-scale real-world evidence supporting these findings remains scarce in China.

Notably, compared with published data in RCTs, patients in our real-world study had a higher prevalence of adverse baseline features, including a higher proportion of R-ISS III and a greater percentage of HRCAs [16]. This enrichment likely reflects methodological and practice-related factors rather than intrinsic population differences. A key reason is differing risk criteria: we used contemporary mSMART guidelines (classifying 1q21 + as high-risk [12]), whereas the latest IMWG definition just updated in 2025 [7]. We focused on 1q21 + given its high frequency in Chinese MM patients [17] and our prior evidence linking its co-occurrence with Myc rearrangement to poorer outcomes [18]. In patients with only del(17p), t(14;16), t(4;14), or these plus 1q21+, rates were 39% (D-VRd) and 29.4% (VRd), still much higher than RCT studies [4, 19]. Additionally, D-VRd was often selectively given to recognized high-risk patients during the study period, further enriching this subgroup and explaining the higher proportions of adverse features like PCL/EMD versus other reports.

Given that HRCAs are well-established predictors of poor prognosis [3, 8], improved outcomes in this HRMM subgroup is of particular importance.Prognosis worsens as the number of HRCAs increases. UHiR MM patients have a much higher risk of early relapse and worse outcomes compared to those with ≤1HRCA [3, 11, 20–23]. Although daratumumab has improved PFS in these patients, their long-term prognosis remains suboptimal, with a 36-month PFS rate of approximately 53% [22]. Current research is exploring intensified regimens, such as daratumumab, cyclophosphamide, bortezomib, lenalidomide, and dexamethasone(D-CVRd) or daratumumab, carfilzomib, lenalidomide, and dexamethasone(D-KRd) followed by tandem ASCT, to further improve outcomes [7, 24]. In this study, D-VRd significantly improved 1-year PFS in UHiR patients, suggesting its value as a first-line option for those ineligible for more intensive therapies, though longer follow-up is needed.

Subgroup analyses were conducted for del(17p) and t(4;14) that are rarely studied in NDMM due to limited sample sizes [3, 22]. Our results demonstrated that D-VRd show the benefit was less pronounced for t(4;14). This may be attributed to the small number of isolated t(4;14) (n = 16) in our cohort. Recent studies indicate that in the era of immune therapy, isolated t(4;14) may be no longer of prognostic significance, while its co-occurrence with 1q21 + or del(17p) remains predictive of poor outcomes [25]. Subgroup analysis did not show a statistically significant PFS benefit for D-VRd in standard-risk patients, unlike in high-risk subgroups. However, formal interaction tests were non-significant for key baseline factors (all P > 0.05). This indicates that the observed differential benefit is not statistically robust and more plausibly reflects limited power in the standard-risk subgroup due to short-term follow-up, fewer progression events, and a smaller sample size—a pattern also noted in other real-world studies.

Although ASCT after induction treatment is the first-line therapy for eligible patients with NDMM [1], its adoption rate in China is approximately 35% in this study. This disparity may be attributed to factors including patients’ preconceived understanding and financial constraints. Studies confirm sequential ASCT post-induction improves outcomes [26]. In transplant-ineligible patients, adding daratumumab also prolonged both PFS and OS [27]. In our study, ASCT rates were comparable between the D-VRd and VRd groups. Although a statistically significant survival advantage of D-VRd over VRd was not reached in the transplant-eligible cohort, this likely reflects the established efficacy of VRd followed by ASCT as a standard regimen [26], as well as the limited sample size and follow-up duration in our subgroup analysis [16]. Importantly, formal interaction analysis showed no significant difference in the treatment effect of D-VRd based on transplant eligibility, indicating that the therapeutic benefit of D-VRd is consistent in direction across both transplant-eligible and transplant-ineligible patients.

In the efficacy analysis stratified by transplant status, the D-VRd regimen demonstrated superior depth of response (≥ CR) in both transplant-eligible and ineligible patients, with the benefit most pronounced after induction. Notably, the between-group difference diminished post-transplant, underscoring ASCT’s pivotal role. Consistent with RCT evidence supporting D-VRd as a first-line option regardless of transplant status [19], our study demonstrates its efficacy in improving early outcomes. However, the early PFS and OS benefits observed here require cautious interpretation given the short median follow-up (16.1 months for D-VRd). For long-term efficacy, MRD negativity is a well-established predictor of survival, including in daratumumab-based therapy [11, 28]. Although MRD assessment was not standardized across centers in our study, available data suggest a higher rate of MRD negativity in the D-VRd group. Collectively, the deeper responses, higher MRD negativity trend, and early survival advantage all support the capacity of the D-VRd regimen to induce high-quality remissions.

The overall incidence of AEs was slightly higher in the D-VRd group. Hematologic AEs, especially leukopenia, were more common with D-VRd, while PN was more frequent in the VRd group. Although AE rates in this real-world study were lower than those reported in randomized controlled trials (RCTs)—likely due to heterogeneous data collection—the AE profile was consistent with RCTs [4], offering practical guidance for prophylaxis and management in clinical practice.

Our study has several limitations.First, there are intrinsic methodological constraints including heterogeneity in diagnostic practices and data collection across participating centers, potential patient selection bias due to clinical decision-making, and calendar time differences between the prospective D-VRd cohort and the historical VRd control.Second, maintenance therapies were heterogenous-while most patients receiving D-VRd induction therapy opted for DR maintenance, some switched to bortezomib-or ixazomib-based maintenance due to tolerability issues.Third, the irregular MRD assessment precluded the evaluation of response depth, a limitation we plan to address in future studies.Furthermore, the definition of HRCAs, while based on contemporary guidelines, differs from the latest international standards, which may affect cross-study comparability. Despite these limitations, this study provides the first multicenter, prospective real-world evidence from China demonstrating the superior efficacy of D-VRd over VRd in NDMM, particularly in high-risk and transplant-ineligible patients.

Conclusions

This multicenter real-world study in China demonstrates the superior efficacy of the D-VRd quadruplet regimen over VRd in frontline NDMM treatment, particularly for high-risk and transplant-ineligible patients. The regimen showed a manageable safety profile. These findings support D-VRd as a preferred frontline option for NDMM, especially in high-risk populations.
