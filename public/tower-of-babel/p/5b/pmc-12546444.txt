Applications of musculoskeletal ultrasound in inflammatory arthritis
Authors: Noor Nooh, Rimaz Turkawi, Mark Maybury, Caroline Cardy, Ilfita Sahbudin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Musculoskeletal ultrasound is useful for early diagnosis of inflammatory arthritis.•Ultrasound facilitates clinicians to distinguish different types of inflammatory arthritis.•Efforts to standardise ultrasound training and competency in rheumatology are progressing, especially in the UK.•Artificial intelligence technology has the potential to enhance diagnostic consistency, reducing variability in image interpretation and improving patient care.

Introduction

Musculoskeletal ultrasound is increasingly used in rheumatology as a diagnostic and disease-monitoring tool for patients with inflammatory arthropathies. The underpinning principle that drives this development is the ability of ultrasound to detect subtle joint and tendon inflammation that is not clinically apparent, thereby detecting inflammation earlier in the disease process. The main advantages of ultrasound include real-time visualisation, good patient tolerability, the ability to scan multiple joints in a short time, cost-effectiveness and the absence of ionising radiation.1

Ultrasound pathology in inflammatory arthritis

One of the diagnostic challenges in clinical rheumatology is to differentiate between inflammatory and non-inflammatory causes of joint pain and swelling. This is crucial as it drives the therapeutic decision whether to start a patient on disease-modifying antirheumatic medications. Conventionally, this diagnostic information is obtained from history taking, physical examination and laboratory tests, including serology and acute phase markers. However, symptoms like joint pain, swelling and stiffness can also be caused by non-inflammatory conditions such as fibromyalgia and osteoarthritis, which often co-exist in patients with rheumatic disease. It is within this context that musculoskeletal ultrasound has the greatest benefits by quantifying the presence of inflammation and degenerative changes, thereby facilitating clinical decision-making.

Musculoskeletal ultrasound plays a key role in accurately distinguishing between different types of inflammatory arthritis early in the disease process.2, 3, 4, 5 This review will summarise the typical ultrasound findings that are commonly observed in several types of inflammatory arthritis, including rheumatoid arthritis, spondyloarthropathy and crystal arthropathy.

Rheumatoid arthritis

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic progressive multisystem condition with inflammatory arthritis as the primary manifestation.6 Delay in treatment may lead to irreversible joint damage and a high level of joint disability. Therefore, early detection of joint inflammation is vital to identify those who require immunosuppression therapy to prevent disease progression.6

The European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) published evidence-based recommendations on the use of imaging in RA.7 The guidance proposed the use of ultrasound to improve the diagnostic certainty of RA in cases when the diagnosis is unclear based on clinical criteria alone. The guidance also concluded that ultrasound is superior to clinical examination in the detection of joint inflammation and recommends that ultrasound imaging should be used for an accurate assessment of joint inflammation.7

Rheumatology investigators have reported the utility of ultrasound as a risk prediction tool in RA. Spondyloarthropathy

Spondyloarthropathy is a spectrum of disease that includes psoriatic arthritis, axial and peripheral spondyloarthritis, reactive arthritis and enteropathic arthritis.9 These diseases share common clinical and genetic traits, including inflammation of the axial skeleton, presence of HLA-B27 antigen and peripheral joint manifestations, such as asymmetrical oligoarthritis, dactylitis and enthesitis.9

Ultrasound is useful in assessing enthesitis, a hallmark feature of spondyloarthropathy.9 Enthesitis is inflammation at the insertion of a tendon, ligament or joint capsule to a bone​. Enthesitis is difficult to assess clinically. Tenderness on palpation of entheseal sites is a marker of enthesitis, but this clinical sign can also be caused by chronic pain or degenerative disease; thus, ultrasound plays an important role in detecting subclinical enthesitis and quantifying the extent of enthesitis.4,10

In a study of patients with psoriatic arthritis, ultrasound is better compared to clinical examination in the detection of enthesis in patients with psoriatic arthritis and concurrent fibromyalgia.5 Multiple studies have reported that ultrasound is useful to differentiate inflammatory synovitis or enthesitis from other conditions such as osteoarthritis and mechanical joint pain in patients with psoriasis and musculoskeletal pain.11, 12, 13 Therefore, ultrasound facilitates clinicians to identify whether the specific musculoskeletal pain is related to inflammatory or non-inflammatory causes and treat accordingly. Ultrasound can also distinguish between RA and psoriatic arthritis at the early stages of disease, which is useful as often radiographic signs and inflammatory markers are normal at this stage. A cross-sectional ultrasound study of patients with RA and psoriatic arthritis showed the differences in ultrasound signatures between the two diseases. Ultrasound-detected extensor enthesitis of distal interphalangeal joints, peritendon inflammation of finger extensor tendon and tissue oedema were more frequent in early psoriatic arthritis. In contrast, ultrasound extensor carpi ulnaris tenosynovitis was more common in RA. Crystal arthropathy

Crystal arthropathy is characterised by the accumulation of crystals in articular cartilage and surrounding soft tissues, leading to acute or chronic inflammation.15 Ultrasound imaging is effective in identifying and distinguishing between different types of crystal deposits, such as urate in gout and calcium pyrophosphate dihydrate (CPPD) crystals in CPPD arthritis. This is useful in cases where synovial fluid aspiration is not amenable.16 Due to the high reflectivity of CPPD aggregates in hyaline cartilage, ultrasound can detect even small deposits (less than 1 mm) as hyperechoic spots. The accumulation of CPPD crystals in cartilage leads to chondrocalcinosis. On ultrasound, this appears as hyperechoic deposits within the hyaline cartilage.17 In contrast, urate crystals deposit on the surface of hyaline cartilage, enhancing the chondro-synovial interface and resulting in the double contour sign (Fig. 3C–E). The EULAR 2023 guidance on imaging for crystal arthropathies recommends ultrasound for the diagnostic assessment of gout, as ultrasound can identify inflammation, early joint damage and crystal deposition.18 Additionally, the guidance also states that synovial fluid analysis is not required to confirm a gout diagnosis in cases where ultrasound-detected urate crystal deposition is observed (such as the double contour sign or tophi). In the context of monitoring disease, the EULAR guidance proposed considering ultrasound as a disease-monitoring tool in only in selected patients, for example, those with frequent gout flares despite adherence to urate-lowering treatment.18 However, studies are needed to assess whether monitoring gout with imaging is superior to the current practice of monitoring without imaging. Rheumatology investigators have developed various scanning protocols for crystal arthropathies; bilateral ultrasound scanning of knees, wrists and hips showed excellent accuracy and feasibility for CPPD diagnosis,19 while a reduced scanning protocol of a targeted joint plus two joints bilaterally showed excellent accuracy (>90%) in both gout and CPPD arthritis.20

Implementation challenges

The British Society for Rheumatology Ultrasound Special Interest Group (BSR US SIG) published the recommendations for rheumatology ultrasound training in the UK in 2021. A working group within the BSR US SIG formulated seven proposals, which were presented to the broader US SIG and agreed upon via a Delphi consensus process.21

Musculoskeletal ultrasound is a mandatory component in rheumatology training in some countries, including Germany, Italy and Norway.22 However, the UK has yet to do this. Ultrasound is an optional element in the latest UK rheumatology curriculum.23 Currently, trainees interested in ultrasound training rely on short courses provided by national societies rather than structured formal training within rheumatology programmes.24

Artificial intelligence in musculoskeletal ultrasound imaging

The use of artificial intelligence (AI) technology in medical imaging is a rapidly expanding area of research.25,26 Ultrasound, being a highly operator-dependent imaging modality, can be influenced by examiners’ experience. AI technology in image recognition and interpretation has the potential to address challenges in physician-dependent assessments, including variabilities in image acquisition. Automated systems can offer rapid and consistent assessments, which improve access to quality diagnostics.25,26

ARTHUR is an AI-assisted ultrasound robot used for patients with suspected arthritis and RA. When compared to a rheumatologist with scanning expertise, the ARTHUR robot showed a comparable level of accuracy in detecting joint inflammation.27 Training specialists is costly and time-consuming, although AI technology also entails significant expenses.25,26 The prohibitive cost of AI technologies remains a key barrier to adoption in the UK.28

Conclusion

In conclusion, musculoskeletal ultrasound is becoming more important in rheumatology clinical practice. Ultrasound markers such as synovitis and tenosynovitis in RA, enthesitis in spondyloarthropathy and crystal deposition in gout, facilitate clinicians to reach the right diagnosis quicker, especially in the early disease phase. Ultrasound is easily accessible, patient-friendly and non-invasive; therefore, the use of ultrasound as a point-of-care tool in rheumatology is likely to become more common. However, one of the main limitations is that ultrasound is unable to visualise structures outside the acoustic window, ie ultrasound cannot visualise structures that are obscured by bone.29 In addition, ultrasound is not well-suited to visualise deep-seated structures like the hip joint. Alternative imaging modalities such as MRI or CT are required in this context. In the future, the integration of AI systems promises to enhance consistency and speed of assessments. These advancements hold great potential to transform rheumatological care and streamline patient management.
