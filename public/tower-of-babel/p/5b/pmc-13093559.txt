Recurrent Cytopathic Effects and Prolonged Viral Shedding in an Immunocompromised Patient With Malignant Lymphoma Treated With Rituximab and Bendamustine: A Case of Coronavirus Disease 2019 Relapse
Authors: Takahisa Ohira, Taku Ogawa, Tomoko Tanaka, Shoichi Sakaguchi, Youichi Suzuki, Hong Wu, Tomoyuki Yamada, Yuriko Shibata, Kazuki Iwaki, Ayana Okazaki, Akira Ukimura, Takashi Nakano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA 79‐year‐old woman with malignant lymphoma initially recovered from mild coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID‐19), but later developed pneumonia. Despite continuous PCR positivity, cytopathic effect (CPE) temporarily became negative before re‐emerging with clinical deterioration. This case shows that PCR results alone cannot reliably determine viral infectivity, and once‐negative CPE can become positive again.

Introduction

Prolonged viral shedding has been documented in patients with coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID‐19), particularly among immunocompromised individuals [1, 2, 3]. Additionally, a phenomenon termed “relapse” has been observed, wherein previously improved symptoms reemerge [4]. However, cases exhibiting both extended viral shedding and recurrence of cytopathic effects (CPEs) have not been well characterized, and their clinical and virological features remain incompletely understood. We report a case of a patient with underlying malignant lymphoma and rheumatoid arthritis who experienced a relapse in both symptoms and CPE. The recurrence of CPE in immunocompromised patients suggests that the virus may persist in a latent state within the patient's body and potentially proliferate again in response to alterations in the host's condition. This case is significant in elucidating the pathophysiology of COVID‐19 recrudescence.

Case History/Examination

We report the case of a 79‐year‐old female diagnosed with rheumatoid arthritis in 2005 who was initially treated with sulfasalazine and prednisolone, with methotrexate added in 2010. In August 2020, she presented with anemia, elevated lactate dehydrogenase levels, and splenomegaly detected via abdominal computed tomography (CT) at a local facility. She was referred to the Department of Hematology at Osaka Medical and Pharmaceutical University Hospital. Blood tests revealed the following results: hemoglobin, 8.2 g/dL; platelets, 120,000/μL; and lactate dehydrogenase, 1580 U/L. Malignant lymphoma was suspected but was not definitively diagnosed following bone marrow aspiration.

Bone marrow biopsy showed hypercellular marrow with infiltration of atypical large B lymphoid cells positive for CD20 and CD79a. Epstein–Barr virus‐encoded RNA in situ hybridization (EBER‐ISH) was negative. Flow cytometric analysis demonstrated a marked imbalance in the κ/λ light chain ratio, suggesting monoclonal B‐cell proliferation. Given the patient's long‐term methotrexate therapy for rheumatoid arthritis, methotrexate‐associated lymphoproliferative disorder (MTX‐LPD) was considered in the differential diagnosis. Methotrexate was discontinued, and the patient was observed for approximately three months; however, no significant clinical improvement or reduction in splenomegaly was observed. In light of the absence of EBV infection, the presence of clonal B‐cell expansion, persistently elevated lactate dehydrogenase levels, and progressive splenomegaly, the overall clinical findings were considered consistent with a primary splenic B‐cell lymphoma. Based on this working diagnosis, systemic chemotherapy was initiated.

Treatment began with one cycle of the cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone regimen in November 2020, followed by four cycles of the cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, etoposide, and prednisone regimen, totaling five chemotherapy cycles. Positron emission tomography‐computed tomography performed in March 2021 after the fourth cycle indicated complete remission. However, tumor progression noted in May 2021 suggested treatment resistance, leading to further treatment with one cycle each of the ifosfamide, carboplatin, and etoposide and cyclophosphamide, methotrexate, and dexamethasone regimens, followed by three cycles of the rituximab and bendamustine (RB) regimen, which resulted in complete remission. RB therapy was extended, and the patient completed the fifth cycle in mid‐May.

The patient had not received any SARS‐CoV‐2 vaccination because of concerns about potential adverse reactions. Immunoglobulin levels and CD4 lymphocyte counts were not routinely measured during the clinical course.

In late May 2022, the patient presented with fever and general fatigue and underwent a polymerase chain reaction (PCR) test for severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS‐CoV‐2) at a local clinic, which resulted in a positive diagnosis of COVID‐19. Despite the underlying medical conditions, she was admitted to another hospital for 10 days. During hospitalization, she was treated with remdesivir (100 mg every 24 h [initially 200 mg]) and sotrovimab without complications such as pneumonia and experienced a mild course of COVID‐19 (Figure 1). Subsequently, to proceed with the 6th course of RB therapy, her cycle threshold (Ct) value progression was evaluated in an outpatient setting but failed to meet the hospital's criteria for COVID‐19 isolation clearance, requiring a Ct value of ≥ 30. This led to repeated postponement of her admission. Therefore, we assessed the presence of direct CPEs and infectivity using nasopharyngeal swab specimens. When the viral fluid extracted from the swab was applied to Vero cells, no CPEs were observed after 1 week, allowing for hospitalization without COVID‐19 isolation, despite her inability to achieve the required Ct value.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment

Rehospitalization was scheduled for mid‐August 2022, but immediately before admission, she experienced fever again, necessitating an investigation of the cause post‐admission. The real‐time reverse transcription PCR (RT‐PCR) test upon admission showed a Ct value of 24.96, which did not meet the hospital's COVID‐19 isolation clearance criteria of a Ct value of > 30. Despite the disappearance of the CPEs (Figure 2a), no specific infection control measures for active COVID‐19 were implemented based on the previous negative CPE result. Chest radiography revealed reticular opacities in both lower lung fields, indicating pneumonia as the cause of fever. Chest CT showed predominant ground‐glass opacities in both lower lobes, with partial consolidation at the lung bases. Sputum cultures did not reveal any significant pathogenic bacteria, and acid‐fast bacillus smears were negative in three consecutive tests. Garenoxacin (400 mg every 24 h) was administered 2 weeks later without showing effectiveness, followed by initiation of cefozopran (1 g every 8 h). Steroid therapy was also administered owing to suspected COVID‐19–related organizing pneumonia but was ineffective.

(a) Negative control, which shows no cytopathic effect (CPE). (b) CPE, indicating that the nasopharyngeal swab specimen obtained from the patient contains viable severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2.

On the 56th day of rehospitalization, repeat SARS‐CoV‐2 real‐time RT‐PCR was performed, and the Ct value was found to have decreased to 20.68. Consequently, CPEs were reassessed using the same specimen, and the result was positive for SARS‐CoV‐2 (Figure 2b). The patient was diagnosed with moderate COVID‐19, which was considered to be due to viral relapse rather than reinfection. Remdesivir (100 mg every 24 h [initially 200 mg]) was administered for 9 days. Following treatment, COVID‐19 pneumonia improved, and on the 89th day of rehospitalization, the patient was discharged in stable condition. During hospitalization, however, chemotherapy for the underlying malignant lymphoma could not be administered.

The SARS‐CoV‐2 strain isolated from the nasopharyngeal swab specimen after CPEs reappeared was investigated to determine its variant. Initially, the virus detected before CPE became negative was labeled by the World Health Organization as the Omicron variant and identified as the Pango lineage BA.2/P337S. However, after CPE reappeared, the detected SARS‐CoV‐2 strain was identified as the Pango lineage BA.2.77. Compared with the original Wuhan strain, the BA.2/P337S strain exhibited two amino acid substitutions, whereas the BA.2.77 strain exhibited three amino acid substitutions, resulting in a total of five amino acid changes observed during the clinical course. The patient was readmitted in August 2022, and during that period, the variant surveillance conducted by the National Institute of Infectious Diseases in Japan did not report any detection of the BA.2.77 lineage. Therefore, the infection control team determined that the recurrence of CPE in this case was attributable to relapse rather than reinfection.

In this study, viral cultures were performed using TMPRSS2‐expressing cells. CPE was assessed by observing the cultured cells for 7 days; if no cellular degeneration was observed during this period, the sample was deemed CPE‐negative.

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

After discharge, the patient resumed outpatient rituximab and bendamustine therapy. She completed the 11th course in March 2025, after which chemotherapy was discontinued at her request. The patient has maintained a complete response (CR) of the lymphoma since November 2023 and continues regular follow‐up. As of January 2026, the serum soluble interleukin‐2 receptor level was 526 U/mL. During follow‐up, there have been no episodes suggestive of recurrent COVID‐19 and no COVID‐19–related symptoms. No additional SARS‐CoV‐2 PCR testing or CPE assays using nasopharyngeal swab specimens were performed after discharge. As of January 2026, the patient remains alive and clinically stable.

Discussion

This patient was scheduled for readmission for chemotherapy because the pneumonia symptoms and fever disappeared, and the viral CPE was no longer present. However, immediately before admission, the patient again developed pneumonia, and the Ct value on real‐time RT‐PCR decreased. However, COVID‐19 relapse or reinfection was not considered. Consequently, the pneumonia was misdiagnosed as post‐COVID‐19 organizing pneumonia, which led to the administration of systemic steroids. Additionally, isolation of the patient with active COVID‐19 was delayed. Past reports have indicated that organizing pneumonia often occurs relatively late in the course of COVID‐19 [5, 6, 7]. Because organizing pneumonia responds well to steroids, another differential diagnosis should have been considered when the response to steroids was poor. If this had been done, the reappearance of CPEs might have been detected early. Organizing pneumonia is an important differential diagnosis for pneumonia after COVID‐19 [8]. However, it is crucial to consider the possibility of pneumonia due to COVID‐19 recurrence. This case highlights the fact that both conditions should be considered when diagnosing post‐COVID‐19 pneumonia.

Numerous cases have been reported where prolonged viral shedding continues after COVID‐19, particularly in immunocompromised patients [1, 2, 3]. Similar to this case, other reports have simultaneously evaluated the presence of CPEs [9]. Similarly, in patients with COVID‐19 with a treatment history of RB, prolonged positivity for SARS‐CoV‐2 has been confirmed via PCR for an extended period [10]. Furthermore, the recurrence of COVID‐19 has been reported in patients receiving anti‐CD20 monoclonal antibody therapy. It is desirable to confirm the presence or absence of CPEs to determine actual infectivity. However, assessing the presence of CPE is not readily available in all facilities; therefore, interpretations are generally based on reports investigating the correlation between the presence of CPEs and Ct values [9, 11]. It is anticipated that even if a patient remains PCR‐positive, they are sometimes no longer infectious after COVID‐19; conversely, patients without CPEs might experience CPE recurrence. Therefore, the question of how long a patient with COVID‐19 can remain a source of infection remains a significant and unresolved issue, particularly for immunocompromised patients.

Although some studies have suggested that sotrovimab lacks efficacy against Omicron variants, our case demonstrates clinical improvement following sotrovimab administration. Recent research by Bruel et al. provides a potential explanation for this apparent discrepancy. Their study showed that sotrovimab not only maintains some neutralizing activity against Omicron subvariants like BQ.1.1 and XBB.1.5, but also importantly elicits significant antibody‐dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) activity which is largely preserved against these variants [12]. This dual mechanism of action—combining both neutralizing and non‐neutralizing immune responses—may explain why sotrovimab can remain clinically effective despite reduced neutralization potency. Based on these considerations, we decided to use sotrovimab for the treatment of Omicron infection in our case.

In our case, Omicron/BA.2/P337S was initially detected, and when CPE became positive again 3 months later, BA.2.77 was detected. We confirmed this by analyzing 1271 amino acids in the S gene. Compared with the original Wuhan strain, the BA.2/P337S strain exhibited two amino acid substitutions, whereas the BA.2.77 strain exhibited three amino acid substitutions. Thus, a total of five amino acid changes were observed during the clinical course, although these changes do not represent five substitutions occurring sequentially between the two variants. Among these, the viral strain detected immediately before the disappearance of CPE contained two amino acid substitutions, P337S and Y369S, compared with the original Wuhan strain. It is conceivable that these substitutions may have reduced viral infectivity in Vero cells, thereby contributing to the absence of detectable CPE despite PCR positivity. However, previous studies have reported that although these substitutions are located within the receptor binding domain (RBD), they are not included in the receptor binding motif (RBM) [13, 14], which is more directly involved in ACE2 binding. Therefore, it is considered less likely that these amino acid substitutions alone were responsible for the disappearance of CPE, and other factors may have contributed to the discordance between PCR positivity and the absence of detectable CPE in this case.

According to a previous report, whole‐genome sequencing in an immunosuppressed patient revealed a mutation of 12 bases within 4 months [15]. The S gene encodes a spike protein of 1273 amino acids, representing approximately 12% of the total genome of SARS‐CoV‐2, and the S gene is five times more frequently mutated than other regions [15, 16, 17]. Considering these facts, it is presumed that the mutation frequency in our case was somewhat higher than that reported previously. However, this patient was aware that she was immunocompromised and had implemented universal masking and thorough infection control measures, even at home. In addition, in October 2022, when CPE was re‐positive, there were no reports of BA.2.77 in the nationwide genomic surveillance of the National Institute of Infectious Diseases in Japan [18]. Therefore, we conclude that re‐positive CPE should be considered a relapse and not a reinfection in immunocompromised patients with pneumonia after COVID‐19. While we observed 5 amino acid substitutions in this case over a 3‐month period, it is crucial to note that this number is significantly lower than the approximately 100 nucleotide differences typically observed between BA.2 and BA.5 lineages, which were predominant in the community at the time of the second CPE positivity [18]. This stark contrast strongly supports the likelihood of viral relapse rather than reinfection.
