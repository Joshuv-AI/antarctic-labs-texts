Left pulmonary artery sling in an infant: a vascular anomaly with a high impact on breathing
Authors: Melpomeni Bizhga, Blerina Saraci
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryAn infant presented with persistent stridor and recurrent respiratory distress since birth. Imaging revealed a left pulmonary artery sling causing significant tracheal compression. Surgical reimplantation of the left pulmonary artery to the main pulmonary artery with concurrent slide tracheoplasty resulted in full recovery. This case highlights the importance of early recognition of vascular anomalies in infants presenting with unexplained airway obstruction.Keywords: Paediatrics (drugs and medicines), Respiratory system, Cardiovascular medicine

Background

Left pulmonary artery (PA) sling (LPAS) is a rare congenital vascular anomaly in which the left PA arises aberrantly from the right PA and courses between the trachea and oesophagus to reach the left lung. The anomalous left PA passes over the right main stem bronchus near its origin and turns to the left, coursing between the lower trachea posteriorly and oesophagus anteriorly to reach the left pulmonary hilus, thereby indenting these structures and displacing the trachea to the left. This anomalous course can compress the distal trachea and right main bronchus leading to respiratory distress in infancy.

The first case of PA sling was reported by Glaevecke and Doehle in 1897 as a post-mortem finding in a 7-month-old infant with severe respiratory distress.1 Contro et al introduced the term ‘vascular sling’ to distinguish this entity from a vascular ring.2 To date, several cases have been recorded in published reports. LPAS accounts for <0.1% of congenital heart defects and is frequently associated with complete tracheal rings or other airway malformation.3 The first successful surgical repair of PA sling was performed in 1953 by Potts, Holinger and Rosenblum4

Early diagnosis is crucial, as delayed recognition can result in progressive airway compromise, pulmonary hypertension and failure to thrive.

Case presentation

An infant was admitted to the paediatric intensive care unit with difficulty breathing, with a history of noisy breathing since birth, worsening over the preceding 2 months. Ambulatory treatment with steroid aerosols and antibiotics for bronchiolitis and pneumonia showed some relief of difficulty breathing without relieving the breathing noise. Parents reported frequent episodes of respiratory distress, particularly during feeding and upper respiratory infections. She was delivered vaginally at term with birthweight of 2.6 kg, had exclusive breastfeeding for the first 2 months, but formula was introduced at 3 months of age. There was a polydactyly of the right hand. No other anomalies were noticed. There was no history of cyanosis or cardiac murmur noted at birth.

On examination, the infant was conscious, tachypneic with a respiratory rate of 58 breaths per minute, with consistent intercostal retractions and biphasic stridor. Decreased breath sounds were heard on the right side. Heart rate was 150 beats per minute, and no cardiac murmur was present. Pulse oximetry was 88% on room air. During salbutamol aerosol therapy, the child experienced desaturations up to 78%, and steroids failed to improve breathing and stridor.

Investigations

A chest radiograph showed hyperinflation of the right lung and narrowing of the distal tracheal shadow (figure 1).

Echocardiography revealed a normal structure of the heart, situs solitus and normal biventricular function but was unable to reveal any anomaly of great arteries.

Flexible bronchoscopy revealed severe external compression of the distal trachea with evidence of indentation and complete tracheal rings in the lower part of trachea with significant stenosis (figure 2).

Flexible bronchoscopy view: extrinsic compression of the trachea (indentation and stenosis) in the lower part of the trachea because of left aberrant vascular sling.

CT angiography (contrast-enhanced) confirmed the diagnosis of LPAS. The left PA was seen arising from the posterior aspect of the right PA, coursing between the trachea and oesophagus, compressing the distal trachea and right main bronchus. Differential diagnosis

Viral laryngotracheitis has similar clinical signs with LPAS such as stridor and difficulty breathing that are present in both, but in infants and children aged <6 months, viral laryngitis is an exception. In LPAS, symptoms present from first days of life; thus, long-lasting symptoms and failure to conventional therapy should lead to further diagnostic up.5

Another differential diagnosis should be subglottic stenosis because in both LPAS and subglottic stenosis, stridor is biphasic. X-rays can help in differentiation, especially in this case when overinflation of the right side is evident.

Vascular ring (double aortic arch and right aortic arch with aberrant subclavian artery) is a cardiovascular anomaly that mimics LPAS, and complete differentiation is made through angiographic CT. Bronchoscopy views are similar, especially in cases with severe stenosis and complete vascular rings; differentiation between different vascular anomalies is possible by angiographic CT.

Children with tracheomalacia and/or bronchomalacia have similar clinical signs, but less severe, intermittent and improved from steroids and antibiotics.

Foreign body aspiration is necessary to be excluded, but a careful history, lack of penetration syndrome and symptoms present from birth can help in differentiation at presentation, and radiological and bronchoscopy evaluation exclude such a possibility.6

Treatment

The patient underwent surgical correction via median sternotomy under cardiopulmonary bypass. The left PA was detached from the right PA and reimplanted into the main PA anterior to the trachea. Intraoperative findings confirmed significant tracheal narrowing due to complete tracheal rings; therefore, a slide tracheoplasty was performed concurrently.

Postoperative management included mechanical ventilation for 48 hours, followed by gradual weaning to spontaneous breathing. The infant received short-term inhaled corticosteroids and antibiotics to prevent postoperative airway inflammation and infection.

Outcome and follow-up

The postoperative course was uneventful. The infant was discharged 28 days after surgery with normal oxygen saturation and no respiratory distress. Follow-up bronchoscopy at 3 months demonstrated a widely patent trachea with no residual compression. At the 6-month follow-up, the child had normal growth and development, with resolution of stridor and no recurrence of respiratory symptoms.

Discussion

LPAS is a rare but potentially life-threatening cause of airway obstruction in infants. The condition arises from abnormal embryologic development of the sixth aortic arches, resulting in the left PA arising from the right PA and coursing between the trachea and oesophagus. The resultant tracheal compression can lead to severe respiratory compromise early in life.7 It may be present with common symptoms in infant age as respiratory distress, wheezing and difficulty feeding and may be under-evaluated by paediatricians, leading to further distress and morbidity and mortality if undertreated.

Echocardiography is the initial investigation of choice, but CT or MRI angiography provides definitive anatomical delineation. Bronchoscopy is essential to assess the degree of airway compression and identify associated tracheal anomalies, especially of stenosis with complete tracheal ring.

Surgical reimplantation of the left PA to the main PA anterior to the trachea is the standard treatment. In cases with tracheal stenosis, slide tracheoplasty is performed simultaneously. Advances in surgical techniques have significantly improved survival rates, with most patients achieving normal respiratory function postoperatively.8

Learning points
