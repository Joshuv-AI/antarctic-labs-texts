A rare encounter - Unveiling the mysteries of biliary hamartoma: A case report
Authors: Deepika C.A., Poornachandra Thejeswi, Shivananda Prabhu, Jyothi Kini
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
IntroductionBiliary hamartomas are rare congenital development anomaly of bile ducts, which are detected incidentally. They often present as multiple lesions on liver surface which resembles metastatic lesions. We report a case of acute calculous cholecystitis ultimately diagnosed to have asymptomatic multiple biliary hamartomas.Case descriptionA 42-year-old male with no co-morbidities presented with pain in upper abdomen associated with fever and vomiting. Contrast enhanced computed tomography (CECT) of abdomen showed acute calculous cholecystitis, hepatomegaly with fatty changes in liver. On laparoscopy the liver was found to have grey-white nodular lesions of about 0.5 cm in diameter scattered on the surface of both the lobes. One of the lesion was biopsied along with cholecystectomy.DiscussionBiliary hamartoma commonly referred to as “von Meyenburg complexes” are uncommon lesions found in the liver which are usually asymptomatic. In this case the patient presented with symptoms of acute cholecystitis but the biopsy report from liver lesions proved to be benign biliary hamartoma which on initial impression looked like multiple liver secondaries.ConclusionWe have described a case of an adult with multiple biliary hamartoma which was an incidental finding. Biliary hamartoma is a rare entity which can sometime mimic metastasis in the liver. Thus, histopathological confirmation is essential before planning any further treatment.

Introduction

One of the uncommon causes of a benign abnormality of the intrahepatic bile ducts is biliary hamartoma. Other names for it include biliary micro hamartoma, multiple bile duct hamartoma, and von Meyenburg complex [1]. The prevalence of multiple biliary hamartoma [MBH] is ~3 % at autopsy [2], however, prevalence on imaging is <1 % as most hamartomas are <5 mm in size and often not detected. There have only been 17 examples reported in the literature to date, making this occurrence extremely uncommon [3]. MBH is reportedly three times more common in women than men [4]. Although its aetiology is uncertain, it is commonly linked to polycystic liver disease and congenital hepatic fibrosis.

Small groups of mildly dilated bile ducts encircled by fibrous tissue are known as bile duct hamartomas. Their communication with the biliary tree is non-existent. A microcystic network of biliary spaces, potentially containing bile, makes up the hamartomas. These lesions might manifest as single changes or as many lesions that affect both liver lobes at the same time [5]. When viewed macroscopically, they resemble greyish-white nodules that are dispersed throughout the liver parenchyma and range in diameter from 0.1 to 1.5 cm. Histologically characterised by a dense fibro collagenous stroma around a focal disorder of dilated, convoluted bile ducts. Few case reports suggest alteration in liver function on the long run and risk of malignant transformation also have been documented. The treatment of such patients remains a clinical challenge because of the small number of documented instances and the absence of recognised guidelines.

Most people won't have any symptoms, and the condition is discovered by accident. The disease has no specific clinical symptoms or signs. Distinguishing it from other potential causes of numerous liver lesions, such metastasis, is crucial. We would like to describe an incidental diagnosis of biliary hamartoma in a patient who had acute calculous cholecystitis. This case report has been described corresponding to the SCARE criteria [6].

Case description

A 42 yrs. old male came with history of pain in right hypochondrium for 1 week associated with fever and vomiting. On presentation his vital signs were stable. Clinically there was no jaundice. On physical examination there was tenderness in the right hypochondrium with rest of the abdomen being normal. Systemic examination was unremarkable. Haematological examination showed raised leucocyte counts, erythrocyte sedimentation rate and C-reactive protein were notably increased. And tests for Malaria, IgM Dengue and Leptospirosis were negative.

Liver function test showed no raise in bilirubin or enzyme levels. Alkaline phosphatase and gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT) test were in normal limits. Serological tests for hepatitis B and hepatitis C were negative. As there was no suspicion of malignancy, tumour markers were not checked preoperatively. An abdominal contrast enhanced computed tomography [CECT] scan demonstrated distended gall bladder with hypodense calculi in the neck, largest 13 × 8 mm, wall thickening of 7 mm with rim of peri-cholecystic fluid and adjoining fat stranding. The impression on the imaging was acute calculus cholecystitis and hepatomegaly with fatty changes in liver (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Showing features of acute calculus cholecystitis and hepatomegaly with fatty changes in liver.Fig. 1

Therapeutic intervention

In view of CECT findings and clinical presentation, the patient was taken up for laparoscopic cholecystectomy under general anaesthesia. Intra op findings on laparoscopy were, loss of anatomical differentiation between liver bed and gall bladder margin. Gall bladder was filled with frank pus which was aspirated and sent for culture sensitivity. Liver surface was found to be studded with multiple sub centimetric greyish white lesions involving both lobes (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Intra Op image showing greyish white lesions involving surface of both lobes of the liver.Fig. 2

Intra Op image showing greyish white lesions involving surface of both lobes of the liver.

Due to difficulty in dissection, inability to delineate triangle of safety due to extensive adhesions and loss of plain, decision was taken to convert to open cholecystectomy. Cholecystectomy was done through right subcoastal incision accompanied with biopsy of liver lesion and drain placement.

Pathology

Microscopic examination of liver lesion biopsy section showed medium to small sized ductules. Many of them dilated and irregular, embedded in a dense collagenous stroma. The ductules were lined with small cuboidal to flattened cells with round to oval nuclei. Some of the ducts showed inspissated bile in the lumen. Adjacent liver parenchyma showed regenerative hepatocytes and periportal chronic inflammatory infiltrates (Fig. 3).Fig. 3Dilated bile ductules lined with cuboidal epithelium, embedded in a dense collagenous stroma (Hematoxylin and Eosin).Fig. 3

Dilated bile ductules lined with cuboidal epithelium, embedded in a dense collagenous stroma (Hematoxylin and Eosin).

Gall bladder sections showed ulceration of the lining epithelium of the gall bladder wall with dense inflammatory infiltrates and granulation tissue. The inflammation was seen extending into serosal layer suggestive of acute cholecystitis.

On immunohistochemistry examination CK7, CK19 was positive in bile ductules (Fig. 4).Fig. 4Immunohistochemistry examination demonstrating CK7, CK19 being positive in bile ductules.Fig. 4

CK 20 was negative in bile ductules. Ki67 was <10 %.

Hence the diagnosis of bile duct hamartoma [von Meyenburg complex] was made.

Discussion

Von Meyenburg originally reported bile-duct hamartomas, commonly referred to as “von Meyenburg complexes,” as uncommon hepatic lesions in 1918 [7]. Based on 875 autopsy series, the frequency is estimated to be 0.69 %; nevertheless, there have been additional reports of an autopsy rate as high as 3 % [2]. Biliary hamartomas are composed of small, disorganised clusters of dilated cystic bile ducts lined by a single layer of cuboidal cells and surrounded by an abundant fibro collagenous stroma [1,[7], [8], [9]]. They come from a congenital bile duct abnormality caused by the embryonic ductal plate remodelling that either persists or does not occur throughout ontogenesis [10,11]. It is believed that they originate from remains of the embryonic bile duct that have not succeeded in involute [1]. They may converse with the biliary tree, but they don't usually.

Bile duct hamartomas are clinically asymptomatic and are found incidentally during laparotomies and autopsies. They usually present as multiple and small well-circumscribed nodules ranging from 1 mm to 1.5 cm on imaging studies and in some cases as a single large mass [12]. In ultrasound biliary hamartomas may present as either hypo echoic or anechoic small nodules; in rare cases they have a hyper echoic appearance. CECT shows hypodense small hepatic nodules. On MRI [Magnetic Resonance Imaging] the lesions are hypo intense on T1-weighted images and markedly hyper intense on T2-weighted images [2,13]. The appearance is often referred as “starry night” [14].

In the case reported by D. Zeng, Y. Wan the patient had reported with pain abdomen and the MRI imaging had shown typical appearance of biliary hamartoma. The patient did not receive any specific treatment and the imaging findings remained same during follow up. They also concluded that liver transplantation is advised for certain patients with diffuse biliary hamartoma because they frequently experience recurrent problems in liver function [14]. However, the clinical presentation of bile duct hamartomas can sometime mimic metastatic disease to the liver. Despite having a normal MRI image, pathological confirmation is frequently needed for a definitive diagnosis because preoperative diagnosis might be challenging.

Ultrasonography, computed tomography, and magnetic resonance imaging can all be used to identify the condition. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) is one method that is particularly useful in this regard, as it shows several irregularly shaped lesions with a diameter of approximately 10 mm. When liver cystic lesions mimic liver metastasis on imaging the diagnosis might be challenging. It is advised in these situations to get a liver biopsy followed by a histological assessment. Notably, the blood analysis has limited specificity and is less effective due to inconsistent results, hence may not be an ideal tool for diagnosis and screening purpose.

Biliary hamartomas fall under the umbrella term “fibrocystic liver diseases” and other conditions included are choledochal cysts, polycystic liver disease, Caroli's disease, and congenital liver fibrosis which can be considered as differential diagnosis for biliary hamartoma. In Caroli's disease, there will an established communication between the cystic lesion with biliary tree in contrast with polycystic liver disease and biliary hamartoma. To diagnose a polycystic liver disease there must be >20 cystic lesions with size being larger compared to hamartomas and MR cholangiography will be the investigation of choice. But in case of polycystic liver disease a thorough family history will be essential.

When it comes to managing patients with biliary hamartoma, the approach can range from routine follow-up in cases where the disease is asymptomatic to active treatment in cases where the disease is symptomatic or has a complicated course such as administration of ursodeoxycholic acid and/or antibiotics [11].

In our instance, these lesions were only visible during a laparoscopy and were not identified by radiological imaging. Bile duct hamartomas are benign asymptomatic lesions without any clinical significance; however, rare incident of transformation into cholangio-carcinoma has been reported [[15], [16], [17]]. In some studies the alteration in liver function also been reported [11].

Conclusion

In this case we found biliary hamartoma as an incidental finding. This condition being a benign liver pathology sometimes may clinically mimic metastatic cholangio-carcinoma or liver secondaries indicating the importance of pathological confirmation of malignancy before considering treatment of any metastatic cancer.
