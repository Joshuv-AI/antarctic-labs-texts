Management of iatrogenic ascending aortic dissection after percutaneous intervention of chronic total occlusion
Authors: Ahmad Safdar, Laura Young, Jaikirshan Khatri
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study highlights the prevalence and conservative management strategies of iatrogenic ascending aortic dissection after percutaneous intervention (PCI) of chronic total occlusion (CTO).MethodsRetrospective analysis on patients who underwent CTO PCI from one medical center from 2020 to 2022 was performed by two operators. Data was obtained from the Prospective Global Registry for the Study of CTO database.ResultsOut of 318 patients, there were six reported cases of iatrogenic ascending aortic dissection. The causes of dissection included guiding catheter trauma, antegrade injection, two retrograde injection, and two cases of balloon rupture after lithotripsy. IVUS‐guided stenting of the coronary back to the origin to seal the entry of the dissection flap occurred in two patients. All patients had a conservative management approach. No patient had to undergo urgent surgery.ConclusionWhile the incidence remains rare, complications, including aortic dissection, are more frequent in CTO PCI as compared to non‐CTO PCI. IVUS can be used to complete coronary intervention so long as the patient remains clinically and hemodynamically stable. The outcomes of patients who experience iatrogenic aortic dissection during CTO PCI with stable coronary disease seem to be better than in patients with acute coronary syndrome.

INTRODUCTION

Percutaneous intervention (PCI) is an effective treatment of angina in patients who suffer symptoms that are refractory to conservative medical therapy. The benefits of PCI must be carefully weighed against the risk of a peri‐procedural complication. In the case of chronic total occlusion (CTO) PCI, there is an increased risk of peri‐procedural complications compared with patent vessel intervention. While not unique to CTO PCI, ascending aortic dissection risk may be increased in CTO PCI due to the requirement for multiple and potentially more aggressive guiding catheters. The inciting event can either be a direct injury of the sinus of Valsalva with the guiding catheter or dissection in the coronary artery that then extends retrogradely into the aorta. There is no established management strategy. This case‐series aims to highlight the prevalence of ascending aortic dissection after CTO PCI and the management strategies used when this complication arose.

METHODS

Study design

This study was a retrospective analysis on patients who underwent CTO PCI from one medical center from 2020 to 2022 performed by two operators. Data were obtained from the Prospective Global Registry for the Study of CTO (PROGRESS‐CTO) database. Out of 318 patients, there were six reported cases of iatrogenic ascending aortic dissection.

Definitions with inclusion criteria

Coronary CTO is defined as a complete blockage of a coronary artery with thrombolysis in myocardial infarction (TIMI) 0 flow for at least 3 months. CTO PCI was only offered to patients after confirmation of myocardial viability with stress echocardiography, single‐photon emission CT, or cardiac MRI. Patients were all started on conservative therapy with medications and lifestyle modifications, and CTO was only offered to patients with refractory angina. While there are several scoring systems, the PROGRESS‐CTO Intervention score was used. The four angiographic characteristics it utilizes are proximal cap ambiguity, circumflex coronary artery CTO, moderate/severe proximal vessel tortuosity, and the absence of interventional collaterals to predict technical success. Successful CTO PCI was defined as TIMI 3 flow and 0% residual stenosis.

Procedure

Each patient had extensive discussions with the operators before scheduling CTO PCI. Table 1 includes patient characteristics. All cases complicated by iatrogenic aortic dissection were elective procedures. Patients were all pretreated with unfractioned heparin with a goal ACT of >250 s. The guiding catheter, CTO devices, CTO approach and management of aortic dissection were all determined by the operator.

RESULTS

Prevalence and patient characteristics

Out of 318 patients who underwent CTO PCI with two different operators, six patients suffered iatrogenic aortic dissection. The majority of patients (83%, 5/6) were male. Every patient had a medical history of hypertension, type 2 diabetes, and previous CABG. The average left ventricular ejection fraction was 58% before the procedure. Canadian Cardiovascular Society grading of angina (CCS score) for each patient was class II or greater, meaning all patients experienced angina with moderate exertion. Unsuccessful prior CTO PCI attempts were noted in 66% (4/6) of patients. CTO PCI attempts were made on the left circumflex artery in three patients, the right coronary artery (RCA) in two patients, and the left anterior descending artery in one patient.

Most dissections (66%, 4/6) began at the left coronary sinus, with 1 starting at the right coronary sinus and 1 at the sinotubular junction. Based on Dunning's classification, there was an equal distribution (2/6) of cases of the three types of aortic dissection. Case 6 was the only patient with retrograde extension to the aortic arch. The presumed cause of dissection was as follows: guiding catheter trauma, antegrade injection, two retrograde injections, and two cases of balloon rupture after lithotripsy. Cases 2 and 3 underwent IVUS‐guided stenting of the coronary back to the origin to seal the entry of the dissection flap, as the CTO had already been crossed with a guidewire. Cardiothoracic surgery (CTS) was consulted in 4/6 (66%) of cases. Computed tomography angiography (CTA) was performed on four of the six patients, while two underwent echocardiograms. All patients (4/6) who had CTA imaging were admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) for close monitoring. All patients had a conservative management approach. Impulse control with beta blockers and antihypertensives was used in each case. Every patient remained hemodynamically stable and chest pain free. One patient had an episode of non‐sustained ventricular tachycardia that was managed by increasing the dosage of the beta blocker. No patient had to undergo urgent surgery. Case 5 was admitted for the longest time as the patient elected for CABG 1 week after failed CTO PCI. The median length of stay was 5 days (Table 2).

DISCUSSION

This case series highlights the conservative management approach taken by two operators for iatrogenic aortic dissections after CTO PCI. While the incidence remains rare, complications including aortic dissection are more frequent in CTO PCI as compared to non‐CTO PCI. In a single‐center experience of 336 consecutive CTO procedures, 18 cases of iatrogenic aortic dissection were recorded with an incidence of 1.8%. 1 Additionally, a recent single‐operator case series showed that among 956 CTO PCI cases, iatrogenic aortic dissection occurred in 8 patients for an overall frequency of 0.83%. 2 In comparison, Dunning et al. found 9 cases out of 43,143 cardiac catheterizations that had coronary artery‐aortic dissections for an overall incidence of 0.02%. 3

Advanced atherosclerosis is likely the leading cause of increased incidence of aortic dissection in CTO PCI compared to non‐CTO PCI. Heavy calcified arteries are by nature more prone to dissection as the operator must be more aggressive in guiding catheter manipulation to deliver stents or balloons. 4 Particularly with retrograde crossing, a forceful contrast injection into the false lumen has been thought to be a mechanism for aortic dissection. 5 Previous studies have highlighted that the retrograde approach has been associated with higher risk of aortic dissection compared to antegrade approach. Shorrock et al. retrospective analysis found that patients with aortocoronary dissection were more likely to undergo attempts with the retrograde approach (67% vs. 25%, p = 0.036). In our study, 4/6 (66%) of patients had dissection after crossing attempts with the antegrade approach. 1 It is important to note that the retrograde approach is usually used after antegrade crossing failure, as occurred in 2/6 of our patients who had iatrogenic aortic dissection.

While the RCA sinus is known to be more prone to aortic dissection, 4/6 of our patients had dissection that started at the LCA sinus. It is thought that the increased incidence of RCA sinus dissection is due to the hemodynamic force vector is directed to the right side convexity of the ascending aorta. 3

The outcomes of patients who experience iatrogenic aortic dissection during CTO PCI with stable coronary disease seem to be better than in patients with acute coronary syndrome, likely due to increased antithrombotic agents used in patients with acute presentations. 3 Protamine was administered to our patients who were on therapeutic anticoagulation after the dissection was noted. All patients in our study remained stable after the procedure with the continuation of antiplatelet agents.

The aim of this study is to highlight the management of iatrogenic aortic dissection. Once a dissection is suspected, it is imperative to avoid further guide catheter injections to mitigate the risk of hydraulic expansion of the dissection. Photo C shows the CTA confirming type A aortic dissection limited to root and proximal ascending aorta. Patients should have formal imaging with either transesophageal echocardiogram or CTA. Before formal imaging, all patients in the study had an immediate bedside echocardiogram performed to assess for pericardial effusions and left ventricular dysfunction. CTS was consulted in 4/6 patients (66%), and the surgical team recommended CTA for all patients, which revealed the extent of the dissection. The other two patients had formal transesophageal echocardiograms that showed stable dissection. Urgent surgical intervention is normally indicated when the dissection extends more than 40 mm, in acute aortic valve insufficiency or hemopericardium. 3 Fortunately, none of our patients required acute surgery. Follow‐up imaging prior to before discharge was obtained in all patients who had CTS involvement. All cases were managed conservatively with respect to the aortic dissection. A conservative management approach focusing on serial imaging, aggressive impulse control and close follow‐up proved to be efficacious. There is an emerging consensus that patients with Dunning type I and II iatrogenic aortic dissection can be managed conservatively. This idea stems from the Registry on Aortic Iatrogenic Dissection, which supports conservative management of IAD. 6 However, the best management for patients with Dunning type III aortic dissection is unclear. Our study highlights and supports conservative management for type III aortic dissections, even with retrograde extension to the aortic arch, which can be efficacious. Case 5's aortic dissection was managed conservatively and remained stable. The reason for the 19‐day hospitalization was that the patient elected to undergo coronary artery bypass grafting for failed PCI. All other patients had outpatient follow‐up and underwent successful CTO PCI in the future. The decrease in mortality and incidence of iatrogenic aortic dissection is likely due to improved recognition of the complication in patients undergoing CTO PCI and advancements in CTO PCI procedures. 7 Moreover, the reduction in mortality may be attributed to interventionalists and cardiac surgeons increasingly favoring a more conservative approach in the management of iatrogenic aortic dissections.

Limitations include retrospective data collection that subjects our study to incomplete or missing data, omitting long‐term outcomes of patients and a small sample size from only two operators. Our study highlights the importance of recognizing iatrogenic aortic dissection as a possible complication to CTO PCI. A conservative management approach appeared to be successful with our patient population.
