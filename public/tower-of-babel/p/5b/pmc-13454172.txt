Remnant cholesterol is independently associated with liver fibrosis in metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease patients and rats
Authors: Ruifang Li, Rong Ren, Guifeng Li, Ping Han, Lili Zhao, Jia Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Few studies have explored the relationship between remnant cholesterol (RC) and liver fibrosis in metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD). Therefore, this study aims to explore the association between RC levels and liver fibrosis in both biopsy-proven MAFLD population and Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats. This current study included 280 participants and 15 SD rats. For MAFLD population, all participants underwent liver biopsy and blood tests. Logistic regression analysis was used to evaluate the association between RC and liver fibrosis and the diagnostic capability of RC was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. For MAFLD rats, pathological and hematological analysis were used to study the association between RC and liver fibrosis. In the MAFLD population, RC remained significantly associated with liver fibrosis after adjustment for confounding factors (OR:1.21, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.09–1.49, p < 0.001). In addition, RC and liver fibrosis remained significantly associated with liver fibrosis when triglycerides (TGs) levels were less than 1.7 mmol/L (OR: 1.13, 95% CI: 1.03–1.56, p = 0.006), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) levels were less than 3.4 mmol/L (OR: 1.18, 95% CI: 1.08–1.43, p < 0.001), or HDL-C (high-density lipoprotein cholesterol) levels were more than 1.0 mmol/L (OR: 1.20, 95% CI: 1.08–1.47, p < 0.001). In the MAFLD rats, rats with fibrosis exhibited higher RC levels (p < 0.001) and elevated RC was significantly correlated with liver fibrosis (r = 0.819, p < 0.001). Higher RC level is significantly correlated with liver fibrosis in the MAFLD population and rats.

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) is one of the most prevalent chronic liver diseases, and is characterized by excessive lipid accumulation within the liver1–3. The worldwide incidence and prevalence rates of MAFLD are on a significant rise, as of 2019, approximately 25% of the general population were reported to have MAFLD4,5.In addition, if the fatty acid persists within hepatocytes, de novo lipogenesis may be stimulated, resulting in an increasing risk of hepatic fibrosis, that may progress to hepatocellular carcinoma or even liver failure6,7. However, there still less beneficial interventions for preventing hepatic fibrosis, thus, extensive exploration of the risk factors related to MAFLD-related fibrosis is urgently needed.

In recent years, studies have focused on the role of dyslipidemia, especially cholesterol, in the progression of MAFLD8. Studies have confirmed that excessive accumulation of cholesterol within the liver contributes to cellular toxicity and proinflammatory cascade, including necrosis, inflammation, and fibrosis in patients with MASH5. Results from a cohort study showed that patients with elevated low-density lipoprotein (LDL-C) levels had higher risk of the appetence of MAFLD9–11. In addition, clinical studies have confirmed that the patients with lipid abnormalities remains higher risk of MAFLD even after effective regulation on serum levels of high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), LDL-C and triglycerides (TG)12,13.

Therefore, remnant cholesterol (RC), as an unconventional lipid, has garnered the attention on MAFLD field. RC is the metabolic remnants of triglyceride-rich lipoproteins (TGRLs), it is defined as the metabolic remnants including chylomicrons, very-low-density lipoproteins (VLDLs), and intermediate-density lipoproteins (IDLs)14. The relationship between RC and the incidence of cardiovascular disease (CVD) has been extensively explored15–17. Although researchers on role of RC in metabolic-related diseases were limited, there were still studies reported that higher level of RC resulted in increased risk of developing MAFLD18.In addition, the study conducted by Wang Y has also reported a positive link between RC and liver stiffness19. However, the association between RC and liver fibrosis in biopsy-confirmed MAFLD population have not yet been explored. Hence, this current cross-sectional study is designed to investigate the association between RC and liver fibrosis in biopsy-confirmed MAFLD population.

Methods

MAFLD population

The data of the current study were obtained from the Department of Hepatology, Tianjin Second People’s Hospital. Adult MAFLD patients who underwent liver biopsy from January 2017 to December 2021 were enrolled in this study. The exclusion criteria were listed as follows: (i) alcohol consumption of more than 210 g/week for men or more than 140 g/week for women; (ii) a history of viral hepatitis; (iii) other liver diseases (Wilson’s disease, autoimmune liver diseases, primary biliary cholangitis, and drug-induced liver injury) and (iv) severe liver disease. Finally, 280 biopsy-proven MAFLD patients were included in this study.

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the local hospital, and all participants provided written consent prior to the study. The IRB approval number is No. [2021]55. All the methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations, and all the methods are reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

Data collection

Anthropometric data, history of diabetes, smoking and drinking habits were obtained from medical record. The height, weight, and waist circumference (WC) were measured and recorded. Blood samples were collected after fasting for 8–10 h. Serum levels of aspartate transferase (AST), alanine transferase (ALT), glutamine transferase (GGT), TG, total cholesterol (TC), LDL-C, and HDL-C were measured using an autoanalyzer (Hitachi, Ltd, Tokyo, Japan). RC was calculated as TC subtracted from HDL-C and LDL-C; body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by the square of height (m2).

Animals and model establishment

Eight-week-old male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats were obtained from Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co. Ltd. (Beijing, China). All the rats were housed under pathogen-free animal conditions. The rats were divided into 3 groups: the heathy control group, the MASH with fibrosis group and the MASH without fibrosis group. All of the rats were allowed free access to water and rodent food. The animal experiments were approved by the Animal Experimentation Ethics Committee of Nankai University, and the animal study approval number is No. 2021-SYDWLL-000338.

To establish the NASH model, SD rats received L-amino acid-defined high-fat diet (CDAHFD) (Cat# A06071302; Research Diets, New Brunswick, NJ) for a total of 8 or 12 weeks20. In the heathy controls, rats were fed with normal chow diet (Cat# 38057, Purina Lab, Missouri, USA). The rats were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 2% pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg). After sacrifice, the blood and liver of rats were collected for further analysis.

Histological assessments

The rats were sacrificed with isoflurane. Liver samples were firstly fixed in 4% neutral formalin solution for 24 h and then embedded in paraffin. To detect the collagen deposition in livers, liver samples were cut into slices and were stained with Masson’s trichrome and Sirius red. Images were obtained through optical microscope (Olympus, Japan). Pathology figures and liver fibrosis stages were evaluated by two liver pathologists according to the METAVIR scoring system21.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 24.0 (IBM, NY, USA), MedCalc 20 (MedCalc Software, Ostend, Belgium), as well as GraphPad Prism 9.4 (GraphPad Prism Software Inc., San Diego, CA) were used for statistical analysis. Continuous variables were expressed as median (interquartile range) and categorical variables were presented as numerically (proportionally). Differences between continuous variables and categorical variables were assessed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) test and the chi-square test, respectively. The relationship between RC and liver fibrosis was assessed by Spearman’s correlation coefficient test or logistic regression analyses. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were also constructed to determine the optimal cutoff values of the RC for predicting liver fibrosis. All the statistical tests were repeated three times and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical characteristics of the study population

The present study included 280 participants (Table 1), with 144 (51.4%) males and 136 (48.6%) females, with a median age of 46 years (31, 56). Compared with the MAFLD patients without fibrosis, MAFLD patients with fibrosis were older, with increased levels of RC, LDL-C, TC, TG, ALT, and AST levels and increased BMI, and WC but decreased serum level of HDL-C levels. Moreover, male sex, smoking, and diabetes were more common in MAFLD patients with fibrosis than in MAFLD patients without fibrosis.

The relationships between liver fibrosis and RC levels were examined by logistic regression analysis (Table 3). In the total participants, RC exhibited a significant adjusted OR in Models 1–3. After all potential confounders, including sex, age, diabetes status, smoking status, drinking status, BMI, WC, ALT level, AST level, and GGT level, were adjusted in Model 3, RC remained significantly associated with diabetes (T2: OR:1.14, 95% CI: 1.06–1.32, p < 0.001; T3: OR: 1.21, 95% CI: 1.09– 1.49, p < 0.001). In the female subgroup, RC was also found significantly associated with hepatic fibrosis in Model 3 after all confounders were adjusted (T2: OR:1.14, 95% CI: 1.04–1.50, p = 0.002; T3: OR: 1.19, 95% CI: 1.05– 1.67, p = 0.01). In the male subgroup, RC remained significantly associated with fibrosis in Model 3 (T2: OR:1.09, 95% CI: 1.03–1.33, p < 0.001; T3: OR: 1.18, 95% CI: 1.05– 1.66, p = 0.009).

Diagnostic performance of RC using the ROC curve

The ROC curve showed that the best cutoff value of RC level was 0.96 mol/L, with 58.21% sensitivity and 81.01% specificity for the diagnosis of liver fibrosis (area under the curve [AUC] = 0.723, 95% CI 0.667-0.775; Figure 1).

Pathological features and serum analysis of MAFLD-related fibrotic rats

To induce MASH-related hepatic fibrosis, rats were received CDAHFD for 8 or 12 weeks to accelerate the progression from hepatic steatosis to MASH with fibrosis. The results of Masson and Sirius Red staining showed that there was no hepatic fibrosis in rats only treated with CDAHFD for 8 weeks, but there was obvious fibrosis in rats treated with CDAHFD for 12 weeks (Figure 2A). Serum levels of ALT and AST were slightly raised in rats treated with CDAHFD for 8 weeks, and significantly increased in rats treated with CDAHFD for 12 weeks (Figure 2B, C). Taken together, our results demonstrated that MASH-related hepatic fibrosis rats were successfully induced through CDAHFD.

Relationships between TC, HDL-C, LDL-C, RC levels, and liver fibrosis in rats

Spearman’s correlation coefficient test was used to assess the relationships between TC, HDL-C, LDL-C, RC levels and liver fibrosis in SD rats (Table 5). Discussion

The main finding of the current study was that serum level of RC was significantly associated with liver fibrosis in the biopsy-confirmed MAFLD population and in rats. In addition, we also reported that RC was remained associated with fibrosis even when serum levels of TG, LDL-C, and HDL-C levels were controlled within the normal range. Moreover, MAFLD patients with elevated RC levels were more likely to develop liver fibrosis, especially males. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first cross-sectional study to explore the relationship between RC and hepatic fibrosis in a biopsy-proven MAFLD cohort as well as in SD rats.

RC is considered as a risk factor for CVD independent of conventional LDL-C levels22. Studies on the relationship between RC and MAFLD are scarce and limited. Recently, several studies have suggested that RC may be a crucial determinant of MAFLD onset. The results from a retrospective study revealed that RC is a risk factor that is independently correlated with the occurrence of MAFLD23. However, their study did not exclude the effects of viral hepatitis and autoimmune liver diseases, which may influence the accuracy of the results. In addition, a study using data from NHANES III also revealed that RC was a risk factor for the development of MAFLD and predicted all-cause, cardiovascular, and cancer-related mortality24, however they did not consider alcohol consumption, which is a risk factor for fatty liver disease. A study of 14,251 patients revealed that compared with conventional lipid profiles, the RC level could predict NAFLD in the general population and exhibited superior diagnostic performance in males25. Justin Chin et al. analyzed 2900 Caucasian women and found a positive association between RC levels and the severity of liver fat26. Huang et al. conducted a cross-sectional study and found that MAFLD patients with higher levels of serum RC levels were more likely to have severe hepatic steatosis27. All of these above studies have a common limitation for the lack of biopsy pathologic features, which has been regarded as the “gold standard” for MAFLD diagnosis. In addition, these previous studies still failed to explore the relationship between the RC level and MAFLD-related hepatic fibrosis. Nevertheless, the current study analyzed the association between RC and the occurrence of hepatic fibrosis in a biopsy-proven MAFLD population, hence, the findings of this prevention study were more accurate.

In this study, we also reported that the RC level was significantly associated with liver fibrosis when TG, LDL-C, or HDL-C serum levels were controlled within the appropriate range according to guidelines. Atherogenic dyslipidemia28, characterized by hypertriglyceridemia and elevated levels of LDL-C and decreased levels of HDL-C, is thought to predict metabolic disorders, including NAFLD, obesity and diabetes29–31. Notably, in this study, the association between the RC level and liver fibrosis remained significant when HDL-C serum levels were greater than 1.0 mmol/L or TG and LDL-C serum levels were less than 1.7 mmol/L and 3.4 mmol/L, respectively. Previous studies have reported that increased RC concentrations are associated with increased atherosclerosis risk even in individuals with normal TG levels32,33. It has been confirmed that no causality exists between low HDL-C levels and the risk of atherosclerosis; however, decreased HDL-C levels are a strong indicator of increased RC34. After entering the bloodstream, HDL particles can penetrate the intima and media of vessels and leave the vascular wall35, whereas RC might become trapped in the intima and transform into foam cells, resulting in inflammation35. Importantly, TGRL accelerates the remodeling of HDL-C into smaller, cholesterol-poor particles without exerting protective effects on atherosclerosis28. As indicated by our results, increased serum RC levels and the risk of liver fibrosis could not be underestimated in MAFLD, even if TG levels are well controlled within the normal range.

The hypothesis that RC may promote MAFLD progression can be explained by the inflammatory response caused by disordered lipid metabolism. Varbo et al. reported that increased LDL-C levels were associated with atherosclerosis but not inflammation, whereas elevated RC levels could result in low-grade inflammation as well as ischemic heart disease33. In addition, RC level was found to be associated not only with a higher risk of CVD but also with an increased incidence of systemic inflammation36. Lipoprotein lipase (LPL) mediates the hydrolysis of peripheral TG to produce triglyceride-rich lipoproteins (TGRLs), and the majority of remnant TGRLs ultimately reenter the liver and are taken up by hepatocytes. Hepatocytes digest and endocytose these remnants, allowing the resecretion of smaller lipids as VLDLs, which are subsequently released into the bloodstream. MASH occurs because the synthesis or import of lipids in hepatocytes surpasses their export or degradation37–39. Furthermore, these small lipids, including VLDL, may be absorbed by macrophage scavenger receptors without limit, exhibit cytotoxic and proinflammatory properties within macrophages and thereby promote the transformation of macrophages into foam cells14,40,41. Given that current interventions inadequately adjust the TG concentration to optimal levels, exploring the influence of RC on the occurrence and progression of MAFLD not only improves the accuracy of MAFLD diagnosis, but also sets the stage for targeted interventions aimed at enhancing fat metabolism.

The present study observed that elevated RC is significantly associated with liver fibrosis in the MAFLD population, even when TG, LDL-C, or HDL‐C was at the appropriate level. Population with MAFLD are at higher risk of liver fibrosis when combined with elevated RC levels. The current study’s findings expand RC’s application and provide new approaches to preventing liver fibrosis in MAFLD patients. Calculating RC is an affordable method. It is necessary to measure RC along with monitoring other lipid markers in MAFLD patients, even if traditional lipid components are at appropriate levels.

This study benefited from the use of liver biopsy-based samples, ensuring the reliability of the results. Moreover, this study also adjusted for potential confounders and constructed several statistical models, enhancing the credibility of the conclusions. Furthermore, we included SD rats in this study to verify the reliability of the conclusions. However, this study has several limitations. First, this was a cross-sectional study; thus, we could determine only a correlational relationship between the RC level and hepatic fibrosis. In addition, we were unable to draw conclusions on the causal relationship between them. Second, this study lacked data on dietary, physical exercise, and diabetes-related serum markers, such as insulin, in the MAFLD population, which are very important factors for MAFLD progression.

Conclusions

The results of the current cross-sectional study revealed that elevated RC levels are significantly associated with liver fibrosis in a biopsy-confirmed MAFLD cohort and in rats. The findings of the current study expand the application of RC and provide new information for preventing liver fibrosis in populations with MAFLD. It is important to calculate the RC level along with monitoring other lipid compounds in the MAFLD population.
