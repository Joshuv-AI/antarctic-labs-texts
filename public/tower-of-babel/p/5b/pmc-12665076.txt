JA Regulates Caffeine Biosynthesis in Tea Leaf for Resistance Against Fungal Infection and Antagonises With GA to Balance the Defence–Growth Trade‐Off via CsDELLA‐JAZ‐MYC2‐MYB184‐TCS1 Module
Authors: Zhili Ye, Ziqing Zheng, Jinsong Wang, Qinyu Zhong, Xiaoyu Mu, Zhoufang Yuan, Yin Huang, Zainab Ghazala, Hao Zuo, Yaling Li, Xiaoyu Liang, Yujia Qi, Xiongyuan Si, Yanrui Zhang, Enhua Xia, Jian Zhao, Penghui Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCaffeine is a defensive alkaloid primarily accumulated in tea leaves to defend against pathogens. But the regulatory mechanism for caffeine biosynthesis in response to fungal infection and the trade‐off between specialised metabolite and plant growth remain elusive. Here, we report that jasmonic acid (JA)‐regulated CsMYB184 is the key for caffeine biosynthesis in tea leaves. Exogenous MeJA promoted caffeine biosynthesis in tea leaves and enhanced the resistance against fungal pathogens. JASMONATE ZIM‐domain proteins (JAZs) interacted with CsMYB184 to repress CsMYB184 transactivation of TCS1. Meanwhile, JA effector CsMYC2 directly bound to and activated CsMYB184 transcription, but inhibited plant growth by directly modulating GA inactivation through gibberellin 2‐oxidase 4 (CsGA2ox4). By contrast, gibberellins (GA) stimulated tea plant growth and inhibited caffeine biosynthesis, rendering tea plants more susceptible to fungal pathogens, via competitive DELLA proteins interacting with JAZs to relieve JAZ inhibition of CsMYB184. CsDELLA‐JAZ‐MYC2‐MYB184‐TCS1 formed an activation–repression loop to regulate developmental and hormonal control of caffeine biosynthesis in response to fungal pathogens, which balance the tea plant growth and defence response trade‐offs. The study reveals JA‐GA antagonistic regulation of caffeine biosynthesis as one of the innate immunity defences, meanwhile balancing the growth and defence of tea plants via crosstalk between JA and GA signalling.Keywords: biotic stress, caffeine, Camellia sinensis , phytohormone crosstalk, resistance, transcriptional regulation

Introduction

Tea has become the second most popular nonalcoholic beverage consumed after water, due to containing rich metabolites with multiple health benefits, such as caffeine (Yang and Hong 2013). Those compounds not only contribute to tea flavour and health functions (Zhang et al. 2020), but also play crucial roles in tea plant interactions with environmental stresses (Chen, Lin, et al. 2023; Zeng et al. 2021). While tea plants are also often exposed to various biotic and abiotic stresses, they have evolved various defence mechanisms, such as densely spaced trichomes to cover the apical buds and young leaves, thick circular waxes deposited on leaf surfaces and various specialised metabolites accumulated in the young tissues to survive the adversary environments (Li, Fu, et al. 2022; Li et al. 2020). In particular, the apical buds and young leaves and stems of tea plants can synthesise a large number of chemically diverse defensive compounds to build up the chemical defence and innate immune system. Especially, tea characteristic secondary metabolites, such as caffeine, catechins and theanine, are not only evolved to defend against the biotic and abiotic stresses, but also contribute to various tea flavours and health functions of tea (Gu et al. 2022; Jing et al. 2024; Li et al. 2023; Zhu et al. 2019, 2022).

Caffeine, a purine alkaloid with strong neuron stimulation activity, is essential for the tea quality and health benefits, but also plays roles in plant pollination, allelopathy and defence against herbivore attack and pathogen infection (Fernandes et al. 2012; Kim et al. 2011; Wright et al. 2013; Zhou et al. 2020). In tea plants, tea caffeine synthase (TCS1) is the key N‐methyltransferase (NMT) responsible for caffeine biosynthesis (Kato et al. 2000). CsMYB184 and other transcription factors, such as CsNAC7, CsS40, CsbHLH1 and CsbHLH80, have been characterised to regulate TCS1 or NMT genes and the caffeine biosynthesis (Jin et al. 2024; Li et al. 2024; Li, Ye, et al. 2022; Ma et al. 2022; Yao et al. 2023). CsMYB184 is also involved in the regulation of both trichrome initiation and development and galloylated cis‐catechins biosynthesis in tea plant development and is a selected gene during tea domestication (Li, Fu, et al. 2022; Li et al. 2020). Although data showed that CsMYB184 is regulated by hormones such as jasmonic acid (JA), abscisic acid (ABA) and insect attack, the exact role of CsMYB184 in the regulation of caffeine biosynthesis in tea leaf defence response remains to be explored (Li, Fu, et al. 2022; Li, Ye, et al. 2022).

As an important hormone involved in the regulation of plant development and defence responses against mechanical damage, herbivorous insects and necrotrophic pathogens, JA signalling has been extensively studied in plants (Pieterse et al. 2014; Wasternack and Hause 2013). JA signalling is ubiquitous in the plant kingdom, and the JASMONATE ZIM‐domain (JAZ) family proteins are the JA signalling repressors (Chini et al. 2007; Howe et al. 2018; Thines et al. 2007). Under regular conditions with low JA/jasmonoyl‐isoleucine (JA‐Ile) levels, the stabilised JAZ proteins interact and repress the transcription regulators of JA responses, MYCs (Howe et al. 2018; Li et al. 2021). But when bioactive JAs are increased and perceived, JAZ proteins are degraded, resulting in the release of transcription factors MYCs from JAZ repression and activating downstream JA responses, such as plant development, metabolism, defences against pathogen infection and insect attack (Howe et al. 2018; Jin et al. 2023). MYC2 and paralogues serve as the effectors and master regulators of the JA signalling pathway by specifically binding to the G‐box element in the promoters of downstream genes or directly interacting with other transcription factors (Fernández‐Calvo et al. 2011; Kazan and Manners 2013). Conservatively, JA signalling, CsJAZ1 and CsMYC2 also regulate catechins biosynthesis and tea plant resistance to insect attack (Li, Zhang, et al. 2022; Lv et al. 2023; Zhu et al. 2022).

Gibberellins (GAs) primarily growth‐regulating hormones that also regulate the balanced growth and defences, through interacting with other hormones, such as JA (Hou et al. 2010; Song et al. 2014; Yang et al. 2012). DELLA repressor proteins have been identified as the major GA signalling components (Boss and Thomas 2002; Claeys et al. 2014). The stabilised DELLA proteins can competitively interact with JAZ proteins to amplify the JA signalling or with MYC2 to inhibit MYC2 sequestration by JAZ to modulate secondary metabolism (Hong et al. 2012; Hou et al. 2010; Panda et al. 2022). In Arabidopsis, DELLA could sequester JA responsive MYC2, leading to suppression of sesquiterpene synthase genes (Hong et al. 2012). In Solanum, MYC1 and MYC2 were interfered with by DELLA in steroidal glycoalkaloids biosynthesis through protein–protein interaction (Panda et al. 2022). Additionally, the JA‐associated MYC2 can directly bind to and activate the promoter of genes involved in GA catabolism, such as gibberellin 2‐oxidases (GA2oxs), to promote GA inactivation (Jin et al. 2023; Panda et al. 2022).

Although JAZ‐DELLA regulation of growth‐defence trade‐offs has been extensively described in several plants, there are fewer studies to investigate the regulation of specialised metabolites and their role in immunity by JAZ‐DELLA antagonism. Here, we found that the JA and GA signalling interplay and coordinative regulation of specialised caffeine biosynthesis in tea leaves is mediated by CsMYB184. As the key regulator of caffeine accumulation in young leaves of tea plants, CsMYB184 expression also responds to JA and GA hormones and pathogen infection. Furthermore, JAZ proteins, CsJAZ1, CsJAZ12 and CsJAZ13, were identified as the caffeine repressors and interacted with the caffeine activator CsMYB184 to repress the TCS1 expression at the post‐transcription level. Meanwhile, CsMYC2 mediated the JA signalling regulation of CsMYB184 and indirectly regulated caffeine biosynthesis and also directly modulated GA inactivation through modulating CsGA2ox4 expression to participate in growth regulation. CsJAZ12‐CsDELLA1/2 protein interaction complexes integrate JA and GA signalling to balance the growth and defence of tea plants. Our study demonstrated the mechanism about caffeine biosynthesis regulation by fungal infection. The JA and GA signalling cross‐talk regulates specialised metabolite biosynthesis in tea plants under normal growth conditions or pathogen infection.

Results

Caffeine Plays a Key Role in Colletotrichum gloeosporioides Resistance of Tea Plants

Caffeine is a well‐known defensive alkaloid, but how caffeine inhibits fungal growth and its function on fungal infection in tea cultivars remain elusive. Firstly, the role of caffeine in the resistance to pathogens was investigated. The growth of Colletotrichum gloeosporioides strain, the fungal pathogen in tea fields causing fungal anthracnose, was significantly inhibited in the presence of 1 mM caffeine (Figure 1A). The growth of fungal pathogens was almost completely repressed by 2 mM caffeine in the medium (Figure 1A). RNA‐seq data for C. gloeosporioides strains (the strains treated by 0 and 2 mM were for RNA‐seq analysis) in response to caffeine showed that a total of 2493 genes with differential expression (1227 down regulation and 1266 upregulation) were obtained (Figure S1A; Table S1). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis showed that those genes, particularly the down regulation genes, were enriched in the ‘Ribosome’ ‘DNA replication’ and ‘RNA polymerase’ pathways (Figure S1B), indicating that caffeine inhibited fungal growth by suppressing the DNA replication and mRNA transcription and translation processes in the fungal pathogen.

To understand the role of caffeine against the fungal pathogen of tea plants, C. gloeosporioides strain was inoculated on the about 2‐year‐old tea cutting seedling leaves at 2 days after 2 mM or 0 mM (Mock) caffeine feeding, respectively. The results showed that 2 mM caffeine feeding could significantly reduce the lesion area and increase resistance to C. gloeosporioides (Figure 1B,C). Furthermore, we also found that when the caffeine pathway was repressed by downregulation of the caffeine activator gene CsMYB184 (Li, Ye, et al. 2022) or caffeine biosynthetic enzyme gene TCS1 (Kato et al. 2000), the resistance of tea leaves to C. gloeosporioides was significantly decreased (Figure S2). More importantly, the tea plant cultivars with obvious resistance against C. gloeosporioides infection, such as ZN113, LJ and WNZ, had significantly higher caffeine contents and smaller lesion areas than the susceptible cultivars, such as AJBC, HSZY and FY (Figure 1D–F). Furthermore, the correlation between disease resistance and caffeine content in 14 tea cultivars showed a significant negative correlation between lesion area and caffeine content up to −0.69 (p = 0.006) (Figure 1G; Figure S3).

To further explore the physiological function of caffeine in tea plants, we analysed the caffeine distribution in young tea leaves. Bismuth potassium iodide staining showed that the caffeine (yellow colour) is distributed throughout the tea leaves, but the distribution is uneven (Figure 1H; Figure S4A). The caffeine was mainly accumulated in mesophyll cells, particularly the palisade cells. Interestingly, caffeine was also detected in epidermal cells and cuticle, as shown by anatomic analysis and cuticular wax profiling with gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) (Figure S4B). To confirm the caffeine distribution in cuticle, the total wax was extracted using chloroform. Liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS) analysis indicated the significant metabolic variation between cuticle and cuticle‐removed leaves. In the leaves, caffeine and epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) were the dominant compounds, but only caffeine was detected in cuticle with the LC–MS method (Figure 1I; Figure S4C). Then we further observed the dynamic variation of caffeine in the cells of tea plants during fungal infection. Bismuth potassium iodide staining showed that an array of caffeine was redeposited in the epidermal cells and cuticle during C. gloeosporioides infection (Figure 1J).

JA Signalling Regulates Caffeine Biosynthesis and Enhances Resistance Against the Fungal Pathogen

To explore how caffeine participates in responding to the fungal infection of tea plants, we subjected tea leaves to C. gloeosporioides infection. Transcriptome analysis revealed that fungal infection significantly upregulated caffeine biosynthetic genes, particularly TCS1, compared with the control (CK) (Figure 2A; Table S2). Concurrently, caffeine also increased under fungal infection (Figure 2B).

JA is a defensive plant hormone that plays a critical role in resistance to both biotic and abiotic stresses. Our investigation into the JA biosynthesis pathway showed that fungal infection synergistically activated the expression of JA biosynthetic genes (Figure 2A; Table S3). Metabolic analysis confirmed elevated levels of JA and JA‐Ile in response to fungal infection (Figure 2C). The activation of both caffeine and JA metabolic pathways by fungal infection indicates that caffeine and JA work together to enhance tea plant resistance to the stress.

To explore the interaction between JA signalling and caffeine biosynthesis, we used exogenous methyl jasmonate (MeJA) to simulate JA treatment for tea leaves from about 2‐year‐old cutting seedings. Transcriptome data showed that MeJA treatment not only significantly activated the expression of endogenous JA biosynthetic genes (Figure 2D; Table S4) but also significantly enhanced the caffeine production (Figure 2E), along with increased expression of caffeine biosynthetic genes (Figure 2D; Table S4).

We also examined the role of JA in tea plant resistance to fungal stress. MeJA treatment improved resistance to fungal anthracnose disease and significantly reduced the lesion area caused by C. gloeosporioides strain (Figure 2F,G). On the other hand, the JA biosynthesis inhibitor, sodium diethyldithiocarbamate (DIECA), clearly repressed the endogenous JA and JA‐Ile biosynthesis (Figure S5), as well as the caffeine biosynthesis and the expression of TCS1 and CsMYB184 compared with the control (CK) (Figure 2H,I). Meanwhile, we found that the CsMYB184 transcript increased significantly in tea leaves under MeJA treatment (Figure 2J,K). A previous study has shown that purine alkaloids are exclusively synthesised in young tea leaves and decreased over leaf maturation (Zhang et al. 2022). We also found that the profiling of purine alkaloids was similar to the profiling of JA and JA‐Ile in developing tea leaves (Figure 2L; Figure S6A,B). Gene expression pattern analysis showed that the expression pattern of CsMYB184 was highly similar and seemingly correlated with the expression patterns of JA signalling regulator genes CsJAZs and CsMYC2 over tea leaf development (Figure S6C).

Given that JA signalling plays a central role in defence metabolism, caffeine may be regulated directly by JA signalling. To explore the transcriptional regulation mechanism of JA‐associated caffeine biosynthesis, the cis‐elements in the 1.5‐kb promoter region of CsMYB184 were screened. Interestingly, two G‐box motifs were identified, which are binding sites of the JA signalling regulator CsMYC2 (Figure 2M). The yeast‐one hybrid (Y1H) assay confirmed the binding of CsMYC2 to the CsMYB184 promoter (Figure 2N). Further, CsMYC2 activated the CsMYB184 promoter‐driven β‐glucuronidase (GUS) reporter gene compared with controls in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves (Figure 2O) and Arabidopsis protoplasts (Figure 2P). When CsMYC2 was knocked down in tea leaves with antisense oligodeoxynucleotide (asODN), this led to the repressed expression of CsMYB184 and TCS1, as well as the reduced caffeine content, compared with the sense ODN (Figure 2Q,R). Summarily, those results indicate that JA plays a crucial role in caffeine biosynthesis in tea leaves under fungal infection.

JA Signalling Repressor JAZs Negatively Regulate Caffeine Biosynthesis and Fungal Resistance in Tea Plants

Since CsMYB184 is the key activator of caffeine biosynthesis in tea plants (Li, Ye, et al. 2022), JA induced CsMYB184 transcription, and JAZs act as the repressors of JA signalling, JA signalling‐regulated CsMYB184, we further investigated their molecular mechanisms. Totally, 12 JAZ genes were confirmed from 13 annotated JAZs in the tea genome (Figures S7 and S8). Among them, five CsJAZs, including CsJAZ1, CsJAZ7, CsJAZ11, CsJAZ12 and CsJAZ13, interacted with CsMYB184 in a yeast‐two hybrid (Y2H) assay (Figure 3A).

In those five CsJAZ genes, only CsJAZ1, CsJAZ12 and CsJAZ13 were highly expressed in tea leaves, correlating with caffeine biosynthetic genes (Figure 3B; Figure S9; Table S5); meanwhile, only CsJAZ1, CsJAZ1 and CsJAZ13 could be significantly induced by fungal infection (Figure 3B; Table S6). Thus, the CsJAZ1, CsJAZ12, and CsJAZ13 were chosen to further study the functions in JA regulation of caffeine biosynthesis.

Bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assay further confirmed the interactions between CsJAZ1, CsJAZ12 and CsJAZ13 with CsMYB184. CsMYB184‐YFPN and CsJAZs‐YFPC fusion protein constructs were coinfiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves in various combinations for BiFC assay of their in Planta interaction. The green fluorescent protein (GFP) fluorescent signals could be observed in the nucleus and/or cytoplasm for CsMYB184 in combination with CsJAZ1, CsJAZ12, and CsJAZ13, respectively (Figure 3C; Figure S10). The physical interaction of CsJAZ1, CsJAZ12 and CsJAZ13 with CsMYB184 was also validated by Glutathione S‐Transferase (GST) pull‐down assays with the Escherichia coli ‐expressed recombinant proteins GST‐CsJAZ1, GST‐CsJAZ12, GST‐CsJAZ13 and Maltose‐Binding Protein (MBP)‐CsMYB184 (Figure 3D–F).

JAZ proteins contain two conserved domains: the ZIM (zinc‐finger inflorescence meristem) domain in the N‐terminal region and the Jas domain in the C‐terminal region. To identify which domain in CsJAZ1, CsJAZ12 and CsJAZ13 is responsible for interaction with CsMYB184, these proteins were divided into N‐terminal fragments (CsJAZ1NT, CsJAZ12NT and CsJAZ13NT), which contain the ZIM domain and C‐terminal fragments (CsJAZ1CT, CsJAZ12CT and CsJAZ13CT), which contain the Jas domain (Figure 3G). Similarly, the CsMYB184 protein was divided into an N‐terminal fragment (CsMYB184NT), containing the R2R3 DNA binding domain and a C‐terminal fragment (CsMYB184CT) (Figure 3G).

Y2H assays showed that full‐length CsJAZ1, CsJAZ12 and CsJAZ13 interacted with CsMYB184CT but not with CsMYB184NT (Figure 3H), suggesting that the C‐terminal part of CsMYB184 is required for interaction with CsJAZ1, CsJAZ12 and CsJAZ13 proteins. Moreover, as shown in Figure 3I, full‐length CsMYB184 interacted with CsJAZ1NT, CsJAZ12NT and CsJAZ13NT, but not with CsJAZ1CT, CsJAZ12CT or CsJAZ13CT in Y2H assays, implying that the N‐terminal parts of CsJAZ1, CsJAZ12 and CsJAZ13 are required for interaction with CsMYB184 protein. BiFC assays further confirmed that the N‐termini of CsJAZ1, CsJAZ12 and CsJAZ13, along with the C‐terminus of CsMYB184, are required for their interactions (Figure 3J; Figure S10).

Given that the JA signalling repressors CsJAZ1, CsJAZ12 and CsJAZ13 interact with CsMYB184, we further investigated whether these CsJAZ proteins could affect the transactivation of TCS1 by CsMYB184 and thereby participate in JA‐induced caffeine biosynthesis. A transactivation activity assay was performed in yeast to confirm the transcriptional activation activity of CsMYB184. Full‐length CsMYB184, CsMYB184NT and CsMYB184CT were fused with the GAL4 DNA binding domain (BD) in the pGBKT7 vectors. The resulting constructs were then transformed into the AH109 yeast strain. The growth of yeast strains harbouring full‐length CsMYB184, CsMYB184CT or CsMYB184NT on selective medium, along with an α‐galactosidase (α‐gal) assay, indicated that CsMYB184 and CsMYB184CT proteins have strong transcriptional activation activity compared to the empty vector used as the negative control (Figure 4A). However, the transcriptional activation activity of the CsMYB184NT protein, which contains the conserved R2R3 DNA binding domain, was completely abolished (Figure 4A). These data suggest that CsMYB184 functions as a transcriptional activator via its C‐terminal part.

To further investigate the functions of the N‐terminal and C‐terminal parts of CsMYB184, CsMYB184NT and CsMYB184CT were fused with an MBP tag to generate recombinant proteins in E. coli strains (Figure 4B). The result of an electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) showed that the TCS1 promoter can be recognised by MBP‐CsMYB184NT, which contains the R2R3 DNA binding domain, but not by MBP‐CsMYB184CT (Figure 4C). To determine whether the interaction between CsMYB184 and CsJAZ1, CsJAZ12 and CsJAZ13 could disrupt CsMYB184's binding activity to the TCS1 promoter, we performed an EMSA assay using CsJAZ1, CsJAZ12, CsJAZ13, CsMYB184 proteins and the TCS1 promoter. The EMSA assay indicated that CsMYB184 could directly bind to the TCS1 promoter, but this binding activity was significantly repressed by the addition of CsJAZ1, CsJAZ12 or CsJAZ13 proteins (Figure 4D). Our data demonstrate that the N‐terminal part of CsMYB184 is necessary for interaction with the TCS1 promoter, while CsJAZ1, CsJAZ12 and CsJAZ13 can inhibit the interaction by forming JAZ‐MYB complexes.

To explore whether CsJAZ1, CsJAZ12 and CsJAZ13 act upstream of CsMYB184 to regulate JA‐induced caffeine biosynthesis, we conducted a transactivation/repression assay with the TCS1 promoter (Figure 4E). Expression of CsMYB184 activated the TCS1 promoter‐driven luciferase reporter gene by more than threefold compared to a no‐effector assay. However, the activation of the TCS1 promoter by CsMYB184 was completely repressed by CsJAZ1, CsJAZ12 or CsJAZ13 (Figure 4F). These results indicate that CsJAZ1, CsJAZ12 and CsJAZ13 negatively regulate the TCS1 gene by directly interacting with CsMYB184, the key activator of TCS1.

To verify the roles of CsJAZ1, CsJAZ12 and CsJAZ13 in caffeine biosynthesis, we conducted an antisense oligodeoxynucleotide (asODN) interference experiment to achieve targeted gene knockdown in tea leaves as previous method (Yue et al. 2025). CsJAZ1, CsJAZ12 and CsJAZ13 were simultaneously suppressed in tea leaves. After 72 h of asODN treatment (CsJAZ1, 12, 13‐KD), transcript levels of CsJAZ1, CsJAZ12 and CsJAZ13 were significantly reduced compared with the sense ODN treatment, as confirmed by quantitative real‐time PCR (qRT‐PCR) (Figure 5A).

Metabolic analysis revealed that caffeine content was significantly increased in CsJAZ1, 12, 13‐KD leaves, consistent with higher expression of TCS1 (Figure 5B,C). Fungal infection assays demonstrated reduced H2O2 level in CsJAZ1, 12, 13‐KD leaves, as shown by DAB (3,3′‐Diaminobenzidine) staining at the early stage of C. gloeosporioides inoculation (Figure 5D). This reduction in H2O2 corresponded with inhibited fungal pathogen growth and enhanced resistance to C. gloeosporioides infection (Figure 5E,F).

To further investigate the roles of CsJAZ1, CsJAZ12 and CsJAZ13 in tea plants, we created Agrobacterium‐mediated transgenic hairy roots with ectopic overexpression of CsJAZ1 (CsJAZ1OE), CsJAZ12 (CsJAZ12OE) or CsJAZ13 (CsJAZ13OE) (Figure 5G–J). Gene expression analysis showed downregulation of TCS1 in CsJAZ1OE, CsJAZ12OE and CsJAZ13OE roots compared to GUS control roots (Figure 5K). Furthermore, representative transient overexpression of CsJAZ12 in tea leaves (Figure 5L,M) led to reduced TCS1 expression and caffeine concentration compared with the control (Figure 5N,O). Our data indicate that CsJAZ1, CsJAZ12 and CsJAZ13 negatively regulate caffeine biosynthesis by interacting with CsMYB184, thereby modulating tea plant resistance to fungal pathogens.

Fungal Infection Modulated Growth of Tea Plants Mediated by JA Signalling

Fungal infection has increased the caffeine biosynthesis and JA biosynthesis, here we further found that fungal infection significantly inhibited the growth of tea cutting seedlings (Figure 6A,B) or tea seedlings (Figure S11). Morphological examination of the parenchymal cells of the fungal treatment plants at the first internode (Between first leaf and second leaf) showed that their average length was significantly decreased compared with the control plants (Figure 6C,D). GA profiling analysis showed that the level of bioactive GA4 was significantly reduced, but the level of inactive GA34 was significantly increased in fungal treatment plants compared with the control plants (Figure 6E), suggesting that fungal infection promotes the catabolism of bioactive GAs. We then investigated the expression patterns of genes related to GA catabolism. Given that GA 2‐oxidase (GA2ox) is important in maintaining GA homeostasis in plants by catabolising bioactive GAs through hydroxylation (Jin et al. 2023), the expression patterns of CsGA2oxs were determined in fungal infection plants. In the tea genome, at least nine GA2ox genes were identified, of which CsGA2ox4 was found to be highly expressed in leaves and stems (Figures S12 and S13). qRT‐PCR data showed that CsGA2ox4 was significantly upregulated in fungal treatment plants compared with the control (Figure 6F).

JA often interacts with other phytohormones, such as GA, to balance defence and growth in plants. In order to elucidate the mechanism of fungal infection inhibiting the growth of tea plants, about 2‐year‐old tea cutting seedlings were sprayed with MeJA to assess its effects on growth. We found that exogenous JA treatment significantly inhibited the growth of tea cutting seedlings (Figure 6G,H) and the length of parenchymal cells (Figure 6I,J). We also found that the CsGA2ox4 expression was significantly upregulated in JA treatment plants compared to the Mock control (Figure 6K). Additionally, a similar phenotype was observed when the tea seedlings were treated with MeJA (Figure 6L–O). These data suggest that JA plays a crucial role in modulating tea plant growth.

MYC2 and JAZ proteins are the key components of JA signalling transduction in plants. To explore how JA regulates tea plant growth, we checked the effects of CsMYC2 and CsJAZ12 on the GA metabolism. qRT‐PCR data showed that CsGA2ox4 expression was significantly upregulated in transient‐overexpressing CsMYC2 tea leaves but downregulated in transient‐overexpressing CsJAZ12 leaves, compared with their respective EV (empty vector) control (Figure 6P). While a reverse trend of the expression of CsGA2ox4 was observed when CsMYC2 or CsJAZ12 was downregulated (Figure 6Q). Then CsMYC2 and CsJAZ12 were introduced into wild type (WT) Arabidopsis for overexpression respectively (Yue et al. 2025). Phenotype analysis showed that overexpressing CsMYC2 (CsMYC2OE) led to a shorter hypocotyl length phenotype compared to WT; on the contrary, overexpressing CsJAZ12 (CsJAZ12OE) significantly promoted hypocotyl elongation compared to WT (Figure 6R,S). Gene expression analysis further showed that the expression of AtGA2ox was significantly upregulated in the CsMYC2OE line but downregulated in the CsJAZ12OE line compared with the WT (Figure 6T). Then the bioactive and inactive GA contents were analysed in those Arabidopsis samples. The level of the bioactive GA1 was reduced in CsMYC2OE and increased in CsJAZ12OE compared to WT; however, the inactive GA8 showed a contrary tendency with the GA1 in the CsMYC2OE and CsJAZ12OE plants (Figure 6U). Together, those results suggest that the fungal infection‐associated growth inhibition was regulated by JA regulating CsGA2ox4 via the MYC2‐JAZ module.

EMSA assay showed that the JA signalling regulator CsMYC2 could directly bind to the G‐box element of the CsGA2ox4 promoter region (Figure 7G). Gene expression analysis showed that genes inactivating bioactive GA, such as CsGA2oxs, were dramatically upregulated by MeJA treatment or fungal infection in tea plants, suggesting that bioactive GA may function as the negative factor in the response to biotic stress in tea plants. Profiling of caffeine showed that the combined effect of paclobutrazol (PAC) and MeJA boosted caffeine accumulation compared with MeJA alone in leaves of 1‐month‐old tea seedlings (Figure 8A; Figure S14A), implying that GA may be antagonistic with JA to negatively regulate caffeine biosynthesis.

To further explore the role of GA in caffeine biosynthesis and resistance to fungal stress, about 2‐year‐old tea cutting seedlings or 1‐month‐old tea seedlings were sprayed with GA3 to assess its effects on growth, caffeine biosynthesis and antifungal activity. We found that exogenous GA significantly promoted the growth of tea plants after 3 weeks treatment, particularly the stems (Figure 8B,C; Figure S14B–E). Morphological examination of the parenchymal cells of the GA‐treatment plants at the first internode (Between first leaf and second leaf) showed that their average length was significantly increased compared to Mock plants (Figure 8D,E). Hormone profiling analysis showed that exogenous GA could significantly inhibit endogenous JA and JA‐Ile biosynthesis in tea leaves (Figure 8F). Additionally, we found that GA significantly repressed caffeine concentration in tea leaves (Figure 8G), consistent with the reduced expression level of TCS1 (Figure S14F).

We further evaluated caffeine accumulation in a time course and in a dose‐dependent manner. Two different concentrations of GA3 (50 and 100 μM) were applied for the about 2‐year‐old tea cutting seedlings. For caffeine quantification, leaves were collected at four different time points, including 0, 12, 24 h and 5 days. The results showed that GA‐mediated caffeine accumulation decreased with time in a dose‐dependent manner compared with the Mock control (Figure 8H). Meanwhile, exogenous GA3 (50 and 100 μM) also significantly increased the susceptibility of tea leaves to the fungal pathogen, leading to larger lesion areas on tea leaves (Figure 8I,J). However, caffeine application (2 mM) effectively counteracted the GA3‐induced increase in tea leaf susceptibility to the fungal pathogen (Figure S14G). Together, our findings suggested that GA interplays antagonistically with JA signalling, promoting plant growth, but negatively affecting the caffeine biosynthesis and the fungal resistance in tea plants.

To confirm their roles in the GA signalling pathway, CsDELLA1 and CsDELLA2 were ectopically overexpressed in N. tabacum (CsDELLA1OE and CsDELLA2OE, respectively) (Figure S15B,C). As expected, overexpression of CsDELLA1 or CsDELLA2 led to the typical gibberellin‐deficient dwarf phenotype in transgenic tobacco plants compared with wild‐type (WT) (Figure 9B,C). RNA‐seq assay showed that the expression levels of GIBBERELLIN‐INSENSITIVE DWARF (NtGID) and NtGA2ox genes were significantly upregulated in CsDELLA1OE and CsDELLA2OE plants compared with WT (Figure 9D; Figure S16). Furthermore, similar phenotype was observed when CsDELLA1 or CsDELLA2 were overexpressed in tea leaf‐derived hairy roots (Figure 9E; Figure S15D,E), suggesting that CsDELLA1 and CsDELLA2 are conserved repressors of the GA signalling pathway in plants. Furthermore, we found overexpression of CsDELLA1 or CsDELLA2 in tea leaf‐derived hairy roots led to the elevation of TCS1 expression compared to EV control (Figure 9F). Expression analysis showed that both CsDELLA1 and CsDELLA2 were highly expressed in young leaves, where caffeine is enriched, as indicated by qRT‐PCR assays (Figure S15F,G).

To verify the functions of CsDELLA1 and CsDELLA2 in caffeine biosynthesis, an antisense oligodeoxynucleotide (asODN) interference experiment was performed to create targeted gene knockdown in tea leaves. Inhibiting the expression of CsDELLA1 or CsDELLA2 in tea leaves led to the repression of TCS1 expression (Figure 9G,I) and a decrease in caffeine production (Figure 9H,J) compared with the respective sense ODN controls. Consistent with the reduced caffeine concentration, repressing the expression of CsDELLA1 or CsDELLA2 significantly decreased tea resistance to C. gloeosporioides infection (Figure 9K). Our data suggest that CsDELLA1 and CsDELLA2, as GA signalling repressors, play positive roles in caffeine biosynthesis and resistance in tea plants.

To determine which domain of CsDELLA1 and CsDELLA2 is responsible for interaction with CsJAZ12, the proteins were divided into an N‐terminal fragment (CsDELLA1NT and CsDELLA2NT) containing the DELLA domain and a C‐terminal fragment (CsDELLA1CT and CsDELLA2CT) containing the GRAS domain (Figure 10C). Meanwhile, the CsJAZ12 protein was also divided into an N‐terminal fragment (CsJAZ12NT) and a C‐terminal fragment (CsJAZ12CT) as shown in Figure 3. Figure 10D showed that both CsJAZ12NT and CsJAZ12CT interacted with the C‐terminal parts of CsDELLA1 and CsDELLA2, but not with CsDELLA1NT and CsDELLA2NT in yeast cells, suggesting that the GRAS domain in the C‐terminal parts of CsDELLA1 and CsDELLA2 is required for interaction with CsJAZ12.

We next investigated whether CsDELLA1 and CsDELLA2 could inhibit the interaction between CsJAZ12 and CsMYB184. The results demonstrated that the addition of CsDELLA1 or CsDELLA2 decreased the fluorescence signal in the BiFC assay (Figure 10E,F) and the bioluminescence signal in the Firefly luciferase complementation imaging (LCI) assay (Figure 10G). Furthermore, pull‐down assays were performed to confirm the inhibition of CsDELLA1 or CsDELLA2 on the CsJAZ12 and CsMYB184 interaction (Figure 10H,I). Previous data have showed that CsJAZ12 could inhibit the interaction between CsMYB184 and the TCS1 promoter (Figure 4D), we further found that CsDELLAs could significantly abolish the inhibition of CsJAZ12 on the interaction between CsMYB184 and the TCS1 promoter (Figure 10J). Meanwhile, the CsDELLAs could at least partially recover the inhibition of CsMYB184‐mediated TCS1 promoter activation by CsJAZ12 in Arabidopsis protoplasts (Figure 10K). These data indicate a competitive relationship between CsDELLA1/2 and CsMYB184 for interaction with CsJAZ12.

Discussion

Caffeine: A Broad‐Spectrum Defensive Toxin Against Multiple Biotic Stresses in Plants

Caffeine is a well‐known purine alkaloid that contributes to the flavour of tea, but its physiological functions in plants are not fully understood (Zeng et al. 2021). Initially, caffeine was thought to act as a signalling compound in plants, inhibiting seed germination of competitors or stimulating the nervous system of pollinators (Arnold et al. 2021; Ransom 1912; Wright et al. 2013). Recent studies have shown that caffeine also plays crucial roles in plant–microorganism interactions (Kim et al. 2011; Kim and Sano 2008). Wang et al. reported that endogenous caffeine could suppress fungal growth by inhibiting cell wall expansion (Wang et al. 2016), and our transcriptome analysis further showed that fungal self‐replication was significantly suppressed after caffeine intake.

Coevolution is a result of mutual adaptation between plants and pests or pathogens (Rausher 2001). In caffeine‐free ancestral plants, caffeine's effects against pathogens and pests not adapted to caffeine are more pronounced. A recent study pointed out that transgenic rice producing caffeine in leaves could simultaneously improve broad‐spectrum resistance, including bacteria, fungi and insects, without affecting yield (Park et al. 2022). Caffeine protects plants from pathogens and insects through two pathways: directly inhibiting DNA and RNA replication in pathogens and insects as a phytoalexin (this study), and significantly activating the host plant's endogenous defence system. For example, in tea plants, caffeine can activate the endogenous JA defence pathway in response to elevated CO2 (Li et al. 2016), while in rice, caffeine enhances the salicylic acid (SA) defence pathway by activating calcium signalling (Park et al. 2022). These dual functions of caffeine hold great potential for developing new biological pesticides to replace toxic pesticides, reducing environmental pollution and pesticide residues and improving food health.

Caffeine Biosynthesis as a JA Signalling‐Mediated Tea Plant Resistance Mechanism to Fungal Infection

Caffeine exhibits strong toxicity against multiple biotic stresses in plants, practically the fungi. However, how caffeine biosynthesis responds to fungal stress is not fully understood in plants. The JA pathway is a conserved core defence system in the plant kingdom (Li et al. 2021). Numerous studies have shown that JA signalling plays a crucial role in resistance to disease infections, insect damage and stress signals from neighbouring plants in tea plants, using multiple strategies such as stimulating the endogenous immune system and producing defensive metabolites (Gu et al. 2022; Jing et al. 2024; Li et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2021, 2016; Zhu et al. 2019). Combined transcriptome and metabolism analyses reveal that caffeine biosynthesis and JA biosynthesis and signalling are highly synergistically activated by fungal infection and exogenous MeJA treatment in tea plants (Figure 2), indicating that caffeine biosynthesis is regulated by JA signalling.

JAZ proteins are the conserved repressors of JA signalling in plants (Howe et al. 2018; Li et al. 2021). In the tea genome, at least 12 JAZ proteins have been identified, with CsJAZ1, CsJAZ12 and CsJAZ13 being the major JAZ genes involved in regulating caffeine biosynthesis by interacting with CsMYB184. In yeast cells, CsJAZ1 and CsJAZ12, but not CsJAZ13, can form homodimers and interact with CsMYC2 (Figure S17). Nevertheless, based on their expression patterns in different tissues and under stress conditions, these JAZ proteins exhibited significantly divergent roles in caffeine biosynthesis. Regarding caffeine distribution in developing leaves, CsJAZ12 and CsJAZ13 may redundantly play crucial roles. Both CsJAZ12 and CsJAZ13 were highly expressed in apical buds, and their expression decreased as leaves matured—a trend consistent with caffeine biosynthesis (Figure 3B). The high expression levels of these JAZ repressors might be due to the high JA concentration in young leaves. High levels of JA, on the one hand, promoted caffeine accumulation, but on the other hand, it prevented excessive accumulation through activating some repressors, thus achieving a balance between development and metabolism via interplaying with other hormones, such as GA. In contrast, CsJAZ1 exhibited an inverse expression profile, peaking in mature leaves (Figure 3B), suggesting potential functional redundancy with CsJAZ12 in stress‐responsive caffeine biosynthesis. The functional differences among these different JAZs may result from variations in their promoter regions or differential regulation by upstream factors. Furthermore, CsJAZ12, with a high expression level in leaves, was the primary JAZ protein interacting with DELLA proteins to regulate caffeine biosynthesis in the crosstalk between JA and GA signalling (Figure 10). Additionally, other JAZ proteins, such as CsJAZ4, CsJAZ5, CsJAZ7, CsJAZ9, CsJAZ10 and CsJAZ11, may serve auxiliary roles in fine‐tuning caffeine biosynthesis via DELLA proteins, given their low expression levels in leaves (Figures 3B and 10A). Obviously, the divergence in protein interactions likely stemmed from sequence variations among JAZ proteins (e.g., specific residues in the DELLA‐binding domains).

As caffeine biosynthesis repressors, the N‐terminal of CsJAZs interacts with the C‐terminal of CsMYB184 to inhibit TCS1 activation by CsMYB184's C‐terminal. Additionally, CsJAZ proteins can suppress the interaction between CsMYB184's N‐terminal and the TCS1 promoter by interacting with CsMYB184. Our data uncover the mechanism of CsJAZ negatively regulating caffeine biosynthesis in tea plants at the post‐transcriptional level. At the transcriptional level, we also found that CsMYB184 is regulated by CsMYC2, the master regulator of JA signalling in tea plants. In summary, JA triggers the degradation of JAZ proteins to release its partners, CsMYB184 and CsMYC2, while CsMYC2 further activates CsMYB184 expression, forming a signalling amplification module to quickly respond to external biotic stress in tea plants.

The regulation of alkaloid biosynthesis by JA signalling through the JAZ‐MYC2 protein complex seems conserved across plants. Alkaloids, a diverse group of compounds irregularly distributed across certain families or species, form via metabolic pathways with dramatic variations to protect plants from biotic damage (Leite Dias and D'Auria 2025). Interestingly, these biosynthesis pathways have evolved to utilise JA as a signalling source from biotic stress. For example, MYC2 paralogs directly regulate key nicotine biosynthetic genes, such as putrescine N‐methyltransferase (Yang et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2012). Similarly, in Catharanthus roseus , MYC2 paralogs regulate terpenoid indole alkaloid biosynthesis by activating several AP2‐type transcription factors (Zhang et al. 2011). These regulatory mechanisms of alkaloid biosynthesis pathways exhibit convergent evolution, being recruited by JA signalling (Jiang et al. 2024; Yamada and Sato 2021). This convergent evolution highlights the core role of JA signalling in responding to biotic stress in plants. While the mechanisms of MYC2 regulation in alkaloid biosynthesis differ across pathways or species, CsMYC2 shows high conservation in regulating catechins and volatile biosynthesis, which are widespread in plants (Gu et al. 2022; Kazan and Manners 2013; Yue et al. 2025; Zhu et al. 2022). Further research could elucidate the evolutionary mechanisms of MYC2 in different metabolic pathways or species.

Crosstalk Between JA and GA for Balancing Disease Resistance and Growth in Tea Plants

When plants face multiple stresses, they synthesise large amounts of secondary metabolites to improve resistance. However, this can inhibit growth, as plants balance resistance and growth (He et al. 2022). In this study, we confirmed that JA and GA are crucial hormones antagonistically mediating growth and defence in tea plants. When tea plants were exposed to fungal stress or exogenous MeJA, the expression levels of bioactive GA biosynthetic genes, such as ent‐copalyl diphosphate synthase (CPS) and ent‐kaurene synthase (KS), were not significantly disturbed. Notably, the genes involved in GA inactivation, such as CsGA2ox4, were dramatically activated by these stimuli, suggesting that JA antagonises GA functions by promoting GA inactivation and inhibiting growth to enhance resistance. Our data further showed that promoting tea plant growth with GA significantly reduced resistance to fungal pathogens. Metabolic analysis suggested that reduced caffeine concentration could partially explain the decreased resistance with elevated bioactive GA in tea leaves.

DELLA proteins are repressors of GA signalling (Boss and Thomas 2002; Claeys et al. 2014). Previous studies have shown that GA promotes DELLA protein degradation to participate in metabolic processes, such as anthocyanin biosynthesis (Xie et al. 2016). In tea plants, two conserved DELLA proteins, CsDELLA1 and CsDELLA2, were identified. Overexpression assays in tobacco and tea hairy roots validated CsDELLA1 and CsDELLA2 as traditional GA signalling repressors. Transcriptome analysis showed that the expression of several growth‐related genes was significantly suppressed in CsDELLA1 or CsDELLA2 overexpressing tobaccos (Figure S16). In tea plants, inhibition of the expression of CsDELLA1 or CsDELLA2 significantly suppressed caffeine production, as well as resistance to fungal infection, indicating that DELLA proteins were the activators of caffeine biosynthesis, and further that DELLA proteins were involved in GA negatively regulating caffeine biosynthesis.

The interaction between GA and JA is a critical regulatory mechanism in plants, primarily mediated through the stability of DELLA and JAZ proteins (Yang et al. 2012). This interaction is essential for balancing plant growth and defence responses, particularly in tea plants. DELLA proteins are known to interact with JAZ proteins, mediating the crosstalk between GA and JA signalling pathways (Yang et al. 2012). In tea plants, CsDELLA1 and CsDELLA2 can interact with over half of the CsJAZ proteins, highlighting the complexity of this interaction network. CsDELLA1 and CsDELLA2 interaction with CsJAZ12 disrupts its binding to CsMYB184, allowing CsMYB184 to promote caffeine biosynthesis. Additionally, this interaction facilitates the release of CsMYC2, which further enhances GA inactivation by regulating the CsGA2ox4 gene. This results in the inhibition of plant growth while promoting defence mechanisms. Under normal conditions, JA signalling is suppressed, leading to the accumulation of JAZ proteins, which interact with CsMYB184, CsMYC2 and DELLA proteins. This suppresses defensive metabolism (e.g., caffeine biosynthesis) and promotes GA signalling, thereby supporting plant growth. Under stress conditions, JA biosynthesis and signalling are activated, leading to the degradation of JAZ proteins. This releases CsMYB184, CsMYC2 and DELLA proteins, enhancing defensive metabolism and increasing GA inactivation, which suppresses growth to bolster resistance (Figure 11).

Model of caffeine accumulation regulation in tea plants. Under stressed conditions, JA biosynthesis and signalling are activated, leading to the degradation of JAZ proteins. This releases CsMYB184, CsMYC2, and DELLA proteins. CsMYC2 released could further directly upregulate the abundance of CsMYB184. Then, CsMYB184 positively regulates TCS1 expression and caffeine biosynthesis to enhance tea plant resistance. Meanwhile, GA2ox is activated by CsMYC2 to catalyse the active GA into inactive GA, which further improves DELLA protein stability for competitively interacting with JAZ12 to enhance caffeine biosynthesis, which antagonises the GA signalling to regulate tea plant defence‐growth trade‐offs.

In summary, the study reveals a novel regulatory mechanism involving the CsMYC2‐CsJAZ‐CsMYB184 module, which mediates the development‐dependent distribution of caffeine in developing tea leaves, and the JA‐induced caffeine biosynthesis as part of the tea plant's innate immune response to fungal pathogen infections. The interaction between JA and GA signalling pathways is crucial for balancing plant growth and defence, particularly through the interactions of CsDELLAs‐CsJAZ‐CsMYB184 and CsMYC2‐CsGA2ox4. This crosstalk offers insights into the conserved mechanisms of plant defence and growth regulation across the plant kingdom. This understanding can contribute to the development of new strategies for enhancing disease resistance in crops while minimising the impact on growth, potentially leading to more sustainable agricultural practices.

Experimental Procedures

Plant Materials, Growth Conditions and Pathogen Infection

All tea plant varieties ( Camellia sinensis L.) were grown at the tea plantation of Anhui Agricultural University (Hefei, Anhui Province, China) and were used for gene cloning, phenotype survey and metabolomic analyses. The tea cutting seedlings (Shuchazao and Longjing43 cultivars, about 2‐year‐old) and the tea seedlings (the seeds from Shuchazao cultivar) were cultivated in the artificial climate chamber (25°C, 16/8 h photoperiod, 65% humidity). The pathogenic fungus used in this study, C. gloeosporioides, was identified previously (Jeyaraj et al. 2019), dropped onto potato dextrose agar (PDA) plates, and incubated in an incubator at 28°C. For fungal inoculation, 50 μL of spore suspension (107 conidia ml−1) or 5 mm diameter fungal plugs were inoculated onto the wounded potted tea leaves. The leaves inoculated with the same volumes of sterile distilled water or no fungal plugs served as controls. The lesion area of leaves was measured by ImageJ software after infection. Meanwhile, the leaves were harvested at different times for further analysis. All the tobacco and Arabidopsis plants were grown at 22°C with a 16/8 h photoperiod at approximately 120 μmol m−2 s−1.

Targeted Metabolite Profiling

Caffeine was profiled by high performance liquid chromatographic (HPLC) according to the method described previously (Li et al. 2020). Briefly, about 0.05 g samples were mixed with 1 mL 80% methanol extraction solution. The solution buffer with samples was sonicated for 1 h at room temperature and then shaken overnight at 100 rpm at 4°C. Following centrifugation at 12000 rpm for 10 min, the supernatants were filtered with a Nylon membrane (0.45 μm). Then the caffeine was confirmed according to the caffeine standard compound by HPLC at 278 nm. For quantification of caffeine, the peak area was introduced into the standard curve. At least three biological replicates were included.

For phytohormone analyses, about 0.15 g samples were ground in liquid nitrogen and then extracted with 1 mL ethyl acetate solution. After strongly vortexing, the supernatants were collected by centrifugation at 12000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatants were evaporated by nitrogen and then redissolved in 150–200 μL 50% methanol (v/v) solution. The contents of phytohormone, including JA, JA‐Ile, GA1, GA4, GA8 and GA34, were measured on an LC–MS/MS System (SCIEX Triple Quad 5500) equipped with a C18 column (Phenomenex, 4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm) according to the previous method (Yue et al. 2025), with a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min and a column temperature of 40°C. The injection volume was 5 μL and the mobile phase was water (contained 0.1% formic acid (v/v) and 5 mM Ammonium formate, A) and acetonitrile (B). The mass spectrometer was operated under negative ionic mode with the Electrospray Ionisation (ESI). The gas temperature was set to 320°C, the gas flow rate was set to 6 L/min, and the temperature of sheath gas, with 11 L/min flow rate, was set to 350°C. The data were acquired and analysed with Analyst software. The phytohormone compounds were identified by comparing the standard substances. The peak area was introduced into the standard curve for quantification. At least three biological replicates were included.

Caffeine Extraction From Tea Wax

About 3 g of healthy tea leaves (Shuchazao cultivar) were fully submerged in a glass plate containing 15 mL chloroform. The plate was stirred gently for extraction of cuticular waxes for 15 s at room temperature. The extraction solution was transferred to a new vial, and fresh chloroform (15 mL) was added to the same tea leaves in the plate, and the extraction was repeated for another 15 s. The two chloroform extractions were combined and dried with a mild stream of nitrogen gas. Then the substrate was resuspended with 80% methanol for HPLC and LC–MS analysis or with 600 μL chloroform for GC–MS analysis. For GC–MS analysis, the samples were injected into the GC–MS platform (Shimadzu, Japan) equipped with an Agilent DB‐1 capillary column. The instrument settings follow our previous methods (Zuo et al. 2023). The detection temperature was 320°C, staying for 20 min. The mobile phase was helium with a speed of 1.2 mL/min−1. Caffeine was identified by comparing the mass spectra in the NIST database and comparing the caffeine standard. The ethyl caprate (0.2 μg g−1) was served as the internal standard for quantification. At least five repeats for each sample were performed.

Assessing the Antifungal Effects of Caffeine

To test the effects of caffeine on the growth of fungal pathogens, 5 mm diameter fungal plugs were inoculated, respectively, on the centre of fresh PDA plates containing different concentrations of caffeine (0, 0.5, 1, 2 and 5 mM). After incubation for 3 days at 28°C, the fungal growth area was measured using ImageJ software from three independent biological replicates with more than 10 PDA plates for each caffeine concentration. Meanwhile, the fungal pathogens were collected from the plates containing 0 or 2 mM caffeine for RNA‐seq analysis.

Cloning and Construction of Binary Vectors for Plant Expressing

The open reading frames (ORFs) of CsJAZs, CsMYB184, CsMYC2, CsDELLAs and CsGA2ox4 were amplified from leaf cDNAs prepared from Camellia sinensis L. cv. Shuchazao using gene‐specific primers (Table S8), and the sequences were identified by Sanger sequencing. The ORFs of genes were then subcloned into the binary vector pBI121, respectively, for tobacco transformation or tea plant transformation.

Transformation of Tobacco and Hairy Roots of Tea Plants

For tobacco transformation, the CDSs of CsDELLA1, CsDELLA2 and CsGA2ox4 were recombined into pBI121 and then transformed into A. tumefaciens EHA105 respectively. The tobacco ( Nicotiana tabacum cv, Xanthi) transformation was performed according to the described methods (Shen et al. 2019). For tea seedling hairy root transformation, the pBI121‐CsJAZ1, ‐CsJAZ12 and ‐CsJAZ13 constructs were transformed into A. rhizogenes strain ATCC 15834 by electroporation, respectively. The positive transformants were confirmed by RT‐PCR, and further were used to transform about 3‐month‐old tea seedlings as described by Alagarsamy et al. (2018). At least three independent transgenic lines were generated for each construct and were used for experimental examination.

RNA Extension and Quantitative RT‐PCR (qRT‐PCR)

Plant total RNA isolation and qRT‐PCR were performed as previously described (Li, Fu, et al. 2022). Briefly, about 1 μg of total purified RNA from each sample was used to generate the cDNA using the SuperScript III kit (Invitrogen). qRT‐PCR was carried out on the iQ5 real‐time PCR machine with gene‐specific primers (Table S8). The data were acquired and parsed by the manager software (Bio‐Red). The housekeeping genes CsACTIN or AtACTIN were used to normalise the relative expression of related genes using a modified 2−ΔΔCT method.

Yeast Two‐Hybrid (Y2H) and One‐Hybrid (Y1H) Assays

For Y2H assays, the ORFs of target genes were subcloned in frame with the GAL4 BD into the pGBKT7 or subcloned in frame with the GAL4 AD into the pGADT7 vector, respectively, according to previously described (Li, Fu, et al. 2022). Different combinations were cotransformed into the yeast strain AH109 to identify the protein interactions by the growth test on the SD medium without Trp, Leu, His and Ade (QDO, SD/‐T‐L‐H‐A). For Y1H assays, approximately 2.0 kb CsMYB184 promoter sequence was inserted into pHIS2.1 vector. Similarly, the ORF of CsMYC2 was subcloned into pGADT7 to generate the CsMYC2‐AD construct. The CsMYC2‐AD construct was transformed into Y187 harbouring pHIS2‐proCsMYB184 to examine whether CsMYC2 is the direct target of CsMYB184. The protein and DNA interaction was tested on the SD medium without His.

BiFC And Subcellular Localisation

For BiFC assay, ORFs of target genes (stop codon removed) were subcloned in frame with the YFPN into the pFGC‐YN173 vector, or subcloned in frame with the YFPC into the pFGC‐YC155 vector. The resulting constructs were then transformed into A. tumefaciens strain GV3101, respectively, for transient assays in tobacco leaves. The strains harbouring different constructs were mixed and infiltrated into leaves of about 3‐week‐old tobacco plants (Nicotiana benthamiana) according to the method described previously (Li, Fu, et al. 2022). For subcellular localisation analysis, the ORFs of the genes CsJAZ1, CsJAZ12, CsJAZ13, CsDELLA1 and CsDELLA2 were amplified and cloned, respectively, into the pLGFP1301 vector. The constructs were respectively transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens EHA105 strains for transient expression. Three‐week‐old Nicotiana benthamiana leaves were used for infiltration with strains harbouring the resulting constructs. The Green fluorescent protein (GFP) fluorescence signals were detected using confocal microscopy (Leica DMi8 M; Germany) at 48 h after infiltration.

Pull‐Down Assay

The ORFs of CsJAZ1, CsJAZ12 and CsJAZ13 were subcloned into the pGEX‐4‐T vector to generate GST‐CsJAZ1, GST‐CsJAZ12 and GST‐CsJAZ13, respectively. Similarly, the ORF of CsMYB184 was inserted into the pMAL‐c2x vector to produce MBP‐CsMYB184. The GST, GST‐fused CsJAZ1, CsJAZ12, CsJAZ13 and MBP‐fused CsMYB184 were expressed in Escherichia coli BL21 and purified by amylose resin beads as described previously (Gao et al. 2021). For the in vitro pull‐down assay, about 50 mg of purified GST, GST‐CsJAZ1, GST‐CsJAZ12 and GST‐CsJAZ13 proteins were respectively incubated with equal MBP‐CsMYB184 protein for 2 h at 4°C with gentle rocking in pull‐down buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.4), 200 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM PMSF and 1 mM DTT) containing 150 μL amylose resin beads. After centrifugation, the residue was further washed five times with TBS buffer (10 mM Tris (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA and 1% Triton X‐100). The proteins were resuspended in SDS loading buffer and immunoblotted using the anti‐MBP antibody (Proteintech Group) (1:5000) and anti‐GST antibody (Beyotime) (1:5000). The immunoblotting signals were scanned with the BioRad (ChemiDoc MP) imaging system.

Electrophoretic Mobility Shift Assay (EMSA)

The ORF of CsMYB184CT, CsMYB184CT or CsMYC2 was inserted into the pMAL‐c2x vector, and then the constructs were translated into the E. coli BL21 cells, respectively, for fusion protein purification. The MBP‐fused CsMYB184NT, CsMYB184CT or CsMYC2 proteins were purified by immobilised glutathione beads (Sangon Biotech Co. Ltd., Shanghai, China) as described previously (Gao et al. 2021). The EMSA was performed with about 2 μg of purified proteins using the EMSA kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer's protocol in 6.6% nondenatured polyacrylamide gel.

For competitive EMSA assay between CsMYB184 and CsJAZs, 1 μg MBP‐CsMYB184 protein was incubated with equal or two‐fold GST‐CsJAZ1, GST‐CsJAZ12, and GST‐CsJAZ13 proteins, respectively. Then, the interaction strength between protein complexes and labelled probes was detected using the EMSA kit. The MBP‐CsMYB184 alone with labelled probes was the control. For competitive EMSA assay among CsMYB184, CsJAZ12 and CsDELLA1s, 1 μg MBP‐CsMYB184 protein was incubated with equal GST, GST‐CsJAZ12 or a mix of GST‐CsJAZ12 and GST‐CsDELLAs proteins, respectively. Then, the interaction strength between protein complexes and labelled probes was detected using the EMSA kit.

Promoter Trans‐Activation Assays

Approximately 2.0 kb sequence upstream of the translation start codons of the CsMYB184 and TCS1 was amplified from tea genomic DNA using the special primers (Table S8) and then inserted, respectively, into pGreenII0800‐LUC to generate the luciferase reporter constructs. The effector genes CsMYB184, CsJAZ1, CsJAZ12, CsJAZ13, CsMYC2 and CsDELLAs were cloned, respectively, into pGreenII62‐SK to generate the effector constructs. Different combinations of reporter and effector constructs were cotransfected in Arabidopsis mesophyll protoplasts according to the method described previously (Yoo et al. 2007). Luciferase activities were measured by using a Dual‐Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega) according to the manufacturer's protocol. The Renilla luciferase activity acted as the internal control to normalise the reporter gene expression values. The relative LUC activity was calculated by normalising to that of REN in three biological triplicates.

Firefly Luciferase Complementation Imaging (LCI) Assays

The CsMYB184 and CsJAZ12 were cloned, respectively, into pCAMBIA1300‐nLUC and pCAMBIA1300‐cLUC to generate the CsMYB184‐LUCc and CsJAZ12‐LUCn constructs. Then the resulting constructs were transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 respectively for LCI assays in tobacco leaves. The strains harbouring CsMYB184‐LUCc and CsJAZ12‐LUCn constructs with the CsDELLAs‐pBI121 or the empty pBI121 were mixed (1:1:1) and infiltrated into leaves of about four‐week‐old tobacco plants (Nicotiana benthamiana). After infiltration, tobacco plants were cultured at 22°C for 48 h with a 16/8 h photoperiod. The luminescence was detected after 1 mM luciferin (Sigma, L9504) supplementation with 0.01% Triton X‐100 in darkness. The images of luminescence were captured using a low‐light‐cooled CCD imaging apparatus (NightSHADE evo LB985N).

Treatment on Tea Plants

For hormone treatment experiments, the tender tea shoots of about 2‐year‐old cutting seedlings were sprayed with 100 μM Methyl Jasmonate (MeJA), 100 μM Sodium diethyldithiocarbamate, trihydrate (DIECA) or 100 μM gibberellins (GA3) solution, respectively, while the controls were treated with distilled water. The shoots treated by MeJA were harvested at 0, 12, 24 and 48 h after treatment for metabolism/transcriptome analyses or section analysis and those treated by GA3 were harvested at 0, 12, 24 h and 5 days after treatment for metabolism and transcriptome analyses.

For fungal pathogen treatment, the young leaves of about 2‐year‐old cutting seedlings or tea seedlings were infected with the C. gloeosporioides pathogen according to the method as described previously (Wang et al. 2021). The tea cultivars ( Camellia sinensis var. sinensis ), including AJBC (anjibaicha), HSZY (huangshanzaoya), FY (fuyun), ZN113 (zhenong113), LJ (longjing), WNZ (wuniuzao) and SCZ (shuchazao), were used for treatment. The leaves treated with C. gloeosporioides were harvested at 1 to 3 weeks after treatment for metabolism and RNA‐seq analyses. Meanwhile, the young leaves were also treated by MeJA (100 μM), GA (50 or 100 μM), PAC (50 or 100 μM), caffeine (2 mM) or ODN, and then were infected with the C. gloeosporioides pathogen 1 to 3 days after treatment. The lesion area of leaves was measured by ImageJ software to illustrate the fungal resistance after infection.

Microscopic Observations

For sections, the first internode (between first leaf and second leaf) was collected and fixed in FAA solution (50% ethanol, 5% formaldehyde, 5% acetic acid and 40% distilled water) overnight. The samples were covered with 5% agar after dehydration in ethanol with different concentrations. Approximately 50‐ to 100‐μm‐thick sections were cut using a vibratome (VT1200S, Leica, Germany). Then a microscope (DM3000, Leica, Germany) was used for imaging the sections, and the cell length was measured with the ImageJ software.

Enzymatic Activity Assay

Enzymatic activity assay of CsGA2ox4 was performed according to the method described previously (Jin et al. 2023). Briefly, the full‐length ORF of CsGA2ox4 was introduced into the pMAL‐c2x vector, and then the construct was transformed into the E. coli BL21 cells for fusion protein purification. The MBP‐fused CsGA2ox4 protein was purified by immobilised glutathione beads (Sangon Biotech Co. Ltd., Shanghai, China) as described previously (Gao et al. 2021). The enzymatic reaction mixtures (150 μL) contained 10 μg recombinant CsGA2ox4 protein, 50 μM GA1 or GA4, 5 mM DTT, 13.3 mM α‐ketoglutarate, 13.3 mM ascorbate, 0.67 mM iron(II) and 5 μg μL−1 BSA in 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0). The reaction mixture was incubated in an incubator at 30°C for 1 to 2 h, and then the reaction was stopped by supplying 1/5 volume of 1 M HCl. After that, the ethyl acetate was used for product extraction. After centrifugation at 14000 × g for 1 min, the upper organic phase was isolated and evaporated by nitrogen and then redissolved in 200 μL methanol (v/v) solution before LC–MS/MS analysis.

Caffeine Staining Assay

The fresh cross‐sections of tea leaves were obtained using a vibratome (VT1200S, Leica, Germany). The bismuth potassium iodide was diluted to 0.04 g/mL in acetic acid. An appropriate amount of staining solution was added to fully cover each sample on the slide. After 30 s, the solution was removed with distilled water for observation with an upright microscope (DM3000, Leica, Germany) under bright field.

Transient Transformation and Antisense Oligonucleotides (AsODN) Assay in Tea Plants

The pBI121‐CsJAZ12 or pBI121‐CsMYC2 and pBI121 plasmids were transformed into Agrobacterium GV3101 and then were injected into both sides of the leaves with the main vein as the boundary respectively. After 48 h of culture, tea samples were collected respectively to determine the related gene expression and caffeine content. Gene suppression of CsJAZs, CsMYC2 and CsDELLAs in tea plants using AsODN was carried out as described previously (Xie et al. 2014) with slight modifications. Briefly, the AsODN of target genes was designed and selected respectively using Soligo software (Xie et al. 2014) (Table S8). The tender tea shoots (SCZ cultivar) with leaves were cut off and partially inserted into Eppendorf tubes to incubate with 2 mL of 20 μM AsODN solution in a plant incubator, and the solution with sense oligonucleotides of the corresponding gene as the control. After 18 to 72 h of incubation, the tender shoots were collected for further metabolism and transcriptome analysis. Three independent measurements consisted of 10 tea shoots.

RNA‐Seq Analysis

For RNA‐seq, total RNA was extracted separately from samples with the Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) and was purified. The quality and concentration of total RNA were evaluated by Agilent 2100. RNA‐seq data were generated with the sequencing libraries constructed by the TruSeq Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) on an Illumina HiSeq2000 platform. After removing reads containing adapter, poly‐N and low‐quality reads, the clean data were obtained and mapped to the fungal genome reported previously (Kong et al. 2023), tobacco genome (Edwards et al. 2017) or tea genome (Wei et al. 2018). Transcripts per million (TPM) were introduced to qualify the expression levels of transcripts. At least two biological repeats were carried out. All transcriptome data generated and used in this study can be downloaded from the tea genomic database Tea Plant Information Archive (TPIA) and the NCBI (PRJNA798825).

Bioinformatic Analysis

All heatmap of gene expression patterns were constructed using the TBtools software (Chen, Wu, et al. 2023). The ‘corrplot’ in R package was used for correlation analysis and drawing diagrams (Koutecky 2015). Briefly, the young leaves of different cultivars were used for catechins and caffeine content and lesion area analysis, respectively. Then correlation analysis was performed using the contents of catechins and caffeine with the lesion area in different cultivars. Phylogenetic trees were constructed with MEGA11 software using the neighbour‐joining method with 1000 bootstrap replications, and multiple alignments were constructed using the ClustalW2 program.

GUS Activity Assays

The promoter of CsMYB184 amplified from tea cultivar (SCZ) was subcloned into pBI121 vector to generate proCsMYB184::GUS reporter system. Then the A. tumefaciens strain GV3101 harbouring the resulting construct was infiltrated into the about 4‐week‐old N. benthamiana leaves. Meanwhile, the infiltrated leaves were treated with Dimethyl sulfoxide or 100 μM MeJA. About 2 days after infiltration, the GUS activity was measured by the leaf staining method as described previously (Li, Ye, et al. 2022). For the CsMYB184 promoter activation assay, the GUS driven by the CsMYB184 promoter with CsMYC2 or GFP control was coexpressed in the same N. benthamiana leaf mediated by A. tumefaciens infiltration. About 2 days after infiltration, GUS activity was detected by the leaf staining method.

Statistical Analysis

All data were obtained from at least three independent experiments. The confidence limits of 95% or 99% were defined as significant between two‐tailed data in Student's t‐test. For the Y1H assay, Y2H assay, BiFC and plant and fungal phenotypic display, only representative pictures were shown.
