Environmental impact of food consumption: determinants of carbon and water footprints in an Italian population
Authors: Doriana Lacalaprice, Nicolò Scarsi, Francesco Gianfagna, Andrea Paladini, Stefania Boccia, Roberta Pastorino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderstanding the environmental impact of food consumption is essential for addressing climate change and water scarcity. This study investigates the carbon and water footprints (WFs) of dietary habits in Italy, identifying sociodemographic and dietary determinants influencing these impacts. We conducted a cross-sectional analysis using data from the nationally representative Italian National Food Consumption Survey (INRAN-SCAI) 2005–06, which included 2831 participants. Daily food intake was multiplied by environmental coefficients from the SU-EATABLE LIFE database to estimate carbon (kg CO2 eq) and water (l) footprints. We used multiple regression models to assess associations between environmental indicators and individual characteristics, including age, sex, education, body mass index, geographical area, and adherence to the Mediterranean diet (MD). The mean daily carbon footprint (CF) was 3.53 kg CO2 eq, and the mean WF was 3331 l. Animal-based food groups were the main contributors to both footprints, particularly meat, which accounted for 6.87% of the total CF and 27.54% of the total WF, and dairy products, contributing 20.0% to CF and 21.3% to WF. Higher adherence to the MD was associated with lower carbon (9.84 vs 11.01 kg CO2 eq) and WFs (9356.0 vs 10 348.3 l). Multiple analysis showed this association remained significant for both carbon (β = −0.239) and WFs (β = −206.4), independent of energy intake. Animal-based foods and specific sociodemographic factors substantially influence the environmental impact of diet. Promoting Mediterranean-style, plant-based diet through targeted policies for specific populations could enhance environmental sustainability.

Introduction

Global dietary habits represent a major public health concern, affecting human health and environmental sustainability [1]. A growing body of evidence showed the substantial impact of the current global food systems in terms of ecological footprint, accounting for over 30% of total greenhouse gas emissions, up to 80% of biodiversity loss, and 70% of freshwater depletion [2]. Dietary choices are influenced by a complex interplay of factors, including socioeconomic status, cultural traditions, and individual preferences [3]. As global food demand is expected to increase by 35% to 56% between 2010 and 2050 [4], substantial shifts in dietary habits are required to sustain a growing global population while aligning with the One Health approach [5], and the 2030 agenda for the accomplishment of Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) set by the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) [6]. The association between lower socioeconomic status and lower dietary quality [7], gender-specific differences and diet-related environmental impacts [8], as well as the relationship between education level and dietary emissions, remain inconclusive in the existing literature [9]. To comprehensively assess the ecological footprint of dietary habits, it is crucial to integrate real-world dietary data from national surveys with extensive life cycle assessment (LCA) inventories [10]. Over the past decades, various indicators have been proposed to assess sustainable healthy diets [11], including the water footprint (WF) and carbon footprint (CF) of individual food items, which help quantify the environmental impact of different dietary models [12]. The Mediterranean diet (MD) is widely recognized for its dual benefits on human health and environmental sustainability [13], having been recognized in 2010 by UNESCO as an ‘intangible cultural heritage of humanity’ due to its long-standing stability among Mediterranean populations [14]. This dietary pattern is characterized by a high intake of vegetables, fruits, legumes, whole grains, nuts, and olive oil; moderate consumption of fish and dairy; and limited amounts of red and processed meats and sweets, features that are associated with reduced environmental impact compared with Western dietary models [15]. However, in Italy, adherence to this dietary benchmark has been declining, with a shift towards less healthy, more Westernized dietary patterns [16].

This study aims to assess the environmental impact of food consumption among Italian adults using data from the third Italian National Food Consumption Survey (INRAN-SCAI). Specifically, it aims to estimate the average daily CF and WF of the study sample, leveraging estimates from the SU-EATABLE LIFE (SEL) database [17]. Furthermore, the study aims to identify the food groups with the highest and lowest environmental impact and explore how dietary impact varies based on gender, age, body mass index (BMI), education level, geographic region, and adherence to the MD measured through the Mediterranean Adequacy Index (MAI).

Methods

Study population

We selected participants from the third INRAN-SCAI 2005–06, conducted by the National Research Institute on Food and Nutrition to ensure the representation of the general Italian population. The survey included 1329 households, aimed to assess national food consumption patterns across Italy’s four geographical areas (North-West, North-East, Centre, and South and Islands). Researchers collected data through a 3-day estimated food record, supported by a semi-structured diary. The methodology has been described in detail elsewhere [18]. In total, the study gathered food consumption data from 3323 individuals, of which 2831 adults. In addition to dietary data, we considered sociodemographic and anthropometric variables including age, sex, BMI, education level, and area of residence. The survey was observational and non-invasive, with ethical considerations limited to data collection on dietary habits potentially linked to health outcomes. At the time of the survey, the National Research Institute on Food and Nutrition was part of the National Statistical System and adhered to the principle of statistical confidentiality. As a Public Body, it complied with data protection regulations, and no additional ethical committee review was deemed necessary.

Dietary assessment and food grouping

The dataset from the third INRAN-SCAI survey included 878 single food items, classified according to the FoodEx2 system developed by the European Food Safety Authority to standardize food categorization for exposure assessments [19]. FoodEx2 follows a hierarchical structure comprising 21 primary food groups, which are further divided into progressively detailed subgroups, enabling classification based on both food origin and processing level. Categories range from Level 1 (broad classifications such as grains and grain-based products) to Level 7 (most specific subcategories such as rye bread and rolls). To better align with the study’s dietary objectives, we modified the standard classification, which originally grouped all meat and meat products into a single category. Specifically, we categorized Red Meat, Processed Meat, and Poultry separately, ensuring a more accurate representation of their distinct nutritional profiles and, more importantly, their environmental impacts, particularly in terms of CF and WF [20]. Figure S1 illustrates the contribution of food categories to the CF and WF.

Assigning environmental footprints values

Two researchers (N.S., A.P.) conducted a preliminary literature search in PubMed to identify common methodologies for assessing food-related environmental impacts and locate databases containing environmental footprint estimates derived from LCA. Based on this search, we selected the SEL database, developed by the Barilla Foundation and publicly accessible online, as the reference dataset for CF and WF values [17]. The SEL database provides footprint estimates for various food commodities, making it a suitable resource for environmental impact analysis.

Using data from the third INRAN-SCAI, we matched each food item (N = 878) with corresponding CF and WF data from the SEL database. When direct matches were unavailable (e.g. canned beer), we assigned median values from related food commodity subcategories as conversion factors. After excluding 48 infant foods, 87 dietary supplements, and 130 items lacking reliable CF and WF conversion factors (e.g. soy sauce, equine meat, and beef ham), accounting for approximately 35% of the initial food items, the final analysis included 617 food items (613 for the WF). Figure S2 illustrates the flowchart of the process. Finally, we calculated the environmental impact of each food item by multiplying the CF and WF conversion factors by the respective average daily consumption of each food product [21]. This methodology aligns with standardized procedures, ensuring harmonization across different studies [22].

Definition and components of CF

The CF quantifies the total greenhouse gas emissions associated with the production and distribution of food products, following a LCA approach [17]. This includes carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions from fossil fuel combustion, nitrous oxide (N2O) emissions from nitrogen-based fertilizers, and methane (CH4) emissions from livestock farming and waste decomposition. CF values are expressed in kilograms of CO2 equivalents per kilogram of food product (kg CO2 eq/kg), calculated using global warming potential (GWP) factors to standardize the impact of different greenhouse gases in terms of CO2 equivalence.

Definition and components of WF

The WF quantifies the total volume of freshwater across a food product’s life cycle using a LCA approach [17]. It consists of three components: Green Water (rainwater evapotranspired during crop growth), Blue Water (irrigation and industrial water use), and Grey Water (freshwater required to dilute pollutants to safe levels). WF values are expressed in litres per kilogram of food product (l/kg). Standardized methodological approaches were employed to ensure cross-study comparability [23].

MAI score

We evaluated adherence to the MD using the MAI score, developed by Alberti-Fidanza et al. [24] based on data from the Seven Countries Study of Cardiovascular Diseases [25]. The index calculates the ratio of total energy derived from Mediterranean foods (e.g. bread, cereals, fruits, vegetables, legumes, potatoes, fish, red wine, vegetable oils) to total energy derived from non-Mediterranean foods (e.g. milk, cheese, meat, eggs, animal fats, margarine, sugar, cakes, pies, cookies, sugar-sweetened beverages). We excluded foods presenting incompatibility with the MAI score framework (alcoholic beverages different from wine, mixed processed dishes, coffee) accounting for less than 7% of the total energy intake in the population. Subjects were categorized in high, intermediate and low adherence to the MD according to MAI tertiles.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics summarized the baseline characteristics of the participants, including mean, standard deviation (SD), median, and interquartile range (IQR), while absolute and percentage frequencies described qualitative variables. We performed the comparisons of CF and WF across sociodemographic variables using two-sided t-tests and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to examine differences by sex, BMI, geographical area of residence, and education level. We used multiple linear regression models to investigate associations between environmental impact indicators and individual characteristics, with CF and WF as the dependent variables in separate models. Independent variables included age, sex, BMI, educational level, geographical area, total energy intake, MAI score, and energy density of food consumed (kcal/kg). Statistical significance was set at P < .05. All analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.2).

Results

Participants characteristics

The characteristics of the study population, stratified by sex, BMI, level of education, geographical area of residence, and the MAI score are presented in Table 1. The study population consisted of 2831 participants, of whom 55.1% were women. The mean age of the sample was 48.2 years (SD = 17.1). The largest age group was 35–49 years (28.8%), followed by 18–34 years (24.7%) and 50–64 years (28.2%). Regarding BMI, the average was 24.6 kg/m2 (SD = 3.9). The majority of participants (57.6%) had a BMI within the normal weight range (18.5–24.9 kg/m2), while 31.2% and 8.6% showed BMI levels higher than 25 and 30. In terms of education, most participants have completed secondary education (59.5%), while 19.5% had tertiary education, and 15.2% had primary education. Geographically, 35.2% of participants came from Southern Italy and the Islands, 26.1% in the North-West, 19.8% in the North-East, and 19.0% in Central Italy. The mean MAI score of the sample population was 1.87 (SD = 2.0).

The mean WF was 3330.96 l (SD = 1024.6). The mean daily CO2 emissions per kcal consumed were 1.88 g CO2eq (SD = 0.05), while the average WF per kcal was 177.12 l (SD = 34.87). Water also accounted for a relevant share of the impact (mean = 0.33 kg CO2 eq, SD = 0.19). Other notable contributors included Fish (mean = 0.19 kg CO2 eq, SD = 0.24), Vegetables (0.16 kg CO2 eq, SD = 0.10), and Processed Meat (0.16 kg CO2 eq, SD = 0.20).

Regarding the WF, Dairy had the largest impact (mean = 708.62 l, SD = 428.75), followed by Red Meat and Others, Non-Processed (mean = 657.24 l, SD = 514.02), and Fat and Oils (mean = 520.35 l, SD = 198.85). Other significant contributors were Grain Products (mean = 348.01 l, SD = 144.57) and Non-Alcoholic Beverages (mean = 195.34 l, SD = 167.88).

Overall, more than 50% of the total CF is attributed to the consumption of Red Meat and Others, Non-Processed (30.56%) and Dairy Products (19.98%). Similarly, over 50% of the total WF impact is contributed by Dairy (21.27%), Red Meat and Others, Non-Processed (19.73%), and Fat and Oils (15.62%). Grain Products also contribute substantially to both CO2 (7.91%) and WF (10.45%) ranking as the fourth-largest contributor to the total environmental impact for both indicators. Determinants of environmental impact

Table 3 reports CF and WF values stratified by population characteristics, alongside the corresponding values of kcal/kg of food consumed. Men showed higher CF and WF than women (CF: 3.92 vs 3.21 kg CO2eq; WF: 3691.4 vs 3037.7 l). Younger adults (18–35 years) reported the highest environmental impact (CF: 3.76 kg CO2eq; WF: 3491.8 l), whereas participants aged ≥75 years showed the lowest (CF: 3.12 kg CO2eq; WF: 2948.3 l). Energy intake per kilogram of food followed a similar trend, reflecting a lower environmental impact of their dietary patterns. Participants with secondary education reported the highest CF (3.63 kg CO2eq), WF (3422.0 l), and dietary energy density (939.30 kcal/kg). Those with primary education showed the lowest values (CF: 3.26 kg CO2eq; WF: 3090.6 l). BMI categories did not differ significantly in CF or WF, and energy density remained relatively stable (875.67–942.50 kcal/kg). Regional patterns also emerged. Participants from Southern Italy and the Islands reported the lowest CF (3.17 kg CO2eq) and WF (3133.3 l), despite the highest energy intake per kilogram of food (986.90 kcal/kg). In contrast, individuals in the North-West and North-East had the highest CF (3.72 and 3.75 kg CO2eq) and WF (3398.0 and 3446.8 l), with lower dietary energy densities (889.75 and 922.43 kcal/kg). Total energy intake was associated with higher CF (β = 0.002 per kilocalorie, β = 0.001 per kilocalorie per 1 kg of food) and WF (β = 1.483 per kilocalorie, β = 0.296 per kilocalorie per 100 g of food) in the univariate analysis. Moreover, subjects with the highest adherence to the MD (above the population mean of 1.87) exhibited lower levels of CF and WF over a 3-day period. Specifically, those with higher adherence had a CF of 9.84 kg CO2eq (SD = 3.49) and a WF of 9356.0 l (SD = 2839.1), compared to those below the threshold, who showed a CF of 11.01 kg CO2eq (SD = 4.00) and a WF of 10 348.3 l (SD = 3149.2).

The relationship between demographic, dietary, and lifestyle-related factors and environmental impact indicators (CF and WF) was assessed using multiple linear regression models, as described in Table 4. Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive assessment of the environmental impact of dietary choices using data from the Third INRAN-SCAI survey, with a focus on CF and WF and their relationship with food groups and health determinants.

The averages daily environmental impacts align with prior research despite methodological differences [26–28] confirming animal-based products, especially red meat and dairy, as major contributors to environmental burden [29]. In Italy, meat accounts for over half of CF and WF, underscoring the need for sustainable dietary shifts.

Sociodemographic factors significantly influence dietary environmental footprints. Women exhibited lower CF and WF than men, likely due to a greater preference for plant-based foods and reduced intake of resource-intensive animal product, consistent with previous research suggesting that women generally adopt more environmentally sustainable diets, influenced by health awareness, cultural norms, and lifestyle choices [30]. Age was also a determinant of environmental impact, with individuals over 74 years displaying lower CF and WF with respect to youngers, reflecting reduced food intake and a more plant-based dietary pattern, as reported in previous literature [31]. BMI did not significantly affect environmental impact, although a non-significant trend of increasing calorie intake per kilogram of food consumed was observed. This is due to non-linear increase in total food intake across BMI categories, which may be attributable to the adherence to restrictive diets, attenuating the anticipated increase [32]. Educational attainment was not consistently associated with dietary emissions, corroborating previous literature that found mixed evidence [31]. Finally, geographical variation further highlights the complexity of dietary behaviours. Southern Italy exhibited lower CF, likely due to MD adherence, while north-east regions showed lower WF, possibly reflecting local food preferences and agricultural practices. Regional variations in dietary environmental impacts in Italy are influenced by the types and quantities of foods consumed but also by local food availability and culturally rooted dietary patterns [27]. Furthermore, this study confirms that higher adherence to the MD is associated with significantly lower CF and WF, reinforcing its role as a sustainable dietary model that preserves biodiversity and cultural heritage [33]. These findings suggest that targeted recommendations could support sustainability goals: young adults, men, and Northern residents, with higher CF and WF, may limit animal-based products and follow Mediterranean-style diets rich in legumes, grains, vegetables, and nuts. Participants with a MAI score above the population mean exhibited a lower average an environmental impact of 1.17 kg CO2eq of CF and 0.99 l of WF those scoring below the threshold. The multivariable analysis confirmed that following MD would lead to a reduced impact, independently on covariates and on potential different intake of kilocalories.

Our findings showed that younger adults and men consumed more calorie-dense diets, linked to higher CF and WF. Subjects should be aware of environmental impact per food quantity, when deciding to eat a certain quantity of food. Conversely, older adults and Southern residents had higher energy density but lower CF and WF, indicating that the link between energy density and environmental impact depends on food type. Calorie-dense plant-based foods (e.g. nuts, seeds, oils), integral to the MD, offer high nutrient density with a lower environmental impact than animal products, suggesting that managing dietary energy density could help individuals and policymakers reduce environmental impact while maintaining nutritional adequacy.

Italian dietary patterns have evolved over the past two decades due to socioeconomic changes, updated guidelines, the COVID-19 pandemic, and greater nutritional awareness. Studies report stable consumption of cereals, legumes, and poultry but declines in animal fats, beef, fruits, and vegetables, alongside increases in tropical oils, fish, and nuts [34, 35]. The present study establishes baseline environmental impacts preceding recent sustainability trends.

Some limitations of the present study must be stressed. First, while the environmental impact values were estimated through a rigorous statistical methodology, their imputation based on imputed environmental data with potential approximation, and partial life-cycle accounting excluding transport, storage, cooking, and waste. Another methodological constraint is the use of European-level food categories (FoodEx2) that does not distinguish foods with different environmental or nutritional footprints (e.g. pasta vs rice, or mixed dishes). This may affect results for key groups like cereals and composite foods, where variability can over- or underestimate CF and WF. Despite this uncertainty at the individual food level, overall population-level patterns and associations are likely robust. Furthermore, the exclusion of ultra-processed foods and alcohol in MAI scoring may slightly overestimate MD adherence, particularly in populations with high consumption of these products. This limitation suggests that the MAI slightly overestimate dietary sustainability for certain groups, so adherence levels should be interpreted cautiously, especially across subgroups with different alcohol and ultra-processed food intake. Despite these constraints, the study provides nationally representative, individual-level dietary data enabling temporal comparisons and subgroup analyses. The rigorous SEL methodology [17], ensures a robust, standardized assessment of dietary sustainability.

These findings form a critical descriptive foundation to guide strategies reducing individual dietary footprints and support evidence-based national and European policies.

Future research should incorporate socioeconomic variables such as income and physical activity, employ stronger health indicators, and expand environmental assessments to include biodiversity, land use, and energy consumption for a more comprehensive evaluation of dietary impacts.

This study underscores the importance of dietary interventions aimed at reducing the consumption of high-impact foods (i.e. animal-based products such as red meat and dairy) while promoting more sustainable alternatives. Transitioning towards more plant-based, sustainable dietary patterns, particularly those rich in legumes, starchy roots, and other low-impact foods, is essential not only for reducing the environmental burden of diets but also for addressing pressing global challenges such as climate change and water scarcity. Furthermore, these findings highlight the complexity of environmental impacts linked to dietary behaviours and emphasize the importance of considering multiple factors, including further sociodemographic and lifestyle variables, in future research. A comprehensive understanding of these factors is essential for developing effective strategies to reduce the environmental footprint of diets. Dietary changes, when combined with targeted intervention policies aimed at specific populations and strategic modifications in food systems, have the potential to promote more sustainable diets and address the urgent environmental challenges facing our planet.
