Quality indicators and turnaround time for blood culture in pediatric patients: 1 year of monitoring in 19 Chinese hospitals
Authors: Hanlei Wu, Wanting Liu, Yuan Peng, Liping Qu, Lijuan Lin, Biyun Mo, Xiwu Cao, Hongwei Zhou, Dingqiang Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBlood culture is the primary method for confirming bloodstream infections, yet quality indicators in pediatric blood cultures vary widely due to the lack of targeted management strategies. Therefore, this study aims to provide a foundation for optimizing blood culture quality management in pediatric patients by analyzing blood culture data from 19 hospitals.MethodsBlood culture data in pediatric patients, comprising a total of 44,397 specimens were included in statistical analysis from January 1 to December 31, 2021. Quality indicators, including positive rates, contamination rates, and specimen processing times, were evaluated along with the identification of common clinical microorganisms.ResultsThe positive rates, delivery rates, and contamination rates of blood cultures across 19 hospitals were analyzed. Before the blood culture test, specimen collection predominantly occurred between 10:00 a.m. and 00:59 a.m. the next day, while specimen reception and loading were mainly concentrated from 08:00 a.m. to 11:59 p.m. The pre-analytical turnaround time was the longest between 08:00 a.m. and 08:59 a.m. Gram staining, organism identification and AST reports were most frequently issued between 08:00 a.m. and 11:59 a.m. Among the identified microorganisms, 9 bacterial isolates (n = 929) and 1 Candida species (n = 52) were most common in pediatric patients. The turnaround times for AST reports were 2.90 (2.27–3.55) days for bacterial isolates and 3.88 (3.29–4.56) days for Candida species.ConclusionPediatric blood culture quality indicators showed significant inter-hospital variability. The multiple steps involved in blood culture processing contribute to delays in report issuance, indicating the need for further optimization of the pediatric blood culture workflow.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-025-06279-z.

Introduction

Bloodstream infections (BSIs) are severe systemic diseases and one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality in pediatric patients [1, 2]. The challenge of BSI management lies in the prompt and precise identification of the causative pathogen, as delays or inaccuracies in diagnosis can lead to ineffective antibiotic treatment [3]. Blood culture (BC) remains the gold standard for the etiological diagnosis of BSI, offering vital information to guide clinical decision [4]. The quality of BC processes is typically assessed through several key indicators, including turnaround time (TAT), positive rates, delivery rates, contamination rates, and blood volume, all of which are instrumental in ensuring the accuracy and reliability of diagnostic outcomes [5].

Empirical antibiotics are often started before pathogen identification, which may lead to inappropriate treatment. Clinical data reveal that approximately 46% of empirical antibiotic treatments are suboptimal, increasing mortality by 35% and raising resistance risks [6, 7]. Consequently, shortening the time for BC collection, rapid identification of BC positive strains, and antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) results is crucial. Accelerating these processes has been shown to reduce treatment duration and hospital costs, while improving patient outcomes [8, 9]. Therefore, improving the quality of BC along with accelerating the rapid identification of positive BC strains and AST results are essential for the effective BSI diagnosis and treatment, as well as for minimizing adverse events from antibiotic overuse [10].

Pediatric BC presents unique challenges, primarily due to the physiological and immunological differences between children and adults. Children, particularly those under five years old and school-aged, exhibit higher rates of viral and bacterial infections and possess a less mature immune system, making accurate diagnosis and treatment crucial [11]. The blood collection process in children is often complicated by their limited cooperation, increasing the risk of sample contamination with normal flora. Moreover, the spectrum of pathogens causing infections in children differs from that in adults, and pediatric infections often present with atypical symptoms, complicating the diagnostic process. Thus, BC plays a pivotal role in confirming diagnoses and guiding the selection of appropriate antimicrobial therapies for pediatric patients, ultimately leading to personalized treatment plans that improve clinical outcomes [12, 13].

In China, awareness of pediatric BC quality varies significantly. Differences in positive rates, procedures, and equipment across hospitals highlight the need for standardization [14]. In response to this need, we conducted a multicenter investigation across 19 hospitals in China, aiming to provide a robust scientific foundation for improving the pediatric BC management by analyzing key indicators, thereby enhancing diagnostic accuracy and therapeutic efficacy for BSIs in children.

Materials and methods

Data collection

Data on 44,397 BC specimens were collected between January 1 and December 31, 2021, from 19 general teaching hospitals in Guangdong Province, China. Through a retrospective study, the data of BC laboratory codes, collection times, reception times, loading times, and times to Gram staining report, organism identification report, AST report were extracted for subsequent research. During screening, records with incomplete data were excluded.

The study statistically analyzed the positive rate, the delivery rate, the contamination rate of BC, as well as the TAT for BC pre-analysis, the time to Gram staining report, the time to organism identification report, and the time to AST report. The 19 general teaching hospitals are denoted by letter A-S. Four hospitals used VITEK 2 and Phoenix M50 for identifications, while 15 hospitals used MALDI-TOF MS. Antimicrobial susceptibility testings were routinely performed with VITEK 2 (bioMérieux, Marcy I’Etoile, France) or Phoenix system (BD Biosciences, Sparks, MD, USA).

BC collection

BC collection in all participating hospitals was conducted in accordance with the Chinese Expert Consensus on Standardized Blood Culture Collection in Pediatric Patients [14]. When the body weight and total blood volume in patients are sufficient, two sets of BCs are recommended. Typically, one pediatric aerobic bottle is used per set, and anaerobic bottles are added if there is clinical suspicion of anaerobic infection. For infants and young children, the volume of blood collected per set should not exceed 1% of their total blood volume [15].

Definitions

TAT

A retrospective analysis of the processing time of positive BC was performed in this study. The following different BC indicators were measured: (1) collection time i.e. the time when the specimen was collected by the medical staff; (2) reception time i.e. the time when the specimen was received in the microbiology laboratory; (3) loading time i.e. the time when the specimen was loaded into the BC instrument; (4) time to Gram staining report i.e. the time from specimen collection to Gram staining results; (5) time to organism identification i.e. the time from specimen collection to organism identification results; (6) time to AST report i.e. the time from specimens collection to the AST results (7). Pre-analytical times i.e. specimen collection, reception and loading stages are included.

In order to compare the differences in TAT of 10 microorganisms (including 9 bacteria and 1 fungus), TAT in pediatric BCs were statistically analyzed. Other types of fungi, including Fusarium and non-Candida albicans species, were also detected in this study; however, due to their low proportions, they were excluded from the analysis. When multiple microorganisms are detected in a BC vial, TAT was analyzed as the time when the AST results of all microorganisms were reported.

Positive rate

According to the Expert Consensus on Clinical Practice of Blood Culture Technology for the Diagnosis of Bloodstream Infection, the positive rate is the ratio of the number of positive sets of BC to the total number of BC sets in a unit time (such as monthly, quarterly, annual). A set is defined as blood drawn from a puncture site and injected into one or more BC vials (usually including aerobic and anaerobic vials).

Delivery rate

Due to the particularity of children patients, only the proportion of single and equal or more than two bottles of BC submitted for examination in each hospital were counted.

Contamination rate

It refers to the proportion of BC specimens that are falsely identified as positive due to the presence of non-pathogenic organisms, typically introduced during the specimen collection process. Following previous researches [16, 17], a single positive bottle in a double-bottle submissions for coagulase-negative staphylococci (CoNS), Micrococcus, Corynebacterium, Propionibacterium, or Bacillus spp. was considered contamination in this study.

Moreover, contaminant organisms are generally characterized by a longer time to positivity, which may serve as an important criterion to distinguish them from true pathogens causing bacteremia [18].

The contamination rate was calculated as the number of contaminated sets divided by the total number of BC sets submitted. Hospital J was excluded from the contamination rate analysis due to its extremely small sample size (n = 23) and the absence of any suspected contaminating cultures.

Unable-to-judge rate

When only a single blood culture bottle was submitted and tested positive for a commonly recognized contaminant, the result was considered unable to judge whether it represented true bacteremia or contamination, due to the absence of a paired sample for comparison. It was calculated by the number of unable-to-judge sets divided by the total number of BC sets submitted. Hospital J was excluded from the contamination rate analysis due to its extremely small sample size (n = 23).

Pediatric patients grouping

Pediatric patients were divided into “Children” and “Infants” groups based on the different departments. “Children Group” includes data from pediatric-related wards, the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU), and pediatric outpatient and emergency departments; the “Infants Group” includes data from the Neonatology Department, Baby-Friendly Wards, and the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU). Hospital G, I, P and S were excluded due to the unclear divisions of pediatric departments.

Data processing and statistical analysis

Microsoft Excel was used to create a PivotTable to calculate total test bottles, sets, and positive BC bottles and sets, determining the positive rate as positive BC sets/total BC sets. The delivery rate was calculated based on single-bottle sets and the proportion of multi-set submissions by each hospital. Time periods for each specimen turnaround stage were extracted, and time differences were calculated using TEXT and SUBSTITUTE functions. SPSS software was used to calculate TATs (quartile range - IQR, 25th and 75th percentiles) by using the weighted average method or the median method.

Correlations between the delivery rates and the unable-to-judge rates were analyzed using R. Due to the highly skewed distribution of the unable-to-judge rate, a log10 transformation was applied prior to correlation analysis to improve data normality and linearity. Pearson test was applied, and a p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. In addition, statistical comparisons of the distribution of common microorganisms between children and infants were also performed using R. Fisher’s exact test was applied, and a p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Quality indicators in BC

Between January 1, 2021, and December 31, 2021, data from 31,938 pediatric patients were collected across 19 teaching hospitals, encompassing a total of 44,397 BC specimens. The overall average positive rate for these BCs was 4.08%. As shown in Fig. 1A, the positive rates of BCs in pediatric patients across the 19 hospitals ranged from 1.32% to 10.55%.

The delivery rates of BC specimens are presented in Fig. 1B and detailed in Supplementary Table 1. The proportion of BC specimens collected with two or more vials varied widely, ranging from 0.39% to 100.00%. Notably, the data of nine hospitals showed that over 50% of their specimens were collected with two or more vials, while seven hospitals showed that less than 10% of their specimens were collected with two or more vials.

Figure 1C shows the contamination rate that could be determined based on blood culture submissions in each hospital, defined as cases where one of two submitted bottles tested positive for common contaminants; as well as the unable-to-judge rate, defined as single-bottle submissions that tested positive for common contaminants. Notably, even when unable-to-judge cases are considered contamination, most hospitals remain within the generally accepted threshold for contamination rate (< 3%) [19]. Moreover, contamination and unable-to-judge rates varied substantially across hospitals, which is most likely influenced by the wide variation in delivery rates among institutions.

Furthermore, we performed correlation analyses to investigate the association between the delivery rate and unable-to-judge rate. Strikingly, there was a statistically significant moderate negative correlation between the double bottle submission rate and the log10-transformed unable-to-judge rate (Fig. 1D, Pearson r =–0.495, p < 0.05), indicating that hospitals with a higher proportion of double-bottle blood culture submissions tended to have fewer positive results that could not be definitively classified as either contamination or true bacteremia.

BC pre-analytical times

Pre-analytical times of BC are essential in identifying issues involved in BC procedure, which is conducive to shortening the TAT in the whole process of BC, so as to enhance the diagnostic efficiency and treatment outcomes of BSI in children. Figure 2A illustrates the hourly frequency distribution of specimen collection, reception, and loading times in pediatric BC. The peak times for specimen collection, reception, and loading were 11:00–11:59, 14:00–14:59, and 08:00–08:59, respectively, accounting for 6.7%, 8.5%, and 12.4% of all specimens. Specimen collection predominantly occurred between 10:00 a.m. and 00:59 a.m. the following day, while reception and loading were mainly concentrated between 08:00 a.m. and 11:59 p.m. Lower activity was observed for specimen collection between 01:00 a.m. and 09:59 a.m. and for specimen reception and loading between 00:00 a.m. and 07:59 a.m.

Figure 2B presents the TAT from specimen collection to reception in the microbiology laboratory. The median TAT exceeded 2 h between 06:00 a.m. and 10:00 a.m. and was under 2 h during other periods. Figure 2C details the TAT from specimen collection to loading, with a median TAT exceeding 2 h between 03:00 a.m. and 09:59 a.m. During this period, the TAT was under 4 h between 07:00 a.m. and 09:59 a.m., but some specimens had a TAT exceeding 18 h, highlighting the need for close monitoring and attention to these outliers. TAT analysis of three processes involved in BC reporting

The reporting time distribution of positive BC was initially analyzed in 4-hour intervals. As shown in Fig. 3A, the majority of Gram staining, organism identification, and AST reports were issued between 08:00 a.m. and 11:59 a.m., accounting for 58.77%, 52.32%, and 64.45% of the total reports, respectively. The fewest Gram staining and organism identification reports were issued between 00:00 a.m. and 03:59 a.m., while the lowest number of AST reports were issued between 04:00 a.m. and 07:59 a.m.

Subsequently, the reporting times were analyzed on an hourly basis. Figure 3B demonstrates that the highest proportion of Gram staining, organism identification, and AST reports were issued between 09:00 a.m. and 09:59 a.m., representing 19.6%, 23.0%, and 19.3% of the reports, respectively. The number of reports issued increased sharply starting at 07:00, peaking between 09:00 a.m. and 09:59 a.m. Following this peak, the report volume gradually declined from 10:00 to 13:59, before rising again to a second peak between 17:00 and 17:59. Notably, the number of reports issued between 07:00 a.m. and 05:59 p.m. was significantly higher than during other time periods.

The amount of valid data for Gram staining, organism identification and AST reports of positive BCs in children were 1006, 434 and 1005, respectively. TATs for these reports are shown in Fig. 3C. The median for Gram staining reports TAT was 1.45 (0.90–2.69) days, for organism identification reports was 1.83 (1.29–2.40) days, and for AST reports was 3.15 (2.49–5.70) days.

Pathogen spectrum and age-related differences in pediatric BCs

To compare the distribution and TAT of BC-positive isolates, we analyzed the 10 most common isolates (n = 929). Ranked by frequency, these isolates included CoN Staphylococcus, Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Staphylococcus aureus, Candida, Enterococcus faecium and Enterococcus faecalis, Salmonella, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Streptococcus pneumoniae and Micrococcus luteus, collectively accounting for 71.2% of all isolates. A total of 877 bacterial strains were identified, of which 54.8% (481/877) were Gram-positive and 45.2% (396/877) were Gram-negative. 52 Candida were isolated, representing 5.60% of the 10 most common isolates. The proportion of isolates subjected to AST varied from 58.6% (CoN Staphylococcus) to 82.0% (Enterococcus faecium and Enterococcus faecalis), while 76.9% of Candida isolates underwent AST (Table 1). The median TAT for ASTs differed by microorganism, with the median TATs for common microorganisms ranging from 2.09 days (Escherichia coli) to 3.88 days (Candida) (Fig. 4A).

In addition, the proportions of 11 common pediatric BC pathogens among total positive cases were calculated for children and infants. Fisher’s exact test was used to analyze the differences between the two groups (Fig. 4B). According to the statistical analysis, CoN staphylococci and Streptococcus agalactiae (group B Streptococcus) accounted for significantly higher proportions in positive BCs from infants compared to children (p < 0.05). Discussion

BC results are critical for guiding pediatricians in selecting appropriate antibiotics or anti-fungal agents to treat BSI. Enhancing BC quality and expediting the issuance of AST reports facilitate the diagnosis and treatment of pediatric patients within the optimal therapeutic window. These improvements not only enhance diagnostic accuracy and treatment efficacy but also reduce the risk of treatment failures and complications associated with delayed diagnosis. Therefore, it is imperative to focus on optimizing BC quality [20–22].

The current study found that pediatric BC positive rates ranging from 1.32% to 10.55%. The large variation in BC positivity rates observed among the 19 hospitals may be attributed to several factors. Firstly, the delay between sample collection and incubation may lead to a decline in pathogen viability and false negative results. In pediatric settings, the physical distance between clinical wards and laboratories, especially in the case of manual transportation, may further exacerbate such delays. Secondly, the differences in the types of BC bottles used by various hospitals may affect their performance. For instance, changes in the neutralizing ability of resin-based antibiotics may affect the recovery of pathogens, especially in patients who have previously received antibacterial treatment. Thirdly, hospitals with higher single-bottle submission rates may experience reduced detection sensitivity, as limited blood volume and the lack of confirmatory cultures can lead to underestimation of true bacteremia. These technical and procedural disparities likely contribute to the inter-hospital variation in positivity rates observed in this study.

Notably, according to the previous research results of our team, the overall positive rate of BC in both adults and children ranged from 5.1% to 13.8%, which was notably higher than the pediatric positive rates observed in this study under similar hospital conditions [23]. This disparity may be due to the atypical clinical manifestations of pediatric diseases, leading to fewer BC tests being ordered. Additionally, children and adults are generally affected by different pathogens and have distinct disease spectrums. Pediatric blood collection techniques and specimen processing may also involve unique challenges, requiring more meticulous handling to avoid external contamination or specimen degradation, which could impact the reliability of BC results and clinical decision-making [24].

Chinese guidelines recommend collecting 2–3 BC sets per patient [14]. However, the data from this study indicate that single sets from one puncture site were more common in pediatric patients. Variations in double-set rate and differences in BC procedures among different centers may contribute to the significant discrepancies in positive and contamination rates observed across the 19 centers.

Impressively, there is a statistically significant moderate negative correlation between the double-bottle submission rate and the log-transformed unable-to-judge rate. This finding supports the hypothesis that increasing the proportion of double bottle blood culture submissions may reduce the likelihood of obtaining results that cannot be confidently interpreted. Paired blood culture sets not only allow for more reliable identification of true bloodstream infections but also facilitate the recognition of likely contaminants, particularly when only one of the two bottles is positive. In contrast, when only a single bottle is submitted and yields a positive result for a potential contaminant, clinicians are often left without adequate microbiological context to interpret the finding. Consequently, optimizing submission practices could improve diagnostic accuracy and reduce reporting uncertainty.

Pre-analytical TAT significantly affects the time to final AST report issuance. The Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) suggests that the allowed maximum TAT from the collection to loading is 2 h [25]. However, our study revealed that between 3:00 and 9:59, the median TAT exceeded 2 h. It is possible that the number of specimens decreased in the second half of the night, resulting in less frequency of specimen transportation and specimen loading. It may also cause by the absence of night shifts in some microbiology laboratories. Additionally, the pre-analytical TAT between 7:00 a.m. and 9:59 a.m. exceeded 4 h, probably due to the large amount of specimens in the morning, which led to the delays in specimen transfer and processing.

The median TATs for Gram staining, organism identification, and AST reports were approximately 2 days, 2 days, and 3 days, respectively. The prolonged TAT for Gram staining may be due to differences in night-shift protocols across hospitals. For example, some institutions process all BC bottles that turn positive overnight only in the following morning, which delays the release of Gram staining results. This practice may partly explain why there was no significant difference observed between the TATs for Gram staining and organism identification.

Molecular diagnostic techniques are increasingly used to diagnose BSI and identify bacterial resistance, playing a key role in early infection detection, acute infection assessment, and resistance analysis [26, 27]. For example, droplet digital PCR enables rapid detection of common pathogens and resistance genes, while metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) is also effective for BSI diagnosis [28]. PCR-based identification panels reduce time to pathogen identification and antimicrobial treatment initiation [29]. These techniques enhance detection sensitivity and shorten reporting time, offering promising advancements for timely patient treatment.

Statistical analysis of BC data and timely publication of results can significantly influence operational workflows in clinical microbiology laboratories, ultimately improving BC quality [30]. Microbiology laboratories should actively engage in antimicrobial stewardship to shorten the time required for microbial identification and AST reporting, thereby minimizing the duration of broad-spectrum antibiotic use in infected patients [31]. Consequently, improving the quality of BC specimens, optimizing BC processing process, and accelerating the speed of issuing pathogenic microorganism reports at all steps are all significant advances for the diagnosis and treatment of BSI. These measures provide an important foundation for enhancing treatment efficacy, reducing antibiotic overuse and minimizing complication rates.

Limitations

Data on BC from the 19 hospitals were extracted via the information system for statistical analysis. However, some data sets were incomplete; if they lacked critical time points for specimen processing, they were excluded from the final analysis, which may affect the comprehensiveness of our findings. Moreover, this study lacked the clinical data of patients, which may have limited the accuracy of distinguishing between true infections and contamination. If all single-bottle submissions that yielded commonly recognized contaminants were counted toward the contamination rate, it may lead to a slight overestimation of contamination. Another limitation is that, due to the lack of specific age data in our database, pediatric patients could only be categorized into children and infants based on the departments that submitted BCs. Although this approach may have led to misclassification of patients near the age cutoff, as well as some pediatric outpatient and emergency cases, the overall impact is likely minimal due to the relatively small number of blood cultures submitted from these patient groups. Additionally, while blood collection volume is a recognized quality indicator in BC, it was not statistically evaluated in this study due to data limitations. It is also important to note that the positive rate of BC can be influenced by multiple factors beyond those we analyzed. Furthermore, while this study focused on positive rates, contamination rates, delivery rates, and workflow processes, it did not account for potential differences in BC instruments used across hospitals, which could have influenced the findings.

Conclusion

The positive rate, contamination rate and delivery rate of BC in children varied significantly different across the 19 hospitals, highlighting the need for standardized BC procedures to improve diagnostic accuracy and treatment effectiveness. Before a BC report is issued, specimens undergo three critical stages: collection, reception, and loading. Prolonged TAT at any of these stages can delay the issuance of subsequent reports. In this study, the issuance times for positive BC reports in children were predominantly concentrated in the morning, with a secondary peak in the afternoon. The median TATs for Gram staining, organism identification, and AST reports were approximately 2 days, 2 days, and 3 days, respectively. The most commonly isolated microorganisms in positive pediatric BCs included CoN Staphylococcus, Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Staphylococcus aureus, Enterococcus faecium and Enterococcus faecalis, Salmonella, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Gambogic micrococcus and Candida, with a median TAT for AST reports of about 3 days. In order to improve the diagnostic accuracy and therapeutic outcomes of BSI in children, the workflow of BC in children still needs to be further optimized.
