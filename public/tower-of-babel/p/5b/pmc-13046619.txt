Development and validation of an interpretable machine learning model for predicting portal vein system thrombosis following splenectomy in Wilson’s disease
Authors: Yi Shen, Zhou Zheng, Hui-cong Min, Hui Peng, Long Huang, Wan-zong Zhang, Hui Feng, Qing-sheng Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPost-splenectomy portal vein system thrombosis (PVST) is a serious complication that adversely affects patient prognosis. The aim of this study was to develop and validate a machine learning-based model for early risk prediction of PVST after splenectomy. A total of 594 patients who underwent splenectomy were included in this study. The data were randomly divided into a training set (n = 417) and a validation set (n = 177) in a 7:3 ratio. Clinical characteristics associated with PVST risk were screened using LASSO regression and univariate analysis. Seven machine learning algorithms were applied for model training, and the models’ performance on the validation set was evaluated using the area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, and F1 score. SHAP interpretability analyses and web-based deployment were used to derive clinical insights. In the training set, the key variables identified included splenomegaly grade, splenic thickness, portal vein flow velocity (PVV), portal vein diameter (PVD), portal vein flow (PVF), splenic vein diameter (SVD), postoperative platelet count (POD PLT), and postoperative D-dimer level (POD D-dimer). The XGBoost model outperformed the logistic regression (LR) and random forest (RF) models, achieving an AUC of 0.971 (95% CI: 0.950–0.987), accuracy of 92.8%, sensitivity of 91.2%, and specificity of 94.6%. In the validation set, the XGBoost model exhibited stable performance, with an AUC of 0.970, accuracy of 91.5%, precision of 89.9%, sensitivity of 88.6%, and specificity of 93.5%. the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) summary analysis indicated that PVV, PVF, and POD D-dimer were the top three predictors of PVST risk (SHAP Value = 0.076, 0.074 and 0.064). SHAP dependency plots and force plots provided explanations of the model’s predictions at the feature and individual levels, respectively. The XGBoost model demonstrated excellent discrimination and calibration for predicting PVST risk after splenectomy. Introduction

Wilson disease (WD) is an autosomal recessive disorder of copper metabolism caused by mutations in the ATP7B gene, resulting in impaired biliary copper excretion and pathological accumulation in the liver and other tissues. Its incidence is approximately 1 in 30,000–40,000 [1]. Excess copper deposition in hepatocytes can trigger inflammation, fibrosis, and cirrhosis, which may progress to portal hypertension, splenomegaly, and hypersplenism. Hypersplenism frequently causes pancytopenia, which, in severe cases, necessitates interruption of copper-chelating therapy and compromises quality of life and disease management [2, 3]. To mitigate hypersplenism while sustaining copper-chelating treatment, splenectomy is often considered an effective intervention. However, postoperative PVST remains a common and serious complication. PVST can impair hepatic perfusion, leading to ascites, gastrointestinal bleeding, and further decompensation of liver function, thereby worsening patient prognosis [4]. Although prophylactic antiplatelet agents (e.g., aspirin) or anticoagulants (e.g., low-molecular-weight heparin, warfarin) are commonly employed, optimal timing remains challenging: early administration risks bleeding, while delayed use diminishes preventive efficacy [5]. Therefore, developing a robust predictive model to stratify high-risk patients and identify key risk factors is clinically essential for optimizing PVST prevention and management.

Several studies have developed risk prediction models for post-splenectomy PVST using traditional risk factors. These investigations have identified splenomegaly or increased splenic volume, enlarged splenic vein diameter, enlarged portal vein diameter, reduced portal vein blood flow velocity, and hypercoagulable states (e.g., elevated D-dimer levels and sharp postoperative increases in platelet count) as correlates of postoperative PVST [6–8]. Similarly, Yao et al. developed a nomogram based on 81 patients with Wilson’s disease, revealing that increased preoperative portal vein diameter, decreased blood flow velocity, elevated postoperative D-dimer levels, and increased platelet counts were independent predictors [9]. Although these statistical models have enabled the identification of high-risk patients and informed prophylactic strategies, their accuracy has been limited by sample heterogeneity and unaccounted variable interactions [10]. Moreover, reliance on logistic regression—primarily suited to linear relationships—hinders the capture of complex nonlinear patterns and adaptive learning [11]. Consequently, these methodological limitations constrain the models’ predictive performance and broader clinical applicability.

Machine learning overcomes the limitations of traditional statistical methods by capturing complex nonlinear relationships, handling high-dimensional data, enabling adaptive learning, and assessing feature importance. It can automatically derive insights from data, optimize models, and enhance prediction accuracy. Machine learning is widely applied in fields such as medicine and finance to support clinical decision-making [12]. However, studies using machine learning for post-splenectomy portal vein system thrombosis (PVST) remain scarce. Our previous research demonstrated that postoperative hemodynamic and coagulation alterations are key contributors to PVST following splenectomy for Wilson’s disease (WD)-associated portal hypertension, although these analyses were confined to traditional predictive models [13, 14]. Therefore, the present study employed seven machine learning algorithms to develop and evaluate predictive models using data from post-splenectomy WD patients treated at our institution over the past decade, with internal validation. The optimal model was further refined and visualized using static nomograms and dynamic network diagrams to facilitate individualized clinical predictions.

Materials and methods

Study design and patients

This retrospective cohort study included 594 patients with Wilson’s disease who underwent splenectomy at the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui University of Traditional Chinese Medicine from January 2016 to June 2025. The patients were randomly divided into a training set (n = 417) and a validation set (n = 177) in a 7:3 ratio. In the training set, the median age was 28.0 years (206 males, 211 females). The Child-Pugh classification included 223 class A and 194 class B cases, with PVST occurring in 166 cases (versus no PVST in 251 cases) (Fig. 1).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion Criteria: (1) Patients diagnosed with WD who exhibited clinical manifestations of splenomegaly and hypersplenism. (2) Patients who underwent laparoscopic or open splenectomy. (3) Patients aged 18–65 years with Child-Pugh class A or B (patients with class C were adjusted to class B). (4) Patients with complete preoperative and postoperative data, including complete blood counts, coagulation profiles, biochemical indices, and imaging findings.

Exclusion Criteria: (1) Patients with preoperative diagnosis of PVST (including thrombosis of the portal vein, splenic vein, or mesenteric vein) confirmed by color Doppler ultrasound, computed tomography (CT), or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). (2) Patients with concomitant hepatocellular carcinoma, pancreatic tumors, or those who underwent splenectomy combined with resection of other organs (e.g., cholecystectomy or hepatectomy). (3) Patients with splenomegaly or hypersplenism attributable to other causes (e.g., infectious diseases, hematological disorders, or autoimmune diseases).

Collection of feature indicators

Characteristic indicators for splenectomy patients were extracted from electronic medical records and preoperative/postoperative assessments, encompassing demographic, clinical, laboratory, and hemodynamic parameters to predict postoperative portal vein system thrombosis (PVST). Demographic characteristics included age, gender, and body mass index (BMI). Clinical characteristics encompassed Child-Pugh classification, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) score, and comorbidities (diabetes, hypertension, smoking history, ascites). Laboratory parameters included coagulation indices: preoperative D-dimer (pre-op D-dimer), D-dimer on postoperative day 14 (POD D-dimer), international normalized ratio (INR), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), prothrombin time (PT), and fibrinogen (FIB); hematological indices: white blood cell count (WBC), red blood cell count (RBC), preoperative platelet count (pre-op PLT), platelet count on postoperative day 7 (POD PLT), and hematocrit (HCT); liver function tests: aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), total protein (TP), albumin (ALB), and total bilirubin (TBIL); renal function tests: blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and creatinine (CRE); electrolytes: potassium (K⁺), sodium (Na⁺), and calcium (Ca²⁺); lipid profile: cholesterol (Chol) and triglycerides (TG); and amylase (Amy). Hemodynamic characteristics included portal vein diameter (PVD), portal vein velocity (PVV), portal vein flow (PVF), and splenic vein diameter (SVD). Surgical features comprised the operation method (splenectomy alone [SA] or splenectomy with esophagogastric devascularization [SEGD]), anatomic splenectomy, intraoperative hemorrhage, and operation duration. Laboratory and hemodynamic tests were performed according to methods described in previous studies [13, 14]. The outcome variable was PVST, diagnosed by imaging (ultrasound, CT, or MRI) within 3 months after surgery.

PVST definition

Portal vein system thrombosis (PVST) after splenectomy is defined as thrombus formation in the portal, splenic, or mesenteric vein, resulting in blood flow obstruction. It is diagnosed by imaging (e.g., ultrasound, CT, or MRI) within 3 months postoperatively, manifesting as a hypoechoic mass or filling defect in the vessel lumen. PVST may occur with or without clinical symptoms, such as abdominal pain, distension, or hepatic function abnormalities [15].

Data preprocessing and feature selection

To ensure the completeness and reliability of the dataset, variables with > 30% missing data were excluded. Missing values in variables with ≤ 30% missingness were imputed using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE). The complete dataset was then randomly partitioned into training (70%) and validation (30%) sets. Within the training set, feature selection was performed using univariate analysis combined with LASSO regression to identify overlapping significant predictors. Core features strongly associated with PVST were finalized by integrating evidence from prior literature and established clinical guidelines.

Model development and assessment

Based on the screened features, seven machine learning algorithms were applied to the training set to develop risk prediction models: logistic regression (LR), decision tree (DT), random forest (RF), extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), light gradient boosting machine (LightGBM), support vector machine (SVM), and artificial neural network (ANN). Hyperparameters were optimized using grid search combined with 5-fold cross-validation (Suppl 1). The best-performing model was then constructed accordingly.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, calibration plots, and decision curve analyses (DCA) were generated for each model on the validation set. Discriminative ability was assessed using the area under the ROC curve (AUC), while calibration (i.e., the agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes) was evaluated via calibration curves and Brier scores. Net clinical benefit was quantified using DCA. Additional performance metrics included accuracy, precision, sensitivity, specificity, F1 score, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV). Ultimately, the best-performing models were selected based on these multidimensional metrics to evaluate their generalizability.

SHAP explanation of the model

Machine learning algorithms are often regarded as ‘black boxes,’ making explainability approaches necessary. Therefore, the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) method was used to quantify each feature’s contribution to model predictions. Global interpretations were achieved through feature importance rankings and summary (beeswarm) plots. Dependency plots visualized the nonlinear relationships between key features and the outcome, while local interpretations of individual predictions were provided through force plots.

Web application

A web-based calculator was developed to predict the risk of postoperative PVST in WD patients with splenomegaly and hypersplenism who are undergoing splenectomy. The tool was built using the Streamlit framework and implemented with the XGBoost model, which demonstrated superior performance. To enhance usability, an interactive panel was created for selecting and inputting the eight core variables, allowing for rapid prediction of outcome events.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.5.1) and Python (version 3.10.4). The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Continuous variables with a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared between groups using the independent t-test. Variables with a skewed distribution were expressed as median (interquartile range) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequency (percentage) and compared using the χ² test; if the expected frequencies were < 5, Fisher’s exact test was applied. All tests were two-sided, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 594 splenectomy patients were enrolled in this study, of whom 166 (27.9%) developed postoperative PVST. Patients were randomly divided into a training set (n = 417) and a validation set (n = 177) in a 7:3 ratio; the study flowchart is shown in Fig. 1. Differences between the training and validation sets in clinical characteristics, preoperative parameters, surgical methods, and postoperative data were not statistically significant (all P > 0.05), indicating good comparability between the sets. In the training set, patients with PVST exhibited a greater grade of splenomegaly (P < 0.001), greater splenic thickness (P < 0.001), and significantly higher postoperative D-dimer (P = 0.014), WBC count (P = 0.042), and PLT count (P = 0.030) than those without PVST. Regarding hemodynamics, PVD and SVD were increased in the PVST group (P = 0.005 and P = 0.008, respectively), whereas PVV and PVF were decreased (both P < 0.001). Differences in other biochemical and surgical metrics were not significant. Overall, baseline characteristics were balanced between the training and validation sets, while splenomegaly grade, postoperative hypercoagulability, and hemodynamic alterations were strongly associated with the development of PVST, as shown in Table 1.

Selection of characteristic variables

In the training set, feature selection was performed using LASSO regression combined with univariate analysis on candidate variables. In LASSO regression, as the λ value increased, the coefficients for some variables progressively shrank to zero (Fig. 2A). The optimal λ, defined as one standard error from the minimum via 10-fold cross-validation, was 0.032 (Fig. 2B). The intersection of results from both methods yielded 8 overlapping variables: grade of splenomegaly, splenic thickness, PVV, PVD, PVF, SVD, POD PLT, and POD D-dimer (Fig. 2C).

For grade of splenomegaly, compared with grade I, grades II (OR = 1.948, 95% CI: 1.079–3.516, P = 0.027) and III (OR = 3.892, 95% CI: 2.128–7.117, P < 0.001) were associated with an increased risk. Model Evaluation

Figure 4 presents confusion matrices for multiple machine learning models, illustrating their predictive performance. The XGBoost model demonstrated strong overall performance (accuracy = 92.64%, sensitivity = 88.57%, specificity = 91.43%). Figure 5 depicts the comprehensive performance of the XGBoost model in predicting PVST after splenectomy. In the validation set, model performance remained stable, with an AUC of 0.970 (95% CI: 0.950–0.987), accuracy of 0.915, precision of 0.899, sensitivity of 0.886, and specificity of 0.935. Calibration curves indicated excellent agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes (Hosmer-Lemeshow test P = 0.684; Brier score = 0.104), confirming good calibration. DCA showed that the XGBoost model yielded the highest net benefit across threshold probabilities of 0.1–0.8 (maximum net benefit ≈ 0.36), outperforming other models. The precision-recall (PR) curve (average precision [AP] = 0.956) and F1 score (0.892) further validated the model’s balanced recall and precision, underscoring its robust generalizability and clinical utility. Similarly, Table 3 highlights the superior performance of the XGBoost model among all algorithms (F1 = 0.892, Cohen’s kappa = 0.822, Youden’s J = 0.820), reflecting the strongest classification agreement and predictive reliability. In summary, the XGBoost model excelled in discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility, and was selected as the final predictive model for subsequent SHAP feature interpretation and online risk prediction tool development.

SHAP interpretation of the model

To further elucidate the impact of each variable on postoperative PVST predictions, SHAP analysis was performed based on the optimal XGBoost model. As shown in Fig. 6B, the feature contributions decreased in the following order: PVV, PVF, POD D-dimer, PVD, POD PLT, splenic thickness, SVD, and grade of splenomegaly. Figure 6A illustrates that elevated SVD, PVD, splenic thickness, grade of splenomegaly, POD D-dimer, and POD PLT-indicative of splenomegaly, venous dilation, and postoperative hypercoagulability-were associated with an increased risk of PVST (SHAP value > 0). Conversely, higher PVV and PVF contributed negatively to PVST formation (SHAP value < 0). Finally, force plots were used to verify the interpretability: a patient who developed PVST had a high composite SHAP score (0.61; Fig. 6 C), while a patient without PVST had a lower score (0.39; Fig. 6D).

The color of the dots corresponds to the magnitude of the original feature value, with red denoting higher values and blue denoting lower values. SHAP dependence plots were employed to reveal nonlinear associations between individual features and model predictions (Fig. 7). When POD PLT > 466 × 10⁹/L, POD D-dimer > 14 mg/L, SVD > 11 cm, PVD > 14 cm, splenomegaly grade ≥ II, or splenic thickness > 5 cm (SHAP > 0), the model exhibited a positive effect on PVST prediction within these ranges. In contrast, when PVV > 24 cm/s or PVF > 925 mL/min (SHAP < 0), the model showed a negative effect. These plots intuitively depict the directional influence and threshold characteristics of key variables on model predictions, enhancing the model’s interpretability and clinical utility.

A convenient tool for clinical applications

The final XGBoost predictive model was integrated into an intuitive web-based application to enhance its clinical applicability (Fig. 8). By inputting the actual values of the eight predictive features, the platform automatically calculates the probability of postoperative PVST in individual patients undergoing splenectomy. Additionally, the system generates SHAP-based force plots to visualize each feature’s contribution to the prediction. This online tool enables clinicians to intuitively assess thrombosis risk, thereby supporting early interventions and personalized decision-making. The platform is now live and can be accessed at https://pvst-predictor.streamlit.app/.

Discussion

In this study, we developed and internally validated an interpretable machine learning model for predicting postoperative PVST risk, based on data from 594 patients with WD who underwent splenectomy at the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui University of Chinese Medicine over the past 10 years. Eight key clinical features were identified using LASSO regression and univariate analysis: grade of splenomegaly, splenic thickness, PVV, PVD, PVF, SVD, POD PLT, and POD D-dimer. Seven machine learning algorithms were constructed, with the XGBoost model demonstrating superior performance in both the training and validation sets (AUCs of 0.971 and 0.970, respectively). Global and local interpretability was achieved through SHAP analysis. Deployment of the model as an online prediction platform facilitates clinical application, enabling early identification of high-risk patients, guiding preventive interventions, and ultimately improving prognosis for patients with WD.

In this study cohort, the incidence of PVST was 27.90%, which is slightly lower than the reported incidence of PVST in cirrhotic patients following splenectomy, ranging from 37.5% to 43.5%, and even as high as 91.0% [6]. This difference may be attributed to the unique pathophysiological features of WD, including copper metabolism disorder leading to liver injury and portal hypertension, which differ from the underlying causes of viral hepatitis or alcoholic cirrhosis. However, the median age of our cohort was 28 years, which is relatively young, and this could partly explain the lower incidence of PVST. Despite this, the negative impact of PVST on the prognosis of WD patients should not be underestimated, including liver perfusion disturbances, exacerbation of ascites, and increased risk of bleeding [4, 16]. We believe that WD patients undergoing splenectomy represent a distinct subgroup, requiring personalized risk assessment tools to guide clinical decision-making. Previous studies have attempted to develop PVST risk prediction models. Lin et al. conducted a meta-analysis involving 1807 splenectomy patients and identified factors such as increased splenic vein diameter, increased portal vein diameter, decreased portal vein flow velocity, ascites, and a history of preoperative bleeding as being associated with PVST[16]. Yuan et al., in a study of 315 cirrhotic patients, used LASSO regression and multivariate logistic regression to establish a nomogram model incorporating variables such as portal vein diameter, splenic vein diameter, body mass index, and platelet count [17]. In contrast, this study specifically targets the prediction of PVST in WD patients following splenectomy, minimizing the confounding effect of disease heterogeneity. Using the XGBoost machine learning algorithm, the AUC for the validation set reached 0.970, significantly outperforming logistic regression (0.804), due to the gradient boosting framework’s robustness in handling high-dimensional data and interaction effects. SHAP analysis revealed that portal vein volume (PVV), portal vein flow (PVF), and postoperative D-dimer levels were the top three contributing factors, consistent with the literature indicating portal hemodynamic abnormalities and a hypercoagulable state, thus confirming the central role of these factors in the pathogenesis of PVST.

These selected predictive factors have a solid clinical and theoretical foundation and deserve the attention of clinicians. They can be used for early identification of high-risk patients, enabling timely intervention. The primary predictive factors include PVV, PVF, and POD D-dimer, which ranked among the top three in SHAP analysis. A decrease in PVV and PVF reflects hemodynamic abnormalities following splenectomy under portal hypertension. Specifically, a decrease in PVF (20%-50% reduction) and an increased portal vein diameter (PVD ≥ 13 mm) postoperatively result in a significant reduction in PVV, leading to a “low flow, high resistance” state [14, 18–20]. Additionally, stagnation of the wider splenic vein diameter (SVD) and the altered angle at the splenic-mesenteric junction (e.g., < 100°) disrupts the spiral flow, exacerbating localized turbulence and creating conditions conducive to PVST formation [21, 22]. On the other hand, postoperative D-dimer and platelet (PLT) elevation are widely recognized as markers of coagulation activation and are independently associated with PVST. In WD patients, copper deposition leads to liver fibrosis and portal hypertension, which exacerbates blood flow stasis postoperatively, increasing the likelihood of platelet aggregation and activation of coagulation factors [23, 24]. D-dimer, a marker of fibrin degradation products, reflects the overactivation of the coagulation-fibrinolysis system [25–27]. PLT elevation, in turn, is associated with platelet release following the relief of splenic function [28]. The combined effect of both factors significantly increases PVST risk by enhancing coagulation factor activity (e.g., thrombin generation) and inhibiting fibrinolytic activity (e.g., increased PAI-1 levels) [29]. Other factors, such as the grading of splenomegaly and splenic thickness, also showed a positive contribution, indicating the severity of portal hypertension, possibly promoting thrombosis by increasing blood flow turbulence and endothelial injury. These findings further confirm that hemodynamics and coagulation mechanisms are the core driving factors of PVST following splenectomy in WD. However, another unexpected finding was that Child-Pugh classification and surgical methods (e.g., simple splenectomy vs. splenectomy combined with esophagogastric vascular disconnection) were not independently associated with PVST, which is inconsistent with some studies on cirrhosis [30]. This may be because WD patients generally have relatively preserved liver function (mostly Child-Pugh A/B), and standardized surgical techniques reduce variability. Future studies should clarify the role of these factors in the context of WD-specific characteristics.

Clinical Implications Based on This Study: (1) Preoperative Assessment and Treatment Optimization: PVV and PVF using Doppler ultrasound should be employed to assess hemodynamics. Optimize copper elimination therapy to control liver function and ensure Child-Pugh ≤ B grade. High-risk patients should be considered for preoperative low molecular weight heparin (LMWH) bridging therapy, targeting an APTT of 1.5-2 times the normal level. (2) Intraoperative Intervention: Laparoscopic splenectomy should be prioritized to minimize endothelial injury. Ensure precise ligation of the splenic vein, maintaining a short stump (< 1 cm) to reduce potential complications. (3) Postoperative Monitoring and Prevention: Routine monitoring of PLT and D-dimer levels should be conducted on postoperative days 7 and 14. If preoperative indicators suggest high risk (comprehensive score > 50%), initiate LMWH or warfarin for anticoagulation therapy for 7–14 days. For early-stage PVST (< 3 months post-surgery), immediate escalation to combined anticoagulation and thrombolysis (e.g., urokinase) treatment should be considered. (4) Long-Term Management: Regular follow-up imaging and coagulation testing every 3–6 months should be performed, with real-time predictions using an online XGBoost platform. Patients presenting with symptoms such as abdominal pain, bloating, or bleeding should seek immediate medical attention to rule out thrombosis.

This study has several strengths, including a well-designed sample size and random grouping, which ensure the robustness of the model. The baseline characteristics of the training and validation sets were well-balanced (P > 0.05). XGBoost outperformed other methods in terms of accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and F1 score (0.892 vs. 0.713 for LightGBM). Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) demonstrated the highest clinical net benefit (net benefit across thresholds from 0.1 to 0.8). The use of SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanations) further enhanced the model’s interpretability. The online platform (https://pvst-predictor.streamlit.app/) is the first to implement real-time prediction and visualization of eight variables in WD research, facilitating clinical translation. However, there are several limitations. First, as a single-center retrospective study, selection bias is inevitable and may affect the external generalizability of the model. Second, due to data limitations, potential variables such as disease activity in WD and autoimmune antibody levels were not included in the analysis. Third, the model is based on baseline and early postoperative features, and does not account for dynamic changes, such as postoperative blood flow monitoring or adjustments in treatment. Finally, this study lacked an external validation cohort, and the model performance was assessed only internally, which may limit its robustness. However, this issue may be addressed in the future through collaboration with the Third Medical Center. Despite these limitations, our early model provides a foundation for future multi-center prospective studies.

In summary, the XGBoost model developed in this study demonstrates excellent performance and interpretability in predicting the risk of PVST following splenectomy in WD patients. This model could serve as a clinical tool to help identify high-risk patients and guide personalized prevention strategies, potentially improving prognosis. Future external validation and prospective trials may lead to the widespread application of this model in the management of WD.
