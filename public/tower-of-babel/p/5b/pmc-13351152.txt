Cyano-modulated Zn(II)-Schiff base complexes for lysosome-targeted fluorescence imaging and pH-responsive camptothecin delivery
Authors: Xi Zhang, Wenjie Sun, Xin Li, Yuanzhe Li, Shuo Xiang, Lifang Zhang, Yiping Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Integrating real-time diagnostic imaging with stimuli-responsive therapy into a single nanoplatform represents a core research direction in precision oncology. Nevertheless, the construction of a lysosome-targeted theranostic system that simultaneously combines high-contrast luminescence, biosafety, and spatially controlled drug release characteristics continues to pose formidable challenges. Herein, by modulating the number of electron-withdrawing cyano (–CN) substituents on the ligand backbone, a series of Zn(II)-Schiff base complexes—designated Zn-0CN, Zn-1CN, and Zn-2CN—were rationally designed and synthesized. The cyano-governed donor–acceptor topology induced a pronounced bathochromic shift in the emission spectra (497 nm for Zn-0CN versus 543 nm for Zn-2CN) while concurrently elevating the fluorescence quantum yield of Zn-2CN to 9.2%. Density functional theory calculations revealed that Zn(II) coordination narrows the HOMO–LUMO energy gap and enhances intramolecular charge transfer; notably, the bis-cyano-substituted Zn-2CN exhibited strong charge polarization within the metal-binding region. Zn-2CN nanoparticles selectively accumulated in the lysosomes of 4 T1 breast cancer cells, displaying bright yellow-green fluorescence and outstanding colocalization with a commercial lysosomal red probe. Following camptothecin (CPT) encapsulation, the resulting CPT@Zn-2CN nanoparticles achieved a cumulative drug release of 92% within 36 h at pH 5.6, whereas only 41% leaked at physiological pH 7.4, confirming their pH-triggered drug release capability. The bare Zn-2CN material exhibited negligible cytotoxicity (cell viability exceeding 90% at 100 μg·mL−1). At an equivalent CPT dosage (20 μg·mL−1), CPT@Zn-2CN elicited markedly superior apoptosis induction compared with free CPT (cell viabilities of 20.5% and 37.5%, respectively), an enhancement attributable to drug release activated by the acidic lysosomal milieu. This work establishes a versatile “imaging–drug delivery integration” platform based on Zn(II)-Schiff base chemistry, wherein a cyano-functionalization strategy enables precise tuning of photophysical properties, and the intrinsic pH sensitivity of the imine bond allows for lysosome-responsive drug release. This study demonstrates that such complexes can seamlessly combine bioimaging with microenvironment-responsive chemotherapy, offering a promising strategy for precision oncology.

Introduction

Cancer remains a major threat to global public health, with breast cancer ranking among the leading causes of cancer-related mortality in women worldwide (Bray et al., 2024). Despite advances in early detection and multimodal therapy (Li et al., 2021; Li et al., 2024; Li et al., 2023; Sun et al., 2026), aggressive breast cancer subtypes continue to be hampered by therapeutic resistance, off-target systemic toxicity, and the lack of real-time tools for monitoring subcellular drug biodistribution (Bianchini et al., 2022). These limitations highlight the urgent need for molecular platforms that seamlessly integrate diagnostic imaging with stimuli-responsive drug delivery to enable imaging-guided precision therapy.

Lysosomes, historically viewed as terminal degradation organelles, are now recognized as dynamic signaling hubs that orchestrate cancer progression, metabolic reprogramming, and drug resistance (Ballabio and Bonifacino, 2020; Allemailem et al., 2021; Tang et al., 2020). Malignant cells frequently exhibit aberrantly upregulated lysosomal function—termed “lysosomal addiction”—which confers enhanced stress tolerance and chemoresistance (Amaravadi et al., 2019; Bonam et al., 2019; Levy et al., 2017), making lysosomes highly attractive subcellular therapeutic targets. Recent lysosome-directed strategies include lysosome-targeting chimeras (LYTACs), which exploit the lysosomal degradation machinery to eliminate disease-relevant extracellular proteins such as PD-L1 with degradation efficiencies reaching 90% in preclinical models (Xiao et al., 2025; Banik et al., 2020), as well as pH-responsive nanocarriers—including polymeric micelles, liposomes, dendrimers, and inorganic nanoparticles—engineered for lysosome-triggered drug release (Ding et al., 2022; Gao et al., 2010; Lee et al., 2008; Li et al., 2025a; Li et al., 2025b).

Despite these advances, a critical bottleneck persists: the vast majority of lysosome-targeted delivery platforms lack intrinsic fluorescence signaling capability, creating a “black box” that prevents researchers from tracking the intracellular fate of both carrier and cargo in real time (Luo et al., 2024). With less than 5% of endocytosed therapeutic payload ultimately reaching cytoplasmic targets due to lysosomal entrapment and degradation (Gilleron et al., 2013; Varkouhi et al., 2011), theranostic agents capable of simultaneously reporting subcellular localization and executing therapeutic function are urgently required.

Existing theranostic systems rely primarily on two classes of materials: small-molecule organic fluorophores and transition metal coordination complexes. Commercial lysosomal tracking dyes such as LysoTracker™ Red and LysoTracker™ Green are widely used in colocalization studies but suffer from rapid photobleaching, concentration-dependent fluorescence quenching, and the inability to double as drug carriers (Qiu et al., 2020; Kundu et al., 2022; Lai et al., 2025). In parallel, luminescent Ru(II), Ir(III), and Pt(II) complexes exhibit advantageous photophysical properties—including large Stokes shifts, long phosphorescence lifetimes, and oxygen-independent emission—yet their clinical translation is severely constrained by dose-limiting toxicities (Poynton et al., 2017; Lo, 2015); Li et al., 2019. Ir(III) polypyridyl complexes completely abrogate topoisomerase I activity at concentrations as low as 10 μM through mitochondrial dysfunction and elevated reactive oxygen species (He et al., 2021; Caporale and Massi, 2018). Cisplatin, despite its frontline clinical status, induces nephrotoxicity and neurotoxicity via non-specific DNA crosslinking and glutathione depletion (Dasari and Bernard Tchounwou, 2014; Patel et al., 2024). Palladium(II) complexes, though structurally analogous to platinum drugs, exhibit pronounced hepatotoxicity (Ferraz et al., 2009; Ferraz et al., 2011). Copper(II) agents, despite promising antitumor activities, provoke deleterious oxidative stress in normal tissues (Jiang et al., 2022; Hosseini et al., 2014; Santini et al., 2014); the well-characterized [Cu(phen)2]2+ complex damages erythrocytes through hemoglobin oxidation (Santini et al., 2014; da Silveira et al., 2008). Even ruthenium(III) complexes such as NAMI-A, once regarded as safer alternatives, interfere with cardiac ion channel function and cause cardiotoxicity (Bergamo and Sava, 2011). Collectively, these findings underscore the pressing need for inherently biocompatible theranostic materials.

In this context, Zn(II)-based coordination complexes have emerged as particularly attractive candidates. As the second most abundant transition metal in the human body, zinc is an essential trace element that participates in over 300 enzymatic processes and is subject to stringent homeostatic regulation by dedicated transporter and metallothionein systems (Maret, 2013; Andreini et al., 2006). The d10 electronic configuration of Zn(II) renders it redox-inert under physiological conditions, thereby precluding Fenton-type oxidative chemistry (Kumar et al., 2021), while also eliminating low-lying metal-centered excited states that would otherwise quench luminescence, enabling ligand-centered emission to be rationally modulated through ligand design (Yam et al., 2020). Schiff base ligands, characterized by the azomethine (–C=N–) moiety, provide robust N2O2 chelation for Zn(II) coordination, extended π-conjugated frameworks for efficient fluorescence, and inherent pH sensitivity: the imine bond remains thermodynamically stable at neutral pH but undergoes accelerated hydrolysis under mildly acidic conditions (pH 4.5–6.5), providing a built-in molecular mechanism for lysosome-triggered structural disassembly and drug release (Vigato and Tamburini, 2004; Cozzi, 2004; Zhang et al., 2015; Xin and Yuan, 2012; Zheng et al., 2013). Although electron-withdrawing cyano (–CN) substituents are known to modulate absorption/emission wavelengths and quantum yields in purely organic fluorophores through π-extension and LUMO stabilization (Kim et al., 2021; Mahmood et al., 2022), their role in governing the photophysical and biological properties of Zn(II)-Schiff base theranostic platforms has remained largely unexplored.

Herein, we report the rational design, synthesis, and systematic evaluation of a novel series of Zn(II)-Schiff base complexes—Zn-0CN, Zn-1CN, and Zn-2CN—bearing zero, one, and two cyano substituents, respectively, on the o-phenylenediamine-derived ligand backbone. We demonstrate that progressive cyano substitution systematically narrows the HOMO–LUMO energy gap, bathochromically shifts the emission maximum (from 497 to 543 nm), and enhances the fluorescence quantum yield (from 5.8% to 9.2%). The lead complex Zn-2CN, formulated into DSPE-PEG2000-stabilized nanoparticles, achieves high-fidelity lysosome-targeted fluorescence imaging in live 4 T1 breast cancer cells (Fig. 1). Encapsulation of the topoisomerase I inhibitor camptothecin yields the theranostic construct CPT@Zn-2CN, which exhibits pronounced pH-dependent drug release kinetics (92% cumulative release at pH 5.6 versus 41% at pH 7.4 over 36 h) and significantly amplified in vitro cytotoxicity relative to free CPT. Experimental section

Materials and general instrumental conditions

All commercially available reagents and solvents were of analytical grade or higher and were used as received without further purification unless otherwise stated. 2-Hydroxy-4-methoxybenzaldehyde, o-phenylenediamine, 3,4-diaminobenzonitrile, 4,5-diaminophthalonitrile, and zinc(II) acetate were purchased from Shanghai Bidepharm Technology Co., Ltd. DSPE-PEG2000 was procured from Xi'an Ruiyi Biological Technology Co., Ltd. LysoTracker™ Red, Calcein-AM/PI Double Stain Kit, camptothecin (CPT), and thiazolyl blue tetrazolium bromide (MTT) were obtained from Shanghai Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd. High-glucose DMEM, fetal bovine serum, and penicillin-streptomycin solution were purchased from Thermo Fisher Gibco™. Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) was obtained from Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. All aqueous solutions were prepared using deionized water with a resistivity of 18.2 MΩ·cm, purified through a Milli-Q® ultrapure water system (Merck Millipore).

Synthesis of Schiff base ligands (0CN, 1CN, and 2CN)

The series of Schiff base ligands were synthesized via acid-catalyzed condensation between 2-hydroxy-4-methoxybenzaldehyde and the respective o-phenylenediamine derivative following a general procedure. Under a nitrogen atmosphere, 2.0 mmol of 2-hydroxy-4-methoxybenzaldehyde was dissolved in 15 mL of anhydrous methanol. To this solution, 1.0 mmol of the corresponding o-phenylenediamine derivative was added portionwise under continuous magnetic stirring. The reaction mixture was heated under reflux at 80 °C for 12 h under nitrogen protection. After cooling to room temperature, the precipitated solid was collected by vacuum filtration, washed successively with cold methanol (3 × 10 mL) and deionized water (3 × 10 mL), and dried in a vacuum oven at 60 °C for 12 h to afford the target ligands. Under the identical reaction conditions, 0CN and 1CN were obtained as symmetrical bis-Schiff base adducts with a 2:1 aldehyde-to-diamine stoichiometry; in contrast, the strong electron-withdrawing effect of the dual cyano substituents substantially reduced the nucleophilicity of the second primary amine, resulting in the isolation of 2CN as a 1:1 mono-Schiff base adduct. The isolated yields for 0CN, 1CN, and 2CN were 85%, 78%, and 82%, respectively.

Synthesis of Zn(II)-Schiff base complexes (Zn-0CN, Zn-1CN, and Zn-2CN)

The target complexes were prepared through stoichiometric coordination of the corresponding Schiff base ligand with zinc(II) acetate. For Zn-0CN: 1.0 mmol of ligand 0CN and 1.0 mmol of zinc(II) acetate were dissolved in 10 mL of anhydrous methanol. For Zn-1CN: 1.0 mmol of ligand 1CN and 1.0 mmol of zinc(II) acetate were dissolved in 10 mL of anhydrous methanol. For Zn-2CN: 1.0 mmol of ligand 2CN, 1.0 mmol of 2-hydroxy-4-methoxybenzaldehyde, and 1.0 mmol of zinc(II) acetate were simultaneously introduced into 10 mL of anhydrous methanol to construct the tetradentate N2O2 coordination sphere in situ. All three reaction mixtures were stirred at 80 °C under reflux for 12 h under a nitrogen atmosphere. After cooling to room temperature, the precipitated products were isolated by vacuum filtration, washed thoroughly with methanol (3 × 10 mL) and deionized water (3 × 10 mL), and dried under reduced pressure at 60 °C for 12 h to afford the target complexes as fine powders. The isolated yields for Zn-0CN, Zn-1CN, and Zn-2CN were 88%, 82%, and 85%, respectively. The complete synthetic route is presented in Fig. 2.

Instrumentation and characterization

1H NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker 600 MHz nuclear magnetic resonance spectrometer using deuterated dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO‑d6) as the solvent. UV–Vis absorption spectra were acquired on a Shimadzu UV-2600 spectrophotometer. Fluorescence emission spectra were collected on a HORIBA FluoroMax+ spectrofluorometer. Absolute fluorescence quantum yields were determined using a calibrated integrating sphere. Hydrodynamic diameters and zeta potentials were measured at 25 °C using a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZSP. Cellular fluorescence imaging was performed on a Zeiss LSM 880 laser scanning confocal microscope (CLSM). Cell viability was quantified using a BioTek Synergy H1 hybrid multimode microplate reader. High-resolution mass spectra (HRMS) were recorded on a Xevo G2-XS Q-TOF mass spectrometer using electrospray ionization (ESI). Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were collected on a Nicolet iS50 FTIR spectrometer using KBr pellets over the range of 400–4000 cm−1. Elemental analyses (C, H, N) were performed on an Elementar Vario EL cube analyzer. Zinc content was determined by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES, PerkinElmer Optima 8000). Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) patterns were recorded on a Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer using Cu Kα radiation over a 2θ range of 5–50°.

Simulations

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were carried out using the Gaussian 09 software suite. Geometry optimizations and frontier molecular orbital (HOMO/LUMO) calculations for the ligands and Zn(II) complexes were performed at the B3LYP/6-31G(d) level of theory, with visualization accomplished via GaussView 5.0. Electrostatic potential (ESP) maps and deformation electron density maps were computed using the Dmol3 module.

Preparation of Zn-2CN nanoparticles (Zn-2CN NPs)

Zn-2CN nanoparticles were fabricated via a self-assembly/dialysis method employing DSPE-PEG2000 as the amphiphilic stabilizer. Briefly, 1 mg of Zn-2CN complex and 5 mg of DSPE-PEG2000 were co-dissolved in 1 mL of anhydrous DMSO with mild sonication to ensure complete dissolution. The organic solution was added dropwise into 9 mL of deionized water, and sonication was continued for a further 10 min to complete nanoassembly. The milky dispersion was transferred into a pre-conditioned dialysis bag (molecular weight cut-off: 3000 Da) and dialyzed against 5 L of deionized water at ambient temperature for 24 h, with the ultrapure water being replaced at regular intervals to ensure complete removal of DMSO. The purified nanoparticle dispersion was lyophilized using a freeze-dryer to obtain Zn-2CN nanopowder, which was stored under desiccation at 4 °C until further use.

Preparation of CPT-loaded Zn-2CN nanoparticles (CPT@Zn-2CN)

Drug-loaded nanoparticles were prepared via a co-assembly protocol: 1 mg of Zn-2CN, 1 mg of camptothecin, and 5 mg of DSPE-PEG2000 were co-dissolved in 1 mL of DMSO, slowly added dropwise to 9 mL of deionized water, and subjected to the same sonication procedure described in Section 2.6. After 24 h of dialysis and subsequent lyophilization, CPT@Zn-2CN powder was obtained and stored at 4 °C. A series of CPT standard solutions in DMSO were prepared, and a calibration curve was constructed at the characteristic absorption maximum (λmax = 366 nm) (Fig. S15).

Dynamic light scattering and zeta potential measurements

The hydrodynamic size distributions and surface potentials of Zn-2CN NPs and CPT@Zn-2CN NPs were determined by dynamic light scattering (DLS) and phase analysis light scattering (PALS) using a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZSP. Aqueous nanoparticle dispersions were filtered through 0.22 μm membranes, equilibrated at 25 °C for 120 s, and subsequently analyzed.

In vitro pH-responsive drug release study

The pH-dependent drug release behavior of CPT@Zn-2CN was investigated using a dialysis method under simulated physiological and lysosomal pH conditions. CPT calibration curves were independently established in DMSO, PBS (pH 7.4), and PBS (pH 5.6) by preparing gradient concentrations of CPT, measuring the absorbance at 367 nm, and performing linear regression analysis (Fig. S15). A 1 mL aliquot of CPT@Zn-2CN aqueous dispersion (CPT-equivalent concentration: 1 mg/mL) was sealed within a pre-hydrated dialysis bag (MWCO: 3000 Da) and immersed in 200 mL of PBS buffer (pH 7.4 or pH 5.6) thermostatically maintained at 37 ± 0.5 °C with continuous gentle agitation at 100 rpm. At predetermined time intervals (2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, 16, 24, 28, 32, and 36 h), 2 mL aliquots of the external release medium were withdrawn for UV–Vis absorbance measurement and subsequently returned to the reservoir to maintain sink conditions. The cumulative CPT release percentage at each time point was calculated using the corresponding calibration curve, and the release profiles were plotted as a function of time.

Cell culture

Murine mammary carcinoma 4 T1 cells were employed for in vitro biological evaluation. Cells were cultured in high-glucose DMEM complete medium (supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution) and maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C under a 5% CO2 atmosphere. All procedures were performed aseptically in a Class II biological safety cabinet. Upon reaching approximately 80% confluence, cells were detached using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA and subcultured or seeded for experiments. Mycoplasma testing was conducted at regular intervals; all cultures tested negative for mycoplasma contamination.

MTT cell viability assay

The cytotoxicity of Zn-2CN NPs, free CPT, and CPT@Zn-2CN was evaluated using the standard MTT colorimetric assay. Logarithmic-phase 4 T1 cells were seeded into 96-well flat-bottom culture plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well in 100 μL of culture medium. Wells containing culture medium alone were designated as blank controls. After 24 h of attachment, the spent medium was aspirated and replaced with fresh complete medium containing varying concentrations of the test agents: Zn-2CN NPs at a concentration gradient of 0–100 μg/mL; free CPT and CPT@Zn-2CN at equivalent CPT concentration gradients. Cells were incubated with the treatments for 24 h under standard culture conditions. Subsequently, 10 μL of sterile MTT solution (5 mg/mL in PBS) was added to each well, and the plates were incubated for an additional 4 h at 37 °C. The viable cells' mitochondrial succinate dehydrogenase reduced MTT to insoluble purple formazan crystals. The supernatant was carefully removed, and 150 μL of DMSO was added per well with gentle orbital shaking (150 rpm, 15 min) in the dark to dissolve the crystals. Absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader.

Live-cell confocal fluorescence imaging and lysosomal colocalization

For subcellular localization studies, 4 T1 cells were seeded onto 35 mm glass-bottom confocal dishes at a density of 5 × 104 cells per dish and cultured for 24 h to achieve 60%–70% confluence. The culture medium was replaced with fresh complete DMEM containing Zn-2CN NPs at a concentration of 50 μg/mL, and the cells were incubated for 3 h at 37 °C under 5% CO2 to allow for nanoparticle internalization and intracellular trafficking. The medium was then removed, and cells were washed three times with pre-warmed PBS (37 °C) to eliminate non-internalized nanoparticles. For lysosomal counterstaining, cells were incubated with LysoTracker™ Red (50 nM) in serum-free DMEM for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark, followed by three additional PBS washes. Samples were immediately imaged using a confocal laser scanning microscope. Zn-2CN fluorescence was excited at 405 nm and collected in the green-yellow channel, while LysoTracker™ Red fluorescence was excited at 561 nm and collected in the red channel.

Live/dead cell double-staining assay

The cytotoxicity of the samples was qualitatively evaluated using a Calcein-AM/propidium iodide (PI) double-staining assay. 4 T1 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 1 × 104 cells per well and cultured for 24 h. Cells were then treated with Zn-2CN NPs (50 μg/mL), free CPT (20 μg/mL), or CPT@Zn-2CN (CPT-equivalent dose: 20 μg/mL) for 24 h. The medium was removed, and cells were washed once with PBS. Staining solution containing 2 μM Calcein-AM and 1.5 μM PI in serum-free DMEM was added, and the plates were incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 15 min. After two PBS washes, cells were immediately imaged by confocal microscopy. Calcein-AM (viable cells, green fluorescence) was excited at 488 nm; PI (necrotic/late apoptotic cells, red fluorescence) was excited at 561 nm.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and optical characterization of Zn(II)-Schiff base complexes

The Schiff base ligands 0CN, 1CN, and 2CN and their corresponding Zn(II) complexes were synthesized following the route outlined in Fig. 2. The synthetic routes are straightforward and afforded the target compounds in good yields (78–88%). 1H NMR spectroscopy (Figs. S1–S3) confirmed the successful synthesis of the ligands. Comparative analysis of the 1H NMR spectra of the free ligands and their Zn(II) complexes (Figs. S4–S6) revealed the diagnostic disappearance of the phenolic –OH proton resonances (originally observed at δ 13.53, 13.14–13.08, and 11.99 ppm for 0CN, 1CN, and 2CN, respectively) upon zinc chelation. High-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS, Fig. S8) provided definitive confirmation of the molecular weights. For 0CN, the observed [M + H]+ peak at m/z = 377.1498 matched the theoretical value of 377.1496 (C22H21N2O4+). Similarly, the observed peaks for 1CN ([M + H]+, 402.1452 vs. 402.1448), 2CN ([M + H]+, 293.1039 vs. 293.1033), Zn-0CN ([M + H]+, 439.0635 vs. 439.0631), Zn-1CN ([M + H]+, 464.0588 vs. 464.0583), and Zn-2CN ([M + H]+, 489.0540 vs. 489.0536) were in excellent agreement with theoretical values, confirming the successful synthesis of all target molecules.

Elemental analysis (CHN) and ICP-OES (for Zn) were performed to determine the purity and composition (Table S1). The calculated atomic ratios from the experimental data were in excellent agreement with the theoretical values for all six compounds. For example, Zn-2CN gave C: H: N: Zn ratios of 24.0: 15.8: 4.0: 1.0, closely matching the theoretical C24H16N4O4Zn stoichiometry (24: 16: 4: 1). These results, together with the HRMS data, confirm the high purity and correct stoichiometry of all synthesized compounds.

Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD, Fig. S9) was employed to assess the crystallinity and phase purity of the ligands and complexes. All compounds exhibited sharp and distinct diffraction peaks, indicating good crystallinity. Importantly, the PXRD patterns of the Zn complexes (Zn-0CN, Zn-1CN, Zn-2CN) were completely different from those of their respective free ligands (0CN, 1CN, 2CN), with new, well-defined peaks appearing upon complexation. This observation unequivocally confirms the formation of new crystalline phases resulting from successful metal coordination, ruling out the presence of unreacted starting materials or physical mixtures.

Collectively, the 1H NMR, FTIR, HRMS, elemental analysis, ICP-OES, and PXRD data provide robust, multi-faceted evidence for the successful synthesis, expected molecular structure, and high purity of all three ligands and their Zn(II) complexes. The absence of extraneous peaks in the 1H NMR spectra further attests to the high purity of the materials.

The optical properties of the complexes were systematically characterized by UV–Vis absorption and steady-state fluorescence spectroscopy in DMSO. As shown in Fig. 3a and Figs. S10a, S11a, the absorption spectra of all three Zn(II) complexes exhibited significant bathochromic shifts relative to their respective free ligands. Specifically, the absorption maximum of Zn-0CN shifted from 327 nm to 387 nm (Δλ = 60 nm), Zn-1CN from 335 nm to 402 nm (Δλ = 67 nm), and Zn-2CN from 302 nm to 420 nm (Δλ = 118 nm). To further validate the experimental optical trends, the simulated UV–Vis absorption spectra of the ligands and complexes were performed (Fig. S12). The calculated spectra showed that Zn(II) coordination induced a bathochromic shift in the maximum absorption wavelength (λmax) for all three complexes compared to their respective free ligands. Specifically, the calculated λmax shifted from 317 nm to 360 nm for Zn-0CN, from 319 nm to 356 nm for Zn-1CN, and from 343 nm to 360 nm for Zn-2CN. Fluorescence spectroscopy demonstrated that Zn(II) coordination dramatically enhances the emission intensity (Fig. 3b; Figs. S10b, S11b). Under identical measurement conditions and concentrations (200 μg/mL), the emission intensities of Zn-0CN, Zn-1CN, and Zn-2CN were amplified by factors of 5.42, 5.01, and 4.35, respectively, compared with their corresponding free ligands. This pronounced luminescence enhancement is attributable to multiple cooperative factors (de Silva et al., 1997): (i) Zn(II) coordination imposes conformational rigidity, restricting intramolecular rotations and vibrations of the Schiff base ligand backbone and thereby suppressing non-radiative energy dissipation; (ii) the closed-shell d10 electronic configuration of Zn(II) eliminates low-lying metal-centered excited states that would otherwise function as efficient non-radiative deactivation channels, ensuring that the photophysics is dominated by ligand-centered transitions; and (iii) chelation-enhanced fluorescence (CHEF) arises from the increased rigidity of the π-conjugated system and the suppression of photoinduced electron transfer (PET) processes involving the lone-pair electrons of the imine nitrogen atoms, which become engaged in coordinate bond formation with the Zn(II) center. These findings are consistent with reports on analogous Zn-Schiff base systems, where coordination-induced rigidification has been established as the principal mechanism underlying fluorescence turn-on (Wang et al., 2017).

The emission maxima of Zn-0CN, Zn-1CN, and Zn-2CN were located at 497 nm, 521 nm, and 543 nm, respectively, revealing a progressive bathochromic shift with increasing cyano content (Fig. 3c; Figs. S10c, S11c): Zn-0CN (zero –CN groups) emits cyan-green light, Zn-1CN (one –CN group) shifts to green-yellow, and Zn-2CN (two –CN groups) emits yellow-green light (543 nm). The cyano group exerts its influence by extending π-delocalization through the nitrile triple bond, lowering the LUMO energy, compressing the energy gap, and reducing the energy of the radiative transition, thereby enabling the controlled tuning of emission wavelength (Tsukamoto et al., 2017; Chai et al., 2016). This trend underscores the structural designability and spectral tunability of the Zn-Schiff base platform.

The absolute fluorescence quantum yields of the three complexes were determined as 5.8% (Zn-0CN), 7.5% (Zn-1CN), and 9.2% (Zn-2CN), exhibiting a monotonic increase with progressive cyano substitution (Fig. 3c). The strong electron-withdrawing character of the cyano group enhances the charge-transfer character of the emissive excited state, increases the oscillator strength of the radiative transition, and further rigidifies the ligand conjugated framework, collectively contributing to improved photon emission efficiency (Mondal and Dastidar, 2020). Although the quantum yield of Zn-2CN (9.2%) is moderate compared to optimized organic laser dyes or quantum dots, it is—when combined with the large Stokes shift and the excellent photostability imparted by the Zn(II)-coordinated rigid framework—fully adequate for live-cell fluorescence imaging applications using sensitive confocal microscopy detectors.

Electronic structure analysis by density functional theory

DFT calculations at the B3LYP/6-31G(d) level of theory were performed on the three ligands and their Zn(II) complexes to elucidate the electronic structural origins of the observed optical trends. The computed HOMO and LUMO energies and corresponding energy gaps are presented in Fig. 3d, e and Figs. S13, S14.

For the free ligands, the HOMO–LUMO gaps were calculated as 3.91 eV (0CN), 3.75 eV (1CN), and 3.63 eV (2CN). The progressive gap narrowing with cyano substitution is consistent with the experimentally observed absorption red shifts. Upon Zn(II) coordination, the gaps underwent further substantial reduction to 3.33 eV (Zn-0CN), 3.19 eV (Zn-1CN), and 3.09 eV (Zn-2CN), corresponding to gap contractions of 0.58, 0.56, and 0.54 eV, respectively, relative to the parent ligands. Zn(II) coordination induces rehybridization of the ligand frontier orbitals: the electropositive zinc ion stabilizes the ligand π antibonding orbitals through electrostatic interaction, while the Zn—O and Zn—N coordinate bonds modulate the HOMO energy, collectively narrowing the gap and promoting intramolecular charge transfer (ICT) from the electron-rich phenolate donors to the electron-deficient imine/cyano acceptors, thereby enhancing radiative transition probability (Lim et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2015).

Molecular electrostatic potential (ESP) maps (Fig. 3f, g; Figs. S13c, S13d, S14c, S14d) provided complementary visualization of charge distribution. In the free 2CN ligand, the regions of most negative electrostatic potential are localized around the phenolic oxygen and imine nitrogen atoms of the coordination pocket, while the aromatic rings display relatively neutral potential. Following Zn(II) chelation, pronounced charge polarization is evident, with the zinc center displaying strongly positive electrostatic potential and the coordinated oxygen and nitrogen atoms exhibiting intensified negative potential.

Quantitative Mulliken population analysis further substantiated the charge redistribution. For the free 2CN ligand, the Mulliken atomic charges at the coordination site were + 0.27 (C), −0.511 (O), and − 0.48 Ha·e−1 (N). Upon Zn-2CN complex formation, the corresponding values for Zn, O, and N were + 0.707, −0.588, and − 0.395 Ha·e−1, respectively. The substantially increased charge differential between the metal center and its donor atoms confirms the establishment of a robust intramolecular electrostatic field within the coordination sphere, which facilitates charge-transfer transitions, modulates frontier orbital energetics, and influences the interactions between the complex and biomolecules—factors critical to cellular uptake and subcellular targeting specificity (Elshaarawy et al., 2015; Alfaifi et al., 2019).

Deformation electron density maps (Fig. 3h, i; Figs. S13e, S13f, S14e, S14f) visually depicted the spatial redistribution of electron density induced by Zn(II) coordination, reflecting the polarization of the ligand electronic structure toward the electropositive metal center. This charge reorganization constitutes the microscopic origin of the HOMO–LUMO gap narrowing and the associated modulation of optical properties.

Lysosome-targeted cellular imaging with Zn-2CN

Given its highest quantum yield (9.2%), longest emission wavelength (543 nm), and favorable cyano-mediated biomembrane affinity, Zn-2CN was selected as the lead compound for cellular imaging studies. Zn-2CN was formulated into DSPE-PEG2000-stabilized nanoparticles to enhance aqueous dispersibility and cellular uptake, and the resulting Zn-2CN NPs were applied to live 4 T1 murine breast carcinoma cells.

Dual-channel confocal colocalization experiments were conducted using the commercial lysosome-specific probe LysoTracker™ Red as a reference. 4 T1 cells were incubated with Zn-2CN NPs (50 μg/mL) for 3 h, followed by LysoTracker™ Red counterstaining (50 nM, 30 min). As shown in Fig. 4a and b, Zn-2CN NPs emitted bright yellow-green fluorescence under 405 nm excitation, distributed in a punctate pattern characteristic of endolysosomal compartmentalization. The LysoTracker™ Red signal displayed a highly analogous punctate intracellular distribution. Merged images at both low and high magnification revealed extensive spatial overlap between the two channels, appearing as orange-yellow puncta. Three-dimensional reconstructed fluorescence distribution maps further confirmed the colocalization of the two fluorophores throughout the cellular volume (Fig. 4c). Fluorescence intensity line-scan profiles across representative regions of interest (Fig. 4d, e) demonstrated close alignment of the peaks and troughs of the two signal curves. This size regime offers significant advantages for tumor therapy: nanoparticles in the 10–100 nm range can evade rapid renal clearance (threshold ∼6 nm) and minimize non-specific uptake by the mononuclear phagocyte system (predominant for particles >200 nm), while simultaneously exploiting the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect to achieve passive tumor-targeted accumulation (Xu et al., 2023; Fang et al., 2011). The diameters of 79–91 nm are thus well-suited for tumor-targeted delivery applications.

Zeta potential measurements (Fig. 5b) revealed that Zn-2CN NPs possess a surface charge of −12.68 mV, while CPT@Zn-2CN NPs exhibit a value of −15.34 mV. The negative surface charge originates from the phosphate groups of the DSPE-PEG2000 coating; inter-particle electrostatic repulsion inhibits aggregation and enhances colloidal stability during storage and under physiological conditions.

The practical utility of theranostic agents depends critically on their stability. As shown in Fig. S16a, the Zn-2CN complex dissolved in DMSO exhibited excellent stability when stored at room temperature for 7 days. The intensity of its characteristic UV–Vis absorption peak remained at 99% of its initial value after 7 days, indicating negligible degradation. Furthermore, the colloidal stability of CPT@Zn-2CN nanoparticles dispersed in water was assessed by monitoring their hydrodynamic diameter over 7 days (Fig. S16b). The size remained above 97% of its original value after 7 days, demonstrating good colloidal stability with no significant aggregation. These results confirm that both the molecular complex and its nanoparticle formulation possess sufficient stability for subsequent in vitro biological evaluations.

Preliminary biocompatibility evaluation by confocal imaging (Fig. 5c, d) revealed that 4 T1 cells treated with Zn-2CN NPs (50 μg/mL) displayed bright fluorescence and normal, spread cellular morphology, confirming the excellent biocompatibility and negligible acute cytotoxicity of the carrier. In contrast, cells treated with CPT@Zn-2CN at an equivalent fluorescence intensity exhibited pronounced cell rounding, directly demonstrating that the bare carrier is safe and non-toxic, whereas the drug-loaded formulation can effectively release the therapeutic payload and kill tumor cells.

In vitro pH-responsive drug release behavior

The pH-dependent drug release kinetics of CPT from CPT@Zn-2CN were systematically investigated at physiological pH 7.4 and lysosome-mimetic pH 5.6, with CPT liberation monitored at its characteristic absorption of 367 nm over 36 h.

As shown in Fig. 6a–c, CPT@Zn-2CN exhibited a pronounced pH-responsive release profile. Under neutral pH 7.4, drug liberation proceeded in a slow and sustained manner, with a cumulative release of only 41% within 36 h—a feature that minimizes premature drug leakage during systemic circulation and reduces off-target toxicity to normal tissues. Under mildly acidic pH 5.6, drug release was dramatically accelerated, reaching 92% cumulative release within 36 h. The molecular basis for this pH responsiveness lies in the acid-labile nature of the Schiff base imine (–C=N–) linkage: under neutral to mildly alkaline conditions, the azomethine bond is thermodynamically stable and resistant to hydrolysis; under acidic conditions (pH < 6), protonation of the imine nitrogen renders the carbon atom more electrophilic, facilitating nucleophilic attack by water molecules that leads to imine hydrolysis and structural disintegration of the Zn-Schiff base complex, thereby liberating the encapsulated CPT.

This pH-gated release mode confers dual therapeutic advantages: (i) it enables targeted drug release within the acidic tumor microenvironment (pH 5.6–6.8) and more potently within lysosomes (pH 4.5–5.5), thereby enhancing local therapeutic efficacy; and (ii) it restricts drug release predominantly to acidified intracellular compartments, minimizing systemic drug exposure and associated toxic side effects.

In vitro cytotoxicity and biocompatibility evaluation

The cytotoxicity of Zn-2CN NPs, free CPT, and CPT@Zn-2CN against 4 T1 cancer cells was quantitatively assessed using the MTT assay (Fig. 6d–e).

Across the entire tested concentration range (0–100 μg/mL), Zn-2CN NPs exhibited minimal cytotoxicity, with cell viability remaining above 90% even at the highest concentration tested, confirming the excellent biocompatibility of the nanocarrier. The biosafety mechanisms include: (i) zinc is an essential trace element in humans, with systemic zinc homeostasis being maintained by dedicated transporter and metallothionein systems; the administered nanocarrier doses are unlikely to perturb physiological zinc balance; (ii) the redox-inert d10 configuration of Zn(II) precludes Fenton-type oxidative damage; and (iii) the Schiff base ligands employed, at the concentrations tested, do not engage in non-specific toxic interactions with DNA, proteins, or cellular membranes. This favorable biocompatibility profile stands in stark contrast to heavy metal-based theranostic agents containing ruthenium, iridium, platinum, and copper, for which dose-limiting nephrotoxicity, hepatotoxicity, neurotoxicity, and cardiotoxicity have been extensively documented.

In marked contrast to the non-toxic carrier, CPT@Zn-2CN elicited substantially enhanced cytotoxicity relative to free CPT at equivalent doses (Fig. 6e). At a CPT-equivalent concentration of 20 μg/mL, the cell viability of the free CPT treatment group was 37.5%, whereas that of the CPT@Zn-2CN group was only 20.5%. Relative to free CPT, the antitumor activity of CPT@Zn-2CN was enhanced by 27.2%.

The differential cytotoxicity arises from the distinct cellular uptake and intracellular trafficking pathways of the two formulations: free CPT, being highly hydrophobic, exhibits low transmembrane passive diffusion efficiency and insufficient intracellular drug accumulation; in contrast, CPT@Zn-2CN, after nanoassembly, enters cells efficiently via endocytic pathways, precisely targets lysosomes, and undergoes acid-triggered carrier disassembly, resulting in localized rapid CPT release proximal to the nuclear target topoisomerase I (Pommier, 2013). This spatiotemporally concerted delivery mechanism substantially enhances the chemotherapeutic efficacy.

Live/dead cell double-staining further corroborated the selective killing effect (Fig. 6f). Cells treated with Zn-2CN NPs displayed uniform intense green fluorescence with minimal red apoptotic signal, confirming the absence of carrier-mediated cytotoxicity. Free CPT-treated cells exhibited a mixture of green and red fluorescence, indicative of only partial cell death. In contrast, CPT@Zn-2CN-treated cells showed predominantly red apoptotic fluorescence with sparse green signal, signifying extensive tumor cell killing. These imaging results are in excellent agreement with the quantitative MTT data and validate the superiority of the pH-responsive lysosome-targeted delivery paradigm: the Zn-2CN carrier is intrinsically biosafe, yet upon lysosome-targeted delivery and acid-triggered drug release, it mediates highly efficient and selective cancer cell killing.

To highlight the structure-property relationships governed by cyano substitution, a comparative summary of the three complexes is provided. As the number of electron-withdrawing cyano groups increases from 0 to 2 (Zn-0CN → Zn-1CN → Zn-2CN), a clear trend is observed: the absorption maximum progressively red-shifts from 387 nm to 402 nm to 420 nm; the emission maximum similarly shifts bathochromically from 497 nm to 521 nm to 543 nm; and the fluorescence quantum yield monotonically increases from 5.8% to 7.5% to 9.2%. These systematic improvements in photophysical properties are attributed to the enhanced electron-withdrawing effect, which stabilizes the LUMO, narrows the HOMO-LUMO gap, and increases the charge-transfer character of the emissive state. Consequently, Zn-2CN, possessing the longest emission wavelength (543 nm, yellow-green) and the highest quantum yield (9.2%), was selected as the optimal candidate for subsequent lysosome-targeted imaging and drug delivery studies. This comparative analysis underscores the power of rational substituent engineering in tuning the performance of Zn(II)-Schiff base theranostic platforms.

To further highlight the novelty and advantages of our Zn-2CN-based platform, we compared its key performance metrics with those of representative previously reported theranostic systems (Table S2). As summarized in Table S2, the emission wavelength of Zn-2CN (543 nm) is longer than that of many reported Zn(II) complexes (typically <520 nm) (Singh and Ghosh, 2016; Chethanakumar et al., 2024), which facilitates deeper tissue penetration for in vivo imaging. In terms of biosafety, unlike heavy metal-based agents such as Cu(II), Pt(II), Ru(II), and Ir(III) complexes that exhibit significant multi-organ toxicities (e.g., hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, cardiotoxicity) (Poynton et al., 2017; Lo, 2015; Li et al., 2019), our Zn-2CN is based on the endogenous essential trace element zinc, showing negligible cytotoxicity (cell viability >90% at 100 μg·mL−1). Regarding pH-responsive drug delivery, CPT@Zn-2CN achieved a cumulative release of 92% within 36 h at pH 5.6, whereas many pH-responsive nanocarriers exhibit release efficiencies below 80% or require dual stimulation (e.g., pH + glutathione) to achieve comparable release (Abri et al., 2024; Jafari et al., 2021; Kirubanithy and Santhanam, 2025). For subcellular targeting, while some Zn(II)-Schiff base complexes lack lysosomal targeting capability (Singh and Ghosh, 2016; Chethanakumar et al., 2024), our Zn-2CN NPs demonstrate high-fidelity lysosome-targeted fluorescence imaging with excellent colocalization with LysoTracker™ Red. Finally, in terms of theranostic integration, most literature reports focus either on imaging alone or drug delivery alone, lacking a seamless integration of both functions within a single molecular platform (Chang et al., 2020; Gungor et al., 2024; Cheng et al., 2024; Machado et al., 2024). In contrast, our work integrates bright lysosome-targeted fluorescence imaging, pH-responsive camptothecin release, and effective cancer cell killing into one Zn(II)-Schiff base complex, representing a significant advance toward imaging-guided precision therapy.

Conclusion

In this study, a series of cyano-substituted Zn(II)-Schiff base complexes—Zn-0CN, Zn-1CN, and Zn-2CN—were designed and synthesized, and the regulatory influence of cyano substitution on the complex structure and properties was systematically investigated. The structures and high purity of all new compounds were unequivocally confirmed by a comprehensive suite of characterization methods, including 1H NMR, HRMS, FTIR (with assignment of Zn—N and Zn—O vibrations), elemental analysis, ICP-OES, and PXRD. The Zn-2CN complex and its CPT-loaded nanoparticles demonstrated excellent stability under ambient conditions for 7 days. Spectroscopic characterization combined with DFT theoretical calculations confirmed that cyano substitution and Zn(II) coordination synergistically narrow the HOMO–LUMO energy gap, achieve a continuous bathochromic shift in emission wavelength from 497 to 543 nm, and elevate the quantum yield from 5.8% to 9.2%. The optimal bis-cyano-substituted Zn-2CN was formulated into DSPE-PEG2000-stabilized nanoparticles and enabled high-fidelity lysosome-targeted fluorescence imaging in 4 T1 breast cancer cells. A comparative analysis of the three complexes revealed a clear trend of progressively red-shifted absorption/emission and increased quantum yield with higher cyano content, establishing structure-property relationships. Camptothecin was encapsulated within Zn-2CN nanoparticles to construct the theranostic system CPT@Zn-2CN, which exhibited excellent pH-responsive drug release properties: cumulative release reached 92% at pH 5.6 versus 41% at pH 7.4 within 36 h. The bare Zn-2CN carrier exhibited negligible toxicity toward cancer cells, with cell viability exceeding 90% at 100 μg/mL. At equivalent CPT dosage, CPT@Zn-2CN enhanced the antitumor activity by 27.2% relative to free CPT, a result mechanistically ascribed to endocytosis-lysosome-targeted delivery coupled with acid-triggered site-specific drug release, which elevates the local drug concentration at the target site and improves therapeutic efficiency. Overall, this work demonstrates that Zn(II)-Schiff base complexes constitute a tunable, multifunctional theranostic molecular platform that integrates real-time fluorescence imaging with microenvironment-responsive precision drug release. The intrinsic biocompatibility of zinc, the synthetic modularity of Schiff base chemistry, the acid-labile character of the imine linkage, and the substituent-tunable photophysical properties—now supported by comprehensive structural characterization and stability data—collectively endow this class of materials with substantial preclinical translational potential. Future work will focus on in vivo evaluation of tumor targeting and therapeutic efficacy in animal models, as well as exploring the potential for dual-modal imaging or combination therapy.
