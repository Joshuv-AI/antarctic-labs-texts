Non-linear association between Life’s Essential 8 and diabetic retinopathy: mediating role of depression in US adults with diabetes
Authors: Long Xie, Yu Qin Peng, Wei Qiang Wei, Xiang Shen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLife’s Essential 8 (LE8) is a comprehensive cardiovascular health (CVH) metric that is associated with chronic diseases. This study aimed to investigate the association between LE8 and diabetic retinopathy (DR) and the mediating role of depression in this association.MethodsWe analyzed cross-sectional data from 3,815 adults with diabetes mellitus from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) (1999–2020). LE8 scores (0–100) were calculated based on diet, physical activity, nicotine exposure, sleep health, body mass index (BMI), blood lipid levels, blood glucose levels, and blood pressure. DR was diagnosed using retinal imaging or self-reports. Depression was assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9). Weighted logistic regression and mediation analysis were performed. In addition, machine learning (Shapley additive explanation [SHAP]) was used to explain the importance ranking of variables.ResultsWeighted logistic regression models revealed a significant negative association between LE8 scores and the risk of DR. The LE8 score had strong, dose-dependent protective effects against DR, with each 10-point increase in the score being associated with a 2% reduction in the risk of DR (odds ratio [OR] = 0.98, P < 0.0001) across adjusted models. A threshold effect was observed at the LE8 score of 73.12. The protective effect of the LE8 score was significant below this threshold (OR1 = 0.98, P < 0.0001), whereas it was diminished above this threshold (OR2 = 1.06, P = 0.097). Mediation analysis indicated that PHQ-9 scores mediated 13.66% of the total effect of LE8 (P < 0.0001). SHAP analysis showed that blood glucose levels (35.5% importance) had the greatest impact on risk prediction, followed by modifiable behaviors such as physical activity (18.0%) and sleep health (13.5%).ConclusionsLE8 scores are negatively associated with the risk of DR in US adults with diabetes mellitus, and this association is partially mediated by depressive symptoms. Longitudinal studies are warranted to validate causality and elucidate the underlying mechanisms. Interventions targeting both metabolic and mental health may reduce the disease burden of DR.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24987-0.

Introduction

Diabetic retinopathy (DR) is a common complication of diabetes mellitus (DM) that affects the retinal blood vessels and may lead to vision loss. It poses a significant threat to the quality of life of millions of individuals worldwide, contributing to increased healthcare costs and economic burden [1]. Existing treatment modalities, such as laser therapy [2], pharmacological therapy, and surgery, fail to resolve DR, highlighting the urgent requirement of early detection and intervention strategies [3]. Understanding the complex pathogenesis of DR is crucial, as it is influenced by various lifestyle factors that may be modified to mitigate the associated risks.

A recent study indicated a strong correlation between lifestyle choices and the incidence of DM and related complications, suggesting that adopting a healthier lifestyle reduces the risk of developing DR [4]. Depression is a common comorbidity in patients with DM and has been associated with poor glycemic control and an increased risk of complications [5]. Although numerous studies have emphasized the importance of lifestyle modifications in managing DM [6], a notable gap exists in understanding how lifestyle factors are associated with the development of DR in a non-linear manner. Moreover, the role of depression as a potential mediator in this association warrants further investigation. Despite the established relationships between lifestyle factors and DM-related complications, the interplay among lifestyle factors, mental health, and the risk of DR remains unclear. The psychological burden of managing DM may reduce the ability of patients to adhere to lifestyle modifications, creating a vicious cycle of worsening health outcomes. The prevalence of major depressive disorder (MDD) among individuals with DM is notably higher than in the general population, further complicating the management of DM and related complications [7]. Life’s Essential 8 (LE8) has emerged as an important framework for promoting cardiovascular health (CVH) and wellness. The eight components of this health metric are diet, physical activity, body mass index (BMI), nicotine exposure, blood glucose control, blood pressure regulation, cholesterol management, and sleep quality [8].

In this cross-sectional study, data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) were analyzed to examine the association between LE8 scores and the risk of DR in US adults with DM and the mediating role of depression in this association.

Methods

Study population

The NHANES is a nationwide cross-sectional survey of the non-institutionalized civilian population of the United States and is acknowledged for its intricate sampling method. Information is collected through home interviews and standardized physical examinations at mobile examination centers (MECs), with the data released every 2 years. The Health Statistics Research Ethics Review Board of the National Center approved the protocol of the survey, and all participants provided informed consent before data collection. Details regarding the survey protocol can be found on the official website of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) (https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/Default.aspx). In this study, we used data from NHANES 1999–2020. The initial dataset comprised 110,241 participants. To ensure data completeness and relevance, a rigorous exclusion process was implemented. Participants were excluded if they lacked data for the following variables: DM status (N = 101,989), DR status (N = 178), LE8 metrics (N = 4,063), and depression status (N = 196). These exclusions were necessary to maintain the integrity of the analysis and ensure that all participants had complete data for the key variables under investigation. The final sample consisted of 3,815 participants. Figure 1 presents a flowchart of participant selection from NHANES 1999–2020. Assessment of LE8

LE8 scores, calculated based on diet, physical activity, nicotine exposure, sleep health, BMI, blood lipid levels, blood glucose levels, and blood pressure [8]. ranged from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better health (Supplementary Table 1). A total score of < 50 indicated poor CVH, a total score of 50–79 indicated moderate CVH, and a total score of ≥ 80 indicated high CVH [9]. We adopted these definitions and cutoff points to assess and categorize the scores of health behaviors and factors to examine the relationship between LE8 subscales and the risk of DR.

Assessment of DR

DR was defined as self-reported physician-diagnosed retinal complications related to DM [10, 11].

Assessment of covariates

The following covariates were included in this study: age, sex (male or female), race (Mexican American, other Hispanic, non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, or other races), education level (below high school, high school, or above high school), marital status (married or living with a partner, divorced, unmarried, separated, or widowed), family poverty-to-income ratio (PIR), smoking status (< 100 cigarettes in lifetime or ≥ 100 cigarettes in lifetime), hypertension (yes or no), and systemic immune-inflammation index (SII). According to the response to the question “Have you smoked at least 100 cigarettes in your entire life?" (SMQ020), smoking status was classified as never, former, and now. The family PIR was categorized into three groups: ≤ 1.3, > 1.3 but ≤ 3.5, and > 3.5 [12]. The responses to the BPQ020 and DIQ010 questionnaires were used to diagnose hypertension and DM, respectively. SII was calculated as follows [13]: platelet count (/L) * neutrophil count (/L)/lymphocyte count (/L).

Mediating effects of depression on the association between LE8 and DR

To investigate the mechanisms underlying the association between LE8 scores and DR, we examined the role of depression as a potential mediator of this association. The severity of depression was assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9).

Shapley additive explanation methodology

Shapley additive explanation (SHAP) values are derived from cooperative game theory and reflect the average impact of each input feature on the output of a machine-learning model [14]. In this study, SHAP values were calculated and used to generate feature importance plots to identify the variable with the greatest impact on the risk of DR [15].

Statistical analysis

Our statistical analysis was divided into three parts to investigate the association between risk factors and DR. First, the participants were assigned to two groups based on whether they had DR. Differences in baseline characteristics between these groups were analyzed using the t-test in cases of continuous variables and the chi-square test in cases of categorical variables. These variables were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) and a frequency or percentage, respectively. The complex sample was subsequently analyzed using univariate and multivariate logistic regression models. The variance was estimated to determine the association between factors and the risk of DR. Based on the results, four multiple regression models with expanding adjustment were used to estimate the odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). In addition, multiple imputation was used to account for missing data (as > 25% of data for triglyceride and cholesterol levels were missing, these variables were excluded). The “mediation” package in R (version 4.2.2) was used to assess the mediating effects of depression on the association between LE8 scores and the risk of DR after adjustments were made for age, sex, race, education level, marital status, PIR, SII, smoking status, and hypertension status. A mediating effect was defined as the presence of a significant indirect effect, a significant total effect, and a positive proportion of the mediator effect.

Results

Participants characteristics

The PIR exhibited a highly significant correlation with the risk of DR (P = 0.0002). The proportion of individuals with DR (26.97%) was higher than that of individuals without DR (21.54%) in the low-income category, whereas no significant differences were observed in the middle-class and affluent categories. Furthermore, education level was significantly associated with the risk of DR (P = 0.0048). Individuals with DR were more likely to have a lower educational level (less than 9th grade: 16.90% versus 14.46% in the non-DR group). The prevalence of a higher education level (college graduate or above) was lower in the DR group than in the non-DR group (13.60% versus 16.74%). In addition, hypertension was significantly more prevalent among individuals with DR (75.86%) than among those without DR (69.12%, P = 0.0145). No significant differences in other variables, including age, sex, race, marital status, smoking status, or metabolic indices (SII, UHR [uric acid to HDL-C cholesterol ratio], and TyG [triglyceride Association between LE8 scores and DR risk

Weighted logistic regression models revealed a significant negative correlation between LE8 scores and the risk of DR. In model I (unadjusted), a 10-point increase in LE8 scores was associated with a 2% reduction in the risk of DR (OR, 0.98; 95% CI, 0.97–0.99; P < 0.0001). Model II, which was adjusted for demographic variables such as age, sex, race, education level, and marital status, maintained the significant negative association between LE8 scores and the risk of DR (OR, 0.98; 95% CI, 0.98–0.99; P = 0.0001). In model III, further adjustments for socioeconomic status (PIR) and clinical covariates (SII, smoking, and hypertension) did not diminish the protective effect of LE8 scores (OR, 0.98; 95% CI, 0.97–0.99; P = 0.0001). Participants in the second quartile (Q2) experienced a 30% reduction in the risk of DR across all models (model III: OR, 0.70; 95% CI, 0.56–0.89; P = 0.0038). Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analysis revealed significant differences in the association between LE8 scores and the risk of DR across subgroups stratified based on demographic and clinical variables. Notably, a strong negative association between LE8 scores and DR risk was observed in participants aged ≥ 60 years, with a pronounced dose–response trend (P for trend = 0.0007). In particular, moderate LE8 scores corresponded to a 40% reduction in the risk of DR (OR, 0.60; 95% CI, 0.45–0.80; P = 0.0007). Married individuals experienced a 36% reduction in the risk of DR (OR, 0.64; 95% CI, 0.47–0.87, P < 0.001). In addition, the risk of DR was significantly low in patients with hypertension (OR, 0.67; 95% CI, 0.51–0.87). Threshold effects between LE8 scores and DR risk

The standard linear model showed a significant negative association between LE8 scores and the risk of DR. A two-piecewise linear model showed a threshold effect at 73.12 on the LE8 scale. Below this threshold, the protective effect of LE8 scores on the risk of DR remained significant (OR1, 0.98; 95% CI, 0.97–0.99, P < 0.0001). However, above this threshold, the protective effect of LE8 scores increased slightly but did not reach statistical significance (OR2, 1.06; 95% CI, 0.99–1.14, P = 0.0972) (Table 4). Discussion

This study demonstrated the inter-relationship between lifestyle factors, depression, and the risk of DR in patients with DM. A total of 3,815 adult individuals with DM from NHANES 1999–2020 were included in this study. The results revealed significant associations between adherence to lifestyle factors—specifically diet, physical activity, and smoking—and the risk of DR and depressive symptoms. Furthermore, a threshold effect was observed, wherein LE8 scores below 73.12 significantly reduced the risk of DR. Mediation analysis showed that PHQ-9 scores played a significant but partial mediating role in the relationship between LE8 scores and DR risk. Altogether, the findings highlight the importance of a comprehensive approach to DM management—integrating lifestyle optimization, mental health support, and microvascular complication prevention—with traditional clinical treatments. This approach can improve the outcomes of patients with DR in clinical settings.

Numerous studies have examined the correlation between various health-related factors and the risk of DR. For example, a prospective cohort study of 7,077 patients with type 2 DM (T2DM) found that maintaining a healthy lifestyle, such as at least 150 min of moderate-to-vigorous exercise weekly, reduced the risk of DR by 35%. In addition, the overall risk of microvascular complications decreased by 6% with each increment in the number of low-risk lifestyle factors [16]. Similarly, a study on 3,482 adults with DM showed that high-intensity physical activity significantly decreased the risk of DR (OR = 0.64, P = 0.044), highlighting its potential as a crucial protective factor along with moderate alcohol intake [17]. A study employing Mendelian randomization showed that Dietary habits, especially consuming at least 28 g of cheese daily and limiting alcohol consumption to < 14 g daily, protected against DR progression through anti-inflammatory and metabolic pathways [18]. A review and meta-analysis of 42 studies showed that prolonged sleep duration, poor sleep satisfaction, and obstructive sleep apnea were potential risk factors for DR. It also highlighted significant melatonin deficiency in patients with DR, suggesting the role of circadian rhythm disruption in DR and proposing sleep optimization as a potential treatment [19]. The findings indicated that adherence to a healthy diet may mitigate the risk of DR, which is consistent with the use of LE8, which incorporates diet and sleep health, in this study. Despite differences in population characteristics and sample sizes, this study aligns with previous cohort studies in demonstrating a robust negative association between LE8 scores and DR risk. A study showed that participants in the lowest LE8 quartile (LE8 score < 55) had a 10.23-fold increased risk of DR compared with those in the highest quartile (LE8 score ≥ 85) (OR, 10.23; 95% CI, 3.11–33.61, P < 0.001), which represented the strongest association among all ocular endpoints [20]. This cross-ethnic consistency underscores that CVH metrics (e.g., glycemic control and blood pressure) may represent core determinants of DR risk across genetic backgrounds. Furthermore, the LE8 score has a robust dose-dependent protective effect against diabetic microvascular complications, with higher LE8 scores (80–100) corresponding to a 76% lower risk of the complications (hazard ratio [HR] = 0.24; 95% CI, 0.06–0.98) [21]. These findings are consistent with the protective effects of LE8 scores against DR risk observed in this study.

This study addresses a significant gap in existing literature by exploring how lifestyle factors and depressive symptoms influence the risk of DR in patients with DM. Although studies have established various associations between lifestyle behaviors and DM-related complications [20, 22, 23], the specific interplay among lifestyle factors, depression, and the risk of DR remains unclear. Existing studies have predominantly investigated the bidirectional relationship between depression and DR. For example, MDD has been shown to increase the risk of DR by 29% in patients with type 2 DM, with 19% of this increase being mediated by lipid disorders and chronic inflammation [24]. Furthermore, depression can independently predict the risk of DR (OR = 1.69; 95% CI, 1.08–2.64), with the risk increasing with the severity of depression (P < 0.01). Older adults and those with controlled lipids are at higher risk (+ 42% and + 37%, respectively). In addition to focusing solely on glycemic control, targeted screening of depression in these populations may reduce the risk of DR [25]. This study provides empirical evidence supporting that psychological well-being is critical for the effective management of DM. Our findings suggest that the relationship between lifestyle modifications and DR risk is non-linear and varies across the components of LE8, particularly influenced by depressive symptoms. Notably, the overall effect of LE8 scores on DR risk was quantified at –0.047 (P < 0.0001), with 13.66% of this effect being mediated by depression (PHQ-9 scores) (P < 0.0001).

The prominence of blood glucose and physical activity in SHAP analysis highlights their dual roles in the LE8 framework and DR pathogenesis. Consistently, Shah et al. (2024) showed that continuous glucose monitoring metrics, especially the time in tight range, predicted the risk of DR better than HbA1c, with a strong agreement between metrics indicating sustained hyperglycemia as the key pathogenic factor [26]. Regular physical activity reduces the risk of DR in patients with T2DM by 35% (HR = 0.65), accounting for 23% of the protective effects of lifestyle modifications. These protective effects are mainly attributed to the regulation of HbA1c levels and reduction of systemic inflammation [22]. In this study, the proportion of mediated effects decreased in the fully adjusted model (model III), suggesting that other factors, such as sleep health, also influence the association between LE8 scores and DR risk. A similar study showed that prolonged sleep duration (OR = 1.41) and poor sleep satisfaction (OR = 2.04) collectively increased the risk of DR, with melatonin deficiency (standardized mean difference [SMD] = –0.94) potentially mediating this effect. Therefore, optimizing sleep health represents a promising strategy for preventing DR [19].

The association between LE8 scores and DR risk and the mediating effect of depression on this association may be explained by several mechanisms. Recent studies have emphasized the protective effects of lifestyle factors incorporated in LE8 on the metabolic and inflammatory pathways involved in DR. Mechanistically, glycemic and blood pressure control alleviates retinal microvascular damage by suppressing polyol pathway hyperactivity and advanced glycation end product (AGE) accumulation, which are key drivers of oxidative stress in DR [27]. A study showed that intensive glycemic control combined with dyslipidemia therapy, instead of strict blood pressure management, significantly delayed the progression of DR [28]. This finding highlights the importance of the multidimensional health approach of LE8 in improving microvascular outcomes. Depression can activate the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, resulting in sustained elevation of cortisol, which aggravates insulin resistance and hyperglycemia [29]. This interplay creates a detrimental cycle that further complicates DM. IL-1R1 SNPs (e.g., rs2287047 and rs3917368) can alter the expression or ligand-binding affinity of IL-1R1, amplifying IL-1β-induced microglial proinflammatory responses (TNF-α↑, IL-6↑) that disrupt prefrontal–limbic circuit plasticity—a pathway involved in mood dysregulation [30]. A study demonstrated that Ephrin type-B receptor 1 (EphB1) functions as a mediator of retinal inflammation in DR. It links metabolic stress with retinal inflammation, serving as a dual-function target in the treatment of advanced DR [31]. Enhanced sleep quality and reduced nicotine exposure, which correspond to higher LE8 scores, may indirectly mitigate DR-associated inflammation and metabolic dysfunction. Chronic sleep disruption triggers NF-κB signaling and metabolic inflammation through circadian rhythm dysfunction, NLRP3 inflammasome activation, and gut microbiota-related endotoxemia. Therefore, improving sleep quality may help alleviate neuroinflammation and improve cardiometabolic health [32]. Nicotine is one of the inducible agents activating the NF-κB pathway, which subsequently drives inflammatory responses and contributes to age-related diseases. The interplay among NF-κB-induced vascular damage, inflammatory factors (e.g., IL-8 and TNF-α), and glial cell-driven neurodegeneration results in a vascular–neuroinflammatory pathway in DR, necessitating combination therapy [33]. The concept of the gut–retina axis suggests that dysbiosis related to depression promotes neuroinflammation in DR owing to the release of lipopolysaccharides (LPSs) by Bacteroidetes and Proteobacteria [34]. Interventions involving dietary fiber intake, which is a component of LE8, may prove beneficial for improving the gut microbiota balance and consequently ocular health [35]. Altogether, the mediating role of depression in the association between LE8 scores and DR risk highlights the need for integrated approaches to manage both physical and mental health in patients with DM. This finding aligns with recent studies that have emphasized the importance of psychological factors in the management of chronic diseases.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first large-scale, nationally representative cohort study to reveal a non-linear relationship between LE8 scores and DR risk. Notably, mediation analysis showed that LE8 scores influenced DR risk both directly and indirectly through partial mediation by depression-like symptoms (PHQ-9 scores). However, the limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design of this study inherently restricted the ability to establish a causal inter-relationship between lifestyle factors, DR, and depression. Second, the reliance on self-reported measures introduces potential biases, as participants may underreport or overreport their behaviors, potentially distorting the accuracy of the data. Third, although the sample size was large, it was limited to American adults, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations. Fourth, the lack of experimental validation limits the robustness of the findings, and differences in measurement techniques across study centers may introduce inter-observer variability, complicating the interpretation of the results. Fifth, while the PHQ-9 is a well-validated instrument for assessing current depressive symptoms, it cannot fully capture the cumulative burden of chronic or recurrent depressive disorders; this limitation may obscure distinct long-term influences of depression on DR risk. Last, although weighted logistic regression and machine learning provide valuable insights, they cannot account for unmeasured confounding factors that may influence the observed associations. such as duration and control of diabetes treatment, access to ophthalmologic care, and socioeconomic stressors. To address these limitations, future longitudinal studies should explore causal relationships and incorporate diverse populations to enhance generalizability. In addition, these studies should consider objective measurements and experimental validation to strengthen the evidence. The findings of this study highlight the complex interplay among lifestyle factors, mental health, and DM-related complications, suggesting that targeted lifestyle interventions can improve both psychological and physical outcomes in patients with DN. Addressing the limitations of this study may facilitate the development of novel strategies for preventing and treating DM-related complications.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated the inter-relationships between LE8 components, depression, and DR risk, highlighting the importance of mental health in the management of chronic diseases. Psychological therapies and lifestyle programs that simultaneously address depression and cardiovascular health may synergistically reduce DR risk. Healthcare providers may improve the overall health outcomes of patients with DM by integrating mental health support, such as the assessment and resolution of depressive symptoms; the use of machine-learning methods; and the identification of threshold effects in DM management strategies. Future studies should explore the complex relationships between various lifestyle factors and DR risk, offering more effective strategies for preventing and treating DR.
