Sustained and discontinued operational changes to ethics committees in the post-pandemic era: a qualitative study in Kenya
Authors: Enock Kebenei, Daisy Cheruiyot, Elizabeth Anne Bukusi, Maurice P. Zeegers, Gowri Gopalakrishna
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEthics Committees (ECs) play a vital role in protecting the rights, safety, and welfare of human participants enrolled in research. In special circumstances such as pandemics, ECs conduct a rapid review of research protocols to respond to a Public Health Emergency (PHE). Globally, many ECs made operational changes to their processes during the COVID-19 era in a bid to continue providing research oversight amidst strict measures to combat the spread of the disease. Little is known about any sustained and/or discontinued EC operational adjustments made during the COVID-19 pandemic. This study explored the EC operational changes that were retained and those that were dropped after the COVID-19 pandemic and any preparedness plans for potential future PHEs.MethodsThis explorative qualitative study used in-depth interviews to gather data from target population of EC members, chairpersons, secretaries, and research administrators from selected ECs in Kenya that were operational during the pandemic. Data was collected between July and October 2025. We used purposive and snowballing methods until saturation was reached. We developed codes using both inductive and deductive approaches while adopting the five-stage framework analysis method of data familiarization, thematic framework development, code generation, thematic map generation, and examination of data patterns. We used NVIVO version 12 software to analyze the data.ResultsA total of 16 participants from the 7 selected ECs in Kenya took part in the study. We generated three (3) broad themes and various sub-themes from the analysis of in-depth interviews: (1) EC operational adjustments retained post-pandemic included technological adoption in protocol submissions and virtual meetings, frameworks for decentralized research, and discussants for protocol review meetings (2) EC operational adjustments dropped in the post-pandemic era included special COVID-19 scientific committees, parallel reviews between ECs and national regulator, quick turnaround reviews, and COVID-19 sensitization sessions during EC meetings (3) EC future directions in preparation for other PHEs included reciprocal approvals, community engagement plans, verbal presentation of protocols, continuous ethics education, and additional EC secretariat support.ConclusionsIn Kenya, ECs have retained selective operations that enhance efficiency and have discontinued practices that were directly related to the emergency situation and those that were deemed unsustainable in the long-term.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12910-026-01499-z.

Background

Ethics Committees (ECs) function on the basic principles of upholding autonomy, justice, beneficence, and non-maleficence for the participants in research in order to ensure ethical standards are observed [1]. ECs play a critical role in protecting the rights, safety, and welfare of human participants enrolled in research [2]. As a corollary to the primary intentions of the ECs, an ethical clearance is required prior to the implementation of more than minimal risk studies with the goal of publishing research findings and contributing to the scientific knowledge [3]. Minimal risk studies refer to research protocols in which the potential harm or discomfort to the participants is not greater than those that they would typically encounter in their daily life [4]. Such studies undergo ethics review procedures and maybe approved expeditiously or under an exempt category as determined by the EC. Most ethics review regulations provided and used by ECs are formulated for use in regular everyday circumstances for the conduct of research [2]. However, in situations of public health emergencies, changes to the normal EC operations maybe required to conduct rapid reviews [5].

The World Health Organization (WHO) declares an extraordinary event as a Public Health Emergency of International Concern (PHEIC) if it is determined to constitute a public health risk beyond the borders and requires a coordinated response [6]. Some of the WHO-declared disease outbreaks as PHEIC include the H1N1 influenza pandemic (2009), Ebola (West African outbreak 2013–2015), poliomyelitis (2014), Zika (2016), COVID-19 (2020) and Mpox (2024) [7]. While some of these were region-specific, a global concern arose when the WHO declared COVID-19 as a Public Health Emergency of International Concern (PHEIC) on 30th January 2020 and subsequently terminated this concern on 5th May 2023 [8, 9]. The COVID-19 crisis led to a rise in the number of COVID-19 human health research resulting in increased EC workloads [10].

Human health research carried out during pandemics is essential in addressing questions relating to the control of the disease spread, but scientists face significant ethical challenges in such contexts. PHEs exacerbate challenges to ethical research. The fundamental ethical concerns that arise in pandemic situations include the pressure to deviate from scientific and ethical standards, prioritization of research activities, resource allocation, and balancing of research with pandemic response efforts [11]. In pandemics, there is a need to adopt a broader approach to ethics through the inclusion of the role of ‘duty-bearers’ like sponsors, government agencies, research institutions and publishers. During pandemics, global agencies such as the WHO take leadership roles in ensuring that all efforts towards responding to the PHE embed ethics [12]. The 74th World Medical Association (WMA) general assembly held in Kigali, Rwanda in October 2023 reiterated the need to observe the basic principles that underpin public health ethics during PHEs. In such scenarios, physicians and other healthcare professionals have the obligation to maximize benefits and minimize harm at the population level, help alleviate suffering, show respect for all, demonstrate justice and fairness in resource allocation, and maximize overall health outcomes [13, 14]. During the COVID-19 crisis, the WHO provided guidance for ECs to rapidly review research related to the pandemic without compromising its quality [15]. Many ECs across the world adjusted their operations to respond to the need for rapid review of urgent research.

The functioning of ECs shifted during the COVID-19 pandemic including: adoption of virtual meetings in part linked to lockdowns and reduced in-person contact, amendment of Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) to capture EC operational changes, increase in pandemic-related research protocols, reduction of review turnaround time to respond to the concerns of the pandemic, and an upsurge in reported protocol deviations [16, 17]. In India, some ECs reported a reduction in the financial burden and expenditure as a result of less paperwork and the adoption of virtual meetings [16]. Other adjustments that led to fast-tracked review included the introduction of ad-hoc committees, full prioritization of COVID-19 research, and putting other non-pandemic-linked research on hold [18]. Coping mechanisms of ECs during the COVID-19 pandemic depended on the flexibility and the existing operational conditions within the institutions. For instance, ECs that had implemented electronic submission systems prior to the pandemic transitioned seamlessly to remote working compared to those that were using paper-based submission processes [19]. Lessons learned from the changes during the COVID-19 pandemic inform preparedness and future directions of ECs. Examples are continuous and regular training of EC members on emerging issues and establishment of working collaborations among ECs [19]. Some EC operations introduced during the COVID-19 pandemic were dropped, for example, in Pakistan, the Rapid Turnaround Review (RTR) system was deemed unsustainable beyond the outbreak despite being considered useful by researchers [20].

In Kenya, the National Commission of Science, Technology and Innovation (NACOSTI) delegates the task of ethical clearance of research proposals and assurance of the quality of research to ECs [21]. Currently, there are forty-six (46) accredited ECs in Kenya domiciled at universities, hospitals, research institutions, and Non-Governmental Organizations (NGOs) and distributed across the country. After the ethical clearance is obtained, all human clinical trials must obtain approval from the Kenya Pharmacy and Poisons Board (KPPB) before the issuance of the NACOSTI permit [22]. The KPPB is the national Drug Regulatory Authority in Kenya [23]. In December 2020, the NACOSTI developed and published ethical guidelines for PHEs in response to the COVID-19 pandemic in Kenya. These frameworks guided the accredited ECs for the rapid review of pandemic-related research while upholding the highest ethical standards [24]. In response to the pandemic, some ECs in Kenya made changes to the review system such as streamlining the online review processes, recruitment of more reviewers, and adoption of the joint review process (between ethics committees and the national drug authority at the same time) [25]. The COVID-19 crisis also saw a reduction of EC approval turnaround times at the Kenya Medical Research Institute (KEMRI) [26].

Uninterrupted operations of ECs during pandemics are important for continued oversight of research in crisis times. Although several studies have explored changes made to EC operations during the COVID-19 pandemic, few have highlighted sustainable changes that have been retained five years in the post-pandemic era and those that have ceased operating. This study addresses this gap by exploring EC changes that have been retained and dropped after the pandemic with reasons and the preparedness plans for future PHEs. In this study, the post-pandemic era refers to the five-year period between 30th January 2020 when COVID-19 was initially declared as a disease of PHEIC and July to October 2025 when this research data was collected.

In this study, we based our qualitative enquiry and findings on the institutional resilience theory. This theory denotes the organizational ability to continue with its operations despite the challenges. It highlights the capacity of a system to withstand shocks and pressures through relevant adaptations, transformations, and survival after a crisis [27]. While this study aimed at exploring EC adjustments that aided in continued operations during COVID-19 disease outbreak, institutional resilience theory looks at dynamic adaptations and sustainability measures that organizations put in place for long-term operations.

Objective

This study aimed to explore sustained and discontinued operational changes and future preparedness plans of ECs in Kenya in the post-COVID-19 pandemic era.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was an empirical, explorative, qualitative study conducted to evaluate the operations of selected ECs in Kenya during and post-COVID-19 period. The design of this study complied with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) and the Journal Article Reporting Standards for Qualitative Research (JARS-QUAL) guidelines [28, 29]. Supplementary material 1 provides a detailed description of adherence to these standards which entails a completed COREQ checklist.

Treatment of reflexivity, positionality, and liminality of the researchers

Three authors in this paper play active roles in the bioethics space in Kenya and are members of one of the ECs that took part in this study. We acknowledge that these positionalities may influence data generation, interpretation, and analysis. We adopted specific strategies to mitigate the potential bias that may arise out of these positionalities. To address positionality, the three authors were not interviewed and did not provide primary data for this research. As declared in the COREQ checklist, there was an established relationship with some participants prior to study commencement, and reasons for conducting the study were known and explained at the beginning of interviews. To navigate the dual roles of being a researcher and providing ethical oversight in the country, we had to declare the intention of conducting interviews beforehand. Particularly, participants were told that the interviewer is interested in their EC experiences at the time of COVID-19 pandemic for academic, research purposes, and possibly aid other ECs in the preparation for future pandemics. Cognizant of the potential bias and assumptions that might influence interpretation of the findings, we engaged an external party who is not involved in EC operations in Kenya to transcribe audio interviews and generate initial themes and subthemes. In addition, we conducted duplicate coding of randomly selected interviews for validation and consistency of data interpretation.

Study population and sampling

The target population for this study was EC members, chairpersons, secretaries, and research administrators who were actively involved in the daily operations of ECs during and after the COVID-19 period. We used purposive sampling method to identify specific respondents across the seven ECs based on institutional representations, specific roles during the COVID-19 period, and other socio-demographic characteristics. The seven ECs were selected purposively based on their active involvement in the oversight of research protocols at the height of the COVID-19 pandemic. The selected ECs represent the diversity of institutional categories in Kenya, as demonstrated in Table 1 below. We also adopted the snowballing method, where the initial participants referred us to other potential respondents who fit the inclusion criteria.

Data collection and analysis

We obtained ethical approval from Kenya Medical Research Institute (KEMRI/SERU/CMR/P00307/5150), Aga Khan University, Nairobi (2025/ISERC-075), Kenyatta National Hospital-University of Nairobi (P636/07/2025), and Moi Teaching and Referral Hospital-Moi University Ethics Committee (IREC/1374/2025). We also obtained a research permit from the national regulator (NACOSTI/P/25/4173340). After obtaining ethical approval and permission from the institutional heads to carry out the study, we contacted potential participants, administered written informed consent and carried out in-depth interviews individually in the English language. To avoid personal biases in providing institutional or EC positions, we ensured that a minimum of two respondents were interviewed per selected EC. For participants who chose virtual consent, we shared and received signed copies of informed consent through email while those who were interviewed in-person provided handwritten copies. All interviews were audio-recorded. We conducted iterative data collection, real time transcription, analysis, and team discussions to determine the saturation point. We stopped interviews when no new codes were emerging. Audio-recorded interviews were transcribed verbatim and typed into Microsoft Word. To protect the anonymity of respondents, we removed all direct identifiers such as names and addresses while replacing with unique codes. The code keys are kept separately from audio recordings and transcripts. A systematic and structured approach to thematic analysis was used to transcribe and familiarize with the data, select keywords, code, develop and interpret themes [30]. We developed codes using both inductive and deductive approaches. We deductively generated priori codes based on the interview guides and literature and then inductively based on each interview transcript [31]. We kept refining the codebook iteratively as new themes emerged. We used NVIVO version 12 software to code and enter data in unique files. The five stages of framework analysis method began with the familiarization of raw data obtained from field notes, audio files, and transcripts. The second stage involved the development of a thematic framework with parent codes and subcodes. The third stage entailed of generating codes entailed the process of coming up with potential themes and subthemes. The fourth stage of creating a thematic map involved putting the indicative quotes from transcripts. In the last phase of framework analysis method, we refined the final themes and subthemes through a thorough examination of data patterns. All these five stages have been illustrated in Fig. 1 below with a description of all activities across the entire coding process.

Operationalization of the institutional resilience theory

The institutional resilience theory shaped the process of primary data collection and analysis. The interview guide (supplementary material 2) consisted of questions that helped in determining ECs’ capacity to continue with research oversight amidst the COVID-19 restrictions. Questions relating to retained and dropped EC operational changes were posed. The framework analysis method of generating parent codes, subcodes, and refining themes and subthemes was guided by the need to understand EC’s capacity to continue operating during and after the pandemic.

Findings

Sociodemographic characteristics of respondents

A total of sixteen [15] participants from the 7 selected ECs in Kenya took part in the study. Target participants are largely homogeneous since they shared similar working experiences and operational conditions during the pandemic. Out of 16, slightly more than half of the participants 9(56%) were male. In terms of roles in the EC, 5(31%) served as EC secretary. Participants ranging between 31 and 40 years and 41–50 years of age were each 5(31%). In terms of working experience, 6(38%) of the respondents had between 6 and 10 years of experience in the EC work while in terms of highest level of education completed, 7(44%) had Master’s degree. In terms of host institutions of the ECs that were sampled, 12(75%) participants were from public sector while only 4(25%) were from the private sector. We also categorized host institutions of EC by their mandate and found out that 5(31%) were from research institutions, 7(44%) were from academic institutions and only 4(25%) were from hospitals (Table 1).

We generated three (3) broad themes and various sub-themes from the analysis of in-depth interviews: (1) EC operational adjustments retained post-pandemic (2) EC operational adjustments dropped in the post-pandemic era, and (3) EC future directions in preparation for other PHEs.

We found out that all efforts were made to ensure that research oversight continued during the pandemic regardless of the measures put in place to combat the spread of the disease. ECs took deliberate action to respond to the pandemic by reviewing and approving research related to the crisis. These include operations such as ensuring that documents are submitted for review and that scheduled meetings are conducted.“We had a shared folder; it had to be made accessible so that the secretariat could see it remotely. So, work did not stop. Yes” (PID01).“We have just continued with the virtual [meeting], and we have not lost any time, we have not missed any meeting” (PID16).

Theme 1: Operational adjustments of ECs retained in the post-pandemic era

Interviews uncovered the pandemic-related changes that have been retained and those that have been dropped five years after the initial declaration of COVID-19 as a disease of PHEIC. We begin with the operations that have been retained in the post-pandemic era including (1) protocol discussants (someone who steers a conversation in a meeting) during EC meetings (2) development of new EC frameworks (3) adoption of technology. The major reason for the continuation of these practices was the efficiency of the review process that were associated with such initiatives.

Protocol discussants during EC meetings

Some committees introduced a discussant who presents protocol summary to the members and leads the discussion to aid in decision-making. One of the committee members is assigned a protocol one week before a scheduled EC meeting. They would read the protocol and provide a summary for all other members to understand and provide their input. The protocol discussant is not part of the primary reviewers assigned to evaluate the protocol, but they steer the discussion and review of the allocated protocol. This has been retained beyond the pandemic as it reduces the workload for the EC secretary and facilitates robust review and discussion of a protocol before a final decision is made.“One other thing that did change is that prior to the meeting…a committee member would be identified to be a discussant of that proposal…discuss this proposal to the committee, to give them an understanding of what the proposal is all about, to give them an opportunity to make an informed decision. And that is something that has remained in the committee throughout” (PID15).“The secretary still has a role of assigning and ushering in the discussant, and then the discussant would just provide a summary of the protocol and the reviewers’ comments and then members can now be on board for discussion” (PID16).

Changes in the policy and regulatory frameworks

The policy and regulatory environment of ECs during the COVID-19 pandemic changed to include new frameworks that help the functionality of ECs in such times. Respondents indicated that they developed frameworks that guided researchers to implement studies with the prevailing conditions. Some of the operational documents that were changed to improve EC functions included the SOPs, guidelines, and policies around a decentralized approach to conducting and overseeing research. The guidance documents developed during pandemics have been retained to aid in preparation for future pandemics [24, 32, 33]. Some ECs have adjusted their SOPs, guidelines, and policies that were meant for COVID-19 pandemic to include other Public Health Emergencies. The guidance documents were deemed relevant beyond the pandemic and could be adjusted in preparation for other unforeseen disease outbreaks. “But now we have a standard one. Now, we changed that. It is no longer COVID; it is just a guideline for pandemics” (PID07).

“But now we have a standard one. Now, we changed that. It is no longer COVID; it is just a guideline for pandemics” (PID07).

New policies and guidance on remote post-approval monitoring of projects

Two respondents indicated that COVID-19 led to the development of frameworks for conducting virtual monitoring of projects. Such guidance documents have been utilized beyond the pandemic. It came out that ECs did not have procedures for conducting remote monitoring of study activities before the pandemic. However, due to the pandemic, ECs made deliberate efforts to ensure that post-approval monitoring of research projects continued despite the movement restrictions. ECs have now noted that there is an opportunity to conduct active monitoring of approved research projects remotely which may be seen as efficient and cost-saving“Doing post-approval monitoring physically was a challenge. So, we opted for the virtual. And I think that helped us a lot, and it enabled us to even come up with now clear guidelines on how do you do virtual monitoring” (PID08).

“Doing post-approval monitoring physically was a challenge. So, we opted for the virtual. And I think that helped us a lot, and it enabled us to even come up with now clear guidelines on how do you do virtual monitoring” (PID08).

New policies and guidance on e-consenting and remote data collection

Three respondents stated that the COVID-19 pandemic led to the development of guidelines for e-consenting and conducting remote data collection. There were concerns relating to ethical concerns arising from telephone interviews, for example, maintaining privacy and documentation of e-consent. ECs developed guidelines to ensure that e-consent is documented through proper recording where feasible.“We had not developed some guidelines on conducting virtual interviews. And that again gave us an opportunity to come up with them… how do you ensure privacy of that information; how do you record consent… we had to figure out and advise investigators on how that can be done, virtually, because that then was the only option.” (PID08).

“We had not developed some guidelines on conducting virtual interviews. And that again gave us an opportunity to come up with them… how do you ensure privacy of that information; how do you record consent… we had to figure out and advise investigators on how that can be done, virtually, because that then was the only option.” (PID08).

Adoption of technology

All respondents indicated that ECs adopted technological tools and equipment to ensure continuity of research oversight. ECs introduced technology in two main ways: (1) movement from in-person to virtual meetings, and (2) transition from paper-based to online submission of review documents.

Virtual meetings

Amidst disruptions caused by the pandemic, ECs adapted by moving from in-person to virtual meetings. Respondents discovered the efficiency that came with virtual meetings since the quorum for protocol discussions and approvals was no longer a problem for ECs.“I think one of the first things that happened was that we had to start holding meetings virtually.” (PID01).“The issue of online meetings is fantastic. It has improved our efficiency. So, we have retained that up to now. We still do virtual meetings, and we receive the protocols also virtually, online” (PID11).

“I think one of the first things that happened was that we had to start holding meetings virtually.” (PID01).

“The issue of online meetings is fantastic. It has improved our efficiency. So, we have retained that up to now. We still do virtual meetings, and we receive the protocols also virtually, online” (PID11).

Online submission of review documents

Review documents transitioned from paper-based to electronic (e-mails and web-based systems). Many respondents expressed their satisfaction with the online engagement of members during meetings“Before COVID-19, most of our documentation was hard copy. There was a lot of paperwork; we would print minutes for signing, attendants’ lists, and conflict-of-interest forms, so that members would sign. There were a lot of logistical changes because now these documents, we had to digitize them.” (PID02).“Protocols are sent via email, and then we do reviews, and then we meet online as well. So, that is one key area that we learned from the pandemic. Actually, we have adopted that nowadays, we have very few physical meetings” (PID13).

Theme 2: Operational adjustments of ECs that have been dropped in the post-pandemic era

In this section, we examine EC operational changes that have ceased after the pandemic including (1) COVID-19 scientific review committees (2) working in shifts (3) informing EC members about the pandemic (4) parallel review of clinical trials (between ECs and the national drug authority), and (5) quick turnaround mechanisms. The major reasons for the stoppage of these initiatives were threefold. Firstly, some of the practices were tied to the pandemic and ceased after COVID-19 was declared no longer a disease of international public health concern. Secondly, some practices, such as rapid reviews, may potentially affect ethical review rigor or research protocols when applied continually. Thirdly, practices such as parallel submission of clinical trials, though widely embraced by respondents were associated with concerns around readiness of the entire regulatory system, sustainability and workload in non-pandemic settings.

COVID-19 special scientific review committees

Some ECs reported that they had special scientific committees addressing unique aspects of COVID-19. The committee ensured the scientific rigor of the research proposal before ethics review was carried out. Two respondents indicated that such committees were crucial in highly specialized topics that required subject matter experts. Respondents indicated that the special scientific committee was dissolved when COVID-19-related protocols were no longer prioritized by the ECs.“The COVID-19 scientific review committee was to ensure that by the time the protocol gets to the ethics committee, the science is of rigor… After the disease [COVID-19] was managed, this committee was dissolved.” (PID04).

“The COVID-19 scientific review committee was to ensure that by the time the protocol gets to the ethics committee, the science is of rigor… After the disease [COVID-19] was managed, this committee was dissolved.” (PID04).

Working in shifts

Two participants reported that during the COVID-19 pandemic, secretariat staff worked in shifts to observe social distancing protocols. However, this was stopped when the measures to combat the spread of the disease were relaxed.“We made arrangements on how we were going to be working in shifts…would just ensure that only one is in the office.” (PID10).“Staff in the secretariat started coming on full board, not just in shifts. I think that is because of the absence of the pandemic.” (PID16).

“We made arrangements on how we were going to be working in shifts…would just ensure that only one is in the office.” (PID10).

“Staff in the secretariat started coming on full board, not just in shifts. I think that is because of the absence of the pandemic.” (PID16).

Special sessions relating to the pandemic

Two respondents indicated that during EC meetings, members were informed about the prevention and management of COVID-19, vaccine development, and healthcare access. This was relevant to keeping members up to date.“During COVID, we also had a kind of seminar to support our members’ understanding of the situation” (PID03).

“During COVID, we also had a kind of seminar to support our members’ understanding of the situation” (PID03).

Parallel review of clinical trials (concurrent submissions and reviews among ECs and regulators)

Some ECs participated in the parallel review of urgent clinical trials with the national drug regulatory authority, the KPPB. Parallel system, in this case, means concurrent submissions and reviews of clinical trials by ECs and the KPPB. Some respondents reported that there were parallel submissions and reviews between ECs that were overseeing similar studies during the pandemic. However, this did not continue beyond the pandemic. Seven respondents expressed the importance of engaging in a parallel review of urgent clinical trials in a bid to quickly respond to the disease outbreak.“Two of our members actually participated in the review of COVID-19 clinical trials in a parallel manner” (PID08).“Concurrent reviews still took place. I am not aware, actually, if there was one that had parallel review with PPB. I know there were parallel reviews with other ECs” (PID06).“I recall during that time, again, there were some parallel reviews or joint reviews between one ethics committee and the other or even the ethics committee and the Pharmacy and Poisons Board for some expedited protocols” (PID03).“You can review parallel…it can be done at the same time. The only problem, as I say, is that legally it is Poisons board…to find a way of continuity beyond pandemic” (PID05).“Consider parallel reviews; that would really be helpful. But then, there has to be a lot of capacity building to strengthen the IRB’s capacity to understand what components we should look at?” (PID08).

Quick turnaround mechanisms

Seven respondents indicated that the rapid review and approval of research during the COVID-19 pandemic should have continued.“During that pandemic… people were getting documents in good time. They were being submitted in good time. The scientists would address the issues of ISERC [Institutional Scientific and Ethics Review Committees, which also refers to Ethics Committees (ECs)] in good time. The EC would respond in good time. So, if it happened during that period, why not now, post-COVID?” (PID02).“I feel like, particularly for trials, I don’t think 72 hours was enough. A lot can be missed. Remember, during that time, everyone had emotional issues…many uncertainties. So, I’d say compared to a normal time when there are no pandemics or whatever, I believe the rigor of review went down a bit, mostly because of the timelines” (PID06).

“During that pandemic… people were getting documents in good time. They were being submitted in good time. The scientists would address the issues of ISERC [Institutional Scientific and Ethics Review Committees, which also refers to Ethics Committees (ECs)] in good time. The EC would respond in good time. So, if it happened during that period, why not now, post-COVID?” (PID02).

“I feel like, particularly for trials, I don’t think 72 hours was enough. A lot can be missed. Remember, during that time, everyone had emotional issues…many uncertainties. So, I’d say compared to a normal time when there are no pandemics or whatever, I believe the rigor of review went down a bit, mostly because of the timelines” (PID06).

Theme 3: Future directions of ECs in preparation for other PHEs

In this theme, we examine recommendations that aid in preparation and response in the event of another public health emergency. The following sub-themes emerged: (1) verbal presentation of research protocols (2), community involvement in research oversight (3), reciprocal approvals (4), continuous education of EC members and staff, and (5) consideration of support for EC secretariat staff.

Verbal presentation of research protocols

Three respondents indicated that during PHEs, ECs may consider inviting investigators to present their research protocols verbally. This will allow clarity of issues that maybe misunderstood when written submissions are made. Often, delays in research approvals maybe caused by back-and-forth correspondence between the EC and the investigator.“Sometimes you may ask the investigators to present directly. They can submit the documents, and also present, so that the committee can probe; you know verbal is not the same as paper…the fastest way to expedite a protocol is to submit and have the person summarize, because they may write in a language that you don’t understand.” (PID05).“If we realized that the applications and the documents given to us were not sufficient to explain a situation, we held meetings in between with the applicants to clarify certain issues.” (PID03).

Community involvement in research oversight

In the future, two respondents mentioned the need to involve community members in the oversight of research related to PHEs. This will help in the management of potential myths and misconceptions around health research by providing clarity and education of other members of the society about the research process at the community level.“In case of any other pandemic, …we need to have probably a community member involved. If problems happen, where will they come from? It will come from the community.” (PID05).

Reciprocal approvals during public health emergencies

Four respondents reiterated the importance of considering another EC approval decision, especially in multisite projects. Instead of conducting an independent full review in multisite projects, ECs need to give attention to site-specific concerns and issue a reciprocal approval of the study.“Multi-sites protocol studies or multi-country studies. So, we do have such; they are common, and as our IRB, we do independent review cognizant of the fact that that study has an approval.” (PID16).

“Multi-sites protocol studies or multi-country studies. So, we do have such; they are common, and as our IRB, we do independent review cognizant of the fact that that study has an approval.” (PID16).

Continuous education of EC members and staff

Eleven respondents argued that EC members and staff need to undergo continuous training and education to prepare for pandemic times. Respondents indicated that there is a need for continued engagement of EC members on emerging diseases and associated ethical concerns. It is the responsibility of the EC host institution and the EC accrediting agency to ensure that members undergo continuous ethics education and training.“And then two, it is the issue of continuous education, or what you have said professional development for the staff to prepare them for times like this.” (PID15).“There should be continuous capacity building because some of the pandemics are coming for the first time; they are emerging diseases, and there is very little information available” (PID14).

“And then two, it is the issue of continuous education, or what you have said professional development for the staff to prepare them for times like this.” (PID15).

Support for secretariat or research administrative teams

Four respondents expressed concerns about the lack of attention to the secretariat staff and research administrators that support EC operations. These are officers who ensure that the EC operates optimally through the preparation of documents for review, logistical planning of EC meetings, and corresponding with principal investigators. The support included the infrastructure like computers, laptops, and internet connection to facilitate remote working.“Part of the support also means giving them enough materials like laptops so that even the secretariat team can even work from home effortlessly.” (PID06).“When we are preparing the honorarium specifically for the committee members, the secretariat, we are left out” (PID12).

Discussion

The goal of this study was to explore sustained and discontinued operational changes and future preparedness plans of ECs in Kenya in the post-COVID-19 pandemic era. Our findings demonstrated that five years after the initial declaration of COVID-19 as a PHEIC, ECs in Kenya retained practices that improved operational efficiency, such as having protocol lead discussants during meetings, the use of technology, and policy documents that supported the digitization of the research process. On the other hand, ECs stopped practices that were directly related to the pandemic and those that were deemed unsustainable in the long-term, for instance, special scientific committees, working in shifts, and parallel review of clinical trials. Other key findings from this research included the recommended actions that aid in preparation for future pandemics such as verbal presentation of research protocols, community involvement in the research oversight, reciprocal approvals during public health emergencies and the additional support for the secretariat staff.

Various efforts were made to ensure continuity of EC operations in the midst of the pandemic. Continued ethics reviews and approvals of pandemic-related research is a one way of actively and rapidly responding to the solutions needed to manage the disease. A survey conducted across 125 ECs in Italy presents results that are similar to our findings. It showed that, during the lockdown, only 2% of ECs stopped all operations, 6% conducted minimal work, 47% continued working regularly without prioritizing COVID-19-related research, and 45% continued with regular operations while prioritizing COVID-19 studies [34]. In our study, we found out that ECs in Kenya made all deliberate efforts to ensure continuity of ethical oversight of research and none of them stopped operations completely. However, we did not go in-depth to determine whether ECs in Kenya prioritized COVID-19 related research or not.

Our study revealed the importance of issuing reciprocal approvals for multisite projects to minimize duplication of effort and enhance efficiency. The parallel submission and review of research protocols among ECs and national regulators was also seen to reduce timelines. These findings are similar to those of De Crop et al. (2016), who indicated that double ethics reviews in emergencies delay the process of responding to the problem at hand. Instead, the researchers in Guinea recommended direct dialogue and joint reviews among ECs participating in multisite studies [35]. We postulate that collaborative review efforts of multisite research not only reduce the overall turnaround time, but also act as an avenue for knowledge and resource sharing among the ECs working together.

Our findings showed that some institutions established special COVID-19 scientific committees that would provide technical advice to the scientific community on the emergence of SARS-CoV-2. We found that this structure is similar to the Urgent Public Health Group (UPHG) that was established in the United Kingdom at the height of COVID-19 to evaluate research protocols that were deemed to have the greatest potential impact on the population. The UPHG evaluated the scientific merit and feasibility of protocols that required the attention of the public health authorities [36]. We advance the view that during public health emergencies, there is a need for a pool of experts who study the patterns of the disease and provide an informed opinion to key decision makers in the research process like ethics committees.

Our study revealed the importance of continuous training of EC members on emerging public health concerns. A similar study was conducted in India, in which respondents underscored the necessity of regular training of EC members to respond to the emerging needs and changing demands during pandemics [37]. Similarly, our study revealed the importance of developing policy documents that will guide EC operations during pandemics. A study in India showed that there was a need for amendments to EC guidelines and SOPs to address unique situations during PHEs. In India, it was reported that such guidelines should entail a virtual mode of functioning of ECs and remote study monitoring [37].

Our findings indicated that the inclusion of community members in the process of research oversight and implementation may be useful, especially during PHE. These results are similar to the findings from a study that interviewed EC chairpersons in South Africa, which showed that a community representative within the EC can help in providing insights as to how the community understands COVID-19. The respondents in the South African study showed that effective community engagement and actual representation of the entire community during the COVID-19 pandemic posed a challenge [38]. Since it may not be practically possible to have meaningful representation of all communities participating in research, we posit that ECs need to have provisions for alternate and invited memberships whenever people with lived experiences are required.

While COVID-19 posed unprecedented challenges to the research oversight during and after the pandemic era [39], perhaps, one of its silver linings is the adaptability of ECs. After the COVID-19 pandemic, EC meetings in Kenya are largely conducted virtually and this has prevented cases of meeting failure due to quorum glitches linked to travel logistics. ECs have adopted online submission and tracking systems that benefit researchers, reviewers, and EC staff, among other stakeholders, by reducing the costs linked to photocopies and the logistics of documentation delivery. ECs, as a result, have seen a reduction in the administrative and financial costs.

We recommend continuous updating of the policy and regulatory environment of EC operations to respond to the rapidly evolving research ethics landscape and peculiarities of PHEs. It is essential to continually carry out training for EC members on the emerging ethical issues during public health emergencies. We put forward the idea that flexibility of EC operations during PHEs may aid in ensuring continued ethical oversight of research. EC operational documents such as SOPs, guidelines, and policies are living documents that maybe adapted to respond to the changing demands during PHEs.

The findings of this research pointed out sustained innovations such as digitization of EC processes and implementation of frameworks for decentralized research. With the emergence of the use of technology in conducting research, there is a need for ECs to adjust their operational frameworks to accommodate this new phenomenon. We posit that innovations such as remote monitoring of approved research projects will not only optimize the use of available resources but also enhance active oversight of research.

On the other hand, findings also revealed some practices that were abandoned in the post-pandemic era, including special COVID-19 scientific committees, parallel reviews, quick turnaround reviews, and COVID-19 sensitization sessions during EC meetings. We argue that ECs need to institutionalize the culture of sensitizing reviewers on contemporary and emerging concepts. Training sessions on the different aspects of COVID-19 may have improved the capacity of EC members to review and provide ethical oversight of complex protocols. Similarly, ECs should be carrying out sensitization and training sessions to continually build capacity on emerging topics. In addition, while many respondents expressed satisfaction with parallel reviews, the abandonment of this practice could be associated with governance culture and regulatory rigidity. We recommend reconfiguration of the current sequential system of clinical trial reviews at EC and NDA levels. Though it was necessary to abandon special COVID-19 scientific committees, we posit that such committees could have been restructured to provide technical support to ECs in the scientific review of complex research protocols.

Reflection of the relationship between findings and the institutional resilience theory

While the institutional resilience theory promotes organizational ability to anticipate and respond to the crisis, these research findings provide practical preparedness plans for future unprecedented PHEs. ECs in Kenya and other similar settings may adopt strategies of reviewing research protocols expeditiously without compromising ethical and scientific rigor in a bid to respond to the pandemic. This may be done by adopting flexible protocol submission systems such as verbal presentation of research protocols, use of digital systems, and parallel submission of protocols between ECs and other regulatory agencies.

The institutional resilience theory emphasizes the strategic management of uncertainties. Our findings relate to this concept by focusing on human resource development and technological investment. For proper adaptation to shocks of PHEs and maintenance of core functions, ECs in Kenya and similar LMIC settings need to continually sensitize reviewers on emerging and remerging diseases of public health concern. The EC secretariat supports the proper functioning of the committee before, during, and after review meetings. EC host institutions should provide financial infrastructural, and professional development support to the EC secretariat for continuity in unforeseen circumstances.

Addressing potential ethical tensions from the research findings

Research findings highlight possible internal tensions between timeliness and robustness in ethics review during PHEs. Respondents associated discontinued practices like quick turnaround reviews in emergency situations with potential threats to ethical rigor and quality of research protocols. We postulate that while rapid reviews are useful in the context of public health emergencies, ECs must approach with caution to avoid the possibility of compromising the ethical and scientific rigor of the process. In particular, we recommend proper reconfiguration of practices such as quick turnaround reviews and parallel submission of research protocols under appropriate governance conditions. Such efficient practices could be implemented beyond pandemics to reduce the overall turnaround time of ethical and regulatory processes.

Policy and practice implications

The research governance systems should remain resilient and adaptable in public health crises such as those posed by unprecedented disease outbreaks. Sustained innovations such as digitalization of EC processes and efficient running of review meetings should be institutionalized for long-term adoption and co-learning across the different ECs in similar settings. Notably, national regulators that accredit and oversee functions of ECs should cement such best practices in the form of regulations, policies, and guidelines. In addition, regional and continental bodies may adopt best practices as templates for success in the quest for improved research governance systems and a strengthened clinical trial ecosystem.

There is a need for continual institutional learning and improvement of research governance systems in low-and middle-income countries. Instead of abandoning some of the best practices, such as quick turnaround and parallel reviews, ethics and regulatory bodies need to identify specific areas of improvement that will promote continuity of protocol review efficiency beyond pandemics.

We recommend deliberate investment in the EC human capital through continued capacity development as well as infrastructural and financial support for both the committee and the secretariat. It is the responsibility of the national EC accreditation agencies and host institutions to ensure that proper mechanisms are in place for continual capacity building in emerging concepts and secretariat support.

Respondents expressed the importance of issuing reciprocal EC approvals in situations where multiple institutions are involved. We contend that national EC accrediting institutions, regional, and continental research platforms should formalize such arrangements through collaborative review arrangements and reliance agreements. To improve the overall turnaround time for multicenter clinical trials where multiple ECs are involved, reciprocal approvals maybe necessary.

Limitations

One of the limitations of this study is the possible recall bias as it was conducted five years after the initial pandemic declaration and some respondents may have some difficulties in remembering the past events. Additionally, we purposively selected seven ECs in Kenya that were actively reviewing clinical trials during the COVID-19 pandemic. This selection criteria may have left out pertinent insights from other ECs that did not review clinical trials during the period of interest.

Conclusion

Five years after the initial COVID-19 pandemic declaration, ECs in Kenya which adapted operations to accommodate the PHE have retained some practices. These include those that enhance their overall function and efficiency. ECs also feel better prepared to adapt their services should another PHE arise in the near future.
