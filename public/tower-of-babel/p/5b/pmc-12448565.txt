Factors associated with self-care behavior among heart failure patients in India: A systematic review
Authors: Prima J. J. D’Souza, Mitchelle S. Lewis, Ganesh Paramasivam, Judith A. Noronha, P Kusumavathi, Tom Devasia, Linu S. George
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Self-care is crucial for heart failure management and complements pharmacological treatments; however, various factors hinder effective self-care, adversely affecting clinical outcomes and leading to poor quality of life. This systematic review aimed to identify factors influencing self-care behaviors among heart failure patients in India. The search was conducted in six databases, PubMed, CINAHL, Scopus, ProQuest, Web of Science, and Embase; between January 2012 and September 2024, 676 articles were reviewed. After removing duplicates, seven articles were included for analysis on the basis of the study inclusion criteria. The PRISMA guidelines has been used in this review. We included observational, cross-sectional, experimental, quasiexperimental, and qualitative studies published in English and conducted in India. The included studies were critically appraised using the Joanna Briggs Institute’s risk of bias tool. The data were synthesized narratively. The findings revealed various factors that influence self-care behavior, which were divided into favorable and hindering factors. The factors that favored self-care behavior were self-efficacy, disease-related knowledge, situational awareness, adequate family income, financial support, health insurance, supportive family, patient willpower, and confidence in healthcare. However, low education, lack of knowledge about heart failure, difficulty understanding health information, limited time spent by healthcare providers, low health literacy, low self-efficacy, financial difficulties, negative emotions, and lack of structured care plans hindered self-care. These findings underscore the need for health policies that prioritize patient education, accessible care and adequate financial support. Strengthening such policies could improve self-care adherence among heart failure patients, enhancing clinical outcomes and quality of life.

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) affects 56.5 million people globally.[1] The prevalence of HF has increased due to advancements in HF management, as there are improved survival rates following myocardial infarction and an aging population.[2] Currently, the incidence of HF is relatively high among older adults. However, there is potential for an increase in HF cases among young adults in the future.[3] According to the European Society of Cardiology Heart Failure Association atlas, the estimated prevalence of HF in India is 1%, with an incidence rate of 0.5–1.7 per 1000 person-years.[2]

Self-care is essential in managing HF, enhancing patient outcomes, and improving quality of life.[45] Various factors, including sociodemographic and clinical factors, influence self-care behavior in patients with HF. Despite global advancements in HF management, patients often experience poor quality of life, low functional ability, frequent hospitalizations, and increased mortality rates. However, in India, where HF is prevalent, identifying factors that affect self-care behaviors is crucial for developing targeted interventions and improving patient outcomes. Although limited literature is available on what influences self-care behavior among HF patients in India, studies conducted in developing countries, such as Nepal and Ethiopia, provide some insights.[67]

Numerous factors influence the self-care behavior of HF patients. A systematic review was conducted to identify the system-related and individual factors related to self-care among HF patients. Review reported that confidence in self-care, health literacy, knowledge related to the disease, support systems, age, cognitive dysfunction, and depression are commonly measured at the individual level. Very few were at the microsystem level, that is, interactions between individuals, families, neighbors, and friends, but none were found at the macro- or exosystem level of the model. The studies included the United States, Brazil, Italy, Sweden, Germany, the Netherlands, Poland, Iran, North Korea, Taiwan, China, Japan, and Ethiopia.[8] Among the several factors being studied, one of the studies sought to identify the role of health literacy, depression, disease knowledge, and self-efficacy with self-care. They reported that health literacy does not directly affect self-care. However, depression and self-efficacy negatively affect self-care.[9]

Another major factor influencing self-care behavior among HF patients is the economic burden that impose high costs on healthcare systems.[2] Countries with higher socioeconomic status have managed to decrease the disease burden due to better healthcare systems.[10] HF has a global economic impact estimated at $346.17 billion.[11] According to the American Heart Association (AHA), the average cost of an HF case is $5,380. In India, the out-of-pocket cost for HF treatment is INR 82,766 ($1,139), with an average total expenditure per person of INR 133,663 ($1,839).[12] This shows that, there is an increased economic burden on patients and families due to healthcare costs and out-of-pocket expenditures.[13] Financial burden significantly influences adherence to self-care behaviors, which are vital for patients with HF to maintain a better quality of life.[4]

This systematic review aligns with sustainable development goals 3, good health and well-being, and 4, quality education. For sustainable development, ensuring good health and promoting well-being is important. The component of self-care is essential for preventing the deterioration of the condition. For self-care to be effective, patients with the disease must have adequate education to understand why it is necessary to follow self-care behaviors and take responsibility for their health.[14]

This systematic review was conducted to identify factors influencing self-care among HF patients in India, addressing a gap in culturally specific interventions. Additionally, to plan effective interventions for the HF self-care, one must understand the factors that contribute to the self-care behavior of an individual. This information will help healthcare providers create tailored interventions to improve self-care among HF patients. Identifying the factors contributing to self-care adherence is crucial for researchers and clinicians in planning effective care. This study aims to comprehensively analyze and synthesize the factors associated with self-care behavior among patients diagnosed with HF in India. Researchers have identified key determinants necessary for adherence to self-care recommendations, such as disease-specific education, social support, and disease knowledge, which are vital for patients with HF. This review contributes to the knowledge base, provides valuable insights into healthcare strategies, enhances patient education, and improves the self-care practices of individuals with HF in India.

Materials and Methods

This systematic review explored the factors associated with self-care behavior in patients with HF in India. This review was conducted on the basis of the criteria of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines.[15] The protocol was registered with the International Registration of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) CRD42023465463.

Eligibility criteria

The population, concept, context (PCC) strategy was adopted to determine the search strategy and the inclusion criteria [Figure 1]. The review included observational, cross-sectional, qualitative, experimental, and nonexperimental studies. Studies published as editorials, letters, theses, brief communications, or short reports were excluded. The setting was restricted to India, as the researchers were interested in area-specific factors in developing self-care interventions specific to the area. The studies were published in English, and the publication period was restricted to January 2012 and September 2024, as the researchers were interested in understanding the recent factors affecting patients with HF.

Search terms used to develop search strategy

Population

This review focused on patients with HF in India. Adult patients above 18 years of age were included.

Concepts

The concept of the review is the self-care behavior of heart failure patients, which encompasses activities and decisions patients make to manage symptoms, adhere to treatment regimens, and maintain overall health.

Context

The context involves exploring the factors influencing self-care behaviors in HF patients. These factors include demographic, clinical, and psychological factors that influence self-care.

Databases and search strategies

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across six electronic databases, including PubMed/MEDLINE, CINAHL, Scopus, ProQuest, Web of Science, and Embase. We also searched Google Scholar to find potential studies. The search was conducted by combining key search terms via Boolean operators (“AND” and “OR”), including both Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text keywords. Study selection process

The articles retrieved from each database were imported into Rayyan software, and duplicates were removed. Using the software, two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts to select eligible articles for full-text screening. The third reviewer then screened the articles that were in conflict, and a final decision was made on the inclusion of studies. After completing the title and abstract screening process and resolving any conflicts, the articles eligible for full-text screening were retrieved and screened against the preset criteria. Any discrepancies among the reviewers regarding the inclusion or exclusion of articles were resolved through discussion to achieve consensus. Before starting the data extraction, two studies were piloted, and a research team’s consensus was obtained to proceed with the data extraction from the articles.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Two researchers assessed the quality of the included full-text articles via a critical appraisal checklist developed by the Joanna Briggs Institute.[16] Three qualitative, three cross-sectional, and one quasiexperimental study were included in the quality assessment. While the quality of the studies was checked, each item on the checklist was given one point. The scores were converted into percentages, with categories of good (80-100%), fair (50-80%), and poor (≤49%) [Table 1]. The data from all the studies, including the authors’ names, year of publication, state, study settings, design, results on associated factors, and self-care behavior, were extracted.

Data synthesis

Owing to the heterogeneity in the study designs of the included studies, a narrative synthesis was conducted, whereas a meta-analysis could not be performed. The factors were identified and summarized as favoring and hindering self-care.

Ethical consideration

All research ethics were taken into consideration when conducting and publishing this review, adhering to the required standards for the review studies.

Results

Selection of sources of evidence

A total of 676 studies published in English were identified via the search strategies. After deduplication, 41 duplicate studies were removed, leaving 635 articles for title and abstract screening. We excluded 616 studies after title and abstract screening, and 19 articles were included for full-text screening. Ultimately, seven articles were included in the final review. Among the seven studies included, only four reported self-care behavior in HF patients.[17192021] One study reported that 59% of patients monitored their fluid intake, 36.3% monitored symptoms, 27.7% checked pedal edema, 22.7% monitored weight daily, and 18.8% had a regular follow-up.[20] Another study reported that 10% were unsatisfactory, 44.3% were moderately satisfied, and 45.7% were satisfied with their self-care behaviors.[19] A correlational study reported that 77% of patients had average self-care practices, and 23.2% had good self-care practices, as measured by the HF self-care index.[17] Two articles reported that 50% of patients were irregularly taking medication.[2021] The details of the findings are presented in Table 3.

Factors favoring self-care behavior in HF patients

In this review, two qualitative studies reported on the factors that favored self-care behavior among HF patients.[2122] Being aware of the disease, confidence, self-efficacy, spiritual learning, and willpower were the intrinsic favoring factors for self-care behavior. Supportive family and community, financial support, living with family, good relationships and trust, and having health insurance were the extrinsic favoring factors of self-care.[21] Another study reported that factors of self-care are at various levels, such as the patient, caregiver, and healthcare provider levels. Patient-level factors include motivation from healthcare providers to improve self-care behavior and telephone follow-up. The caregiver-level factor was the support received by the patient from the family members, and the healthcare-related factors included advice to improve their behavior, such as quitting smoking and alcoholism.[22] From these studies, we can identify that the main theme for favoring self-care behavior is the presence of strong support systems at the individual, society, and healthcare levels. Factors hindering self-care behavior in HF patients

According to a qualitative study on the factors hindering HF self-care, 63.6% of patients had low health literacy, and 37.5% had high health literacy.[2122] Concerning educational level, studies reported that approximately 50% of the study participants had only primary education, and 24% were illiterate.[1723] A qualitative study on factors of self-care behavior revealed that 77.1% of patients were of lower socioeconomic status.[20] Among the quantitative studies, one reported a statistically significant association between self-care behavior, educational status (P = .01), and income (P = .004).[19] However, another study reported no significant associations (P > .05) between self-care for HF and age, sex, marital status, educational level, NYHA class, duration of illness, or duration of treatment.[17] The other barriers to HF care were patients’ difficulty understanding health information, lack of quality time with doctors during visits, and low self-efficacy.[23] Another study reported emotional stress and a lack of detailed plans for self-management as barriers.[22] Having negative emotions, including losing hope or stigma toward the disease and experiencing a financial burden, affects an individual’s self-care behavior.[20] Patients with cardiovascular diseases did not adhere to the medication regimen. The reasons for the lack of adherence to the treatment were negligence and absent-mindedness.[18] Some beliefs are ingrained in patients, including disease/symptom interpretation, considering themselves a burden to the family, trust issues, surrendering to God’s will, refusing the existing problem, and attaining a passive role. The other factors are awareness related to disease, disease management, one’s situation, emotional disturbances such as anxiety, coping mechanisms, and stigma when one is diagnosed or living with the disease.[20] We can infer that the main barriers to effective self-care behavior are educational status and the economic burden that patients face. Additionally, the outlook of the disease by themselves and the caregivers can influence their behavior. The details of the findings are given in Table 3.

Application of the framework for self-care for heart failure patients

After scrutinizing the data, the researchers concluded that by utilizing the middle-range theory of self-care illness of heart failure by Riegel Barbara, we could visualize the holistic view of factors affecting self-care. The theory explains that self-care is vital for healthy and unhealthy individuals, but the constructs differ. People suffering from long-term chronic illness have different meanings in regard to self-care, as self-care is essential for controlling the disease process, decreasing or managing symptoms, and improving the survival rate. This theory provides an overall view of the self-care concepts required to manage disease. This theory defines self-care as the “process of maintaining health as the central phenomenon through health-promoting practices and managing illness.” There are three components under the central umbrella of self-care: self-care maintenance, which explains adherence; self-care monitoring, which includes changes in routine and testing; and self-care management, which encompasses changes in diet or medications based on treatment. This theory aims to explain the process so that patients with chronic illness can obtain optimal care while maintaining symptoms.[24]

On the basis of the results of the findings from the extracted articles, the factors that favor and hinder self-care behavior can be placed in the components mentioned above [Figure 3]. The factors interact with each other on the basis of the constructs. Self-care maintenance requires continuous support from family, community, and healthcare providers, and the absence of a support system hinders them from self-care; self-care monitoring includes follow-up care from healthcare along with support, whereas hindering factors can include a negative outlook toward themselves and the disease. The last factor, self-care management, includes adherence to treatment, and the hindering factors are a lack of knowledge and an economic burden due to the disease.

Discussion

In managing HF, self-care behavior is critical for preventing symptom exacerbation, improving patient outcomes, and leading a good quality of life. However, adherence to self-care practices among HF patients in India remains suboptimal due to various sociocultural and clinical factors. The aim of this review was to explore the factors associated with self-care behavior among patients with HF in India with the intent of identifying culturally specific factors, enabling researchers to develop more personalized, patient-centric interventions. This review highlights the favorable and hindering factors for self-care in HF patients. This discussion is based on this classification, and researchers want to characterize the differences in identifying the factors in research conducted globally and in India. In this review, the favorable factors identified were doctor-patient trust and motivation; doctor-patient relationships; motivation by healthcare providers; good healthcare access; support systems, including family, community, and finance; gratitude toward the system; self-efficacy and willpower; and patient confidence in self-care.

A qualitative study reported the facilitators and motivators for self-care behavior in HF patients.[20] The European Heart Failure Self-Care Behavior Scale and Self-Care of Heart Failure Index (SCHFI) are the questionnaires most frequently used to assess self-care among HF patients; however, this review revealed that only one study used the SCHFI, in which 77% of patients reported average self-care behavior.[17] This study’s findings are supported by other studies conducted elsewhere that reported good adherence to self-care behavior.[2526]

Several factors could contribute to self-care behavior in patients with HF, which may facilitate or hinder their self-management of the disease. Patients with illiteracy, low education, low health literacy, rural residence, lack of knowledge of HF self-care, lack of social support, multiple comorbidities, a greater number of noncardiovascular comorbid conditions, five or more medications, prior hospitalization, NYHA class II-IV, and lack of patient education from healthcare settings reported lower self-care behavior.[26272829303132]

This review identified various factors that influence self-care behavior in HF patients. These results indicate that sociodemographic factors, knowledge, health literacy, and healthcare-related factors impact inadequate self-care behaviors. Considering the importance of various factors influencing self-care, it is important to pay attention to these factors in HF self-care while planning interventions. However, studies have also revealed that female sex, increased age, unemployment, no comorbidities, NYHA class III and IV, adequate knowledge, good economic status, better physical function, self-efficacy, duration of symptoms, and adequate social support are positively associated with adherence to self-care behaviors.[6727333435] However, Fetensa et al.[25] (2021) reported that female sex and comorbidities negatively affect self-care behavior. In contrast, urban residents and those with a duration of HF of more than 1 year had good adherence to self-care practices.[35]

A study conducted in the USA among HF patients reported a strong positive relationship between self-efficacy and self-care. Depression was negatively associated with self-care, whereas knowledge was not associated with self-care behavior.[36] In this review, we found that self-confidence was a favorable factor for self-care behavior in patients, which was similar to the findings of the study conducted in Korea.[35] There are various results concerning the contribution of the NYHA class to self-care behaviors. The present review revealed no significant differences between the NYHA class and self-care behavior.[17] In Ethiopia, patients in advanced NYHA classes exhibit good self-care behavior.[2737] Studies conducted in Iran reported that spiritual health, perceived social support, hope, quality of life, and HF knowledge were predictors of self-care.[3438]

The findings from this review revealed that healthcare-related factors such as good relationships with healthcare providers, advice, motivation from the doctor for following healthy behaviors, access to healthcare, and regular follow-up are positive factors that favor good self-care behavior. However, the nonavailability of healthcare providers was a negative factor.[2122] A study in line with this review reported that limited access to healthcare, lack of social support and financial strain hindered self-care adherence.[39]

Strengths, limitations and recommendations

Focusing on HF patients in India has provided valuable insight into culturally and regionally relevant factors associated with self-care, addressing specific gaps in the literature. Identifying the factors that affect self-care may help develop targeted interventions and policies to improve self-care and reduce hospital readmissions. The findings of this review have several limitations. First, a limited number of studies were identified from India in this review area. Second, the majority of the studies were conducted with small sample sizes. Third, the included studies were heterogeneous, making it difficult to draw strong conclusions. Finally, few studies included caregivers, health care providers, and HF patients; therefore, the results did not solely reflect the patients’ viewpoints. Periodic review and updating of the articles are recommended.

Conclusion

This review identified self-care behavior and various factors that influence self-care behavior among HF patients. However, the review could not reach a firm conclusion because of the heterogeneity of the studies. To meet the sustainable development goal of good health and well-being, planners and policymakers must design self-care interventions focusing on these factors to improve the well-being of HF patients. Additionally, this review highlights the need for further research on HF self-care among the Indian population to bridge the existing gaps in understanding the reasons for poor self-care behaviors among HF patients in India.
