Gender disparities in high-risk pulmonary embolism: a nationwide analysis of management strategies and clinical outcomes
Authors: Adam Folman, Marlon V Gatuz, Lucky R Cuenza, Maguli S Barel, Rami Abu-Fanne, Ariel Roguin, Ofer Kobo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPulmonary embolism (PE) remains a significant cause of cardiovascular mortality. Despite advancements in diagnosis and treatment, gender disparities in high-risk PE management and outcomes remain poorly understood. This study aimed to investigate gender-based differences in the presentation, treatment, and outcomes of patients with high-risk PE.MethodsWe analyzed data from the National Inpatient Sample for patients with high-risk PE from 2016 to 2021. High-risk PE was defined as PE with cardiogenic shock, mechanical ventilation, mechanical circulatory support, or vasopressors. Multivariable logistic regression was used to examine gender-based differences in management and outcomes.ResultsOf 45,140 patients with high-risk PE, 51.4% were female. Female patients were older (mean age 65.2 vs. 63.4 years, p < 0.001) and more likely to present with cardiac arrest (35.4% vs. 30.4%, p < 0.001). After adjustment, females were more likely to receive systemic thrombolysis (aOR 1.17, 95% CI 1.11–1.23, p < 0.001) but less likely to undergo catheter-directed embolectomy (aOR 0.92, 95% CI 0.86–0.99, p = 0.034) or surgical embolectomy/thrombectomy (aOR 0.83, 95% CI 0.73–0.93, p = 0.002). Female patients experienced higher risk of all-cause mortality (aOR 1.22, 95% CI 1.18–1.27, p < 0.001) and major adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events (aOR 1.21, 95% CI 1.16–1.25, p < 0.001).ConclusionSignificant gender disparities exist in the management and outcomes of high-risk PE. Female patients have higher mortality rates despite being more likely to receive systemic thrombolysis. Further research is needed to understand and address these disparities to improve outcomes for all patients with high-risk PE.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-025-05235-0.Keywords: Pulmonary embolism, High-risk pulmonary embolism, Gender disparities, Risk factors, Outcomes

Introduction

Pulmonary embolism (PE) is a significant cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, with an estimated annual incidence of approximately 1 per 1000 persons in the United States [1]. The incidence of PE has been continuously rising over the past two decades, with recent studies showing an increase from 0.1% of emergency department visits in 2010–2012 to 0.2% in 2017–2018 [2]. The mortality rate associated with PE varies depending on its severity, with high-risk PE carrying a particularly poor prognosis. In-hospital mortality for high-risk PE has been reported to be as high as 28.3%, with 30-day all-cause mortality reaching 30.2% [3]. Despite advancements in diagnosis and treatment, PE remains a leading cause of cardiovascular death, following only myocardial infarction and stroke [4].

Despite the substantial burden of PE, there are notable gaps in our understanding of gender disparities in its presentation, management, and outcomes, particularly in high-risk cases. While some studies have suggested that female may have higher rates of severe clinical features, such as hypotension and right ventricular dysfunction [5], others have found no significant differences in PE severity between genders [6]. Furthermore, conflicting data exist regarding gender differences in treatment approaches, with some research indicating that female are less likely to receive reperfusion therapy when controlled for PE severity [5], while other studies have found no disparities in the use of reperfusion therapy [6, 7].

The impact of gender on in hospital mortality in high-risk PE patients remains poorly understood, with limited data available from large-scale, contemporary studies. The objective of this paper is to address these knowledge gaps by examining gender disparities in high-risk PE using insights from the National Inpatient Sample (NIS). By leveraging large-scale, nationally representative data from the NIS, this study seeks to provide a comprehensive analysis of gender-specific patterns in high-risk PE, ultimately informing more tailored approaches to diagnosis, management, and risk stratification for this critical patient population.

Methods

Data source

The National Inpatient Sample (NIS), available since 1988, is one of the largest publicly available all payer inpatient healthcare databases in the United States. The NIS dataset includes approximately 7 million hospital admissions annually, representing a 20% stratified sample of all discharges from U.S. community hospitals, excluding rehabilitation and long term acute care facilities. The NIS is part of the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project (HCUP) and is designed to provide estimates of inpatient utilization, access, costs, quality, and outcomes at local, regional, and national levels across the nation [8].

Study design and population

In this retrospective study, we analyzed adult patients (aged 18 years and older) who were hospitalized with a primary discharge diagnosis of high-risk PE from 2016 to 2021, stratified by gender. Patient selection was based on the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) diagnosis codes, which were introduced in 2016 and offered more detailed information than the former ICD-9 system. Table S1 lists the ICD-10 codes related to patient and procedural characteristics. For each hospital discharge, we recorded patient demographics such as age, gender, race, admission day (weekday or weekend), expected primary payer, and median household income based on ZIP code. We also looked at patient disposition at discharge. We excluded records with missing information on age, gender, elective status, admission type and day, and mortality from our analysis (refer to Fig. 1 for the study flow diagram). Each discharge record included data on up to 30 diagnoses.

Flow diagram

In this study, a “high-risk PE” was defined as PE with cardiogenic shock (ICD-10 code R57.0), mechanical ventilation (ICD-10 codes 5A19054/35Z/45Z/55Z), mechanical circulatory support (MCS) (ICD-10 codes 5A1522G/5A15A2G/5A15A2H), or vasopressors (ICD-10 codes 3E030XZ/3E033XZ/3E040XZ/3E043XZ) [9, 10] Supplementary Table S1 contains a comprehensive list of ICD-10-CM codes used to identify PE. These codes also helped classify procedural details during hospitalization, such as systemic thrombolysis, catheter-directed thrombolysis, ultrasound-facilitated catheter-directed thrombolysis, catheter-directed embolectomy, surgical embolectomy or thrombectomy, and inferior vena cava (IVC) filter placement.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of interest was the difference in all-cause in-hospital mortality between male and female patients with high-risk PE. Secondary outcomes included gender differences in clinical presentation and comorbidities, utilization of diagnostic modalities and treatment strategies, and in-hospital adverse events. These adverse events encompassed stroke, MI, death of any cause (MACCE), major bleeding, intracranial hemorrhage (ICH), and non-ICH bleeding events. Additionally, we evaluated hospital length of stay and total hospitalization costs. MACCE was defined as a composite of all-cause mortality, acute ischemic CVA or transient ischemic attack and cardiac complications. Gastrointestinal, retroperitoneal, intracranial, intracerebral hemorrhage, as well as periprocedural hemorrhage, unspecified hemorrhage or requiring blood transfusion were defined as major bleeding. The treatments that the participants also received including invasive management procedures such as systemic thrombolysis, catheter directed thrombolysis, ultrasound facilitated catheter directed thrombolysis, catheter directed embolectomy, surgical embolectomy/thrombectomy and inferior vena cava (IVC) filter placement.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed on IBM SPSS version 29. Continuous variables were presented as median and interquartile range, due to skewed data, and categorical data were presented as frequencies and percentages. Pearson’s chi square test was used to compare categorical variables, and continuous variables were compared using t-test or the Kruskal Wallis test, as appropriate. Sampling weights were used to calculate the estimated total discharges as specified by AHRQ. Multivariable logistic regression models were utilized to examine the association between gender and in-hospital outcomes among patients with high-risk PE. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were produced by these models, adjusted for baseline differences between groups. Adjustment was made for a range of covariates such as weekend admission, hospital bed size, region and location/teaching status, VF, VT, AF, HF, hypertension, valvular heart disease, dyslipidemia, chronic liver disease, chronic lung disease, ethnicity, primary expected payer, smoking status, dementia, obesity, hematologic malignancies, chronic kidney disease, anemia, thrombocytopenia, coagulopathies, diabetes mellitus, malignancies, cardiac arrest., outcomes were adjusted for all of the above and also for including systemic thrombolysis, catheter-directed thrombolysis, ultrasound-facilitated thrombolysis, catheter-directed embolectomy, surgical embolectomy/thrombectomy, and inferior vena cava (IVC) filter placement.

Result

Of a total of 1,105,295 patients with a primary diagnosis of acute PE, 45,140 (4.1%) were classified as having high-risk and were included in this analysis. The study population comprised 21,965 (48.6%) male and 23,275 (51.4%) female patients. Female patients were significantly older than male patients (mean age 65.2 vs. 63.4 years, p < 0.001). Regarding ethnicity, White patients constituted the majority in both groups, but with a higher proportion among males (66.5% vs. 62.1%), while Black patients were more prevalent among females than males (26.5% vs. 21.9%, p < 0.001).

In terms of record characteristics, female patients had a higher incidence of cardiac arrest (35.4% vs. 30.4%, p < 0.001) and acute cor pulmonale (24.2% vs. 21.7%, p < 0.001), while male patients more frequently presented with ventricular fibrillation (4.2% vs. 3.2%, p < 0.001) and ventricular tachycardia (8.3% vs. 6.2%, p < 0.001).

Regarding comorbidities, female patients had a higher prevalence of valvular heart disease (8.9% vs. 7.2%, p < 0.001), chronic lung disease (25.4% vs. 24.0%, p < 0.001), obesity (28.5% vs. 21.9%, p < 0.001), and anemia (44.4% vs. 38.2%, p < 0.001). Conversely, male patients more frequently had heart failure (37.1% vs. 33.5%, p < 0.001), atrial fibrillation/flutter (27.2% vs. 20.5%, p < 0.001), dyslipidemia (31.8% vs. 31.2%, p = 0.169), smoking history (35.8% vs. 25.9%, p < 0.001), and hematologic malignancy (14.2% vs. 11.5%, p < 0.001). In-hospital procedures and outcomes

Crude rates

Table 2 showed the analysis of in-hospital management and clinical outcomes of patients with high-risk PE stratified by gender. Regarding management strategies, female patients were significantly more likely to receive systemic thrombolysis compared to male patients (17.5% vs. 15.5%, p < 0.001). However, Male patients had numerically higher rates of catheter-directed thrombolysis (7.3% vs. 7.0%, p = 0.260) and catheter-directed embolectomy (7.5% vs. 7.2%, p = 0.130), but these differences were not statistically significant. Males were also more likely to undergo surgical embolectomy/thrombectomy (2.9% vs. 2.5%, p = 0.002) and receive IVC filter placement (15.2% vs. 14.3%, p = 0.008). Females had marginally higher rates of ultrasound-facilitated catheter-directed thrombolysis (1.5% vs. 1.4%, p = 0.363), but this difference was not statistically significant.

In terms of clinical outcomes, female patients experienced significantly higher rates of all-cause mortality (45.5% [26060] vs. 42.1% [20370], p < 0.001) and major adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events (MACCE) (49.6% vs. 45.5%, p < 0.001). The incidence of major bleeding was similar between genders (13.8% for females vs. 13.6% for males, p = 0.033). When examining specific types of bleeding, intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) rates were identical (3.2% for both genders, p = 0.713), gastrointestinal bleeding was more common in male patients (8.2% vs. 7.5%, p = 0.004), retroperitoneal bleeding was significantly more frequent in females (2.5% vs. 1.3%, p < 0.001), and procedure-related bleeding was slightly more prevalent in female patients (1.0% vs. 0.9%, p < 0.001).

Male patients had a longer mean length of stay compared to female patients (9.8 days vs. 8.9 days, p < 0.001) and the mean total hospital charges were higher for male patients ($186,559.7 vs. $172,881.0, p < 0.001).

Figure 2 illustrates the disposition of patients with high-risk PE stratified by gender. Male patients were more likely to be discharged directly home compared to female patients (69.3% vs. 60.3%). Conversely, female patients had higher rates of discharge to intermediate care facilities (15.7% vs. 11.1%) and home health care services (18.2% vs. 13.1%). Male patients were slightly more likely to be discharged to short-term facilities (2.3% vs. 1.9%) and to leave against medical advice (1.2% vs. 0.7%). These gender-based differences in disposition were all statistically significant, with p-values less than 0.001.

Multivariable logistic analysis

The multivariate analysis showed several significant findings (Table 3). After adjusting for various factors, females were significantly more likely to receive systemic thrombolysis (aOR 1.17, 95% CI 1.11–1.23, p < 0.001) compared to males. However, they were less likely to undergo certain invasive procedures, including catheter-directed embolectomy (aOR 0.92, 95% CI 0.86–0.99, p = 0.034), surgical embolectomy/thrombectomy (aOR 0.83, 95% CI 0.73–0.93, p = 0.002), and IVC filter placement (aOR 0.86, 95% CI 0.82–0.91, p < 0.001). Interestingly, there were no statistically significant differences between genders in the use of catheter-directed thrombolysis (aOR 0.97, 95% CI 0.90–1.04, p = 0.406) or ultrasound-facilitated catheter-directed thrombolysis (aOR 1.08, 95% CI 0.92–1.27, p = 0.338).

Discussion

In this large, nationally representative cohort of 45,140 patients hospitalized with high-risk pulmonary embolism (PE), we identified significant gender-based disparities in both management strategies and clinical outcomes. Despite being more likely to receive systemic thrombolysis, women experienced significantly higher rates of major adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events (MACCE) and all-cause in-hospital mortality compared to men. These differences persisted even after adjusting for baseline characteristics and comorbidities. Additionally, women were less likely to undergo invasive procedures such as catheter-directed embolectomy, surgical thrombectomy, and IVC filter placement. Notably, women presented with more severe clinical features, including higher rates of cardiac arrest and acute cor pulmonale. These findings underscore a critical paradox: although women received more aggressive medical therapy, their clinical outcomes were worse, suggesting that current treatment paradigms may inadequately address gender-specific factors in high-risk PE.

The higher mean age of female patients in our study aligns with previous studies [11–13] which reported that female with acute PE were older by 3.7–7.7 years compared to men. This trend is further supported by studies from Korea and Japan, which also found a higher incidence of PE among older female [14–16].

A large-scale study by Jarman et al. found that female with PE were more likely to have severe clinical features, including higher rates of hypotension and shock, higher pulmonary artery pressures, and more frequent evidence of right ventricular dysfunction on echocardiography [5]. This aligns with our observation of higher rates of cardiac arrest and acute cor pulmonale in female patients.

The increased prevalence of PE in older female may be attributed to age-related physiological changes such as reduced mobility, higher rates of obesity, higher incidence of concomitant diseases and hormonal shifts post-menopause that alter coagulation dynamics [12, 17]. Estrogen modulates coagulation through complex pathways, promoting a prothrombotic state, particularly in premenopausal women, while postmenopausal hormonal shifts exacerbate endothelial dysfunction and alter thrombotic risk profiles. Furthermore, gender differences in vascular physiology—such as smaller pulmonary artery diameters and differential right ventricular remodeling responses—may predispose women to more severe hemodynamic compromise in PE. Women may also exhibit heightened inflammatory responses and platelet reactivity, influencing both clot burden and the risk of adverse events following thrombolytic therapy [18–20].

Our multivariate analysis revealed significant gender disparities in the management and outcomes of high-risk PE. Consistent with our findings, a study by Sedhom et al. demonstrated that female were more likely to receive systemic thrombolysis compared to men [21]. Additionally, our results align with a study by Sathianathan et al., which reported higher rates of inferior vena cava (IVC) filter placement and catheter-directed thrombolysis in male patients compared to females [22].

Despite the more frequent use of advanced therapies like systemic thrombolysis in female, our study showed higher in-hospital mortality rates and MACCE among female patients with high-risk PE. A study by Sedhom et al., aligns closely with our observations. Their analysis revealed that in-hospital mortality was significantly higher among female in both unadjusted analysis (30.7% vs. 27.8%, P < 0.001) and after propensity score matching, despite female receiving advanced therapies more frequently [21]. Similarly, a multicenter retrospective study by Morosato et al., which included patients from five Italian centers who underwent catheter-directed treatments (CDTs) for high-risk and intermediate-high risk PE, found that female patients had higher in-hospital mortality rates (20% versus 8.9%, OR 2.53 95% CI 1.09–6.41, p = 0.038) and higher all-cause mortality at 1-year follow-up (29.1% versus 12.7%, HR 2.50 95% CI 1.15–5.43, p = 0.022) [23].

This raises the idea that systemic thrombolysis may not adequately address larger or more organized clot burdens seen in severe cases, where mechanical removal (which women were less likely to receive) might be more effective [24–25]. This suggests that current risk stratification models may underestimate the severity of PE in women or inadequately capture gender-specific pathophysiology. Future research should investigate whether female-specific treatment thresholds or procedural considerations could improve outcomes.

The lower rates of catheter-directed embolectomy and surgical thrombectomy among female patients may be multifactorial. Patient-related factors such as advanced age, higher burden of comorbidities (e.g., anemia, obesity), and higher rates of cardiac arrest at presentation may have influenced eligibility for invasive procedures. Additionally, institutional or physician-related biases cannot be excluded. Prior studies suggest that unconscious gender bias may impact procedural decision-making, particularly under emergent conditions where time-sensitive interventions are required [26].

Our study on gender disparities in high-risk PE management and outcomes has significant clinical implications, highlighting the need for gender-specific treatment strategies. However, limitations of using the National Inpatient Sample (NIS) include lack of detailed clinical data, potential coding inaccuracies, absence of post-discharge information, and limited data on specific medications and dosages. The database’s reliance on ICD coding may lead to misclassification bias, as the accuracy of coding can vary. Despite these limitations, our study has several strengths. The NIS provides a large, nationally representative sample, allowing for robust analysis of trends and outcomes across diverse healthcare settings. Future research combining multiple databases and incorporating more detailed clinical data could further elucidate the complex interplay of factors influencing gender disparities in high-risk PE management and outcomes.

Conclusion

Our comprehensive analysis of gender disparities in high-risk PE reveals a critical paradox: despite receiving more aggressive medical therapy, female experience significantly worse outcomes. Female patients, who were older and presented with more severe clinical features, received systemic thrombolysis more frequently but were less likely to undergo invasive procedures. Most strikingly, they experienced higher rates of major adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events and all-cause mortality. These findings demand an urgent reevaluation of high-risk PE management. Current approaches may not adequately address the unique characteristics of female patients. Implementing gender-specific protocols is crucial to improve outcomes for all patients with high-risk PE, potentially saving lives and reducing the burden of this critical cardiovascular condition [27–34].
