Deep Learning Enables Identification of Antimicrobial Peptides Through Mechanochromic Fingerprints
Authors: Jiali Chen, Che‐Lun Chin, Qingzhen Zhu, Ling Chao, Kaori Sugihara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAntimicrobial peptides (AMPs) are promising antibiotic alternatives, but their diverse modes of action make functional classification slow and labor‐intensive. Here we introduce a rapid and scalable strategy for AMP identification that integrates low‐cost, self‐assembled polydiacetylene (PDA) sensors with hyperspectral imaging and deep learning. AMP–PDA interactions generate mechanochromic spectral fingerprints that capture subtle differences in affinity, conformation, and penetration depth in membranes. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) trained on full spectral datasets accurately distinguished seven AMPs at two concentrations with 96.79% accuracy, whereas conventional two‐wavelength colorimetric response failed entirely. The rich chemical information embedded across the entire visible wavelength spectral region reveals that mechanochromic polymers encode far more detail than previously recognized. These findings establish PDA mechanochromism, when paired with high‐throughput spectral imaging and deep learning, as a powerful and accessible platform for rapid AMP screening and, more broadly, as a foundation for scalable, information‐dense biosensing technologies.Keywords: artificial intelligence, biosensor, computational biology, computer science, convolutional neural network, deep learning, hyperspectral imaging, nanotechnology, pattern recognition

Introduction

Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) are promising antibiotic alternatives, but their diverse modes of action make functional classification slow and labor‐intensive. Here we introduce a rapid and scalable strategy for AMP identification that integrates low‐cost, self‐assembled polydiacetylene (PDA) sensors with hyperspectral imaging and deep learning. AMP‐PDA interactions generate mechanochromic spectral fingerprints that capture subtle differences in affinity, conformation and penetration depth in membranes. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) trained on full spectral datasets accurately distinguished seven AMPs at two concentrations with 96.79% accuracy, whereas conventional two‐wavelength colorimetric response failed entirely. The rich chemical information embedded across the entire visible wavelength spectral region reveals that mechanochromic polymers encode far more detail than previously recognized. These findings establish PDA mechanochromism, when paired with high‐throughput spectral imaging and deep learning, as a powerful and accessible platform for rapid AMP screening and, more broadly, as a foundation for scalable, information‐dense biosensing technologies. AMPs are one of the prime candidates as alternatives to conventional antibiotics in the current crisis of antimicrobial resistance (AMR). They are short amino acid chains (6–50 residues) that act as innate immune components across eukaryotes, bacteria, and archaea [1]. They exhibit broad‐spectrum antimicrobial activity mainly through lipid membrane disruption [2], a mechanism that limits the development of bacterial resistance since lipids are not directly DNA‐encoded. These attractive properties have fueled intense scientific interest and substantial investment in the discovery and optimization of next‐generation AMPs [3, 4].

Polydiacetylene (PDA) is a mechanochromic polymer that changes its color from blue to red upon various stimuli, including external forces [5, 6, 7], heat, [8, 9], and biomolecules [10, 11]. Interestingly, its amphiphilic structure enables incorporation into lipid bilayers, rendering biological membranes mechanosensitive [12, 13]. Jelinek and coworkers demonstrated that the color change of PDA embedded inside phospholipids can partially reflect the functional activity of AMPs, paving the way for PDA‐based AMP sensing [14]. However, this pioneering work, along with subsequent studies, [15, 16] relied on either color changes observable by the naked eye or colorimetric responses (ratiometric quantification based on absorbance at two wavelengths), which lack the resolution necessary to distinguish among different peptide properties.

To extract richer information from PDA color transitions, we recently implemented hyperspectral imaging. This technique records a full transmission spectrum at every pixel in bright‐field mode, allowing precise color identification and spatial mapping across materials. Within minutes, 484,416 spectra can be collected from a single image, providing ultrahigh‐throughput spectral data. Using spectra‐mapping software, we found that the resulting spectral distribution acts as a unique fingerprint capable of distinguishing different stimuli, such as pH variation and heat [17]. These results highlight the potential of PDA for more advanced spectral sensing through improved data acquisition and analysis.

In this study, we present a novel strategy for AMP identification that integrates PDA sensing with hyperspectral imaging and deep learning (Figure 1). By leveraging the large datasets generated through hyperspectral imaging, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) were trained to classify seven AMPs at two concentrations (14 classes in total), achieving 96.79% prediction accuracy for both peptide identity and concentration. This high performance was enabled by the use of full spectral information, which contains far more detail than the colorimetric responses employed in conventional PDA sensing.

Results

TRCDA/DMPC Hybrid PDA Films Were Assembled on Glass by the Langmuir–Blodgett Method

To facilitate the implementation of hyperspectral microscopy, PDA was formed on glass coverslips. 10,12‐Tricosadiynoic acid (TRCDA) and dimyristoylphosphatidylcholine (DMPC) were mixed at a molar ratio of 8.5:1.5 and deposited on glass surfaces using the Langmuir–Blodgett technique, followed by UV irradiation (Figure 2a,b). The assembled PDA has a conjugated backbone that absorbs around 650 nm, appearing blue (Figure 2a). The blue PDA is metastable, thus mechanical or chemical stimuli can twist its backbone, reducing π‐conjugation and increasing the energy gap. This causes a color transition from blue to red and induces fluorescence. The sensitivity of this response can be enhanced by adding other lipids [18] such as DMPC in this study (Figure 2c). However, increasing the PDA's sensitivity also makes it more susceptible to salts in solution. To address this issue, the modified protocol included pre‐incubating the assembled monomers in buffer solution with 150 mM NaCl, and then polymerized afterward, thereby minimizing salt‐induced color changes (Figure 2c). In the past, we and other researchers have explored various PDA compositions to tune sensitivity, stability, membrane charge, and membrane‐mimetic properties, and have found that lipid additives and membrane fluidity strongly influence PDA responses [10, 19, 20, 21]. Among them, TRCDA/DMPC was selected in the present work because of its high sensitivity and stability, as the PDA system here primarily serves as a matrix for capturing the physicochemical properties of AMPs.

(c) Left: Effect of DMPC addition on peptide sensitivity. Right: Effect of pre‐incubation with HEPES buffer solution (150 mM NaCl) on PDA stability under salt.

Hyperspectral Microscopy Visualized PDA Microdomains Shifting From Various Shades of Blue to Red by Peptide Stimuli

These blue PDA films were characterized by hyperspectral imaging with a pixel size of 0.2 µm and an image size of 696 × 696 pixels, yielding 484,416 spectra acquired within six minutes. The obtained spectra were first analyzed using the Spectral Angle Mapper (SAM) algorithm, a non‐AI method that classifies spectra by similar shapes [17]. Before stimulation, the blue PDA crystals already exhibited diverse absorption spectra with the main peaks around 650 nm, indicating the presence of microdomains that appear as slightly different shades of blue (Figure 3a Before). These spectral variations primarily originate from intrinsic structural differences rather than film thickness as the SAM algorism normalizes intensity and classifies spectra based on their shapes [22]. After incubation with mastoparan 8, the red PDA also displayed multiple spectra with main peaks around 550 nm, suggesting the existence of microdomains showing slightly different shades of red (Figure 3a After). These results demonstrate that both blue and red PDAs comprise microdomains with distinct spectral characteristics, each transitioning from a specific blue to a specific red upon peptide stimulation, thereby encoding extremely rich information. Such complex spectral diversity has been overlooked in conventional vesicle‐based PDA sensing, where heterogeneous signals are ensemble‐averaged and further simplified into colorimetric responses using only two wavelengths (e.g., 550 nm/650 nm), resulting in a substantial loss of information [23, 24].

A Standard CNN, a Multiscale CNN, and a Multiscale CNN‐BiLSTM Were Trained Using 5‐Fold Cross‐Validation

Next, blue PDA films were incubated with seven types of AMPs; indolicidin, magainin 1, magainin 2, oritavancin, mastoparan, mastoparan 8, and mastoparan X, (Figure 3b) either at 8 µM or 128 µM. These peptides were selected based on the criteria that i) they exhibit clear PDA blue‐to‐red transitions, and ii) they are all clinically relevant. AMP based on beta sheets such as defensins were excluded as they did not present clear PDA color transition (Figure S4). Hyperspectral imaging was performed before and after peptide incubation from seven samples, yielding 3,390,912 spectra for each class (peptide and concentration). Before feeding the spectra into deep learning models, several data cleaning steps were applied: (i) raw transmission spectra were converted to absorption spectra; (ii) hyperspectral images before and after peptide stimulation were registered to co‐localize PDA crystals; (iii) noisy spectral regions below 450 nm and above 800 nm were removed; (iv) baselines were corrected; (v) pixels corresponding to glass substrates were excluded; (vi) spectra were averaged over 100, 700, or 1500 pixels; and (vii) these averaged spectra were used directly or red and blue spectra were combined through addition, subtraction, or alignment to create four feature types (Figure 3c and Figure S1: Red, Add, Subtract, Align).

These features were used to train a model based on CNN. CNNs are deep learning architectures that automatically extract hierarchical features from data using learnable convolutional filters [25]. The model used here consists of three convolutional blocks for multi‐scale spectral feature extraction, followed by three fully connected layers for dimensionality reduction and classification, batch normalization layers for training stability, dropout layers for overfitting prevention, and max pooling layers for spatial downsampling. We compared three custom CNN architectures: a standard CNN, a multiscale CNN [26], and a multiscale CNN combined with a BiLSTM (Figure 3d) [27]. The multiscale CNN added three parallel convolutional branches with kernel sizes of 176, 68, and 27 to capture global spectral shapes, major peaks, and minor peaks corresponding to the blue and red PDA bands. The CNN‐BiLSTM model further integrated a bidirectional Long Short‐Term Memory (LSTM) layer to capture correlations among hierarchical features for robust sequential classification. All networks were trained using 5‐fold cross‐validation across 8 GPUs. Finally, 100 entirely new data from new PDA samples were used for validation. Prediction results were determined by majority voting from the five folds and presented as percentages.

CNN‐BiLSTM Identified Both Peptide Type and Concentration With 96.79% Accuracy

Prediction accuracy reached 96.79% when the multiscale CNN‐BiLSTM was combined with Add‐based features averaged over 700 pixels (Figure 3e). Among the three parameters tested, namely the model type (CNN, multiscale CNN, multiscale CNN‐BiLSTM), the feature type (Red, Add, Subtract, Align), and the number of pixels averaged (100, 700, 1500), the feature type had the strongest effect. Subtract‐based features performed significantly worse than others. Interestingly, combining blue and red PDA spectra (Add‐based) resulted in higher accuracy than using only red spectra (Red‐based), indicating that the entire blue to red transition carries more information than the red spectra alone [17]. The second most influential parameter was the number of pixels averaged. Averaging over 700 pixels improved accuracy by enhancing the signal to noise ratio, while further averaging to 1500 pixels provided no additional benefit, suggesting saturation (results from other averaging conditions are shown in Figure S3). The confusion matrix for the best case (CNN‐BiLSTM with Add‐based features averaged over 700 pixels) shows that most classes achieved over 95% prediction accuracy, most classes achieved over 95% prediction accuracy, with only minor misclassifications (Figure 3f). The model successfully identifying both peptide type and concentration, which was not possible using the conventional vesicle approach with colorimetric response‐based analysis. An exception was magainin 1 at 128 µM, which reached 93% accuracy, with the remaining 7% misclassified as magainin 1 at 8 µM. This occurs when the PDA response does not perfectly correlate with peptide concentration. Another exception was oritavancin at 128 µM, which showed 87% accuracy, with 12% misclassified as magainin 1 (128 µM) and 1% as indolicidin (128 µM). At lower concentrations, 12% were predicted as indolicidin (8 µM) and 1% as mastoparan 8 (8 µM), indicating that some peptides induce similar PDA responses. (Figure S5). Analysis was also performed using a negative control (addition of HEPES buffer solution to PDA without AMP), which yielded similar results (Figure S6).

Occlusion Sensitivity Analysis Confirmed That the Model Primarily Relied on the Blue and Red Spectral Bands

To clarify how deep learning overcomes this limitation, we applied occlusion sensitivity analysis for identifying which wavelengths the neural network relies on for classification. Occlusion sensitivity analysis is a widely used interpretability method in artificial intelligence [28]. It evaluates the contribution of different input regions by systematically masking parts of the input and observing the resulting changes in model confidence. In our system, spectral segments of approximately 8 nm were masked to assess the importance of each region. Regions whose occlusion markedly reduced classification accuracy were identified as critical for the model's decision‐making.

Occlusion sensitivity scores from ten correctly classified datasets revealed that the CNN models primarily relied on spectral regions between 470–550 nm and 550–680 nm for classification (Figure 4b), corresponding to the red and blue absorption bands, respectively. In the intermediate region around 550 nm, the scores decreased, indicating that this wavelength provided limited information for accurate classification. These results confirm that the model leveraged both the entire blue and red spectral regions (not only at 640 nm and 540 nm) to make accurate predictions. PDA absorption spectra exhibit a characteristic double‐peak structure (Figure 2a). Conventionally, analysis has focused on the dominant peaks (e.g., 640 nm and 540 nm) because of their higher reproducibility relative to the secondary peaks. However, our results demonstrate that the previously neglected secondary peak also contributes meaningfully to the decision process. Representative examples of occlusion sensitivity scores aligned with spectral shapes further support this interpretation (Figures 4c and S2). At 8 µM indolicidin, the increase in the red peak was very subtle (almost invisible), suggesting that the model relied mainly on the reduction of the blue peak. In contrast, at 128 µM, a small red peak began to emerge, allowing the model to also utilize the red band for classification.

What Does Misclassification Imply?

Some data were misclassified as other peptide types, a tendency most pronounced for oritavancin. This suggests that certain peptides may interact with PDA membranes through similar mechanisms. (Table 1) summarizes the physicochemical properties of seven AMPs used in this study. Indolicidin is a short, tryptophan‐rich cationic peptide that perturbs lipid packing at the interface between polar headgroups and hydrophobic tails. Its ability to partition into diverse bilayer compositions underlies its broad antimicrobial and hemolytic activities [29, 30]. Magainins are positively charged amphipathic peptides with similar α‐helical structures but subtle mechanistic distinctions. Magainin 1 binds anionic lipids and induces curvature [31], while magainin 2 lies parallel to the membrane surface [32], promoting vesicle leakage and nanopore formation through membrane tension and lipid density reduction. Mastoparans are cationic peptides carrying four positive charges. Among them, mastoparan X notably accelerates lipid flip‐flop, facilitates its own transbilayer translocation, and disrupts membranes through the formation of transient pores [33]. Oritavancin is a glycopeptide‐based antimicrobial agent that disrupts bacterial membranes by reorganizing lipid domains and degrading exposed bilayer edges [34]. It simultaneously compromises barrier integrity and alters lipid microdomain structure, exerting dual effects on permeability and membrane organization [35].

Our model misclassified 12% of oritavancin (128 µM) as magainin 1 (128 µM), and 12% of oritavancin (8 µM) as indolicidin (8 µM). Since oritavancin and indolicidin are both potent antimicrobial agents against Gram positive S. aureus., they may share similar functions, although oritavancin is less charged and more hydrophobic than indolicidin. In contrast, magainin 1 kills only Gram‐negative E. coli. Nevertheless, both oritavancin and magainin 1 are moderately cationic (+2 vs. +3) and slightly hydrophobic (logP = 4.1 vs. GRAVY = 0.22), sharing some physicochemical properties. The minimal misclassification across other peptides indicates that their interactions with PDA are multifaceted and do not rely on single parameters such as electric charge or hydrophobicity.

The Present Data Benchmarks the Characterization of Stimulus‐Induced Mechanochromic Transitions Using Hyperspectral Imaging and ML

Hyperspectral imaging across different wavelength regions, has been previously employed in a variety of fields. Zhang and coworkers utilized a hyperspectral Penalized Reference Matching algorithm with Stimulated Raman Scattering (PRM–SRS) microscopy to map the subcellular distributions of sphingosine and cardiolipin in human brain tissue, demonstrating its ability to resolve lipid subtypes at the subcellular level [47]. Dong's group introduced spectral phasor single‐molecule localization microscopy (SP‐SMLM), which integrates hyperspectral and super‐resolution imaging, to study organelle‐specific polarity and lipid composition remodeling in living cells [48].

Recent development of deep learning has further accelerated the advancement of its analytical methods owing to its automatic feature extraction capabilities and robustness to noise [49]. Manifold and coworkers developed UwU‐Net, a deep learning framework for hyperspectral images, which achieved superior performance in label‐free prediction of nuclear, mitochondrial, and endoplasmic reticulum structures using hyperspectral SRS microscopy [50]. Yi and coworkers developed a ML approach for simultaneous prediction of lipid and protein oxidation in mutton meat using visible‐near‐infrared hyperspectral imaging [51]. Zhang and coworkers introduced DeepChem, a high‐speed multiplex chemical imaging that enables deep learning to identify cellular components accurately from noisy hyperspectral images [52]. Magnussen and coworkers demonstrated quantitative chemical analysis of fungi based on IR hyperspectral imaging combined with deep learning [53]. These rapid advancements in the field demonstrate that hyperspectral imaging and deep learning are a powerful combination, enhancing analytical efficiency and precision, reducing dependence on expert interpretation, and improving accuracy compared to traditional methods. However, previous approaches have primarily analyzed hyperspectral images captured at a single state or condition of the material, without considering how the spectra or image features change in response to stimuli. The present work benchmarks the first application of deep‐learning‐enabled hyperspectral imaging to mechanochromic materials, enabling us to decipher the complex information encoded in their color transitions. A key challenge in tracking such color transitions by hyperspectral microscopy is the precise alignment of PDA crystals before and after stimulation, as the applied stimuli often induce subtle shape deformations. Further improvements in image‐registration algorithms are expected to enhance both classification accuracy and overall robustness.

Conclusion

In this study, we demonstrate a new approach to AMP identification that unites PDA mechanochromic sensing with hyperspectral imaging and deep learning. This combination elevates PDA assays from simple color reporters to information‐rich analytical systems. Using full spectral profiles rather than two‐point colorimetric values, our model accurately identified seven AMPs at two concentrations with a prediction accuracy of 96.79%, whereas the conventional method failed. These results reveal that mechanochromic transitions store far more biochemical information than previously recognized. AMPs interact with PDA membranes through distinctive patterns of affinity, conformation, orientation and penetration depth, shaped by their charge, hydrophobicity, Boman index, and secondary structure. These interactions produce subtle optical fingerprints that cannot be perceived by the human eye yet become accessible through deep learning. This establishes a conceptual shift in PDA‐based sensing toward high‐dimensional spectral interpretation that captures the complexity of peptide‐membrane interactions. Taken together, our findings show that mechanically responsive materials, when examined with hyperspectral imaging and interpreted with machine learning, can provide a powerful platform for molecular identification and screening. Expansion of this concept may enable the identification of peptides with membrane‐interaction properties closely resembling clinically successful compounds such as polymyxins, rather than merely general membrane‐active peptides that ultimately failed during clinical development. The discovery that the entire visible wavelength spectral region contains rich and useful information highlights the unrealized potential of mechanochromic polymers. This work positions PDA mechanochromism as an emerging modality for high‐throughput AMP discovery and more broadly as a foundation for intelligent biosensing technologies that extract meaning from subtle mechanical and optical responses.
