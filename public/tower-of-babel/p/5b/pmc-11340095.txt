Orthodontic movement after regenerative endodontic procedure in mature permanent tooth associated with dens invaginatus: a case report with 4-year follow-up
Authors: Isabella Martínez, Rodrigo Oyonarte, Guillermo Concha, Claudia Brizuela
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRegenerative endodontic procedures (REPs) are innovative treatments aimed at restoring damaged dental structures. However, the effect of orthodontic movement on REP-treated teeth is not well understood and may have significant long-term consequences. This study aimed to evaluate the impact of orthodontic movement on a mature permanent tooth associated with dens invaginatus that has undergone a regenerative endodontic procedure (REP).Case presentationThis report describes the case of a 13-year-old healthy male who presented with pulp necrosis and a chronic apical abscess (tooth number 2.2). Following REP according to the American Association of Endodontists' guidelines, the patient began non-extraction orthodontic treatment with fixed appliances after a 9-month healing period, which lasted 17 months in the upper arch. Subsequent follow-ups at 24, 36 and 48 months post-REP revealed an asymptomatic state with minimal cervical discoloration and diminished cold sensitivity. Radiographic analyses revealed periapical healing, mild apical remodeling on tooth 2.2, and moderate apical remodeling on other maxillary incisors. The treated tooth displayed a positive response to both REP and orthodontic treatment, yet further research is required to determine the long-term effects of orthodontics on REP-treated teeth.ConclusionOrthodontic movement following REPs in mature permanent teeth is feasible and do not seem to prone teeth to orthodontic tooth resorption. Our experience indicates that a 9-month healing period allows successful orthodontic outcomes following REPs. Introduction

Regenerative endodontic procedures (REPs) are biologically based procedures designed to replace damaged tooth structures and regenerate the pulp-dentin complex [1]. Over the past two decades, the use of REPs, particularly for immature teeth with pulp necrosis and apical periodontitis, has notably increased [2].

Several case studies have demonstrated the potential benefits of using a regenerative approach on mature teeth. These cases reveal promising outcomes in terms of symptom resolution, apical periodontitis healing [3–5], and vital tissue ingrowth within the root canal system, as highlighted by Arslan et al. [6]. As a result, clinical trials have gradually recognized REPs as promising treatment options for mature teeth suffering from pulp necrosis and apical periodontitis [7–10], but are still controversial and there is a lack of information on their long-term prognosis. However, recently published clinical practice guidelines do not recommend their applicability to general dental practice, due to the limited and low-quality evidence available [11].

The risk of root resorption due to orthodontic movement of endodontic treated teeth continues to be a subject of rigorous debate. Existing research suggests no marked discrepancy in root resorption between endodontically treated and vital teeth when exposed to identical orthodontic forces [12, 13] and there’s even evidence that supports that endodontically treated teeth exhibit relatively less root resorption than teeth with vital pulps [14, 15].

With increasing interest in regenerative endodontics, understanding the effects of orthodontic movements on REP-treated teeth is becoming essential [16]. The long-term implications of such interventions need to be as well characterized as those for conventionally treated endodontic teeth [12]. To date, successful orthodontic movement of REP-treated teeth has been documented in a limited number of case reports [17–20], and the absence of clinical guidelines underscores the need for detailed reporting. Thus, the finding of orthodontic movement in REP-treated teeth not only holds clinical significance but also has the potential to enrich our collective understanding and guide treatment planning.

This case report contributes to his emerging body of literature by detailing the successful orthodontic treatment of a mature nonvital tooth following REPs and a brief healing period, offering insights into the therapeutic nuances and outcomes of such cases.

Report

This case report was written according to the Case Reports (CARE) 2013 guidelines [21, 22]. A timeline of events associated with the management of this patient can be found in Fig. 1.

Flowchart according to CARE 2013 guidelines

A 13-year-old hispanic male patient, with incomplete permanent dentition and inactive orthodontic treatment, was referred to the dental clinic at Universidad de Los Andes, Santiago, Chile, for evaluation and orthodontic treatment. His primary concern was to continue orthodontic treatment previously started at another clinic. His medical history was noncontributory. In his dental history, no history of dental trauma was reported. Previous orthodontic appliances had been removed, requiring a new orthodontic assessment. The patient's orthodontic diagnosis included skeletal class I relation, with an Angle’s class III ¼ cuspid, subdivision left molar relationship, dolichofacial, cervical maturation stage 5 [23]. Clinically, he presented with agenesis of tooth 4.5, persistence of deciduous teeth, mild gingivitis associated with bacterial plaque, incorrect sagittal occlusal relationships, overjet and overbite, and a dental midline deviation relative to the facial midline (Fig. 2A to C). On radiographic examination, an apical lesion was observed in tooth 2.2, and incipient caries were present. The therapeutic objectives considered in the first instance involved the endodontic management of tooth 2.2, oral hygiene instruction, and caries preventive management. Once achieved, it was proposed to proceed with orthodontic management.

Clinical photographs (A to C) at initial diagnosis, (D to F) after completion of REPs, (G to I) 9 months after REPs and at cementation of superior appliances, (J to L) after completion of orthodontic treatment

During the initial endodontic consultation, mild vestibular swelling was observed near tooth 2.2, which exhibited normal mobility and probing depths. The tooth itself was asymptomatic upon palpation and percussion and showed a negative response to vitality tests, in contrast to control teeth, which responded normally. Radiographic evaluation of tooth 2.2 revealed complete root development with thin root dentinal walls and an extensive periapical radiolucent area associated with dens invaginatus type I (Fig. 3A). A sinus tract was observed, and a periapical radiograph was taken (Fig. 3B). CBCT of the involved tooth was performed, and images revealed an initial CBCT Periapical Index score of 5 + D (Fig. 4A to C).

The endodontic diagnosis was pulp necrosis and chronic apical abscess, associated with the dens invaginatus type I anomaly.

REPs management

Prior to initiating the REP, both the patient and their guardian were provided with an in-depth understanding of the procedure, its potential risks, and the anticipated outcomes. Upon gaining a comprehensive grasp, they provided their acknowledgment and approval by signing an informed consent form. The treatment commenced under the meticulous guidance of a surgical operating microscope (OPMI pico; Carl Zeiss AG, Göttingen, Germany). The affected tooth was treated following the American Association of Endodontists (AAE) guidelines for REPs [24].

Follow-up appointments took place at 9, 24, 36 and 48 months (Fig. 3). The outcome was evaluated radiographically and clinically, CBCT images were obtained at 9, 36, and 48-month recall sessions. The patient remained completely asymptomatic during the 48-month follow-up. Clinically, no tenderness to percussion or palpation was noted, mild discoloration was observed, and the periodontal examination revealed no pocket depths > 3 mm and normal physiological mobility. At 48 months recall, the tooth showed a delayed response to the cold test using 1,1,1,2-tetrafluoroethane compared with the control tooth. CBCT imaging revealed intact periapical bone structures (CBCT PAI = 0) (Fig. 4), dentin bridge formation in the middle third of the root, and calcification of the canal in its coronal third (Fig. 4) at the 48-month recall. Neither the patient nor their guardian expressed any dissatisfaction with the discoloration of the tooth (Fig. 2). The patient will be kept under long-term review to evaluate treatment success and to check any evidence of signs and/or symptoms of inflammation or infection.

Orthodontic management

Given the clinical characteristics, the patient’s previous history of orthodontic treatment, and the patient’s family’s intention to finish the treatment as soon as possible, treatment objectives were set to correct Overjet and Overbite, achieve canine Class I, improve midline discrepancy, manage 4.5 agenesis, and control for the developing third molars. A non-extraction orthodontic treatment (Straightwire technique, MBT prescription) was planned, keeping tooth 8.5, and considering its future replacement with an osseointegrated implant.

Orthodontic treatment was resumed once the patient was discharged from the Endodontic Clinic, starting in the lower arch while endodontic treatment began (Fig. 2D to F) and bonding orthodontic appliances in the upper arch after a 9-month healing period of the REP-treated tooth (Fig. 2G to I). The treatment steps included leveling and aligning the dentition, coordinating both arches, correcting dental midlines, overjet, and overbite relationships, and finally performing finishing and detailing procedures. Special efforts were made to perform orthodontic treatment by applying gentle forces at the initial stages, especially on the upper incisor segment. After the initial healing period, and leveling of the dentition, the treatment was conducted in a regular fashion.

The archwire sequence began with a 0,014″ NiTi wire, progressing through rectangular NiTi wires, to end on Stainless Steel rectangular wires on the upper and lower arches. Overjet, overbite, and midlines were improved on 0,019 × 0,025″ Stainless Steel wires using open NiTi coil springs, interproximal stripping in the lower cuspids and incisors, and guiding the occlusion using short class III intermaxillary elastics bilaterally from upper to lower canines. Lower third molars were extracted one year after lower arch bonding.

Fixed orthodontic appliances were removed after 26 months of treatment in the lower arch and 17 months in the maxillary arch (Fig. 2J to L). Fixed retainers were bonded to upper incisors and lower canines and incisors, and a removable Hawley-type retainer for nighttime wear was delivered.

At radiologic examination on the panoramic radiograph, mild apical root remodeling could be observed on the REP-treated tooth 2.2 and the lower incisor segment. Moderate root remodeling was present on teeth 1.2, 1.1, and 2.1 (Fig. 5A and B).

Discussion

The current body of literature suggests that orthodontic treatment is generally viable for endodontically treated teeth, albeit outcomes can vary significantly based on factors such as the magnitude of orthodontic forces, the integrity of the canal seal, any preexisting trauma, and the clinical symptoms exhibited [19, 25–27]. The associated risk of resorption due to orthodontic forces remains controversial, with studies reporting increased [28], decreased [15, 29] and unchanged risk [13, 14].

Based on the available literature, only six case studies have addressed orthodontic movement of REP-treated teeth, and the evidence is conflicting and contradictory [15–17, 19, 20, 30]. Successful orthodontic movement of REP-treated teeth has been shown in only four recent case reports [17–20], and the common factor among all these studies is that the treated teeth were immature permanent teeth.

The absence of definitive clinical guidelines for post-REP orthodontic movement underscores the need for further research in this area. Our case report endeavors to bridge this gap by presenting the effects of orthodontic treatment on a mature permanent tooth post-REP.

The management of REP-treated teeth is unfamiliar to most orthodontic practitioners according to a national survey conducted in the UK [31], and long-term complications are largely unknown and often unreported. The absence of adequate information is especially true in mature permanent teeth, as all the above case studies addressing the orthodontic movement of REP-treated teeth refer exclusively to immature permanent teeth. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first case report that addresses the orthodontic management of mature REP-treated teeth. Moreover, the radiologic assessment of the final panoramic radiograph shows a milder apical remodeling response on the REP-treated tooth than on the neighboring maxillary incisors after a non-extraction orthodontic treatment with fixed appliances.

Performing REPs by cell homing on mature teeth is still controversial and presents its challenges, including limited access to stem/progenitor cells, a narrower apical space, and greater complexity in disinfection due to anatomical variations [3]. Indeed, a recent study identified age as the primary factor negatively affecting success; the regenerative potential of adults is notably lower than that of younger individuals [32]. Additionally, the microenvironment of the canal space, epithelial cell remnants of Hertwig's root sheath, and apical papilla can also be diminished in adult individuals. Conversely, younger individuals may have a wider apical foramen and may also possess stem cells with a greater regenerative capacity; adult patients might require alternative approaches to ensure success. For this reason, it is not recommended to perform REPs by cell homing for adults or patients older than 30 years [11, 32, 33].

With respect to disinfection, maintaining a sterile working environment is crucial for all endodontic procedures, including regenerative therapy [34, 35]. Nonetheless, regenerative treatments rely on living cells and natural physiological processes, so eliminating bacteria and ensuring the survival of endogenous cells can be complex undertakings, especially when using a bleeding technique. Given that the extent of bacterial infection and the etiology of pulp necrosis might affect the regenerative process, different diagnoses may yield different outcomes, where caries, severe trauma, and asymptomatic apical periodontitis are considered risk factors for achieving the secondary and tertiary outcomes proposed by the AAE [32, 36, 37].

Despite these limitations, our case report describes the successful orthodontic treatment of a mature nonvital tooth associated with dens invaginatus post-REPs. According to the European Society of Endodontology guidelines [38], REP-treated teeth that undergo orthodontic treatment may be more prone to inflammation and apical root resorption, but this guideline refers only to immature teeth [12]. In this case, apical root resorption and root length were compared between tooth 1.2 and tooth 2.2, where we could evidence apical resorption and root length shortening on tooth 1.2 and minimal changes on tooth 2.2 (Fig. 5C to J). We suggest that a REP-treated mature tooth may behave like an endodontically treated tooth, with less risk of apical root resorption and thus maintaining root length. This might be explained by histological findings in which the tissue formed inside the canal space resembled fibrous connective tissue rather than pulp-like tissue [7]. The favorable outcomes, centered around both the patient and the clinician, suggest that regenerative endodontic procedures could act as a safeguard against root resorption in mature teeth subjected to orthodontic treatment.

Moreover, as the available literature suggests, during the initial phase of orthodontic movement, there are increased levels of hypoxia-inducible factor-1α, which activates several genes that collectively stimulate neoangiogenesis, which is considered a survival mechanism to ensure proper vascularization of the dental pulp [39]. It is yet to be proven whether the new tissue formed by REPs can initiate immune and inflammatory responses and produce reparative dentin [40] as do pulp tissue, but this mechanism could explain and enable the newly formed tissue to undergo a remodeling process after tooth movement.

Therefore, our findings suggest that orthodontic movement following REPs in mature permanent teeth is feasible and does not seem to prone teeth to orthodontic tooth resorption. Nonetheless, the predictability of outcomes and the ideal healing period before orthodontic movement is initiated remain to be established. Our experience indicates that a 9-month healing period allows successful orthodontic outcomes following REPs. Further research is imperative to elucidate the immune and inflammatory responses in REP-treated teeth subjected to orthodontic movement and to reduce the healing period required for successful treatment. There is a need for high-quality data, with standardized protocols for endodontic treatment interventions and orthodontic treatment and techniques, to structure an applicable clinical guideline for orthodontic movement after REPs.

Conclusion

This case reports the effects of orthodontic treatment on mature teeth treated with regenerative endodontic procedures (REPs). The potential for long-term complications underscores the need for more research and vigilant follow-up. Future studies should aim to refine clinical guidelines for post-REP orthodontic care.
