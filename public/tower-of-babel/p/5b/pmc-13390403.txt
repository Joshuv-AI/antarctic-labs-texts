Early osmotherapy exposure and subsequent incident acute kidney injury in neurocritical care patients: a MIMIC-IV retrospective cohort study
Authors: Shenshen Du, Shaojie Zhang, Weicheng Lai, Qimeng Wu, Shuang Cui, Shuqi Xu, Bo Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOsmotherapy is frequently used in neurocritical care to manage cerebral edema and intracranial hypertension. However, its association with subsequent new-onset acute kidney injury (AKI), and whether early serum sodium or chloride burden materially attenuates this association, remain uncertain.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study using MIMIC-IV version 3.1. Adult first-ICU neurocritical care patients were analyzed with a 48-h landmark design. The primary risk set included patients who were alive, remained in the ICU, and were AKI-free at 48 h after ICU admission. The primary exposure was any osmotherapy, defined as mannitol or hypertonic saline administered within the first 48 ICU hours, compared with no osmotherapy. The primary outcome was incident AKI after the 48-h landmark through ICU day 7 or ICU discharge. Multivariable regression was used as the main adjusted model; propensity-score weighting, overlap weighting, doubly robust models, and modified Poisson regression were used as complementary estimators. Sodium/chloride burden analyses were conducted in the lab-complete subset.ResultsThe final 48-h landmark cohort included 2,756 adult neurocritical care patients, of whom 624 (22.6%) developed incident AKI after the landmark. Any osmotherapy was administered to 365 patients (13.2%). In the doubly robust overlap-weighted logistic model, any osmotherapy was associated with subsequent incident AKI (OR, 1.48; 95% CI 1.12–1.96; P = 0.006). The corresponding doubly robust overlap-weighted modified Poisson estimate was RR 1.30 (95% CI 1.08–1.57; P = 0.006). Sodium/chloride burden analyses included 2,455 lab-complete patients. Early sodium burden (OR, 0.99; 95% CI 0.92–1.07; P = 0.792) and chloride burden (OR, 1.01; 95% CI 0.94–1.09; P = 0.743) did not materially attenuate the observed association in exploratory adjustment models. In exploratory clinical relevance analyses, patients with subsequent AKI had higher hospital mortality and lower creatinine-based renal recovery at ICU and hospital discharge. Dose-proxy and hypertonic saline concentration-specific analyses were exploratory because dose capture was incomplete and 23.4% hypertonic saline exposure was sparse.ConclusionsAmong adult neurocritical care patients who were alive, remained in the ICU, and were AKI-free at the 48-h landmark, early osmotherapy exposure was associated with a higher risk of subsequent incident AKI. This association was not materially attenuated by early serum sodium or chloride burden in exploratory adjustment models. These findings support careful renal monitoring in patients receiving clinically indicated osmotherapy, while residual confounding by indication precludes causal interpretation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40560-026-00917-z.

Introduction

Osmotherapy is widely used in neurocritical care to control intracranial pressure, reduce cerebral edema, and support the management of severe neurologic injury. Mannitol and hypertonic saline are the two most commonly used agents and are applied across traumatic brain injury, intracerebral hemorrhage, subarachnoid hemorrhage, postoperative neurosurgical care, intracranial tumors, and other neurologic conditions requiring intensive monitoring [1–3].

AKI is common among critically ill patients and is associated with higher mortality, longer ICU stay, and greater resource use [4–6]. In neurocritical care, AKI may further complicate fluid management, osmotherapy titration, hemodynamic optimization, medication dosing, and neurologic prognostication. However, the renal risk associated with osmotherapy is difficult to distinguish from the severity of the underlying neurologic condition because osmotherapy is typically administered to patients with more severe disease and higher treatment intensity.

Several mechanisms may link osmotherapy to kidney injury. Mannitol has been associated with osmotic nephrosis, intravascular volume shifts, and tubular stress in susceptible patients [7–9]. Hypertonic saline increases sodium and chloride exposure, and hyperchloremia has been associated with renal vasoconstriction and AKI in critically ill populations [10–12]. Nevertheless, serum electrolyte abnormalities may also reflect disease progression, impaired renal handling, free water balance, fluid administration, and escalation of critical care therapy rather than a direct nephrotoxic effect of osmotherapy.

Previous studies have mainly focused on selected diagnoses or specific agents, such as mannitol exposure in intracerebral hemorrhage or comparisons between mannitol and hypertonic saline in traumatic brain injury [13–15]. Evidence from broader neurocritical care populations remains limited. Moreover, whether early serum sodium and chloride burden accounts for the association between osmotherapy and subsequent incident AKI has not been fully evaluated.

To address these uncertainties, we used MIMIC-IV to evaluate the association between early osmotherapy exposure and subsequent incident AKI in a broad neurocritical care population. We designed the analysis as a 48-h landmark cohort study to separate early osmotherapy exposure from later new-onset AKI. The primary objective was to compare any osmotherapy administered within the first 48 h after ICU admission with no osmotherapy among patients who were alive, still in the ICU, and AKI-free at the 48-h landmark. Secondary objectives were to examine strategy-specific associations for mannitol only, hypertonic saline only, and combined therapy. Exploratory objectives were to assess whether early serum sodium and chloride burden, hypertonic saline concentration, available dose-proxy measures, calendar period, and alternative AKI definitions influenced the observed association. This study differs from prior work by including a broader neurocritical care population, applying a landmark design focused on subsequent incident AKI, prioritizing any osmotherapy as the primary exposure while presenting strategy-specific analyses as secondary, and evaluating whether physiologic sodium/chloride burden accounts for osmotherapy-associated AKI risk.

Methods

Data source

This retrospective cohort study used MIMIC-IV version 3.1. MIMIC-IV is a large, deidentified electronic health record database from Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center, containing 546,028 hospitalizations and 94,458 ICU stays between 2008 and 2022 [16, 17]. The database includes demographics, admissions, ICU stays, diagnoses, procedures, laboratory measurements, medication and fluid administration records, urine output, and derived critical care variables. Because MIMIC-IV contains deidentified data, individual informed consent was waived. The establishment of MIMIC-IV was approved by the institutional review boards of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology and Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center. The database-access author completed the required Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative training and obtained permission to access MIMIC-IV, with certification number 42304428.

Study population

We identified adult patients aged 18 years or older with a first ICU stay who met criteria for neurocritical care. Neurocritical care status was defined using neurologic diagnosis codes, neurosurgical procedure codes, and ICU service information, including traumatic brain injury, intracerebral hemorrhage, subarachnoid hemorrhage, subdural hemorrhage, epidural hemorrhage, intracranial tumor, postoperative neurosurgical ICU care, and other neurologic conditions requiring intensive care. Detailed code definitions are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

The primary analysis used a 48-h landmark design. The exposure window was the first 48 h after ICU admission, the landmark time was 48 h after ICU admission, and the outcome window began after the 48-h landmark. The primary risk set included adult first-ICU neurocritical care patients who were alive, had not been discharged from the ICU, and were free of AKI at the 48-h landmark. Patients with end-stage kidney disease or chronic dialysis were excluded. Patients who developed AKI before or at the 48-h landmark, including AKI within 0–24 h and AKI between 24 and 48 h, were excluded from the primary risk set because the outcome had already occurred before follow-up began. Patients who died or were discharged from the ICU before the 48-h landmark were excluded because they were not observable for subsequent incident AKI after the landmark. Exposure ascertainment

The primary exposure was any osmotherapy administered within the first 48 h after ICU admission. Osmotherapy included mannitol or hypertonic saline administered during the 0–48 h exposure window. The comparator group consisted of patients with no recorded osmotherapy during the same window. Secondary strategy-specific exposures were defined as no osmotherapy, mannitol only, hypertonic saline only, and combined mannitol plus hypertonic saline. Strategy-specific comparisons used no osmotherapy as the reference category and were interpreted as secondary analyses rather than the primary analysis.

Candidate item IDs, medication names, drug labels, and order names were identified from ICU item dictionaries and hospital prescription records and then reviewed before analytic variable construction. Non-intravenous administration, inhaled or nebulized preparations, nasal therapies, enteral administration, flush orders, and other nontherapeutic records were excluded. Hypertonic saline was further classified by concentration when recorded, including 3% sodium chloride and 23.4% sodium chloride. Concentration-specific analyses were considered exploratory because 23.4% hypertonic saline exposure was infrequent.

Dose-proxy variables were derived from ICU input events when actual administration records and compatible units were available. Mannitol dose was summarized as cumulative grams during the first 48 h. Hypertonic saline exposure was summarized as estimated sodium dose in mEq from concentration and administered volume for 3% and 23.4% saline. Because dose capture and unit harmonization were incomplete, dose-proxy variables were used only for supplementary description and exploratory analyses and were not interpreted as definitive dose–response evidence.

Exploratory descriptive analyses of clinically relevant renal outcomes available in MIMIC-IV were performed. These included ICU mortality, hospital mortality, new renal replacement therapy after the 48-h landmark or during the hospitalization, and creatinine-based renal recovery near ICU and hospital discharge. Creatinine recovery was defined descriptively as discharge-window creatinine less than 1.5 times baseline creatinine among patients with available measurements. These analyses were descriptive and were not used to redefine the primary outcome.

Sodium and chloride burden

Serum sodium and chloride burden were evaluated as physiologic exposure markers rather than direct measures of administered electrolyte load. These measures were selected because serum electrolyte trajectories integrate administered fluids, osmotherapy, renal handling, free water balance, illness trajectory, and treatment response. Sodium burden was defined as the time-weighted area above 145 mmol/L, reflecting hypernatremia. Chloride burden was defined as the time-weighted area above 110 mmol/L, a threshold selected a priori based on commonly used definitions of hyperchloremia in critical care literature [11, 12]. Burden values were calculated using trapezoidal integration to account for irregular laboratory sampling intervals. Hypernatremia and hyperchloremia were defined as any sodium value greater than 145 mmol/L and any chloride value greater than 110 mmol/L, respectively.

Exploratory electrolyte-burden models were structured as follows: sodium burden, chloride burden, hypernatremia, and hyperchloremia were each evaluated in separate logistic regression models adjusted for the base covariates and osmotherapy strategy. In the strategy-specific model additionally adjusted for electrolyte burden, sodium burden and chloride burden were entered simultaneously. These models were intended to assess whether adjustment for early serum electrolyte burden materially attenuated the osmotherapy–AKI association; they were not designed as formal mediation or causal pathway analyses.

Outcome definition

The primary outcome was subsequent incident AKI, defined as new-onset AKI occurring after the 48-h landmark through ICU day 7 or ICU discharge, whichever occurred first. AKI was defined according to KDIGO criteria using serum creatinine and urine output [18]. Patients who developed AKI before or at the 48-h landmark were excluded from the primary risk set. In the primary definition, baseline creatinine was defined using the available early creatinine window because outpatient baseline creatinine was not consistently available in MIMIC-IV. A sensitivity analysis used the minimum creatinine from 24 h before ICU admission to ICU admission as an alternative pre-ICU baseline definition. Urine-output criteria were calculated using recorded body weight when reliable weight data were available; for patients without reliable weight data, urine-output-based staging was not applied, while creatinine-based criteria remained available.

Covariates and missing data

Covariates were selected a priori based on clinical relevance, plausibility as confounders, and availability in MIMIC-IV. They included age, sex, race, admission type, comorbidities, baseline renal function, neurocritical care diagnosis category, neurosurgical procedures, first-day Glasgow Coma Scale score, SOFA score, mechanical ventilation, vasopressor use, kidney-relevant medications, baseline blood urea nitrogen, hemoglobin, albumin, and bicarbonate. Admission year and calendar period were evaluated in sensitivity analyses. Early urine output was handled carefully because it may function as a disease severity marker, treatment response, mediator, or component of AKI ascertainment. The core model did not adjust for outcome-window urine output; a sensitivity model additionally adjusted for first-24 h urine output measured before outcome follow-up. Missingness, model-specific sample sizes, and weight diagnostics are summarized in Supplementary Table S2. Established comorbidity and severity measures, including Charlson comorbidity, SOFA score, and Glasgow Coma Scale, were referenced using standard descriptions [19–21].

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as medians with interquartile ranges, and categorical variables as counts with percentages. Reporting was guided by observational-study reporting principles [22]. Baseline characteristics were compared according to any osmotherapy exposure, and covariate balance was assessed using standardized mean differences [23]. The primary analysis compared any osmotherapy with no osmotherapy in the 48-h landmark risk set. Logistic regression estimated odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals. Because AKI was not rare, modified Poisson regression with robust variance estimated risk ratios [24]. Propensity scores for any osmotherapy were estimated using prespecified demographic, comorbidity, neurologic, renal, severity, and treatment-intensity covariates [25, 26]. Inverse probability of treatment weighting and overlap weighting were applied [27–29]. Doubly robust models combined propensity score weighting with outcome regression adjustment [30]. The exposure contrast and covariate set were defined before outcome modeling. Conventional multivariable regression was used as the main adjusted model. Propensity-score weighting, overlap weighting, doubly robust models, and modified Poisson regression were used as complementary estimators. Overlap weighting was emphasized as the primary weighted estimator because it targets the clinical-overlap population and achieved the best covariate balance in this cohort; this emphasis was based on balance diagnostics rather than on effect-size magnitude. Results were interpreted according to consistency across complementary estimators rather than a single selected model.

Secondary strategy-specific analyses compared mannitol only, hypertonic saline only, and combined therapy with no osmotherapy. Exploratory analyses evaluated sodium burden, chloride burden, hypernatremia, hyperchloremia, hypertonic saline concentration, and dose-proxy measures. Sensitivity analyses evaluated admission year, calendar period, alternative baseline creatinine, first-24 h urine output adjustment, and selected subgroup interactions. Formal interaction tests were performed by adding multiplicative interaction terms between osmotherapy exposure and subgroup variables to fully adjusted logistic regression models; P values for interaction were obtained from Wald tests. All findings were interpreted as associations rather than causal effects.

Results

Cohort construction and 48-h landmark risk set

Among 10,707 adult first-ICU neurocritical care patients with an ICU stay of at least 24 h, 123 were excluded because of end-stage kidney disease or chronic dialysis. Sodium/chloride measurement sufficiency was not required for the primary osmotherapy–AKI risk set; 1,789 patients had insufficient sodium/chloride measurements and were retained when otherwise eligible for the primary analysis. We then excluded 5,288 patients with AKI within 0–24 h and 893 with AKI between 24 and 48 h. A further 65 patients died before 48 h, and 1,582 were discharged alive from the ICU before 48 h. The final 48-h landmark primary risk set therefore included 2,756 patients who were alive, still in the ICU, and AKI-free at the landmark. Subsequent incident AKI after the 48-h landmark occurred in 624 patients (22.6%). The sodium/chloride burden analyses were restricted to the lab-complete subset of 2455 patients (Fig. 1).

Primary association between any osmotherapy and subsequent incident AKI

Within the 48-h exposure window, 365 patients received any osmotherapy and 2,391 received no osmotherapy. In the primary complete-case model, any osmotherapy was associated with subsequent incident AKI after full adjustment (OR, 1.43; 95% CI 1.09–1.89; P = 0.011). The association was directionally consistent after overlap weighting (OR, 1.44; 95% CI 1.09–1.89; P = 0.009) and in the doubly robust overlap-weighted logistic model (OR, 1.48; 95% CI 1.12–1.96; P = 0.006). Modified Poisson models yielded similar estimates, including a fully adjusted RR of 1.29 (95% CI 1.07–1.56; P = 0.008) and a doubly robust overlap-weighted RR of 1.30 (95% CI 1.08–1.57; P = 0.006). Sodium and chloride burden

Sodium burden, chloride burden, hypernatremia, and hyperchloremia did not materially attenuate the observed association between osmotherapy and subsequent incident AKI in exploratory adjustment models. In 0–48 h models, sodium burden was not associated with AKI (OR, 0.99; 95% CI 0.92–1.07; P = 0.792), and chloride burden was also not associated with AKI (OR, 1.01; 95% CI 0.94–1.09; P = 0.743). Hypernatremia had an OR of 1.04 (95% CI 0.77–1.40; P = 0.804), and hyperchloremia had an OR of 1.14 (95% CI 0.86–1.50; P = 0.359). These secondary and exploratory estimates are summarized in Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table S3. No formal mediation analysis, causal pathway model, or mechanistic interaction analysis was performed for sodium or chloride burden.

Dose, hypertonic saline concentration, sensitivity, and interaction analyses

Dose-proxy availability was incomplete: mannitol dose was available for 72 of 168 mannitol-exposed patients (42.9%), and HTS estimated sodium dose was available for 132 of 271 HTS-exposed patients (48.7%). Median available mannitol dose was 117.5 g (IQR, 75.0–200.0), and median available HTS estimated sodium dose was 513.0 mEq (IQR, 280.5–918.0). Parsimonious exploratory models did not show clear dose–AKI associations for mannitol log-dose (OR, 0.75; 95% CI 0.37–1.54; P = 0.440) or HTS sodium log-dose (OR, 1.13; 95% CI 0.77–1.65; P = 0.536). In concentration-specific descriptions, 116 patients received 3% HTS only, 1 received 23.4% HTS only, and 15 received both 3% and 23.4% HTS; therefore, reliable adjusted modeling for 23.4% HTS was not feasible. Adjustment for admission year or calendar period did not materially change the primary association. Additional adjustment for first-24 h urine output attenuated but did not reverse the association. These sensitivity and exploratory analyses are summarized in Supplementary Table S4 and Supplementary Figure S3A-D.

Exploratory clinical relevance analyses

Patients with subsequent AKI had higher hospital mortality than those without subsequent AKI (67/624 [10.7%] vs 114/2,132 [5.3%]) and higher ICU mortality (49/624 [7.9%] vs 64/2,132 [3.0%]). New renal replacement therapy after the 48-h landmark or during hospitalization was rare (4/624 [0.6%] among patients with subsequent AKI and 0/2,132 [0.0%] among those without subsequent AKI). Among patients with available discharge-window creatinine measurements, creatinine recovery to less than 1.5 times baseline occurred in 520/571 (91.2%) patients with subsequent AKI versus 1,654/1,688 (99.1%) patients without subsequent AKI near ICU discharge, and in 497/542 (91.9%) versus 1,600/1,663 (97.4%) near hospital discharge. These descriptive analyses are summarized in Supplementary Table S6.

Discussion

Principal findings

In this 48-h landmark cohort of adult neurocritical care patients who were alive, still in the ICU, and free of AKI at the landmark, subsequent incident AKI occurred more frequently among patients exposed to early osmotherapy than among those without osmotherapy. The association between any osmotherapy within the first 48 ICU hours and subsequent new-onset AKI remained directionally consistent after multivariable adjustment, overlap weighting, doubly robust adjustment, and modified Poisson regression. However, estimates were attenuated or imprecise in several sensitivity and strategy-specific analyses. Early serum sodium and chloride burden did not materially attenuate the observed AKI association in exploratory adjustment models.

Interpretation of binary and strategy-specific findings

We prioritized any osmotherapy versus no osmotherapy as the primary analysis because this comparison reflects the central clinical question of whether early osmotherapy exposure is associated with subsequent incident AKI in patients who remain at risk after 48 h. Strategy-specific analyses were secondary and less precise because the mannitol-only, hypertonic-saline-only, and combined-therapy groups were smaller. These results should not be interpreted as evidence that one osmotherapy strategy is definitively more nephrotoxic than another. Rather, they suggest that excess AKI risk occurs in the clinical context in which osmotherapy is administered.

Residual confounding by indication

Residual confounding by indication remains central to interpretation. Patients receiving osmotherapy often have more severe neurologic injury, greater intracranial hypertension risk, more frequent mechanical ventilation, higher likelihood of neurosurgical intervention, and greater exposure to other ICU therapies that may influence renal perfusion and AKI risk. Although we adjusted for measured neurologic severity, comorbidities, renal function, and treatment intensity and used propensity score and doubly robust approaches, MIMIC-IV does not fully capture intracranial pressure, neuroimaging severity, clinician intent, timing of neurologic deterioration, or detailed fluid management decisions. Therefore, the observed association should not be interpreted as proof of a direct nephrotoxic effect of osmotherapy.

Sodium and chloride burden

The absence of a clear adjusted association between early serum sodium/chloride burden and subsequent AKI, together with the limited attenuation of osmotherapy estimates after adjustment for sodium and chloride burden, suggests that early hypernatremia, hyperchloremia, or time-weighted electrolyte burden alone did not account for the osmotherapy–AKI association in these exploratory models. Serum sodium and chloride burden are physiologic markers influenced by administered fluids, osmotherapy, renal handling, free water balance, illness trajectory, and treatment response; they are not direct measures of cumulative administered sodium or chloride load. Thus, the negative burden findings do not exclude a renal contribution from fluid composition or osmotherapy dose, and they should not be interpreted as formal mediation evidence; rather, they argue against early serum electrolyte burden as the sole explanatory marker in the available exploratory adjustment models.

Dose and hypertonic saline concentration

The exploratory dose-proxy and concentration-specific analyses should be interpreted cautiously. Dose capture was incomplete, and compatible units were not consistently available. Although parsimonious models did not show clear dose–AKI associations, these analyses were underpowered and vulnerable to selection and measurement error. Hypertonic saline formulations also differ substantially in osmotic content and clinical use. In this cohort, 23.4% hypertonic saline exposure was extremely sparse, precluding reliable adjusted concentration-specific modeling. These results therefore provide descriptive context rather than definitive evidence regarding dose–response or concentration-specific renal risk.

Clinical implications

These findings should not discourage clinically indicated osmotherapy in neurocritical care. Mannitol and hypertonic saline are often used to manage cerebral edema and intracranial hypertension, and withholding indicated therapy could be harmful. Instead, early osmotherapy exposure may identify a group of patients at higher risk for subsequent incident AKI. Structured renal monitoring should include serum creatinine, urine output, hemodynamics, volume status, vasopressor exposure, concomitant nephrotoxins, and evolving electrolyte abnormalities.

Exploratory clinical relevance analyses suggest that subsequent AKI was associated with worse short-term clinical and renal-status markers, including higher ICU and hospital mortality and lower creatinine-based recovery by ICU or hospital discharge. However, whether enhanced monitoring changes these downstream outcomes cannot be determined from this observational dataset and should be evaluated in future prospective or externally validated studies.

Strengths

This study has several strengths. It used a large critical care database and included a broad neurocritical care population rather than a single diagnosis group. The 48-h landmark design clarified the temporal relationship between early osmotherapy exposure and subsequent new-onset AKI. AKI was defined using KDIGO creatinine and urine-output criteria when reliable data were available. The analysis distinguished the primary binary exposure from secondary strategy-specific comparisons and applied multivariable adjustment, propensity score weighting, overlap weighting, doubly robust models, modified Poisson regression, sensitivity analyses, and formal interaction testing. The study also evaluated serum sodium and chloride burden and added exploratory analyses of dose proxies and hypertonic saline concentration.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was an observational study and could not establish causality. Residual confounding by indication may persist despite adjustment and weighting. Second, intracranial pressure, neuroimaging findings, detailed treatment indications, and clinician intent were not fully captured. Third, prescription records may not perfectly reflect actual administered doses, and inputevents-based dose-proxy variables were incompletely available; dose–response analyses were exploratory and should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of dose–response. In addition, sparse 23.4% hypertonic saline exposure limited concentration-specific modeling. Fourth, baseline creatinine uncertainty may have caused AKI misclassification because true outpatient baseline creatinine was not consistently available. Fifth, urine output may be affected by documentation quality, body-weight availability, diuretics, osmotherapy, and evolving AKI physiology. Sixth, the 48-h landmark design improves temporal ordering but excludes patients with early AKI, early death, or early ICU discharge, which may introduce selection bias and limits generalizability to patients surviving and remaining AKI-free at the landmark. Seventh, secular trends in neurocritical care and AKI ascertainment may have influenced results, although calendar adjustment did not materially change the primary association. Eighth, MIMIC-IV is a single-center database, and findings may not generalize to other institutions or osmotherapy protocols. Finally, although exploratory descriptions of ICU mortality, hospital mortality, renal replacement therapy, and discharge-window creatinine recovery were included, MIMIC-IV does not provide reliable longitudinal post-discharge kidney follow-up. Therefore, long-term renal recovery, persistent dialysis dependence after discharge, and the impact of monitoring strategies on patient-centered renal outcomes could not be robustly evaluated. External validation with longitudinal renal follow-up is needed.

Conclusions

Among adult neurocritical care patients who were alive, remained in the ICU, and were free of AKI at the 48-h landmark, early osmotherapy exposure was associated with a higher risk of subsequent new-onset AKI during ICU follow-up. This association remained directionally consistent after overlap weighting and doubly robust adjustment, although attenuation and imprecision in strategy-specific and sensitivity analyses suggest that residual confounding by indication and early ICU treatment intensity likely contributed to the observed excess AKI risk. Early sodium and chloride burden did not materially attenuate the observed AKI association in exploratory adjustment models. These findings support careful renal monitoring in neurocritical care patients receiving osmotherapy, rather than avoidance of clinically indicated osmotherapy.
