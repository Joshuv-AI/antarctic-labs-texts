OscillomeR infers ultradian oscillations and targets of the Hes family
Authors: Xiaochan Xu, Palle Serup
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe Hes family, basic-helix-loop-helix transcription factors and downstream effectors of Notch signaling, regulate the fate choices of pancreatic progenitors, muscle stem cells, neuronal progenitors, and presomitic mesoderm cells. Bioluminescence imaging (BLI) has revealed ultradian oscillatory dynamics of Hes-family members Hes1, Hes5, and Hes7. However, identifying which of the Hes target genes also oscillate remains challenging due to the time-consuming and costly nature of tracking individual target genes using BLI. Here, we propose OscillomeR, a computational framework that reconstructs ultradian oscillations from RNA-sequencing data to identify oscillatory target genes at high throughput. OscillomeR predicts thousands of oscillatory genes in synchronized or unsynchronized cell types, identifying both known and novel Hes-family targets. It also captures the dynamic rewiring of gene-regulatory networks during cell differentiation. Overall, OscillomeR is an effective tool for elucidating the functions of oscillatory transcription factors at the genomic scale.Keywords: Hes family, Notch signaling, bHLH transcription factors, single-cell RNA-seq, ultradian oscillations, oscillatory gene targets, gene-regulatory networks

Introduction

Oscillation of the master transcription factors (TFs) that govern cell-fate decisions has been observed in various developing organs. The oscillations known as “molecular clocks” can be synchronous or asynchronous among cells within different regions of the organism.1 Many oscillatory TFs are members of the basic-helix-loop-helix (bHLH) gene family, including the Hes TFs. During somite development, Hes7 and Hes1 oscillate synchronously across presomitic mesoderm (PSM) cells,2,3 and the absence of Hes7 in mice leads to disorganized segmentation, indicating its role in maintaining anterior-posterior polarity and regulating the periodic somitogenesis. In neuronal progenitors, Hes1 and Hes5 oscillate asynchronously4,5; oscillatory Hes1 is necessary for progenitor proliferation and for repressing genes that promote neuronal differentiation. Hes1 also oscillates asynchronously in muscle stem cells (MuSCs), facilitating proliferation and inhibiting the myogenic regulatory factor (MRF) MyoD.6,7 Recently, Hes1 has been found to oscillate in mouse pancreatic progenitors (PPs), helping maintain the proper proportions of multipotent and bipotent cell fates.8,9

The oscillatory behavior of these Hes genes complicates the identification of their target genes and the full dissection of their biological functions. Given that the period of Hes oscillations is as short as ∼2 h in mice and ∼5 h in humans,10 directly tracking these oscillations in individual cells requires a rapidly decaying reporter. The ubiquitinated firefly luciferase has a half-life of about 10 min and is usually used to monitor the precise gene expression in tracking experiments.3 The experiments are already costly and time consuming when using time-lapse imaging to trace these Hes genes alone. It is impractical to screen for downstream targets of Hes without a list of candidates. Therefore, it is compelling to develop methodologies to infer the target genes of oscillatory TFs, including both directly bound and indirectly regulated targets, in a high-throughput fashion. Such approaches are indispensable for comprehensively investigating and characterizing the functional roles of oscillatory dynamics in directing differentiation.

Using mRNA levels to trace the propagation of oscillations in a gene-regulatory network (GRN) has advantages over protein levels, as mRNA is typically less stable than protein.11,12 The mRNA levels change on a faster timescale and are more likely to oscillate if transcription regulation oscillates (Figure 1A). Thus, mRNA levels provide more informative data for tracing oscillations. Monitoring mRNA oscillations has been achieved in synchronized PSM cells from embryos using microarrays13,14 and in induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived segmentoids using bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq).15 Before differentiation into somites, the iPSC-derived PSM cells are considered a synchronous, homogenous cell population. The previous study harvested PSM cells at different HES7 oscillation phases and obtained temporal transcriptomic dynamics with bulk RNA-seq while monitoring HES7 reporter activity.15 The study identified 220 genes that oscillate with HES7 at the mRNA level in PSM cells.

However, bulk RNA-seq is inapplicable to asynchronous oscillatory cell types such as neural stem cells, MuSCs, and PPs, since the oscillatory signal would be averaged out and obscured. The recent single-cell and single-nucleus RNA-seq (scRNA-seq and snRNA-seq) technologies16,17,18 have inspired the development of algorithms to reconstruct asynchronous cyclic signals from single-cell transcriptomic data.19,20,21,22,23 These algorithms primarily address the challenges of selecting the cyclic genes and reconstructing the cyclic order of cells. For instance, Oscope22 infers groups of potential oscillatory genes involved in cell ordering by fitting a two-dimensional (2D) sinusoidal function to gene pairs and clustering the top pairs. The method allows Oscope to recover the cyclic order of cells without prior knowledge of which genes are oscillatory. Another algorithm, scPrisma,23 which integrates known marker genes corresponding to different cycle phases, orders cells by enhancing the cyclic signal with a spectral projection approach. These algorithms have demonstrated impressive performance in analyzing cyclic signals such as the cell cycle and circadian clock. Recently, OscoNet,24 an important extension of Oscope that adds a well-calibrated non-parametric test for selecting oscillatory genes at a defined false discovery rate (FDR) threshold, showed strong performance by identifying SOX2 as a validated oscillator in quiescent glioblastoma stem cells.25 This work underscores the potential of computational methods to uncover weak and short-lived ultradian oscillatory signals, including those driven by TFs such as the Hes genes. Building on this progress, it is compelling to develop a more general framework that applies to both synchronized and asynchronous systems and remains robust to shorter, noisier oscillations, particularly in developmental settings where single-cell data often capture many cells in similar states but different oscillation phases.

Here, we introduce OscillomeR, a computational framework designed to reconstruct ultradian oscillations using only the knowledge of an oscillatory master gene. OscillomeR integrates maximal information coefficient (MIC),26 uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP),27,28 and dynamic time warping (DTW)29 to reliably predict weak cyclic signals even with small sample sizes. Its accuracy is demonstrated by the precise inference of oscillatory genes and phase shifts in simulated data and PSM cells. Furthermore, OscillomeR effectively predicts oscillations in asynchronous cell types such as MuSCs and PPs. The framework suggests that Hes1 plays a crucial role in regulating the satellite cell state in MuSCs and predicts that Myf5 and Megf10, as MRFs, oscillate with Hes1 at the mRNA level. It also identifies GRN rewiring during early PP differentiation, showing that distinct groups of genes oscillate with Hes1 across different cell fates. When applied to the cell cycle, circadian clock, and other oscillatory systems, OscillomeR outperforms other algorithms in flexibility and sensitivity. It assists in investigating how TFs’ oscillations propagate to and regulate a spectrum of functional gene sets.

Results

OscillomeR infers oscillatory genes with high-dimensional mRNA expression data

We developed OscillomeR to characterize TF-driven oscillatory dynamics and identify oscillatory genes from bulk RNA-seq and scRNA-seq. In this study, Hes is the oscillating TF. The algorithm has three main steps (Figure 1): (1) select highly variable genes likely to oscillate with Hes; (2) reconstruct an oscillatory trajectory and infer a DTW-regularized pseudo-phase axis for Hes oscillation; and (3) classify genes into oscillatory and non-oscillatory groups by comparing phase-resolved expression to oscillatory versus constant models.

Step 1: Select genes that are likely to oscillate downstream of Hes

We defined two criteria for selecting genes likely to oscillate with Hes. First, oscillatory genes exhibit high cell-to-cell variability in expression. Second, correlation analysis with Hes must capture in-phase (peaking with Hes), anti-phase (peaking at Hes minimum), and out-of-phase genes that peak between the Hes maximum and minimum. To identify such genes, we applied MIC, an information-theory-based metric that quantifies the non-linear association between a gene’s expression and Hes (Figure 1B). Unlike the linear Pearson and the monotonic Spearman correlation coefficients, which mainly detect in-phase or anti-phase relationships, MIC also recovers correlations from out-of-phase oscillations with Hes. Additionally, a geometric metric, the Q value of expression variation expected to be significantly lower among Hes-peak cells than in random subsets, can help distinguish true oscillations from monotonic changes over time when strong differentiation is anticipated and may also yield comparable high MIC values.

Step 2: Infer a pseudo-phase axis for Hes oscillation

Having selected genes of interest, we reconstruct the oscillatory trajectory by visualizing a circular manifold on which cells align by phase, allowing estimation of each gene’s amplitude and phase relative to Hes (Figures 1C and 1D). We embed step-1 genes into a low-dimensional space using principal-component analysis (PCA) followed by UMAP, which, owing to their oscillatory behavior, yields a looping manifold that preserves local cell-cell relationships. Absolute time differences between neighboring cells remain unknown, as cells with the same oscillation phase can be separated by multiple cycles. The loop represents a phase portrait rather than real time, with similarly phased cells positioned nearby within one Hes cycle. To extract gene oscillations along this manifold, we choose an arbitrary starting point (shared across genes) and a traversal direction (clockwise or counterclockwise), connect the k-means cluster centers to form a polygonal trajectory, and project each cell to its nearest point on this polygon. We then generate a one-dimensional pseudo-phase axis for Hes oscillation.

To regularize this axis, we apply DTW and align the ordered Hes expression to a sine function on (0, 2π] (Figure 1D and methods). This DTW-based normalization preserves the Hes oscillation pattern while mapping similar Hes expression levels onto a canonical sine-wave template in place of the unknown true waveform. After this transformation, Hes expression peaks at π/2 and troughs at 3π/2, yielding a normalized oscillation phase within (0, 2π] for each cell.

Step 3: Identify oscillatory genes by quantifying oscillatory likelihood

Finally, we identify oscillatory genes from the phase-resolved transcriptomic profiles, obtained by averaging gene expression across cells assigned to each Hes phase (Figure 1E). To quantify how likely a gene is oscillatory, we fit its phase-ordered expression to an “oscillatory” sine function model on [0, 2π] and compare this fit to a “constant” model. An F statistic yields a p value, which is adjusted across genes using Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) FDR. Genes whose oscillatory fit is significantly better than the “constant” fit (FDR below a chosen cutoff) are predicted to be oscillatory, with smaller FDR values indicating more oscillatory-like gene expression along the pseudo-phase axis. OscillomeR also estimates amplitude, peak and trough levels, and phase shift relative to Hes, enabling additional constraints on oscillatory gene sets.

Together, the aforementioned three steps define the OscillomeR pipeline for reconstructing Hes-driven oscillatory dynamics and calling oscillatory genes. We next assessed its performance on synthetic data with predefined oscillatory genes.

Validation of OscillomeR on synthetic data

We tested OscillomeR on simulated data comprising 500 genes across 1,000 samples, including 100 predefined oscillatory genes with heterogeneous phase shifts relative to a designated master (Hes) gene and 400 non-oscillatory controls (Figure S1A and methods). Gaussian noise was added to all genes, and a sinusoidal component with random amplitudes and random phase shifts in [0, 2π] relative to Hes was superimposed on the oscillatory subset. To span diverse expression behaviors, we included representative patterns such as purely oscillatory, monotonic, and variable-period trajectories; in addition, 50 of the 100 oscillatory genes exhibited waveforms sharper than a pure sine. Monotonically increasing genes were used to mimic differentiation-driven changes, which can confound oscillation detection.

Oscillatory genes were robustly enriched for high MIC, regardless of waveform shape. However, at large sample sizes, monotonic genes also showed high MIC with Hes and could mix with oscillatory genes. Incorporating the geometric variability metric (Q value) separated differentiation-like monotonic genes from true oscillators, indicating that step 1 of OscillomeR effectively selects genes likely to oscillate with Hes while minimizing confounding from differentiation genes (Figure S1B).

OscillomeR successfully captured the oscillation dynamics (Figure S1C) and accurately identified the oscillatory genes, as evidenced by the high area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (Figure S1D). At an FDR of 0.05, OscillomeR achieved uniformly low false-positive rates (FPR ≤ 0.0075) while false-negative rates decreased markedly with increasing sample size, particularly under high noise (from 0.23 at n = 35 to 0.04 at n = 500 for δ = 0.15), highlighting high specificity and sample-size-dependent gains in sensitivity. It also correctly inferred the phase shifts relative to Hes for the oscillatory genes, with strong agreement between predicted and true amplitudes and peak phases across both sinusoidal and sharp oscillatory trajectories (Figures S1E–S1G).

Limited sample sizes can be a challenge to data analysis, particularly for rare cell types in scRNA-seq or bulk RNA-seq with few samples due to cost constraints. We assessed OscillomeR’s performance relative to sample size and found that larger sample sizes enhanced the separation between oscillatory and non-oscillatory genes, consistently improving ROC performance across sizes 35, 100, and 500 under different noise levels (Figures S1C and S1D). However, OscillomeR effectively reconstructed oscillations and categorized genes into oscillatory and non-oscillatory clusters, even with smaller sample sizes (e.g., sample size n = 35).

Methodologically, OscillomeR does not assume that oscillations are strictly sinusoidal. Instead, its core inference relies on non-linear dependence measures (MIC), phase-space geometry with respect to a master oscillatory TF, and manifold learning, which together capture consistent cyclic structure even when temporal trajectories are asymmetric, sharply peaked, or pulse-like. As long as target genes exhibit a coherent, recurrent relationship to the master oscillator in phase space, OscillomeR can detect them as oscillatory. Thus, our approach does not rely on a pure sine-wave assumption but instead leverages non-linear dependence and geometric structure in phase space to accommodate a broad range of biologically realistic oscillation shapes. This flexibility makes OscillomeR adaptable to various systems.

In summary, based on a single known oscillatory TF, OscillomeR uses non-linear dependence measures, low-dimensional manifold embedding (UMAP), and dynamic temporal mapping in phase space to identify potential targets (transcripts oscillating at the same frequency as the putative regulator) in a bulk RNA-seq or scRNA-seq dataset. It does so with high sensitivity, robustly recovering both oscillation presence and timing (amplitude and phase) across a range of noise levels and sample sizes, and it is reliable even with small sample sizes.

Predicting oscillatory genes in PSM cells

With OscillomeR performing robustly on synthetic oscillations, we next evaluated it on real biological oscillations in human iPSC-derived PSM cells, approximated as a homogenous population with short-term synchronized HES7 oscillation. A previous study15 tracked two cycles of HES7 oscillation by temporally resolved bulk RNA-seq across 16 time points in two experimental replicates (Figure 2A). It identified 220 genes oscillating with HES7. Using the resulting 32 bulk RNA-seq samples, we applied OscillomeR to predict oscillatory and non-oscillatory genes without using time information and evaluated performance by recovery of the 220 oscillatory genes, exclusion of non-oscillatory controls, and accurate inference of phase shifts relative to HES7.

Using HES7 as the master oscillatory gene, OscillomeR predicted 2,315 oscillatory genes with FDR <0.1, including the well-known oscillators DKK1 and LFNG, which ranked above HES7 (Figure 2B). Of the 220 previously known oscillatory genes, 167 (75.9%) were recovered (Figure 2B). As negative controls, housekeeping genes (n = 20) and monotonically increasing genes (n = 41) or decreasing (n = 32) “differentiation” genes (Figure S2A) were ranked as non-oscillatory (Figures 2C and S2B), consistent with their stable or monotonic time-series profiles. Thus, OscillomeR accurately distinguished oscillatory from non-oscillatory genes using a small bulk RNA-seq sample size without time information.

We next assessed phase-shift predictions, a key aspect of gene-regulatory analysis, because genes with similar phase shifts relative to HES7 are likely to share upstream regulatory inputs. OscillomeR identified oscillatory genes with diverse phase shifts relative to HES7, particularly including 416 in-phase (peak phase shift <1/4π from HES7 peak) and 476 anti-phase (peak phase shift <1/4π from HES7 valley) genes (Figure 2D). After mean-centering and averaging expression across predicted in-phase or anti-phase genes at each time point, in-phase profiles aligned with HES7 peaks and troughs, whereas anti-phase profiles showed opposite dynamics (Figure 2D). This agreement extended to previously annotated in-phase and anti-phase genes, which clustered according to OscillomeR-inferred peak phase (Figure 2E). Together, these results show that OscillomeR accurately recovers both membership and phase shifts of oscillatory genes relative to HES7 from bulk RNA-seq data without time cues.

OscillomeR identified a broad set of oscillatory genes in PSM cells (Figures 2B and 2E; Table S1). Among the newly identified genes were Notch ligands (e.g., DLL1) and downstream targets of Notch signaling (e.g., NEUROG3) in-phase with HES7, and cell-cycle genes (e.g., AURKA and CDCA2) anti-phase with HES7 (Figures S2C and S2D). These findings suggest that HES7 oscillation may propagate to previously unappreciated pathways.

OscillomeR is equally applicable to scRNA-seq, as it exploits phase-resolved expression differences rather than explicit time labels. Unlike temporally sampled bulk RNA-seq, scRNA-seq of PSM cells profiles multiple segmentoids across HES7 phases and offers higher transcriptomic resolution, enabling the removal of differentiated cells by clustering and reducing bias from mixed cell types. We applied OscillomeR to scRNA-seq data from four independent PSM experiments30,31,32 (Figure 2A and methods) and compared oscillatory gene predictions (Table S2) with those from bulk RNA-seq (Table S1).

Enrichment of known oscillatory, housekeeping, and differentiation genes was similar between scRNA-seq and bulk RNA-seq (Figure 2F). From the scRNA-seq, 1,038 genes were identified with FDR <0.05, of which 111 overlapped with bulk RNA-seq predictions at the same threshold, including 19 genes previously reported as oscillatory genes (Figure 2G). Discrepancies likely reflect differences in induction protocols, sequencing depth, and thresholds as well as differentiation-related confounding in data.

The scRNA-seq analyses of purified PSM populations correctly treated housekeeping genes (GAPDH and ACTB) and differentiation markers (TBX6 and TCF15) as non-oscillatory despite strong monotonic trends (Figures S2B, S2E, and S2F). Genes with an oscillatory pattern that are obscured by mixed differentiation processes in bulk data (e.g., ID1 and REPO3, Figure S2E) are correctly identified as oscillatory in scRNA-seq (Figure S2F), highlighting the advantage of cell-type-resolved scRNA-seq.

Gene Ontology analysis of oscillatory genes from both bulk (Table S3) and scRNA-seq (Table S4) data showed enrichment for early embryonic processes, including somitogenesis and the Wnt signaling pathway known to oscillate in the PSM.33,34 OscillomeR also recovered numerous cell-cycle genes oscillating with HES7, suggesting a link between HES7 oscillation and cell proliferation, consistent with recent evidence from long-term single-cell tracking experiments in mouse embryo tails.35 These predicted cell-cycle genes tended to peak at specific phases of HES7 oscillation (Figures 2H and S2G), with most peaking around 0.5π different from the HES7 peak. Some cell-cycle genes, including previously identified CCND1 and CCND2,15 peaked with HES7. while CCNE1, CCNE2, and CDC6 peaked out-of-phase with HES7 (Tables S1 and S2).

Together, these results indicate that OscillomeR accurately infers oscillatory genes and their phase shifts in PSM cells, excludes non-oscillatory and differentiation-driven genes, and stratifies oscillatory genes into functionally coherent modules, thereby revealing the broader biological significance of TF oscillation dynamics.

OscillomeR recovers cell-cycle genes with scRNA-seq

To assess the generality of OscillomeR across cyclic processes, we applied it to cell-cycle dynamics in HeLa cells using scRNA-seq data36 (Figure 3A). 725 HeLa cells were confirmed to be in the normal cell cycle, indicating the presence of oscillatory dynamics in cell-cycle genes (methods). We then used a single known cell-cycle gene (CCNB1, CCNE2, or CDC20) as the master gene and inferred oscillation phases with OscillomeR. OscillomeR successfully reconstructed oscillatory trajectories consistent with the chosen master gene (Figures 3B–3D).

To validate that the inferred oscillation corresponds to the cell cycle, we examined well-characterized cell-cycle genes that peak at distinct phases (G1, G1/S, S, G2, G2/M, and M)37 (Figure S3). Regardless of whether CCNB1, CCNE2, or CDC20 was used as the master gene, all phase-marker groups were predicted to oscillate, indicating that OscillomeR robustly identifies oscillatory cell-cycle genes with minimal prior knowledge and great flexibility. Notably, within each group, genes oscillated in-phase, whereas different groups oscillated out-of-phase, suggesting that OscillomeR can resolve biologically meaningful phase differences across the cell cycle.

For each reconstructed cell cycle, we averaged expression within each phase-marker group across OscillomeR-inferred phases and observed peak expression in the expected order from G1 through M (or in reverse), matching their known cell-cycle timing (Figure 3D). These results show that OscillomeR can handle cyclic programs of varying duration and biological function, underscoring its broad applicability to diverse oscillatory processes in scRNA-seq data.

OscillomeR benchmarks the existing methods for inferring cyclic signaling

Several computational tools have been developed to identify and remove cell-cycle effects from scRNA-seq data. We next compared them with OscillomeR and tested whether they can be integrated to enhance cyclic reconstruction. We focused on Oscope22 and scPrisma23 and embedded them within the OscillomeR framework (Figure 3E) to reconstruct HES7 oscillation in PSM cells (Figure 3F).

OscillomeR and Oscope differ mainly in gene selection: OscillomeR uses MIC to select genes tracking HES7, whereas Oscope identifies frequency-matched oscillatory clusters via a paired-sine model. Using either set as input to OscillomeR yielded highly correlated gene amplitudes (Pearson correlation coefficient = 0.89; Figure 3G). However, OscillomeR alone identified more candidate oscillatory genes with significant amplitude (N = 221) than the OscillomeR-Oscope pipeline (N = 161) (Figure 3H), although further validation is needed to assess the sensitivity and specificity of these additional predictions.

scPrisma lacks an inherent gene-selection step and depends on prior knowledge of oscillating genes, making it less suited to HES7 but useful for cell ordering. Using the same OscillomeR-selected genes, we ordered cells with either OscillomeR or scPrisma and then applied OscillomeR for gene calling. Amplitudes from the two analysis pipelines remained highly consistent (Pearson correlation coefficient = 0.85; Figure 3G), and the OscillomeR–scPrisma pipeline produced a smaller set of oscillations (N = 40), 80% of which overlapped with those from the other pipelines (Figure 3H).

Thus, OscillomeR matches or exceeds the performance of existing cyclic-analysis tools while providing broader oscillatory gene discovery.

Predicting oscillatory genes in MuSCs

We next applied this tool to asynchronous MuSCs, where understanding the functions of Hes1 oscillation is critical, yet current knowledge about oscillatory genes in these cells is sparse and limited. MuSCs, also known as satellite cells, are tissue-resident cells crucial for muscle repair and regeneration.38,39,40,41 These cells transition from a quiescent state to an activated proliferating state upon injury and then differentiate toward a myogenic lineage, a process regulated by Notch signaling42,43 and MRFs such as Pax7, MyoD, Myf5, myogenin (Myog), and MRF4.44,45 Previous studies have shown that Hes1, MyoD, and Dll1 oscillate in MuSCs6,7,46 with a period of 2–3 h in mice, as revealed by bioluminescence tracing experiments. The oscillatory Hes1 and its downstream MyoD and Dll1 help maintain the proliferative stem cell pool. However, the influence of Hes1 oscillation on the dynamics of other MRFs is not well understood, and the range of target genes remains unexplored. Therefore, we use OscillomeR to predict oscillatory genes in the published scRNA-seq data of mouse MuSCs47(Figures S4A–S4C and methods).

OscillomeR reconstructed the Hes1 oscillation from the scRNA-seq data (Figure 4A), predicting 1,125 genes (FDR < 0.05, amplitude >0.5) as oscillatory in this cell population (Figure S4D). Intriguingly, by gene set enrichment analysis, the oscillatory genes were most enriched in the signature gene set of skeletal muscle satellite cells (normalized enrichment score [NES] = 7.04, p value = 1.17e−29, Figures 4B and 4C). Among these were MRFs Pax7 and Myf5, and one of the regulators of MyoD, Zfp36,48 suggesting a dominant role of Hes1 in regulating the MuSC state. Known oscillatory genes, Myod1 and Dll1 were found to oscillate with low amplitudes (Figure 4D), showing different phase shifts compared to Hes1. Dll1 was in-phase with Hes1, while Myod1 peaked slightly earlier (later). Another key regulator of the MuSC myogenic program, Megf10,49,50 showed an oscillatory pattern in-phase with Hes1, consistent with Hey1, Egr1, and Dusp1 (Figure 4D). Genes involved in MuSC activation and differentiation, such as Klf5, Mt1, and Pitx2, also oscillated with Hes1 (Figure 4E) at transcription level. However, we did not observe an oscillatory pattern of Myog, which was only detected at a minimal expression level in the cells (Figure 4F).

The oscillatory genes were also significantly enriched in the Notch signaling gene set (NES = 3.15, p value = 3.32e−05, Figures 4G and 4H). Within this set, Hey1 oscillates in-phase with Hes1, while Rbpj, Runx1, Smad3, and Egfr oscillate out-of-phase with Hes1 (Figure 4H). Genes related to cell proliferation, such as Ccnd1 and Myc, also oscillate out-of-phase with Hes1 (Figure 4H). Notch receptors, including Notch1, Notch2, and Notch3, were expressed in MuSCs but did not exhibit significant oscillation, although Notch1 displayed a very low amplitude of oscillation (Figures 4E and 4F).

In addition to Notch signaling, Gene Ontology enrichment analysis suggests that Hes1 oscillation influences various pathways, including transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) signaling, as well as biological processes, such as regulation of cell migration in MuSCs (Figure S4E). Therefore, OscillomeR predicts oscillatory dynamics of MRFs such as Myf5 and Megf10, as well as other functional gene groups, thereby extending its applicability to asynchronous cell types such as MuSCs.

Dynamic GRN rewiring revealed by oscillatory genes during embryonic pancreas development

Up to this point, the method has been applied exclusively to a single-cell population. To further evaluate its capabilities, we investigated whether this approach could also uncover more intricate processes, such as capturing cell-fate transitions. The mouse PPs are an ideal model for this goal. Mice deficient for Hes1 suffer from pancreatic hypoplasia,51 and Hes1 oscillates with a period of ∼1.5 h in mouse embryonic PPs (embryonic day 10.5 [E10.5]–E12.5), as shown by bioluminescence experiments tracking Hes1 protein levels in individual cells.8 A mathematical model of the PP differentiation GRN further demonstrates that Hes1 has a higher amplitude in bipotent progenitors (BPs) than in multipotent progenitors (MPCs).9 Both studies8,9 suggest that Hes1 oscillation is crucial for maintaining proper cell proportions of pro-acinar cells (PACs) and BPs during their differentiation from MPCs. As a downstream target of Hes1, Dll1 is the principal Notch ligand in MPCs and it is known to oscillate,8 while Neurog3,51,52 which is required for endocrine lineage differentiation, has recently also been found to oscillate in human iPSC-derived endocrine progenitors.53 Sox9,54 which is co-expressed with Hes1 in MPCs and BPs, is also downstream of Notch signaling, but its oscillatory dynamics in PPs have not been characterized. Therefore, we used OscillomeR to reconstruct Hes1 oscillation and infer other potential targets of Hes1 in different PP populations.

Different PP populations were isolated from the scRNA-seq of early embryonic mouse PPs55 (Figure S5A). Subclustering of these cells captures the time course of different differentiation stages: a homogeneous MPC population (Hes1+Ptf1a+Nkx6-1+) differentiates into BPs with higher Hes1 and early PACs (ePACs) with low Hes1 expression (Figure 5A). Compared with MPCs, ePACs and BPs pronouncedly increased expression of Ptf1a and Nkx6-1, respectively. Consistent with prior knowledge, Hes1 shows a higher amplitude in BPs than in MPCs and ePACs (Figure 5B), benchmarked by the housekeeping genes Gapdh and Actb, which exhibited constant levels throughout the oscillation phase (Figure S5B). TFs co-expressed with Hes1 such as Sox9, Hnf1b, Hes6, and Rest oscillate within all three cell populations with a higher or comparable oscillatory tendency rank than Dll1 (Figure 5C). The mRNA level of Sox9 is highly predicted by OscillomeR, and its protein level shows heterogeneous expression in mouse PPs at the protein level,56 indicating that Sox9 might be an oscillator in mouse MPCs, ePACs, and BPs. Intriguingly, Rest,57 a major negative regulator of endocrine differentiation, also exhibits an oscillatory pattern similar to that of Hes1 and Sox9. OscillomeR identified that, like Hes1, master TFs of different lineages also oscillate in early PPs.

(B) Hes1 oscillation is reconstructed with scRNA-seq data of MPCs, BPs, and ePACs. Dashed lines indicate the range of smoothed Hes1 levels in MPCs.

(C) Oscillatory patterns of pancreatic genes across various cell types are illustrated. Gray shading marks constant expression across Hes1 oscillation phases.

(D) Heatmap comparing cell-type-specific oscillatory genes between MPCs and BPs.

(E) Heatmap comparing cell-type-specific oscillatory genes between MPCs and ePACs.

In (D and E), only genes that showed obvious transitions in oscillatory patterns between two cell types were compared.

Focusing on the master lineage regulators (Nkx6-1, Sox9, and Neurog3 for BPs; Ptf1a, Dll1, and Jag1 for ePACs), we predicted distinct groups of oscillatory genes across PP cell types. Previous studies have observed low fluctuations of Neurog3.58,59 We predicted with OscillomeR that Neurog3 oscillates in anti-phase with Hes1 at the mRNA level in PPs. We also found evidence of stable expression of a subset of genes across PPs: Nkx6-1 shows uniformly high expression in BPs, Ptf1a is constantly low in BPs, and Jag1 is stably expressed in MPCs (Figure 5C).

Additionally, during the differentiation from MPCs to BPs, 367 genes (e.g., Sp1, Foxp4, Cebpa, and Lrp5) transition from non-oscillatory to oscillatory, while 281 oscillatory genes (e.g., Etv5 and Ankrd42) become non-oscillatory (Figure 5D). Similarly, during the differentiation from MPCs to ePACs, 729 oscillatory genes that oscillate in MPCs (e.g., Bcl11a and Zfp276) lose their oscillatory pattern in ePACs, and 144 genes that are non-oscillatory in MPCs (e.g., Wnt7b and Pgdb5) begin to oscillate in ePACs (Figure 5E). These results suggest that Hes1 regulates a larger number of genes in BPs and gradually reduces its influence on genes in cells differentiating toward the PAC fate. Consequently, the oscillatory genes in each population are enriched in different biological processes. For example, oscillatory genes in MPCs are centered on cell proliferation (Figures S5C and S5D), while in BPs they are specifically associated with Wnt, TGF-β, Erk, Hippo signaling, and cholesterol homeostasis (Figures S5C and S5E). In ePACs, oscillatory genes are involved in protein secretion, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, and tubulogenesis (Figures S5C and S5F).

Intriguingly, analysis of the predicted cell-cycle S-phase and G2/M-phase genes shows that OscillomeR also predicted their oscillation with Hes1 in MuSCs, MPCs, BPs, and ePACs (Figures S6A and S6B). These findings suggest that, as in HES7 in the synchronized PSM, Hes1 oscillation is also linked to cell proliferation in asynchronous cell types during development.

The above results demonstrate that OscillomeR captures the rewiring of the cell-type-specific GRN during lineage segregation, suggesting it can be used to investigate dynamic regulatory mechanisms across various developmental and differentiation processes.

Discussion

Numerous efforts have been devoted to understanding the function of molecular clocks, especially the oscillation of TFs in biological systems, using experimental as well as theoretical approaches. Besides the critical roles of the bHLH family in development discussed here, the oscillation of nuclear factor κB maintains the transcription of its target genes,60 and the oscillation of p53 enhances the process of DNA damage repair.61 The key to retrieving these functions is to depict how the oscillatory dynamics of the TFs are sustained and propagated on the GRN. In this study, we provide an efficient computational tool, OscillomeR, to fill the gap between the rare prior knowledge of the network motifs encoding oscillatory gene expression and the high demand for the prediction of functional oscillatory gene sets. The method applies to both synchronous and asynchronous oscillations with modest sample-size requirements and is enabled by single-cell resolution, such as scRNA-seq technology. It has demonstrated the ability to predict known and novel oscillating genes and their phase shifts relative to reference genes across various cell types and species. It is flexible and user-friendly to use alongside Seurat62 and can fit with other goals, for instance, filtering out cell-cycle effects on gene expression. It benefits complex functional studies of oscillators by identifying oscillatory genes in a high-throughput fashion, which is essential for dissecting the effects of oscillation on the different cellular processes and functions investigated.

OscillomeR evaluates each gene’s tendency to oscillate with the master TF and outputs quantitative parameters containing information on timing and levels of peak and trough. The prediction based on mRNA levels indicates oscillatory dynamics at the transcriptional level. Users can refine the gene list using additional criteria. Thus, for example, oscillating genes that are always expressed at high levels may be less informative for inferring GRNs and could be excluded by imposing selection for a specific ratio between peak and trough. Genes with shorter half-lives of their corresponding proteins might be more interesting, since mRNA-level oscillations are likely preserved at the protein level and can be experimentally validated by immunostaining or bioluminescence.

Our analyses demonstrate that OscillomeR can capture oscillators with periods that differ from that of the reference gene, as illustrated by the detection of cell-cycle dynamics, whose period is substantially longer than the Hes oscillation. In principle, this framework is not intrinsically constrained by the length of the reference period and can detect ultradian oscillators with periods both shorter and longer than those of the master gene. However, in its current implementation, OscillomeR is anchored to a single reference oscillator and is best suited for oscillations with similar periods or simple harmonics. Extending the underlying geometric and statistical metrics to systematically identify coupled oscillators with more divergent periods represents an important direction for future methodological development.

OscillomeR further suggests a mechanistic link between Hes oscillation and cell proliferation. Although the ∼2 h Hes periodicity is clearly distinct from the much longer cell-cycle duration, Hes may function as an ultradian regulator that modulates, rather than matches, cell-cycle progression. Because cyclins are regulated post-translationally through changes in protein stability, repeated ultradian transcriptional pulses could be integrated over longer timescales via the stability of downstream proteins or the cumulative impact on target gene expression, thereby influencing proliferation without requiring a 1:1 correspondence between oscillation period and cell-cycle length. Moreover, the cellular response to Hes oscillation may depend on cell-cycle phase, such that the same ultradian signal is interpreted differently across distinct stages of the cell cycle. In a broader context, predicting and analyzing coupling between multiple biological oscillators is both fundamentally important and highly complex, and extending OscillomeR to systematically investigate such oscillator coupling represents an attractive avenue for future work.

One limitation of the algorithm is that the time direction of the reconstructed oscillatory trace remains undetermined, as there is no inherent indication of whether the oscillation progresses clockwise or counterclockwise along the UMAP trajectory. However, this ambiguity can be resolved through experimental approaches that track specific oscillatory genes and the reference gene in real time. For instance, in bulk PSM data, examining time-series bulk RNA-seq data can confirm whether the reconstructed oscillation aligns with or opposes the actual temporal coordinates.

OscillomeR can be used solely with scRNA-seq and integrated with the GRN inference from multimodal profiling data. The directed GRN can be inferred from protein-binding site analysis with chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq),63 TF footprinting analysis with assay for transposase-accessible chromatin using sequencing (ATAC-seq),64 or genetic perturbations with Perturb-seq65 in the same cell population. Combining OscillomeR and the directed architecture of GRNs may potentially improve its performance in multiple ways. Especially now, our approach identifies oscillatory genes associated with Hes, thereby providing a prioritized set of candidate Hes-regulated genes while recognizing that some associations may also arise from shared upstream signals or parallel network dynamics. GRNs may help predict the direction of oscillation propagation among the oscillatory genes by identifying upstream and downstream relationships. Overall, OscillomeR is a general workflow that focuses on uncovering oscillation dynamics from omics data and predicting oscillation propagation in GRNs.

Methods

Synthetic data simulating gene expression

We simulated gene-expression data for 500 genes measured over 1,000 time points. Each gene was assigned to one of six expression patterns: oscillatory (n = 50), oscillatory with sharp shape (n = 50), monotonically increasing (n = 100), long period 5 times (n = 100) or 4.5 times (n = 100) of the master gene, and constant expression (n = 100). For oscillatory genes, expression was modeled as y(t) = Asin[(t + φ)π], where A denotes amplitude and φ is a phase shift sampled uniformly from [0, 2]. The master gene had amplitude A = 1 and phase shift φ = 0. Monotonic genes followed a linear function, y(t) = 0.2Aπt + b, where b was sampled from [−1.5, −0.5]. Long-period genes used the same sinusoidal form as the oscillatory genes but with the period extended by factors of 5 and 4.5, respectively. Sharp oscillatory genes were generated by raising the sinusoidal signal to the 10th power, y(t) = Asin10[(t + φ)π]. To approximate experimental variability, we added Gaussian noise with standard deviation δ = 0.05 (low noise) and δ = 0.15 (high noise) to all expression values.

Bulk RNA-seq and scRNA-seq data of different cell types

OscillomeR applies to both synchronous and asynchronous cell populations and requires that the cells be homogeneous at the differentiation stage. The PSM cells in the experiments for the bulk RNA-seq data (GEO: GSE116929)15 are considered homogeneous and synchronized. The raw count matrix of the 32 samples from two experimental repeats is divided by the total counts for each sample and multiplied by a scale factor (mean of the total counts of all the samples). The rescaled counts are then transformed with the natural logarithm, i.e., log-norm.count = ln(count + 1). A similar normalization was also used for scRNA-seq. For the scRNA-seq data, a few standard procedures might be needed to extract the desired cell types from the existing datasets. The feature matrix from each scRNA-seq dataset was processed using the Seurat package’s standard analysis pipeline,62 including normalization, highly variable feature selection, and clustering. Subclustering of the initial cell cluster containing the desired cell types is necessary to confirm and purify the cell population with the expected gene-expression markers. PSM cells were isolated as clusters with high expression of PSM markers (TBX6, MSGN1, and HES7) from scRNA-seq datasets GEO: GSM5975290,30 GSM5975292,30 GSE195467,31 and GSE220563.32 The cells close to the PSM clusters are excluded if they have high expression of anterior PSM markers (MEOX1, TCF15, and RIPPLY1), the tail bud marker (HOXA10), or neuromesodermal progenitor/neural progenitor markers (SOX2 and HES5). The anteroposterior-axis-related genes were examined, and the cell population was refined by reclustering PSM cells and excluding cells with low WNT3A and TBX6 expression and high MEOX1, FGF18, and MESP2 expression. HeLa cells (HeLaS3 wild type, GEO: GSM4224315)36 that have gene expression indicating the existence of the normal cell cycle marked by low E2F5 are removed for analysis. MuSCs (GEO: GSE171794)47 are classified with marker genes including Pax7, Hes1, Stmn1, Cdkn1c, Myog, Lgals1, H2az1, Mt1, Nppc, and Gpx3. The tip-trunk cells from the PPs (GEO: GSE115931)55 are recognized by markers Hes1, Sox9, Nkx6-1, Neurog3, Dll1, Jag1, and Ptf1a and are excluded by other lineage markers Chga, Cdh5, and Pecam1. Users should also be aware of additional confounders in RNA-seq data that may affect the inference of oscillations. For example, genes whose expression patterns could obscure or mimic oscillatory behavior can be excluded during the MIC-based gene-selection step, and dropout events (i.e., undetected low-abundance expression) can be imputed using existing methods such as adaptively thresholded low-rank approximation (ALRA).66 In practice, users are expected to implement their own preprocessing and quality-control pipeline to mitigate such confounders before applying OscillomeR.

Oscillation reconstruction with bulk RNA-seq and scRNA-seq data

We use normalized feature counts for the following oscillation reconstruction. We restrict the oscillation analysis to genes sufficiently expressed in the cell population, filtering out those with very low abundance or detection rates for which reliable oscillatory inference is not feasible. To further stabilize the estimation of oscillatory patterns, cells are binned by their position along the inferred circular trajectory, thereby smoothing the mean expression profile and reducing the influence of individual zero measurements on the associated test statistics. The oscillatory model additionally includes parameters for amplitude (A) and basal expression level (b), which help distinguish true oscillatory signals from low-level noisy expression without requiring an explicit zero-inflated count model. Highly variable features are then selected, and the MIC with the reference gene is calculated with the minerva package.26 Features with MIC above a threshold are retained for subsequent PCA and UMAP projections. To order the cells, we form a polygonal trajectory from the k-means centers in the 2D UMAP using two selected principal components (typically PC1 and PC2, although alternative PCs can be used if the leading components primarily capture batch effects or other confounders). Each cell is then aligned to the closest point on the trajectory. After a direction on the trajectory is chosen, cells follow the order of their aligned positions on the trajectory. The gene expression of the reference gene is further mapped to a sine function with DTW,67,68 and each cell gets its oscillation phase in (0, 2π]. Gene expression is aggregated for cells with the same oscillation phase as the reference gene. For each gene, the aggregated expression over (0, 2π] is smoothed using loess fitting, and oscillation parameters, including amplitude and peak phase, are estimated.

For each gene, we derive a p value from the F statistic by fitting phase-ordered expression to an “oscillatory” sine function model on [0, 2π] and to a “constant” model, then apply BH FDR correction across all genes. We implement additional statistics from two alternative methods for users’ references: (1) the OscoNet bootstrap-based method, which permutes cell order for one gene in each pair when estimating distances and uses this null distribution to obtain an FDR for oscillatory behavior; and (2) the GeneCycle69 package, which is designed for detecting periodically expressed genes in time-series data. These complementary approaches provide external benchmarks and confirm that OscillomeR achieves competitive performance, as quantified by explicit classification.

Incorporation of scPrisma and Oscope with OscillomeR

To incorporate Oscope with OscillomeR, we replaced OscillomeR’s feature-selection procedure with the gene cluster selected by Oscope, then performed the following OscillomeR analysis. To incorporate scPrisma with OscillomeR, we used the features selected by OscillomeR, then applied scPrisma to obtain the cell order for the oscillation. The cell order was used for the subsequent OscillomeR analysis.

Calculating gene ontology and gene set enrichment

Gene Ontology enrichment of oscillatory genes predicted by OscillomeR in each dataset was calculated with clusterProfile,70 and the p value was adjusted with the BH method. Genes were ranked by adjusted p value (FDR) to calculate the gene set enrichment score. The ranks were used to calculate the enrichment score with fgsea71 and for all the gene sets obtained from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) with msigdbr.72 p values were adjusted with the BH method.
