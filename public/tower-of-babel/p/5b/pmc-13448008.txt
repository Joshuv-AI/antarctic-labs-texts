When Moderate Is Severe: Bivalvular Functional Regurgitation
Authors: Asad Ghafoor
Published: 2026

We have long lived with the time-honored assumption that, because it has the worst prognosis, severe disease merits the most innovative interventions. In a quarter century, we have revolutionized the management of valvular heart disease with minimally invasive transcatheter solutions. What started with transcatheter replacement of aortic valves has evolved to include transcatheter repair and replacement of mitral and tricuspid valves. The heart team approach has led to multidisciplinary collaboration, improving outcomes. Importantly, the significant gain in safety associated with minimally invasive transcatheter solutions compared with surgery has allowed us to push these envelopes. The progress has created an opportunity, a platform to study those with less-than-severe disease. The time has come to understand coexisting moderate bivalvular disease beyond isolated valve disease.

Little is known about the epidemiology and prognosis of moderate bivalvular functional regurgitation (FR). In this issue of Structural Heart, Nitsche et al.1 explore the burden and outcome of moderate bivalvular FR in a large-scale observational cohort study of 13,000 patients. Significant bivalvular FR, defined as the presence of moderate functional mitral regurgitation (FMR) and moderate or greater-than-moderate functional tricuspid regurgitation (FTR), was observed in 22% of patients. Only 2.1% of this population was referred for mitral valve intervention. From a heart failure subtype analysis, 56% had heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, 24% had heart failure with mildly reduced ejection fraction, and 20% had heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF). Echocardiographic markers of disease severity showed larger left atrial and left ventricular dimensions in severe FMR compared with moderate bivalvular FR. Interestingly, echocardiographic parameters of right heart structure showed equivalent right ventricular and right atrial dimensions between severe FMR and moderate bivalvular FR, probably indicating an equal predisposition to right heart failure. After a median follow-up of 68 months, patients with severe FMR had a 5-year mortality rate of 62% versus a mortality rate of 52.8% for the moderate bivalvular FR group. Most importantly, when adjusted by heart failure subtype, mortality was similar between severe FMR and moderate bivalvular FR in HFrEF. The fact that details regarding guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) were not provided remains a significant limitation in the analysis of this cohort.

A review of available literature shows that increasing mitral regurgitation severity is associated with poor prognosis.2 This prognostic association in moderate FMR may be greater in patients with HFrEF.3 Similarly, although long-term outcomes such as mortality increase with severe FMR, mortality rates are similar between moderate and severe FMR when treated with medical therapy alone and lower when treated with mitral transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (m-TEER).4 In addition, patients with moderate FMR have a higher heart failure hospitalization risk when treated with medical therapy alone. In the EXPANDed studies, global postmarketing data were analyzed to evaluate the safety and effectiveness of third- and fourth-generation MitraClip systems (Abbott, Abbott Park, Illinois).5 At baseline, patients with moderate and severe FMR had similar left ventricular ejection fractions. Significant reduction in mitral regurgitation was achieved in both the moderate and severe FMR cohorts. Ejection fraction significantly increased from baseline in moderate FMR. Statistically significant reduction of left ventricular end-systolic volume occurred in the moderate FMR group to a degree that was not seen in the severe FMR group. The 1-year risk of heart failure hospitalization was significantly reduced in both groups after intervention with m-TEER. These findings are particularly promising in light of previously known neutral outcomes in the surgical repair of moderate ischemic mitral regurgitation.6 In fact, there was an increased risk of neurological events and no significant impact on left ventricular remodeling, survival, or reduction in heart failure hospitalization. In total, these findings suggest a benefit to early transcatheter intervention in FMR, in which (unlike surgery) there is certainly no signal of harm.

Less is known about the impact of minimally invasive interventions in patients with moderate FTR. There have been no randomized clinical trials specifically designed to evaluate this cohort. However, the severity of tricuspid regurgitation driving long-term outcomes is understood. In the reported observational cohort, the worst 5-year outcomes (65.4% mortality) were in patients with isolated severe FTR.1 The tricuspid regurgitation space is undergoing a quiet revolution, first with the approval of transcatheter tricuspid edge-to-edge repair after the publication of the TRILUMINATE trial—which showed the superiority of transcatheter tricuspid edge-to-edge repair over medical therapy alone for the primary composite end point of mortality, heart failure hospitalization, and improvement in quality of life7—and then the approval of the EVOQUE valve system (Edwards Lifesciences, Irvine, California) for transcatheter tricuspid valve replacement based on the positive outcomes of the TRISCEND-II trial. TRISCEND-II demonstrated the superiority of transcatheter tricuspid valve replacement over medical therapy alone for the primary composite endpoint, primarily driven by symptom improvement and quality of life.8 Both these landmark trials excluded patients with less-than-severe FTR.

Currently, the European and American guidelines recommend m-TEER only for patients with moderate-to-severe FMR who remain symptomatic despite optimized GDMT. A volumetric analysis of the MITRA-FR, COAPT, and RESHAPE-HF2 trials show that the benefit of m-TEER is limited to patients with left ventricular end-diastolic volumes less than 220 ml,9 probably indicating that the benefit is greater in patients with FMR closer to moderate than severe. This observation is also evident in populations selected for FMR trials, where the mean effective regurgitant orifice area (EROA) was 0.41 cm2 in the earlier COAPT trial and down to 0.25 cm2 in the later RESHAPE-HF2 trial, gradually approaching the moderate range. In the currently ongoing EMPOWER trial, investigators are studying the benefit of the Carillon device (Cardiac Dimensions, Kirkland, Washington) in patients with larger ventricles (left ventricular end-diastolic diameter ≥60 mm) and any degree of FMR (even mild). Additional trials to evaluate the benefit of m-TEER in moderate FMR are needed and will pose several unique challenges. These include enrollment criteria that need to accommodate the dynamic nature of FMR, implementation of appropriate GDMT, and addition of artificial intelligence–based tools that can help standardize FMR severity quantification. Accurate estimation of FMR remains a diagnostic challenge owing to its dynamic nature. Severity can vary with preload, afterload, and limitations associated with transthoracic echocardiographic assessment in obese and ventilated patients. Ideal assessment requires an expert echocardiographer and euvolemic state. EROA can be underestimated with transthoracic echocardiography. Therefore, the ability to obtain transesophageal echocardiographic imaging with 3D multiplanar reconstruction is crucial. In borderline cases, incorporation of regurgitant fraction into the assessment—as regurgitant fraction ≥50% indicates severe FMR regardless of the EROA or regurgitant volume—is essential.10 Lastly, in a pioneering research study, machine learning algorithms were trained on echocardiograms from observational cohorts and validated. Patients graded as having severe mitral regurgitation had higher 1-year mortality. The authors concluded that an automated multiparametric machine learning model for grading mitral regurgitation severity was feasible, fast, and highly accurate and predicted 1-year mortality.11

From a clinician’s viewpoint, symptomatic patients with moderate bivalvular FR despite appropriate GDMT merit interventions when the mortality signal is comparable to that of patients with isolated severe FR. These interventions need to be studied in well-designed randomized clinical trials. Further, we face the uphill task of changing perceptions in the clinical community, where only severe disease is deemed worthy of interventions—an age-old belief from an era in which surgical solutions were the sole option. Minimally invasive transcatheter procedures are far safer than surgery and therefore tip the balance in favor of earlier intervention. We do not have to wait for moderate to become severe; we can act sooner with less risk and earlier delivery of benefit.
