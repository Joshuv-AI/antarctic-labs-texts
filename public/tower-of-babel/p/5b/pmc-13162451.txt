Neonatal carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I deficiency with severe hyperammonemic coma: the first report from Palestine
Authors: Anas K Assi, Amro Odeh, Habeeb H Awwad, Hanin Kassem, Imad Dweikat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCarbamoyl phosphate synthetase I deficiency (CPS1D) is the most proximal and severe urea cycle disorder, often presenting in the neonatal period with rapidly progressive hyperammonemia. Early symptoms are often nonspecific, posing significant diagnostic challenges and contributing to delays in targeted management.Case presentationWe report a term female neonate (37 + 6 weeks, 2600 g) who initially presented with transient tachypnea of the newborn and was managed with continuous positive airway pressure. At 36 h of life, she developed sudden desaturation and bradycardia requiring emergent intubation, and laboratory evaluation revealed severe hyperammonemia. Plasma amino acid analysis demonstrated markedly elevated glutamine and alanine with severely reduced citrulline, and urine organic acid analysis revealed undetectable orotic acid—a biochemical profile consistent with a proximal urea cycle defect. Despite immediate initiation of intravenous sodium benzoate and arginine at guideline-recommended doses, serum ammonia escalated to a peak of approximately 3300 µmol/L. Peritoneal dialysis was initiated as the sole available extracorporeal modality, given the absence of neonatal hemodialysis capacity within the regional healthcare network, and achieved a gradual biochemical reduction; however, no neurological recovery occurred. Whole-exome sequencing identified a previously unreported homozygous likely pathogenic CPS1D variant (NM_001875.5:c.4172 C > A; p.Thr1391Lys). The patient progressed to multiorgan failure and died on day six of life.ConclusionThis case illustrates the fulminant course of neonatal-onset CPS1D and the diagnostic difficulty posed by an initial nonspecific respiratory presentation. Notably, despite early ammonia detection and prompt initiation of guideline-directed management, the outcome was fatal—reflecting the intrinsic biological severity of the disorder and the absence of access to high-efficiency extracorporeal clearance and liver transplantation rather than a failure of timely recognition alone. A high index of suspicion for urea cycle disorders in neonates with unexplained encephalopathy or sudden decompensation, early metabolic evaluation, and rapid genomic testing remain essential components of management, even where definitive therapeutic options are limited. Introduction

Urea cycle disorders (UCDs) are rare errors of nitrogen metabolism that impair toxic ammonia excretion [1, 2]. Carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I (CPS1) catalyzes the first, rate-limiting step of this cycle; its autosomal recessive deficiency (CPS1D) represents the most proximal and typically most severe UCD [3]. Affecting 1 in 50,000 to over 1,000,000 live births, CPS1D carries an exceptionally high risk of mortality and irreversible neurological injury [3]. Affected neonates appear healthy at birth but deteriorate rapidly within 24–72 h of protein intake, exhibiting nonspecific symptoms—such as respiratory distress, lethargy, and coma—that frequently mimic sepsis or transient tachypnea and consequently delay diagnosis [2, 4].

The characteristic biochemical profile of this proximal block includes extreme hyperammonemia, elevated glutamine and alanine, markedly low citrulline, and minimal or absent urinary orotic acid [4]. Because this profile is indistinguishable from N-acetylglutamate synthase (NAGS) deficiency, molecular genetic testing is essential for a definitive diagnosis. Ultimately, early recognition remains critical, as the magnitude and duration of hyperammonemia directly dictate the severity of neurotoxicity and adverse outcomes.

Acute management of CPS1D follows established UCD emergency protocols, including immediate cessation of protein intake, provision of high-calorie intravenous glucose with or without lipids to suppress catabolism, administration of nitrogen-scavenging agents and arginine, and early initiation of extracorporeal ammonia removal when available [1]. Despite aggressive therapy, neonatal-onset CPS1D frequently proves refractory to medical management. Liver transplantation remains the only definitive curative option; however, many neonates deteriorate too rapidly to undergo transplantation, and overall mortality for neonatal CPS1D remains high, with survivors often experiencing severe neurodevelopmental impairment [1].

Here, we report the first genetically confirmed report of neonatal-onset CPS1D from Palestine. This case expands the global mutational spectrum of CPS1 and highlights the fulminant clinical course, diagnostic challenges, and therapeutic limitations associated with severe neonatal CPS1D, particularly in resource-limited settings. This report is presented in accordance with the CARE guidelines [5].

Case presentation

A female newborn was delivered at 37 weeks of gestation to first-cousin parents by normal vaginal delivery, with a birth weight of 2600 g. Shortly after birth, she developed tachypnea and was diagnosed with transient tachypnea of the newborn. She was managed with continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) for 24 h, followed by supplemental oxygen via nasal cannula. Blood cultures were obtained, and empirical intravenous ampicillin and gentamicin were initiated.

At 36 h of life, the infant experienced sudden bradycardia and oxygen desaturation, necessitating emergent endotracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation. Chest radiography demonstrated normal cardiac size with bilateral pulmonary infiltrates (Fig. 1). Transthoracic echocardiography revealed structurally normal cardiac anatomy, good biventricular systolic function, mild tricuspid regurgitation, and no evidence of pulmonary hypertension. Arterial blood gas analysis showed severe metabolic acidosis (pH 7.15, PCO₂ 21 mmHg, HCO₃⁻ 10.3 mmol/L). Concurrently, exactly at 36 h of life, the first serum ammonia measurement was obtained and was markedly elevated at 1000 µmol/L (reference range 21–95 µmol/L). Abnormal movements were observed, and phenobarbital was administered. Given the extreme hyperammonemia and suspicion of an inborn error of metabolism, the infant was urgently transferred to our center.

A chest X-ray showed bilateral pulmonary opacities and a mildly prominent cardiomediastinal silhouette without definitive cardiomegaly

Upon admission to our neonatal intensive care unit at 47 h of life—resulting in a delay of approximately 11 h from the initial detection of hyperammonemia at the referring facility—the infant was intubated and mechanically ventilated. Neurological examination revealed a deeply comatose state, with no spontaneous eye opening and withdrawal only to painful stimuli. She was tachycardic (heart rate 184 beats per minute), while blood pressure was initially within the normal range for age. Laboratory evaluation demonstrated a critically elevated serum ammonia level of 1606 µmol/L, severe metabolic acidosis, and elevated serum lactate. Standard emergency management for hyperammonemia was initiated immediately upon this admission (at 47 h of life), consisting of an intravenous bolus of sodium benzoate and arginine followed by continuous infusion. Enteral feeding was withheld, and an orogastric tube was placed for gastric decompression. Despite maximal medical therapy, serial ammonia measurements continued to rise, reaching 2600 µmol/L and subsequently 2540 µmol/L within the first day of admission, and eventually peaking at approximately 3300 µmol/L. The infant remained deeply comatose, ventilator-dependent, and without spontaneous respiratory effort. Transfontanellar ultrasound showed no evidence of intraventricular hemorrhage or structural brain abnormalities.

Given persistent and extreme hyperammonemia refractory to medical therapy, urgent extracorporeal clearance was indicated. However, neonatal hemodialysis and continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) were unavailable at our institution. Following consultation with a pediatric nephrologist at a regional tertiary referral network, it was confirmed that hemodialysis was unavailable for any patient weighing less than 5 kg. Consequently, peritoneal dialysis was planned as the only accessible modality due to these strict resource limitations. The initial attempt was deferred due to intraoperative hypotension; following hemodynamic stabilization, a peritoneal dialysis catheter was inserted on 9 September 2025, and dialysis was initiated. After initiation of dialysis, serum ammonia levels gradually declined from a peak of approximately 3300 µmol/L to 1600 µmol/L, then 1200 µmol/L, and eventually 700 µmol/L over several days. This state of extreme hyperammonemia (> 1000 µmol/L) persisted continuously for approximately 72 h, spanning from its initial detection at 36 h of life until a biochemical reduction was finally achieved following the initiation of peritoneal dialysis on day four of life. Despite biochemical improvement, there was no neurological recovery; the infant remained deeply comatose, ventilator-dependent, and without spontaneous respiratory effort (Table 2).

Comprehensive metabolic investigations were performed. Plasma amino acid analysis demonstrated markedly elevated glutamine (≈ 1200 µmol/L; reference range 300–700 µmol/L) and alanine (≈ 900 µmol/L; reference range 200–500 µmol/L), with severely reduced citrulline (< 5 µmol/L; reference range 10–45 µmol/L), consistent with a proximal urea cycle defect. Urine organic acid analysis was unremarkable, with undetectable orotic acid. This biochemical profile supported a diagnosis of carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I (CPS1) deficiency or N-acetylglutamate synthase deficiency.

Whole-exome sequencing identified a homozygous missense variant in the CPS1 gene, NM_001875.5:c.4172 C > A p.(Thr1391Lys), which was classified as likely pathogenic. This variant has been submitted to ClinVar (accession number SCV007101763). It is absent from the gnomAD v4.1.0 population database and, to our knowledge, has not been previously reported in the literature. A different missense change affecting the same amino acid residue, p.(Thr1391Met), has previously been reported as likely pathogenic in ClinVar (accession number VCV000552749.19), supporting the functional importance of this residue. The p.(Thr1391Lys) variant affects a highly conserved residue within the CPS1 catalytic domain and is predicted to be deleterious by multiple in silico tools, including REVEL (0.91), AlphaMissense (0.954), CADD (26.8), PolyPhen-2 (1.00; probably damaging), SIFT (0; deleterious), and MutationTaster (1; disease-causing). According to ACMG/AMP variant interpretation guidelines, the variant was classified as likely pathogenic based on PM2, PM5, PP3, and PP4. The PP4 criterion was supported by the highly specific phenotype and biochemical profile, including extreme neonatal hyperammonemia, markedly elevated glutamine and alanine, very low citrulline, and absent urinary orotic acid, consistent with CPS1 deficiency. The variant call was supported by 105 high-quality sequencing reads on an Illumina-based exome platform. Given the strong sequencing quality metrics and clear homozygous call, orthogonal Sanger confirmation was not performed. Additionally, while parental segregation analysis was recommended, the parents declined further testing; the absence of these confirmatory studies represents a limitation of our genetic evaluation. In the context of the highly characteristic biochemical findings, these results are consistent with a molecular diagnosis of autosomal recessive carbamoylphosphate synthetase I deficiency.

During her NICU course, the infant developed progressive multiorgan dysfunction. She remained ventilator-dependent with persistent hypoxemia despite high ventilatory settings and developed refractory hypotension requiring escalating doses of norepinephrine, adrenaline, and dopamine. Blood cultures grew Staphylococcus haemolyticus, and a rectal swab was positive for carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae, for which she was treated with meropenem, vancomycin, and colistin. She also developed coagulopathy, anemia, and thrombocytopenia, requiring multiple blood product transfusions.

On the fifth day of admission, the infant suffered sudden cardiovascular collapse with severe metabolic acidosis and profound hypoxia, progressing to asystole. Cardiopulmonary resuscitation was performed for approximately 20 min without return of spontaneous circulation, and death was pronounced.

Discussion

Carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I deficiency (CPS1D) is a rare but devastating proximal urea cycle disorder that most commonly presents in the neonatal period with rapid-onset hyperammonemic encephalopathy and high mortality [6, 7]. Affected infants are typically asymptomatic at birth and deteriorate within the first days of life after protein intake, progressing to seizures, coma, and respiratory failure [6, 8]. Our patient followed this classic neonatal-onset course, initially presenting with presumed transient tachypnea of the newborn before developing profound hyperammonemia and encephalopathy by the second day of life, consistent with previously reported lethal neonatal CPS1D cases [7, 8].

The biochemical profile of CPS1D reflects a proximal block in the urea cycle, characterized by extreme hyperammonemia, elevated glutamine and alanine, low or undetectable citrulline, and normal or low urinary orotic acid due to intact ornithine transcarbamylase activity [9, 10]. This pattern was observed in our patient and helped distinguish CPS1D from other urea cycle disorders such as OTC or ASS deficiencies. Early diagnosis is often delayed because initial symptoms—tachypnea, poor feeding, lethargy, or presumed sepsis—are nonspecific, as occurred in our case. Several reports emphasize that delayed ammonia measurement contributes significantly to poor outcomes, underscoring the need for immediate ammonia testing in any neonate with unexplained neurological or respiratory deterioration [10].

Molecular genetic testing is essential for definitive diagnosis. The Carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I (CPS1) gene is large and highly heterogeneous, with over 260 pathogenic variants reported, most of which are private to individual families [6, 11]. Funghini et al. demonstrated that many CPS1 mutations are novel, highlighting the diagnostic challenge when clinical suspicion is low [11]. Our patient harbored a previously unreported homozygous CPS1 variant, further expanding the mutational spectrum. Importantly, CPS1D exhibits marked genotype–phenotype variability. Klaus et al. described two siblings with identical CPS1 mutations but dramatically different outcomes—one fatal neonatal case and one adult-onset, diet-responsive disease—suggesting that residual enzyme activity and regulatory mechanisms may influence disease severity [12].

Despite initiating aggressive, guideline-directed medical therapy upon admission—including nitrogen scavengers, arginine, and dialysis [13]—our patient’s hyperammonemia remained refractory. This poor response to medical management is consistent with prior neonatal series [7, 8]. Batshaw et al. reported high mortality among neonatal-onset urea cycle disorders even with consortium-level care, reflecting the aggressive nature of this phenotype [7].

Liver transplantation (LT) remains the only definitive curative therapy for CPS1D. Several reports document successful outcomes following LT in selected patients, including neonatal auxiliary transplantation and adult living-donor transplantation in late-onset disease [9, 11]. Nevertheless, transplantation in CPS1D poses challenges, particularly in the neonatal period, due to rapid clinical deterioration and limited time for intervention. In our case, LT was considered; however, the patient’s acute instability and lack of immediate access to transplantation services precluded definitive treatment. Additionally, caution is required when selecting related donors, as heterozygous carriers may have reduced CPS1 activity, potentially leading to post-transplant metabolic complications [13].

Because this represents the first genetically confirmed neonatal CPS1D case reported from Palestine, the regional healthcare infrastructure is highly relevant to understanding the disease trajectory and management limitations. First, as highlighted previously, high-efficiency neonatal extracorporeal clearance (hemodialysis or CRRT for infants < 5 kg) is currently unavailable within the local healthcare system, leaving peritoneal dialysis as the only accessible modality. Second, urgent pediatric liver transplantation is not locally performed. Accessing such life-saving procedures necessitates complex cross-border medical referrals, which are logistically demanding and inherently time-consuming, effectively rendering them inaccessible during a rapidly progressive, ultra-acute neonatal metabolic crisis. Third, while established referral pathways exist to transfer decompensating neonates from local maternity hospitals to specialized tertiary metabolic centers—as was successfully done in this case—the initial, non-specific clinical presentation often mimics common neonatal conditions, which inevitably risks delaying initial recognition, targeted testing, and subsequent transport.

This case reinforces several important clinical lessons. CPS1D should be included in the differential diagnosis of any neonate with unexplained encephalopathy or respiratory distress unresponsive to standard therapy, and immediate serum ammonia measurement should be considered mandatory in this setting. Rapid metabolic consultation and early genetic testing remain essential — not because they guarantee survival, but because they enable prompt initiation of guideline-directed management, accurate family counseling, and, in eligible patients, timely referral for liver transplantation before irreversible neurological injury has occurred. Critically, however, this case demonstrates that even when hyperammonemia is detected within the first 36 h of life and standard management is promptly initiated, the fulminant neonatal-onset phenotype of CPS1D may prove refractory to all available medical therapies. Early diagnosis is therefore a necessary but not sufficient condition for survival in severe neonatal CPS1D; outcomes are ultimately constrained by the intrinsic biological severity of the disorder, the speed of ammonia accumulation, and the availability of definitive interventions including high-efficiency extracorporeal clearance and liver transplantation. Our report represents the first genetically confirmed case of CPS1D from Palestine, identifying a novel homozygous likely CPS1 variant and expanding the global mutational spectrum. This finding highlights the need for improved access to metabolic diagnostics and genomic resources in underrepresented populations, particularly in regions with high rates of consanguinity where autosomal recessive disorders are disproportionately prevalent.

Conclusion

This case of fatal neonatal-onset carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I deficiency (CPS1D) illustrates the devastating trajectory of proximal urea cycle disorders even when hyperammonemia is detected within the first 36 h of life and emergency management is initiated promptly according to established guidelines. The patient’s rapid progression to extreme hyperammonemia, irreversible encephalopathy, and multiorgan failure—despite metabolic workup and transfer to a tertiary center—reflects the intrinsic severity of fulminant neonatal CPS1D rather than a failure of early recognition alone. The characteristic biochemical profile and homozygous likely pathogenic CPS1D variant confirmed the diagnosis, reinforcing the value of early metabolic evaluation and rapid genomic testing as essential tools for definitive diagnosis, family counseling, and informed management planning—while acknowledging that these measures do not guarantee survival in the most acute presentations.

This case therefore serves as a cautionary illustration of the biological limitations of current medical therapy in acute neonatal CPS1D. Survival in such presentations may ultimately depend on timely access to high-efficiency extracorporeal clearance and liver transplantation, neither of which was accessible in our resource-limited setting. Rather than endorsing early diagnosis as a reliable pathway to improved outcomes, our findings emphasize that neonatologists must maintain a high index of suspicion for urea cycle disorders in any neonate with unexplained deterioration, while simultaneously advocating for systemic improvements in regional access to specialized metabolic services, neonatal extracorporeal clearance capacity, and pediatric transplantation infrastructure.
