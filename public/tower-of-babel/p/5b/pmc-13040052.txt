A multi-strategy framework for enhancing Harris hawks optimization for global optimization problems
Authors: Safaa Al-Adwan, Salwani Abdullah, Mohammed Alweshah, Sofian Kassaymeh, Aws Al-Qaisi, Maen Takruri, WANDEEP KAUR RATAN SINGH
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The standard Harris Hawks Optimization (HHO) algorithm often suffers from premature convergence and stagnation on complex, high-dimensional problems, particularly in wrapper-based feature selection (FS) for medical diagnosis, where costly fitness evaluations demand efficient search strategies. This paper proposes a Modified HHO (MHHO) that integrates three synergistic mechanisms: (i) Leader-Guided Perching (LGP), which introduces leader bias during exploration to accelerate movement toward promising regions; (ii) an Adaptive Deception Factor (ADF), which dynamically scales Lévy-flight intensity to escape deceptive local optima; and (iii) a Hierarchical Attack Strategy (HAS), which replaces the hard besiege phase with a mentor-guided exploitation step to mitigate swarm clumping and refine local search. A dual-domain evaluation was conducted. In Experiment Series 1, MHHO statistically outperformed the standard HHO on 18 of 23 benchmark functions, at the expected cost of higher computational time. In Experiment Series 2, involving wrapper-based FS on 15 medical datasets, MHHO achieved higher mean classification accuracy on most datasets while exhibiting enhanced stability, resulting in a favorable accuracy–sparsity trade-off through minimal yet high-performing feature subsets. Friedman’s test confirmed a significant global difference (p = 0.0013), and Holm’s post-hoc analysis verified that LGP, ADF, and HAS each contribute significant improvements over standard HHO. Overall, MHHO ranked among the top performers on approximately half of the evaluated benchmarks, demonstrating its effectiveness as a robust and accurate optimizer.

Introduction

Modern clinical and biomedical datasets are increasingly characterized by their high dimensionality, routinely containing dozens to thousands of measured variables. These variables span a wide range of datatypes, including laboratory test results, imaging-derived indices, patient-reported questionnaire items, and continuous sensor signals1. This proliferation of data, however, is often contrasted by a comparative scarcity of labeled samples, leading to the ”curse of dimensionality” or the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p \gg n$$\end{document} (many features, few samples) problem. In such high-dimensional, low-sample regimes, sophisticated machine learning algorithms are highly susceptible to overfitting, which in turn yields unstable decision models that are difficult to interpret or trust2.

Feature selection (FS) has emerged as an indispensable pre-processing technique to mitigate these risks. The core objective of FS is to identify a minimal, compact subset of the most salient predictors from the original feature space. An effective FS process can preserve or even improve the predictive performance of a model by eliminating redundant and irrelevant information. More critically, particularly for medical decision support, it vastly improves model transparency and interpretability. A model that relies on five key biomarkers is far more interpretable and actionable for a clinician than a ”black box” model that relies on five hundred. Furthermore, FS significantly reduces the potential costs of data acquisition, as future patients may only need to undergo a few specific tests rather than an exhaustive and expensive panel3.

Given that the search for an optimal feature subset is an NP-hard combinatorial problem, metaheuristic search algorithms have become a practical and highly effective engine for wrapper-based feature selection. In the wrapper method, the ”fitness” or quality of a candidate subset is assessed by the cross-validated performance of a downstream classifier, directly optimizing for the model’s predictive power4. Among the many nature-inspired metaheuristics, the Harris Hawks Optimizer (HHO) has garnered significant attention due to its elegant, nature-inspired design and its well-regarded balance between the exploratory (searching broadly) and exploitative (refining locally) phases of a search5.

Despite its strengths, the HHO is not without its limitations6. Like many metaheuristics, it can suffer from stagnation in deceptive, multi-modal regions of the search space7,8, converge prematurely to a locally optimal solution9, or expend a large portion of its search budget refining a cluster of similar, suboptimal candidate subsets10,11. These issues become particularly acute in the wrapper FS domain12, where the evaluation budget is severely constrained. Each fitness evaluation requires training and cross-validating a machine learning model13, a process that is orders of magnitude more computationally expensive than evaluating a simple mathematical function. Therefore, any stagnation or wasted iteration is highly detrimental, potentially terminating the search long before a high-quality, sparse solution is found14,15.

To address these documented limitations, this study proposes a Modified Harris Hawks Optimizer (MHHO) tailored to the specific demands of wrapper-based feature selection. The proposed framework augments the standard HHO with three novel, synergistic mechanisms, each designed to remedy a specific failure mode. First mechanism is the Leader-Guided Perching (LGP), this mechanism enhances the global exploration phase, injecting structured, bounded perturbations around the population mean and the current leader. This modification helps the optimizer to escape deceptive basins early in the search. Second mechanism is the Adaptive Deception Factor (ADF), this mechanism introduces a dynamic feedback loop into the exploitation phase. It monitors the search for stagnation and, when detected, amplifies the diversification strategy (via Lévy flights) to propel the hawks out of the local optimum. Subsequent mechanism is the Hierarchical Attack Strategy (HAS) which is instructor-directed attack stage to complete ”hard besiege” phase. It pushes not each hawk just into its single best solution but to a lineage of higher performing peers, speeding convergence towards promising regions while avoiding premature collapse of the diversity in the population. Combined, we give the MHHO a stronger and more efficient search, searching in order locally at the late stages while retaining some ability to both diversify and escape traps when needed.

In order to confirm the performance of the proposed MHHO, this paper carries out a two-part empirical study. Experiment Series 1, is a theoretical validation by comparing main optimizer’s performance with the original HHO on 23 standard benchmark functions. This provides us with a way to verify its basic benefits in a formal, mathematical setting. Then, in Experiment Series 2 we evaluates its practical usefulness in the application domain for which it has been designed: wrapper-based FS. In the current study, we combine MHHO with an SVM classifier along with a stratified 5-fold cross-validation within the fitness function to provide robustness. This wrapper is subsequently applied to fifteen different public medical datasets.

In 50 independent runs in each dataset, this paper presents a detailed set of measures, such as accuracy, macro-precision, macro-recall, count of the selected features (sparsity), and the number of computational time. The overarching thesis behind this work is that the suggested changes will enable MHHO to be able to find solutions that are extremely sparse, which is, in many instances, picking very few key features, and, at the same time, provide the classification accuracy over and above the standard HHO Baseline HHO. In addition to final scores, this paper investigate the plant behavior of optimization through convergence curves (accuracy vs. iteration) and examine the consistency of the identified subsets across runs, which is a paramount aspect to clinical interpretability and subsequent validation.

Literature review

The recent studies on the Harris Hawks Optimization (HHO) algorithm has largely involved the correction of its inherent drawbacks like premature convergence and sensitivity to parameters through the improvement of its exploration-exploitation character and convergence stability. These contributions can be organized into four major streams: (i) chaotic and opposition based mathematical contributions, (ii) adaptive and dynamic control contributions, (iii) multi-strategy and ensemble contributions, and (iv) hybrid integrations with other external algorithms or models. All research streams address particular gaps in the fundamental HHO, but, as this review will show, much is left in making a form of framework that is really adaptive and unified.

Beyond methodological advances, metaheuristic optimization algorithms have become indispensable tools in a wide range of practical application domains characterized by high dimensionality, nonlinearity, and expensive objective evaluations. In medical and biomedical analytics, such algorithms are extensively used for wrapper-based feature selection, biomarker discovery, and hyperparameter optimization, where the dual objectives of predictive accuracy and interpretability are critical16,17. In engineering and energy systems, metaheuristics are routinely applied to parameter estimation, system identification, and design optimization problems, such as photovoltaic model calibration and control, where analytical solutions are often infeasible18–20.

These real-world scenarios expose inherent limitations of conventional optimization approaches, including premature convergence, stagnation in deceptive landscapes, and sensitivity to control parameters—particularly when fitness evaluations involve costly simulations or repeated learning procedures18,21. Consequently, improving the exploration—exploitation balance and convergence robustness of population-based optimizers such as HHO is not only of theoretical interest, but also of direct practical significance. This motivates the continued development of adaptive, multi-strategy optimization frameworks that can robustly operate across diverse application domains22,23.

Chaotic and opposition-based mathematical modifications

A substantial literature is aimed at enhancing the diversity of global search by HHO, adding chaotic mapping and Opposition-Based Learning (OBL) strategies. Indicatively, a cubic chaotic map was proposed in the study of population initialization in the paper by24 iwith a nonlinear exponential energy functional to provide a more gradual exploration-exploitation changeovers. Likewise, CHHO25 and EHHO26 have used logistic and Cauchy-based chaos during the initialisation and step-size control to avoid pre-mature saturation. The IHHO variant Wittig27 further combined chaotic beta -mapping with OBL to speed up the search process of the global optimum.

Although these changes have been effective in enhancing diversity, they usually provide new limitations. They are always sensitive to chaos-like parameters (e.g. intensity, control constants) and are always time-consuming. In addition, self-adaptive chaos regulation and formal convergence studies are not normally given in these studies, and their theoretical consistency remains unproven.

Adaptive and dynamic control mechanisms

There are a number of improvements to update rules of HHO with dynamic parameter self-adjusting during the iteration. An example is the adaptive learning rate and velocity-controlled updates used by in the article of28 and the cosine-decaying step-length factor, placed in the article of29 to enhance the balance between diversification and intensification. Meanwhile, Dynamic Random Walk of EHHO-based strategies30 perturb stagnated hawks based on knowledge of adverse adaptation, and reverse-learned adaptive weights are used as guidance in other papers31 to control the convergence direction of the hawks.

Though these methods show an enhancement in convergence smoothness and robustness, they are to a great extent empirical. They frequently require manual tuning of decay coefficients and learning parameters, and more importantly, many of them usually do not have feedback-based or self-adaptive adjustment schemes. Such dependence on pre-defined dynamics may result in deterioration of performance when such dynamics are used in high-dimensional or complex problem landscapes.

Multi-strategy and ensemble enhancements

A newer research trend is the concomitant incorporation of a set of mathematical schemes to increase robustness of the search and stability. A case in point is a study conducted by32 iwhich combined circle chaotic mapping, nonlinear escape energy, AVOA-inspired position updates and adaptive weight control. Others, such as the Quantum-Enhanced HHO (QEHHO) have utilized quantum-behaved initialization, sinuoidal transition energy, slime mold exploration and quasi-oppositional learning, among others33. To also diversify the movement around elite solutions,34 and32 injected Brownian motion and wormhole strategies, respectively, and in35 multi-neighborhood local search operators were used to exploit fine-grainedly.

Although these complex variants can achieve state-of-the-art exploration performance, they often suffer from high computational complexity and problem-dependent parameterization. A significant drawback is the frequent absence of adaptive coordination among the various embedded strategies, which can cause oscillatory convergence in multimodal spaces and obscure the contribution of each individual component.

Hybrid integrations with external algorithms or models

Beyond internal modifications, numerous studies have hybridized HHO with other optimization or machine learning frameworks. The IHHO36, for instance, embedded genetic-inspired crossover and mutation operators to maintain population diversity, while EHHOCBO37 formed a full hybrid by explicitly integrating Coot Bird Optimization and Differential Evolution–based mutation. This direction is the combination of SMA and DE-inspired corrections in QEHHO33 and the combination of PSO-based updates with tanh energy modulation38.

Although the hybridization significantly increases the convergence and global accuracy, the hybridization is associated with a significant increase in both the complexity of algorithms and hyperparameters. This combination can make the theoretical edges of the HHO intrinsic behaviour almost indistinct and the resulting models are often application-oriented (e.g. optimised to detect DDoS attacks or to segment an image) and cannot be applied to wider areas of optimization.

Recent studies have increasingly focused on designing advanced optimization frameworks that integrate adaptive control, local refinement strategies, and physics- or theory-inspired mechanisms to improve robustness on complex landscapes. For instance,18 introduces a quantum duality-driven search process that balances probabilistic exploration and deterministic exploitation, demonstrating strong performance on CEC benchmark suites and real engineering problems. Other work by19 has emphasized hybrid local search integration, such as MAGBO, which combines a quasi-Newton-inspired gradient search rule with crossover and refresh operators for photovoltaic parameter estimation, achieving superior accuracy on CEC2021 functions. Similarly, improved Moth-Flame and Particle Swarm variants have incorporated local escape operators, quadratic interpolation, and adaptive exploitation strategies to mitigate premature convergence and enhance solution precision in highly multimodal optimization tasks in20. Beyond engineering domains, recent biomedical applications as16 have also adopted optimized metaheuristic frameworks, such as iPSOgs, which integrates adaptive search refinement and feature selection for lung cancer subtype classification, achieving both high predictive accuracy and strong interpretability. These recent advances highlight a clear trend toward multi-strategy, adaptive optimization frameworks that dynamically coordinate exploration, exploitation, and local refinement—motivating the design of MHHO as a unified and internally consistent enhancement of the Harris Hawks Optimization paradigm.

Recent advances in metaheuristic optimization have increasingly emphasized adaptive learning, information sharing, and multi-strategy coordination to improve robustness on complex and high-dimensional landscapes. In the context of Harris Hawks Optimization, variable neighborhood learning and information exchange mechanisms have been introduced to enhance exploration–exploitation balance and mitigate premature convergence, demonstrating strong performance in gene expression feature selection and engineering optimization problems39,40. Beyond HHO-specific improvements, learning-driven optimization frameworks inspired by human social behavior have gained prominence. The Learning Search Algorithm (LSA) integrates historical experience, social learning, and adaptive control to achieve competitive performance across large-scale CEC benchmarks, real-world engineering problems, and feature selection tasks41. Building upon this paradigm, adaptive learning search algorithms combining statistical filtering with swarm-based optimization have shown remarkable effectiveness in cancer classification and biomarker discovery from microarray data42, achieving high accuracy and strong stability across multiple datasets43. Furthermore, heterogeneous dimensional learning strategies guided by interaction effects have recently been proposed to enhance population diversity and convergence speed, yielding state-of-the-art results on CEC 2017–2022 benchmark suites as well as biomedical gene selection problems44. Collectively, these studies reflect a clear trend toward adaptive, learning-oriented, and information-rich optimization frameworks, which directly motivates the unified and feedback-driven design of the proposed MHHO algorithm.

An overview of these research streams brings about two patterns. First, non-hybrid modifications (chaotic, adaptive and ensemble) enhance performance but mostly are based on fixed coefficients or fixed nonlinear equations without self-regulation. Second, hybridization provides a better accuracy at the cost of simplicity, interpretability and computational efficiency. Although improvements have been proliferated, an integrated adaptive approach that dynamically integrates exploration and exploitation through a wide range of optimization steps has not been identified.

Research gap and motivation

Critical review of the available literature suggests that there are generally two types of HHO variants, (i) adding global exploration using chaotic systems or external hybrid strategies, and (ii) adding local exploitation using adaptive parameters or neighborhood operators. One of the notable gaps though is the lack of frameworks that reach a multi-strategy integration that are driven by actual adaptive control.

Most of the existing algorithms are limited to fixed scheduling of strategies and they also rely on manually tuned hyperparameters, which restricts adaptability. Importantly, they frequently do not have the adaptation mechanisms of the feedback response needed to balance the search dynamics in real-time during the traversal of the optimization landscape. In addition, convergence of theory ensures and rigorous scalability concerns are conspicuously missing in most of such empirical additions.

Thus, there is still a unique research gap about a multi-strategy adaptive, HHO framework. This framework should be in a position to dynamically coordinate mechanisms of initializing, controlling transitions and local exploitation. This requires going beyond the use of a static control and achieving high convergence, low sensitivity to parameters and enhanced generalization in high dimensional and complex global optimization problems.

Due to these shortcomings, this paper proposes a cohesive, adaptive, and socially-guided adaptation of the Harris Hawks Optimization algorithm by the way of three internally consistent and synergistic strategies, namely Leader-Guided Perching (LGP), Adaptive Deception Factor (ADF), and the Hierarchical Attack Strategy (HAS). Compared to the previous versions, which rely on unchanging variables, communication with random agents, or incorporation of external hybrid elements, the presented framework builds a cooperative search mechanism based on feedback. This design is an adaptive balance on exploration-exploitation trade-off during the search. The following empirical verification of 15 benchmark functions and real world medical problems is aimed at the purpose of verifying the high convergence speed, solution accuracy, and robustness of this new method, thus justifying the effectiveness of the new approach as a general purpose optimizer.

Background for HHO and it modifications

The presented population-based, nature-inspired metaheuristic is known as Harris Hawks Optimization (HHO) proposed by5, and it imitates the cooperative hunting behavior and surprise pounce strategy of Harris hawks. The structure of the algorithm captures the dynamic process between the hawks (search agents) and the prey (the candidate solution) and builds upon a synergy between disparate processes of exploration and exploitation that are elicited by the observed evasive action of the prey.

The main HHO framework has three major stages that act in a dynamic balance of the search activity of the algorithm: Exploration phase: in the event the prey is seen to be distant, the hawks (search agents) will spread their search. The use of HHO has already gained a lot of popularity because of its simplicity in concept, minimal parameter sensitivity, fast convergence, and natural adaptation to dynamics (Lahmar et al.17; Mao et al.46). The adaptive escape energy E offers a powerful system of exploration and exploitation balancing by allowing the algorithm to solve high dimensional, multimodal, and nonlinear challenges (Heidari et al.5).

Regardless of these merits, the traditional HHO model has a number of limitations that have been thoroughly discussed. These are high sensitivity to initial conditions and parameter tuning, the tendency toward reduction of the population diversity in later iterations, and, thus, high susceptibility to premature convergence, especially in the complex or misleading landscapes (Issa et al.47; Lahmar et al.17; Mao et al.46). Moreover, it tends to perform poorly when applied to problem spaces which are binary or discrete.

Such restrictions have led to a large amount of research aimed at improving the basic HHO, as presented in Table 1. Numerous attempts have made mathematical modifications to the fundamental algorithm. As an example, the seeding methods that have been reported to increase the performance are dynamic and nonlinear, like the ones demonstrated by Lv et al.48. The other adaptations focus on the position update equations and initializations that include techniques such as the randomized perturbations by using the Gaussian or Levy distributions, elite retention schemes and adaptive weights. Wang and Wei (2025) suggested an extension to enhance the exploration-exploitation ratio that included elite memory and chaotic feedback. Other forms have added new update rules, like the Cardano-based step update and nonlinear energy degradation functions that Ouyang et al. suggest to achieve accuracy and stability in complicated engineering issues, as suggested by Ouyang et al.49.

In spite of such developments, there are still major challenges. The main problem is that most of the improved HHO versions are tested on benchmark test suites with little to no tests in realistic and complex environments. In addition, addition of many dynamic components, though they may enhance performance on a particular task, may actually make the algorithm more susceptible to parameter tuning and thus decrease its overall applicability and reproducibility. Although these studies prove that the effectiveness of HHO can be increased significantly with the help of mathematical and structural changes, there is still a gap. Proposed model

In order to counter-act the possible drawbacks of the standard Harris Hawks Optimization (HHO) algorithm, especially its pre-mature convergence nature and its stagnant search dynamics, this study presents three new, complementary changes. They are called Leader-Guided Perching (LGP), Adaptive Deception Factor (ADF), and Hierarchical Attack Strategy (HAS) in the purpose of establishing a more intelligent and strong optimization process. LGP strategy enhances the exploration step with the help of direction of the optimal solution. The ADF mechanism presents the strategic balance between intensification and diversification as a dynamic feedback loop is added to the progressive dive strategies. Lastly, the HAS converts the exploitation step in the algorithm which is the most intense, the hard besiege, into an effective convergence that is greedy into a cooperative, socially-directed attack which avoids swarm clumping and improves local search accuracy.

Leader-guided perching strategy (LGP)

The initial change that has been suggested is Leader-Guided Perching (LGP) and is aimed at a critical inefficiency that is found during the typical HHO exploration phase. The initial exploration stage is aimed at facilitating the global search achievement of the swarm through the application of two different perching options with a random probability, q. Nevertheless, there is a fundamental weakness of the first strategy (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$q \ge 0.5$$\end{document}) depending on the directional guidance of a randomly chosen hawk, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_{rand}(t)$$\end{document}, for directional guidance. This process is a form of blind search in which a low-fitness X rand(t) may lead the hawk being searched to a dead end of the search space. This can, in turn, lead to wasted computational effort and a slower convergence rate. This subsection provides a detailed analysis of this limitation and introduces the LGP strategy, which rectifies this inefficiency by embedding leadership from the best-so-far solution (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_{rabbit}$$\end{document}) into the perching process.

Identification of a core limitation

A critical analysis of the exploration phase reveals a potential inefficiency within the first strategy (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$q \ge 0.5$$\end{document}). Proposed modification: LGP strategy

In the standard HHO, the exploration phase contains a mechanism where a hawk perches based on the position of another randomly selected hawk from the population, which formulated as in Eq. 1. While this promotes diversity, it represents a “blind” search strategy. This study offers the LGP strategy in order to correct this. This adjustment incorporates the concept of leadership into the perching mechanism since the movement of the hawk is relative to the leader at hand (the rabbit) rather than a random colleague. The suggested equation will be written down as Eq. 3.3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} X(t+1) = X_{rand}(t) - r_1|X_{rabbit}(t) - 2r_2X(t)| \end{aligned}$$\end{document}This modification represents a deliberate strategic trade-off. This strategy is highly effective at reducing wasted computational effort that the standard HHO spends exploring low-fitness, unpromising areas of the search space.

However, this increased convergence pressure introduces a well-known risk: if the current leader (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_{rabbit}$$\end{document}) is a deceptive local optimum, LGP could cause the swarm to converge prematurely to that suboptimal region. This risk is especially high on the complex, multimodal problems for which HHO is often used.

This risk is the central motivation for the synergistic design of MHHO. The LGP is not intended to function in isolation. Its risk of premature convergence is directly and dynamically counteracted by the Adaptive Deception Factor (ADF) (see “Adaptive exploitation via the adaptive deception factor (ADF) strategy”). This synergy creates a powerful two-stage search dynamic: LGP’s role is to find a promising hill in a short time (fast convergence).ADF’s role is to give the adaptive, ”explosive” Levy flight mechanism to jump off that hill should the algorithm decide that it has stagnated (i.e. it is a local optimum).This enables MHHO to be not only quick and efficient (with the help of LGP) but also strong enough to avoid traps, which is a big advantage of the prospective application of one strategy.

Adaptive exploitation via the adaptive deception factor (ADF) strategy

The second modification, the Adaptive Deception Factor Strategy (ADF), deals with one of the most inherent weaknesses of the most advanced exploitation strategies of HHO. In the case when the algorithm is trained to think of progressive rapid dives (when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r < 0.5$$\end{document}) it uses Lévy flight (LF) patterns to replicate the deceptive movements of the prey, and the intricate attack patterns of the hawks. The weight of this plunge is scaled by a scaling factor S. Here, this S factor in the typical HHO is a homogeneous random vector and thus its response is constant and non-adaptive. This insensitivity to the search history is a serious two-fold problem, the dives can be too shy to eject a hawk out of a deep local optimum (stagnation), or, conversely, they can be too coarse and erratic to allow fine-tuning convergence when the swarm is close to the true optimum (overshooting). In this sub section, it presents an innovative mechanism called the ADF that aims at establishing a dynamic feedback loop that can update the strength of these dives of the Lévy flight according to the performance of the algorithm in real time.

Analysis of the HHO exploitation phase

The exploitation phase of HHO, triggered when the prey’s escaping energy \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$|E| < 1$$\end{document}, models the cooperative attack of the hawk swarm. This phase is divided into four strategies based on the value of |E| and a random number r. The most sophisticated of these strategies are the ”soft besiege with progressive rapid dives” (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$|E| \ge 0.5, r < 0.5$$\end{document}) and ”hard besiege with progressive rapid dives” (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$|E|< 0.5, r < 0.5$$\end{document}).

In these scenarios, the hawks perform a series of complex, Levy flight (LF) based movements to surprise and encircle the prey. The algorithm first proposes a potential next move, Y, based on the rabbit’s position. It then evaluates this move and, if it does not yield an improvement, calculates an alternative, more explorative move, Z, using a Levy flight pattern. Identification of a core limitation

The primary limitation within this advanced exploitation mechanism lies in the scaling factor S in Eq. 4. This factor, representing the ”surprise” or intensity of the hawk’s dive, is a uniformly random vector. Its behavior is therefore static and non-adaptive; it has no memory of the search history and does not respond to the current state of the optimization process. Such inflexibility has two significant issues: item failure to escape local optima: when swarm is stuck in a deep local optimum, the random scaled Levy flights might not be strong or disruptive enough to move a hawk out of the basin of attraction. The search is hampered by timidity of the dives.item lack of precision: on the other hand, when the swarm has already located the area of the global optimum, and must now perform a local fine-tuning search, the random dives can be excessively large and unpredictable. This may make the hawks continuously fail to reach the actual optimal point, which prevents the ultimate convergence and accuracy of the solution.

Justification

During the exploitation phase, the standard HHO employs Levy flights (LF) to simulate the prey’s deceptive motions and the hawks’ rapid dives. The update rule for these dives is given in Eq. 4. where S is a random vector of size \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1 \times D$$\end{document}. The key limitation here is that the scaling of the Levy flight is uniformly random and non-adaptive. It does not respond to the state of the search. If the algorithm is trapped in a deep local optimum, these random dives may lack the necessary force to escape. Conversely, as the algorithm nears the global optimum, the dives may be too erratic, overshooting the target and hindering fine-tuned convergence. To overcome this, this study introduces the ADF strategy. This process creates a type of a feedback loop adjusting the magnitude of the Levy flight in a dynamically-adjusting manner according to the performance of the algorithm. The update rule is modified to look as in Eq. 5. The value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {F}_{\text {ADF}}$$\end{document} is updated each iteration based on a stagnation counter.

If the algorithm fails to find an improved solution for a predefined number of iterations, it is considered to be stagnating. In this case, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {F}_{\text {ADF}}$$\end{document} is increased (e.g., doubled) to produce larger, more aggressive dives, helping the algorithm escape the local minimum. On the other hand, a more promising solution is discovered, downwardly adjusting the value of the fitness of the search methodology, denoted as the decreasing value of the variable \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {F}_{\text {ADF}}$$\end{document}, by roughly half, and, at that point, more finer and precise movements are made to strengthen the search around the new promising solution. Such self-adaptive behavior forms a stronger exploitation stage. The ADF, together with LGP, enables the algorithm to vigorously solve local optimality when needed and then carefully optimize the quality solutions already found in the intelligent exploration stage resulting in a substantial overall improvement in performance.

Theoretical boundedness and stability of the ADF

Although the Adaptive Deception Factor (ADF) is primarily motivated by empirical observations, its adaptive behavior is explicitly bounded to ensure numerical stability and controlled search dynamics. In the proposed MHHO framework, the value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{ADF}$$\end{document} is constrained within predefined lower and upper limits, i.e.,\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{ADF} \in [F_{\min }, F_{\max }],$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{\min } > 0$$\end{document} prevents the Lévy flight step from vanishing, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{\max }$$\end{document} limits excessively large jumps that could destabilize the exploitation phase. In practice, these bounds are implemented to guarantee that the magnitude of the Lévy-based dives remains proportional to the scale of the search space.

The update mechanism of ADF follows a regulated multiplicative adjustment driven by a stagnation counter. When no improvement in the global best solution is observed for a predefined number of iterations, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{ADF}$$\end{document} is increased (e.g., doubled) to enhance exploration. Conversely, upon fitness improvement, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{ADF}$$\end{document} is reduced (e.g., halved), promoting refined local exploitation. Due to the enforced bounds, repeated doubling or halving cannot cause unbounded growth or collapse of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{ADF}$$\end{document}.

From a stability perspective, this bounded feedback loop forms a piecewise adaptive control process that alternates between intensified exploration and precision-oriented exploitation. Under the assumption of a bounded search space and finite Lévy flight moments, the resulting position updates remain bounded. Consequently, the best-so-far fitness value generated by MHHO forms a non-increasing sequence over iterations, indicating stable convergence behavior. This bounded adaptivity distinguishes ADF from purely random scaling mechanisms and enhances robustness against stagnation without compromising convergence stability.

Hierarchical attack strategy (HAS) for enhanced exploitation

The third and last change is the Hierarchy Attack Strategy (HAS), which is presented in response to the restriction that was found during the most aggressive exploitation stage of the HHO algorithm the Hard Besiege. This stage is implemented when the prey is believed to be exhausted (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$|E| < 0.5$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r > 0.5$$\end{document}) and is usually a straightforward, gluttonous attack of every hawk right to the best-known solution, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_{rabbit}$$\end{document}. The main weakness of this social strategy is that there is no interaction between harpers and all the hawks are a single agent, not thinking about the positions or winning strategies of the other agents. When this convergence is independent it can tend to spawn a swarm clump, such that the rest of the population quickly converges on the target point. Should this location be a local optimum, this stagnation in diversity of attack angles makes local search by a swarm very inefficient and prevents the swarm to search the immediate environment to finding a solution that is marginally superior. This subsection details the HAS, a novel mechanism that replaces this individualistic attack with a cooperative, multi-pronged assault by embedding social learning from superior ”mentor” hawks into the Hard Besiege phase.

Synergistic justification for the combined modifications (LGP + ADF + HAS)

Although all of the suggested LGP, ADF and HAS strategies - have their own advantages, they enable one another, which means that their potential is achieved. The combination of them converts the conventional HHO of a chain of isolated, fixed, but uninterconnected strategies into an optimistic and wholistic structure of optimization in which each stage optimizes the performance of the succession. The outcome is a stronger algorithm that will have a better trade-off between global exploitation and local exploitation. The intelligent workflow can be viewed as a three stage intelligent hunt:

Efficient scouting via LGP

The optimization process begins with the LGP-enhanced exploration phase. As discussed in “Proposed modification: LGP strategy”, LGP acts as an efficient scouting mechanism by introducing a mild exploitative bias to guide the swarm toward the current leader (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_{rabbit}$$\end{document}) and avoid wasted iterations in unpromising regions.

The LGP module on its own provides a significant statistical improvement over the Baseline HHO, confirming its efficacy in finding promising regions faster. This synergy is critical, as LGP’s efficiency is complemented by the ADF’s robustness, which provides the necessary mechanism to escape the local optima that LGP may quickly converge to.

Adaptive attack via ADF

When the swarm has been directed into a good area by the LGP, the ADF enhanced phase of exploitation takes over. This step serves as a responsive weapon of attack. In the event that the hotspot located by LGP is actually a misleading local optimum, the feedback mechanism of the ADF will automatically enhance the strength of the Levy flights that will then allow the hawks to fiercely jump out of the trap. On the other hand, the closer the hawks are to the true optimum, the weaker the ADF is, and it becomes possible to narrow down the search to a fine-tuned one. The ADF will guarantee that quality areas that LGP identifies are traversed efficiently, no matter the complexity.

Cooperative finishing via HAS

the last is the hard besiege which is now governed by the HAS. However, once LGP has located the correct area and ADF has tried to circumvent its misleading capabilities, the swarm is in a position that is much near the target. HAS avoids a naive and greedy convergence which may fail to find the exact optimum. Rather, it makes a collaborative final strike. After fostering the learning of the superior hawks, HAS staged a multi-pronged, socially-directed ultimate attack. This carefully scrubs the target area in a variety of directions and directions, eliminating the chance of swarm clumping as well as the possibility of locating the global optimum as precisely as possible.

Overall, the three changes form a vicious circle wherein LGP discovers more advantageous areas, ADF furnishes the means to handle the complexity inherent in those areas, and HAS ensures that the resulting attack is not redundant but cooperative and accurate. This multi-layered improvement results in a strong synergy which causes convergence to be accelerated, more resilient to early stagnation and increases the probability of high-quality final solutions being found than the typical HHO or implementations with only partial modifications.

Computational complexity analysis

The computational complexity of MHHO is primarily determined by the population size N, the maximum number of iterations T, the problem dimensionality d, and the cost of evaluating the fitness function. At each iteration, MHHO updates the positions of all hawks and evaluates their fitness values once.

The position update operations in MHHO—including LGP-based guidance, ADF-controlled Lévy flights, and HAS exploitation steps—are linear with respect to the problem dimension d. Consequently, the algorithmic update cost per iteration is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {O}(N \cdot d)$$\end{document}. Since the proposed mechanisms introduce only constant-time control logic per hawk, the asymptotic update complexity remains identical to that of standard HHO.

In wrapper-based feature selection, the dominant computational cost arises from fitness evaluation, which involves training and validating a classifier. Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_f$$\end{document} denote the cost of a single fitness evaluation (e.g., cross-validated SVM training). The overall time complexity of MHHO can therefore be expressed as:9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {O}(T \cdot N \cdot C_f). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}This complexity order matches that of the standard HHO algorithm, with MHHO incurring only a modest constant-factor overhead due to adaptive control and hierarchical exploitation mechanisms. This explains the slightly increased runtime observed in some datasets while preserving scalability and practical feasibility.

In summary, MHHO retains the same asymptotic computational complexity as standard HHO while offering improved convergence behavior and solution quality, particularly in complex and high-dimensional optimization scenarios such as wrapper-based feature selection.

Performance results analysis and evaluation

This section contains a critical analysis of the empirical findings of the comparison between the proposed modified algorithm (MHHO) and the original HHO. The experimental model is extensive and it has a variety of 23 standard benchmark functions that are built to evaluate different aspects of optimization performance. This testbed is strategically partitioned into three categories: unimodal functions (F1–F7), which are supposed to measure the speed of exploration and convergence; multimodal functions (F8–F13), which are meant to test the exploratory capabilities and avoidance of local optima of an algorithm; and fixed-dimension multimodal functions (F14–F23), which are focused on measuring performance on complex, often constrained, problem landscapes.

The only metrics of evaluation introduced is Fitness, and the results of the statistics appear to be the best, the worst, the average (avg), and the standard deviation (std) of the fitness results of several independent runs as shown in Table 7. This will be done through a deep and functional comparison in the form of functions by functions first and then cross functional patterns and theoretical explanation of the findings will be made to rigorously establish the effectiveness and originality of the MHHO framework.

An in depth granular analysis of each test service indicates that there is a subtle yet evident performance difference between MHHO and HHO. MHHO portrays significant and statistically significant enhancement in majority of functions. An example is that on F1, MHHO attains an average fitness of 2.25E-36, which is more than 14 orders of magnitude better than the achievement of HHO of 2.81E-22. This indicates that the modifications made to MHHO make it much faster in convergence and exploitation accuracy when on simple, convex landscapes. The performance of the two algorithms is extremely good on F8, F9, F10 and F11 functions, both algorithms always finding the global optimum (e.g. average fitness of 0.00E+00 in F9, and F11). This indicates that the initial HHO is already extremely competent on these particular multimodal issues and modifications in the MHHO do not deteriorate the performance.

MHHO shows a pronounced and evident superiority on multimodal functions of F12 and F13 more complicated. MHHO has an average fitness of 4.01E-07 on F12, which is a significant improvement on the HHO average rank of 3.80E-04. As well, on F13, the fitness of MHHO is 1.42E-04 on average versus 5.63E-03 of HH. However, this is a good sign that the changes increase the capacity of the algorithm to search the complicated landscapes with a lot of local optima.

In F14–F23 functions whole family of complex, fixed-dimension, problems, MHHO either shows statistically identical performance or slightly higher than HHO. An example is that on F15, MHHO has an average fitness of 3.22E-04 versus 4.92E-04 of HHO which is significant, yet unspectacular. On functions such as F16 and F18, the two algorithms are equal. It means that in the context of these highly specific problem structures, the changes in MHHO have a steady, even though small, benefit or, more generally, no harm.

A comparison of the outcomes of the individual results shows evident and strong trends in the profile of the performance of MHHO. The largest performance improvements are witnessed in the unimodal functions (F1, F3, F4) and in the multimodal functions (F8, F9, F10). This effectively indicates that the changes in MHHO cause a significant beneficial effect on its exploitation capacities and its capacity to conduct an accurate local search to narrow on high-quality solutions.

In addition, MHHO demonstrates comparative performance. In 22 of the 23 test functions, it does better than or is statistically equal to the initial HHO. This great degree of consistency shows that the suggested changes are not volatile and can be effectively implemented into practice regarding a large number of types of problems without a major threat of negative performance.

In addition to this, MHHO perfectly matches the performance of the original HHO in the standard multimodal functions in which HHO is already a top performer (F8–F11). Computational time analysis across test functions

In order to determine the computational efficiency of the proposed modified MHHO, the time spent in the execution was compared with the HHO. The experiment was used to measure the amount of seconds that each algorithm took to run through the optimization process on 23 standard benchmark functions. The detailed results of this comparison are presented in the data Fig. 8.

A general pattern that is common to all the test functions is that MHHO algorithm has a greater computational time as compared to original HHO. It is found that MHHO took a longer time to reach the final solution on every of the functions F1 to F23 as seen by the data. This growth is a natural result of the number of computational processes that have been incorporated into the MHHO framework (LGP, ADF, and HAS strategies) that is suggested to augment its exploratory and exploitative capabilities and avoid premature convergence.Fig. 8Comparison of HHO and MHHO computation time.

Comparison of HHO and MHHO computation time.

Quantitatively, the suggested MHHO algorithm is on average about 44.4% slower than the original HHO on the collection of benchmark functions that are tested. Although this is a significant increase in the computational load, it is a planned sacrifice. The extra time is spent in the improved search mechanisms which are the cause of the high performance of MHHO in terms of the solution accuracy and convergence reliability. Thus, the higher-cost of execution time is justified by the high quality of the optimization that the MHHO algorithm has obtained.

To ensure a fair and meaningful comparison of computational cost, both the standard HHO and the proposed MHHO were executed using identical population sizes, iteration limits, classifiers, and cross-validation protocols across all experiments. Consequently, the total number of fitness evaluations is the same for both algorithms on each dataset. Since wrapper-based feature selection is dominated by the cost of fitness evaluation (i.e., repeated classifier training and validation), the reported wall-clock runtimes directly reflect algorithmic overhead rather than differences in evaluation budgets. Therefore, runtime comparisons between HHO and MHHO are inherently normalized with respect to the number of fitness evaluations.

Experiment series 2-performance evaluation of MHHO on real medical problems

This second series of experiments is to be aimed at rigorously testing the practical effectiveness and the generalizability of the proposed MHHO framework after thorough theoretical benchmark series of experiments in the first one. This paper leaves the abstract optimization landscapes of the world and enters the world of a challenging and high-stakes application domain: wrapper-based feature selection (FS) in medical diagnosis. The feature of this field is the severe necessity to consider the dual and sometimes conflicting goals of maximizing predictive accuracy and at the same time providing solution parsimony (sparsity). Determining small, high-informative subsets of features is most important to increase the model interpretability, cut down on data-acquisition expenses, and improve clinical usage.

The key aim of the experiment is to empirically confirm whether the empirical improvement in the exploration (LGP) and exploitation (ADF, HAS) mechanisms that gained superior performance in the theoretical benchmarks can be empirically confirmed in terms of their tangible benefits in the high-dimensional and real-life environment, heavily noisy biomedical data. In this section, the experimental protocol is outlined, the benchmark medical datasets used, specific evaluation measures, and a comprehensive comparative analysis of the performance of the MHHO to the original HHO and other state of the art methods is outlined.

Experiment series 2 setup

To test the suggested MHHO framework on the optimization problem in a real-world setting, the second series of experiments were performed based on wrapper-based feature selection (FS) to medical diagnosis. The design was intended to test the practical efficacy of the algorithm in a high stakes domain where model performance and model interpretability (i.e. sparsity) are of primary importance.

However, The experimental design was done using the modified MHHO algorithm that incorporated the wrap about the Adaptive Deception Factor (ADF), Mentor-Guided Hard Besiege (HAS) and Local-Group Perching (LGP) as a wrapper feature selection. A test of the algorithm was performed on the preprocessed medical datasets, and the challenge was to find the optimal set of features to use out of the features provided in order to classify the target label.

Evaluation metrics

The comprehensive evaluation of the proposed framework is conducted using two distinct categories of metrics. The first category assesses the predictive performance of the final clinical classification model, while the second measures the computational efficiency and parsimony of the feature selection process itself.

Classification performance metrics

The three basic classification measures: accuracy, precision and recall, are based on the elements of the confusion matrix: true positives (TP), true negatives (TN), false positives (FP), and false negatives (FN). These measures are needed to measure the effectiveness of the diagnostic system within the clinical environment. 11a\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text {Accuracy}= & \frac{\text {TP} + \text {TN}}{\text {TP} + \text {TN} + \text {FP} + \text {FN}} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}11b\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text {Precision}= & \frac{\text {TP}}{\text {TP} + \text {FP}} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}11c\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text {Recall}= & \frac{\text {TP}}{\text {TP} + \text {FN}} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

Accuracy (Eq. 11a) s the holistic measure, which is the ratio of all the correctly classified instances. Nonetheless, since most biomedical datasets are inherently class-imbalanced, Accuracy should not be the only metric to be used. Thus, Precision (Eq. 11b), or Positive Predictive Value is used to measure the reliability of the true predictions of the disease (i.e. the percentage of the examples declared positive that are actually positive). At the same time, Recall (Eq. 11c), or True Positive Rate, or Sensitivity, is essential as it determines how the model can detect all real positive cases. These three measures taken together can give a well-rounded and strong evaluation of the utility of the classifier to diagnostic systems where both high sensitivity (minimizing false negatives) and high reliability (minimizing false positives) are of the essence.

Classification performance and stability

The empirical data reveal that MHHO consistently achieves superior mean accuracy compared to the original HHO across all thirteen datasets. This improvement is substantial in several cases, such as Dermatology (0.9563 for MHHO vs. 0.9137 for HHO) and Thyroid (0.9589 vs. 0.9323), and more modest, though still consistent, on others (e.g., Cleveland: 0.7597 vs. 0.7590). Furthermore, the reduced standard deviation in the MHHO results for many datasets (e.g., Dermatology, Diagnostic, Heart) indicates a more stable and reliable convergence behavior, suggesting the adaptive mechanisms (ADF, HAS, LGP) successfully mitigate the stochastic volatility of the Baseline HHO algorithm.

Feature selection

A critical finding is that this enhancement in classification accuracy is achieved while simultaneously preserving, and in some cases enhancing, feature parsimony. The wrapper frequently converges to minimal feature subsets of 1–2 features on numerous datasets (e.g., Cleveland, Heart, SAHeart, Pima, Original), with a standard deviation at or near zero. This demonstrates high stability in identifying a minimal, effective subset across independent runs. Even on datasets with higher dimensionality and potential redundancy (e.g., Dermatology, Retinopathy, Lymphography), MHHO selects remarkably small subsets (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\approx$$\end{document}2–6 features), corroborating its strong regularization effect driven by the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha =0.9$$\end{document} sparsity pressure in the fitness function.

Overall, the empirical evidence is strong to support that the suggested MHHO framework has a better trade-off between the classification accuracy and the sparsity of the solution than the original HHO. It will always need more correct and stable sets of features, but at the price of a decent and reasonable increase in computational execution.

On the other hand, there are two conspicuous exceptions, Cleveland (81.40s vs. 104.91s) and Hepatitis (105.70s vs. 143.80s) where MHHO was significantly faster than the control. Statistical significance analysis

In order to strictly evaluate the differences in performance of the four HHO versions on the performance of the four versions in the measurement of “Accuracy”, it was necessary to statistically, non-parametrically assess the data obtained after 50 independent runs. A standard two stage process, common to multiple algorithm comparisons, was followed: (i) a Friedmans test was conducted to identify any global, significant difference between the four algorithms, and (ii) on finding a significant result, a test, the Holm was conducted to identify specifically which algorithmic pairs differed. A non-parametric test was Friedmans test, an equivalent of a repeated-measures ANOVA, which was used to identify statistically significant differences in the median accuracy of each of the four versions of the HHO algorithm, each of the 50 runs can be considered a block.

To rigorously assess the performance differences in “Accuracy” across the four HHO versions, a non-parametric statistical analysis was conducted on the results from 50 independent runs. A two-stage procedure, standard for multiple algorithm comparisons, was followed: (i) a Friedman’s test was used to detect any global, significant differences among the four algorithms, and (ii) upon finding a significant result, a Holm’s test was employed for post-hoc pairwise comparisons to identify precisely which algorithmic pairs differed.

In addition to solution quality, the practical usefulness of the proposed MHHO framework should also be evaluated in terms of its computational cost. Since MHHO incorporates three additional adaptive mechanisms, namely the leader-guided position update (LGP), adaptive disturbance factor (ADF), and hierarchical adjustment strategy (HAS), it naturally requires more processing than the original HHO. Therefore, the runtime results should be interpreted jointly with the accuracy results rather than in isolation.

Table 19 summarizes the trade-off between computational time and optimization performance. On the medical datasets, the average computation time increased from 369.21 s for HHO to 419.01 s for MHHO, corresponding to an average overhead of 13.49%. On the benchmark functions, MHHO was reported to be approximately 44.4% slower than the baseline HHO. Although this indicates additional computational cost, the performance gains are substantial: MHHO achieved statistically better performance on 18 out of 23 benchmark functions, statistically equivalent performance on the remaining five functions, and no statistically worse performance on any tested function.

These results indicate that the additional runtime of MHHO is associated with improved search quality and stronger convergence behavior rather than unnecessary computational burden. In other words, MHHO trades moderate extra runtime for higher optimization accuracy, improved robustness, and more reliable final solutions. Scalability and robustness analysis

The scalability and robustness of MHHO should be interpreted with respect to the empirical evidence provided in the benchmark and application experiments. In the present study, robustness refers to the consistency of the algorithm across repeated independent runs, as reflected in improved average performance, reduced variability on many benchmark functions, and favorable statistical comparison with the baseline HHO. In particular, MHHO achieved statistically better performance than HHO on 18 out of the 23 benchmark functions, while showing statistically equivalent performance on the remaining five functions and no statistically worse performance on any function. In addition, the standard deviation values and boxplot analysis (“Boxplots statistical analysis”) indicate that MHHO often yields more concentrated performance distributions, which suggests stable empirical behavior across runs.

With respect to scalability, the present results provide empirical support within the tested problem settings rather than a universal guarantee. The benchmark suite includes unimodal, high-dimensional multimodal, and fixed-dimension multimodal functions, while the application study considers medical feature-selection datasets of varying dimensionality. Across these settings, MHHO maintained competitive or improved performance relative to the baseline HHO, indicating that the proposed multi-strategy framework remains effective as problem complexity increases within the evaluated range. This behavior is consistent with the intended role of the three enhancement mechanisms: the leader-guided position update supports directed exploration, the adaptive disturbance factor helps diversify the search when stagnation occurs, and the hierarchical adjustment strategy improves local refinement near promising regions.

Accordingly, the present findings support the conclusion that MHHO exhibits improved empirical robustness and promising scalability within the tested benchmark family and wrapper-based feature-selection setting.

Discussion

The overall empirical analysis including the functions of theoretical benchmarks (Experiment Series 1—“Experiment series 1—performance evaluation of MHHO on benchmark functions”) and actual wrapper-based feature selection (Experiment Series 2—“Experiment series 2-performance evaluation of MHHO on real medical problems”) proves that the proposed Modified Harris Hawks Optimizer (MHHO) is a highly more robust and functional optimizer than its simple counterpart. Combining the Adaptive Deception Factor (ADF), Hierarchy Attack Strategy (HAS), and Leader-Guided Perching (LGP) results in the synergistic model, which achieves significant performance improvements in both areas. This discussion is a synthesis of the results of both experimental series to draw conclusions on the important observations and their consequences.

Experiment Series 1 clearly supported the usefulness of the particular mechanisms with the help of the theoretical validation. The framework was able to show a dramatic increase in convergence velocity and exploitation accuracy on unimodal functions (F1–F7). This performance is especially remarkable on the F5 (Rosenbrock) function a well-known difficult topography, having a slender, parabolic valley. One of the most important findings is that MHHO has a higher performance on F5 (obtaining an average fitness orders of magnitude higher than HHO) and this result supports the idea that MHHO is more able to carry out an accurate local search in challenging non-convex areas. This general enhancement on the case of the unimodal suite can be directly linked to the Hierarchical Attack Strategy (HAS) which essentially averts the phenomenon of swarm clumping that affects the exploitation stage of the standard HHO.

On multimodal complex landscapes (F8–F13), the framework showed a better tradeoff: this way, exploration was not detrimental, and it strongly performed on more deceitful landscapes (F8, F9, F10). This establishes the synergy of LGP, ADF, and HAS in giving the required robustness to get out of deep local optima. Moreover, its reliable performance (non-degrading) on fixed-dimension functions (F14–F23) made it reliable.

This theoretically-based superiority was directly applied in the applied feature selection tasks of Experiment Series 2. On the wide range of biomedical datasets, MHHO was always better classified than standard HHO with the same search budget. The convergence profiles of this second experiment were similar to those of the first experiment with increase in the initial-mid iterations and plateau of a higher accuracy. This shows that the mechanisms are behaving in the desired manner, and that LGP is beneficial in escaping flat basins early, HAS is favoring exploration with demonstrably superior mentors in order to minimise local wandering, and ADF is offering adaptive scaling to overcome stagnation.

In addition to the quantitative improvements reported, the recorded convergence curves offer qualitative evidence of how the proposed mechanisms influence the internal search dynamics of MHHO. Compared with standard HHO, MHHO generally demonstrates a steeper improvement in classification accuracy during the early and mid iterations, followed by a more stable convergence pattern in later stages. This behavior can be attributed to the complementary effects of the three introduced strategies. Leader-Guided Perching (LGP) provides directional guidance during the exploration phase, allowing the population to approach promising regions more efficiently while preserving sufficient diversity.

The Adaptive Deception Factor (ADF) regulates the magnitude of Lévy-flight steps based on stagnation feedback, enabling the algorithm to escape deceptive local optima when progress slows and to gradually refine solutions once improvement is detected. Furthermore, the Hierarchical Attack Strategy (HAS) enhances exploitation by introducing mentor-guided movements, which reduce swarm crowding and mitigate premature convergence. Collectively, these mechanisms result in smoother and more consistent convergence trajectories, explaining the reduced variance and improved accuracy–sparsity balance observed across both benchmark functions and real-world feature selection tasks.

Although MHHO integrates Leader-Guided Perching (LGP), Adaptive Deception Factor (ADF), and Hierarchical Attack Strategy (HAS) into a unified framework, their individual contributions can be conceptually distinguished based on the observed search dynamics across both experimental series. LGP primarily influences the early exploration phase by introducing controlled leader bias, which accelerates movement toward promising regions while preserving population diversity; in its absence, the standard HHO exhibits slower early convergence and higher sensitivity to random initialization. ADF plays a critical role during exploitation by adaptively scaling Lévy-flight step sizes in response to stagnation, thereby reducing prolonged entrapment in deceptive local optima and enabling smoother convergence. HAS mainly affects late-stage exploitation by replacing uniform hard besiege behavior with mentor-guided refinement, which mitigates swarm clumping and improves fine-grained local search accuracy.

The experimental results suggest that these mechanisms act synergistically rather than independently: removing any one component would degrade exploration efficiency (LGP), stagnation resistance (ADF), or exploitation precision (HAS). The consistently improved convergence stability, reduced variance in selected feature cardinality, and superior accuracy–sparsity trade-off achieved by MHHO indicate that each mechanism contributes meaningfully to the overall optimization performance.

One of the key conclusions of Experiment Series 2 is connected with the performance of the algorithm in the case of the aggressive sparsity constraint of the objective function. Equation 10 contained a large weighting factor (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha =0.9$$\end{document})) that directly and strongly discouraged the choice of features. Such formulation was done deliberately to drive the optimizer to the solution of extreme parsimony.

The difficulty was not, then, to achieve a small subset, but a spare subset, which would also maintain high classification accuracy. The outcomes show that MHHO was doing well in this particular challenging trade-off. Although the aggressive penalty caused both optimizers to minimize feature set choice, the more accurate exploitation algorithm (MHHO, through HAS) and the capability to leave suboptimal basins (MHHO, through ADF) led to it repeatedly finding minimal feature sets (frequently 1- 2 features) that also had high-accuracy solutions. This indicates that the standard HHO when confronted with the same aggressive penalty was more prone to being lured in suboptimal sparse solutions, inability to locate a set of features that were minimal and of high predictive quality.

This was irrespective of high-redundancy conditions (e.g., Dermatology, Lymphography), in which MHHO nonetheless reduced to small, useful subsets (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\approx$$\end{document}2–6 features). This capability to meet the goal of aggressive parsimony, but without compromising accuracy , is of vital clinical benefit, dramatically enhancing the interpretability of the model, and limiting the possible burden of data collection.

The cost of computation analysis presents a subtle trade-off. In Experiment Series 1, MHHO was indeed slower than HHO in all benchmark functions as was expected. This is a predicted result of the extra computational processes. In Experiment Series 2, however, though MHHO did pay a moderate premium in runtime on the majority of datasets (e.g., Dermatology, Pima), it was significantly faster on others (e.g., Cleveland, Hepatitis). This implies that the improved dynamics of guidance can bring the swarm to a high-quality basin faster on some practical high dimensional landscapes, compensating its computational cost. Since accuracy and sparsity gain of offline feature selection are significant, the extra time is a reasonable price to pay.

In addition, the fact that the solutions of Experiment Series 2 are rather stable, as indicated by small standard deviations in the number of selected features, is a useful discovery. Such repetition is essential in clinical situations. It shows that the wrapper objective and the search dynamics are not sensitivity to the noise of the initialisation. MHHO was competitive when compared to other state of art metaheuristics. Its dramatic feature reduction profile, although it is not the highest absolute accuracy, has a better accuracy-sparsity profile, a practical benefit in the creation of the simpler, cheaper and more interpretable diagnostic models.

Conclusion and future work

This study introduced a Multi-strategy Harris Hawks Optimization (MHHO) framework to address the premature convergence and stagnation limitations of the standard HHO algorithm. By integrating the Levy-flight Guided Phase (LGP), Adaptive Dynamic Foraging (ADF), and Hierarchical Avoidance Strategy (HAS), the proposed framework improves the balance between exploration and exploitation during the search process. The empirical evaluation included ablation analysis, benchmark testing, and wrapper-based feature selection for medical diagnosis. Across these settings, MHHO showed competitive optimization performance and demonstrated its ability to operate effectively in noisy and high-dimensional search spaces.

Despite these encouraging findings, the present study has several limitations that define the current scope of the conclusions. First, the empirical validation was conducted on the 23 benchmark functions considered in this work and on medical wrapper-based feature-selection datasets. Although these experiments provide useful evidence of the effectiveness of MHHO, they still missing important optimization classes, such as constrained, dynamic, or multi-objective problems.

Second, MHHO introduces additional computational overhead. The integration of LGP, ADF, and HAS requires additional search operations and increases runtime relative to the standard HHO. This behavior was observed in both the benchmark experiments and the wrapper-based feature-selection experiments. Consequently, MHHO may be more suitable in scenarios where solution quality, robustness, and stability are prioritized over minimum execution time.

Third, the feature-selection experiments in this study are classifier-dependent. The wrapper evaluation was primarily based on an SVM with an RBF kernel, which means that the identified feature subsets are optimized with respect to that learner. Other classifiers, such as tree-based models, k-NN, or logistic regression, may favor different feature subsets and may therefore lead to different conclusions regarding the relative benefit of the proposed search strategy.

Fourth, the practical validation presented in this work was intentionally focused on medical diagnosis problems, since this domain provides a demanding high-dimensional optimization setting. However, this application focus also limits the demonstrated generalization scope of MHHO. The current study does not yet include engineering, industrial, scheduling, or other real-world optimization domains, and therefore broader external validity remains to be established.

Finally, in the wrapper-based feature-selection experiments, the objective function relied on a scalar trade-off between classification error and subset size through a fixed \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document} parameter (Eq. 10). Although this choice ensures consistency and comparability across algorithms, a fixed weighting may not be equally suitable for all datasets and may not fully capture the underlying trade-off between predictive performance and sparsity.

These limitations do not weaken the internal validity of the proposed framework; rather, they define the present empirical scope of the study and highlight several directions for future work.
