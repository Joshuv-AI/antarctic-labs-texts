Oral nanoliposomes functionalized with cRGD and polydopamine for enhanced antimalarial efficacy of disulfide bond-modified dihydroartemisinin prodrug
Authors: Liqing Chai, Xiaomin Niu, Xiaoning Li, Jingjing Han, Fugui Guo, Rongrong Wang, Xiaohui Zhou, Xiaozhou Dong, Guoshun Zhang, Ruili Wang, Guolian Ren
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMalaria remains a global health crisis, with Plasmodium resistance underscoring the urgent need for advanced drug delivery strategies. To overcome the limitations of oral antimalarials, such as hepatic first-pass metabolism and insufficient drug accumulation in parasites, intestinal M cell-mediated lymphatic transport and Plasmodium-triggered drug release are effective strategies. Plasmodium parasites maintain a weakly acidic intracellular environment with high levels of glutathione (GSH). Polydopamine (PDA) remains stable in the gastrointestinal tract and degrades in the parasite’s weakly acidic, GSH-rich environment, making PDA nanoliposomes (PNLs) a promising oral delivery system for Plasmodium-responsive drug release. Additionally, cyclic arginine–glycine–aspartic acid (cRGD) can specifically target intestinal M cells, promoting lymphatic transport and thereby reducing the first-pass effect. We developed cRGD- and PDA-modified nanoliposomes (cRPNLs) to address the challenges associated with oral administration and loaded them with a disulfide bond (–SS–)-modified dihydroartemisinin (DHA) prodrug (DSSC) to simultaneously deplete GSH and induce lethal oxidative stress, thereby offering an enhanced antimalarial mechanism. In vitro, cRPNLs exhibited pH- and GSH-responsive DHA release. Ex vivo fluorescent imaging confirmed that cRPNLs targeted Peyer’s patches with minimal distribution in the liver. Crucially, both PNLs and cRPNLs reduced GSH levels in infected erythrocytes and elevated ROS by 2.0-fold, outperforming unmodified DHA. Pharmacokinetic and in vivo antimalarial pharmacodynamic studies revealed that cRPNLs exhibited significantly higher exposure and Plasmodium inhibition rates compared to PNLs and free DHA. These results highlight cRPNLs as a potent oral nanoplatform that combines M cell-mediated lymphatic uptake with parasite-triggered drug release, significantly improving antimalarial efficacy.Keywords: cRGD, polydopamine, oral delivery, M cell, dihydroartemisinin prodrug, oxidative stress

Introduction

Malaria, a pernicious infectious disease caused by Plasmodium parasites invading red blood cells (RBCs), has historically posed a significant threat to human health and survival (WHO 2024). Since the groundbreaking discovery of artemisinin (ART) as an efficacious treatment for malaria in 1972, its derivatives, including dihydroartemisinin (DHA), have consistently served as first-line antimalarial agents administered orally (WHO 2001). The unique endoperoxide bridge within ART drugs undergoes iron-catalyzed reductive homolytic cleavage, generating carbon-centered radicals that subsequently react with heme. This process leads to the formation of a potent array of reactive oxygen species (ROS), ultimately resulting in the destruction of the parasites (Adebayo et al. 2020; Yang et al. 2020; Yadav et al. 2025).

However, the suboptimal water solubility, rapid first-pass metabolism, short half-life, and lack of targeting specificity have collectively compromised the oral bioavailability and antimalarial efficacy of ART derivatives (Chai et al. 2021; Das et al. 2024; Rathi et al. 2024). Studies conducted in both human and animal subjects have demonstrated that DHA is absorbed rapidly, with concentrations reaching a peak approximately 0.5–1.5 h after oral administration and subsequently declining due to its short half-life of 0.2–2.5 h (Rijken et al. 2011; Benjamin et al. 2015; Chotsiri et al. 2017). Due to the absence of parenteral formulations, data regarding the absolute bioavailability of ART and DHA in humans remain unavailable. However, ARTs are believed to pass through the gut membrane relatively easily, and high oral clearance values suggest significant first-pass metabolism of the compound, leading to low bioavailability (Gordi et al. 2005). This is consistent with findings in rats (Chai et al. 2021). Moreover, the emergence of ART-resistant strains has threatened the long-term effectiveness of these compounds, while no alternative drug has demonstrated sufficient efficacy to replace them (Lu et al. 2017; Wang et al. 2019; Hanboonkunupakarn et al. 2022). Therefore, refining the molecular structure and developing advanced oral delivery systems for existing ART derivatives are anticipated to be critical strategies for enhancing antimalarial efficacy (Singh et al. 2014; Kumari et al. 2019).

Parasites meticulously maintain a precisely controlled acidic microenvironment (pH 5.2–5.8), which not only enhances the robust activity of proteases but also facilitates the efficient catabolism of hemoglobin from RBCs for nutrient synthesis (Kapishnikov et al. 2021; Li et al. 2024). Concurrently, these organisms maintain elevated levels of glutathione (GSH) to protect against oxidative stress-induced damage (Atamna and Ginsburg 1997; Wang, Ren, et al. 2021; Siddiqui et al. 2022). In contrast to nonresistant strains, drug-resistant parasites exhibit a significantly enhanced reducing microenvironment (Vega-Rodríguez et al. 2015; Phompradit et al. 2017; Zhao et al. 2024). Disulfide bonds (–SS–), as functional groups participating in GSH reactions, facilitate the release of active components from prodrugs containing –SS– within the intracellular reducing environment (Wang, Ren, et al. 2021; Ma et al. 2024). This process depletes GSH reserves and induces oxidative damage, ultimately accelerating parasite demise. Therefore, our objective is to leverage the distinctive Plasmodium microenvironment to design –SS– modified DHA (DSSC, also known as a DHA prodrug), which will then be used to prepare pH/GSH dual-responsive nanoliposomes (NLs), with the aim of achieving superior antimalarial efficacy.

The oral route remains one of the most prevalent drug administration, yet it presents substantial challenges in delivery due to the complex physiology of the gastrointestinal tract (Homayun et al. 2019). Nanomaterials have garnered increasing attention as a promising tool to enhance the solubility of poorly soluble drugs and modulate their release profiles, particularly for targeted drug delivery (Kirtane et al. 2021; Liu et al. 2025). In this study, we selected polydopamine (PDA) as the carrier material to achieve satisfactory stability within the gastrointestinal tract while enabling pH/GSH-responsive release. PDA is synthesized via the 1,4-Michael-type reaction of dopamine (DA) monomers under alkaline and aerobic conditions (Zeng et al. 2018; Szukalska et al. 2024). Notably, PDA exhibits robust stability in strongly acidic environments (pH 1–3), but undergoes depolymerization in weakly acidic (pH 5) or strongly alkaline (pH 13) conditions (Bernsmann et al. 2009; Lynge et al. 2011; Zeng et al. 2018; Zhang et al. 2020; Szukalska et al. 2024). Studies have demonstrated that PDA-coated drugs exhibit minimal alterations in particle size and morphology after incubation in simulated gastric and intestinal fluids for 2 h (Ding et al. 2017). Furthermore, when PDA was utilized as a synthetic biomaterial layer to form an intestinal synthetic epithelial lining on the surface of the Yorkshire pig’s intestinal tract, it demonstrated exceptional stability and degraded by only 28% within six hours, highlighting its sustained stability in the intestine (Li, Wang, et al. 2020). Additionally, the amino groups on PDA undergo Michael-type addition in the presence of –SH groups, facilitating drug release when activated by GSH (Wang et al. 2022; Li et al. 2024). Consequently, by leveraging the pH and GSH-responsive properties of PDA-coated nanoliposomes (PNLs), we aim to enhance stability during transit through the gastrointestinal tract, trigger drug release at the target site while depleting GSH, and thereby achieve the dual objectives of drug accumulation in parasites and augmented oxidative stress.

The intestinal lymphatic pathway serves as an optimal route for drug transport, effectively circumventing first-pass metabolism (Zhang et al. 2021; Dinh and Yan 2023). A promising approach for oral drug delivery via the lymphatic system involves targeting microfold cells (M cells) (Islam et al. 2019; Managuli et al. 2020; Miao et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2021; Dinh and Yan 2023; Del Castillo and Lo 2024). Specifically, M cells are located within the follicle-associated epithelium of Peyer’s patches (PPs) and play a pivotal role in small intestine immunity. Unlike other small intestinal cells, M cells lack brush borders and mucous coverage, and their basement membranes feature invaginations, which contribute to high transport efficiency (Islam et al. 2019; Zhang et al. 2021; Dinh and Yan 2023; Del Castillo and Lo 2024). Moreover, M cells do not contain microsomal enzymes and exhibit minimal lysosomal activity, allowing NLs to remain intact during transit from the lymph into the bloodstream, thereby effectively bypassing gastrointestinal first-pass metabolism (Managuli et al. 2020; He et al. 2023). Integrins, members of the cell adhesion receptor family, are highly expressed in M cells but not in the intestinal epithelium (IE) (Mohammadi et al. 2020; Song et al. 2021). The arginine–glycine–l-asparagine (RGD) peptide, renowned for its high affinity to integrins, has been shown to effectively target M cells (Yu, Meng, et al. 2024). However, the RGD peptide is highly susceptible to chemical degradation due to the presence of an aspartic acid residue in its structure, by cyclizing the RGD peptide (cRGD), the exposed N- and C-terminals are removed, rendering it resistant to enzymatic degradation and thus more suitable for oral delivery (Zhu et al. 2021). Utilizing cRGD-modified NLs to target intestinal M cells may offer a viable strategy to overcome first-pass metabolism.

Based on the aforementioned strategies, we have developed a cRGD- and PDA-dual modified oral DSSC NLs delivery system to enhance its antimalarial efficacy. Initially, primary NLs encapsulating DSSC were synthesized. These DSSC NLs were subsequently coated with PDA to form PNLs, which were further functionalized with cRGD peptides on the surface of the PNLs, resulting in the formation of cRGD-modified PNLs (cRPNLs). Upon oral administration, cRPNLs can specifically target M cells, thereby improving oral bioavailability. In infected RBCs (IRBCs), the PDA shell rapidly degraded, releasing DSSC and disrupting –SS– while simultaneously depleting GSH, thus enhancing the antimalarial effects of the generated DHA. Experimental results demonstrated that cRPNLs constituted a more effective anti-malaria therapeutic approach than PNLs and free DHA.

Materials and methods

Materials and animals

DHA was obtained from the Nanjing Jingzhu Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China). 1-Ethyl-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide hydrochloride (EDCI), 4-dimethylaminopyridine (DMAP), 3,3-dithiodipropionic acid, acetic anhydride, N-hexadecyl alcohol, PDA, granulesten, and cholesterol were all sourced from Aladdin Industrial Corporation (Shanghai, China). cRGD was acquired from Shaanxi Bangshi Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Hancheng, China). Vitamin E polyethylene glycol succinate (TPGS) was purchased from MedChemExpress (Monmouth Junction, NJ). Caco-2 cells (human epithelial colorectal adenocarcinoma cells) were generously provided by Professor Bin Zheng from the School of Pharmacy at Shanxi Medical University (Taiyuan, China).

All other reagents used were of analytical grade.

Male Sprague Dawley (SD) rats (180–220 g, Certificate No. SCXK (Shanxi) 2019-0004) were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Center of Shanxi Medical University (Taiyuan, China). Male C57BL/6J mice (18–22 g, Certificate No. SCXK (Beijing) 2024-0003) were sourced from Beijing Huafukang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). This study received approval from the Ethics Committee of Shanxi Provincial People’s Hospital Affiliated to Shanxi Medical University (Approval No. 2022-140). All animal procedures adhered to the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals established by Shanxi Medical University.

Synthesis of DSSC

DSSC was synthesized through a three-step process. First, 3,3-dithiobispropionic acid (315.39 mg, 1.5 mmol) and acetic anhydride (3 mL) were added to a flask and stirred for 3 h at 30 °C. The resulting mixture was then evaporated using toluene to obtain 3,3-dithiodipropionic anhydride (DTPA). Second, N-hexadecyl alcohol (242.44 mg, 1.00 mmol) and DMAP (18.33 mg, 0.15 mmol) dissolved in dichloromethane were added to DTPA under stirring at 35 °C. After a 24 h reaction period, the progress of the reaction was monitored by thin-layer chromatography (TLC). The developing solvent was prepared with a volume ratio of cyclohexane:ethyl acetate:glacial acetic acid = 2:1:0.1. The target bands on the TLC plate were identified and eluted using an appropriate volume of dichloromethane. Each elution cycle lasted for 8 h and was repeated twice. Upon completion of the elution process, the filtrate was separated and collected. Following vacuum evaporation, the white intermediate product was obtained and accurately weighed. Finally, DHA (426.52 mg, 1.50 mmol), DMAP (18.33 mg, 0.15 mmol), and EDCI (192.70 mg, 1.00 mmol) dissolved in dichloromethane were added to the intermediate product (434.69 mg, 1.00 mmol) and stirred for 48 h at 35 °C. Upon completion of the reaction, DSSC product was obtained and subsequently purified by TLC as described above. The developing solvent was prepared using a mixture of cyclohexane, ethyl acetate, and glacial acetic acid in a volume ratio of 3:1:0.1. The structure of DSSC was confirmed by high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) and proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR), as well as carbon-13 nuclear magnetic resonance (13C NMR).

Sensitivity of DSSC to pH levels and GSH

DSSC was dissolved in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 20% ethanol, with or without 10 mM GSH, under different pH conditions of 1.2, 5.5, and 7.4. Changes in DSSC over time and the quantification of DHA were analyzed using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC, LC-2030, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) coupled with a post-column derivatization system (LC-10AT, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) (Guo et al. 2021; Wang, Ren, et al. 2021). For DHA analysis, a C18 HPLC column (5 μm, 250 × 4.6 mm; Agilent Technologies, Inc., Santa Clara, CA) was employed. The mobile phase consisted of 20% methanol, 50% acetonitrile, and 0.1 M ammonium acetate buffer (pH 4.0), delivered at a flow rate of 1.4 mL/min to ensure high separation efficiency. The eluate was mixed with the post-column reagent (1 M potassium hydroxide in 90% ethanol) at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min within a 5 m stainless steel capillary (inner diameter 0.5 mm) housed in a reaction chamber maintained at 70 °C. Detection was performed at a wavelength of 289 nm. A similar method was applied for DSSC analysis, except that the mobile phase comprised 30% methanol, 65% acetonitrile, and 5% ammonium acetate buffer (0.1 M, pH 4.0), delivered at a flow rate of 1.6 mL/min. Detection for DSSC samples was conducted at a wavelength of 240 nm.

Preparation of DSSC NLs

DSSC NLs were prepared using an ethanol-injection method (Abdelbary et al. 2019). Specifically, 2.00 mg of DSSC, 30.00 mg of soybean phospholipid, 3.00 mg of cholesterol, and 1.75 mg of TPGS were dissolved in 400.00 μL of anhydrous ethanol. This solution was then slowly injected into water under stirring conditions at 1200 rpm and 60 °C. Stirring was continued for an additional 2 h post-injection to obtain the DSSC NLs. A single-factor analysis was employed to optimize the formulation. In this study, the DSSC-to-soybean phospholipid (DSSC/SP) ratio, soybean phospholipid-to-cholesterol (SP/CH) ratio, and TPGS content were identified as key influencing factors. Particle size, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential were selected as evaluated parameters.

Preparation of PNLs and cRPNLs

cRPNLs were prepared according to a previously described procedure, with minor modifications (Bi et al. 2018; Li et al. 2024). DSSC NLs were incubated with a 0.6 mg/mL DA solution in tris buffer (10 mM, pH 8.5) under gentle rotation at 25 °C in the dark for 12 h. The reaction mixture, which visibly changed from transparent to grayish black, was then subjected to ultrafiltration centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C, resulting in concentrated PNLs. Subsequently, the concentrated PNLs were resuspended in an equal volume of tris buffer (10 mM, pH 8.5), and 2 mg/mL cRGD was added. After overnight agitation at 600 rpm at 25 °C, the cRPNLs were harvested. To confirm the successful conjugation of cRGD, infrared spectroscopy analysis was performed.

Characterization of PNLs and cRPNLs

The particle size, PDI, and zeta potential of both the PNLs and cRPNLs were measured using a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS unit (Nano ZS90, Malvern, Malvern, UK) at 25 °C. Morphological characterization was performed via transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Hitachi, Chiyoda, Japan) operated at 80 kV. Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra of PDA, cRGD, NLs, PNLs, and cRPNLs were recorded using a Thermo Nicolet IS5 FT-IR spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). The scan range was from 4000 to 1000 cm−1 with a resolution of 4 cm−1 and 16 scans.

Evaluation of entrapment efficiency and drug-loading capacity

The entrapment efficiency (EE) and drug-loading capacity (DL) of PNLs and cRPNLs were determined using the low-speed centrifugation method. After the preparation of PNLs and cRPNLs, unencapsulated DSSC remained in the system in a suspended form. The content at this stage represents the total amount of DSSC (W2, mg). PNLs and cRPNLs were centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 10 min to remove the unencapsulated drug. The supernatant was collected and subjected to demulsification by adding four times its volume of acetonitrile to release the DSSC encapsulated within the NLs (W1, mg). Primary stability of PNLs and cRPNLs

The primary stability of PNLs and cRPNLs was evaluated by assessing the particle size, PDI, zeta potential, EE, and DL (n = 3). The samples were stored in PBS (pH 7.4) at 4 °C for 28 days. The particle size, PDI, zeta potential, EE, and DL of DSSC were measured on days 1, 7, 14, and 28.

In vitro release study of PNLs and cRPNLs

To investigate the in vitro drug release profile, 1 mL of PNLs and cRPNLs (DSSC 2mg/mL) were added to disposable dialysis bags (MWCO: 3500 Da) immersed in PBS under different pH conditions of 1.2, 5.5, and 7.4 containing 20% ethanol with or without 10 mM GSH (Cui et al. 2020; Lin et al. 2020). The total volume of the system was 10 mL, which was incubated at 37 °C with stirring at 100 rpm using an air bath constant temperature shaker (SHA-BA, Ronghua Instrument Manufacturing Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China). At predetermined time intervals (0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, and 24 h), 0.2 mL aliquots were withdrawn and diluted with four times its volume of acetonitrile, so that DHA could be determined by HPLC with post-column derivatization, as described in Section 2.3. At the same time, an equivalent volume of fresh medium was replenished immediately.

In vivo pharmacokinetic study

Pharmacokinetic studies of PNLs, cRPNLs, and DHA suspension (DHA-sus) were conducted in healthy male SD rats (180–220 g), with five animals included in each group. Each formulation, including PNLs, cRPNLs, and DHA-sus (in 0.5% CMC-Na), was administered via oral gavage at an equivalent DHA dose of 10 mg/kg. This dose was determined based on the normalized conversion of surface area to the clinical dose for the human body and aligns with the dosing regimens reported in prior studies (Zheng, Pan, et al. 2024). Serial blood samples were collected from the jugular vein into heparinized tubes and centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 10 min to isolate plasma. The plasma concentrations of DHA were quantified by HPLC–MS/MS following a liquid–liquid extraction procedure (Chai et al. 2021). Chromatographic separations were performed on a HPLC system (Agilent Technologies, Inc., Santa Clara, CA) equipped with a C18 analytical column (50 mm × 2.1 mm, 5 μm; Agilent Technologies, Inc., Santa Clara, CA) and a Dikma C18 guard column (4.0 mm × 3.0 mm, 5 μm; Dikma Technologies Inc., Foothill Ranch, CA). Isocratic elution was conducted using a mobile phase consisting of 90% acetonitrile and 10% ammonium acetate at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. The target analytes were quantified by an API 3200 Triple Quadrupole Mass Spectrometer (AB SCIEX LLC, Redwood City, CA) via multiple reaction monitoring of the transitions m/z 302.5 → 163.3 for DHA and m/z 300.3 → 209.2 for ART (internal standard). Pharmacokinetic parameters were determined using noncompartmental analysis based on individual plasma concentration–time data.

Ex vivo M cell targeting studies

The Ex vivo targeting study of M cells was conducted by referring to previously published reports (Managuli et al. 2020). DiR-PNLs and DiR-cRPNLs were prepared by incorporating DiR dye into PNLs and cRPNLs, respectively. These formulations were orally administered to overnight-fasted mice at a dose of 2 mg/kg, with each group consisting of three animals. At 1 h and 3 h post-administration, the mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation following anesthesia induced with isoflurane, and their intestines and livers were collected. The intestines were thoroughly washed, and tissues containing PPs were dissected into 1 cm segments. The patches appear as small, protruding, whitish to grayish nodules (diameter 2 mm) embedded in the outer intestinal wall, as described in a previous study (Pastori and Lopalco 2014). The entire intestines, livers, and segments containing PPs were imaged using an IVIS in vivo optical imaging system (Xtreme, Bruker, Billerica, MA) with excitation/emission wavelengths set at 675/720 nm.

In addition to quantifying the fluorescence of segments containing PPs, precise observations were made regarding the fluorescence differences between individual PPs and the IE (Miao et al. 2021; Song et al. 2021). Dil solution, Dil-PNLs, and Dil-cRPNLs were orally administered to mice at a dose of 4 mg/kg. After 1 h, the mice were euthanized, and both the PPs and IE located more than 1 cm away from the PPs were excised. Subsequently, each tissue sample was cryo-sectioned, stained with DAPI, and analyzed using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM; FV1000, Olympus, Hachioji, Japan). Furthermore, quantitative analysis of distributional differences was performed, with each group comprising three animals. Each kind of tissue sample was mixed with PBS and subjected to vigorous stirring for five minutes, followed by centrifugation at 13,000 rpm for 10 minutes. The supernatant was subsequently collected, and fluorescence was quantified using a fluorescence plate reader (Varioskan Flash, Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA).

In vitro intracellular ROS and GSH–GSSG detection

A 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFDA) probe was utilized to assess the production of ROS in nanoparticle-treated IRBCs. Specifically, DHA solution (in RPMI1640 containing 0.00025‰ DMSO, DHA-sol), DSSC solution (in RPMI1640 containing 0.00025‰ DMSO, DSSC-sol), blank PDA nanoliposomes (BPNLs), PNLs, and cRPNLs were added to 1 mL of malaria parasite K173-IRBCs with a parasitemia rate of 10% and incubated for 24 h. After incubation, the samples were washed and centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 10 min to remove the supernatant, followed by co-incubation with the DCFDA probe for 30 min. Excess probe was removed by washing with PBS. Fluorescence intensity was measured using a microplate reader at an excitation wavelength of 485 nm and an emission wavelength of 520 nm.

Furthermore, the total glutathione (T-GSH) content and oxidized glutathione (GSSG) levels in IRBCs were quantified using a GSH assay kit (Elabscience Biotech Co. Ltd., Wuhan, China). The concentration of reduced GSH was calculated by subtracting the GSSG concentration from the T-GSH content. The same treatment and incubation protocol used for the ROS assay was applied. After incubation, unbound NLs were removed by washing the IRBCs three times with PBS. The cells were then resuspended in PBS, lysed with an equal volume of saponin solution on ice for 15 min, and centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 min to separate the supernatant. The resulting pellets were analyzed using the manufacturer’s instructions provided with the GSH assay kit.

In vivo antimalarial studies

The antimalarial efficacy of PNLs, cRPNLs, and DHA-sus was evaluated in a Plasmodium berghei K173-infected C57BL/6J male mouse model (Li et al. 2024; Zhao et al. 2024). Mice were intraperitoneally inoculated with 1.0 × 107 parasitized RBCs suspended in saline. A modified four-day suppression test was employed to assess the antimalarial activity. Seventeen groups, each consisting of eight mice, were randomly assigned to different treatment regimens. One group served as an untreated control, while the remaining 16 groups were infected and treated 2 h post-infection. Infected groups received either saline (vehicle control), DHA-sol, PNLs, or cRPNLs at doses of 6.9, 13.7, 27.4, 54.8, and 109.8 µmol/kg via oral gavage daily for four consecutive days. On days 5 and 11, a single drop of blood was collected from each mouse via tail-tip puncture. The collected blood samples were subjected to Wright-Giemsa staining for the preparation of thin blood smears to evaluate the degree of parasitemia. This analysis encompassed the infection rate, inhibition rate, ED50. Subsequently, under isoflurane anesthesia, three mice from each group (normal, control, DHA-sus, high-dose PNLs, and high-dose cRPNLs) were randomly selected for cardiac blood sampling to determine hematological parameters and evaluate liver function (ALT and AST levels). Following this procedure, euthanasia was performed via cervical dislocation, and major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys, were excised for histopathological evaluation using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. In addition, survival fraction was analyzed from day 5 onwards.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 22.0 (Armonk, NY) to evaluate the differences between groups. A p value of less than .05 was considered indicative of statistical significance. All data were expressed as means ± standard deviation (SD).

Sensitivity of DSSC to pH levels and GSH

The temporal changes in DSSC degradation and the corresponding percentage of DHA production under various environmental conditions are depicted in Figure 1(B). These figures illustrate the efficiency of DSSC conversion to DHA at different pH levels and GSH concentrations. The data reveal that at a pH of 1.2, approximately 80% of the prodrug was converted to DHA within 2 h. After 24 h, the conversion rate reached approximately 86% at a pH of 5.5, while it remained at only 25% at physiological pH 7.4. The results demonstrate that DSSC can be converted into DHA under varying pH conditions, although the conversion rates differ across these conditions. Specifically, the transformation rate increases progressively under pH levels of 7.4, 5.5, and 1.2. Additionally, at pH 7.4, the presence of 10 mM GSH significantly accelerated the degradation process, underscoring the sensitivity of the prodrug to this reducing agent. Collectively, these findings confirm that the drug rapidly degraded in the reducing and slightly acidic environment characteristic of the malaria parasite, leading to the release of DHA.

Preparation and characterization of PNLs and cRPNLs

The optimized NLs formulation was achieved with a DSSC/SP mass ratio of 1:15, a SP/CH mass ratio of 10:1, and a TPGS concentration of 5%, respectively (Figure S2). As illustrated in Figure 2(A), PNLs and cRPNLs were successfully synthesized, exhibiting a brownish-black appearance. TEM images confirmed the uniform spherical morphology of the NLs (Figure 2(A)). The size and size distribution of these NLs were characterized using dynamic light scattering, as shown in Figure 2(B). Compared to NLs, the average sizes of PNLs and cRPNLs decreased progressively. Specifically, the particle sizes were 183.77 ± 2.83 nm for NLs, 141.27 ± 1.16 nm for PNLs, and 94.30 ± 1.65 nm for cRPNLs, with PDI of 0.33 ± 0.02, 0.24 ± 0.02, and 0.21 ± 0.01, respectively. Notably, the zeta potentials of PNLs and cRPNLs did not show significant differences (Table 1). For cRPNLs, the EE was 85.87% ± 2.70%, and the DL was 5.02% ± 0.10% (Table 1). The stability assessment of the NLs at 4 °C over 28 days, as shown in Figure 2(D–I), indicated no significant changes in particle size, PDI, DL, or EE, suggesting excellent preliminary stability.

In vitro drug release behavior

To evaluate the gastrointestinal stability and triggered release properties of PNLs and cRPNLs, in vitro release experiments were performed in PBS under different pH conditions (1.2, 5.5, and 7.4) containing 20% ethanol, with or without 10 mM GSH (as shown in Figure 3). Additionally, the stability of the nanocarriers in PBS at pH 7.4 supplemented with 20% ethanol was concurrently assessed (as depicted in Figure S3). The results demonstrated that under simulated gastrointestinal conditions and residence time (pH 1.2 for 2 h and pH 7.4 for 24 h), negligible drug release was observed from both PNLs and cRPNLs, indicating excellent gastrointestinal stability. At pH 5.5, the release of DHA from PNLs and cRPNLs significantly increased, with cumulative release rates reaching 74.23% ± 1.81% and 84.86% ± 1.45%, respectively, within 24 h. Under conditions of pH 5.5 and the presence of 10 mM GSH, the release of DHA was further enhanced, with cumulative release rates reaching 82.58% ± 2.85% for PNLs and 88.60% ± 0.99% for cRPNLs, respectively. At pH 7.4 and the presence of 10 mM GSH, the cumulative release rates of DHA from PNLs and cRPNLs were 74.78% ± 3.21% and 73.41% ± 1.68%, respectively. Notably, the release of DHA was significantly higher under conditions of pH 5.5 and the presence of 10 mM GSH compared to conditions of pH 7.4 and the presence of 10 mM GSH (p < .05). This suggests that the release behavior was strongly influenced by both pH and GSH concentration.

Cytotoxicity and hemolysis assay

In order to evaluate the safety of the preparation, we systematically investigated the cytotoxicity of NLs on Caco-2 cells and evaluated their blood compatibility. As shown in Figure 4(A), after co-culturing with various concentrations of PNLs and cRPNLs for 24 h, the viability of Caco-2 cells remained above 80% within a concentration range of 5–100 μg/mL. In accordance with the established criteria outlined in ISO 10993-5: 2009, substances that maintain cellular viability exceeding 80% are classified as nontoxic. Therefore, these findings indicate that the carriers exhibit acceptable safety within this concentration range. Additionally, Figure 4(B) demonstrates that after a 3 h incubation with varying concentrations of PNLs and cRPNLs, all RBC samples remained intact, with no significant hemolysis observed. The absorbance measurements of the supernatant from the sample groups, conducted using an ultraviolet spectrophotometer, indicated that the hemolysis ratio of PNLs and cRPNLs at different concentrations was consistently below 5%. This further suggests that the preparations possess excellent biocompatibility and are safe for use.

Ex vivo M cell targeting studies

Ex vivo fluorescent imaging of whole intestinal tracts, livers, and segments containing PPs in mice was performed using an IVIS optical imaging system following oral administration of free DiR, DiR-PNLs, and DiR-cRPNLs at various time intervals (Figure 5). One hour post-administration, significant fluorescence was observed across different intestinal segments regardless of the presence of PPs. However, this intensity had markedly diminished by the 3 h time point (Figure 5(A)). The overall intestinal fluorescence signal was stronger in the NLs groups compared to the free DiR group, suggesting that PDA encapsulation enhances drug stability within the gastrointestinal tract. The fluorescence signals of free DiR, DiR-PNLs, and DiR-cRPNLs in the liver exhibited a progressive decline (Figure 5(B)). A significant difference was observed at 1 h post-administration (Figure 5(D)), indicating reduced hepatic distribution after NLs delivery, with cRGD modification further minimizing liver accumulation. In Figure 5(C,E), DiR-cRPNLs demonstrated significantly stronger and more localized fluorescence specifically in the PP regions compared to free DiR and DiR-PNLs at 1 h (p < .05). These results suggest that DiR-cRPNLs accumulated to a greater extent in the small intestine’s PP regions compared to DiR-PNLs.

Quantitative analysis further validated and substantiated the aforementioned results. In vitro intracellular ROS and GSH–GSSG detection

To assess the impact of each preparation on the oxidative stress in IRBCs, the levels of ROS and GSH were measured. As shown in Figure 7(A), the ROS levels in DHA-sol and DSSC-sol groups were significantly elevated by approximately 4.1-fold and 5.7-fold, respectively, compared to the untreated control group. This indicated that DHA-sol and DSSC-sol induced oxidative stress in IRBCs. Notably, BPNLs alone resulted in a pronounced increase in oxidative damage than DHA-sol alone (p < .001). Following treatment with PNLs, ROS levels were markedly elevated (p < .001), increasing by 1.4-fold and 1.4-fold, respectively, compared with DSSC-sol and BPNLs treatment alone, and by 2.0-fold compared with DHA-sol. However, no significant difference was observed between the PNLs and cRPNLs treatments, suggesting that the addition of the cRGD peptide did not influence ROS production. The effects of each preparation on GSH depletion, as shown in Figure 7(B), mirrored their impacts on ROS. Specifically, both DHA-sol and DSSC-sol individually caused a reduction in GSH levels, with DSSC-sol exhibiting a more pronounced effect (p < .001). BPNLs also showed a significant reduction compared to DHA-sol (p < .001), while PNLs demonstrated the most potent effect among all treatments, surpassing both DSSC-sol and BPNLs.

In vivo safety study

To evaluate biosafety, adverse reactions were monitored on days 5. As shown in Table 4 and Figure 8, the primary hematological indices and liver function parameters (including ALT and AST) in both the PNLs and cRPNLs groups remained within normal ranges. In contrast, the model group infected with malaria exhibited significantly abnormal hematological and liver function indices (p < .001). Similarly, histopathological examination revealed substantial damage to the liver and spleen in model group (Figure 9), with less severe damage observed in the treatment groups. Specifically, the model group displayed marked accumulation of inflammatory cells in hepatic vasculature and hemosiderin deposition. The distinction between red and white pulp in the spleen was indistinct, with reduced and loosely arranged white pulp cells and minor hemosiderin deposition. Following drug treatment, DHA-sus and NLs groups demonstrated varying degrees of improvement. The DHA-sus group still exhibited signs of inflammation and malarial pigmentation, whereas no significant abnormalities were observed in either PNLs or cRPNLs groups. These findings suggest superior biocompatibility and enhanced antimalarial efficacy of the NLs.

Discussion

ARTs are characterized by a pronounced first-pass effect, with a liver extraction ratio of 0.9, and are significantly influenced by intestinal CYP450 enzymes (Xing et al. 2012; Chai et al. 2021). In this study, we employed an M-cell-targeted lymphatic transport delivery system to reduce the first-pass effect and enhance oral bioavailability. Specifically, we developed cRGD and PDA-modified NLs for oral ART delivery. In vitro release studies, which simulated gastrointestinal emptying time, demonstrated the stability of the NLs. Cumulative release reached approximately 80% under weakly acidic conditions and in the presence of GSH, indicating excellent stability and triggered-release properties. Pharmacokinetic analysis revealed increased in vivo exposure of cRGD-modified drugs. Imaging confirmed significant drug enrichment in PPs and reduced liver accumulation. These findings suggest that cRGD and PDA-modified NLs can improve the drug stability in the gastrointestinal tract after oral administration, enhancing lymphatic transport and improving oral bioavailability.

It is noteworthy that the particle size of NLs was significantly reduced after modification with PDA and cRGD peptides, as observed in similar studies previously reported (Zhu et al. 2016; Li et al. 2024). This phenomenon can be attributed to the lipid bilayer structure at the core of the liposomes. First, the lipid bilayer is a vesicular structure whose molecular arrangement can be readily influenced by factors such as charge and hydrogen bonding (Cong et al. 2023). The hydroxyl and amino groups in PDA and cRGD peptides may promote tighter packing of the lipid chains through intermolecular interactions. Second, the rigid cyclic structure of cRGD may fill the pores within the PDA layer, forming a denser composite coating. This not only reduces the thickness of the surface modification layer but also compresses the liposome core, thereby leading to a further reduction in the particle size. Furthermore, in the pharmacokinetic study described in this article, the control group utilized a DHA-sus. Given that commercially available DHA formulations are primarily oral tablets, administering these directly to rats is impractical. To closely simulate the drug delivery form of commercially available products, the rats were administered the drug in suspension form, mimicking the state of the drug after the disintegration of oral tablets.

In addition to increasing systemic drug exposure, enhancing Plasmodium-responsive drug release and augmenting oxidative damage to parasites are effective strategies for improving treatment outcomes. In this study, we utilized two distinct strategies to augment oxidative stress in Plasmodium-IRBCs. First, the interaction between PDA and the GSH sulfhydryl groups within Plasmodium leads to a reduction in GSH concentration, thereby intensifying oxidative stress. Concurrently, the degradation of PDA facilitates drug release within Plasmodium, enhancing the efficacy of drug delivery. Second, –SS– exhibit reduction-sensitive characteristics and can react with high concentrations of GSH, resulting in the cleavage of the –SS– while consuming GSH. This process subsequently increases oxidative stress levels. The application of –SS– containing prodrugs results in the formation of DHA and concurrent depletion of GSH. Both DSSC and PDA independently diminish parasite GSH levels, exacerbate oxidative stress, and increase drug sensitivity. The combination of these two approaches yields superior therapeutic outcomes.

M cells are predominantly situated in PPs (Islam et al. 2019; Zhang et al. 2021; Dinh and Yan 2023; Del Castillo and Lo 2024), therefore, evaluating the targeting efficiency of the preparation to PPs is tantamount to assessing its capacity to enter PPs via M cells and subsequently reach the lymphatic circulation. The ex vivo imaging, CLSM, and fluorescence quantification analyses demonstrated that DiR-cRPNLs accumulated to a significantly greater extent in the small intestinal PPs compared to DiR-PNLs, thereby confirming the enhanced targeting efficacy of cRPNLs toward M cells. This finding also clarifies the previously observed pharmacokinetic outcomes. The encapsulation with PDA improved the stability and retention of NLs within the gastrointestinal tract. The cRGD modification further promoted M-cell-mediated lymphocyte circulation, and reduced hepatic distribution, thereby mitigating the first-pass effect. Consequently, cRPNLs exhibited the highest AUC in the pharmacokinetic results. Notably, the hepatic distribution of PNLs was obviously reduced, suggesting that these NLs may enhance transport through the mesenteric lymph nodes to the lymphatic system, thereby improving oral bioavailability (Zheng, Luo, et al. 2024).

In vivo safety studies have demonstrated that NLs have no significant impact on liver function or blood routine indices, indicating their favorable safety profile. However, the control group showed marked abnormalities in both pathological sections and blood routine results. Previous reports have noted significant alterations in the liver and spleen tissues of Plasmodium-infected mice, characterized by the deposition of malarial pigment in liver tissue, extensive infiltration of inflammatory cells in hepatic sinusoids, and accumulation of malarial pigment in the splenic medulla (Wang, Li, et al. 2021; Zuo et al. 2022). In this study, H&E staining revealed that the liver and spleen of the control group displayed pronounced malarial pigmentation and inflammatory changes compared to the normal group, corroborating previous findings. In contrast, the liver and spleen in the PNLs and cRPNLs groups showed substantial improvement. Hematological results in these groups were comparable to those of the normal group, consistent with the observed Plasmodium inhibition rates. Additionally, PNLs and cRPNLs did not exert significant effects on other organs. Collectively, these findings confirm that cRPNLs exhibit excellent biocompatibility and therapeutic efficacy, positioning it as a promising and safe candidate for oral antimalarial treatment.

DHA demonstrates the highest therapeutic efficacy among ARTs and functions as the active in vivo metabolite of artemether, arteether, and artesunate (Navaratnam et al. 2000; Chai et al. 2021; Das et al. 2024). Commercial formulations include DHA tablets as well as combination therapies incorporating piperaquine or trimethoprim. However, there is increasing concern about the emergence of resistance to this compound. Developing derivatives to counteract such resistance is of critical importance. It has been reported that –SS– exhibits GSH responsiveness and has been utilized in anti-tumor and anti-malaria research, and derivatives featuring –SS– in DHA have been documented (Li, Guo, et al. 2020; Li et al. 2021; Wang, Ren, et al. 2021; Ren et al. 2022). However, their studies have been restricted to intravenous administration routes. The design of DSSC and oral nanocarriers has shown enhanced therapeutic effects compared to DHA alone, along with improved administration routes compared to those mentioned above, suggesting promising development prospects and significant application potential.

Conclusions

In summary, DSSC NLs functionalized with cRGD and PDA were successfully synthesized. These NLs possess lymphatic transport properties and pH/GSH-responsive drug release capabilities. The developed NLs can be relatively stably retained in the gastrointestinal tract and transported via the M cell and PPs pathways of the intestine, thereby minimizing first-pass metabolism and enhancing oral bioavailability. In IRBCs, these NLs significantly elevated ROS levels while reducing GSH levels, with minimal cytotoxic side effects, indicating their potential therapeutic efficacy. These findings suggest that cRPNLs hold promise as an antimalarial agent and provide a novel approach for the oral delivery of drugs with high first-pass effects.

In this study, the procedures involving animal trauma included intraperitoneal injection, jugular vein cannulation, tail-tip blood sampling, and cardiac blood collection. The intraperitoneal injection and tail-tip blood sampling were associated with minimal discomfort and therefore did not necessitate the use of anesthesia. For the remaining procedures, isoflurane anesthesia was administered to ensure minimal discomfort and pain. At the conclusion of the study or for tissue extraction, animals were euthanized via cervical dislocation under deep isoflurane-induced anesthesia. Rationale for using animals: animal models are indispensable for evaluating in vivo pharmacokinetics, efficacy, and safety of new drugs. Our study aimed to validate the antimalarial efficacy of our constructed NLs. Mice and rats serve as valuable tools in biomedical research due to their significant similarities to humans, despite some differences, making them essential for assessing drug effectiveness and safety.
