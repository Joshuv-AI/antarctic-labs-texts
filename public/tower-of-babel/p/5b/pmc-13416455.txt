The nasal septum: A novel mucosal donor site for fornix reconstruction
Authors: Claude Kaufmann, Katja C Iselin, Gunther Pabst
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeFornix reconstruction in the setting of symblepharon formation conventionally relies on oral mucosa, nasal turbinate mucosa, amniotic membrane, or conjunctival autografts, each bearing distinct limitations such as limited tissue availability, donor site morbidity, inadequate stability, or absence of goblet cells. This report introduces the nasal septum as a novel mucosal donor site that overcomes the inherent shortcomings of traditional graft sources.ObservationsA 63-year-old woman with extensive conjunctival cicatrization and symblepharon resulting from toxic epidermal necrolysis underwent fornix reconstruction using nasal septal mucosa as the donor tissue. A 3 × 3 cm mucosal graft was harvested from the posterior dorsal nasal septum under general anesthesia and secured to the reconstructed fornix following adhesiolysis and cicatricial tissue release. Throughout the subsequent 2-year follow-up period, the reconstruction remained stable, with no evidence of recurrent symblepharon or fornix shortening. The incorporated graft demonstrated sustained lubrication and preserved tissue integrity. The nasal donor site healed without complication.Conclusions and importanceThis case establishes nasal septal mucosa as a promising alternative graft tissue for fornix reconstruction, offering distinct advantages over conventional grafts. Its characteristics (presence of goblet cells ensuring adequate lubrication, capacity for large-scale tissue harvest, inherent tensile strength conferring structural stability, and minimal donor site morbidity) make it suitable for complex reconstructive scenarios. The utilization of nasal septal mucosa expands the reconstructive options available to oculoplastic surgeons and warrants prospective evaluation to define its role as a standard technique in fornix reconstruction.Keywords: Fornix reconstruction, Mucosal graft, Nasal mucosa, Nasal septum, Toxic epidermal necrolysis

Introduction

Surgical symblepharon correction involves scar lysis and fornix reconstruction using graft tissue such as mucosal or amniotic membrane grafts. According to Henderson and Collin, the ideal graft tissue prevents postoperative scarring, promotes normal tissue formation including goblet cells, and resembles conjunctiva cosmetically.1 Although autologous conjunctival transplants represent the optimal choice, they are unavailable in bilateral disease and limited by safety concerns in unilateral cases requiring large grafts.2 Amniotic membranes offer unlimited availability but lack long-term stability, epithelial stem cells, and goblet cells.1 Oral (labial, buccal, or hard palate) mucosal grafts provide a high degree of stability and maintain epithelial morphology,3 but the sturdiness of the hard palate mucosa may irritate the cornea,4 and larger grafts of the lips and cheek risk donor site complications.5 Finally, oral mucosa lacks the goblet cells essential for tear film support. In contrast, high densities of goblet cells are present in vaginal and rectal mucosa (where again donor site complications and pain are limiting factors)6 and in the nasal mucosa.7 Complications from harvesting the middle or inferior turbinate (bleeding due to the presence of larger vessels, crusting, empty nose syndrome) or the cartilaginous septum (perforation)8 prompted investigation of alternative sites such as the nasal floor.9 In the following, we describe the removal and use of nasal mucosa primarily from the osseous part of the dorsal nasal septum in Cottle areas 3 and 4. This study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Case report

A 63-year-old woman developed progressive conjunctival cicatrization resembling mucous membrane pemphigoid following toxic epidermal necrolysis.10 Clinical findings included persistent conjunctivitis, loss of plica semilunaris and lacrimal caruncle, fornix shortening, symblepharon formation, cicatricial entropion, trichiasis, punctate keratopathy, and recurrent corneal epithelial defects with infections (Fig. 1A). Systemic immunosuppression with oral steroids temporarily controlled the inflammation; of the steroid-sparing agents used, rituximab, mycophenolate mofetil and infliximab proved insufficient, while cyclophosphamide was effective, but discontinued due to systemic side effects. Topical treatment included autologous serum for dryness and dexamethasone for inflammation. Trichiasis responded poorly to cryotherapy, requiring continuous epilation and bandage contact lens protection. Progressive inferior fornix shortening ultimately prevented lens insertion, necessitating fornix reconstruction with amniotic membrane, which failed after resorption. Subsequent reconstruction utilized nasal septal mucosa as a more durable graft. Systemic prednisolone (1 mg/kg; Spiricort®, Spirig HealthCare AG, Egerkingen, Switzerland) was initiated perioperatively to prevent inflammatory exacerbation.

Surgical technique

Based on the extent of the symblepharon, we estimated that a graft size of 2.5 × 2.5 cm would be necessary to completely cover the recipient site without tension. Since the size of the harvest site on the dorsal septum is not critical, a slightly larger graft size was chosen to account for any potential tissue shrinkage after harvest.

The procedure was performed under general anesthesia. Following application of decongestant drops and local anesthetic to the right septum (Fig. 2A), a hemitransfixation incision exposed the anterior septal edge. Because of concurrent nasal obstruction from septal deviation and synechiae due to the underlying disease, septoplasty was performed and deviated segments excised. The nasal mucosa on the right side, in Cottle areas 3 and 4, was carefully detached from the cartilage and bone, along with the perichondrium and periosteum. A 3 × 3 cm mucosal graft was harvested from the posterior dorsal septum (Fig. 3), a location chosen to prevent anterior perforation-related complications, which would lead to nasal breathing obstruction and crusting. The mucosal graft was used as harvested without further preparation; the periosteum/perichondrium (approximately 0.1–0.3 mm thick) was deliberately left intact on the deep surface of the graft, as it provides the collagen framework essential for flexural strength and mechanical integrity.

The donor site was covered with crushed reimplanted cartilage anteriorly, and a septal splint placed to create a moist chamber facilitating secondary re-epithelialization. A hemitransfixation suture was applied, and nasal packing with a breathing tube was inserted and removed on postoperative day 2.

For the fornix reconstruction (Fig. 2B), a bridle suture was placed through the lower eyelid, the symblepharon was detached from the inferior limbus through a crescent-shaped incision and the fibrovascular tissue was excised. The overlying conjunctiva then retracted to the inner eyelid margin. The mucosal graft was attached to the inferior limbus and horizontal palpebral fissure with 8-0 polyglactin mattress sutures. The reconstructed inferior fornix, measuring just over 1 cm deep, was secured in depth with a silicone tube, which was fixed transpalpebrally using double-armed 4-0 silk anchoring sutures tied externally over a cerclage band on the lower eyelid. The mucosal graft was attached to the recessed conjunctiva along the inner margin of the eyelid using 8-0 polyglactin mattress sutures. Excess graft tissue was trimmed at the limbus and eyelid margin, triamcinolone injected sub-Tenon superiorly, and a 21mm bandage contact lens applied. Postoperative treatment included topical steroid and antibiotic ointments. Anchoring sutures were removed at six weeks postoperatively. Systemic steroid therapy was tapered over one month and discontinued.

The postoperative course was uneventful with complete nasal mucosa healing (Fig. 1B). Throughout the two-year follow-up period, which ended with the patient's death from gastrointestinal malignancy, there was no fornix shortening or symblepharon formation. The graft maintained adequate lubrication and enabled the patient to wear bandage contact lenses again.

Discussion

In contrast to the amniotic membrane initially used, the nasoseptal mucosal graft enabled stable fornix reconstruction throughout the two-year follow-up period. By preventing postoperative scarring, maintaining goblet cell-mediated lubrication, and providing favorable cosmetic appearance, the nasoseptal mucosal graft closely approximates the ideal graft tissue.1 Despite these advantages, nasal mucosa remains underutilized in ocular surface reconstruction, possibly reflecting ophthalmologists' reluctance to harvest it.6 This concern is justified, and we therefore advocate an interdisciplinary approach with otorhinolaryngologic harvest, as free nasal mucosal grafts are standard procedures in this specialty, for example for skull base reconstruction.11 It is worth noting that nasal tissue has also been employed in eyelid reconstruction in a fundamentally different context: composite chondromucosal grafts combining septal cartilage with overlying mucosa have been described as structural replacements for the tarsal plate in full-thickness eyelid defects.12 The technique presented here, by contrast, employs a mucosal graft without cartilage to replace scarred conjunctiva and reconstruct the fornix. With this distinction in mind, the nasoseptal graft offers several advantages: adequate harvest size, preservation of olfactory mucosa, and fewer complications compared to turbinate, oral, or hard palate harvest.

Despite these advantages, the technique carries potential donor- and recipient-site morbidity that warrants consideration. At the nasal donor site, recognized complications of septal mucosal harvest include septal perforation, prolonged crusting, delayed remucosalization, squamous metaplasia, and—after more extensive harvest—nasal saddling.8,13 In our patient, several features of the technique were specifically intended to limit these risks. The graft was harvested primarily from the osseous portion of the posterior dorsal septum (Cottle areas 3 and 4), away from the anterior cartilaginous septum where perforation is most likely and most symptomatic. The site of mucosal excision is located caudally on the dorsal septum. Cranially, toward the nasal bridge, a wide margin of unaffected tissue remains, preserving adequate septal support; thus, the development of a saddle nose deformity can be excluded. Since the harvest was unilateral and was performed over bone rather than cartilage, the septal cartilage and the contralateral mucoperichondrium or mucoperiosteum remained intact, thereby avoiding the through-and-through cartilaginous defect that underlies classic anterior symptomatic septal perforation.8 The exposed donor bed was further covered and protected with a septal splint for two weeks to create a moist chamber, promoting secondary re-epithelialization—an approach analogous to free mucosal grafting of the nasoseptal flap donor site, which reduces crusting and shortens remucosalization time.13,14 Performing the harvest jointly with otorhinolaryngology, for whom free nasal mucosal grafts are routine, provides a further safeguard.11 At the recipient site, two phenomena merit attention. Because nasal mucosa is goblet-cell rich, excess mucous production may occur; in our case this secretory capacity was advantageous and restored adequate lubrication, but persistent hypersecretion could, by analogy with other mucous-membrane grafts, be managed conservatively with graft excision, cryotherapy, or botulinum toxin injection.15 In addition, chronic ocular-surface exposure in cicatrizing disease can promote epithelial keratinization or squamous metaplasia of the graft, as reported for both nasal and oral mucosal grafts.6 Meticulous recipient-bed preparation, appropriate graft sizing with trimming of redundant tissue at the limbus and eyelid margin, sustained surface lubrication, and close postoperative surveillance help to mitigate these risks. In the present patient, neither donor- nor recipient-site complications were observed throughout the two-year follow-up. Nonetheless, awareness of these potential sequelae remains important when adopting this technique.

The septal graft's thin periosteal or perichondrial layer with overlying mucosa and goblet cells provides superior tensile strength and morphology compared to inferior turbinate or alternative donor sites. Naumann and colleagues concluded their pioneering work on nasal turbinate mucosal grafts in 1990 by questioning the ideal nasal donor site.7 Based on our limited experience presented here, we propose the nasal septum as an alternative for ocular surface reconstruction.
