Factors influencing insomnia among nurses in Wuhan, China, two years after the COVID-19 outbreak: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Li Wen, Zhiying Wan, Fangli Li, Yunyan XianYu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFrontline nurses at designated hospitals endured psychological distress and sleep problems during the COVID-19 pandemic. However, the long-term effects have not yet been thoroughly investigated. This study examines their sleep status and related factors two years later to inform staffing and mental health support.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted at a large tertiary COVID-19-designated hospital in Wuhan, China, in 2022. Using convenience sampling, we recruited 552 clinical nurses. Participants completed the Insomnia Severity Index (ISI), Perceived Stress Scale (PSS), and Maslach Burnout Inventory-Human Services Survey (MBI-HSS). Quantile regression was used to evaluate the relationship between variables and insomnia. The findings were interpreted through the lens of the 3P model of insomnia in this cohort.ResultsThe median ISI-7 score was 5.00 (7.00), and 6.88% of nurses had moderate-to-severe insomnia. According to the quantile regression results, female gender, the nucleic acid testing site and self-reported COVID-19-related posttraumatic stress symptoms, which were interpreted as predisposing factors, were associated with insomnia at the 10th and 10th-50th quantiles, respectively. Average weekly working hours (> 60 h and 41-60 h) attributed to precipitating factors were associated with insomnia at the 10th and 75th quantiles. Emotional exhaustion and perceived stress were associated with insomnia across all quantiles, while personal accomplishment was associated with it at the 75th quantile, aligning with the model’s perpetuating factors.ConclusionsInsomnia remains a health concern among nurses two years after the outbreak. The 3P model associates insomnia with vulnerabilities, traumatic exposure, and ongoing occupational stress. Multi-level, theory-based interventions should target these factors to support nurses’ well-being and reinforce healthcare system resilience.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04498-y.

Background

Healthcare workers, particularly nurses, comprise the largest segment of the medical workforce. They play a critical role during public health crises. Nurses are exposed to significant psychological challenges when working in high-pressure environments. Previous studies have shown increased anxiety, depression, and stress among nurses in such situations [1, 2]. These psychological concerns can diminish work efficiency and the quality of patient care [2, 3]. Insomnia is especially prevalent and can have debilitating effects [4–6].

Insomnia refers to dissatisfaction with sleep quantity or quality, such as difficulty falling asleep, difficulty maintaining sleep, or early morning awakening, and being unable to fall back asleep [7]. It is associated with hypertension, cardiovascular disease, burnout, anxiety, and depression [8, 9].

The COVID-19 pandemic had an unprecedented global scope and duration. It posed a profound and sustained threat to the mental health of medical personnel. Nurses in Wuhan, the initial epicenter, experienced particularly acute impacts. Research conducted during the peak of the pandemic found that 52.8% of frontline nurses in the city experienced insomnia [10]. Another study reported that 60% of this group had poor sleep quality [11]. Evidence from prior pandemics suggests that psychological sequelae among healthcare workers may persist for extended periods after outbreak resolution [12]. However, most existing research on nurses’ psychological well-being has focused on the acute phase, leaving a substantial gap in understanding the long-term effects as the pandemic evolves into endemic management.

Insomnia has many causes. To provide a clear integrative framework, this study applies the 3P model of insomnia [13, 14], which conceptualizes chronic insomnia as the result of predisposing, precipitating, and perpetuating factors. Predisposing factors are pre-existing vulnerabilities that increase the risk of developing insomnia. Precipitating factors are stressful life events or environmental pressures that trigger an acute episode of insomnia. Perpetuating factors are cognitive and behavioral patterns that sustain the insomnia state. Among nurses, common predictors can be understood through this lens. Demographics and work-related variables often serve as predisposing or precipitating factors [10, 11, 15]. Gender, professional experience, direct care of COVID-19 patients, and trauma exposure are associated with poor sleep [10, 16]. Acute and chronic stress [10, 17] and burnout [18–20] also play significant roles that may precipitate initial sleep disturbances and perpetuate them over time. Burnout is defined by emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and low personal accomplishment [21]. It correlates with sleep problems in meta-analyses, with emotional exhaustion being a key perpetuating factor of impaired sleep quality [19]. Additionally, perceived stress has emerged as a key correlate of insomnia among nurses during the pandemic [15].

It is essential to assess this cohort’s psychological health at a defined post-crisis interval. Two years after the initial COVID-19 outbreak in Wuhan, available data on nurses’ insomnia remain limited. This study aims to identify the associated factors among nurses from a former COVID-19 designated hospital guided by the 3P model. Quantile regression was employed to uncover differential effects across the spectrum of insomnia severity. The findings are intended to inform targeted and theory-driven support strategies in the post-pandemic period.

Method

Study design

A descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted.

Study setting and sample

This study included 552 nurses from a Grade A tertiary hospital in Wuhan, China, between March 7 and 15, 2022. This was two years after the COVID-19 outbreak. China and the entire world have now entered the “post-pandemic” or pandemic-normalized stage of epidemic prevention and control. Convenience sampling was used. Inclusion criteria required participants to be registered nurses worked during the pandemic. Exclusion criteria included resignation during the two years.

The sample size was determined based on requirements for multiple linear regression. These recommend 5 to 10 times the number of independent variables, plus an additional 20% for potential data loss [22]. The study included 15 variables (11 sociodemographics, 1 perceived stress, and 3 burnout dimensions). Thus, the required sample size was 94 to 188. Our final sample of 552 exceeds this, ensuring reliable findings.

Measurements

Demographic questionnaire

A demographic questionnaire was developed to collect information on gender, age, marital status, education, professional title, years of work, current department, monthly income level, average weekly hours, jobs during the pandemic, and self-reported COVID-19-related posttraumatic stress symptoms.

Measurement of self-reported COVID-19-related posttraumatic stress symptoms

In this study, COVID-19-related posttraumatic stress symptoms among nurses were assessed through self-reporting. If nurses reported having one or more DSM-5 symptom clusters tied to the outbreak, they were identified as having COVID-19-related posttraumatic stress symptoms. Re-experiencing: intrusive memories, nightmares, or flashbacks related to the outbreak. Avoidance: unwillingness to discuss work or to avoid reminders like people, places, or activities. Hyperarousal: being constantly on guard or easily startled, especially around infection risks. Negative alterations in cognition/mood: feeling emotionally distressed when recalling outbreak events.

The 7-item Insomnia Severity Index (ISI-7)

The Insomnia Severity Index (ISI) was developed by Morin in 1993 [23] to assess sleep over the past two weeks. It has 7 self-assessment questions, each scored from 0 (none) to 4 (extremely). The total score ranges from 0 to 28. Higher scores indicate more severe insomnia and stress. Scores: 0–7 no insomnia, 8–14 mild, 15–21 moderate, and 22–28 severe. The Chinese ISI-7 version has been validated [24]. Cronbach’s α was 0.812 in this study.

Perceived Stress Scale-10 Items (PSS-10)

The PSS, created by Cohen, Kamarck, and Mermelstein [25], measures stress and the extent to which a respondent’s life feels uncontrollable. It has 10 items, each rated on a 5-point Likert scale (never = 0 to always = 4), for a total score of 0–40. Higher scores indicate greater stress. Cutoffs: 0–13 low, 14–19 medium, and 20 + high. It is reliable for a Chinese population [26]. Cronbach’s α was 0.793 in this study.

Maslach Burnout Inventory-Human Services Survey (MBI-HSS)

Nurses’ burnout was measured by the Chinese version of the Maslach Burnout Inventory-Human Services Survey (MBI-HSS) for Medical Personnel (MP) [27, 28]. It contains 22 items with three dimensions: emotional exhaustion (EE, 9 items), depersonalization (DP, 5 items), and personal accomplishment (PA, 8 items). Each item was rated on a seven-point Likert scale. For EE and DP, higher scores mean more severe burnout. For PA, lower scores mean more severe burnout. Scores of 19–26 or ≥ 27 on EE, 6–9 or ≥ 10 on DP, and 34–39 or ≤ 33 on PA indicated moderate or high burnout for each dimension [27, 29]. The Cronbach’s α of the MBI-HSS was 0.847 in this study. The Cronbach’s α for the three dimensions was 0.774, 0.868, and 0.861, respectively.

Data collection

The questionnaire was released through WenJuanXing. It was converted into a QR code. Data collectors were trained to ensure all understood the scales. Clinical departments were visited to inform participants about the study’s objectives. Participants accessed the anonymous questionnaires via QR code after providing informed consent. Surveyors provided instructions to ensure complete responses on-site using participant IDs. Data confidentiality was strictly maintained. All items in the questionnaire are mandatory; it is not possible to skip any or submit a blank questionnaire. The mandatory responses and the on-site verification ensured the completeness of the questionnaire collection. A total of 552 nurses completed the questionnaires voluntarily.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS version 25.0 for Windows [30] and R version 4.4.1. Categorical data are presented as counts or percentages. Because the continuous variables did not follow a normal distribution, data were described using the median, interquartile range (IQR), maximum, and minimum values. Univariate analysis of insomnia scores was conducted using the Mann-Whitney U test or the Kruskal-Wallis H test as appropriate. Spearman’s rank correlation examined relationships among insomnia, burnout, and perceived stress. Before constructing the multivariate model, we evaluated multicollinearity among the predictor variables using ordinary least squares (OLS) linear regression. The variance inflation factors (VIFs) of the independent variables ranged from 1.060 to 4.731 (See Supplementary Table 1), all of which were less than 5 [31]. Therefore, there was no multicollinearity in this model. Due to the apparent skewness in the ISI-7 score (skewness 1.148, kurtosis 1.325), quantile regression analysis was used to estimate the effects of variables on the severity of insomnia at the 10th, 25th, 50th, 75th, and 90th percentiles. A two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The demographic and occupational characteristics of the participants

The average age of participants was 28.61 ± 4.71 years, ranging from 20 to 56. The majority of participants were female (97.46%). 54.35% were married. 95.11% had a Bachelor’s degree or above. The average working years of the participants were 6.10 ± 4.65 years. 61.05% received the junior title. 43.48% of the monthly income fell within the range of 3,000 to 5,000 yuan, and 42.21% was between 5000 and 10,000 yuan. 48.55% of participants worked an average of 20–40 h per week, while 46.38% worked 41–60 h per week. 79.17% of participants provided care for COVID-19 patients in inpatient ward settings in Wuhan in 2020. 20.65% reported having COVID-19-related posttraumatic stress symptoms.

Correlation among insomnia, burnout, and perceived stress

Insomnia was significantly positively correlated with perceived stress (r = 0.441, p < 0.001), emotional exhaustion (r = 0.525, p < 0.001), and depersonalization (r = 0.395, p < 0.001). Insomnia was negatively correlated with personal accomplishment (r=-0.089, p = 0.036).

Multivariable quantile regression on the score of ISI-7 among nurses

Table 5 describes the findings of the quantile regression analysis of insomnia among nurses. Quantile regression analysis showed that being female and the nucleic acid testing site were protective factors for insomnia at the 10th quantile. Other departments did not show any independent, statistically significant effects at any quantile. Average weekly working hours ranging from 41 to 60 h at the 75th and exceeding 60 h at the 10th percentiles were associated with insomnia. Insomnia is more affected by self-reported COVID-19-related posttraumatic stress symptoms at lower quantiles (10th-50th). Personal accomplishment was associated with insomnia at the 75th quantile. Emotional exhaustion and perceived stress were significantly associated with insomnia from the 10th percentile to the 90th percentile. And the intensity of their effects increased with the quantile of insomnia.

Discussion

This cross-sectional study identified a 27.72% prevalence of insomnia among nurses two years after the outbreak. Although this is a notable decrease from rates reported during the pandemic’s peak [32], it remains significantly higher than pre-pandemic levels [33]. The 3P model provides a valuable framework for interpreting these cross-sectional findings. While the study design does not allow assessment of temporal trajectories, the model helps contextualize the results. The quantile regression results of this study show that gender, the nucleic acid testing site, and having self -reported COVID-19-related posttraumatic stress symptoms act as predisposing/protective factors for insomnia in the early stage. Average weekly working hours are essential precipitating factors for different insomnia quantiles. Emotional exhaustion, personal accomplishment, and perceived stress act as perpetuating factors and have significant impacts on the entire quantile or higher quantiles of insomnia. The acute impact of the pandemic may have diminished for many, yet strong perpetuating factors likely sustain symptoms in vulnerable individuals.

Predisposing and protective factors

The findings underscore factors that may influence baseline vulnerability. At the lowest percentile of insomnia, female gender and the department where the nucleic acid testing site were associated with a lower ISI-7 score. Possible reasons for this include, on the one hand, the relatively small sample size of male and nucleic acid testing nurses in this study. On the other hand, women may be better at early identification and taking active self-regulation strategies when symptoms are mild. The short-term high-intensity tasks and clear sense of mission prevented it from becoming a chronic stressor, reducing the initial risk of insomnia. The non-significance of conventional high-pressure departments (such as the ICU) in the multivariate model suggests that the environmental impact in these departments may have complex mechanisms. However, this effect was only significant at the 10th percentile, suggesting that after insomnia progresses to moderate to severe stages, other factors may mask the gender and department differences as a basic predisposing factor.

Self-reporting having COVID-19-related posttraumatic stress symptoms had a significant positive effect at the 10th-50th percentiles. According to the 3p model of insomnia, COVID-19-related posttraumatic stress symptoms are closely linked to the model’s predisposing factors. Although there is debate about whether epidemic stressors strictly meet the DSM-5 PTSD criteria A, evidence confirms that these experiences can cause clinically significant posttraumatic stress symptom clusters [34]. These symptoms can disrupt sleep by causing excessive arousal, intrusive thoughts, and avoidance behaviors [35]. Nurses who reported significant posttraumatic stress symptoms are more likely to feel unsafe, which disrupts their bodies’ ability to stay balanced and prevents restful sleep. However, at higher quantiles, the impact of psychological trauma is no longer significant. Possible reasons for this include that when the degree of insomnia is severe, other stronger maintaining factors may mask its influence. Additionally, long-term severe insomnia not only may lead to neuroendocrine system disorders but also may mask the specific neurobiological markers of psychological trauma by affecting the stress response and cognitive function [36, 37]. Early identification of psychological trauma symptoms among nurses and providing structured peer support and psychological first aid can help prevent acute stress from turning into chronic stress symptoms, making it a valuable and cost-effective investment.

Precipitating factors

Working hours, as a precipitating factor, are a continuous and high-intensity source of work pressure. Compared with working less than 40 h per week, working 41–60 h per week has significant positive effects on higher insomnia quantiles. This finding indicates that excessive working hours do not uniformly affect all nurses, and the harm is particularly prominent among those with moderate to severe insomnia levels. Working hours exceeding 60 h per week are significant at the 10th percentile. This indicates that extreme workloads play an important role in early triggering. Previous studies have also shown that long-term shift work has a significant negative impact on nurses’ sleep patterns [38]. Long working hours not only reduce nurses’ sleep time but may also increase arousal levels by disrupting physiological systems, affecting sleep onset and maintenance, and leading to a decline in sleep quality. Irregular working hours can destabilize the sleep-wake rhythm, leading to chronic insomnia. Managers should improve nurses’ work schedules, reasonably control working hours, and provide mandatory rest guarantees and psychological support.

Perpetuating factors

A key finding of this study was the significant impact of job burnout, especially emotional exhaustion and personal accomplishment. The results showed that emotional exhaustion is significantly present across all insomnia quantiles; 51.45% of nurses have a severe level of personal accomplishment, which is significant at the higher quantiles of insomnia (75th). This is consistent with a meta-analysis finding that low personal achievement is often less strongly associated with adverse outcomes than emotional exhaustion [39]. In the 3P model, they were classified as perpetuating factors. A state of high burnout levels fosters pre-sleep rumination and cognitive hyperarousal, disrupting sleep [40]. The resulting non-restorative sleep, in turn, further depletes emotional resources. This creates a self-sustaining cycle that perpetuates insomnia even after initial triggers have subsided [19]. Addressing burnout can help break this cycle. Implementing rotating recovery shifts to ensure regular, sufficient rest, increasing work autonomy, providing constructive feedback, and establishing a mechanism for recognizing meaning may help nurses recover physically and mentally, reduce burnout, and restore healthy sleep patterns.

Perceived stress also emerged as a significant risk factor, primarily functioning as a perpetuating factor within the 3P framework. It keeps a state of physiological hyperarousal [41], incompatible with sleep, particularly among individuals with high sleep reactivity [42, 43]. These factors help maintain insomnia long after the initial cause has resolved. Consequently, adding stress management techniques, such as cognitive-behavioral strategies and mindfulness, to regular support programs for nurses is vital to help reduce this ongoing problem.

Limitations and strengths

This study has several important strengths. It was conducted at a critical juncture, two years after the initial COVID-19 outbreak, and focused on nurses working at a former COVID-designated hospital in the pandemic’s epicenter, offering valuable insights into long-term effects. Using standardized, internationally validated tools (ISI, PSS, and MBI) also improves the reliability and comparability of the results. Quantile regression was used to analyze the influencing factors of insomnia.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, this study employed the 3p model of insomnia as the explanatory framework, aiming to provide a structured framework for understanding the findings. However, this study adopted a cross-sectional design, which prevented the establishment of temporal and causal relationships between variables. Therefore, classifying the variables as predisposing, precipitating, and perpetuating factors was a post hoc theoretical deduction based on the classic literature and theoretical logic of this model. Future research should employ longitudinal designs to track the long-term trajectory of mental health recovery in this cohort. Secondly, this sample was nurses who were still employed at a former COVID-19-designated hospital in Wuhan. There may be a selective bias in terms of sample characteristics. Therefore, the conclusions of this study cannot be directly generalized to all nurses in Wuhan or to the entire nursing profession nationwide. Multi-center sampling across different regions and levels would help make the findings more widely applicable. Thirdly, the data were self-reported, which can introduce bias. Nurses who left the profession were not included, so the psychological impact of the pandemic may be underestimated. Future studies should employ mixed methods, incorporating objective measures (e.g., actigraphy for sleep) and qualitative interviews, to provide a more comprehensive understanding. Finally, although key variables were examined, other potentially important factors, such as social support, psychological resilience, and leisure activities, were not included. Including a broader range of possible risk and protective factors in future studies would help build stronger predictive models. In addition, the measurement of the working environment was limited to department classification and did not include more detailed objective indicators (such as the patient-nurse ratio, department-specific stressors). Future research could adopt a multi-level model to simultaneously measure stress characteristics at the department level and exposure at the individual level, thereby more accurately decomposing the impact of the environment.

Conclusions

This cross-sectional study demonstrates that insomnia remains a significant health concern among nurses working at a former COVID-designated hospital two years after the COVID-19 outbreak. Interacting factors, including demographics and pandemic-related traumatic predispositions, work-related precipitating factors, and perpetuating factors such as burnout and chronic stress, drive this chronicity. These findings support a shift towards targeted interventions that address these dimensions by reducing vulnerabilities, preventing trauma, and breaking the cycles of exhaustion. Creating trauma-informed and supportive work environments is essential to protect nurses’ well-being and ensure the healthcare system’s resilience in future crises.
