Weight stigma in Latin America and the Caribbean: a call to action
Authors: Stuart W Flint, Taissa Vila
Published: 2026

As noted in the call for papers,1 research has emerged from across the world evidencing the prevalence and impact of weight stigma and discrimination. Despite sporadic reports from across Latin America and the Caribbean (LAC), there is a paucity of empirical evidence from this region. Two years on, this Special Collection represents the first coordinated body of evidence on weight stigma and discrimination from the region, fulfilling what the call for papers set out to achieve. Through original articles, reviews and commentaries, this collection of papers contributes with the much needed empirical evidence, and in doing so, provides the foundations and impetus to call for multi-level action that addresses weight stigma from policy to practice.

To provide an understanding of what is known about weight stigma within LAC, Viola and colleagues synthesize findings from over 100 studies across the region.2 They report that weight stigma — including bullying and discrimination — is prevalent across many settings in society including healthcare, schools, media and workplaces. This is further supported by Vázquez-Velázquez and colleagues’ national study of the attitudes, stigma and knowledge of healthcare professionals and the general population in Mexico.3 With a total of 3859 participants (2597 healthcare professionals and 262 general population), they present evidence of weight stigmatizing attitudes in healthcare professionals, including in those who provide care for people living with obesity in clinical settings. Both healthcare professionals and the general population living with obesity report higher weight bias internalization. The study evidences some of the harms of weight stigma experiences in healthcare, where, for instance, 79% of people living with obesity avoid medical visits and over 50% feel judged by doctors.

Focusing on the experience of weight stigma, Scagliusi and colleagues systematically reviewed 46 studies from across LAC. In line with evidence from across the world, their review identified that experiences of weight stigma are associated with emotional harm, social exclusion, bullying, moral stereotypes, and family-related issues.4 However, specific to LAC, their review also identified the experiences of weight stigma were rooted in relational dynamics that are shaped by moral judgements, social exclusion and family interactions, a finding that is culturally distinct from patterns documented in North America and Europe. Their review was complemented by Saquimux Contreras and colleagues’ study that explored the patient experience of attending a gynaecology clinic in southeast Brazil.5 Their study found that experiences of weight stigma influenced the way that women perceived their body weight and shape, and subsequently, how they shift perceptions of nutrition and physical activity towards social actions that reflect and reproduce weight bias and stigma.

Recognizing the pervasiveness and impact of weight stigma as evidence in the articles above, Chávez-Manzanera and Vázquez-Velázquez report on the newly created clinical practice guidelines for managing overweight and obesity in adults in Mexico.6 These guidelines propose healthcare professional education and early awareness, as well as the adoption of non-stigmatising clinical practice that reflects the complex, multifactorial nature of obesity, and support improved communication strategies. The Mexican guideline is a big step in the right direction and should serve as an example for the region.

Interventions to address weight stigma are desperately needed, not just in Latin America and the Caribbean, but across the world. de Oliveira da Fonseca and colleagues conducted a randomised controlled trial and feasibility assessment to assess the acute effects of a mindfulness-based eating intervention on internalised weight stigma in people living with obesity and anxiety.7 They report improvements in a range of psychological outcomes despite no improvement in inhibitory food control in the mindfulness eating group. Underlining the importance of weight bias internalisation, de Oliveira da Fonseca and colleagues report that higher weight bias internalisation was associated with higher anxiety and lower body appreciation.

In LAC, obesity is a top public health priority, and with that, an increase in policy, typically focused on prevention, has been observed. However, research suggests that policy is also a key driver of weight stigma, influencing public knowledge and opinion, whilst acting as a structure that dictates healthcare access and safety standards. In doing so, policy affects health on national and local levels. Using a Weight Stigma Heat Map (WSHM) to assess thirteen national food-based dietary guidelines and four technical documents from LAC countries, Lopez-Arana and colleagues8 report that weight loss is commonly portrayed as a universal goal, the use of alarmist language about obesity, and that obesity was framed as entirely modifiable through individual agency without recognition of individual differences and clinical context. This framing ignores the vast evidence that obesity is not a matter of personal choice, but rather may result from a wide array of biological, psychological, social, economic and environmental factors and that there is no one combination of factors that determines weight status. On a positive note, they identified that a more inclusive, health-promoting environment was adopted by Saint Vincent and the Grenadines,8 resisting direct associations between weight and behaviour that can lead to the moralization of populations whose policies aim to serve.

Collectively, papers within this Special Collection highlight the need for healthcare professional education to address misconceptions about the controllability and causes of obesity,9 and to promote more empathetic care for people living with obesity. The collection also evidences the widespread nature of weight stigma across society in LAC, and thus, the need for interventions to improve public attitudes towards people living with obesity and challenge stereotypes, biases and discrimination. These interventions must reflect current scientific evidence about the complex,8 multifaceted causes of obesity, where obesity is driven by biological mechanisms.
