Urban Voids and Neighborhood Health: Assessing Associations Between Vacant Land and Adverse Health Behaviors in Chicago
Authors: Liang Chen, Helen C S Meier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the relationships between vacant land and key adverse health behaviors, including smoking, insufficient sleep, and no leisure‐time physical activity (No LPA), across census tracts in Chicago, Illinois. Using both global regression and geographically weighted regression (GWR), we evaluated whether neighborhood vacant land ratios (VLRs) were associated with the prevalence of these adverse health behaviors and assessed how these associations varied spatially across the city. We found significant spatial clustering in both vacant land and health behavior indicators, and the spatial clustering patterns of neighborhood vacancy and adverse health behaviors were broadly consistent. In global models, higher VLRs were associated with higher prevalence of adverse health behaviors; after accounting for spatially autocorrelated errors, the associations remained robust for smoking and insufficient sleep but were attenuated for No LPA. GWR results further revealed clear spatial non‐stationarity, with stronger positive local associations concentrated in low‐income neighborhoods on the south and west sides. When overlaid with Healthy Chicago Zones (HCZs), the strong vacancy‐behavior associations aligned primarily with the West, Southwest, Near South, and Far South zones, highlighting these HCZs as priority areas where vacancy was most strongly linked to adverse health behaviors. Our findings support theories of neighborhood disorder and spatial inequality, emphasizing that vacant land is a potentially modifiable environmental determinant of health behaviors and calling for tailored interventions that consider local social and economic contexts to improve community health and advance health equity.Keywords: vacant land, health behavior, neighborhood disorder

Introduction

The Challenge of Vacant Land

Vacant land continues to be a persistent challenge in many American cities, particularly those grappling with ongoing population decline and economic downturns (G. Kim et al., 2020; J. Lee et al., 2019). Scholars have long acknowledged vacant land as urban eyesores and highlighted the array of problems it can bring to urban environmental, social, and economic systems (Bowman & Pagano, 2004; Chen & Conroy, 2024; Newman et al., 2018). Former industrial sites often carry the legacy of long‐term contamination, with pollutants that can leach into nearby soil and water over time (Bjelland, 2004; Sivak et al., 2021). Abandoned residential parcels frequently become informal dumping grounds, contributing to sanitation hazards and localized environmental degradation (Branas et al., 2011). When left unmaintained, these spaces are also prone to overgrowth by invasive vegetation, which can create ideal conditions for pests and disease vectors to thrive (Berland et al., 2020). Beyond environmental concerns, vacant land is both a contributor to and a signal of neighborhood disorder, escalating violence and crime, fostering a sense of insecurity, and eroding community cohesion (Branas et al., 2012; Hadavi et al., 2021). Vacancy can also accelerate disinvestment: concentrated, long‐term vacant lots can deter development, and the costs of monitoring and maintenance may impose substantial fiscal strain on local governments (Newman et al., 2018). In this sense, widespread vacancy can reshape the physical and social landscape of neighborhoods, undermining their livability, visual appeal, and capacity to attract and retain residents and investment.

In addition to its consequences for communities, vacant land is increasingly recognized as a critical contextual factor linked to both physical and mental health outcomes of residents. One study found that higher rates of neighborhood vacancy were associated with increased prevalence of non‐communicable diseases, such as high blood pressure, asthma, and diabetes, in the context of shrinking cities (Zhu et al., 2024). Other research has primarily focused on the impact of vacant land on mental health, emphasizing its role in contributing to neighborhood disorder and attracting violence and crime, which can foster feelings of fear, unease, and stress among residents, leading to adverse health outcomes (Sivak et al., 2021). Branas et al. (2012) discovered a link between increased vacancy levels and a heightened risk of assaultive violence at the block‐group level. Crime and the fear of crime are widely acknowledged as key factors influencing mental health and overall well‐being (Lorenc et al., 2012). Intervention studies further suggest that modifying vacant land conditions can yield measurable mental health benefits. South et al. (2018) demonstrated that greening vacant lots could significantly reduce depression, particularly in low‐income neighborhoods.

Although existing literature has demonstrated that vacant land is significantly associated with health outcomes at the neighborhood level, far less is known about how urban vacancy relates to health behaviors, which are key determinants of long‐term health. Health behaviors are recognized as critical mediating mechanisms linking broader structural and ideological environments to individual health outcomes (Hu et al., 2014; Liu et al., 2022; Short & Mollborn, 2015). Practically, they are estimated to contribute to approximately 40% of all deaths in the United States each year (McGinnis et al., 2002; Short & Mollborn, 2015). As urban vacancy serves as both an important contributor to and a key marker of neighborhood disorder, investigating its link to health behaviors is essential for a more comprehensive understanding of how the urban environment shapes health. Such inquiry also informs the development of more effective interventions aimed at mitigating the adverse health consequences of neighborhood disorder.

Vacant Land and Health Behaviors

Health behaviors, also known as health‐related behaviors, refer to actions and routine practices that influence health status and risk of mortality (Short & Mollborn, 2015). A large body of interdisciplinary scholarship spanning social epidemiology and urban studies demonstrates that health behaviors are not solely the product of individual preferences; rather, they are patterned by neighborhood contexts that structure opportunity, shape routine activities, and influence chronic psychosocial stress (Cohen et al., 2003; Diez Roux & Mair, 2010; Housman & Dorman, 2005; Liu et al., 2022). Vacant land is a particularly salient neighborhood contextual feature not only because it is highly visible and often spatially concentrated in disadvantaged neighborhoods, but also because it is widely interpreted as a key indicator of neighborhood decline and disorder (Bowman & Pagano, 2004; Chen, 2024; Garvin et al., 2013). Accordingly, vacant parcels may influence health behaviors by shaping both residents' perceptions of local environments and the disorder‐related features of these environments. First, vacant land functions as a visible marker of neighborhood disorder and an “on‐the‐ground” cue of decline, neglect, and weakened informal social control that can shape residents' perceptions, stress responses, and day‐to‐day behavioral choices (Garvin et al., 2013). Second, vacant land can also generate and intensify disorder by creating conditions that facilitate incivilities and opportunistic crime, exacerbate environmental hazards, and undermine social interaction and collective efficacy; these processes may elevate chronic psychosocial stress and, in turn, influence health behaviors (Benediktsson, 2014; Hill et al., 2005; Qin et al., 2025; Skogan, 1990/1992). In combination, these roles suggest dual pathways: vacant land may influence health behaviors directly as a salient disorder signal, and indirectly by worsening disorder‐related conditions that affect health behaviors through heightened perceived threat and stress, constrained outdoor activity, and weakened social cohesion.

Neighborhood disorder encompasses the visible and invisible characteristics of communities, including both physical and social indicators that may suggest a decline in order and community cohesion, ultimately diminishing the overall quality of life (Gracia, 2014; Qin et al., 2025). Vacant land plays a multifaceted role in the dynamics of neighborhood disorder. Its presence often serves as a visible symbol of neglect and abandonment, signaling a lack of investment and care within the community (Bowman & Pagano, 2004). Moreover, vacant lots can become hotspots for various forms of illicit activities, including illegal dumping, drug dealing, and vandalism (Branas et al., 2018; Hadavi et al., 2021; Stern & Lester, 2021). These activities not only create a sense of insecurity and fear among residents but also deter potential investors and homebuyers, perpetuating a cycle of disinvestment and decline (Newman et al., 2018). Neglected vacant land can also lead to environmental hazards such as overgrowth of weeds, breeding grounds for pests, and increased risk of fire hazards, further exacerbating the deterioration of the neighborhood's physical environment (Berland et al., 2020). Furthermore, vacant lots can impede social interactions and cohesion among residents, as they often serve as barriers to community engagement and collective action (Garvin et al., 2013). Collectively, the presence of vacant land can significantly contribute to neighborhood disorder by fostering an environment of blight, insecurity, and disconnection within the community (Skogan, 1990/1992).

Neighborhood disorder not only reflects existing problems within a community but also fosters an environment that encourages unhealthy behaviors, particularly in relation to crime and the fear of crime. When disorder is prevalent, it often creates a perception of lawlessness and vulnerability among residents, which may lead to increased instances of criminal activity and further heighten the fear of crime within the community (Gracia, 2014; O’Brien et al., 2019). Scarborough et al. (2010) conducted an investigation in Kansas City, Missouri, finding that perceived neighborhood disorder was strongly associated with residents' fear of crime. The fear of crime can have a pervasive effect on residents' daily lives, permeating their thoughts and behaviors with a sense of unease. Residents may find themselves avoiding outdoor activities, opting instead to remain indoors where they perceive safety. The criminology literature has illustrated that fear of crime can confine individuals' social and physical activities (Liska et al., 1988; Skogan & Maxfield, 1981). Shareck and Ellaway (2011) found that individuals who perceiving high crime in their neighborhood were more likely to be current smokers. “Fear of crime is a major urban stressor” (Nasar & Jones, 1997). Some other studies have demonstrated the significant link between stress and sleep disorders (Germain et al., 2003; E.‐J. Kim & Dimsdale, 2007; Lo Martire et al., 2020). Unhealthy behaviors associated with neighborhood disorder can ultimately contribute to adverse health outcomes.

Research Question

Previous studies have illustrated that vacant land both signals and contributes to neighborhood disorder, and neighborhood disorder can promote unhealthy behaviors, ultimately influencing health outcomes. However, while these suggest a clear pathway linking vacant land to health behaviors, few research has thoroughly investigated the relationship between vacant land and health behaviors. Understanding this relationship is crucial for comprehending the role of vacant land in influencing long‐term health outcomes. To address this gap, our study aims to explore the associations between vacant land and three key neighborhood‐level health behavior measures (smoking prevalence among adults, the percentage of adults sleep less than 7 hr, and the percentage of adults without leisure‐time physical activity) using Chicago, Illinois, the largest midwestern city, as the sample. Specifically, we examined whether there were consistencies in the spatial patterns between vacant land ratios (VLRs) and health behavior measures across neighborhoods. Moreover, we also investigated how the associations between vacant land and unhealthy behaviors varied across space.

This research holds significant implications for urban planning and public health initiatives. Understanding the relationship between vacant land and health behaviors can inform strategies to address the challenges related to neighborhood disorder and promote healthier communities. By understanding the spatial patterns of vacant land and unhealthy behaviors, policymakers can target interventions more effectively, potentially reducing health disparities and improving overall community well‐being. Additionally, this study contributes to the broader understanding of the complex interplay between urban environments and population health, offering insights that can guide future research and policy efforts aimed at fostering healthier urban environments.

Materials and Methods

Unit of Analysis and Sample

Neighborhood remains an elastic concept with varied definitions (R. J. Lee & Newman, 2021). In this study, we use census tract as the unit of analysis because the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) 500 Cities/PLACES project reports health data at the census‐tract level, which is the finest geographic resolution available for these estimates.

In this study, we selected Chicago, Illinois, the most populous city in the U.S. Midwest, as our sample city. Chicago has consistently been a geographic focus in the literature on vacant land (e.g., Chen, 2024; Hadavi et al., 2021; Stewart et al., 2019). In 2015, Chicago had 24,251 vacant lots (Figure 1), totaling 7,132 acres (310,686,028 square feet), which accounted for 4.8% of the city's total area (Chicago Metropolitan Agency for Planning (CMAP) 2015 Land Use Inventory). The distribution of vacant lots across Chicago exhibited significant spatial disparities. Predominantly, vacant lots were clustered in neighborhoods primarily situated on the south and west sides of the city, areas that also served as focal points for low‐income and minority neighborhoods (Anderson & Minor, 2020). Moreover, many studies have highlighted substantial discrepancies in health outcomes between White and minority residents in Chicago (Hunt & Whitman, 2015; Hunt et al., 2015; Oyana & Margai, 2010). Given those pronounced spatial variations, using Chicago as the sample city allows us to delve into how the spatial pattern of vacant land correlates with disparities in health behaviors across neighborhoods. In addition, Chicago provides a relatively large sample size for analysis. We excluded census tracts lacking available statistics and those in O’Hare International Airport, resulting in a sample size of 793 census tracts.

Vacant land distribution across census tracts in Chicago, IL in 2015. Source: CMAP 2015 Land Use Inventory.

Measures and Data

We quantify vacant lots within neighborhoods into a neighborhood‐scale metric, namely the VLR. This ratio represents the percentage of a neighborhood's total land area classified as vacant land. Consequently, if a neighborhood contains no vacant lots, its VLR is zero. Data on vacant lots were sourced from the Land Use Inventory maintained by the Chicago Metropolitan Agency for Planning (CMAP). This data set undergoes regular updates and serves various purposes, including regional planning, policy evaluations, and local technical planning assistance activities. Aligning with other data sets, we used the land use data from 2015. Within the CMAP Land Use Inventory, vacant land is defined as undeveloped land lacking intact buildings and infrastructure and is categorized as “vacant land” or “undeveloped land.”

In this study, we examined the measures of three major health behaviors: smoking, insufficient sleep, and no leisure‐time physical activity (No LPA). The smoking indicator represents the prevalence of current smokers among adults aged 18 and over. The insufficient sleep indicator is measured by the percentage of adults aged 18 and over who sleep less than 7 hr. The non‐LPA indicator denotes the percentage of adults aged 18 and over who report no leisure‐time physical activity. All those measures are assessed at the census tract level. We obtained the health behavior data from the CDC's 500 Cities/PLACES project. This project is a collaboration between CDC, the Robert Wood Johnson Foundation, and the CDC Foundation. PLACES offers model‐based, population‐level analyses and community estimates of health measures for all counties, places (incorporated and census designated places), census tracts, and ZIP Code Tabulation Areas (ZCTAs) across the United States. Since 2016, the CDC updates and releases PLACES data annually. Each year's release data are estimates for previous years. In this study, we used the PLACES data released in 2017. Specifically, the smoking and No LPA data are estimates for 2015, while the insufficient sleep data are estimates for 2014.

We controlled for key neighborhood‐level demographic and socioeconomic characteristics that prior research has consistently linked to health. Since age and race are both significant factors relating to population health and inequalities (Williams & Sternthal, 2010; Zanjani et al., 2006), we included the senior population ratio and the non‐White population ratio as primary covariates. To capture neighborhood socioeconomic conditions, we incorporated median household income as a tract‐level indicator of socioeconomic status, consistent with prior work on neighborhood SES and health (Robinette et al., 2017). We also included the homeownership rate to account for neighborhood housing stability, which has been associated with health and well‐being (Rahman, 2026; Rahman & Steeb, 2024). Senior population ratio is the percentage of individuals who were 65 years old and over. Non‐White population ratio is the percentage of individuals who did not identify as White alone. Median household income was measured as the tract‐level median household income (in 2017 U.S. dollars). Homeownership rate is the percentage of housing units occupied by owners. We obtained the neighborhood demographic and socioeconomic data from the 2013–2017 American Community Survey (ACS) 5‐year estimates.

In this study, we merged data sets from various sources based on 2010 census tract boundaries in the 2010 TIGER/Line Files and Shapefiles from the U.S. Census Bureau. All spatial data processing and analyses were conducted using the NAD83/StatePlane Illinois East coordinate reference system (EPSG: 3435).

Analytical Procedures

This study consists of two analytic steps. In the first step, we analyzed the spatial patterns of neighborhood‐level VLR and each of the three health behavior measures (smoking, insufficient sleep, No LPA). In particular, we employed the Anselin Local Moran's I statistic to identify statistically significant spatial clusters and outliers for the VLR and each health behavior measure. We also observed Moran's scatterplots to characterize the direction and strength of spatial dependency for each variable. In the second step, we estimated a series of regression models to assess both global and local associations between vacant land and health behaviors. We first fit global ordinary least squares (OLS) models to test whether tract‐level VLR was associated with each health behavior measure, adjusting for tract senior population percentage, non‐White population percentage, median household income, and homeownership rate. We then estimated spatial error models (SEM) to account for spatially autocorrelated errors attributable to unobserved spatially structured neighborhood processes and to obtain valid statistical inference under spatial dependence. Finally, we conducted geographically weighted regression (GWR) to examine whether and how the associations between VLR and health behavior measures varied across neighborhoods in Chicago. To enhance interpretability and policy relevance, we further assessed how spatial variation in the estimated associations corresponded to Healthy Chicago Zones (HCZs), identifying zones in which the relationships between vacant land and adverse health behaviors were relatively stronger.

Results

Spatial Patterns

Figure 2 presents 12 panels organized as a four by three layout. Each row corresponds to one study variable: VLR, prevalence of smoking, prevalence of insufficient sleep, and prevalence of no leisure‐time physical activity (No LPA). Each column provides a complementary view of spatial patterning. The first column maps the tract‐level distribution of each variable, with values classified into six quantile‐based groups. The second column shows results from the cluster and outlier analysis (Anselin Local Moran's I), which identifies statistically significant local spatial autocorrelation patterns. The cluster/outlier type distinguishes: high‐high (HH) tracts (high values surrounded by high values), low‐low (LL) tracts (low values surrounded by low values), high‐low (HL) outliers (high values surrounded primarily by low values), and low‐high (LH) outliers (low values surrounded primarily by high values). Statistical significance was evaluated at the 95% confidence level (p < 0.05). The third column presents the Moran's scatterplot for each variable, where the x‐axis represents the standardized (z‐scored) tract value and the y‐axis represents the spatial lag. Points in the scatterplot correspond to the same local association types shown in the cluster/outlier maps (HH, LL, HL, LH), illustrating the direction and strength of local spatial dependence.

Spatial patterns of vacant land ratios and neighborhood measures of smoking, insufficient sleep, and no leisure‐time physical activity (No LPA) across census tracts in Chicago, IL (N = 793). VLR, vacant land ratio.

Generally, Figure 2 suggests pronounced spatial clustering in vacant land and each adverse health behavior across Chicago census tracts. In the spatial distribution map of VLRs (Figure 2: a1), higher VLR values were concentrated primarily on the south and west sides of the city, whereas lower VLR values were more common in the north and portions of the lakefront area. The three health behavior measures display broadly similar geographic patterning (Figure 2: b1, c1, d1): higher prevalences of smoking, insufficient sleep, and No LPA were concentrated in many of the same south‐ and west‐side tracts, while comparatively lower prevalences were more evident in northern/lakefront neighborhoods.

Results from the cluster and outlier analysis (Figure 2: a2, b2, c2, d2) further indicate that these patterns reflect statistically significant local spatial dependence rather than random variation. For VLR, large HH clusters were evident on the south and west sides, while LL clusters were more prevalent in the north/lakefront portions of the city; HL and LH outliers occurred but were relatively sparse and spatially scattered. Comparable cluster structures were observed for the three health behaviors. Each measure exhibited sizable HH clusters in parts of the south and west sides and LL clusters in the north/lakefront area, suggesting that elevated levels of unhealthy behaviors tended to co‐occur geographically with elevated neighborhood vacancy.

The Moran's scatterplots (Figure 2: a3, b3, c3, d3) corroborate these findings by showing a clear positive relationship between tract‐level standardized values and their spatial lags, consistent with positive spatial autocorrelation. The strength of spatial dependence was substantial for all variables, with the fitted relationships indicating relatively high explained variation in the scatterplots (R‐squared: 0.62 for VLR, 0.77 for smoking, 0.88 for insufficient sleep, and 0.85 for No LPA). Overall, Figure 2 demonstrates that vacant land and adverse health behaviors were not evenly distributed across the city but instead exhibited coherent spatial clustering, with high‐burden areas concentrated on the south and west sides.

Spatial error models (SEM) revealed substantial residual spatial dependence across outcomes, as indicated by large and statistically significant spatial error parameters (λ = 0.742 for smoking, 0.766 for insufficient sleep, and 0.894 for No LPA; all p < 0.001). Accounting for spatially autocorrelated errors attenuated the estimated associations between VLR and the outcomes. VLR remained positively associated with smoking (β = 0.063, SE = 0.017, p < 0.001) and insufficient sleep (β = 0.054, SE = 0.012, p < 0.001), but the association with No LPA was reduced and no longer statistically significant (β = 0.016, SE = 0.018, NS). Notably, SEM fit improved markedly relative to OLS for all outcomes, as reflected in lower AIC values (smoking: 3554.8 vs. 3826.4; insufficient sleep: 3054.4 vs. 3297.6; No LPA: 3726.3 vs. 4353.3), supporting the presence of spatially structured unobserved factors and the appropriateness of spatial error correction.

Given global models are not able to capture spatial heterogeneity in the associations between vacant land and adverse health behaviors, we employed GWR to further examine spatial variations in these associations across neighborhoods. In GWR models, the same demographic and socioeconomic variables in the global models were controlled.

Figure 3 summarizes the GWR results, highlighting spatial heterogeneity in the association between VLR and each health behavior outcome. The left column maps the local VLR coefficients from the GWR models; only coefficients that are statistically significant at p < 0.05 are displayed, with non‐significant tracts shown in gray. The right column maps the local R‐squared values, indicating how well the local model explains outcome variation across tracts.

Geographically weighted regression maps for associations between vacant land ratio and neighborhood measures of smoking, insufficient sleep, and no leisure‐time physical activity (No LPA) in Chicago, IL (N = 793).

For smoking (row a), significant local VLR coefficients were predominantly positive across many tracts, with the strongest positive associations concentrated in parts of the south and west sides. A smaller set of tracts in the northwest exhibited significant negative coefficients. The corresponding local R‐squared surface showed moderate‐to‐high explanatory power across the city (approximately 0.44–0.92). For insufficient sleep (row b), the VLR coefficients were likewise spatially heterogeneous but were again primarily positive where significant, with clusters of stronger positive associations evident across multiple neighborhoods (notably in portions of the south and west sides). Local model fit was consistently high (approximately 0.54–0.97), indicating that the set of predictors explained insufficient sleep prevalence particularly well in many areas, though the strength of fit still varied geographically. For No LPA (row c), the local VLR coefficients showed the greatest spatial differentiation: significant positive associations were concentrated in selected neighborhoods (including areas on the south side and parts of the west side), while significant negative coefficients appeared in a smaller number of tracts. The local R‐squared values ranged from approximately 0.39–0.94, with higher explanatory power in several contiguous areas and weaker fit in others, underscoring that the determinants of No LPA may be more locally contingent.

Overlaying the GWR maps with the Healthy Chicago Zones (HCZ) map (https://www.chicago.gov/content/dam/city/depts/cdph/community‐health/hcez/downloads/HCEZ‐Playbook.pdf, accessed on 18 February 2026) revealed that the vacancy‐behavior associations were not evenly distributed across HCZs. The significant positive local VLR coefficients for smoking and insufficient sleep appeared to concentrate largely within the West, Southwest, Near South, and Far South zones. In contrast, the Northwest and North Central zones showed comparatively fewer tracts with significant positive VLR effects, and in some areas the association was weak or even negative, indicating that vacancy was less consistently linked to these behaviors in those northern zones. Patterns for No LPA were more spatially selective overall, with significant positive coefficients clustering in parts of the Near South/Southwest and selected Far South areas, while many tracts were non‐significant.

Discussion and Conclusion

Vacant land has long been regarded as both a symptom of and a contributor to neighborhood disorder. While prior studies have established links between neighborhood disorder and adverse health behaviors, there remains limited understanding of how vacant land specifically relates to adverse health behaviors. A deeper understanding of the spatial relationship between vacant land and health behaviors can enhance our insight into how neighborhood disorder shapes long‐term population health. Aiming at addressing the literature gap, improving the current understanding of urban vacancy, and informing targeted policies and practice, this study uses spatial analysis to examine the associations between VLR and three key adverse health behavior measures (smoking prevalence, insufficient sleep prevalence, and no leisure‐time physical activity (No LPA) prevalence) at the census tract level in Chicago.

Our findings reveal significant spatial clustering effects of both vacant land and the three health behavior measures, highlighting consistent spatial patterns between vacant land and health behavior measures. The GWR models indicate that the associations between VLR and health behavior measures vary across space, and other neighborhood contextual factors may influence these associations to some extent.

First, this study highlights that neighborhood‐level VLRs and health behavior measures are spatially clustered and autocorrelated in Chicago. There are observable spatial consistencies in the clusters of VLRs and these health behavior measures. This primarily indicates that health disparities have a spatial dimension and are related to locations and places. Yohan et al. (2020) pointed out that attributes of geographic context, including physical environment, social deprivation, and health care accessibility, can lead to spatial inequalities in health outcomes. Schulz et al. (2002) found that the residential segregation of African Americans in aging urban communities was a major contributor to health disparities in Detroit. Lynch et al.’s (2021) investigation in Milwaukee revealed that historical redlining, a place‐based practice of racism, was associated with present‐day health outcomes at the neighborhood level. Gibbons et al. (2020) found that spatial segregation was associated with clusters of poor health, and the poor self‐rated health clusters identified by their research were broadly consistent with the adverse health behavior clusters and high vacancy clusters identified in this study. Aligned with prior studies, this study affirms the spatial components of health disparities and further illustrates key adverse health behaviors, such as smoking, insufficient sleep, and No LPA, also exhibit spatial clustering. Furthermore, the alignment between clusters of high vacancy and adverse health behaviors suggests that neighborhood disorder, which is often manifested through concentrated vacant lots, could be a significant geographic contextual factor shaping health behaviors.

In this study, we observed associations between increased neighborhood‐level VLRs and higher prevalence of smoking, insufficient sleep, and No LPA. However, because vacant land is a physical marker of broader neighborhood disorder and often co‐occurs with other environmental stressors, such as crime, littering, and poor living conditions, this finding may not sufficiently support that vacant land is a direct driver of specific health behaviors. Instead, neighborhood disorder, as a contextual condition signified by vacant lots, may be the underlying factor shaping these behaviors. Given this, our finding is in line with prior studies, highlighting the robust link between neighborhood disorder and adverse health outcomes. Hill et al.’s (2005) study on 2,402 disadvantaged women in disadvantaged neighborhoods in Chicago, Boston, and San Antonio suggested that neighborhood disorder was linked to worse health and this link was mediated by psychological and physiological distress. Drawing upon data from the Los Angeles Family and Neighborhood Survey, Bjornstrom et al. (2013) found that neighborhood disorder was negatively associated with health and this association could be explained by perceptions of reduced social cohesion and heightened danger. In addition, prior studies have suggested that vacant land can contribute to heightened perceptions of stress and fear (Newman et al., 2018), and that elevated stress and fear are associated with smoking and insufficient sleep (Kumar et al., 2021; Lawless et al., 2015). Within this context, our findings may further indicate that vacant land, beyond symbolizing neighborhood disorder, could be part of the broader set of environmental conditions that elevate perceived stress and fear among residents, which in turn lead to increases in unhealthy behaviors.

We further examined the spatial variation in the associations between vacant land and the three adverse health behavior measures. The results show that the relationships between vacant land and both smoking and insufficient sleep are significantly positive across most census tracts in Chicago, with the exception of neighborhoods on the north side and in the downtown area. Those neighborhoods with non‐significant local associations are characterized by lower levels of vacant land, higher income levels, and higher property values. This pattern may indicate that while vacant land is associated with adverse health behaviors in many areas of the city, socioeconomic factors substantially shape the strength of these relationships. As such, planning and public health interventions addressing vacant land should be tailored to the socioeconomic context of each neighborhood to more effectively reduce disparities in health behaviors. Moreover, although the spatial error model did not reveal a significant relationship between vacant land and No LPA, the GWR results indicated that significant local relationships did exist in certain neighborhoods, especially in low‐income areas on the south side of the city. This finding also underscores the importance of accounting for spatial heterogeneity when assessing how vacant land relates to health behaviors.

By overlaying the univariate clustering maps and GWR maps with the Healthy Chicago Zones map, we identified that clusters characterized by high vacancy rates and high prevalence of adverse health behaviors were predominantly concentrated in the West, Southwest, Near South, and Far South zones. Notably, these four zones also exhibited significant associations between vacant land and adverse health behaviors. This highlights the need to prioritize these areas in planning and public health interventions. Efforts to address vacant lots, stabilize neighborhoods, and reduce broader signs of disorder in these zones may contribute to improvements in health behaviors and overall community well‐being.

While our study provides valuable insights into the relationship between vacant land and health behaviors, it is important to acknowledge several limitations that warrant cautious interpretation of our findings. First, while we find statistically significant associations between vacant land and health behaviors, these behaviors could be influenced by a wide range of co‐occurring neighborhood conditions, including pollution, littering, unsafety, and poor housing quality, which are not fully captured in our models. As such, vacant land should be understood as a marker of broader neighborhood disorder rather than a direct causal factor. Second, our sample is limited to Chicago, which may constrain the generalizability of our results to other cities. Future research endeavors should encompass a broader range of urban settings to validate and extend our findings across diverse geographical contexts. Third, although CDC's 500 Cities/PLACES data have been widely used in public health research and urban studies, it is worth noting that these data may introduce bias when used at small geographic scales. Finally, our study employs a quantitative research approach at the neighborhood level, which may overlook nuanced factors and variables that could influence the observed associations. Subsequent investigations could adopt qualitative research methods and explore the phenomenon at different geographical scales, including broader or narrower scales, or even at the individual level, to capture a more comprehensive understanding of the complex interplay between vacant land and health behaviors.

The findings of our study hold significant implications for policymakers and urban planners alike. First, the identification of spatial clustering effects of vacant land and health behavior measures underscores the importance of targeted interventions in areas where these clusters are prominent. Policymakers could use this information to prioritize resources and implement tailored initiatives aimed at mitigating neighborhood disorder and improving public health outcomes in these specific regions. Second, the consistent spatial patterns observed between vacant land and health behavior measures suggest the need for integrated approaches that address both neighborhood disorder and public health concerns simultaneously. Third, the spatial variation in associations across neighborhoods underscores that socioeconomic context plays a crucial role in shaping how vacant land relates to health. Effective interventions must therefore be tailored to neighborhood‐specific conditions, recognizing that the same landscape feature may have different implications depending on local socioeconomic and environmental factors. Finally, these findings point to the need for continued research examining the mechanisms linking neighborhood disorder to health behaviors. Evaluating the long‐term impacts of interventions that stabilize neighborhoods, reduce disorder, or repurpose vacant land will help determine which strategies most effectively promote health equity across urban communities.
