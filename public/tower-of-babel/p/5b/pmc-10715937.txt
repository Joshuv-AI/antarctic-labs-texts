How to Get Back Into the True Lumen During Chronic Total Occlusion Percutaneous Coronary Intervention∗
Authors: Emmanouil S. Brilakis, Salman Allana, Khaldoon Alaswad
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

Getting back in the true lumen after extraplaque (formerly “subintimal”1) guidewire crossing during chronic total occlusion (CTO) percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) is challenging. Several techniques have been developed for both antegrade and retrograde re-entry (Figure 1).2 At present, antegrade re-entry is achieved through the use of dedicated devices or various guidewires. CTO Crossing Strategy Classification and Nomenclature

CART = controlled antegrade and retrograde tracking; CTO = chronic total occlusion; LAST = limited antegrade subintimal tracking; STAR = subintimal tracking and re-entry.

Device-based re-entry is most often achieved using the Stingray balloon (Boston Scientific), a self-orienting, flat balloon with 2 180˚-opposed and offset exit ports. The flat balloon “hugs” the vessel, automatically positioning 1 exit port toward the true lumen. A stiff-tipped guidewire is advanced through the exit port, either directly into the distal true lumen (“stick and drive” technique) or followed by a polymer-jacketed guidewire that is more likely to advance into the distal vessel (“stick and swap” technique). The self-orienting flat balloon device is expensive, is not available in many countries, requires a long learning curve, can be hard to deliver to the re-entry zone, and is not always successful. The ReCross microcatheter (IMDS) or other dual-lumen microcatheters can also be used for re-entry.

The STAR technique involves the advancement of a knuckled, polymer-jacketed guidewire until it spontaneously re-enters into the distal true lumen, usually at a bifurcation point.4 The STAR technique carries high restenosis and reocclusion rates if stenting is performed; hence, it is usually used when all other crossing strategies have failed and is followed by balloon angioplasty only, without stent implantation, as an “investment” procedure. Two months later, PCI with stent implantation is usually performed to allow time for the dissections to heal and to minimize stent length.5 These re-entry techniques are based on 2-dimensional views and the injection of contrast medium to determine the wire position relative to the true lumen. Intravascular imaging adds another visual dimension and enables intentional wire manipulation to enter the true lumen. However, intravascular imaging–guided re-entry can be difficult for interventional cardiologists to master.6

In this issue of JACC: Case Reports, Sandesara et al7 expertly describe an innovative 4-F optical coherence tomography (OCT)-guided catheter for antegrade re-entry. Visualization of the true lumen was achieved with OCT, followed by rotation of the catheter until its “exit ramp” was oriented toward the true lumen. A high tip-stiffness guidewire was then directed toward the true lumen under OCT visualization and achieved re-entry, as confirmed by both angiography and OCT. Despite many years of anticipation, imaging-guided re-entry is currently available only for peripheral vessels. Sandesara et al7 should be congratulated for providing the first clinical description of coronary image-guided re-entry.

The OCT-guided re-entry catheter offers several advantages over self-orienting, flat balloon–based re-entry. First, and foremost, it allows targeted advancement of the guidewire without contralateral contrast medium injection, as is needed for the self-orienting, flat balloon. Second, it allows direct visualization of the distal true lumen and confirmation of intraluminal wire position. Third, it may allow faster re-entry with fewer guidewire manipulations, minimizing the risk of hematoma formation. Fourth, it may reduce the volume of contrast agent and dose of radiation.

OCT-guided re-entry has limitations. First, image interpretation and true lumen detection may be challenging, especially for operators who do not routinely use intravascular imaging. Second, its deliverability remains to be determined, given the OCT-guided re-entry catheter’s large distal profile (4-F vs 3.2-F for the self-orienting, flat balloon device), especially through calcification and tortuosity. Third, saline or contrast medium flushing of the catheter is needed to displace blood and to enable OCT beam penetration and visualization of the vessel wall, which may create or enlarge an extraplaque hematoma. Fourth, the cost of the device will likely have a significant impact on adoption. Fifth, it is yet to be determined whether most CTO PCI operators can quickly master the OCT-based re-entry technique. Sixth, OCT provides a reflection of the true lumen because OCT does not penetrate red blood cells. The operators used true lumen pulsation to confirm the true lumen location; pulsation might not be visible in low-pressure, distal circulation.

At present, the self-orienting, flat balloon device and STAR remain the key antegrade re-entry modalities, with great anticipation for the recently U.S. Food and Drug Administration-approved Triumph catheter (Teleflex), which has 6 wire exit ports for precise wire advancement and clear visualization. It also allows aspiration to decrease the size of the extraplaque hematoma and improve the likelihood of re-entry.8 The use of the catheter with 6 wire exit ports and the OCT re-entry catheter could increase the use of antegrade dissection and re-entry, which has been declining in recent years.9

Regardless of which device is used for re-entry, meticulous attention to technique can optimize the likelihood of success.2 First, strong guide catheter support is needed for all antegrade dissection re-entry techniques. Second, a guide extension should be routinely used, both to increase support and to minimize extraplaque hematoma formation. Third, antegrade dissection should be performed using orthogonal projection and contralateral injections to confirm that the wire is advancing in the right direction. Although we should “trust the knuckle,” the most commonly used dissection device, we should also verify that the wire is not entering a side branch or going outside of the vessel architecture. Fourth, antegrade contrast medium injections should not be performed. Fifth, guidewire manipulations at the distal cap should be minimized to reduce the risk of hematoma. Sixth, CTO PCI operators should develop experience and expertise in both knuckle wire advancement and the use of whichever antegrade re-entry device they have access to.

Although the retrograde approach often allows crossing, especially of complex occlusions, it also carries an increased risk of complications.10 Antegrade dissection and re-entry carries a lower risk and may be the only option for success when antegrade wiring fails and there are no retrograde options or when retrograde crossing fails.

Advancements in devices, techniques, and skillsets have increased the success rates and safety of CTO PCI among expert CTO PCI operators. New techniques, such as OCT-guided CTO PCI, could increase the success rates of many more operators. Mastering distal true lumen re-entry is of critical importance for CTO PCI, and the novel OCT re-entry catheter is a long-awaited and highly anticipated addition to our equipment on the road to making CTO PCI easier to master, more successful, and safer. Larger studies of OCT-based re-entry are the next step to establish the utility of this device in CTO PCI.
