Engaging youth in systems thinking for active recreation: piloting a Collective Intelligence approach
Authors: Jiani Ma, David Cao, Michael Hogan, Kristy A Bolton, Jo Salmon, Maja Vilhelmsen, Harriet Koorts
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractYouth active recreation is influenced by complex interactions among factors in the active recreation system. Systems approaches can help identify ways to intervene; however, young people are rarely involved in this process. This study piloted the use of Collective Intelligence (CI), a participatory systems approach, to engage young people in identifying barriers to active recreation and co-creating solutions. Three CI workshops were conducted in Victoria, Australia, involving nine participants aged 12–17. In each workshop, participants generated and prioritized barriers, co-created systems maps, and collaboratively designed solutions aligned to the mapped systems. Field notes captured reflections on facilitation and youth engagement. Across workshops, 48 barriers were identified and grouped into eight categories: Shyness and social skills, Lack of support, Financial, Time, Space and equipment, Public Transport, Safety, and Medical. Participants then co-created solutions across individual (n = 5), community (n = 11), and government (n = 5) levels. Individual-level strategies focused on enhancing social skills; community-level ideas focused on public transport and inclusive spaces; Government actions included subsidies for extracurricular activities. Reflections highlight the value of CI to engage young people in articulating interconnected influences in their lived experience with active recreation. Key strengths of the process included making complexity visible and actionable. Challenges included uncertainty of upstream issues, uneven participant engagement, conflation of terms, and maintaining focus. This study offers actionable strategies to potentially overcome these challenges for anyone who wants to engage young people in systems thinking. The inclusion of a youth researcher was valuable in supporting young people to contribute their ideas.

Background

Participation in active recreation—such as walking, cycling, and active play—offers promising opportunities to increase young people’s (aged 12–17) physical activity levels and improve their health and wellbeing. Despite clear benefits (Garcia et al. 2022), participation rates in Australia are low with ∼7.4% of adolescents (aged 15–17) participating in active recreation (Australian Sports Commission 2023). This low prevalence may be partly due to the inherent complexity of active recreation behaviours influenced by intersecting individual, social, and environmental factors (Garcia et al. 2022). Many structured programmes designed to enhance youth active recreation have focused on school and community settings (Owen et al. 2025). However, these programmes often overlook the complex levels of influence, leading to limited success in creating effective and sustainable changes in behaviours (Koorts et al. 2022).

Systems thinking approaches, which explicitly recognize and address this complexity, have gained increasing recognition in public health research (Rutter et al. 2019). These approaches, including systems mapping, facilitate structured, holistic exploration of the multifaceted determinants of health behaviours such as active recreation. Recent evidence demonstrates that, when combined with consumer involvement, systems approaches enable comprehensive mapping of system factors and identification of key leverage points (Emke et al. 2024), improved participant buy-in and consensus (Lee et al. 2022), and contextualization of interventions across multiple levels (Frerichs et al. 2020). Despite these advancements, research employing systems thinking in health research predominantly engages adult stakeholders, thereby potentially limiting the relevance and impact of research outcomes for young people (Nagata et al. 2025, Prescott and Hetrick 2025).

Active involvement of young people in research and decision-making processes is increasingly recognized as essential for developing relevant, effective, and sustainable strategies for improving health behaviours (Nicholson et al. 2024). However, youth involvement in improving health behaviours is not common practice (Queral et al. 2025); and when used, substantial practical and methodological challenges in authentically involving youth can occur. Challenges include their capacity to articulate system-level insights, maintaining equitable participation, and overcoming power imbalances in adult–youth interactions (Tinner 2024, Prescott and Hetrick 2025).

Collective Intelligence (CI) is uniquely suited for facilitating the active engagement of diverse groups, leveraging their collective expertise and lived experiences to identify barriers and co-create contextually appropriate solutions, as evidenced by its applications in social and health sciences (e.g. Hogan et al. 2015, Groarke and Hogan 2016). CI is a structured and facilitated process that employs Interpretive Structural Modelling (ISM) to enhance collaborative problem-solving, actively involve participants through meaningful decision-making roles, and systematically synthesize diverse perspectives into actionable outcomes (Warfield 2006, Hogan et al. 2015). It is designed to maximize the potential of group work by organizing the knowledge, experience, and reasoning of participants with a range of diverse backgrounds, perspectives, and values. In youth physical activity research, CI has been applied to map the barriers to and identify strategies in implementing motor competence interventions in school settings (Ma et al. 2021a, 2021b). Broader application of the CI approach aligns with several recommendations for youth engagement in research, such as mutual learning, shared decision-making, and youth empowerment (Nicholson et al. 2024, Tinner 2024, Prescott and Hetrick 2025). A recent study involving young people has demonstrated the efficacy of this approach in mental health contexts, showing that youth participants can actively contribute sophisticated insights when effectively facilitated, particularly through scenario-based engagement, interactive methods, and visual tools (Colleran et al. 2021). However, documented challenges include managing varied group dynamics, ensuring equitable participation among young people with differing confidence levels, and overcoming young people’s uncertainty or discomfort when discussing complex, systemic issues (Colleran et al. 2021, Tinner 2024).

The present study piloted the use of CI to engage young people in systems mapping and collective problem-solving related to active recreation. The aim was to (i) identify barriers and solutions to enhance active recreation as perceived by young people during the CI process and (ii) provide reflections on applying CI with young people and recommendations for future research.

Methods

Recruitment and sampling

Youth participants were recruited through a combination of social media ads via Meta platforms (including Facebook and Instagram), flyer distribution within community venues and local networks (e.g. libraries, community announcement boards), and convenience sampling. Potential participants accessed a Plain Language Statement (PLS) and prescreening questionnaire to confirm eligibility, with an option to refer interested individuals. Eligibility criteria for participants within the youth workshops included being aged between 12 and 17 years and living in Victoria, Australia. Eligible youth participants were asked to provide their parent/carer’s contact details. Parents of eligible youth received an email link with a PLS, which they reviewed with their child to ensure understanding before giving electronic consent. This approach ensures that youth and parents are fully informed and actively engaged in the consent process. Parents of eligible youth were asked to provide electronic consents and third-party consents before the workshop. Ethical approval was obtained from Deakin University (HEAG-H 156_2023).

There is no universally accepted ideal sample size for CI research. Therefore, we followed precedents in previous CI research involving young people (e.g. Colleran et al. 2021), which suggests that groups of 4–8 participants tend to foster optimal engagement and contribution in CI sessions. Participants were compensated with a $50 gift-card for their time and participation upon completion of the workshops.

Workshop design and structure

Three CI workshops were conducted in-person with younger (aged 12–14) youth (n = 3) and older youth (aged 14–17) (n = 6). Workshop 1 included two females and one male aged 15–17 years; Workshop 2 involved three female participants aged 12–14 years; and Workshop 3 included two females and one male aged 14–17 years. Each workshop involved two phases. The CI workshops took place between March and May 2024. The duration for each workshop was 2, 2, and 2.5 h, respectively. All three workshops were facilitated by the lead researcher (J.M.) and a youth researcher (D.C.) affiliated with the Centre for Multicultural Youth (CMY) in Victoria. A youth researcher is a young person who is trained in research, facilitation, and co-design skills to actively contribute to research projects that impact their communities. J.M. is a female researcher who holds a PhD in Physical Activity and Health, and a trained facilitator for CI workshops. D.C. is a young male who has 3 years of experience working with young people. D.C. experiences barriers to physical activity as a young person growing up in Australia and undertook training in CI and systems thinking through directed reading, mock workshops, and mentoring meetings.

Phase 1: Identifying and structuring barriers using Interpretive Structural Modelling

In all three workshops, participants identified barriers to active recreation through a structured idea generation process. Participants were then asked to vote on the barriers they believed to be most critical. ISM was then employed to map the interdependencies among the top ranked barriers.

The workshop began with an introduction to systems thinking using accessible, age-appropriate language and visual aids (via Canva) to explain concepts and the workshop process. This was followed by guided brainstorming where participants individually and collectively listed barriers they experienced or perceived related to active recreation participation. Facilitators then guided the groups through the ISM process, prompting discussions about the causal relationships between barriers, and capturing these relationships on the systems map (Ma et al. 2021a).

Phase 2: Co-creating solutions

Participants worked on developing solutions to the barriers identified in Phase 1. The session was structured to ensure that solutions were proposed at multiple levels: individual, community, and governmental. By incorporating solutions at these different levels, the workshop aimed to create a set of recommendations that could be tailored to various implementation settings.

To enhance engagement, participants were encouraged to use storytelling, visual aids, drawing exercises, and role-playing scenarios. These methods allowed young people to express their ideas in non-verbal formats when needed. A list of supplementary engagement and reflection methods used is provided in Supplementary File 1. Solutions addressing barriers to active recreation were recorded and categorized by the facilitation team, ensuring that all contributions were valued and included.

Data sources

Discussions during the workshops were audio-recorded with participant consent and transcribed for analysis. This ensured that the nuanced reasoning and discussion were accurately captured and analysed. Observational notes were taken by the facilitators during each workshop to document participant engagement, group dynamics, and the effectiveness of different facilitation strategies. These notes were used to reflect on and refine workshop practices.

Data analysis

Systems maps

To address the first aim (i.e. identifying barriers and solutions to enhance active recreation), the facilitation team (J.M. and D.C.) collated all barriers across the three workshops and performed a paired comparison analysis (Warfield and Cardenas, 2002). Specifically, after immersing in the data to gain an overall understanding, the facilitation team initiated the process of barrier comparison and open coding. The team iteratively compared pairs of barriers to evaluate their conceptual similarities, with input and oversight from MH. These conceptually similar barriers were then grouped under higher-order categories. The ISM-generated systems maps provided insights into the perceived relationships between barriers to active recreation. Proposed solutions generated during the workshops were then mapped to their corresponding barrier categories.

Field notes and observations

To address the second aim (i.e. providing reflections and recommendations on applying CI with young people), facilitators’ field notes, workshop observations, and transcriptions were synthesized through a structured, reflexive process. Data were organized thematically around the strengths, challenges, and actionable strategies related to applying CI with young people.

Workshop outcomes and reflections

Workshop outcomes (systems maps and solutions)

A total of 48 barriers related to active recreation participation were identified. These were organized into eight barrier categories (see Fig. 1a, and Supplementary File 2 for the full set of barriers). Systems maps revealed the interconnected nature of these barriers, where barriers such as cost and equipment challenges exacerbated other barriers including parental willingness and time availability (see Fig. 1b for an example from Workshop 3). For instance, one participant highlighted how poor infrastructure increases reliance on costly alternatives:

‘If parks had equipment for teens, I wouldn’t have to pay for expensive activities like trampolining or rock climbing.’ (Participant 1, 15yrs)

Additionally, time constraints were frequently mentioned, with one participant noting,

‘After a full day of school and homework, I don’t have the energy to take a long, expensive trip just to do something active.’ (Participant 3, 14yrs)

Through the ISM structuring process, these individually expressed barriers were collectively examined for their causal interdependencies, producing systems maps that revealed how barriers aggravate one another (Fig. 1b). By reflecting on the systems map collectively (Fig. 1b), participants focused on the generation of solutions for the following barrier categories: ‘Space and Equipment, Financial, and Time’ given that barriers in these categories are key drivers of negative influence in the system of interdependent barriers (i.e. these barriers are located on the left in system maps, which indicates they significantly aggravate the barriers to the right). Overcoming these barriers has the potential to increase chances of resolving other barriers in the map. The full set of solutions co-created across three workshops is provided in Supplementary File 2. For example, one participant discussed solutions related to equipment and transport:

‘If public transport was better and cheaper, more people could actually get to places to play.’ (Participant 2, 14 yrs)

Discussion

This exploratory study demonstrates that CI can be practically applied with young people aged 12–17 to facilitate systems thinking about active recreation, while also highlighting important process considerations for researchers planning similar work. The findings contribute to growing evidence on the benefits of engaging young people in systems thinking through participatory approaches.

Consistent with research highlighting the value of visual and interactive methods for simplifying abstract systems concepts (Chinapaw and Altenburg 2023), the CI process enabled participants to recognize how barriers to active recreation often interact and amplify each other across individual, social, and environmental levels. This system-level insight adds a novel dimension to youth physical activity research, which typically conceptualizes barriers in isolation. Drawing on the system-level insights, we recommend that policymakers ensure age-appropriate spaces and accessible transport; schools protect time for PA and coordinate after-school opportunities; and community organizations reduce cost barriers through subsidized equipment and free try-out events, thereby addressing multiple interacting barriers to active recreation.

A distinctive contribution of this study lies in the identification of potential interactions among barriers. The CI process made these dynamics visible and, crucially, guided participants towards developing system-level solutions. Similar participatory systems mapping work with young people shows that co-created maps can reveal leverage points for encouraging health behaviour change (Frerichs et al. 2020, Pronk and Lee 2024).

Study limitations

This study was exploratory in nature and thus our primary aim was not to produce generalizable findings but to gather insights into the process of engaging young people in systems thinking. Basic demographic information was collected to contextualize the sample (e.g. age and gender), while more detailed sociodemographic data (e.g. ethnicity, socioeconomic status) were not collected to minimize participant burden, given the small sample size. The relatively small sample size, limited information about diversity, and limited number of workshops reflect the exploratory aims of the study and were shaped by both methodological intent and contextual constraints.

Despite extensive outreach through social media, paid advertising, community organizations, and physical postering, participation remained lower than anticipated due to scheduling conflicts and limited parental availability. The underrepresentation of male participants may limit the diversity of perspectives. The involvement of a youth researcher helped mitigate some recruitment barriers through grassroots outreach and youth-appropriate communication, though future studies would benefit from stronger partnerships with youth-serving organizations. Additionally, while facilitator reflections provided valuable context, they may introduce bias, as participant feedback was not systematically collected. Future research should incorporate structured postworkshop evaluations or follow-up interviews to more robustly capture participant experiences.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated CI as a practical approach for facilitating systems mapping with young people. By centring youth as experts in their own lived experiences, the CI process enabled participants to co-create contextually relevant solutions. The structured, visual, and relational nature of CI supported deeper reflection and systems thinking among participants. Future research should build on these foundations by integrating youth-generated insights with stakeholder perspectives to develop implementable, multilevel strategies for promoting active recreation.
