Trans-renal ureteral occlusion for the management of persistent intraperitoneal bladder leak
Authors: Jeremy Bolton, Abdullah Al-Khanaty, Goran Mitreski, Mark Brooks, Damien Bolton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Surgical repair is the recommended management for intraperitoneal bladder perforation. In certain cases, repair may be unsuccessful or non-feasible, resulting in refractory urine leak. Ileal conduit formation remains an alternative, howeve it may not be suitable for all patients. In this case, an intraperitoneal bladder perforation occurred during TURBT. Laparoscopic repair was unsuccessful, as were bilateral nephrostomies in controlling the urine extravasation. The patient was not a candidate for surgical urinary diversion. We occluded both ureters using stents, microvascular plugs and embolisation coil and glue. Post intervention the urine leak resolved and the patient was discharged with nephrostomies in-situ.

Introduction

Iatrogenic intraperitoneal bladder perforation is a rare but serious complication of TURBT. The standard management of intraperitoneal bladder perforation is immediate surgical repair of the bladder defect, insertion of indwelling urethral catheter and an intraperitoneal abdominal drain. However, in the setting of advanced pelvic malignancy definitive repair of the bladder defect may be unsuccessful due to poor tissue healing. This may result in a refractory urine leak, with subsequent sequelae including urinoma and abscess formation, urosepsis and renal failure.1

Surgical urinary diversion remains an option but carries significant morbidity and may be unsuitable for frail or palliative patients.2, 3, 4 In such circumstances, permanent transrenal ureteral occlusion can provide a less invasive alternative to surgery.5 Transrenal antegrade ureteral occlusion has been reported in the management of ureteral fistula in the context of obstetric procedures, trauma, radiotherapy and inflammatory conditions, but has never been shown to have efficacy in management of vesical pathologies. We present a case utilising transrenal ureteral occlusion to manage a refractory intraperitoneal bladder leak following TURBT.

Case presentation

An 80-year-old male with macroscopic haematuria underwent an elective TURBT. His medical history included prostate cancer treated by external beam radiotherapy, congestive heart failure, atrial fibrillation, ischaemic heart disease, severe pulmonary hypertension and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. A large bladder tumour was identified in the superior-posterior area of the generally very thin bladder wall. Complete macroscopic resection of the bladder tumour was performed, which was complicated by an intraperitoneal bladder perforation, identified intra-operatively. The patient was considered by the anaesthetic team as unfit for an open repair via laparotomy. Management was via continuation of slow continuous irrigation, commencement of broad spectrum antibiotics and early return to theatre for laparoscopic bladder repair. A one-centimetre bladder tear was identified in the superolateral wall with associated fluid within the peritoneal cavity. The defect was unable to be completely closed due to the friability of the adjacent bladder wall tissue, and consequently a partial repair was undertaken using a V-loc suture (Medtronic, Minnesota, USA). An omental flap was positioned over the defect. An 18Fr urethral catheter and a 14Fr intraperitoneal abdominal drain were inserted.

Following bladder repair, the patient complained of persistent abdominal pain. Examination revealed abdominal tenderness and distension with associated elevated peritoneal drain tube outputs. On day five post TURBT, he developed rapid atrial fibrillation with associated haemodynamic instability. He was resuscitated and transferred to a tertiary hospital. Emergency bilateral nephrostomy tube insertion was undertaken, but despite placement of bilateral nephrostomy tubes and ongoing urethral catheter drainage, intraperitoneal abdominal drain tube output remained elevated.

Investigations

Histopathology from the TURBT yielded 6 g of resected tissue. Final histopathological diagnosis was prostate adenocarcinoma, Gleason score 4 + 5 = 9 (Grade group 5) with intraductal growth and comedonecrosis, and patchy sarcomatoid and squamous differentiation.

Treatment

Due to the ongoing leakage, bilateral ureteric occlusion was performed via the nephrostomy tubes 48 days post initial bladder repair. Via Interventional Radiology, a combination of microvascular plugs and embolisation material with coil and glue to obstruct the lumen of the ureter bilaterally, using the following methodology.

The existing bilateral nephrostomies were initially exchanged for 8Fr, 45cm BRIGHT TIP® sheaths (Cordis, Florida, USA) positioned within the proximal ureters. Via wire control, covered 10 × 40 mm Fluency™ Plus Endovascular Stent Grafts (BD, New Jersey, USA) were then deployed at the level of the inferior sacroiliac joint. Both grafts were tied ex-vivo at their tips (with the stent partially opened) with 4.0 silk suture, running the suture along the distal-most interstices of the stent. The sutures were then tied off, promoting a non-deployable distal stent margin fluted in appearance (Fig. 3). The stents were then deployed. On the left, a MVP 5™ Micro Vascular Plug System (Medtronic, Dublin, Ireland) was also deployed in addition to coil embolisation with 5 mm pushable coils (Nester® embolisation coils, Cook Medical, Indiana, USA). On the right, coil embolisation alone was utilised as the fluted stent had less bypass of contrast down the ureter on antegrade contrast imaging. Histoacryl® (Braun, NSW, Aus) mixed with Lipodol Ultra fluid® (Guerbert LLC, New Jersey, USA) in approx. 40:60 ratios (glue/lipiodol) were injected under image guidance to fill the fluted nose cone of the stent/coil construct. Outcome and follow up

Twenty-four hourly peritoneal drain tube output resolved to zero and this was subsequently removed on day 3 following ureteral occlusion. The patient recovered well post intervention and was discharged day 6 post ureteric occlusion. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication of this case report and accompanying images.

Discussion

Urinary leakage from the bladder refractory to standard management is challenging to treat and may require unconventional therapeutic approaches.5 We present a case of successfully utilising transrenal ureteral occlusion to manage a refractory intraperitoneal bladder leak following TURBT.

In the present case, post-radiation changes and the friable nature of the bladder tissue are thought to have contributed to the failure of the bladder leak to resolve after initial repair. Urinary diversion was contraindicated due to the anaesthetic risks associated with laparotomy.

There is a paucity of literature on how to optimally manage intraperitoneal perforations after bladder repair is unsuccessful and when surgical urinary diversion is not feasible. Minimally invasive techniques have been described to achieve ureteral occlusion in the treatment of ureteric fistula secondary to obstetric procedures, trauma, radiotherapy, or brachytherapy for prostate cancer.6 No studies have described the use of this technique to manage bladder leak secondary to iatrogenic bladder perforation.

Ureteral occlusion can be classified into direct or transrenal approaches. They all require a functional nephrostomy to avoid post-procedure renal failure.6 Direct approaches include percutaneous ureterostomy and ureteral clipping.7 In ureteric clipping, stainless-steel clips are guided via an introducer sheath placed into the retroperitoneum.8,9 Farrell et al. described complete resolution of urinary leak following ureteric clipping in seven of eight women.9 This procedure requires a second percutaneous access for the introducer sheath, is time consuming and carries a high risk of complications.

Transrenal approaches to ureteral occlusion have been in use since the 1970s. Reddy et al. initially described fulguration with unipolar current applied to a 2-cm length of ureter in four patients just distal to the PUJ via the existing nephrostomy tract.10 It lead to occlusion in all patients after 4-10 days, with permanent results (up to a follow up of 21 months). Fulguration however is painful and requires sedation.

Most other transrenal approaches use various embolisation materials (off label use) applied via existing nephrostomy access.5 The ideal material should immediately occlude the ureters following embolisation with cessation of urinary flow; (b) confer a low risk of recanalisation; c) not dissolve after exposure to urine; (d) and not erode or fistulate through the ureteric wall.11,12

The use of cyanoacrylate glue was first reported in three patients by Gunther et al.13 Success rates were initially high, however there were poor long-term results, as glue softens when exposed to urine resulting in recanalisation and further interventions. This technique has been modified many times and is now usually combined with other devices including coils and vascular plugs.

In Schild et al. study of 52 patients, ureteral occlusion was seen in 69% of patients (mean follow up of 7.9 months [range 0.25 - 61 months]).14 Issues arose with devices bursting during deployment, as well as deflation and migration. Non-detachable balloons (Fogarty catheter) have also been they require externalisation of two catheters.

Nylon and silicone plugs have also been described. Kinn et al. placed nylon plugs into the distal ureter via 26F access.15 Eighty seven percent of patients (n = 15) had improvement in ureteral leak (mean follow up 6 months). Plug migration was observed in half of patients. The limitation of plug devices is the requirement for large calibre access.

Coils ± gelfoam or glue have also been described and have the largest evidence base. In total, occlusion with coils and gelform has been used in 141 ureters with a success rate in 97% with a follow up between 0.5 and 29 months.1,4,8,16 We used this particular agent in both ureters. It can be performed using small calibre access via nephrostomy. We also additionally placed glue within the lumen of the distal ureter to induce fibrosis as literature has reported coils alone may take several weeks to induce sufficient fibrosis.

Amplatzer vascular plugs (AVP) have also been described with a latex cover or as a scaffold to secure coils and/or glue.17, 18, 19, 20 Pieper used latex plugs, which successfully occluded 9 out of 10 ureters (range 10 - 462 days).17 Concern have been raised regarding dislocation of latex-covered AVP, with some authors advocating for the use of AVP as a scaffold for glue or embolisation.6 Success rates reported in literature have ranged between 90 and 100% (follow up 7 - 14 months).18, 19, 20

In our case, we sought to construct a durable ureteric plug that extended over a long segment. On the left side, we found a microvascular plug alone failed to occlude the covered stent but addition of fibred microcoils produced complete occlusion. On the right side a combination of fibred microcoils and glue was successful. While this is a moderately expensive approach blocking the lumen of a covered stent is likely to produce a durable occlusion. As the microvascular plug proved ineffective on its own on the left side we changed our approach on the right side - we used a combination of fibred microcoils and glue within the covered stent for the right side. In our experience, we found no added value to using a microvascular plug. It is expensive (∼$1500 Australian dollars) and added little value in occluding urine flow. We found that coils and glue alone within a cover is more efficacious in completely occluding the ureters, with the added benefit of it being significantly less costly approach.

In select cases, occlusion of the ureteric lumen via nephrostomy access can be undertaken bilaterally, in order to provide for resolution of ongoing intraperitoneal bladder leakage in patients unfit for open surgical repair or diversion. This is best performed using multiple agents to act as an intraluminal scaffold and non-recanalisable plug, and represents another option available to clinicians in the management of complex vesical pathology.

Conclusion

Trans-renal ureteral occlusion represents a viable rescue strategy for refractory intraperitoneal urine leak when surgical options are exhausted or contraindicated. Our case demonstrates that bilateral ureteral occlusion using a combination of covered stents, microvascular plugs, and embolic material can achieve durable resolution of urine extravasation. This approach may be considered in patients with prohibitive surgical risk, offering a minimally invasive alternative to urinary diversion. Further case series are needed to refine patient selection and optimal embolisation technique.
