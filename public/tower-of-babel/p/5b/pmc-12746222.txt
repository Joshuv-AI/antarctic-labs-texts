Innovative insights into the effects of a high‐sucrose diet on pancreatic health and the therapeutic role of empagliflozin in type 2 diabetes: An experimental study
Authors: Emad Gholami, Ali Fardin, Fazeleh Iranshahi, Alireza Malek, Ali Saravani, Faranaz Aghvami, Houman Parsaei, Habib Yaribeygi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractType 2 Diabetes (T2D) is a growing global health issue, often aggravated by excessive sugar intake. Chronic high‐sucrose diets contribute to insulin resistance, oxidative stress, and pancreatic dysfunction, worsening metabolic health. Sodium‐glucose co‐transporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors, like empagliflozin, show potential in improving glycemic control and metabolic parameters, but their effects on pancreatic efficiency in sugar‐induced T2D are not well understood. This study aimed to explore the effects of empagliflozin on metabolic and pancreatic protection in a high‐sucrose diet‐induced T2D model. Male Wistar rats were divided into four groups: normal, normal‐treated, diabetic, and diabetic‐treated (n = 8 per group). Diabetes was induced with a 35% sucrose solution for 8 weeks, followed by a low‐dose streptozotocin (STZ) injection. Treated groups received empagliflozin (15 mg/kg/day) for the duration. Biochemical markers, including fasting blood sugar (FBS), lipid profile, insulin levels, and oxidative stress markers, were measured. Insulin resistance (HOMA‐IR) and pancreatic function (HOMA‐B) were assessed. Histological analysis of pancreatic tissues was performed. The high‐sucrose diet increased FBS, insulin resistance, and oxidative stress, while decreasing pancreatic function and islet diameter. Empagliflozin treatment lowered FBS (p = 0.001), improved insulin sensitivity (p = 0.001), reduced triglycerides (p = 0.001), and LDL (p = 0.05). It also enhanced antioxidant enzymes, reduced lipid peroxidation (MDA, p = 0.001), and preserved pancreatic islet structure (p = 0.001). A high‐sucrose diet negatively affects metabolic health and pancreatic function. Empagliflozin mitigates these effects by improving metabolism, reducing oxidative stress, and preserving pancreatic integrity, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic agent in diabetes related to excessive sugar consumption.

INTRODUCTION

Diabetes, particularly type 2 diabetes (T2D), has reached epidemic proportions globally, with increasing prevalence rates. 1 According to the World Health Organization (WHO), diabetes is one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide, affecting millions of individuals across all age groups. 1 The rise in diabetes prevalence is largely attributed to various factors, including lifestyle changes, sedentary behavior, and dietary habits. 2 The pathophysiology of type 2 diabetes is complex and involves a progressive failure of pancreatic insulin secretion, combined with peripheral insulin resistance. 2 One of the most significant complications associated with this disorder is pancreatic dysfunction. 3 The pancreas, which plays a critical role in glucose metabolism by producing insulin, is often unable to secrete sufficient amounts of this hormone in the face of increased demand. Over time, this dysfunction exacerbates the disease and contributes to the worsening of hyperglycemia and associated complications, including cardiovascular disease, nephropathy, and neuropathy. 3 , 4

Among the various risk factors for type 2 diabetes, one of the most prominent is the excessive intake of sugar. 5 A diet high in sucrose, fructose, and other refined sugars has been shown to increase the risk of insulin resistance, a hallmark feature of type 2 diabetes. 5 Additionally, sugar consumption can lead to dysregulated glucose metabolism, ultimately resulting in impaired insulin signaling and pancreatic beta‐cell dysfunction. 5 , 6 The long‐term effects of such dietary patterns are particularly concerning, as they contribute not only to the development of type 2 diabetes but also to the increased burden of its complications. 6 In the context of human diets, carbohydrates represent one of the most significant macronutrients, comprising a substantial portion of daily caloric intake. While carbohydrates are a primary source of energy for the body, the shift towards consuming an increasing proportion of simple carbohydrates, particularly refined sugars, has raised concerns about its impact on metabolic health. 7 The rising intake of high‐carbohydrate foods, especially those high in added sugars, has been linked to a number of adverse effects on the body, including obesity, insulin resistance, and pancreatic dysfunction. 7 This dietary shift has contributed to the growing incidence of type 2 diabetes and its associated complications. 7 , 8

Another key factor in the progression of type 2 diabetes is oxidative stress, which is often exacerbated by high sugar consumption. 9 , 10 Oxidative stress occurs when the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) exceeds the body's antioxidant defense mechanisms. 10 This imbalance can lead to cellular damage and inflammation, which are known to play a pivotal role in the development of insulin resistance. Furthermore, oxidative stress has been implicated in pancreatic beta‐cell dysfunction, making it a significant contributor to the pathogenesis of type 2 diabetes. 10 The accumulation of ROS in the pancreas can impair insulin secretion, exacerbate insulin resistance, and ultimately worsen the overall metabolic state. 10

The management of type 2 diabetes involves various therapeutic strategies, with a focus on improving insulin sensitivity and restoring pancreatic function. One such approach involves the use of sodium‐glucose co‐transporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors, such as empagliflozin. Empagliflozin has been shown to effectively reduce blood glucose levels by inhibiting the reabsorption of glucose in the kidneys, leading to increased urinary glucose excretion. 11 In addition to its glycemic‐lowering effects, empagliflozin has been demonstrated to improve metabolic profiles, reduce oxidative stress, and alleviate some of the burden on pancreatic function in individuals with type 2 diabetes. 11 , 12 The drug also has potential benefits in mitigating cardiovascular complications, making it a promising therapeutic option for managing type 2 diabetes and its associated comorbidities. 12 , 13

However, while empagliflozin has been shown to improve various aspects of metabolic health in type 2 diabetes, its effects on pancreatic function, particularly in models of diabetes induced by high sugar consumption, remain unclear. The role of empagliflozin in enhancing pancreatic efficiency, especially in the context of high‐sucrose diet‐induced type 2 diabetes, has yet to be thoroughly explored. Therefore, the objective of the present study is to develop an animal model of diabetes induced by high sucrose consumption and to investigate the impact of this model on insulin resistance, pancreatic function, and overall metabolic health. Furthermore, this study aims to assess the potential therapeutic effects of empagliflozin in improving pancreatic efficiency and metabolic outcomes in this experimental setting.

METHODS

Animals and experimental design

Male Wistar rats (8–10 weeks old), weighing 150–180 grams at the start of the study, were obtained from the Physiology Research Center of Semnan University of Medical Sciences (Semnan, Iran). All animals were housed in standard polypropylene cages (two rats per cage) in a controlled environment with a temperature of 22 ± 2 °C, relative humidity of 55% ± 5%, and a 12‐hour light/dark cycle. Rats had ad libitum access to standard rodent chow (Pars Co., Iran), and non‐diabetic groups received free access to tap water. All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee and conducted in accordance with the NIH Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Drug administration

Empagliflozin (Jardiance®, Boehringer Ingelheim) was administered orally to the treatment groups (C + EMPA and D + EMPA) at a dose of 15 mg/kg/day via gavage for 8 consecutive weeks. The selected dose was based on previous rodent studies demonstrating its efficacy in improving glucose metabolism and reducing oxidative stress in diabetic conditions. 14 , 15 The control and diabetic groups received an equivalent volume of normal saline as vehicle.

Induction of type 2 diabetes mellitus

To induce a model of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), rats in the diabetic groups (D and D + EMPA) were given 35% sucrose solution in place of regular drinking water throughout the entire 8‐week experimental period, mimicking chronic high‐carbohydrate intake. To accelerate disease onset, and following established protocols used in diet‐induced diabetes models, a single low‐dose intraperitoneal injection of streptozotocin (STZ; Sigma‐Aldrich, USA) at 30 mg/kg body weight, freshly dissolved in 0.1 mol/L citrate buffer (pH = 5.5), was administered after 14 days of sucrose feeding.

To confirm successful induction of hyperglycemia, fasting blood glucose (FBS) levels were measured 7 days post‐STZ injection using tail vein sampling and a glucometer (Accu‐Chek®). Animals with FBS levels >250 mg/dL were considered diabetic and included in the study. FBS was also reassessed at the end of the 8‐week period to monitor glycemic status.

Biochemical and hormonal assessments

At the end of the 8‐week intervention, animals were anesthetized using a high dose of ketamine (80 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg), and blood samples were collected via cardiac puncture. Blood was immediately centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 minutes at 4 °C to separate plasma, which was then stored at −80 °C until analysis.

Plasma concentrations of total cholesterol, triglycerides (TG), and low‐density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol were measured using enzymatic colorimetric kits (Pars Azmun, Iran), following the manufacturer's protocols. Plasma insulin levels were quantified using a rat‐specific commercial ELISA kits. The Homeostatic Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance (HOMA‐IR) and β‐cell function (HOMA‐B) were calculated using the following formulas 16 , 17 : HOMA‐IR = [FBS (mg/dL) × Insulin (μU/mL)]/405,HOMA‐B = [360 × Insulin (μU/mL)]/[FBS (mg/dL) – 63].

Tissue collection and oxidative stress assays

Immediately after euthanasia, pancreatic tissues were excised, washed in ice‐cold saline, and divided into two portions. One portion was fixed in 10% buffered formalin for histological analysis, and the other was snap‐frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C for oxidative stress assays.

The frozen tissues were homogenized in phosphate buffer (pH 7.4), and the supernatants obtained after centrifugation were used to assess oxidative stress markers. The enzymatic activity of superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT) was measured using the methods of Winterbourn 18 and Aebi, 19 respectively. Glutathione (GSH) levels were determined based on the method of Tietz, 20 and malondialdehyde (MDA), an index of lipid peroxidation, was measured using Satoh's method. 21 All biochemical assays were performed in duplicate.

Histological analysis

Formalin‐fixed pancreatic tissues were processed and embedded in paraffin. Sections of 5 μm thickness were stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) and examined under a light microscope at 400× magnification. A scale bar of 100 μm was included in representative images (see Figure 4). Pancreatic islet morphology and cellular integrity were assessed qualitatively by a blinded histopathologist.

Statistical analysis

The Shapiro Wilk test was used to examine the normal distribution of all data. Then, one‐way ANOVA was used to assess any differences between groups. In addition, Tukey's test was applied post hoc. In all steps, p < 0.05 was considered as the significant difference. The presented data are expressed as the mean ± SD (standard deviation).

Ethical considerations

All ethical concerns about caring and using the experimental animals were carefully considered throughout this study. Also, this study was approved by the ethics committee of the Semnan University of Medical Sciences (IR.SEMUMS.AEC.1402.013).

RESULTS

The impact of a high‐sucrose diet‐induced diabetes on metabolic, biochemical, and histological parameters, as well as the therapeutic effects of empagliflozin, were comprehensively evaluated in this study.

Fasting blood sugar (FBS) levels

Diabetes induction with a high‐sucrose diet led to a significant increase in fasting blood glucose levels compared to the normal group (p = 0.001), indicating severe hyperglycemia as a hallmark of type 2 diabetes (Figure 1). However, treatment with empagliflozin effectively reduced FBS levels in the diabetic‐treated group (p = 0.001), demonstrating its potent glucose‐lowering effect by enhancing renal glucose excretion.

FBS levels in all groups. *p = 0.001 and **p = 0.01: significantly different from normal (N) group. # p = 0.001: significantly different from diabetic (D) group.

Biochemical analysis

The diabetic group exhibited a substantial elevation in plasma insulin levels (0.66 ± 0.007 μIU/L) compared to the normal group (0.29 ± 0.05 μIU/L, p = 0.001), suggesting the development of insulin resistance due to prolonged high‐sucrose intake. In contrast, empagliflozin administration significantly lowered insulin levels in the diabetic‐treated group (0.32 ± 0.07 μIU/L, p = 0.001), indicating improved insulin sensitivity and reduced pancreatic burden. Similarly, triglyceride (TG) levels were markedly increased in diabetic rats (252 ± 16 mg/dL) compared to normal controls (112 ± 14 mg/dL, p = 0.001), reflecting the dyslipidemic effects of chronic high‐sucrose consumption. Treatment with empagliflozin significantly attenuated this rise (139 ± 14 mg/dL, p = 0.001), suggesting its beneficial role in lipid metabolism regulation.

Although cholesterol (Chol) levels did not differ significantly among groups (p = 0.09), low‐density lipoprotein (LDL) levels were elevated in the diabetic group (20.7 ± 2.3 mg/dL), indicating increased cardiovascular risk. Empagliflozin treatment significantly reduced LDL levels (17.3 ± 2 mg/dL, p = 0.05), further supporting its protective metabolic effects (Table 1).

Insulin resistance and pancreatic function

A high‐sucrose diet led to severe insulin resistance, as indicated by significantly elevated HOMA‐IR levels (p = 0.001), and compromised pancreatic function, as evidenced by altered HOMA‐B values. Empagliflozin administration markedly reduced HOMA‐IR while improving HOMA‐B scores, demonstrating its efficacy in restoring pancreatic efficiency and improving glucose metabolism (Figure 2).

HOMA‐IR and HOMA‐B levels in all groups. *p = 0.001 and **p = 0.01: significantly different from normal (N) group. # p = 0.001: significantly different from diabetic (D) group.

Oxidative stress markers

Chronic high‐sucrose consumption induced oxidative stress, reflected by reduced activities of key antioxidant enzymes (CAT and SOD) and elevated levels of malondialdehyde (MDA), a marker of lipid peroxidation. These changes contributed to pancreatic β‐cell dysfunction and increased metabolic stress. Treatment with empagliflozin significantly enhanced the antioxidant defense system, as evidenced by increased CAT and SOD activities, higher glutathione (GLT) levels, and a marked reduction in MDA concentrations compared to the untreated diabetic group (p = 0.001). These findings underscore the antioxidant potential of empagliflozin in mitigating oxidative damage in T2D (Figure 3).

CAT (Unit/mL), SOD (Unit/mL), GLT (nmol/mL) and MDA (nmol/mL) levels in all groups. *p = 0.001: significantly different from normal (N) group. # p = 0.001: significantly different from diabetic (D) group.

Histological findings

Histological evaluation revealed severe structural damage in the pancreatic islets of diabetic rats, characterized by a significant reduction in the diameter of the islets of Langerhans (p = 0.001) and widespread necrosis, indicative of β‐cell destruction. In contrast, empagliflozin treatment led to a significant increase in islet diameter (p = 0.001) and preserved cellular integrity, and prevented further necrotic damage. These findings highlight the cytoprotective role of empagliflozin in maintaining pancreatic tissue integrity under hyperglycemic conditions (Figures 4 and 5).

DISCUSSION

The present study aimed to investigate the effects of empagliflozin, an SGLT2 inhibitor, on metabolic parameters, oxidative stress, and pancreatic function in an experimental model of type 2 diabetes (T2D) induced by a high‐sucrose diet. This model is particularly innovative as it mimics dietary patterns common in modern societies, where the intake of refined sugars has dramatically increased, contributing to the rising prevalence of T2D. While previous research has explored the effects of high‐fat diets and high‐fructose diets on pancreatic function and diabetes progression, the specific effects of a high‐sucrose diet on pancreatic efficiency and the potential benefits of empagliflozin treatment in this context have not been thoroughly investigated. Our study not only addresses this gap but also highlights the novel role of empagliflozin in mitigating the harmful effects of excessive sugar consumption on metabolic health.

Impact of high‐sucrose diet on fasting blood sugar (FBS)

A major finding of this study is the significant increase in fasting blood glucose (FBS) levels in the diabetic group induced by a high‐sucrose diet. This result is consistent with previous studies, which have shown that diets rich in sucrose contribute to hyperglycemia and insulin resistance. For instance, a study by Stanhope et al. (2009) demonstrated that excessive fructose and sucrose intake leads to impaired glucose tolerance and increased fasting glucose levels in rodents and humans. 22 The high‐sucrose diet used in our model led to a considerable increase in FBS levels compared to the normal group, underscoring the detrimental effects of sugar on glucose metabolism. 23

Empagliflozin treatment significantly lowered FBS levels in the diabetic‐treated group. This observation supports the findings of previous clinical and preclinical studies demonstrating the glucose‐lowering effects of SGLT2 inhibitors. 24 , 25 Empagliflozin works by inhibiting the sodium‐glucose co‐transporter 2 in the kidneys, thereby increasing urinary glucose excretion and reducing blood glucose levels. The observed reduction in FBS in the current study mirrors similar results from trials such as the EMPA‐REG OUTCOME trial, which showed that empagliflozin significantly reduced HbA1c levels and improved glycemic control in patients with T2D. 26 , 27

What makes our study particularly innovative is the use of a high‐sucrose diet model, which closely mimics the modern dietary patterns contributing to T2D. 28 The fact that empagliflozin was able to effectively reduce FBS in this context highlights its potential as a therapeutic agent in managing hyperglycemia associated with excessive sugar intake, a growing concern in the global population.

Biochemical analysis: insulin, lipid profile, and oxidative stress

Our study found that the high‐sucrose diet significantly increased plasma insulin levels, reflecting insulin resistance. This aligns with the findings of other studies, where chronic exposure to high‐sucrose diets has been shown to impair insulin signaling and promote insulin resistance. 29 , 30 For example, studies by Lourido et al. (2021) and Epstein et al. (2023) demonstrated that high sugar intake can lead to increased insulin secretion in response to elevated blood glucose levels, a hallmark of insulin resistance. 29 , 30

Treatment with empagliflozin significantly reduced insulin levels in the diabetic‐treated group, suggesting an improvement in insulin sensitivity. This is consistent with the known effects of SGLT2 inhibitors, which have been shown to improve insulin sensitivity by reducing glucose reabsorption in the kidneys and lowering circulating glucose levels. 11 The reduction in plasma insulin levels in the empagliflozin‐treated group in our study reflects an improvement in insulin resistance, which is a key target in T2D management. Notably, this effect is particularly important in the context of high‐sucrose diet‐induced insulin resistance, as sucrose‐induced hyperglycemia often exacerbates insulin resistance and pancreatic dysfunction. 31

Our findings regarding triglyceride (TG) levels also align with previous research. The diabetic group exhibited significantly elevated TG levels compared to the normal group, consistent with the dyslipidemic effects of high‐sucrose diets. 32 Previous studies, such as those by Song et al. (2024), have demonstrated that high‐sucrose intake leads to increased hepatic lipogenesis and elevated circulating TG levels. 33 Empagliflozin treatment reduced TG levels in the diabetic‐treated group, which is consistent with studies showing that SGLT2 inhibitors have beneficial effects on lipid metabolism. 12 For example, in the EMPA‐REG OUTCOME trial, empagliflozin was associated with a reduction in TG levels in T2D patients. 34

The reduction in TG levels in our study further supports the notion that empagliflozin not only improves glycemic control but also contributes to better lipid metabolism, thus mitigating one of the common metabolic complications associated with T2D. 12 The significant reduction in TG levels and the lack of significant changes in cholesterol (Chol) levels also suggest that empagliflozin may have a more pronounced effect on triglyceride‐rich lipoproteins, which are a key contributor to cardiovascular risk in T2D. 12 , 34 , 35

Regarding oxidative stress, our study found significant increases in markers of oxidative stress, such as malondialdehyde (MDA), and a reduction in antioxidant enzymes, including superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT), in the diabetic group. This is in line with previous studies that have demonstrated that high‐carbohydrate diets exacerbate oxidative stress, which in turn contributes to insulin resistance and pancreatic dysfunction. 36 , 37 However there is not enough evidence about the role of a high‐sucrose diet in the absence of a high‐fat diet. The role of oxidative stress in T2D has been well‐established, with studies indicating that reactive oxygen species (ROS) play a critical role in the pathogenesis of pancreatic β‐cell dysfunction and insulin resistance. 38

Empagliflozin treatment significantly increased the activities of CAT and SOD while reducing MDA levels, indicating its antioxidant effects. This finding is consistent with studies showing that SGLT2 inhibitors, including empagliflozin, have antioxidant properties and can reduce oxidative stress. 24 For instance, studies by Yaribeygi et al. (2023) and Uthman et al. (2022) have shown that empagliflozin reduces ROS levels and enhances antioxidant defenses in T2D models. 24 , 39 The ability of empagliflozin to mitigate oxidative stress in the high‐sucrose diet model highlights its dual benefits in improving both metabolic and oxidative stress markers.

Histological findings: pancreatic function and islet preservation

One of the most striking findings of our study is the significant reduction in the diameter of the islets of Langerhans in the diabetic group, indicative of pancreatic β‐cell dysfunction. This observation aligns with previous studies showing that chronic high‐fat and high‐sucrose intake induces pancreatic β‐cell apoptosis and dysfunction, 37 but there is not enough evidence about the effects of a high‐sucrose diet alone. The high‐sucrose diet in our model not only induced insulin resistance but also resulted in structural damage to the pancreas, which likely contributed to the exacerbation of hyperglycemia and metabolic dysregulation.

Empagliflozin treatment significantly preserved the diameter of the islets of Langerhans and prevented cellular destruction and necrosis within the islets. This finding is particularly important as it highlights the potential of empagliflozin in preserving pancreatic function in a model of T2D induced by excessive sugar consumption. While previous studies have shown that SGLT2 inhibitors can improve pancreatic function and reduce β‐cell apoptosis in models of T2D induced by high‐fat diets, 40 the specific effect of empagliflozin in a high‐sucrose diet‐induced T2D model has not been previously reported. Our study fills this gap and provides evidence that empagliflozin can protect pancreatic islets from the destructive effects of a high‐sucrose diet, suggesting its potential to improve both metabolic and pancreatic health in patients with T2D.

Novelty and clinical implications

The major novelty of our study lies in the development and validation of a high‐sucrose diet as a relevant experimental model for type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), which closely mimics modern dietary patterns characterized by excessive intake of refined sugars. 7 , 41 While the detrimental effects of high‐fat or high‐fructose diets on insulin resistance and pancreatic β‐cell dysfunction have been extensively studied, the specific metabolic and pancreatic consequences of chronic sucrose consumption remain underexplored. Moreover, to our knowledge, the therapeutic impact of empagliflozin on pancreatic structure and oxidative status in a sucrose‐induced model of T2DM has not been systematically investigated.

Our findings provide new mechanistic and translational insights by demonstrating that chronic sucrose intake leads to significant metabolic disturbances, oxidative stress, and histological damage to pancreatic islets—hallmarks of early T2DM pathophysiology. Importantly, empagliflozin not only improved glucose and lipid profiles, but also mitigated oxidative damage and preserved islet architecture, suggesting protective effects that may extend beyond its known glycosuric action.

From a clinical standpoint, these findings are timely and relevant. The global surge in dietary sugar consumption, particularly sweetened beverages and processed foods, parallels the increasing burden of T2DM, especially in younger populations. Our results highlight the potential utility of SGLT2 inhibitors such as empagliflozin as early pharmacological interventions in individuals with diet‐induced metabolic risk. These agents may serve not only as effective glycemic regulators, but also as modulators of oxidative stress and protectors of pancreatic β‐cell integrity in the context of high‐sugar diets.

Although animal studies cannot be directly extrapolated to humans, the implications are noteworthy. Empagliflozin may have a broader role in clinical management—potentially benefiting patients with prediabetes, metabolic syndrome, or early‐stage T2DM driven by poor dietary habits. Future clinical trials are warranted to evaluate whether similar pancreatic‐protective effects of empagliflozin are observed in human cohorts with high‐sucrose dietary exposure.

Limitations and future directions

While our findings clearly demonstrate that empagliflozin improves glucose and lipid metabolism, reduces oxidative stress, and preserves pancreatic islet morphology in a high‐sucrose diet‐induced model of type 2 diabetes, the precise underlying mechanisms remain to be elucidated. In particular, potential contributions from anti‐inflammatory and anti‐apoptotic pathways were not investigated in the current study. As our primary aim was to validate and characterize this novel animal model and to confirm the beneficial metabolic and oxidative outcomes of empagliflozin, mechanistic studies were beyond the intended scope. Future research should explore these molecular pathways, including inflammatory signaling and apoptosis‐related cascades, to provide a more comprehensive understanding of empagliflozin's therapeutic actions.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that a high‐sucrose diet exacerbates hyperglycemia, insulin resistance, oxidative stress, and pancreatic dysfunction, contributing to the progression of T2D. Empagliflozin effectively mitigates these impairments by improving glycemic control, reducing oxidative stress, and preserving pancreatic islet structure and function. The novel use of a high‐sucrose diet in this model provides valuable insights into the harmful effects of excessive sugar consumption and highlights the therapeutic potential of empagliflozin in managing T2D induced by dietary patterns common in modern societies.
