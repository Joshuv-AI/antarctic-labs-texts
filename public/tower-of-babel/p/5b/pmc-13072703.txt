Resveratrol inhibits renal ischemia and reperfusion injury in diabetes via reducing oxidative stress, inflammation, and apoptosis
Authors: Daojing Gong, Xiaobo Chen, Ziqiang Dong, Huan Chen, Ziqiang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe diabetic kidney is susceptible to renal ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) injury, which is associated with enhanced oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, and cell apoptosis. Resveratrol (RSV) plays a crucial role in protecting various organs against ischemia/reperfusion damage by exerting antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-apoptotic effects. However, the molecular mechanism of RSV protection against renal I/R injury in diabetic conditions remains unclear. The aim of the present study was to examine whether RSV attenuates renal I/R injury in diabetes and further clarify the underlying mechanisms.MethodsStreptozotocin-induced diabetic rats and high glucose-cultured HK-2 cells were, respectively, treated with RSV before renal ischemia and hypoxia/reoxygenation induction. In vitro, cell viability, intracellular reactive oxygen species levels, and apoptosis were measured. In vivo, renal function, histology, oxidative stress level, inflammatory cytokines, and apoptosis were determined. Furthermore, the expression levels of B-cell lymphoma-2 (Bcl-2), Bcl-2-associated X protein (Bax), cleaved caspase-3, nuclear factor-erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2), heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), and nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) were detected.ResultsRSV improved renal function, reduced oxidative stress, inhibited inflammatory responses, and decreased apoptosis after renal ischemia/reperfusion in diabetes, accompanied by increased expression of Nrf2 and HO-1, and decreased expression of TLR4 and NF-κB. The beneficial effects of RSV were abolished by selective inhibition of Nrf2 with ML385.ConclusionThese findings indicate that RSV could attenuate renal I/R injury in diabetes, possibly by enhancing Nrf2/HO-1 signaling and suppressing the TLR4/NF-κB pathway.

Introduction

Renal ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) injury is a major cause of acute postoperative renal dysfunction and renal failure. It commonly occurs in patients undergoing major renal surgeries (e.g. kidney transplantation and partial nephrectomy), leading to serious complications such as delayed graft function, graft rejection, and acute kidney injury [1]. The mechanisms of I/R-induced kidney damage consist of multiple highly integrated pathophysiological processes, including mitochondrial energy exhaustion, oxidative stress, calcium overload, inflammation, endoplasmic reticulum stress, and apoptosis [2]. Of note, the outburst of oxidative and proinflammatory factors is pivotal for the initiation of renal I/R injury. During reperfusion, high levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) are generated due to the sudden regaining of oxygen, exerting detrimental effects on cell proteins, lipids, and DNA. These effects result in cell apoptosis and necrosis [3].

Diabetes mellitus is a common clinical metabolic disease, which can lead to long-term damage, dysfunction, and failure of various organs, particularly the eyes, kidneys, nerves, heart, and blood vessels [4]. Diabetes mellitus-associated kidney disease, also known as diabetic nephropathy, is the major cause of end-stage renal disease worldwide [5]. Hyperglycemia increases basal ROS levels in diabetic kidney tissue, inducing a state of chronic oxidative stress [1]. Studies involving animal models have confirmed that diabetic rats are more susceptible to renal I/R injury compared with normal rats [1,6]. Patients with diabetes exhibit increased vulnerability to renal I/R injury, leading to higher rates of morbidity and mortality in clinical practice [7]. Therefore, the prevention of renal I/R injury in diabetes is of great clinical significance.

Resveratrol (RSV) is a plant-derived polyphenolic compound mainly found in grapes, berries, peanuts, and other fruits. Since its discovery, RSV has attracted great research interest due to its antioxidative, anticancer, anti-inflammatory, antineurodegenerative, and antiaging properties [8]. Recent research has shown that RSV is a potential scavenger of ROS due to its antioxidant effect [9]. RSV exerts its antioxidant properties by regulating the activity of antioxidant enzymes through transcriptional regulation [10]. It significantly mitigates I/R-induced organ and tissue injury, including the heart [11], liver [8], uterus [12], kidneys [13], and retina [14]. Nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) is one of the key signaling pathways that regulate cellular inflammatory and oxidative responses [15]. It has been reported that RSV protects the tissue from I/R injury by enhancing the activity of Nrf2 and its downstream products, including heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) [16]. During reperfusion, external or internal inflammatory signals stimulate transmembrane toll-like receptors (TLRs) and further activate nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB). This process promotes the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines and infiltration of immune cells, resulting in cellular damage [1]. By downregulating the TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway, RSV can inhibit inflammatory responses and improve renal function after renal I/R injury [17]. However, few studies thus far have investigated the effects of RSV on renal I/R injury in the context of diabetes.

Considering the rapidly rising incidence of diabetes, further investigation is warranted to explore effective measures against kidney I/R injury in patients with diabetes. In the present study, we established models of renal I/R injury in streptozotocin (STZ)-induced diabetic rats to evaluate whether RSV reduces oxidative stress, inhibits inflammation, and decreases cell apoptosis via the Nrf2/TLR4/NF-κB pathway in a high-glucose environment, thereby alleviating renal I/R injury.

Materials and methods

Animals

Male Sprague–Dawley rats (weight: 200 ± 230 g) were purchased from the Experimental Animal Center of China Three Gorges University (Yichang, China). All rats were housed in an environment with controlled temperature (23 ± 1 °C) and relative humidity (50–70%) under specific pathogen-free conditions and a 12-h light/dark cycle with free access to food and water. All animal experimental procedures were approved by the Experimental Animal Management and Use Committee of Hubei Provincial Center for Disease Control and Prevention (202520469) and strictly followed the Guidelines of the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Establishment of diabetes models

All rats were adaptively fed with a standard diet for 1 week before the experiment. After fasting overnight, rats were intraperitoneally injected with a single dose of 65 mg/kg streptozotocin (STZ; cat. no. S0130; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) solution diluted in citrate buffer (0.1 mM, pH 4.5) as previously described [1]. Three days after STZ injection, tail vein blood was collected to measure glucose levels using a glucometer (Terumo, Tokyo, Japan). Only animals with three consecutive measurements of random blood glucose levels >16.7 mM were considered diabetic. All rats were provided with adequate standard chow and water ad libitum for another 8 weeks. At termination, rats were weighed and subjected to renal I/R as described in the following section.

Experimental groups

The animal experiments were conducted in two stages. In the first stage, rats were randomly divided into five groups (n = 8 per group) to determine the optimal dose of RSV: (1) diabetic sham-operated group (DS); (2) diabetic I/R group (DI/R); (3) 5 mg/kg RSV-pretreated diabetic I/R group (DI/R + RSV 5); (4) 10 mg/kg RSV-pretreated diabetic I/R group (DI/R + RSV 10); and (5) 20 mg/kg RSV-pretreated diabetic I/R group (DI/R + RSV 20). In the second stage, rats were randomly divided into four groups (n = 8 per group) to explore underlying mechanisms: (1) DS group; (2) DI/R group; (3) DI/R group pretreated with the optimal dose of RSV determined in the first stage (DI/R + RSV); and (4) DI/R + RSV group treated with ML385 (DI/R + RSV + ML385). RSV (cat. no. R5010; Sigma-Aldrich) was diluted in 10% dimethyl sulfoxide/sterile saline and administered through intraperitoneal injection once daily for seven consecutive days and once again prior to ischemia. ML385 (a Nrf2 inhibitor) (cat. no. HY-100523; MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) was intraperitoneally administered for 6 h at a single dose of 30 mg/kg before the induction of renal I/R.

Establishment of rat models of renal I/R injury

All rats were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (60 mg/kg) and placed on a homeothermic table to maintain core body temperature at 37 °C. After an abdominal midline incision, the kidneys were exposed and right nephrectomy was performed. Next, the left kidney vessels were clamped for 45 min to induce ischemia, followed by clamp removal for 24 h of reperfusion. Sham-operated animals underwent the same procedure without vascular occlusion. All rats were sacrificed by cervical dislocation at the end of reperfusion. The left kidneys were removed and sliced into two coronal sections for further analysis. One section was fixed in 10% phosphate-buffered formalin, whereas the other section was immediately stored at −80 °C. Plasma samples were also collected for renal function assessments.

Cell culture

The human renal proximal tubular epithelial cell line HK-2 was obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). HK-2 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. Cells were seeded and incubated overnight before being used for experiments.

High-glucose culture and hypoxia/reoxygenation (H/R) model

At 24 h after cell seeding, the medium was replaced with a high-sugar medium (glucose 30 mM) for 36 h of cell culture. The cells treated with high glucose were randomly divided into five groups to determine the optimal dose of RSV: control group; H/R group; H/R + 25 μM RSV group; H/R + 50 μM RSV group; and H/R + 100 μM RSV group. Next, cells were assigned into the following four groups to explore potential mechanisms: control group; H/R group; H/R + RSV group; and H/R + RSV + ML385 group. A cellular hypoxic environment was induced and maintained in an incubator filled with a gas mixture composed of 94% N2, 5% CO2, and 1% O2. After 12 h of hypoxia, 3 h of reoxygenation was achieved by continuous gas flow with a gas mixture composed of 95% air and 5% CO2. RSV and 5 μM ML385 were added 1 h prior to H/R stimulation, respectively. Cells were collected at the end of treatment.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed using a Cell Counting Kit-8 according to the instructions provided by the manufacturer (cat. no. CK04-100; Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan). Cells were seeded in 96-well plates and treated with Cell Counting Kit-8 solution (10 μL per well). After 3 h of incubation, the absorbance at 450 nm wavelength was measured with a microplate reader to calculate the proportion of viable cells.

Intracellular ROS detection

Intracellular ROS levels were measured using 6-carboxy-2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) (cat. no. D6883; Sigma-Aldrich). Briefly, cells were incubated with 10 μM DCFH-DA for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. Subsequently, the medium was discarded and cells were washed thrice with serum-free medium. ROS levels were estimated based on the intensity of fluorescence images captured using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus IX51) through Image-Pro advanced software.

Flow cytometry analysis

After 3 h of reoxygenation, the collected cells were stained with fluorescein isothiocyanate-conjugated annexin V and propidium iodide according to the instructions provided by the manufacturer (cat. no. 40302ES60; Yeasen Biotechnology Co., Ltd, Shanghai, China). Cell apoptosis was detected by flow cytometry (CytoFLEX S; Becman Coulter Inc., Brea, CA, USA).

Serum assays

Whole blood was centrifuged at 2000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C to obtain serum. Renal function was assessed by measuring blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and serum creatinine (SCr) levels using a Hitachi 7060 automatic biochemistry analyzer (Hitachi, Ltd., Tokyo, Japan).

Histological observation

After tissue fixation and embedding, sections (thickness: 4 μm) were gradually subjected to deparaffinization and rehydration, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (cat. no. C0105S; Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) and terminal deoxynucleotidyl-transferase-mediated dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL; cat. no. 11684795910; Roche, Basel, Switzerland) reagents. Morphological assessments were performed by two experienced renal pathologists who were unaware of the assigned treatments. The degree of I/R-induced renal damage was graded histologically using a scale from 0 to 4 according to the criteria outlined by Jablonski et al. [18]. The apoptosis index (apoptotic cells/total cells × 100%) was calculated from five random fields per section with Image-Pro plus software version 6.0 (Media Cybernetics, Inc., Rockville, MD, USA).

Superoxide dismutase (SOD) and malondialdehyde (MDA) measurement

SOD is an important enzyme in oxidative stress, and MDA is a terminal product of lipid peroxidation. The SOD activity and MDA concentration in renal tissue homogenates were measured using commercial kits (cat. no. A001-3-2 and A003-1-2, respectively; Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China) according to the instructions provided by the manufacturer.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Levels of tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interleukin-1β (IL-1β) in homogenized renal tissues were tested using ELISA commercial kits (cat. no. E-EL-R2856 and E-EL-R0012, respectively; Elabscience Biotechnology Co., Ltd, Wuhan, China) according to the instructions provided by the manufacturer. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm wavelength.

Western blotting

Proteins were extracted from renal tissues or cells using a Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Protein Extraction Kit (cat. no. P0027; Beyotime Biotechnology) according to the instructions provided by the manufacturer and quantified by the bicinchoninic acid method. Lamin B and β-actin were used as the internal loading control of nuclear and cytoplasmic protein, respectively. Protein from each sample (40 μg) was subsequently separated on 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gels and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes. The blots were blocked with 5% skimmed milk and incubated with primary antibodies against Nrf2 (cat. no. 16396-1-AP; 1:2,000), NF-κB p65 (cat. no. 80979-1-RR; 1:1,000), HO-1 (cat. no. ab68477; 1:1,000), lamin B (cat. no. 66095-1-Ig; 1:20,000), β-actin (cat. no. 60004-1-Ig; 1:5,000; all Proteintech Group, Inc.), TLR4 (cat. no. AF7017; 1:1,000; Affinity Biosciences), B-cell lymphoma-2 (Bcl-2; cat. no. GB154830; 1:1,000; Servicebio), Bcl-2-associated X protein (Bax; cat. no. BS-0127R; 1:1,000; BIOSS), and cleaved caspase-3 (cat. no. 9661 T; 1:1,000; Cell Signaling Technology), respectively. Next, the blots were incubated with appropriate secondary goat-anti-mouse (cat. no. 115-035-003) or goat-anti-rabbit (cat. no. 111-035-003) horseradish peroxidase-conjugated antibody (both 1:10,000; Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories, Inc.) for 1 h at room temperature. Finally, proteins were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence reagents (cat. no. NCI5079; Thermo Fisher Scientific), and band densities were quantified with a gel analysis software (ChemiDoc™XRS+; Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. The means of different groups were compared by one-way analysis of variance followed by the Student–Newman–Keuls test. All statistical tests were performed using GraphPad Prism software version 7.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA), with p values <0.05 denoting statistically significant differences.

Results

At 8 weeks after STZ injection, the diabetic rats showed obvious characteristics of diabetes, including hyperglycemia, weight loss, polydipsia, and polyphagia (Table 1). Compared with the age-matched controls, the blood glucose was significantly increased and the body weight was significantly reduced in diabetic rats.

RSV mitigates renal histological damage and ameliorates kidney dysfunction in diabetic rats

As shown in Figures 1(A,B), diabetic rats undergoing renal I/R displayed severe renal tubular damage including tubular dilatation, loss of the brush border, cast formation, and cell lysis. However, RSV pretreatment attenuated the histopathological changes, with a more significant effect observed at a concentration of 20 mg/kg (p < 0.05). After I/R induction, renal function worsened in diabetic rats, as indicated by significantly increased levels of SCr and BUN. However, RSV pretreatment ameliorated renal dysfunction, as demonstrated by significantly decreased levels of SCr and BUN, with a more significant effect observed at a concentration of 20 mg/kg (Figures 1(C,D), p < 0.05).

RSV alleviates I/R-induced oxidative stress and inflammation in diabetic rats

As shown in Figures 2(A,B), we found a significant increase of MDA levels together with a significant reduction in SOD activity in diabetic rats following renal I/R. However, compared with the DI/R group, the RSV pretreatment group was characterized by alleviated oxidative abnormalities, as indicated by markedly decreased MDA levels and increased SOD activity, with a more significant effect observed at a concentration of 20 mg/kg (p < 0.05). In addition, I/R induced obvious increases in the levels of TNF-α and IL-1β in diabetic rats (Figures 2(C,D), p < 0.05). However, the RSV pretreatment group exhibited significantly reduced TNF-α and IL-1β levels compared with the DI/R group, with a more significant effect observed at a concentration of 20 mg/kg (p < 0.05). These results suggest that RSV could prevent oxidative stress and inflammation following diabetic renal I/R.

RSV inhibits I/R-induced renal apoptosis in diabetic rats

The degree of apoptosis in renal tubular epithelial cells was evaluated by TUNEL and western blotting assays. Following the occurrence of I/R, cell apoptosis obviously increased in diabetic rats. RSV pretreatment significantly reduced the degree of apoptosis. However, administration of ML385 inhibited the positive effects elicited by RSV (Figures 3(A,B), p < 0.05). As shown in Figures 4A–D, compared with the DS group, the expression of Bax and cleaved caspase-3 in the DI/R group was significantly increased, whereas that of Bcl-2 was significantly decreased. RSV pretreatment significantly reduced Bax and cleaved caspase-3 levels and increased Bcl-2 expression. Furthermore, administration of ML385 eliminated the effects of RSV on these proteins (p < 0.05). These data demonstrate that RSV could inhibit renal tubular apoptosis in diabetic rats after I/R stimulation.

RSV activates the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway and blocks TLR4/NF-κB signaling after I/R induction

As shown in Figures 4A and 4E–H, there was a slight decrease in the expression of Nrf2 and its downstream target factor HO-1 in the DI/R group compared with the DS group (p < 0.05). However, the decreased expression of Nrf2 and HO-1 was significantly promoted in the DI/R + RSV group, which was further suppressed in the DI/R + RSV + ML385 group (p < 0.05). In addition, compared with the DS group, protein expression of TLR4 and NF-κB p65 was significantly upregulated in the DI/R group (p < 0.05). RSV pretreatment significantly inhibited the I/R-induced activation of the TLR4/NF-κB pathway, which was reversed by ML385 administration (p < 0.05). Taken together, these results show that the renoprotective effect of RSV on kidney I/R was related to Nrf2/HO-1 pathway activation and TLR4/NF-κB signaling inhibition.

RSV enhances cell viability and reduces oxidative stress after H/R stimulation of HK-2 cells

HK-2 cells treated with H/R showed an obvious decrease in cell viability, which was prevented by RSV pretreatment (Figure 5A, p < 0.05). Cell viability improved as the concentration of RSV increased, with a more significant effect observed at a concentration of 100 μM. We detected the intracellular ROS levels to evaluate the degree of oxidative stress in the cells. Moreover, the ROS levels were markedly increased in the H/R group compared with the control group (Figure 5(B)). However, RSV pretreatment reduced ROS levels after H/R stimulation, with a more significant effect observed at a concentration of 100 μM (p < 0.05). These findings indicate that RSV prevents HK-2 cells from H/R damage by reducing oxidative stress.

RSV protects HK-2 cells against H/R-induced apoptosis

Compared with the control cells, the degree of apoptosis was significantly increased in the H/R-treated cells (Figures 5C and D, p < 0.05). RSV exerted a protective effect against apoptosis induced by H/R; however, this effect was inhibited by ML385. Similarly, the expression of Bax and cleaved caspase-3 was increased, whereas that of Bcl-2 was decreased after H/R (Figures 6(A–D)). Treatment with RSV reduced Bax and cleaved caspase-3 expression and raised Bcl-2 levels, and these effects were inhibited by ML385 (p < 0.05). These data further confirm that RSV inhibits apoptosis in HK-2 cells after H/R stimulation.

RSV enhances Nrf2/HO-1 signaling and inhibits the TLR4/NF-κB pathway after H/R induction

As shown in Figures 6A and 6E–H, the expression levels of Nrf2 and HO-1 were slightly decreased in the H/R group compared with the control group (p < 0.05). RSV significantly upregulated the expression of Nrf2 and HO-1 in HK-2 cells subjected to H/R, and this effect was offset by ML385 (p < 0.05). The expression levels of TLR4 and NF-κB p65 were significantly increased after H/R stimulation, although treatment with RSV reduced their expression. ML385 increased the downregulated expression of TLR4 and NF-κB p65 in cells exposed to H/R (p < 0.05). These results indicate that the renoprotective effect of RSV involves the Nrf2/TLR4 pathway.

Discussion

The main objective of this study was to provide evidence that RSV can protect kidneys from I/R injury under a high-glucose environment and to elucidate the underlying mechanisms. In the in vivo study, we used STZ-induced diabetic rats to confirm that RSV alleviates renal dysfunction via antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-apoptotic effects after renal I/R. In addition, RSV reduced cell apoptosis and oxidative stress in H/R-induced HK-2 cells cultured in high-glucose medium. Furthermore, the enhancement of Nrf2/HO-1 signaling and inhibition of the TLR4/NF-κB pathway are the underlying mechanisms of RSV renoprotection in the setting of hyperglycemia.

I/R injury is a phenomenon which commonly occurs during hypovolemic conditions, surgery, transplantation, and septic shock. Studies have shown that it is responsible for several severe complications, such as acute kidney injury, delayed graft function, and allograft loss, thereby posing a great challenge to treating physicians [17]. Although the mechanism of renal I/R injury is not fully understood, it may be closely related to excessive production of oxygen free radicals, calcium overload, mitochondrial dysfunction, and inflammatory reaction [19,20]. Patients with diabetes, especially those with diabetic nephropathy, are susceptible to I/R injury. Previous studies have shown that diabetes aggravates renal I/R injury by promoting oxidative stress, inflammation, and apoptosis [1,5,21,22]. Furthermore, the protective measures against I/R injury are ineffective in a high-sugar environment. Schenning et al. revealed that the protective effect of ischemic preconditioning on glomerular endothelial cells against I/R injury was lost in the setting of hyperglycemia [23]. Gao et al. also demonstrated that diabetes blocked the cardioprotective effect of sevoflurane postconditioning against I/R injury [24]. However, in the present study, we found that RSV alleviated renal I/R injury in terms of histological improvements and decreased BUN and SCr levels compared with control. These results suggest that RSV can exert a renal protective effect to combat I/R injury, even in the setting of hyperglycemia.

Oxidative stress results from an imbalance between ROS production and scavenging capability. Under normal physiological conditions, ROS generated during cellular metabolism can be cleared by endogenous antioxidant enzymes, such as SOD, which can catalyze the dismutation of superoxide anion radicals into hydrogen peroxide and oxygen [22]. During reperfusion, ROS are overproduced due to abrupt restoration of oxygen and result in cellular dysfunction through lipid peroxidation, protein degradation, and DNA damage. MDA is a product of lipid peroxidation and can indicate the degree of oxidative stress. Nrf2 acts as a key redox-sensitive regulator of the antioxidant defense system by promoting the expression of its target antioxidant genes, including HO-1, and resists oxidative damage [22]. As a natural antioxidant, RSV is used to treat diseases related to oxidative stress, such as I/R injury. Because diabetes often weakens the effects of various protective factors on I/R injury, it remains unclear whether RSV can exert antioxidant effects against diabetic I/R injury. Previous studies have demonstrated the antioxidant properties of RSV which include decreasing MDA production and increasing glutathione levels to alleviate diabetic hepatic I/R damage [4]. Xu et al. also confirmed that RSV protected the myocardium from I/R injury in diabetic rats through enhancing the expression of Nrf2 and further inhibiting oxidative stress in diabetic heart [25]. Consistent with these studies, our investigation showed that RSV pretreatment significantly upregulated the expression of Nrf2 and its downstream target gene HO-1 both in vivo and in vitro, while inhibition of Nrf2 markedly reversed the beneficial effect of RSV. Based on previous research data and our findings, we can conclude that RSV enhances the antioxidant power of the Nrf2 pathway and reduces I/R-induced oxidative damage in the diabetic kidney.

The TLR4 signaling pathway, which plays an important role in both innate and adaptive immune systems, is an essential cascade during I/R injury [26]. TLR4 is a transmembrane receptor that can be activated by I/R injury, thereby triggering the TLR4 signaling pathway [27]. The activation signal of TLR4 is transmitted via the adaptor protein myeloid differentiation primary response 88 (MyD88), which is associated with activated TLRs and can enhance the subsequent signaling processes, thereby activating the inflammation-related protein NF-κB [28]. NF-κB is an essential transcription factor that regulates the expression of numerous genes, including those encoding adhesion molecules, chemokines, and cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β [29]. The NF-κB signaling ultimately triggers inflammatory responses and leukocyte recruitment. Although diabetic nephropathy has been regarded as a nonimmune disease, a growing body of evidence has confirmed the pivotal role of inflammation in its pathological progression [30,31]. Moreover, diabetes further aggravates renal I/R injury through TLR4/NF-κB-mediated inflammatory reaction [1]. These results indicate that TLR4 stimulation and the subsequent activation of the NF-κB pathway may represent a potential therapeutic target for ischemic acute kidney injury. Previous studies have shown that RSV can effectively alleviate myocardial and renal I/R injury, potentially by reducing the expression of TLR4/NF-κB in myocardial and renal tissues, thereby improving inflammation [17,32]. Wang et al. also demonstrated that RSV attenuated renal I/R injury in diabetic rats through the inhibiting TNF-α-induced inflammation and inactivation of cyclooxygenase 2 [33]. In the current study, we consistently observed that the upregulation of TLR4 and NF-κB induced by I/R or H/R stimulation was inhibited by RSV pretreatment in a high-glucose environment. Taken together, these results indicate that RSV can exert its anti-inflammatory effect to combat renal I/R injury under high-glucose condition, and this effect is associated with the inhibition of TLR4/NF-κB-mediated inflammatory signaling in the kidney.

Apoptosis is a physiological cell death mechanism that occurs after renal I/R induction [34,35] and is characterized by the activation of either intrinsic or extrinsic pathways. Cell apoptosis increases under pathological conditions, leading to tissue damage and loss of function [36]. Given the established role of oxidative stress and inflammation in triggering tubular apoptosis following ischemic injury, we next evaluated whether RSV attenuates apoptosis in diabetic kidneys. The TUNEL assay results indicated that RSV pretreatment reduces the number of apoptotic cells caused by I/R injury, suggesting that RSV has an anti-apoptotic effect. Bcl-2 is an anti-apoptotic protein that acts as the gatekeeper of mitochondria, inhibiting cell apoptosis by reducing oxyradical-mediated membrane damage and suppressing the release of pro-apoptotic proteins [37]. In contrast to Bcl-2, Bax (a key member of the Bcl-2 family) can promote apoptosis by forming oligomers on the outer mitochondrial membrane, thus creating a channel for the release of cytochrome C [38]. Caspase-3 is also a pro-apoptotic protein synthesized in the form of a precursor enzyme and activated through proteolytic cleavage. Our results showed that RSV increased Bcl-2 levels and downregulated the expression of both Bax and cleaved caspase-3 in vivo and in vitro, demonstrating that RSV inhibited cell apoptosis by repressing the caspase cascade.

Conclusions

Collectively, the results of our study indicate that inhibition of oxidative stress, inflammation, and apoptosis with RSV attenuates renal I/R injury in diabetes by enhancing Nrf2/HO-1 signaling and blocking the TLR4/NF-κB pathway. Moreover, RSV may be a therapeutic drug to ameliorate renal I/R injury in patients with diabetes.
