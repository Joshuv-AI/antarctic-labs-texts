TE_Bench: a foundational benchmarking workflow for transposable element annotation pipelines
Authors: Hannah P Kania, Sierra A Seifert, Anne D Yoder
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMethods for computational discovery of transposable elements (TEs) in DNA sequences are under continual development. Although many TE annotation pipelines have been published, differences in their methods are often opaque and can lead to inconsistent results. As new methods emerge, there is a growing need for an informative and reproducible strategy to evaluate pipeline performance.ResultsWe developed TE_Bench, a user-friendly TE annotation benchmarking workflow that streamlines data generation and visualization for systematic comparison of annotation pipeline performance. TE_Bench can automate simulation of DNA sequences containing artificially evolved TEs from a database or accept user-provided real data to quantify how well test pipelines detect TEs relative to a reference annotation. To accommodate users with varying starting points, TE_Bench is housed as a Snakemake workflow with several options. The data it generates can be used to determine which TE annotation pipeline to use for a specific task, or to inform future improvements to pipelines by revealing shortcomings. We demonstrate the utility of TE_Bench in both contexts by benchmarking EDTA, RepeatModeler2, and Earl Grey using simulated and real DNA sequences. With simulated data, we assess the impact of variables including TE class and nested structure on annotation quality, and with real data, we consider whether read type and mapping method influence downstream annotation. In their default configurations, RepeatModeler2 and Earl Grey perform similarly, outperforming EDTA.ConclusionsTE_Bench is an extensible, open-source workflow that supports community-driven TE annotation benchmarking using simulated ground-truth or real genomes. Background

Transposable elements (TEs) are powerful drivers of genomic disruption and diversity. Active TEs can upset gene expression, reshape epigenetic landscapes, and contribute to pathological processes including inflammation and tumorigenesis [1–3]. Even elements that have lost the ability to transpose due to mutations or host-mediated silencing don’t lose their influence. Inactive TEs can act as recombination substrates that drive genomic structural rearrangements as well as reservoirs of regulatory sequences that can be co-opted over time [3, 4].

Identifying TEs and other repetitive regions in DNA sequences is a critical first step in many genomic investigations [5–7]. Despite their ubiquity, TE discovery remains a challenging computational problem. TE sequences vary widely in age, subfamily structure, degree of fragmentation, nesting complexity, and the presence of mosaic architectures, in which multiple TE families and simple or tandem repeats are combined within a single element (e.g., SVA elements) [8]. These characteristics complicate the development of a single algorithm that can accurately capture the full diversity of TEs. Although existing discovery pipelines are excellent repeat-finders, they often struggle to classify TEs, delineate their boundaries, and leverage biological signs of insertion without also introducing numerous false positives [9, 10]. Consequently, the gold standard for TE annotation is computational discovery followed by manual curation, where experts refine TE family consensus sequences by inspecting alignments of their members in the genome [5, 11]. While effective, this process is time- and labor-intensive, limiting its scalability in the context of rapidly expanding genomic data.

Recent advances have sought to reduce reliance on manual curation while improving annotation quality. Tools such as Earl Grey [12], TETrimmer [13], and MCHelper [14] refine TE consensus sequences identified by established pipelines like RepeatModeler2 [15], emulating and in some ways improving on manual curation. Additional software complement these efforts by meeting the demands posed by developments in genomic sequencing and analysis. Specifically, pantera [16], panEDTA [17], and GraffiTE [18] were developed to address the complexities of polymorphic TE insertions, long-read sequencing, and graph-based genomes. As a result, the number of available TE annotation tools has grown substantially, with some designed for standalone use and others intended for integration into complex pipelines [9]. However, annotation quality can vary considerably depending on the pipeline used to generate it [8, 11, 19, 20], making pipeline selection a nontrivial task for users.

Although some studies invoke benchmarks to compare annotation pipelines, a standardized and widely-applicable benchmarking workflow has not yet been published [10, 21]. Existing efforts outside of tool-specific validation do not fully meet this need, as they address only individual components of the benchmarking process rather than providing a unified, end-to-end framework. TEgenomeSimulator [22] and TEvarSim [23] advance simulation of ground-truth data by enabling the controlled generation of synthetic genomes and transposable element landscapes, but they do not support downstream comparison of whole-genome TE annotations. denovoTE-eval [21] introduces a workflow to compare the performance of de novo TE detection tools, yet it lacks an automated wrapper for generalizable reuse on new annotation pipelines. More broadly, benchmarking strategies in the literature range from genome-wide, base-pair level measures of sensitivity and precision, to summary metrics that emphasize structural accuracy and element completeness (e.g., NTE50, LTE50, and FLC) [24], to categorical schemes classifying annotations as “perfect,” “good,” “present,” or “not found” [15]. This lack of standardization hinders comparisons across studies and limits reproducibility.

To address these challenges, we developed TE_Bench, an automated, end-to-end Snakemake-based [25] benchmarking solution designed to support efficient, data-informed selection of TE annotation pipelines. First, TE_Bench invites users to simulate customizable DNA sequences with known TE insertions. Under the hood, GARLIC [26] makes statistically informed decisions on repeat placement, mutations, and nesting within simulated sequences. Since highly curated TE annotations are not available for most genomes, sequence simulation is necessary to generate reliable reference TE annotations. Once simulated sequences are submitted to any number of TE annotation pipelines, TE_Bench compares the resulting test annotations to the reference annotation in a series of analyses considering TE class, divergence, and nested structure. In addition to base-pair level performance metrics, TE_Bench returns publication-ready visualizations of performance to facilitate expeditious identification of high-performing pipelines, or to lay the groundwork for deeper investigations into deficiencies in computational TE discovery. In our case study, we discuss these outputs in the context of running EDTA v2.2.0 [20], RepeatModeler2 v2.0.5 [15], and Earl Grey v4.4.0 [12] on five species-specific simulated DNA sequences. We also include an example of alternatively using TE_Bench to evaluate the understudied effects of read type and mapping strategy on downstream TE annotation of real DNA sequences.

Implementation

Our benchmarking workflow is organized across three stages: DNA sequence simulation, reference annotation file generation, and benchmarking (Fig. 1).

TE_Bench workflow overview

Each stage automates a different step of TE_Bench, as follows: Stage 1: GARLIC Sequence Simulation; Stage 2: Simulated Annotation File Generation; Stage 3a: Comprehensive Benchmarking; Stage 3b: Selective TE Benchmarking. The stages can be run independently for flexibility. For example, a user who already has reference and test TE annotation files can skip to Stage 3 for immediate results. More detailed instructions covering installation and execution of TE_Bench are available on our GitHub repository (https://github.com/hkania/TE_Bench).

TE_Bench provides Snakefile options to automate all benchmarking stages. Although designed to run in order, the options are compartmentalized to accommodate users who wish to skip DNA sequence simulation in favor of supplying their own data directly to comprehensive and/or selective benchmarking. To ensure extensibility as new TE annotation softwares are released, TE_Bench can accept test annotation output from any software so long as it adheres to a standard 8-column ‘.csv’ format outlined on our GitHub repository (https://github.com/hkania/TE_Bench). Scripts that convert the TE annotation files output by EDTA, RepeatModeler2, and Earl Grey to 8-column ‘.csv’ files are available on our GitHub repository for reuse or reference.

GARLIC DNA sequence simulation

Simulation_Snakefile outlines a job execution plan that automates the generation of simulated DNA sequences based on a model genome and user-specified TE database, either Repbase [27] or Dfam [28]. Users should consider that Repbase is behind a paywall as of publication of this manuscript. Dfam has a larger volume of TE sequence data, which impacts the computational load of the rules within Simulation_Snakefile and therefore runtime.

To execute Simulation_Snakefile, users must supply a ‘.fasta’ file, a tandem repeat ‘.bed’ file, a gene annotation ‘.gtf’ file, and a RepeatMasker ‘.align’ file to specify their model genome. These files can be downloaded from the UCSC Genome Browser [29] (https://genome.ucsc.edu/) or may be user-supplied, provided they match UCSC Genome Browser file formatting. Importantly, the files must share a base name for Snakemake to parse them appropriately, for example, the base name droMel in the following file set: droMel.fa, droMel.bed, droMel.gtf, and droMel.align.

Before running Simulation_Snakefile, users must define configuration parameters by modifying the provided ‘config_simulation.yaml’ file or by using the following config flags at the command line: --size to specify the sequence length in nucleotides to simulate (default is 1000), --num_seqs to specify the number of sequences to simulate per model (default is 2), --repbase to expect TE sequence data from Repbase (default is false), --dfam to expect TE sequence from Dfam (default is true), and --test to run simulation using the TE_Bench test data (default is false).

Internally, Simulation_Snakefile uses modified scripts from GARLIC to simulate sequences. Specifically, ‘createModel.pl’ reads the user-supplied files and returns summaries of the model’s tetramers, repetitive element inserts, and simple sequence repeats across GC bins of 10 kb. Size, divergence, and frequency of insertion into other elements are noted for each repeat, and this information is used by ‘createFakeSequence_repbase.pl’ or ‘createFakeSequence_dfam.pl’ to guide simulation of realistic, non-functional DNA sequences containing artificially evolved and nested TEs. Please see the original GARLIC manuscript for more details [26]. Then, the TE_Bench scripts ‘seqkit.sh’ and ‘split_inserts.py’ separate the multi-FASTA sequence files output by GARLIC. Additional details for all associated scripts can be found in Supplementary Material 2.

When sequence generation completes, users can expect to have a log file, simulated ‘.fasta’ files, and simulated ‘.inserts’ files that can be used to build a reference annotation file with AnnotationGen_Snakefile. For ease of use, the TE_Bench repository includes a Snakemake profile that configures Simulation_Snakefile for execution on SLURM-managed high-performance computing environments so that sequence generation can run in the background, as it can take hours to complete. The repository also includes conda environments with ‘perl v.5.32.1’ and ‘seqkit v.2.10.1’ dependencies.

For detailed advice on how to run Simulation_Snakefile, users can refer to the User Guide “Simulation_Snakefile, Workflow Stage 1” in the TE_Bench README file.

Annotation file generation

AnnotationGen_Snakefile outlines a job execution plan that automates the generation of TE annotation ‘.gff’ and ‘.csv’ files for simulated reference DNA sequences.

To execute AnnotationGen_Snakefile, users must have already used Simulation_Snakefile to produce ‘.fasta’ files, ‘.insert’ files, and a ‘rule_garlic_sequence_generation’ log file for their genomic model of interest.

Before running AnnotationGen_Snakefile, users must define configuration parameters by modifying the provided ‘config_annotationgen.yaml’ file or by using the following config flags at the command line: --num_seqs to specify the number of sequences simulated with Simulation_Snakefile per model (default is 2) and --GARLIC_LOG_FILE to specify the location of the log file generated during the associated simulation run (default is input/testdata/droMelTEST_garlic_test.log for testing).

Internally, AnnotationGen_Snakefile uses a custom bash script, 'garlic_gff_gen.sh', and two awk scripts, 'sequence_start_stop.awk' and 'nested_gff.awk', to generate ‘.gff’ files for the associated simulated sequences. This combination of scripts extracts GARLIC-generated attributes and start and stop positions for each TE insertion, using NCBI Blast+ [30] to locate all nested TE boundaries since GARLIC does not delineate the base pair locations of modeled TEs nested within others. Once the ‘.gff’ files are produced, 'Garlic_gff_to_csv.R' converts them into 8-column ‘.csv’ files compatible with Benchmark_Snakefile. Additional details for all associated scripts can be found in Supplementary Material 2.

When annotation generation completes, users can expect to have ‘.gff’ and ‘.csv’ files for each sequence simulated in a GARLIC simulation run to use as ground-truth reference data in subsequent benchmarking. For ease of use, the TE_Bench repository includes a Snakemake profile that configures AnnotationGen_Snakefile for execution on SLURM-managed high-performance computing environments so that sequence generation can run in the background, as it can take hours to days to complete.

For detailed advice on how to run AnnotationGen_Snakefile, users can refer to the User Guide “AnnotationGen_Snakefile, Workflow Stage 2” in the TE_Bench README file.

Benchmarking

Benchmark_Snakefile outlines a job execution plan that automates the generation of standardized performance metrics and comparative visualizations for a given set of TE annotation data on both comprehensive and selective levels. On a comprehensive level, Benchmark_Snakefile produces TE annotation performance metrics and comparative visualizations for all reference TE elements. On a selective level, Benchmark_Snakefile produces TE annotation performance metrics and a subset of comparative visualizations using a filtered set of reference TEs; for example, for all reference LTR elements. If Simulation_Snakemake was executed, users can optionally use Benchmark_Snakefile to visualize the relationship between reference TE divergence and element coverage by a test annotation, as GARLIC produces the necessary TE consensus sequence data.

To execute Benchmark_Snakefile, users must supply reference and any number of test ‘.csv’ files in the expected 8-column format. Users wishing to pursue the divergence versus coverage analysis mentioned above must also supply the directory containing the '.insert' files for the model at hand.

Before running Benchmark_Snakefile, users must define configuration parameters by modifying the provided ‘config_benchmark.yaml’ file or by using the following config flags at the command line: --stg3a to run comprehensive benchmarking, --stg3b for selective benchmarking, --clean_eg, --clean_rm, --clean_edta for data parsing the annotation outputs of the respective annotation pipelines on the reference FASTA file(s), --radar for producing performance metric score visualizations, --nest for producing coverage visualizations based on TE nested status, --pie for producing coverage pie charts, --iden for performing percent identity analysis, --redundancy for producing redundancy and misclassification heatmaps (default for aforementioned flags is true), --RM2_gff_ext, --EG_gff_ext, --EDTA_gff_ext with the extensions of ‘.gff’ files produced from those respective annotation pipelines for data parsing (defaults are _RM2.gff, _EG.gff, and _EDTA.gff), --RM2_fasta_ext with the extension of the FASTA file(s) produced from RepeatModeler2 for the reference FASTA(s) (default is _RM2.fa), --seq_len to specify the length of the reference sequence in base pairs (default is 100000000), --inserts_dir to specify the directory containing ‘.inserts’ file(s) if running an identity analysis (default is input/annotationdata/testing), --extras to denote the TE classes outside of LINE, LINE-dependent, LTR, and DNA to include in identity analysis (default is “Unknown”), --column to specify the column to sort on for selective benchmarking (default is V5, column 5), and --target_types to specify the variables of interest within the specified column for selective benchmarking (default is LTR).

Internally, Benchmark_Snakefile runs multiple scripts to generate performance metrics and visualizations. 'EG_gff_to_csv.R', 'EDTA_gff_to_csv.R', and 'RM2_gff_to_csv.R' convert TE annotation data from EarlGrey, EDTA, and RepeatModeler2 into 8-column ‘.csv’ files. 'generate_stats.py' calculates performance metric scores at the base-pair level which are used by 'view_stats.py' to produce radar plots that compare multiple test TE annotations [see Performance Metrics and Supplementary Material 2 for further metric calculation details]. 'plot_covered.py' produces a new ‘.csv’ file per test annotation file with an additional column containing the percent of each reference element covered by the test annotation and a complementary coverage pie chart depicting how many elements within each major TE classification (LTR, LINE, etcetera) are covered 80% or more by the test annotation. 'create_new_csv_id_ana.py' extends the input reference ‘.csv’ file by adding a column containing the percent identity of each reference TE relative to its family consensus sequence. 'plot_identity_vs_coverage.py' uses the ‘.csv’ file output by create_new_csv_id_ana.py to generate class-specific regression plots depicting percent coverage of reference TE by the test annotation versus percent identity. 'calculate_overlap_csvs.py' generates three new ‘.csv’ files given a reference ‘.csv’ file: one containing nested elements, one containing host elements that harbor nested elements, and one containing elements not involved in nesting. These files are used by 'plot_nesting.py' to generate bar charts showing TE annotation coverage stratified by TE nesting status.

'bpoverlap.py' reads each test annotation ‘.csv’ file and produces a summary including the counts of overlapping base pairs within a test annotation and how those counts are dispersed by TE categorization and locus-level overlap. TE loci are classified as redundant when more than 95% of their base pairs overlap with another annotated TE, as partially overlapping when 0–95% of their base pairs overlap with another TE, and as contained (nested) when 100% of their base pairs were located within another annotated TE. The 'bpoverlap.py script' is designed to be used alongside 'redundancy_heatmap.py' to assess the extent of redundancy within each test annotation and to identify broader classification inconsistencies between the test and reference annotations. Using the test and reference '.csv' files, 'redundancy_heatmap.py' determines both the degree of overlap between elements within the test annotation and the number of test annotation elements corresponding to each TE base pair in the reference annotation. For each pair of TE classes, the overlap score is calculated by dividing the number of overlapping base pairs between the two classes by the smaller of the two class coverage values, thereby measuring how completely the smaller class is represented within the larger one. Biologically, a high overlap score indicates that one TE class is largely contained within or repeatedly co-annotated with another class, suggesting potential redundancy, nested insertions, or ambiguity in classification between the two TE families, whereas a low overlap score indicates that the classes are generally distinct in their genomic distribution. The script outputs a visualization containing two heatmaps [see Misclassification and Redundancy Analysis and Supplementary Material 2 for further details].

Selective benchmarking uses 'filter_csv.R' to generate new reference and test annotation ‘.csv’ files filtered on a TE attribute specified by the user, for example, ‘.csv’ files containing only LTR elements identified using the fifth ‘.csv’ column. These ‘.csv’ files are input to the aforementioned benchmarking scripts to generate performance metrics and visualizations as well as coverage ‘.csv’ files for the filtered TE annotation data. Additional details for all associated scripts can be found in Supplementary Material 2.

When benchmarking completes, users can expect many new files to propagate depending on their configuration of Benchmark_Snakefile, all of which are outlined in the associated GitHub User Guide.

For output information and detailed advice on how to run Benchmark_Snakefile, users can refer to the User Guide “Benchmark_Snakefile, Workflow Stage 3” in the TE_Bench README file.

Results

To demonstrate the utility of our benchmarking workflow, we conducted a case study examining three popular de-novo TE annotation pipelines: EDTA, RepeatModeler2, and Earl Grey. Specifically, we evaluated the performance of each in TE annotation of five 100 Mb simulated DNA sequences modeled on the reference genomes of Arabidopsis thaliana, Drosophila melanogaster, Homo sapiens, Myotis lucifugus, and Saccharomyces cerevisiae [see Supplementary Material 3 for files used in GARLIC model building]. The simulated sequences and raw TE annotation data are publicly available as tarball archives within the associated data repository [https://doi.org/10.7924/r4r509, raw_anno_data.tar]. We present benchmark results primarily for the M. lucifugus simulated sequence. Readers interested in benchmark results for the remaining models can see Supplementary Material 1. To demonstrate TE_Bench’s applicability to TE annotations of real DNA sequences, we also present results comparing annotation quality between five D. melanogaster consensus sequences built using variable sequencing platforms and read mapping strategies.

Performance metrics

Firstly, for every user-specified TE annotation pipeline, TE_Bench compares its output annotation file to the reference annotation file to generate performance metrics heavily cited in preexisting benchmarking efforts: sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, precision, FDR, F1 score, and Matthew’s correlation coefficient (MCC) (Fig. 2) [12, 20, 21].

TE annotation performance metric calculations. a Illustration of 1:1 bp comparisons between reference and test annotations with associated benchmarking true and false calls. b Matthew’s Correlation Coefficients measure the quality of binary classifications and range from − 1.00 to 1.00, where 1.00 is a perfect annotation score and − 1.00 represents complete disagreement with the reference. c Standard benchmarking performance metrics returned by TE_Bench

All metrics are calculated using true positive (TP), true negative (TN), false positive (FP), and false negative (FN) counts across the entire underlying DNA sequence. Namely, for every test annotation to reference annotation comparison, a nucleotide is counted as TP if it’s within a predicted TE in both the test and reference annotations, TN if it’s not within a predicted TE in both the test and reference annotations, FP if it’s within a predicted TE in the test annotation but not the reference annotation, and FN if it’s within a predicted TE in the reference annotation but not the test annotation (Fig. 2a). Counts depend on the scale of benchmarking invoked [see Supplementary Material 2 generate_stats.py]. Unlike the other metrics, MCC accounts for all four confusion matrix outcomes, making it a particularly valuable standalone indicator of pipeline performance. MCC ranges from − 1, indicating that all predictions made by a pipeline are false, to 1, indicating that all predictions are true (Fig. 2b) [21, 31].

In addition to returning performance metrics in plain-text format, TE_Bench provides publication-ready radar plots to visualize them. In our case study, radar plots summarize the annotation performance of EDTA, RepeatModeler2, and Earl Grey on the five species-specific sequences we simulated (Fig. 3).

Radar plots summarize annotation quality across test pipelines and genomic models. a Performance metrics for annotations of all TEs in the Myotis lucifugus simulated sequence. EG - Earl Grey, RM - RepeatModeler2. b Performance metrics for annotations of all TEs in the Arabidopsis thaliana simulated sequence. c Performance metrics for annotations of all TEs in the Drosophila melanogaster simulated sequence. d Performance metrics for annotations of all TEs in the Homo sapiens simulated sequence. e Performance metrics for annotations of all TEs in the Saccharomyces cerevisiae simulated sequence, the best whole-sequence annotation performance achieved by all three pipelines. See Supplementary Material 2 table S3-S7 for raw performance metric scores. Note that in all plots, FDR (False Discovery Rate) is inversely related to performance compared to the other metrics shown and is complementary to precision. Consequently, a FDR of 0 represents perfect performance, corresponding to a precision of 1

RepeatModeler2 (average MCC = 0.85) and Earl Grey (average MCC = 0.83) outperform EDTA (average MCC = 0.64) at predicting TEs across all inputs except the M. lucifigus simulated sequence. Although their relative performance is consistent, the absolute performance of the pipelines changes depending on the input sequence. For example, annotations of the H. sapiens sequence are markedly less sensitive than annotations of the S. cerevisiae sequence, likely reflecting differences in the TE composition of the represented genomes (Fig. 3b&e). While the S. cerevisiae genome contains only Y-Prime and LTR TEs, the H. sapiens genome additionally contains non-LTR TEs such as LINEs and SINEs that are challenging to accurately reconstruct due to truncation, fragmentation, and weak structural signatures [10, 32, 33]. While the performance metrics returned by TE_Bench provide a high-level assessment of TE annotation quality, more thorough analysis is necessary to elucidate the factors that influence annotation performance.

Class analysis

Beyond performance metrics, TE_Bench returns adaptable plots to examine pipeline performance more closely in view of TE class, nested structure, and divergence from their consensus sequences. An example output of our workflow depicts TE class-specific detection by EDTA, RepeatModeler2, and Earl Grey in the M. lucifugus simulated DNA sequence (Fig. 4).

Pie charts compare annotation coverage across TE classes. Pie charts output by Stage 3a of TE_Bench depict the relative abundance of TEs by subclass in the reference TE annotation (inner ring) and the percent of elements in each subclass covered by a particular test annotation (outer ring). A TE is defined as covered when the test annotation overlaps at least 80% of its total length in base pairs, regardless of test annotation classification. TE coverage in the M. lucifugus simulated sequence as annotated by EDTA, RepeatModeler2, and Earl Grey is shown, demonstrating the value of visualizing coverage by subclass when choosing an annotation pipeline. Earl Grey’s comparably poor coverage of LINE-dependent TEs was found to be the result of an internal RepeatMasker command which masked small tRNA-derived SINEs during annotation [see Supplementary Material 1, Figure S3]

By default, a reference TE is considered found if it’s greater than 80% percent covered by a test pipeline’s annotation. Output pie chart plots from TE_Bench can inform comparisons of class-specific annotation within and between test pipelines. However, they cannot stand alone as evidence of one pipeline’s superiority over another because they do not account for the non-TE parts of input DNA sequences. For instance, a dummy pipeline that annotates the entire input sequence would cover all reference TEs 100% but incur an enormous FDR (see Performance Metrics). In our case study, we see that EDTA finds the least DNA, LINE, and LTR elements in the M. lucifugus simulated sequence relative to RepeatModeler2 and Earl Grey. As expected, because Earl Grey uses RepeatModeler2 for initial repeat-finding before alignment extension and refinement, we observe that Earl Grey improves on RepeatModeler2’s detection of DNA and LINE elements. However, Earl Grey fails to retain more than 50% of the LINE-dependent elements that are correctly identified by RepeatModeler2. We observe a similar loss of sensitivity for LINE-dependent elements from RepeatModeler2 to Earl Grey in annotations of the A. thaliana simulated DNA sequence, but not the H. sapiens sequence [see Supplementary Material 1, Figure S1-S2]. To follow up on the observed discrepancy, we hypothesized that relative to abundant 7SL-derived Alu SINEs in humans, smaller and less complex tRNA-derived SINEs such as Ves and RatheE1 that predominate in M. lucifugus and A. thaliana, respectively, are excluded from annotation during Earl Grey’s final RepeatMasker step, which in the analyzed version of Earl Grey, 4.4.0, is hardcoded to ignore small RNA genes. When we internally removed the -norna flag in the RepeatMasker step, the percent of LINE-dependent TEs found by Earl Grey v4.4.0 in the M. lucifugus sequence increased from 32.6 to 87.1, surpassing RepeatModeler2 (Fig. S1, See Supplementary Material 1 Table S2 & S3). Although the fix was straightforward, the problem it solved would not have been apparent without benchmarking annotations of sequences representative of SINE diversity. TE_Bench empowers users to quickly test multiple pipelines on multiple sequences to uncover new problems that deserve attention in the TE annotation space.

Nested structure analysis

Another feature of TE_Bench enables users to investigate challenges in computational TE annotation that arise from the biological propensity of some TEs (“nested”) to insert into other TEs (“hosts”) (Fig. 5a).

Bar charts compare annotation coverage distributions between nested and host TEs. a Illustration of a nested TE sequence, where host elements (black) harbor at least one nested TE (grey) within their length. b Bar charts output by Stage 3 of TE_Bench depicting coverage distributions of nested versus host TEs in the M. lucifugus simulated sequence. Reference nested and host TEs are assigned to coverage bins based on the percent of their length that is correctly annotated by a test pipeline. For example, reference TEs that are fully annotated are assigned to the > 80% coverage bin, while reference TEs that are only 40% annotated are assigned to the 20–40% coverage bin

Nesting creates chimeric DNA sequences, in which distinguishing one TE from another is algorithmically difficult [9–11, 21]. To evaluate a pipeline’s competency in resolving nested TEs, TE_Bench separately calculates the coverage of reference nested TEs and reference host TEs. In our case study, we observe a marked difference in the coverage distributions of nested versus host TEs in the M. lucifugus simulated sequence (Fig. 5b). While binning nested TEs by the percent that they are covered by EDTA, RepeatModeler2, and Earl Grey creates a bimodal distribution, indicating that the test pipelines either find nested TEs (> 80% coverage) or not (< 20% coverage), filtering instead for only host TEs creates a flatter distribution with no predominant peak. The larger proportion of host TEs relative to nested TEs that fall into intermediate coverage bins suggests that precise delineation of host TE boundaries remains a weakness in the annotation pipelines that we tested. However, since host TEs are older than their nests by definition, poorer coverage of hosts might also reflect difficulties in defining the start and end positions of elements that have accumulated more mutations over time. Regardless, users should exercise caution when annotating TEs known to harbor other TEs, such as TIR DNA transposons in eukaryotes [34]. Running our benchmarking workflow before formal annotation generation may help inform whether added measures like manual curation post computational discovery are necessary to improve annotation quality.

Divergence analysis

Modeling divergence of TEs from their consensus sequences across diverse genomic data is a distinct advantage of simulating sequences using GARLIC. Resulting reference TE annotations inherently vary, as some genomic models feature higher or lower frequencies of fragmented TEs compared to others. A plot provided by TE_Bench equips users to inspect the relationship between the identity of reference TEs and the percent that they are covered by a test pipeline (Fig. 6).

Regressions consider the relationship between TE identity to consensus and coverage by a test annotation. Scatter plots output by Stage 3 of TE_Bench depict the strength of correlations between TE identity to consensus and coverage by Earl Grey in the M. lucifugus simulated sequence. A positive correlation is observed for reference DNA elements, suggesting that Type II TEs that are more similar to their consensus sequences are more likely to be annotated by Earl Grey. Subclass-specific scatter plots can be examined at finer granularity to investigate how annotation pipelines respond to divergence within specific TE superfamilies, such as Tc-Mariner DNA elements (grey box)

TE_Bench computes the percent identity of a reference TE to its family consensus as the percent of identical bases between the two sequences. By plotting identity against coverage, we can evaluate how test pipelines respond to elements that are increasingly divergent from their family’s consensus sequence; for example, we observe a correlation (r = 0.43) between the identity of M. lucifugus DNA elements and Earl Grey’s coverage of them (Fig. 6). Comparing distributions across TE classes or subclasses can indicate which types of elements might be especially recalcitrant to detection if sufficiently mutated. Alternatively, comparing distributions across test pipelines can shed light on the varying sensitivities of their repeat-finding modules. EDTA, RepeatModeler2, and Earl Grey use the same software for discovery of non-LTR TEs: RepeatScout [35], which identifies and iteratively extends short repeats, and Recon [36], which identifies longer repeats via exhaustive inter-genome alignments. Thus, although we didn’t find evidence of different sensitivities among our test pipelines (i.e. different percent identity thresholds at which they fail to adequately cover elements in a TE family), it’s an important variable to bear in mind with the advent of new software, especially for researchers interested in older, fragmented TEs.

Misclassification and redundancy analysis

Misclassification and redundancy, where multiple elements correspond to a singular base pair or locus, are known challenges for TE annotation pipelines [8, 10]. The redundancy summary files and heatmaps produced by TE_Bench examine annotation misclassification and redundancy at different scales and together help explain how redundant annotations, especially when misclassified, can distort associated annotation performance metrics. For example, if a reference LINE element is classified as an LTR in the test annotation, those base pairs simultaneously contribute FNs for the LINE class and FPs for the LTR class in selective, class-specific benchmarking.

Furthermore, a single TE annotated as several overlapping predictions may present as found on account of base pair-level coverage, despite being only partially recovered at the locus level.

TE_Bench visualizes test annotation misclassifications by comparing overlapping base pairs between TE classes in the reference and test annotations (Fig. 7a).

Heatmaps consider test annotation misclassification and redundancy. a Heatmap demonstrating the relationship of base pairs within each reference TE class that are recovered and assigned to each EDTA class, normalized by class coverage. A value of 1 means there is a strong correlation in where the genomic sequence is annotated, and values approaching 0 mean little to no shared genomic space between the compared reference and EDTA classes. The heatmap is based on total overlapping base pairs, not element counts, so a value of 1 on the diagonal does not necessarily mean the test annotation recovered all reference elements. Instead, it means that the presented test annotations within a class categorization overlap base pairs within the correct reference class. b Heatmap showing how much genomic sequence is shared between pairs of TE classes within the EDTA annotation set, normalized by the smaller class’s total coverage. A value of 1 indicates complete overlap of the smaller class’s genomic sequence with the compared class, while values approaching 0 indicate little to no shared genomic regions between classes. Values greater than 1 indicate that overlapping or fragmented annotations are causing repeated counting of shared genomic regions, reflecting strong redundancy or over-segmentation in the EDTA annotation set

Off-diagonal enrichment in the lefthand matrix highlights potential classification inconsistencies between reference and test annotations. In our case study of M. lucifugus, we observed substantial off-diagonal signal between reference DNA elements and EDTA-annotated LTR elements (Fig. 7a). An overlap value of 0.61 between reference DNA elements and EDTA LTR elements suggests a tendency for EDTA to classify portions of DNA elements as LTR-derived sequence.

Notably, a test annotation can show high base-pair overlap with the reference while still being fragmented into redundant, smaller calls, and thus within-annotation redundancy can inflate annotation sensitivity while obscuring deficiencies in boundary resolution and locus-level accuracy. TE_Bench helps identify cases where multiple TE predictions cover the same locus, single elements are split into multiple overlapping annotations, or nested calls repeatedly annotate the same sequence by combining a test-versus-test heatmap with summary outputs that quantify overlapping rows in the test annotation (Fig. 7b & Supplementary Material 4). In our case study, we observe that EDTA’s annotation of M. lucifugus features values greater than 1 for DNA, LTR, and LINE elements, meaning that annotations of those classes overlap themselves enough (via fragmentation or redundancy) that the same genomic regions, at the base pair level, are being counted multiple times by EDTA (Fig. 7b).

Workflow application: sequencing platform analysis

Finally, our benchmarking workflow can be applied to TE annotations of real DNA sequences, provided a highly curated reference annotation file exists. We demonstrate this utility by investigating whether DNA sequencing platform influences downstream TE annotation. DNA sequencing platforms from PacBio to Illumina can introduce biases that propagate into TE annotation [9, 10, 37, 38], yet we found only two studies that quantify the impact of different sequencing technologies on TE detection [39, 40]. One study is limited to haplotype-resolved nonreference TE insertions, and the other is a preprint comparing TE detection between pipelines developed for long reads. Once sequencing reads are generated and cleaned, a common next step is to map reads to a known reference, an additional step that can introduce variability or potential sources of error to downstream genomic analyses. To understand how sequencing platforms and mapping strategies might impact TE annotation, we first mapped Illumina 250 bp paired end reads and PacBio HiFi reads from ISO1 D. melanogaster individuals to release 6 of the D. melanogaster reference genome assembly. We leveraged BWA [41], minimap2 [42], and pbmm2 [43] in parallel to gain perspective across widely used read mapping strategies in their default configurations. We then used samtools consensus to generate consensus sequences from each of the 6 read type and mapping strategy combinations (Table 1, column 1). The consensus sequences were input to EDTA, RepeatModeler2, and EarlGrey to produce TE annotations, and the resulting annotations were analyzed using Stage 3 of our benchmarking workflow. We considered the published TE annotation of the D. melanogaster reference assembly as the ground truth [see Supplementary Material 2 for supplementary methods]. We find that EDTA, RepeatModeler2, and Earl Grey perform best on consensus sequences built from long reads mapped with BWA (Table 1). Although preferred to short reads, long reads were not a decisive factor in achieving optimal downstream TE annotation—annotations of consensus sequences built using long reads mapped with minimap2 or pbmm2 yield lower MCC scores than annotations of consensus sequences built using Illumina short reads mapped with BWA (Table 1). Prior studies attest that minimap2 sometimes misaligns reads from highly repetitive regions [44]; therefore, when applicable, we suggest using BWA if its higher computational time can be accommodated. Our findings are an important reminder to carefully consider how biases incurred during DNA sequencing, mapping, and consensus generation might propagate into TE annotations, as previously reported [39]. When applied to simulated or well-characterized real data, TE_Bench can illuminate biases and inform corrective measures, like fine-tuning mapping strategies.

Discussion and conclusions

Here we present TE_Bench, a user-friendly workflow for benchmarking TE annotation pipelines which is conveniently automated within the Snakemake workflow management system. We chose to measure sensitivity and specificity, among other statistical metrics, at the base-pair level because of the downstream flexibility it enables. Beyond comprehensive scoring, base-pair level computations can evaluate nesting and the identities of reference TEs relative to their consensus sequence. We demonstrate the immediate value of TE_Bench as a tool to help users differentiate between pipelines in the context of their own data. Additionally, we illustrate its promise as the foundation for an increasingly needed standard benchmarking strategy to evaluate advancing annotation software. In a case study of TE_Bench, we show that the performance of annotation pipelines EDTA, RepeatModeler2, and Earl Grey varies depending on species, TE class/subclass, TE nested structure, and TE divergence. Furthermore, real D. melanogaster sequencing data reveals that annotations produced by the three pipelines we tested are sensitive to both read type and mapping strategy. Altogether, our findings underscore that the complex biology of TEs necessitates bioinformatics approaches guided by biological expertise.

Future directions

Ideally, the results automatically returned by TE_Bench will indicate which TE annotation pipeline to use for a specific task. However, more analysis is needed to understand why pipelines perform differently, and how to improve their performance. Specifically, for annotation software that rely primarily on repeat-finding algorithms to identify TEs, particular elements may go undiscovered due to low copy number or high sequence divergence to other repeats. In the current implementation of TE_Bench, we do not yet support explicit evaluation of copy number or fragmentation. However, sequence identity-based analyses are inherently influenced by both fragmentation and divergence, and thus partially reflect those factors. The identity versus coverage plots provided by our workflow are a starting point toward addressing these questions. Future research might explore how narrower definitions of sequence divergence, such as number of copies, indels, transitions, and/or transversions relative to consensus sequences, impact coverage distributions.

Secondly, the performance metrics output by TE_Bench can be influenced by redundant or fragmented annotations, which may inflate or distort agreement between the test and reference annotation files. To contextualize these biases, TE_Bench includes dedicated misclassification and redundancy modules that quantify cross-class overlap and self-overlapping annotations. Future extensions incorporating locus- or family-resolved clustering and element-level matching would further disentangle true biological signal from annotation fragmentation and duplication. Lastly, although pipelines that integrate TE class-specific annotators, such as EDTA, characteristically struggle with FDR, our benchmarking workflow can expedite testing different combinations of class-specific annotators to improve class-specific TE discovery.

Another avenue for future work is to extend the case study presented here by analyzing additional species models to quantify how TE annotation is impacted by increasing TE diversity and phylogenetic distance from model species [11]. For example, EDTA produces poorer annotations with higher rates of misclassification as phylogenetic distance increases from rice and maize, the species for which the tool was originally developed [19]. For researchers interested in species which lack curated TE data, we recommend using TE_Bench with data from phylogenetically related species for the best approximation of how annotation pipelines might perform on sequences belonging to the species of interest [11, 19]. Fortunately, open-source databases like Dfam continue to grow, broadening representation of the tree of life captured by submitted TE sequences and libraries. With improvements to the quality and accessibility of ground-truth data, the potential applications of TE_Bench will only expand. For instance, TE_Bench provides a framework for systematically evaluating the effects of an annotator’s access to either a broader TE repertoire or one that is more closely related to the target species.

Limitations

At this time, TE_Bench supports benchmarking mainly at the base-pair level to maximize flexibility in relating performance metrics to other variables such as nesting and divergence, which are evaluated at the same level. Therefore, some challenges associated with TE annotation pipelines are largely obscured from consideration, such as family-level redundancy.

TE_Bench is currently configured to accept annotation data generated by the pipelines that we tested. To test different annotation pipelines, users are responsible for properly formatting their output annotation files for submission to our workflow. Instructions on doing so are available on the TE_Bench GitHub repository (https://github.com/hkania/TE_Bench).

Furthermore, GARLIC does not use a random seed when building genomic models or when generating simulated sequences. This could create run-to-run variation that may be reflected in the simulated sequences themselves, and ultimately in the performance of test annotation pipelines. Over two GARLIC model builds using genomic data from release 6 of the D. melanogaster reference genome assembly, we generated 19 100 Mb simulated sequences and calculated 95% confidence intervals summarizing the observed performance of EDTA, RepeatModeler2 and Earl Grey on the simulated sequences [see Supplementary Material 2 table S8]. We discovered no significant variation in any performance metric across the three pipelines that could be attributed to the non-deterministic behavior of GARLIC or of the pipelines themselves. The maximum observed confidence interval width was Earl Grey’s sensitivity at 0.030188.

The annotation results presented in our case study were obtained by running EDTA, RepeatModeler2, and Earl Grey using default parameters, except one Earl Grey run in which disabling an internal RepeatMasker flag, –norna, improved the annotation of small tRNA-derived SINEs (Fig. S3). For users interested in customizing parameters to their annotation task, we provide a table outlining the predicted impacts of modifying the command-line flags for EDTA, RepeatModeler2, and Earl Grey, which could be tested using TE_Bench [see Supplementary Material 1, Tables S1-S3].

Finally, we note that the ISO1 D. melanogaster reads used in our sequencing platform analysis were not from the same individual. While transposition rates vary within D. melanogaster populations, they are minimal; thus, we expect non-reference TE insertions within the ISO1 strain to have a small if not negligible impact on our benchmarking results [45].
