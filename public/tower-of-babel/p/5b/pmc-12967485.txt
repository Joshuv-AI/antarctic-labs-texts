Dual association patterns between microglial activation and neuronal health in Alzheimer's disease: a whole‐brain MRSI/PET study
Authors: Yaoyu Zhang, Xiao‐Hang Qian, Huixiang Zhuang, Wenqi Zhang, Jialin Hu, Yibo Zhao, Yudu Li, Wen Jin, Chang Xu, Ziyu Meng, Wenli Li, Siyue Chen, Xu‐Feng Jiang, Tengfei Guo, Ya‐Dong Li, Jian Ye, Zhi‐Pei Liang, Biao Li, Miao Zhang, Huidong Tang, Yao Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONMicroglial activation can either support neuronal function or exacerbate damage, contributing to Alzheimer's disease (AD) progression. We investigated spatial relationships among microglial activation, neuronal health, and amyloid beta (Aβ) in the AD spectrum.METHODSForty healthy controls, 37 patients with mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and 62 patients with AD underwent whole‐brain high‐resolution 1H‐magnetic resonance spectroscopic imaging (MRSI), [1 8F]DPA‐714, and [1 8F]AV‐45 positron emission tomography (PET). Regional and voxel‐wise analyses assessed changes and associations of microglial activation with N‐acetylaspartate (NAA) and Aβ.RESULTSMCI and AD patients showed higher microglial activation and lower NAA, correlating with cognitive decline. In controls and MCI, microglial activation correlated positively with NAA and Aβ in early amyloid‐accumulating regions. Conversely, negative correlations with NAA emerged in the hippocampus in MCI and extended to temporal and occipital regions in AD.DISCUSSIONFor the first time, we identified two distinct spatial association patterns between [1 8F]DPA‐714 PET and NAA, shedding light on the complex interplay between neuroinflammation and neuronal health in AD.

BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is characterized by amyloid deposition, neurofibrillary tangles, neuronal loss, and neuroinflammation, with microglial activation playing a central role in neuroinflammation by either supporting neuronal function (anti‐inflammatory) or exacerbating cell damage (pro‐inflammatory). 1 , 2 Dysregulated microglia–neuron interactions are key contributors to neurodegeneration and cognitive decline in AD. 3 Emerging evidence suggests that microglial responses are highly heterogeneous across disease stages and brain regions, exhibiting both beneficial and detrimental roles in the pathogenesis of AD. 4 In the early stages of AD, animal studies demonstrate that reactive microglia surround and clear amyloid beta (Αβ) plaques and support neuronal repair. 5 As the disease progresses, chronic microglial activation drives tau pathology and exacerbates neuronal damage. 6 Genome‐wide studies have further revealed phenotypic and functional microglial diversity across brain regions, which may underlie the selective regional vulnerability observed in AD. 7 , 8 For example, microglia‐mediated synaptic pruning may be reactivated in a region‐ or circuit‐specific manner, potentially protecting neurons from excitotoxicity or leading to excessive synaptic elimination that drives cognitive decline. 9 , 10

Previous positron emission tomography (PET) studies with the 18‐kDa translocator protein (TSPO) radioligands revealed the biphasic roles of microglial activation in AD. On the one hand, correlations between microglial activation and Aβ deposition are stronger in mild cognitive impairment (MCI) than in AD. 11 Higher initial microglial activation is associated with better clinical prognosis in follow‐up studies, indicating its protective role in the early stages of AD. 12 , 13 , 14 On the other hand, increased microglial activation in AD has more widespread correlations with glucose hypometabolism 15 and tau deposition, 11 propagating jointly with tau across Braak stages 16 and mediating key steps in tau spread, neurodegeneration, and cognitive impairment. 17 These findings underscore the detrimental roles of microglial activation in driving disease progression. Despite these insights, the spatial–temporal relationship between heterogeneous microglial activation and neuronal health remains poorly understood. Understanding the in vivo temporal and topographical dynamics of microglia–neuron interactions across the whole brain is important for identifying key regions and time points that may guide more effective treatment strategies and improve our ability to assess intervention outcomes.

N‐acetylaspartate (NAA) is a well‐established biomarker of neuronal health that can be measured non‐invasively using 1H‐magnetic resonance spectroscopic imaging (MRSI). 18 Previous single‐voxel magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) studies consistently reported decreased NAA levels in regions such as the precuneus, posterior cingulate cortex, and hippocampus in MCI and AD patients. 19 These reductions have been associated with synaptic loss, 20 AD pathologies, 20 , 21 and cognitive decline. 22 , 23 Animal studies also show reduced hippocampal NAA levels, accompanied by increased neuroinflammation, as evidenced by [18F]DPA‐714 PET and markers for astrogliosis (glial fibrillary acidic protein [GFAP]) and microgliosis (CD11b). 24 , 25 However, traditional MRS/MRSI techniques are limited by low spatial resolution, restricted brain coverage, and long scanning time, which have confined most studies to region‐based analyses in one or two brain regions. To date, no study has simultaneously investigated the in vivo whole‐brain spatial–temporal relationship between microglial activation and NAA distribution.

This is the first study to map the co‐varying spatial relationships between microglial activation and neuronal health across the AD continuum, utilizing combined [18F]DPA‐714 PET, [18F]AV‐45 PET, and whole‐brain 1H‐MRSI. Specifically, [1 8F]DPA‐714 is a second‐generation TSPO PET ligand with higher affinity and improved signal‐to‐noise ratio (SNR) compared with first‐generation TSPO tracers. 26 It has been widely used to assess microglial activation and neuroinflammatory processes in AD and has demonstrated sensitivity to disease‐related changes. 12 , 14 , 27 [1 8F]AV‐45 (florbetapir) is a PET tracer with high affinity for fibrillar Aβ, and its cortical uptake reflects the presence and burden of Aβ plaques. 28 It is extensively validated and widely applied in clinical and research studies of AD. 29 In this study, a total of 139 participants were included in the analysis: 40 Αβ‐negative cognitively normal (CN) subjects, 39 Αβ‐positive MCI patients, and 62 Αβ‐positive AD patients. We applied spectroscopic imaging by exploiting spatiospectral correlation (SPICE), 30 , 31 , 32 a novel high‐resolution 3D 1H‐MRSI technique, to map whole‐brain NAA levels with a nominal spatial resolution of 2.0 × 3.0 × 3.0 mm3. We hypothesize that microglial activation has differential effects on neuronal health, which are closely related to the pathological progression of AD. This study aims to (1) compare in vivo AD‐related spatial changes of microglial activation and NAA at both regional and voxel levels and (2) investigate the spatial relationship among microglial activation, NAA, and Αβ load across CN, MCI, and AD stages.

METHODS

Participants

A total of 166 participants aged 50 years or older were enrolled in this study between April 2021 and May 2024. Patients with MCI and AD were recruited from the memory clinic of Ruijin Hospital, and CN subjects were recruited through local advertisement. All subjects underwent neurological examinations, including Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) and global Clinical Dementia Rating. 23 , 27 Consensus clinical diagnoses of MCI and AD were made by a panel of three experienced neurologists (X.Q., S.C., and H.T.) based on the Peterson criteria 33 and the 2011 National Institute on Aging–Alzheimer's Association (NIA‐AA) criteria, 34 respectively. According to the latest diagnostic criteria for AD 2 and to ensure that the observed changes were more likely linked to AD rather than other types of dementia, only Αβ‐positive patients were included.

Exclusion criteria included (1) psychiatric or other neurological diseases; (2) pregnancy or renal failure (critical for PET imaging); (3) autoimmune and systematic inflammatory diseases; (4) use of corticosteroids, non‐steroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs, and benzodiazepines (interfere with TSPO imaging); (5) structural brain lesions, such as infarctions, hemorrhages, and traumatic brain injury; and (6) contradictions for magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Ruijin Hospital (approval number: 2021‐46) and conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Helsinki declaration and its later amendments. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their designated representatives.

Blood samples were drawn for each participant to determine their TSPO genotype. All subjects were classified as high‐affinity binders (HABs), mixed‐affinity binders (MABs), or low‐affinity binders (LABs), based on the rs6971 polymorphism within the TSPO gene. Two participants with TSPO LAB were excluded. Among the remaining participants, two patients with AD were MAB, whereas all other participants were HAB, which is aligned with previous studies showing the majority (>96%) of Asian population are HAB. 27 , 35

Image acquisition

All PET and MRI scans were acquired on a 3.0T Biograph mMR hybrid PET/MRI system (Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany), equipped with a 12‐channel phase‐array head coil. TSPO PET images were simultaneously acquired with structural images and MRSI data. Αβ PET images were acquired on a separate day within 2 weeks of the first scan, simultaneously with another set of structural images. All subjects were instructed to lie still and keep awake during the scans.

PET imaging

For the TSPO PET imaging, a mean dose of 240.32 ± 40.90 MBq [18F]DPA‐714 was injected intravenously, and static PET data in list mode were collected during the 60‐ to 90‐min post‐injection window. This time frame was selected based on prior kinetic studies indicating that tracer uptake reaches a relative equilibrium between tissue compartments during this period, thereby supporting the validity of standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR) calculation. 12 , 36 For Αβ PET imaging, a mean dose of 242.41 ± 42.52 MBq [18F]AV‐45 was injected intravenously, and static PET acquisition was performed 40 to 60 min after the bolus injection. Attenuation correction was performed based on the Dixon method with an additional model‐based bone compartment to improve the accuracy of standard uptake value (SUV) estimation. 37 The PET emission data were reconstructed using ordered subset expectation maximization (OSEM) algorithm (21 subsets, four iterations) and post‐filtered with an isotropic 2‐mm full‐width at half‐maximum (FWHM) Gaussian kernel. The image parameters after reconstruction are as follows: 127 slices, gap = 0.5 mm, matrix size = 344 × 344, voxel size = 2.1 × 2.1 × 2.0 mm3.

Magnetic resonance spectroscopic imaging

The detailed imaging parameters of MRSI were as follows: field of view (FOV) = 240 × 240 × 160 mm3, matrix size = 122 × 78 × 42, voxel size = 2.0 × 3.0 × 3.0 mm3 (right–left × anterior–posterior × superior–inferior), repetition time (TR) = 160 ms, echo time (TE) = 1.6 ms, flip angle = 27°, echo space = 1.76 ms, scan time = 12 min 35 s. For data acquisition, we included the following key features to achieve whole‐brain coverage in a reasonable scan time, with high spatial resolution, and with robustness: (1) short‐TR, ultrashort‐TE, Ernst‐angle‐excitation free induction decays (FID) MRSI for high SNR efficiency and fast encoding speed; (2) extended echo‐planar spectroscopic imaging (EPSI) trajectories with ramp samplings to simultaneously cover the kx and t dimensions and phase encodings to densely sample the ky and kz dimensions of inner k‐space; (3) blipped EPSI trajectories to sample outer k‐space sparsely for high‐resolution water reference signals; (4) elimination of water and lipid suppression pulses; and (5) built‐in navigators to track and correct subject motion and field drift during the scan. 38 , 39

For MRSI data processing, we adopted a customized pipeline, including four key components. First, spectral basis functions for each metabolite were prelearned with physics‐based (through quantum simulations) and machine learning (from training data) priors. Second, lipid and water signals were removed using a union‐of‐subspaces model, leveraging the high‐resolution lipid and water signals and prelearned spectral basis functions. Third, the spatiospectral signals of metabolites were reconstructed using the prelearned union‐of‐subspaces model through a Bayesian estimation formulation, with additional spatial and probabilistic distribution constraints. Fourth, metabolite concentrations were quantified voxel‐wise by fitting a parametric spectroscopic model to the reconstructed signals. 31 , 40 , 41 , 42 , 43 Note that this processing pipeline achieved robustness to motion and system imperfections due to several advanced features, including navigator signals for motion and field corrections and high‐resolution water reference signals for B0 correction. The details are included in the Supplementary Method.

Magnetic resonance imaging

The structural images were acquired using a T1‐weighted magnetization‐prepared rapid gradient echo (MPRAGE) sequence with the following parameters: FOV = 250 × 250 × 192 mm3, voxel size = 1.0 × 1.0 × 1.0 mm3, TR = 1900 ms, TE = 2.44 ms, flip angle = 9°.

Image processing

The image processing pipeline is summarized in Figure S1. The processing details, including preprocessing, region‐level analysis, voxel‐level analysis, and statistical analysis, are described below.

Preprocessing

All structural images were preprocessed using the FreeSurfer software (version 7.4.1, http://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu), including intensity normalization, skull stripping, cortical parcellation according to the Desikan–Killiany atlas, and segmentation of subcortical structures. The structural images acquired with AV‐45 PET were aligned to the structural images acquired with DPA‐714 PET and MRSI images using rigid transformation implemented by Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs). 44 The latter set of structural images were normalized to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) brain template using non‐linear transformation.

All PET images were skull stripped using the SynthStrip tool, 45 followed by co‐registration with their corresponding structural images using affine transformation. For DPA‐714 PET, the cerebellar gray matter (GM) mask obtained from each subject's structural MRI was warped into the native PET space, and the mean uptake within this region was calculated. DPA‐714 SUVR images were generated by dividing each voxel value by this mean. Cerebellar GM was used as a pseudo‐reference region, following established TSPO PET protocols. 14 , 46 AV‐45 SUVR images were computed using the same procedure, except that the whole cerebellum served as the reference region, as it is relatively spared from Aβ deposition and widely used for AV‐45 normalization. 47 , 48 NAA images were aligned with the structural images using the transformation matrices derived from the unsuppressed water images acquired with MRSI and normalized using the unsuppressed water MRSI signals as a reference.

Quality assessment for MRS data

We adopted a customized quality assessment process for the MRS data, which includes (1) B0 field drift, (2) B0 field inhomogeneity, (3) subject head motion, (4) measurement noise, and (5) residual nuisance signals from background water and lipids. Specifically, the B0 field drift and inhomogeneity effects were characterized based on the FWHM of water peak given its excellent SNR. We chose 15 Hz as the acceptance threshold, which was slightly larger than previous reports (NAA FWHM 11‐13 Hz). 49 , 50 This is because water signals have shorter T2 (T2,water ≈ 60 ms), while metabolite signals have longer T2 (T2,NAA ≈ 240 ms). This will lead to an intrinsic linewidth mismatch of 1πT2,water−1πT2,NAA≈ 4 Hz. Taking this factor into account, our 15‐Hz threshold on water FWHM should be equivalent to ∼11 Hz threshold in NAA FWHM, which is comparable to previous studies. For subject motion, our sequence collects motion navigators at every TR (160 ms), from which data with significant motion corruption can be detected and discarded from subsequent data processing. 39 , 51 If the portion of the motion corrupted data is too large (>20%), then all the MRSI data would be discarded. In our study, seven subject data were rejected due to such severe motion effects. The measurement noise effect was characterized by the Cramér‐Rao lower bounds (CRLB of the NAA. We chose 20% as the acceptance threshold. 52 The residual nuisance signals were detected based on the goodness of fit. Specifically, we conducted a Kolmogorov–Smirnov test to determine whether the signal in a voxel largely deviated from the physical model of metabolites due to the presence of residual nuisance signals. Voxels that did not pass the test were rejected in our analysis.

Region‐level analysis

Twenty regions of interest (ROIs) were used for statistical analyses, including superior frontal cortex, middle frontal cortex, inferior frontal cortex, superior temporal cortex, middle/inferior temporal cortex, medial temporal cortex, fusiform gyrus, medial occipital cortex, lateral occipital cortex, superior parietal cortex, inferior parietal cortex, supramarginal gyrus, precuneus, anterior cingulate, posterior cingulate, sensorimotor cortex, hippocampus, amygdala, thalamus, and striatum. These ROIs were registered to the original DPA‐714 PET and MRSI images, from which mean values of the imaging measures were extracted for each region.

A composite AV‐45 SUVR was computed by taking the average of the weighted mean SUVRs from the GM of four cortical regions (i.e., frontal, cingulate, lateral parietal, and lateral temporal), as previously described by Landau et al. 47 A cutoff of 1.11 was used to classify all subjects into an Αβ‐positive and Αβ‐negative status.

Voxel‐level analysis

To mitigate potential biases arising from tissue mixing and non‐specific binding, all PET and MRS data underwent partial volume correction (PVC) following the approach of Bhogal et al. 53 Each voxel's signal was modeled as a linear combination of gray‐ and white‐matter contributions using tissue probability maps derived from SPM12 segmentation of T1‐weighted images, with weighting coefficients estimated via linear regression as implemented in the ExploreASL toolbox. 54 All subsequent voxel‐wise correlation analyses were performed on PVC‐corrected data.

All PET images were normalized to the MNI space based on the transformation matrices from PET‐to‐structural co‐registration and structural‐to‐MNI normalization. NAA images were normalized to the MNI space based on the transformation matrices from water MRSI‐to‐structural co‐registration and structural‐to‐MNI normalization. All transformations were implemented in ANTs. Spatial smoothing was conducted with an 8‐mm FWHM Gaussian kernel.

To investigate voxel‐wise group differences in DPA‐714 SUVR and NAA, two‐sample unpaired t‐tests were applied to the smoothed maps using the FSL (https://fsl.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl) randomize function with 5000 permutations. To examine voxel‐wise associations between MMSE scores and DPA‐714 SUVR or NAA, voxel‐wise linear regression analyses were performed between MMSE scores and the maps of DPA‐714 SUVR and NAA, also using the FSL randomize function with 5000 permutations.

To explore voxel‐wise correlations between microglial activation, NAA, and Αβ load, biological parametric mapping (BPM) analyses were conducted using the BPM toolbox. 55 , 56 To estimate baseline correlations likely attributable to background uptake and non‐specific signal, we defined a conservative reference group with minimal evidence of amyloid pathology, neuroinflammation, and neurodegeneration. The criteria were as follows: (1) age < 60 years old; (2) amyloid negativity, defined as a composite AV‐45 SUVR < 1.11 across frontal, cingulate, lateral parietal, and lateral temporal cortices 47 ; (3) absence of elevated TSPO signal, defined operationally as composite DPA‐714 SUVR values within 2 standard deviations (SD) of the CN group across predefined cortical regions 17 ; (4) absence of neurodegeneration, defined as mean cortical NAA values within 2 SD of the CN group. Using these conservative criteria, we identified 11 CN subjects to serve as a reference group. Voxel‐wise correlation analyses within the reference group revealed negligible DPA–NAA correlations and only minimal, spatially restricted DPA–AV45 correlations. This reference group was subsequently used for baseline correction in the remaining CN subjects (n = 31), as well as in the MCI and AD groups. To do that, z‐score maps were generated for each subject and each modality using the reference group mean and standard deviation at each voxel. 11 , 57 A group GM mask was then obtained by averaging individual GM masks with a threshold at 0.5. BPM correlations within the group GM mask were investigated.

Statistical analysis

To compare demographic information between groups, continuous variables were analyzed using one‐way ANOVA for normally distributed data and the Kruskal–Wallis test for non‐normally distributed data. Test of normality was performed using the Shapiro–Wilk test in SPSS 20. Post hoc pair‐wise comparison was performed using the Tukey–Kramer method. Categorical variables were compared using the chi‐squared test.

To compare differences in AV‐45 SUVR, DPA‐714 SUVR, and NAA across anatomical regions between groups, analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was applied with age, sex, and education as covariates. False discovery rate (FDR) correction using the Benjamini–Hochberg method with p < 0.05 was applied across three pair‐wise comparisons and 20 ROIs (60 total comparisons). To avoid dichotomous interpretations based solely on statistical significance, effect sizes (Cohen's d) were further calculated for all regions.

To assess the overall relationship between neuronal health (NAA) and microglial activation (DPA‐714 SUVR), we implemented linear mixed‐effects models (LMMs) using the Python statsmodels package (version 0.14). Models were estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML), and all continuous predictors (NAA, DPA‐714 SUVR, age, and years of education) were z‐score standardized. 58 In all models, NAA was treated as the independent variable and DPA‐714 SUVR as the dependent variable, with age, sex, and education included as covariates. Random intercepts were specified for participants and ROIs to account for within‐subject and regional variability. Furthermore, to explore the associations of DPA‐714 SUVR with AV‐45 SUVR and NAA in anatomical regions, Pearson's correlation analyses were performed in CN, MCI, and AD groups.

For the BPM analysis of PET images, a voxel p < 0.001 with an extent threshold of 50 voxels was used. For the BPM analysis between PET and NAA images, a voxel p < 0.01 with an extent threshold of 50 voxels was used. Surviving clusters were assessed for significance using SPM's built‐in FDR correction at the cluster level (p < 0.05), implemented via the Benjamini–Hochberg method adapted for neuroimaging data. For voxel‐wise group comparisons and linear regression with MMSE scores of DPA‐714 SUVR, family‐wise error (FWE)‐corrected p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For voxel‐wise group comparisons and linear regression with MMSE scores of NAA, multiple comparisons were corrected using the threshold‐free cluster enhancement (TFCE) algorithm, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

To evaluate how NAA and DPA‐714 SUVR jointly relate to cognitive performance across all patients, we applied another LMM to include NAA, DPA‐714 SUVR, and their interaction as fixed effects, age, sex, and education as covariates, and diagnostic group and brain regions as random intercepts to account for group‐level and regional variability.

RESULTS

Demographics

Of the 166 participants, 11 dropped out or could not complete the experiment, seven had severe head motion during scanning, and nine had poor MRSI image qualities, including streaking and shadowing artifacts. As a result, 40 Αβ‐negative CN controls, 37 Αβ‐positive MCI patients, and 62 Αβ‐positive AD patients were included for data analysis. The demographic information of these 139 subjects is summarized in Table 1. No significant differences in age, sex, and education were found among the three groups. The MMSE scores were significantly lower in MCI compared to CN (p = 0.001) and in AD compared to both MCI and CN (p < 0.001).

For microglial activation, the ROI‐based analysis (Figure 2B and Table 2) showed significantly increased DPA‐714 SUVR in the middle frontal cortex (p = 0.035, Cohen's d = 0.572), lateral occipital cortex (p = 0.030, Cohen's d = 0.822), middle/inferior (p = 0.001, Cohen's d = 1.079) and medial temporal cortices (p < 0.001, Cohen's d = 1.036), fusiform (p = 0.001, Cohen's d = 1.072), superior (p = 0.035, Cohen's d = 0.603), and inferior (p < 0.001, Cohen's d = 0.966) parietal cortices, supramarginal gyrus (p = 0.013, Cohen's d = 0.630), precuneus (p = 0.003, Cohen's d = 0.747), posterior cingulate cortex (p = 0.002, Cohen's d = 0.861), hippocampus (p = 0.007, Cohen's d = 0.708), and amygdala (p < 0.001, Cohen's d = 1.138) in the MCI group compared to CN subjects. In AD patients, significant increases in DPA‐714 SUVR were observed in all cortical regions (p ≤ 0.006 for all, Cohen's d ≥ 0.636 for all), except for the anterior cingulate (p = 0.927, Cohen's d = 0.020) and sensorimotor (p = 0.454, Cohen's d = 0.187) cortices, compared to CN subjects. Comparing MCI and AD groups, DPA‐714 SUVR significantly increased in the occipital, parietal and temporal cortical regions in AD, including the lateral occipital cortex (p = 0.011, Cohen's d = 0.483), precuneus (p = 0.004, Cohen's d = 0.691), posterior cingulate cortex (p = 0.039, Cohen's d = 0.500), inferior parietal cortex (p = 0.002, Cohen's d = 0.663), supramarginal gyrus (p = 0.026, Cohen's d = 0.551), middle/inferior temporal cortex (p = 0.001, Cohen's d = 0.683), and fusiform gyrus (p = 0.006, Cohen's d = 0.585). No significant difference was found in the anterior cingulate cortex (p = 0.693, Cohen's d ≤ 0.150), sensorimotor cortex (p = 0.693, Cohen's d ≤ 0.187), thalamus (p = 0.277, Cohen's d ≤ 0.341), or striatum (p = 0.071, Cohen's d ≤ 0.372) across all three groups. Notably, these regional differences remained largely preserved after adjusting for mean global DPA‐714 SUVR.

For neuronal health, the ROI‐based analysis (Figure 2C and Table 2) revealed significantly decreased NAA levels in the precuneus (p = 0.021, Cohen's d = −0.501), medial occipital cortex (p = 0.023, Cohen's d = −0.585), and posterior cingulate cortex (p = 0.050, Cohen's d = −0.427) in the MCI group compared to CN subjects. In AD patients, NAA significantly decreased in the superior (p = 0.004, Cohen's d = −0.692) and middle (p = 0.002, Cohen's d = −0.621) frontal cortices, medial (p = 0.002, Cohen's d = −0.714) and lateral (p = 0.003, Cohen's d = −0.633) occipital cortices, anterior (p = 0.003, Cohen's d = −0.689) and posterior (p < 0.001, Cohen's d = −1.044) cingulate cortices, and extensive regions in the parietal cortex, including the precuneus (p < 0.001, Cohen's d = −1.013), superior (p < 0.001, Cohen's d = −0.678) and inferior parietal cortices (p < 0.001, Cohen's d = −0.820), and supramarginal gyrus (p = 0.001, Cohen's d = −0.750) compared to CN subjects. Significant decreases in NAA were also observed in subcortical regions, including the hippocampus (p = 0.001, Cohen's d = −0.831), amygdala (p = 0.023, Cohen's d = −0.504), and striatum (p < 0.014, Cohen's d = −0.509). Comparing MCI and AD groups, lower NAA were observed in supramarginal gyrus (p = 0.050, Cohen's d = −0.548), anterior (p = 0.050, Cohen's d = −0.578) and posterior cingulate (p = 0.050, Cohen's d = −0.573) cortices, and hippocampus (p = 0.050, Cohen's d = −0.567). As shown in Figure 5, worsening MMSE scores were associated with increased DPA‐714 SUVR, mainly in the temporal and parietal cortices, including the precuneus, middle and inferior temporal cortex, and fusiform, as well as the occipital and cingulate regions in the combined MCI and AD groups. Similarly, positive correlations between MMSE scores and NAA were identified in the precuneus, middle and superior temporal, inferior and superior parietal, middle and inferior frontal, and cingulate cortices. All significant clusters were corrected for multiple comparisons using the TFCE method with p < 0.05. These findings suggest that both microglial activation and neuronal health are closely related to cognitive decline, with distinct spatial patterns reflecting the progression of neurodegenerative changes.

Furthermore, using LLMs, we found that a higher NAA level was significantly associated with better cognitive performance (β = 0.042, 95% CI [0.011, 0.073], p = 0.009), while a higher DPA‐714 SUVR was significantly associated with worse cognitive performance (β = −0.103, 95% CI [−0.133, −0.072], p < 0.001). Moreover, the interaction between DPA‐714 SUVR and NAA levels was significantly correlated with cognitive performance (β = 0.048, 95% CI [0.017, 0.080], p = 0.003). These findings are in line with the voxel‐wise correlation results and suggest that decreased neuronal health and increased microglial activation jointly contribute to worse cognitive outcomes along the clinical AD continuum.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we investigated the spatotemporal relationship between microglial activation, NAA, and Αβ deposition using hybrid 3D‐MRSI/PET imaging. Correlations between microglial activation and NAA varied across disease stages. In CN subjects, microglial activation correlated positively with NAA, whereas in AD patients this relationship shifted to negative. MCI patients exhibited both positive and negative correlations, indicating a transitional stage with mixed effects. Voxel‐wise analysis revealed similar patterns of positive associations between microglial activation, NAA, and Αβ load in CN and MCI groups in regions susceptible to Αβ deposition. However, in MCI and AD patients, these correlations turn increasingly negative and spread from the hippocampus to temporal and occipital cortices. Furthermore, changes in microglial activation and NAA were associated with cognitive impairment in MCI and AD patients, indicating their collective contribution to AD progression. All results remained after including binding affinity as a covariate. For all analyses, including age, sex, and education as covariates did not materially affect the results.

Positive spatial associations between microglial activation and neuronal health in Aβ‐related regions in CN and MCI

In the CN group, microglial activation was found to be positively correlated with Αβ load and NAA levels, particularly in cortical regions prone to early Aβ accumulation. The positive associations between microglial activation and Aβ deposition observed here align with prior studies reporting similar patterns in Aβ‐negative elderly individuals using 11C‐labeled Pittsburgh Compound‐B and 11C‐peripheral benzodiazepine receptor (PBR28) tracers. 59 These findings in a non‐pathological context may reflect physiological roles of microglia in immune surveillance or early responses to emerging Aβ, consistent with histological evidence of microglial clustering around plaques during early amyloidogenesis. 5 Notably, microglial activation was also positively associated with NAA levels, a marker of neuronal viability, 18 in similar cortical regions. The observation, also corroborated by region‐wise positive correlations in the CN group, aligns with the hypothesis that microglial activation in healthy individuals may serve homeostatic roles, possibly via anti‐inflammatory phenotypes or neurotrophic support mechanisms that help clear debris or preserve synaptic integrity. 60 , 61

In the MCI stage, these positive associations persisted but decreased in regions such as the frontal, parietal, and cingulate cortices. These areas, known for early Aβ deposition, include key hubs like the precuneus and superior frontal gyrus, essential for networks such as the default mode and salience networks. 62 , 63 , 64 Microglial activation in these regions may help sustain neuronal health longer, delaying dysfunction in critical areas during disease progression.

Negative spatial associations between microglial activation and neuronal health in hippocampus and extended cortices in MCI and AD

We observed that increased microglial activation correlated with decreased NAA levels in regions such as the hippocampus and parahippocampal gyrus in MCI patients. These areas correspond to Braak stages II and III 65 and are suggested to be associated with early tau accumulation and isolated memory impairments. 66 In AD patients, the negative correlations extended to broader regions, including the middle/inferior temporal and occipital cortices. These regions are covered by Braak stages IV and V, which are suggested to mark the onset of general cognitive decline and executive dysfunction. 66 It is noteworthy that the spatial association between NAA and microglial activation differs from that between NAA and Aβ deposition in MCI and AD patients. The latter is primarily observed in the frontal cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, and precuneus. 48 These distinct patterns may indicate different pathological roles of Aβ and microglial activation and their effects on neuronal health. When Aβ clearance fails, microglia may adopt a pro‐inflammatory phenotype, initiating a vicious cycle of neurodegeneration characterized by excessive synaptic pruning and reduced trophic support, leading to neuronal dysfunction and death. 4 , 13 Consistent with this, increased microglial activation has been associated with glucose hypometabolism and cortical atrophy in MCI and AD patients. 15 , 67 Animal studies demonstrated a similar progression: Increased neuroinflammation (indicated by [18F]DPA‐714 PET and markers like GFAP and CD11b) precedes a decline in NAA levels, measured by single‐voxel MRS on the hippocampus. 25 Additionally, evidence suggests that microglial activation drives tau spreading, 6 , 68 supported by in vivo PET studies showing colocalization of microglial activation and tau in early‐onset amnestic MCI, with joint propagation along Braak stages. 16 , 65 Our findings reveal negative associations between microglial activation and neuronal health that begin in the hippocampus and parahippocampus of the MCI stage and extend to the temporal and occipital cortices as the disease progresses. These findings underscore the role of microglial activation in neuronal dysfunction. Although the negative associations between microglial activation and neuronal health occur in tau‐susceptible regions, whether they are related to tau deposition and propagate along Braak stages needs to be further clarified leveraging tau PET.

Dual association patterns between microglial activation and neuronal health in MCI

In MCI patients, we observed dual association patterns between microglial activation and neuronal health: increased NAA levels in Aβ‐related regions and decreased NAA levels in tau‐related regions. This coexistence of positive and negative inflammatory associations aligns with previous observations in animal models and post mortem AD brain samples. 69 Our findings suggest that the MCI stage represents a transitional period, where microglial activation shifts from predominantly homeostatic in CN individuals to predominantly detrimental in AD patients. The spatial heterogeneity of these effects reflects region‐specific vulnerability to microglial dysregulation, consistent with evidence of microglial immunophenotype variability across brain regions. 7 This spatial and temporal heterogeneity highlights the importance of using whole‐brain neurometabolic imaging and the potential for targeted therapeutic interventions in the MCI stage.

To test whether the observed results were specific to AD, we performed sensitivity analyses using a separate group of age‐matched amyloid‐negative patients (n = 24) with a clinical diagnosis of MCI (Aβ‐MCI). Detailed results are included in the supplementary results, Tables S3 and S4, and Figures S4 and S5. Briefly, our results indicate that the increase of microglial activation and its positive association with amyloid deposition and neuronal health is not specific to AD, as observed in both amyloid‐positive and amyloid‐negative MCI patients. Nevertheless, consistent with previous reports, 70 , 71 these microglial effects appear more prominent in amyloid‐positive MCI. Importantly, the negative correlation between microglial activation and neuronal health in the hippocampus observed in amyloid‐positive MCI patients is absent in the Aβ‐MCI group, suggesting a critical disease‐specific detrimental role of microglial activation in AD compared to MCI due to non‐AD causes.

Associations of cognitive impairment with microglial activation and neuronal dysfunction

Our findings demonstrate that both microglial activation and neuronal dysfunction are closely associated with cognitive impairment in AD. The observed correlation between MMSE scores and microglial activation aligns with previous studies. In a cross‐sectional study of 109 participants across the AD continuum, increased [11C]PBR28 SUVR was associated with higher Neuropsychiatric Inventory Questionnaire severity scores in the frontal, temporal, and parietal cortices. 72 Similarly, Hamelin et al. 14 reported that higher initial [18F]DPA‐714 binding was linked to better clinical outcomes in MCI and early AD, whereas subsequent increases in microglial activation were associated with worsening cognition. A longitudinal study further demonstrated that microglial activation in the anterior temporal lobe predicted cognitive decline in MCI and AD patients over a 3‐year period. 73 Building on our prior work, 23 which revealed that NAA levels in parietal‐temporal regions correlate with MMSE scores in prodromal and demented AD patients, this study utilized a whole‐brain voxel‐level analysis to extend these findings. We identified significant correlations between decreased NAA and MMSE scores in the precuneus, temporal, parietal, and cingulate cortices, which play a critical role in memory, executive functions, and attention. These regions are particularly susceptible to tau accumulation during middle and late Braak stages, further underscoring their importance in cognitive impairment.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. While this study focuses on the association between microglial activation and NAA, other metabolites potentially affected by neuroinflammation were not examined. For instance, myo‐inositol, a glial marker, has been suggested to relate to neuroinflammation, although findings to date are inconsistent. 25 , 74 Future studies should investigate the associations and interactions between neuroinflammation and other metabolites. Also, tau pathology and apolipoprotein E (APOE) genotype are critical factors that closely interact with microglia during AD progression. However, the absence of tau measurements and APOE genotyping in our cohort limited our ability to comprehensively analyze the relationship between microglial activation and neuronal functions, particularly in the context of tau deposition and genetic risk. Third, although the sample size was moderate, the study did not include Aβ‐positive CN subjects, who represent a critical preclinical stage. Finally, while our findings demonstrate stage‐ and region‐dependent effects of microglial activation on neuronal functions, the lack of longitudinal data limited our ability to confirm the downstream impacts of these interactions on cognitive decline over time. Future studies should incorporate tau imaging, APOE genotyping, Aβ‐positive CN subjects and longitudinal assessments to validate these findings further and extend their applicability beyond the current cohort.

CONCLUSION

In summary, using high‐resolution, whole‐brain 3D‐MRSI in combination with TSPO and Αβ PET imaging, our study mapped the topography of microglial activation and its differential effects on neuronal health in the AD spectrum. Microglial activation exhibited dual‐stage‐dependent associations: positive correlations with neuronal health in CN and MCI in amyloid‐susceptible regions, negative correlations with neuronal health in MCI and AD in the hippocampus and extended cortical regions. These findings demonstrate the potential of combined 3D‐MRSI and PET imaging as an in vivo approach to mapping distinct microglial activation states and their spatial covariance with neuronal health, providing valuable insights for developing targeted treatments and evaluating intervention outcomes in AD.
