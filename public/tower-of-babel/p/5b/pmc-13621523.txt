Autologous collagen-induced chondrogenesis versus mesenchymal stem cell therapy combined with high tibial osteotomy for medial knee osteoarthritis: a retrospective study
Authors: Yun Hwan Kim, Bo Seung Bae, Jae Woong Jung, Chan Sik Kim, Seon Ae Kim, Mi-La Cho, Suo Kim, Sung Bin Sorn, Asode Ananthram Shetty, Jianmo Li, Seok Jung Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCartilage restoration techniques combined with high tibial osteotomy (HTO) have increasingly been used to delay conversion to total knee arthroplasty. Among these, autologous collagen-induced chondrogenesis (ACIC) and mesenchymal stem cell (MSC) therapy have gained significant research attention; however, their comparative therapeutic efficacy remains unclear.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 102 patients with medial compartment knee osteoarthritis who underwent HTO combined with cartilage repair (ACIC, n = 70; MSC, n = 32). Arthroscopic and radiographic parameters, including International Cartilage Repair Society (ICRS) grade, cartilage defect size, Kellgren–Lawrence (KL) grade, and mechanical alignment, were evaluated preoperatively and at second-look arthroscopy performed during hardware removal (mean, 27.2 ± 12.2 months). Clinical outcomes, including Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC), Visual Analog Scale (VAS), and International Knee Documentation Committee Subjective Knee (IKDC) scores, were also assessed preoperatively and at the last clinical follow-up (mean, 58.11 ± 30.85 months).ResultsICRS grade significantly improved, and cartilage defect size significantly decreased in both groups (all p < 0.001). KL grade also showed significant postoperative improvement in both groups (p < 0.001). No significant differences were observed between groups in ICRS grade (p = 1.000), cartilage defect size (p = 0.202), and KL grade (p = 0.529). Both groups showed significant clinical improvements in WOMAC, VAS, and IKDC scores (all p < 0.001). There were no significant differences between the two groups in clinical outcomes (WOMAC, p = 0.430; VAS, p = 0.307; IKDC, p = 0.823).ConclusionsBoth ACIC and MSC combined with HTO resulted in significant arthroscopic, radiographic, and clinical improvements in patients with varus knee osteoarthritis. No significant differences were observed, indicating comparable effectiveness of the two techniques when performed in conjunction with HTO.Keywords: Osteoarthritis, High tibial osteotomy, Autologous collagen-induced chondrogenesis, Mesenchymal stem cells, Cartilage regeneration

Background

Knee osteoarthritis is a highly prevalent degenerative joint disorder affecting millions of individuals worldwide, particularly those aged > 50 years. The disease is characterized by progressive cartilage degeneration, subchondral bone sclerosis, synovial inflammation, osteophyte formation, and loss of joint function [1, 2]. High tibial osteotomy (HTO) is a well-established surgical intervention aimed at realigning the mechanical axis of the lower limb, thereby offloading the affected medial compartment and delaying the progression to total knee arthroplasty (TKA) [3–6]. The long-term survival and stability of HTO have been confirmed in multiple cohort studies, showing favorable outcomes in selected patients [4–7].

Despite successful correction of varus deformity, residual cartilage defects remain a critical limitation of HTO [8, 9]. Although HTO effectively redistributes load across the knee joint, conversion to TKA has been reported in approximately 11.2–35.8% of patients at 10 years after surgery, with a mean survival of approximately 12 years [3, 10]. Second-look arthroscopic studies following HTO have reported partial improvement in cartilage status, including softening and surface repair; however, these findings are largely derived from short- to mid-term follow-up, and complete restoration of hyaline cartilage remains uncommon [10, 11]. Recent studies have revealed that the addition of cartilage repair procedures to HTO may be associated with more durable long-term outcomes. Bode et al. [12] reported that patients who underwent HTO combined with cartilage repair showed a superior 10-year survival rate of 94.3% compared with those treated with isolated HTO. Jung et al. [8] showed that additional clinical outcome improvements after HTO were observed only in patients who achieved successful cartilage regeneration following combined cartilage repair procedures.

Various cartilage repair techniques have been combined with HTO to enhance joint preservation by addressing residual cartilage pathology [13]. Microfracture has long been used as a traditional cartilage repair technique and was widely applied in clinical practice in the past. However, with the emergence of newer biologic cartilage restoration strategies such as bone marrow aspirate concentrate (BMAC) and autologous chondrocyte implantation (ACI), an increasing number of studies have revealed that these approaches may provide more favorable structural and clinical outcomes than microfracture, particularly with respect to the durability of repair tissue [14]. BMAC has gained attention because of its technical simplicity and cost-effectiveness, with several studies reporting encouraging short- to mid-term outcomes. ACI has shown reliable cartilage restoration in selected patients, especially younger individuals with small- to medium-sized focal chondral lesions [15]; however, robust evidence demonstrating its superiority or long-term durability remains limited [15].

Mesenchymal stem cell (MSC) therapy has emerged as a promising biological treatment for cartilage regeneration owing to its chondrogenic differentiation potential, paracrine effects, and immunomodulatory properties. Compared with BMAC, MSC-based therapy provides a more standardized and concentrated cellular composition, which may result in more consistent regenerative responses and improved cartilage quality [16]. Several studies have reported that MSC implantation leads to notable improvements in pain, function, and cartilage structure in patients with knee osteoarthritis [7, 16–18]. However, the relatively high cost and procedural complexity of MSC therapy remain barriers to its widespread clinical use [7]. Notably, autologous collagen-induced chondrogenesis (ACIC) has been introduced as a single-stage, scaffold-based cartilage repair technique that aims to overcome some of the limitations of ACI. Unlike ACI, which requires a two-stage procedure and cell expansion, ACIC allows for in situ chondrogenesis using a collagen scaffold, reducing procedural complexity and donor-site morbidity. Early clinical and preclinical studies have reported favorable structural outcomes with ACIC, revealing its potential as a pragmatic alternative cartilage repair strategy [19]. When combined with HTO, both ACIC and MSC therapy provide biologically augmented approaches that may enhance cartilage regeneration in the mechanically optimized environment created by realignment surgery. Nevertheless, direct comparative evidence evaluating MSC therapy and ACIC in conjunction with HTO remains limited, highlighting the need for further investigation.

The purpose of this study was to compare the arthroscopic, radiologic, and clinical outcomes of HTO combined with ACIC and MSC therapy. In this study, we hypothesized that both ACIC and MSC therapy combined with HTO would lead to improvements in arthroscopic and radiologic parameters, accompanied by enhanced clinical outcomes, and that the overall results of the two treatment strategies would be comparable.

Methods

Study design and patient selection

This retrospective cohort study was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines and included patients with medial compartment knee osteoarthritis who underwent ACIC and MSC therapy combined with HTO between January 2016 and December 2022. This study received approval from the Institutional Review Board (UJA2024-03 A), and informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study. Clinical data were retrospectively collected from institutional electronic medical records, including demographic information, preoperative clinical and radiographic findings, operative details, and postoperative outcomes. A total of 111 patients were initially identified, of whom 77 were allocated to the ACIC group and 34 to the MSC therapy group.

Patients with a history of prior knee surgery (n = 4) or simultaneous bilateral MSC therapy combined with HTO (n = 1), were excluded. Patients with inflammatory arthritis, or significant medical comorbidities who underwent cartilage repair combined with HTO were not identified in the cohort and were therefore not applicable as exclusion criteria [7, 20]. Finally, patients with less than 2 years of clinical follow-up after cartilage repair combined with HTO were excluded (ACIC: n = 3, MSC: n = 1). Following application of the exclusion criteria, 70 patients in the ACIC group and 32 patients in the MSC group were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). With the final sample (ACIC, n = 70; MSC, n = 32) and a two-sided alpha of 0.05, the study had approximately 80% power to detect a moderate between-group difference in continuous outcomes (Cohen’s d = 0.60) using an independent two-sample comparison. Treatment selection was influenced by disease severity and patient preference, with MSC therapy more frequently chosen in patients with advanced cartilage degeneration.

Flowchart of the study

Surgical indications of ACIC and MSC therapy combined with HTO were as follows: (1) symptomatic medial compartment osteoarthritis lasting at least 3 months, classified as Kellgren–Lawrence (KL) grade II or higher, accompanied by varus deformity (≥ 5° and < 15°); (2) aged 40–65 years with a body mass index (BMI) < 35 kg/m²; (3) preserved knee motion, defined as flexion greater than 120° and flexion contracture less than 15°; and (4) absence of severe knee instability, defined as grade I or less on physical examination, including the Lachman test, anterior and posterior drawer tests, and varus/valgus stress tests and (5) chondral defect size greater than 2 cm2 of medial femoral condyle in preoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and arthroscopic assessments during primary surgery [7, 9] (Fig. 1).

Surgical techniques

After arthroscopic identification of chondral defects on the medial femoral condyle, the lesion surface was meticulously prepared using ring curettes and gouges. Multiple perforations of the subchondral bone were then created using a 2-mm Kirschner wire. Carbon dioxide (CO2) gas insufflation was subsequently applied over the articular surface to facilitate precise application of the biological material.

Cartilage repair using autologous collagen-induced chondrogenesis

The ACIC technique used in the present study was based on marrow stimulation combined with implantation of a porcine-derived atelocollagen scaffold to enhance endogenous cartilage repair [19]. The atelocollagen composite gel was prepared using two 1-mL syringes connected to a Y-shaped mixing catheter. One syringe contained 1 mL of fibrinogen (Greenplast; Greencross Corporation, Seoul, Korea), and the other contained 0.9 mL of atelocollagen (COLTRIX® CartiRegen, Ubiosis Co. Ltd., Seongnam, Korea) mixed with 0.1 mL of thrombin (50 IU). COLTRIX® CartiRegen consists of 3% type I atelocollagen, the fibrinogen solution contains 72–110 mg/mL of fibrinogen, and the thrombin solution contains 500 IU/mL, corresponding to 50 IU in 0.1 mL. During application, the contents of both syringes were simultaneously delivered and mixed through the Y-shaped catheter as the material was injected into the cartilage defect [19, 21, 22] (Fig. 2).

Cartilage repair using mesenchymal stem cell therapy

Human umbilical cord blood-derived mesenchymal stem cells (hUCB-MSCs) were prepared using a commercially available product, CARTISTEM®. Each vial contained 1.5 mL of hUCB-MSCs (7.5 × 106 cells) suspended in a 4% hyaluronic acid–based hydrogel. The hUCB-MSC–hyaluronic acid hydrogel mixture was implanted into the prepared drill holes under arthroscopic guidance [7, 23] (Fig. 3).

High tibial osteotomy

In both groups, an open-wedge HTO was performed and stabilized using an anatomical locking metal-block plate (Ohtofix; Ohtomedial Co., Ltd., Goyang-si, Republic of Korea). A biplanar osteotomy technique was used to laterally shift the mechanical axis, targeting the medial aspect of the lateral tibial spine [24, 25].

Post-operative rehabilitation

Both groups followed an identical postoperative rehabilitation protocol. Patients were advised to perform isometric quadriceps and hamstring exercises, whereas knee flexion was restricted to 90° during the first 4 weeks postoperatively. Continuous passive motion therapy was recommended for 4 h daily for 4–6 weeks after surgery. Partial weight-bearing was permitted at 6 weeks postoperatively, with progression to full weight-bearing encouraged at 3 months. Crutches were used for 3 months postoperatively [7, 19].

Arthroscopic findings and radiologic evaluation

Arthroscopic findings and radiologic outcomes were evaluated preoperatively and postoperatively at the time of hardware removal, which was performed at least 1 year after the primary surgery. Cartilage status was evaluated using the International Cartilage Repair Society (ICRS) grading system and measurement of cartilage defect size. Varus alignment and the severity of medial compartment knee osteoarthritis were assessed by comparing the hip–knee–ankle (HKA) angle and KL grade between the preoperative and postoperative states. Arthroscopic and radiologic outcomes were independently assessed under blinded conditions by two observers who were not involved in the surgical procedures. Interobserver agreement was complete with no discrepancies observed.

Clinical evaluation

Clinical outcomes were evaluated preoperatively and postoperatively at the time of hardware removal using the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) [26], visual analog scale (VAS), and International Knee Documentation Committee (IKDC) subjective knee scores [27]. All clinical outcomes were reviewed from medical records. No interobserver discrepancies were observed.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables are presented as the mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test.

Between-group comparisons (ACIC vs. MSC) were conducted using the independent t-test for normally distributed variables and the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed variables. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Within-group comparisons of preoperative and postoperative outcomes were performed using the paired t-test for normally distributed variables and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for non-normally distributed variables.

Arthroscopic cartilage status assessed using the ICRS grading system and cartilage defect size, as well as radiographic outcomes, including the KL grade and HKA angle, were analyzed using nonparametric statistical methods for both between-group and within-group comparisons.

In addition to statistical comparisons, the clinical relevance of changes in patient-reported outcome measures was assessed using the minimal clinically important difference (MCID). The MCID was defined as an improvement of ≥ 12.5 points for the IKDC score [6], ≥ 16.1 points for the WOMAC total score [28], and a reduction of ≥ 1.5 points on the VAS pain scale [29], based on previously published literature in patients undergoing HTO or with knee osteoarthritis.

Results

Demographics and baseline characteristics

There were no significant differences between the two groups with respect to age, sex distribution, BMI, time to hardware removal, duration of clinical follow-up, meniscal procedures (meniscectomy or repair), or the prevalence of medical comorbidities, including diabetes mellitus, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and chronic kidney disease (all p > 0.05). Baseline preoperative variables were comparable between the ACIC and MSC groups (Table 1). There were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of cartilage status, including ICRS grade and cartilage defect size, radiographic parameters such as KL grade and HKA angle, or patient-reported outcome measures, including WOMAC, VAS, and IKDC scores (Table 2).

Arthroscopic findings

Both the ACIC and MSC groups exhibited significant postoperative improvement in cartilage status as assessed by second-look arthroscopy (Figs. 4 and 5). The mean ICRS grade significantly improved from 3.76 ± 0.43 to 1.63 ± 0.54 in the ACIC group and from 3.84 ± 0.37 to 1.72 ± 0.52 in the MSC group (both p < 0.001). Similarly, the mean cartilage defect size showed a marked reduction after surgery in both groups, decreasing from 6.28 ± 2.78 to 1.15 ± 0.73 cm² in the ACIC group and from 6.76 ± 2.11 cm² to 1.21 ± 0.84 cm² in the MSC group (both p < 0.001). When comparing the magnitude of improvement between groups, there were no significant differences in the changes in ICRS grade (p = 1.000) or cartilage defect size (p = 0.202) between the ACIC and MSC groups (Table 3).

Radiographic outcomes

Radiographic evaluation revealed significant postoperative improvement in both osteoarthritic severity and lower limb alignment in the ACIC and MSC groups. The mean KL grade significantly decreased from 3.16 ± 0.37 to 2.01 ± 0.36 in the ACIC group and from 3.28 ± 0.58 to 2.22 ± 0.61 in the MSC group (both p < 0.001). In addition, the HKA angle showed a substantial correction toward neutral alignment after surgery in both groups. The mean HKA angle changed from 7.76 ± 1.84° to − 1.81 ± 1.39° in the ACIC group and from 8.11 ± 1.90° to − 1.97 ± 1.09° in the MSC group (both p < 0.001). Between-group comparison of change values revealed no significant differences in the magnitude of KL grade improvement (p = 0.529) or HKA correction (p = 0.134) between the ACIC and MSC groups (Table 3).

Clinical outcomes

Clinical outcomes were evaluated preoperatively at the time of hardware removal using the WOMAC, visual analog scale (VAS), and IKDC subjective knee scores (Table 4). All patients in both the ACIC and MSC groups achieved the MCID for WOMAC, VAS, and IKDC scores.

Discussion

The most important finding of the present study is that both ACIC and MSC, when performed in conjunction with HTO, resulted in notable improvements in arthroscopic, radiographic, and clinical outcomes in patients with varus knee osteoarthritis. Notably, these favorable outcomes were observed in a clinically challenging patient population consisting of middle-aged patients in their late fifties with medium-to-large chondral defects averaging 6–7 cm² in the medial compartment. This finding suggests that joint-preserving strategies combining cartilage restoration with mechanical realignment can be effective even in patients with advanced cartilage damage and varus alignment.

Importantly, the extent of postoperative improvement did not differ significantly between the ACIC and MSC treatment groups across arthroscopic, radiographic, and clinical outcomes. This result indicates that both cartilage restoration techniques provide comparable overall benefits when performed in conjunction with HTO. Although numerous studies have evaluated the clinical efficacy of ACIC and MSC-based therapies, investigations directly assessing cartilage restoration using second-look arthroscopy following ACIC combined with HTO remain limited. In particular, the ACIC technique used in the present study involves implantation of an atelocollagen gel into the chondral lesion. Although this approach shares the concept of collagen-based scaffolding with autologous matrix-induced chondrogenesis [30], it differs in that cartilage regeneration is induced through implantation of a collagen gel instead of coverage with a collagen membrane. To date, retrospective studies evaluating cartilage status, KL grade, mechanical alignment, and clinical outcomes following ACIC combined with HTO are relatively few. Kim et al. [19] reported significant improvement in all clinical outcomes (Lysholm score, preoperative 52.6 ± 10.7 vs. postoperative 79.7 ± 6.8; KOOS, 68.3 ± 11.4 vs. 90.2 ± 3.4; IKDC, 39.1 ± 4.1 vs. 81.6 ± 7.8; all p < 0.05) at 6-year follow-up and improvement in magnetic resonance observation of cartilage repair tissue (MOCART) score at 3 years in a prospective study of 30 patients treated with ACIC (mean, 78.5 ± 9.6). However, their cohort consisted of isolated ACIC treatment cases and included relatively young patients (mean age, 39 years; range, 19–61 years) with medium-sized chondral lesions (mean defect size, 4.6 cm²). More recently, Lee et al. [21] reported successful joint preservation using ACIC combined with HTO for large steroid-induced osteonecrotic defects of the knee, with favorable clinical, radiographic, and arthroscopic outcomes at mid-term follow-up. In comparison with previous ACIC studies [19], the present investigation is notable in that it provides clinically relevant data in a more challenging patient population, including a larger sample size, an older cohort in their late fifties, and relatively larger chondral lesions with varus alignment.

In the present study, MSC therapy combined with HTO also showed favorable postoperative outcomes. Several previous studies have reported encouraging results following MSC therapy in conjunction with HTO [17, 20, 31]. Song et al. [20] evaluated cartilage restoration using second-look arthroscopy at the time of hardware removal after MSC therapy combined with HTO and reported that approximately 88% of patients showed improvement to ICRS grade II or higher at a mean follow-up of 20.2 ± 6.5 months. Jung et al. [17] reported a one-year prospective comparison of hUCB-MSC implantation versus microdrilling combined with HTO in varus knees with ICRS grade ≥IIIB chondral lesions, showing comparable preoperative weight-bearing line ratios between groups (24.6 ± 8.6 vs. 19.9 ± 13.7, p = 0.360) but significantly greater arthroscopic cartilage coverage (7.2 ± 1.9 vs. 5.2 ± 2.1 cm², p = 0.023; 4.7 ± 1.4 vs. 3.0 ± 1.6 cm², p = 0.020) and a higher proportion of predominantly hyaline-like repair tissue in the hUCB-MSC group (66.7% vs. 20%, p = 0.041). Consistent with these previous reports, our study also revealed significant arthroscopic improvement following MSC therapy combined with HTO. These findings further support the cartilage restoration potential of MSC therapy, particularly in the short- to mid-term period following surgery. Importantly, our results confirm these favorable outcomes in a cohort of patients with medial compartment knee osteoarthritis treated with MSC therapy in conjunction with HTO and evaluated using second-look arthroscopy.

With respect to postoperative ICRS scores, no significant difference was observed between the ACIC and MSC groups. In both groups, multiple perforations served as a common trigger for cartilage repair by facilitating the recruitment of bone marrow-derived progenitor cells and the formation of a fibrin clot within the defect. In addition, both treatment strategies incorporated a three-dimensional hydrogel matrix, atelocollagen gel in the ACIC group, and a hyaluronate-based hydrogel in the MSC group, which may have stabilized the multiple perforation-induced repair clot and enhanced local cell retention. Furthermore, the favorable biomechanical environment created by HTO, including medial compartment unloading and reduced shear stress, may have optimized the conditions for cartilage repair and attenuated potential differences between the two approaches. Under such conditions, the regenerative processes in both groups may converge toward a similar macroscopic repair phenotype.

Radiographic assessment revealed significant improvement in KL grade in both the ACIC and MSC groups, without a significant difference between the two treatment modalities. A plausible explanation for this finding is the dominant biomechanical effect of HTO, which redistributes load away from the medial compartment and reduces abnormal joint stresses. Because both treatment groups underwent identical alignment correction, the radiographic response is likely to have been driven primarily by biomechanical factors rather than differences in cartilage restoration strategies. However, improvement in KL grade should not necessarily be interpreted as evidence of superior cartilage regeneration [32].

The absence of significant differences in clinical outcomes between the ACIC and MSC groups may be explained by several complementary mechanisms. The biomechanical unloading effect achieved through HTO may have contributed substantially to improvements in patient-reported functional outcomes by reducing medial compartment loading. However, because patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) primarily reflect subjective symptoms and perceived knee function instead of structural cartilage quality, differences in cartilage restoration do not necessarily translate directly into proportional differences in clinical scores [33]. In addition, clinical symptoms in osteoarthritic knees are influenced by multiple pain generators beyond articular cartilage, which may further attenuate technique-specific effects [34].

In this study, the prevalence of dyslipidemia did not differ significantly between the ACIC and MSC groups, revealing that lipid status was unlikely to have influenced the selection of the cartilage repair method. Previous studies have shown that abnormal lipid profiles are associated with impaired chondrocyte function, reduced extracellular matrix synthesis, and accelerated progression of osteoarthritis [35–37]. Baudart et al. [35] revealed that patients with osteoarthritis had an approximately twofold higher risk of dyslipidemia compared with individuals without osteoarthritis (OR, 1.98; 95% CI, 1.43–2.75), supporting a potential link between lipid metabolic abnormalities and osteoarthritis. Furthermore, Courties et al. [37] proposed the concept of a metabolic syndrome–associated osteoarthritis phenotype, in which metabolic disturbances such as dyslipidemia contribute to chronic low-grade inflammation and subsequent joint degradation. In this context, hypercholesterolemia has been identified as an independent risk factor for both hand and generalized osteoarthritis, irrespective of age, sex, or BMI. However, the extent to which dyslipidemia affects the outcomes of surgical cartilage restoration remains unclear. Because the present study was not designed to evaluate the impact of dyslipidemia on clinical or structural outcomes, no definitive conclusions can be drawn regarding its prognostic role. Similarly, the effects of other metabolic comorbidities, including diabetes mellitus and chronic kidney disease, on articular cartilage integrity remain incompletely understood.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design inherently carries a risk of selection bias and limits causal inference. Second, the lack of randomization may have resulted in unmeasured confounding factors influencing treatment allocation and outcomes. Dyslipidemia was assessed only as a categorical baseline variable, and the study was not designed to evaluate its association with clinical or structural outcomes; therefore, potential metabolic influences on cartilage repair could not be adequately analyzed. Third, cartilage repair was evaluated using the ICRS grading system, which primarily reflects macroscopic morphological characteristics and may not fully capture differences in the histological or biochemical quality of the regenerated cartilage. In addition, the timing of second-look arthroscopy varied among patients because it was performed at the time of hardware removal, which may have introduced variability in the stage of cartilage maturation at evaluation. Finally, the relatively small sample size and the short- to mid-term follow-up period may limit the generalizability of the findings and preclude assessment of the long-term durability of cartilage restoration. Future prospective studies with larger cohorts and longer follow-up periods are warranted to validate these findings and clarify long-term outcomes.

Conclusion

Both ACIC and MSC therapy can be performed in conjunction with HTO for patients with varus knee osteoarthritis requiring cartilage restoration. When combined with mechanical realignment, these cartilage repair techniques can provide notable clinical benefits, accompanied by favorable arthroscopic findings and radiographic changes suggesting osteoarthritis stabilization. Within the limitations of this retrospective study, ACIC showed outcomes comparable to those of MSC therapy in terms of cartilage status, radiographic parameters, and clinical outcomes. Accordingly, ACIC can be considered a pragmatic treatment option alongside MSC therapy in selected patients undergoing realignment surgery.
