Assessing the role of Subacute Encephalopathy with Seizures in Alcoholics (SESA) syndrome in the development of status epilepticus: a case report and systematic review with individual patients’ analysis
Authors: Michelangelo Dasara, Fedele Dono, Giacomo Evangelista, Paolo Quintieri, Sara Cipollone, Davide Liviello, Rossella Ferrante, Stefano L Sensi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSubacute Encephalopathy with Seizures in Alcoholics (SESA) is a rare neurological syndrome characterized by altered mental status, periodic EEG discharges, and neuroimaging abnormalities, typically occurring in individuals with chronic alcohol use. Its progression to status epilepticus (SE), including refractory and super-refractory forms, is poorly documented.MethodsWe present a super-refractory status epilepticus (SRSE) case in a patient with confirmed SESA and conduct a systematic review of the literature to explore the relationship between SESA and SE. A systematic literature review was performed using MEDLINE (accessed through PubMed), Scopus, EMBASE and Google Scholar, in accordance with the PRISMA guidelines. Data were extracted on demographics, EEG and imaging findings, treatment approaches, and outcomes.ResultsOur patient developed SRSE in the setting of chronic hepatic dysfunction. The patients responded to a combination of propofol and ketamine and obtained long-term seizure control with vagus nerve stimulation (VNS). Type 3 oligoclonal bands were detected in the CSF, although it is considered a non-specific marker of neuroinflammation. The literature review identified 7 cases of SE in SESA, with variable clinical presentations. While levetiracetam and valproic acid were the most employed treatments, benzodiazepines were notably absent from first-line protocols. Phenytoin and lacosamide resulted as favorable alternatives.ConclusionSE in the context of SESA requires tailored therapeutic strategies due to hepatic comorbidities and diagnostic ambiguity with NCSE. Our report supports the cautious use of propofol and ketamine in SRSE, as well as the efficacy of VNS in long-term seizure control.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-026-04936-z.Keywords: Alcohol assumption, Epilepsy, Systematic review, Status epilepticus, SESA syndrome

Introduction

Subacute Encephalopathy with Seizures in Alcoholics (SESA) is a rare neurological disorder resulting from chronic alcohol abuse, presenting with transient confusional states and seizures [1]. Diagnosis is primarily clinical, though supported by electroencephalogram (EEG) recordings which reveal lateralized periodic discharges (LPDs), brief potentially ictal rhythmic discharges (BIRDs), and lateralized rhythmic delta activity (LRDA) [2]. Complementary, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) findings may show cortical atrophy, chronic microvascular leukoencephalopathy, and cortical-subcortical T2 fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) hyperintensities, alongside increased diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) signal [3, 4]. To date, nearly 40 cases of SESA have been documented in the literature, highlighting its potential threat to neuronal integrity and the risk of permanent neurological sequelae, such as hippocampal and focal cortical atrophy [5].

The etiology of SESA remains poorly understood. Chronic cerebrovascular pathology has been suggested as a probable pathogenic factor, although the strength of this association is debated [1, 6]. Experimental studies indicate that SESA is linked to the downregulation of postsynaptic γ-aminobutyric acid type A (GABA-A) receptors and disregulation of N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptors. This imbalance in the cerebral excitatory-inhibitory system is central to the development of seizures as well as status epilepticus (SE) [7].

The relationship between SESA and SE continues to be a topic of discussion. Some reports propose that SESA may represent a non-convulsive SE (NCSE). However, these studies often lack adherence to the Salzburg criteria for NCSE diagnosis, complicating the reliability of their findings. Although the clinical presentation of SESA is typically associated with periodic or rhythmic lateralized EEG abnormalities, these findings may reflect the underlying encephalopathy rather than an ictal pattern. Thus, while SESA can potentially premote SE onset, specific risk factors have yet to be identified.

In this article, we present a case of SRSE in a patient diagnosed with SESA, and we also provide a systematic literature review regarding the association between SESA and SE.

Methods

Case report

Data relevant to the reconstruction of this case report were retrospectively retrieved from the patient’s medical records. Written authorization for the processing of sensitive data was obtained prior to data extraction, and all information was handled in compliance with applicable data protection regulations. Patient-identifying information was removed to ensure confidentiality.

Systematic review

The results of our systematic review were reported following the guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement.

Two authors (M.D. and F.D.) performed independent research from the first available data until January 2026 using the following terms: (“Status Epilepticus” OR “Non Convulsive Status Epilepticus” OR “Epileptic State” OR “New onset Status Epilepticus”) AND (“SESA” OR “Subacute Encephalopathy with Seizures in Alcoholics”) AND (outcome* OR prognosis OR prognostic OR mortality OR survival OR recovery OR “treatment response” OR response* OR refractory OR “seizure control” OR relapse OR recurrence OR disability OR “neurological outcome” OR “functional outcome”). The following electronic databases and data sources were systematically searched: MEDLINE (accessed through PubMed), Scopus, EMBASE and Google Scholar.

As per the inclusion criteria, we selected: (1) articles written in English; (2) case reports, case series, or observational studies reporting at least one adult case of SE in patients diagnosed with SESA syndrome. SE was defined according to the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) criteria. For cases described before the publication of the current ILAE definition, only reports providing accessible clinical information and EEG data allowing a reliable classification were included. Exclusion criteria were: (1) non-English language articles; (2) reviews, editorials, conference abstracts without sufficient clinical details, or animal studies; (3) studies not reporting patients with SESA syndrome and status epilepticus; (4) reports describing exclusively pediatric cases (age < 18 years) or studies in which adult cases could not be clearly separated; (5) cases lacking sufficient clinical information and/or EEG data to allow reliable classification of SE according to ILAE criteria; and (6) duplicate reports of the same case (in which case the most complete report was retained).

For each eligible report, we extracted the following data using a standardized data collection form: patient demographics (age, sex), alcohol use history, prior epilepsy history (yes/no), status epilepticus (SE) type and semiology (convulsive or non-convulsive), clinical presentation, neuroradiological findings, hematological/metabolic data, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) findings, and electroencephalogram (EEG) features. We also extracted treatment-related variables, including acute SE management (e.g., benzodiazepines, intravenous ASMs, anesthetics when applicable), chronic ASM therapy, and clinical outcomes (SE resolution, recurrence, functional recovery at discharge and/or follow-up, and mortality when reported).

Data extraction was performed independently by four reviewers (G.E., F.D., M.D., S.S.), and disagreements were resolved by consensus. Due to expected heterogeneity across reports and the predominance of case reports/case series, quantitative pooling (meta-analysis) was not planned. Data were synthesized through descriptive statistics (when feasible) and qualitative narrative synthesis, and results were summarized in structured tables highlighting clinical, EEG, imaging, treatment, and outcome patterns.

The primary endpoint was the characterization of status epilepticus (SE) in patients diagnosed with SESA syndrome, including SE type (convulsive or non-convulsive) and EEG patterns. Secondary endpoints included treatment response (resolution of SE and need for escalation of therapy), recurrence of seizures/SE, neuroimaging and CSF findings, and clinical outcomes at discharge and/or follow-up (neurological recovery and mortality when reported).

The methodological quality of included studies was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklists for Case Reports and Case Series [8]. Each item was rated as “Yes”, “No”, “Unclear”, or “Not applicable”. Overall quality was classified as high, moderate, or low according to the proportion of items rated “Yes” (high: ≥75%, moderate: 50–74%, low: <50%). The certainty of evidence for the main outcomes was evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) [9] approach. Given the predominance of case reports and case series, the overall certainty of evidence was expected to be low or very low and was interpreted accordingly.

Results

Case report

A 41-year-old man was admitted to our Emergency Room (E.R.) due to a severe metabolic imbalance characterized by hyperbilirubinemia (17,70 mg/dL), hyperammonemia (70 mol/L), and severe hyponatremia (121 mmol/L) due to an acute alcohol intoxication. The patient’s past medical history showed heroin and alcohol addiction started at the age of 29, and focal epilepsy with unknown aetiology diagnosed at the age of 12 and treated with Levetiracetam (LEV) 1000 mg/day and eslicarbazepine (ECB) 400 mg/day. According to the medical chart, the patient has always shown a good therapeutic adherence without reported seizure relapse in the previous 4 years. The patient’s past functional and neurological status were otherwise unremarkable.

The neurological examination only showed mild confusion without any sign of focal neurological deficits. A computerized tomography (CT) of the head showed no abnormalities. Thus, the patient was hospitalized and sodium levels were gradually adjusted. During the hospitalization, the patient experienced a urinary infection and was successfully treated with antibiotic therapy (i.e., III generation cephalosporin). His neurological conditions improved, and there were no further signs of confusion or any neurological signs.

At day 30, the patient showed brief episodes characterized by behavioural arrest and slight bilateral palpebral myoclonus. A CT scan with angiography ruled out any cerebrovascular disease. A concomitant EEG showed 2 Hz high-amplitude (100–150 uV) generalized periodic discharges (GPDs) with superimposed fast activity (+ F) and poly-spikes (Fig. 1A). Thus, according to the Salzburg criteria, a diagnosis of NCSE was made. The patient was first treated with two boluses of intravenous (i.v.) diazepam (DZP) 10 mg with no clinical-EEG improvement. Consequently, i.v. loading doses of LEV 3000 mg and phenytoin (PHT) 750 mg were administered 30 min apart, with a brief transient EEG improvement (Fig. 1B). Therefore, according to the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) criteria, a diagnosis of refractory Status Epilepticus (RSE) was made. The patient was then transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) and underwent continuous EEG (cEEG) monitoring. First, an i.v. bolus of propofol (PPF) 2 mg/Kg was administered, followed by continuous i.v. infusion at a rate of 1.7 mg/kg/h combined with LEV 3000 mg and PHT 600mg daily, achieving an EEG burst suppression pattern. After 24 h, a first anaesthetic weaning was attempted, unfortunately leading to reappearance of the GPD + F pattern. Thus, PPF dosage was implemented at 2.2 mg/kg/h and combined with continuous 0.1 mg/kg/h i.v. midazolam (MDZ). An MRI scan of the brain showed a T2-fluid attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) hyperintense signal in the central portion of the pons as well as in the left cortical-subcortical occipital cortex with signs of restriction in the diffusion weighted image (DWI) sequences. No areas of positive contrast enhancement were detected. In addition, mild diffuse cortical atrophy and mild cerebellar atrophy were also detected. Magnetic Resonance Venography (MRV) and Angiography (MRA) were unremarkable. (Fig. 2A-F).

A new weaning was tried after 48 h, which was unsuccessful. According to ILAE criteria, a diagnosis of super-refractory status epilepticus (SRSE) was made. Hence, propofol was stopped and a bolus of i.v. Ketamine (KTM) 0.5 mg/kg followed by continuous i.v. infusion at a rate of 0.3 mg/kg/h was started. In addition, the ketogenic diet was introduced.

A cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis showed normal protein level (20 mg/ml) with normal cellularity (0 cell/mm3). However, type 3 oligoclonal bands (OCB) were detected. The autoimmune and microbiological panel did not show any abnormalities. A new MR scan of the brain with contrast, angiography, and venography sequences was performed, which did not show any further pathological modifications. After 96 h, an anaesthetic withdrawal was attempted, resulting in a successful awakening of the patient who was then transferred to our Neurologic Unit (Fig. 1C).

In the suspicion of an underlying inflammatory/autoimmune condition, i.v. methylprednisone 1 g per 5 days was administered without any related side effects. A total body CT and testicular echo were also performed without evidence of tumour pathology. During the hospitalization, daily seizure clusters characterized by behavioural arrest and bilateral palpebral myoclonus were detected. The anti-seizure treatment was then gradually modified by switching LEV and PHT to brivaracetam (BRV) 200 mg/day and lacosamide (LCS) 600 mg/day. Even though the therapy was implemented, the patient kept showing daily focal seizures. Thus, perampanel (PER) 8 mg was gradually introduced with < 50% seizure frequency reduction. A whole-exome sequencing analysis was performed, showing the RELN gene heterozygosis variant of uncertain significance (VUS) c.2015 C > T (p.Pro672Leu). At day 90, the patient was dismissed to a rehabilitation unit, still with daily seizures. A Vagal Nerve Stimulator (Sentiva®, Livanova) was then implanted along with a biweekly titration scheme to reach a current output of 1.75 mA after 14 weeks. After 6 months, seizure frequency was reduced by > 75%, while seizure freedom was reported at a 9-month post-implantation follow-up visit. Ketogenic diet was stopped due to patient’s poor adherence.

Literature search

The literature search yielded 245 (PubMed: 10 results; Google Scholar: 225; EMBASE: 8; Scopus: 2). 41 duplicates have been identified. Of the 204 reports screened, the full texts of 5 articles were reviewed for eligibility, and all of them met the selection criteria and were considered eligible for the review (Fig. 3) [2, 4, 5, 10–12]. All patients had a confirmed diagnosis of SE in line with ILAE criteria. According to the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklists for Case Reports and Case Series, the overall quality of all included papers was classified as high (Supp. Table 1).

Demographics, clinical features and SE characteristics

Data from 7 adult patients diagnosed with SESA syndrome who developed SE were extracted from the included reports (6 males; mean age 66.4 ± 9.2 years). A prior history of epilepsy was reported in only one case (1/7, 14.3%), while two patients had experienced alcohol-withdrawal seizures in the year preceding the index event. Data regarding the alcohol withdrawal interval were inconsistently reported and showed marked variability across individuals.

Regarding SE semiology, focal non-convulsive status epilepticus (NCSE) represented the most frequent presentation (5/7, 71,4%), while one patient presented with generalized NCSE (1/7, 14,3%) and one with focal convulsive SE (CSE) (1/7, 14,3%). One patient exhibited both focal CSE and subsequent NCSE during the same clinical episode.

EEG, neuroradiological and laboratory findings

EEG findings were consistently characterized by lateralized epileptiform activity. LPDs were reported in most cases (6/7), sometimes associated with LRDA or BIRDs. One report described frequent focal spike-and-wave complexes with progressive hemispheric propagation.

Neuroradiological evaluation showed heterogeneous but recurrent patterns. MRI abnormalities compatible with peri-ictal changes were frequently reported when MRI was performed, including cortical/subcortical DWI/FLAIR alterations involving temporal–limbic regions (i.e., amygdala/hippocampus) and/or neocortical areas, with occasional thalamic involvement. CT frequently showed diffuse cerebral atrophy and/or chronic microangiopathic ischemic changes; in one case, a prior ischemic stroke was documented.

Hematological/metabolic abnormalities were variably described (available in 6/7 cases), with hyponatremia among the most recurrent findings. CSF analysis was performed in 5 patients, showing elevated protein levels in two cases, while pleocytosis was described in one.

Treatment and outcomes

Treatment strategies primarily relied on i.v. ASM, most commonly LEV and valproic acid (VPA); LCS and PHT were also used. Data on benzodiazepine use as first-line therapy were not systematically reported. Escalation to anesthetic agents (e.g., MDZ and/or PPF) was required in several cases, consistent with refractory SE presentation.

Hospitalization length was reported in six patients (mean 35.5 ± 26.7 days). SE resolution was achieved in all included cases (7/7, 100%). Relapse was described in four patients, occurring between 30 and 300 days after the index episode when reported; poor ASM compliance was explicitly identified as the main contributing factor in two relapsing cases.

Key patterns identified

Overall, across the included cases, SESA-related SE most frequently presented as focal non-convulsive SE, with EEG consistently showing lateralized epileptiform abnormalities (predominantly LPDs, often associated with LRDA and/or BIRDs). When MRI was available, peri-ictal cortical-subcortical signal changes, frequently involving temporal-limbic regions, were commonly reported. Although SE resolution was achieved in all cases with ASM-based management, relapse occurred in a relevant proportion of patients, often in association with poor treatment adherence.

Discussion

SESA syndrome has been recognized as an entity within the ictal–interictal continuum associated with chronic alcoholism, characterized by encephalopathy, periodic or generalized EEG discharges, and neuroradiological alterations.

Several mechanisms may contribute to the development of this condition. Chronic alcohol consumption may worsen seizure control through increased blood levels of excitotoxic compounds such as glutamate, aspartate, and homocysteine. In addition, alcohol-related hepatic enzyme induction can enhance the catabolism of ASM, thereby reducing serum concentrations and potentially facilitating breakthrough seizures [13]. Dehydration and electrolyte disturbances, frequent conditions in individuals with chronic alcoholism, represent additional proconvulsant factors. Notably, chronic alcohol intake has also been linked to a systemic pro-inflammatory state, with elevated cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6, which may contribute to blood–brain barrier dysfunction and increased neuronal excitability, thereby promoting seizure susceptibility [14]. In our case, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis revealed type 3 oligoclonal bands (OCB), raising the suspicion of central nervous system immune activation. However, this OCB pattern is generally regarded as non-specific; within the context of SESA it may more plausibly reflect reactive inflammatory processes secondary to neuronal injury rather than a primary autoimmune etiology.

Both CSE and NCSE have been reported in patients with SESA. While CSE is typically identifiable on clinical grounds, the diagnosis of NCSE remains more challenging and requires EEG confirmation. Clinically, NCSE often manifests with altered mental status or behavioral changes without prominent motor features, substantially overlapping with metabolic and toxic encephalopathies [15]. This overlap makes the differentiation between SESA-related encephalopathy and NCSE particularly complex and highlights the central role of EEG in evaluation. Several authors have proposed that SESA represents an intrinsically epileptic condition and, in some instances, have equated it with NCSE [2]. However, this interpretation is not universally accepted. Indeed, multiple reports describe patients with SESA exhibiting periodic or rhythmic EEG abnormalities without fulfilling standardized criteria for NCSE, including Salzburg criteria. When current electroclinical definitions are applied, such EEG patterns may fall within the ictal-interictal continuum rather than represent unequivocal SE. Importantly, a large portion of historical literature on SESA predates the widespread adoption of modern EEG criteria, which may partly explain discrepancies in diagnostic interpretation across studies. For these reasons, we deliberately avoid considering SESA per se as synonymous with SE. Instead, SESA should be viewed as a syndrome characterized by an encephalopathic state with a high propensity to develop seizures and, in a subset of cases, overt SE. Adhering strictly to validated electroclinical criteria is essential to avoid overdiagnosis of NCSE, which may lead to unnecessary exposure to aggressive and potentially harmful treatment escalation, particularly in medically fragile patients with hepatic dysfunction.

In the present case, SE diagnosis was supported by evolution of EEG findings into a pattern fulfilling Salzburg criteria for NCSE, associated with subtle ictal clinical features and pharmacological responsiveness. Although the presence of pre-existing focal epilepsy and multiple systemic comorbidities could raise doubts about appropriateness of a SESA diagnosis, prior epilepsy has not been established as an exclusion criterion. On the contrary, a history of epilepsy may represent a vulnerability factor facilitating transition from alcohol-related encephalopathy to overt SE under conditions of metabolic stress and neurotoxicity. In this context, long-standing seizure freedom prior to admission, followed by abrupt onset of encephalopathy and SE associated with alcohol-related metabolic derangements, supports SESA as the precipitating syndrome rather than an exacerbation of chronic epilepsy.

The diagnostic workup in suspected SESA-related SE should integrate systemic, CSF, and neuroradiological findings, particularly in cases where the clinical presentation overlaps with other toxic–metabolic encephalopathies. As discussed above, CSF analysis may show non-specific abnormalities including elevated protein and occasionally immunological markers that likely reflect reactive inflammatory mechanisms rather than primary autoimmune inflammation.

Neuroimaging findings can provide additional supportive evidence. SESA has been associated with structural brain vulnerability and peri-ictal changes, sometimes involving temporal-limbic regions. In the present case and in the reviewed literature, neuroradiological alterations may reflect acute seizure-related injury and/or chronic alcohol-related brain damage, contributing to network instability and seizure susceptibility. While imaging findings are not pathognomonic, their integration with EEG and clinical data may improve diagnostic confidence and help guide treatment escalation decisions in equivocal presentations.

Management of SE in patients with suspected SESA requires particular attention, mainly due to frequent systemic comorbidities, especially hepatic dysfunction. Although BDZ should be used cautiously in individuals with hepatic insufficiency due to the risk of worsening encephalopathy [16] and cholestasis [17], they remain the recommended first-line therapy for SE. Notably, in our literature review, early BDZ administration was never documented, potentially reflecting clinician reluctance rooted in concerns about hepatically mediated adverse effects. This observation supports the notion that, in this population, safety concerns may prevail over established first-line efficacy in acute management. It should nevertheless be emphasized that BDZ use in patients with chronic alcohol consumption is not contraindicated per se, as BDZ are routinely employed for alcohol withdrawal syndromes and psychiatric manifestations [18]. Therefore, adherence to ILAE guidelines for SE management should also be maintained in this patient population, except in individuals with clinically significant hepatic compromise, where risk–benefit must be individualized.

With regard to second-line therapy, the available literature most frequently reports the use of LEV and VPA. LEV represents a reasonable option due to its favorable efficacy and safety profile. Conversely, VPA should be used with caution as it may exacerbate hyperammonemia and potentially worsen the encephalopathic state [19]. Although PHT undergoes hepatic metabolism, the risk of inducing hepatic failure appears lower than with VPA [20]. The ESETT trial [21] and recent real-world evidence [22] have highlighted comparative efficacy of VPA, PHT, and LEV, with some data favoring PHT in acute SE, particularly in new-onset presentations [22]. Nonetheless, PHT dosing should be carefully adjusted based on hepatic function and serum albumin concentrations given its nonlinear pharmacokinetics, highly protein bound, and risk of accumulation. LCS represents an additional therapeutic option [23]. Multiple studies support its favorable safety and tolerability profile, with minimal drug-drug interactions and lower propensity for sedation or cardiorespiratory depression compared to other ASMs, making it a valuable alternative in complex scenarios with hepatic vulnerability.

Data regarding RSE or SRSE in SESA remain limited. Only a small number of documented cases report use of propofol in this clinical setting, and caution is warranted due to the risk of propofol infusion syndrome (PRIS), particularly in patients with hepatic dysfunction. In our case, SRSE was successfully resolved through PPF and KTM, which has established efficacy in SRSE. KTM is primarily metabolized hepatically via CYP2B6 and CYP3A4; however, its pharmacokinetic profile is relatively stable and it is generally well tolerated even in moderate hepatic impairment [24]. Moreover, it does not significantly exacerbate hepatic encephalopathy, supporting its role as a relatively safe adjunct in this fragile population.

Long-term management remains highly relevant in SESA, as the syndrome may lead to persistent structural alterations and progressive network instability, potentially promoting the development of epilepsy [15]. This highlights the need not only for acute SE resolution but also for relapse prevention, long-term ASM optimization, and adherence-oriented follow-up strategies. In our patient, long-term seizure control was achieved through vagus nerve stimulation (VNS). In this context, VNS represents a viable option in patients who develop refractory epilepsy following SESA-related SE. Evidence supports VNS efficacy across drug-resistant epilepsy etiologies [25], and in our case it was associated with sustained seizure freedom, further emphasizing its potential therapeutic value in post-SESA epileptogenesis.

Finally, although acute survival in reported cases appears generally favorable when SE is recognized and treated appropriately, the long-term burden of relapse, recurrent SE, and functional decline can be substantial. Therefore, an integrated strategy including early recognition, guideline-based acute management, and structured long-term follow-up is essential to improve outcomes in this population.

Conclusion

Our findings underscore the substantial diagnostic challenges posed by SE in the setting of SESA, where clinical presentation and EEG patterns may closely overlap with NCSE and metabolic encephalopathies, particularly in patients with chronic alcohol use and hepatic comorbidities. In this context, rigorous application of standardized electroclinical criteria is crucial to ensure diagnostic accuracy and to prevent misclassification, which may lead to inappropriate or delayed treatment. From a therapeutic perspective, management requires a careful balance between prompt seizure control and safety considerations related to hepatic dysfunction. ASM and anesthetic choices should therefore be individualized. In our case, a stepwise, multimodal approach incorporating propofol, ketamine, and ultimately VNS proved effective, highlighting the value of tailored strategies in addressing both the acute SE phase and the long-term management of SESA-associated epilepsy.
