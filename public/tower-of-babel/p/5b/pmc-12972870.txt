Extension of the minimally invasive pectus repair to adult patients: strategies for success
Authors: Nour B. Odeh, Abdulrahman Senjab, Rawan M. Zeineddine, Ahmed E. Khedr, Juan M. Farina, Amjed A. Al, Jesse J. Lackey, Dawn E. Jaroszewski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pectus excavatum (PE) is the most common congenital deformity of the anterior chest wall and is increasingly recognized as a clinically relevant condition in adult patients. While minimally invasive repair of pectus excavatum (MIRPE) is well established in pediatric and adolescent populations, early attempts to apply this technique in adults were associated with higher complication rates. These challenges were largely attributed to increased chest wall rigidity, more severe and asymmetric deformities, and greater mechanical forces acting on intrathoracic support bars. Over the past two decades, however, progressive refinements in surgical technique, bar stabilization, and perioperative management have enabled the safe expansion of MIRPE to adult populations. This review summarizes the contemporary literature on MIRPE in adults, focusing on patient selection, technical considerations, perioperative strategies, and reported outcomes. Adult MIRPE presents distinct technical challenges and frequently requires modifications such as routine thoracoscopic guidance, forced sternal elevation, reinforcement of intercostal spaces, use of multiple bars, cross-bar configurations, and enhanced bar fixation techniques to achieve durable correction. These technical adaptations have contributed to reductions in bar displacement, recurrence, and major complications in more recent series. Postoperative pain management remains a critical component of adult repair, and the adoption of multimodal analgesic protocols, including intercostal nerve cryoablation, has been associated with reduced opioid requirements and shorter hospital length of stay. Contemporary reports from experienced, high-volume centers demonstrate consistent improvements in patient-reported quality of life (QoL), symptom burden, and cosmetic satisfaction following adult MIRPE. Improvements in cardiopulmonary function have been described in selected patients, particularly when complete anatomic correction is achieved, although objective physiologic benefits remain variable across studies. Adult MIRPE is technically demanding, and outcomes appear closely linked to surgeon experience and institutional volume. With careful patient selection and modern surgical strategies, MIRPE can be performed safely and effectively in adult patients, including those over 30 years of age. Further prospective studies focused on adult populations are needed to better define long-term outcomes and durability following bar removal.

Introduction

Pectus, including the most prevalent variant, pectus excavatum (PE) or “funnel chest”, is the most common congenital deformity of the anterior chest wall (1). Reports of PE prevalence vary by location and ethnicity, with Caucasian and Asian races having a higher reported incidence (2,3). In a recent publication, 0.5%, 5% and 0.4% of 2,687 adult Dallas Heart Study subjects were noted to have PE when reviewing chest imaging [depending on the index of assessment: Haller index (HI) 3.25, correction index (CI) ≥10%, or both, respectively] (1). More males undergo surgical correction, but the prevalence is likely equal (1,3).

In many patients, symptoms associated with PE will worsen with aging (4,5). Common symptoms include exercise intolerance, chest pain, decreased endurance, and exertional dyspnea (4). Symptoms may be secondary to underlying cardiac compression, which results in reduced right ventricular dimensions, restricted stroke volume, abnormal strain, and potentially compromised systolic and diastolic function (6,7). Most patients experience significant improvement in quality of life (QoL) and symptoms after surgical repair (8). Improvements in objective measures, including cardiopulmonary exercise testing, are reported [increased maximum oxygen consumption (VO2 max), oxygen pulse, maximal ventilation, and right ventricular stroke volume] (6).

Pectus is commonly identified and repaired in late childhood or adolescence when the chest wall is malleable. Patients who are not symptomatic or who forego surgical intervention may present as adults due to worsening symptoms. The repair of adults with PE presents challenges compared to pediatric cases. A minimally invasive repair [minimally invasive repair of pectus excavatum (MIRPE)] approach to pediatric pectus repair was introduced in 1998 as an alternative to open procedures and has rapidly become the standard of care in children and adolescents due to its less invasive nature and excellent outcomes (6,9). Although some would argue that “minimally invasive” is an underestimation of the invasiveness involved in utilizing multiple intrathoracic bars to force elevation of the anterior chest wall, excision of abnormal cartilage and sternal osteotomy (Ravitch procedure) are no longer thought to be necessary to repair deformities even in the adult with increased chest wall rigidity (10). There has been a learning curve and evolution of techniques to allow safe performance of MIRPE in the more difficult adult patient. Initial efforts to adapt MIRPE into older patients were fraught with reports of bar migration and higher complications, including some life-threatening (11). Over the past two decades, the introduction of technique modifications to the original MIRPE has significantly improved the safety and outcomes of the procedure in the repair of the adult patient (12). These modifications are particularly critical in patients ≥30 years, who are more challenging and notably have higher risks of complications (13-15). As clinical recognition of PE in adults has grown, so has the impetus for surgical correction (13,14). A review of minimally invasive pectus repair in the adult population is presented with discussion of technique modifications, pain management, and outcomes.

Methods

We conducted a comprehensive literature search to identify clinical studies reporting outcomes of MIRPE in adult patients. Searches were performed in the PubMed/MEDLINE database from database inception through August 31, 2025. The search strategy included combinations of the following keywords and MeSH terms: “pectus excavatum”, “pectus repair”, “Nuss procedure”, “minimally invasive repair of pectus excavatum”, “adult”, and “thoracic surgery”. We also reviewed reference lists of relevant reviews and primary studies to capture additional reports not identified in the initial search. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at Mayo Clinic Arizona (No. 23-010389). Written informed consent was obtained from all individual participants.

Inclusion criteria: (I) studies published in English; (II) studies reporting clinical outcomes in patients undergoing MIRPE or variants thereof; (III) cohorts including adult patients, defined as age ≥16 years for consistency with previously published reviews, with specific emphasis on studies with older adult subgroups when available.

We excluded studies that reported exclusively pediatric populations without separate adult outcome data, case reports with fewer than six adult patients, and articles focusing solely on open or hybrid (non-MIRPE) techniques without distinct MIRPE outcomes. Given the limited literature on older adult cohorts, we included all available studies that reported outcomes for adults beyond the pediatric age range and highlighted the relative scarcity of large, dedicated adult series.

Indications for surgery

Indications for surgical repair in adults should be individualized, with the final decision weighted on balancing the benefits of the procedure versus the risks. For the more difficult to repair adult population, the benefits of the procedure need to greatly outweigh the risks. In pediatric pectus repair, having a HI greater than 3.25 and symptoms is considered an indication for repair (16): adult patients, however, should undergo a more extensive evaluation and documentation of medical issues. Other potential medical comorbidities that could cause similar symptoms should be investigated and ruled out. A HI (≥3.25) or CI (≥10–20%) is generally required for insurance coverage. More importantly, when considering surgery for the adult patient, evidence for significant compression that is causing a cardiopulmonary deficit should be present before considering surgical intervention. An echocardiogram for assessment of chamber size and function is important and ideally includes right ventricular strain evaluating cardiac efficiency (17). Transthoracic echocardiograms may be inadequate due to poor acoustic windows and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is a good alternative for evaluation of resting cardiac function (18). Many patients manifest symptoms during exercise and cardiopulmonary exercise testing can be used to identify abnormalities with the percentage of predicted O2 pulse and peak VO2 max.

Considerations for older patients

Especially important for ages over 30 years when the difficulty of repair, recovery, and risk of complications is significantly greater, significant deficits with QoL implications before surgery are considered. More difficult, older patients may be considered for referral to a high-volume center for improved outcomes (15,19,20). Challenges in adult repair

Surgery for adults with PE can be challenging largely due to the increased chest wall calcification and rigidity that occurs with aging, but other factors can come into play that may increase difficulty, including a larger, heavier chest wall and greater length of the defect (21). Adults also may have increased chest adipose and breast tissue as well as pectoral muscle mass, which increases the difficulty of access and visualization (14,16). Breast and pectoral implants may have been placed to cosmetically address the deformity and should be removed prior to pectus repair, as they hamper access to the chest and significantly increase the infection risk. In one study, patients who underwent concurrent breast implants had higher rates of pleural effusion requiring thoracentesis (17.4% vs. 4.8%, P=0.008) and wound infections (8.6% vs. 0.5%, P<0.001) (3). Achieving chest elevation and getting bars to remain in the proper position remains challenging with adult PE repair.

Early publications of MIRPE reported significant bar displacement rates in adults (as high as 33%) (22-24). Unfortunately, even recent publications from centers with limited experience in adult repair have reported prohibitive complications and bar migration rates (11,25). This has led some centers to recommend against surgical intervention in adult patients (26,27). Technique modifications and surgeon experience are critical aspects of a safe and successful MIRPE in adults. In pediatrics, a minimum of 25 cases are reported to yield average outcomes; however, centers performing over 70 cases annually were able to achieve lower complication rates and shorter hospital stays (28). Miller et al reported that two-thirds of all Nuss-related complications occurred during the first 9 months of learning experience (6-year study) (29). In adult repairs, with a much greater level of difficulty, the number of required cases is likely doubled (14,28).

Technique modifications for adult MIRPE

The original MIRPE procedure introduced by Dr. Nuss has undergone several recommended modifications since its introduction in 1998 (Table 2) (9,12). The most significant of these was the addition of thoracoscopy. Adequate visualization during surgery is imperative for safety and injury prevention (12). Technique modifications for successful adult MIRPE repair

Forced sternal elevation

The use of sternal elevation, prior to attempting mediastinal dissection and bar passage, was initiated in pediatrics using a vacuum bell attached to the anterior chest wall (26). Sternal elevation decreases the risk of cardiac injury by improving visualization and negates the need to maneuver under the defect and around the compressed mediastinal structures (Video 1) (52). It also decreases rotational forces and intercostal trauma associated with bar placement. For the adult patient with chest rigidity, significant force is required to elevate the chest wall (32). A bedside retractor, such as the Rultract retractor (Rultract Inc., Cleveland, OH, USA) or the Crane (Primemed Inc., Korea) works well in adults when attached to the operating table railing (32,53).

Intra-operative video of a patient with pectus excavatum demonstrating sternal elevation technique at our institution using a bedside retractor.

The sternum is secured to the retractor with a perforating bone clamp, such as a Lewin bone clamp (V. Mueller NL6960; CareFusion, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA), sternal screws, or wire. Handheld retractors can also be placed through a subxiphoid incision for manual elevation. The rule for maintaining a ‘critical view’ should be followed to safely perform the procedure. This rule states that the surgeon can always visualize the distal end of thoracoscopic instruments placed into the chest (52). Without sternal elevation, this may not be possible, and injury can occur during blind passage (32).

Placement of multiple bars

Multiple bars are often required to achieve sufficient lift and stability and are recommended for adult repair. The use of two versus one bar has been shown to reduce pain by distributing the chest weight more evenly across the depressed area and may decrease bar migration risks (12). Evidence suggests that multi-bar configurations yield superior reshaping in severe deformities, hence better cosmetic results, without greatly increasing operative time or complexity (Figure 1A-1E) (45,54).

In a retrospective review of 361 adults (207 patients aged 30 years and older), multiple bars were placed in 99% of patients (56%: 2 bars, 44%: 3+ bars) (38). Other large-volume centers have also reported a higher incidence of multiple bar use in adult patients (15). Park et al. placed 2 or more bars in 94.4% of adults (46). Pilegaard et al. noted ≥2 bar placement in 70% patients older than 30 years (20). In Pawlak et al.’s comparison of surgical outcomes by age group, 63% of patients older than 20 years required ≥2 bars, versus 47% of patients under the age of 20 (P=0.0003) (44). Use of multiple bars is also advantageous when the defect is long or asymmetric, as this helps achieve a more symmetric elevation and lowers the risk of recurrence or residual deformity (Figure 2A-2E) (45-47).

Bar stabilization

Once a bar has been placed, it must be stabilized to prevent migration or rotation (40). Bar stabilization can be a greater challenge for adults due to the increased forces on the bar. The lateral stabilizer was first described by Nuss et al. as an adjunct to the original MIRPE procedure and decreased bar rotation in pediatric patients (14.3% to 4.6%) (55). However, the success of using lateral stabilizers in adult patients was significantly lower. Various methods have been described in the literature to help mitigate the risk of bar migration and rotation. These approaches include multipoint rib fixation, both medial and lateral, with either pericostal fixation or circumferential rib and/or sternal fixation (49,53). Multiple fixation points with FiberWire (Arthrex Inc., Florida, USA), a braided polyethylene, are a more durable alternative for adults compared to Polydioxanone (Ethicon, Inc., New Jersey, USA) or Ethibond Excel sutures (Ethicon, Inc., New Jersey, USA), which can more easily break. This suture absorbs cyclic loads and can decrease the risk of significant bar rotation (49).

Connecting the bars together laterally was introduced by Park in 2015 however the instrumentation to perform these links was not available in the U.S. until 2024. Currently, Zimmer Biomet, Inc. (Jacksonville FL), KLS Martin, Inc. (Tuttlingen, Baden-Wuerttemberg) and Primemed (Scranton, Pennsylvania) have bar bridges available, however anterior medial positioning is only possible with TruLink (Zimmer Biomet, Inc., Jacksonville FL) (Figure 2E, Figure 3A,3B). The bridge technique has been reported to be effective in decreasing bar displacement to near zero, with centers reporting no bar dislocations in any of their cases during limited follow-up (46,50).

Intercostal space reinforcement

For the MIRPE procedure to be successful, the bars must elevate the sternum and adjacent depressed ribs in all areas that are compressed into mediastinal structures (32). The more medial the entrance sites of the bars are, the greater the pressure upward against the sternum (Video 2).

Intra-operative video of a patient with pectus excavatum demonstrating medial insertion and exit sites of the dissector for planned bar insertion.

The pressure of the chest wall is distributed to the bars, which are held in place by a combination of the intercostal muscles and the bar positioning across the ribs (48). In pediatric cases, the pressure on the bars is low (48). In a rigid, large adult, this pressure will be significant. The most common cause of bar migration and procedure failure in adults is stripping or tearing out of the intercostal muscle, which allows the bar to move posterior and lateral (48). If the muscle cannot support the bar, it will tear, and the bar will migrate laterally and posteriorly within the intercostal space. The bar will no longer push anteriorly on the sternum, and the repair is lost (Video 3) (56).

Illustrative video demonstrating posterior-lateral bar migration resulting from intercostal stripping.

Reinforcement of the intercostal space can be performed with a ‘hammock’ figure-of-eight suture passed twice around the rib above and below the intercostal space that will hold the bar (Video 4). A #5 FiberWire (Arthrex, Inc., Naples, FL) suture can be used for good durability and less risk than sternal wire (49,57). The sutures are placed before the bars are inserted and tied down under the muscle. When the bar is in place, the weight of the chest wall will be distributed to the suture (49).

Thoracoscopic view of the reinforcement “hammock stitch” which is performed by placing a stitch around the rib about and rib below of the intercostal space that the bar will subsequently be inserted into.

Other techniques which can be employed to prevent muscles from stripping include placement of stabilizers medial at the bar entrance site (Figure 3C), medial linking of bars and cross bars (Figure 3D) (38,41).

Placing bars crossed helps redistribute the weight and reduce intercostal muscle stripping (41,58). In a multicenter study by Ersoz et al., the cross-bar technique had a significantly lower recurrence rate (0.5% compared to 3.3%) versus the parallel-bar technique (P<0.04) (42). Patients with parallel bars, however, noted a higher patient satisfaction rate with outcomes (mean satisfaction score of 9.40±0.95 compared to 9.13±1.11 for the crossbar technique (P<0.05). The crossbar technique was noted to be especially advantageous in patients with long-segment depression at the narrowest point and one vertebral level below it (42). Park first recommended this technique in 2016 for the correction of what he termed ‘Grand Canyon’ defects (58). Despite the advantages offered by the cross-bar technique, several limitations exist, including increased technical complexity, longer operative times, and limited data on long-term outcomes.

Adequate postoperative pain management

One criticism of MIRPE in adults is a higher level of postoperative pain. Studies indicate that adults experience a higher incidence of extended postoperative pain after MIRPE compared to pediatrics (7% vs. 1%) (11). Multiple factors contribute to the intensity and duration of postoperative pain among adults, including increased age, thoracic rigidity, severity of deformity, the number and positioning of bars placed, and the selected perioperative analgesic strategy (15). Some of the more common recent postoperative pain prevention strategies are presented below.

Multimodal drug regimen

With the current opioid epidemic, minimizing narcotic use should be a priority (11). Higher opioid requirements and prolonged postoperative pain can delay hospital discharge and are associated with increased risks of complications and readmission. A multimodal analgesic regimen can optimize pain control while minimizing opioid use (59). Blocking the N-methyl-D-aspartate receptors with ketamine or Mu-opioid receptors with methadone intraoperatively can significantly improve postoperative pain (60). Utilizing gabapentinoids can also target the affective component of pain, stimulate descending inhibition, inhibit descending serotonergic facilitation, and reduce inflammatory mediators (61). Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, acetaminophen, and muscle relaxants should also be considered postoperatively. An intraoperative intercostal nerve block with local anesthetic is recommended, and the addition of Dexamethasone may prolong analgesic effects (59,62). Despite these adjuncts, most patients will require opioids.

Epidural

Multiple studies examined outcomes associated with epidural analgesia and have found their analgesia to be improved or, at least, equivalent to some other pain modalities, particularly, patient-controlled intravenous analgesia, and oral narcotics alone (11,21). There have been reports of paralysis in the pediatric population, epidurals are no longer used for pediatric and adolescent cases at most institutions. Epidurals have been associated with longer hospitalizations, though.

SubQ catheters

Subcutaneous continuous catheter infusions have been shown to be at least equivalent to epidurals for pain control, with shorter hospital stays reported (21). Subcutaneous catheters were introduced as a solution; however, some sites noted an increased infection risk with their use (63).

Cryoablation

Intercostal nerve cryoablation has been increasingly utilized for postoperative pain. Cryoablation has been shown to significantly reduce opioid requirements and shorten the time to hospital discharge after pectus repair. A systematic review and meta-analysis of over 13,103 patients reported intercostal nerve cryoablation reduced the length of hospitalization by 1.8 days (95% CI: −2.12 to −1.42), and reduced inpatient and outpatient opioid consumption by 186 morphine mg equivalents (95% CI: −255.72 to −38.26) compared to thoracic epidural analgesia (64). In a retrospective review of 729 adult MIRPE patients (mean age 30.9 years) comparing epidural analgesia, subcutaneous catheters, and cryoablation, patients in the cryoablation group also required significantly lower doses of morphine equivalents (P<0.001) and had the shortest hospital stay (mean, 1.9±1.5 days; P<0.001). Less than 17% of cryoablation patients stayed >2 days, compared to 94% in the epidural group and 48% in the subcutaneous catheter group (P<0.001). Neuropathic pain has been a concern for the use of cryoablation; however, most studies have reported no significant difference in the rates of neuropathic pain long term (21,64,65). Zobel et al. did report an increased incidence of neuropathic pain in their older patient group (3 patients) (66). Other reported complications include one center reporting adult patients with cryoablation experienced a higher incidence of pleural effusion requiring thoracentesis (P=0.024) (21). Most centers perform cryoablation during the procedure with increased operative time. The device is costly, however, decreased hospitalization and patient benefits may prevail. At least one study has shown that the total cost of the pectus repair utilizing cryoablation is equivalent or less when total hospitalization costs are considered (64).

Timing of bar removal

The intrathoracic bars are recommended for temporary implants as erosion into the sternum and skeletal chest wall can occur over time. There is also the consideration, especially in aging patients, for reduced efficacy of chest compressions should emergent CPR be required (21). The optimal timing for adults with the implants is unknown, many centers recommend 3 or more years before implant removal (our paper, any other adult papers with lengths reported at least 3 years) without direct evidence to support. There are no long-term studies evaluating the optimal duration for bars in adults. Pediatric literature reports increased regression rates of around 2.6% when bars are removed early (67). Many large pediatric centers recommend that the implants remain in place at least 3 years (22,33,38,68). Infante et al. reported that keeping bars for >3 years did not increase adverse events or negatively impact QoL compared to shorter durations. Patients with underlying connective tissue disorders, who may experience delayed or impaired remodeling due to altered collagen structure, should be considered for an extended bar duration (69).

Outcomes and benefits

Increased experience with complex repairs and advancements in surgical techniques has improved outcomes in adult PE repair. Recent literature reports shorter operative times, lower complication rates, and enhanced patient-centered outcomes (12,13). QoL is also reported to improve in adults who have undergone MIRPE (8). One retrospective study showed statistically significant improvements in all dimensions of health-related QoL as assessed by the Short Form-36 (SF-36) questionnaire (70). Another prospective observational study reported that over 90% of patients described their QoL as better or much better at 3, 12, and 36 months postoperatively, with cosmetic results satisfying more than 90% of patients (19).

The data for improved cardiopulmonary capacity following MIRPE in adults has been variable. Several large patient studies report significant improvements in multiple cardiac and pulmonary parameters (6,7,38). This contrasts with a few small patient series reporting no significant improvements (71,72). It is critical that all cardiac compression has been relieved after surgical repair. Centers reporting no postoperative improvements while performing a limited number of cases and utilizing single bar repairs call into question the adequacy of the pectus repair, especially in the adult patient (7). Complications

Surgeon experience significantly influences outcomes, especially in challenging adult cases (28,56). In pediatric cases, an annual institutional case-volume of 73 cases per year is recommended for optimal outcomes and the more difficult adult patient repairs should at a minimum mirror this (28). There are limited publications comparing complications in adults and pediatric patients undergoing PE repair and most reports are from centers that perform predominantly pediatric surgery. Several adult-only publications from centers with experience operating on predominantly older patients report notably lower complication rates versus other centers. Within stratification of the adult population, there is clear evidence that older patients (≥30 years) increase risks of complications, including bar rotation and fracture. Conclusions

PE repair in adults presents distinct technical challenges compared to pediatric cases due to increased chest wall rigidity and greater patient size and weight. Modifications to the MIRPE have allowed it to be safely and successfully expanded into surgical repair of the adult. Cryoablation, especially when integrated into multimodal pain protocols, has improved postoperative outcomes with less need for opioids and shorter hospitalization. Postoperative symptom relief, cardiopulmonary improvement, and a better QoL can be expected when complete anatomical correction is achieved. Future investigations should focus on prospective, adult-specific cohorts to better define optimal patient selection, durability of anatomic and physiological benefits, and long-term outcomes following bar removal. Although a few comparative studies evaluating pain management strategies exist, more adult-focused studies are needed to further refine postoperative care and optimize recovery in this population.
