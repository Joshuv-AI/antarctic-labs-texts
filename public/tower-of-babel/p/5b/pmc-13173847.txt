Surgical management of 32 primary giant mediastinal neoplasms: a retrospective single-center analysis
Authors: Qihang Zhu, Zhe He, Jing Zhan, Xiaojing Yao, Shuang Zhao, Kai Chen, Qingrong Ma, Wei Liang, Guibin Qiao, Haiping Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrimary giant mediastinal neoplasms are extremely uncommon. Large-sample studies are rare yet urgently needed.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis of consecutive cases who underwent excision surgery for giant mediastinal neoplasms between January 2019 and December 2023. Demographic, surgical, and prognosis data were retrospective collected and analyzed.ResultsThirty-two cases were analyzed. The most prevalent location was prevascular compartment (21 cases, 65.6%), followed by visceral compartment (9 cases, 28.1%), and paravertebral compartment (2 cases, 6.3%). Eighteen cases underwent preoperative biopsy, of which 15 consistent with postoperative outcomes. Seven cases underwent preoperative embolization. Median thoracotomy was the most common surgical approach (23 cases, 71.9%). The median operation time was 214 min (interquartile range, IQR: 149–252), with a median blood loss of 500 ml (IQR: 200–800). Thymic tumors were the most prevalent (13 cases, 40.6%), followed by mesenchymal (9 cases, 28.1%), germ-cell (5 cases, 15.6%), pulmonary malignant neoplasms (2 cases, 6.2%), and other rare types (3 cases, 9.4%). The pathological classification demonstrated a significant association with prognosis (p = 0.006), with pulmonary malignancies exhibiting the worst outcomes. No significant overall survival difference was observed in malignant tumor patients with versus without local organ invasion (p = 0.896). R2 resection correlated with significantly poorer survival compared to R0 (p = 0.001).ConclusionsWith varied pathological types and complex surgical procedures, giant mediastinal tumors demand multidisciplinary treatment in experienced centers. Complete surgical resection is key to a good prognosis.Keywords: Giant mediastinal tumors, Preoperative embolization, Surgery, Multidisciplinary treatment

Introduction

Mediastinal neoplasms represent an uncommon disease. According to several studies involving large sample of healthy individuals who underwent chest computed tomography (CT) screening, the incidence of mediastinal tumors ranging from 0.73% to 0.90% [1–3]. Giant mediastinal tumors primarily depend on their location, dimensions, and extent of invasion, as there is no universally accepted definition. In general, the term “Giant” in the context of mediastinal tumors refers to a size of at least 10 cm in the greatest dimension. There is currently a lack of precise data regarding the morbidity rate, but this clinical condition is considered to be extremely rare.

The sources of organization in mediastinum are diverse and plentiful. Therefore, pathological and imaging manifestations are intricate. The most common primary pathological types include thymoma, benign cysts, lymphoma, neurogenic tumors, and others [4]. Meanwhile, the space of mediastinum is narrow and contains a number of vital organs such as the aorta, main veins, heart, large airways, esophagus, and nerves. Within the relatively restricted anatomical region that is densely populated with critical anatomical structures, giant mediastinal tumors inevitably compress and may even invade these anatomical structures. Surgical resection is the important tool to cure mediastinal tumors. However, both complete resection and organ protection are essential, which renders the surgical procedure exceptionally challenging and risky. This study aimed to investigate the clinical characteristics, surgical treatment approaches, perioperative management strategies, pathological features, and prognosis of giant mediastinal tumors through a retrospective analysis of 32 consecutive primary giant mediastinal neoplasms that underwent surgery in a single institution.

Methods

Participants and data collection

Two thoracic surgeons (Zhu Q and He Z) retrospectively reviewed patients with giant mediastinal neoplasms between January 2019 and December 2023 at Department of Cardiothoracic Surgery, The First Affiliated Hospital of Guangdong Pharmaceutical University. We divided the mediastinum into three compartments according to the International Thymic Malignancy Interest Group (ITMIG) classification system, namely prevascular, visceral, and paravertebral compartments [5]. When a tumor extends across the prevascular, visceral, or paravertebral mediastinum, it should be classified as a certain mediastinal lesion if the centroid of its largest cross-sectional area is located within the certain mediastinal region. Subsequently, divide the left and right sections based on the midline of the sternum. The inclusion criteria were defined as follows: (1) mediastinal neoplasms confirmed by enhanced CT scan, (2) the maximum diameter of the tumor is greater than or equal to 10 cm, (3) surgical treatment with the aim of complete resection, (4) no neoadjuvant therapy received. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) the maximum diameter is less than 10 cm, (2) metastatic tumors. Upon completion of the initial screening by the two physicians, their findings will be independently verified by each other. In the event of any controversial or inconclusively confirmed cases, these will be re-evaluated and finalized by the two corresponding authors (Qiao G and Xiao h).

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians, as applicable. The Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Guangdong Pharmaceutical University approved this study, which was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, 1975.

We analyzed patient demographics; clinical and radiological findings, such as chief complaint, course duration, tumor location; surgical findings, such as surgical approaches, operation types, extended resections, intraoperative critical incidents, blood loss, operation time, R0 or R2 resection; postoperative events, such as complications and prognosis. R0 resection was defined as the absence of any visible residue, while R2 resection was defined as the presence of visible residue. Postoperative pathological examination of surgical margins did not identify any cases of microscopic residual tumor (R1), so R1 resection was not included in the analysis.

Perioperative management

Enhanced chest CT scan is the essential tool to evaluate the tumors. Systematic examinations of the abdominal cavity, head, and bones were conducted using appropriate methods to rule out the possibility of metastasis. Cardiac and pulmonary function tests were conducted to assess the patient’s tolerance to surgical procedures. Preoperative biopsy was not mandatory. A multidisciplinary team, comprising experts from the thoracic department, oncology department, vascular interventional department, and anesthesiology department, collectively determined the treatment plan prior to initiating therapy. The tumors with rich vascular supply were embolized by the interventional department. The definition of ‘rich vascular supply’ was based on enhanced chest CT and CTA evaluations, including obvious tumor enhancement, identifiable feeding arteries, and high vascular density. Preoperative embolization will be performed by vascular interventional department when tumors meeting these criteria, and surgery was performed within 48 h after embolization. The operations were performed by a fixed team. The majority of patients were extubated in the anesthesia recovery room following surgical procedures and returned to the thoracic department. If patients who cannot be extubated immediately following assessment are transferred to the ICU for further treatment.

Follow‑up

Whether to accept adjuvant therapy is made based on a comprehensive evaluation of the patient’s pathological characteristics, the extent of surgical resection, and the recommendations from the oncology department. In general, patients underwent their first imaging follow-up three months postoperatively and subsequently underwent follow-ups every six months until five years postoperatively. Radiologically suspected recurrence did not require histological confirmation. The primary modality for follow-up is enhanced CT. Positron emission tomography combined with CT (PET/CT) may be considered in certain patients when necessary. The overall survival (OS) was calculated from the date of surgery to the date of death or the last follow-up, with the final follow-up date being January 2025.

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables were first tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Due to the small sample size and non-normal distribution of all continuous variables (all p < 0.05), they are reported as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs); categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. OS was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared between groups using the log-rank test. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), while statistical significance was considered when p < 0.05.

Results

From January 2019 to December 2023, a total of 35 cases of giant mediastinal tumors were resected. Among these, one case was confirmed as a giant coronary arteriovenous fistula. Additionally, informed consent could not be obtained in two cases. Consequently, 32 cases were included in the final analysis for this study.

The second common location was visceral compartment (9 cases, 28.1%), including 6 cases on left and 3 case on right. The demographic data of the patients are presented in Table 1. The median age at operation was 51-year-old (IQR 31–64). Twenty-one cases (65.6%) were male, and eleven cases were female. Twenty-two cases (68.8%) were current or former smokers. The primary symptom reported by 11 patients (34.4%) was chest tightness, accompanied by shortness of breath and dyspnea. Nine patients (28.1%) were reported as asymptomatic. 6 patients (18.8%) were reported as cough and pectoralgia. 2 cases (6.2%) were reported as dysphagia, and another 2 cases (6.4%) reported symptoms related to myasthenia. The other 2 cases (6.4%) reported superior vena cava syndrome (SCVC). The majority of patients (13 cases, 40.6%) received surgery within 1–3 months following the onset of symptoms. 11 cases (34.4%) received surgery within 1 month. The rest were over three months. Preoperative biopsies were performed in 18 cases (56.3%), with 16 cases undergoing transthoracic needle core biopsies and the remaining 2 cases undergoing electronic bronchoscopy procedures. The results of the puncture biopsy and postoperative pathology were consistent in 15 cases. Necrotic tissue was identified in one case, while postoperative pathological diagnoses revealed two inconsistent cases, which were classified as mixed germ cell tumor and pulmonary blastoma, respectively.

The others were left/right thoracotomy, T-shaped, and L-shaped incision. The median operation time was 214 min (IQR, 149–252 min). The median blood loss was 500 ml (IQR, 200–800 ml). Six patients underwent R2 resection due to residual tumor involving the aortic capsule or superior vena cava inlet. A patient may have undergone the resection of one or more affected tissues as part of a surgical intervention. The median follow-up time was 29.4 months (IQR, 21.5–45.1 months). The median survival time was not reached. As well as the one and two-year survival rates were 81.3%, and 74.6%, respectively. We conducted survival analysis by pathology group (Table 4). Overall comparison showed that pathology group was closely correlated with prognosis (p = 0.006, Fig. 4a). Pairwise comparison showed a worse prognosis in patients with lung malignant tumors. The difference between thymic tumors, mesenchymal tumors, germ cell tumors and other rare tumor types was not significant. For malignant tumors, comparative analysis demonstrated no statistically significant difference in overall survival between patients with local organ invasion and those without (p = 0.896; Fig. 4b). However, the resection status was significantly closely associated with prognosis. Patients who underwent R2 resection had a worse outcome compared to those who underwent R0 resection (p = 0.001, Fig. 4c).

Discussion

Due to the rare morbidity, the incidence of giant mediastinal tumors cannot be accurately assessed. The majority of previous literature were case reports [6–15]. To our knowledge, only two prior studies have reported on the prognosis of relatively large sample sizes following surgical intervention [16–17]. Giant mediastinal tumors exhibit a wide range of different histologies [18]. The radiological manifestations, and pathological diagnosis vary from case to case. Despite these tumors having variable clinical presentations, complete resection is considered the first-line treatment. The internal structure of mediastinum presents a complex configuration characterized by narrow interstices, and tumors frequently exert compressive forces or invasive growth into critical tissues. Our analysis of 32 resected cases demonstrated that mediastinal organ invasion was not a significant predictor of prognosis in univariate analysis. Furthermore, R0 resection status consistently correlated with improved outcomes.

The primary chief complaint associated with giant mediastinal tumors ‌is‌ respiratory system-related symptoms, including chest tightness, shortness of breath, and dyspnea. A certain proportion of patients ‌are‌ asymptomatic and ‌detected‌ through imaging examinations, which ‌is‌ attributed to the fact that most giant mediastinal tumors ‌exhibit‌ primarily expansile and slow growth. This implies that the prevalence of giant mediastinal tumors might be underreported, particularly in developing countries where limited awareness of healthy examinations contributes to undiagnosed cases. Our findings indicate that the pathological results of preoperative core needle biopsy were largely consistent with the histological examination results of surgical specimens and can serve as a component of preoperative assessment. However, several problems still exist. Firstly, there remain some negative or inconclusive pathological results. The potential cause may be attributed to the insufficient biopsy experience. For example, the failure to avoid regions with extensive necrosis during the puncture procedure may have compromised to obtain viable tumor tissue. Furthermore, the pathological complexity of certain tumors may lead to diagnostic inaccuracies in small specimens. Secondly, preoperative biopsies incur time and financial costs, potentially delaying treatment or increasing economic burdens. Thirdly, there remains a possibility for needle path transfer theoretically. In fact, clinical characteristics and radiological features can serve as components of a clinical decision support system in the selection of the initial management strategy [19]. The enhanced CT serves as a critical modality for assessing the location, extent of invasion, and growth characteristics of giant mediastinal tumors [20]. Preoperative biopsy may be omitted for tumors which enhanced CT indicates resectable, or for those tumors whose pathology can be preliminarily inferred based on imaging findings, or in cases presenting with emergency symptoms (such as airway compression with dyspnea) to prevent delayed treatment. Nevertheless, preoperative biopsy remains essential in cases where enhanced CT indicates unresectable tumors (such as invasion of the aortic arch or heart), the pathological type is unclear and requires confirmation to guide neoadjuvant therapy (for example, suspected lymphoma or germ cell tumor), or there is a high suspicion of a malignant tumor that may benefit from preoperative medical treatment.

A multidisciplinary team (MDT) serves as a crucial approach during the entire treatment process. For tumors with abundant blood supply, preoperative embolization by vascular intervention team is an important tool to reduce perioperative hemorrhage [21–23]. The peripheral space of the tumor becomes more clearly following embolization, which facilitates resection. Furthermore, the size of tumors may be reduced after embolization [22, 24]. High-resolution CT and CT angiography (CTA) are instrumental in evaluating tumor vascularity and preliminarily mapping feeding vasculature [17, 21]. As for primary giant mediastinal neoplasms, the blood supply may originate from diverse sources. The internal thoracic artery and bronchial arteries are important sources. Prevascular mediastinal tumors predominantly derive vascular supply from the internal thoracic artery, while visceral mediastinal lesions are typically perfused by bronchial, thyroid, or esophageal arteries, and paravertebral mediastinal tumors primarily receive blood supply from intercostal arteries. Some tumors may have multiple sources of blood supply. A 26-year-old female patient with Castleman disease underwent two consecutive embolization procedures, targeting the internal thoracic artery and bronchial arteries. Surgical procedures were performed within 24 h following embolization. Preoperative embolization demonstrates safety in our experience and literature review, with no major complications documented [21, 25].

Regardless of the pathology, all giant mediastinal tumors have the potential to cause compression or invasion of critical structures, leading to respiratory insufficiency or hemodynamic instability [26–27]. Effective and timely communication with anesthesiologists throughout the perioperative period is essential. The potential risks associated with anesthesia should be thoroughly evaluated by anesthesia team. Single-lumen endotracheal intubation is enough for the majority of patients undergoing intended median sternotomy. For patients with an intended T-shaped incision who may require lobectomy based on preoperative assessment, double-lumen endotracheal intubation is necessary. In extreme cases, airway compression becomes significantly pronounced, potentially necessitating the implementation of more advanced life support measures, such as extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) [28–29]. The insertion of central venous catheters and invasive blood pressure monitoring should be performed prior to surgery. It should be noted that if the superior vena cava or innominate veins are invaded, it is imperative to avoid the neck or subclavian puncture approaches and opt for the femoral vein puncture approach instead. The patient’s hemodynamics are at risk of sudden deterioration during the procedures. The sudden removal of a large tumor can lead to the release of pressure on important organs, or the stimulation of nerves in the mediastinum due to surgery operation, potentially leading to arrhythmias, including ventricular fibrillation and cardiac arrest. Timely and effective interventions, including pharmacological management by anesthesiologists and prompt cardiopulmonary resuscitation by surgeons, can restore cardiac rhythm in a timely manner without poor prognosis. According to our experience, timely postoperative extubation facilitates the early recovery of patients. In our cases, patients who can extubate after operation can get out of bed the next day after operation. But for patients with airway compression or intraoperative accidents, short-term ICU monitoring is also the right choice.

For surgical teams, incision selection constitutes a paramount preoperative consideration in giant mediastinal tumor management. Median sternotomy remains the gold-standard approach for most cases. When confronting tumors demonstrating significant lateralization with unilateral thoracic cavity extension and mediastinal tissue invasion, A T-shaped incision that combines median sternotomy with anterolateral thoracotomy should be considered. Posterolateral thoracotomy represents the preferred alternative for dorsally positioned unilateral tumors adjacent to the pericardium. Critical to achieving R0 resection is the en bloc removal of involved anatomical structures, ensuring complete tumor eradication with histologically negative margins. Based on the findings of our case series, the lungs represent the most commonly involved organ, with wedge resection constituting the predominant concurrent procedure, followed by pericardial resection. Either the left or right innominate vein can be safely ligated without significant risk of postoperative cerebral edema. Due to its course across the anterior mediastinum, the left innominate vein is frequently involved by tumor invasion. The superior vena cava (SVC) must remain patent; if compromised by tumor invasion, SVC reconstruction is mandatory. Previous studies indicate artificial blood vessels are predominantly used for SVC reconstruction [30]. Currently, autologous pericardial patch has been proven as reliable material for the reconstruction of the superior vena cava [31]. We utilized autologous pericardial tissue for the reconstruction of the superior vena cava, achieving a satisfactory recent patency rate. Extended resections may involve multivisceral procedures, including gastrointestinal and chest wall resection-reconstruction. Our survival data analyses demonstrate that anatomically complete resection provides statistically superior prognosis compared to subtotal resection, even in locally invasive disease. This evidence underscores the critical importance of achieving radical resection margins for resectable involved structures.

Thymic tumors remain the most common pathological type of giant mediastinal tumors, followed by mesenchymal tumors. The pathological sources are diverse, including benign, low-grade malignant and malignant pathological types. Surgical resection constitutes the most critical component of treatment in our cases. However, the adjuvant therapy remains indispensable. Due to heterogeneous pathological types and varied adjuvant treatment regimens among patients, analysis of subsequent therapies was precluded in this study.

Our study has certain limitations. As a retrospective study, the results generated are dependent on the reliability of data collection and the study sample size. Two authors, each with over a decade of experience in thoracic surgery, conducted data collection separately and cross-reviewed each other’s work. Two corresponding authors, each with over 15 years of experience in thoracic surgery, as principal decision-makers, jointly determined the inclusion or exclusion of patients in order to minimize data loss and ensure consistency. In addition, subgroup stratification by histopathological classification or disease stage was precluded by insufficient cohort size and the rarity of distinct pathological entities. Similarly, due to the small sample size and limited outcome events, multivariate analysis was not performed, and the identified prognostic factors (pathological type, resection status) are based on univariate analysis. Future multi-center studies with larger sample sizes are needed to verify independent prognostic factors. Despite these limitations, this study provides valuable single-center data on 32 cases of giant mediastinal tumors (a relatively large sample for this rare disease), detailing preoperative embolization criteria, extended resection experience, and prognostic factors. These findings supplement the existing literature and provide clinical references for the management of giant mediastinal tumors.

Conclusion

Giant mediastinal tumors have diverse pathological types and complex surgical procedures, requiring multidisciplinary treatment in experienced centers. A targeted surgical plan needs to be developed based on a comprehensive consideration of imaging features, pathological types, and clinical characteristics. For all resectable tumors, thorough surgical resection remains the primary method of curative treatment.
