HAMA microneedles patch loaded with muscle-targeting engineered apoptotic vesicles and attenuating sarcopenia by regulating mitochondrial homeostasis
Authors: Jiayao Zhang, Zhao Lin, Haiqi Ding, Mingjuan Zhang, Yibin Su, Linhai Yang, Dingqiang Chen, Xuanhao Chen, Jun Luo, Fuli Wen, Guoyu Yu, Zineng Yan, Jie Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSarcopenia is an age-related disorder characterized by the progressive loss of skeletal muscle mass and function, primarily driven by mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and impaired satellite cell activity. Conventional therapeutic strategies have shown limited efficacy in restoring muscle performance. Apoptotic vesicles (ApoVs) derived from mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) have recently emerged as promising therapeutic agents capable of delivering bioactive cargos to regulate cellular metabolism and attenuate tissue aging. However, the targeted delivery and functional stability of ApoVs remain major challenges.ResultsWe engineered human umbilical cord MSC-derived ApoVs with the muscle-specific aptamer A01B and encapsulated them in a methacrylated hyaluronic acid (HAMA) hydrogel microneedle patch to enhance tissue targeting and enable sustained release. In vitro, ApoVs were efficiently internalized by C2C12 myotubes and alleviated dexamethasone-induced atrophy by promoting proliferation, reducing oxidative stress, restoring mitochondrial structure and function, and modulating the PTEN–PI3K–AKT signaling pathway. miRNA profiling identified miR-21-5p as a key ApoV-delivered effector that activates this pathway. In vivo, treatment with the aptamer-functionalized ApoV microneedle system significantly improved muscle mass, myofiber cross-sectional area, mitochondrial oxidative capacity, and functional endurance in sarcopenic mice compared with controls.ConclusionsOur findings demonstrate that the muscle-targeted delivery of engineered ApoVs via HAMA microneedles effectively reverses key hallmarks of sarcopenia by restoring mitochondrial homeostasis and activating PI3K/AKT signaling through miR-21-5p. This microneedle-based ApoV platform provides a promising cell-free therapeutic strategy for ameliorating muscle atrophy and functional decline.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12951-026-04813-4.

Introduction

Sarcopenia is an age-related, progressive degenerative disorder characterized by the loss of skeletal muscle mass and function, and it has become a major public health challenge in aging societies worldwide. According to the World Health Organization, the prevalence of sarcopenia is approximately 10–30% in individuals aged ≥ 65 years and may reach up to 50% in those aged ≥ 80 years [1, 2]. Sarcopenia is closely associated with marked functional decline, reduced quality of life, and increased mortality risk, posing a substantial threat to health and independent living in older adults [3, 4].

Cellular senescence is tightly linked to oxidative stress [5, 6]. During aging, the balance between the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and antioxidant defenses is disrupted, resulting in redox dyshomeostasis. ROS are highly reactive and can induce oxidative damage to biomacromolecules, including proteins, lipids, and DNA. Mitochondria are a major source of ROS, and the electron transport chain (ETC) generates ATP during oxidative phosphorylation. However, a fraction of electrons can leak from the ETC and react with oxygen to form ROS such as superoxide (O2−). With aging, mitochondrial dysfunction leads to reduced ATP synthesis and decreased ETC efficiency, thereby increasing electron leakage and ROS generation [7–9]. Persistent ROS accumulation causes oxidative injury in muscle cells, promotes structural disruption and functional impairment of muscle fibers, and ultimately contributes to the decline in skeletal muscle mass and strength.

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) are adult stem cells with multilineage differentiation potential and can give rise to osteogenic, chondrogenic, and adipogenic lineages [10]. They are widely distributed in tissues such as bone marrow, adipose tissue, umbilical cord, and dental pulp [11, 12]. Compared with other sources, umbilical cord tissue is readily accessible, cost-effective, and enables relatively gentle isolation of MSCs [13]. Moreover, umbilical cord-derived MSCs secrete multiple tolerance-related factors, including transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1) and interleukin-10 (IL-10), and exhibit potent immunomodulatory properties, with a low propensity to trigger T cell-mediated immune responses in vitro, thereby offering strong translational promise [14]. MSC transplantation has shown therapeutic potential in injuries and degenerative diseases of bone, cartilage, heart, and the nervous system, potentially through cell replacement and paracrine regulation. However, accumulating evidence indicates that only a small fraction of transplanted MSCs survive and integrate in vivo; thus, apoptosis-associated products may contribute substantially to the therapeutic outcomes [15]. Apoptosis is a key process for maintaining tissue homeostasis [16]. As an important class of terminal products generated during apoptosis, apoptotic vesicles (ApoVs) can carry diverse functional cargos, including nucleic acids, proteins, and even organelles, and play critical roles in immune modulation and tissue repair [17–19].

Targeted delivery to specific organs or tissues can enhance the accumulation of bioactive agents at diseased sites while minimizing systemic adverse effects. Although naturally derived bioactive vesicles are enriched with membrane proteins and other surface components, they often lack tissue-specific homing capability. Because vesicle surface molecules govern recognition, binding, and uptake by target cells, surface engineering can reprogram their biodistribution and targeting properties [20]. For instance, displaying targeting ligands such as antibodies, peptides, carbohydrates, or receptor ligands on vesicle surfaces can improve affinity and specificity toward desired cells/tissues [21–23]. Nucleic acid aptamers are short oligonucleotides obtained via in vitro selection and can bind target molecules with high affinity and specificity. They provide efficient and programmable tools for molecular recognition and targeted delivery, with broad prospects in biomedical applications [24].

After administration, biological vesicles often face several challenges, including rapid clearance, insufficient tissue retention, and limited targeted delivery efficiency. To overcome these limitations, various biomaterial-assisted delivery platforms have been developed, including hydrogels, microneedle patches, and their composite systems [25, 26]. These platforms can protect the bioactivity of vesicles, enhance their local tissue retention, and enable controlled or sustained release. Hydrogel microneedles combine the favorable biocompatibility of hydrogels with the minimally invasive delivery capability of microneedles. Owing to their relatively simple fabrication process, low cost, high drug-loading capacity, and good patient compliance, hydrogel microneedles have become an important research direction in the field of transdermal delivery. To date, hydrogel microneedles have been widely explored for various biomedical applications, including tumor therapy [27], wound repair [28], disease diagnosis and dynamic monitoring [29], and vaccine delivery [30], demonstrating substantial potential for biomedical applications.

This study is the first to investigate the key role of mesenchymal stem cell-derived apoptotic vesicles in alleviating muscle atrophy and to develop a composite delivery system based on degradable HAMA hydrogel microneedles (Fig. 1). Our results demonstrate that HUMSCs-derived ApoVs exert therapeutic effects by delivering bioactive components enriched in miR-21-5p. Mechanistically, these ApoVs regulate the PTEN–PI3K/Akt signaling pathway, thereby improving mitochondrial function and the cellular senescence phenotype in DEX-induced atrophic C2C12 myotubes. Ultimately, this approach promotes the recovery of muscle mass and function, providing a novel strategy for the treatment of muscle atrophy.

Methods

C2C12 myoblast culture and differentiation

Mouse C2C12 myoblasts were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. Cells were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution (100 IU/mL penicillin and 0.1 mg/mL streptomycin). Cells were routinely passaged in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂.

For myotube differentiation induction, when C2C12 cells reached 70–80% confluence, the growth medium was replaced with differentiation medium consisting of high-glucose DMEM (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 2% horse serum (Horse Serum, Cat. No. 16050122, ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution. The differentiation medium was refreshed every 48 h, and cells were induced for 6 days to form mature multinucleated myotubes. Cell fusion and myotube formation were observed and documented using an inverted optical microscope (DM IL LED, Leica, Germany). Differentiated C2C12 myotubes were subsequently used for biochemical analyses.

In vitro sarcopenia model of myotubes

In this study, dexamethasone (DEX)-induced myotube atrophy was employed as an in vitro model of sarcopenia. Following the differentiation of C2C12 cells into myotubes, the differentiation medium was replaced with fresh differentiation medium containing 50 µM DEX (Solarbio, Beijing, China), and cells were cultured for an additional 24 h to induce myotube atrophy. Morphological changes of myotubes were observed and documented using an inverted optical microscope (DM IL LED, Leica, Germany).

Culture of human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells

Human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (HUMSCs) were obtained from the National Stem Cell Translational Resource Center. HUMSCs were cultured in α-minimum essential medium (α-MEM; Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. Upon reaching 80% confluence, cells were passaged at a 1:3 ratio. Cells from passages 3 to 5 were used in all experiments to ensure optimal cell viability and stability.

Isolation and characterization of HuMSC-derived apoptotic vesicles

HUMSCs at optimal growth conditions were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and the culture medium was replaced with α-MEM containing 10% exosome-depleted FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin supplemented with 250 nM staurosporine (STS; Beyotime Biotechnology, China) to induce apoptosis. HuMSC-derived apoptotic vesicles (ApoVs) were isolated using an optimized protocol based on a previous report [31, 32]. Briefly, culture supernatants were collected 12 h after apoptosis induction and centrifuged at 800×g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove cellular debris. The supernatants were then centrifuged at 12,000×g for 30 min at 4 °C to pellet ApoVs. The pellets were resuspended in PBS and washed twice by centrifugation at 12,000×g for 30 min at 4 °C. Finally, the ApoVs-enriched components were resuspended in 200 µL of PBS and stored at -80 °C until use. All procedures were performed at 4 °C to maintain sample integrity.

The protein concentration of ApoVs was quantified using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China). The morphology of ApoVs was examined by transmission electron microscopy (TEM; HT7800, Hitachi, Japan), and their size distribution was determined by dynamic light scattering (DLS; Zetasizer Nano S90, Malvern Panalytical, UK). Western blot analysis was performed to assess the expression of specific apoV markers (see Supplementary Materials for details). Additionally, compositional analysis of ApoVs was conducted by RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) (see Supplementary Materials for details).

Cellular uptake of ApoVs

To track the cellular uptake of ApoVs, the vesicles were labeled with DiI fluorescent dye (C1036, Beyotime Biotechnology, China). ApoVs were incubated with DiI working solution for 30 min at room temperature in the dark, followed by centrifugation at 12,000×g for 30 min at 4 °C to remove unbound dye. After washing twice with PBS, the labeled ApoVs were resuspended in PBS. DiI-labeled ApoVs were co-incubated with C2C12 cells for 24 h at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ in the dark.

After incubation, cells were washed three times with PBS to remove non-internalized ApoVs and then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 30 min at room temperature. The cytoskeleton was stained with Tubulin-Tracker Green (C1051S, Beyotime Biotechnology, China), and nuclei were counterstained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; 0100 − 20, SouthernBiotech, USA). Fluorescence images were acquired using a laser scanning confocal microscope (SP8, Leica, Germany) to analyze ApoVs internalization.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; CK04, Dojindo, Japan). C2C12 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 10³ cells/well with 100 µL of complete culture medium per well and cultured in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 24 h to allow cell attachment. Subsequently, cells were treated with different concentrations of DEX and ApoVs and cultured for an additional 24 h.

After incubation, the culture medium was discarded, and 100 µL of fresh medium and CCK-8 reagent were added to each well. Cells were incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 2 h. Absorbance values (OD values) were measured at 450 nm using a multimode microplate reader (SpectraMax iD5, Molecular Devices, USA).

Detection of cell proliferation by EdU assay

C2C12 cells were seeded in 24-well plates at an appropriate density. After cell attachment, cells were treated with complete medium containing dexamethasone (DEX) or complete medium containing DEX and ApoVs, and cultured for 24 and 48 h at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. According to the manufacturer’s instructions of the EdU Cell Proliferation Assay Kit (C10310, RiboBio, China), the culture medium was replaced with 100 µL of medium containing 50 µM EdU, and cells were incubated for an additional 2 h. Cells were then fixed and permeabilized. Subsequently, cells were stained with Apollo staining solution for 10 min at room temperature, and nuclei were stained with DAPI. Finally, cells were observed under the fluorescence microscope.

Apoptosis detection

To detect DEX-induced apoptosis and the effect of ApoVs on apoptosis, cells were seeded in 6-well plates with four replicates and incubated for 24 h. Experimental cells were digested and collected, and stained using the Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China). Apoptosis levels were detected and analyzed by flow cytometry (BD Accuri C6 Plus). Data analysis was performed using FlowJo IM 10 software (v10.8.1).

Cell cycle analysis

Cell cycle analysis was performed using a Cell Cycle Detection Kit (CX007S, CellorLab, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After different treatments, cells were digested with trypsin and collected, washed once with PBS, and centrifuged to remove the supernatant. Cells were resuspended in 70% ethanol pre-cooled at − 20 °C and fixed at − 20 °C for at least 2 h. After fixation, cells were washed twice with PBS, centrifuged to remove the supernatant, and incubated with propidium iodide (PI)/RNase staining solution for 60 min at room temperature in the dark. Flow cytometry analysis was performed using a flow cytometer (Accuri C6 Plus, BD, USA) to determine the percentage of cells in G0/G1, S, and G2/M phases. Data were analyzed using FlowJo software (v10.8.1).

Senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) staining

Senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) staining was performed using the SA-β-gal Staining Kit (C0602, Beyotime Biotechnology, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, adherent cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. After washing with PBS, β-galactosidase staining working solution was added, and cells were incubated overnight (12–16 h) at 37 °C in the dark. After staining, images were observed and captured under a light microscope (DM IL LED, Leica, Germany). At least three fields were randomly selected from each group, and the percentage of SA-β-gal-positive cells relative to the total number of cells was counted, with at least 20 cells counted per field, to evaluate the degree of cellular senescence.

ATP assay

C2C12 cells were seeded in 6-well plates with 2 mL of culture medium and divided into CON group (PBS), DEX group (50 µM dexamethasone + PBS), and DG group (50 µM dexamethasone + ApoVs). The concentration of dexamethasone used for C2C12 cells in this study was based on previous research. ATP concentration in cells was determined using a commercial kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China). Absorbance values were measured using a microplate reader (SpectraMax iD5, MD, USA).

Determination of mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm)

The effect of dexamethasone and ApoVs on mitochondrial membrane potential was assessed using the Mitochondrial Membrane Potential Assay Kit (JC-1 method) (HY-K0601, MCE, USA). Briefly, cells from different treatment groups were incubated with JC-1 staining working solution at 37 °C for 20 min in the dark. After incubation, cells were washed twice with PBS and observed under a fluorescence microscope for image acquisition. When mitochondrial membrane potential is normal, JC-1 aggregates in mitochondria and produces red fluorescence. When mitochondrial membrane potential decreases, JC-1 exists as monomers in the cytoplasm and produces green fluorescence. Fluorescence intensity was analyzed using ImageJ software, and mitochondrial membrane potential changes were expressed as the red/green fluorescence intensity ratio.

Determination of reactive oxygen species

Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels after dexamethasone and ApoVs treatment were detected using the ROS Assay Kit (S0033S, Beyotime Biotechnology, China). Briefly, C2C12 cells from different treatment groups were incubated with 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) staining working solution at 37 °C for 20 min in the dark. After incubation, cells were washed three times with serum-free medium to remove extracellular DCFH-DA, and images were captured under a fluorescence microscope. Green fluorescence intensity was analyzed using ImageJ software to quantify ROS levels.

Assessment of oxidative stress

The malondialdehyde (MDA) content in cell lysates was determined using the Lipid Peroxidation MDA Assay Kit (S0131S, Beyotime Biotechnology, China). The cell samples were lysed, and the resulting lysates were used to measure MDA content according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance values were measured using a microplate reader, and results were calculated according to standard curves.

Experimental animals

All animal experimental protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Fujian Provincial Hospital Affiliated to Fuzhou University (Approval No.: IACUC-FPII-SL-20250208 [0608]). Animal care and experimental procedures were conducted in strict accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Seven-week-old male C57BL/6J mice were purchased from the Laboratory Animal Center of Fujian Provincial Hospital Affiliated to Fuzhou University. All mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility under controlled conditions with a temperature of 22–25 °C, relative humidity of 50–60%, and a 12-hour light/dark cycle, with free access to food and water.

Construction of muscle atrophy mouse model and treatment

The dexamethasone-induced muscle atrophy mouse model was employed in this study. Dexamethasone is widely used to establish muscle atrophy mouse models, which exhibit pathological features consistent with aging-induced sarcopenia, both characterized by selective reduction of type II muscle fibers. Mice were subjected to daily intraperitoneal injections of dexamethasone (20 mg/kg body weight) for model establishment.

Seven-week-old C57BL/6J male mice were selected, acclimatized for 7 days, and randomly divided into 4 groups (n = 6 per group): the control group (CON group, normal mice + PBS), the dexamethasone group (DEX group, atrophic mice), the dexamethasone + human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cell-derived apoptotic vesicle group (DEX+aptamer-ApoVs group), and the loaded HUMSC-ApoVs microneedle group (HAMA-MN@aptamer-ApoVs group). Mice in the DEX+aptamer-ApoVs group received aptamer-ApoVs injections every 7 days at a dose of 100 µg per administration. Mice in the DEX+HAMA-MN@aptamer-ApoVs group were treated by continuous application of HAMA microneedle array patches on the dorsal skin, with the patches replaced every 7 days. Each patch contained 100 µg of aptamer-ApoVs. On day 14, body weight and grip strength were measured, after which the mice were euthanized and tissue samples were collected for subsequent histopathological analyses.

Grip strength test in mice

Grip strength of mice was measured using a grip strength meter. Before testing, the instrument was calibrated to zero (0 g), and each mouse was gently handled to minimize stress. The mouse was then placed horizontally on the grip plate and allowed to grasp the grip bar with its forepaws. The tail was gently pulled backward until the mouse released the bar. Each mouse was tested three consecutive times, and the mean value was calculated. Grip strength was normalized to body weight to account for individual differences.

Forced swimming test

To evaluate skeletal muscle function in mice, a forced swimming test (FST) was conducted. The apparatus consisted of a transparent cylindrical glass tank (30 cm in height and 20 cm in diameter) filled with water to a depth of approximately 20 cm. Each mouse was placed individually into the water for free swimming while being continuously recorded. Videos were captured from a top-down perspective to obtain a complete view of the swimming trajectory, which was later analyzed to assess muscle endurance and locomotor performance.

In this study, we implemented a SiamMask-based tracking algorithm to extract the swimming trajectories of mice automatically. The initial region of interest (ROI) of the mouse was defined in the first frame, and the SiamMask model was applied for frame-by-frame tracking throughout the video sequence. Both the mask generation and bounding box refinement modules were enabled to enhance localization accuracy. The centroid of the segmented mask was computed in each frame to obtain the real-time position of the mouse, forming the raw trajectory dataset. To minimize tracking noise and motion artifacts, the raw trajectory data were downsampled and processed with a Savitzky-Golay smoothing filter, ensuring trajectory continuity and accuracy. The refined trajectory points were then saved as text data, while the swimming paths were dynamically visualized by overlaying red trajectory lines and position markers on the video frames.

Preparation of HAMA-MN@ApoV

The preparation of HAMA-MN@ApoV involves the following steps: (A) Mixing: apoptotic vesicle suspension is added to the pre-prepared 25% (w/v) HAMA-50 hydrogel precursor solution, ensuring thorough mixing for uniform distribution. (B) Molding and Deaeration: The precursor solution is dropped into pre-prepared silicone molds and subjected to deaeration using a vacuum pump to remove air bubbles. (C) Pre-drying: The mixture is placed in an oven at 30–35 °C for heating, undergoing multiple heating and concentration steps to achieve the desired viscosity. (D) Photocrosslinking: The solution is exposed to 405 nm UV light for 20 s to complete the crosslinking process. (E) Drying: The samples are dried by heating at 30–35 °C for more than 12 h until completely dry. (F) Demolding: After crosslinking, the samples are carefully demolded to obtain the final HAMA-MN@ApoV microneedle patches. For the preparation of pure HAMA microneedles (HAMA-MNs), all steps remain the same except for the omission of the apoptotic vesicle suspension.

Characterization of HAMA-MN

The morphology of HAMA-MN@ApoV was characterized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Zeiss Sigma 300, Germany) and laser confocal microscopy (SP8, Leica, Germany).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were repeated three times, and the data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism (9.0, Graph Software, USA). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post-hoc test for multiple comparisons was applied to determine statistical significance between three groups, while Student’s t-test was applied to determine statistical significance between 2 groups. To compare the data at different time points, two-way ANOVA and Sidak tests for multiple comparisons were performed. Value of p < 0.05 was considered significant. The data was expressed as mean values and standard deviation (SD).

The content of the other methods is detailed in the Supplementary Material.

Results

ApoVs promote C2C12 cell proliferation and inhibit apoptosis

We isolated ApoVs from HUMSCs and characterized them using multiple approaches. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) showed that ApoVs exhibited a typical spherical vesicular morphology (Fig. 2A). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis indicated that ApoVs were predominantly 100–500 nm in diameter (Fig. 2B). Western blotting further confirmed the high expression of the apoptosis-associated markers Caspase-3 and cleaved Caspase-3 (C-Caspase-3) in ApoVs (Fig. 2C). To evaluate cellular uptake, ApoVs were labeled with DiI and co-incubated with C2C12 myoblasts; confocal imaging demonstrated efficient internalization of ApoVs by C2C12 cells (Fig. 2D). C2C12 cells were then differentiated into myotubes, and an in vitro atrophy/sarcopenia-like model was established using 50 µM dexamethasone (DEX). CCK-8 assays showed that ApoVs increased C2C12 cell viability, with 1 µg/mL producing the most pronounced pro-proliferative effect compared with 0.25, 2.5, and 5 µg/mL (Fig. 2E).

Consistently, EdU incorporation assays revealed a significant increase in the number of EdU-positive cells following ApoV treatment compared with the control group (Fig. 2G). Colony formation assays further showed elevated colony numbers in the ApoV group, indicating enhanced proliferative and clonogenic capacity (Fig. 2I–J). Cell-cycle analysis by flow cytometry demonstrated that the DEX+ApoVs group had a markedly higher proportion of S-phase cells, accompanied by a reduced G1-phase fraction, compared with the DEX-only and control groups (Fig. 2F). Ki67 immunofluorescence staining corroborated these findings, showing substantially stronger Ki67 signals in the DEX+ApoVs group than in the DEX group (Fig. 2H). Collectively, these data suggest that ApoVs possess favorable physicochemical and biological properties, are efficiently taken up by muscle cells, and promote proliferation under DEX-induced stress, supporting their potential therapeutic utility in sarcopenia-related muscle impairment.

ApoV-derived miRNAs contribute to the regulation of cellular energy metabolic homeostasis

To elucidate the molecular composition of ApoVs, we performed small RNA sequencing of ApoV-associated miRNAs. The relative abundance of the top 20 most highly expressed miRNAs in ApoVs is shown in Fig. 3E. Predicted target genes were then subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses and categorized into biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF) (Fig. 3A, B), providing an overview of the regulatory landscape potentially mediated by ApoV miRNA cargos.

To further explore their relevance to mitochondrial dysfunction and energy metabolic imbalance in sarcopenia, we focused on GO terms related to mitochondrial function. These enriched terms indicated that the predicted target genes of ApoV-associated miRNAs were closely associated with mitochondrial activity, energy metabolism, and cellular homeostasis (Fig. 3C). KEGG pathway analysis further indicated significant enrichment in the PI3K–Akt and mTOR signaling pathways, which are tightly linked to cell growth, metabolism, and energy homeostasis (Fig. 3D). Together, these omics-level findings suggest that HUMSCs-derived ApoVs may participate in maintaining cellular metabolic homeostasis through their miRNA cargos, and provide a basis for identifying key functional miRNAs and guiding subsequent mechanistic studies.

ApoVs ameliorate oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction in sarcopenic myotubes in vitro

ROS immunofluorescence analysis showed that, under DEX challenge, the 1.0 µg/mL ApoVs group exhibited the lowest ROS levels, indicating that ApoVs at this dose markedly suppressed DEX-induced oxidative stress (Fig. 4A, B). To directly assess mitochondrial ultrastructural alterations, we performed transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Mitochondria in the control group displayed well-defined cristae and intact morphology, whereas the DEX group showed pronounced swelling and occasional membrane disruption. ApoVs treatment alleviated mitochondrial swelling and rupture, preserved cristae integrity, and restored a healthier mitochondrial morphology (Fig. 4C), suggesting that ApoVs help maintain the balance between mitochondrial fusion and fission to re-establish mitochondrial structural homeostasis.

We next evaluated senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) staining. The proportion of β-gal-positive cells was significantly increased in the DEX group, confirming successful induction of an atrophy/senescence-like phenotype. In contrast, ApoVs reduced SA-β-gal positivity, with the most prominent reduction observed at 1.0 µg/mL (Fig. 4D, H). Flow cytometric apoptosis assays further showed that ApoVs effectively inhibited DEX-induced apoptosis (Fig. 4F).

Regarding energy metabolism, DEX markedly reduced ATP production in C2C12 myotubes, whereas ApoVs partially restored ATP levels. Consistently, ApoVs significantly decreased malondialdehyde (MDA), a lipid peroxidation marker of oxidative stress (Fig. 4I).

Mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) is a key driver of oxidative phosphorylation and ATP synthesis. JC-1 staining revealed increased JC-1 monomer signals and decreased J-aggregate formation in the DEX group, indicating ΔΨm depolarization. ApoVs treatment reduced monomer levels and increased J-aggregates, suggesting recovery of ΔΨm and improved mitochondrial function (Fig. 4G).

Finally, we examined the expression of mitochondrial dynamics-related molecules. DEX promoted mitochondrial fission, as evidenced by upregulated Drp1 and downregulated Mfn1; ApoVs reversed these changes and restored the expression of the mitochondrial trafficking mediator Miro and mitochondrial DNA-related genes (mtCO2 and mtND1) (Fig. 4E, J). Collectively, these data demonstrate that ApoVs mitigate DEX-induced mitochondrial dysfunction by suppressing oxidative stress, preserving mitochondrial dynamics, and improving ΔΨm and ATP production.

ApoVs inhibit DEX-induced C2C12 myotube atrophy and promote C2C12 hypertrophy

Giemsa and crystal violet staining demonstrated that DEX markedly impaired myogenic differentiation and myotube formation (Fig. 5A). Consistently, the DEX group displayed thinner myotubes with a significantly reduced mean myotube diameter/area compared with the control group, indicating successful induction of an atrophic phenotype. In contrast, ApoVs treatment noticeably restored myotube morphology, leading to a recovery increase in myotube diameter/area, suggesting that ApoVs counteract DEX-induced myotube atrophy and promote myotube growth.

Myosin heavy chain (MyHC) is a key structural component of mature myotubes and is widely used as a marker of myotube differentiation and maturation. Myogenin (MyoG), a member of the myogenic regulatory factors (MRFs), plays an essential role in initiating myoblast differentiation and fusion. To further assess the effects of ApoVs on myogenesis, we performed immunofluorescence staining of MyHC and MyoG. The Dex+ApoVs group exhibited significantly higher MyHC and MyoG signals than the DEX group (Fig. 5B–D), indicating that ApoVs not only alleviated DEX-induced myotube atrophy but also promoted C2C12 myogenic differentiation/fusion, which may ultimately facilitate de novo myotube formation and maturation.

In skeletal muscle, the ubiquitin–proteasome system (UPS) degrades sarcomeric proteins to adapt to changes in muscle activity or stress [33]. The rate-limiting step of ubiquitination, which determines subsequent proteasome-dependent degradation, is catalyzed by E3 ubiquitin ligases. Two canonical muscle-specific E3 ligases upregulated during atrophy are atrogin-1/MAFbx and MuRF1. Atrogin-1 targets proteins associated with the sarcomeric Z-disc, whereas MuRF1 contributes to myofibrillar protein breakdown and is closely linked to myofibril disassembly. In contrast, MyoD (myogenic differentiation antigen) is a myogenic lineage-determining transcription factor that initiates and locks in the myogenic differentiation program; notably, ectopic expression of MyoD can even drive non-muscle cells toward myogenic lineage. Additionally, myostatin (MSTN), a member of the TGF-β superfamily produced by skeletal muscle, is a well-recognized negative regulator of muscle growth.

We further validated these key regulators at both the transcriptional and translational levels using qPCR and Western blotting. qPCR results showed that ApoVs markedly upregulated multiple myogenesis-related genes while suppressing the transcription of atrophy-related genes (Fig. 5F, G). Western blotting corroborated these findings by demonstrating increased expression of myogenic proteins and reduced levels of atrophy-associated factors, including atrogin-1, MuRF1, and myostatin (Fig. 5E).

Regulation of gene expression in C2C12 myotubes treated with ApoVs

To further elucidate the regulatory effects of ApoVs on C2C12 myotubes, DEX-induced atrophic C2C12 myotubes were co-incubated with ApoVs in vitro for 24 h. mRNA-seq was then performed on the atrophic myotube model before and after ApoVs treatment, which were designated as the Control and ApoVs groups, respectively. Principal component analysis (PCA) demonstrated good within-group reproducibility and a clear separation between groups (Fig. 6A). Differentially expressed gene (DEG) analysis revealed a total of 2,073 significantly altered genes in the ApoVs group compared with the Control group, including 873 downregulated and 1,200 upregulated genes (Fig. 6B-D).

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis indicated that ApoVs-associated DEGs were significantly enriched in multiple mitochondria metabolism–related biological processes and functional terms, including “positive regulation of cell cycle”, “ATP-dependent activity”, “response to reactive oxygen species”, and “myoblast proliferation” (Fig. 6F). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis further showed that, compared with the Control group, the ApoVs group was significantly enriched in the “PI3K/AKT signaling pathway”. To further validate these pathway-level changes, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed and revealed an upregulated enrichment trend of the “PI3K-AKT” gene set following ApoVs intervention (Fig. 6G). Collectively, these results suggest that ApoVs may regulate C2C12 proliferation and myogenic differentiation, at least in part, through activation of the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway (Fig. 6E).

Fabrication and characterizations of HAMA-MN@aptamer-ApoVs

We used the muscle-targeting aptamer A01B (5′-CAGGAGCCGAGAACCGGTTGGTGGGTAATCCTGTTAGCGC-3′) for surface modification of ApoVs [34, 35]. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and dynamic light scattering (DLS) showed that aptamer-modified ApoVs retained their characteristic vesicular morphology and size distribution, indicating that the modification procedure did not appreciably compromise vesicle integrity (Fig. 8A, B). Moreover, compared with unmodified ApoVs, aptamer-decorated ApoVs showed enhanced uptake by C2C12 cells (Fig. 8C). In addition, HAMA hydrogel-based microneedles (HAMA-MN) were fabricated as a delivery vehicle for ApoVs. Optical microscopy images showed a regular pyramidal microneedle geometry with a base diameter of ~ 400 μm and a height of ~ 600 μm. A total of 400 microneedles (20 × 20 array) were uniformly distributed on an 8 mm × 8 mm square patch (Fig. 8D). The force-displacement curve shows that as the applied load increases, the microneedle array exhibits nonlinear compressive deformation behavior, indicating its good mechanical strength, compressive resistance, and structural integrity (Figure S2). The sharp tips are consistent with the basic geometric requirements for efficient skin penetration. To visualize the distribution of aptamer-ApoVs within HAMA-MN, ApoVs were labeled with DiI (red) and the HAMA-MN was labeled with a green fluorescent dye. Confocal imaging showed that the red fluorescence was distributed throughout the microneedle matrix (Fig. 8E), which was consistent with the scanning electron microscopy observations (Fig. 8F).

To evaluate the in vitro release profile, DiI-labeled HAMA-MN@ApoVs were incubated in PBS (pH 7.4) at 37 °C, and the DiI fluorescence intensity was longitudinally monitored using an in vivo imaging system. The microneedle platform enabled sustained release of ApoVs over 12 days (Fig. 8G). In parallel, the cumulative release at each time point was quantified using a BCA assay. The release curve displayed a typical biphasic pattern, featuring an initial burst release within the first 48 h (approximately 30–40%), followed by a prolonged slow-release phase (Fig. 8H). In vitro degradation experiments show that the HAMA hydrogel microneedles can gradually degrade over 7 days, thus supporting the formation of a local drug reservoir after insertion, and may promote the sustained release of ApoVs by gradually degrading the matrix (Figure S4). H&E staining of the punctured tissue revealed a distinct microchannel at the microneedle insertion site, confirming the effective penetration capability of the microneedles (Fig. 8I).

ApoVs ameliorate muscle atrophy and enhance muscle strength

Next, we evaluated the in vivo therapeutic efficacy of the HAMA-MN@aptamer-ApoVs composite delivery system in a mouse model of muscle atrophy. DEX-induced atrophic mice were randomly assigned to four groups (Control, DEX, DEX+aptamer-ApoVs, and DEX+HAMA-MN@aptamer-ApoVs). Macroscopic observations, body weight changes, and grip strength tests demonstrated that, compared with the DEX group, treatment with aptamer-ApoVs increased both body weight and muscle strength in mice, accompanied by an enlargement of the gastrocnemius (GA) muscle volume. Furthermore, the HAMA-MN@aptamer-ApoVs group showed a further enhancement of these improvements (Fig. 9B, F). Consistently, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining revealed a significant increase in GA myofiber diameter in both the aptamer-ApoVs and HAMA-MN@aptamer-ApoVs groups relative to the DEX group (Fig. 9C). We further assessed mitochondrial oxidative capacity of muscle fibers using succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) staining. DEX markedly reduced SDH activity in GA tissue, whereas aptamer-ApoVs partially restored SDH activity and improved mitochondrial oxidative capacity (Fig. 9D). In addition, a forced swimming test was performed to evaluate muscle endurance. Compared with DEX-treated mice, the aptamer-ApoVs group exhibited prolonged swimming time and more completed laps; notably, mice receiving HAMA-MN@aptamer-ApoVs displayed more continuous and regular circular trajectories, indicating the most pronounced improvement in limb strength and endurance (Fig. 9E). Type I (slow-twitch/oxidative) fibers are enriched in mitochondria, rely predominantly on oxidative metabolism, and show superior fatigue resistance. Immunofluorescence analysis showed that DEX significantly decreased the proportion of type I fibers in GA tissue; this reduction was reversed by aptamer-ApoVs, and the most evident restoration was observed in the HAMA-MN@aptamer-ApoVs group, highlighting the potential of this composite delivery system to improve muscle quality and function (Fig. 9G).

Discussion

In this study, we systematically evaluated how ApoVs derived from HUMSCs regulate energy metabolism and myogenic differentiation in aging-associated skeletal muscle. ApoVs exhibit favorable biocompatibility and therapeutic feasibility. Our results demonstrate that ApoVs markedly alleviated DEX-induced cellular senescence phenotypes and myotube atrophy, accompanied by changes in PI3K/AKT pathway–related proteins, thereby improving mitochondrial morphology and function. To further enhance tissue targeting, we functionalized the ApoV membrane surface with an aptamer to increase affinity toward muscle tissue. Finally, engineered ApoVs were delivered using dissolvable HAMA microneedles; mice treated with this composite delivery system exhibited improved muscle mass and functional performance.

Stem cell transplantation has long been a major strategy for aging-related diseases; however, the key mechanisms underlying its in vivo therapeutic effects remain incompletely understood [12, 36, 37]. Meanwhile, the limited availability of MSC sources, high costs of acquisition and expansion, stringent requirements for storage and transportation, and reduced potency after in vitro passaging collectively hinder clinical translation. Notably, multiple studies indicate that a fraction of transplanted stem cells undergo apoptosis in vivo and release apoptosis-associated vesicles, including ApoVs [38]. Accordingly, increasing attention has been directed to ApoVs as cell-free effectors in disease intervention. Compared with conventional extracellular vesicles, represented by exosomes, ApoVs may offer an advantage in terms of recoverable yield during preparation; however, this advantage depends on the cell source, apoptosis-induction protocol, isolation method, and quantification strategy [39, 40]. In addition, because most transplanted stem cells undergo apoptosis in vivo, apoptotic bodies, as products released after stem cell apoptosis, may play a more important role in regulating tissue homeostasis. ApoVs have been reported to participate in apoptotic cell clearance, antigen presentation, and immune modulation. Although ApoVs have been investigated in wound healing, diabetes, and intrauterine adhesions [41–43], whether they can regulate mitochondrial homeostasis and thereby benefit sarcopenia remains insufficiently explored.

Studies on sarcopenia commonly focus on improving the metabolic microenvironment of skeletal muscle. As the major cellular hub for energy production, mitochondria generate ATP to support muscle contraction; mitochondrial dysfunction therefore compromises energy supply, resulting in reduced muscle strength and endurance [44–46]. In parallel, aging is associated with excessive mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, which induces oxidative stress, further impairs myocytes and mitochondrial function, and accelerates muscle wasting. In our in vitro experiments, ApoVs markedly reversed DEX-induced apoptosis and promoted cell proliferation, with an apparent enhancement as the ApoV dose increased. Morphological assessment of C2C12 myotubes showed that DEX induced shortened and atrophic myotubes, whereas ApoV treatment substantially mitigated these changes; concurrently, myogenic differentiation markers were upregulated and atrophy-related factors were downregulated.

Mechanistically, our data suggest that ApoVs may regulate intracellular mitochondrial function predominantly via the PI3K/AKT signaling axis. Cellular state transitions associated with energy metabolism typically rely on precisely orchestrated metabolic reprogramming. As a central pathway integrating nutrient and growth cues, the PI3K/AKT/mTOR network profoundly influences satellite cell activation, differentiation, and effector functions by modulating mitochondrial biogenesis, respiratory chain activity, and substrate utilization [33, 47, 48]. AKT activation can stimulate mTOR signaling and enhance mitochondrial biogenesis and expansion, helping maintain mitochondrial abundance and functionality to meet cellular energetic and metabolic demands. Moreover, AKT can reinforce antioxidant defenses through multiple routes, thereby mitigating oxidative stress–induced injury. For example, AKT can regulate FOXO-associated processes and can promote nuclear factor erythroid 2–related factor 2 (Nrf2)–mediated expression of antioxidant enzymes (e.g., SOD and GPx), enhancing ROS-scavenging capacity [10, 49]. Phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN), a canonical negative regulator of PI3K signaling, dephosphorylates PIP3 to PIP2 and thereby suppresses PI3K/AKT pathway activity. Previous studies have linked PTEN activity to restrained muscle growth and repair; in Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) models, PTEN inhibition has been shown to improve pathophysiology and muscle function, supporting PTEN as a potential therapeutic target [50, 51].

miRNA sequencing revealed a high abundance of miR-21-5p in ApoVs. miR-21-5p plays an important role in skeletal muscle–related biological processes. For instance, miR-21-5p has been reported as one of the more abundant miRNAs in porcine skeletal muscle and shows markedly differential expression between embryonic day 90 (E90) and postnatal day 100 (D100), suggesting a role in muscle development; the same study proposed that miR-21-5p may act, at least in part, through the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway [52, 53]. Based on these findings, we speculate that the enhanced phosphorylation of PI3K/AKT-associated proteins observed after ApoV treatment may be attributable to miR-21-5p delivery.

Nucleic acid aptamers are oligonucleotide sequences capable of specifically recognizing target molecules with high affinity and selectivity, and have therefore been widely used in biosensing and targeted delivery. Compared with conventional protein-based targeting ligands, aptamers are often more stable against pH and temperature variations, smaller in size, and easier to synthesize chemically, offering improved cost control and batch-to-batch consistency [54–56]. In this study, the aptamer was covalently conjugated to the lipid membrane surface of ApoVs through Schiff base reaction. This modification likely contributed to the enhanced muscle-targeting ability of aptamer-ApoVs, as reflected by their improved binding and uptake in muscle-related models, thereby supporting more efficient local delivery of ApoVs to atrophic muscle tissue.

In this study, HAMA hydrogel microneedles were employed as a transdermal delivery platform for ApoVs. The microneedles penetrated the skin barrier and delivered ApoVs into the subcutaneous microenvironment. After insertion, the hydrogel matrix could serve as a local ‘drug depot’, supporting gradual ApoVs release and subsequent diffusion, thereby helping to prolong therapeutic exposure. Compared with standard transdermal patches, microneedles create microchannels in the skin, markedly enhancing transdermal transport of macromolecules and nanoscale carriers. Moreover, microneedle-based systems can enable high local accumulation at the lesion site and may also facilitate entry into systemic circulation through the generated microchannels, offering the possibility of efficient systemic delivery. For dissolvable microneedles, the matrix can provide physical protection to vesicles during administration and reduce ex vivo degradation; meanwhile, the local “drug depot” formed after insertion supports sustained release, prolonging exposure and helping maintain a relatively stable effective concentration, thereby improving therapeutic durability [57–59].

Composite delivery strategies that integrate biological vesicles with hydrogel materials have been widely applied in tissue engineering and regenerative medicine for various diseases. However, most existing studies have focused on exosomes or small extracellular vesicles, whereas the application of apoptotic vesicles (ApoVs) released during apoptosis in skeletal muscle disorders remains relatively limited. Meanwhile, microneedle-based drug delivery systems, as minimally invasive delivery platforms, have been extensively explored for the treatment of various diseases, but their application in muscle atrophy-related disorders remains underdeveloped. Given that ApoVs also possess a lipid membrane structure, this study adopted surface-engineering strategies commonly used for exosomes/extracellular vesicles and enhanced the muscle-targeting ability of ApoVs through modification with a specific nucleic acid aptamer. Based on this rationale, we developed a cell-free delivery strategy that integrates muscle-targeted engineered ApoVs with HAMA hydrogel microneedles, providing a new therapeutic approach for the intervention of muscle atrophy and related muscle dysfunction.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, although the DEX-induced muscle atrophy model recapitulates several pathological features associated with sarcopenia, including muscle fiber atrophy, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and muscle weakness, it primarily reflects glucocorticoid-driven catabolic muscle loss and does not fully reproduce the chronic, systemic, and multifactorial nature of primary age-related sarcopenia [60]. Therefore, future studies using naturally aged mice or complementary sarcopenia-related models, such as hindlimb unloading and denervation models, are needed to further validate the therapeutic efficacy and translational potential of the proposed strategy. In addition, ApoVs are intrinsically heterogeneous, and further optimization is required for their isolation, purification, characterization, batch-to-batch consistency, and large-scale production. Finally, before clinical translation, the long-term biodistribution, clearance, immunogenicity, biosafety, storage stability, and potential off-target effects of aptamer-modified ApoVs delivered via HAMA microneedles should be systematically evaluated.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that ApoVs derived from HUMSCs effectively alleviate muscle atrophy and enhance muscle strength. We further confirm that ApoVs reduce oxidative stress and suppress excessive mitochondrial fission in skeletal muscle, thereby improving mitochondrial homeostasis. Moreover, ApoVs regulate the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway by delivering miR-21-5p, which in turn promotes myogenic differentiation—an effect that is crucial for counteracting aging-associated myotube atrophy. Overall, our findings provide new mechanistic insights into ApoV-mediated repair in aging tissues and support a promising cell-free therapeutic strategy for the clinical management of sarcopenia.
