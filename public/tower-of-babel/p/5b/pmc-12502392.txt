The constructs of health literacy in children: a systematic review
Authors: Sara Zamani Bakhtiarvand, Zohreh Rahaei, Hossein Ali Sadeghian, Farhad Fatehi, Sepideh Soltani, Armin Zareiyan, Don Nutbeam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHealth literacy encompasses competencies enabling individuals to access, comprehend, evaluate, and use health information effectively. While research emphasizes its importance during childhood, limited consensus exists on health literacy conceptualization for children aged 9–12 years—a critical developmental period characterized by concrete operational thinking. This systematic review aimed to identify and synthesize key components of health literacy frameworks specifically used for children in this age group.MethodsA systematic review was conducted in 2024 following PRISMA 2020 guidelines and registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024588355). Six databases (PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, ProQuest, Google Scholar, and ScienceOpen) were searched without temporal restrictions using terms related to “health literacy” and “children.” Studies were included if they detailed health literacy conceptualization for children aged 9–12 years, were peer-reviewed English publications with accessible full-text, and demonstrated high methodological quality. Hybrid deductive-inductive thematic analysis was applied using Nutbeam’s framework as theoretical foundation.ResultsFrom 11,586 initial records, 18 studies met inclusion criteria representing diverse geographic contexts (66.7% Western, 33.3% Eastern) and methodological approaches (35% qualitative, 24% quantitative, 41% mixed-methods). Four overarching themes emerged: [1] Skills (functional, interactive, and critical literacy competencies); [2] Teaching-learning processes (pedagogy, educator-learner dynamics, content delivery, learning environments, and assessment methods); [3] Contextual factors (socioeconomic, cultural, and demographic influences); and [4] Individual factors (psychological traits and cognitive abilities). While Nutbeam’s tripartite model was referenced in 77% of studies, it required substantial adaptation for pediatric populations. Teaching-learning processes emerged as a novel dimension in 50% of studies, highlighting pedagogical approaches absent from adult-focused frameworks.ConclusionsHealth literacy in children aged 9–12 years extends beyond adult-centric models to encompass distinct developmental and educational dimensions. Teaching-learning processes emerged as a foundational component, emphasizing schools as critical platforms for health literacy development. Our findings support developing child-centered frameworks integrating cognitive development theory, educational pedagogy, and socio-ecological perspectives. Future research should focus on longitudinal studies, culturally responsive interventions, and child-specific assessment tools. This framework provides evidence-based guidance for developing age-appropriate health literacy interventions and assessment tools.Keywords: Health literacy, Children, Pedagogy, Thematic analysis, Cognitive development

Background

Health literacy (HL) represents a fundamental determinant of lifelong health outcomes, defined by the World Health Organization as “the personal characteristics and social resources needed for individuals and communities to access, understand, appraise and use information and services to make decisions about health” [1, 2]. Despite growing recognition of its importance during childhood developmental stages [3–5], conceptual frameworks for understanding health literacy in children remain underdeveloped and inconsistent, creating significant gaps in both theory and practice [6].

Children’s rapid cognitive and social growth creates distinct opportunities for establishing foundational health competencies that differ fundamentally from those required by adult populations [7–9]. The transition from caregiver dependence toward emerging personal autonomy in health-related decision-making occurs prominently between ages 9–12 years [10, 11], positioning this demographic as pivotal in health literacy development. This developmental window presents unique cognitive, social, and emotional characteristics that necessitate targeted approaches to both health literacy conceptualization and intervention design [7]. Moreover, interventions during this period demonstrate significant potential to influence long-term health behaviors while impacting immediate outcomes including academic performance and healthcare engagement [12].

Developmental considerations

From a cognitive development perspective, children aged 9–12 years operate within Piaget’s concrete operational stage, characterized by enhanced logical thinking about concrete objects and events, improved understanding of cause-and-effect relationships, and increased capacity to organize and classify information [13–15]. This developmental stage enables systematic processing of health information, though children require concrete examples and experiential learning approaches rather than abstract theoretical presentations [16, 17].

This period marks a crucial educational transition when children typically shift from learning basic reading skills to using reading as a tool for acquiring new knowledge [18], creating optimal conditions for health literacy interventions requiring systematic information processing. Vygotsky’s social learning theory provides additional theoretical foundation, emphasizing that learning occurs through social interaction and cultural mediation [19, 20]. During this developmental window, children demonstrate increasing ability to model health behaviors observed in their environment, develop greater awareness of social norms, and respond to peer influences [21, 22].

Theoretical framework adaptation

Nutbeam’s widely-cited tripartite model provides a framework for understanding health literacy competencies, categorizing HL into three progressive levels: functional (basic reading and writing skills applied to health contexts), interactive (advanced cognitive and social skills for active participation in health decisions), and critical (skills to critically analyze and act upon health information) [23, 24]. The framework’s structured approach to health literacy competencies and recognition of the progressive nature of health literacy development makes it particularly suitable for examining developmental progressions in children. Furthermore, this framework serves as the theoretical foundation for analyzing how existing research conceptualizes health literacy in the target age group, providing a systematic lens through which to examine alignment with established developmental principles.

However, this model requires adaptation for middle childhood contexts, as conceptualizations of health literacy for children vary widely, encompassing diverse competencies and learning outcomes [10]. Contemporary researchers emphasize the need for an integrated approach to describing and developing health literacy dimensions in children, considering their personal attributes and contextual embeddedness [7, 10].

Contemporary health literacy has evolved from its original conceptualization as basic reading and writing skills to encompass a multidimensional construct incorporating various domains, including physical, food, nutrition, and media literacy [4, 23, 25–28]. Although research demonstrates that these literacies represent distinct concepts with unique approaches to the education-health relationship [29], health literacy research benefits from incorporating these related literacy domains, as they provide foundational competencies that enhance health decision-making during childhood development [6, 8, 28].

This integration is particularly critical given that childhood represents a formative period where health literacy competencies are acquired, with evidence demonstrating associations between children’s health literacy levels and essential health behaviors, including physical activity, sleep patterns, and overall well-being [30]. This expanded conceptualization recognizes that effective health literacy extends beyond traditional cognitive processing to include embodied knowledge and physical competencies that enable children to navigate health-related decisions throughout their developmental trajectory [31, 32].

Current gaps and limitations

Despite these theoretical advances, significant empirical and conceptual gaps persist in the field. Existing health literacy frameworks and assessment tools were primarily developed for adult populations [6, 33–39], failing to account for key developmental factors such as concrete operational thinking patterns and social learning processes that characterize middle childhood. This creates a significant empirical gap: while interventions during this developmental window show substantial potential to influence long-term health behaviors and impact immediate outcomes including academic performance and healthcare engagement [7, 12, 33, 38, 40], the lack of developmentally appropriate conceptual frameworks limits effective intervention design and implementation.

Child-specific definitions of health literacy must be operationalized as developmentally appropriate conceptualizations that account for: [1] cognitive abilities specific to concrete operational thinking [2], social contexts including peer and family influences, and [3] learning preferences that emphasize experiential and concrete approaches rather than abstract theoretical concepts. The absence of such definitions creates barriers to effective intervention and risks perpetuating health inequities, particularly given that educational settings provide ideal environments for health literacy development [41–43].

Study rationale and objectives

This systematic review addresses a critical conceptual and empirical gap in the health literacy literature. While previous reviews have examined measurement tools or health literacy across broad pediatric age ranges [6, 39], none have specifically analyzed how researchers conceptualize and operationalize health literacy for children aged 9–12 years. This age-specific focus is essential because this developmental period represents a unique intersection of cognitive capabilities, social influences, and educational opportunities that requires distinct theoretical approaches and practical interventions.

The conceptual gap is particularly pronounced for the 9–12 age range due to the convergence of several factors: the transition to concrete operational thinking, the shift from learning-to-read to reading-to-learn, increasing peer influence, and the gradual assumption of health-related responsibilities. Understanding how existing research has addressed these developmental considerations is essential for developing effective, theoretically grounded interventions and assessment approaches.

This systematic review aims to identify, synthesize, and critically analyze how health literacy has been conceptualized, defined, and operationalized in research involving children aged 9–12 years, with specific attention to theoretical frameworks used, developmental considerations incorporated, and alignment with established cognitive and social development principles.

Methodology

Study design and registration

This systematic review adheres to the PRISMA 2020 guidelines [44] and was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (registration number: CRD42024588355). The primary objective was to identify and synthesize evidence regarding the components of health literacy in children aged 9–12 years.

Search strategy

We conducted a comprehensive literature search on November 21, 2024, across six electronic databases: PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, ProQuest, Google Scholar, and Science Open. The search included English-language, peer-reviewed journal articles with accessible full-text versions published from database inception through November 2024, with no temporal restrictions applied to ensure comprehensive coverage.

Our keyword selection employed a systematic four-step approach: [1] preliminary literature review to identify relevant terminology [2], consultation with Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms [3], examination of keywords from seminal articles, and [4] expert consultation with faculty specializing in health literacy research. The core search strategy combined “health literacy” and “child” as primary terms, supplemented by synonyms including “children,” “pediatric,” “paediatric,” “health knowledge,” “health information,” and “information literacy” to capture diverse terminology across research traditions and geographic contexts.

Boolean operators were systematically applied, with OR operators combining synonyms and AND operators connecting essential search components. Database-specific strategies were developed through iterative testing to achieve an optimal Number Needed to Read (NNR) between 12 and 15 across all databases, balancing efficiency with comprehensiveness (Table 1).

Study selection

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were included if they: [1] examined health literacy components in children aged 9–12 years [2], provided explicit details about conceptual models or assessment tools [3], were published as peer-reviewed journal articles in English [4], offered accessible full-text versions, and [5] demonstrated high methodological quality based on critical appraisal.

Exclusion criteria comprised: [1] studies lacking explicit details about health literacy conceptual models or tools [2], inaccessible full-text articles [3], research focusing specifically on children with clinical disorders [4], unpublished materials (conference abstracts, trial protocols) [5], gray literature, short communications, commentaries, or letters [6], non-English publications, and [7] studies receiving low quality assessment scores.

Selection process

Study selection followed a multi-stage systematic approach with clearly defined reviewer roles. All retrieved articles were imported into EndNote reference management software, and duplicate records were identified and removed by the primary researcher (Z.R.). Two researchers (S.Z.B. and H.S.) independently screened titles to exclude obviously irrelevant studies, achieving excellent inter-rater reliability (Cohen’s kappa κ = 0.87). These researchers subsequently assessed abstracts independently, resolving disagreements through discussion and, when necessary, consultation with a third reviewer (F.F.).

Of 76 studies identified for full-text review (65 from databases plus 11 from additional sources), eight were excluded due to inaccessible full texts despite author contact attempts. The remaining 68 studies underwent independent critical appraisal by two researchers, achieving substantial inter-rater reliability (Cohen’s κ = 0.81).

Quality assessment

Two researchers independently assessed methodological quality using study-specific critical appraisal tools appropriate for the diverse research designs encountered. We employed the Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) checklists for cohort, case-control, and qualitative studies [45], the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for cross-sectional studies [46], and the Consensus-based Standards for the Selection of Health Measurement Instruments (COSMIN) checklist for tool development studies [47, 48].

Inter-rater disagreements were resolved through structured discussion using a standardized protocol, with a third reviewer making final determinations when consensus could not be achieved. Following comprehensive screening and quality assessment, 18 studies demonstrating the highest methodological quality and relevance were selected for inclusion.

Data extraction

We developed a standardized data extraction form using Microsoft Excel to capture: journal name, article title, publication year, author(s), country, study population characteristics, research methodology, and identified components of children’s health literacy. Trained researchers performed data extraction following a standardized protocol, with a subset undergoing dual extraction to verify consistency. Data Extraction

Data analysis

We conducted hybrid thematic content analysis, combining deductive and inductive strategies to comprehensively examine health literacy components in children aged 9–12 years. This approach balanced theoretical grounding with empirical discovery, ensuring established frameworks were honored while remaining open to emergent themes.

We selected Nutbeam’s three-level framework as our deductive foundation after systematically evaluating available models including WHO definitions [49], Bloom’s Revised Taxonomy [50], frameworks by Paakkari [38] and Bröder [51], and Sørensen et al.‘s integrated model. Although Sørensen et al.‘s integrated model provides comprehensive coverage through its 4 × 3 matrix of competencies and health domains, its complexity appears to exceed the cognitive and experiential capabilities typically found in 9–12 year-olds [52]. Otten et al. have shown that existing health literacy frameworks demonstrate dimensional overlap, yet Nutbeam’s hierarchical progression uniquely corresponds with developmental patterns observed in middle childhood [53]. Nutbeam’s framework provided the optimal theoretical foundation through its three-level structure, while allowing identification of additional pediatric-specific components through inductive analysis.

Coding process

Health literacy components from included studies were initially coded according to Nutbeam’s framework. Three researchers independently applied this coding to establish inter-coder reliability (Cohen’s κ = 0.79). Components aligning with functional, interactive, or critical health literacy dimensions were systematically categorized, while components not fitting Nutbeam’s framework underwent inductive thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke’s approach (2006). Researchers generated initial codes, identified patterns, and developed themes through iterative analysis.

Deductive and inductive findings were subsequently integrated to create a comprehensive framework. We resolved discrepancies through structured discussion and team consensus. Analytical rigor was ensured through independent coding by multiple researchers, regular team meetings, audit trail maintenance, and expert consultation. Analysis was conducted using Microsoft Excel with systematic organization linking each component to its source study and thematic classification.

Search results

Following the PRISMA 2020 flow diagram (Fig. 1), our systematic search identified 11,586 articles across all databases. After removing 2,951 duplicate records, 8,635 unique articles remained for screening. Title screening excluded 8,377 irrelevant articles, while abstract screening resulted in exclusion of an additional 258 articles. Seventy-six studies proceeded to full-text review and critical appraisal, with 18 studies ultimately meeting all inclusion criteria and quality standards for final inclusion.

Results

Study characteristics

The final synthesis comprised 18 peer-reviewed articles, predominantly published between 2018 and 2020, reflecting increasing scholarly attention to children’s health literacy conceptualization (Fig. 2). Studies represented diverse geographic contexts: Germany, United States, Canada, Thailand, and Australia each contributed two studies, while single studies originated from Iran, Taiwan, United Kingdom, Czech Republic, South Korea, China, Finland, and Ireland. This distribution provided global representation, with 66.7% of studies conducted in Western countries and 33.3% in Eastern contexts.

Distribution of included articles by publication year

The literature demonstrated considerable methodological diversity. Mixed-methods approaches were most prevalent (41%), followed by qualitative methods (35%) and quantitative approaches (24%). This methodological heterogeneity offered multiple perspectives on child health literacy conceptualization and measurement, enhancing the comprehensiveness of our findings.

Quality appraisal

Methodological quality assessment of 68 full-text articles was independently conducted by two reviewers using design-appropriate critical appraisal tools, achieving substantial inter-rater reliability (κ = 0.81). Studies were categorized as included, excluded, or borderline based on methodological rigor and relevance, with 18 studies meeting the predefined threshold for high methodological quality.

Qualitative and exploratory studies scored 8–9/10 on the CASP checklists, cross-sectional studies achieved 7/8 on the JBI checklist, and the conceptual/opinion paper scored 5/6 on the JBI Text and Opinion checklist. Tool development and validation studies consistently received Good or Excellent ratings on the COSMIN checklist. These findings confirm the methodological robustness of the included evidence base (Table 3).

Theoretical framework application

Analysis of theoretical frameworks revealed varied approaches to health literacy conceptualization. Nearly half (44%) applied Nutbeam’s complete tripartite framework encompassing functional, interactive, and critical health literacy dimensions. For instance, Bollweg et al. [3] adapted the European Health Literacy Survey using all three dimensions for fourth-grade German students, while Senahad et al. [54] incorporated functional, interactive, and critical components in their Thai children’s health literacy assessment tool.

An additional 33% utilized selected components of Nutbeam’s framework, particularly emphasizing engagement, evaluation, and understanding dimensions, as demonstrated by Lane & Aldoory [55] in their pragmatic measurement approach. The remaining 22% employed alternative theoretical models or developed novel frameworks specific to their research contexts, such as Longmuir et al. [56], who developed a physical literacy framework incorporating motivation, confidence, and physical competence.

Theme 1: skills

The Skills theme comprised three sub-themes directly corresponding to Nutbeam's framework, demonstrating its continued relevance while revealing child-specific adaptations.

Functional Health Literacy was conceptualized as basic abilities to access, understand, and use health information, emphasizing age-appropriate reading comprehension and numeracy skills in health contexts. Doustmohammadian et al. [4] demonstrated this through their Food and Nutrition Literacy scale, assessing children's ability to read food labels and understand nutritional information. Similarly, Wanichsaithong & Prasertsom [59] emphasized functional literacy in their oral health assessment tool, focusing on children's comprehension of oral health materials and effective use of educational resources.

Interactive Health Literacy extended beyond individual skills to encompass social and communicative competencies. Jenkins et al.[58] highlighted children's engagement with health information through discussion and interpersonal skills development in library settings. Kågström et al. [65] exemplified this dimension through their mental health literacy scale, assessing children's communication skills about emotions and help-seeking abilities from peers and professionals. This dimension particularly emphasized peer-to-peer health communication and collaborative health decision-making, reflecting the inherently social nature of childhood learning.

Critical Health Literacy comprises analytical and evaluative skills, though adapted for children's cognitive capabilities. Rather than focusing on abstract critical analysis, studies examined children's ability to compare health information sources and evaluate simple health claims. Baek& Lee [60] demonstrated this through their focus on children's ability to extract key health messages and identify reliable information sources. Bollweg et al. [3] incorporated critical evaluation skills in their adapted survey, assessing children's ability to interpret and evaluate health information for informed decision-making.

Theme 2: teaching-learning processes

This novel theme emerged from nine studies (50%) and represents a significant departure from adult-focused models, capturing educational dimensions essential to child health literacy development through five sub-themes.

Teacher and Learner Dynamics addressed the reciprocal relationship between educators and children, emphasizing teacher health literacy competence and child-centered pedagogical approaches. Paakkari et al. [69] emphasized teacher confidence in seeking and managing health information as crucial for effective instruction. Otten et al. [53] demonstrated through their curriculum-based intervention how teachers'self-confidence and health specialist knowledge directly influenced children's health literacy acquisition.

Content focused on curriculum design and health topic selection appropriate for children's developmental stage. Senahad et al. [54] exemplified this by developing age-appropriate content covering nutrition, exercise, medication use, and hygiene for 9-10-year-old Thai children. Jefferies et al. [63] demonstrated content adaptation by focusing on motor skills and physical fitness topics relevant to children's immediate experiences.

Pedagogy encompassed instructional methods and learning strategies, with studies consistently favoring interactive, experiential approaches over traditional didactic methods. Longmuir et al.[56] emphasized hands-on physical activities and experiential learning in their physical literacy assessment. Otten et al.[53] demonstrated the effectiveness of curriculum-based interactive approaches that engaged children through practical learning experiences.

Learning Environment addressed physical and social contexts facilitating health literacy development. Baek & Lee[60] emphasized supportive school environments as ideal settings for health promotion programs. Jenkins et al. [58] highlighted how public libraries provided unbiased, supportive environments for children's critical health literacy development, free from health-related labeling or judgment.

Assessment and Feedback captured evaluation methods and feedback mechanisms appropriate for children. Lane & Aldoory[55] emphasized developmentally appropriate assessment approaches considering children's cognitive stages. Wanichsaithong & Prasertsom[59] demonstrated this through visual stimuli and interactive components in oral health literacy assessment, making evaluation more engaging for young learners.

Theme 3: contextual factors

Eleven studies (61% of the total) addressed contextual influences, organized into three sub-themes.

Socioeconomic Factors included economic conditions, parental education, housing stability, and peer influences. Qiao et al.[70] demonstrated the significant impact of parents'literacy levels and socioeconomic status on children's health literacy development in their Chinese cohort. Liao et al.[62] emphasized how parental health literacy directly influenced children's abilities, while Amin et al.[67] highlighted peer networks'role in shaping food literacy attitudes and behaviors.

Cultural Factors encompassed social norms and values shaping health understanding. Otten et al.[53] demonstrated how cultural norms and values significantly impact children's health literacy development and must be considered in curriculum design.

Demographic Factors included age and gender influences on health literacy development. Qiao et al. [70]and Otten et al.[53] identified age-related developmental considerations and gender differences in health literacy acquisition patterns.

Theme 4: individual factors

This theme captured personal characteristics that moderate health literacy development, identified in eleven studies (61% of the total).

Psychological Factors included motivation, self-esteem, and resilience. Britton et al.[61] demonstrated the central role of self-confidence and motivation in physical literacy development. Jefferies et al.[63] incorporated resilience as a key physical literacy component, while Longmuir et al.[56] emphasized motivation and self-confidence as fundamental elements. Jenkins et al.[58] highlighted how children's self-esteem significantly influenced their engagement with health information and critical thinking abilities.

Cognitive Factors encompassed information processing abilities, executive function, and developmental readiness. Doustmohammadian et al.[4] assessed cognitive dimensions including decision-making and planning abilities in their food literacy scale. Kågström et al.[65] incorporated cognitive elements related to recognizing thinking patterns and their mental health impact. Qiao et al.[70] demonstrated how cognitive and decision-making abilities directly influenced children's health-related quality of life.

Implementation and assessment variations

Significant variability emerged in health literacy teaching and assessment across educational contexts. Teaching approaches ranged from traditional textbook-based instruction to innovative digital interventions. Knisel et al.[68] utilized gamification strategies with immediate feedback mechanisms in physical activity programs, while Baek& Lee[60] employed storytelling and role-playing activities through their LEAD program.

Assessment methods varied considerably, from standardized questionnaires adapted from adult instruments[3] to novel child-specific measures incorporating visual elements and interactive components[59]. This variability highlighted the ongoing challenge of developing appropriate measurement tools for children's health literacy competencies.

Geographic and cultural patterns

Framework usage patterns emerged across different cultural contexts. Studies from Eastern contexts more frequently applied Nutbeam's complete framework, with examples including Doustmohammadian et al.[4] from Iran and Qiao et al.[70] from China utilizing comprehensive tripartite approaches. Western studies more commonly developed hybrid approaches combining multiple theoretical perspectives, as demonstrated by Longmuir et al.[56] from Canada and Jenkins et al.[58] from the UK. This difference may reflect varying educational philosophies and health literacy conceptualization approaches across cultural contexts.

Discussion

This systematic review examined health literacy conceptualizations in children aged 9–12 years, providing an integrative synthesis of theoretical frameworks and empirical evidence from 18 high-quality studies. Through hybrid deductive-inductive thematic analysis, four central dimensions emerged: skills, teaching-learning processes, contextual factors, and individual factors. Each dimension contributes uniquely to a more comprehensive and developmentally appropriate understanding of child health literacy (Fig. 2).

The evolving role of nutbeam’s framework in child health literacy

Nutbeam’s tripartite model demonstrated sustained prominence, being referenced wholly or partially by 77% of included studies, underscoring its continued conceptual relevance [2, 73]. However, our synthesis reveals that significant adaptations are required when applying this framework to children. Studies implementing the complete Nutbeam framework (44%) frequently incorporated additional child-specific elements, suggesting researchers recognize the framework’s inherent limitations for pediatric populations.

Notably, geographic patterns emerged in framework application: Eastern studies more frequently applied complete theoretical frameworks [4, 62], while Western studies emphasized individual skills [58], suggesting cultural differences in health literacy conceptualization that merit deeper comparative exploration.

Functional health literacy in children

The functional dimension was consistently identified across multiple studies [3, 4, 54, 57–60], revealing child-specific adaptations that distinguish pediatric applications from adult models. Wanichsaithong & Prasertsom [59] developed an oral health literacy tool emphasizing visual comprehension and age-appropriate language, while Doustmohammadian et al. [4] focused on food label reading skills adapted for elementary school cognitive levels.

Interactive health literacy: the social learning dimension

Interactive health literacy emerged as particularly relevant for children, with studies emphasizing engagement [55, 58, 60, 63, 66] emotional skills [4, 62], and interpersonal competencies [55, 65]. Kågström et al. [65] developed a mental health literacy scale highlighting peer communication and help-seeking behaviors, while Liao et al. [62] emphasized self-expression and interpersonal management as core competencies. These findings align with developmental psychology principles demonstrating that middle childhood is characterized by increased peer interaction and social learning [20].

Critical health literacy: adapted for concrete operational thinking

Critical health literacy demonstrated interesting adaptations for children’s cognitive capabilities. Rather than abstract critical analysis, studies focused on analyzing familiar contexts [3, 4, 53, 60, 62], evaluation of accessible health information [3, 58, 60, 67], and domain-specific health literacies including physical [56, 61] and mental health literacy [65]. For example, Amin et al. [67] developed a food literacy assessment focusing on children’s ability to evaluate simple nutritional claims rather than complex policy analysis, reflecting the concrete operational thinking characteristic of this developmental stage.

Teaching-Learning processes: A novel dimension

The identification of teaching-learning processes as a distinct dimension represents this review’s most significant contribution, appearing in 50% of studies. This theme extends beyond traditional health literacy models to encompass pedagogical factors essential for children’s learning. Evidence consistently supports experiential, participatory approaches—including gamification, storytelling, digital tools, and peer learning—over traditional didactic methods [74, 75]. These findings align not only with developmental psychology theories emphasizing active learning, but also with frameworks such as the U.S. National Health Education Standards. The latter advocate for age-appropriate, skills-based instruction to promote health literacy. Together, these perspectives reinforce schools’ role as critical environments for both social and cognitive development [6, 53, 76, 77].

Educator competence and Child-Centered approaches

Five studies highlighted the critical role of educator competence and child-centered approaches [53, 59, 60, 64]. Nash et al. [64] examined school leaders’ reflections on health literacy programs, revealing that teacher training and confidence directly influenced program effectiveness. Similarly, Otten et al. [53] demonstrated that teachers’ self-confidence in health education significantly impacted student outcomes, supporting the need for specialized health education preparation.

Age-Appropriate content and curriculum integration

Six studies emphasized age-appropriate content selection and curriculum integration [53, 59, 60, 62–64]. Baek & Lee [60] developed the LEAD program specifically designed around children’s developmental readiness, while Liao et al. [62] emphasized connecting health concepts to children’s real-life experiences. Studies consistently favored interactive, experiential approaches over traditional didactic methods [53, 56], supporting Vygotsky’s zone of proximal development theory, which emphasizes that children learn optimally through scaffolded, interactive experiences [20].

Contextual factors: Socio-ecological influences

Contextual factors emerged in 61% of studies, revealing the embedded nature of children’s health literacy development within broader social systems. While contextual and structural determinants were not the focal point of most reviewed studies, their indirect influence was evident [78].

Socioeconomic influences

Economic conditions and parental education significantly influenced health literacy outcomes [62, 70]. Qiao et al. [70] found that children from higher socioeconomic backgrounds demonstrated superior health literacy competencies, while housing stability emerged as a protective factor. Socioeconomic status, parental education and health literacy, rurality, and access to healthcare services were recurrently cited as shaping children’s exposure to health information and their capacity to process it [79–82].

Peer influences were particularly prominent [59, 65, 67, 70], with Kågström et al. [65] specifically highlighting peer communication about mental health as a critical component of adolescent health literacy.

Cultural and demographic variations

Cultural variations were evident: Eastern studies emphasized family-community integration [62, 70], while Western studies focused on individual competencies (Jenkins et al., 2022). Demographic factors, including age and gender differences, were identified by Qiao et al. [70] and Otten et al. [53], revealing developmental variations within the 9–12 age range requiring targeted interventions. Gender dynamics were minimally addressed, despite emerging evidence suggesting girls often display higher health literacy competencies—potentially due to socialized norms around communication and help-seeking behaviors [83–87].

Individual factors: psychological and cognitive foundations

Individual characteristics emerged as significant moderators of health literacy development in 61% of studies. Individual psychological and cognitive attributes emerged as salient moderators of health literacy acquisition [7, 88].

Psychological components

Self-esteem was particularly prominent across eight studies [53, 56, 58, 61–63, 67, 69] with higher self-confidence consistently associated with greater health literacy engagement. Self-efficacy, cognitive flexibility, emotional readiness, and language proficiency were consistently associated with children’s engagement in health decision-making. Children with higher confidence and autonomy more readily questioned and acted upon health information, whereas those with limited cognitive or linguistic capacity faced comprehension barriers. These findings support Bandura’s self-efficacy theory, demonstrating that children’s beliefs about their capabilities directly influence health literacy acquisition [89].

Cognitive capabilities

Cognitive capabilities, including information processing and executive function, were identified as crucial moderators [4, 53, 62, 65, 70]. Liao et al. [62] emphasized cognitive and social skills as foundational components, while Qiao et al. [70] demonstrated that cognitive and decision-making abilities directly influenced health-related quality of life. These insights highlight that health literacy is deeply intertwined with psychosocial development—not merely a cognitive skillset [90–92]. These findings align with Piaget’s concrete operational stage theory, demonstrating that children’s cognitive development patterns directly influence health information processing capabilities [13, 14].

Implications for theory and practice

Theoretical Implications

The findings support the need for a child-centered health literacy framework integrating developmental psychology principles with educational theory. The four-dimensional model identified provides a comprehensive foundation accounting for: [1] developmental readiness based on concrete operational thinking capabilities; [2] pedagogical mediation through appropriate teaching methods and learning environments; [3] social learning context including peer, family, and community influences; and [4] individual psychological and cognitive variability that moderates learning outcomes.

While Nutbeam’s tripartite model continues as a foundational framework, its application in child health literacy requires contextual refinement through integrating developmental, motivational, and sociocultural perspectives.

Educational practice implications

The prominence of teaching-learning processes directly supports schools as critical platforms for health literacy development [53, 60, 64, 66]. This necessitates a shift from traditional didactic approaches toward interactive, experiential, and developmentally appropriate pedagogies.

Educational interventions should prioritize [1] interactive, experiential pedagogies as demonstrated effective by Longmuir et al. [56] and Otten et al. [53]; [2] age-appropriate assessment methods providing immediate feedback as advocated by Lane & Aldoory [55]; and [3] content delivery connecting to children’s lived experiences as demonstrated by Liao et al. [62].

Policy and assessment implications

Health literacy policies must recognize the multidimensional nature revealed in this review. Policy frameworks should [1] mandate teacher training in developmentally appropriate methods as supported by Nash et al. [64]; [2] ensure equitable access across socioeconomic contexts as highlighted by Qiao et al. [70]; and [3] develop culturally responsive curricula reflecting diverse population needs as suggested by Otten et al. [53].

Assessment tools should integrate successful approaches including visual comprehension for younger children [59], multidimensional evaluation [4], and authentic performance assessment within developmental capabilities rather than abstract knowledge testing [67]. Rather than adapting adult measures, child-specific tools should [1] assess skills within age-appropriate contexts as demonstrated by Wanichsaithong & Prasertsom [59] and Doustmohammadian et al. [4]; [2] evaluate pedagogical effectiveness as shown by Otten et al. [53]; and [3] account for contextual factors as emphasized by Qiao et al. [70].

Limitations and future directions

This review has several limitations. Access restrictions to some potentially relevant full-text articles may have affected synthesis completeness. The predominance of studies from high-income countries (55% Western contexts) limits generalizability to low- and middle-income settings. Language restrictions to English-language publications may have excluded relevant research from non-English speaking regions. Additionally, the methodological heterogeneity of included studies, while enriching the synthesis, limits definitive conclusions about the relative importance of different dimensions.

Future research should: [1] conduct longitudinal studies tracking health literacy development across the 9–12 age range; [2] systematically explore cultural variations in health literacy conceptualization; [3] develop intervention research focusing on pedagogical innovations as suggested by the teaching-learning processes theme; and [4] examine optimal combinations of interactive pedagogies for different health topics. Additionally, more attention is needed on underexplored dimensions such as the role of gender, neurodiversity, and familial influences in health literacy acquisition.

Conclusion

This review demonstrates that health literacy in children aged 9–12 years requires distinct dimensions—particularly pedagogical and contextual factors—that warrant standalone attention. The four-dimensional model provides a comprehensive framework extending beyond traditional adult models to capture the developmental and educational realities of children.

By identifying four core constructs across the literature, including a previously underexplored teaching-learning dimension, we demonstrate that existing adult-centric models like Nutbeam’s, while useful, are insufficient to fully capture the developmental and educational realities of younger populations. These findings necessitate educational policies mandating teacher preparation in interactive pedagogical approaches and curriculum standards that prioritize experiential learning over traditional didactic methods.

The practical implications for educational practice, policy development, and assessment tool creation provide clear directions for stakeholders seeking to enhance health literacy development in children. This four-dimensional framework provides a systematic blueprint for future empirical studies to investigate how pedagogical mediation, contextual embedding, and individual psychological factors interact with traditional skill-based competencies to optimize health literacy trajectories in diverse pediatric populations.
