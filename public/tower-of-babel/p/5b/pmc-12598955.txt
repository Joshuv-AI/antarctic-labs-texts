Association between hospitalisations for ambulatory care-sensitive conditions and primary healthcare physician specialisation: a longitudinal ecological study in Belo Horizonte, Brazil
Authors: Gregório Victor Rodrigues, Marcelo Pellizzaro Dias Afonso, Luis Guilherme de Mendonça, Saulo Pedro, Henrique Pickler da Silva, César Macieira, Veneza Berenice de Oliveira
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesAmbulatory care sensitive conditions (ACSCs) are conditions for which the provision of timely and skilled primary care can reduce risks of hospitalisation when preventing, treating or controlling a disease. For this reason, hospitalisations for ACSC have been commonly employed by health systems as an indicator of effectiveness for the primary level of care. This study aims to evaluate whether the provision of primary care services by physicians with residency training in family medicine is associated with rates of general hospitalisations for ACSCs in the Brazilian Unified Health System network in the city of Belo Horizonte, Brazil.DesignLongitudinal ecological study using a Generalised Linear Model for Gamma-distributed variables.SettingPrimary healthcare centres in Belo Horizonte, Brazil, from January 2017 to December 2021, aggregated at the primary healthcare centres level.ParticipantsData aggregated at the primary healthcare centre level, encompassing socioeconomic, professional and health-related variables.Primary outcome measuresIncidence rates of hospitalisations for ACSCs, adjusted for age and sex.ResultsAfter adjusting for age, sex and socioeconomic variables using the Health Vulnerability Index, a higher concentration of family physicians was significantly associated with a lower incidence of hospitalisations for ACSCs. If all physicians in the primary care network were family physicians, compared with a scenario in which none were, an estimated 11.89% reduction in hospitalisations would be expected (95% CI 7.3% to 16.3%, p<0.05). Subgroup analyses confirmed these results across different levels of social vulnerability (low, medium, high, very high), time periods (prepandemic: 9.59% reduction; 95% CI 3.80% to 15.00%, p<0.05; pandemic: 15.93% reduction; 95% CI 9.76% to 20.91%, p<0.05) and groups of causes of hospitalisation (eg, diabetes: 32.80% reduction; 95% CI 22.5% to 41.7%, p<0.05). Furthermore, an estimated 10.58% reduction in hospitalisation costs was observed (95% CI 2.60% to 17.90%, p<0.05).ConclusionsThe findings suggest that specialisation in family medicine positively impacts health outcomes by reducing hospitalisations for ACSCs. These results can inform the development of evidence-based public policies to enhance primary care effectiveness.

Introduction

Primary healthcare (PHC) serves as the foundational pillar and initial point of contact within well-structured national health systems, ensuring readily accessible, uninterrupted, comprehensive and well-coordinated healthcare services for the population.1

In Brazil, the predominant organisational model for PHC under the Unified Health System (SUS) is the Family Health Strategy (FHS). FHS teams typically include 1 general practitioner, 1 professional nurse, 1 or 2 assistant nurses and 4–12 community health agents, collectively responsible for the well-being of approximately 2000–3500 individuals on average.2

Until recently, the discussion surrounding the need for medical professionals, particularly physicians, in the organisation and expansion of Brazil’s FHS was rarely addressed in debates on the universality of SUS.3 This scenario has shifted with recent federal government investments in initiatives aimed at providing medical professionals for PHC centres (PHCCs), such as the National Program to Support the Training of Medical Specialists (Pro-Medical Residency),4 the Valuation Program for PHC Professionals (PROVAB),5 the Brazilian More Doctors Program (PMM),6 the Doctors for Brazil Program7 and the reestablished PMM.8

However, these programmes have predominantly prioritised the quantitative expansion of medical personnel, often overlooking the qualitative aspect of their training. In Brazil, as in many low- and middle-income countries, there is limited evidence supporting the need for PHC physicians to have specialised training in family medicine. Consequently, policymakers and healthcare administrators frequently uphold the common assumption that PHC is a non-complex field that can be managed by any physician, even those without specialised training.9

In this context, the present study investigates the influence of physicians with Residency Training in Family Medicine (RTFM) on PHC outcome indicators. In Brazil, physicians can specialise in family medicine through a 2-year medical residency programme or by obtaining a certification after 4 years of professional experience in the field.

A commonly used indicator to assess the effectiveness of PHC services is ambulatory care sensitive conditions (ACSCs), which refers to conditions for which timely and high-quality PHCs can reduce hospitalisation risks by preventing, treating or controlling disease progression. Based on the Brazilian list of conditions sensitive to PHCs,10 this study analysed data from approximately 600 000 hospitalisations (2017–2021) in Belo Horizonte, Brazil, geolocated to 152 PHCCs. The objective was to assess whether the presence of family physicians with RTFM, as opposed to generalist physicians without specialised PHC training, was associated with reduced hospitalisation rates for ACSC.11

While previous analyses have highlighted the role of socioeconomic vulnerability in explaining variations in these hospitalisations,12 this study incorporates those factors and expands the investigation by examining the contribution of medical specialisation. By integrating both dimensions—social determinants and physician training—we aim to provide new insights into how the composition of PHC teams can influence health outcomes.

Understanding how medical specialisation impacts health outcome indicators is essential for informing evidence-based public policies in SUS. These insights can contribute to the rational allocation of limited healthcare resources, ensuring their optimal use in strengthening Brazil’s FHS.13

Methods

The present work is a longitudinal ecological study with a quantitative approach, conducted with secondary data from Hospital Admission Authorisations (AIH) related to the population of patients assigned to the coverage area of the PHCC within Belo Horizonte, from 2017 to 2021.

Belo Horizonte is the capital city of the state of Minas Gerais, Brazil. The population estimated by the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics for 1 July 2020 was 2 521 564 inhabitants, making it the sixth most populous municipality in the country, the third in the Southeast Region and the first in Minas Gerais.

The services and health units in Belo Horizonte are distributed territorially across nine health districts. According to the Belo Horizonte City Hall (PBH), the city had 152 health centres served by 596 FHS teams during the study period. Regarding the hospital network, data from DataSUS, illustrated in the graph (figure 1), has established the number of available hospital beds, demonstrating the relative stability of the existence of beds within SUS. In addition to the well-established health network, the choice of this municipality for study is due to its high coverage of PHC services and its high concentration of family physicians (FPs) compared with the average among Brazilian capitals.

The unit of analysis targeted by this research consists of the enrolled population for each of the PHCCs in Belo Horizonte, from January 2007 to December 2021. Hence, each month, each health centre is represented in the database as a row.

Data sources and variables

The data acquired and used in this study were consolidated from five public data sources, namely14: (1) The National Register of Health Establishments (CNES) was used to identify the health professionals allocated in each health centre of Belo Horizonte during each month between 2017 and 2021; (2) The National Medical Residency Commission System (SisCNRM) was used to identify health professionals who completed the RTFM. Thus, in each competency, a boolean marker was obtained to identify whether, at that time, the acting physician had or had not concluded an RTFM; (3) The SUS Hospitalisation System was used to acquire AIH from patients residing in Belo Horizonte between 2017 and 2021, filtering for International classification of diseases (ICD) version 10 codes listed in the Brazilian list of ambulatory care-sensitive conditions10; (4) The 2010 Population Census database was used to identify the number of patients, by gender and age who live in each of the sector’s census data within the coverage areas of the health centres; (5) The Belo Horizonte map site System (http://bhmap.pbh.gov.br/) was used to acquire all the addresses in Belo Horizonte and the addresses of health centres in order to geolocate hospitalisations using the Address Postal Code (CEP), as well as geolocating the population using the Sector Code.

Considering such data, the independent variable refers to the exposure of patients to physicians assigned to PHC teams. According to the education of the physicians, the patients will be exposed to those who have participated or not in the RTFM. However, because hospitalised patient addresses were only available at the postal code (CEP) level, precisely defining PHC teams’ coverage areas was not possible. To address this, the PHCC level was used as the unit of analysis, and the explanatory variable was calculated as the proportion of weekly physician workload for each category divided by the total medical workload of the healthcare team in the entire PHCC. That is, by competency, the variable demonstrates the concentration of each of the categories in the health centre. In addition to the explanatory variable capturing the medical factor, the Health Vulnerability Index (IVS) was also used as a covariate (the index was divided by 100 for better readability) in order to capture possible confounding factors related to social determinants of health. The IVS was calculated based on Demographic Census data, incorporating variables related to sanitation, housing, education, income, infrastructure and socioeconomic factors. Each component was weighted and normalised to classify areas into low, medium, high and very high-risk categories (Belo Horizonte, 2012).15

Similarly, AIH data was aggregated at the level of areas covered by the PHCC. Due to the imprecision of postal code data in the AIH database, a proportional allocation method was used: when a postal code corresponded to multiple PHCCs, which typically occurs at the boundaries of health centre catchment areas, hospitalisations were distributed proportionally based on the number of addresses with the respective CEP, according to publicly available address information retrieved from the Belo Horizonte map site system. Figure 3 demonstrates the process for selecting AIHs for analysis according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Given that hospitalisation data were proportionally distributed rather than assigned to discrete units, the dependent variable was treated as continuous. Furthermore, to modulate time, the variable of years was added as a dummy variable.

The incidence rate ratio of hospitalisations for ACSC was analysed observing the exposure of interest, which refers to the concentration of medical assistants trained in the family medicine and community in the respective health centres in each competency.

Statistical analysis

A Generalised Linear Model (GLM) for Gamma-distributed variables was used, which is suitable for skewed healthcare data. To minimise multicollinearity, we centralised the variables before analysis. Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), and variables with VIF>5 were excluded. A stepwise selection approach16 was used to determine control variables. In the univariate analysis, the variables that present a p value<0.20 were selected for the multivariate analysis using the Forward method. In the multivariate analysis, the Backward method was applied, which consists of removing one variable at a time from the analysis due to its non-significant p value, repeating the procedure until only significant variables remain in the final model. For the multivariable model, a significance level of 5% was adopted.

Based on the final model estimates, hypothetical scenarios (eg, if all physicians were trained in family medicine) were derived by applying the regression coefficients to simulate expected changes in ACSC hospitalisation rates.

Subgroup analyses were conducted to evaluate the effect of physician specialisation within different contexts, including: social vulnerability categories (low, medium, high and very high), classified according to Belo Horizonte16; time periods, categorised as prepandemic and pandemic, with the pandemic period starting in March 2020, as defined by the WHO; groups of causes of hospitalisation for ambulatory care-sensitive conditions (ACSCs), based on the Brazilian list.10

Patient and public involvement

It was not appropriate or possible to involve patients or the public in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

The results of the Gamma GLM coefficients are presented, for all hospitalisations due to ACSC, pooled together into specialised physicians, shown in figure 5. Compared with the baseline year of 2017, this study observed a significant reduction in the incidence of hospitalisations for ACSC, which was associated with a higher concentration of physicians specialised in family and community medicine. To illustrate the magnitude of this association, the model results were interpreted through two hypothetical scenarios derived from the estimated regression coefficients. In the first scenario, 100% of the medical hours within each FHS team were provided by RTFM-trained physicians. Compared with a second scenario in which none of the physicians held this specialisation, the model estimated a significant reduction of 11.89% in ACSC-related hospitalisations (95% CI 7.3% to 16.3%, p<0.05). Conversely, when interpreting the reverse contrast, moving from full RTFM coverage to none, the model estimated a 13.49% increase in hospitalisations (95% CI 7.8% to 19.4%, p<0.05). These findings underscore the potential benefits of a fully specialised medical workforce in primary care and suggest that the absence of such training may negatively affect care quality and increase the demand for hospital services. Furthermore, the study also analysed the effect of the IVS as a covariate. The results showed that for each one-point increase in the IVS (on a scale of 100), there was a corresponding positive effect on hospitalisations for ACSCs, ranging from 3.4% to 3.6%.

To detail the result of the specialist physician, a separate model was performed with each type of specific group of cause of hospitalisation for ACSC. The most substantial and statistically significant reductions were observed for nutritional deficiencies (−39.54%, p=0.010), diabetes (−32.80%, p<0.001), airway diseases (−19.40%, p=0.001), asthma (−19.52%, p=0.003) and angina (−19.17%, p=0.005). Finally, to further explore the potential economic impact of RTFM, an additional model was estimated using hospitalisation costs as the dependent variable, rather than hospitalisation counts. Cost data were adjusted to present-day values using the Broad Consumer Price Index (IPCA) for each year.17 The results showed a reduction of 10.58% in hospitalisation costs (95% CI 2.60% to 17.90%, p<0.05). This indicates that increasing the proportion of medical hours dedicated to RTFM-trained physicians to 100%—meaning that all physicians working in an FHS team have RTFM specialisation—could potentially reduce hospitalisation costs by approximately 10.58% for all ACSC causes.

Discussion

The present study offers important insights into the effects of RTFM on healthcare outcomes in the city of Belo Horizonte. While the findings suggest a positive association between the presence of RTFM-trained physicians and reduced hospitalisations for ACSCs, caution is needed when considering the external validity of these results—that is, their generalisability to other contexts and health systems.18

Nonetheless, from the standpoint of internal validity, the hypothesis that RTFM is the most suitable medical specialisation for delivering effective PHC and capable of reducing ACSC hospitalisations appears to be well supported. This interpretation aligns with previous evidence reported within the Brazilian context,11 19 reinforcing the plausibility of the observed associations. Among the various findings of the study, several deserve particular attention and are discussed in detail below.

An important point to note is that, although the overall effect on hospitalisations for ACSCs associated with the concentration of physicians with RTFM was a reduction, examining each type of hospitalisation for ACSCs separately revealed that the effect of RTFM is not uniform across all conditions. Some conditions showed a significant reduction in hospitalisation risk, suggesting a plausible positive impact of RTFM. Among the most notable cases, diabetes presented a substantial reduction, indicating a strong association between RTFM and improved management of chronic conditions. In contrast, for other conditions—including acute conditions such as infectious gastroenteritis, ear infections and infectious skin diseases, as well as chronic conditions such as anaemia and immunisable diseases—the effect of RTFM was not statistically significant or suggested a possible increase in hospitalisation risk, though without statistical significance.

Additionally, some ACSC conditions presented a significant increase in hospitalisations, which may suggest different interpretations. One possible explanation for the increased incidence of certain ACSCs—such as preventable conditions (eg, tuberculosis, syphilis)—is that the greater diagnostic capacity of well-trained physicians (with RTFM) led to more accurate detection of cases that truly required hospitalisation. This increased diagnostic precision may have identified cases that would otherwise go undiagnosed or be inadequately referred to tertiary care. On the other hand, the lack of significant effects on some ACSCs could be related to systemic factors inherent to the healthcare system, rather than the specific impact of RTFM. For example, the absence of a significant effect on vaccine-preventable diseases may reflect the effectiveness of the SUS immunisation programme, which operates largely independently of physician performance.

The issue of the COVID-19 pandemic was also present during the study period, and evidence shows that the pandemic drastically changed the morbidity profile of hospitalisations.20 Considering the availability of data up to December 2021, our study conducted a subgroup analysis to compare the impacts of RTFM before and during the pandemic, which began in March 2020. When analysing pre-pandemic data only, the study found a significant reduction in the incidence of hospitalisations for ACSCs associated with a higher concentration of RTFM-trained physicians. Similarly, when analysing pandemic-period data only (admissions dated March 2020 or later), the study also found a significant reduction in ACSC hospitalisations linked to a higher concentration of RTFM-trained physicians. Given the overlap of CIs, it is possible to conclude that the protective effect of RTFM concentration observed before the pandemic was sustained during the pandemic period. This finding is noteworthy and may be interpreted as follows: since RTFM-trained physicians are formally educated in comprehensive care, they likely possess greater expertise in managing COVID-19 cases while maintaining the management of ACSCs, even under challenging operational conditions during the pandemic.21 22 Additionally, it is plausible to assume that prior care provided before the pandemic—particularly for chronic conditions—helped maintain disease control to some extent, thereby reducing the incidence of acute exacerbations and, consequently, the number of hospitalisations, despite reduced surveillance during the pandemic.

In addition, the main model includes the IVS variable, demonstrating that social vulnerability is a crucial factor influencing hospitalisation outcomes. The results show an increase in hospitalisations for every 1-point increase in the IVS (on a scale of 100). This indicates that social vulnerability has an even greater impact on hospitalisation rates than the availability of physicians themselves, which is consistent with the findings reported in the literature.23

Taking into account all the points raised, it is important to consider the economic impact of RTFM, as the reduction in hospitalisation counts can have significant financial repercussions, given that hospitalisations place a substantial burden on the SUS coffers. In recent years, the rapid and progressive growth of healthcare spending has raised concerns about the sustainability of national health systems.24 This issue affects all countries, regardless of their level of development, as resources are always limited. Therefore, resource allocation inevitably involves a decision based on the concept of opportunity cost—investing in one area means forgoing the benefits of another alternative investment.25 In the context of human resources, this means that decisions regarding which type of healthcare professionals to invest in should involve a proper cost-effectiveness evaluation. Although this specific cost-effectiveness analysis was not performed in the present study, the indication of potential savings through reduced hospitalisations already provides evidence that RTFM can positively impact the economic dimension of healthcare.

Nonetheless, the present study has some limitations. The most evident of these concerns the study design itself. As an ecological study, it is important to recognise that findings reported at the ecological level do not necessarily reflect associations at the individual level, which could lead to ecological fallacy.26

Another limitation relates to the study population. It is well known that, within the scope of the SUS, the exposed population does not necessarily correspond to the entire resident population, as some individuals are not exclusive users of SUS and may benefit from supplementary health coverage. Consequently, using the total resident population as the denominator, while the numerator only includes SUS hospital admissions (AIH), could potentially lead to bias. Specifically, it may cause an overestimation of hospitalisation rates in areas with lower health plan coverage, while leading to an underestimation of the indicator in areas with greater coverage by health plans. Paradoxically, this limitation may demonstrate the robustness of the findings. Belo Horizonte has shown minimal variation in population size, with an estimated growth of only 9% between 2010 and 2020.27 This growth is also uneven across the municipality, with areas of greater expansion generally corresponding to regions with more unfavourable socioeconomic conditions. As a result, it is possible that hospitalisation rates for more vulnerable areas are overestimated in comparison to other areas, since a more precise denominator would likely result in higher population estimates for these regions. Despite this residual bias, the findings still demonstrated better outcomes for RTFM specialisation, which further underscores the strength of the observed effects. Additionally, the hospitalisation data used in this study were obtained from public databases, which are anonymised to protect individual privacy. Consequently, only the zip code (CEP) is available, rather than the exact residential address. Due to the inaccuracy of the CEP, it was necessary to employ a geolocation method for AIHs, which involved a relatively imprecise approach to calculate the proportion of admissions based on the number of residential addresses assigned to each health centre within the CEP area.28

Finally, some potentially significant confounding factors were not explored in this study. These include more detailed characteristics of healthcare professionals, such as involvement in teaching activities, length of service, time spent with the same team, age and sex of family medicine physicians in residency, and other related factors. Future research should consider incorporating these variables to obtain a more comprehensive analysis of some of the factors influencing healthcare outcomes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the findings of this study highlight the significant positive impact of RTFM on reducing hospitalisations and healthcare costs related to ACSCs. By demonstrating that an increased concentration of RTFM-trained physicians in PHC settings is associated with lower hospitalisation rates and reduced costs, the study provides robust evidence in favour of investing in specialised training for family physicians. However, despite this evidence, systemic and policy-level challenges persist. At the federal level, the Ministry of Health of Brazil, when formulating the National Primary Care Policy (PNAB),29 did not establish the requirement for family and community medicine training to occupy positions in FHS teams. Instead, policies like PROVAB5 have encouraged the occupation of these positions by recently graduated physicians, motivated by residency admission bonuses. This approach contradicts the principle of longitudinality, which is fundamental to primary care. Additionally, the Ministry has focused on investing in programmes such as More Doctors and Doctors for Brazil, without fully considering the recommendations of the Brazilian Society of Family Medicine (SBMFC) for the mass formation of Family and Community Medicine (MFC) professionals to meet the country’s needs.30 31

At the municipal level, similar shortcomings are observed. In cities like Belo Horizonte, there remains an insistence on maintaining traditional assistance models, such as the Semashko model,32 which favours the hiring of clinical doctors, paediatricians and gynaecologists rather than family and community doctors. This fragmented approach to healthcare staffing hinders the implementation of comprehensive and continuous care models, which are central to PHC principles.

Furthermore, it is crucial to address the managerial perspective on human resource allocation in healthcare, which inherently involves considerations of opportunity cost.33 Considering the progressive increase in healthcare spending worldwide, especially in low- and middle-income countries like Brazil, the decision to prioritise specialised training in family and community medicine should be driven by cost-effectiveness analyses and evidence-based policymaking.25

In light of these findings, prioritising RTFM specialisation in hiring practices and public health policies may contribute to improving the quality, efficiency and sustainability of PHC in Brazil.
