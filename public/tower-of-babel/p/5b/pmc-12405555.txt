Preclinical safety and feasibility of a bupivacaine-loaded hydrogel for pain relief after spinal surgery
Authors: Jasper G Steverink, Suzanne Bruins, Floris R van Tol, Hein R Jonkman, Kwame B Amponsah, Jessica Marvela, Andre J Smorenburg, Bas J Oosterman, Susanna Piluso, Jorrit-Jan Verlaan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPain and side effects of analgesics hinder postoperative recovery after instrumented spine surgery. Current locoregional blocks and sustained-release formulations are limited by rapid systemic absorption. We evaluated the local and systemic safety of a bupivacaine-loaded hydrogel, co-implanted with pedicle screws, in a sheep spine surgery model. Gelatin-based ring-shaped hydrogels containing bupivacaine crystals (305 mg total) were implanted with screws in eight sheep (Group C). Control groups included screws only (Group A), screws with unloaded hydrogels (Group B), and subcutaneous (SC) bupivacaine infiltration (150 mg, Group D). Plasma drug levels, systemic safety parameters, and histological host reaction at 6 and 56 days were assessed. All animals recovered without complications. Group D showed a Cmax of 138.3 ± 68.5 ng/mL at 1 h, while Group C had a lower Cmax of 50.4 ± 24.5 ng/mL at 28 h. The elimination half-life (T1/2) in Group C was 6.5 times longer than in Group D (67.6 ± 15.6 vs. 10.7 ± 5.5 h). Histology showed no differences in host reaction between groups. In conclusion, bupivacaine-loaded hydrogel was well-tolerated locally and systemically, with prolonged drug release and lower systemic exposure than SC infiltration. Introduction

Every day, thousands of spinal surgical interventions are performed1. Because of positive effects on patient well-being and increasing healthcare demands, acceleration of the postoperative recovery process is receiving growing attention2,3. However, both pain and side effects of analgesic treatment affect the majority of patients undergoing spine surgery, thereby delaying hospital discharge4–8. Severe postoperative pain typically lasts for 3 days after spine surgery, and can significantly influence surgical outcomes and patient satisfaction8–11. Poorly controlled pain following skeletal surgery is a common clinical problem, and increases the risk of chronic postoperative pain12–14. Currently, treatment of postoperative pain relies heavily on opioids8. Systemic opioid administration can provide effective analgesia, but can lead to a plethora of unwanted side effects such as paralytic ileus, respiratory depression, dependence and addiction15. The rapid increase in opioid consumption over the past 25 years and concomitant increase in opioid-related deaths illustrate the magnitude of these risks16–20. The coexistence of inadequate pain relief and opioid-related side effects highlights an unmet clinical need for improved analgesia after spinal surgery. Various solutions are in development to provide non-opioid analgesia after surgery, and thus reduce the risk of opioid-related side effects. Local anesthetics are employed in wound infiltration, regional anesthesia such as erector spinae plane blocks, and continuous wound infusion21–24. However, all are limited by duration of action or practical issues such as the need for specialized personnel or the use of external cannulas.

To provide non-opioid sustained analgesia and overcome the drawbacks of short durations of action and cannulas, we have recently reported on a ring-shaped hydrogel-based bupivacaine delivery formulation for co-implantation with pedicle screws during spine surgery25. The active ingredient bupivacaine inhibits action potential conduction by blocking voltage-gated sodium channels in sensory neurons, shown to be abundantly present in bony tissues26–28.

The concept of a ring-shaped, implantable drug delivery formulation yields several requirements for successful clinical application: (i) the formulation should be able to encapsulate and slowly release local anesthetics in sufficient doses to provide analgesia for the duration of severe pain following spine surgery;8,10 (ii) the formulation should not interfere with optimal screw positioning and surgical workflow; (iii) the formulation should be composed of a biocompatible and biodegradable material, to prevent local toxic effects or excessive immune system activation that could compromise postoperative recovery29.

In this study, a sheep model for instrumented spine surgery is employed to quantify the systemic pharmacokinetics of the bupivacaine-loaded hydrogel and compare with bupivacaine HCl subcutaneous infiltration30. In addition, the local and systemic toxicity of both the empty hydrogel matrix (E-matrix) and drug-loaded hydrogel (B-matrix) co-implanted with pedicle screws are investigated by histological comparison with the pedicle screw-only clinical reference situation.

Materials and methods

Material preparation

Bupivacaine-loaded hydrogel rings (B-matrix) were prepared as described previously25,31. In short, commercially available gelatin (type A from porcine skin; low endotoxin) functionalized with 3-(4-Hydroxyphenyl)propionic acid (GelTyr), was mixed with oxidized beta-cyclodextrin and photocross-linked in the presence of riboflavin-5-monophosphate and sodium persulfate to obtain the hydrogel matrix. Matrices were then washed and subsequently incubated in bupivacaine solution for drug loading. Following drug loading, matrices were incubated in a sodium bicarbonate 0.1 M solution at pH 8.5 to induce bupivacaine crystal formation. The domed surface of matrices was dip-coated by partial submersion in a poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) solution. Excipient hydrogel matrix rings (E-matrix) were prepared in a similar manner, with the exception that the drug-loading step was omitted from production. Two batches of E-matrix (batches 28 and 29) and B-matrix (batches 30 and 31) were manufactured. Both E-matrix and B-matrix were packed in aluminum pouches and underwent 25 kGy gamma sterilization (STERIS, Ede, Netherlands). Bacterial endotoxin content per unit was quantified using Ph. Eur. Method 2.6.1432.

Quantification of bupivacaine dose and in vitro release

Bupivacaine dose was extracted from B-matrix samples of both batches (N = 3) by incubation of B-matrix in 0.1% formic acid as described previously31. This solution was subsequently analyzed using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) to obtain the bupivacaine dose per unit. In vitro drug release was studied by submerging B-matrix (N = 5 per batch) in 20 mL of 0.01 M citrate buffer pH 6.0 at 37 °C under continuous agitation25. The solution was replaced at predetermined time points, after which residual drug was extracted by incubation in 20 mL of 0.1% formic acid solution. At each time point, the bupivacaine concentration was quantified using HPLC and used to construct a cumulative release curve.

Animals

Ethical approval was obtained from the local ethical committee prior to the start of the experiment (Medanex Clinics, Diest, Belgium, dossier number EC MxCl 2021–176). The experiment and its reporting adhered to the ARRIVE guidelines, EU directive 2010/63 and the Belgian Royal Decree regarding the Protection of Laboratory Animals. All procedures were performed in a GLP-licensed animal facility (Medanex Clinics). All eligible animals underwent parasite monitoring by fecal egg count testing, blood analysis and clinical assessment of health prior to inclusion. Upon arrival at the test facility, animals were allowed a 14-day acclimatization period before the experiment. Before surgery or bupivacaine infiltration, animals were housed in groups. After the procedure, the animals were individually housed in a small pen, allowing contact with other sheep in the same room. Straw bedding was provided in all housings and hay and water were available ad libitum except in case of preoperative fasting. All included animals received a temperature chip.

Twenty-four Swifter ewes with a mean preoperative weight of 75.2 ± 7.5 kg and age of 3.5 ± 0.9 years underwent surgical implantation of eight pedicle screws, described in more detail below. Sheep were randomly assigned to either of the three following groups: pedicle screws-only (A), pedicle screws equipped with E-matrix (B), or pedicle screws equipped with B-matrix (C). Prior to surgery, six sheep underwent subcutaneous infiltration of 30 mL bupivacaine HCl 0.5 w/v%, accumulating to a total dose of 150 mg (group D), serving as positive control (Aurobindo Pharma, Baarn, Netherlands). Following 96 h of plasma samples to quantify bupivacaine levels, a 2-week cool-down period to ensure absence of bupivacaine in the plasma and allow for another health assessment, the six sheep were re-used for the surgical part of the experiment. Surgical intervention

Surgery was performed according to a previously described procedure31. In short, general anesthesia was induced using detomidine 0.04 mg/kg IV and propofol 2–5 mg/kg IV. Rocuronium bromide 0.05 mg/kg was administered IV for muscle relaxation. Antibiotic prophylaxis consisted of amoxicillin 1 g/clavulanic acid 100 mg IV. Perioperative analgesia was provided by IV buprenorphine (6 μg/kg) and meloxicam (0.5 mg/kg). Blood loss and vital parameters were monitored during surgery. The sheep were positioned in the ventral recumbency position, and after shaving, disinfection and draping of the animal’s back, a midline incision was performed. The posterior spinal muscles were then dissected laterally to expose the spinous processes, lamina, facet joints and medial part of the transverse process of L1-L6. Bilaterally in vertebra L2 until L5, 5 mm wide holes were drilled in a 45-degree transversal angle where the transverse process meets the lamina. These holes provided access to the pedicles. Following probing of drilling trajectories to ensure integrity of the medial wall, pedicle screws and, if applicable, test items were inserted. Tightening of the last screw was marked as t = 0. Sheep randomized in group A received eight pedicle screws. Sheep in group B received eight pedicle screws and the same number of E-matrices. Sheep in group C received eight pedicle screws and eight B-matrix, equating to a total dose of 305 mg bupivacaine HCl. In case E-matrices or B-matrices dislodged from their intended location under the screw head, they were replaced. After implantation, the wound was rinsed with 500 mL 0.9% NaCl and closed in layers.

Animal follow-up

Following SC infiltration of 150 mg bupivacaine HCl, plasma samples were retrieved at predetermined time points up to 96 h after administration. Sheep were assessed daily for the occurrence of adverse events. Provided blood values were within normal ranges, sheep that underwent SC infiltration were included in the surgical groups. After surgery, sheep were monitored daily for clinical well-being, body weight and the occurrence of adverse events, and underwent plasma sampling to quantify systemic bupivacaine levels. PK samples were taken at predetermined time points between 0 up to 144 h post-implantation for all animals included in group C (where 0 h is the time point when the last screw has been tightened). Additional PK sampling was performed up to 360 h post-implantation for animals with a planned survival time of 56 days. Plasma samples were analyzed for bupivacaine content by a GLP-licensed facility (Ardena, Assen, Netherlands) and pharmacokinetic data was analyzed by a GLP-accredited contract research organization (DGr Pharma, Oudenbosch, Netherlands) using WinNonLin software. In addition, hematology and biochemistry parameters were quantified pre-inclusion as well as 6 and 56 days after infiltration or implantation. In groups A, B and C, two sheep per group were terminated after 6 days of follow-up. The remaining six sheep per group were terminated after 56 days of follow-up. Termination was performed through induction of anesthesia using detomidine and propofol, followed by intravenous administration of 200 mg embutramide. Structured necropsy and macroscopic examination of the surgical site and specified organs was performed directly after termination.

Histological analysis

In each sheep, two randomly selected screw implantation locations were sent for histopathological processing and analysis by a GLP-licensed contract pathologist (Anapath Services, Liestal, Switzerland). Two of the four instrumented vertebrae were resected en bloc with adjacent muscle attached and intact dorsal fascia. Vertebral levels were fixated in 4% buffered formaldehyde and further trimmed down under fluoroscopic guidance after 7 days to improve fixative penetration, resulting in a final slice thickness of ± 2 cm. After thorough fixation, samples were trimmed in the transversal plane and embedded in poly(methyl methacrylate) resin, sawed by a diamond band saw in transversal sample direction (transversal cut through the E-matrix or B-matrix but longitudinal cut through the pedicle screw). Embedded samples were then ground and polished to a final thickness of approximately 40–60 µm and stained with Paragon (toluidine blue and basic fuchsin). An adapted scoring system, described by ISO 10,993–6:2016(E), was applied by the study pathologist as described in Supplementary Tables S1 and S233. Host reaction was scored both at the muscle and bone level. In addition, device osseointegration, bone resorption and implant degradation at the implant site were evaluated (Supplementary Table S3).

Additionally, the spinal cords of each animal at implantation site, at thoracic and cervical sites and the tissue samples collected from the spleen, liver, kidneys, lungs, heart, aorta, brain, local lymph nodes (lumbar aortic and medial iliac), sciatic nerve, eyes and skin above implantation site were trimmed, processed by paraffin embedding and underwent hematoxylin/eosin (HE) staining according to the ISO 10,993–11:2016 standard.

Statistical analysis

All clinical and histopathological data were collected in Excel (Microsoft Corp, Redmond, WA, USA). All data were reported as mean and standard deviation. Analysis of variance was used to compare clinical outcomes between surgical groups, with post-hoc Tukey’s test in case of significance. Histological toxicity scores were calculated and analyzed as recommended in the ISO10993-6:2016(E) standards33. Statistical analysis was performed using Rstudio version 4.1.2 (Boston, MA, USA).

Results

Characteristics of test items

Two batches of B-matrix and E-matrix hydrogel rings were produced. Due to the similarities in the preparation process, the presence of drug was the only distinguishing factor between B-matrix and E-matrix. B-matrix batch 30 contained a dose of 46.7 ± 3.3 mg and B-matrix batch 31 a dose of 29.0 ± 3.7 mg bupivacaine HCl. In vitro drug release was similar for both batches, displaying a first-order release profile over 72 h (Fig. 2). Endotoxin content was below the detection limit of 10 EU per matrix. In-depth characterization of mechanical and physical properties of the hydrogel rings were reported previously25.

In vitro release curves of both B-matrix batches used in the animal study.

Animals

Postoperative recovery was uncomplicated in most animals. No significant differences regarding blood loss (p = 0.487) and duration of surgery (p = 0.311) were found between groups. Body weights remained stable throughout the experiment in all groups (Supplementary Fig. S1). Four minor adverse events (AEs) occurred in group A (three transient neurological symptoms and one wound infection), six minor (four transient neurological symptoms, one fever and one disturbed wound healing) and one major (persisting neurological deficits) AEs in group B and three minor AEs in group C (two transient neurological symptoms and one disturbed wound healing). The cases of disturbed wound healing were empirically treated with antibiotics. The transient neurological symptoms occurred in the first cases, and a relationship with positioning was suspected. Animal position was changed to ventral recumbency with hind legs extended caudally and hanging from the surgery table for all further surgeries, and no further (transient) neurological deficits were observed.

Hematology & clinical chemistry

To assess overall well-being of animals, and to allow detection of inflammatory processes, blood sampling for quantification of hematological and chemical parameters was performed at baseline and 6 and 56 days after surgery. Outcomes of specific interest were muscle damage markers (CK, LDH and AST) and bone damage markers (calcium), as toxic effects of bupivacaine on muscle and bone tissue have been reported in vitro and in vivo34. Moreover, as implant infection is a feared potential complication in orthopedic surgery, various innate and adaptive immune system cells were quantified35. These included leukocyte, monocyte, neutrophil and lymphocyte counts. As was expected following a surgical intervention with extensive muscle dissection, muscle damage markers significantly increased 6 days after surgery and returned to near-baseline levels after 56 days of implantation, with similar trends observed in all surgical groups (Fig. 3A–C). Similar trends were observed for immune cells (Fig. 3E–H). Serum calcium levels were stable throughout the follow-up period (Fig. 3D).

Bupivacaine plasma levels

To determine the pharmacokinetics of B-matrix and compare with a clinically used bupivacaine HCl solution, sheep in group C and D underwent frequent plasma sampling. The pharmacokinetic results of groups C and D are visualized in Fig. 4 and summarized in Table 1. Mean Cmax in group C was 50.4 ± 24.5 ng/mL, compared to 138.3 ± 68.5 ng/mL in group D (p < 0.01). The highest Cmax recorded was 104 ng/mL in group C, and 240 ng/mL in group D. Median tmax was 28 h in group C (range 8–72 h) and 1 h in group D (range 1–1 h). Appreciating the differences in dose between groups C and D (305 mg vs. 150 mg), the dose-normalized area-under-the-curve extrapolated to infinity (AUCinf) values were 20.6 ± 12.3 and 6.5 ± 1.5 ng*h*mL−1 for groups C and D (p = 0.053), respectively. Elimination half-life was significantly longer in group C compared to group D (t1/2 67.6 ± 15.6 vs. 10.7 ± 5.5 h, p < 0.001). Bupivacaine was still present in the blood in concentrations < 5 ng/mL in three out of six surviving sheep after 15 days.

Histopathology of implant site

To study and quantify the tissue effects of both E-matrix and B-matrix in the surgical wound, implantation sites containing a pedicle screw and test item were macro- and microscopically analyzed and compared to the clinical reference situation (i.e., pedicle screw-only). Host reaction was scored according to ISO-standards at both the bone (Supplementary Table S4) and muscle level (Supplementary Table S5). Compound scores are shown in Table 2. Macroscopically, all samples showed areas of soft tissue focal necrosis and sclerosis around the implant site, which were histologically correlated with necrosis and fibrosis (Supplementary Fig. S2). Atrophy of the longissimus muscle was present in almost all cases. All microscopic samples contained a screw inserted into the vertebral body through the pedicle. In groups B and C, the E-matrix or B-matrix were present as black, acellular material. All samples across the groups showed equal histopathological findings at the implantation site, with interindividual variation in severity.

After 56 days, the host reaction at muscle level evolved to an inflammatory reaction, consisting mainly of macrophages, incidental giant cells and lymphocytes (Fig. 5). In addition, the muscle around the screws showed myofiber necrosis and fibrovascular granulation tissue compatible with wound healing, as well as multifocal presence of hemosiderin and scattered hemorrhages at this time point. At the bone level, minor inflammatory infiltrates with a low degree of neovascularization and fibrosis were observed. Hemosiderin and scattered hemorrhages were present, as well as new bone formation towards the screw that multifocally attached to the screw thread (osseointegration) (Table 2). Minor bone resorption was observed in case of direct contact between bone surface and pedicle screw or ring. No to minimal differences in total host reaction score between groups were present. Necrosis decreased between 6 and 56 days, at which time it was least severe in the B-matrix group. In contrast, bone formation at the implant bed was minimally superior in the B-matrix group, while osseointegration was similar. Bone resorption values were similar between 6 and 56 days in all groups.

Histopathology of systemic organs according to the ISO 10,993–11:2016 standard, annex F did not reveal any systemic macroscopic or microscopic findings during necropsy that could be attributed to the E-matrix or B-matrix. In all samples, the skin directly above the surgical site was histologically characterized by a minor inflammatory infiltrate of mixed cells, most likely associated with the postoperative cutaneous healing process36.

Discussion

This experiment evaluated the pharmacokinetic, hematological, and histological properties of a bupivacaine-loaded sustained release hydrogel (B-matrix) in a sheep model for spine surgery. Properties were compared to unloaded hydrogel (E-matrix) and pedicle-screw only groups. In addition, pharmacokinetic parameters were compared to the kinetics of SC bupivacaine infiltration. The sheep was selected as animal model, as spinal anatomy, body weight and circulating volume are comparable to humans37–43. In addition, the pharmacokinetic properties of bupivacaine in sheep are well-established44,45.

Following B-matrix implantation (group C), bupivacaine plasma levels remained well below known systemic safety thresholds46,47. Median time to Cmax after B-matrix implantation was 28 h. After SC bupivacaine infiltration (group D), all animals reached Cmax at the first sampling timepoint, 1 h after administration (tmax). This likely indicates that the actual Cmax has occurred within 1 h after SC infiltration. Indeed, 30 min is reported as tmax for SC bupivacaine infiltration, meaning that the Cmax values for group D are likely underestimations48. Underestimating the Cmax of SC infiltration hampers precise comparison of bupivacaine administration via B-matrix versus infiltration and is a shortcoming of the present study. Due to the occurrence of a plateau in plasma levels in group C, underestimation of Cmax is far less likely in this group.

We have previously shown that obtained in vitro curves correspond well to release in vivo31. Elimination half-life was significantly longer in group C compared to group D. This significant decrease in elimination indicates the presence of absorption rate-limited elimination, or flip-flop kinetics49. Slow systemic absorption of drugs is a desirable characteristic of a sustained-release formulation, as it indicates the sustained and concentrated presence of drugs at the target site. The anatomical location of B-matrix after implantation resembles that of the erector spinae plane block, a local anesthetic-based nerve block employed during spine surgery which provides significant pain relief and opioid reduction, although these effects are limited to 24 h22,23.

With regards to postoperative recovery, all sheep developed subcutaneous seroma near the surgical wound, which subsided over the course of 2 weeks. Seroma is a commonly reported finding in cattle undergoing surgery, due to the formation of dead space in the subcutaneous compartment after surgery50. Animal studies in which large volumes of dead space are surgically induced report seroma in up to 100% of cases51. Surgical techniques such as quilting could have reduced the incidence of seroma and thereby have improved postoperative recovery52. The incidence of surgical site infection in the present study is within range of the incidence reported in the literature and was equally distributed between groups53. The incidence and severity of the macroscopic lesions at necropsy was similar among the groups, and thus deemed associated with the surgical intervention rather than the presence of B-matrix or E-matrix.

Microscopic evaluation after 56 days revealed an inflammatory response with giant cells. The findings are in accordance with reports on different hydrogel formulations encapsulating bupivacaine for sustained release54. Heffernan et al. encapsulated bupivacaine in a synthetic poly(N-isopropylacrylamide-co-dimethylbutyrolactone acrylamide-co-Jeffamine M-1000 acrylamide) hydrogel at 4 wt.%, a drug concentration eightfold higher than currently used in clinic. The gel was administered to a pig incisional wound and histological analysis was performed after 14 and 49 days. Resembling the histological features in this study, an inflammatory response, characterized by infiltration macrophages and multinuclear giant cells, as well as the formation of fibrous granulation tissue were observed. In the present study, the hydrogel is gelatin-based. Hydrogels from natural polymer are generally well-tolerated by the body and their elasticity closely mimic the surrounding tissues55–57. The ISO 10,993 standard defines a score difference between 0.0 and 2.9 as no or minimal host reaction, 3.0 to 8.9 as slight, 9.0 to 15.0 as moderate and ≥ 15.1 as severe host reaction compared to a reference material33. None of the histological parameters scored in the present study exceed the 2.9-point difference with the clinical reference group of screw-only implantation, indicating excellent local tolerance of both the hydrogel material and the bupivacaine-loaded hydrogel. In accordance with previous animal studies, no additional toxic effects of bupivacaine compared to an excipient-only hydrogel were observed58. Encapsulation of bupivacaine as crystals has been demonstrated to decrease in vitro toxicity, which can provide further explanation for the minimal differences between B-matrix and E-matrix local toxicity25.

Strengths of this study are the robust setup, the use of a large-animal model, and extensive characterization of local and systemic safety parameters. For pharmacokinetic comparability, a clinically used reference group of SC bupivacaine infiltration was chosen. For local and systemic histological and hematological comparability, a clinically relevant reference group of pedicle screw-only implantation was selected. The dose difference between the two batches of B-matrix is a limitation of this experiment and reflects the current developmental stage of the formulation. To prevent underestimation of local toxic effects due to lower doses, only implant sites equipped with the highest-dose B-matrices were microscopically analyzed. Although this study focused on safety parameters, the lack of pain quantification is a limitation. However, pain models in sheep rely on laterality and thus have limited in spinal surgery59. As frequent blood sampling precluded the use of heart rate measurements or behavioral scores to reliably estimate only postsurgical pain in sheep. Moreover, blood sampling was performed more frequently in group C, which would further hamper comparison of pain between groups. The safety of B-matrix has recently been evaluated in a first-in-man clinical study, and implantation did not lead to related serious adverse events60. The study of B-matrix’s analgesic effects is an object of future randomized clinical studies. A recent review on hydrogels for sustained release of local anesthetics listed drug loading and sustained release, biocompatibility, mechanical stability, scalability, sustainability and interdisciplinary collaboration as major determinants of success61. The present manuscript reports a mechanically robust hydrogel constructed from a natural and abundant polymer, capable of encapsulating high bupivacaine doses which are released over a sustained period of time, developed in collaboration with academia.

Conclusion

In conclusion, a robust sustained-release formulation of bupivacaine, designed for use in spine surgery, was developed and tested in a large animal model. B-matrix provided sustained and low bupivacaine plasma levels compared to the reference subcutaneous bupivacaine infiltration. All plasma levels in this study remained well below toxic thresholds. B-matrix and its excipient material were well-tolerated, and all animals recovered uneventfully from surgery. There were none to minimal histological differences in the average total host reaction score at muscle and bone levels among the groups. Therefore, the host reaction at muscle and bone levels were considered associated with the implantation of the screw and non-related to the B-matrix or E-matrix. There were no signs of systemic toxicity that could be attributed to B-matrix or E-matrix. The current findings facilitate clinical testing of B-matrix for safe and effective use in instrumented spine surgery.
