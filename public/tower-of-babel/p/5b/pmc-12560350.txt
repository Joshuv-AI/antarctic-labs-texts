The effect of a Web-Based BF education program for adolescent pregnant women in the third trimester on BF practices
Authors: Veysel Can, Mehmet Bulduk
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIt is crucial to prepare adolescent mothers—who represent a vulnerable group where the importance of breastfeeding is increasingly emphasized—for the BF experience. A method on the rise or increasingly popular for promoting breastfeeding is through web-based education interventions. The aim of this study was to evaluate the effect of a web-based breastfeeding education program, developed by the researchers, on breastfeeding outcomes among adolescent pregnant women in the postpartum period.MethodsThis randomized controlled study involved a sample of 100 pregnant adolescents, aged 15–19 years, who were in their third trimester of pregnancy. The study commenced after obtaining ethical approval and permissions from the hospital where the research was conducted.A web-based breastfeeding education program was administered to the experimental group. Data were collected using a Socio-Demographic Data Form, the breastfeeding Self-Efficacy Scale Short Form (Antenatal Version), the breastfeeding Self-Efficacy Scale Short Form (Postnatal Version), and the LATCH breastfeeding Diagnosis and Assessment Scale. Data collection occurred during the third trimester (pretest) and the 1st and 8th postnatal weeks (posttest). Out of the initial 100 mothers, 90 completed the study.ResultsThe results indicated that adolescent mothers in the experimental group scored higher than those in the control group in the 1st and 8th postnatal weeks. Additionally, rates of exclusive breastfeeding and the intention to breastfeed for up to two years were higher in the experimental group compared to the control group.ConclusionsIt was concluded that the web-based breastfeeding education program effectively improved breastfeeding self-efficacy, breastfeeding success, exclusive breastfeeding rates, and intentions to breastfeed for up to two years.

Background

Adolescent pregnancy remains a global concern for both developed and developing countries. According to the World Health Organization, approximately 21 million girls between the ages of 15 and 19 become pregnant each year [1]. Adolescent motherhood, a consequence of adolescent pregnancy, continues to be a significant social issue, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. Adolescent mothers face numerous challenges during pregnancy, childbirth, and the postpartum period, one of which is the initiation and maintenance of breastfeeding (BF) [2–5].

Both WHO and the United Nations Children's Fund (UNICEF) highlight the critical importance of BF, recommending that BF should begin within the first hour after birth, continue exclusively for the first six months, and then be complemented with appropriate nutrition until the child is two years old [2]. The importance of BF is even greater for infants born to adolescent mothers. Research shows that BF initiation rates and duration are lower among adolescent mothers compared to adult mothers [3]. Low BF rates in this group may be attributed to ineffective BF practices, underscoring the need for targeted education and support [5, 6].

Studies have demonstrated that BF education positively influences adolescent mothers'attitude toward BF, resulting in higher initiation rates and longer BF durations [6, 7]. The prenatal period is particularly important for nurses to prepare and encourage mothers to breastfeed, as it provides a relatively stress-free environment where the mother can gradually and confidently approach BF [5, 8]. During this time, it is essential to develop BF self-efficacy, as it directly impacts BF success. Mothers with low BF self-efficacy often struggle to initiate and maintain BF [9].

One promising way to support BF is through web-based health interventions, which are becoming an integral part of everyday life for national and global populations. Web-based educational programs allow for enhanced communication between patients, caregivers, and health professionals, offering benefits such as improved access to information and increased satisfaction [10, 11]. Digital platforms also provide alternative support systems where women can share experiences and receive social support [11]. To effectively educate adolescent mothers, BF education and counseling methods should be tailored to the characteristics of this age group. Doing so increases the effectiveness and sustainability of education.

Adolescents tend to learn better when their visual, tactile, and auditory senses are engaged. Given their curiosity, self-directed learning, inquisitiveness, and widespread use of technology, web-based programs can effectively improve their health behaviors [10, 12]. Studies have shown that web-based BF education is effective, feasible, and acceptable in improving BF outcomes, promoting BF initiation, enhancing BF education, and encouraging mothers to continue BF [13]. The aim of the present study is to examine the effectiveness of a web-based breastfeeding education program given to pregnant adolescents.

Study design

This study was conducted using a randomized controlled pretest–posttest experimental design, which included one intervention group and one control group.

Study setting

The study was conducted in the Obstetrics and Gynecology Outpatient Clinic of Van Training and Research Hospital, University of Health Sciences, in Van city located in the east of Türkiye, between April and October 2024.

Population and sample

The population of the study consisted of pregnant adolescents, aged 15–19 years, who attended the Health Sciences University Van Training and Research Hospital’s Obstetrics and Gynecology Outpatient Clinics. The sample size was calculated using the G*Power program. A two-tailed t-test was used because the research question was two-tailed. An effect size of 0.8, representing a large effect size, was used, and the significance level was set at 0.05 (95% confidence interval). An allocation ratio of 1 was applied to ensure equal numbers in the experimental and control groups. Based on the power analysis, a total of 84 participants were required (42 in each group). To account for potential dropouts, the target sample size was increased to 100 (50 participants in each group).

Initially, 133 mothers were contacted, but 25 were excluded due to lack of internet access, 1 was excluded due to twin pregnancy, and 7 were excluded because their husbands or mothers-in-law did not provide consent. Pretest data were collected from 100 participants. By the end of the study, data from 90 mothers were successfully collected (n = 47 in the control group, n = 43 in the WEB-BEP group) (Fig. 1). The remaining 10 participants were excluded due to being unreachable by phone (6 mothers), having their newborns admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit (2 mothers), and preterm births (2 mothers).Fig. 1 Consort Flow Diagram

Inclusion criteria

Participants were included in the study if they were aged between 15 and 19 years, had not previously received planned BF education, were between 28 and 37 weeks gestation (third trimester), gave birth at term, had no health issues preventing BF, could read, write, and respond to questions, had access to a computer, tablet, or smartphone, and had regular internet access.

Exclusion criteria

Participants were excluded if they had multiple pregnancies, physical or mental health problems, communication disabilities, a history of breast surgery, or if their newborns were considered high-risk.

Training content

After the BF training content was developed, it was reviewed by experts in the field and subsequently finalized. The training covers a comprehensive range of topics, including the structure of the breast, the role of hormones in BF, the composition and importance of breast milk, the significance of BF, the process of milk production, methods to increase milk supply, techniques for successful BF, the use of syringes for inverted or flat nipples, the duration and frequency of BF, signs of hunger, indicators that the baby is not receiving enough milk, proper positioning of the baby on the breast, holding techniques, methods for expressing milk, feeding the expressed milk to the baby, milk storage guidelines, breast care, common breast issues, and maternal nutrition during BF.

Website design

The website is fully compatible with computers, tablets, and mobile devices, allowing pregnant women to access it from any device (computer, tablet, or phone) at any time. The researcher professionally filmed the videos covering the entire training content. Once the videos were finalized, they were reviewed by experts. Based on expert feedback and recommendations, the videos were uploaded to the website. Pregnant women could log in to the website at their convenience to access and watch the training videos. For the first user login, you can register to the application by entering your e-mail address and creating a password via the sign up tab, and all subsequent logins are made automatically. There are two separate panels on the website, namely user login and administrator login. The frequency of pregnant women logging into the web page, the dates they log into the website, and the duration of their stay are determined by the web counter.

Implementation of the study

A pilot study was conducted with six pregnant women who met the inclusion criteria. During the pilot study, the participants successfully registered on the website, logged in, and watched the videos without any issues. Simple randomization was performed using the Random Allocation Software [19] to ensure homogeneous distribution of participants between groups. According to the randomization list generated by the software, adolescent pregnant women were assigned to the respective groups and included in the study.

After providing detailed information about the study to the pregnant women in both the WEB-BEP and control groups, verbal consent was obtained, followed by written consent through the"Informed Consent Form."In the third trimester, pre-test data were collected by the researcher via face-to-face interviews using the Socio-Demographic Data Form and the BF Self-Efficacy Short Form Scale (Antenatal Version). After collecting the pre-test data from the WEB-BEP group, participants were given access to the website. They were informed that subsequent logins to the website would be automatic and were instructed on how to navigate and use the website effectively.

In the 1 st and 8th weeks postpartum, home visits were conducted for both the WEB-BEP and control group to administer the posttests. During these visits, the LATCH BF Identification and Assessment Scale and the BF Self-Efficacy Short Form Scale (Postnatal Version) were applied. Additionally, participants were asked about their mode of delivery, the time of the first BF after birth, exclusive BF level, Intention to Breastfeed for up to 2 years and the sex of the baby. After the posttest data were collected, any BF issues experienced by the mothers in the control group were addressed and resolved by the researcher.

Ethical Declaration

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the XXX Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee under approval number 2024/04–25. Additionally, study permission was secured from the hospital where the research was conducted. The principle of “Informed Consent” was observed by thoroughly informing the mothers about the study. The principle of “Respect for Autonomy” was upheld by ensuring participants were aware that participation was entirely voluntary, and they could choose to withdraw from the study at any time. The principle of “Confidentiality and Protection of Privacy” was followed by assuring the participants that all information provided would remain confidential. Written and verbal consent was obtained from all participants. Furthermore, the study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki, ensuring the protection of personal rights throughout the research.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 26. Descriptive statistics were presented as numbers (n), percentages (%), means (x̄), standard deviations (SD), and minimum (min) and maximum (max) values. For comparisons of categorical variables, Chi-Square (χ2) and Fisher’s Exact Test (FE) were used, while independent sample t-tests were applied for continuous variables. McNemar’s test was employed for repeated measurements of categorical variables. For repeated measurements of continuous variables, ANOVA (with Bonferroni correction for post hoc tests) was used when there were more than two measurements, and dependent sample t-tests were used when there were only two measurements. The reliability of the scales was assessed using Cronbach’s Alpha coefficient.

To assess the normality of the data, skewness and kurtosis values were examined, and values between + 1.5 and −1.5 were considered indicative of normal distribution. All analyses were conducted at a 95% confidence interval, and a p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Effect size was calculated using Eta squared (η2), with the following interpretations: η2 less than 0.01 indicates a very small effect size, between 0.01 and 0.06 indicates a small effect size, between 0.06 and 0.14 indicates a medium effect size, and greater than 0.14 indicates a large effect size.

While the BFSES scores between the groups did not differ significantly during the antenatal period and were similar (F = 0.41, p > 0.05), there was a statistically significant difference in BFSES scores between the groups at postpartum week 1 (F = 179.3, p < 0.001) and week 8 (F = 183.4, p < 0.001). According to the post-hoc analysis, this difference was attributed to the increase in the mean BFSES scores of the experimental group. The effect size for this increase in the experimental group was large, with η2 = 0.671 at Week 1 and η2 = 0.676 at Week 8, compared to the control group.

In the control group, the difference between the BFSES scores at the antenatal stage (X̄ = 46.1 ± 7.7), postpartum week 1 (X̄ = 48.9 ± 5.6), and week 8 (X̄ = 48.7 ± 8.6) was not statistically significant (F = 2.798, p > 0.05).

In contrast, the BFSES scores in the experimental group showed a steady increase from the antenatal stage (X̄ = 45.8 ± 6.1) to week 1 (X̄ = 62.1 ± 3.1) and week 8 (X̄ = 67.1 ± 2.3). This increase was statistically significant (F = 130.9, p < 0.001). Post-hoc analysis revealed that the 1st-week BFSES score of the experimental group was significantly higher than the antenatal measurement, and the 8th-week score was significantly higher than both the antenatal and 1st-week measurements. This increase had a large effect size (η2 = 0.749).

Table 3 also shows a statistically significant difference in LATCH scores between week 1 (X̄ = 6.2 ± 2.0) and week 8 (X̄ = 7.4 ± 2.4) (t1(88) = 8.883, p < 0.05). This difference had a large effect size (η2 = 0.778). Intergroup and intragroup comparisons were performed to determine whether the difference was due to the experimental group, control group, or both.

At week 1, the LATCH values in the control group (X̄ = 4.7 ± 1.5) were significantly lower than those in the experimental group (X̄ = 7.8 ± 1.5) (t2(88) = 10.717, p < 0.05), with a large effect size (η2 = 0.566). Similarly, at week 8, the LATCH score in the control group (X̄ = 5.6 ± 2.1) was significantly lower than that in the experimental group (X̄ = 9.3 ± 0.5) (t2(88) = 11.188, p < 0.05), and this difference also had a large effect size (η2 = 0.587).

In the within-group comparison, the difference in LATCH scores between week 1 and week 8 in the control group was 0.851, while the difference in the experimental group was 1.558. This difference was statistically significant and was driven by the increase in the experimental group’s mean scores (t(88) = 2.730, p < 0.05), though the effect size for this increase was small (η2 = 0.078).

In Table 4, a statistically significant difference was observed in the number of exclusively breastfed infants between the groups at week 1 (FE = 9.062, p < 0.05) and week 8 (χ2 = 18.984, p < 0.05). Upon analyzing the within-group comparisons, it was determined that this difference was primarily due to changes in the control group. In the control group, a statistically significant difference was found between week 1 and week 8 (p < 0.05). However, in the experimental group, there was no statistically significant difference between week 1 and week 8 (p > 0.05). Discussion

Web-based interventions have been shown to be among the most effective methods for promoting BF and have positive effects on BF rates and outcomes [13, 20, 21].

In the present study, BF self-efficacy and BF achievement of mothers in the WEB-BEP group were found to be higher than those in the control group. BFSES and LATCH scale scores of those in the WEB-BEP group showed a gradual increase at 1 and 8 weeks postpartum (H2 and H3 hypothesis was accepted).

There are only a few studies examining web-based BF education for adolescent mothers. In one study involving adolescent mothers, it was found that BF self-efficacy and BF success significantly increased during postnatal follow-ups in a technology-based BF intervention, with higher scores in the intervention group compared to the control group [21].

A study conducted in Jordan reported that a web-based BF education program delivered during the antenatal period effectively increased BF knowledge, attitude, and self-efficacy after birth [22]. Similarly, in Thailand, adolescent mothers with higher digital literacy were found to have better BF outcomes than those with lower digital literacy [23].

In Malaysia, a Whatsapp-supported BF education program demonstrated that mothers in the intervention group had higher BF self-efficacy and knowledge levels during postpartum follow-ups compared to the control group [24]. In Iran, a smartphone-supported BF education program for primiparous women significantly improved the self-efficacy of the intervention group compared to the control group [25].

In the USA, a smartphone-based multi-behavioral health education and support intervention was found to increase BF levels [26]. A meta-analysis on technology-based BF education, including web-based interventions, concluded that such interventions are effective and feasible for improving exclusive BF rates, BF duration, self-efficacy, and BF success [27]. Additionally, a meta-analysis of seven experimental studies conducted between 2018 and 2021 in the USA, Israel, India, Indonesia, and Brazil, reported that smartphone-supported BF education interventions significantly increased BF rates and success [28].

It has been reported that rates of exclusive BF during the first six months are lower among adolescent mothers compared to adult mothers globally [23, 29]. In the current study, the rate of mothers who exclusively breastfed their infants in the WEB-BEP group was notably high, and the exclusive BF rates at the 1 st and 8th weeks postpartum were higher compared to the control group (H1 hypothesis was accepted). These findings are supported by the literature, which reports that technology-based BF programs, including web-based BF education, increase exclusive BF rates [20, 30, 31].

For instance, a study that implemented a mobile application-based BF education program found that exclusive BF rates were higher in the intervention group compared to the control group at the 1 st and 2nd follow-ups postpartum [20]. Similarly, a study involving web-based BF education reported that exclusive BF rates in the intervention group were higher than in the control group at week 4 [31]. Another study that developed and implemented a mobile BF education program during the antenatal period found that exclusive BF rates in the intervention group were twice as high as those in the control group at the 1 st, 4th, and 6th weeks postpartum [30]. Additionally, a study conducted in Brazil with adolescent mothers revealed that a BF education intervention was effective in promoting exclusive BF for the first six months [32]. Similarly, a study that evaluated the effect of a smartphone-based counseling program delivered by a postpartum multidisciplinary BF support team reported higher BF rates in the experimental group at six weeks and three months postpartum compared to the control group [33].

Despite the World Health Organization's (WHO) recommendation, only approximately 45% of infants worldwide are breastfed up to the age of two years. The BF rate among adolescent mothers remains below this global average [1, 23, 29]. In the present study, adolescent mothers in the WEB-BEP group showed a higher intention to breastfeed up to two years compared to those in the control group. Although there are studies in the literature indicating that BF education interventions can increase BF intention and support BF up to two years, there are also studies suggesting these interventions may not always be effective [20, 32, 34–36]. A study conducted in the USA reported that mothers with a strong intention to exclusively breastfeed for the first six months and to continue BF until two years had higher rates of exclusive BF for six months and continued BF up to two years [37]. Factors affecting breastfeeding duration change as breastfeeding develops. Adolescent mothers face many challenges during their changing motherhood experiences as the child grows and returns to social and work activities. Additionally, spousal support and other sources of social support, lifestyle, level of education, socioeconomic level, internet access and many other factors can affect breastfeeding duration [5, 32, 35, 38]. Extra support is needed for disadvantaged groups such as poverty, living in rural areas, and low education level. [4, 5, 38]. In addition, the family (extended, nuclear), living far from the city center, whether the pregnancy was planned or not are factors affecting the level of breastfeeding due to participation in breastfeeding education [5]. Since the current study was conducted with pregnant adolescents who had similar sociodemographic conditions and internet access, it cannot be generalized to the entire pregnant adolescent population.

However, there are no studies in the literature specifically examining BF duration up to two years among adolescent mothers. More research is needed to investigate the duration of BF up to two years in both adolescent and adult mothers.

Conclusion

Studies focusing on adolescent pregnant women and mothers should be specifically designed, or data on young mothers should be reported separately in studies that include a wide age range. Adolescent mothers have distinct characteristics, needs, and challenges. Therefore, BF education interventions should be tailored to their unique circumstances and planned, implemented, and integrated into nursing services in a manner that engages adolescents. The provision of BF education by nurses in primary health care settings during the antenatal period will enhance the effectiveness of these interventions. Moreover, further research is needed to examine both exclusive BF during the first six months and extended BF up to 24 months.
