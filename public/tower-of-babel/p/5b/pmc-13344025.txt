Exploitation or economic prosperity: corporate activities and Aboriginal self-determination in media discourse
Authors: Jacob Cubis, Cassandra JC Wright, Clement Bresson, Teegan Wattam, Royce Ramsamy, Beau Cubillo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe political and regulatory environment in which people live can have profound impacts on health and wellbeing through practices that prioritise commercial development and interest over public health. For Aboriginal People, this is further undermined through corporate influence over Aboriginal autonomy, political and social participation, cultural preservation, economic and land control and, free prior and informed consent and decision-making capacity over their interests, referred to as self-determination.MethodsThis study applies a content analysis to understand the intersection of this issue within media discourse focused on the Northern Territory of Australia. Articles were located through Factiva and Newsbank searches targeting commercial activities from the extractive resource, alcohol, and food industries that impact Aboriginal People and/or Communities from 2015 to 2025.ResultsOne hundred and sixty-one articles were included in the analysis. The dominant narrative identified in the media articles focused on the economic contributions commercial development provided to Aboriginal Peoples (more commonly found in commercial news outlets and trade magazines), while a prominent counter-narrative focused on harm to Country and failures to consult Traditional Owners (more common in articles by the public broadcaster and Indigenous-owned media). Our findings challenge the notion of a single-victim narrative by illustrating how multiple stakeholders (i.e. industry, government, media, and at times civil society) collectively influence public discourse, which can either hinder or promote self-determination. Further, centring self-determination requires a suspension of assumptions about what is best for Aboriginal Peoples.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12992-026-01218-8.Keywords: Commercial determinants of health, Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Health, Corporate political activities, Australian media

Positionality statement

The lead author of this study, JC, is a Larrakia and Wadjigan Aboriginal man from the Northern Territory (NT) of Australia with a background in criminology and experience working within the NT legal system. The second author, CW, is a non-Indigenous mid-career researcher with expertise in alcohol and other drugs, public health, and commercial determinants of health. The third author, CB, is a non-Indigenous early career researcher with a background in community-based entrepreneurship research and twelve years’ experience in Aboriginal Community and enterprise development in remote NT. Author TW is a Larrakia and Wadjigan woman from the NT with expertise in speech pathology and health science. Author RR, a Kuku Yalanji Aboriginal man from Queensland, Australia, has expertise in Aboriginal health research, including in alcohol and other drugs. Senior author BC is a Larrakia and Wadjigan Aboriginal man from the NT with expertise in Indigenous health and wellbeing, Indigenous research methodologies, and the commercial determinants of Indigenous health and nutrition. This research study was undertaken on Larrakia land in Darwin, NT, Australia, but had a wider focus on the NT with global significance for Indigenous People. Despite authors JC, TW and BC being accepted members of the Larrakia Nation and Bulgul Community, we undertake this research study with the intention of not speaking on behalf of our Indigenous communities but rather from the position of Aboriginal individuals who have lived experiences with commercial developments. The positionality of this authorship team enables insightful attention to Aboriginal governance, leadership, and relationships in the NT. With four of six authors being Aboriginal, we are also appropriately positioned to interpret the framing and representation of Aboriginal People and communities. Our team incorporates public health and other disciplinary backgrounds, enabling insider-outsider reflection on the study’s contribution to the public health field.

Introduction

Throughout this article, the term (‘Indigenous’) is respectfully applied to reference the global Indigenous Community, while (‘Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander’) is used to explicitly reference Indigenous Peoples of Australia. It is acknowledged that these terms do not fully capture the unique language, social, cultural, and political diversity of Indigenous Peoples; we respect the inherent rights and ongoing connections that Indigenous Peoples have to the lands and waterways on which they reside [1]. The term (‘Aboriginal Communities’) may be used throughout, reflecting that while Torres Strait Islander individuals and families may reside in the NT, the Traditional Owners1 in the NT only refers to Aboriginal People’s.

Studies have illustrated that media reporting on Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples and affairs in Australia has largely been framed through a lens of deficit, with narratives of failure, dysfunction, and violence [2, 3]. This is a global experience of Indigenous People with studies from other colonised countries producing similar findings [4]. Evidence further suggests that Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander health topics attract media coverage that promotes racial stereotypes and often lacks Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander voices [5, 6]. While this is likely influenced by public and policy discourse more broadly, it may be further compounded by framing in health literature, which has also been found to focus on the failures of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander People to improve their health and wellbeing outcomes, with focus more commonly on individual responsibility than the systemic inequalities and structural racism that intersect with and shape health and social outcomes [7]. The emphasis on individual responsibility additionally silences the influences of economic, environmental, and political determinants on the health and wellbeing of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples, despite substantial evidence illustrating the magnitude of their impacts on populations [8, 9], these framings can be amplified through mainstream media [10]. The news media, in turn, play a role in shaping the Australian public’s perceptions and beliefs, which contribute to the construction of attitudes towards Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples, Communities, and affairs [10–12]. Beyond mere stigma, shaping public narratives is a critical tool of colonisation worldwide which establishes and maintains power structures and racial hierarchies [12].

The media is therefore both a reflection of public narratives as well as a tool that can be used to create it. Both have implications for political power of Indigenous Peoples. A 2019 study examined mainstream media reporting relating to 11 key initiatives and events in Australia over a 45-year period in which Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander People sought self-determination and justice, such as the Barunga Statement and Treaty 1988 campaign, Apology to the Stolen Generation 2008 and Uluru Statement from the Heart 2017 [13]. [13]. While there are Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander specific media platforms that all provide narratives that challenge the stereotypes and negative discourse, such as National Indigenous Television (NITV), a subsidiary of Special Broadcasting Service (SBS), Koori Mail, Australian Broadcasting Corporation (ABC) Indigenous and National Indigenous Times, these entities sit outside of mainstream influence. Meanwhile, the state of affairs for mainstream media in Australia has been heavily criticised due to a lack of diversity in ownership, with almost all media being owned by only three corporations [14]. This has implications including practices such as reproduction of articles across publications, lack of local voices and potential biases stemming from vested interests of owner corporations as recently highlighted by UNESCO Expert Mechanism on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples conducting a global study to strengthen media with Indigenous voices [15].

The political and regulatory environment in which people live can have a profound impact on health and wellbeing through practices that prioritise commercial development and interests over public health [16]. Commercial actors (i.e., organisations, businesses, non-profits) are known to undertake actions aimed at reducing cost, increasing profits, or promoting political or reputational goals [17]. These activities, sometimes termed ‘Corporate Political Activities’ (CPA) [18], can influence public policy and have an impact on society or the wider economy. One pathway to this is through influencing agenda setting and debates [19], which can contribute to shaping the public’s perception of who is responsible for causing health issues [20]. This is only one aspect, albeit important, of the Commercial Determinants of Health (CDoH), which encompass the systems, practices, and pathways used by commercial actors that impact people’s health and wellbeing, either positively or negatively [21]. Emerging evidence suggests that CDoH are having a profound impact on Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander health, wellbeing, and self-determination principles by shaping the environment in which Aboriginal People live [22, 23]. Research has identified industries such as tobacco, unhealthy food, fossil fuel, and alcohol as being responsible for at least a third of global deaths per year [21]. While there has been limited research to date on the Indigenous CDoH globally [22], early research suggests that factors such as colonisation, cultural and social disruption and systemic inequities are amplifying the CDoH contribution to health disparities [23, 24]. While there is ample international research that identifies the influence of CPA and corporate actors on policy and the potential for this to impact health outcomes through a range of actions and objectives [18], the explicit understanding of this from an Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspective is not well documented.

A previous study conducted by Crocetti et al. examined the representation of key commercial industries, companies, and activities influencing Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander health in Australia, focusing on media representation of commercial activities and representation of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander People [25]. The study highlighted three case studies that occurred between 2018 and 2022, which predominantly showed the negative impacts of commercial activity on Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander People. However, the authors acknowledged that the study was limited by its explicit focus on health in the search terms, which may not fully encompass Indigenous understandings of health. This study therefore aims to further build upon this work by gaining a deeper understanding of how the representation of commercial actors and activities is framed in relation to Aboriginal organisations, people, and communities, particularly in the NT.

Methods

Context and setting

This study focuses on the NT, a federal Territory comprising 20% of Australia’s landmass but accounting for only 1% of the Australian population. Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander People’s account for 26.3% of the NT population compared to 4% nationally [26]. Aboriginal People play a major role in the economy of the NT, through the ownership of approximately 47% of the NT’s land mass and 80% of intertidal zones through the Aboriginal Land Rights Act (ALRA) 1976 (Cth) [27] 5% under the Native Title Act 1993, which are managed through land councils representing the Traditional Owners of their respective regions. The passing of ALRA led to the establishment of four Land Councils in the NT (Northern Land Council, Central Land Council, Tiwi Islands Land Council and Anindilyakwa Land Council). The Land Council’s role is to ensure that Aboriginal Traditional Owners exercise their rights under ALRA to prior, informed consent when engaging in social, commercial, and economic development activities taking place on land owned under ALRA [28].

The extractive resource industry represents a significant proportion of the NT Gross State Product, approximately one-third [29]. In recent years, there has been much political debate about allowing hydraulic fracturing techniques (‘fracking’) in the NT. A moratorium was initially put in place by the Labor Government following its election in 2016, before the (‘Pepper Inquiry’) was conducted in 2018, which made recommendations to reduce the risks associated with fracking. The government at the time committed to implementing measures that paved the way for fracking exploration to commence and supported the development of necessary infrastructure [30]. The Country Liberal Party Government, elected in 2024, committed to supporting the development of fracking and major mining projects in general. Alcohol-related policies have also received significant attention in recent years. The Australian Government Parliamentary Joint Committee on Human Rights (2016) led to a 2018 review of NT alcohol legislation (‘Riley Review’), which resulted in policies affecting alcohol retail licensing and pricing [31], some of which have since been repealed. In relation to food insecurity in remote Aboriginal communities of the NT a major focus has been placed on responding, with a parliamentary inquiry exposing the impact of food insecurity in 2020. This contributed to the development of a National Strategy for Food Security in Remote Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Communities [32]. All represent major events that have changed policies affecting Aboriginal Peoples in the NT.

Many colonised countries have their own ‘version’ of the NT, a remote area never heavily populated by settlers due to environmental conditions, but which is of interest to corporations due to resources, land access or unique opportunities otherwise (i.e., Northern Territories of Canada, and regional area of Alaska, United States). Despite global differences in land ownership and rights, Indigenous Communities in regional and remote areas of other colonised countries face similar challenges and opportunities in relation to corporations’ activities and government regulation over how land is used.

Theoretical approach

The Indigenous inquiry lens applied to this study positions the researchers within a critical space that aims to unsettle dominant colonial paradigms and the validity of knowledge rooted in Eurocentric epistemologies and ontologies by centring the lived realities of Aboriginal People through acknowledging their ways of knowing, being and doing [33, 34]. Four authors of this paper are Aboriginal People, JC, TW, RR and BC, residing in the NT, with three, JC, TW, and BC, being relationally connected to several Aboriginal groups (Larrakia and Wadjigan) within the NT. The Aboriginal authors integrate this identity into the research as Aboriginal People who are not just researching Aboriginal affairs but are faced with the realities of commercial activities impacting themselves, their families, and their communities. This research was grounded in the assumption that the relationship between corporate activities and Aboriginal self-determination is not a straightforward or linear process, but a complex web of interactions by a range of actors that have real-world impacts. It is recognised that commercial activities have challenged Aboriginal self-determination and governance; however, it is also acknowledged that these activities contribute to Aboriginal employment, capacity building, and financial contributions to organisations and Traditional Owners. This study employs this reflexivity and positionality to offer a nuanced understanding of the complex social and political implications of this research for Aboriginal People in the NT, from the perspective of those who live these realities [35].

Study design

This study applies a content analysis to online news media focused on extractive resources, the alcohol and food industry, and actors and activities impacting Aboriginal People in the NT over a 10-year period, from 2015 to 2025. These industries were selected following a preliminary scoping of areas that have been subject to significant events attracting political inquiries and substantial media interest. This includes, but is not limited to, the Pepper inquiry into hydraulic fracturing [30] the Tiwi Islands legal challenge to the Santos Barossa gas pipeline in the Timor Sea, the Gilbert review into the development of a Dan Murphy’s store commissioned by Woolworths group, the NT Government’s review on alcohol polices and legislation [31] and the Australian Government Parliamentary inquiry into Remote Food Security [32].

A content manifest analysis method was employed in this study, which focuses on analysing the explicit, surface-level communication and framings of news media articles through identifying, counting and coding visible data [36, 37]. Data were drawn from media articles due to the role the discourse has in shaping public understanding and perception of issues that impact public policy. Online news media was targeted as a prime news source accessed by 79% of Australians [38]. Trade magazines were also included as a key media source, wholly produced by industry actors, which may contain additional information about CPA and the framings of these activities by actors from the included industries themselves. A 10-year timeframe was selected as public policy scholars recommend this as the minimum time to examine a full policy cycle and corresponding beliefs about policy issues [39].

Search strategy

The search strategy targeted Australian online news media from July 1st, 2015, to July 1st, 2025. Sources included Factiva and Newsbank. The primary source was the Factiva (Dow Jones) database, which included all major Australian news media publications except the ABC. Due to this limitation, the search terms were applied in the Newsbank Incorporated database, limiting the source to only ABC articles. Search terms combined using Boolean operators to join three key concepts: 1) the industry of interest, 2) Aboriginal Peoples and 3) Northern Territory, with separate searches for each industry. For example, the following combination of terms was used to identify extractive resources industry target articles, (“mining” or “mine*” or “extractive resource*” or “mineral” or “energy” or “renewable*” or “resource extract*” or “fracking” or “exploration”) AND (“sector” or “compan*” or “commercial” or “firm” “peak bod*” or “business*” or “enterprise*” or “corporat*” or “organisation” or “Industr*”) AND (“Aboriginal” or “Indigenous” or “First Nations” or “First Peoples” or “Traditional Owners”) AND (“NT” or “Northern Territory”) NOT “breaking news” NOT “blog”. Settings to limit articles to news articles and trade magazines, the English language, the Australia region, and a 10-year timeframe were selected. See (Additional File 1) for full details.

Article selection

Articles were included if they were: online news articles; if they were published between 01 July 2015–01 July 2025; published in an Australian news media source; published in English language; contained reference to the NT; contained representation of corporate activities by the alcohol, food, or extractive resources industries and if they referred to Aboriginal People or Communities of the NT. Articles were excluded if they were other article types such as blogs, breaking news, websites; if they were published outside of this date range; were published in outlets from outside of Australia; and did not contain reference to either corporate activities by the relevant industries or to Aboriginal People of the NT.

Articles were first title screened by an Aboriginal first author, JC. Those meeting the inclusion criteria were downloaded. Duplicates were manually removed. Full-text screening of the included articles was independently conducted by two reviewers: one Aboriginal author, JC, and one non-Aboriginal author, CB. Conflicts were resolved by the Aboriginal senior author, BC. See (Additional File 2) for the reason for exclusion that was provided for all full texts.

Data extraction and analysis

Data were extracted into an Excel document by two researchers, independently, JC and CB, using a coding framework developed by JC, CB, and BC. Codes incorporated elements from several key concepts derived from (Ulucanlar’s CPA strategy model), as well as information specific to Aboriginal Nations and Organisations including relevant government policies and legislation or programs referenced in the media articles. Furthermore, data on framing and representation were extracted using a framework that adapted concepts from Russell [40] and Crocetti et al.‘s [25], approach that considered framing relating to harms, benefits for Aboriginal People and Communities, causation (what/who was identified as the main problem), connotation (positive, negative, or neutral tone) and whose voices were represented (Aboriginal People, Community, Leaders, or industry or third party) and any proposed solutions and responsibilities. The articles were each read line-by-line, and phrases that were linked to one of the frameworks parents’ headings were extracted into the relevant column. Each phrase could be coded multiple times where relevant to multiple codes. Any disagreements relating to the tone of mixed codes were resolved through a process of discuss with senior author BC, as this research is theoretical positioned within an Indigenous lens the reliability and tone is related to Indigenous ways of knowing being and doing. This was measured not against statistical inter-code reliability but rather how Indigenous lived realties were reflected and framed within the media portraying to governance, aspiration and priorities, leadership, engagement benefit to community; this was adapted from Hartfield et al. [41]. The data were collated and synthesised by the first author, JC, and the second author, CB, then cross-checked and discussed with co-authors a narrative review approach was applied for this step [42, 43].

Results

A total of 8,429 articles were identified (7,739 from Factiva and 690 ABC articles added from Newsbank). 1,912 duplicates were removed. A further 6,262 articles were excluded in the title screening process. The remaining 255 articles were independently screened for full text. Full text screening resulted in 94 articles being excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria. Characteristics

Within the articles that relate to the extractive resources industry, significant media coverage included fracking in the Beetaloo basin located in the Central Northern part of the NT (n = 25) by Tamboran Resources and Beetaloo Energy Australia, formerly Empire Energy. While Origin Energy initially held shares in the Beetaloo Basin for fracking, the company sold its shares to Tamboran to pursue cleaner energy opportunities. Other media coverage of extractive resources includes the development of the Barossa offshore oilfields by South Australia and Northern Territory Oil Search (Santos) and the subsequent sea pipeline project located in the Timor Sea, which aims to replace the Bayu-Undan oilfield and supply natural gas to the processing facilities at (‘Darwin LNG’) an onshore export terminal (n = 21). Another case with significant media coverage involved Energy Resources Australia (a majority shareholder of Rio Tinto) attempting to negotiate with Traditional Owners (the Mirarr People) and shareholders to restart the previously closed Ranger uranium mine, located 230 km East of Darwin near the township of Jabiru (n = 12). Most extractive resources projects reported in the media took place in remote areas (n = 76), followed by offshore areas (n = 21), with only six articles reporting on activities in the urban Darwin area. Alcohol industry media representation was dominated by a proposal to build a Dan Murphy’s store in Darwin (n = 24) by Woolworths Group Limited (which demerged from Endeavour Drinks in 2021), with only a limited number of articles in remote settings (n = 3). Food industry-related articles were primarily focused on NT’s remote communities (n = 19), with the majority focusing on food insecurity and remote stores, and lacking representation from major food companies. Articles frequently referred to important legislative acts or inquiries that impact Aboriginal interests in the NT included the Aboriginal Land Rights Act 1976 (NT) (n = 14), Offshore Petroleum and Greenhouse Gas Storage Act 2006 (Cth) (n = 9), Aboriginal Sacred Sites Act 1989 (NT) (n = 7) and the Native Title Act 1993 (NT) (n = 5). Throughout the included articles, all four Aboriginal Land Councils in the NT, the Northern Land Council, Central Land Council, Tiwi Land Council, and the Anindilyakwa Land Council were referenced. In addition to this, Aboriginal Community Controlled Organisations (ACCOs), such as Danila Dilba and the Aboriginal Medical Services Alliance Northern Territory (AMSANT), were also represented alongside individual advocates.

The dominant narrative identified in the media articles focused on the economic contributions commercial development provided to Aboriginal Peoples (more commonly found in commercial news outlets and trade magazines), while a prominent counter-narrative focused on harm to Country and failures to consult Traditional Owners (more common in articles by the public broadcaster and Indigenous-owned media).

Key concepts positioned within the media analysis

Six key concepts relating to corporate development and activities impacting Aboriginal People were constructed from the (n = 161) news media articles: Impact of commercial activities on Aboriginal health and wellbeing; Socio-economic outcomes; Corporate social responsibility; Engagement and partnerships; Third-party activist engagement and Policy and government intervention.

Impact of commercial activities on Aboriginal health and wellbeing

Aboriginal concepts of health and wellbeing, such as connection to culture, Country, body, spirituality, mind and emotions, community, family, and kinship [44], were represented across articles on all three industries, in both positive and negative frames. The media articles cite the harm that the extractive resource industry may cause to Aboriginal Country. For example, in one article a Garawa Elder expressed that “the company (Glencore) is trying to destroy it (sacred site) and we feel really afraid about it and we don’t want another one like that Western Australia one (Juukan Gorge) were they (Rio Tinto) damaged the site (sacred site), just blew it up” (Para 1345). Articles also outline that corporate actors (i.e., ERA, a major corporate actor) attempted to continue their practices by applying for a renewed lease for the operation despite Traditional Owners voicing concerns about the potential harms to Country from the Jabiluka Uranium deposit in which their majority shareholder, Rio Tinto, voting against it in support of Mirarr Traditional Owners’ opposition [45]. In contrast, other articles covered findings from the 2018 Pepper Inquiry into hydraulic fracturing, which concluded that the risks of fracking to the environment could be adequately managed. Statements from corporate actors attempted to demonstrate that no harm was done to Aboriginal Country. For example, the NT director of the Australian Petroleum Production and Exploration Association published an NT news article stating that any potential environmental risk can be managed safely [46], despite concerns raised by Traditional Owners. In a separate case, a 2021 ABC article covered the Anindilyakwa Aboriginal Corporation (representing the Traditional Owners on Groote Island) regarding a study that found high levels of manganese in hair and nail samples of local Aboriginal People, attributed to the potential dust from the South 32 Gemco mine site [47]. The article reported that South 32 Gemco immediately addressed the dust issue.

Several industry-related media articles reported on the potential risk of harm caused by alcohol outlets being established in Darwin near three Aboriginal dry communities, with the voices of Aboriginal health organisations prominently featured compared to the voices of community members. An article published in the NT News states that the National Aboriginal Community Controlled Health Organisation (NACCHO), Northern Territory Council of Social Services (NTCOSS) and Australian Medical Association (AMA) issued an open letter saying the alcohol outlet risks undermining progress made since the introduction of government reforms to tackle the NT’s high rates of alcohol-related harm, in the open letter it is stated that “We know that the more alcohol you sell, the more harm it causes in our community, We ask you to stop this plan to profit from People who are at their most vulnerable” [48]. The alcohol industry activities within media articles identify the harms associated with alcohol, which include Fetal Alcohol Spectrum Disorder, road safety concerns, domestic violence, food insecurity, and family conflicts [49]. These articles frequently featured short quotes from corporate actors whose statements typically focused on their responsible practices. This is highlighted through Woolworths and Endeavour moving the proposed site of the Dan Murphy’s outlet, “We’ve done the right thing; we have looked for and committed to another site. Now I’m calling on you to support us” [50]. 7). The food related articles primarily represented remote store suppliers’ and community voices on the challenges of food security, and the essential service that remote stores play in having positive impacts on health and wellbeing outcomes in Aboriginal Communities. Several issues contributing to food insecurity is raised within the articles, such as supply chains and high prices for essential items. An article published in the Daily Mail Online in 2024 reports that a mystery shopper who conducted research in remote NT and WA identified that the prices of essential everyday food items were higher than in non-remote regions. As stated by an Aboriginal Tiwi Island resident, “I didn’t realise that it was that much to support a little family, you want to be independent, but you can’t because the prices at the shops are really high” [51]. The articles that discuss the potential solutions and frame the food industry (i.e., retail stores) as providing a path forward but little focus on the responsibility of government and the private sector evident through the absence of big food companies.

Socio-economic outcomes

The articles present corporate development and activities as providing socio-economic benefits for Aboriginal communities, the wider NT, and the Australian economy. Quotes attributed to corporate actors and, in some cases, Aboriginal People described developments as increasing the availability of jobs and training for Aboriginal People, especially in remote regions, predominantly by extractive resource industries. For example, an article published in the NT News in 2016 reports on Australian Abrasive Minerals Pty Ltd working with the Atitjere community in Central Australia, conducting “several training modules with the Atitjere community and (…) expanding these to ensure interested community members will be qualified for employment once mining commences” [52]. Media articles quoted corporate actors promoting the number of local Aboriginal People employed through their developments, such as the 230 positions with Jemena secured by people from Aboriginal Communities [53], or the jobs Empire Energy has created in the Beetaloo Basin, but also “in Borroloola, Cape Crawford, Katherine and Darwin” [54]. A Mudburra-Jingili Traditional Owner was quoted saying that Origin “was giving jobs to anyone willing to work in Elliott and Marlinja communities” [55]. Corporate developments have been reported in the media as having changed the opinions of several Aboriginal People about fracking: as one of the Traditional Owners puts it, “I was really against fracking three or four years ago; I was one of the People that went to Canberra to go (protest) against fracking. What changed my mind was that I came here, and Empire gave me employment to come and see for myself what it’s all about and it’s pretty good” [54]. Various corporate actors and their Aboriginal partners also talk about the future jobs to be created, such as the “The Jawoyn Association said the agreement (with miner Vista) will create jobs and extra royalties for the association” [56]. The benefits of royalty payments are discussed as an important socio-economic outcome for Aboriginal Peoples throughout extractive resource articles. These are primarily described as positive and building the local economy, described as “sit-down money” [57]. One Traditional Owner working for Empire Energy is quoted as stating that “this (the gas project) will benefit employment for my people and opportunities in the future for construction and roads, better schooling, and more housing opportunities. Right now, we’re struggling with a lot of stuff and hopefully this will contribute to better opportunities for generations” [54]. Santos describes that training, education and employment are “a universal foundation for human flourishing, (…) the building blocks for individual social and economic empowerment, and just as importantly, for stronger, more resilient families and communities” [58].

In a separate article on fracking the Beetaloo Basin, the executive director of the Menzies Research Centre (a think tank affiliated with the Liberal Party) argues that the gas industry can bring the “benefits of modernity” to the negatively framed local Indigenous population [57]. “Darwin could be transformed from a government-funded service centre for Indigenous misery to the new Dallas (Texas USA), or at the very least something resembling Perth, a city where lawyers, accountants, engineers and truck drivers live comfortably on the back of mining” [57]. Community-owned enterprises, a different type of corporate actor, are also reported to deliver significant benefits through their operations. This is, for example, the case of the Gumatj-operated bauxite mine in East Arnhem Land, described as a holistic operation in which “the stringybark trees the Yolngu (Aboriginal People group) clear to make way for mining are milled by Yolngu workers and sold for roof trusses and as kitchen chopping boards. The Yolngu make their own bricks, build their own houses” [59]. Traditional Owners attribute reduced youth suicide in the community to the enterprise [59].

In an alcohol focused article, Traditional Owners describe their fight to resume control of the local social club, which was taken over by the local council. A Traditional Owner is quoted as saying, “On one side we have the Territory Government being so supportive of our community enterprise, but on the other side our local council doesn’t seem to get it that our enterprise needs to be in our hands … . We’re the ones creating new jobs, improving housing, building new community facilities, supporting youth and culture. We haven’t received a penny from the club profits in years, which threatens our capacity” [60]. The food industry in the media articles was framed as a challenging aspect of remote community life, with food security a top concern. One media article published in Food and Drink Business in reports on awards promoted by Coles celebrating businesses, including Australian Garlic Producers, with its “Initiatives with the Warrabri Aboriginal community at Ali Curung in the Northern Territory” [61]. The Article reports that “the company is in the process of developing a sustainable garlic industry resulting in future employment opportunities for the community” [61]. The benefits of employment are also reported in the case of the community stores featured in the set of media articles, such as the Barunga store [62].

Corporate social responsibility

The articles refer to Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) measures relating to Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples. This is particularly explicit in the extractive resource industry is particularly present, with claims in articles such as “the gas industry takes its social license seriously” [46]. One key measure described was training and employment of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander People and subcontracting practices to Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander-owned businesses. Other cases of CSR are diverse and are reported to occur through formal negotiations and informal approaches. Sporting initiatives are mentioned in various articles, with Origin Energy funding a football oval in Elliott [55], Tamboran upgrading the Elliott oval as well and “kitting out the new Elliott Hawks AFL women’s team as well as the men’s” (Smith, 2023b, para. 27). An article in The North West Star describes pipeline builder Jemena’s community contributions to eight organisations in the Barkly and Mount Isa regions, including support for Anyinginyi Health Aboriginal Corporation in Tennant Creek (Barry, 2018). Tamboran also stated they would purify Elliott’s water supply [63]. A major initiative reported on several times is the establishment of the $10 m Barossa Aboriginal Future Fund, which aims to “improve health, education, housing, community resilience and economic outcomes for Aboriginal people in the Northern Territory. It would also invest in programs that enable Aboriginal people to maintain cultural practices and carry out cultural obligations” [64]. In the food related, articles written during the pandemic reported on the efforts by major Australian supermarket chains to assist remote Aboriginal communities during panic buying and supply issues. For example, an article published in The Age in 2020 described how “at one point, Woolworths directed its own suppliers to independent wholesaler Metcash to help supply remote Indigenous communities in the Northern Territory” [65].

Engagement and partnerships

The efforts of corporate actors to engage with and establish partnerships with Aboriginal communities are a recurring concept in media articles. This is particularly evident when reporting on corporate developments and activities that impact Aboriginal communities within the ALRA or Native Title Act, with the appropriate Land Councils playing a role in negotiations with corporate actors. Aboriginal groups or individuals regularly questioned the non-existence or insufficiency of consultations by corporate actors with Traditional Owners, which has generated significant media attention. Corporate actors within the media acknowledge that consulting is not always adequately undertaken, blaming it on “unworkable” processes and highlighting the complexities to develop and sustain partnerships [66]. Inadequate consultation and the risks of this to land and Community were discussed in articles about urban areas, i.e., Darwin. In an ABC article, a Larrakia Traditional Owner states, “We read about [the Middle Arm precinct] but there’s never been any consultation. The only notification I’ve received is by email, I’ve never spoken to anyone”, although it is acknowledged Middle Arm is not currently under the jurisdiction of any ALRA or Native Title Act but clearly has an impact to community self-determination [67]. In the alcohol industry, articles similarly featured Larrakia Traditional Owners claims of lack of consultation surrounding the development of an alcohol outlet. Other articles following Woolworths’ own review into this development included statements from Woolworths acknowledging inadequate consultation, “we (Woolworths) did not do enough in this community to live up to the best practice stakeholder engagement to which we hold ourselves accountable” [68].

In remote areas, lack of consultation was a key feature of two major projects that had government backing (Barossa pipeline and fracking of the Beetaloo Basin). Media articles also contain reports of positive relationships built through consultations, such as NT News’ report that the Jawoyn Association and a gold mining company worked together on an agreement that can be “a real game-changer for the prosperity of the Jawoyn People”, according to the chair of the association [56]. Partnership approaches were also reported to be successful for the rehabilitation of the Rio Tinto mine on Yolngu Country [69], and between rare earths miner Tivan and Larrakia Energy [70]. Articles with positive sentiment towards these partnerships were more commonly reported in commercial news publications, such as The NT News, The Australian, or in trade magazines. One event that was positively featured by both public and commercial outlets was a joint mining venture on Winchelsea Island on Groote Eylandt between Anindilyakwa Advancement Aboriginal Corporation and AUS China International Mining [71]. However, later articles described anti-corruption investigations related to the deal [72].

Third-party activist engagement

Several media articles described activists as playing two roles in corporate development projects (predominantly extractive resource projects) that impact NT Aboriginal communities. In some articles, activists are positioned as using Aboriginal People as a gateway to push their own agendas, often attempting to halt economic developments through lengthy delays. Other articles describe activists as providing a platform for Aboriginal People to protest and advocate against corporate interests, thereby protecting their interests. Extractive resource industry representatives identify activists as impeding development, as stated by Origin CEO Gordon Cairns: “The activists are saying you shouldn’t be drilling there, and gas is wrong and fracking is bad—but these people (Aboriginal) want us to come,” [73]. An article published in The Australian by Northern Territory Senator Jacinta Nampijnpa Price (at the time Shadow Minister for Indigenous Affairs) uses Tasmanian Senator Jonno Duniam’s term “environmental lawfare” to describe practices undertaken by the activist groups (Price, 2024), that frustrate the courts, bring important projects to a standstill, and puts vital industries – and the thousands of Aussie (Australian) jobs they create – at real risk. Former Labor MP, Joel Fitzgibbon, was quoted following the Santos decision: “Hopefully the broader community is beginning to see activist lawfare for what it is, ideological and a threat to our living standard” [74].

In contrast the Environmental Defenders Office a non-profit legal centre representing Munkara in the Santos development of the coast of Tiwi Islands was referred to by the Federal Court Judge Natalie Charlesworth as “distorting and misrepresenting” the words of Traditional Owners Munkara v Santos NA Barossa Pty Ltd (No 3) [2024] FCA 9. However, other articles illustrated concern from Aboriginal groups regarding the potential for damage to sacred sites, land and waterways, and the climate associated with extractive resource projects. Nicholas Milyari Fitzpatrick from Seed mob (an Indigenous youth-led climate justice network) stated “we connect and empower Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander young People to take action together to protect our land, water, communities and our future” [75]. The role of activists working with Aboriginal communities was similarly highlighted in the alcohol industry regarding the planned development of a Dan Murphy’s alcohol outlet. Several articles report on Woolworths or Endeavour executives suggesting activists have been putting words in the mouths of Aboriginal Community residents or “speaking on their behalf” [50]. This was refuted in another article by a Larrakia Elder who stated, “There are a lot of people out there that have got a brain and we do know what we’re talking about—it’s coming from our heart, it’s not coming from anywhere else” [76].

Policy context and government intervention

The media highlighted the government (both at the Territory and federal levels) as having a key role in mediating the impact (positive or negative) of corporate activities on Aboriginal People in the NT through policies and legislation that can protect Aboriginal People’s rights, support development, or both. Several cases involve governments circumventing their own legislation by ‘cutting red tape’ to simplify development processes, with many federal and NT politicians stressing the economic benefits created by corporate actors and their activities as justification. This position is illustrated in the following quote from a senior NT Minister in 2020: “We expect them (corporate actors) to comply with Aboriginal Areas Protection Authority (AAPA) and their environmental requirements as well, but this is about supporting jobs” [77]. In the alcohol industry, articles similarly highlight how the red tape was cut to build a Dan Murphy’s outlet, despite the liquor commission and courts blocking the development due to potential risk of harm to local Aboriginal communities. For Chief Minister Michael Gunner, the court decision against the development was a “kick in the guts for responsible drinkers” and offered a legislative loophole to enable the project to proceed [78]. An article published in the ABC in 2020 reports that “Coinciding with the location change, the NT Small Business Minister Paul Kirby pushed new laws through Parliament to “cut red tape” and force a decision on the new application and several others within 30 days” [76]. In the food related, The Australian Competition and Consumer Commission (ACCC) were criticised by a former senior investigator for not responding to alleged price gouging by Outback Stores in remote communities however all investigations were dropped [79]. One article described a positive partnership between Aboriginal Organisations, Outback Stores and the NT and Commonwealth Governments to open a store in the remote community of Barunga [62].

The policy and legislative frameworks were also important, as they were referenced in articles describing significant battles in the judicial system, both at the federal and local levels. The media reported on the interactions between Traditional Owners of the McArthur River Mine region and the Glencore-owned mine indicating to the Darwin Local Court that they would enter a guilty plea for “carrying out work on a sacred site” which conducted “minor works” by installing metal handrails “in the vicinity” of the Damangani sacred site to carry out environmental sampling [80]. Media articles report on the legal tactics used by activists to delay or halt corporate actors’ projects, as well as those employed by corporate actors to secure approval for their developments. One ABC News article describes corporate actors threatening charities with legal action to recover legal costs, as seen in Munkara vs Santos NA Barossa Pty Ltd [2023] FCA 1348, which provides strong disincentives for charities to support Traditional Owners in the judicial system [81].

Discussion

Over the 10-year timeframe investigated, a variety of cases involving corporate activities were presented in online news media, revealing discourse that highlights links to Aboriginal People’s self-determination principles and the complex relationships with health, economy, politics, and governance. This intertwines with decision-making power over lands, waterways, and the governance of systems, as well as the development and implementation of strategies aligned with the aspirations of Aboriginal People in the NT [82, 83]. Our findings reveal several competing narratives regarding the representation of corporate activities that intersect with these self-determination principles, with a network of actors contributing to the discourse, including Traditional Owners, activists, Industry representatives, land councils, and Territory and Federal Government officials. A key contribution of this study is that introducing self-determination produces more than a harm-benefit binary.

The benefits to Aboriginal People from commercial developments were typically collapsed into economic benefits associated with the creation of wealth. These articles typically do not discuss potential harms associated with corporate activities. More granular approaches are, however, required, as the framing of these articles varies significantly. Indeed, in this category, some articles adopt a deficit-based approach, stressing the context of poverty and framing extractive resource projects as needed for Aboriginal People to rise from poverty and use the voices of Aboriginal People to reinforce this, without acknowledging the structural inequities that make this difficult [84]. This negative framing echoes previous research about media discourse that places responsibility on Aboriginal People [3, 85]. Several articles, however, do stress partnership approaches and recognise the leadership of Aboriginal People and Organisations; however, it is unclear whether Aboriginal aspirations are being addressed beyond a socioeconomic lens that focuses on economic prosperity. A competing narrative is one of conflict between Aboriginal groups and corporate interests, exemplified by a few emblematic cases that have attracted significant media coverage, such as Dan Murphy’s in the alcohol industry, the Santos Barossa gas project, the McArthur River Mine in Borroloola, and ERA’s uranium mine. In both narratives, Aboriginal People are more typically framed as passive recipients of either benefits or harms.

In cases where Aboriginal People opposed developments (often due to concerns about potential harms), Aboriginal People were framed as depriving the rest of the NT (and Australia) of the benefits derived from these projects, in terms of economic returns, employment, or product availability. This is a stark contrast to the principles of self-determination and the fundamental principles of Indigenous health and wellbeing. Globally there have been other examples of this such as the Dakota Access Pipeline in the USA impacting the Standing Rock Sioux Tribe who believe the Fort Laramie Treaty 1851 Article II was breached “undisturbed use and occupation” of lands [86] or the Tapajós hydropower complex impacting the livelihoods of the Munduruku Tribe in Brazil in favour of economic expansion [87].

With recent emphasis on the ‘Commercial Determinants of Health’ [21], the public health field would likely take a relatively skeptical view of positive stories in the articles included in the study, noting the difference in the framing between commercial and public news publications, with commercial news more likely to frame both Aboriginal People and activists as a threat to corporate developments. Scholars have previously critiqued media bias relating to the mining industry, with a historical overlap between ownership of both media and mining corporations [88], although this is no longer the case. While some articles explicitly highlighted CSR activities, other articles also highlighted many other cases of corporations undertaking actions that could be interpreted as CPA [18], such as government lobbying, using financial benefits or employment opportunities to sway Aboriginal Peoples, threatening Aboriginal groups and communities to be held responsible for the loss of opportunities for the Australian People. A second key contribution of our study is reflections on the limits to this CPA model when concerning Indigenous affairs due to its narrow focus on corporate and policy intersections. The CPA model also neglects the role of self-determination for Aboriginal Peoples, through framing of ‘marginalised populations’ [18], as vulnerable recipients of harm and exploitation and hence can reproduce deficit narratives unless adapted with Indigenous governance and self-determination at the centre.

The CDoH literature has recently outlined the role of civil actors who can “raise their collective voices, articulate alternative visions, and hold commercial actors and governments accountable” [89]. Systemic racism and failures of systems to protect Indigenous interests makes it increasing difficult for Indigenous People to gain a seat at decision making levels. This position is also complex for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples, where enacting self-determination may not be in opposition to corporate activities in some cases, despite having a clear impact to health. The articles describe support for corporate development initiatives for reasons that are important to Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples (i.e., improved livelihoods through employment and training opportunities) in areas where other opportunities are limited, a concept that has long been acknowledged in the public health field as a determinant of health and inequity [90]. A recent commentary highlighted the limits of the CDoH in terms of its narrow vision of health, compelling scholars to engage in interdisciplinary research. But even within health, there is room to encompass Indigenous concepts of health and wellbeing, and the power relations which shape these outcomes. Several articles in our study highlight the aspirations of Aboriginal People to be engaged actors driving corporate activities, for instance, the mining partnership on Winchelsea Island. Given the emphasis in Australian policies on wealth generation to achieve self-determination [91], this is unsurprising. Friel et al. argue that social progress requires alternative visions such as greater public investment, cooperatives, and social enterprises [89]. However, it is essential that Aboriginal People’s aspirations and views on what constitutes social progress in relation to commercial systems are at the forefront, and it is unclear whether this differs from the thinking of the largely non-Indigenous public health field.

Furthermore, the findings highlight the range of other actors that play a crucial role in mediating the benefits and impacts (both positive and negative) of corporate activities on Aboriginal Peoples. Policies not only set boundaries on what actions can be taken to support, slow, or block developments, but governments are also shown to be active actors, changing policies to move these boundaries in favour of corporate development when deemed politically important. Activists were also described as playing an influential role in conflicts, sometimes reported as manipulating Aboriginal People and their culture, and creating divisions in Aboriginal communities, to achieve objectives that do not align with the best interests of Aboriginal communities. This requires further investigation, as it may reveal undermining of Aboriginal People’s self-determination. Conversely, this narrative positions Aboriginal People as passive and may silence strategic civil action led by Aboriginal People. These narratives all provide different perspectives on the question of the impact of corporate actors on the self-determination of Aboriginal Peoples in the NT and who gets to decide what’s best for Aboriginal communities. The media articles analysed portray Aboriginal Peoples as influenced or manipulated by either corporate actors or activists or making decisions out of necessity [22, 23]. Exceptions exist, and articles referring to community-owned enterprises in all three sectors, as well as positive long-term partnerships between corporate actors and Aboriginal groups. There is a need for greater development of Aboriginal community-based enterprises that support Aboriginal principles of self-determination, which may lead to improved health and wellbeing outcomes [92, 93].

Of course, the data in this study are limited to what is published in large enough media outlets to be captured in media databases, which may not capture the full extent of media discourse. However, the study included both public and commercial news and likely captured the vast majority of media that reaches enough of the population to yield influence over public discourse. It is not possible to measure the degree of influence that corporations have over commercial news represented in this study (nor of political or other influences), however the inclusion of public broadcasters at least provides room for alternative narratives. This study enabled insights into the media discourse on this contentious issue, however, only captures the voices and narratives curated by media. While the study provides valuable contributions to the CDoH field, further research unpacking where self-determination of Aboriginal People fits in this field is important. A significant strength of this study is its Aboriginal leadership in conception, design, conduct and interpretation of the research, which has been limited in the commercial determinants of health field to date. While our findings may present ideas that challenge dominant public health discourse on CDoH, it is important that Indigenous lens are considered as the field develops. The study represents media in relation to corporate activities and Aboriginal Peoples of Australia, however given colonisation and capitalism are global phenomena, as is deficit discourse in media representation of Indigenous Peoples, we contend that lessons from this study should give pause for thought in the broader CDoH and public health fields. In Australia, there has been investment in understanding how commercial determinants impact on Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander People’s health (NHMRC, 2024), but it is not yet known how the field will navigate findings that may conflict with traditional (Western) public health understandings or priorities, which is also important for the global field to heed attention to.

Conclusion

The mainstream media discourse over a 10-year period between 2015 and 2025 identifies narratives on the representation of Aboriginal People in the NT in relation to corporate activities. Our findings challenge the notion of a single-victim narrative by illustrating how multiple stakeholders (i.e., industry, government, media, and at times civil society) collectively influence public discourse, which can either hinder or promote self-determination. Narratives commonly emphasise economic benefits of corporate activities for Aboriginal People, with Aboriginal People commonly framed in deficit narratives as passive beneficiaries. Even within narratives focused on mutually beneficial partnerships, it is unclear to what extent Aboriginal aspirations are addressed beyond a socioeconomic lens that primarily benefits corporate actors. Narratives focused on harms to Aboriginal People also frequently use deficit framing. Greater consideration of self-determination of Indigenous Peoples in commercial determinants of health literature can ensure that the concept of health encompasses the broader values of Indigenous health and variety of ways that both commercial entities and the public health field itself may shape the wellbeing of Indigenous Peoples.
