Isolation, culture, and in vitro functional validation of intratumor bacteria from lung cancer patients
Authors: Yifan Sun, Jianghui Tong, Yafang Tan, Yuejiao Liu, Dong Li, Huan Yu, Yuling He, Jianjie Li, Bo Jia, Hongchao Xiong, Yujia Chi, Ruifu Yang, Ziping Wang, Yujing Bi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveCulturomics and 16 S rDNA sequencing were applied to identify lung tumor-resident microorganisms. In vitro characterization revealed potential functions of these cancer-associated microorganisms.MethodsEighteen clinical lung cancer (LC) tissues samples were collected. All the samples were cultured by culturomics, and analyzed with 16 S rDNA sequencing. Four isolated isolates belonging to the genus Staphylococcus were detected to find out the possible functions in vitro. A549 cells were infected with supernatant of these bacteria, and cell index which reflected cell proliferation was determined by Real-Time Cell Analysis (RTCA). ELISA was employed to detect levels of proinflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6) in THP-1 cells stimulated with bacterial culture supernatants.ResultA total of 12 bacteria were cultured and identified, most of which belonged to the Staphylococcus genus. Bacillus was detected through both methods. In vitro experiment, the cultured strains promoted cellular proliferation, and enhanced the levels of proinflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6) as well.ConclusionBacteria from LC tissues was isolated and identified. Lung tumor-resident microbiota was described. LC tumor-resident bacteria cause tumor development mediated by enhancing tumor cell proliferation and proinflammatory cytokines releasing by macrophages in the tumor microenvironment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04237-4.Keywords: Microbiota, Lung cancer, Culturomics, 16S rDNA sequencing, Cellular proliferation, Proinflammation

Introduction

Lung cancer constitutes a severe malignancy that poses a profound threat to human health. Clinically, it is characterized by symptoms such as hemoptysis, chest discomfort, and coughing [1]. Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) represents the predominant form of this neoplasm, within which adenocarcinoma is the most common subtype [2]. Over half of the patients present with locally advanced or metastatic disease at the time of diagnosis, and the 5-year overall survival rate is less than 15% [3]. Inflammation caused by microbial infections may contribute to cancer initiation and progression [4]. Microbial heterogeneity has been incorporated as a core hallmark of cancer in recent updates [5]. Growing evidence indicates the lung microbiome contributes to cancer pathogenesis [6, 7].

A substantial body of research has elucidated that tumor-associated microbiota plays a crucial role in various biological processes, including carcinogenesis, metastasis, and the development of drug resistance [8, 9]. Deborah Nejman systematically analyzed seven distinct types of human tumor tissues alongside their corresponding normal tissue counterparts, revealing the pervasiveness of bacterial presence across these tumor types, with lung cancer demonstrating a bacterial DNA positivity rate of around 16.8% [10]. Healthy lungs, historically considered sterile, are now known to harbor a diverse microbiota. Some research has linked pulmonary microbial communities to the initiation and progression of lung cancer [11, 12]. For example, Greathouse’s sequencing analyses of neoplastic and non-neoplastic lung tissues identified a higher alpha diversity within the microbiota of malignant tissues, with distinctive species differentials observed among various pathological classifications [13]. Lung tissue microbiota in cancer patients showed increased taxonomic diversity and enrichment of Bacteroidetes relative to emphysema cohorts [14]. Tsay et al. demonstrated that lower airway dysbiosis caused by oral bacterial microaspiration promotes IL-17-driven inflammation, accelerating lung tumorigenesis [15].

Culturomics, a sophisticated high-throughput bacterial culturing methodology, facilitates the isolation of human-originated bacterial strains, which can subsequently be employed for in-depth phenotypic and functional characterizations [16, 17]. Our team has previously provided insights into pulmonary and oral microbiota alterations in patients with lung cancer using culturomics and 16 S rDNA amplicon sequencing [18]. However, the application of culturomics for the culture and detection of bacteria in LC tissues has been scarcely reported.

We utilized integrated culture-dependent and culture-independent methodologies to profile the microbiota within lung tumors across a cohort of 18 lung cancer patients. We reported the bacteria diversity and richness of microbiota from LC tissues and applied experiments in vitro to support the plausibility of the strains isolated from LC tissues can contribute to a proinflammatory effect and cellular proliferation.

Materials and methods

Patient recruitment and samples collection

The recruited patients came from Peking University Cancer Hospital. The inclusion criteria were as follows: pathologically diagnosed of NSCLC; did not receive surgery operation, radiotherapy or systemic therapy before sample collection; without community acquired pneumonia, acute bronchitis, acute exacerbation of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease bronchiectasis with infection or asthma; had no fever; without prior history of other malignant diseases. Small pieces (~ 1–4 cm3) of LC tissues were taken from surgically resected lung specimens with patients’ informed consent. The entire experimental procedure was performed in strict accordance with institutional guidelines under aseptic conditions to maintain sterility and prevent contamination. To monitor potential contamination during surgical sampling, environmental controls were processed in parallel and under identical conditions to the LC tissue samples. Concurrently, Day 1 culture controls were established. Tissue samples were deemed suitable for downstream experiments only when the Day 1 environmental controls showed no detectable microbial growth. To eliminate the possible contamination of the operation, we collected samples from within the tumor. All reagents and equipment were rigorously maintained as sterile, and all sample manipulations were exclusively performed within a biosafety cabinet. Samples were confirmed as tumor tissue on the basis of histopathological assessment.

The sample was split into two aliquots: one for bacterial culture and the other for 16 S rDNA sequencing. Fresh samples were collected in sterile tubes, kept on ice, and transported to the laboratory within 1 h. Culturomics was performed immediately after sample collection, while aliquots were preserved at − 80 °C for subsequent high-throughput sequencing.

DNA extraction, 16 S rDNA amplification and sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from all samples using the Hipure Bacterial DNA Kit (Magen, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. PCR amplification targeted the V3-V4 region of 16S rRNA using specific primers (341F: 5’-CCTAYGGGRBGCASCAG-3’; 806R: 5’-GGACTACNNGGGTATCTAAT-3’), amplicons were sequenced using the Miseq platform. A Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Scientific) and Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Santa Clara, CA, USA) were used to assess the quality of the library. Paired-end sequencing (2 × 250 bp) was conducted on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, United States). Operational taxonomic units (OTU) were clustered at 97% similarity using UPARSE (version 7.0.1001 http://drive5.com/uparse/) following chimera removal, with representative sequences extracted for each cluster. For each representative sequence, the Silva database was used based on the Mothur algorithm to annotate taxonomic information. OTU abundance information was normalized using a standard of sequence number corresponding to the sample with the fewest sequences. DNA extraction, library preparation, sequencing and data analysis was conducted by Novogene Bioinformatics Technology Co., Ltd (Beijing, PR China).

Culturomics

Culturomics employs high-throughput multi-condition cultivation to enhance bacterial diversity detection and isolate pure cultures [19]. LC tissues were recovered in 1 mL of PBS and homogenized with a FastPrep-24 (MP Biomedicals) during two cycles (speed 6, 45 s). Lung homogenates were pre-incubated in blood culture bottles supplemented with 5% blood. Methods for dilution, isolation, and identification adhered to established procedures developed in our laboratory [18, 20]. On days 1, 3, and 6, enriched cultures were sampled via syringe, serially diluted, and plated on 5% sheep blood Columbia agar. Aerobic cultures (37 °C, 24 h) and anaerobic cultures (37 °C, 72 h) were performed. Colonies were identified by MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry (MS) (Autof MS1000 V1.1.12). Unidentified isolates (score < 9, database V1.1.12) underwent 16 S rRNA sequencing with primers 27 F (5′-AGAGTTTGATCMTGGCTCAG-3′) and 1492R (5′-GGTTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3′). Sequencing results were analyzed by the NCBI BLAST algorithm4 for homologous sequence searches with type strains [21].

Bacterial strains

We exclusively selected strains that were both isolated on the day of initial cultivation and exhibited optimal morphological characteristics for subsequent experimental analyses. Strains of S. warneri−01, S. hominis−22, S. capitis−22, S. nepalensis−09 originated from representative bacterial isolates that we obtained from patient samples in this study. Lactobacillus rhamnosus was isolated from fecal samples of a healthy individual and preserved in our laboratory.

Cytokines detection by ELISA

A549 and THP-1 cells were purchased from Beina Biological Co, Ltd. (Henan, China). The RPMI 1640 medium (8122151, Gibco, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, 900 − 108, GEMINI) is employed to cultivate the cells at 37 ℃ with 5% CO2. THP-1 cells (5 × 10⁵/ml) were plated in 12-well plates and differentiated into macrophages using 100 ng/ml PMA (356150010, Thermo Fisher) for 48 h at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. All bacterial strains were cultured in brain heart infusion (BHI) broth. Four bacterial strains cultured from LC tissues and another negative control strain L. rhamnosus, were cultivated aerobically at 37 ℃ to the end of log phase. Finally, the culture supernatants of the aforementioned bacteria 0.5 ml were added into 12-well plates for 16 h. At intervals of 4 and 8 h, the supernatant after infection was collected, centrifuged, and stored at −80 ℃. Cytokine concentrations (IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6) in bacterial-stimulated THP-1 supernatants were determined by pre-coated ELISA kit (Dakewei, Human IL-1β, 1110122; Human TNF-α, 1117202; Human IL-6, 1110602) according to the instructions of the kit. Concentrations of IL-1β, TNF-α and IL-6 were calculated from the standard curve for each sample.

Cell proliferation detection by RTCA

RTCA is an impedance-based technology for continuous, label-free monitoring of cellular proliferation, viability, adhesion, and morphological changes in real-time [22]. A total of 2 × 104 A549 cells were seeded into the wells of a plate. After 12 h, supernatant of S. warneri, S. hominis, S. capitis, or S. nepalensis was added to the well at a concentration of 10% (v/v). The plate was placed on the RTCA xCELLigence (Agilent, esight) for 148 h. BHI was used as a blank control.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were carried out using GraphPad Prism 8.0. Student’s t-test and paired t-test was performed to compare the variables of the two sample groups. One-way ANOVA analysis was used to test the data of more than three groups. The results were depicted as mean values ± standard deviation (SD), derived from three separate experimental replicates. No formal statistical correction for multiple comparisons was applied due to limited group number. All statistical assessments were two-tailed, and p-value of less than 0.05 was deemed indicative of statistical significance.

Results

Characteristics of the clinical cohorts

We recruited 22 patients with unilateral lobar masses from Beijing Peking University Cancer Hospital between February 2021 and August 2022, of which 18 were newly diagnosed with NSCLC via histological confirmation but 4 were not. The clinical characteristics of patients are listed in Table 1. In total, 10 males and 8 females were enrolled. Additionally,9 smokers were included. The average age was 61.9 years old, and 6 patients had a family history of cancer. There were 13 cases (72.2%) diagnosed with adenocarcinoma, 5 cases (27.8%) with squamous cell carcinoma. Genetic testing in this cohort detected only EGFR mutations and KRAS mutations, with the highest proportion of EGFR mutations (61.5%), one KRAS mutation (7.7%), and no driver gene alterations detected in three individuals (23.1%).

Characteristics of bacteria isolated from the lung cancer tissues via culturomics

Figure 1 showed the culturomics workflow and 18 fresh NSCLC tissue samples were cultured. Following culture on blood agar, 287 isolates were purified from 5 samples, and 12 species of bacteria were identified, which belonged to 6 genera, 6 families, 6 orders, 6 classes and 3 phyla (Table 2). The average detection rate of bacteria per sample was 27.8%. More strains were isolated under anaerobic conditions than under aerobic conditions. Additionally, despite extended culturing, no novel bacterial isolates were recovered beyond those obtained during initial sampling. This demonstrates the importance of establishing an optimal primary subculture window for maximal bacterial recovery.

Microbial composition of lung cancer tissues using 16 S rDNA sequencing

In addition to culture, the LC microbiota was profiled by sequencing the V3-V4 region of the 16 S rDNA gene. At the phylum level, the three dominant phyla were Proteobacteria, Firmicutes and Actinobacteriota (Fig. 2A). We further analyzed differential abundance across 18 samples at genus level. The 18 samples varied widely, and the majority of them belong to others categories (Fig. 2B). Consistent with the culturomics results, Bacillus was detected at a high rate in the 18 samples by 16 S rDNA sequencing. These results indicate significant individual differences in the microbial composition of lung cancer tissues.

Comparison of lung cancer bacteria via culture-dependent and culture-independent methods

Bacteria were cultured from 5 LC tissues. We compared microbiota composition between culture-dependent and culture-independent methods among the 5 samples. Based on the sequencing data, three dominant phyla were Proteobacteria, Firmicutes, and Actinobacteriota (Fig. 3A). We observed a moderate difference among the five LC samples at genus level, such as, in sample T1, the dominant genus was Pantoea, whereas in sample T22, the dominant genus was Glutamicibacter (Fig. 3B). From culturomics results, the 12 identified species belonged to three phyla, including Firmicutes (67%), Actinobacteriota (25%), and Proteobacteria (8%), which was similar with sequence result. The genus that accounted for the largest proportion is Staphylococcus, accounting for 59% (Fig. 3C). Other genera included Bacillus, Propionibacterium, Acinetobacter, Microbacterium, and Tessaracoccus. To evaluate the differences between the culturomics and sequencing results, we compared the genera with top 10 relative abundance identified in 18 samples and the genera isolated from 5 samples. The result showed that only Bacillus (1/10, 10%) could be detected and isolated by both methods (Fig. 3D). We also separately compared the sequencing and culture results of each sample at the genus level. Except for T1, which has two genera that can be discovered, there are no overlapping bacteria for the other samples between two methods (Figure S1).

Evaluation of the tumor-derived bacterial in vitro

Cell proliferation and cytokines levels were detected. The four Staphylococcus strains (S. warneri−01, S. hominis−22, S. capitis−22, S. nepalensis−09) were prioritized for functional analysis based on their established relevance to pulmonary pathologies in literature and higher isolation frequency across multiple samples in our cohort. A549 cell was stimulated with different Staphylococcus supernatants, and cell proliferation was recorded by RTCA. Cell index showed that after stimulation with the culture supernatant of all strains, cell proliferation significantly accelerated (Fig. 4A). At 148 h, bacterial supernatants significantly increased cell index versus controls (Fig. 4B), demonstrating their tumor-proliferative role.

Proliferation and pro-inflammation in vitro. A Detection of different Staphylococcus strains promoting cancer cell line proliferation through real-time cell analysis (RTCA). B Statistic analysis of cell index after stimulating for 148 h. C TNF-α content in the supernatant of four strains co-incubated with THP-1. D IL-1β content in the supernatant of four strains co-incubated with THP-1. E TNF-α content in the supernatant of four strains in the heated or unheated group. F IL-1β content in the supernatant of four strains in the heated or unheated group. Data are expressed as mean ± SD from 3 independent experiments, * p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01, *** p < 0.001

To access their potentials to induce cytokine production, supernatants from four strains were added to THP-1 cells. L. rhamnosus was set as the negative control group to exclude the influence of BHI itself and common metabolites of bacteria on THP-1. After stimulation with supernatants for 16 h, the secretion levels of TNF-α in THP-1 derived macrophages were significantly higher than those in the control group (Fig. 4C). The results of IL-1β were similar to those for TNF-α, with the supernatants from four bacterial strains significantly increasing the secretion of IL-1β by THP-1 derived macrophages compared to the control group (Fig. 4D). The secretion level of IL-6 was relatively low, and only at 16 h in the S. capitis group IL-6 was significantly higher than those in the control group (Figure S2).

To further clarify whether the heat-resistant non-protein components or the heat-sensitive protein components of bacterial metabolites mediate the release of macrophage-related inflammatory factors, we conduct investigation by heat-treating bacterial culture supernatants. The heat-treated supernatants from S. capitis, and S. nepalensis exhibited significantly reduced levels of TNF-α and IL-1β, compared to the unheated groups (Fig. 4E and F). This suggested that protein components in bacterial supernatants were involved in mediating the release of inflammatory factors. At the same time a certain amount of pro-inflammatory cytokines was still released after heat treatment, indicating that non-protein components in bacterial supernatants also participated in mediating the release of related pro-inflammatory cytokines. In addition, among four strains, S. capitis showed more obvious pro-inflammatory effect.

Discussion

The relationship between cancer and the microbiota is being comprehensive investigation [23, 24]. The affliction of LC imposes a substantial burden, and the exploration of the pulmonary microbiome has provided a novel paradigm for the investigation of this pathology. Accumulating evidence indicates that microbiota elucidates disparities in the lung microbiota between individuals with lung cancer and those of a healthy cohort, correlating these variations with the initiation and progression of lung carcinoma [25, 26]. Lung microbiota dysbiosis may modulate the risk of malignancy at multiple levels, including activation of oncogenes and chronic inflammation [27, 28]. At present most studies have been predicated on sequencing techniques. To study the role of bacteria in LC, it is necessary to isolate the pathogenic bacteria from clinical samples. In this study, LC tissues samples were examined using both two methods, and the results were compared between them. Sequencing profiled the microbiota composition in LC tissues, and culturomics provided partial validation for the sequencing findings; more importantly, culturomics provided microorganisms for further study. The present study represents an innovative approach in the utilization of culture-based methods to isolate bacterial strains from human lung cancer tissues, followed by in vitro experimental validation of partial isolated strains. The disparity between these two omics approaches underscores that most microbial taxa in the biosphere resist laboratory cultivation, despite rigorous simulation of their hypothesized cultivation parameters [29]. Likely reasons involve fastidious growth conditions, dormant forms, and reliance on host metabolic products [30].

The lung tissue microbiota was clearly distinct from the microbiota reported at other body sites [31]. Moreover, it was dominated by Firmicutes at phylum level and showed increased in Streptococcus and Bacillus at genus level [32]. In our study, we found that only Bacillus could be detected and isolated by culturing and sequencing, which was similar with previous results [33, 34].

In the context of culturing methods, to maximize the isolation of microorganisms from lung cancer tissues, both aerobic and anaerobic culture conditions were established. A greater number of bacteria were isolated under anaerobic conditions, indicating the necessity of setting anaerobic conditions for culturing bacteria from lung cancer tissues. Current research reveals that dysregulation of anaerobic bacterial populations may contribute to cancer pathogenesis. Notably, enrichment of Ruminococcus in lung adenocarcinoma tissues has been associated with poor clinical prognosis, while the hypoxic niche characteristic of squamous cell carcinoma may provide a selective advantage for anaerobic bacteria such as Clostridium [35, 36]. The predominance of facultative anaerobes among the isolates suggests a potential correlation with the adaptive capabilities of these microorganisms to the fluctuating microenvironment [37, 38].

A total of 12 bacterial species was isolated, of which 7 species were classified as belonging to the genus Staphylococcus. Bacteria in the genus Staphylococcus are Gram-positive cocci 1 μm in diameter, which can cause many forms infection, such as S. aureus causes toxic shock syndrome by releasing of superantigens into the blood stream [39]. Certain bacterial species have been reported to be associated with human cancers, for instance, S. aureus has been demonstrated to induce the infection-associated malignant transformation of oral epitheliums through COX-2 activation, thereby potentially contributing to tumorigenesis [40]. Recent research reveals that S. warneri and its toxin Warnericin trigger human inflammation by disrupting key host cell processes and directly activating immune cells [41]. The bacterial Staphylococcus species have also reported their correlation with lung diseases. Cystic fibrosis patients are colonized by S. aureus expressing various virulence factors including toxins, and Staphylococcal Cassette Chromosome mec types, and have the potential for biofilm formation [42]. S. nepalensis releases corisin, a peptide conserved in diverse staphylococci, to induce apoptosis of lung epithelial cells, which facilitate the progression of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis [43].

While many Staphylococcus bacteria are associated with various diseases, most strains used are not isolated from the clinical samples but were purchased from internationally recognized standard bacterial strain preservation centers, such as American type culture collection [44, 45]. Culturomics can identify bacteria at strain level, and successfully isolating living bacteria is crucial for carrying out subsequent experimental work [16, 46]. In this study, we isolated 7 species of the genus Staphylococcus from the tissues of patients with NSCLC. Theses strains were used for subsequent experiments, making the results more credible.

Whether the tumor is caused by inflammation or inflammation caused by tumor, there is infiltration of inflammatory substances in the tumor tissue, and inflammation will further promote the development of tumor [47]. The predominant pathological characteristic of cancer is the extensive infiltration of inflammatory cells within the tumor microenvironment [48]. Macrophages constitute a pivotal immune cell population in the tumor microenvironment, and M2 macrophages, in particular, facilitate tumor progression through the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines [49]. Tumor-associated macrophages are capable of producing a significant array of inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 [50]. TNF-α, also known as tumor necrosis factor, can facilitate tumor angiogenesis by promoting the expression of vascular endothelial growth factor, and it mediates inflammation-associated carcinogenic processes [51]. IL-6 is highly expressed in a variety of malignant tumors and serves as a primary mediator in the inflammation-induced oncogenesis process [52]. IL-1β is a master regulator of proinflammation its upstream regulator NOD-like receptor thermal protein domain associated protein 3 in both solid tumor and hematologic malignancies [53]. Therefore, the significant increase of supernatant TNF-α and IL-1β in the S. warneri, S. capitis, and S. nepalensis group confirms that these strains have a stronger proinflammatory effect. These novel findings hold significant clinical implications for human pulmonary oncology, particularly given that bacterial infections are prevalent in approximately 70% of lung cancer cases and substantially impact both disease progression and overall survival rates. Our findings of altered pulmonary microbiota composition in lung cancer patients may functionally contribute to tumor progression through mechanisms similar to those described by Jin et al., where commensal microbiota were shown to promote lung cancer development via γδ T cell activation and IL-17 production [35].

Although we have screened potential pathogenic bacteria via culture or sequencing, the sample size is limited, and more clinical samples are needed for verification. Critically, our findings on bacterial-driven tumor proliferation and inflammatory responses are derived exclusively from in vitro cell culture models. While these systems provide mechanistic insights, they cannot fully replicate the complexity of human tumor microenvironments. The potential in vivo and clinical implications must be more explicitly delineated as testable hypotheses requiring further validation; additionally, the reverse translational validation and underlying mechanisms warrant deeper investigation.

The association between lung microbiota and LC is complex and poorly characterized. Therefore, identifying potential pathogenic bacteria in patients is of great significance. This study utilized both culture-dependent and culture-independent methods on human LC tissues, resulting in the isolation of 14 bacterial species. In vitro experiments revealed that these patient-derived strains could promote cellular proliferation and induce the release of inflammatory cytokines, thereby leading to a pro-inflammatory tumor microenvironment. In the future, it is important to study the relationship between lung microbiota and LC by isolating patient-derived strains through culturomics, combining this data with sequencing data, and assess the mechanism of disease development.
