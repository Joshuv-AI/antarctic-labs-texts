Precision theranostics in cervical Cancer: Harnessing stimuli-responsive hydrogels for tumor microenvironment-targeted therapy and diagnosis
Authors: Yu Zhang, Yueda Lu, Sidi Li, Fangqi Zheng, Yonggan Dong, Heng Tang, Xianwen Wang, Jianye Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCervical cancer continues to pose a significant threat to women's health worldwide, with conventional therapies often limited by drug resistance and systemic toxicity. Stimuli-responsive hydrogels have recently emerged as promising platforms in precision oncology, owing to their ability to enable spatiotemporally controlled drug delivery, integrate multimodal theranostic functions, and dynamically interact with the tumor microenvironment (TME). This review highlights the stimuli-responsive hydrogels as precision theranostic platforms capable of targeting the unique TME of cervical cancer. We systematically categorize hydrogel systems based on their response mechanisms—pH, temperature, enzymes, light, magnetic fields, and redox conditions—and directly align these with clinical applications such as chemotherapy, gene therapy, immunotherapy, and real-time diagnostics. We further explore the integration of advanced technologies including 4D printing, organoid models, and artificial intelligence (AI) for personalized treatment planning and dynamic adaptability. By offering a comprehensive and application-oriented perspective, this review provides innovative insights into the design of smart hydrogels that enhance therapeutic precision, reduce off-target effects, and improve diagnostic sensitivity in cervical cancer.Keywords: Stimuli-responsive hydrogel, Cervical cancer, Theranostics, Precision medicine, Smart drug delivery

Introduction

Cervical cancer is a prevalent malignant tumor among women worldwide and continues to pose a serious threat to women's health and survival [1]. According to the latest statistics from the World Health Organization (WHO), there were approximately 661,000 new cases and 348,000 deaths globally in 2022, with age-standardized incidence and mortality rates of 14.1 and 7.1 per 100,000 people, respectively. Notably, approximately 85 % of cases occur in developing countries, where healthcare resources are often limited [2]. Persistent infection with high-risk human papillomavirus (HR-HPV), particularly HPV16 and HPV18, is the primary etiological factor in cervical carcinogenesis. The viral oncoproteins E6 and E7 encoded by HR-HPV promote tumorigenesis by targeting and degrading the host tumor suppressor proteins p53 and retinoblastoma (pRb), thereby disrupting cell cycle control and DNA repair pathways and ultimately leading to cellular immortalization and malignant transformation [3]. Although widespread HPV vaccination has substantially reduced the incidence of early-stage cervical cancer, the management of advanced, recurrent, or metastatic disease remains a major clinical challenge. Conventional therapeutic modalities, including surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy, can achieve local control but often fail to eradicate occult metastatic lesions. These approaches are frequently associated with severe adverse effects, such as irreversible fertility loss, radiation-induced proctitis, and myelosuppression [[4], [5], [6]]. Moreover, chemotherapeutic agents such as cisplatin and paclitaxel exhibit high systemic toxicity. Their administration often results in collateral damage to healthy tissues. A significant proportion of these agents bind to plasma proteins or are metabolized and excreted before reaching the tumor site, leading to suboptimal drug accumulation at the lesion site and diminished therapeutic efficacy [7,8]. In addition to therapeutic limitations, current diagnostic strategies for cervical cancer face multiple challenges. Early-stage disease is often asymptomatic and easily overlooked; HPV screening coverage remains inadequate in many primary-care settings; and cytological assessments, while widely used, have limited sensitivity and are highly operator-dependent. Furthermore, sensitive, user-friendly tools for early detection are lacking, and patients with advanced disease are frequently diagnosed beyond the optimal therapeutic window. These challenges underscore the urgent need for drug delivery systems capable of selectively targeting tumor sites and effectively eradicating cancer cells, alongside more sensitive and accessible diagnostic tools to facilitate early detection and timely intervention.

In recent years, the convergence of precision medicine and nanotechnology has led to the development of stimuli-responsive hydrogels as smart drug delivery systems, offering innovative solutions to overcome key challenges in the treatment of cervical cancer. Compared with conventional hydrogel-based drug delivery materials, these advanced materials present unique advantages, such as spatiotemporally controlled drug release, multimodal theranostics and microenvironment remodeling capabilities. Stimuli-responsive hydrogels can selectively accumulate at tumor sites through passive or active targeting [9] and respond to specific signals from the tumor microenvironment (TME) [10,11] or external stimuli [12,13]. This enables on-demand drug release, significantly increasing the local drug concentration at the tumor site while minimizing systemic toxicity. The three-dimensional network structure of hydrogels allows for the codelivery of chemotherapeutic agents, immunomodulators, and contrast agents (e.g., superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles), which facilitates simultaneous imaging via magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or fluorescence. This integration enables real-time monitoring of therapeutic efficacy, providing valuable data for the dynamic adjustment of treatment protocols [14,15]. Some functionalized hydrogels can synergistically overcome therapeutic resistance within the TME by degrading the fibrotic stroma of tumors, modulating the phenotype of immunosuppressive cells (e.g., tumor-associated macrophages), or providing sustained release of antiangiogenic factors [16,17]. These properties contribute to a more effective and adaptive approach to cancer treatment.

This review focuses on the precise diagnosis and treatment of cervical cancer, with a particular focus on stimuli-responsive hydrogels. It systematically integrates the oncogenic mechanisms and distinctive features of the cervical TME to formulate a multifunctional theranostic platform. Unlike traditional reviews that classify materials on the basis of solely composition or single-response mechanisms, this work redefines the paradigm by aligning various stimuli types (e.g., pH, temperature, light, magnetic field, enzymatic activity, and redox conditions) with specific clinical applications, including localized chemotherapy, immunomodulation, and fertility preservation. Furthermore, the review outlines a future trajectory wherein closed-loop systems are established, incorporating dynamic 4D-printed constructs, patient-derived organoid-based digital twins for predictive modeling, and machine learning-assisted optimization for hydrogel formulation. Finally, the review addresses translational challenges by proposing cohesive strategies that encompass technical, regulatory, and ethical dimensions.

Overview of cervical cancer

Pathogenesis of cervical cancer

The etiology of cervical cancer is multifactorial, with persistent infection by HR-HPV being the most prevalent and primary causative factor. HPV-16 and HPV-18 are the predominant types implicated [18,19]. HPV is a group of small, nonenveloped DNA viruses [20], with a genome encoding six early genes (E1, E2, E4, E5, E6, and E7) and two late genes (L1 and L2), the latter of which are involved primarily in capsid protein synthesis [21]. The expression of E6 and E7 indicates active infection and is pivotal in driving cellular transformation. The E6 and E7 oncoproteins encoded by HR-HPV promote tumorigenesis by specifically degrading the host tumor suppressors p53 and retinoblastoma (pRb), thereby disrupting cell cycle regulation and impairing DNA damage repair, ultimately leading to cellular immortalization and malignant transformation [3]. Mechanistically, E6 possesses multiple p53-binding domains and, upon binding, recruits the cellular ubiquitin ligase E6-associated protein (E6AP), promoting proteasomal degradation of p53 and suppressing apoptosis. E7 binds to pRb and disrupts its tumor-suppressive functions, resulting in deregulated cell cycle progression and uncontrolled cellular proliferation (Fig. 1B) [22]. Under normal circumstances, the E2 protein negatively regulates the transcription of E6 and E7; however, the integration of HR-HPV DNA into fragile sites of the host genome (e.g., 3p and 13q) frequently disrupts the E2 gene, leading to sustained overexpression of the E6 and E7 oncogenes (Fig. 1A) [23,24]. This disruption drives the malignant transformation of cervical epithelial cells, resulting in abnormal hyperplasia, the formation of cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN), and potential progression to invasive cervical carcinoma [25].

A critical feature of HR-HPV-driven carcinogenesis is its ability to evade host immune surveillance, thereby establishing persistent infection, which is a prerequisite for malignant progression [26]. HPV achieves immune evasion through multiple mechanisms, including the upregulation of immune checkpoints, dysregulation of cytokine-mediated inflammation, and modulation of host immune responses by early viral proteins [27]. Specifically, HPV enhances the expression of immune checkpoint molecules such as PD-1, PD-L1, and CTLA-4, thereby suppressing antitumor immune responses. Concurrently, it alters the cytokine milieu by upregulating proinflammatory factors (e.g., TGF-β and IL-10) and downregulating antiviral cytokines, further weakening the host immune defense (Fig. 1C). In addition, early viral proteins such as E5, E6, and E7 directly interfere with host immune pathways, facilitating immune escape and viral persistence [28].

In addition to the molecular mechanisms of HPV-induced transformation, increasing attention has been given to the ecological evolution of tumors postinitiation. As CIN progresses to invasive carcinoma, malignant epithelial cells establish a complex pathological interplay with surrounding stromal components. This remodeled local environment, known as the TME, not only reinforces HPV-mediated immune evasion but also fosters therapeutic resistance through intricate regulatory networks. The TME thus plays a critical role in sustaining malignancy and shaping the response to treatment.

Characteristics of the cervical cancer microenvironment

The TME, a dynamically evolving pathological niche, comprises heterogeneous cellular components, bioactive mediators, and specialized extracellular matrices. This complex microecosystem not only underpins malignant proliferation and metastasis but also profoundly influences therapeutic efficacy through multiple regulatory pathways. In cervical cancer, the TME is characterized by a distinctly immunosuppressive landscape underpinned by diverse immune evasion mechanisms [29]. From a cellular standpoint, the cervical cancer TME is enriched with immunosuppressive cell subsets that impair effector T-cell function [30]. At the molecular level, immune checkpoint pathways serve as critical regulatory hubs, with inhibitory costimulatory signals such as the PD-1/PD-L1 axis [31], CTLA-4 [32], and LAG-3 [33], modulating T-cell activity through distinct immunoregulatory mechanisms.

This section introduces three aspects of the cervical cancer TME (acidic environment, enzyme overexpression, and redox imbalance) and highlights their relevance as endogenous triggers for stimuli-responsive hydrogel-based therapies [34]. Owing to the Warburg effect, cervical cancer cells exhibit aberrantly upregulated glycolysis, leading to sustained lactate production even under normoxic conditions. Consequently, the extracellular pH within the TME decreases significantly (typically pH 6.5–6.8). This acidified microenvironment facilitates immune evasion by simultaneously impairing CD8+ T-cell cytotoxicity and promoting regulatory T-cell (Treg) infiltration and activation, thereby establishing an immunosuppressive barrier [35]. Moreover, hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) overexpression enhances lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA) expression and monocarboxylate transporter activity, further accelerating lactate efflux and acidification, thereby perpetuating a self-amplifying pathological loop [[36], [37], [38]].

Capitalizing on this hallmark feature, researchers have engineered pH-responsive smart hydrogels capable of undergoing targeted swelling or degradation in acidic environments, facilitating the localized release of therapeutic agents such as chemotherapeutics (e.g., doxorubicin) and immune checkpoint inhibitors (e.g., anti-PD-1 antibodies). For example, Jalalvandi et al. developed a chitosan (CS)-based pH-sensitive hydrogel for the delivery of doxorubicin in cervical cancer and demonstrated enhanced drug retention and tumor-specific release in response to acidic pH [10]. In parallel, the overexpression of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), particularly MMP-2 and MMP-9, within the cervical cancer TME plays a central role in promoting tumor invasion and metastasis. MMPs degrade key components of the basement membrane, such as type IV collagen, thereby facilitating tumor cell dissemination (Fig. 1D) [39,40].

Furthermore, the HPV oncoproteins E6/E7 upregulate MMP expression via activation of the MAPK/ERK and PI3K/AKT signaling pathways, synergizing with HIF-1α to establish a prometastatic positive feedback loop [41,42]. Leveraging this pathological feature, enzyme-responsive hydrogels have been developed by incorporating MMP-specific substrate peptides, enabling site-specific and stimuli-triggered drug release within the TME. For example, Chen et al. designed peptide-based hydrogels utilizing Ac-I3SLKG-NH2 and Ac-I3SLGK-NH2 sequences, of which the former is selectively cleaved by MMP-2. This cleavage-mediated structural collapse facilitated the controlled release of the anticancer peptide G(IIKK)3I-NH2 (G3), achieving a cumulative release efficiency of 74.5 % within 72 h. This system significantly suppressed HeLa cell proliferation and effectively induced apoptosis in vitro [11]. In addition to enzymatic dysregulation, redox imbalance constitutes another hallmark of the cervical cancer TME and is characterized by widespread metabolic reprogramming and aberrant redox signaling. Compared with normal cells, tumor cells exhibit markedly elevated intracellular glutathione (GSH) levels, creating a highly reductive intracellular environment. This GSH-rich state enables tumor cells to mitigate oxidative stress by neutralizing reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby preserving redox homeostasis, which is essential for sustaining rapid proliferation [[43], [44], [45]]. Concurrently, mitochondrial dysfunction and the overactivation of NADPH oxidases lead to excessive ROS accumulation (e.g., hydrogen peroxide, H2O2), which in turn induces genomic instability and activates oncogenic signaling pathways such as the NF-κB and HIF-1α pathways [46,47]. To exploit these redox vulnerabilities, GSH/ROS-responsive hydrogel systems have been engineered for site-specific chemotherapeutic delivery. These platforms undergo structural transformation in response to oxidative or reductive triggers within the TME, enabling on-demand drug release. For example, Jun et al. developed a ROS-sensitive hydrogel embedded with carbon dots (CDs) that exerted antitumor effects through a combination of ROS-scavenging activity and photothermal therapy (PTT), ultimately achieving significant tumor volume reduction.

The unique characteristics of the cervical cancer TME—acidic pH, MMP overexpression, and redox imbalance—collectively contribute to resistance to conventional treatment modalities. In this context, endogenous stimuli-responsive smart drug delivery systems offer spatiotemporal precision in therapeutic intervention. This multistage strategy—comprising environmental sensing, targeted delivery, and dynamic response—represents a promising paradigm for overcoming the off-target toxicity of traditional chemotherapy and the limited efficacy observed in current immunotherapeutic approaches.

Cervical cancer treatment strategies

Conventional cervical cancer treatments include surgery (for early-stage, >90 % 5-yr survival in IA but with recurrence and complications risks) [[48], [49], [50]], radiotherapy (for locally advanced, 40–60 % 5-yr survival but with toxicity and radioresistance issues) [[51], [52], [53]], chemotherapy (systemic efficacy limited by toxicity and drug resistance) [8,53], and targeted therapy (e.g., bevacizumab improving survival in combination therapy) [5,54]. Cervical cancer treatment methods.

The pursuit of novel therapeutic strategies is driven by the need to overcome the inherent limitations of conventional treatments. Among these, local precision therapy, gene therapy, and immunotherapy-based combination approaches have emerged as prominent areas of ongoing research. Local precision therapy, typically administered via intratumoral injection, minimizes systemic exposure and off-target effects. However, its clinical utility is limited by challenges such as heterogeneous intratumoral drug distribution and procedural risks associated with repeated drug administration, including bleeding, infection, and potential tumor seeding [53]. Nanodrug delivery systems, exemplified by albumin-bound paclitaxel (Abraxane), have improved tumor targeting through enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effects. Nevertheless, their lack of dynamic responsiveness to the TME limits their therapeutic efficacy in cervical cancer [55]. Gene therapy offers another promising avenue, with RNA interference (siRNA) and CRISPR/Cas9 technologies enabling targeted silencing of oncogenic HPV E6/E7 genes, thereby reactivating tumor suppressor pathways involving p53 and Rb. In vitro studies have shown that such interventions can inhibit HeLa cell proliferation by more than 70 % [56]. Additionally, oncolytic virotherapy, involving genetically modified viruses such as adenoviruses, has demonstrated selective cytotoxicity toward HPV-positive cells, achieving tumor volume reductions of approximately 50 % in preclinical models. However, clinical translation remains hindered by low in vivo transduction efficiency and immunogenicity concerns, including potent host immune responses elicited by viral vectors [57].

Cervical cancer treatment is undergoing a paradigm shift from traditional modalities—surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy—toward more precise and individualized therapeutic strategies. Despite notable progress, each approach faces distinct limitations. Future research must focus on refining treatment regimens by integrating advanced technologies such as stimuli-responsive hydrogels that can adapt to patient-specific tumor microenvironmental cues. The development of intelligent drug delivery platforms, alongside the identification of novel immunotherapeutic targets, holds significant potential to advance precision medicine in cervical cancer management.

Cervical cancer diagnosis strategies

According to the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) Guidelines, the standard diagnosis of cervical cancer relies on clinical evaluation, imaging studies, and pathological confirmation [[58], [59], [60], [61], [62], [63]]. Diagnostic processes should be implemented within a multidisciplinary framework to support the development of personalized diagnostic and treatment strategies.

Despite advances, the clinical diagnosis of cervical cancer remains limited by several factors. First, cervical biopsy—the current gold standard—is subject to sampling error, particularly in cases of microinvasive carcinoma or glandular lesions such as adenocarcinoma. Although conization can increase diagnostic accuracy, it is an invasive procedure and may compromise fertility. Second, imaging modalities have notable limitations: MRI demonstrates only moderate sensitivity (∼70 %) for detecting early parametrial invasion, and PET/CT has limited accuracy (50–70 %) for detecting lymph node metastases smaller than 5 mm, potentially resulting in staging inaccuracies.

To overcome these diagnostic challenges, stimuli-responsive hydrogels have emerged as promising adjuncts in cervical cancer diagnostics. By incorporating functional components such as biosensors or contrast agents, these hydrogels can respond to TME-specific cues, such as acidic pH or overexpressed enzymes, by releasing fluorescent signals or imaging contrast at the lesion site. This strategy enhances the detection of microlesions, early stromal invasion, and micrometastases with improved spatial resolution. Furthermore, the inherent biocompatibility and tunable degradability of hydrogels reduce interference with surrounding normal tissues, offering a minimally invasive, real-time, high-precision diagnostic tool. Such systems may serve to increase the accuracy and safety of conventional diagnostic modalities, thereby advancing the paradigm of precision oncology in patients with cervical cancer.

Overview of stimuli-responsive hydrogels

Stimuli-responsive hydrogels

Stimuli-responsive hydrogels, a class of intelligent materials endowed with environmental sensing capabilities, are characterized by their ability to undergo dynamic restructuring of their molecular network in response to external stimuli such as temperature, pH, light, magnetic fields, or specific chemical environments. These structural changes result in various responsive behaviors, including swelling, contraction, color changes, or controlled substance release. Owing to their excellent biocompatibility, tunable physicochemical properties, and ability to interact with the biological environment, these hydrogels have significant applications in advanced fields such as controlled drug delivery systems, biosensing interfaces, tissue engineering scaffolds, and environmental monitoring devices [64]. Stimuli-responsive hydrogels are typically three-dimensional network structures composed of hydrophilic polymers that are crosslinked either physically or chemically. These materials can absorb large amounts of water while maintaining their structural integrity [65]. The unique ability of these hydrogels to respond to environmental triggers such as pH, temperature, redox conditions, enzymatic activity, or light, coupled with their biocompatibility, makes them invaluable for applications in drug delivery, tissue engineering, wound healing, and biosensing. Stimuli-responsive hydrogels can be broadly classified into two major categories on the basis of their polymer origin: natural-based hydrogels and synthetic-based hydrogels. These materials exhibit significant differences in performance and applications while also achieving complementary advantages through semi synthetic strategies [66]. Owing to their excellent biocompatibility and responsiveness to environmental stimuli, natural compound hydrogels are highly favorable for applications in drug delivery and tissue engineering. These hydrogels are primarily composed of natural polymers sourced from biological organisms, such as polysaccharides, proteins, and nucleic acids. Synthetic compound hydrogels allow precise control over their properties through molecular design, making them highly suitable for multifunctional combination therapies. Classification of natural and synthetic hydrogels.

Both natural and synthetic hydrogels offer distinct advantages and limitations in terms of performance and application potential (Table 3). Natural hydrogels derived from natural polymers such as proteins or polysaccharides are highly desirable because of their excellent biocompatibility and biodegradability. These materials can effectively mimic the extracellular matrix and promote cell adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation, making them ideal for applications in tissue engineering, drug delivery, and regenerative medicine [67,68]. Moreover, their natural origin minimizes their environmental impact, contributing to sustainability in biomedical applications [69]. However, natural hydrogels typically exhibit low mechanical strength and are prone to deformation or rupture under stress, which limits their application in high-load environments [70,71]. Additionally, their rapid degradation rate, poor stability, and difficulty in precisely controlling physical and chemical properties further restrict their use in long-term or complex applications [69,70].

Comparison between natural and synthetic hydrogels.

In contrast, synthetic hydrogels enhance mechanical strength through chemical crosslinking, making them suitable for applications in high-load environments. The physical and chemical properties of synthetic hydrogels can be finely tuned through molecular design to meet specific functional requirements [69]. Moreover, synthetic hydrogels generally exhibit slower degradation rates and greater stability, which makes them more suitable for long-term use [69,70]. However, their artificial nature can lead to potential biocompatibility issues, such as triggering immune responses or toxicity. In addition, some synthetic hydrogels are difficult to degrade completely, which limits their widespread biomedical application. Furthermore, the production and disposal of synthetic hydrogels can impose environmental burdens [69,70].

To harness the advantages of both natural and synthetic hydrogels, researchers have developed composite hydrogels. By combining natural and synthetic components, composite hydrogels retain the biocompatibility and biodegradability of natural hydrogels while significantly enhancing their mechanical strength and stability [72]. While natural hydrogels excel in terms of biocompatibility and cell adhesion, they suffer from limited mechanical properties and stability. Conversely, synthetic hydrogels perform well in terms of mechanical properties and design flexibility but may lack biocompatibility and degradability. The development of composite hydrogels offers a promising solution to these challenges, expanding their potential applications in the biomedical field.

Unique value of different stimuli-responsive hydrogels in cervical cancer diagnosis and treatment

While various nanodrug delivery systems (e.g., liposomes, polymeric micelles) and local implant technologies have shown potential in cervical cancer therapy, stimuli-responsive hydrogels have emerged as particularly compelling candidates. This is largely attributed to their unique three-dimensional network architecture, high water content, and capacity for dynamic environmental responsiveness. Compared to liposomes and micelles [73,74], hydrogels not only exhibit superior drug loading capacity and prolonged local retention, but also enable on-demand drug release in response to tumor microenvironmental stimuli (e.g., pH, enzymes, ROS) or external stimuli (e.g., light, magnetic fields), thereby minimizing systemic toxicity and improving therapeutic precision [75]. Furthermore, unlike surgically implanted local depots, many hydrogels possess injectable properties and the ability to undergo in situ gelation, significantly enhancing clinical applicability and patient compliance [76]. Although physical targeting techniques such as ultrasound-targeted microbubble destruction (UTMD) can enhance drug penetration, they often lack sustained release capabilities [77]. In contrast, hydrogels can integrate multiple treatment modalities (e.g., chemotherapy, PTT, immunomodulation), enabling multimodal synergistic therapy [78]. Thus, stimuli-responsive hydrogels not only address the limitations of conventional nanocarriers but also provide an integrated platform for precision theranostics in cervical cancer.

In the diagnosis and treatment of cervical cancer, stimuli-responsive hydrogels offer distinctive adaptability and application value depending on their response mechanisms. On the basis of the nature of the stimuli, these hydrogels are generally categorized into endogenous and exogenous response types. Endogenous stimuli originate from the TME of cervical cancer and include factors such as pH, specific enzymatic activity, and redox conditions. In contrast, exogenous stimuli involve externally applied physical triggers such as light and magnetic fields. Notably, temperature stimuli represent a unique dual-response mechanism: they can be endogenous (arising from physiological temperature) or induced via exogenous sources such as photothermal or magnetothermal conversion. Summary and comparison of different stimuli-responsive rydrogels.

As advanced carriers for precision medicine in the treatment of cervical cancer, intelligently responsive hydrogels exhibit substantial translational potential owing to their environmental sensitivity and multifunctional therapeutic synergy. Temperature-responsive hydrogels regulate drug release through lower or upper critical solution temperature (LCST/UCST) mechanisms. LCST-type hydrogels undergo a phase transition from hydrophilic to hydrophobic in response to physiological or externally applied hyperthermia (typically 41–45 °C), facilitating enhanced drug diffusion and tumor ablation. The incorporation of biocompatible materials such as gelatin or poly(N-isopropylacrylamide) (PNIPAM) further ensures clinical safety [79]. Light-responsive hydrogels leverage the tissue-penetrating capabilities of near-infrared (NIR) light to achieve precise, spatiotemporal drug release via synergistic photothermal and photodynamic effects. Photothermal conversion elevates the local temperature, directly inducing tumor apoptosis and triggering controlled drug release. Simultaneously, photoacoustic imaging (PAI) enables integrated diagnostics and therapy [80]. Magnetically responsive hydrogels are engineered with superparamagnetic nanoparticles (e.g., Fe3O4 or γ-Fe2O3), allowing targeted delivery under external magnetic field guidance. Alternating magnetic fields induce localized magnetothermal effects that enhance drug release and provide direct thermal ablation. These hydrogels also offer MRI contrast capabilities, enabling real-time treatment monitoring [81,82]. pH-responsive hydrogels exploit the slightly acidic TME by modulating their swelling behavior through protonation or deprotonation of ionizable groups (e.g., carboxyl or amino groups), thereby achieving tumor-specific drug release [83,84]. Enzyme-responsive hydrogels incorporate cleavable linkers—such as peptide sequences sensitive to MMPs—enabling site-specific degradation and activation of therapeutic cascades. These systems autonomously respond to pathological enzyme levels, obviating the need for external intervention [85,86]. Redox-responsive hydrogels are tailored to the high GSH and ROS levels characteristic of tumors. They enable precise, on-demand drug release through the cleavage of dynamic covalent bonds (e.g., disulfide linkages). Moreover, GSH depletion can sensitize tumors to chemotherapy and augment oxidative stress-mediated cytotoxicity [87,88]. All types of smart hydrogels share advantages such as biodegradability, biocompatibility, and tumor-targeted controlled release. By integrating multimodal therapeutic strategies—including hyperthermia, chemotherapy, and immunomodulation—and combining therapeutic and diagnostic functionalities, these systems hold transformative promise for overcoming drug resistance, reducing systemic toxicity, and enhancing deep-tissue delivery. Collectively, these findings offer innovative and personalized solutions for next-generation cervical cancer therapy.

Application of stimuli-responsive hydrogels in cervical cancer treatment

Stimuli-responsive hydrogels, as advanced drug delivery systems, offer distinctive advantages in the treatment of cervical cancer. By responding to alterations in the TME or external physical stimuli—such as temperature, light, magnetic fields, pH, and enzymatic activity—these hydrogels undergo structural or functional changes that enable precise, controlled drug release and targeted delivery (Fig. 2). These materials hold significant promise in addressing the inherent heterogeneity, high recurrence rate, and metastatic potential of cervical cancer, thereby overcoming the limitations of conventional chemotherapy. Through mechanisms such as localized high-concentration drug accumulation, spatiotemporally controlled release, and multimodal therapeutic synergy, stimuli-responsive hydrogels facilitate effective tumor cell eradication while minimizing systemic toxicity.

Temperature-responsive hydrogels

Temperature-responsive hydrogels represent a class of intelligent biomaterials that leverage the synergistic interplay between the phase transition behavior of polymer chains and external temperature stimuli (Table 5). These hydrogels are crosslinked polymer networks capable of swelling in aqueous environments and retaining substantial amounts of water. Upon exposure to environmental temperature variations, these hydrogels undergo volume changes or phase transitions [89]. As a key subset of smart materials, temperature-responsive hydrogels undergo reversible or irreversible phase transitions in response to temperature fluctuations. The core mechanism involves rapid sol‒gel phase transformation of the polymer chains, triggered by changes in the hydrophobic‒hydrophilic balance when the temperature reaches a critical threshold. This transformation results in the formation of physically or chemically crosslinked network structures [90]. Temperature-responsive hydrogels are primarily categorized into two types on the basis of their phase transition mechanism. The first type is lower critical solution temperature (LCST) hydrogels. These hydrogels transition from hydrophilic to hydrophobic when the temperature exceeds a critical threshold, leading to a gel-to-liquid transition [91]. The second type is UCST hydrogels, where gelation occurs when the temperature falls below the critical value, and the polymer‒solvent system, initially phase-separated at lower temperatures, becomes a homogeneous solution at elevated temperatures [79]. Temperature-responsive hydrogels offer significant advantages in the context of cervical cancer therapy. Their temperature-sensitive nature allows for precise, on-demand drug release control, which can be triggered by physiological body temperature or external heat sources such as NIR light. This enables spatiotemporally selective drug release, thereby avoiding systemic exposure to chemotherapeutic agents and minimizing toxicity to healthy tissues. Moreover, the sustained-release and long-acting properties of these hydrogels help maintain therapeutic drug concentrations, reducing the frequency of drug administration [92,93]. When combined with hyperthermia, the application of temperatures in the range of 41–45 °C not only induces direct tumor cell damage but also causes vasodilation, thereby enhancing the penetration of therapeutic agents into deep tissue regions [94,95]. From a materials perspective, temperature-responsive hydrogels incorporate biocompatible natural polymers (such as gelatin and CS) or tunable synthetic materials (e.g., PNIPAM), which combine biocompatibility with biodegradability. This reduces the risk of foreign body reactions and eliminates the need for secondary surgical removal, providing an efficient and safe platform for targeted tumor therapy [96,97].

PEG-based triblock copolymers are typically composed of two hydrophilic PEG segments alternately linked with a single hydrophobic segment, such as polypropylene glycol (PPO), polylactic acid (PLA), or polycaprolactone (PCL), resulting in a "hydrophilic‒hydrophilic‒hydrophilic" triblock structure (e.g., PEG‒PPO‒PEG or PEG‒PCL‒PEG) [98]. The temperature-responsive behavior of these copolymers arises from the conformational change in the hydrophobic segment, which becomes less soluble upon heating. Moreover, the hydrophilic PEG segment interacts with water molecules via hydrogen bonds, maintaining the system's dynamic equilibrium. When the temperature exceeds the critical phase transition temperature (e.g., LCST), the hydrophobic interactions of the hydrophobic segment are enhanced, promoting the self-assembly of copolymer molecules into micelles or three-dimensional network structures, resulting in reversible sol‒gel transition behavior [99]. This property can be finely tuned by adjusting the block ratio, molecular weight, and end-group modifications. For example, increasing the length of the hydrophobic segment lowers the phase transition temperature [100]. The PEG-PCL-PEG (PECE) system responds to heat via aggregation of the hydrophobic PCL segment. At low temperatures (<25 °C), PCL is extended because of the hydrophilic shielding afforded by PEG, resulting in an injectable sol. At 30–37 °C, PCL forms a physically crosslinked core via hydrophobic interactions, with PEG forming a hydrophilic shell. The storage modulus (G′) increases from 10 Pa to 1000 Pa, forming a semicrystalline micellar network. The phase transition temperature can be regulated by adjusting the PCL/PEG ratio; for example, increasing the PCL molecular weight from 2000 to 4000 Da lowers the gel temperature from 34 °C to 28 °C, allowing the material to adapt to different tissue environments [101]. The PLGA-PEG-PLGA system utilizes a dual mechanism. The hydrophobic PLGA segments form the micelle core via van der Waals forces, whereas PEG dehydration leads to micelle shrinkage. At 37 °C, a 20 wt% solution achieves a G′ of 450 Pa, with shear-thinning allowing injection through a 26G needle (Fig. 3A) [102]. The PDLLA-PEG-PDLLA system exhibited concentration‒temperature synergy. At 20 wt%, PDLLA segments form 85 nm micelles at 35 °C, increasing tumor penetration and retention [103]. These materials demonstrate significant potential in terms of their designable functionality and intelligent controlled release capabilities. The PECE hydrogel achieved sustained cisplatin release over 14 days, with local concentrations 8 times greater than those achieved via intravenous administration [101]. The PLGA-PEG-PLGA hydrogel released 25 % EGF in 72 h, significantly inhibiting HeLa cell migration and reversing cisplatin resistance [102]. The PDLLA gel showed dual-drug release at pH 6.5, reducing the tumor volume by 63 % [103]. Through molecular engineering, temperature-responsive hydrogels provide a new paradigm for precision tumor therapy. The injectability, high local concentration maintenance, and microenvironment regulation of these materials offer advanced solutions for cancer treatment.

A poloxamer (Pluronic) is a thermosensitive hydrogel composed of polyoxyethylene-polyoxypropylene-polyoxyethylene (PEO-PPO-PEO) triblock copolymers. Its temperature-responsive mechanism results from the PEO and PPO blocks: below the critical gelation temperature (CGT), the poloxamer molecules are dispersed as single chains in solution. Above CGT (typically 25–36 °C), the hydrophobic PPO segment dehydrates, forming a dense micellar core, whereas the hydrophilic PEO segment forms a micellar shell, creating a three-dimensional gel structure [104]. In Wang et al.'s study, a blend of 22 % Poloxamer 407 and 5 % Poloxamer 188 had a CGT of 35 °C, ensuring gelation after vaginal administration to prolong the residence time [105]. Another study demonstrated that a 27 % poloxamer solution with a CGT as low as 24 °C improved intravaginal stability [104]. This temperature-dependent phase transition allows easy injection and in situ solidification. At 20 °C, the poloxamer behaves as a Newtonian fluid (viscosity of 0.38 Pa s), whereas at 37 °C, the storage modulus (G′) increases to 16998 Pa, forming a strong gel that prevents drug leakage and controls drug release [105]. Poloxamer gels, including hydrophobic drugs such as cisplatin and carboplatin, have excellent biocompatibility, simple preparation, and broad drug-loading capacity. Poloxamer gels have shown significant efficacy in the treatment of cervical cancer. A cisplatin-loaded gel achieved a tumor inhibition rate of 63.1 %, which was higher than that of intravenous treatment (51 %) [105]. Carboplatin-loaded gels reduce tumor recurrence, demonstrating efficient local chemotherapy with minimal systemic toxicity [104]. These gels offer a new approach for high-efficacy, low-toxicity cervical cancer treatment.

The thermosensitive double-network hydrogel P(AEtMA-Cl-DEAEA)/PA enables dynamic cell release via a temperature-regulated hydrophobic-hydrophilic balance. At body temperature, hydrophobic P(AEtMA-Cl-DEAEA) promotes cell attachment and proliferation. Cooling to 20 °C enhances polymer hydrophilicity, triggering nonenzymatic detachment of approximately 32.6 % of HeLa cells, with the released cells remaining viable [106]. PNIPAM-based thermosensitive nanoparticles utilize their LCST ≈33 °C. Above the LCST, these materials contract hydrophobically, release encapsulated components, and swell at lower temperatures to form a porous scaffold that promotes cell growth. No toxicity was observed in HeLa and HEK293 cells, with increased metabolic activity compared with that of the controls [107]. P(AEtMA-Cl-DEAEA)/PA enhances mechanical strength via a polyacrylamide network, mimicking natural cartilage tissue and withstanding dynamic in vivo environments (Fig. 3B) [106]. In contrast, PNIPAM nanoparticles support efficient cell growth with a nanoporous structure, offering easy synthesis and excellent biocompatibility [107]. P(AEtMA-Cl-DEAEA)/PA is ideal for controlled release in cell therapy, while PNIPAM scaffolds are suitable for temperature-responsive drug delivery and wound healing, avoiding cell membrane damage from traditional enzymatic detachment.

Beyond synthetic polymers, genetically engineered biomimetic polypeptides have emerged as advanced thermoresponsive materials. A representative example is the O-CUBE hydrogel, constructed from elastin-like polypeptides (ELPs) and a coiled-coil crosslinking domain. This system exhibits sharp thermal transition at physiological temperature, forming a stable network within minutes that excellently encapsulates and supports the growth of HeLa cells, guiding them to form tumor-like spheroids. This injectable platform highlights the potential of protein-engineered hydrogels to recreate complex 3D tissue-like architectures for cell-based studies and modeling cervical cancer [108].

As carriers for cervical cancer treatment, temperature-responsive hydrogels utilize hydrophobic aggregation and reversible phase transitions for spatiotemporal drug release through dynamic crosslinked networks. The advantages of this method include injectability, biodegradability, and tunable response sensitivity. However, challenges in controlling the mechanical strength and degradation rate, and addressing TME interference remain. Future development will focus on interdisciplinary integration and preclinical validation, with temperature-responsive hydrogels poised to revolutionize cervical cancer treatment.

Light-responsive hydrogels

Light-responsive hydrogels are intelligent materials that respond to specific light stimuli and undergo reversible or irreversible structural changes such as swelling, contraction, or degradation, enabling drug release or localized therapy [109]. These hydrogels are categorized into photothermal-responsive, photochemical-responsive, and photomechanical-responsive types [[110], [111], [112]]. These hydrogels are categorized into photothermal-responsive, photochemical-responsive, and photomechanical-responsive types [80], while photodynamic therapy (PDT) generates ROS through photosensitizers under light irradiation, triggering tumor apoptosis [111]. Light-responsive hydrogels offer several advantages for cervical cancer treatment. The precise targeting mechanism ensures that drug release is localized to the tumor site, minimizing damage to healthy tissues [113,114]. Additionally, NIR light offers deeper tissue penetration, activating photosensitizers or photothermal agents within deep-seated tumors, thus expanding treatment coverage [113]. These hydrogels can also be combined with PAI for simultaneous therapy and diagnosis. Studies have shown that light-responsive hydrogels with high-concentration photosensitizers maintain >90 % cell viability, demonstrating excellent biocompatibility [115]. Hydrogel materials, with tunable physicochemical properties and excellent biocompatibility, offer unique advantages in PTT. Various material systems achieve photothermal responses and therapeutic effects through distinct mechanisms. An oxidized alginate (OA)-based hydrogel, loaded with polyaniline (PANI) and poly (sodium 4-styrenesulfonate) (PSS) nanoparticles, uses the π‒π conjugated structure of PANI to absorb NIR light. Under acidic conditions, the doped state of PANI enhances NIR absorption, and PSS maintains a protonation environment, enabling sustained photothermal performance (ΔT up to 53 °C in 5 min) even at neutral pH. The degradation rate of OA is tunable, indicating its injectability for long-term therapy [116]. A peptide-engineered hydrogel system with gold nanorods (GNRs) utilizes localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) to absorb NIR light (808 nm), converting it into heat (ΔT up to 55 °C in 5 min). This system electrostatically adsorbs chemotherapy drugs such as DOX and leverages pH responsiveness and photothermal triggering for controlled drug release. Compared with monotherapy, combined therapy was more effective against HeLa cells (Fig. 4A) [117]. Another system, the PVA/CS layered microneedle, generates localized thermal effects through NIR absorption by gold-core mesoporous silica-shell (AuMSS) nanorods, reducing HeLa cell viability to less than 20 % with combined therapy (Fig. 4B) [118]. A recent carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC)-based hydrogel featuring ROS-cleavable thioketal crosslinks enables on-demand drug release via a dual photochemical mechanism. Under NIR irradiation, co-loaded ICG simultaneously generates heat (57 °C) and ROS, which cleave the thioketal linkages, triggering rapid DOX release (67 % in 24 h). This light-triggered ROS cascade enables synergistic chemo-PTT, potently inhibiting HeLa cells (6.7 % viability) for precise on-demand delivery [119]. A polydopamine (PDA)-modified PTMC core-shell scaffold leverages NIR absorption for photothermal conversion (ΔT up to 25 °C in 3 min) and dual drug release, which inhibits tumor cell proliferation and promotes tissue repair (Fig. 4C) [120].

The diverse design strategies of hydrogel materials in PTT reflect the dynamic interplay between material systems and functional requirements. OA-based hydrogels focus on conductive polymers and self-doping mechanisms to address the performance degradation of traditional photothermal materials in neutral environments. Its tunable degradation rate is conducive to long-term therapeutic interventions, although the absence of active targeting may limit the localized accumulation of drugs [116]. The peptide‒GNR composite system achieves precise synergy between PTT and chemotherapy by leveraging noble metal plasmonics and RGD peptide-mediated active targeting [117]. However, the rapid degradation of the peptide network may compromise the long-term efficacy of the treatment. The PVA/CS MN system integrates both physical penetration and chemical responsiveness to overcome tumor barriers, yet its relatively low PTT conversion efficiency limits its ability to induce high-temperature ablation effects [118]. The core-shell scaffold, which utilizes PDA for broad absorption and thermosensitive controlled release, enables sequential therapy, but its complex structure may complicate fabrication processes [120]. While all four approaches exploit the porous structure and stimuli-responsive nature of hydrogels for PTT-drug synergy, they emphasize different aspects: some prioritize material stability and longevity, whereas others focus on innovative integrations of targeting mechanisms and physical penetration strategies. Future research should aim to strike a balance between targeting efficiency, PTT conversion, and degradation kinetics, while also addressing the biocompatibility of PTT agents and the depth of tissue penetration to facilitate clinical translation.

Light-responsive hydrogels enhance PDT through photosensitizer loading, controlled release, and TME optimization. Poloxamer-based thermosensitive hydrogels (GHCAC) integrate cerium nanoparticles (CeNPs) and calcium peroxide (CaO2) to target and retain chlorin e6 (Ce6), a photosensitizer. When activated by NIR light, Ce6 generates singlet oxygen (1O2) to induce tumor apoptosis. CeNPs decompose H2O2 in the TME, alleviating hypoxia and improving PDT efficacy. Additionally, Ce6 oxidizes L-arginine (L-Arg), releasing nitric oxide (NO) and enabling synergistic PDT and gas therapy. The system's "single injection, multiple irradiations" strategy prolongs the drug action time and reduces toxicity (Fig. 4D) [121].Another CS–liposome composite hydrogel encapsulates zinc phthalocyanine (ZnPc), improving dispersibility and generating ROS upon light excitation. The bioadhesive properties of CS ensure sustained drug release and localized photodynamic killing. This system results in a greater than 95 % reduction in HeLa cell viability due to enhanced cellular uptake and controlled drug release [122].Both of these light-responsive hydrogel systems effectively optimize the delivery and photoactivity of photosensitizers, taking advantage of their biodegradability, local retention, and photodynamic effects to offer highly efficient, low-toxicity strategies for tumor therapy. By improving drug delivery efficiency and enhancing therapeutic efficacy, they provide a promising direction for precision medicine in cancer treatment.

pH-responsive hydrogels

The core characteristic of pH-responsive hydrogels is their sensitivity to pH changes, enabling targeted drug release at tumor sites. The TME typically has a weakly acidic pH (6.5–7.0), whereas normal tissues have a near-neutral pH (∼7.4) [114]. pH-responsive hydrogels undergo swelling or contraction in response to pH changes on the basis of pH-sensitive functional groups in their molecular chains [123]. Acidic groups (e.g., carboxyl -COOH) remain protonated at low pH, causing hydrogel contraction via hydrogen bonds or hydrophobic interactions. At high pH, deprotonation results in swelling due to increased electrostatic repulsion. Similarly, basic groups (e.g., amino -NH2) protonate at low pH, promoting swelling through electrostatic repulsion, and deprotonate under alkaline conditions, leading to contraction [83,84]. Additionally, pH changes can influence a hydrogel's dynamic crosslinking structure through factors such as crosslinking density, and ionic strength [124]. In tumor therapy, pH-responsive hydrogels allow precise drug release at tumor sites, minimizing toxicity to healthy tissues [125]. Moreover, by adjusting the pH-responsive range of the hydrogel, on-demand drug release can be achieved [126]. pH-responsive hydrogels are useful not only for drug delivery but also for tumor detection and therapeutic monitoring [123,127]. Different studies have achieved precise responses to the acidic pH of the TME by controlling the chemical composition and structure of hydrogels, with mechanisms involving primarily ionic interactions, hydrophobic–hydrophilic balance transitions, and coordination bond dissociation (Table 6).

Carboxymethyl chitosan (CC) and CS-based hydrogels offer unique advantages in pH-responsive drug delivery. CC-modified hollow mesoporous silica nanoparticles (CCs-HMSNs) create a pH-responsive gating system through covalent crosslinking between carboxyl and amino groups. Under neutral conditions, CC molecules aggregate to seal mesoporous channels. In acidic environments, protonation of carboxyl groups causes polymer chain disaggregation and gel formation, opening pores and accelerating drug release. This system achieved a drug loading capacity of 64.74 μg/mg, with a release rate of 56 % at pH 6.0 over 108 h, which was significantly greater than the 17 % release at neutral pH. The mesoporous structure improves drug loading efficiency and induces HeLa apoptosis [128]. CS-based in situ-formed hydrogels utilize pH-sensitive Schiff base crosslinking and degrade rapidly in acidic environments (pH 4.5). Low crosslinking density gels degrade within 6 days, whereas highly crosslinked gels take 11 days. The degradation rate is due to accelerated hydrolysis of imine bonds and protonation of unreacted amino groups. The swelling ratio of the 3 % gel at pH 4.5 is 15 % greater than that at neutral pH [10]. CCs-HMSNs release drugs such as DOX, with nearly 70 % inhibition of HeLa cells at 96 h, and show biocompatibility. CS delivers the nonhormonal contraceptive FeGl and DOX, achieving 95 % sustained release of DOX over 72 h and 80 % mesenchymal stem cell survival [10]. These systems demonstrate high drug loading, controlled release, and excellent biosafety for tumor-targeted therapy and local drug delivery.

In addition to CS, numerous pH-responsive hydrogel materials have been developed. Yamamoto et al. created a peptide amphiphile (C16-VVAEEE) that self-assembles into a nanofiber network under weakly acidic conditions, enhancing drug retention and achieving selective cell killing by disrupting membrane integrity in tumor cells (pH < 7) [129]. Kaushik et al. constructed a zein‒pectin (PEC) composite hydrogel, utilizing enhanced hydrophobic interactions to enable rapid DOX release in acidic environments, promoting tumor cell uptake via charge reversal [130]. For multiple pH responses, Mekonnen et al. developed a sodium deoxycholate hydrogel that modulates drug release through protonation of the carboxyl group, in synergy with dendrimers to increase the efficacy of resveratrol and DOX [131]. Kuddushi et al. designed a polyvinyl alcohol/ionic liquid composite system that regulates drug diffusion rates via pH-dependent hydrogen bond reorganization and is suitable for topical application with tumor-specific drug release [132]. Guan et al. developed a nanodiamond carrier that releases drugs efficiently in an acidic intracellular environment, combining high drug loading with excellent biocompatibility [133]. Raspberry-like hydrogels formed by polyglutamate cholesterol nanoparticles (RBHPs) show significant membrane disruption ability, achieving 78 % hemolysis at pH 5.0, and when combined with DOX, reduce HeLa cell viability to 23 % within 24 h [134]. A dual-drug-loaded system, Na-DOC-hyd-RESV + G4.5-DOX, increased tumor suppression by 2.3-fold through sequential release [131]. These studies collectively highlight that optimizing the pH responsiveness and drug release kinetics of hydrogels via molecular design, in conjunction with controlling biodegradability and functionalization for targeted delivery, can effectively balance therapeutic efficacy and systemic toxicity. This approach provides critical technological pathways for the advancement of precision cancer therapy.

pH-responsive hydrogels offer distinct advantages in the treatment of cervical cancer by enabling localized, high-concentration drug delivery while minimizing systemic toxicity. This is achieved through targeted, controlled drug release and modulation of the TME. Nevertheless, several challenges remain within current technologies: the pH heterogeneity within the TME may diminish response efficiency; the interference of complex biological fluids can compromise material stability; the long-term biocompatibility and safety of degradation products from certain synthetic materials require further investigation; and ensuring batch-to-batch consistency in large-scale production remains a significant hurdle.

Enzyme-responsive hydrogels

Enzyme-responsive hydrogels can undergo structural or functional alterations upon exposure to specific external enzymes, playing pivotal roles in the treatment of cervical cancer (Table 7). These hydrogels offer significant advantages in tumor therapy owing to their unique mechanisms. By selectively recognizing enzymes that are overexpressed in the TME (e.g., MMPs and hyaluronidases) and incorporating enzyme-cleavable bonds (e.g., peptide sequences), they enable precise degradation or drug release at lesion sites. This facilitates highly selective targeted therapy while minimizing off-target toxicity to normal tissues [85,86]. Moreover, the self-triggering nature of these hydrogels, which rely on endogenous bioenzymes, eliminates the need for external stimuli, thus reducing the risk of tissue damage associated with techniques such as PTT, which is particularly beneficial for deep-seated tumors [135,136]. Additionally, enzymatically released drugs can activate downstream cascade effects (e.g., promoting immune cell infiltration or enhancing DNA damage), dynamically adapting to tumor heterogeneity. Regions with greater malignancy and enzyme activity experience accelerated drug release, enabling on-demand therapeutic interventions [87,114]. This system can also be synergistically integrated with immunotherapy. For example, the release of anti-PD-1 antibodies in combination with chemotherapeutic agents, can alleviate immunosuppression and enhance T-cell-mediated cytotoxicity. From a material design perspective, the use of natural enzyme substrates (such as gelatin and collagen) or programmable synthetic peptides ensures excellent biocompatibility and biodegradability. Furthermore, the integration of pH/temperature-responsive units addresses the complexities of the TME, providing an efficient and safe platform for precision and personalized cancer therapy [137,138].

Enzyme, magnetic, redox and dual responsive hydrogels for the treatment of cervical cancer.

The Aurora kinase B (AURKB)-responsive hydrogel Gel S/AZD integrates the AURKB substrate sequence (RRKS) into a self-assembling peptide backbone (Fig. 5A), providing targeted therapy for cervical cancer cells [139]. Its mechanism relies on AURKB-mediated phosphorylation. When activated in the TME, the hydrophobic peptide Nap-Phe-Phe-Arg-Arg-Lys-Ser-OH (S) is phosphorylated to the hydrophilic Sp form, causing the nanofiber structure to disintegrate and release the inhibitor AZD1152-HQPA (Fig. 5B). This phosphorylation‒driven gel‒sol phase transition minimizes burst drug release and induces tumor cell polyploidization and apoptosis by inhibiting expression at pH 3. Compared with the free drug, the hydrogel led to a 68.6 % reduction in tumor volume in vivo, with reduced systemic toxicity. Similarly, the short peptide hydrogel Ac-I3SLKG-NH2 targets the MMP-2-overexpressing TME for on-demand release of the anticancer peptide G3 via an MMP-2-specific cleavage site (SL) [11]. The response of the hydrogel is based on enzymatic cleavage of the peptide, breaking it into fragments (Ac-I3S and LKG-NH2) that disrupt the β-sheet structure and collapse the nanofiber network. This cleavage resulted in 74.5 % release efficiency of G3 within 72 h, significantly inhibiting HeLa cell proliferation and inducing apoptosis (Fig. 5C), with excellent biocompatibility (NIH 3T3 cell viability >90 %) (Fig. 5D). Both systems utilize enzymatic reactions for controlled drug release, but their mechanisms differ: the AURKB system depends on phosphorylation-induced hydrophilic‒hydrophobic balance changes [139], whereas the MMP-2 system disrupts peptide assembly through bond cleavage [11]. Compared with chemotherapy, gel-S/AZD inhibits key kinase pathways, leading to superior tumor suppression [139]. The Ac-I3SLKG-NH2 system enhances the local peptide concentration and reduces systemic toxicity via an on-demand release mechanism [11]. AURKB- and MMP-2-responsive hydrogels enable precise drug delivery by mimicking natural enzyme substrates, increasing their efficacy through the inhibition of oncogenic signaling (AURKB) and disruption of the tumor stroma (MMP-2). Both exemplify the advantages of "smart materials" in overcoming chemotherapy-related toxicity and resistance. Future research should focus on optimizing the enzyme response, expanding therapeutic strategies, and advancing clinical translation, offering novel approaches for treating solid tumors such as cervical cancer.

Magnetic-responsive hydrogels

Magnetic-responsive hydrogels are functional materials composed of hydrogel matrices and magnetic nanoparticles (e.g., γ-Fe2O3, Fe3O4), offering unique advantages in tumor therapy, especially in drug delivery, hyperthermia, and imaging (Table 7). These hydrogels combine hydrophilic polymer networks with magnetic nanoparticles, imparting biocompatibility, remote controllability, and multifunctionality (e.g., drug delivery, magnetic hyperthermia, and MRI). In tumor therapy, magnetic-responsive hydrogels utilize drug delivery and magnetothermal effects to achieve therapeutic outcomes. The core mechanism of targeted drug release involves controlling the location and timing of drug delivery via an external magnetic field (e.g., alternating magnetic field, AMF). Upon exposure to an AMF, magnetic nanoparticles realign their magnetic moments with the field direction, generating localized heat [81,82].This heat causes the hydrogel matrix to expand or contract, altering its pore structure and promoting drug diffusion [140]. Since the magnetic field can guide nanoparticles to tumor sites, drug release directly targets tumor tissue, minimizing damage to surrounding healthy tissues. Magnetic field guidance allows for precise drug delivery to tumor sites, improving therapeutic efficacy and reducing side effects [141]. The use of AMF enables on-demand drug release, preventing fluctuations in drug concentrations typical of traditional chemotherapy [82,140]. Additionally, these hydrogels can be combined with other stimuli (e.g., temperature and pH) for more intelligent drug release strategies. Moreover, the heat generated by magnetic nanoparticles in an AMF not only triggers drug release but also directly induces tumor cell death through hyperthermia, further enhancing therapeutic outcomes [82,142].

The core components of magnetic hydrogel-based drug delivery systems typically include magnetic nanoparticles (such as Fe3O4) and natural or synthetic polymer matrices, which collectively endow the carriers with multiple functionalities including magnetic responsiveness, environment-sensitive drug release, and good biocompatibility.

Magnetic targeting represents a pivotal mechanism in stimuli-responsive drug delivery systems. By incorporating the superparamagnetic Fe3O4 nanoparticles, the drug-loaded system can be directionally enriched at the tumor site under the guidance of an external magnetic field, thereby significantly enhancing local drug accumulation while simultaneously minimizing systemic exposure and associated off-target toxicity. For example, in both the carboxymethyl cellulose (Mag-CMC)-based core–shell nano-system and the gelatin-alginate magnetic hydrogel, the Fe3O4 component successfully achieved magnetically guided positioning [13,143]. Similarly, (PNIPAm) copolymer hydrogels and PEG hydrogel-coated magnetic nanoparticles demonstrated enhanced tumor-specific accumulation through the same approach [4,144].

In addition to physical targeting, various active targeting strategies have been integrated to improve cellular uptake efficiency. For instance, the modification of magnetic thermosensitive hydrogels (MHG-FA) with folic acid (FA) enables specific binding to folate receptors (FR), which are overexpressed on the surface of cervical cancer HeLa cells, thereby facilitating receptor-mediated endocytosis. When assisted by an external magnetic field, cellular uptake can be increased by up to 15-fold [3]. In another study, the incorporation of RGDS peptides into the PEG hydrogel coating facilitated similar targeting effects through recognition of integrin αvβ3, significantly enhancing the internalization of particles by HeLa cells [121].

Regarding drug release behavior, pH-responsive characteristics are widely used to achieve triggered drug release in the TME. The abundant carboxyl and amino groups in systems, such as gelatin-alginate hydrogels, CMC and its functionalized derivatives (Mag-CMC@FCA), undergo protonation in the acidic TME (pH 4–6.5), weakening the electrostatic interactions between the drug and the carrier, thereby accelerating drug release [13,143]. The Mag-CMC@FCA system exhibited a significantly increased swelling ratio at pH 7.4, and its release behavior followed the Korsmeyer-Peppas model, achieving colon-targeted and sustained release through a diffusion-swelling synergistic mechanism [143]. Similarly, the drug-loaded gelatin-alginate hydrogel exhibited slow release at physiological pH, but release was significantly accelerated under acidic conditions, highlighting its favorable intelligent drug release properties [13].

Thermoresponsiveness is another important regulatory mechanism. PNIPAm-based magnetic hydrogels incorporate hydrophilic comonomers such as vinylpyrrolidone (VP) to adjust their LCST to 37 °C, enabling phase transition and contraction at body temperature. This achieves sustained release of DOX with a release period extended to over 100 h, effectively avoiding burst release [144].

To enhance therapeutic efficacy, some studies have also introduced metabolic regulation and combination therapy strategies into magnetic-responsive hydrogel systems. For example, the Mag-CMC system employed ampicillin (AMP) as a metabolic interferent, which competitively inhibits methotrexate (MTX)'s metabolic enzymes, reducing MTX biodegradation by up to 35 % and prolonging its serum half-life, thereby enhancing efficacy and reducing dosing frequency [143]. Furthermore, the magnetic component Fe3O4 not only enables targeting but also endows the system with magnetothermal therapy capabilities and MRI imaging functionality, achieving theranostic integration [13,126]. For instance, PEG hydrogel-coated magnetic nanoparticles can induce localized hyperthermia under an alternating magnetic field, promoting tumor cell apoptosis [145].

These multifunctional systems generally exhibit excellent biocompatibility and biosafety. Cytotoxicity tests demonstrate that blank carriers have low toxicity to normal cells, and hemolysis rates are within acceptable biomedical standards [13,143,145]. Functionalized carriers such as Mag-CMC@FCA and gelatin-alginate hydrogels display high drug-loading capacity, sustained release behavior, and effective tumor growth inhibition while minimizing systemic side effects [13,143]. Additionally, some carriers also possess antibacterial properties and biodegradability, further broadening their potential for clinical application [143].

In summary, magnetic responsive hydrogel systems offer a comprehensive therapeutic platform that integrates magnetic targeting, active molecular targeting, pH-/thermo-responsive drug release, metabolic regulation, and multimodal therapy. These systems present a promising strategy for achieving efficient, low-toxicity treatment of cervical cancer. Future research should prioritize in-depth in vivo evaluations, optimization of magnetic field application protocols, and the development of next-generation intelligent designs to facilitate clinical translation.

Redox-responsive hydrogels

Redox-responsive hydrogels offer significant advantages in tumor therapy, leveraging the unique redox characteristics of the TME. Compared with normal cells, tumor cells exhibit a distinct redox state, with elevated intracellular GSH levels and high ROS concentrations [146]. These hydrogels incorporate oxidation sensitive bonds (e.g., disulfide, thioether, or selenide bonds) that cleave in tumor regions, enabling precise drug release while reducing systemic toxicity (e.g., cisplatin-induced cardiotoxicity). The degradation products can form nanoparticles, which accumulate in the tumor via the EPR)effect [87,88]. Moreover, redox-responsive hydrogels can deplete intracellular GSH, inhibiting resistance proteins (e.g., P-gp) and thus reversing chemotherapy resistance (e.g., cisplatin resistance in ovarian cancer). This mechanism can synergize with oxidative stress therapy or combine with photosensitizers for photodynamic or sonodynamic therapy, forming multimodal strategies to increase therapeutic efficacy [[147], [148], [149]]. In terms of material design, both natural polymers (e.g., hyaluronic acid, CS) and synthetic polymers (e.g., PEG-SS-PCL) offer good biocompatibility and programmable degradability. Additionally, these hydrogels can integrate immunomodulators (e.g., PD-L1 inhibitors) and imaging probes (e.g., Gd3+) to achieve theranostics, making them efficient, low-toxicity carriers for precision tumor therapy [146,147]. The following sections discuss recent advancements, molecular design strategies, mechanisms, and translational prospects of redox-responsive hydrogels in the treatment of cervical cancer (Table 7).

The redox-responsive mechanisms of hydrogels in cervical cancer therapy offer unique advantages, primarily through intelligent regulation of structure and function via dynamic chemical bonds or ROS-sensitive groups. For example, H2O2-mediated gelatin/hyaluronic acid composite hydrogels utilize H2O2 in two roles [150]. It acts as an electron donor for horseradish peroxidase (HRP), inducing crosslinking between phenolated gelatin and hyaluronic acid to form a 3D network. Simultaneously, H2O2 oxidatively cleaves polymer chains, producing low-molecular-weight hyaluronic acid (LMW-HA). This dual mechanism results in time-dependent changes in the Young's modulus of the hydrogel, precisely regulating the cell cycle in cervical cancer cells. Soft hydrogels induce G2/M phase arrest, whereas stiff hydrogels promote proliferation, demonstrating the ability of the mechanical microenvironment to control tumor cell behavior. Another example is the dual-crosslinked network (DCN) hydrogel, which responds to multiple stimuli through dynamic imine bonds and benzoxaboronate esters [151]. These bonds respond to H2O2 and exhibit changes under neutral and acidic conditions. The system completely dissociates in 10 mM H2O2 within 6 h, and its shear-stiffening properties and self-healing abilities provide a platform for controlled drug release and cell encapsulation. A recent study developed a gelatin-based dual-loading hydrogel (Gel-MP@Gel@6-MP) that covalently conjugates 6-MP via a GSH-cleavable C–S bond while physically entrapping another drug portion. This design enables rapid initial release followed by sustained GSH-triggered release over 96 h. Following a single peritumoral injection, the system achieved a tumor inhibition rate of 46 % with minimal systemic toxicity, demonstrating its capacity for microenvironment-specific activation and enhanced therapeutic precision [152]. In a diselenide bond (Se-Se)-crosslinked carbon dot (dsCD) hydrogel system, Se-Se cleaves in the high-ROS cervical cancer microenvironment, causing the dispersion of CDs and increasing hydrogel conductivity, mechanical stretchability, and pressure sensitivity [165]. This structural change also endows the material with ROS scavenging capabilities (63 % DPPH clearance rate), downregulating HIF-1α and VEGF expression to inhibit tumor growth. The addition of NIR photothermal effects (ΔT up to 12.3 °C) specifically induces necrosis in cervical cancer cells [153]. The polymer dot (PD-Cu2+) composite hydrogel, which is based on copper ion coordination, utilizes copper and catechol group interactions to form a redox-responsive unit. In the high-GSH TME, Cu2+ catalyzes the oxidation of GSH, enhancing hydrogel swelling by 94.2 %, reducing viscosity by 95.14 %, and improving tumor tissue penetration. Cu2+-mediated GSH depletion also downregulates key signaling pathways (NF-κB and STAT3) and inhibits cervical cancer cell proliferation [154].

In therapeutic applications, gelatin/HA hydrogels modulate cell adhesion receptor signaling pathways (e.g., CD44 and RHAMM) by regulating the LMW-HA content and inhibiting epithelial‒mesenchymal transition (EMT). The ROS-responsive DCN hydrogel enables selective drug release by targeting high H2O2 concentrations in the TME. Both systems show low cytotoxicity (HeLa viability >80 %) and 3D encapsulation potential; live/dead assays confirmed over 85 % HeLa cell survival in the DCN hydrogel [150,151]. The dsCD hydrogel reduced the cervical cancer volume through ROS scavenging combined with photothermal effects; histopathological analysis revealed tumor cell pyknosis and reduced inflammatory infiltration [153]. The PD-Cu2+ hydrogel modulated the redox balance by regulating SOD2 and catalase (CAT) activity, inhibiting the expression of metastasis-related genes (e.g., Slug), and increasing E-cadherin expression [154]. These studies highlight that redox-responsive hydrogels, through mechanisms such as dynamic chemical bond reorganization, ionic valence state conversion, and signaling pathway intervention, provide novel strategies for minimally invasive intervention and synergistic therapy, offering precision diagnosis and treatment for cervical cancer.

Multiresponsive hydrogels

In recent years, the application of hydrogel materials in the biomedical field has expanded significantly. The design of multiresponse mechanisms in hydrogels offers new opportunities for precise drug delivery and synergistic therapy. Multiresponsive hydrogels, which are based on dynamic covalent chemistry and natural polymers, present unique advantages. Through molecular design, different research teams have achieved synergistic responses to multiple environmental stimuli (Table 7).

pH and temperature responsive hydrogels offer significant advantages in precision therapy for cervical cancer. These hydrogels are designed on the basis of their biomimetic regulation of TME characteristics, such as the acidic environment (pH ~ 5.0–6.5) and local temperature increase (≈37.5–40.0 °C) typical of tumors [155,156]. They are typically formed by copolymerizing pH-sensitive groups (e.g., carboxyl, amino) with temperature-responsive units (e.g., PNIPAM), enabling reversible swelling-shrinking or phase transitions in response to changes in acidity and temperature [157]. In cervical cancer lesions, the acidic microenvironment promotes protonation of pH-sensitive groups, causing hydrogel pore expansion, whereas an increase in local temperature accelerates the conformational transition of thermosensitive segments, increasing drug release kinetics [158]. This mechanism minimizes systemic toxicity and maintains effective drug concentrations at the lesion site, significantly improving bioavailability and therapy. Furthermore, the dual-responsive nature confers in situ adaptability, ensuring structural stability in normal physiological environments while enabling a rapid response in TME. This optimizes the balance between targeting, safety, and sustained efficacy [159], providing an innovative solution for precision interventional therapy of cervical cancer. A composite hydrogel based on CMC and HA forms a temperature-sensitive network crosslinked by β-glycerophosphate (GP), which undergoes a sol‒gel transition at body temperature (37 °C). Under acidic conditions (pH 4.0), the CS amino groups protonate, promoting drug release. CD44, an HA ligand, targets cervical cancer cells, increasing drug accumulation at the tumor site [160]. Another system uses PF127 and thiolated chitosan (TC) nanoparticles. PF127 undergoes thermosensitive micellization at temperatures between 17.8 and 23.2 °C, whereas the pH responsiveness of TC arises from the solubility changes of TC under acidic conditions (pH 4.5–5.6), promoting drug release by destabilizing the nanoparticles [161]. The mucoadhesive properties of TC enhance its residence time in the vaginal environment, whereas nanoparticle "reservoir effects" prolong its sustained release, reducing the burst release of DOX within 24 h [161]. In synthetic systems, a PVA and ester-functionalized ionic liquid hydrogel (C16EMorphBr) achieves dual-responsiveness. An increase in temperature (25 → 37 °C) triggers rearrangement of PVA's hydrophobic segments, whereas acidic conditions (pH 5.0) hydrolyze the ester group in C16EMorphBr, enlarging gel pores and increasing DOX release to over 80 % [158]. Additionally, this system exhibits self-healing properties via boronate dynamic bonds, with a shear recovery efficiency of 90 %. The high adhesion strength ensures tissue conformity postadministration [158]. Compared with these systems, all three hydrogels combine biocompatible matrices (polysaccharides or PVA) with functional additives (GP, TC, or ionic liquid). Natural polysaccharide systems (CS/HA, TC) emphasize biological targeting, whereas synthetic polymers (PVA) offer enhanced mechanical property regulation. pH sensitivity is realized through different chemical mechanisms: protonation of CS amino groups, thiol group oxidation in TC, or ester hydrolysis in C16EMorphBr [158,160,161]. Therapeutically, DOX serves as a model drug, showing pH-dependent cytotoxicity. CS/HA enhances drug enrichment through targeted adhesion to HeLa cells, whereas the PF127/TC and PVA/C16EMorphBr systems prolong the drug action time via sustained nanoparticle release and self-healing, respectively. These systems effectively reduce systemic toxicity and improve local therapeutic efficacy [158,160,161]. Additionally, Zhao et al. developed a DCN) hydrogel with zwitterionic poly (MPC) as the backbone, utilizing dynamic imine bonds and benzoxaboronate esters for multistimuli responsiveness. The DCN hydrogel undergoes selective degradation in the TME, releasing drugs in response to pH changes [151]. Another innovative approach by Veisi et al. integrated diselenide bond crosslinking and MnO2 nanoparticles, enhancing synergistic chemoradiotherapy. Diselenide bonds are cleaved under low-dose radiation, whereas MnO2 generates oxygen, alleviating tumor hypoxia and increasing radiosensitivity [162]. pH responsiveness via protonation of carboxyl groups further enhances drug release, resulting in a 3.8-fold increase in release under acidic conditions. These studies underscore key principles in multiresponsive hydrogel design: dynamic covalent bonds (imine, disulfide, diselenide) provide reversible crosslinking, natural polymers (CS, HA) ensure biocompatibility, and inorganic nanoparticles (MnO2) expand functionality, offering new avenues for precision chemotherapy and immunotherapy.

Chapter summary

This chapter provides a systematic review of the application prospects and challenges of stimuli-responsive hydrogels as advanced drug delivery systems in the treatment of cervical cancer. Various types of hydrogels (including thermo-, photo-, pH-, enzyme-, magnetic-, redox-responsive, and multi-responsive systems) can perceive specific intrinsic signals within the TME—such as acidic pH, overexpressed enzymes, and elevated levels of ROS/GSH—or respond to external physical stimuli (e.g., temperature, light, magnetic fields). This leads to precise structural or functional transformations, thereby enabling spatiotemporally controlled release and targeted delivery of drugs, as summarized in Table 8, which outlines the correspondence between various response mechanisms and clinical applications. Their core advantages include the ability to achieve high local drug concentrations at the lesion site, significantly reduce systemic toxicity, and thereby overcome the limitations of conventional chemotherapy. They also effectively overcome the high interstitial pressure barrier in tumor tissues, improving drug penetration and retention in deep tumor regions. Furthermore, these smart materials can integrate multiple treatment modalities (such as chemotherapy, PTT, photodynamic therapy, immunotherapy, and gas therapy), resulting in synergistic effects and potential reversal of chemoresistance. By responding to endogenous biological signals or exogenous triggers, they enable on-demand drug release, better adapting to tumor heterogeneity. Moreover, as they are often based on natural polymers or biocompatible synthetic materials, their degradation products are generally safe, eliminating the need for secondary surgical removal and endowing them with good biocompatibility and biodegradability.

The optimal hydrogel platform for cervical cancer theranostics is not a one-size-fits-all solution but should be selected based on the clinical scenario. For broad-spectrum targeting of the primary tumor's acidic TME, pH-responsive systems offer a straightforward approach. When high specificity for aggressive, invasive disease is needed, enzyme-responsive hydrogels are paramount. For applications demanding external control and real-time monitoring, light- and magnetic-responsive systems are superior, albeit with higher technical requirements.

The future lies in the rational design of multi-responsive hydrogels that sequentially or synergistically respond to multiple cues (e.g., pH + enzyme, or magnetic + temperature). This approach can create sophisticated feedback loops that mimic biological systems, offering unprecedented precision. Furthermore, the integration of these smart materials with emerging technologies like 4D printing, AI-driven design, and patient-derived organoid models will be crucial for translating their potential from the laboratory to the clinic, ultimately enabling truly personalized and effective therapy for cervical cancer patients.

Stimuli-responsive hydrogels for monitoring and diagnosis of cervical cancer

CRISPR/Cas system-based DNA hydrogel sensors

DNA hydrogel sensors based on the CRISPR/Cas system integrate molecular recognition with signal amplification, enabling highly sensitive detection of cervical cancer biomarkers. These sensors typically consist of CRISPR/Cas proteins, DNA-crosslinked hydrogels, and isothermal amplification components (Table 11). Pian et al. developed a CRISPR/Cas12a-driven DNA hydrogel sensor using a polyacrylamide-DNA composite (Fig. 6A). After recombinase polymerase amplification (RPA) of target HPV DNA, activated Cas12a exhibits trans-cleavage activity, cleaving the crosslinked DNA within the hydrogel to alter its permeability (Fig. 6B). This process allows for quantitative HPV DNA detection via capillary liquid-level displacement, with a detection limit of 1 aM and an assay time of 30 min (Fig. 6C and D) [15]. Another study designed a bifunctional sensor using HA-ssDNA crosslinked hydrogels. Cas12a-mediated ssDNA cleavage or HAase-triggered HA degradation releases entrapped water, forming a diffusion wet area (Fig. 6E). Combined with smartphone imaging analysis, this system simultaneously detects HPV 16 DNA (detection limit: 66.95 fM) and HAase (detection limit: 0.15 U/mL) [14]. Both approaches utilize the high specificity of CRISPR/Cas systems and the stimuli-responsiveness of DNA hydrogels. The first offers device-independent portability through physical distance readouts, whereas the second expands clinical utility with multitarget responsiveness.

Multifunctional Electrochemical Hydrogel Sensors

Electrochemical hydrogel sensors convert biomolecular interactions into electrical signals through the synergistic effects of conductive materials and biorecognition elements (Table 11). Qiu et al. developed an electrochemical sensor using DNA hydrogels. In this system, hybridization chain reaction (HCR)-generated long dsDNA activates Cas12a, which cleaves ssDNA crosslinkers, releasing embedded methylene blue (MB) and inducing electrochemical signal variation for ultrasensitive detection of the NF-κB p50 transcription factor (Fig. 6F) [163]. Kim et al. designed a mineralized low-viscosity injectable hydrogel (LVI-gel) loaded with Cu2+-polymer dots (PD-Cu2+). GSH/ROS in the TME reduce Cu2+, altering hydrogel conductivity and mechanical properties. Combined with wireless sensing, this system enables real-time monitoring of cancer cells (e.g., HeLa) and suppresses tumor-associated genes such as NF-κB (Fig. 6G) [154]. The key difference between these systems lies in signal transduction: the former uses DNA cleavage-mediated release of electroactive molecules, whereas the latter relies on metal ion redox reactions to modulate conductive networks. Qiu et al.’s sensor avoids immobilization steps for homogeneous detection but depends on complex HCR amplification. In contrast, Kim et al.'s LVI-gel integrates therapeutic and monitoring functions, although its mineralized structure may affect long-term biocompatibility. Overall, electrochemical sensors offer high sensitivity and quantitative benefits but require optimization of material conductivity and biological stability for application in complex in vivo and in vitro environments.

Photothermal/photoacoustic responsive hydrogel sensors

Photothermal/photoacoustic-responsive hydrogel sensors offer unique advantages for cervical cancer monitoring and theranostics through diverse mechanisms (Table 11). Jun et al. developed a ROS-responsive conductive hydrogel (dsCD-Hydrogel) with diselenide-crosslinked CDs embedded in a PVA/alginate dual-network matrix. In the cancer microenvironment, high ROS levels cleave diselenide bonds, releasing CDs and enhancing conductivity (52 %), mechanical stretchability (122 %), and strain-pressure sensitivity (91 %). This sensor works synergistically with NIR PTT, enabling wireless monitoring of TME pressure dynamics (Fig. 7A) and downregulating VEGF/HIF-1α expression for integrated diagnosis and therapy [153]. In contrast, Borum et al. engineered a DNA hydrogel using MB-Dox conjugates with photoacoustic properties. J-aggregate formation within DNA-bound MB-Dox redshifts the photoacoustic peak (680 → 730 nm), and nuclease degradation-driven release causes blueshifting. Multiwavelength PAI tracks drug release kinetics in real-time (Fig. 7B), achieving 72.2 % tumor growth suppression with biodegradability [164]. Zeng et al. fabricated platinum porphyrin-modified polyacrylamide-HEMA hydrogels (PtTFPP-PAM-co-PHEMA) into micropatterned oxygen-sensing films via photolithography (Fig. 7C). Phosphorescent oxygen quenching monitors cellular metabolic oxygen consumption, whereas microgrooves guide HeLa cell alignment (Fig. 7D, E, F), revealing correlations between cell elongation and groove width [165]. These studies highlight distinct mechanisms and applications. Jun et al. leveraged photothermal effects for microenvironment-specific sensing and synergistic PTT [153]. Borum et al. and Zeng et al. used photoacoustic/phosphorescent properties, with Borum et al. focusing on drug release kinetics and Zeng et al. tracking oxygen metabolism and cellular morphology [164,165]. In terms of theranostic integration, Jun et al.'s wireless sensing with PTT shows the highest clinical potential, whereas Zeng et al.'s micropatterned design provides a precision tool for single-cell metabolism research [153,165]. These photoresponsive hydrogel sensors pave the way for multimodal precision diagnosis and therapy for cervical cancer.

Challenges and clinical translation

Cervical cancer, the fourth most common malignant tumor in women globally, affects more than 300,000 people annually. In the pursuit of innovative therapeutic strategies, stimuli-responsive hydrogels have emerged as a research focus because of their customizable physicochemical properties and TME-responsive capabilities. However, translating this technology into clinical practice faces multiple challenges. Concurrently, emerging technologies such as 4D printing, organoid models, and AI offer novel pathways to enhance hydrogel system functionality.

Standardization of manufacturing and performance reproducibility

Batch-to-batch variability. Fluctuations in crosslinking density during hydrogel synthesis are prevalent and directly impact drug release kinetics and material degradation rates, leading to inconsistent therapeutic outcomes. This inconsistency poses significant risks in anticancer therapies requiring precise dosage control [166]. Precise control of complex compositions. Advanced hydrogel systems often integrate multiple functional components (e.g., natural/synthetic polymers such as PEG/CS and nanomaterials such as Fe2O3 or liposomes). Achieving a uniform spatial distribution of these components at the micro/nanoscale is challenging and affects drug loading efficiency and release profiles [166].

Insufficient precision in controlled drug release

Challenge of tumor heterogeneity. While the cervical cancer microenvironment typically has a pH range of 6.5–6.8, significant intratumoral heterogeneity exists, with some regions exhibiting near-normal pH, reducing system responsiveness. Furthermore, the significant interpatient variability in MMP-2/9 expression levels complicates the standardization of enzyme-triggered peptide crosslinker design [166]. Difficulty in maintaining the therapeutic concentration. Although thermosensitive PLGA-PEG-PLGA hydrogels enable sustained EGF release to inhibit recurrence, the initial burst release can cause local concentrations to exceed the therapeutic window. High EGF concentrations may stimulate tumor growth, whereas low concentrations are often subtherapeutic. This narrow therapeutic window severely limits its clinical utility [102].

In vivo dynamic monitoring and efficacy assessment

The lack of real-time monitoring technology. Current hydrogel systems lack noninvasive monitoring capabilities, preventing real-time feedback on drug release kinetics and the tumor response. While gold nanoparticle-integrated hydrogels hold potential for PAI, their signal stability and resolution within the complex in vivo environment remain insufficient for clinical needs [167]. Lag in efficacy assessment. Traditional assessments relying on imaging and biopsies fail to reflect the microscopic biological effects of hydrogel therapy promptly. For example, AURKB-responsive supramolecular hydrogels effectively inhibit cervical cancer cell growth, but real-time monitoring of induced apoptosis and polyploidization is lacking. Clinical efficacy assessment typically requires several weeks [168].

Biocompatibility and immune response complexity

Immunogenicity of exogenous materials. Synthetic polymer components can trigger foreign body responses, leading to fibrous capsule formation that impedes drug diffusion. Even PEGylation strategies to reduce protein corona formation may be compromised by preexisting anti-PEG antibodies in humans, potentially causing accelerated blood clearance (ABC) phenomenon [169]. The complexity of immune responses is further heightened by interactions between vaginal microbiota and the material used in local cervical applications. Safety of degradation products. The long-term retention of nanocomposite materials (e.g., gold nanoparticles) may cause cumulative tissue toxicity. Animal studies indicate that approximately 53.3 % of gold nanoparticles fail to be released from alginate matrices within 42 h, and their long-term biotransformation pathways remain unclear [170].

Current status of clinical translation

AAlthough significant progress has been achieved in the basic research of stimuli-responsive hydrogels for the treatment of cervical cancer, their clinical translation still needs to be implemented, with most studies still confined to preclinical validation. It's delighted that several related hydrogel-based platforms have already entered clinical practice. The most established translational example is the use of hydrogels as radioprotective spacers. By injecting biodegradable hyaluronic acid- or PEG-based hydrogels into the anatomical space between the cervix and rectum, the rectum can be effectively pushed away from the high-dose radiation zone. The most well-established clinical example is the use of hydrogels as radioprotective spacers. Biodegradable hydrogels based on hyaluronic acid or PEG are injected into the anatomical space between the cervix and rectum to physically displace the rectum away from high-dose radiation fields. Clinical studies have confirmed that a 10 mL injection of Respacio® hydrogel significantly increases the cervix–rectum distance and reduces rectal radiation exposure (D2cc) to 60.9 ± 3.4 Gy, thereby lowering the risk of radiation-induced proctitis [171]. Although these hydrogels are non-therapeutic in nature, their clinical success has validated both the biocompatibility of the materials and the feasibility of transvaginal precision injection, laying the groundwork for future deployment of “therapeutic” smart hydrogels.

Beyond radioprotection, hydrogel-based local drug delivery platforms used in other solid tumors (such as the use of OncoGel™ for esophageal cancer) serve as direct translational references for cervical cancer therapy [172]. These precedents support the clinical potential of intelligent hydrogels loaded with chemotherapeutic agents (e.g. cisplatin) or immunomodulators, which are now transitioning from preclinical stages toward early-phase clinical trials. However, several critical challenges must be addressed to realize large-scale clinical translation. First, scalable manufacturing and GMP-compliant quality control remain key barriers. The inherently complex synthesis and drug-loading processes of hydrogels must be optimized to achieve high reproducibility and batch-to-batch consistency, which are essential prerequisites for clinical entry [166]. Second, long-term biosafety must be rigorously evaluated. Potential issues include prolonged retention of degradation byproducts, immunogenicity risks (e.g., anti-PEG antibodies), and possible adverse effects on the reproductive system, all of which require systematic studies in large animal models [167,169]. Additionally, clinical trial designs will necessitate novel endpoint metrics, such as monitoring in situ drug concentration, TME modulation via biopsy, and early consultation with regulatory bodies to address the complex approval pathway for combination products.

While radioprotective hydrogel spacers have already become clinically established, drug-loaded stimuli-responsive hydrogels are approaching a pivotal moment in their translational trajectory, with Phase I clinical trials on the horizon. Their successful clinical deployment will require interdisciplinary collaboration—spanning materials science, pharmacology, oncology, and regulatory affairs—to overcome bottlenecks from production to patient delivery. As the next generation of drug delivery platforms, they hold great promise to provide a novel, effective, and low-toxicity paradigm for cervical cancer treatment, advancing the field toward precision medicine.

Perspectives and conclusion

Perspectives

Addressing the aforementioned challenges, interdisciplinary technological convergence is paving new avenues for stimuli-responsive hydrogels in cervical cancer diagnosis and treatment. Cutting-edge technologies such as 4D printing, organoid models, and AI are driving hydrogel systems toward greater intelligence, personalization, and dynamic responsiveness (Fig. 8).

The integration of stimuli-responsive hydrogels with emerging technologies, including 4D printing, patient-derived organoid models, AI, image-guided navigation, and soft robotics, which drive synergistic optimization of material design while significantly advancing diagnostic precision and therapeutic outcomes in cervical cancer management.

4D Printing: dynamically customized diagnosis and therapy

By incorporating time-responsive properties into hydrogel materials, the 4D printing of hydrogels offers transformative potential for cervical cancer treatment. This technology enables dynamic morphological and functional adaptations in response to tumor-specific stimuli (e.g., pH, temperature, or enzymatic activity), ensuring precise therapeutic alignment [173]. For example, hydrogels can rapidly release high-concentration chemotherapeutics in the tumor core to target cancer cells while gradually releasing immunomodulators at the periphery to remodel the immune microenvironment, increasing efficacy while minimizing damage to healthy tissue [174]. Postoperatively, these intelligent materials conform to wound contours, progressively contract during tissue regeneration, and release proangiogenic factors to promote repair and reduce scarring, significantly lowering the risk of complications [175]. Experimental validation has shown that 4D-printed pH-responsive hydrogels effectively suppress tumor growth in simulated tumor environments [176]. However, clinical implementation requires critical issues such as material biocompatibility, large-scale production cost control, and individualized adaptation.

Organoid models: precision therapeutic prediction

Patient-derived tumor organoid models address the challenge of predicting treatment efficacy in cervical cancer. These models mimic native TME dynamics, providing a physiologically relevant platform for evaluating novel hydrogel therapies [177]. For example, organoids can simulate patient-specific variations in tumor acidity and enzymatic activity, allowing researchers to observe drug release kinetics from pH/enzyme dual-responsive hydrogels and predict therapeutic outcomes. This "in vitro drug testing" paradigm helps identify optimal hydrogel formulations and drug combinations for individual patients, reducing the risks of empirical treatment and conserving medical resources and time [178,179]. Chemoresistance, a major challenge in the treatment of cervical cancer, can be achieved through long-term organoid culture. By coculturing organoids with drug-loaded hydrogels for several weeks, resistance evolution can be tracked, revealing gene expression signatures in resistant cell populations [180,181]. Additionally, integrating organoid technology with genomic sequencing and proteomic profiling will offer insights into molecular-level responses to hydrogel-based interventions, further refining personalized treatment strategies.

AI and machine learning: a powerful engine for intelligent design

AI is deeply penetrating the entire hydrogel R&D pipeline, from molecular design to clinical application, and catalhesizes intelligent transformation in cervical cancer therapy. Machine learning algorithms can decipher hydrogel structure‒activity relationships, guiding high-performance material design [182,183]. By training neural networks to recognize the nonlinear mapping between polymer molecular weight, crosslinking density, and drug release profiles, formulations meeting specific requirements can be inversely designed [169]. Furthermore, deep learning models can integrate multiomics data to predict patient responses to hydrogel therapy. The input parameters include tumor gene expression profiles, imaging features, and hydrogel drug release kinetics, whereas the outputs include the probability of a therapeutic response [184,185]. AI systems can analyze intraoperative images to guide hydrogel implantation in real time. For example, colposcopic image recognition can identify tumor margins, calculate optimal injection sites and dosages, and guide transvaginal implantation paths via augmented reality (AR) technology [186]. Additionally, by leveraging the sensing capabilities of hydrogels, AI can analyze real-time therapeutic feedback data (e.g., released drug concentration, tumor biomarker changes) to dynamically adjust treatment regimens (e.g., remotely triggering secondary drug release via external stimuli), enabling closed-loop adaptive therapy with potential for early recurrence monitoring.

Image-guided technology: real time assurance for precision control

The integration of modern imaging technologies with functionalized hydrogel design enables real-time visualization, monitoring, and precise control throughout the treatment process. Intraoperative real-time imaging guidance optimizes the location and distribution of hydrogel injection sites. In cervical cancer brachytherapy, CT-guided transvaginal injection of 10 mL of Respacio® hydrogel significantly increased the cervix‒rectum distance and reduced the rectal D2 cc dose to 60.9 ± 3.4 Gy [171]. Future integration of endoscopic ultrasound visualization needles is expected to further increase implantation accuracy. Furthermore, functionalized nanoparticles endow hydrogels with theranostic capabilities. For example, Fe3+-loaded hydrogels provide T2-weighted MR signals for real-time monitoring of radionuclide retention doses [187]. Introducing this system into the cervical cancer clinical setting can specifically address challenges such as intracavitary radiotherapy dose blind spots, pelvic lymph node metastasis, and damage to adjacent organs. Vaginal injection of aptamer-modified MRI-contrast hydrogels loaded with radionuclides enables real-time visualization of the dose distribution and responsive drug release triggered by the acidic cervical microenvironment; simultaneous loading of HPV antigenic peptides or immune adjuvants can activate specific T cells to eliminate metastases; and during combined microwave ablation, hydrogels enhance the thermal effect and demarcate ablation boundaries in real time, preventing damage to the bladder and rectum.

Shape-memory hydrogels and soft robotics: revolutionizing minimally invasive intervention

Shape-memory hydrogels and soft robotics facilitate precise drug delivery and navigation. Their core advantage lies in leveraging material compliance, adaptability, and programmable deformation capabilities to achieve ultraminimally invasive and highly precise operations. This technology can access deep cervical regions via natural orifices, transforming them into complex structures triggered by body temperature or external stimuli to conform tightly to tissue and minimize damage. Applications significantly enhance early diagnosis (e.g., precise biopsy, adaptive imaging and in situ detection) and minimally invasive therapy (e.g., targeted drug delivery, precise ablation and mechanical debulking) while optimizing postoperative management (e.g., intelligent biodegradable stents and continuous monitoring). Shape-memory hydrogels can be designed in a low-temperature compressed state, delivered to the target site transvaginally through a small incision, and triggered to expand by body temperature for noninvasive implantation. Zhang et al. developed an octopus-inspired cervical stent with a pH-responsive Janus patch structure that autonomously unfolds and adheres to specific cervical regions in the vaginal environment. Compared with traditional surgery, this technique significantly reduces trauma and is suitable for outpatient immediate treatment [188].

The five emerging technologies, 4D printing, organoid models, AI, image-guided techniques, and shape-memory hydrogels/soft robotics, are interconnected and synergistic, collectively propelling hydrogel systems toward intelligence, personalization, and dynamic responsiveness. They operate not in isolation but as an integrated technological network. Organoid models simulate patient-specific TME (pH, enzyme activity, etc.), providing a crucial platform for predicting drug release kinetics and resistance evolution; their generated data constitute the starting point for precision therapy. AI deeply engages throughout the chain, usese machine learning to analyze hydrogel structure‒activity relationships, optimizes molecular design, integrate multiomics data to predict patient responses, and even provides real-time intraoperative navigation (e.g., via colposcopy) for hydrogel implantation. Guided by AI design instructions and validated by organoid data, 4D printing enables the dynamic customization of hydrogel structures, endowing them with the ability to undergo morphological and functional changes in response to specific stimuli (e.g., pH and enzymes) in vivo, achieving differential drug release in the tumor core versus periphery and enabling postoperative adaptive repair. Image-guided techniques (e.g., CT/MRI-contrast nanoparticles) ensure precise intraoperative implantation, real-time visualization of hydrogel distribution and drug release throughout the treatment, and combination with AI for dynamic feedback control. Shape-memory hydrogels and soft robotics focus on minimally invasive delivery, utilizing their compliance and programmable deformation capabilities to achieve noninvasive or minimally invasive implantation via natural orifices (e.g., the vagina) and deployment to conform tightly to target tissue (e.g., triggered by body temperature), significantly increasing precision and reducing damage.

Together, these five technologies form a closed-loop "Design-Predict-Fabricate-Implement-Monitor-Optimize" system. Organoids provide personalized predictions; AI drives intelligent design; 4D printing and shape-memory materials enable precision fabrication and dynamic response; imaging ensures precise implementation and real-time monitoring; AI further analyzes feedback data to dynamically adjust treatment strategies; and organoids can recapitulate resistance mechanisms to optimize the next design cycle. Their functions are complementary: 4D printing and shape-memory materials address dynamic response; imaging and AI overcome monitoring blind spots; and organoid models bridge the clinical prediction gap. This deep integration synergistically overcomes the core barriers to hydrogel application. Ultimately, the synergistic effects of these technologies will significantly advance cervical cancer therapy toward greater personalization, minimal invasiveness, and intelligence.

Conclusion

Stimuli-responsive hydrogels are shifting the paradigm for cervical cancer diagnosis and treatment from "generalized therapy" to a new era of "dynamic precision intervention." In response to persistent challenges such as chemoresistance, systemic toxicity, and insufficient diagnostic sensitivity, these materials respond to dynamically specific signals within the TME, integrating multifunctional advantages like targeted delivery, real-time monitoring, and microenvironment remodeling, offering cervical cancer patients individualized solutions that enhance efficacy while reducing toxicity. Moreover, their intrinsic theranostic capabilities support concurrent therapy and diagnostics, paving the way for intelligent treatment cycles. The intelligent evolution of stimuli-responsive hydrogels will be increasingly driven by the deep integration of interdisciplinary technologies. 4D printing endows hydrogels with "living" dynamic structures capable of deforming in response to TME signals, enabling postsurgical drug gradient release and tissue adaptation. Patient-derived organoids, combined with the digital twin concept, can replicate TME heterogeneity in vitro, predicting hydrogel drug release kinetics and resistance evolution in advance to optimize personalized regimens. AI methods can decipher structure–activity relationships to inversely design materials with more precise response thresholds and integrate multiomics data to predict treatment response, guiding clinical decision-making. For clinical translation, closed-loop theranostic systems are emerging where sensing-capable hydrogels provide real-time feedback on drug concentrations and tumor biomarkers, enabling externally triggered secondary drug release (e.g., via light or magnetic stimuli) to form a real-time "monitor-treat-adjust" cycle.

However, to accelerate these advanced systems from bench to bedside, sustained efforts must address several critical research priorities. This necessitates proactively tackling the regulatory hurdles inherent in developing complex, dynamic drug-device combination products, which includes establishing standardized biocompatibility and long-term safety profiles for their sophisticated degradation products. Furthermore, the design of pivotal clinical trials must evolve to demonstrate not just feasibility but a clear superior efficacy and improved patient outcomes compared to the current standard of care. Concurrently, the field must develop robust, reproducible manufacturing processes, potentially leveraging microfluidics, to overcome critical batch-to-batch variability and ensure the scalable synthesis and incorporation of functional components like nanoparticles and sensitive ligands. Beyond manufacturing, a deeper, practical integration of AI with hydrogel technology is essential, moving from concept to application by utilizing AI for the real-time analysis of in situ sensor data to enable adaptive treatment adjustment and by leveraging AI-powered image guidance to optimize hydrogel implantation precision and dosimetry. Ultimately, while stimuli-responsive hydrogels are reshaping cervical cancer treatment with unprecedented precision, establishing a clear roadmap focused on overcoming these specific technical, manufacturing, and clinical compliance barriers is paramount to accelerating their translation and solidifying their role as a core technological platform for improving patient survival outcomes.
