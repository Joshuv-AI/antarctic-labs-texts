Lycopene improves the developmental competence of vitrified-thawed oocytes by reestablishing redox balance and modulating Rock1 expression
Authors: Songmao Li, Qing Zhang, Li Zhang, Yongjian Ma, Feng Chen, Di Sun, Baicai Yang, Yanquan Li, Qianhui Hu, Fanghao Guo, Meiling Zhang, Wen Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeOocyte vitrification is a critical technique for female fertility preservation; however, it is often accompanied by cryodamage that compromises subsequent embryo development. Moreover, limited research has assessed the long-term effects on offspring derived from vitrified oocytes. While antioxidants are frequently used to mitigate vitrification-induced oxidative stress and have been shown to enhance embryo development, their impact on implantation and offspring outcomes remains underexplored. Lycopene (LYC), a potent natural antioxidant, has not yet been evaluated in the context of oocyte vitrification.MethodsOocytes were collected from 4-week-old C57/BL6J female mice. Fresh oocytes served as the negative control (n = 393). Vitrified-thawed oocytes were cultured in post-warming, IVF, and embryo culture media supplemented with 0 µM (positive control, n = 479), 0.05 µM (n = 485), 0.1 µM (n = 499), or 0.2 µM (n = 461) lycopene. Blastocysts from the fresh, vitrified, and LYC-0.1µM groups were transferred into the uterine horns of 3.5-dpc pseudopregnant 8-week-old ICR female mice to assess in vivo development.ResultsSupplementation with 0.1 µM lycopene significantly improved the blastocyst formation rate in vitrified oocytes (75.56 ± 8.69% vs. 66.53 ± 6.94%, P = 0.029). Lycopene treatment elevated intracellular glutathione (GSH) levels (P < 0.001) and reduced reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels (P = 0.014). Notably, 0.1 µM lycopene modulated Rock1 expression during early embryogenesis, especially at the 4-cell stage, suggesting a potential role in developmental regulation. Despite improved preimplantation development, live birth rates following embryo transfer were similar between vitrified groups with or without lycopene, and both remained significantly lower than the fresh group (P = 0.008). None of the offspring had any apparent birth defect. No significant differences were observed among offspring in birth weight, growth trajectory, major organ morphology, gonadosomatic indices, or follicles counting among offspring.ConclusionsLycopene exerts a protective effect against vitrification-induced oxidative stress by restoring redox homeostasis within oocytes and modulating Rock1 expression during early embryo development. Importantly, lycopene treatment did not compromise offspring health, supporting its potential as a safe adjunct to improve vitrification outcomes.Keywords: Oocyte, Vitrification, Lycopene, Oxidative stress, ROS, Rock1, Offspring

Introduction

Oocyte vitrification is an indispensable part widespread used in assisted reproductive technology and female fertility preservation. The metaphase II (MII) oocytes are very sensitive to cryopreservation stimuli due to their special characteristics, such as being larger than other cell types and having a small surface area to volume ratio, a high water content, and a meiotic spindle [1]. Vitrification has been shown to impair the developmental competence of oocytes, as evidenced by decreased fertilization rates, embryo quality, implantation rates, pregnancy outcomes, and live birth rates compared to fresh controls [2–6]. Consequently, there is sufficient necessity to explore optimized strategies.

Oxidative stress is a major contributor to cryodamage during oocyte vitrification [2]. The process involves exposure to cryoprotectants, liquid nitrogen (LN₂), and extensive in vitro handling, all of which can trigger excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), overwhelming the oocyte’s intrinsic redox defense mechanisms [7]. While physiological ROS levels play essential roles in cell signaling and oocyte fertilization [8], excessive ROS can damage DNA, proteins, lipids, mitochondria, the endoplasmic reticulum, and spindles [9–11]. These disruptions lead to reduced ATP production, spindle abnormalities, and increased apoptosis [12]. Eventually, even in surviving oocytes, sublethal damage, caused by vitrification, can lead to fertilization failure or embryo development obstruction. To counteract this, antioxidant supplementation has emerged as a promising strategy to reduce vitrification-induced damage [13].

Exogenous antioxidants, particularly non-enzymatic types, are typically applied to counter oxidative stress. For example, melatonin acts through its receptors to activate the Nrf2 antioxidant pathway, increasing the expression of antioxidative enzymes and preserving mitochondrial and spindle integrity [14]. It also directly scavenges ROS, promoting in vitro maturation of GV-stage mouse oocytes [15]. Similarly, resveratrol enhances antioxidant enzyme activity and reduces oxidative damage [16]. It activates SIRT1, leading to PGC1α deacetylation and upregulation of NRF1 and NRF2, thereby promoting mitochondrial biogenesis and restoring redox balance in oocytes [17].

A further limitation of vitrification lies in the uncertain long-term impact on offspring. Despite its widespread use in assisted reproductive technology and fertility preservation, few studies have tracked the obstetrical outcomes of vitrified oocytes and followed up the offspring. A 2022 systematic review of 13 studies evaluating obstetrical and neonatal outcomes including live birth rate, cesarean section incidence, gestational age, birth weight, Apgar score, and congenital anomalies, suggested no apparent safety concerns [18]. Regretfully, only one extended follow-up evaluated children until 6 years of age without strict control and with extremely high loss of follow-up (17/110) [19]. Thus, further long-term evaluations are urgently needed. Additionally, most studies examining antioxidant intervention in vitrified oocytes assess outcomes only up to the blastocyst stage, leaving the effects on implantation, pregnancy, and offspring largely unknown.

Lycopene (C40H56) is a carotenoid found abundantly in red fruits such as tomatoes, watermelons, and red grapefruits [20]. As a natural antioxidant, its singlet oxygen-quenching capacity surpasses that of β-carotene and α-tocopherol by two- and ten- fold, respectively [21]. Recent studies have highlighted the protective effects of lycopene on the reproductive system. In rats on a high-fat diet, lycopene enhanced placental function and fetal growth by increasing total antioxidant capacity [22]. It also mitigated ovarian injury and infertility in rats induced by toxicants such as furan [23], cisplatin [24], and methotrexate [25] through reducing malondialdehyde (MDA) levels and boosting glutathione (GSH), glutathione reductase, and superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity. In bovine oocytes, lycopene supplementation during in vitro maturation significantly reduced intracellular ROS, improved blastocyst yield, and lowered apoptotic cell proportions [26]. However, information on the effects of lycopene on oocyte vitrification remains limited to date. Considering the established antioxidant activity of lycopene and its demonstrated effects in reproductive systems, its application in the context of oocyte vitrification represents a promising area for further study.

In this study, we investigated the effects of lycopene supplementation during the post-warming and embryo culture phases on the developmental competence of vitrified mouse oocytes and evaluated potential long-term impacts on offspring. This work aims to establish lycopene as a promising antioxidant for improving vitrification outcomes and to provide critical insight into the safety and developmental consequences of antioxidant-treated, vitrified oocytes.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

G-IVF plus (Cat No.10136, IVF medium) and G-GAMETE (Cat No.10126, handling medium) were obtained from Vitrolife (Gothenburg, Sweden). SAGE 1-Step SPS (Cat No. 67010060A, embryo culture medium) was bought from LifeGlobal (Guilford, USA). Lycopene (Cat No. L9879, purity ≥ 90%) and all other chemicals were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA), unless otherwise specified. Lycopene powder was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, Cat No. D2650) to prepare a 2 mM stock solution and stored at − 80 °C. On the day of the experiment, this stock was diluted in either IVF medium or embryo culture medium to achieve final working concentrations of 0.05, 0.1, or 0.2 μM. DMSO was excluded from both negative and positive control groups.

Animals and oocyte collection

C57/BL6J female mice, CD-1® (ICR) female mice, and CD-1® (ICR) male mice were maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions at the Laboratory Animal Center of the International Peace Maternity and Child Health Hospital, School of Medicine, Shanghai Jiao Tong University. Animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Review Board of the International Peace Maternity and Child Health Hospital (Approval No. GKDW-A-2024–12).

A total of seventy 4-week-old C57/BL6J female mice were intraperitoneally injected with 10 IU pregnant mare serum gonadotropin (PMSG), followed by 10 IU human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) 48 h later. Fourteen to fifteen hours post-hCG injection, mice were euthanized via CO₂ asphyxiation followed by cervical dislocation. Cumulus-oocyte complexes (COCs) were harvested from the ampullae under a stereomicroscope at 37 °C, and then digested in 0.3 mg/mL hyaluronidase for 3 min to remove cumulus cells. After triple rinsing in handling medium, metaphase II (MII) oocytes with normal morphology and visible first polar bodies were selected (n = 2317).

Vitrification and thawing

Commercial vitrification (solutions F1 and F2) and warming kits (solutions W1–W4) were obtained from Aivfo (Chengdu, China). MII oocytes were vitrified using the cryotop method. At room temperature, oocytes were equilibrated in F1 solution for 5 min, transferred to F2 for 30–40 s, then loaded with minimal volume onto cryotop straw and immediately plunged into LN₂ for storage (≥ 24 h). For thawing, the oocytes were quickly immersed into W1 solution (preheated to 37 °C). Within 1 min, oocytes were transferred into W2 solution for 3 min, and then transferred through W2 (3 min), W3 (5 min), and W4 (1 min) at room temperature. After thawing, oocytes were recovered in recovery medium (IVF medium) for 2 h before in vitro fertilization and then considered viable if there was no dark/degenerated or contracted ooplasm and no cracked zona pellucida [27]. Oocytes were processed in batches of 15–20.

Lycopene supplementation and experimental grouping

A 2 mM lycopene stock solution (1 mg lycopene in 1 mL DMSO) was diluted in IVF or embryo culture medium to obtain final concentrations of 0.05, 0.1, and 0.2 μM. Fresh oocytes (n = 393) served as the negative control (fresh group). Vitrified-warmed oocytes not exposed to DMSO or lycopene comprised the positive control (vitrification group, n = 479). Experimental groups were designated LYC-0.05 μM (n = 485), LYC-0.1 μM (n = 499), and LYC-0.2 μM (n = 461), with lycopene supplementation throughout recovery, IVF, and embryo culture. Post-warming survival numbers were 464, 466, 488, and 449 oocytes, respectively, for the vitrification, LYC-0.05 μM, LYC-0.1 μM, and LYC-0.2 μM groups.

In vitro fertilization (IVF), in vitro culture (IVC), and embryo transfer (ET)

All media mentioned in this section were overlaid with mineral oil. The cauda epididymis of 8-week-old male ICR mice was minced in 200 μl IVF medium to release sperms. After 1 h of capacitation, motile sperms from the droplet periphery were counted via hemocytometer and transferred into fresh 100 μl IVF droplets at a final concentration of 2–5 × 10⁶/ml. Approximately 40 oocytes were added to each droplet and incubated for 6 h at 37 °C with 6% CO₂. Good sperm motility was confirmed by sperm binding and movement of oocytes within the droplets.

Zygotes were washed three times and cultured in groups of 15 in 30 μl embryo culture medium droplets at 37 °C, 6% CO₂ until the blastocyst stage. Development was assessed at defined timepoints post-fertilization: pronuclei formation (defined as fertilization, 6 h), 2-cell (24 h), 4-cell (48 h), morula (72 h), and blastocyst (96 h). The embryo formation rate for 4-cell embryos, morula, and blastocysts was calculated based on the number of 2-cell embryos.

Blastocysts cultured to 96 h were transferred into uterine horns of 3-day-postcoitum pseudopregnant 8-week-old ICR female mice mated with vasectomized 10–12-week-old males. Each group included three recipients, with 16 blastocysts transferred per recipient. Live pups were delivered and fostered by surrogate mothers.

Assessment of ROS and GSH levels of oocytes

The changes of intracellular levels of ROS and GSH of oocytes after 2 h in recovery medium were assessed by DCHFDA and Cell Tracker Blue CMF2HC staining, respectively. 16 to 20 oocytes of each group were transferred to handling medium supplemented with 10 μM 2',7'-H2DCFDA for ROS measurement or 10 μM CMF2HC Dye for GSH determination and incubated 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. Then, oocytes were washed three times in 0.1% FBS-DPBS and immediately observed under the fluorescence microscope (ZEISS, Germany) under a 20× objective lens with blue filters (Ex: 470 nm/Em: 525 nm) for ROS or UV filters (Ex: 365 nm/Em: 445 nm) for GSH. The ImageJ software (version 1.46, National Institutes of Health, USA) was used to analyze the mean fluorescence intensities of oocytes. The appropriate fluorescence channel was selected, and thresholds were adjusted to isolate regions of interest. Using the oval selection tool, each oocyte was outlined, and fluorescence intensity was measured. Mean intensity values were used for statistical comparison. Experiments were independently repeated in triplicate.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

MII oocytes, 2-cell embryos, 4-cell embryos, and blastocysts (50 per group per replicate) were collected for total RNA extraction using a RNeasy Micro Kit (Qiagen, Germany). Reverse transcription was performed using a QuantiTect Reverse Transcription Kit (Qiagen, Germany). Custom primers were designed as follows: Rock1 forward primer: 5′-GCCTGATAACATGCTGCTGGATAAG-3′; Rock1 reverse primer: 5′-TGTGTCACATCGTACCATACCTTCC-3′; Hmgb3 forward primer: 5′-GTGCCAAGGGTCCTGCTAAA-3′; Hmgb3 reverse primer: 5′-GTCAATCACAGCGCTCTCCTA-3′.

qPCR was conducted using 1 μl of cDNA in a 20 μl reaction mix with 10 μl SYBR Green qPCR SuperMix (Vazyme Biotech, China) and 0.2 μM of each primer (final concentration). The cycling conditions were 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 42 cycles with 2 steps (95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 34 s). Relative Rock1 mRNA levels were quantified using the 2–ΔΔCt method, with Hmgb3 as the internal reference due to its stable expression across mouse oocyte and preimplantation stages [28]. Experiments were independently repeated in triplicate.

Observations of the offspring

A total of 29, 12, and 16 pups were evaluated by blinded investigators in the fresh, vitrification, and LYC-0.1 μM groups, respectively. Body weights were recorded at birth (day 0), day 3, day 7, and weekly thereafter. Females and males were separately weighed from day 14 and separately fed from day 21.

On day 63, all offspring were euthanized via CO₂ asphyxiation and cervical dislocation. Major organs, including the gonads (ovary/testis), liver, spleen, kidney, and heart, were collected. Gonads were weighed to calculate gonadosomatic indices (GSI = [gonad weight (mg)/body weight (g)] × 100). All organs were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) overnight at 4 °C and embedded in paraffin.

Ovaries were serially sectioned at 5 μm and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Follicles were counted in every fifth section based on established morphological criteria to determine follicle stage (primordial, primary, secondary, antral, atretic) [29]. Other organs were also sectioned, H&E-stained, and examined microscopically (Olympus, Japan).

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using SPSS 27.0 (IBM, USA). The results of oocyte and embryo outcomes were analyzed on a per-replicate basis, while the analysis of offspring assessment was performed per litter. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± SD, and categorical variables as n (%). One-way ANOVA with LSD post hoc tests was used for continuous data, while the Chi-squared test was applied to categorical comparisons. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Lycopene improves the embryo developmental competence of vitrified oocytes

To evaluate the effect of lycopene on the survival rate of vitrified-thawed oocytes, the surviving oocytes of each group were counted after vitrification, thawing, and 2-h recovery. The survival rate for the vitrification group was 96.95 ± 1.83%. In groups exposed to varying concentrations of lycopene (0.05, 0.1, 0.2 μM), labeled as LYC-0.05 μM, LYC-0.1 μM and LYC-0.2 μM, the survival rates were 96.07 ± 1.93%, 97.92 ± 1.51%, and 97.26 ± 1.62%, respectively. There was no significant difference in the survival rate of vitrified-thawed oocytes among these lycopene groups (Fig. 1B).

To further explore the developmental potential post-IVF, cleavage and blastocyst formation rates were recorded (Table 1, Fig. 1H). Fertilization, 2-cell, 4-cell, and morula formation rates did not differ significantly across all groups. However, vitrification significantly reduced the blastocyst formation rate compared to the fresh group (66.53 ± 6.94% vs. 81.26 ± 6.10%, P = 0.002). Supplementation with 0.1 μM lycopene significantly improved the blastocyst rate relative to the vitrification group (75.56 ± 8.69% vs. 66.53 ± 6.94%, P = 0.029), approaching that of the fresh group (75.56 ± 8.69% vs. 81.26 ± 6.10%, P = 0.192). In contrast, the LYC-0.05 μM and LYC-0.2 μM groups did not exhibit significant improvements (66.16 ± 8.28% and 64.88 ± 8.79%, respectively, P = 0.926 and P = 0.679), and remained significantly lower than the fresh group (P < 0.001 for both). Consequently, 0.1 μM lycopene was selected for further investigation.

Lycopene restores redox balance in vitrified-thawed oocytes

To evaluate oxidative stress status, intracellular ROS and GSH levels were quantified in oocytes from the fresh, vitrification, and LYC-0.1 μM groups (Fig. 2). ROS levels were significantly elevated in the vitrification group compared to the fresh group (P < 0.001), while 0.1 μM lycopene significantly reduced ROS levels (P = 0.014). Conversely, vitrified oocytes had significantly reduced GSH levels compared to fresh oocytes (P < 0.001), but lycopene treatment restored GSH to levels comparable with the fresh group (P < 0.001).

Lycopene repairs the expression of Rock1 across oocyte and preimplantation embryo stages

To investigate whether lycopene regulates Rock1 expression, mRNA levels were quantified in oocytes and embryos. Vitrification induced a modest, non-significant upregulation of Rock1 in MII oocytes (fold change = 1.17, Fig. 3). However, Rock1 was significantly downregulated in embryos derived from vitrified oocytes at the 2-cell (P < 0.05) and 4-cell (P < 0.01) stages, though not at the blastocyst stage (Fig. 3). Lycopene supplementation restored Rock1 expression at all stages, with a significant increase at the 4-cell stage (P < 0.01, Fig. 3). Previous studies have reported stage-specific roles of ROCK, with peak expression between the MII and morula stages and critical involvement in 4-cell-stage regulation, possibly explaining why expression levels normalized at the blastocyst stage despite improved developmental outcomes.

Lycopene showed a mild but non-significant increase in live birth rate compared to vitrification alone. No congenital malformations were observed.

Discussion

Vitrification has demonstrated clear advantages over slow-freezing techniques [30–32] and has become the dominant method for oocyte cryopreservation since 2013 [33]. In this study, the survival rates of vitrified-thawed oocytes across all groups exceeded 95%, indicating extremely high efficiency and surpassing many previously reported outcomes [34, 35]. The absence of any significant effect of lycopene on oocyte survival suggests the vitrification protocol itself was robust and reproducible.

ROS functions as a double-edged sword: while essential as signaling molecules in numerous physiological processes [36], their excessive accumulation can cause extensive cellular damage. Elevated ROS levels can induce DNA strand breaks, protein oxidation, and lipid peroxidation, impairing organelles such as mitochondria and endoplasmic reticulum, ultimately leading to developmental delays or arrest in oocytes and embryos [9, 37]. Oocytes and embryos feature natural regulatory mechanisms, such as enzymatic and nonenzymatic antioxidants, that effectively mitigate the harmful effects of ROS [38]. Additionally, a defensive mechanism exists in vivo, where oocytes and embryos are protected against oxidative stress by oxygen scavengers present in follicular or oviductal fluids [39]. However, the antioxidant systems can be overwhelmed by an excessive production of ROS due to disturbances from in vitro operations [39]. During oocyte cryopreservation, exogenous ROS from the environment and procedural reagents accumulate, causing oxidative stress-induced damage to mitochondria and the endoplasmic reticulum [7]. Studies have shown that lycopene can scavenge ROS in bovine oocytes and improve the cleavage rate of embryos under heat shock conditions [40]. Lycopene was considered a protective agent against embryonic anomalies, yolk sac vasculogenic, and placental defects induced by nicotine for its ability to alleviate oxidative stress [41]. In our study, 0.1 μM lycopene obviously eliminated the intracellular ROS in vitrified-thawed oocytes after a 2-h recovery. And the level of GSH was raised as similar as fresh control, which suggested the reestablishment of redox balance and beneficial to following fertilization and embryo development.

The rho-associated coiled-coil-containing protein kinases (ROCKs), downstream effectors of the small GTPase RhoA, act as signaling molecules regulating various cellular processes including cell proliferation, adhesion, polarity, cytokinesis, and survival [42]. Rock1, one of the two ROCK isoforms, participates in oocyte maturation by modulating actin-binding proteins such as cofilin, profilin, and tubulin [43]. Inhibition of Rock1 was harmful to normal oocyte or embryo development [44, 45]. However, amelioration in fertilization and cleavage rates of vitrified feline oocytes was observed when Rock1 was suppressed, suggesting that vitrification induces alterations in Rock1 expression [46].

The raise of blastocyst formation rate revealed that lycopene at a concentration of 0.1 μM enhanced the embryonic developmental ability of oocytes under vitrification. It was analogous that in vitro development of porcine embryos was improved by 0.1 μM lycopene via reducing the oxidative stress [47]. Real-time quantitative PCR in this study tracked the transcriptional levels of Rock1 from oocytes to blastocysts. Vitrification led to upregulation of Rock1 in MII oocytes, consistent with earlier findings suggesting that ROCK activity may be altered by cryopreservation and that its inhibition can improve vitrified oocyte performance [46, 48]. Post-fertilization, a consistent trend of downregulation was noted at each embryonic stage compared to fresh counterparts. In the LYC-0.1 μM group, an antagonistic effect was observed against the alterations in Rock1 caused by vitrification. Previous reports have shown that Rock1 expression in porcine oocytes decreases from the germinal vesicle (GV) to metaphase I (MI) stage, then increases from MI to MII and continues rising through to the morula stage [49]. During the 4-cell to morula transition, treatment with the ROCK inhibitor Y27632 caused a dose-dependent reduction in blastocyst formation. At 15 μM, Y27632 completely arrested development at the early 4-cell stage, although some embryos progressed to the 8-cell and morula stages [49]. These findings underscore the critical importance of ROCK activity during the 4-cell stage. In line with this, our results suggest that lycopene may enhance embryo development by preserving appropriate Rock1 expression from the MII oocyte stage through to the blastocyst stage, particularly at the 4-cell transition.

The first successful live birth from a vitrified oocyte was reported globally in 1999 [50] and by 2013, vitrification had gradually become the mainstream method for oocyte cryopreservation [33]. Lower pregnancy rate [51] and live birth rate [52] but similar birth weight, and early development in childhood were recorded in clinical [53]. The most extended follow-up continued assessment of children until they were just 6 years old [19], far from reaching adulthood. Regretfully, research on human adult offspring from vitrified oocytes is hindered by limited samples, a brief history of usage, high costs, and significant follow-up attrition. Moreover, antioxidants were increasingly considered as effective to reduce cryo-damage, and many studies evaluated their effect on embryo development. Some effective antioxidants, such as melatonin [54] and C-phycocyanin [55], have been studied in human oocytes for their potential to reduce cryodamage, their long-term effects on offspring remain largely unexamined.

Generally, mammalian such as mouse, bovine, and porcine embryos have been considered as models for studying biochemical and physiological regulations of human [47]. Here, we utilized mice embryo transfer to confirm whether vitrification could have long-term effect on offspring. The live birth rate was lower in the vitrification group compared to the fresh control, which was consistent with the clinical outcomes previously reported [52]. Lycopene did not significantly improve live birth outcomes, suggesting its beneficial effects are primarily limited to early embryogenesis. Then, these pups were kept in observation until 9-week-old, when they were considered adults. Pups’ weight presented significant but small differences only on day 7 between the fresh and vitrification group, which were still in normal range and considered natural development. No abnormalities in the morphology, growth curves, or paraffin sections of major organs may indicate a safety profile for oocyte vitrification. Additionally, the gonadosomatic indexes for both sexes and follicle counts suggested that vitrification did not impair the fertility of the offspring. We think these results indicated that oocyte vitrification had no adverse effect on the development of offspring until adulthood. Similar conclusions may be speculated in vitrified oocytes with lycopene exposure. Statistically significant differences of weight on days 21, 28, and 35 in males between the fresh and LYC-0.1 μM group were small and considered biologically insignificant. None of other outcomes presented abnormalities in the LYC-0.1 μM group. The counts of atretic follicle among female offspring were significantly smaller in the LYC-0.1 μM group than those in the other groups, maybe suggesting a subtle protective effect of lycopene on ovarian reserve. However, these findings should be cautiously interpreted, particularly given the small sample size and substantial biological variability. Overall, we speculated that lycopene enhances the developmental potential and usability of vitrified-thawed oocytes without compromising offspring health. These results preliminarily support the safety and potential utility of lycopene as an adjunct to improve outcomes in oocyte cryopreservation.

This study is limited by the relatively small number of embryo transfer recipients and the lack of data on older generations. Considering the limitations of this study, including small sample size, limited follow-up duration, and lack of functional testing, expanded validation studies are needed to verify these findings and their implication regarding the implantation results and the long-term health of the offspring. Recent reports have highlighted an increased risk of metabolic disorders among children conceived through assisted reproductive technologies [56]. Future work will focus on expanding sample size, assessing reproductive and metabolic outcomes into later life, and examining multigenerational effects.

Conclusions

Lycopene exerts a protective effect against vitrification-induced oxidative stress by restoring redox homeostasis and modulating Rock1 expression across preimplantation stages. While lycopene did not significantly impact live birth rates, it improved early embryonic development. It was speculated that neither vitrification nor lycopene had exhibited adverse effect on offspring growth, fertility, or organ health. Our results have laid a preliminary foundation for further research.
