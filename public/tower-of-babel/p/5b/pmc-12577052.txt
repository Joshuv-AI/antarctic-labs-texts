Complete genome sequence analysis of Boeremia exigua, a fungal pathogen causing leaf spot disease of Panax notoginseng
Authors: Shuang Ma, Teng Wang, Zhongjian Chen, Tie Zhang, Mengmeng Cheng, Gao Xiong, Yong Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn 2022, a new leaf spot disease caused by a strain of Boeremia exigua (Desm.) Aveskamp was discovered on fully expanded leaves. However, the genomic basis and pathogenic mechanisms of the pathogens responsible for Panax notoginseng leaf spot remain poorly understood. This study reports, for the first time, the complete genome sequence of B. exigua, the pathogen causing P. notoginseng leaf spot in China. We performed sequencing, de novo assembly, and genomic analysis of strain HEI-C.ResultsThe HEI-C genome was estimated to be approximately 26.35 Mb in size, with a 53.14% GC content. Among the 11,701 predicted genes, 11,478 (98.09%) showed significant similarity to genes in the NCBI nr database. Phylogenetic analysis based on the HEI-C ITS sequence and whole genome revealed that HEI-C is most closely related to Boeremia sp. Among its coding genes, 412 putative CAZy genes belonging to five CAZy classes were identified, which are associated with plant cell wall degradation. A total of 252 secondary metabolite gene clusters of various types were predicted. Additionally, using the PHI (Pathogen–Host Interactions) database, we identified 47 effector proteins and 91 virulence-enhancing factors.ConclusionThe complete genome sequence of B. exigua, the causal agent of P. notoginseng leaf spot, enhances our understanding of its pathogenicity and virulence. Therefore, this study provides a theoretical foundation for investigating its pathogenic mechanisms and developing improved disease management strategies.Keywords: Panax notoginseng, Boeremia exigua, Whole genome sequencing, CAZy genes, Effectors

Background

P. notoginseng is a perennial herb of the family Araliaceae. As a traditional Chinese medicinal plant, it has a long history of cultivation [1]. P. notoginseng is among the most highly valued medicinal perennial herbs worldwide [2]. It has a narrow ecological range and is primarily cultivated in Wenshan, Yunnan Province, where the climate and soil conditions are most suitable for its growth [3, 4]. However, during cultivation, P. notoginseng is vulnerable to microbial contamination, which compromises its safety and efficacy [5]. In July 2024, ~ 20% of P. notoginseng plants grown under pine forests in Qiubei County, Wenshan, Yunnan, exhibited leaf spot symptoms. The pathogen Boeremia exigua was identified as the causal agent. The first confirmed infection of P. notoginseng by B. exigua occurred in March 2024. However, the disease was initially observed in July-August 2022, when a novel leaf spot appeared on fully expanded leaves at a plantation in Xundian, Yunnan Province, China [6].

B. exigua has a broad host range and has been isolated from over 200 plant genera [7]. In recent years, its host range has continued to expand. In 2020, the first case of B. exigua-associated leaf spot on white clover in China was reported [8]. In 2022, the pathogen was reported to cause leaf spot on Khaya grandifoliola and K. senegalensis in Brazil [9]. In 2024, B. exigua was linked to leaf spot on Hydrangea macrophylla in Korea [10], Chinese researchers found that it also caused spot blight in cowpeas [11]. Overall, B. exigua is a plant pathogen affecting 45 species across 31 genera and 19 families, causing symptoms such as leaf spots, spot blight, and tuber rot [12].

The lifestyle and pathogenic mechanisms of B. exigua remain insufficiently understood. In recent years, fungal genome sequencing, enabled by next-generation sequencing technologies, has been widely applied in plant disease management research [13], advancing genomics-based approaches. However, to date, no genomic characterization of B. exigua has been reported. To investigate the gene regulatory network of B. exigua, we sequenced, assembled, and annotated the complete genome of strain HEI-C. This genomic data is expected to provide valuable insights into the pathogenic mechanisms underlying disease development. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of the genome of B. exigua, the causal pathogen of P. notoginseng leaf spot disease.

Materials and methods

Strains and culturing conditions

The B. exigua strain HEI-C was initially isolated from diseased leaves of P. notoginseng. These leaves were collected as raw materials from the primary P. notoginseng cultivation areas in Wenshan, Yunnan Province, China. The strain was deposited at the P. notoginseng Agricultural Engineering Laboratory, P. notoginseng Research Institute, Wenshan, China. The B. exigua strain was cultured on PDA medium (composed of 200 g potato, 20 g dextrose, and 15 g agar per liter of distilled water) and incubated at 25 °C for 7 days. Sterile filter paper (90 mm diameter) was placed in sterilized Petri dishes, and 3 mL of sterile water was added to each to maintain a moist environment. Healthy leaves were placed in the dishes, and one side of each leaf was wounded using a disposable sterile needle. Cultured mycelial plugs (6 mm diameter) were inoculated onto the wounds, while leaves inoculated with sterile agar plugs served as the control (n = 10). The leaves were then incubated at 25 °C with 80% relative humidity for seven days. Seven days post-inoculation, fungi were re-isolated from newly developed lesions on the inoculated leaves.

For morphological observations, cultures were grown on PDA medium at 25 °C in the dark for 30 days. The taxonomic identity of B. exigua was determined based on a combination of morphological characteristics and analysis of the ITS and RPB2 region of the rDNA gene sequences [6].

Genomic DNA extraction and high-throughput sequencing

For whole-genome sequencing, B. exigua HEI-C was grown on solid PDA medium and incubated in the dark at 25 °C for seven days. The mycelia were then harvested and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. Genomic DNA was extracted using the NucleoBond® HMW DNA Kit (MN NucleoBond, Germany, 740160.20). The extracted DNA was used to construct sequencing libraries, which were sequenced on both the Illumina MGISEQ PE150 and Nanopore Sequel platforms by Sangong Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The average genome coverage was 58× for Illumina and 86× for Nanopore.

Genome survey and assembling

Trimmomatic v0.36 was used to remove adaptor sequences and low-quality bases from the paired-end Illumina raw reads [14]. Subsequently, FastQC v0.11.2 was employed to assess read quality [15]. The raw sequencing data contained low-quality reads and junctions. To ensure high-quality data for downstream analysis, Fastp was used to filter the raw data, yielding clean reads. Finally, these clean reads were then used for a genome survey based on the k-mer algorithm and for genome sequence correction.

Subread data from the Nanopore Sequel sequencing platform were output in Hierarchical Data Format. To facilitate genome assembly, the sequence files were converted into FASTA format using the “fasta” command in Samtools v1.9. BUSCO v5.3.2 was used to assess the accuracy and completeness of the genome assembly, gene sets, and transcripts. Based on the OrthoDB database (https://www.orthodb.org/), BUSCO constructed a set of single-copy orthologous genes representing major evolutionary lineages [16]. This gene set was then compared with the assembled genome. The accuracy and completeness were evaluated based on the comparison ratio and integrity. A proportion of Complete BUSCOs exceeding 80% indicated a high level of genome assembly integrity. Finally, all predicted genomic information was used to generate a circular genome map with the CIRCOS tool [17].

Gene function annotation

The prediction of gene functions was performed through a sequential process. Initially, BLASTP searches with an E-value threshold of 1e−5 were conducted against the Nr v20210824 and Swiss-Prot v20210824 databases [18–20]. The gene functions were then inferred based on the best-matching alignments from these searches. Subsequently, the eggNOG-mapper software was used to generate annotations based on eggNOG v2.1.5, utilizing the eggNOG v4.5 and HMMER databases [21]. To obtain InterPro annotations, a Perl script provided by EBI (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/) was utilized. Subsequently, Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analyses were then performed to assign functional annotations. To assess the potential involvement of these genes in biological pathways, all gene models were aligned (E-value < 1e−5) to the KO database. Functional predictions and classifications of putative genes were further refined using the KOG database [22]. The assembled sequences were also mapped to Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways using the KEGG Automatic Annotation Server [23].

CAZy family analysis

Using the CAZy database (http://www.cazy.org) as a reference, HMMER3 was applied to compare gene aggregation sequences against the CAZy database [24]. HMM domain information for the entire CAZy family was obtained using the HMM algorithm, with a filtering threshold set at E-value < 1e−5.

PHI prediction and secondary metabolism gene cluster analysis

The genes related to pathogen-host interactions were predicted using the PHI v4.8 database (http://www.phi-base.org/). To quantify different types of genes, BLASTP was employed to compare the predicted protein sequences with the protein sequences of the complete genome. In this study, PHI-base lethal-phenotype genes refer to pathogen genes whose knockout results in pathogen lethality, as defined by PHI-base, and are distinct from host-targeting toxins. Secondary metabolic gene clusters were predicted using the antiSMASH v6.0.0 pipeline [25, 26].

Results

Infection symptoms and phenotypic characteristics of HEI-C

In 2024, the author conducted a survey of P. notoginseng disease in Qiubei County, Wenshan City, Yunnan Province, and found that P. notoginseng leaf spot was prevalent, with a disease incidence of ~ 20%. Nearly round or irregular dark brown spots of varying sizes appeared on affected leaves, and in severe cases, these spots coalesced (Fig. 1A). To identify the pathogens responsible for P. notoginseng leaf spot, diseased leaves exhibiting typical symptoms were collected from the field, and the pathogens were isolated. The isolated strains were then inoculated onto leaves in vitro. After the HEI-C strain was cultured in vitro for five days, the leaves developed typical infection symptoms (Fig. 1B). These tests confirmed strain HEI-C as the causative agent based on Koch’s postulates. After seven days of growth on potato dextrose agar (PDA), HEI-C mycelia appeared green and dense, with white aerial mycelium, irregular margins (Fig. 2A), and a blue colony underside (Fig. 2B). After 10 days, the mycelium turned olive green (Fig. 2C), and the colony underside became inky blue (Fig. 2D). By 30 days, the mycelium appeared dark brown (Fig. 2E) and the colony underside blue-black (Fig. 2F). Under the microscope, the ascus disc and ascus fruit were dark brown (Fig. 2G, a, b, I, f, J and g), and the mycelium was brown, smooth, and septate (Fig. 2G, c). The conidial peduncle (Fig. 2H and d), mature ascospore (Fig. 2H and e), and nearly spherical brown ascospores were visible at 30 days. Based on morphological characteristics, the pathogen isolated from P. notoginseng leaf spot was initially identified as Boeremia.

Molecular identification of HEI-C

Pathogenic fungus HEI-C underwent DNA extraction and PCR amplification. Following sequencing, two fragments of the rDNA-ITS (Accession No. PV017704) and RPB2 (Accession No. PV820597) region were obtained. The multiplre sequences were subjected to a BLAST search in the NCBI database and compared with similar strains (Fig. 3A). The results indicated that HEI-C shared sequence similarity with strain of Boeremia exigua. Based on the NR database comparison of the HEI-C genome (Accession No. JBLFBN000000000), HEI-C showed the highest number of matching sequences with Boeremia exigua (Fig. 3B). A total of 11,478 genes (98.09%) exhibited significant matches to entries in the NCBI nr database. Due to the high similarity between the ITS sequence and that of the Boeremia lycopersici strain, an ANI (Average Nucleotide Identity) comparison was performed between the genome of the HEI-C strain and those of Boeremia exigua MPI-SDFR-AT-0100 (GCA_020726555.1), Boeremia lycopersici CBS378.67 (GCA_022609225.1), and Boeremia exigua CU02 (GCA_029590635.1) to ensure accurate classification. The genome similarity between HEI-C and Boeremia exigua MPI-SDFR-AT-0100 was 96.24% (Fig. 3C). Based on these results, along with morphological identification, the strain was classified as Boeremia exigua.

General genome features

To gain a more comprehensive understanding of the B. exigua strain HEI-C, we performed whole-genome sequencing and analyzed the complete genome (Fig. 4). The genome was evaluated using K-mer spectrum analysis based on Illumina paired-end sequencing data. A total of 3,370,198,376 bases and 23,685,732 reads were generated The initial genome size of the B. exigua HEI-C strain was estimated to be 26.35 Mb, with an overall GC content of 53.14% (Fig. 4; Table 1). The assembled genome comprised twenty-five contigs, with a contig N50 of 2.20 Mb. The longest contig was 3.73 Mb, and the average contig length was 1.39 Mb (Table 1).

Carbohydrate-active enzymes

In fungi, CAZys facilitate host cell wall degradation, enabling nutrient uptake [27]. CAZys are categorized into five major classes: glycoside hydrolases (GHs), glycosyl transferases (GTs), carbohydrate esterases (CEs), auxiliary activity enzymes (AAs), and polysaccharide lyases (PLs). A total of 412 putative CAZy genes were identified in B. exigua through comparison with the CAZy database. Among them, 159 encode GHs, 30 GTs, 57 CEs, 24 PLs, and 142 AAs (Fig. 8). This CAZy gene profile provides a foundation for exploring the mechanisms underlying the fungal pathogenicity of B. exigua.

Secondary metabolite-associated gene clusters

The gene clusters associated with secondary metabolism in B. exigua are presented in Fig. 9. A total of 252 gene clusters were identified through comprehensive genomic analysis. These included 52 type I polyketide synthase (T1PKS), 11 type III PKS (T3PKS), 24 terpene, 41 nonribosomal peptide synthase (NRPS), 68 nonribosomal peptide synthase like (NRPS-like), 23 NRPS-like-T1PKS, 15 NRPS-T1PKS, and 18 NRPS-like-terpene clusters (Fig. 9). Among these, 104 clusters were identified as PKS and NRPS types, accounting for 41.26% of the total predicted gene clusters, as shown in Fig. 9.

Discussion

B. exigua is synonymous with Phoma exigua. In 2010, scientists established a new genus, Boeremia, based on morphological characteristics and polygenic sequence analysis, and reclassified P. exigua as Boeremia [28]. According to the taxonomy provided by NCBI, B. exigua is currently classified as “Ascomycota, Dothideomycetes, Pleosporales, Didymellaceae, Boeremia.” In 2004, Boerema summarized the cultural characteristics of these strains. However, natural variation within this group and population-level differences following host infection led to changes in their morphological and physiological traits during in vitro culture [29]. Consequently, identification of pathogenic fungi based solely on morphology is unreliable. In our manuscript, we identified strain HEI-C as B. exigua using a multi-step approach. First, we examined its morphological characteristics. Then, we conducted pathogenicity tests. Finally, we performed phylogenetic analysis using the ITS sequence and whole genome data. This integrative approach enabled accurate identification of strain HEI-C as B. exigua.

Furthermore, the overall genome features of B. exigua HEI-C, a strain of B. exigua isolated from China, were described. Genome assembly was performed using a combination of high-throughput Illumina MGISEQ PE150 and Nanopore Sequel sequencing platforms. DataData from both platforms were integrated to generate a de novo genome assembly, which was then characterized in terms of genome size, gene content, and other relevant genomic features. Genome assemblies of representative fungal species available in GenBank show that fungal genome sizes range from 2.2 Mb to 1.1 Gb, with an average of 40.8 Mb and a median of 35.4 Mb [30]. The genome size of B. exigua was estimated using both k-mer and read coverage analyses and found to be approximately 26 Mb. This is smaller than those of other reported fungal pathogens of P. notoginseng, such as Alternaria panax BNCC115425 (34.76 Mb) [31] and Phytophthora cactorum 10,300 (59.3 Mb) [32]. The total number of predicted genes in B. exigua was 11,701, compared to 11,838 and 18,189 in A. panax BNCC115425 and P. cactorum 10,300, respectively. On average, fungal genomes encode 11,128 protein-coding genes, with a median of 11,142. However, the number of genes varies substantially among fungal species [30]. Although the exact causes of gene number variation at broader phylogenetic levels are difficult to determine, they are primarily attributed to differences in the rates of gene duplication and gene loss within gene families. These processes influence key traits such as nutritional strategies and the capacity to adapt to diverse hosts or environments [33]. Currently, the relationship between gene number, degree of fungal specialization, and pathogenicity toward P. notoginseng remains unclear. Further comparative genomic analyses are needed to better understand the pathogenic mechanisms of different pathogen species.

To investigate the genome architecture of B. exigua, the repetitive elements within its genome were analyzed. The results showed that repetitive elements constituted ~ 8.0% of the entire B. exigua genome. Transposable elements (TEs) include both retrotransposons (Class I elements) and DNA transposons (Class II elements). Long terminal repeats (LTRs) are a major class of retrotransposons. As observed in other fungi, the majority of transposable elements (TEs) in B. exigua were Class I LTR retrotransposons (LTR-RTs), predominantly from the Gypsy family [34–36]. All LTRs identified among the repetitive elements in B. exigua were classified as Gypsy elements. In addition, the retrotransposons (Class I elements) of B. exigua also included long interspersed nuclear elements (LINEs). LINEs can induce genomic changes such as rearrangements, insertions, or deletions, and provide raw material for genome evolution [37]. DNA transposons (Class II elements) mobilize via a “cut-and-paste” mechanism [38]. Interestingly, only one type of DNA transposon was identified in the B. exigua genome: TcMar-Fot1. The role of these transposons in B. exigua remains unclear. Notably, the abundance of repetitive elements (8% of the genome), including transposons and retrotransposons, suggests that they may drive adaptive evolution [39]. These elements could facilitate functional diversification of virulence-associated genes or epigenetic regulation of pathogenicity traits [40, 41]. From an agronomic perspective, the prevalence of repetitive regions in the genome may have practical implications. Repetitive elements might contribute to chromosomal instability or rapid adaptation to host resistance genes, posing challenges for durable disease control [42, 43].

Microbial CAZymes constitute a vast, intricate, and redundant enzymatic system involved in the degradation of plant cell walls. These enzymes play a pivotal role as virulence factors in fungal pathogens. To investigate the genetic basis of pathogenicity, CAZymes in B. exigua HEI-C were identified and annotated. The CAZymes include glycoside hydrolases (GHs), glycosyltransferases (GTs), carbohydrate esterases (CEs), auxiliary activity enzymes (AAs), and polysaccharide lyases (PLs). AAs contribute to the oxidative degradation of lignocellulose [44]. Certain CAZymes exhibit synergistic interactions, whereby the combined activity of multiple enzymes exceeds the sum of their individual activities. For instance, synergy among polynanase, cellobiohydrolase, and β-glucosidase results in a highly efficient cellulase degradation system [45]. Plant cell walls are composed of 35–50% cellulose, 20–35% hemicellulose, and 15–20% lignin [46]. Many fungal pathogens must first breach the plant cell wall to colonize the host [47]. The five types of CAZymes facilitate cell wall degradation, thereby enabling infection of P. notoginseng. Thus, the pathogenic mechanism of B. exigua HEI-C was further examined from a genetic perspective.

The PHI-base database, accessible online, catalogs genes associated with experimentally validated pathogenicity, virulence, and effectors in fungal pathogens. Using PHI-base, several virulence and effector-related genes were predicted in the B. exigua genome. In this study, 47 effectors were identified at the genomic level. These effectors are predicted to be key factors in pathogen–host interactions. Further analyses, such as in planta expression studies, can be conducted to validate these effector candidates. When integrated with genomic information, these effectors may contribute significantly to the development of disease management strategies for effective control of Boeremia leaf spot in P. notoginseng. Prior to this study, no information was available regarding the genome structure of B. exigua. Therefore, this research represents an initial step toward understanding the genome organization and pathogenic mechanisms of B. exigua.

Conclusion

Strain HEI-C of the fungus B. exigua, which causes leaf spot in P. notoginseng, has had its genome sequenced-representing the first reported genome sequence of this species. The genome of strain HEI-C exhibits several distinct characteristics. It is rich in functional genes, cell wall–degrading enzymes, and pathogen–host interaction (PHI) elements. Moreover, B. exigua strain HEI-C demonstrates the capacity to synthesize a diverse array of secondary metabolites and gene components. These findings enhance our understanding of the genomic biology and gene regulatory network of B. exigua. Overall, the genomic sequence data generated in this study provide a theoretical foundation for future investigations into the interactions between B. exigua and P. notoginseng.
