Ssrp governs germline development independent of the FACT complex in Drosophila spermatogenesis
Authors: Xiaoning Tan, Yuhan Lou, Zhiyong Yin, Jiaqi Zhao, Jia Wang, Jichen Jin, Yunbo Wang, Yongmei Xi, Xiaohang Yang, Huimei Zheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe heterodimeric FACT complex (SSRP1/Ssrp and SUPT16H/Dre4) is primarily recognized as a chromatin remodeler. While mutations in this complex are linked to human intellectual disability and it maintains neural stem cell fate in flies, single-cell RNA sequencing reveals robust FACT complex expression in human testicular germ cells. Nevertheless, its specific functions during spermatogenesis remain unexplored.MethodsThis study utilized Drosophila melanogaster as a model to investigate the roles of FACT complex during spermatogenesis. Germline-specific and somatic-specific knockdowns of Ssrp and dre4 combined with immunostaining were performed to assess their functions. Bulk and single-cell RNA sequencing analyses were conducted on Ssrp-deficient testes to investigate transcriptomic changes.ResultsOur study uncovers context-dependent functions for Ssrp. In testis somatic cells, Ssrp collaborates with Dre4 to sustain cyst stem cell populations via estrogen-related receptor-mediated glycolytic activation. Remarkably, germline Ssrp operates independently of Dre4, governing transit-amplifying divisions and meiotic progression. Germline-specific Ssrp depletion, but not dre4 knockdown, induces male sterility, characterized by spermatogonial accumulation, mitotic asynchrony, and meiotic arrest within primary spermatocytes. Bulk and single-cell RNA sequencing analyses of Ssrp-deficient testes reveal systemic transcriptomic dysregulation, including suppression of metabolic programs (glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation) and activation of MAPK/EGFR signaling. Ssrp loss disrupts sister centromere cohesion during meiosis I, as well as diminished ATP levels and aberrant CENP-A accumulation, suggesting a dual regulatory nexus linking energy metabolism to chromosomal stability.ConclusionsThis work reveals previously unknown, context-dependent functions of Ssrp during Drosophila spermatogenesis. Ssrp emerges as a multifunctional orchestrator essential for both somatic and germline compartments of germline development. These findings provide crucial foundational insights into reproductive disorders associated with Ssrp dysfunction and underscore the importance of the FACT complex in male germ cell development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13578-025-01478-8.Keywords: FAcilitates chromatin transcription complex, Ssrp, Spermatogenesis, Glycolysis, Oxidative phosphorylation, CENP-A

Introduction

The FAcilitates Chromatin Transcription (FACT) complex, a heterodimeric histone chaperone composed of SSRP1 and SUPT16H, has emerged as a critical regulator in developmental processes, with mutations linked to intellectual disability in humans [1, 2]. Our previous research has showed that the FACT complex binds to the promoter region of the estrogen-related receptor (ERR)—a transcriptional activator governing aerobic glycolytic switching [3–5]—to promote glycolytic metabolism and maintain neural stem cell fate in the Drosophila central brain [6]. Notably, single-cell RNA sequencing data from the Human Protein Atlas reveal robust expression of both SSRP1 and SUPT16H in human testicular germ cells (https://www.proteinatlas.org). This observation aligns with emerging data highlighting striking proteomic similarities between brain and testicular tissues [7], as well as clinical correlations linking cognitive function to semen quality and testicular activity [7, 8]. Despite these associations, the biological role of the FACT complex in testicular physiology remains unexplored. This motivates our investigation into its potential roles during spermatogenesis.

Spermatogenesis is a highly conserved process from Drosophila to humans [9]. Drosophila is well established as an excellent model for the study of spermatogenesis [10, 11]. An adult male Drosophila contains a pair of testes, each testis is a coiled tube with a closed apical end and a basal end that connects to the seminal vesicle. At the apical of the testes, there is a well-structured microenvironment comprising terminally somatic cells (hub cells), germline stem cells (GSCs), and somatic cyst stem cells (CySCs) [12]. Each GSC asymmetrically divides to produce a self-renewing GSC and a committed gonialblast (GB). The GB undergoes four rounds of mitotic transit amplification (TA), forming a 16-cell spermatogonial cyst interconnected via cytoplasmic bridges (ring canals) and synchronized by branched fusome structures [12–15]. Post-mitotic cells then enter meiosis, ultimately yielding 64 haploid spermatids that subsequently undergo terminal differentiation processes including nuclear compaction, flagellar axoneme assembly, and individualization to produce motile spermatozoa [15, 16].

During the process of spermatogenesis, significant energy expenditure is required to support the differentiation and maturation of germ cells, with the metabolic profiles continue to evolve through the development phase [17]. Adenosine triphosphate (ATP), the primary energy-carrying molecule found in all living cells, is predominantly produced via two metabolic pathways: glycolysis and mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) [18]. It is well-established that GSCs exhibit a metabolic preference for glycolysis, utilizing its metabolic intermediates to sustain long-term regenerative capacity. The differentiation of germ cells is associated with an upregulation of mitochondrial function and increased OXPHOS activity [17]. Disruptions in this metabolic choreography—whether through ERR-mediated glycolytic impairment [19]; mitochondrial fusion/fission defects (Marf/Opa1/Drp1) [20–22], or ATP synthase dysfunction [23]—result in catastrophic failures manifesting as GSC depletion, meiotic arrest, and spermiogenic defects (e.g., failed axoneme assembly). Interestingly, ATP synthase has also been shown to perform functions independent of its role in OXPHOS. A study indicates that ATPsynα or ATPsyn-βlike subunit localizes at meiotic centromeres, a process dependent on the presence of the histone H3 variant CENP-A [24], underscoring the multifaceted relationship between metabolism and germ cell development.

While the FACT complex typically functions as an obligate heterodimer across diverse systems, such as embryonic stem cells [25], neural stem cells [6], and numerous cancer cell types [1], this study reveals multifaceted roles of Ssrp during Drosophila spermatogenesis. We demonstrate that somatic depletion of Ssrp or dre4 (fly orthologs of SSRP1/SUPT16H) disrupts CySC maintenance and non-cell-autonomously impairs germ cell differentiation via ERR-dependent metabolic regulation. Strikingly, germline-specific depletion uncovered divergent functions of Ssrp. Ssrp knockdown in germ cells induces male sterility, whereas dre4 knockdown does not exhibit this effect. The reduction of Ssrp levels in spermatogonia resulted in an accumulation of spermatogonial cells and meiosis arrest, accompanied by impaired sister centromere cohesion and decreased ATP levels. Integrated bulk and single-cell RNA sequencing analyses reveal that Ssrp loss triggers systemic transcriptomic dysregulation, including suppression of glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation, concomitant with hyperactivation of MAPK/EGFR signaling. These results not only provide critical insights into the pathogenesis of Ssrp-associated reproductive disorders but also unveil a novel, Dre4-independent role for Ssrp in male germ cell metabolism and chromosome dynamics.

Result

Somatic FACT complex mediates CySC maintenance and germline cell differentiation via ERR in Drosophila testes

To explore the roles of FACT complex during spermatogenesis, we employed the upstream activating sequence (UAS)/Gal4 system [26] to manipulate the expression levels of Ssrp or dre4, each targeted by at least two RNAi lines. Tj-Gal4, a somatic Gal4 driver expressed in cyst stem cells (CySCs) and cyst cells, was used to specifically knock down Ssrp or dre4 in Drosophila testes. All RNAi lines were verified by BLAST analysis to exhibit no predicted off-target effects. When driven by Tj-Gal4, these lines effectively reduced the corresponding target protein levels, as confirmed by immunostaining (Supplemental Fig. 1A–D). In control males, the testes exhibited appropriate size with mature sperm present in the seminal vesicle (Fig. 1A). Conversely, Tj > Ssrp RNAi and Tj > dre4 RNAi males displayed significantly smaller testes with nearly empty seminal vesicles (Fig. 1A). Male fertility rates were markedly reduced to 5.4% in Tj > Ssrp RNAi males and completely diminished to 0% in Tj > dre4 RNAi males (Fig. 1B). Dissected testes were stained for Zinc-finger homeodomain protein 1 (Zfh1), a marker for CySCs and somatic cyst cells surrounding the hub cells [27]. We observed that Zfh1 signals were significantly reduced in both Tj > Ssrp RNAi and Tj > dre4 RNAi testes (Fig. 1C, upper lanes). Statistical quantification of Ssrp+ cells showed a significant overall decrease in Tj > Ssrp RNAi testes; whereas in Tj > dre4 RNAi testes, an increase in Ssrp+ cells was observed specifically at the testis apex (Supplemental Fig. 1F–H). Furthermore, the sperm individualization process was absent in Tj > Ssrp RNAi and Tj > dre4 RNAi testes (Fig. 1C, lower lanes). In contrast, control testes revealed elongated spermatids along with cystic bulges (CBs) and waste bags (WBs), visualized using antibodies specific for activated Caspase-3 (Fig. 1C, lower lanes). Intriguingly, immunofluorescence staining showed that knocking down one component of the FACT complex in cyst cells using Tj-Gal4 resulted in reduced expression of the other component (Supplemental Fig. 1A–E).

Our previous work showed that FACT complex regulates neural stem cell fate by modulating the expression of ERR genes and maintaining a high metabolic ratio of glycolysis/oxidative phosphorylation [6]. To determine whether this mechanism operates in the testis, we employed ATP-SPARK (separation of phases-based activity reporter of kinase) method [28] to visualize the ATP levels in the somatic depletion of Ssrp testes. Consistent with observations in brain neural stem cells, Ssrp knockdown in somatic cells of testes also displayed elevated ATP synthesis (Fig. 1D). Given that upregulated ATP levels in brain was caused by decreased ERR levels in Ssrp deficiency brains and the phenotypes were rescued by overexpressing ERR [6], we overexpressed ERR in Tj > Ssrp RNAi testes. This restored testis size and the number of Zfh1-positive cells (Fig. 1E) as well as the population of Ssrp-positive cells (Supplemental Fig. 1G–H), and fertility rates were also largely rescued (Fig. 1F). It is logical to believe that lack of Ssrp or Dre4 in testicular somatic cells leads to defective FACT complex which causes spermatogenesis defects. As for the differences of Ssrp+ cells in Ssrp and dre4 RNAi treatments, it is possible that Ssrp or Dre4 may have some unknown FACT complex independent functions. Collectively, these results suggest that Ssrp collaborates with Dre4 as a functional heterodimer within the FACT complex to control CySCs development via ERR, which in turn affects germ cell differentiation non-cell-autonomously.

Ssrp deficiency in early germ cells affects male fertility independent of Dre4

To investigate the role of FACT complex in germ cells, we used two germline-specific Gal4 drivers to knock down Ssrp or dre4. nos-Gal4 mainly expresses in the GSCs and early spermatogonia; and Bam-Gal4 drives in spermatogonia [29]. We found that nos > Ssrp RNAi males exhibited severely atrophied testes with completely empty seminal vesicles (Supplemental Fig. 2A) and 0% fertility (Supplemental Fig. 2B). Unexpectedly, knocking down dre4 using the same Gal4 driver didn’t affect the testis morphology (Supplemental Fig. 2A), and the fertility rate was indistinguishable from that of the control males (Supplemental Fig. 2B). Similarly, Bam > Ssrp RNAi males displayed reduced testicular size with no mature sperm in their seminal vesicles (Fig. 2A), accompanied by a significant reduction in fertility rate (Fig. 2B). In contrast, knocking down dre4 with Bam-Gal4 didn’t showed any obvious phenotypes (Fig. 2A–B).

To eliminate potential off-target effects associated with RNAi, we conducted immunofluorescence staining, Western blot analysis, and genetic rescue experiments to validate our findings. In the control testes, Ssrp immunostaining levels were robust; however, in Bam > Ssrp RNAi testes, there was a marked reduction of signals within spermatogonial cells and even later-stage spermatocytes (Fig. 2C, Supplemental Fig. 3). Genetic rescue demonstrated that the expression levels of Ssrp were recovered in Bam > Ssrp RNAi/UAS-Ssrp testes and the testis morphology of Bam > Ssrp RNAi/UAS-Ssrp males closely resembled that of wild-type controls (Fig. 2D). Furthermore, the fertility rate of Bam > Ssrp RNAi/UAS-Ssrp males was comparable to that of the control males (Fig. 2E). Western blot analysis demonstrated that the knockdown of either Ssrp or dre4 resulted in a significant decrease in their respective protein expression level (Fig. 2F). Importantly, germline knockdown of Ssrp (Bam > Ssrp RNAi) did not affect Dre4 expression (Supplemental Fig. 3), confirming phenotype specificity. These data demonstrate that Ssrp knockdown phenotypes are not attributable to off-target effects. Together, Ssrp or Dre4, was unlikely to function as a cohesive FACT complex in early germ cells and Ssrp function within early germ cells is necessery for male fertility.

Ssrp knockdown in spermatogonia causes prolonged TA divisions and abnormal cell proliferation

To further explore the roles of Ssrp in early germ cells in Drosophila testes, we focused on its function in spermatogonia by disrupting its levels with Bam-Gal4. In testes, deep DAPI staining highlights the early-stage nuclei at the apex, the nuclei of clusters of elongated spermatozoa at the tail. We noticed that the region containing deeply stained nuclei at the apex of Bam > Ssrp RNAi testes was significantly expanded as compared with the control (Fig. 3A–B). Bam serves as a spermatogonial cells marker and is a key regulator of TA division, which is essential for germ cell differentiation in testes [30, 31]. Our results showed that Bam-positive signals were coincide within the expanded TA region (Fig. 3C–D), indicating the accumulation of spermatogonial cells in the absence of Ssrp.

To investigate the abnormal accumulation of spermatogonial cells induced by Ssrp knockdown, we used a Phospho-Histone H3 (PH3) antibody to detect the proliferation status in the testes. Phosphorylation of histone H3 at serine 10 is a hallmark chromatin modification exclusively occurring during late G2/M phase transition in both mitotic and meiotic divisions [32]. We found the number of PH3-positive cells and the distance from PH3-positive cells to the tip of testis were both increased in Bam > Ssrp RNAi males compared with those of control (Fig. 3E–G), indicating the delayed mitosis of spermatogonial cells. Interestingly, we did not observe PH3-positive 32-cell cysts in Bam > Ssrp RNAi testes (Supplemental Fig. 4). These data suggest that lack of Ssrp in spermatogonia causes abnormal germ cell proliferation.

Ssrp is necessary for the meiosis I of primary spermatocytes

Vasa is expressed in germ cells, including spermatogonia and spermatocytes. Vasa signals are intense at the apex and prominent in early primary spermatocytes, while they are undetectable in spermatids within control testes (Fig. 2D). In Bam > Ssrp RNAi testes, signals were distributed almost evenly from the apex to the tail of the testes (Fig. 2D). To pinpoint down the developmental defects during the spermatogenesis in Bam > Ssrp RNAi testes, we conducted a stage-wise phenotipic analyses in the testes. DE-cadherin (DE-cad) was used to mark cyst cells and hub cells. The cyst stage was identified based on the number of germ cells within the tightly packed clusters. Results showed that in Bam > Ssrp RNAi testes the numbers of GSCs, GBs and 2-cell cysts were similar to that of the control, while the numbers of 4-cell cysts and 8-cell cysts were almost doubled (Fig. 4A–B). It is most likely the expansion of TA region was due to the accumulation of spermatogonia cells. Based on this result and our early observtion that no PH3-positive 32-cell cysts were detected in Bam > Ssrp RNAi testes, we concluded that lack of Ssrp attenuated the rates of TA divisions and blocked meiosis.

We next labeled the centrosomes of early germ cells with γ-tubulin antibody [33] to comfirm the meiosis blockage in the Bam > Ssrp RNAi testes. Results revealed no significant differences between Ssrp RNAi and control testes at 2 to 8 cell-cyst stages, where centrosomes remained on the same side at the prophase of mitosis and occupied opposite poles of each spermatogonia at the metaphase (Fig. 4C, left). At the later 16-cell cyst stage, characteristic V-shaped centriole pairs within the centrosome were observed during prophase of meiosis I in primary spermatocytes from control testes (Fig. 4C, middle, arrowheads). In contrast, in Bam > Ssrp RNAi testes at this stage, the centrosomes appeared as mainly as large dots and bar-like structures in the cyst of 16 primary spermatocytes, indicating defected meiosis. While we observed 32-cell cysts in control testes, they were completely absent in Bam > Ssrp RNAi testes (Fig. 4C, right).

Since the mitochondrial structures in meiosis I exhibited a rod shape, and at meiosis I telophase, mitochondria form a butterfly-like pattern [34], we employed ATP5A antibody to label mitochondrial morphology for identifying stages during spermatogenesis. We observed the butterfly-like mitochondria in the control testes (Fig. 4D, left), indicating 32 cell stage. However, no butterfly-like mitochondria were detected in the Bam > Ssrp RNAi testes (Fig. 4D, right), indicating defected meiosis. Meanwhile, the individualization process was absent in Bam > Ssrp RNAi testes. Furthermore, in control testes, cystic bulge and waste bag structures (labelled by Caspase-3) and individualization complex (marked by Phalloidin) were obvious, indicating normal individualization process. And those structures were not detected in Bam > Ssrp RNAi testes (Supplemental Fig. 5). Together, we conclude that Ssrp functions to promote germ cells transition to 32-cell stage during spermatogenesis and without Ssrp cell cycle arrested at meiosis I.

Reduction of Ssrp in spermatogonia leads to decreased energy metabolism

To gain better understanding of the regulatory network mediated by Ssrp in testes, we conducted RNA-seq analysis of control and Bam > Ssrp RNAi testes. Total of 15839 genes were identified within the transcriptional profiles, and differentially expressed genes (DEGs) underwent further analysis [p-value < 0.05; log2 fold-change (FC) > 1 or log2FC < − 1]. Among 4608 DEGs, there were 2692 upregulated and 1916 downregulated genes that exhibited significant alterations in Bam > Ssrp RNAi testes as compared with the controls (Fig. 5A, Table S1). Gene ontology (GO) analysis was performed for the DEGs induced by spermatogonia Ssrp RNAi. The top-ranked downregulated genes, consistenting with the observed reproductive phenotypes, demonstrated enrichment in genes associated with spermatogenesis, flagellated sperm motility, sperm axoneme assembly, and sperm individualization (Fig. 5B, Table S2). Upregulated gene-enriched GO terms were identified and are presented in Supplemental Fig. 6A. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis revealed that oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and glycolysis/gluconeogenesis pathways were significantly down-regulated (Fig. 5C, Table S3), indicating that Ssrp knockdown impacts energy metabolism processes. The top-ranked upregulated KEGG pathways were shown in Supplemental Fig. 6B.

ATP is mainly generated through the metabolic pathways of glycolysis and OXPHOS, both of which were downregulated in Bam > Ssrp RNAi testes. We observed a significant decrease in ATP levels within Ssrp RNAi spermatogonial cells (Fig. 5D). Furthermore, we found that overexpression of ERR in Bam > Ssrp RNAi testes was insufficient to rescue male sterility (Supplemental Fig. 6C). Interestingly, the suppression of glycolysis by knocking down ERR alone in wild-type spermatogonial cells did not show obvious defects in spermatogenesis (Supplemental Fig. 4C). These two pieces of information imply that, in spermatogonial cells, ERR is not directly involved in the regulation of glycolysis which affects spermatogenesis. Based on these findings, we conclude that the role of Ssrp in early germ cells differs from its function in somatic cells, where it acts as a subunit of FACT complex in neural stem cells and cyst cells.

We then examined the potential roles of OXPHOS in spermatogonial cells. Mitochondrial fusion is typically associated with a high level of OXPHOS [35]. We assumed that disruption of genes responsible for mitochondrial fusion would, at least partially, mimic the low levels of OXPHOS phenotype observed upon knockdown of Ssrp in spermatogonia. As expected, knockdown of Optic Atrophy 1 (Opa1), which regulates the fusion of the inner mitochondrial membranes, in spermatogonia indeed decreased ATP levels (Fig. 5F). At the same time, we observed prolonged TA division (Fig. 5F). Additionally, depletion of Opa1 also resulted in a reduced fertility rate (Fig. 5E). These results were reminiscent of phenotypes recognized in Bam > Ssrp RNAi testes. However, spermatocytes could largely go through meiosis and exhibited a marked disruption in the synchronous differentiation of 64 haploid spermatids in Bam > Opa1 RNAi testes (Supplemental Fig. 7, yellow arrows), which was different from the phenotype of Bam > Ssrp RNAi testes. Together, our data demonstrate that reduction of Ssrp in spermatogonia leads to decreased energy metabolic pathways, and downregulation of oxidative phosphorylation, not glycolysis, partially responsible for the defects associated with Ssrp deficiency in spermatogonia.

Single cell sequencing reveals transcriptome changes in Ssrp knockdown testes.

To delineate the germline-specific regulatory architecture of Ssrp, we conducted single cell sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis of freshly dissected testes from control and Bam > Ssrp RNAi groups. Following rigorous quality control filtering, we retained 13218 high-quality testicular cells (6,399 wild-type and 6819 Ssrp knockdown cells).

Transcriptomic profiling revealed that approximately 15921 and 14,671 genes were detected in the control and knockdown groups, respectively. The dimensionality of the gene and/or cell expression matrix was reduced to two primary axes and visualized using t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE), which clustered similar cells together (Fig. 6A). Utilizing published marker genes [36], we identified three testicular somatic cell types and five spermatogenic cell types in control group: cyst cells (CC), hub cells (HC), muscle cells (MS), spermatogonia (SPG), early spermatocytes (SPC), late SPC, early spermatids (SPT), late SPT; and two testicular somatic cell types and six spermatogenic cell types in Bam > Ssrp RNAi group: CC/HC, MC, SPG, early SPC, late SPC1, late SPC2, early SPT and late SPT (Fig. 6A–B, Supplemental Figs. 8 and 9). To further investigate the origin and maturation of germ cells, we combined data from control and Bam > Ssrp RNAi groups to re-cluster germline subpopulations and conducted pseudotime trajectory analysis based on clustering for various differentiated stages of spermatogenesis (Fig. 6C–D,). Moreover, we performed two dissection repeats for each group, and the major cell clusters were independent of batch effects and technical variables, while differences between the two groups were preserved. Notably, we observed a deviational trajectory in spermatogonial development in Bam > Ssrp RNAi groups (Fig. 6D, green arrow), which may represent a source of meiotically arrested spermatocyte subpopulations. Cellular component analysis showed that the cellular composition analysis revealed alterations in the proportions of various testicular cell types, including an increase in the proportion of spermatogonial cells following Ssrp knockdown in spermatogonia. Subsequently, we analyzed DEGs across the five spermatogenic cell types between control and Bam > Ssrp RNAi groups. The number of upregulated and downregulated genes in each cluster was presented in Supplemental Fig. 11 (Table S4). In the spermatogonial cluster, KEGG analysis revealed that downregulated genes were significantly enriched in pathways related to Glycolysis/Gluconeogenesis, Citrate cycle (TCA cycle), and Oxidative phosphorylation (Fig. 6E, Table S5), whereas no significant enrichment was observed for upregulated genes. Lack of Ssrp in spermatogonia affects meiotic sister centromere cohesion

As the reduced OXPHOS partially accounted for the phenotypes associated with Ssrp deficiency in spermatogonia, we sought to identify additional underlying factors. Previous studies have demonstrated that defects in CENP-A or ATP synthase α/β/γ disrupted the adhesion of sister centromeres during pre-meiosis, leading to abnormal spermatogenesis [24]. Furthermore, Ssrp has been identified as an interaction partner of CENP-A in Drosophila S2 cell lines [37]. We assessed the levels of CENP-A in Bam > Ssrp RNAi testes and found that the expression of CENP-A mRNA within spermatogonia cluster was elevated in single cell sequencing data (Fig. 6F). Protein levels of CENP-A were also increased in testes subjected to Ssrp knockdown by Western blot analysis (Fig. 6G). We subsequently performed immunostaining on spermatocytes using CENP-A as a centromere marker. In male Drosophila, meiotic prophase I lacks conventional synapsis features between homologous chromosomes and is instead divided into sub-stages S1-S6 based on nuclear and spindle morphologies [38]. At stages S5/6, the four Drosophila chromosomes are organized into three distinct territories: both the second and third autosomes form large territories while the X–Y chromosomes create a third territory alongside the fourth chromosome. Typically at stage S5/6, an average of 6.5 centromere foci can be observed; two foci each per second and third autosomal territories, one or two per fourth chromosomal territory, and one or two per X–Y territory [39]. In stage S5/6 nuclei from Ssrp-depleted spermatocytes, an increase in centromere foci compared to controls was observed (Fig. 6H). Quantitative analysis revealed a significant increase in centromere foci per nucleus at late prophase I (S5/6), with averages rising from 6.7 in control samples to 8.3 following Ssrp reduction (Fig. 6I). Given that homologous chromosomes are typically unpaired at this stage, except for the fourth chromosome, these results suggest that maintaining sister centromere cohesion during late prophase I is compromised upon reduction of Ssrp levels. Defects of sister centromere cohesion could be one of the additional factors involved in meiosis arrest in Bam > Ssrp RNAi testes.

Discussion

The two subunits of the FACT complex, SSRP1/Ssrp and SUPT16H/Dre4, have typically been shown to function together across various cell types in the regulation of DNA replication, DNA repair, and RNA transcription [1, 40–42]. In this study, we discovered a paradigm shift in its functional organization during Drosophila spermatogenesis. While Ssrp and Dre4 function cooperatively in somatic cyst cells to maintain CySC populations through ERR-mediated glycolytic regulation, a mechanism recapitulated in neural stem cells, their roles diverge fundamentally in the germ cells. Strikingly, our data show that germline-specific depletion of Ssrp induces male sterility through pleiotropic defects spanning mitotic synchrony, meiotic progression, and energy metabolism. However, dre4 knockdown shows no comparable phenotypes, demonstrating subunit-specific functional autonomy.

We observed distinct subcellular localization of Ssrp and Dre4 in the Drosophila testis: both are expressed in cyst cells, whereas only Ssrp is detectable in germ cells (Supplemental Figs. 1 and 3). The independent function of Ssrp in developing germ cells probably arises from meiosis-specific chromatin remodeling demands distinct from mitotic systems. In mitotic divisions, sister chromatid cohesion is synchronously released during anaphase to ensure equal segregation, mediated by the canonical cohesin complex containing Rad21/Scc1 [43, 44]. In contrast, meiotic divisions require a two-step cohesion release. First for homologous chromosome separation in meiosis I, then for sister chromatid segregation in meiosis II, which depends on meiosis-specific cohesins like Rec8 in humans [43, 44] and SOLO/C(2)M in Drosophila [45]. This extended maintenance of centromeric cohesion through meiosis I places unique demands on chromatin stability, particularly at CENP-A-enriched centromeres where Ssrp probably interact with CENP-A and act to stabilize cohesion complexes without Dre4 partnership. Such divergence is further amplified by later germline-specific chromatin transitions like histone-protamine replacement [46], wherein Ssrp may recruit alternative unknown interactors to fulfill specialized nucleosome remodeling roles. While these adaptations appear specifically tailored to spermatogenesis, the potential conservation of this Ssrp function in oogenesis remains an open question meriting future exploration, considering the distinct chromatin and metabolic landscapes characterized by a significant reliance on mitochondrial inheritance within female germ cells [47].

The metabolic equilibrium between glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation is essential for stem cell maintenance and differentiation [48]. In Drosophila neural [6] and testicular somatic systems (Fig. 1), Ssrp-Dre4 cooperativity regulates ERR-dependent glycolytic activation, as evidenced by neuroblast proliferation defects and cyst stem cell loss upon their depletion—phenotypes rescued by ERR overexpression. In contrast, germline Ssrp depletion disrupts both metabolic pathways, contrasting with somatic contexts. This dual metabolic impairment aligns with germ cell differentiation's oxidative phosphorylation dependency, as demonstrated by Opa1 knockdown partially mimicking Ssrp-deficient spermatogonial defects. Notably, our omics analyses reveal other signaling pathways associated with energy metabolism, such as pyruvate metabolism, Pentose phosphate pathway are significant downregulated in Ssrp-deficient spermatogonia (Figs. 5C and 6E). Pyruvate metabolism serves as a critical hub linking glycolysis to the TCA cycle, thereby supporting central bioenergetic flux, while the PPP supplies essential precursors for biosynthesis and redox homeostasis [49, 50]. The coordinated downregulation of these supporting pathways underscores the breadth and severity of the metabolic dysregulation caused by Ssrp loss. Collectively, our findings establish Ssrp as a multifunctional metabolic regulator with germline-specific roles beyond canonical FACT complex activities.

Notably, the observed elevation of CENP-A levels in Bam > Ssrp RNAi testes presents an intriguing paradox. A previous study demonstrated that Ssrp interacts with CENP-A in Drosophila S2 cell lines [37], and knockdown of CENP-A or ATP synthase α disrupt the adhesion of sister centromeres during pre-meiosis, leading to abnormal spermatogenesis [24]. We observed impaired sister centromere cohesion marked by increased centromere foci in Bam > Ssrp RNAi testes (Fig. 6H). However, it is perplexing that the levels of CENP-A were elevated in Ssrp RNAi testes. It is possible that unbalanced CENP-A levels may ultimately lead to disrupted sister centromere cohesion phenotype. In addition, a potential feedback response from CENP-A to compensate the loss of Ssrp could not be excluded.

The discovery of upregulated MAPK/EGFR signaling in Ssrp RNAi testes adds another layer of complexity to this regulatory network (Supplemental Figs. 6A and 11C). The MAPK/EGFR signaling pathway plays a crucial role in various developmental processes, including cell survival, growth, proliferation, and differentiation [51]. In Drosophila testes, this pathway is well-established within somatic cells that it is critically important for their encapsulation of germ cells; and this interaction is vital for germ cell differentiation and maturation [52–55]. Furthermore, the MAPK/EGFR signaling pathway also play important roles in germ cells. It exhibits dose-dependent effects on germline differentiation; a low dose of EGFR signaling is required for spermatogonia TA-divisions, while a high dose of EGFR signaling promoted germline cell terminal differentiation in Drosophila testes [56]. Downregulation of Dsor1 (MEK), which acts downstream of EGFR in spermatogonia, leads to an upregulation of EGFR signaling within the germline and results in defects in germline differentiation [57]. In our study, by manipulating Ssrp levels in spermatogonial cells, we identified defects not only in spermatogonia TA divisions but also meiotic abnormalities in spermatocytes as well as issues with terminal differentiation of germline cells. Whether the abnormal MAPK/EGFR signaling pathway causes the phenotypes associated with Ssrp defects or merely represents a concomitant result remains to be elucidated. Further investigations into the relationship between Ssrp and the MAPK/EGFR signaling pathway will be interesting.

In summary, our findings delineate a compartment-specific functional partitioning of the FACT complex within the Drosophila testis. While Ssrp and Dre4 function cooperatively in somatic cyst cells to maintain metabolic and CySC populations, Ssrp operates independently in the germline to orchestrate both metabolic programs and meiotic chromosome dynamics. The germline-specific depletion of Ssrp triggers a multifaceted crisis, encompassing a severe bioenergetic deficit, via disruption of glycolysis/oxidative phosphorylation, and a failure in centromeric cohesion, collectively leading to aberrant TA divisions, meiotic arrest, and sterility (as integrated in Fig. 7). In conclusion, this work redefines the FACT complex from a static heterodimer to a context-dependent regulatory module. We elucidate the divergence of Ssrp functions in germ cells by revealing a Dre4-independent role for Ssrp in coordinating germline metabolism and chromosome dynamics. Our work underscores that "housekeeping" chromatin regulators can evolve reproductive-specific modalities, offering foundational insights into human infertility linked to SSRP1/SUPT16H mutations.

Method and materials

Drosophila stock

Drosophila lines used in this study were listed as below: w1118, sp/Cyo; TM2/TM6B, bam-GAL4/Cyo, nos-GAL4, Tj-GAL4/Cyo, UAS-mCD8GFP/Cyo, Ssrp RNAi (THU2221, THU5317), Dre4 RNAi (THU1558, BS34344, V106049), opa1 RNAi (THU0811), ERR RNAi (THU2474), sp/Cyo; UAS-Ssrp 3 × HA/TM6B (FlyORF: F001903), UASt-ERR (BS83690), UAS-ATP-SPARK/Cyo;TM2/TM6B (Gift from Hai Huang, Zhejiang university). Flies were reared on standard cornmeal medium at 25°C and in a relative humidity of 40–60%, and crosses were set up at either 25°C or 29°C. Flies were purchased from Tsinghua Drosophila Center (THU), Bloomington Drosophila Stock Center (BS), Zurich ORFeome Project (FlyORF) and Vienna Drosophila Resource Center (V).

Fertility test

A single two-day-old male from different genotypes was arranged to mate with three wild-type virgin females aged 2–5 days in tubes. The tubes were replaced every 24 h, and the presence of larvae was monitored over a period of two days. The detection of larvae indicates that the male flies are fertile; conversely, their absence suggests infertility. A total of 20 male flies in each genotypes were selected for this study. The fertility rate is calculated as the total number of fertile flies divided by the total number of flies in the group. This experiment was conducted three times to ensure reliability. Data analysis was performed using Student's t-test.

Immunostaining and imaging

The testes of 3-day-old adult males were dissected in schneider’s insect medium (SD), fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (diluted by 0.3% PBST) for 20 min, and washed three times in 0.3% PBST. After blocking for 1 h in 0.3% PBST containing 5% BSA, they were incubated with the primary antibodies Chicken anti-GFP (1:500, Abcam); Mouse anti-Eya (1:50, DSHB); Guinea Pig anti-Vasa, [1:10,000, Gifted from Yu Cai (Temasek Life Sciences Laboratory, Singapore)]; Rabbit anti-cleaved caspase3 (1:100, Cell Signaling Technology); Mouse anti-Bam (1:50, DSHB); Rabbit anti-Phospho-Histone H3 (PH3) (1:500, Millipore); Mouse anti-ATP5A (1;1000, Abcam); Rat anti-DE-cadherin (1:50, DSHB); Rat anti-CENP-A (1:500, Proteintech); Mouse anti-γ-tubulin (1:200, sigma-aldrich); Mouse anti-Zfh-1 (1:1000, our lab); Rabbit anti-Ssrp (1:2000, our lab) at 4°C overnight, and then washed three times in 0.3% PBST. The testes were incubated with secondary antibodies (Jackson ImmunoResearch) for 1 h at room temperature, and washed three times in 0.3% PBST before mounted by antifade mounting medium with DAPI (Beyotime). The samples were visualized and pictured by confocal microscope (Leica STELLARIS5; Olympus FV1000).

Antibody generation

dre4 cDNA fragment encoding amino acid residues 1–309 was cloned into a pET-28a. Protein fragments were expressed in E.coli BL21, purified by SDS-PAGE and used to generate polyclonal antibodies in guinea pigs according to the published protocol [58].

Western blot

Total protein extracted from 60 to 80 male testes were prepared in the RIPA lysis buffer [50 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS, 1% IGEPAL CA‐630 and complete protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche)] and quantified using a BCA assay (Beyotime). Equivalent amounts of total protein per sample were diluted with loading buffer, boiled for 5 min, and stored at -20˚C until use. Samples were subjected to SDS-PAGE and transferred to a PVDF membrane, and immunoblotted at 4˚C overnight with antibodies. The primary antibodies used were mouse anti-CENP-A (1:500, Abcam); rabbit anti-Ssrp (1:2000, our lab); guinea pig-Dre4 (1:2000, our lab). The secondary antibodies were HRP-conjugated (1:5000). Blots were treated with ChemiLucent ECL detection reagents (Millipore) and protein bands were visualized using a Chemiluminescence Imaging System (Clinx Science Instruments, Shanghai, China).

RNA extraction and RNA‑Seq analysis

We used 3-day-old Bam > w1118 flies as controls (Control) and Bam > Ssrp RNAi (Ssrp-KD) flies in this experiment. Testes from 50 male flies of each genotype were dissected in cold SD. Total RNA was prepared from the isolated testes using TRIzol (Invitrogen), following the recommendations of the manufacturer. The quality and integrity of the total RNA were verified using a 2100 Bioanalyzer RNA Nano chip device (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA). The concentration was measured using an ND-430 1000 spectrophotometer (NanoDrop, Wilmington, DE). Indexed RNA-Seq libraries were prepared. This experiment included purification of poly (A) mRNA with oligo-dT magnetic beads, RNA fragmentation, synthesis of double stranded cDNA using SuperScript II reverse transcriptase, ligation of indexed Illumina adapters, and amplification using limited-cycle PCR. The mRNA of each library was sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform located at Novogene Co., Ltd., China.

Single-cell suspensions, library preparation and sequencing

3-day-old testes were used to profile cells from four independent samples (two control samples vs. two Ssrp-KD samples). Testes (a mixture of 80 testes per sample) were freshly dissected in cold SD. Cell counter was used for quality inspection, and the concentration was adjusted according to the quality inspection result. The target concentration was 700–1200 cell/μL, the cell activity was greater than 90%, and the agglomeration was less than 15%. NovaSeq X Plus was used to carry out the single-cell RNA-sequencing. Cells quantified for library construction were used to build libraties followed by SOP《Chromium Next GEM SingleCell3_v3.1_Rev_D》(10X Genomics). Qualified libraries were sequenced using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 PE150 sequencing strategy.

scRNA-seq data processing

The raw BCL files were processed through Cell Ranger software (v7.0; 10X Genomics) for FASTQ conversion, genome alignment (Drosophila melanogaster BDGP6.32 reference), and UMI-based quantification, implementing strict sequence filtering criteria: low-quality barcodes (Qscore < 20) were discarded, UMI sequences underwent error correction via Hamming distance clustering, and EmptyDrops (FDR = 0.01) discriminated true cell-containing droplets from ambient RNA. Transcript quantification required reads to uniquely map to exonic regions with ≥ 50% overlap, generating cell-by-gene matrices that were imported into Seurat (v4.1.1) for integrated analysis. Initial quality control excluded genes detected in < 3 cells and cells expressing < 200 genes, followed by mitochondrial content-based filtration (cells with > 20% mitochondrial reads removed) and doublet exclusion via Scrublet (score > 0.25). Post-filtering datasets (Control: 6,399 cells; Ssrp-KD: 6,819 cells) underwent log-normalization (scale factor = 10,000) and variance stabilization using 1,100 highly variable features (HVFs) identified through mean–variance relationship modeling. Linear dimensionality reduction was first performed using principal component analysis (PCA), retaining the top 5 principal components that collectively explained 78.3% of the variance. These principal components then informed nonlinear manifold learning through Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) and t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE). Graph-based clustering was subsequently implemented using the FindNeighbors (k-nearest neighbors = 20) and FindClusters (resolution parameter = 0.2) algorithms, which resolved six transcriptionally distinct cell clusters within each genotype group. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were considered significant with a fold change more than 2 and the P value lower than 0.05.

Statistical methods

All statistical data in this study were analyzed and obtained by GraphPad Prism software. The statistical method was unpaired two-tailed Student's t-test. The images were processed by Olympus FV1000 confocal laser microscope software FV10-ASW 4.2 Viewer, Leica fluorescence microscope software LAS X Office, Adobe Photoshop and Adobe illustrator.
