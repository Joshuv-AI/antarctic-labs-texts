Axitinib promotes stemness and vasculogenic mimicry in triple-negative breast cancer by disrupting THBS1-CD47 axis-mediated endothelial-tumor cell communication
Authors: Lei Lei, Huanhuan Zhou, Xiaojing Lai, Zeng Wang, Rongguo Li, Chengyong Du, Xiangming He, Lingbin Du, Yabing Zheng, Xiaojia Wang, Xiying Shao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is an aggressive subtype with limited therapeutic options. Although anti-angiogenic agents like Axitinib are used in cancers, resistance emerges through poorly understood mechanisms involving stemness and vasculogenic mimicry (VM). Here, we investigated the regulatory role of thrombospondin-1 (THBS1), an anti-angiogenic protein, in Axitinib-mediated signaling and its impact on TNBC. The effects of Axitinib on VM, angiogenesis, and stemness in TNBC were investigated through in vivo models and in vitro endothelial-supernatant cultures. Bioinformatics analyses (GSE230643 and CellChat), immunohistochemistry of clinical samples, exosome transfer assays, and xenograft models were employed to explore how Axitinib regulates THBS1-mediated endothelial-TNBC intercellular communication. Further bioinformatics analyses, combined with in vitro and in vivo experimental validations, were performed to elucidate the mechanism of the THBS1-CD47 axis in TNBC. Axitinib treatment increased stemness and promoted VM in TNBC models, despite tumor growth inhibition. Bioinformatics analyses revealed the Axitinib-dependent THBS pathway between endothelial and endothelial-like tumor cells. Axitinib-induced downregulation of THBS1 expression in HUVEC cells promoted stemness and angiogenesis of recipient TNBC cells via exosomes. Mechanistically, endothelial-derived THBS1 promoted CD47 expression in recipient TNBC cells, thereby attenuating the activity of PI3K/Akt and MAPK/ERK signaling pathways, and inhibiting stemness, angiogenesis, and VM. Importantly, Axitinib disrupted this THBS1-CD47 axis by downregulating endothelial-derived THBS1 expression. Axitinib induces the suppression of THBS1 expression in endothelial cells, thereby enhancing stemness and VM in recipient TNBC via disruption of the THBS1-CD47 axis. Restoring THBS1 signaling may represent a promising strategy to overcome Axitinib resistance.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10495-026-02388-x.

Introduction

Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is the most aggressive breast cancer (BC) subtype, characterized by the absence of estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) expression[1]. This subtype exhibits aggressive clinical behavior, high metastatic potential, and limited treatment options beyond cytotoxic chemotherapy [1, 2]. Although recent advances in targeted therapies have improved outcomes for selected patients, intrinsic and acquired resistance to therapy remains a major clinical problem and a critical barrier to achieving durable therapeutic responses [1, 3, 4]. Emerging studies have implicated that this resistance may arise from the tumor microenvironment (TME), where crosstalk between different types of cells enhances tumor survival and adaptation [5–7]. These complexities underscore the urgent need to identify the underlying mechanisms of resistance and develop more effective, targeted interventions.

Tumor vascularization plays a pivotal role in TNBC progression and therapy resistance, as tumors can adopt multiple vascularization strategies to sustain nutrient supply and survival under therapeutic pressure [8–10]. In addition to classical angiogenesis, which relies on endothelial cell sprouting from pre-existing blood vessels, aggressive tumors can also develop vasculogenic mimicry (VM), a non-endothelial vascularization program in which tumor cells themselves form patterned, fluid-conducting, vessel-like networks [11, 12]. Unlike classical angiogenic vessels, which are lined by endothelial cells and supported by a basement membrane, VM channels are lined directly by tumor cells, stain positive for periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) but negative for endothelial markers such as CD31/CD34, and are embedded within a glycoprotein-rich extracellular matrix devoid of endothelial lining[8, 13–15]. This non-endothelial vascular network directly connects to the host circulatory system, providing an alternative perfusion pathway that sustains tumor growth and facilitates hematogenous metastasis [8, 13–15].

Increasing evidence suggests that VM is strongly associated with tumor aggressiveness, metastasis, hypoxia adaptation, poor prognosis, and resistance to anti-angiogenic therapies in multiple malignancies, particularly TNBC [16, 17]. Due to the absence of an endothelial lining in VM channels, these channels exhibit inherent insensitivity to agents that are targeted against vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), such as bevacizumab and tyrosine kinase inhibitors, enabling tumors to bypass anti-angiogenic blockade[16, 18, 19]. Mechanistically, VM formation is driven by tumor cell plasticity, specifically an epithelial-to-endothelial transition in which tumor cells acquire endothelial-like characteristics through the expression of vascular endothelial (VE)-cadherin, upregulation of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), and activation of key signaling pathways, including PI3K/Akt, MAPK/ERK, Notch, and VEGF receptor signaling [14, 18–20]. Importantly, cancer stem cell (CSC)-like populations are considered major drivers of VM formation due to their high plasticity and transdifferentiation capacity [21, 22]. Concurrently, the acquisition of stemness properties, characterized by enhanced self-renewal, differentiation potential, and expression of pluripotency-associated markers, further contributes to therapeutic resistance by replenishing drug-tolerant CSC populations [4, 18, 23, 24]. Notably, crosstalk between endothelial cells and tumor cells via secreted factors or extracellular vesicles (EVs) has been shown to coordinate both VM formation and stemness maintenance [25–27].

Thrombospondin-1 (THBS1) is a multifunctional, secreted matricellular protein that modulates cell–matrix interactions and cell signaling in the TME [28, 29]. Importantly, THBS1 functions are context dependent: while many studies document an anti-angiogenic and tumor-suppressive role for THBS1, other reports link THBS1 to invasive phenotypes or poor prognosis in specific tumor contexts, reflecting complex, cell-type-specific effects [28, 30, 31]. It is reported that THBS1 can potently inhibit angiogenesis through interactions with receptors such as CD36 and CD47 and by antagonizing NO-cGMP signaling [32, 33]. However, the role of THBS1 in the context of TNBC remains inadequately explored, as does its potential impact on angiogenesis and stemness.

Given the clinical urgency posed by therapy resistance in TNBC and the mechanistic links between endothelial-tumor communication, VM, and CSC biology, we sought to investigate whether anti-angiogenic therapy (Axitinib) remodels endothelial-derived signaling in a manner that modulates VM and stemness. Using integrated bioinformatics analysis, exosome analyses, and functional validation in vitro and in vivo, we demonstrate that endothelial-derived THBS1 inhibits VM and stemness by promoting CD47 expression in recipient TNBC cells. This process is disrupted by Axitinib. These findings underscore the significance of THBS1 as a pivotal mediator of treatment resistance and suggest therapeutic strategies targeting the THBS1-CD47 axis to overcome Axitinib resistance.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells HUVEC (YDT-0292), human TNBC cell lines MDA-MB-231 (YDT-0384) and Hs578T (YDT-0269), and mouse BC cells 4T1 (YDT-0024) were obtained from INDIT Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China). HUVECs were cultured in PUMC-HUVEC-T1 Cell Complete Medium (Pricella, China, CM-0675). TNBC cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, BDBIO, China, L100-500), and 4T1 cells were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute-1640 (BDBIO, China, L103-500). All media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, SERANA, China, s-mx-015) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S, Gibco, USA, 15,140–122). Cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2.

After HUVECs were treated with Axitinib (10 or 20 μg/mL) for 24 h, the cells were washed three times with PBS and then incubated in serum-free medium for an additional 24 h. The resulting conditioned medium was collected for downstream functional assays, thereby minimizing potential residual Axitinib carryover.

Cell transfection

To establish HUVEC cell lines overexpressing THBS1, the human THBS1 CDS sequence (NM_003246) was synthesized and inserted into the pcDNA3.1 vector (Youbao, China, VT1001). Once the cells had reached 80% confluence, cells were transfected with recombinant plasmids or empty plasmids using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, USA, 11,668–019) for 24 h. The CD47 knockdown lentivirus (shR-CD47) and its corresponding control were obtained from Aikangde (Suzhou, China). MDA-MB-231 cells were infected with the aforementioned lentivirus (MOI = 200) for 24 h. Subsequently, cells were subjected to 1 μg/mL puromycin (Thermofisher, USA, A1113802) selection. The efficiency of transfection was verified by Western blotting. Detailed information regarding the lentiviral vectors is presented as follows:

shR-CD47: gatcgGCCTTGGTTTAATTGTGACTTCTCGAGAAGTCACAATTAAACCAAGGCTTTTTT.

Animal experiments

Female BALB/c nude mice (5 weeks old) were obtained from Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd (Jiaxing, China) and housed under specific pathogen-free conditions. Three independent experiments were performed, each involving different experimental groups. The animal experiments were approved by the Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee of Zhejiang Cancer Hospital (Approval No. 2020–07-003). All procedures strictly followed the institutional guidelines for animal welfare and the ARRIVE guidelines.

In 4T1 syngeneic model (Axitinib efficacy and VM analysis), mice were subcutaneously injected with 4T1 cells (5 × 105 cells/100 μL). Once the tumor volume reached approximately 100 mm3, the mice were randomly divided into two groups (n = 6) and administered either a vehicle control or Axitinib (30 mg/kg/day, MCE-HY-10065) via oral gavage for a duration of two weeks, with tumor diameters measured every three days. The animals were euthanized when the tumor volume reached approximately 1000 mm3 (but remained below 1500 mm3), and tumor tissues were subsequently collected for histological analysis.

In MDA-MB-231 xenograft model, mice were subcutaneously injected with MDA-MB-231 cells (5 × 106 cells/100 μL). When tumors reached approximately 100 mm3, mice were randomized into four groups (n = 6): vehicle, Axitinib (30 mg/kg/day, oral gavage for two weeks), hTHBS1 (0.5 mg/kg/day, subcutaneous injection for 10 days, MCE-HY-P70725), or combination. Tumor size was measured every 3 days and animals were euthanized when the tumor volume reached approximately 1000 mm3.

In CD47-knockdown xenograft model (functional validation of the THBS1-CD47 axis). Mice were subcutaneously injected with stable CD47 knockdown (sh-CD47) or control MDA-MB-231 cells (5 × 106 cells/100 μL). When tumors reached approximately 100 mm3, mice received daily subcutaneous injection of hTHBS1 (0.5 mg/kg/day) or vehicle for 10 days. Tumor size was measured at three-day intervals and animals were euthanized when the tumor volume reached approximately 1000 mm3.

PAS/CD31 double staining and Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

VM in tumor tissue sections was assessed by PAS/CD31 double staining. Paraffin sections were baked at 65℃ for 2 h, deparaffinized and rehydrated through the following sequential immersions: xylene I (3 min), xylene II (15 min, repeated twice), xylene I (3 min), 100% ethanol (3 × 5 min), and distilled water (3 × 5 min on a shaker). Antigen retrieval was performed by immersing the slides in preheated antigen retrieval buffer (1 × , prepared from a 50 × stock solution) at 95–100℃ for 15 min, followed by cooling to room temperature. The slides were rinsed three times with PBST and the tissue sections were circled with a hydrophobic pen. Non-specific binding sites were blocked with an immunohistochemical blocking solution (5% BSA) for 15 min. Subsequently, the tissue sections were incubated with the primary antibody (CD31) at 4 °C overnight. After returning to room temperature for 30 min, the sections were washed three times with PBST and incubated with the corresponding secondary antibody for 1 h. Following three PBST washes, the slides were rinsed under running water for 5 min, and DAB working solution was applied in the dark for 3–10 min. For PAS staining, the procedure was carried out according to the PAS Staining Kit instructions (Servicebio, PAS-G1008-100ML). Briefly, the sections were incubated with PAS oxidizing reagent for 10–15 min, washed with distilled water, incubated with Schiff reagent for 25–30 min in the dark, and then washed under running water for 5 min. Nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin for 30 s, differentiated briefly, and blued under running water. Finally, the sections were dehydrated through graded ethanol, cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral gum. Slides were air-dried and stored at room temperature until imaging.

For IHC of clinical TNBC samples and xenografts, sections were incubated with antibodies against THBS1, OCT4, NANOG, SOX2, and CD47 (see Table 1), followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies and DAB detection. Sections were counterstained with hematoxylin. This study involving human tissues was approved by the Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee of Zhejiang Cancer Hospital (Approval No. 2020–07-003). All procedures were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee. IHC staining intensity was quantified using ImageJ software with the IHC Profiler plugin. Briefly, digital images were imported into ImageJ and analyzed using the IHC Profiler workflow under the DAB stain mode. Images were converted to 8-bit grayscale, and thresholding was applied using identical settings for all samples to ensure consistency. For each specimen, five independent high-power fields were randomly selected and analyzed. The integrated optical density (IOD) and positive staining area were measured, and the mean optical density (MOD) was calculated as MOD = IOD/positive staining area. The average MOD from all analyzed fields was used for statistical analysis. Cell viability assay

HUVEC cells were seeded in 96-well plates (3 × 103 cells/well) and treated with various concentrations of Axitinib (0–25 µg/mL) for 24 h. Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Yeasen, 40203ES76) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well and incubated for 2 h. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Thermofisher, 51,119,570). The percentage of viable cells was calculated relative to the untreated control. A dose–response curve was generated by fitting the data to a four-parameter logistic nonlinear regression model using GraphPad Prism 6.0. The IC25 value, defined as the concentration that reduces cell viability by 25% (i.e., 75% viability), was interpolated from the fitted curve. The calculated IC25 was 17.42 µg/mL. This value served as a conservative reference for selecting subtoxic concentrations (10 and 20 µg/mL) in subsequent intercellular communication experiments, thereby avoiding confounding effects from excessive cytotoxicity.

Western blotting (WB) analysis

Cells or tissue samples were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were measured by BCA assay (Thermo, USA, 23,228). Equal amounts (20–40 μg) were separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, IPVH00010), blocked in 5% non-fat milk in TBST, and incubated with primary antibodies (see Table 1). After HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent (Biosharp, BL520B) and captured with a chemiluminescence imaging system (SH-523). β-actin serves as an internal control. Triplicate biological replicates were performed for all experiments.

Tumor sphere formation assay

To evaluate cancer stem cell-like properties, TNBC cells (3 × 103 cells/well) were seeded in ultra-low attachment 6-well plates (Corning, 3471). Cells were cultured in serum-free DMEM/F12 medium (BI, 01–172-1ACS) supplemented with 5 μg/mL insulin (Prospec, CYT-27), 20 ng/mL epidermal growth factor (EGF, Prospec, CYT-217), 10 ng/mL fibroblast growth factor (FGF, Prospec, CYT-218), and 1 × B27 (Gibco, 12,587,010). The cells were cultured for ten days, during which spheres were monitored daily and imaged using microscopy.

Tube formation (in vitro angiogenesis) assay

After adding 70 μL of the Matrigel (Corning, 356,234) to the 96-well plate that had been pre-cooled at 4 °C, the plate was immediately placed into the incubator for subsequent use. Hs578T (1 × 104 cells/well) and MDA-MB-231 (1.5 × 104 cells/well) cells treated with conditioned supernatant or exosomes were seeded onto the Matrigel and incubated for 4 h and 6 h. Tube formation was imaged using a microscope and quantified using ImageJ software.

Isolation and characterization of exosome and microvesicles (MVs)

Exosomes were isolated from HUVEC cell culture supernatant by differential ultracentrifugation (Beckman Coulter Optima XPN-100). Conditioned medium was centrifuged (4 °C) at 300 × g for 10 min, 2,000 × g for 10 min, and 10,000 × g for 30 min to remove cells and debris. Samples were centrifuged at 4 °C and 120,000 × g for 70 min. After centrifugation, carefully discard the supernatant and resuspend the pellet by adding 10 mL of PBS to each tube. Repeat the centrifugation under the same conditions, remove the supernatant, and finally resuspend the exosome pellet in 100 μL of PBS within an ultrafiltration tube. Exosome characterization methods are as follows.

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM): Exosomes were adsorbed onto formvar-carbon coated grids (3 min), stained with 2% phosphotungstic acid (2 min), and air-dried. Images were captured by transmission electron microscope (Hitachi, HT7800) to evaluate size, shape, and membrane integrity. Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis (NTA): Size distribution and concentration were measured using a NanoSight NS500 system (Malvern Panalytical). Exosomal markers (CD63 and CD9) were confirmed by WB assay.

MVs were isolated from HUVEC-conditioned medium by differential centrifugation. Briefly, conditioned medium was sequentially centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min and 2,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove cells and cellular debris. The supernatant was subsequently centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C, and the resulting pellet containing MVs was collected and washed once with PBS. After an additional centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 30 min, the MV pellet was resuspended in PBS for downstream analyses. The morphology of isolated MVs was examined by TEM, and particle size distribution was analyzed using NTA. MV-associated markers, including ARF6 and VAMP3, were confirmed by WB assay. THBS1 levels in MV and Exo fractions were compared by Western blot under identical loading conditions (equal protein amount per lane).

Immunofluorescence detection of exosome uptake

Purified exosomes were labeled with PKH67 Green Fluorescent Cell Linker Dye (Umibio, UR52302) at a final concentration of 5 μM for 10 min at room temperature, protected from light. To remove unbound dye, the labeled exosomes were then diluted in a large volume of 1 × PBS and re-isolated using the same differential ultracentrifugation protocol of exosome isolation. The resulting pellet was resuspended in 200 μL of PBS. Recipient TNBC cells were cultured in confocal dishes and incubated with the PKH67-labeled exosomes for 24 h. Following incubation, the cells were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, and counterstained with DAPI (Servicebio, G1012) to visualize nuclei. Exosome uptake was subsequently visualized and confirmed using a confocal laser scanning microscope.

Bioinformatics

The mouse breast cancer single-cell sequencing data (GSE230643) were downloaded from the public database. Single-cell data were preprocessed and analyzed using R language Seurat V5. Dimensionality reduction and clustering analysis resulted in 13 distinct cell subgroups, which were visualized using UMAP for dimensionality reduction. Single-cell data were annotated based on the expression of specific marker genes for each cell type. The InferCNV package was used to detect copy number variations (CNVs) in epithelial cells, and tumor cells were annotated based on CNVs.

Differential analysis between the control group (Control) and the drug-treated group (Axit) was performed using the Seurat package function FindMarkers on tumor-associated endothelial cells, tumor cells, and endothelial cells. A volcano plot was generated using ggplot2, with blue representing downregulated differentially expressed genes and red representing upregulated genes. Gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were performed on differentially expressed genes (DEGs) using the ClusterProfiler package. Cell–cell communication analysis between tumor-associated endothelial cells, endothelial cells, and tumor cells was performed using the R language CellChat package. The communication network between groups was visualized using the built-in function of CellChat. Incoming and outgoing communication strength heatmaps and scatter plots were created. The CD47 pathway was selected for further analysis, and a chord diagram was generated to illustrate the interaction between CD47 and LAMININ signaling.

Triple-negative breast cancer data from the TCGA database were downloaded and grouped based on high and low expression levels of CD47 and THBS1. Differential analysis between groups was conducted using the R language Limma package, and a volcano plot was generated using ggplot2. Heatmaps were created using the Pheatmap package. DEGs were subjected to enrichment analysis using the ClusterProfiler package, and enrichment bar plots and bubble plots were generated using ggplot2.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate unless otherwise specified. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 6.0. Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test was used for comparisons between groups. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05 (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001).

Results

Axitinib promotes vasculogenic mimicry in vivo and supernatant from Axitinib-treated HUVEC cells facilitates stemness properties and vasculogenic mimicry in TNBC cells

To investigate the mechanisms underlying resistance to Axitinib in BC, its therapeutic efficacy was first evaluated in a mouse xenograft model. Following tumor establishment, Axitinib (30 mg/kg/day) was administered via oral gavage for two weeks, with tumor diameters measured every three days during the treatment. The animals were euthanized when the tumor volume reached approximately 1000 mm3 (but remained below 1500 mm3), and tumors were harvested for subsequent analysis. Although Axitinib significantly suppressed breast tumor growth overall (Fig. 1A-C), the tumor-inhibitory effect of Axitinib appeared to attenuate during the later stages of treatment (approximately days 10–16), as evidenced by a steeper growth curve slope compared to the earlier phase (Fig. 1C), suggesting the potential emergence of an early adaptive response following Axitinib administration in BC. The cytotoxic effects of Axitinib on HUVEC cells were assessed to determine appropriate experimental concentrations. Within the dose range of 0–10 μg/mL, Axitinib did not impair the viability of HUVEC cells. A modest increase in viability was observed at low concentrations (< 10 μg/mL); however, this trend did not reach statistical significance. In contrast, Axitinib at ≥ 10 μg/mL produced a significant, dose-dependent reduction in cell viability (Fig. 1E). Biphasic or non-monotonic dose–response patterns have been previously reported for anti-angiogenic agents, including VEGF-pathway inhibitors, and the subtle increase at low doses may reflect compensatory metabolic or adaptive endothelial responses rather than genuine proliferation [34]. The dose–response curve was fitted using a four-parameter logistic model, and the IC25 value was calculated as 17.42 μg/mL. The IC25 is advantageous for subtoxic/sublethal dose selection in mechanistic studies or combination therapies, providing a more conservative estimate than the IC50 and helping to avoid strong cytotoxicity that may interfere with downstream molecular analyses. Based on this analysis, two concentrations were strategically chosen, 10 μg/mL (below IC25, minimal cytotoxicity) and 20 μg/mL (approximating the IC25, subtoxic), for comprehensive evaluation of Axitinib’s biological effects in subsequent experiments. To investigate the mechanism by which Axitinib induces VM in TNBC, supernatant from HUVEC cells treated with different concentrations of Axitinib (0, 10, and 20 μg/mL) was used to treat Hs578T and MDA-MB-231 cells. The results revealed that supernatant from Axitinib-treated HUVEC cells upregulated the expression of stemness markers, including OCT4 (Octamer-Binding Transcription Factor 4), NANOG (NANOG Homeobox Protein), and SOX2 (SRY-Box Transcription Factor 2), with the more consistent promoting effect observed at 20 μg/mL (Fig. 1F). Moreover, supernatant from Axitinib-treated HUVEC cells exhibited an increase in tumor sphere formation, indicating enhanced cellular stemness (Fig. 1G). Importantly, exposure to supernatant from Axitinib-treated HUVEC cells promoted angiogenesis of TNBC cells (Fig. 1H). Collectively, these data indicate that Axitinib may facilitate stemness properties and angiogenesis through cell communication between normal endothelial cells and TNBC cells, thereby contributing to the development of resistance to Axitinib and highlighting the need for further studies on Axitinib.

Axitinib potentially suppresses THBS-mediated intercellular communication between endothelial and endothelial-like tumor cells in TNBC

UMAP visualization of the murine breast cancer single-cell transcriptome (GSE230643) revealed distinct clustering patterns corresponding to 13 cellular subpopulations (Fig. 2A and S1A). Based on canonical marker gene expression, these distinct clusters were annotated into eight major cell types, including epithelial, neutrophil, fibroblast, endothelial, B cell, T cell, macrophage, and monocyte (Figure S1A and S2A left panels). Tumor cells were further identified among epithelial cells using InferCNV-based copy number variation analysis. Endothelial cells showed heterogeneous subclusters including endothelial-like tumor cells (Fig. 2A right panel). DEGs between Axitinib-treated (Axit) and control groups were analyzed and visualized via volcano plots in endothelial-like tumor cells, tumor cells, and endothelial cells, respectively (Figure S1B). Notably, only upregulated DEGs were identified in tumor-like endothelial cell group, while tumor cell group exhibited the highest number of DEGs. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses revealed that in tumor-like endothelial cell group, DEGs were predominantly associated with calcium ion transport, extracellular structure organization, and Hippo signaling pathway (Figure S1C). In tumor cell group, DEGs were primarily enriched in the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, MAPK signaling pathway, and ubiquitination-related activities (Figure S1D). In endothelial cell group, DEGs were mainly related to ubiquitination, ribosomal activities, and mitochondrial functions (Figure S1E). Importantly, CellChat analysis uncovered distinct patterns of intercellular communication between control and Axitinib-treated conditions (Fig. 2B). Under control conditions, endothelial-like tumor cells showed no communication with the other two cell types (left panel). Discussion

TNBC remains a clinical challenge due to its aggressive nature and limited therapeutic options [1–4]. While anti-angiogenic agents like Axitinib have been approved for certain cancers, their efficacy in TNBC is constrained by emerging resistance mechanisms [10, 39]. In this study, we identify a previously unrecognized mechanism by which Axitinib treatment remodels endothelial-tumor communication to promote VM, stemness, and resistance in TNBC. Specifically, Axitinib downregulates THBS1 expression in endothelial cells and their exosomes, which in turn inhibits CD47 activation in recipient TNBC cells, leading to enhanced PI3K/Akt and MAPK/ERK signaling, VM formation, and stemness properties. These findings link anti-angiogenic therapy, EV signaling, and tumor plasticity in a mechanistic framework that has implications for overcoming therapeutic resistance.

VM, in which aggressive tumor cells form perfusable, endothelial-free vascular channels, is highly prevalent in TNBC and has been linked to resistance against anti-angiogenic therapies [8, 40]. The formation of VM is regulated by multiple signaling pathways, including PI3K/Akt, MAPK/ERK, Notch, and VEGF receptor pathways, as well as by hypoxia-driven transcriptional programs involving HIF-1α, FOXC2, and Twist1[16, 18, 41–44]. Importantly, VM is functionally distinct from classical angiogenesis because it reflects tumor cell transdifferentiation rather than endothelial sprouting from pre-existing vessels, and it may provide a compensatory blood supply when canonical angiogenic pathways are inhibited[13, 20]. Recent reviews further emphasize that VM is particularly relevant in TNBC, where its presence contributes to treatment failure and represents a major adaptive mechanism under therapeutic pressure [8, 41]. Our observations indicate that, despite initially effective tumor growth suppression, Axitinib promotes VM and stemness in BC. Consistently, emerging literature indicates that canonical anti-angiogenic agents can paradoxically foster alternative vascularization strategies and treatment escape. For example, bevacizumab has been shown to increase VM formation in glioblastoma [45], and anti-angiogenic TKIs (such as sunitinib) can promote VM in renal cell carcinoma[46], Axitinib may inadvertently promote adaptive responses that facilitate tumor survival [47–49]. Similarly, FOXC2-driven VM has been shown to confer resistance to anti-angiogenic therapy across multiple solid tumor types, an effect that can be reversed by FOXC2 suppression [16]. In TNBC, anti-angiogenic or anti-tumor pressure can enrich these plastic populations and activate VM-associated signaling networks, including TWIST1-driven programs and other EMT/stemness-associated pathways [10, 50]. In our xenograft model, although Axitinib effectively suppressed tumor growth overall, the tumor-inhibitory effect attenuated during the later stages of the treatment period, as evidenced by a steeper growth curve compared to the earlier phase. This temporal pattern is more indicative of an early adaptive response rather than fully established acquired resistance. The distinction between early and late phases of resistance to anti-angiogenic therapy is now well recognized. Montemagno et al. comprehensively reviewed resistance mechanisms and explicitly categorized them into an “immediate early” stage, involving rapid tumor cell adaptation, and a “late” stage, driven by microenvironmental remodeling [51]. This framework accommodates phenotypic changes that emerge within days to two weeks, consistent with the time course of our observation.

Similarly, in our study, Axitinib treatment upregulates stemness markers and enhances tumor sphere formation, underscoring its role in fostering a stem-like, resistant phenotype. This outcome is consistent with reports that CSCs are intimately connected to VM, and CSC subpopulations can directly form VM channels and secrete pro-VM factors [41, 52], suggesting that treatment-induced plasticity may contribute to VM-associated resistance rather than merely reflecting a secondary phenotype. In effect, anti-angiogenic treatment appears to select for vascular plasticity and cellular de-differentiation in TNBC, enabling tumors to adapt by non-endothelial vascularization and enhanced invasiveness [10, 53, 54]. Together, these studies provide strong literature support for the concept that the attenuation of Axitinib efficacy we observed within a relatively short period of time reflects an early adaptive tumor response, driven at least in part by VM induction and enhanced stemness, which may subsequently evolve into more durable resistance mechanisms. These findings underscore the notion that conventional angiogenesis inhibitors alone may be insufficient in the treatment of TNBC unless the compensatory VM and stemness programs are also addressed.

A central focus of this study was the intercellular signaling patterns reshaped by Axitinib. Our findings indicate that after Axitinib treatment, endothelial THBS signaling is weakened in its outgoing capacity, while that of endothelial-like tumor cells is enhanced. THBS1 is a multifunctional matricellular protein that has been demonstrated to possess anti-angiogenic properties through its interactions with receptors such as CD36 and CD47 [32, 33]. Recent studies have emphasized its immunoregulatory and context-dependent roles in the TME [28, 30, 55, 56]. In our work, THBS1 expression was reduced in TNBC tissues and inversely correlated with stemness markers, consistent with previous reports associating low THBS1 expression with poor prognosis [57–60].

Importantly, we identified that THBS1-mediated signaling via endothelial-derived exosomes is critical for maintaining anti-tumorigenic communication with TNBC cells. The involvement of exosomes as carriers of THBS1 expands a growing appreciation of EVs as biologically active messengers in the TME. Recent studies have documented that exosomal cargo, including both proteins and noncoding RNAs, can reprogram recipient cells to alter angiogenesis, stemness, and therapy sensitivity [61–64]. A growing body of research has begun to emerge in the scientific literature that specifically examines the impact of exosomal THBS1 on the behavior of recipient cells [29, 65–67]. This study contributes to the existing body of research by demonstrating that THBS1 mediates endothelial-TNBC intercellular communication via exosomal transfer, thereby suppressing stemness features and angiogenesis in TNBC cells, at least partially. Conversely, Axitinib can downregulate the expression of THBS1 in endothelial cells and their derived exosomes, thereby inhibiting THBS1-mediated intercellular communication.

Mechanistically, the communication between THBS1 (expressed by endothelial cells) and CD47 (expressed by TNBC cells) has been identified as a key mechanism influencing VM and stemness in TNBC cells. CD47 has emerged as a pleiotropic modulator in cancer biology, not only as an immune checkpoint but also as a signaling receptor that can influence cell survival and plasticity, and it is increasingly recognized as a therapeutic target in oncology [68, 69]. In TNBC, CD47 is not only relevant to immune escape but is also associated with stem-like properties and poor prognosis, and blocking CD47 can suppress stem cell/EGF signaling in TNBC models[70, 71]. In this study, endothelial-THBS1 is delivered to TNBC cells via exosomes and induces the expression of the cell-surface receptor CD47 in recipient TNBC cells. Moreover, the interaction between THBS1 and CD47 (integrin-associated protein) is well documented [72]. It has been reported that THBS1 suppression can enhance the efficacy of checkpoint blockade by disrupting the THBS1-CD47 interaction, highlighting the complex, cell-type-specific actions of THBS1. In our experiments, TNBC cells receiving THBS1-rich exosomes exhibit increased CD47 expression and markedly reduced PI3K/Akt and MAPK/ERK activity, which in turn suppresses stemness marker expression, angiogenic ability, and VM capacity. The involvement of these pathways is particularly significant, as PI3K/Akt and MAPK signaling are frequently dysregulated in TNBC and contribute to VM, stemness, and drug resistance [18]. In contrast, exosomes from Axitinib-treated endothelial cells (with low THBS1) fail to induce CD47 and instead allow these pathways to drive stem-like phenotypes and angiogenesis. Knockdown of CD47 in TNBC cells abrogated the inhibitory effects of THBS1, confirming the significance of this axis. Consequently, disruption of this THBS1-CD47 paracrine circuit by Axitinib effectively enhances stemness and angiogenesis in recipient TNBC cells.

Clinically, these insights have important therapeutic implications. Firstly, the preservation or restoration of endothelial THBS1 signaling during anti-angiogenic therapy has the potential to mitigate the compensatory emergence of VM and stemness. In our xenograft experiments, hTHBS1 partially reversed Axitinib-induced VM, and combination therapy demonstrated superior anti-tumor efficacy than monotherapies. Secondly, therapies that stabilize the THBS1-CD47 signaling axis in tumor cells could be explored, although caution is warranted because CD47 blockade is itself being pursued clinically for immune activation, highlighting the complex, context-dependent biology of CD47 in tumors and immunity. Furthermore, the prognostic value of THBS1 expression in TNBC suggests its potential as a biomarker for stratifying patients likely to benefit from anti-angiogenic therapies.

Although this study provides significant insights into the role of the THBS1-CD47 axis in TNBC, several limitations merit consideration. Firstly, the xenograft models and exosome transfer models used may not fully recapitulate the complexity of the human TNBC microenvironment. Secondly, the precise molecular mechanisms by which Axitinib regulates THBS1 expression in endothelial cells remain unclear, requiring further exploration of epigenetic or post-translational modifications. Additionally, while we focused on CD47, other receptors, such as ITGA3 and SDC1, may also contribute to THBS1 signaling in a context-dependent manner. Future studies using patient-derived organoids and spatial transcriptomics could provide deeper insights into the cellular crosstalk governing THBS1-mediated effects. Integrating multi-omics data from longitudinal patient samples could refine predictive biomarkers for Axitinib response. In addition, a promising direction for future research involves investigating the therapeutic potential of ThBS1-mimicking peptides or CD47 inhibitors.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our work unveils a previously unrecognized mechanism of Axitinib resistance in TNBC, driven by suppression of endothelial-THBS1 and subsequent disruption of the THBS1-CD47 signaling axis. The promotion of stemness and VM by this adaptive response ultimately results in the limitation of the efficacy of Axitinib. By linking stemness, VM, and TME communication, our work positions THBS1 as a pivotal regulator of therapeutic resistance and emphasizes the necessity for combinatorial strategies targeting endothelial-tumor communication. These findings refine our understanding of anti-angiogenic therapy resistance and point to THBS1-CD47-centric interventions as promising avenues to improve the durability of responses in TNBC patients.
