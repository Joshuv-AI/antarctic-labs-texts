Near-infrared light–driven biomass conversion
Authors: Longfei Hong, Huiyan Zhang, Liangdong Hu, Rui Xiao, Sheng Chu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Current photocatalytic technologies mainly rely on the input of high-energy ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) light to obtain the desired excited states with adequate energy to drive redox reactions, precluding the use of low-energy near-infrared (NIR) light that occupies ~50% of the solar spectrum. Here, we report the efficient utilization of NIR light by coupling the low-energy NIR photons with reactive biomass conversion. A unique mechanism of photothermally synergistic photocatalysis was revealed for the selective biomass conversion under NIR light. Using biomass-derived 5-hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF) conversion as a model reaction, it was found that NIR and UV-vis light featured markedly different reaction patterns. 5-Formyl-2-furancarboxylic acid (FFCA) was almost exclusively produced under NIR light, whereas UV-vis light favored the formation of 2,5-diformylfuran (DFF) as the major product. This work provides a paradigm for sustainable and selective chemical synthesis using the Earth’s abundant resources, sunlight and biomass.

INTRODUCTION

The intermittency of sunlight limits its widespread application in global energy infrastructure (1–3). A potential solution is the use of semiconductor photocatalysis to convert intermittent solar energy into storable and transportable energy-rich chemicals (4–6). To accomplish this goal, the photocatalysts should satisfy the prerequisite requirement of band edges matching with the redox potentials of surface reactions. On the conventional photoreactions of overall water splitting and CO2 reduction, the bandgap of photocatalysts needs to be larger than 1.6 eV, on consideration of theoretical minimum potential, additional kinetic overpotentials, and other losses (7–10). Consequently, the commonly reported photocatalytic systems can only use the ultraviolet (UV) and visible (vis) light, while the low-energy near-infrared (NIR) light occupying ca. 50% of the solar radiation has been largely unexploited. Although strategies including upconversion (11, 12) and interband electron transitions (13–15) have been used to harness the NIR light, the efficiencies are usually low and the systems are complex, potentially limiting their practical applications. With regard to these issues, it is imperative to develop efficient and simple NIR-driven photocatalytic system to maximize the use of solar energy. Here, we report the efficient utilization of NIR light by coupling the low-energy NIR photons with reactive biomass conversion. As the largest renewable carbon resource with an annual production of 170 billion metric tons (16–18), biomass serves as an ideal substrate to couple with the underutilized NIR light. Compared to the conventional water oxidation, biomass oxidation is much more facile due to the markedly lower potential (19–22), which couples well with the low-energy NIR photons that are abundant in the solar spectrum (Fig. 1A). In addition, biomass conversion can upgrade biomass waste into higher-value chemicals instead of producing O2 by-product bearing negligible economic value. Notably, using biomass-derived 5-hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF) conversion as a model reaction, an excellent HMF conversion performance of 5.24 mmol g−1 hour−1 with a high HMF–to–5-formyl-2-furancarboxylic acid (FFCA) conversion selectivity over 95% were achieved under NIR light. This is inaccessible under conventional ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) light illumination, wherein HMF was converted into the intermediate product of 2,5-diformylfuran (DFF). A unique photothermally synergistic photocatalytic mechanism was revealed for the NIR-driven reaction (Fig. 1B). The strategy was demonstrated for the selective conversion of other biomass derivatives and outdoor natural sunlight as well.

RESULTS

Photocatalytic performance

HMF is a biomass-derived platform feedstock that can be converted into various industrially important molecules such as DFF and FFCA (Fig. 2A) (23–26). The photocatalytic tests of HMF conversion were carried out in a fixed-bed reaction chamber equipped with a quartz window under NIR light illumination (fig. S1). No product was detected without light or catalyst. A series of sulfide photocatalysts (Bi2S3, WS2, and MoS2) were screened first due to their appropriate bandgaps and excellent optoelectronic properties (27–29). The sulfide samples were fabricated through a hydrothermal process (see Methods for details). Among the samples, MoS2 displayed the highest activity and selectivity for HMF conversion to FFCA under NIR irradiation (fig. S2). Consequently, MoS2 was chosen for further study.

To improve the HMF conversion kinetics, nonprecious NiOOH was selected to load on MoS2 as a cocatalyst by a facile photodeposition method, because NiOOH has been widely reported as a superior biomass oxidation electrocatalyst (30–32). An impressive HMF conversion rate of 5.24 mmol g−1 hour−1 and high selectivity of 95.4% for FFCA production were achieved (Fig. 2B), which was much improved compared to unmodified MoS2 (2.51 mmol g−1 hour−1 and 67.2%, respectively). The NiOOH loading amount was optimized based on the HMF conversion performance and 0.93 wt % was found to be the optimal concentration (fig. S3). It is worth noting that the light illumination during the photodeposition process does not affect the physicochemical properties of MoS2 (fig. S4). The apparent quantum yield (AQY) was determined to be 1.28% with 850-nm monochromatic light, which was higher or comparable to the reported efficiencies for photocatalytic biomass conversion under UV-vis light (33, 34). Increasing the HMF concentration can enhance the HMF conversion rate. For instance, the reaction rate with 50 mM HMF was 2.3 times higher than that with 20 mM HMF (fig. S5).

Intriguingly, the product selectivity of HMF conversion strongly depended on the irradiation wavelength (Fig. 2C). The optical transmission properties of different wavelength filters (350, 550, and 850 nm) are shown in fig. S6. High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis of the resultant electrolyte after the reaction quantified two products: FFCA and DFF (Fig. 2D). Time course of HMF oxidation product indicated the conversion ratio reached ~40% after 10-hour reaction (fig. S7A), and the concentration of DFF was first increased to a maximum at 1 hour and then decreased (fig. S7B), implying DFF was the reaction intermediate to generate FFCA. 5-Hydroxymethyl-2-furan-carboxylic acid, a possible intermediate product from HMF to FFCA, was not detected. FFCA was almost exclusively produced under 850-nm wavelength irradiation (95.4% selectivity), while DFF was the major product under 350- and 550-nm irradiation (82.4 and 79.9% selectivity, respectively). The phenomenon of HMF-to-DFF conversion under UV-vis light is consistent with the previous reports, whereas HMF-to-FFCA conversion under NIR light has not been demonstrated (Fig. 2E and table S1). The unique behavior of DFF-to-FFCA conversion under NIR light is attributed to a synergetic photocatalytic-photothermal effect, which will be discussed in detail in the following section. Furthermore, the stability of NiOOH/MoS2 photocatalyst was evaluated. There was no activity loss in terms of FFCA yield and selectivity during the recycled tests of seven runs, highlighting the excellent stability of the catalyst (Fig. 2F). Characterization results also demonstrated the stable physicochemical properties of photocatalyst during the reaction (fig. S8).

Photocatalyst characterization

The NiOOH/MoS2 photocatalyst was characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) measurements (Fig. 3A and fig. S9). The sample exhibited a nanoflower morphology with an average diameter of approximately 1 to 2 μm. The high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) showed the lattice fringes with a spacing width of 0.61 nm (Fig. 3B), which were assigned to the (110) plane of cubic MoS2. The surrounding amorphous layers with 1 to 2 nm thickness were attributed to NiOOH. The energy-dispersive x-ray spectrometry mapping images of NiOOH/MoS2 indicated the uniform distribution of NiOOH on MoS2 (fig. S10). X-ray diffraction analysis displayed characteristic peaks corresponding to the hexagonal structure of MoS2 (JCPDS 37-1492) (35), as shown in fig. S11. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis confirmed the NiIII state from NiOOH (Fig. 3C and fig. S12). In Raman spectrum of NiOOH/MoS2 (fig. S13), the band at 477 and 552 cm−1 is associated with the (NiIII-O) and (NiIII-O), respectively (36).

UV-vis–NIR diffuse reflectance spectroscopy indicated that both MoS2 and NiOOH/MoS2 photocatalysts exhibited excellent optical absorption performance for full solar-spectrum harvesting (Fig. 3D). The loading of NiOOH did not observably affect the light absorption property. The bandgap of MoS2 was estimated to be 1.07 eV (fig. S14). The conduction band (CB) position of MoS2 was determined to be −0.37 V versus reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE) according to the Mott-Schottky test (fig. S15). Therefore, the valence band (VB) position of MoS2 photocatalyst was calculated to be 0.70 V versus RHE. The energy band positions were shown in Fig. 3E. Both CB and VB positions straddled the potentials of EO2/O2− (−0.33 V) and EHMF/DFF (−0.19 V), demonstrating its capability for HMF oxidation and oxygen reduction under NIR light irradiation.

The photocurrent density spectra, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) Nyquist plot, and time-resolved photoluminescence (TR-PL) were investigated to understand the photogenerated charge carrier transfer properties (Fig. 3, F to H). From the comparison of the data of MoS2 and NiOOH/MoS2, it was found that the loading of NiOOH could effectively accelerate the photogenerated carrier transport, reduce the electron-hole pair recombination rate, and thus provide more carriers for HMF conversion. Next, we investigate the mechanism of wavelength-dependent product selectivity of HMF conversion under UV-vis and NIR light irradiation.

The mechanism of wavelength-dependent product selectivity

The mechanism of wavelength-dependent product selectivity was investigated through combined experimental measurements and theoretical calculations. It was found that there was a photothermally synergistic photocatalytic effect of the NiOOH/MoS2 photocatalyst under NIR light irradiation, in contrast with the merely photocatalytic effect under UV-vis light. The NIR light irradiation induced localized heating on the NiOOH/MoS2 catalyst surface, converting light energy into thermal energy, thereby increasing the temperature of reaction system and accelerating the reaction rate. As depicted in Fig. 4 (A and B), the temperature of NiOOH/MoS2 increased from 25.5° to 46.6°C within 2 min and then reached a relative steady state under 850-nm irradiation, while the temperature of NiOOH/MoS2 only rose to ~29°C under 350- and 550-nm irradiation. There was an increase of 20°C in the steady state between the 850- and 350/550-nm irradiation, which was ascribed to the stronger photothermal effect under the 850-nm NIR light irradiation. A simplified simulation using COMSOL estimated a local temperature of ~50°C on the catalyst under 850-nm irradiation (Fig. 4B), which was consistent with the experimental observations.

Deep insight into the origin of the wavelength-dependent product selectivity was studied. On the basis of the Arrhenius equation k = Ae−Ea/RT, the apparent activation barrier (Ea) for FFCA synthesis under NIR irradiation (850 nm) was calculated to be 31.0 kJ mol−1, much lower than 41.8 kJ mol−1 under UV-vis irradiation (Fig. 4C). Compared to UV-vis irradiation, NIR irradiation had a synergetically photothermal-photocatalytic effect that boosted the reaction kinetics and lowered the activation barrier. In addition, the Ea was determined to be 57.3 kJ mol−1 in the dark under the thermocatalytic conditions from an external heat source, much larger than that under NIR irradiation. This demonstrated that the NIR irradiation coupled the photocatalytic and photothermal effects to reduce the reaction energy barrier for FFCA synthesis, leading to a superior catalytic activity that is inaccessible with the individual function. DFT calculations were performed to gain insights into the effect of different light irradiation on the reaction selectivity. The reaction energetics of the HMF-FFCA pathway under UV-vis (25°C) and NIR (50°C) irradiation were calculated and compared, as shown in Fig. 4D. Under UV-vis (25°C) irradiation, the reaction energy barrier for DFF-FFCA was 0.91 eV, which was notably higher than that for HMF-DFF (0.79 eV). Therefore, the second step of DFF-to-FFCA was the rate-limiting step in the whole process, rationalizing the DFF was the main product under UV-vis irradiation. In contrast, under NIR irradiation (50°C), the rate-limiting step switched to the first step of HMF-DFF. Both the reaction energy barriers for HMF-DFF and DFF-FFCA were decreased, while the energy barriers for the later one was decreased more pronounced by 0.38 eV. Our calculations agree well with the experimental findings of the wavelength-dependent product selectivity.

Moreover, the adsorption energies (Ead) of HMF, DFF, and FFCA on catalyst surface were also calculated (fig. S16). The negative Ead value indicates the exothermic process of reactant adsorption. DFF exhibited the lowest adsorption value, implying its easiest adsorption as an intermediate and facilitating the further conversion to FFCA. It was found that the NIR irradiation strongly promoted the desorption of FFCA product, favoring the generation of FFCA. A control experiment showed that the desorption amount of FFCA was much higher than those of HMF and DFF when heating the reactor system to 50°C (fig. S17).

To better understand the wavelength-dependent product selectivity, we have performed several reference experiments (fig. S18). First, we have carried out the photocatalytic HMF conversion under UV-vis light and NIR light (with applied cooling). First, we have carried out the photocatalytic HMF conversion under UV-vis light and NIR light (with applied cooling). The products were mainly DFF for both UV-vis and NIR light (with applied cooling), and the decrease of temperature substantially hindered the conversion of DFF to FFCA under NIR light, indicating the key role of photothermal effect on DFF to FFCA conversion process. In addition, we have added comparative experiments using DFF as the reaction substrate (dark at elevated temperature, NIR light with cooling). It was found that the DFF conversion rate under NIR (light + heat) far exceeded the net subtotal of single heat (dark + heating) and single light (NIR light + cooling), indicating a photothermally synergistic photocatalytic effect. On the basis of the above analysis, a tentative mechanism of NIR-driven selective FFCA production is proposed (Fig. 4E). On the one hand, NIR light irradiation promotes the activation of some reactants and reduces the activation energy barrier for FFCA synthesis via the synergistic photocatalytic-photothermal effect. On the other hand, the thermal energy provided by NIR light irradiation increases the local temperature, which promotes the desorption of FFCA from the catalyst surface and facilitates the generation of FFCA. As a result, NIR light induced a photo- and thermal-synergistic catalysis that not only efficiently enhance the charge carrier generation via a photocatalytic process but also have a photothermal effect to elevate the local temperature to further enhance the reaction kinetics.

Besides, the active species for HMF conversion were investigated by electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) measurements and control experiments with the addition of different scavenger reagents (fig. S19). The EPR spectra showed the presence of superoxide radical anion (O·−) and holes (h+) active species in the reaction system, while the signal of hydroxyl radical (·OH) was not detected (fig. S19, A to C). This is not unexpected considering the moderate photooxidation capability of MoS2 is insufficient for O2 oxidation into ·OH, which is beneficial for preventing the formation of overoxidation product such as CO2. In addition, with p-benzoquinone and triethanolamine added as O2·− and h+ scavengers, respectively, the HMF conversion was both strongly inhibited, indicating that O·− and h+ were the primary active species in the reaction (fig. S19D). The generation of reactive oxygen species under different light irradiation was also investigated, as shown in fig. S20. It was found that there was no obvious difference in reactive oxygen species in the reaction system under NIR and UV-vis light irradiation, implying that reactive oxygen species were not crucial factors in determining the product selectivity (fig. S20). Furthermore, HMF did not transform under N2 atmosphere, indicating that O2 was the oxidant for the selective photooxidation of HMF to FFCA. On the basis of the above results and the related knowledge in the literatures, we proposed the possible reaction pathways of HMF conversion to FFCA, as depicted in fig. S21. Under an aerobic condition, HMF was first transformed into DFF and lastly into FDCA through a similar successive oxygenation and dehydration process.

The generality of NIR light driven biomass conversion

To explore the potential application of NIR light driven system, the system was applied to the conversion of raw biomass, exemplified by using birch wood. Because of the structural robustness of raw biomass, an acid pretreatment was used to depolymerize (hemi)cellulose into soluble sugar fragments, with insoluble lignin component removed by filtration (Fig. 5A). Subsequently, the sugar solution was converted into HMF by a catalytic isomerization and dehydration process with a yield of 43.5%, along with the formation of fructose, levulinic acid, and formic acid as minor products (fig. S22). Using the biomass-derived HMF as the feedstock, the FFCA selectivity reached up to 82% under NIR light irradiation, indicating the versatility to transform real-world raw biomass waste into FFCA with high selectivity (Fig. 5B). However, the conversion rate of biomass-derived HMF (2.60 mmol g−1 hour−1) was lower compared to that of pure HMF substrate (5.24 mmol g−1 hour−1) under identical conditions. This is attributed to the presence of competitive glucose and fructose conversion on NiOOH/MoS2 catalyst, as revealed by control experiments with the introduction of pure additives (fig. S23).

Besides HMF, we also investigated the photocatalytic conversion performance of other biomass-derived compound, such as glucose (fig. S24). Under UV and visible light irradiation, glucose was mainly converted to gluconic acid with selectivity of 79.3 and 69.2%, respectively. In contrast, the main product was switched into glucaric acid (76.9%) under NIR light irradiation. Although quantitative differences exist between different systems, similar qualitative trend indicates the feasibility of using NIR light to tune the product selectivity in photocatalytic biomass transformation. The ideal system would harness solar radiation from the natural environment. Hence, we performed outdoor tests of NIR light driven photocatalytic HMF conversion experiments under Fresnel lens–focused sunlight (Fig. 5C). During experiments, the quartz window of the reactor overlapped with the focal point of the Fresnel lens, thereby concentrating sunlight and providing sufficient temperature for photocatalytic HMF conversion (fig. S25). As shown in Fig. 5D, the highest FFCA selectivity of 93.1% was achieved at noontime (12:00 p.m.). Lower FFCA selectivity was obtained in the morning (9:00 a.m. to 11:00 a.m.), which was associated with the lower reactor temperature under weak illumination intensity. During the 6-hour outdoor test, the system achieved an average AQY efficiency of 0.91%, comparable to the result of indoor laboratory experiments (1.28%). In addition, full-spectrum outdoor experiment was also performed. It was found that full-spectrum sunlight illumination substantially enhanced the yield of FFCA by a factor of 7.8 compared to that under NIR light due to the enhanced light intensity, and there was no obvious loss of FFCA selectivity (fig.S26). The outdoor tests demonstrate the feasibility of using natural sunlight to provide the thermal and photoenergy to synergistically catalytic power the selective biomass conversion.

DISCUSSION

In summary, we demonstrated the efficient utilization of low-energy NIR light by coupling with reactive biomass conversion. Using biomass-derived HMF conversion as a model reaction, an excellent HMF conversion performance (5.24 mmol g−1 hour−1) with a high FFCA product selectivity of 95.4% were achieved under NIR light using NiOOH/MoS2 photocatalyst, while UV-vis light favors the formation of DFF as the major product. Our mechanistic studies revealed that a unique photothermally synergistic photocatalytic mechanism was responsible for the selective FFCA generation under NIR light. NIR light irradiation not only reduced the activation energy barrier for FFCA synthesis but also promoted the desorption of FFCA product. In addition, NIR light–driven biomass conversion was demonstrated for different biomass derivatives and outdoor natural sunlight as well. The present work not only demonstrates that NIR light is a promising tool for biomass valorization but also offers an opportunity to control the reaction pattern and product selectivity in other important yet challenging organic transformations.

METHODS

Synthesis of photocatalysts

Nanoflower MoS2 was synthesized by a simple hydrothermal method. Typically, 0.179 g of Na2MoO4 and 0.163 g of CH4N2S were dissolved in 40 ml of deionized water and then transferred to a hydrothermal reactor with a volume of 50 ml and reacted at 220°C for 18 hours. The mixture was washed several times alternately with deionized water and ethanol and dried at 80°C to obtain pristine MoS2. Both Bi2S3 and WS2 were synthesized by hydrothermal method according to previous reports (37, 38).

NiOOH cocatalyst was loaded on MoS2 photocatalyst by a photodeposition method. A total of 100-mg Ni(NO3)2 and 200-mg NaIO3 were dissolved with 30 ml of deionized water, then 100-mg MoS2 was added and sonicated for 30 min. The photoreactor was subsequently vacuumed and irradiated for 40 min under a 300-W Xe lamp for NiOOH photodeposition.

Photocatalytic biomass conversion

Photocatalytic biomass conversion was conducted in a sealed reactor under oxygen atmosphere. Typically, 10 mg of photocatalyst was added into 50 ml of 20 mM HMF solution. After full dispersion with sonication, 1 M NaOH solution was added to adjust the solution pH to 12. A 300-W Xe lamp (Aulight Co. Ltd.) equipped with different bandpass filters (λ = 350, 550, and 850 nm) was used as the lighting source. A total of 100 μl of sample was taken from the reactor at given time intervals and analyzed by HPLC (Agilent 1260 Infinity II) to determine the concentrations of HMF and products. Shodex SUGAR SH1011 column was used for product separation. The mobile phase of the HPLC was 0.5 mM H2SO4 at a flow rate of 1.0 ml/min. In the cyclic experiment, photocatalyst was separated by filtration, washed with deionized water, dried at 80°C for 12 hours, and the next run was performed under the same experimental conditions.
