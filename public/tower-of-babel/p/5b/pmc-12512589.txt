Body mass index mediates the association between the dietary index for gut microbiota and kidney stones: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Jian Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough previous studies have evaluated the association between the Dietary Index for Gut Microbiota (DI-GM) and kidney stone formation, highlighting the potential role of diet-modulated gut microbiota in urolithiasis; however, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. In particular, the mediating role of body mass index (BMI) in the relationship between DI-GM and kidney stone risk has not yet been thoroughly investigated.MethodsThis cross-sectional study used data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) spanning 2007–2018. We applied weighted multivariable logistic regression models, restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis, and subgroup analyses to examine the relationship between DI-GM and kidney stones. Additionally, mediation analysis was conducted to evaluate the potential role of BMI.ResultsThe present study comprised a total of 29,988 participants, of whom 2,852 had a history of kidney stones. Fully adjusted model demonstrated an inverse association of DI-GM with kidney stones (odds ratio = 0.96, 95% CI: 0.93–0.99, P = 0.004). RCS analysis indicated a non-linear association (P for non-linearity < 0.001). Mediation analysis estimated that BMI accounted for 9.8% of the total effect of DI-GM on kidney stones risk.ConclusionWhile prior studies have demonstrated a link between DI-GM and kidney stones, our findings extend this knowledge by identifying BMI as a significant mediator of this relationship. These results provide new insights into the potential pathways linking diet, gut microbiota, body weight, and kidney stone risk. Further longitudinal studies are needed to confirm these findings and clarify causal relationships.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24292-w.

Introduction

Kidney stones, commonly known as nephrolithiasis, represent a prevalent urological disorder, affecting approximately 10% of the population in the United States (US) [1]. With shifts in diet and lifestyle, alongside the rising prevalence of obesity and metabolic syndrome, the incidence of kidney stones is anticipated to increase, potentially exacerbating public health challenges and healthcare expenditures [2]. In the US alone, the medical costs of kidney stones surpass $2 billion annually [3]. Despite advancements in medical technology and therapeutic approaches, the recurrence rate of kidney stones remains substantial, with nearly 50% of patients experiencing recurrence within 5 to 10 years [4]. Given the high recurrence rates and the considerable financial burden of this condition, implementing effective preventive strategies is of paramount importance.

The gut microbiota, consisting of over 100 trillion microorganisms, forms a dynamic ecosystem that is critical for host health, contributing to intestinal barrier integrity, immune regulation, and metabolic homeostasis [5, 6]. Several studies have implicated gut microbiota in kidney stone formation [7–11]. Notably, the composition and function of the gut microbiome are significantly influenced by dietary patterns [12]. To evaluate the dietary impacts on gut microbiota composition, the Dietary Index for Gut Microbiota (DI-GM) was recently developed as a scoring tool. It derived from a systematic review of dietary components influencing gut health, has been validated through its association with markers of gut microbiota diversity, such as urinary enterodiol and enterolactone levels [13]. DI-GM offers a novel approach for exploring diet-gut health relationships and holds promise for investigating microbiota-related diseases. The DI-GM has since been used to investigate microbiota-related disease outcomes, including depression [14]. More recently, two large cross-sectional studies utilizing NHANES 2007–2018 reported inverse associations between DI-GM scores and kidney stone prevalence, highlighting the potential of gut-friendly dietary patterns in stone prevention [15, 16]. However, these studies did not investigate underlying mechanisms, such as the role of obesity, a major modifiable risk factor for nephrolithiasis.

Obesity, characterized by the excessive adiposity (body mass index (BMI) ≥ 30 kg/m²), affects 42.4% of US adults and is projected to rise globally, reaching 14% of men and 20% of women by 2030 [17, 18]. This metabolic disorder is a well-established risk factor for kidney stones [19]. Emerging evidence also highlights the critical role of gut microbiota in the onset and progression of obesity [20]. Given the interconnected roles of diet, gut microbiota, and adiposity in kidney stone pathogenesis, it is plausible that BMI may mediate the relationship between DI-GM and kidney stones.

To build upon and extend existing findings, we conducted a cross-sectional analysis of NHANES 2007–2018 data with two primary objectives: (1) to confirm the association between DI-GM scores and the prevalence of kidney stones in a large, nationally representative sample of US adults, and (2) to evaluate the potential mediating role of BMI in this relationship.

Methods

Study population

This cross-sectional study utilized publicly accessible data from the NHANES 2007–2018. NHANES is a series of multi-stage, complex surveys conducted by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), designed to gather health and nutritional data from non-institutionalized US population. The NHANES research protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics, and all participants gave written informed consent.

In our study, we analyzed data from adults aged 18 years and older, collected during the 2007–2018 cycle. After excluding participants with missing data on kidney stones (N = 25,613), those with missing data on the components of DI-GM (N = 3,966), and individuals with extreme energy intake (< 500 or > 6000 kcal/day) (N = 411), we were left with 30,312 participants. Further exclusions for missing BMI data (N = 314) resulted in a final sample of 29,988 participants, of whom 2,852 had a history of kidney stones, and 27,136 did not (Fig. 1).

Assessment of DI-GM

According to research by Bezawit E. Kase et al., 14 specific foods and nutrients were identified as either beneficial or unfavorable to gut microbiota health, were selected to contribute to the DI-GM [13]. The beneficial components included fermented dairy, chickpeas, soybeans, whole grains, fiber, cranberries, avocados, broccoli, coffee, and green tea, while the unfavorable components comprised red meat, processed meat, refined grains, and high-fat diets (≥ 40% of energy from fat). However, green tea was excluded from the final DI-GM score due to NHANES not recording the specific type of tea consumed. Each component was assigned a score of 1 if a participant’s intake was above the sex-specific median for beneficial foods or below the median for unfavorable foods. Conversely, a score of 0 was given if the intake pattern was the opposite. The individual scores for each component, classified as either beneficial or unfavorable to gut microbiota health, were summed to calculate separate dietary index scores for each category. These two scores were then combined to obtain the DI-GM scores, which ranged from 0 to 13. Participants were further divided into four groups based on the DI-GM scores: 0–3, 4, 5, and ≥ 6. In this study, we used the first 24 h dietary recall data to estimate the DI-GM score.

Assessment of kidney stones

The history of kidney stones was assessed by a questionnaire, “Have you ever been diagnosed with kidney stones?” Those who responded affirmatively to this question were classified as having a history of kidney stones.

Assessment of mediator variable

In this study, BMI was treated as a mediator variable. It was calculated by dividing a participant’s weight in kilograms by the square of their height in meters.

Covariates

Our study adjusted for a range of covariates, encompassing demographic, socioeconomic, behavioral, and health-related factors. Demographic variables included age, sex, and race/ethnicity, which was categorized as Mexican American, Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, or other ethnicities, along with educational attainment. Socioeconomic status was evaluated using the poverty-to-income ratio (PIR), divided into three categories: <1.3, 1.3–3.5, and > 3.5. Behavioral factors considered included smoking status (Never: lifetime consumption of < 100 cigarettes; Former: ≥100 cigarettes consumed but abstinent at time of assessment; Now: ≥100 cigarettes consumed with ongoing tobacco use, either occasional or daily), alcohol use, and physical activity levels. Physical activity was measured using metabolic equivalent (MET) scores, calculated as MET-min/week = activity days × session duration × MET intensity, and categorized into low (< 600 MET-min/week), moderate (600-3,000), or high (> 3,000) [21]. Health conditions assessed included hypertension (defined by physician diagnosis, antihypertensive medication use, systolic blood pressure (BP) ≥ 130 mmHg, or diastolic BP ≥ 80 mmHg), diabetes (diagnosed clinically, through hypoglycemic medication use, hemoglobin A1c level ≥ 6.5%, or fasting glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL), hyperlipidemia (defined by total cholesterol ≥ 5.0 mmol/L, LDL ≥ 3.0 mmol/L, triglycerides ≥ 1.7 mmol/L, or use of lipid-lowering drugs), and chronic kidney disease (CKD), identified based on an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 60 mL/min/1.73 m², calculated using the CKD-EPI equation [22].

Statistical analysis

In accordance with the NHANES analytical guidelines, we constructed a 12-year combined weight by merging data from six consecutive survey cycles. Missing values were addressed using the random forest imputation method [23]. Baseline characteristics were summarized as weighted means with standard errors (SE) for continuous variables and as numbers with weighted percentages for categorical variables. To examine the relationships between DI-GM (and its individual components) and kidney stones, multivariate weighted logistic regression models were employed. Additionally, restricted cubic spline (RCS) model was used to model the dose-response relationship between DI-GM and kidney stones. Mediation analysis was conducted to assess the potential mediating effect of BMI. Stratified analyses were performed based on key demographic and clinical factors, including sex, age, race/ethnicity, PIR, education level, smoking status, drinking status, physical activity levels, and the presence of hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, and CKD. Furthermore, sensitivity analysis was performed by removing missing values and analyzing the complete dataset to validate the stability and reliability of the results. All statistical analyses were carried out using R software (version 4.1.3), with specific analyses conducted using the missForest package for missing data imputation, the survey package for complex sampling design, and the mediation package for mediation effect estimation. A two-tailed P-value threshold of less than 0.05 was applied to determine statistical significance.

Further analysis revealed that the dietary index beneficial to gut microbiota was also significantly associated with a lower risk of kidney stones after adjusting confounding factors (OR = 0.94, 95% CI: 0.90–0.98, P = 0.009). Conversely, the dietary index unfavorable to gut microbiota did not show a significant association with kidney stone risk in any of the models (P > 0.05).

When analyzing DI-GM score categories, participants with a score of 6 or higher had a significantly lower risk of kidney stones (OR: 0.79, 95% CI: 0.68–0.92, P = 0.002) compared to those with a score of 0–3.

Sensitivity analyses

The subgroup analyses results are presented in Table S1. A significant interaction effect was observed between DI-GM and kidney stones in sex (P for interaction = 0.046). Specifically, the relationship between DI-GM and kidney stones was significant in females (OR: 0.94, 95% CI: 0.90–0.98, P = 0.005) but not in males (OR: 0.99, 95% CI: 0.95–1.03, P = 0.558).

An additional sensitivity analysis was conducted using complete data (Table S2). The results revealed that the association between DI-GM and kidney stones remained significant after adjusting confounding factors (OR: 0.95, 95% CI: 0.92–0.99, P = 0.010).

Discussion

In this large cross-sectional study based on NHANES data, we identified a significant inverse and non-linear association between DI-GM and the prevalence of kidney stones in the general US adult population. This relationship remained robust across sensitivity analysis and was particularly pronounced in females. Moreover, mediation analysis revealed that BMI partially mediated the association between DI-GM and kidney stones, accounting for 9.8% of the observed effect. These findings support the emerging hypothesis that gut microbiota related dietary patterns may influence kidney stone formation and underscore BMI as a potential mediator in this pathway.

Calcium oxalate (CaOx) stone is the predominant type of kidney stones, making up approximately 76% of all cases [24]. Urinary oxalate is a significant risk factor for the development of CaOx stone, with the gut playing a vital role in regulating oxalate balance and maintaining homeostasis [25]. Previous studies have primarily focused on Oxalobacter formigenes, a bacterium that degrades oxalate by using it as its sole energy source [26]. However, other oxalate-degrading bacteria, such as Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus, also contribute to clearing oxalate from the gut, thereby lowering plasma and urinary oxalate levels and reducing the risk of stone formation [27]. However, recent evidence suggests that supplementation with Oxalobacter formigenes may not effectively reduce urinary oxalate levels or prevent stone formation [28–30]. A meta-analysis involving eight studies revealed that patients with kidney stones exhibit characteristic gut microbiota dysbiosis [31]. This dysbiosis is characterized by increased abundances of Bacteroides, Escherichia, and Shigella, along with a decreased abundance of Prevotella [31]. Additionally, compared to the control group, the gut microbiota of stone patients showed altered metabolic functions, including disruptions in lipid, carbohydrate, and energy metabolism, as well as glycan synthesis and amino acid biosynthesis [31]. These findings underscore the complex interaction between gut microbiota and kidney stone formation. Therefore, maintaining gut microbiota balance may be important for the prevention of kidney stones.

Given the emerging role of gut microbiota in kidney stone pathogenesis, diet becomes a particularly important modifiable factor. A previous population-based metagenomics analysis found that individual difference in gut microbiome composition could be influenced by various factors, including lifestyle, diet, diseases, and medications, with diet being the most critical factor [32]. In recent years, researchers have increasingly emphasized the role of overall dietary patterns rather than isolated food items in chronic disease development. Compared to focusing on individual nutrients, dietary pattern analysis better reflects the synergistic or antagonistic effects of multiple food components. Accordingly, dietary pattern indexes such as the Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII) and Healthy Eating Index (HEI) have been widely used to evaluate dietary quality from the perspectives of inflammation and adherence to dietary guidelines, respectively, and these indices have been reported to be linked to kidney stones [33, 34]. However, they do not capture the influence of diet on the gut microbiome, which is increasingly recognized a crucial mediator linking dietary factors to metabolic health outcomes.

To address this gap, a comprehensive analysis of 106 studies identified 14 foods or nutrients associated with the gut microbiota and developed a new dietary index, DI-GM, aimed at assessing the impact of diet on the gut microbiota health [13]. In the present study, we applied the DI-GM to investigate its association with kidney stones risk. Our results demonstrated that higher DI-GM scores were significantly associated with a lower prevalence of kidney stones. Specifically, four components, including chickpeas, coffee, fiber and whole grains, were significantly negatively correlated with stone risk. These components demonstrated their potential to prevent stone formation through beneficial regulation of the gut microbiota. The study indicated that raffinose in chickpeas can selectively promote the growth of Bifidobacterium, which plays an important role in maintaining gut health and promoting oxalate degradation [27, 35]. Similarly, polyphenols in coffee, particularly chlorogenic acid, also stimulate Bifidobacterium growth [36]. Additionally, the intake of total grains can enhance the gut microbiota’s utilization of whole grain components (especially dietary fiber), thereby promoting the production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) [37]. SCFAs could help maintain gut health, regulate the immune system, and inhibit inflammation [38], which may play a protective role in kidney stone prevention. While these mechanisms are biologically plausible, our study did not directly assess microbial composition or related metabolic markers, and therefore, these hypotheses warrant further validation in future mechanistic or interventional studies.

Our findings are consistent with two recent NHANES-based studies. Yan et al. reported a similar inverse association between DI-GM and kidney stone prevalence using NHANES 2007–2018 data [16], while Wang et al. identified diabetes as a potential mediator in this relationship [15]. Unlike these two studies, which excluded individuals with missing covariate data, we used random forest imputation to handle missing values, allowing us to retain the largest possible sample size. We also excluded individuals with implausible energy intake to reduce potential bias. In addition to these methodological improvements, we applied RCS models to explore nonlinear associations and examined the individual contributions of each DI-GM food component, thereby providing more detailed insights into the dietary factors associated with stone risk. Overall, our study offers robust evidence of an inverse association between DI-GM and kidney stone risk, suggesting that encouraging the consumption of gut-friendly foods such as fiber and coffee may be an accessible and cost-effective strategy to support microbial health and mitigate kidney stone formation.

Beyond the overall association, we observed a significant sex difference in the relationship between DI-GM and kidney stones (P for interaction < 0.05), with the protective effect of DI-GM evident only in females. This sex-specific association may be explained by differences in gut microbiota composition and dietary habits between males and females. A previous population-based cohort study involving 1135 individuals reported significant differences in the overall gut microbiome composition between sexes, with females exhibiting greater microbial diversity [39]. Moreover, females tend to prioritize a healthy diet than males, favoring vegetables over meat [40, 41]. These dietary patterns may result in higher DI-GM scores among females, thereby potentially reducing the risk of kidney stone formation in this group.

Importantly, our mediation analysis revealed that BMI partially mediated the relationship between DI-GM and kidney stones, suggesting that gut-friendly diets may reduce stone risk partly through improved weight management. Obesity is a well-recognized risk factor for kidney stone formation, driven by systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and alterations in urinary composition, such as increased excretion of uric acid, calcium, and oxalate, along with reduced citrate levels and urinary pH, all of which create a pro-lithogenic environment [42–44]. Recent research emphasizes the critical role of maintaining a healthy weight during adulthood in reducing the risk of developing kidney stones [45]. Emerging evidence suggests that the impact of dietary patterns on the gut microbiota may be an effective strategy for weight management and metabolic health [46]. For example, the Mediterranean diet, rich in fruits, vegetables, and unsaturated fats, has demonstrated the ability to modulate gut microbiota composition, enhancing metabolic outcomes and reducing inflammation [46]. In contrast, Western diets, typically low in fiber and high in sugars and saturated fats, can disrupt gut microbiota balance and promote obesity and related metabolic disorders [46]. Considering these findings, dietary patterns that support a healthy gut microbiota may offer a promising strategy for managing obesity and preventing its related complications, such as kidney stones.

To our knowledge, this is among the first studies to utilize a large-scale public database to investigate the association between the DI-GM and kidney stones, and critically, we employed mediation analysis to assess whether BMI mediates this relationship, a novel aspect not extensively explored in previous NHANES-based studies on DI-GM and kidney stones. However, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, like other studies utilizing NHANES data, the cross-sectional design restricts our ability to determine causal relationship between DI-GM and kidney stones. Second, the complete DI-GM included 14 dietary components, as the NHANES dietary questionnaire did not include data on green tea consumption; thus, it could not be considered. Third, both dietary information and the history of kidney stones were self-reported, which introduces the possibility of recall bias and may lead to an underestimation of asymptomatic kidney stones. Furthermore, the composition of kidney stones was not reported, which limited our ability to assess associations between DI-GM and specific stone types (such as calcium oxalate, calcium phosphate, and uric acid). Finally, although we adjusted for demographic, lifestyle and comorbidity related factors, the potential influence of unmeasured confounders cannot be ruled out. Therefore, future prospective cohort studies with large sample sizes are needed to validate our findings.

Conclusion

Our study contributes to the growing body of evidence linking diet, gut microbiota, and kidney stone risk by demonstrating that higher DI-GM scores are associated with a lower prevalence of kidney stones and identifying BMI as a significant mediator in this relationship. Future large-scale prospective studies are needed to validate these findings and investigate the underlying mechanisms.
