Nanopuerarin Improves Doxorubicin Therapeutic Efficacy by Inhibiting Angiogenesis via Regulating NSUN2 in Hepatocellular Carcinoma
Authors: Na Feng, Junjie Chang, Honglei Yu, Tongfei Li, Lin Lei, Xiaomin Huang, Dandan Shi, Rui Liu, Wenjun Zhang, Xiju He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains a leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide. Doxorubicin (Dox), a conventional chemotherapeutic agent, exhibits unsatisfactory efficacy in HCC due to its poor tumor response, severe cardiotoxicity, and drug resistance. It is urgent to develop strategies to mitigate the side effects and enhance the chemosensitivity of conventional chemotherapy drugs. Traditional Chinese medicine has gained research interest in cancer treatment due to its homology with food and minimal side effects. Puerarin (Pue) has been identified as an effective inhibitor of tumor cell proliferation. However, puerarin’s poor water solubility and bioavailability limit its pharmaceutical application. In this study, a novel puerarin nanoparticle (nanoPue) was developed to overcome its limitation. NanoPue could significantly inhibit tumor growth and enhance the chemotherapeutic effect of Dox in HCC models. NanoPue could increase the perivascular coverage within the tumors; decrease the expressions of NSUN2, p-AKT, VEGFA, and MMP-9; and reduce endothelial cell tube formation. In summary, nanoPue improves Dox therapeutic efficacy by decreasing angiogenic factors via regulating NSUN2 in HCC. This study may provide a potential avenue for advancing nanoPue to enhance chemotherapeutic agents in HCC therapy and supply a theoretical support for the clinical application of nanoPue.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) accounts for approximately 90% of all primary liver cancers and is the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide. While considerable progress has been made in HCC treatment, the 5-year overall survival (OS) of patients remained at about 30%. Radiotherapy and chemotherapy remain common clinical therapies for HCC. Doxorubicin (Dox) is a classic chemotherapy drug for liver cancer. However, its dose-dependent irreversible cardiotoxicity poses a major obstacle to clinical use. − Therefore, reducing the side effects and enhancing the Dox sensitivity are urgent therapeutic goal. Studies showed that the natural products combined with Dox could mitigate its side effects and enhance its chemosensitivity. − Low-toxicity natural products have increasingly been used as adjuvant therapies to alleviate cancer symptoms in the recent decades. ,

Puerarin (Pue), an isoflavone derivative from Gegen, can protect cardiovascular and cerebrovascular systems, alleviate hypertensive myocardial remodeling, and exert anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects. − We had previously confirmed that puerarin can ameliorate myocardial remodeling and biomechanical properties of carotid artery in spontaneously hypertensive rats. , A recent study demonstrated that puerarin could reverse platinum-based drug resistance and potentiate oxaliplatin (OXA) efficacy in breast cancer. Nevertheless, puerarin’s poor water solubility and bioavailability limit its application in pharmaceutical application.

In this study, a novel puerarin nanopreparation was prepared to improve its pharmacokinetic profile and tumor-specific accumulation. A current study has demonstrated that puerarin nanoemulsion remodels the tumor interstitial microenvironment by reducing collagen deposition and interstitial fluid pressure, thereby enhancing intratumoral drug penetration and chemotherapeutic efficacy in murine breast cancer. In recent years, another research on metal ion–cell interactions reveals that numerous metal ions can induce tumor cell death. Aluminum (Al3+) can significantly inhibit overexpressed matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) in tumor cell. Based on this knowledge and puerarin’s chemical structure, the phenolic hydroxyl group (PhOH) of puerarin was coordinated with Al3+ to form self-assembled puerarin nanoparticles (nanoPue).

In this study, aluminum nanopuerarin (nanoPue) particles were prepared. We evaluated the effects of nanoPue combined with Dox using cell-based assays and xenograft models in nude mice. Our data demonstrated that nanoPue could enhance Dox sensitivity to HCC. These findings suggest that nanoPue can serve as an adjuvant therapy to enhance chemosensitivity in HCC.

Materials and Methods

Cell Lines and Main Reagents

Human hepatocellular carcinoma cell line (HepG2) was provided by the Chinese Academy of Sciences (TCHu 72). Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were obtained from the ATCC (PCS-100-013). HepG2 cells were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S, Gibco, USA). HUVECs were cultured in ECM medium (ScienCell, USA) supplemented with 5% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S), and 1% endothelial cell growth supplement (ECGS, ScienCell, USA). All cells were maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator.

Puerarin standard was purchased from Beijing Spectrum Technology Co., Ltd. (R30646, Beijing, China). Doxorubicin was purchased from Aladdin Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (D107159, Shanghai, China). ELISA Assay Kit was purchased from Neobioscience (EMC103.48, Shanghai, China). TUNEL Apoptosis Assay Kit (C1086), Masson’s Trichrome Staining Kit (C0189S), RIPA lysis buffer (P0013B), SDS-PAGE Sample Loading Buffer (5×) (P0015L), and PMSF (ST505) were obtained from Beyotime Biotechnology. Al (NO3)3·9H2O was purchased from Aladdin Biotechnology Co. Ltd. (S492260, Shanghai, China). NSUN2 antibody (20854-1-AP), Ki67 antibody (27309-1-AP), VEGFA antibody (26157-1-AP), AKT (10176-2-AP), and p-AKT (66444-1-Ig) antibody were purchased from Proteintech (Wuhan, China). MMP-9 antibody (10375-2-AP) was purchased from Immunoway (USA).

Preparation and Characterization of NanoPue

Al (NO3)3·9H2O (37.5 mg, 0.1 mM) and puerarin (41.6 mg, 0.1 mM) were dissolved in 5 mL of methanol. After being treated at 120 °C for 24 h, the brown powder was centrifuged at 10,500 rpm for 15 min and washed five times sequentially with ethanol. NanoPue morphology and size were characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Hitachi S4800+ EDS) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Tecnai 20, FEI, Hillsboro, OR, USA). The puerarin concentration in nanoPue was quantified by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC, Agilent LC1100) at the wavelength of 250 nm. The hydrated particle size and zeta potential were determined by the dynamic light scattering (DLS). The dispersibility was assessed by direct observation after 7 days of storage.

Cell Cytotoxicity Assays

Cell proliferation was monitored with the Real-time Unlabeled Cell Analyzer (RTCA, xCELLigence RTCADP) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, HepG2 cells (3 × 103 cells/well) were seeded in E-Plate 96-well plates. After 24 h of adhesion, cells were treated with Pue or nanoPue (The concentration of nanoPue refers to that of the puerarin component). The xCELLigence program recorded cell impedance every 15 min for 100 h, and viability was analyzed by the system. HepG2 cell viability was also assessed with a CCK-8 kit (Beyotime) following the manufacturer’s protocol.

Flow Cytometry Analysis

HepG2 cells were seeded in 6-well plates for 24 h, 4 groups as follows: control, NP (10 μM nanoPue), D (1 μM Dox), and DNP (10 μM nanoPue and 1 μM Dox). Subsequently, cells were collected by centrifugation, resuspended in binding buffer to a concentration of 1–5 × 106 cells/mL. The experiment was performed following the Annexin V-FITC apoptosis detection kit instructions (Beyotime) and using a Cytoflex flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter).

Animals and Xenografted Tumor Model

Twenty-four BALB/C nude mice (4–6 weeks old, 18–20 g) were purchased from Charles River Company. Animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Hubei University of Medicine (approval No.SYXK2019-31). Mice were inoculated subcutaneously at the right flank with 1 × 107 HepG2 cells. When the tumors reached approximately 100 mm3 (V = length × width2/2), mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 5/group): control (saline, ip, eod), NP (nanoPue, 15 mg/kg, ip, eod), D (Dox, 3 mg/kg, iv, eod), DNP (Dox + nanoPue, with a 2 h interval between Dox and nanoPue administration, iv/ip, respectively, eod). Body weight was recorded every other day, and tumor size was measured with Vernier calipers. Mice were sacrificed after 10 days, and samples from tumor tissues were collected for immunohistochemistry, immunofluorescence, Western blot, and other analyses.

TUNEL Staining

The tumor tissues of each group were separated, dehydrated, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned. For TUNEL staining, slides were dewaxed with xylene and gradient of ethanol solutions, and subsequent processing was performed according to the protocol provided by Beyotime for their TUNEL Apoptosis Assay Kit.

Immunofluorescence, HE, and Masson Staining

The tumor tissues of each group were separated and dehydrated, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned. Slides were dewaxed with xylene and a gradient of ethanol solutions, permeabilized with 1% Triton X-100, and blocked with 5% goat serum. The primary antibodies were incubated with slides overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with fluorescent secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were stained with DAPI. Slides were observed by using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, IX73). Sections were also stained with Hematoxylin–Eosin staining (HE) and Masson’s Trichrome staining and observed under a microscope (Olympus, BX53F2C).

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

Blood samples were collected from mice into citrate anticoagulant tubes and centrifuged, and the supernatant was collected. VEGF concentration in mouse blood serum was measured using an ELISA kit (Neobioscience) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Expression levels were calculated from the standard curve and expressed as ng/mL.

Cell Transfection

HUVECs in logarithmic growth phase were plated in six-well plates. At 40–50% confluency, cells were transfected with 2.5 μg of shNC or shNSUN2 plasmid mixture using Lipofectamine 3000 (ThermoFisher, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After 48 h, cells were collected and total protein was extracted. The shNSUN2 sequences were shRNA-1 GGAAGAAATGGACTACCTT and shRNA-2 CTGAAGTACGAACCAGATT.

Tube Formation Assay

HUVECs treated with different drugs were cultured for 24 h with conditioned medium. Then, 2 × 104 cells/well were plated in 96-well plates coated with 50 μL of Matrigel and incubated for 6 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The tube formation was imaged by microscopy (Olympus, Afghanistan) and analyzed using ImageJ.

Western Blot Analysis

Total protein from cells and tumor tissues was extracted using RIPA Lysis Buffer supplemented with PMSF (RIPA/PMSF = 100:1). Protein concentration was quantified using a BCA Protein Assay (Beyotime). Samples were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk for 1 h at room temperature, incubated with specific primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, and then incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Immunoreactive bands were visualized using an ECL chemiluminescence kit (Beyotime).

Statistical Analysis

A statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad prism8.0. The results were expressed as the mean ± SD. The analysis of variance was completed using a one-way ANOVA. A p value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Synthesis and Characterization of NP

The chemical structure of Pue is shown in Figure F. As described in the Materials and Methods, the phenolic hydroxyl group of Pue coordinated with AL3+ to form self-assembled nanopuerarin (nanoPue). HPLC analysis showed a concentration of 2.5 mg/mL for nanopuerarin, including 1.5 mg/mL of puerarin (Figure S1). The nanoPue dispersion appeared light-brown, with predominantly homogeneous nanoparticles (Figure A). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images confirmed the spherical shape and uniform size of nanoPue (Figure B,C). DLS analysis of nanoPue indicated a hydrodynamic diameter of 443 ± 85 nm and a positive zeta potential (Figure D,E).

(D) Hydrated particle size of nanoPue (443 ± 85) measured by DLS. (E) Zeta potential of nanoPue measured by DLS. (F) Chemical structure of puerarin (PubChem CID: 5281807).

NanoPue Exhibited Superior Antitumor Effects in HepG2 Cells

HepG2 cells treated with different doses of Pue and nanoPue were detected by RTCA. Results showed that both Pue and nanoPue could reduce HepG2 cell viability in a dose-dependent manner (Figure A,B), but nanoPue exhibited higher cytotoxicity at lower doses. The effect of nanoPue and Dox on HepG2 cell activity was determined by a CCK-8 assay (Figure C,D). Cell activity decreased gradually with increasing concentration; the IC50 of Dox was calculated to be 1 μM, and that of nanoPue was 10 μM. Based on the IC50 concentration of nanoPue, we evaluated the effects of free AL3+ on cell viability. As shown in Figure E, free AL3+ (0–10 μM) within the nanoPue composite system showed no significant cytotoxicity. Synergy between nanoPue and Dox was analyzed using CompuSyn software (Figure F,G), indicating a strong synergistic effect (Combination Index, CI <1). Flow cytometry confirmed that nanoPue significantly sensitized HepG2 cells to Dox-induced apoptosis (Figure H,I).

(E) Effect of different AL3+ concentrations on cell viability (CCK-8). *P < 0.05 vs Ctrl. (F) Cells were treated with nanoPue or Dox. (G) Fa-CI plot of different nanoPue: Dox molar ratios. NanoPue Enhanced the Sensitivity of Dox

To verify the enhancement effect of nanoPue on Dox against HCC, we used HepG2 xenograft tumor-bearing models. Compared to the control group, both nanoPue and Dox alone reduced tumor size (Figure A). The DNP group exhibited significantly smaller tumor size and weight compared with the Dox or nanoPue groups alone (Figure A,C). No significant body weight loss (Figure B) or treatment-related deaths were observed. These results indicated that nanoPue combined with Dox had a better tumor-inhibiting effect compared to either drug alone. Furthermore, HE staining confirmed that nanoPue combined with Dox promoted more effective tumor cell death (Figure D). To evaluate nanoPue toxicity, H&E staining of heart, liver, and kidney organs was performed, no inflammation or necrosis was found in mice treated with nanoPue, or the combination (Figure E).

TUNEL assay demonstrated a higher proportion of apoptotic tumor cells in the DNP group and revealed that nanoPue enhanced tumor cell sensitivity to Dox (Figure F). Ki67 results showed that nanoPue combined with Dox could inhibit tumor cell proliferation effectively (Figure G). Therefore, nanoPue effectively inhibited tumor growth in vivo and enhanced Dox sensitivity.

NanoPue Inhibited Angiogenesis and Increasd Pericellular Coverage in Tumors

Angiogenesis is crucial for tumor proliferation and metastasis. To explore whether nanoPue’s inhibitory effect was related to angiogenesis, Masson staining was performed. Collagen is a major component of the extracellular matrix (ECM) and an indicator of stromal desmoplasia. Masson staining showed that collagen deposition (blue) decreased significantly in the DNP group (Figure A), implying nanoPue combined with Dox could alleviate desmoplasia by reducing collagen deposition.

NanoPue inhibited tumor angiogenesis. (A) Masson’s Trichrome staining. Blue: collagen, red: cytoplasm. Scale bar: 100 μm. (B) Representative immunofluorescence images of CD31 (green) and α-SMA (red). Scale bar: 50 μm. (C) Representative images of HUVEC tube formation. Scale bar: 200 μm. *P < 0.05 vs Ctrl, # P < 0.05 vs D group.

Tumor vasculature is imperative for hematogenous metastasis. CD31 demonstrates endothelial cells and assesses tumor angiogenesis. α-SMA expression reflects perivascular coverage, indicating vascular maturity and stability. Immunofluorescence for CD31 and α-SMA in tumors showed that compared to the control group, CD31 (green) was significantly weaker in the DNP group, indicating nanoPue inhibited angiogenesis and reduced microvessel density and branching when combined with Dox (Figure B). Additionally, the level of α-SMA (red) increased significantly, suggesting vascular normalization. These results indicated that nanoPue enhanced the Dox sensitivity by inhibiting angiogenesis and increasing perivascular coverage to promote vascular normalization. To further explore the mechanism of nanoPue, a tube formation assay was performed in HUVECs. The result showed that the DNP group exhibited incomplete tube-like vascular structures and significantly reduced formation extent, directly confirming nanoPue’s inhibitory effect on tumor angiogenesis (Figure C).

NanoPue Decreased the Expressions of NSUN2, p-AKT, VEGFA, and MMP-9

NSUN2, an RNA methyltransferase highly expressed in tumor cells, is upregulated in tumorigenesis and development, representing a novel therapeutic target. Recent studies found NSUN2 methylates vascular endothelial cells and regulates angiogenesis. , Given the enhanced anticancer effect of nanoPue combined with Dox in HCC, we explored its effect on NSUN2. Immunofluorescence and Western blotting revealed significantly decreased NSUN2 expression in the DNP group (Figure A,B). The expression of several tumor angiogenic factors was explored in cancer tissues. As NSUN2 was downregulated by nanoPue, the expression of p-AKT, VEGFA, and MMP-9 decreased (Figures C, , and A,B), similar results were observed in HUVECs (Figure C). Furthermore, nanoPue combined with Dox significantly decreased serum VEGF levels in the xenograft (Figure D).

NanoPue Decreased p-AKT, VEGFA, and MMP-9 Expressions by Downregulating NSUN2 in HUVECs

To further verify nanoPue’s antitumor effect on NSUN2, we knocked down NSUN2 in HUVECs (Figure A). When NSUN2 was knocked down, the inhibitory effect of nanoPue on p-AKT, VEGFA, and MMP-9 expression was attenuated (Figure B), and its antiangiogenic effect in tube formation assays was diminished (Figure C). These results confirmed that nanoPue exerted its effects primarily through downregulating NSUN2.

Discussion

Puerarin is widely used in treating cardiovascular diseases. Recent studies demonstrated its anticancer mechanisms against multiple cancer types. , In this study, puerarin was complexed with aluminum to form nanopuerarin particles (nanoPue), improving its solubility and bioavailability. NanoPue combined with Dox could significantly inhibit tumor growth in vivo. These results demonstrated that nanoPue could improve Dox chemosensitivity and inhibit HCC metastasis. Thus, nanoPue represents a potential adjuvant therapy for Dox in the HCC treatment.

Angiogenesis is pivotal in HCC progression. Malignant tumors require the continuous formation of abnormal blood vessels. Dysfunctional endothelial cells, disrupted basement membranes, and impaired cell junctions contribute to leaky tumor vessels, compromising therapy efficacy. The tumor vascular leakage may compromise the efficacy of anticancer therapies. Restoring normalized tumor vasculature could optimize drug delivery. CD31 marks endothelial angiogenesis, while α-SMA reflects perivascular coverage and vascular maturity. Our data showed that nanoPue could downregulate CD31 and upregulate α-SMA, implying it could promote tumor vascular normalization.

NSUN2 is highly expressed in various tumors , and regulates angiogenesis by methylating vascular endothelial cells. , We found that NSUN2 is highly expressed in HCC xenografts, and nanoPue significantly decreased its expression. Mechanistically, NSUN2-mediated m5C hypermethylation can activate the PI3K/Akt pathway to drive cancer progression. Our findings revealed that nanoPue could decrease NSUN2 and p-AKT expressions in HCC. The PI3K/AKT pathway, downstream of receptors such as VEGF, is critical. PI3K inhibitors reduce VEGF expression in breast cancer. , VEGF, a key angiogenesis regulator, is essential for HCC development. In our study, we found that nanoPue significantly decreased p-AKT and VEGFA expression consequently to the downregulation of NSUN2, indicating its anticancer effect was mediated by NSUN2 downregulation.

MMP-9, a matrix metalloproteinase, plays an important role in tumor invasion, metastasis, and angiogenesis. , MMP-9 enhances tumor cell invasion, metastasis, and angiogenesis by stimulating VEGF expression. − In this study, nanoPue markedly decreased MMP-9 expression, consequently due to the downregulation of NSUN2. Based on these results, we propose that nanoPue decreases p-AKT, VEGF, and MMP-9 expression through downregulating NSUN2, thereby enhancing Dox sensitivity in HCC.

Finally, knocking down NSUN2 in HUVECs could attenuate nanoPue’s inhibitory effects on p-AKT, VEGFA, MMP-9 expression, and tube formation, which further confirmed that nanoPue exerted antitumor effects primarily via NSUN2 regulation.

While this study elucidates the antitumor efficacy of nanopuerarin (nanoPue) and provides preliminary insight into its mechanisms against HCC, several limitations remain. First, the involvement of the PI3K/AKT pathway was not experimentally validated by using inhibitors. Second, NSUN2-knockout (KO) animal models were not employed to confirm nanoPue’s target specificity. Additionally, direct molecular targets of nanoPue beyond NSUN2 remain unidentified. Future investigations will delineate nanoPue’s precise targets and elucidate its antiangiogenic mechanisms, particularly through NSUN2-regulated pathways, to comprehensively unravel its therapeutic potential in HCC.

In this study, we developed self-assembled puerarin nanoparticles (nanoPue) to overcome puerarin’s poor solubility and bioavailability. NanoPue potently inhibited HCC growth by targeting NSUN2, improved tumor penetration, and sensitized HCC to Dox, thereby enhancing the drug’s anticancer efficacy. In summary, nanoPue could serve as an adjuvant therapy for chemotherapeutic drugs in HCC treatment. Its relatively simple and scalable preparation also grants nanoPue significant potential for clinical translation.
