Comparison of High-tensile Suture and 16-gauge Wire for Prophylactic Fixation in Total Hip Arthroplasty: A Biomechanical Study
Authors: Sergio F. Guarin Perez, Gongyin Zhao, Ichiro Tsukamoto, Joshua R. Labott, Diego J. Restrepo, Ta-Wei Tai, Alexander W. Hooke, Chunfeng Zhao, Rafael J. Sierra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundProphylactic cerclage fixation may reduce fracture risk after total hip arthroplasty. Traditionally performed with wire or cables, high-tensile suture is a novel alternative not previously evaluated. This study compared peak torque to failure of cemented and cementless femoral stems using either 16-gauge wire or high-tensile suture in a synthetic femur model.MethodsSixteen collarless triple-taper (TT) stems (8 calcar wire, 8 calcar suture) and 27 taper-slip (TS) stems were tested: 7 each with calcar suture, diaphyseal wire, and diaphyseal suture, and 6 with calcar wire (one excluded). Stems were press-fit or cemented into composite femora. Fixation was applied above the lesser trochanter (calcar) or 2 cm below (diaphyseal). Sutures were tensioned to 80 N. A servohydraulic system applied a 2000 N load with 1°/s internal rotation until failure. Power analysis supported 7 specimens per group to detect a 30 Nm difference. Two-sample and noninferiority t-tests were performed.ResultsFor TT stems, peak torque was 92.7 ± 11.4 Nm with calcar wire and 102.6 ± 41.4 Nm with calcar suture; suture was noninferior (P = .038). For TS stems, torque was 148.2 ± 38.6 Nm (calcar wire), 118.3 ± 30.2 Nm (calcar suture), 164.9 ± 21.5 Nm (diaphyseal wire), and 159.4 ± 30.0 Nm (diaphyseal suture). Noninferiority in TS stems was not established. Diaphyseal suture exceeded calcar suture (P = .038).ConclusionsHigh-tensile suture was comparable to wire for calcar fixation in collarless TT stems. In TS stems, noninferiority was not demonstrated. Suture constructs may offer practical advantages and warrant further study.

Introduction

Prophylactic fixation is often used during total hip arthroplasty (THA) to prevent periprosthetic femoral fractures (PPFx) or to stabilize intraoperative calcar cracks [1,2]. Traditionally, stainless steel wires or cables have been used for this purpose [3], but these materials can cause soft tissue irritation, radiographic interference, and long-term mechanical complications such as breakage and debris near the joint [4]. Recent discussion has focused on not just whether to use prophylactic fixation, but where—proximally at the calcar or distally along the diaphysis—though biomechanical evidence guiding this choice remains limited [5].

High-tensile sutures, such as tape-based cerclage constructs, are increasingly used in orthopaedic subspecialties like sports medicine, offering advantages including lower abrasiveness, radiolucency, and more controlled tensioning [6,7]. Their potential use in joint arthroplasty is gaining interest, particularly as an alternative to wire or cable fixation, but their mechanical performance in the context of femoral stem fracture prophylaxis has not been clearly established.

The purpose of this study was to evaluate whether high-tensile suture provides noninferior mechanical strength compared to 16-gauge stainless steel wire in 2 commonly used femoral stem designs: a cementless collarless triple-taper (TT) stem and a cemented taper-slip (TS) stem.

Specimens and implants

Fourth-generation composite synthetic femora (Model 3403, Sawbones, Pacific Research Laboratories, Vashon, WA, USA) were used for all constructs. These femora consisted of a 17 pounds per cubic foot solid foam cancellous core with a cortical shell, a 13-mm medullary canal, and an overall length of 455 mm. Two femoral stem designs were tested; the cemented TS stem was an Exeter (44-0, Stryker, Kalamazoo, MI, USA), and the cementless collarless TT stem was an Actis (size 4 standard offset, 130° neck angle, DePuy Synthes, Warsaw, IN, USA). All constructs used a 28-mm femoral head with 0-mm offset. A No. 2 (5 metric), 66-inch polyethylene FiberTape suture with a preformed closed loop (TigerLink, Arthrex, Naples, FL, USA) was used for all suture constructs.

Stem preparation and implantation

TS stems were implanted using a standard cementation technique. The femoral canal was broached to the final size, cleared of debris, and 40 g of Simplex bone cement (Stryker, Kalamazoo, MI, USA) was introduced manually. No distal cement restrictor was used, as the sawbone model allowed for full canal fill. Stems were held in position until cement cured, and all constructs were allowed to cure for 24 hours at room temperature before testing.

Collarless TT stems were implanted using a press-fit technique. The femoral canal was broached to the final size according to the manufacturer guidelines, cleared of debris, and the stem was impacted manually using a mallet until securely seated. Proper axial alignment was ensured using visual and manual confirmation. No cement was used in these constructs.

Fixation technique

High-tensile suture or 16-gauge stainless steel wire was applied for prophylactic fixation. For calcar fixation, constructs were wrapped above the lesser trochanter. For diaphyseal fixation (TS stems only), fixation was applied 2 cm distal to the lesser trochanter. All high-tensile suture constructs were tensioned using the FiberTape Cerclage Tensioner (AR-7800, Arthrex, Inc., Naples, FL, USA) to a standardized force of 80 N, in accordance with manufacturer guidelines [8]. Wire constructs were manually twisted until secure by a fellowship-trained arthroplasty surgeon using the same technique for every specimen to ensure consistency. Mechanical testing was performed using a servohydraulic test system (Model 312, MTS Systems, Eden Prairie, MN, USA). The distal femur was potted in urethane resin (Smooth-Cast 300, Smooth-On Inc., Macungie, PA) using a custom mold to ensure consistent positioning. A 6° wedge was placed under the condyles to align the femur for axial torque testing. A custom jig and fixture were used to attach the femoral head to the testing machine.

Each construct was axially loaded at 20 mm/min until a 2000 N compression load was reached, after which axial position was held constant. The femoral head was then internally rotated at 40°/sec up to 60°, or until failure occurred. Internal rotation angle and torque were recorded at 128 Hz. Peak torque at failure was the primary outcome measure. The initial rotation protocol was adapted from Windell et al. [9], with angle increased from 40° to 60° to ensure fracture, based on pilot testing. Photographs were taken of all fractured constructs, and fracture patterns were documented. Fractured bone fragments were reconstructed when possible, to assess propagation patterns. Cement fragments were not recovered.

Data analyses

Prior to study initiation, a power analysis was conducted based on a standard deviation of 14.8 Nm for peak torque [9]. A sample size of 7 per group provided 90% power to detect a 30 Nm difference. For noninferiority analysis, 8 specimens per group were estimated to provide 90% power to detect a noninferiority margin of −25 Nm, assuming a true difference of −2 Nm.

Two-sample t-tests were used to compare mean torque values between groups. P values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.4.1 (Dotmatics, Boston, MA) and BlueSky Statistics version 10.3.4 (Bluesky, Chicago, IL).

Fracture patterns were documented visually post-testing. Across groups, failure consistently occurred in the proximal femur, typically as spiral or oblique fractures originating at or near the lesser trochanter. No differences in fracture location were observed between fixation types.

Discussion

Calcar cracks have traditionally been treated using wire or cable cerclage [2,10], but these methods can cause soft tissue abrasion and radiopacity, limiting their utility [3]. More recently, fixation tools commonly used in sports medicine—such as high-tensile sutures—have attracted interest in hip arthroplasty due to their low-profile design and favorable handling characteristics [11]. High-tensile suture systems typically have a higher upfront cost, often reported as 2-3 times that of standard 16-gauge wire, although pricing varies across centers. Despite this difference, their radiolucency, more controlled tensioning, and lower risk of fretting near the stem collar may justify selective use (Table 4). Modern cerclage cables have been shown to provide greater mechanical strength than monofilament wires [12]. Future studies directly comparing high-tensile suture with cables are warranted to determine whether the findings of this study translate to current clinical practice.

To our knowledge, this is the first biomechanical study demonstrating that high-tensile fixation in collarless TT stems is noninferior to wire fixation in terms of peak torque to failure. Additionally, in TS stems, high-tensile suture fixation at the diaphysis resulted in higher torque to failure than fixation at the calcar, suggesting that fixation location may influence construct strength.

Although variability differed between constructs, noninferiority was assessed using a predefined margin and confidence interval–based interpretation rather than variability alone. In the TT calcar comparison, the lower bound of the 95% confidence interval for the mean difference remained above the noninferiority margin despite wider dispersion in the suture group, supporting the stated noninferiority conclusion.

One possible explanation for the observed differences in cerclage response between stem types relates to differences in stem geometry and fixation method. Collarless TT stems rely on metaphyseal fixation and may permit more uniform load distribution along the proximal femur, potentially allowing adjunctive cerclage constructs to contribute more effectively to torsional resistance. In contrast, cemented TS stems transfer load differently, with greater reliance on cement–bone interactions, which may limit the relative mechanical contribution of calcar-level cerclage. This hypothesis warrants further investigation in studies specifically designed to evaluate failure mechanisms.

Collectively, these findings suggest that adjunctive fixation may provide a mechanical benefit in collarless stem designs. Prior studies have shown that stem geometry influences fracture patterns and mechanical behavior [13], and our findings support further exploration of how adjunctive fixation like high-tensile sutures could optimize outcomes in high-risk patients.

In TS stems, high-tensile suture fixation at the calcar or diaphysis did not meet noninferiority thresholds compared to wire, although the absolute differences in torque were modest in the diaphyseal comparison. TS stems are widely used cemented designs with excellent long-term survivorship, primarily due to low rates of aseptic loosening [14]. However, emerging data suggest they may carry a higher risk of PPFx compared to composite beam designs or collarless TT stems [[15], [16], [17]]. While our goal was to test whether high-tensile suture could supplement TS constructs in a way similar to wire, our data could not confirm noninferiority. Prior biomechanical work has suggested that adding diaphyseal fixation to TS stems can shift the fracture site distally to more favorable locations [18], but in our study, high-tensile suture did not demonstrate equivalent mechanical resistance to wire fixation under the testing conditions used.

The performance of calcar suturing may be influenced by the fixation method of the stem itself. In our study, high-tensile suture met noninferiority thresholds in collarless TT stems but not in cemented TS stems. Further biomechanical work comparing these constructs directly is warranted.

This study has several limitations. While cadaveric models are considered the gold standard for biomechanical fracture analysis, we chose validated fourth-generation composite femora to reduce inter-specimen variability and ensure consistent geometry and mechanical properties across constructs. This model allowed for controlled comparisons between fixation techniques and has been used extensively in prior THA studies to evaluate fracture risk and construct strength [9,[19], [20], [21]]. In addition, this study did not include cerclage cables as a comparison group, which limits direct extrapolation of findings to other contemporary fixation strategies used for fracture prevention in THA. Only one implant design was used per stem type, potentially limiting generalizability to other manufacturers. The modest sample size in each group increases sensitivity to outliers and may have limited our ability to detect statistical differences. One TS stem construct did not fracture during the initial test due to a rotational fixture error. Although this specimen was subsequently retested, it was excluded from analysis to maintain consistency with our a priori plan to include only first-test results. The cementless TT stems lacked biological fixation (osseointegration), while the cemented TS stems were tested after 24 hours of curing. Although this creates inherent differences in fixation biology, our goal was to model early postoperative fracture risk, before osseointegration typically occurs—usually around 90 days postop [[22], [23], [24]]. The exact tension applied to the wires was not measured or calibrated, but all fixations were performed by a single fellowship-trained surgeon to maintain consistency. In contrast, high-tensile suture constructs were applied using a calibrated mechanical tensioner set to a standardized force, further reducing technique-related variability. This study focused on peak torque to failure as the primary outcome, consistent with prior biomechanical investigations of prophylactic fixation [25]. However, the lack of a formal stress analysis, such as finite element modeling or strain mapping, limits the ability to fully characterize failure mechanics. Incorporating these techniques in future studies may provide further insight into stress distribution across constructs.

Conclusions

High-tensile suture fixation at the calcar was noninferior to wire in collarless TT stems in terms of peak torque to failure. In TS stems, high-tensile suture fixation did not demonstrate noninferiority at either the calcar or diaphyseal locations. Nonetheless, high-tensile suture may offer practical advantages, including nonabrasiveness, radiographic invisibility, and adjustable tension, which could be clinically beneficial in select THA cases. Further clinical and biomechanical studies are warranted to better define their role in PPFx prophylaxis.
