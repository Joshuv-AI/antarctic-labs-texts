A Case Report of Bart Syndrome with Brachydactyly of the Great Toe: A Rare Entity
Authors: En Hyung Kim, Sun Woo Son, Shin Ae Yoon, Ji Yeoun Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBart syndrome is characterized by the triad of aplasia cutis congenita (ACC), epidermolysis bullosa (EB), and nail abnormalities. We report a rare case of Bart syndrome presenting with ACC, nail abnormalities, and congenital brachydactyly of the left great toe in a neonate with genetically confirmed dominant dystrophic EB. The diagnosis was supported by next-generation sequencing, which identified a heterozygous COL7A1 c.6007G>A (p.Gly2003Arg) variant. The patient was managed conservatively with topical antibiotics, nonadherent dressings, and temporary keratinocyte allograft application, resulting in spontaneous epithelialization without major complications. This case underscores congenital brachydactyly as a rare extracutaneous association of Bart syndrome and highlights the diagnostic value of molecular testing, as well as the potential effectiveness of conservative management in achieving a favorable outcome.Keywords: Bart syndrome, epidermolysis bullosa, aplasia cutis congenita

Introduction

Bart syndrome is a rare inherited disorder characterized by the triad of aplasia cutis congenita (ACC), epidermolysis bullosa (EB), and nail abnormalities.1 Although an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern is reported in most cases, sporadic cases have also been described.2 ACC—congenital absence of skin—is a rare anomaly that most often involves the scalp. However, in Bart syndrome (type VI ACC by Frieden classification), lesions typically involve the medial and/or dorsal surface of the lower extremities.3

Inherited EB comprises a group of genetically and phenotypically heterogeneous skin disorders, characterized by recurrent blistering and erosions due to mechanical fragility of the skin. The four major types are EB simplex (EBS), junctional EB (JEB), dystrophic EB (DEB) and Kindler EB (KEB). DEB results from pathogenic variants in COL7A1 (type VII collagen) and may be inherited in either a dominant (DDEB) or recessive (RDEB) manner. In a review of 55 publications, RDEB was the most frequent EB subtype associated with Bart syndrome (41.4%), followed by DDEB (22.8%).4

Patients with RDEB generally exhibit a more severe clinical phenotype with marked cutaneous fragility, lifelong blistering, and extensive extracutaneous involvement. Notably, musculoskeletal complications are common extracutaneous manifestations in severe RDEB but are less typical in DDEB and in Bart syndrome. Most such musculoskeletal problems are acquired as sequelae of repeated blistering and present as webbing, encasement, and contracture of the hands and feet (pseudosyndactyly or mitten deformities).5 Reports of congenital musculoskeletal abnormalities, in contrast, are rare. Current treatment of Bart syndrome is largely supportive and focuses on gentle wound care, prevention of secondary infection, and avoidance of trauma, whereas surgical intervention, including skin grafting, may be considered for extensive or complicated cases.4 Here, we report an uncommon case of Bart syndrome with congenital brachydactyly of the great toe.

Case Report

A male neonate was born at 39 weeks gestation via cesarean section to a 31-year-old mother. Apgar scores at 1 and 5 minutes were 9 and 10, respectively. The newborn was transferred to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) at our hospital for evaluation of congenital localized absence of skin. On physical examination, there was localized absence of skin over the medial, lateral, and pretibial aspects of the left lower extremity, extending onto the dorsum of the foot (Figure 1A and B). The lesion had sharply demarcated borders covered by an ultrathin translucent membrane, with underlying vascular structures readily visible. The scalp and mucous membranes were unaffected at presentation. In addition, the patient had multiple erosions (Figure 1B and C) and nail abnormalities involving both thumbs (Figure 1D and E). The mother reported no medical conditions or medication use during pregnancy, and there was no family history of similar skin lesions or blistering disorders. Prenatal evaluations were unremarkable. The weight, height, head circumference, and vital signs were all within normal range for gestational age. Screening abdominal and cranial ultrasonography and echocardiography performed within the first week of life were unremarkable. Newborn metabolic screening was also normal.

Initial presentation. Localized absence of skin on the medial (A) and lateral (B) aspects of the left lower extremity. (C) Erosion on the dorsum of the right foot. Nail abnormalities of the right (D) and left (E) thumbs.

On postnatal day 4, the patient developed a bulla on the left plantar surface (Figure 2A). Thereafter, hemorrhagic bullae intermittently developed at various sites with minor trauma or friction and resolved spontaneously (Figure 2B–D). The discoloration of both thumbnails also resolved spontaneously over 5 weeks (Figure 3A–C). On further examination, the patient had shortening of the left great toe, and the left sole (Figure 3D). Plantar length (from the crease below the second toe to the heel) measured 7cm on the right and 6cm on the left. No additional foot deformities were identified. The clinical findings of congenital absence of skin on the lower extremity, epidermolysis bullosa and nail abnormalities supported a diagnosis of Bart syndrome. The clinical appearance of Bart’s syndrome is frequently used to diagnose this disease. Genetic testing was pursued to specify the EB subtype and confirm the diagnosis. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) identified a heterozygous pathogenic variant in COL7A1 (c.6007G>A) with autosomal dominant inheritance pattern, consistent with DDEB. NGS also detected a heterozygous nonsense variant in LAMA3 (c.2233C>T). This loss-of-function allele indicates carrier status for autosomal recessive JEB, without expected clinical impact in the heterozygous state and does not account for the patient’s phenotype.

We provided conservative wound care with topical antibiotics and nonadherent dressings. The patient received daily saline wet dressings and was applied with mupirocin 2% ointment, chlorhexidine acetate–impregnated petrolatum gauze under a soft silicone foam dressing for 12 days. On postnatal day 8, the previously visible vessels were no longer evident (Figure 4A). Subsequently, to facilitate epithelialization, the plastic surgery team placed a keratinocyte allograft over the skin lesion on postnatal day 14. The allograft was removed after 3 days (Figure 4B), and the previous dressing regimen was resumed. For intermittent nonhealing erosions (Figure 4C), ofloxacin ophthalmic ointment was applied. Spontaneous epithelialization occurred, and the skin lesions healed over the course of approximately one month (Figure 4D). By the fifth week, the clinical team assessed that the lesions sufficiently healed to be manageable at home. The parents received detailed wound care instructions, and the patient was discharged. Two months after discharge, he visited our dermatology clinic for outpatient follow-up; he was in good health, and the previous lesions had healed with scarring (Figure 5).

Discussion

We present a rare case of Bart syndrome associated with DDEB, ACC, and congenital brachydactyly of the left great toe. DDEB results from heterozygous, dominantly inherited variants in COL7A1, most commonly glycine substitutions; in our patient, c.6007G>A (p.Gly2003Arg) was identified. The NGS result was particularly important in this case, because Bart syndrome represents a clinical phenotype that may occur in different EB subtypes. Thus, molecular confirmation was essential to establish the diagnosis as DDEB and to better understand the patient’s prognosis and inheritance pattern. COL7A1 encodes type VII collagen, the principal component of anchoring fibrils that attach the basement membrane to the underlying dermis.6 Consequently, cleavage in DEB occurs within the sublamina densa region, which is the uppermost dermis.7 Clinically, DDEB generally has a milder course than RDEB; blistering—often prominent on the lower extremities—typically begins at or shortly after birth and tends to diminish with age.6

ACC shows wide clinical heterogeneity and has been reported across all major inherited EB subtypes. In a recent literature review, RDEB was the most frequently associated subtype (41.4%), followed by DDEB (22.8%).4 The etiology of ACC remains uncertain; proposed mechanisms include in-utero mechanical factors (eg, friction due to leg-rubbing) and mutations leading to thermolability of type VII collagen, which may preferentially affect warmer, frequently rubbed sites such as the mucous membranes and lower extremities.2

Although acquired musculoskeletal abnormalities—such as pseudobrachydactyly—are well recognized in RDEB, musculoskeletal deformities are uncommon in DDEB. To the best of our knowledge, only one case of Bart syndrome with genetically confirmed DDEB and congenital brachydactyly of the great toes has been reported (Table 1).8 Acquired musculoskeletal abnormalities are thought to result from repeated rupture of bullous lesions, which induces secondary inflammation and fibrosis and, over time, leads to scarring and musculoskeletal deformities. The repeated episodes of scarring may compromise the blood supply to the distal limbs and bones. This has been reported to cause muscle atrophy and hindrance in the growth of distal bones. The distal phalanges of the hands and feet may show shortening or tapered ends.9 Although this mechanism has been described in the context of acquired musculoskeletal abnormalities in EB, the proposed mechanisms of ACC in DEB, including intrauterine limb friction and bullae formation in the setting of COL7A1-related skin fragility, suggest that a similar process could begin during fetal development.10 Therefore, congenital brachydactyly in our patient may share a similar pathogenic pathway, although the precise mechanism remains to be elucidated.

In clinical practice, most patients with Bart syndrome can be managed conservatively with local wound care and measures to prevent secondary infection. Prophylactic systemic antibiotics are not indicated unless infection is suspected, and adhesive or compressive dressings should be avoided to minimize further blistering.

This report describes a case of Bart syndrome with genetically confirmed DDEB, accompanied by ACC, nail abnormalities, and congenital brachydactyly. Congenital foot deformities in DDEB, as in our patient, have rarely been reported. The absence of skin in a localized area at birth is a crucial indicator for diagnosing Bart syndrome at birth which later heals and can obscure the diagnosis. The strengths of this case include molecular confirmation of the EB subtype by NGS and detailed documentation of the clinical course and treatment response. However, this report is limited by its single-case nature, the lack of functional studies to clarify the mechanism of brachydactyly, and the absence of parental genetic testing. Despite these limitations, the genetically confirmed diagnosis of DDEB, together with the characteristic neonatal presentation of Bart syndrome and the rarity of congenital toe deformity in this setting, supports the interpretation that brachydactyly in our patient is more likely to represent an uncommon phenotypic association than an incidental finding. This case therefore expands the clinical spectrum of DDEB-associated Bart syndrome and highlights the importance of careful assessment for rare extracutaneous manifestations.
