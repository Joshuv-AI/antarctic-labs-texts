Ecological flow guarantee rate along the Xijiang River mainstream at different scales based on multiple probability distributions
Authors: Jiqing Li, Xinyi Deng, Jiali Liu, Zhanhong Cheng, Shilang Deng, Xiaoli Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEcological flow is a key determinant of river ecosystem stability. Under climate change and increasing human activities, accurate quantification of ecological flow is increasingly important. Using hydrological observations from four stations, Tian’e, Qianjiang, Wuxuan, and Wuzhou, ecological flow was quantified for the Xijiang River mainstream. To address runoff nonstationarity driven by climate change and human activities, this study calibrated a random forest model using meteorological driving factors from the natural period and applied it to simulate quasi-natural runoff during the change period. The simulation results supported the assumption that the climate runoff relationship in the natural period was transferable for quasi-natural runoff reconstruction. This study used the Kolmogorov-Smirnov (K-S) test, Anderson-Darling (A-D) test and Chi-Square (C-S) test to evaluate five probability distributions to determine the optimal distribution of monthly ecological flow estimates. Then, the month-by-month frequency method was used to calculate the monthly ecological flow of each site at 90% guarantee rate, and the Tennant method was used to evaluate the rationality of the flow in each period of the year. The optimal fitting distribution was mainly the Generalized Extreme Value (GEV) and the P-III distribution. After runoff change points, ecological flow guarantee rates decreased during the change period relative to the natural period, with larger declines at the Tian’e and Qianjiang stations in the upper reaches of the basin and the most pronounced reductions during the flood season from July to October. These findings inform ecological flow management in the Xijiang River Basin by identifying priority reaches and sensitive months, and they provide a transferable framework for assessing ecological flows in regulated river systems to support sustainable freshwater ecosystem protection and water resources management.Keywords: Ecological flow, Random forest model, Ecological flow guarantee rate, Xijiang RiverSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences, Hydrology

Introduction

River ecological flow refers to the portion of river discharge that sustains ecosystem functions while simultaneously supporting human survival and development. It is characterized by critical thresholds, beyond which ecological imbalances may arise, potentially compromising ecosystem stability1,2. Under the combined pressures of climate change and intensive human activities-including socioeconomic development, reservoir impoundment, and large scale water, the natural hydrological regime of rivers has been substantially modified3,4, leading to altered intra- and inter-annual flow patterns and threatening the stability and integrity of river ecosystems5. Given these threshold responses and the substantial modification of natural flow regimes, robust quantification of river ecological flow is required to define ecological flow and to support river management decisions, which is essential for maintaining ecosystem balance, conserving biodiversity, and enabling coordinated development between economic growth and water ecological sustainability6,7.

At present, ecological flow estimation methods are primarily classified into hydrological, hydraulic, habitat simulation, and integrated approaches8. Among these, the hydrological method is most widely applied because of its simplicity, cost-effectiveness, and convenience; however, its application requires a consistent runoff regime that is representative of natural conditions. In rivers subject to increasing human activities, runoff often exhibits nonstationarity. This runoff nonstationarity reflects time varying statistical properties driven by climate change and human activities. Direct fitting of probability distributions to post impact observations can violate the consistency assumption and can bias ecological flow quantiles. Recent studies have attempted to address this issue by reconstructing quasi-naturalized runoff. Hydrology based ecological flow estimation is then performed using the reconstructed series9. For example, Yu et al.10 utilized monthly runoff data from the Wudu Hydrological Station of the Bailong River from 1990 to 2020, integrating seven hydrological methods to estimate ecological flow in the middle reaches and conducting a runoff satisfaction analysis on the coupled estimation results. Chen et al.11applied the Slid-GrU model to reconstruct runoff across different hydrological periods and calculated ecological flow by combining basic and characteristic frequencies. Xia et al.12 reconstructed runoff for the southern Poyang Lake–Ganjiang system using the Distributed Time-Variant Gain Model and derived ecological flow via a weighted probabilistic flow-duration curve. Fang et al.13 employed measured runoff data from 1952 to 2020, used the SWAT model to reconstruct natural runoff, corrected hydrological anomalies, and simulated sub-basin runoff, with the Tennant method subsequently applied to calculate ecological flow and water shortage status under the minimum, appropriate, and ideal criteria.

Despite this progress, most existing studies have addressed only one aspect of the problem. Multiple hydrological methods have been coupled with satisfaction or adequacy analyses, typically at only a few sites. Runoff has been reconstructed for different hydrological periods, and ecological flows estimated using frequency based schemes, probabilistic methods, or flow duration curve approaches. Runoff has been naturalized with process based models, and deficits evaluated using empirical criteria such as the Tennant method. Consequently, long-term trends in ecological flow guarantee rates have seldom been examined, and longitudinal variability along regulated mainstreams has been characterized only to a limited extent. In order to make up for these shortcomings, this study quantified the ecological flow guarantee rate of multiple time scales and spatial locations along the mainstream of the Xijiang River, and used random forest models to reconstruct quasi-natural runoff, thereby addressing runoff nonstationarity and enabling subsequent probabilistic ecological flow estimation. Furthermore, the optimal monthly distribution is selected using multiple goodness-of-fit tests with explicit decision criteria. Ecologically sensitive periods are identified based on intra-annual variability. Compared with prior studies that focus on time varying runoff nonstationary frequency models or on runoff naturalization alone, the novelty of this study is provided by an integrated framework. Change point delineation is combined with random forest based quasi-natural runoff reconstruction. A multi-test decision rule is applied to select month specific distributions. Guarantee rate dynamics are evaluated over long-term time and along river space using a unified station by station framework.

As the mainstream of the Pearl River, the Xijiang River is subject to frequent extreme hydrological events and intensive human activities, which increase the uncertainty of ecological flows and pose substantial challenges for ecological flow management14,15. The Xijiang River mainstream is a key component of the Pearl River Xijiang ecological corridor16. Basin responses to environmental change are important for maintaining aquatic ecosystem stability. In this study, the mainstream of the Xijiang River is examined. An integrated framework is proposed to quantify ecological flow and their guarantee performance under runoff nonstationarity. Specifically, hydrological change points are identified to delineate the natural period and the change period. A random forest model is trained using data from the natural period. It is applied to reconstruct quasi-natural runoff during the change period. This step supports ecological flow assessment under runoff nonstationarity. A systematic month by month and station by station probabilistic framework is developed. Multiple candidate distributions are evaluated using several goodness-of-fit tests and explicit decision criteria. The optimal distribution is selected for each monthly runoff series. Using the reconstructed runoff and the selected distribution models, the temporal evolution and longitudinal spatial variability of ecological flow guarantee rates are quantified. Sensitive periods are identified. Decision relevant information on where and when ecological flow deficits are most likely is provided by this framework. Targeted ecological flow protection, adaptive reservoir operation, and water allocation can be supported in regulated river systems.

Materials and methods

Study area and data

Originating from Maxiong Mountain in Yunnan Province, the Xijiang River forms the largest tributary within the Pearl River Basin. The basin is an important part of China’s national water network and has a high aquatic biodiversity. As one of the most abundant water resources but the most developed areas in China, the basin has experienced a series of factors such as rapid population growth, rapid economic development, extensive inland shipping, and increased reservoir regulation, all of which pose major challenges to water ecological safety.

Four representative hydrological stations (Tian’e, Qianjiang, Wuxuan, and Wuzhou) located along the mainstream of the Xijiang River were selected to investigate the ecological flow guarantee rate, aiming to support basin-scale ecological conservation and water resources management. Daily runoff records were obtained for the Tian’e and Wuzhou stations from 1961 to 2020, and for the Qianjiang and Wuxuan stations from 1961 to 2019. Meteorological data, including daily precipitation, evaporation, temperature, wind speed, and sunshine duration, were collected from 13 meteorological stations across the basin for the period 1961–2020, sourced from the China Meteorological Network. Meteorological forcing was assigned to each hydrological station using a distance weighted ensemble averaging approach based on inverse distance weighting. For each hydrological station, distances from the station to all available meteorological stations were calculated. These distances were converted to weights using an inverse distance weighting scheme. Stations that were closer were assigned higher weights, and stations that were farther away were assigned lower weights. The variables included precipitation, evaporation, temperature, wind speed, and sunshine duration. This step produced a station specific meteorological input time series. Based on these hydrological and meteorological datasets, the analytical framework was implemented as shown in Fig. 2. The framework was organized into three linked components. First, change point detection was applied to delineate the natural period and the change period. Second, quasi-natural runoff in the change period was reconstructed using a random forest model driven by meteorological variables. Third, month specific distribution curve fitting was conducted on the resulting naturalized runoff series to derive ecological flow and to evaluate guarantee rates.

The methodological workflow of this study.

Methods for detecting abrupt change points

Six change point detection methods, including cumulative anomaly, sliding t-test, Pettitt test, hierarchical clustering, Mann-Kendall test, and moving rank sum test (T1-T6), were applied to identify abrupt changes in annual runoff series. To enhance robustness, a mode-based criterion was adopted, whereby a year was regarded as a valid change point when it was detected by at least three methods. This criterion was used to delineate natural and change periods for subsequent runoff reconstruction.

Methods for simulation of runoff series

The random forest model is a nonlinear ensemble learning algorithm built on decision trees. This study used the re-released self-aggregation (bagging) and random feature selection methods to build a decision tree integration model. Due to its fast-learning speed and strong adaptability, this model has been widely used in time series prediction and feature extraction17–19. This study explored the relationship between runoff and its driving factors from the perspective of hydrocausality. A random forest model was constructed using meteorological and runoff data from the natural period, and only meteorological variables were used as input in the calibration and verification stage. Then, the model was used to simulate the changes in runoff during climate change.

The random forest model was implemented in MATLAB R2017b using the TreeBagger function in regression mode. Daily runoff was used as the target variable for each station. Five meteorological predictors were used as inputs: precipitation, evaporation, temperature, wind speed, and sunshine duration. The random forest model was not intended to reproduce regulated runoff during the change period. Instead, quasi-natural runoff was reconstructed to provide a consistent regime for subsequent ecological flow estimation. Only meteorological variables were used as predictors. Reservoir operation variables such as storage and releases were excluded to avoid embedding human activities in the reconstructed series and to preserve the climatic component for ecological flow estimation. The random forest consisted of 2500 regression trees. The parameter nTrees was set to 2500, and bootstrap aggregating was applied. Each tree was trained using bootstrap samples drawn with replacement. On average, each bootstrap sample contained about 63.2% of the original observations. Node splits were selected by minimizing average squared error. Tree complexity was controlled using a minimum leaf size of 1. MinLeafSize was set to 1. No maximum tree depth was imposed. Trees were grown until further splitting was prevented by the stopping rules. At each split, a random subset of predictors was considered to reduce intertree correlation. This feature subsampling parameter was not specified, and the default TreeBagger setting was used. Model evaluation used time ordered splitting rather than random shuffling to reduce overfitting. This approach matched the temporal dependence of hydrological series and avoided information leakage. Out of bag prediction was enabled by setting OOBPrediction to On. The out of bag error curve was examined to confirm that generalization error stabilized as the number of trees increased. The trained random forest model was used to generate runoff predictions for the testing period.

Methods for ecological flow estimation

Ecological flow estimation based on probability distributions requires a runoff series that represents a consistent regime. To address this issue, quasi-natural runoff for the change period was reconstructed using a random forest model trained during the natural period. Observations from the natural period were then combined with the reconstructed runoff to form a naturalized runoff series for each station. After quasi-natural runoff was reconstructed at the daily scale, the daily series was aggregated to a monthly series for subsequent distribution curve fitting and ecological flow estimation. This scale transition was adopted for two reasons. First, ecological flow calculation in this study was distribution based and was performed month-by-month to represent intraannual seasonality. Monthly series provided stable sample sizes for each month. They also reduced the influence of short-term noise and extreme daily fluctuations on distribution curve fitting. Second, many hydrology based ecological flow applications and guarantee rate evaluations were implemented at the monthly scale in prior work. This choice facilitated comparison with previous studies. It also supported management relevant interpretation at monthly and seasonal resolution. Therefore, the random forest model was applied to daily data to capture rainfall runoff responses during reconstruction. Distribution curve fitting and ecological flow extraction were conducted using the aggregated monthly series. Specifically, five candidate distribution functions were fitted to each monthly series, including Normal distribution, Gamma distribution, Generalized Extreme Value (GEV) distribution, P-III distribution, and Logistic distribution. Distribution parameters were estimated using the Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) method. The fitted distributions were evaluated using three goodness-of-fit tests: the Kolmogorov-Smirnov (K-S) test, the Anderson-Darling (A-D) test, and the Chi-Square (C-S) test. The best-fitting distribution for each month’s runoff series was selected as the optimal distribution model, and ecological flow was subsequently calculated using the month-by-month frequency method. The Tennant method was then used to evaluate the reasonableness of the derived ecological flows by classifying flow conditions relative to the multi-year average natural runoff.

Goodness-of-fit testing

Goodness-of-fit tests evaluate the consistency between the observational data and the theoretical data by comparing the observational data with the theoretical values calculated under a given hypothesis or distribution model27. The K-S, A-D, and C-S tests were primarily employed to evaluate the goodness-of-fit of different distribution functions. All goodness-of-fit tests were conducted at a unified significance level of α = 0.05. For each candidate distribution, the null hypothesis that the monthly runoff series followed the specified distribution was accepted when the test statistic did not exceed the critical value at α. When the goodness-of-fit tests yielded inconsistent best fit distributions, the optimal distribution was selected using the rankings from the K-S, A-D, and C-S tests. Two criteria were applied. First, when two or three tests selected the same fitted curve, that curve was accepted as the optimal distribution. Second, when the three tests did not agree, the rank values from the three tests were summed for each fitted curve. The fitted curve with the smallest rank sum was selected as the optimal distribution for that series.

Month-by-month frequency method

Based on the hydrological conditions of the river and ecological objectives, a reasonable guarantee rate was selected using the month-by-month frequency method to derive the ecological flow for each month of the year30. Long-term monthly mean flow data were used to perform frequency ranking and fitted-line analysis for four hydrological stations under five probability distributions. The flow corresponding to a 90% guarantee rate was selected as the basic ecological flow31.

Tennant method

In accordance with the Technical Guidelines for the Calculation of Ecological Water Requirements for Rivers and Lakes, the rationality of the ecological flow estimates was evaluated to ensure scientific validity and practical applicability. In this study, the Tennant method was employed for evaluation. The Tennant method classifies ecological flow into one upper-limit standard, one optimal range, and six minimum thresholds based on recommended flow levels. These classifications were determined according to the proportion of calculated ecological flow as a percentage of the multi-year average flow. The multi-year average flow was defined as the long-term average annual flow of the naturalized runoff series at each station. The naturalized series combined observed runoff from the natural period with reconstructed quasi-natural runoff from the change period. To obtain this reference condition, the complete naturalized daily runoff record was compiled for each station. The long-term average annual flow was computed by averaging discharge over the full record. Detailed classification criteria are provided in Table 1. In the Tennant method, 10% of the average annual flow was treated as a critical boundary. It was interpreted as a minimum short-term survival threshold. Therefore, the poor class was assigned to flows around 10%. The fair class was assigned to flows above this boundary. Flows below 10% were classified as severe degradation. Additionally, based on the seasonal requirements of aquatic organisms, the year was divided into the normal water use period (October to March) and the spawning and rearing period (April to September)32.

Calculation of ecological flow guarantee rate

The ecological flow guarantee rate refers to the ratio of the number of time periods during which the observed river discharge exceeds the ecological flow to the total length of the observation period33. When the ecological flow guarantee rate is estimated using average flows at different time scales, significant variations in results are often observed. Chen et al.34 pointed out that the monthly ecological flow guarantee rate can be effectively characterized by calculations based on monthly average flows, thereby reflecting actual water shortage conditions in river ecosystems. Accordingly, to ensure comparability across time scales and to reflect ecosystem water shortage conditions, ecological flow guarantee rates were evaluated on a monthly basis using the basic ecological flows at four hydrological stations: Tian’e, Qianjiang, Wuxuan, and Wuzhou. Results and discussion

Runoff simulation results

First, six change point detection methods were applied to the annual runoff series at four hydrological stations along the mainstream of the Xijiang River to identify abrupt changes, and the natural and change periods for each station were delineated accordingly. The results are shown in Fig. 3. As shown in Fig. 3, the identified change points varied among methods, indicating method dependent sensitivity. Because change points differed across methods, a conservative rule was applied to define the natural period for training the climate driven random forest model. The training data were required to be as minimally affected by human activities as possible. Therefore, the earliest robustly supported change point was selected as the primary delineation point at each station. Candidate years detected by the six methods were screened for cross method robustness. Years supported by at least three methods were treated as robust candidates, and the first robust candidate was adopted for period separation. If a later change point were used, partially impacted observations could be included in the training period designated as natural, which would weaken the interpretation of reconstructed runoff as a climate driven baseline. For the Tian’e and Qianjiang stations, 1986 and 2002, respectively, were identified as change points by three or more methods. Although 2002 was detected as a change point by all six methods, 1986 was the earliest year supported by more than three methods. Therefore, 1986 was selected as the change point year for both Tian’e and Qianjiang stations. These years were interpreted as the first significant abrupt changes in hydrological regimes at the corresponding stations and were used to delineate natural and change periods. Accordingly, the natural period was defined as 1961 to 1986 for both stations, whereas the change period spanned 1987 to 2020 for Tian’e and 1987 to 2019 for Qianjiang. Using the same criterion, the natural and change periods were defined as 1961 to 1992 and 1993 to 2019 for Wuxuan, and 1961 to 1983 and 1984 to 2020 for Wuzhou, respectively.

Change point detection results for hydrological stations.

Based on baseline period data, a random forest model was developed for each hydrological station. For the three hydrological stations of Tian’e, Qianjiang and Wuzhou, the calibration period was from 1961 to 1975, and the verification period extended from 1976 to the detected change point. For Wuxuan Station, the calibration period was from 1961 to 1981, and the verification period was extended from 1982 to the detected change point. Use NSE and R² to evaluate the simulation performance of the model. As shown in Table 2, during the calibration period, the NSE and R² values of the four hydrostations were above 0.9, indicating that the model had good performance. During the verification period, both NSE and R² are maintained above 0.6 to meet the required accuracy standards. These results show that the relationship between the meteorological factors established by the random forest model under natural runoff conditions and runoff can be extended to the study area and can provide reliable runoff simulation results.

Figure 4 shows the comparison between observation and simulation of daily flow during the study. As shown in the figure, the random forest model can effectively capture the trend of runoff changes during the calibration period and the verification period. In most cases, the analog value matched the observed data well, indicating that the model had excellent performance and high simulation accuracy. However, due to the large and complex space-time variability of extreme precipitation events, the simulation results of extreme runoff events were biased. Especially during the simulation period, the simulated peak flow was always lower than the observed peak flow. The frequency and flow of high-flow events were underestimated, while the frequency and flow of low-flow events were often overestimated. This phenomenon was most pronounced at the Wuxuan and Wuzhou stations in the lower reaches, where runoff volumes were relatively large. This phenomenon may be related to the operation of the reservoir, which was storing water during the high flow period and releasing water during the low flow period. This strategy is called flood control and drought resistance. These results suggest that human activities may have intensified the variability of runoff dynamics in these regions. Reservoir operation can dampen flood peaks and increase low flows. This process can increase the residuals between reconstructed quasi-natural runoff and observed runoff during the change period, particularly for extreme values. In this sense, the reconstruction residuals can be interpreted as an integrated signal of reservoir operation and other human activities. This uncertainty can propagate to distribution curve fitting and ecological flow estimates, particularly for extreme flows.

Ecological flow calculation results

Linear fit of the distribution

Using the January average monthly flow as a representative case, parameter estimates for the five candidate distribution functions are summarized in Table 3, and goodness-of-fit visualizations are provided in Fig. 5. Because ecological flow assessments primarily focus on monthly runoff values corresponding to the 90% guarantee rate35, fitting accuracy in the lower tail of the monthly runoff distribution was emphasized. As shown in Fig. 5, the GEV distribution provided the best overall fit to both the central and lower-tail portions of the monthly runoff data at the Tian’e station. Comparable fitting performance was observed for the P-III and Gamma distributions, with no significant differences detected. In contrast, the Normal distribution tended to overestimate the central part and underestimate the tail, resulting in a poor-fitting effect. The Logistic distribution had the worst performance at the tail, and there was an obvious downward deviation in the fitting curve. At Qianjiang Station, most of the data points coincided well with the generalized extreme distribution curve (including the tail), indicating that its fitting performance was better. In contrast, the Normal distribution showed the poorest fit; the curve generally overestimated January runoff values and exhibited low agreement with the observed data. At both the Wuxuan and Wuzhou stations, the GEV distribution also outperformed the other four distribution types, demonstrating superior fitting accuracy. Overall, the GEV distribution consistently provided the best fit for the January average monthly flow at all four hydrological stations along the mainstream of the Xijiang River. The Gamma and P-III distributions showed comparable fitting performance, with no significant differences observed between them. The Logistic distribution performed worse than these three distributions, whereas the Normal distribution showed the poorest overall fit.

Goodness-of-fit tests

Given that different distribution curves exhibit varying degrees of fit to the same sample data, three statistical tests, the K-S, A-D, and C-S tests, were used to quantify and corroborate the qualitative assessments of the goodness-of-fit for the different distribution models. Using the January average monthly flow data from each hydrological station as an example, the goodness-of-fit test results for the five distribution models from the three methods are presented in Table 4. In the table, black dashed boxes highlight the distributions with the poorest fit, whereas solid boxes indicate the distributions with the best fit. Table 4 shows that different goodness-of-fit tests can yield varying results for the same series. For instance, at the Tian’e station, the GEV distribution was identified as the best fit by both the K-S and C-S tests, whereas the A-D test favored the P-III distribution. However, across the four hydrological stations, the optimal distributions identified by the different methods were consistently concentrated in the GEV and P-III distributions, whereas the Normal distribution predominantly showed the poorest fit.

Ecological flow estimation

Based on the optimal distributions of average monthly flow for the four hydrological stations, the month-by-month frequency method was applied to estimate the basic ecological flow corresponding to the 90% guarantee rate. The ecological flows were then compared with the average monthly flows during the natural and change periods at each station. The results are shown in Fig. 6. As illustrated in Fig. 6, monthly ecological flows at the four hydrological stations were consistently lower than the corresponding monthly average flows during both the natural and change periods. Nevertheless, ecological flow exhibited strong temporal coherence, with a gradual increase from January to May, followed by a sharp rise after May, a peak in July, and a subsequent decline. This model shows that key ecological water needs mainly occur during the high-flow season from June to August.

Variation analysis of the ecological flow guarantee rate

Using long-term average monthly flow series as the basis for calculating ecological flow guarantee rates, variations in guarantee rates across different months, periods, and representative years were examined. Furthermore, the spatial variability of ecological flow guarantee rates was characterized by analyzing flow characteristics at hydrological stations located in the upper, middle, and lower reaches of the basin. The ecological flow guarantee rate is used as a hydrological adequacy metric. This metric evaluates compliance and reliability with the proposed hydrological threshold under the reconstructed regime. By contrast, ecological response refers to biological or habitat outcomes. These outcomes depend not only on threshold exceedance but also on the timing, duration, and magnitude of flow deficits and other environmental controls. Habitat modeling and biological observations were not included. Therefore, the guarantee rate is interpreted as evidence of hydrological adequacy rather than proof of ecological sufficiency.

Characteristics of annual ecological flow guarantee rate variations

Annual ecological flow guarantee rates for each hydrological station are shown in Fig. 7. As shown in Fig. 7, ecological flow guarantee rates, including annual values and those for the normal water use period and the spawning and rearing period, fluctuated noticeably across all hydrological stations. Furthermore, station-specific patterns were observed, as characterized below: (1) A marked and fluctuating decline in ecological flow guarantee rates was observed at all hydrological stations in the early 1990 s, followed by a gradual recovery beginning around 1995. This pattern may be associated with intensified anthropogenic impacts related to major hydropower development, including the Tianshengqiao II and Yantan dams, in the Xijiang River Basin during the late 1980 s and early 1990s. River regulation and reservoir impoundment were inferred to have reduced inflows in the lower reaches, thereby limiting the extent to which ecological water requirements in the river channel were met. (2) Variability in ecological flow guarantee rates at the Tian’e and Qianjiang stations was more pronounced than at the Wuxuan and Wuzhou stations, particularly after 2000. This difference suggests stronger combined influences of climate change and human activities in the upper reaches of the Xijiang River. (3) At the Tian’e and Qianjiang stations, ecological flow guarantee rates during the spawning and rearing period fluctuated more strongly than during the normal water use period. In contrast, this pattern was less evident at the Wuxuan and Wuzhou stations.

The guarantee rate quantifies how often a hydrologically defined flow target is met. It does not provide direct evidence of ecological recovery. Ecological outcomes also depend on the timing, duration, and magnitude of low flow deficits. High overall guarantee rates can still mask short but ecologically relevant low flow episodes. Therefore, guarantee rates are interpreted primarily as an indicator of hydrological adequacy and reliability with the proposed targets. Ecological effectiveness requires additional habitat and biological validation. During both the normal water use period and the spawning and rearing period, ecological flow guarantee rates at the Tian’e and Qianjiang stations declined to varying degrees during the change period relative to the natural period, which may adversely affect aquatic organism growth and reproduction. Ecological flow guarantee rate in different typical years

Ecological flow guarantee rates for hydrological stations along the mainstream of the Xijiang River were analyzed for different typical years, and the results are shown in Fig. 8. Wet, normal, and dry years were classified using the guarantee rate method: years with P ≤ 37.5% were classified as wet years, those with 37.5% < P ≤ 62.5% as normal years, and those with P ≥ 62.5% as dry years. where P (%) denotes the empirical exceedance probability of annual runoff derived from the long-term series, obtained by ranking annual runoff from high to low and assigning a plotting position to each year. Smaller P indicates wetter conditions and larger P indicates drier conditions. In the 60-year series at the Tian’e station (1961 to 2020), 23 wet years were identified. During wet years, 265 months met the ecological flow requirements, accounting for 96% and yielding a wet-year ecological flow guarantee rate of 96% when monthly average flow was compared with ecological flow. Fifteen normal years were identified, and 159 months met the ecological flow requirements (88%), yielding a normal-year ecological flow guarantee rate of 88%. For the 22 dry years, 208 months met the ecological flow requirements (79%), resulting in a dry-year ecological flow guarantee rate of 79%. Similarly, ecological flow guarantee rates at the Qianjiang station during wet, normal, and dry years were 95%, 89%, and 78%, respectively. At the Wuxuan station, the corresponding rates were 95%, 95%, and 82%, respectively. At the Wuzhou station, the corresponding rates were 96%, 95%, and 81% for wet, normal, and dry years, respectively. Across the four hydrological stations, ecological flow guarantee rates were highest during wet years (≥ 95%) and lowest during dry years (approximately 80%). In normal years, ecological flow guarantee rates remained at 95% at the Wuxuan and Wuzhou stations, whereas lower rates were observed at the Tian’e and Qianjiang stations (< 90%). Overall, ecological flow guarantee rates across the four hydrological stations showed little variation during wet years. However, in normal and dry years, higher guarantee rates were observed at the Wuxuan and Wuzhou stations than at the Tian’e and Qianjiang stations, suggesting higher hydrological reliability in meeting the proposed ecological flow.

Monthly ecological flow guarantee rate in different typical years

Monthly ecological flow guarantee rates for each hydrological station during the natural and change periods were calculated separately, and the results are shown in Fig. 9. As illustrated in Fig. 9, ecological flow guarantee rates at Tian’e Station during the natural period exceeded 90% in most months, with the highest value reaching 97% in February. In contrast, rates during the change period showed a marked decline relative to the natural period. The largest reductions were observed in August and September, reaching up to 62%. Notably, from May onward, the ecological flow guarantee rate declined continuously during the change period, with markedly greater variability than during the natural period, and recovery did not begin until October. At Qianjiang Station, the ecological flow guarantee rate during the natural period exceeded 90% for 8 months, and the highest was in April, reaching 96%. In contrast, during the change period, only the guarantee rate in January, February, March and July was higher than the natural period, and the guarantee rate in the remaining months was lower than the natural period. The guarantee rate began to decline significantly in July, falling to a low of 63% in September. In Wuxuan station, the ecological flow guarantee rate during the natural period and the change period was generally higher than that of the other three stations, exceeding 90% in most months. Except from September to November, when guarantee rates during the change period were lower than those during the natural period, higher guarantee rates were observed in the change period for all other months. However, a sustained decline was observed from June during the change period, with fluctuations markedly greater than during the natural period, and recovery did not begin until November. At Wuzhou station, ecological flow guarantee rates from January to July differed only slightly between the natural and change periods. However, from August onward, ecological flow guarantee rates during the change period declined markedly and remained lower than those during the natural period through December.

Overall, at the Tian’e and Qianjiang stations, ecological flow guarantee rates during the change period declined markedly relative to the natural period in most months. By contrast, decreases at the Wuxuan and Wuzhou stations were less pronounced; slight declines were observed only from August to November, whereas modest increases were observed from January to July relative to the natural period. Across all four stations, the most significant reductions in ecological flow assurance during the change period were concentrated in the flood season and its recession phase from August to November, a pattern consistent with findings for the Yellow River mainstream.

To further analyze monthly variation trends in ecological flow guarantee rates and identify sensitive periods for in-stream ecological flow along the mainstream of the Xijiang River, a hierarchical clustering method37 was employed. Using squared Euclidean distance and the within-group linkage method, average intermonth distances were calculated. Months with strongly correlated ecological flow guarantee rates were grouped together based on their similarity. The clustering results are shown in Fig. 10.

(a) Cluster analysis tree diagram of different months; (b) The change of ecological flow guarantee rate of different hydrological stations in the same month.

As shown in Fig. 10(a), the 12 months were clustered into two distinct groups based on ecological flow guarantee rates along the Xijiang River mainstream. Group A spanned November to the following June, whereas Group B covered July to October. When ecological flow guarantee rate variations across stations for the same months were considered (Fig. 10(b)), guarantee rates from November to the following June were relatively high at all four stations, with average values generally exceeding 90%. During this period, the proposed hydrological targets were met more frequently. This pattern may support ecological functioning. However, during the flood season (July to October), the runoff guarantee rate continued to be low, usually below 85%, and even below 80% for several months. This model showed that the ecological flow of the entire basin during this period was generally insufficient, reflecting the insufficient amount of water to meet the ecological needs. Given that this period overlaps to a considerable extent with the fish spawning and rearing season (April to September), it was defined as the ecologically sensitive period of the river ecosystem.

The above analysis shows that during the runoff change period, the ecological flow guarantee rate had decreased to varying degrees compared with the natural period. Spatially, the decline in the ecological flow guarantee rate of Tian’e Station and Qianjiang Station in the upper reaches of the Xijiang River was more significant, indicating that the region should give priority to protecting the ecological flow. In terms of time, the most significant decline in the ecological flow guarantee rate was from July to October. At this time, the ecological flow guarantee rate was generally low, indicating that this period was a sensitive period of river ecological flow. During this period, the water demand in the basin was still relatively high, and the storage effect of the cascade reservoir continued to adversely affect the ecological health of rivers in the lower reaches. In addition, climate change has led to a decrease in the total amount of water in the basin, and the protection of the river’s ecological flow is facing serious challenges. Therefore, in addition to meeting the demand for production and domestic water, the Xijiang River Basin should also be committed to maintaining the demand for natural ecological flow in the basin. Accordingly, priority should be given to ecological flow protection in the upper reaches, and particular attention should be paid to maintaining ecological flow during the flood season.

Several limitations should be noted. First, runoff reconstruction relied on a limited set of meteorological predictors, and additional climate variables and catchment descriptors could further improve the representation of runoff generating processes and reduce uncertainty. Second, ecological flow estimation was conducted from a hydrological perspective, and future work should incorporate habitat and biological response information to establish explicit ecohydrological relationships and derive biologically informed ecological flow. Third, runoff nonstationarity may occur in the climate runoff relationship across the detected change point. Time ordered evaluation within the natural period suggests satisfactory generalization of the random forest model. However, the mapping can deviate under substantial reservoir operation, land use change, or shifts in runoff generation processes that are not fully represented by meteorological predictors alone. Accordingly, human impacts are inferred primarily from systematic deviations between observed and reconstructed runoff. Future work could reduce this uncertainty by incorporating consistent anthropogenic covariates when such data become available. Examples include land use indicators, water withdrawal proxies, and reservoir operation descriptors. Fourth, ecological flow and guarantee rates are derived from month specific distribution curve fitting. They can be sensitive to the selected distribution. Three goodness-of-fit tests were applied and an explicit rank-based rule was used to select the optimal distribution. However, more than one candidate distribution can remain statistically acceptable and can yield slightly different quantile estimates. This distribution selection uncertainty can propagate to ecological flow estimates and guarantee rate calculations. Future work could quantify this effect through sensitivity analyses across the top ranked admissible distributions.

Conclusions

This study combines quasi-natural runoff reconstruction with a monthly probabilistic framework to quantify ecological flow and ecological flow guarantee rates along the mainstream of the Xijiang River. The approach explicitly accounts for runoff nonstationarity by training a random forest model in the natural period and applying it to reconstruct quasi-natural runoff in the change period, and it selects optimal monthly distributions using multiple goodness-of-fit tests to support ecological flow estimation at a 90% guarantee rate. The derived ecological flows were evaluated using the Tennant method, supporting their ecological relevance. The main findings are as follows:

Natural monthly runoff across the four stations was most frequently best described by the GEV and P-III distributions, indicating that these distributions provide a reliable basis for month specific ecological flow estimation in the study region.

Changes in ecological flow guarantee rates at the Tian’e and Qianjiang stations in the upper reaches were more pronounced than those at the Wuxuan and Wuzhou stations, suggesting that ecological flow pressure and its drivers were stronger in the upper reaches.

Compared with the natural period, the ecological flow guarantee rate had decreased during the change period, and the period with the largest decrease was concentrated from July to October. This period was identified as a key ecologically sensitive period, and the river section in the upper reaches was prioritized for ecological flow protection and adaptive reservoir operation.

This period was identified as a key ecologically sensitive period, and the river section in the upper reaches was prioritized for ecological flow protection and adaptive reservoir operation.

The key innovation of this study lies in training a random forest model during the natural period and applying it during the change period to reconstruct quasi-natural runoff, together with a month by month and station by station probabilistic framework that selects optimal distributions using multiple goodness-of-fit tests to estimate ecological flow at a 90% guarantee rate. Practically, the framework identifies priority river reaches and months with elevated ecological flow deficits to support adaptive reservoir operation and water allocation, thereby alleviating ecological stress in regulated river systems and advancing sustainable water and freshwater ecosystem management.
