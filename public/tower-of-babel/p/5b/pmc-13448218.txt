Multiparticle entanglement of nuclear spins in silicon
Authors: Feng Xu, Yi Deng, Guangchong Hu, Kada Yang, Chunhui Li, Chao Wei, Shihang Zhang, Chunhui Zhang, Peng Du, Keji Shi, Mingchao Duan, Tianluo Pan, Zhen Tian, Peihao Huang, Guanyong Wang, Song Liu, Dapeng Yu, Yu He, Tao Xin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Silicon spin qubits offer high-fidelity control and semiconductor manufacturing compatibility. However, the systematic generation and characterization of multiparticle entanglement, a core resource for quantum information science, remain elusive in such systems. Here, we demonstrate the complete preparation of all nine canonical families of four-qubit entanglement between donor spins in silicon, by designing hardware-efficient quantum circuits that use native multi-qubit operations. We employ advanced overlapping tomography and multifaceted verification to fully characterize these states. Our results confirm genuine multiparticle entanglement, resolve their entanglement structure, and assess their robustness under qubit loss. Moreover, we observe significant violations of multiparticle Bell-type inequalities for genuine four-qubit nonlocality. On the prepared Greenberger-Horne-Zeilinger state, we further uncover a noise-induced entanglement dynamics from distillable to bound and finally to separable, with bound entanglement unlocked via joint measurement. This work generates diverse entanglement resources alongside their complete characterization, establishing a foundation for entanglement-enabled quantum applications in silicon.

Introduction

Spin qubits in silicon are a highly promising candidate for implementing quantum information processing tasks1,2. This platform has demonstrated essential characteristics such as long coherence times3 and high-fidelity initialization4, readout5–7, and gate operations above the fault-tolerant threshold8–13. The compatibility with advanced semiconductor nanofabrication8,14 and elevated-temperature operations9,15–17 makes them particularly attractive. These technical advances also enable the demonstration of few-qubit quantum prototypes18–20, which stand on the brink of large-scale integration. However, practical quantum applications, including quantum computational advantage, enhanced sensing, and secure networks, still require various forms of multiparticle entanglement resources in experiments. As the field advances toward large-scale arrays and logical qubits21–23, systematic generation and characterization of entanglement have become imperative for entanglement-enabled applications.

To date, multiparticle entanglement remains underdeveloped, and the scope of accessible entanglement in silicon spin qubits has been confined to easily generated and verified but fragile Greenberger-Horne-Zeilinger (GHZ) states. Even for these states, in-depth entanglement characterization beyond three qubits remains to be achieved. For instance, in gate-defined quantum dots, three-qubit GHZ states have been demonstrated with 88% fidelity24, and the four-qubit GHZ state was initially demonstrated via the observation of parity oscillations19. Recently, a four-qubit GHZ state achieved 63.8% fidelity via state tomography25. In donor spins in silicon, following the demonstration of three-qubit entanglement12,18, the GHZ entanglement has recently been extended to eight qubits21, verified with the fidelity-based entanglement witness. Moreover, observations of quantum nonlocality are still restricted to two-qubit systems26,27. As systems grow, entanglement types diversify, and entanglement structures become increasingly complex, where we not only need to detect (genuine multiparticle) entanglement but also identify how the qubits are entangled. This poses significant challenges for both the generation and characterization of multiparticle entanglement. Thus, establishing the capability for generating diverse entanglement resources beyond GHZ states and employing advanced characterization methods is crucial for advancing towards various quantum information processing tasks using spin qubits in silicon.

Here, we generate diverse four-qubit entanglement among the nuclei on a silicon donor quantum processor that integrates five phosphorus nuclear spins with a shared electron spin. Hyperfine couplings between electron and nuclear spins enable programmable multi-qubit operations across nuclear spins through single electron spin control12,18. Utilizing these native operations, we develop and implement hardware-efficient circuits to prepare representative four-qubit entangled states from all nine entanglement families, and reconstruct density matrices via efficient overlapping tomography. We characterize entanglement separability using witness operators and negativity, and quantify their entanglement structure and entanglement robustness differences under qubit loss. The prepared GHZ state exhibits the violations of multiparticle Bell-type inequalities. Moreover, we generate noise-induced four-qubit bound entanglement on the prepared GHZ state and achieve 7-fold entanglement distillation. This work establishes comprehensive generation and characterization of diverse four-qubit entanglement in silicon spin systems.

Results

Four-qubit entanglement generation

The silicon quantum processor is constructed using a bottom-up fabrication approach that combines scanning tunneling microscopy (STM) hydrogen lithography with nanofabrication18,28,29. On an isotopically purified 28Si substrate, STM is employed to pattern a hydrogen-resist mask with atomic precision. This mask defines open windows for doping phosphorus donor dots, as well as the structure of a SET and in-plane electrodes. Then, a 20 nm epitaxial 28Si layer is subsequently grown to encapsulate the atomic-scale structure, after which Ohmic contacts and a broadband coplanar waveguide antenna are fabricated for electron spin resonance (ESR) and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) control. Figures 1a, b depict our silicon quantum processor, comprising a quantum dot (one electron spin and five phosphorus nuclear spins), a SET, and seven gate electrodes (G1–G5, source S, and drain D) in the STM plane. Here, the electron spin serves as a central mediator, coupling with the nuclear spins through hyperfine interactions, which enable independent addressability and all-to-all coupling of the nuclear spins. Four-qubit entanglement characterization

To characterize the prepared states, we perform quantum state tomography (QST) to reconstruct their density matrices. The standard four-qubit QST typically requires measurements over the full set {X, Y, Z}⊗4 containing 34 = 81 Pauli operators and is exponentially challenging for larger systems. Here, we employ a reconstruction protocol based on overlapping tomography34,35, which utilizes measurements over only 15 chosen Pauli operators and substantially reduces the measurement overhead (see “Methods”). The corresponding measurements can reconstruct the four-qubit density matrices via tensor network learning (see Supplementary Sec. V). This yields a compact tensor network representation of the reconstructed state, whose projective distributions on the Pauli operators most closely match the measurement data. This method delivers higher reconstruction precision than standard QST while requiring fewer measurement settings and sampling complexity. Figure 2 presents the reconstructed density matrices. Genuine four-qubit nonlocality

On the prepared GHZ state, we test multiparticle Bell-type inequalities for genuine multipartite nonlocality. Certifying nonlocality goes beyond entanglement quantification and is critical for distributed quantum computing and quantum networks36. Bell inequality violations for two-qubit nonlocality have previously been reported in silicon-based systems26,27. Here, we experimentally demonstrate violations of multiparticle Bell-type inequalities under different assumptions (see Supplementary Sec. VI). Despite potential loopholes, the observed violations certify the faithful generation, control, and readout of multiparticle entangled states in silicon.

A key property of the Smolin state is that when qubits are locally operated, any pair of them will approach a diagonal mixed state with no residual entanglement. Figure 4d shows that the residual concurrence between N3–N4 is merely 0.08 when local measurements are performed on N1–N2 (see Supplementary Fig. S13 for the density matrices of N3–N4). However, their entanglement can be greatly distilled when joint Bell-state measurements are permitted. In the experiments, qubits N1–N2 underwent a joint Bell-state measurement following the preparation of the Smolin state, which is realized by a Bell-state entangling circuit with subsequent projective measurements on the computational basis. Based on the measurement outcomes c1 and c2 of N1–N2, we reconstruct the conditional density matrices of N3-N4, as shown in Fig. 4e. These states exhibit an average fidelity of 90.0% with the one expected from the experimental ρS, assuming a perfect Bell-state measurement. Figure 4d reports an average concurrence of 0.58 after joint measurement, achieving a 7-fold entanglement distillation.

As illustrated in Fig. 4f, with p increasing, the system enters a bound entangled regime (positive \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\mathcal{N}}}}_{1:3}$$\end{document}N1:3 and zero \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\mathcal{N}}}}_{2:2}$$\end{document}N2:2) at p ∈ [0.40, 0.60]. These special entanglement properties render the Smolin bound-entangled state a valuable resource for future quantum information tasks in silicon.

Discussion

In this work, we report a donor-based silicon quantum processor featuring five nuclear spins with all-to-all connectivity and realize diverse multi-qubit entangling gates between them. Utilizing intrinsic multi-qubit operations, we design efficient circuits for representative four-qubit entanglement states, enabling us to experimentally generate and benchmark these states, thereby verifying genuine and bound entanglement while uncovering differences in entanglement robustness. This work defines state-of-the-art entanglement capabilities in solid-state spin qubits across multiple dimensions: diversity of entangled states, entanglement quality, entanglement nonlocality, and entanglement distillability. The breakthrough shatters the system’s decade-long confinement to producing only GHZ entanglement and significantly expands the scope of achievable entanglement. The demonstrated coherent control of spins in silicon and the ability to generate diverse entangled states with different entanglement properties constitute a key advance toward harnessing entanglement as a resource for multi-qubit algorithms and applications in silicon quantum platforms in the future, such as quantum simulation and advantage53, quantum metrology54, and quantum networks55. The realization of such applications will become increasingly feasible with the ongoing scaling of these systems, driven by both academic and industrial efforts.

The phosphorus donor cluster utilized in experiments aligns with the scalable donor-cluster array architecture, in which neighboring clusters are connected via exchange interactions, a vision supported by recent theoretical and experimental developments21,22,30. The state characterization method combines quantum overlap tomography with much gentler measurement scaling and efficient tensor network representations, making it scalable to larger systems. It is worth noting that our circuit design method is suited for preparing representative entangled states rather than arbitrary states and relies on classical simulation of quantum circuits. Combined with efficient simulation methods, such as tensor networks56 and sparse state simulation57, the method can in principle, be extended to moderately larger systems. Given the rapid progress in silicon spin qubits, this work provides valuable insights for future silicon-based quantum computing.

Methods

Experimental setup

In our quantum device, the SET, capacitively coupled to the donor, serves as a high-sensitivity charge sensor that enables single-shot readout of the electron spin state via spin-to-charge conversion. The electrostatic gates G1-G5 provide precise tuning of both the donor and SET potentials, allowing deterministic electron loading, emptying and readout. Microwave and radio-frequency (RF) pulses are delivered via the on-chip antenna to facilitate coherent control for NMR and ESR. The sample is mounted on the cold finger of a dilution refrigerator (Bluefors, LD400) with a base temperature of 15 mK. A static in-plane magnetic field of 1.35 T is applied along the z-axis to induce Zeeman splitting of both electron and nuclear spins. The source of the SET is connected in series with an LC tank circuit resonant at 302.8 MHz. An RF signal generated by a signal source is split by a power divider; one path is sent to the SET source electrode, while the other serves as the local oscillator for demodulating the reflected signal. The reflected RF signal is first amplified at 4 K using a cryogenic low-noise amplifier, followed by a second-stage amplification at room temperature. The amplified signal is then demodulated using an IQ demodulator (Polyphase, AD0105B). The demodulated output is further amplified, then filtered using a programmable analog filter (SRS, SIM965) with a cutoff frequency of 49.9 kHz, and digitized using a 16-bit digitizer (Spectrum Instrumentation, M2p.5911-x4) at a sampling rate of 500 kHz. The DC gate bias is provided by a low-noise digital-to-analog converter (Basel, SP1060), which was also integrated with an arbitrary waveform generator (AWG) module. High-speed voltage pulses generated by the AWG are combined with the DC bias on the same output channel and filtered through a 60 kHz low-pass filter before being applied to the control electrode. Microwave pulses for electron spin control are generated by a vector signal generator (Keysight, M9384B), while RF pulses for nuclear spin manipulation are generated by an AWG (Zurich Instruments, HDAWG). The two drive signals are combined via a diplexer (Raditek, DPX-1721) and delivered to the on-chip antenna.

As shown in Fig. 1f, the multi-qubit controlled gates among nuclear spins are realized by the ESR-based geometric gates. In designing circuits, due to the relatively weak coupling of A4 ≈ 226 kHz compared to the electron spin driving frequency employed, we adopt distinct strategies for different types of multi-qubit controlled gates. Specifically, when multi-qubit controlled gates do not depend on the spin-up and spin-down states of N4, we directly apply one broadband ESR pulse, where the time required for a 2π rotation gate is 1.35 μs. Otherwise, multi-qubit controlled gates face a cross-talk problem. A straightforward approach to mitigate this frequency cross-talk is to minimize the ESR Rabi frequency to the lowest practicable level. For example, directly applying a hard pulse at the ESR transition frequency of the N4 spin-up state necessitates lowering the electron Rabi frequency to approximately 50 kHz. This reduction aims to prevent cross-talk with the N4 spin-down state. However, such a low Rabi frequency becomes unacceptable given the electron’s coherence time of roughly 23 μs. Therefore, we employ a “mirror-pulse” method to suppress cross-talk without significantly compromising gate speed. As an example, consider frequency addressing of the N4 spin-down state (resonant state) in the circuit. Due to the weak coefficient A4, excessively fast ESR operations can induce cross-talk on the undesired N4 spin-up state (non-resonant state), accumulating unwanted geometric phases in the non-target subspace. To mitigate this effect, in addition to applying the ESR pulse to the resonant state, we also apply a mirror pulse whose frequency is obtained by mirroring the transition frequency of the resonant state about that of the non-resonant state. This ensures that the unwanted phases acquired in the non-resonant subspace are canceled by the mirror pulse. The influence of the mirror pulse on the resonant state must satisfy the condition \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$2\pi m/{f}_{R}=2\pi k/\sqrt{{f}_{R}^{2}+{\Delta }^{2}}$$\end{document}2πm/fR=2πk/fR2+Δ2, where m is an odd integer, k is an even integer, fR is the resonant Rabi frequency, and Δ denotes the detuning frequency between the frequency of the resonant state and the mirror pulse. Here, m = 1, k = 2, and Δ = 2A4. It is worth noting that pulse shaping is also a versatile method capable of significantly mitigating the impact of various types of noise, including cross-talk errors58. Compared to the mirror-pulse technique, it suppresses a broader range of noise types with high bandwidth, but it carries the risk of excessively high instantaneous power and imposes stricter requirements on waveform design and its high-precision implementation59. In our current fabrication process, the antenna on our device cannot yet withstand the highly concentrated peak power generated during pulse shaping, which poses a significant risk of burning out the antenna. Conversely, if we were to limit the peak power for pulse shaping, the pulse duration would often need to be extended, which would inadvertently lead to increased electron decoherence. This is the reason we currently rely on the mirror-pulse technique for cross-talk suppression in our system.

To minimize cross-talk between nuclear spins during NMR driving, when performing a π/2 gate operation on N4, the operation time must be designed such that N4 completes a π/2 rotation while N5 simultaneously undergoes a 2π rotation. This ensures that the driving speed of N4 remains relatively fast while minimizing cross-talk with N5. In addition, although the coherence time of nuclear spins is longer than that of electrons, the deep circuit used in experiments necessitates optimization. For example, entanglement operations for nuclei with shorter coherence times are scheduled later to minimize idle time. NMR operations on different nuclei are driven simultaneously when possible, taking pulse heating effects into account. It is known that pulse-induced heating can affect qubit properties, including coherence time and frequency shifts. These effects have indeed been reported across different semiconductor quantum platforms12,21,22,60–62. In our experiments, although we did not specifically study the pulse-induced effects on coherence time and frequency shifts, we took strategies in advance to mitigate them as much as possible. For example, we reduced power or applied pulses sequentially as much as possible. Consequently, combining these methods helps reduce the impact of pulse-induced heating on our current experiments. Further discussion of this effect is detailed in Supplementary Sec. VIII.

Quantum entangling circuit design

We employ a breadth-first search (BFS) algorithm to design quantum circuits that prepare specific four-qubit states drawn from all nine families of entangled states, starting from the fixed initial product state. Quantum states in the Hilbert space are represented as nodes in a graph, where edges correspond to quantum gate operations from a predefined set. In detail, the state \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left|\phi \right\rangle={\sum }_{i=1}^{16}{\alpha }_{i}\left|i\right\rangle$$\end{document}ϕ=∑i=116αii, where αi is the superposition coefficient, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left|i\right\rangle$$\end{document}i denotes the computational basis of the four-qubit Hilbert space, is encoded into each node of the BFS algorithm using only those computational basis elements for which αi ≠ 0. Each node is uniquely identified by a computational basis term present in the state. It is important to note that a clear distinction exists between the physical state \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left|\phi \right\rangle$$\end{document}ϕ and its BFS-encoded counterpart ϕ, where the latter lacks information about the superposition coefficients. After the BFS procedure, further manipulations are required on the resulting output circuit to reconstruct the target state \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left|\phi \right\rangle$$\end{document}ϕ (see Supplementary Sec. IV). The gate set comprises single-qubit gates and three- and four-qubit controlled gates applied to nuclear spins. The initial and target states are treated as distinct nodes, and BFS is used to identify the shortest path (in terms of gate count) connecting them.

Once an initial circuit structure is obtained via BFS, a post-processing step is applied if the superposition coefficients of the resulting state deviate from those of the target: fixed-angle gates are replaced with parameterized counterparts, and their parameters are optimized to accurately match the target state’s coefficients. Before implementing these circuits in experiments, they are further optimized to enhance the final state fidelity while accounting for physical constraints such as qubit coherence times and gate durations. For example, the order of entanglement between nuclei is determined based on their coherence times. In addition, some single-qubit NMR operations are executed simultaneously rather than sequentially to further shorten the circuit time. Since BFS operates with a finite set of fixed-angle gates, it is well-suited for preparing specific entangled states rather than arbitrary four-qubit states. This makes the approach particularly efficient for benchmarking multiparticle entanglement through the preparation of representative entangled states. Additional details and discussions are provided in Supplementary Sec. IV.

The concurrence is a computable entanglement measure that quantifies quantum correlations in bipartite systems. Its most significant application is in two-qubit systems, where it provides an analytical solution for entanglement quantification. For any bipartite state ρ, the concurrence is defined as: 7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C(\rho )=\max \left\{0,\sqrt{{\lambda }_{1}}-\sqrt{{\lambda }_{2}}-\sqrt{{\lambda }_{3}}-\sqrt{{\lambda }_{4}}\right\},$$\end{document}C(ρ)=max0,λ1−λ2−λ3−λ4,where λi are the eigenvalues of the matrix R = ρ(Y ⊗ Y)ρ*(Y ⊗ Y) in decreasing order, and ρ* denotes the complex conjugate of ρ. If C = 0, the two-qubit state is separable (unentangled). If C > 0, the state is entangled. C = 1 indicates maximal entanglement. The concurrence serves to benchmark the entanglement of both: (i) the remaining qubits following the loss of two qubits, and (ii) the distilled two-qubit states in the Smolin experiment.

Estimation of uncertainties

In our experiments, we estimate the statistical uncertainty of all derived quantities using a Monte Carlo bootstrap resampling approach, following the method detailed in Huang et al.63. Below, we elaborate the procedure step by step: (i) For each measurement setting, the observed outcomes (e.g., nuclear spin configurations ranging from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left|\Downarrow \Downarrow \Downarrow \Downarrow \right\rangle$$\end{document}⇓⇓⇓⇓ to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left|\Uparrow \Uparrow \Uparrow \Uparrow \right\rangle$$\end{document}⇑⇑⇑⇑) are assumed to follow a multinomial distribution. If a projective measurement has K possible outcomes, the probabilities of these outcomes are pk with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sum }_{k=1}^{K}{p}_{k}=1$$\end{document}∑k=1Kpk=1. The parameters pk of this multinomial distribution are directly parameterized using the experimentally observed average probabilities. (ii) We generate 2000 synthetic datasets by random sampling and then calculate the mean and standard deviation of these datasets. The standard deviation is taken as the statistical uncertainty, and all reported uncertainties correspond to one standard deviation (1σ) from the mean.
