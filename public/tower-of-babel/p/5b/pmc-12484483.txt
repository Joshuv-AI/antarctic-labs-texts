Effect of Repetitive Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation on the Symptoms and Brain Imaging in Patients with Fibromyalgia Syndrome: A Randomized Controlled Pilot Trial
Authors: Laura Ackermann, Daniel Zeller, Thorsten Odorfer, György A Homola, Thomas Kampf, Mirko Pham, Hans-Christoph Aster, Claudia Sommer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionRepetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) of the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) has been shown to reduce the symptoms of patients with fibromyalgia syndrome (FMS). We tested whether rTMS of the left DLPFC can reduce the main symptoms in FMS and whether TMS induces changes in brain functional and structural connectivity, cortical gray matter volume, and the metabolites/neurotransmitters GABA and combined glutamate/glutamine (Glx).MethodsTwenty-seven women diagnosed with FMS according to the 2010 ACR criteria were included in a randomized controlled trial. They received either ten sessions of active or sham 10-Hz stimulation over 2 weeks embedded in a longitudinal neuroimaging setup, including one pre-treatment (T1), one post-treatment (T2), and one follow-up (T3) 3 T MRI scan. Pain and pain catastrophizing, depression, daily life/quality of life, and anxiety were assessed using standard questionnaires.ResultsLinear mixed-model analysis of clinical data showed a significant main effect of the main factor time but did not reveal group differences or group–time interactions, indicating a large placebo effect with symptom reduction in both groups.Fractional anisotropy (FA) values of the pontine crossing tract, the sagittal stratum, and the right cingulum in the active rTMS group increased between pre-TMS and the follow-up time points. Subgroup analysis of responders of the treatment group revealed higher functional connectivity between the left DLPFC and the right cerebellum. We did not find evidence for changes in the treatment group in the gray matter of the left DLPFC and for the concentrations of GABA and Glx, but a trend towards decreasing Glx levels for the factor time in all patients could be detected.ConclusionsWhile these results may be due to small sample size and short treatment duration, the findings of increased FA after active rTMS and higher functional connectivity between DLPFC and cerebellum in responders should be further explored.Trial RegistrationAuswirkungen der nicht-invasiven Neuromodulation auf das Gehirn bei Fibromyalgiepatienten. Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? Treatment of patients with fibromyalgia syndrome (FMS) is often unsatisfactory, with a need for new therapeutic approaches.We hypothesized that high-frequency repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (HF-rTMS) of the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex reduces the pain and accompanying symptoms in FMS and causes changes in the brain that can be measured by multimodal brain imaging. What was learned from this study? Our data revealed a significant reduction of symptoms in both the treatment and sham groups, indicating a strong placebo effect of sham rTMS.Responders in the treatment group revealed higher functional connectivity between the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and the right cerebellum.Thus, fronto-cerebellar circuits should be further explored as a potential marker for treatment response. Although rTMS over the left DLPFC led to neurobiological changes, no clinically meaningful advantage over sham stimulation was observed, suggesting limited therapeutic utility for this target in FMS.Studies with alternative targets should have a larger sample size, and extended stimulation protocols are needed to investigate and confirm neurobiological alterations in the brain due to rTMS.

Introduction

Patients with fibromyalgia syndrome (FMS) suffer from chronic widespread pain and associated symptoms like sleep disturbances, fatigue, and cognitive dysfunction. Comorbid depressive and anxiety disorders may also be present [1, 2]. The pathogenesis is assumed to involve changes in the central and peripheral nervous system and to be influenced by genetic, immunological, and environmental factors [3]. However, a clearly defined etiology remains elusive, and the condition is likely to be etiologically heterogeneous [4]. A multimodal treatment approach including non-pharmacological strategies, such as patient education, aerobic exercise, cognitive behavioral therapy, and pharmacological treatment—adapted to comorbidities—has been recommended [2]. The effectiveness of pharmaceuticals is only moderate, and the acceptance by patients is low [5, 6]. Therefore, non-pharmacological therapy has been recommended [7, 8]. Given the only moderate effect size of current treatment options, new, effective therapies for FMS are needed.

Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) is a technique of non-invasive neuromodulation [9] that has been shown to be effective in reducing pain [10], e.g., in neuropathic [11–13] and postoperative [14] pain conditions, chronic migraine [15], and FMS [16–19]. Moreover, analgesic effects of rTMS over the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) were reported in patients with major depression [20].

In patients with FMS, high-frequency rTMS (HF-rTMS) has been applied successfully over the DLPFC [17, 21, 22] (mostly left side) as well as over the left motor cortex (M1) [16, 23–27]. Different stimulation paradigms have been used (for review, see [28–30]). The effect of rTMS in FMS is discussed controversially. Specifically, the question of whether stimulation of the motor cortex or the DLPFC gives better results is still not settled [28, 31], unlike in neuropathic pain, where direct comparison favored motor cortex stimulation [13].

Given the changes in the CNS of patients with FMS, as recently also reported by our group [32], the potential effects of rTMS on the brain, as examined by MRI techniques, are of high interest. It is conceivable that some of the differences in brain structure and connectivity between FMS patients and controls might be reversed by rTMS. The DLPFC has a central role in processing pain and its cognitive perception, and functional as well as structural alterations in this region are associated with chronic pain [33]. Pain reduction upon targeting the DLPFC could be explained by the activation of descending modulatory systems (opioidergic), cognitive and affective modulation of pain, or a combination of these. In conclusion, there is good evidence for choosing the left DLPFC as a therapeutic target for rTMS in FMS [33]. We therefore designed this study to investigate the effects of HF-rTMS of the left DLPFC on resting-state functional connectivity (rs-FC), white brain matter, cortical volume, and metabolite levels and their association with clinical symptoms in patients with FMS. We chose (f)MRI because of its high spatial resolution.

We hypothesized that HF-rTMS of the left DLPFC would improve FMS symptoms, increase gray matter volume of the left DLPFC, integrity of white brain matter, and rs-FC in patients with FMS, thereby facilitating a concomitant increase in inhibitory neurotransmission.

Methods

Study Design

We conducted a randomized controlled pilot trial with 27 women with FMS between October 2019 and September 2020, with a 3-month break between March and June 2020 due to the COVID-19 pandemic. The trial was registered in the German Clinical Trial Register (DRKS-ID: DRKS00019051). On the first day (“pre-rTMS”, T1), patients received an MRI scan of the brain with sequences described previously [32]. All MRI acquisitions included sequences for the research question and an additional sequence to rule out morphological pathologies. Fourteen patients were randomized into the treatment (group A) and 13 into the sham group (group B). Since the side effects induced by true rTMS [34] markedly differ from those associated with sham stimulation, a study design in which patients receive both types of stimulation, interrupted by a wash-out period, was not feasible. Patients were blinded, while the investigator was not. The first out of ten (active/sham) rTMS sessions started after determination of the resting motor threshold (RMT). Because interindividual differences in cortical excitability can be substantial, assessing the resting motor threshold (RMT) is essential to calibrate stimulation intensity in a standardized and participant-specific manner. This ensures that the applied stimulation reliably reaches the cortical threshold while maintaining safety and comparability across individuals. Five treatment days were followed by 2 days of rest. After completing the last five sessions, another MRI scan of the brain was performed at the end of the second week (“post-rTMS”, T2). Side effects were monitored closely by a daily structured interview. To investigate potential long-term effects of HF-rTMS, a third scan (“follow-up”, T3) took place 8–18 weeks after the second MRI. In 19 subjects, imaging could be performed as planned after 8 weeks. Due to the COVID-19 pandemic, seven follow-up scans were delayed after the first lockdown in 2020, which led to a latency from 12 to 18 weeks between the second and third MRI scan in these patients; one subject dropped out for the last scan. Clinical parameters were assessed before the first (“pre-rTMS”, T1) and directly after the tenth rTMS session (“post-rTMS”, T2) as well as 8 weeks after the last session (“follow-up”, T3). Delayed follow-up scans were included in the analysis to preserve statistical power (n = 26, one drop-out at the follow-up). Clinical follow-up data were successfully collected in time for 27 participants.

Subjects

Sample size calculation was based on an assumed probability of the alpha error of 5% and a calculated effect size of 0.7. This cumulative size is based on published data on effects of rTMS on fMRI and pain levels [17, 35] as well as MRI parameters (neuroimaging modalities, regions of interest, signal variance within the MRI sequence, and inter-subject variability) [36–39]. Twenty-nine female patients with FMS aged between 40 and 65 years (mean age 53.3 ± 7.0 years) were recruited at the Department of Neurology at the University Hospital of Würzburg. They were randomized into sham or treatment by the shuffle function from the random module of Python. Figure 1 describes the process of enrollment, randomization, and the study design. All patients had taken part in a previous study on small fiber pathology [40], and some of them also had participated in the previous neuroimaging study investigating CNS characteristics of patients with FMS with or without peripheral nerve pathology [32]. The participants had been diagnosed with FMS according to the ACR criteria of 2010 [41]. Severe comorbidities and possible differential diagnoses had been excluded: pain of origin other than FMS, polyneuropathy, current infection, relevant rheumatic or autoimmune disease, malignancy (within the past 5 years), severe neurological or psychiatric illnesses requiring treatment, drug/alcohol abuse, untreated endocrinological diseases, pregnancy and suicide attempts in the past 2 years [40]. Further exclusion criteria included contraindications for rTMS and MRI, such as a history of epilepsy or seizures, and medical conditions that elevate the risk of seizures, as well as intracranial metallic materials and other implants not labeled as MRI-compatible [42]. MRI scans of the brain had been checked for structural abnormalities prior to the first TMS session. None of the subjects had to be excluded because of abnormalities in the MRI. Pain medication had to be continued without modification during the 4 weeks before the first examination until the last examination.

On the days when MRI acquisitions took place, no pain medication was taken prior to the measurement to avoid interference with brain metabolism. Questionnaires were assessed on days with the regular intake of the home medication. All patients gave written informed consent to the study according to the Declaration of Helsinki. The Ethics Committee of the University of Würzburg Medical Faculty approved the study (140/19).

HF-rTMS Protocol

HF-rTMS was applied using the MagPro X100 stimulator (Medtronic A/S 2740 Skovlunde, Denmark) equipped with an MC-B70 figure-of-eight coil. Left DLPFC (MNI coordinates X, Y, Z = – 43.00, 21.00, 38.00 mm) [43] was located using T1-weighted (T1w) MPRAGE sequences (isotropic voxel size of 1 × 1 × 1 mm) within a neuro-navigation system (Brainsight, Rogue Research, Montreal, Canada). Sham stimulation was performed by placing the coil at a 45° angle to the skull. When the coil is placed parallel to the skull, lines of flux of the magnetic field are induced perpendicular to the coil [8]. Therefore, the magnetic field does not reach the cortex when the coil is positioned at a 45° angle. In total, 1600 stimuli were applied in 40 trains of 10-Hz stimulation at RMT over 20 min (4 s on, 26 s off). Each participant received one daily session over 10 working days. RMT was defined as the minimum stimulation intensity over the left M1 required to elicit motor-evoked potentials (MEPs) amplitudes of at least 50 µV in ≥ 5 out of ten trials. Electromyography (EMG) was recorded from the right first dorsal interosseous muscle using surface electrodes [44].

Data Acquisition

Clinical Data

Clinical data were assessed at baseline (T1), post-rTMS (T2), and a third time as follow-up (T3). Pain intensity/catastrophizing scores were assessed by a numeric rating scale (NRS) (0 = no pain, 10 = worst pain, with distances considered to be equal) [45], the German version of the Neuropathic Pain Symptom Inventory (NPSI-G) and the Pain Catastrophizing Scale (PCS) [46–48]. Depressive symptoms were evaluated by the German version of the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D Scale) [49] called “Allgemeine Depressionsskala” (ADS) with a cut-off value of ≥ 23 for depression. To assess FMS symptoms and quality of life, the Fibromyalgia Impact Questionnaire (FIQ) was used [50]. Anxiety symptoms were investigated by applying the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI-S and STAI-T) [51].

MRI Data

Neuroimaging was acquired on a 3 T Siemens MAGNETOM Prisma fit Scanner (Siemens Healthcare GmbH, Erlangen, Germany) with a 64-channel head coil at the Department of Neuroradiology, University Hospital Würzburg. Structural T1-weighted (T1w) sequence, diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), fieldmap data, resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI) series and MEGA-PRESS (SVS) for spectroscopy (https://www.cmrr.umn.edu/spectro/) were performed. The exact protocols have been published recently [32, 52].

Analysis of MR Data

Brain Morphometry

Cortical reconstruction and volumetric segmentation were conducted on T1w images with the FreeSurfer image analysis suite v6.0.0 (Martinos Center for Biomedical Imaging, Boston, MA, USA, https://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu). The technical details of these procedures are described in prior publications [53, 54]. The Desikan-Killiany Atlas was used for parcellation of the cerebral cortex [55] (ROIs are displayed in the electronic supplementary material 2). Images were automatically processed with the longitudinal FreeSurfer stream to investigate reliable volume and thickness estimates [56–58]. As the region of interest (ROI), the left rostral middle frontal cortex containing the left DLPFC was selected to analyze possible changes in cortical volume induced by HF-rTMS. Volume was measured in mm3.

Structural Connectivity

Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) data were processed with the Oxford Centre for Functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging of the Brain software library (FSL, Oxford, UK, https://www.fmrib.ox.ac.uk) [59]. The same protocol was used that was published in previous work from our group [32]. The ROIs are summarized in the electronic supplementary material 3. We performed an exploratory analysis of all ROIs using a linear mixed model for group differences and changes over time as well as the interaction between group and time.

Functional Connectivity

Resting-state functional connectivity (rs-FC) analysis was performed using SPM12 (Welcome Trust Centre for Neuroimaging, University College London, United Kingdom; https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/) and the CONN Toolbox (RRID:SCR_009550) v19.c (https://www.nitrc.org/projects/conn [60]) running in MATLAB R2020b (The MathWorks Inc, USA). False discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied at the cluster level (p < 0.05). Voxel threshold was p < 0.001.

The electronic supplementary material 4 contains a detailed explanation from all (pre)processing steps to the second-level analysis and a detailed list of the ROIs and seeds.

On the ROI-to-ROI level, an exploratory whole-brain approach was performed, whereas in the seed-to-voxel analysis, the effect of the left DLPFC among the listed regions above was investigated.

Responder analysis was conducted on the ROI-to-ROI and three seed-to-voxel levels, as explained above. Responders were defined as a minimum of 30% score reduction in at least two out of three questionnaires among the treatment group: NRS, FIQ, and ADS. Then a mixed within- and between-subject two-way ANOVA was performed with responders and non-responders.

1H-MR Spectroscopy

Analysis of the MR spectra was performed using the Totally Automatic Robust Quantitation in Nuclear MR (TARQUIN) software version 4.3.11 (https://tarquin.sourceforge.net). TARQUIN is an algorithm for the automatic analysis of short echo-time in vivo 1H-MRS [61, 62]. We extracted the concentrations of neurotransmitters/metabolites of the left and right DLPFC. Based on our hypothesis, statistical analysis was conducted on GABA+ and Glx (combined glutamate and glutamine) in reference to total creatine (tCr) of the left DLPFC. GABA+ was extracted from the differential spectrum (off spectrum – on spectrum). Due to the relatively low concentration of GABA and its spectral overlap with macromolecules and other metabolites, the edited signal acquired using the MEGA-PRESS sequence reflects a composite peak. Therefore, the measured concentration is conventionally referred to as GABA+, indicating that it includes GABA as well as co-edited macromolecular signals and related compounds [63]. As the exact concentrations of glutamate and glutamine are difficult to determine because of overlapping proton resonances and given the sample size, a more restrictive approach was chosen, and therefore only the combined marker Glx is described. We used the relative quantification method with an endogenous marker [64] and therefore defined tCr as a concentration standard showing a stable distribution in the left DLPFC over the different measurements. Quality control measurements for Tarquin are listed in Table S1 in the electronic supplementary material.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and plotted in JASP (JASP Team, 2022), Version 0.16.4, Windows 10). Clinical data, cortical volume, DTI, and metabolic activity were analyzed within a linear mixed model as dependent variables (Dependent Variable ~ Time × Group (1 + Time × Group | ID). The analysis of the factor “time” means changes from pre (T1)- to post-rTMS (T2) or to the follow-up (T3) taken both groups together. The interaction of group and time (factor group × time) stands for changes on the dependent variables between groups over the whole study period. The factor “group” only analyzes the differences between the sham and treatment group across all points in time.

DTI analysis was performed as an exploratory analysis without correction for multiple comparisons. The linear mixed model was chosen because it takes into account that the baseline level of scores may differ from participant to participant. For models with a significant main effect of time, adjustments for multiple comparisons were made using the Bonferroni correction (total of three pairwise comparisons), and adjusted p values are displayed as padj. The confidence interval was set at 95% and the significance level was 0.05.

Pain

Regarding NRS (Fig. 2a, Table 2), the model was highly significant for the fixed factor time, with p < 0.001 (F(2,50) = 8.19). Scores decreased significantly during the stimulation in both groups (padj = 0.01) and then increased again (padj = 0.001).

a Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) and b Neuropathic Pain Symptom Inventory–German (NPSI-G). c Fibromyalgia Impact Questionnaire (FIQ). d Allgemeine Depressionsskala (ADS) sum scores of the treatment and sham group pre-, post-treatment (day 12) and 8 weeks after the last session of rTMS

The NPSI (Fig. 2b, Table 2) was significant for the fixed factor time (p = 0.03, F(2,50) = 3.63). Sum-scores of the NPSI-G of both groups declined significantly pre- to post-rTMS (T1–T2) (padj = 0.03) and then slightly increased again at the follow-up (T3) (padj = 0.57), the difference between pre-rTMS (T1) and follow-up (T3) was not significant (padj = 0.54).

Impact on Everyday Life/Quality of Life and Depression

FIQ (Fig. 2c, Table 2) revealed a highly significant change in scores over time (p < 0.001, F(2,50) = 10.29). After the stimulation (post-rTMS, T2) FIQ scores were lower in the sham and active rTMS group (padj < 0.001), and values rose again at follow-up (T3) compared to post-rTMS (T2) (padj = 0.03).

The ADS score (Fig. 2d, Table 2) showed significant results for the fixed factor time (p < 0.001, F(2,50) = 16.01). Directly after the intervention, the values were reduced compared to the baseline measurement (padj < 0.001) and increased again significantly in the weeks thereafter (padj = 0.003), resulting in no difference in follow-up (T3) depression scores compared to the baseline level (padj = 0.14).

At baseline, 7/14 of the treatment and 10/13 of the sham group were screened positive for symptoms of depression. Post-rTMS (T2) 1/14 of the treatment and 5/13 of the sham had above cut-off values. At follow-up (T3), 6/14 of the treatment and 6/13 of the sham group were above cut-off values.

Pain Catastrophizing and Anxiety

The PCS (Table 2) was significant for the factor time (p = 0.02, F(2,50) = 4.24). Mean values for pain catastrophizing of all subjects together declined significantly between pre-rTMS (T1) and post-rTMS (T2) (padj = 0.03).

Due to the very similar results of the STAI-S and STAI-T, the results of the STAI-S are described here and those of the STAI-T are reported only in Table 2. As in the other questionnaires, there was a significant change over time across all subjects (p = 0.005, F(2,50) = 5.84). The decrease in anxiety scores to a lower level after completion of true or sham stimulations (padj = 0.005) did not show stability 8 weeks later (post-follow-up (T2–T3) padj = 0.90; pre-follow-up (T1–T3) padj = 0.08). None of the questionnaires revealed a group difference or group × time interaction.

Side Effects

Active rTMS did not cause any serious side effects. Commonly reported or observed complaints in the active rTMS group were local pain and tingling at the stimulation site and co-innervation of the trigeminal and facial nerve, resulting in muscle contraction of the mimic muscles (especially the left forehead and eye area). Some patients reported (self-limiting) headaches with a maximum of a few hours. These side effects were not observed by the sham group. They only reported a distinct pressure of the coil on their scalp.

MRI Data

The exploratory character of some of the data should be kept in mind below.

Structural Connectivity: DTI Analysis

Exploratory analysis (not corrected for multiple comparisons) of all 48 ROIs revealed significant results in eight white matter tracts. In the following four tracts, a significant group × time interaction was found (detailed values in Table 3): pontine crossing tract (Fig. 3a; F(2,49.09) = 4.89; p = 0.01), right sagittal stratum (Fig. 3b; F(2,49.05) = 3,46; p = 0.04), right cingulum (hippocampus) (Fig. 3c; F(2,49.12) = 5.47; p = 0.01) and left cingulum (hippocampus) (Fig. 3d; F(2,49.03) = 3.45; p = 0.04). In the right cingulum of the hippocampus, there was an additionally significant main group effect (F(1,25.05) = 10.14), p = 0.004). White matter integrity as measured by fractional anisotropy (FA) values of the pontine crossing tract, the sagittal stratum and the right cingulum in the active rTMS group increased between pre-TMS (T1) and the follow-up (T3), especially between post-rTMS (T2) and the follow-up (T3) compared to the sham group (Fig. 3a–c). In the left cingulum (Fig. 3d), FA values first increased during the active stimulation and then decreased again at the follow-up (T3) to the baseline level, while the FA values of the sham group behaved in opposite ways.

Functional Connectivity

Seed-to-Voxel

A repeated measures ANOVA was performed to investigate the group by scan interaction by comparing connectivity between pre-rTMS (T1) and post/follow-up (T2/T3) differences between patients and controls. No differences could be revealed. The main therapy effects (comparing post-/follow-up- and pre-treatment connectivity measures over the average of all subjects) and main group effects were not found. The results remained negative after accounting for the changes of NPSI, NRS, and FIQ scores in both groups as covariate.

Simple main therapy effects (comparing pre-rTMS/post-rTMS, post-rTMS/follow-up and pre-rTMS/follow-up connectivity measures in either sham or treatment group) were found in both groups (exact data and figures in supplementary material 5.3). From pre- to post-intervention (T1–T2) in the sham group, rs-FC decreased between left DLPFC and a cluster of 187 voxels mainly in the right middle temporal gyrus and supramarginal gyrus (Table S3, Figure S1a).

From pre- to follow-up (T1–T3) in the treatment group, rs-FC increased between left DLPFC and a cluster of 115 voxels in the left lateral occipital cortex and superior parietal lobule (Table S4, Figure S1b).

ROI-to-ROI

The two-way ANOVA did not show any significant results regarding group by scan interaction, main therapy effects and main group effects as explained above. Again, results remained negative after including the changes of NPSI, NRS, and FIQ scores in both groups as covariate.

Simple main therapy effects with increasing rs-FC between pre- and follow-up (T1–T3) measurement were revealed in the treatment group (Fig. 4) between left frontoparietal network, left angular gyrus, left superior frontal gyrus and insular cortex (right and left), supplementary motor cortex (left and right), salience network hub (left and right), right frontal orbital cortex (F(1,12) = 54.60; p-uncorrected = 0.000008; p-FDR (false discovery rate) = 0.004).

Discussion

In this randomized controlled single-blind pilot study, we examined the influence of HF-rTMS over the left DLPFC on the symptoms in patients with FMS in comparison to the effects of sham HF-rTMS on structural and functional connectivity, as well as gray matter volume and metabolic changes at the stimulation site by MRI.

To our knowledge, we are the first to combine a common rTMS protocol with four different modalities of MRI, which leads to an exploratory and hypothesis-generating character of the MRI data. Moreover, as the underlying etiology of fibromyalgia remains insufficiently understood and may vary substantially between individuals [4], interpreting neurobiological findings, especially with respect to treatment effects, remains challenging.

There was no benefit of active treatment compared to the control group, both in terms of major symptoms and associated symptoms (depression and anxiety). We did observe a strong effect of time in both groups with a decrease in symptoms over all patients. This can be interpreted as a placebo effect. In the weeks following the intervention, symptoms mostly worsened again, but did not exceed baseline levels.

Exploratory DTI analysis showed an increase of white matter integrity as measured by FA in the active rTMS group in the right cingulum of the hippocampus, pontocerebellar fibers (part of the middle cerebellar peduncle) and tracts connecting the frontal and temporal lobes with the occipital lobe.

Our fMRI findings propose projections between the left DLPFC and a part of the right cerebellum as candidate marker for rTMS treatment response. HF-rTMS might cause long-term effects of higher rs-FC in pain-processing areas such as frontal, insular, supplementary motor areas and the salience network. MR spectroscopy demonstrated a downward trend of Glx/tCr ratios in both groups.

Clinical Data

Positive results from other rTMS/DLPFC studies on FMS [17, 21, 22] could not be reproduced in our work. Our data align with some studies smaller than ours with similar stimulation protocols performing HF-rTMS of the left DLPFC in FMS [65–67] and showing no differences in clinical outcomes between active and sham group, despite some symptom improvements in the treatment [65] or in both groups [66, 67].

Current guidelines make a level B recommendation of rTMS of the left DLPFC for its probable analgesic efficacy and stimulation of M1 for probable improvement in quality of life [31]. Evidence of HF-rTMS of the left DLPFC in other pain conditions supports the positive recommendation of this therapy. In neuropathic pain, for example, there is a Level A recommendation for HF-rTMS of M1 contralateral to the painful side [31]. Another review highlights rTMS as a safe method with analgesic, antidepressant effects, and reduction of anxiety. To date, the perfect TMS parameters have yet to be determined [30]. However, RCTs used different stimulation protocols, and meta-analyses partially present different views on the optimal stimulation target, parameters [21, 28, 30] and positive effects on symptoms [22, 68, 69]. Our stimulation parameters are based on previous studies of HF-rTMS of the DLPFC or M1 (same parameters in [17, 70], similar in [16, 18, 22, 23, 26, 27, 65, 71] with RMT ≤ 100%). Typical HF-rTMS is performed with 10 Hz, with the number of pulses/session between 1200 and 4000 and RMT between 80 and 120%. Most studies reported ten sessions over 2 weeks, a few up to 15 or 20 over 3–6 weeks (in exceptional cases up to 21 weeks) [28, 30]. Our protocol (10 Hz, 1600 pulses/session at 100% RMT, 10 sessions over 2 weeks) according to [17] was chosen because of the similar study design and to avoid side effects at the stimulation site (e.g., painful innervation of the facial nerve) and overheating of the device.

Although there are more studies in recent years on HF-rTMS over the left M1 than over the left DLPFC, reviews do not favor one over the other [30, 72]. Stimulation of M1 is ascribed a better analgesic [22, 28] and the DLPFC a better antidepressant effect [28]. The opposite view is propagated in another publication [31].

The strong effect of time in most of our clinical data suggests a relevant placebo effect of sham stimulation, so that eventually no beneficial effect of HF-rTMS could be proven. This is exemplified by the average relative reduction of NRS (FIQ) scores after two weeks of intervention: 14% (31%) in the treatment vs. 32% (25%) in the sham group. Comparable reduction of symptom scores in both groups can also be seen in other trials [21, 22, 66]. One study from 2011 revealed reduced average daily pain in the treatment group, but no difference in the average pain scale scores after the intervention compared to sham stimulation. In addition to that, the scores of the daily life-specific symptom score were significantly lower in both groups compared to baseline [67]. The strong placebo effect in our study may be explained by the one-to-one care between the same investigator and patients for the entire study period, which implied discussion of medical history, current medical conditions, and disease management. High expectations and bad experiences with former physicians and levels of personal investment (e.g., long distance from home for almost 2 weeks, taking leave to participate in the study) without everyday stress as well as intrinsic motivation to support research, may have influenced the results as potential selection bias. Hence, patients who are more susceptible to a placebo effect tended to participate. The fear of being in the sham group was a rather small risk considering the hope they put in the trial.

Given the time investment, high technical requirements and equipment, and innovative method, the placebo effect of rTMS might be higher than that of pharmaceutical interventions. Data observing this effect already exists from studies on depression [73].

Symptom reduction through non-pharmacological treatments like aerobic training [74] and psychological methods [75] is well established in FMS [76]. A meta-analysis from 2017 concluded that patients with clinical or induced pain respond more to placebo than healthy controls [77]. Prior experience of effective or ineffective treatment influences placebo analgesia. It was shown that prior effective pain treatment increased the response to placebo analgesia [78]. Learning theories like classical conditioning and expectancy of the patients are known mechanisms for placebo analgesia [79]. As every participant already knew our hospital from prior examinations or even trials, those who have had positive associations and experiences were more likely to participate. These effects may have unintentionally played a role in our study population. Our observation on placebo response and possible mechanistic explanations are reflected in the literature, especially in depression, where placebo response to TMS is a known fact [80, 81]. Future implications could include, for example, minimizing contact with the examiner and recommending continuing with personal daily business, and of course, improvement of blinding. In addition to it, a minimum percentage of symptom reduction should be set before conducting a study as a threshold where improvement is considered significant, taking into account that effects might partially be due to placebo.

fMRI experiments revealed altered brain activity in pain processing areas during placebo analgesia as well as the anticipation of pain. Among others, prefrontal cortical areas are involved [82, 83]. The overlap of neuromodulatory treatment targets and areas responsible for placebo effects could also be shown in depression [81]. Opioidergic prefrontal pathways are also known to be part of placebo analgesia in healthy subjects and those with FMS [84].

Participants had no contact with each other, so the presence or absence of specific side effects did not lead to a group identification. In addition, blinding quality was assured by the fact that the study procedures were identical except for the positioning of the coil. The subjects were supervised by only one investigator to prevent guessing the group by the different behaviors of several investigators. Lastly, it should be noted that the group size was rather small, which limits the ability to detect subtle clinical effects.

DTI Data

Exploratory testing of all 48 ROIs revealed four white matter tracts with significant group × time interaction. These tracts connect the frontal and temporal lobes with the occipital pole (sagittal stratum), brainstem structures with the cerebellum (pontine crossing tract) or are part of the limbic system (cingulum).

Interpretation of the diffusion imaging results must be made with caution, given the exploratory nature of the analyses, the absence of correction for multiple comparisons, and the limited sample size. Looking at the graphs of the FA values of these four areas, one could suggest an opposite development of the values with stable or decreasing FA values in the sham group and increasing values, especially during the follow-up (T3) (pontine crossing tract, sagittal stratum r, cingulum r) in the treatment group. In the left cingulum, a similar trend can be seen between pre- and post-rTMS (T1–T2) with subsequent equalization of the values to the initial level in both groups. Symptom reduction shown in the FIQ correlated with declining FA values of the right sagittal stratum implies that this complex hub could be a candidate marker for treatment response. Since both treatment and sham group show a parallel development of symptoms, whereas the FA values differ over time between sham and verum, the results need to be interpreted with caution.

There are two negative studies with a low sample size investigating the influence of HF-rTMS on DTI so far, mostly in depression [85, 86]. However, one of the two studies found a trend towards increased FA values in the stimulated left prefrontal white matter in the active rTMS group [85]. Another group revealed an improvement of pre-treatment reduced FA values in left middle frontal gyrus after 20 sessions of HF-rTMS of the left DLPFC correlating with decreased depressive symptoms in a double-blind RCT with healthy controls [87]. To our knowledge, the only other rTMS study in FMS patients that examined effects on both gray matter volume and white matter integrity applied stimulation over M1 and reported no significant structural changes in either domain [88].

Functional Connectivity

Although our data did not show effects of HF-rTMS on rs-FC in the linear mixed model, responder analysis in the treatment group revealed several correlations as discussed below. Before diving deeper in the discussion, the exploratory character of the data should be kept in mind regarding the small sample size and multiple comparisons. Altered activity (increased or decreased depending on the exact area) in pain-processing brain regions as effect of rTMS of the left DLPFC supports the theory of rTMS interfering in pain pathways [89, 90]. In addition, remote effects of rTMS of the left DLPFC are assumed and explained as an effect of the embedded stimulation site as node in interacting networks or brain regions [91]. In FMS, one recent double-blind, sham-controlled crossover study conducting ten sessions of rTMS of M1 showed altered (mostly reduced) connectivity in affective, cognitive, and sensory pain processing brain areas. Additionally, rs-FC correlated with a reduction of clinical symptoms. Changes in brain structure could not be found [88]. In our study, rs-FC was increased in treatment responders between the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and the right cerebellum. In our previous work, lower rs-FC with the cerebellum was present in FMS compared to healthy controls [32], which supports the theory of the cerebellum being involved in pain pathways. Higher rs-FC between fronto-cerebellar regions in responders might therefore be an indicator of how treatment affects pain pathways.

Recently, the cerebellum, as part of cortico-cerebellar circuits, has been implicated in modulating placebo analgesia in a mouse study [92]. Fronto-cerebellar pathways from the left DLPFC to the right cerebellum indicate the non-motor tasks of these circuits [93].

Additionally, we found higher rs-FC in the treatment group between frontal areas, the frontoparietal network, and the insular cortex, including the salience network as well as supplementary motor cortices. These regions are involved in the processing of pain; frontoparietal areas are part of the executive attention network [94] and the salience network, where previously remote effects of HF-rTMS of the left DLPFC have been shown [35]. For example, cognitive behavioral therapy in FMS leads to increased activation of the prefrontal cortex and reduces pain by interfering in cognitive control mechanisms of pain [95]. HF-rTMS might add to this effect by stimulating the prefrontal cortex. It is possible that rTMS may influence rs-FC preferentially in the long-term, as changes could be seen in the seed-to-voxel and ROI-to-ROI analysis between pre- and follow-up (T1–T3) measurements.

Future studies with larger sample sizes are needed to confirm the present findings and test the robustness of the observed effects. This is particularly important given the exploratory design and the relatively small number of (non-)responders, which restricts statistical power.

Proton MR Spectroscopy

Although our data did not show a significant group × time interaction or group difference of Glx/tCr and GABA+/tCr, a trend towards significance for the factor time of decreasing Glx/tCr ratios (amount of tCr remained stable over time) was found. Decrease of Glx/tCr was more distinct in the treatment group, but without reaching significance. Since the spectra of Glu and Gln in 1H-spectroscopy overlap, exact differentiation is difficult [96]; hence we focused on the analysis of Glx.

In FMS, studies on metabolite levels are heterogeneous. Some studies have shown elevation of glutamate/Glx [97–104] in the amygdala [104], the PCC [102], the ventrolateral prefrontal cortex [103] and the right posterior but not anterior insula [98], which leads to the conclusion that glutamate might play a role in pain processing in FMS and that increased levels are associated with increased symptoms [105].

In contrast, other studies did not reveal any significant differences of glutamate and GABA+ or Glx/tCr in some of those areas [52, 106] (citation 52: preprint). A positive correlation between Glx levels of the right DLPFC and pain sensitivity scores was shown [107], whereas others investigated a negative correlation between Glx (among other metabolites) and FIQ scores in the left DLPFC [104].

In an HF-rTMS (20 Hz) sham-controlled study in patients with treatment-resistant depression, a correlation between higher concentrations of GABA+ in the left DLPFC and better clinical outcome was found, but no significant metabolite differences between groups after the stimulation. The authors conclude that inhibitory GABAergic neurotransmission might play a role in HF-rTMS over the left DLPFC [108].

Two hypotheses of the decreasing trend of Glx/tCr could be discussed but remain rather speculative considering the lack of literature on this specific topic. It might either be related to placebo analgesia, taking into account that no group difference was observed in our cohort. On the other hand, we see a slightly stronger (but not significant) decrease in the treatment group, which might be due to rTMS. Nevertheless, the study might have been underpowered to detect such a small rTMS-related effect.

Gray Matter Volume

We did not find an increase in gray matter volume of the target area, which is in line with a study performing HF-rTMS of M1 in FMS with a similar design [88]. We restricted our analysis to the cortical area containing the stimulation target. Other studies reporting increased cortical volume/thickness with a prolonged rTMS-DLPFC protocol over several weeks in patients with treatment-resistant major depression or depressive episode [109–111], indicating that 10 sessions of HF-rTMS, as in our study, might not be enough to increase cortical volume.

Limitations

Some limitations of the study should be kept in mind. Although our sample size was comparable to other studies, it may not be sufficient to detect rather small effects in the MRI data. In addition, our MRI results might be partially explained by the short study duration, considering that more than ten sessions might be needed to induce functional, metabolic, and structural alterations in the brain. Studies on depression showed greater clinical effects with more sessions [112, 113], which might also be reflected in neurobiological effects. In addition to it, the exploratory character of DTI data and hypothesis-generating character of the rs-FC and spectroscopy should be kept in mind.

Second, while it was required that participants were on stable medication without changes during the trial period, the influence of medication on the results cannot be completely excluded. Third, a part of the follow-up (T3) scans could not be performed in time due to the COVID-19 pandemic. Fourth, the time delay (ranging from 30 min to 3 h) between the last rTMS session and fMRI may have been too long to detect changes of rs-FC. Fifth, the study was only single-blinded and sham/active stimulation was performed with the same coil but in a different position. However, this was one commonly used method to apply sham stimulation at this time [114].

Conclusions

In this study, HF-rTMS of the left DLPFC did not outperform placebo. Regarding the cost–benefit analysis of this elaborate technique, it should so far only by applied in the context of trials. Nevertheless, these results should be used with caution due to the small sample size and partially exploratory character of MRI data.

Given the susceptibility to placebo responses in FMS, carefully controlled, longitudinal, large-scale, and neuroimaging-based studies with extended stimulation periods investigating the therapeutic benefits of HF-rTMS of the left DLPFC, as well as alternative stimulation sites with potentially more direct involvement in pain, such as M1 in patients with FMS and within subgroups, should be pursued.

Our data provide a framework for an MRI protocol for the analysis of neuromodulatory interventions. Some hypotheses could be drawn: HF-rTMS may influence rs-FC in central pain-processing areas, and fronto-cerebellar circuits are a potential marker for treatment response.
