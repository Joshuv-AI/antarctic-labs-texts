Development of an automated chemiluminescence immunoassay for detection of IgA antibodies against porcine epidemic diarrhea virus spike
Authors: Duan Li, Daoping Zeng, Qi Wang, Leyi Zhang, Yanling Liu, Cuiqin Huang, Changxu Song, Lei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPorcine epidemic diarrhoea virus (PEDV) causes substantial financial losses on pig farming worldwide. Monitoring PEDV-specific IgA antibodies is essential for assessing vaccine efficacy. In this study, we developed a fully automated chemiluminescence immunoassay (CLIA) for the detection of PEDV IgA antibodies in both serum and milk.ResultsThis S1-CLIA utilizes recombinant S1 protein-coated magnetic particles (S1-MPs) for antibody capture and an acridinium ester-labeled mouse anti-pig IgA monoclonal antibody (mpIgA-AE) to generate chemiluminescence signals. Under optimized conditions, S1-CLIA achieved a diagnostic sensitivity of 96.30% and specificity of 98.48%, with a cut-off index of 1.0. S1-CLIA exhibited excellent repeatability (coefficient of variation < 10%) and no cross-reactivity with sera positive for other common swine pathogens. Compared to a commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), S1-CLIA achieved 93.93% agreement rate, but offered a significantly broader dynamic range (35.48-fold). Notably, S1-CLIA results correlated well with virus neutralization titers (VNT), yielding R² values of 0.6660 (serum) and 0.7639 (milk).ConclusionsWith capability of fully automated testing, this novel S1-CLIA presents a robust platform for large-scale evaluation of PEDV immunity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-026-05407-w.Keywords: Porcine epidemic diarrhoea virus, Spike, IgA, Chemiluminescent immunoassay, Antibody detection

Background

Porcine epidemic diarrhoea (PED), caused by PED virus (PEDV), is a highly contagious swine enteric disease characterized by vomiting, watery diarrhoea, and dehydration. Pigs of all ages suffer from PED, while neonatal piglets are more susceptible with mortality rates of up to 100%. Since December 2010, highly virulent PEDV variants re-emerged in China [1], and subsequent outbreaks in USA in 2013, leading to seven million deaths of pigs [2]. Currently, circulating PEDV strains are classified into two major genotypes: GⅠ (classical) and GⅡ (field variant). GⅡ genotype has several sub-groups: GII-a, GII-b and GII-c [3, 4]. Now GⅡb and GⅡc are epidemic mainly in swine farms in China, resulting in enormous economic losses [5, 6].

PEDV is an enveloped, single-stranded, positive-sense RNA virus with a genome of approximately 28 kilobases (kb). It encodes four structural proteins: spike (S), envelope (E), membrane (M), nucleocapsid (N) [7]. S protein plays a decisive role in viral attachment and entry into host cells, comprising two functional domains: S1, responsible for receptors binding and S2, mediates membrane fusion [8]. S1 is capable of eliciting protective immune responses in pigs [9], making it an ideal target for both diagnostic assays and vaccine development.

Vaccination remains as an effective strategy for PED control. To support vaccination programs, several commercial and in-house enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs) have been developed [10–13]. Although ELISA is a convenient and widely used technique, it is limited by being time-consuming, labour-intensive, and having a relatively narrow dynamic range. Given that PEDV IgA is a valuable predictor for immunization effects [14], and serves as a critical “lifeline” for newborn piglets [15], there is a clear need for a reliable diagnostic method to detect IgA in both serum and milk. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop a high-throughput, automated and more sensitive alternative.

Chemiluminescence immunoassay (CLIA) is a widely used analytical tool in life sciences, clinical diagnostics, environmental monitoring, pharmaceutical analysis, and food safety evaluation [16, 17]. It can be divided into three main categories: direct CLIA, chemiluminescence enzyme immunoassay (CLEIA), and electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (ECLIA). Direct CLIA exhibits distinct advantages including low background signals, rapid reaction kinetics, and a simple experimental procedure. In veterinary medicine, direct CLIA has been successfully established for the detection of various pathogens, such as African swine fever virus (ASFV) as well as sheep and bovine brucellosis, enabling automated, highly sensitive detection [18, 19]. In addition, Fe3O4-based magnetic particles (MPs) have been been unitized in CLIA. Advances in functionalized MPs, such as nanocomposites and layered nanomaterials, show great potential to significantly improve detection performance, highlighting their superiority over conventional ELISA [20, 21].

In this study we expressed a recombinant PEDV S1 protein and developed a novel direct CLIA based on MPs and an acridinium ester (AE) system. This assay offers high sensitivity and automated detection of PEDV IgA antibodies, making it a promising tool for PEDV surveillance and diagnosis.

Materials and methods

Cells and viruses

Vero cells (Cat. CL-0242) and human embryonic kidney 293 F (HEK-293 F; Cat. GNHu73) cells, maintained in our laboratory, were sourced from Procell system (Wuhan, China) and Cell Bank of Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China), respectively. Vero cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% (vol/vol) fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37 °C and 5% CO2, whereas HEK293F was cultured in serum-free SMM293-TII medium (Sino Biological, Inc., China) in suspension.

The PEDV strain FJzz02 was isolated from a clinical sample collected from a swine farm in Zhangzhou, Fujian Province, and is currently stored at the Fujian Provincial Key Laboratory for Prevention and Control of Animal Infectious Diseases and Biotechnology. Based on S gene analysis, this strain was classified into G2c genotype.

Samples

Serum samples positive for common swine pathogens were confirmed using commercial ELISA kits and used for cross-reactivity assays. These included samples positive for classical swine fever virus (CSFV; n = 5), porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome virus (PRRSV; n = 8), porcine circovirus type 2 (PCV2; n = 9), SFV ( n = 6), pseudorabies virus (PRV, n = 5), porcine deltacoronavirus (PDCoV; n = 7) and transmissible gastroenteritis virus (TGEV; n = 10). A total of 247 additional serum samples were collected from pigs in farms in Guangdong, Guangxi and Fujian provinces. These samples were tested for IgA antibodies using a commercial ELISA kit (Cat: 06-55550-01, IDEXX Laboratories, Westbrook, ME, USA) and subsequently used for concordance rate analysis. Of these 247 samples: 120 samples from piglets were used for cut-off value determination; 27 samples from sows immunized with a diarrhea vaccine (Wuhan Keqian Biology Co., Ltd.) were used for PEDV neutralizing antibody detection. Furthermore, 97 milk samples were collected from vaccinated sows in farms located in Foshan (n = 23), Jiangmen (n = 41); Zhanjiang (n = 18), and Heyuan (n = 15), and stored at the National Engineering Research Center for Breeding Swine Industry. Following a previously described method with minor modifications [22], all milk samples were centrifuged at 8000 rpm at 4 °C for 8 min to remove fat, cellular debris to eliminate matrix effects. The middle layer (skimmed milk) was collected for subsequent analysis. IgA and neutralizing antibody levels were measured, respectively.

Plasmid construction

The fragment derived from the PEDV strain FJzz02 was codon-optimized, synthesized, and subsequently cloned into the pCAGGS vector that had previously been used in our laboratory by GENEWIZ Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Suzhou, China). The corresponding protein sequence spans amino acids 20 to 793 (aa), and was designated as S1. To facilitate protein purification, the recombinant S1 protein was engineered to include a C-terminal His-tag preceded by a GGGS linker.

Immunoblotting (IB)

Above constructed recombinant S1 plasmid was transfected into HEK-293 F cells for IB to validate protein expression. Briefly, cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and lysed in 200 µL of RIPA lysis buffer (Cat. P0038; Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Cell lysis occurred within 1–2 s, followed by incubation on ice for 2–10 min. The whole cell lysates (WCLs) were then separated using 8% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% skimmed milk in PBS containing 0.5% Tween 20 (PBST) and incubated overnight at 4 °C with a mouse anti-S specific monoclonal antibody (S1-mAb; Cat. PV-949416; Lonyin Biotechnology, Guangzhou, China). Following six washes with PBST, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (Cat. 5220 − 0341; KPL, Milford, USA) at room temperature (RT) for one hour. After five additional washes, protein bands were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence reagent (Cat. P2300; NCM, Suzhou, China).

Expression and purification of proteins

Above constructed S1 plasmid was transfected into HEK-293 F cells for protein purification. Briefly, the plasmids were transfected using polyethylenimine Linear (PEI) with a molecular weight (MW) of 25,000 (Cat.40815ES03; Yeasen, Shanghai, China). Three days later, the cells were harvested and the recombinant S1 protein were purified using a His-tag protein purification kit (Cat.70502-K10; Beaver, Suzhou, China), following through molecular sieves using a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 Gl size exclusion column (Cat. 28–9909-44; GE Healthcare, Chicago, USA). The purified S1 protein were analysis using SDS-PAGE, followed by protein mass spectrometry and deglycosylation assay using PNGase F deglycosylation kit (Cat.P2318S; Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Its concentration was measured using the BCA protein assay kit (Cat. P0009; Beyotime, Shanghai, China).

Determination of virus neutralizing titer

PEDV neutralizing antibody titers (VNTs) in serum and milk samples were determined using a virus fixation–sample dilution method. Briefly, 27 serum and 97 milk samples were pretreated as described above, then supplemented with a penicillin-streptomycin-amphotericin B solution. All samples were serially diluted by a factor of two in cell maintenance medium, starting at a dilution of 1:2, and then mixed with PEDV at a concentration of 50% tissue culture infectious dose (TCID50) per 0.1 mL in an equal volume. All mixtures were incubated at 37 °C in a CO2 incubator for one hour. Then 0.1 mL of each mixture was added to a 96-well cell culture plate (four wells per dilution), along with four wells containing uninfected cells and four wells incubated with 100 TCID50/0.1 mL of PEDV as controls. After one hour incubation, all wells were washed three times using PBS, and 0.15 mL of maintenance medium was added. The cytopathic effect (CPE) was observed daily and recorded on day 4. VNTs were calculated according to a previous study [23].

Conjugation of S1 to MPs

Recombinant S1 protein was coupled to carboxyl-functionalized MPs (JSR, Sunnyvale, USA) using a modified protocol based on our previously reported method [18]. Briefly, 10 mg of MPs were washed and resuspended in 1.0 mL of 0.1 M 2-Morpholinoethanesulphonic acid (MES) buffer at pH 5.0. After four washes, 100 µL each of N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS;10 mg/mL) and 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide (EDC;10 mg/mL) were added, and incubated at RT for 30 min under rotation. Above MPs were washed four times with 1.0 mL of MES buffer, followed by addition of 100 µg of purified PEDV S1 protein. The mixture was then incubated at RT with shaking for 3 h. Next, 100 µL of 10% bovine serum albumin (BSA) was added, and incubation continued for another three hours under shaking. After three washes with PBST, the S1-coupled magnetic particle (S1-MPs) were dispersed in 50 mL PBST containing with 0.01% ProClin™300, and 0.5% BSA.

The prepared S1-MPs were diluted at ratios of 1:50, 1:100, 1:200 and 1:400, before being tested using an automated chemiluminescence analyzer. Briefly, 50 µL of each dilution was taken and 100 µL of in-house-prepared AE-labeled S1-mAb (S1-mAb-AE) was added. After an incubation period of 10 min at 37 ℃ and three washes, substrates A and B (containing H2O2 and NaOH, respectively) were added and the RLU values were recorded. The MPs served as the blank control. In our CLIA, an RLU value of at least 1.0 × 106 was required to pass quality control.

To further observe their dispersion stability, MPs and S1-MPs were placed in a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube at a concentration of 5.0 and 10.0 mg/mL, and their drop at 0, 10, 20, 30, 60 90 min and overnight, were recorded. Then MPs were placed in a glass slide and their aggregation were observed using a microscope.

Conjugation of AE to mouse anti-pig IgA monoclonal antibody (mpIgA)

The mpIgA (Cat. MA-660020; Longyin Biotechnology, Guangzhou, China) was labeled with AE according to previous study with slight modifications [24]. Briefly, 0.5 mg of mpIgA was transferred to 2 mL low-adsorption type tube and mixed well with labeling buffer (carbonate buffer, pH 9.0). Then, a 1.0 mM AE solution was added, and the final volume was adjusted to 1.0 mL using labeling buffer. The mixture was incubated in the dark at 25 °C with shaking at 150 rpm for 30 min. The reaction was terminated by adding 100 µL of a 5% lysine solution. The mixture was incubated for a further 30 min. The conjugate was purified using a G-25 desalting column (1.6 × 2.5 cm). The resulting fraction was designated as mpIgA-AE.

The prepared mpIgA-AE was diluted to 1:100, 1:200, 1:400, and 1:800, and analyzed in triplicate (100 µL per test) using a chemiluminescence analyzer. Substrates A and B (containing H2O2 and NaOH, respectively) were directly added to each dilution. The RLU values were recorded. The dilution buffer was used as the negative control. In our CLIA, an RLU value of ≥ 1.0 × 107 was required to pass quality control.

Experimental procedure

All procedures were conducted in accordance with our previously established method [18]. As shown in Fig. 1, high-capacity S1-MPs were used to capture serum antibodies, subsequently incubated with mpIgA-AE for signal generation. After separation and washing of specific antigen-antibody complexes, substrate A (0.1 M HCl containing 0.1% H2O2) was added and allowed to react for one minute. Substrate B (0.25 M NaOH with 2% Triton X-100) was added to within 5 s to record all RLUs.

Optimization of reaction conditions

To achieve optimal conditions for S1-CLIA, four critical parameters including dilution ratios of MPs, mpIgA-AE, serum and milk samples were systematically optimized. S1-MPs were diluted at ratios of 1:100, 1:200, 1:300 and 1:400, while mpIgA-AE were sequentially diluted at 1:300, 1:600, 1:900, and 1:1,200, respectively. MPs and AE conjugates were formed at each dilution pair and evaluated experimentally. Five positive and five negative samples were selected and randomly assigned to five distinct testing sets for measurement of RLU. The optimal dilution combination was determined based on minimal variation in RLU values among negative samples and maximal signal intensity for positive samples. The P/N ratio was calculated as the ratio of RLU values between positive and negative samples. By comparing the P/N ratios, the optimal reaction conditions for S1-MPs and mpIgA-AE dilutions were established. One pair of positive and negative serum samples were diluted at 1:10, 1:20, 1:50, 1:100, 1:200, 1:300. One pair of strongly positive and negative milk samples were diluted at 1:50, 1:100, 1:200, 1:300, 1:400, 1:500. Their RLU values were measured and P/N values were calculated. By comparing the P/N ratios, the optimal dilution folds for serum and milk samples were established.

Detection procedure

Two-step and one-step procedures were compared. Two-step procedure: 50 µL of S1-MPs, and 100 µL of diluted sample were added to a test tube, and incubated at 37 ℃ for 10 min. After three washes with Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST), 100 µL of mpIgA-AE was added and incubated for an additional 10 min at 37 ℃. After three washes, 100 µL of substrate A was added, followed by a one minute incubation at 37 ℃. Then, 100 µL of substrate B solution was added to measure RLUs within 5 s. Delayed one-step procedure involved in incubation of S1-MPs and diluted sample at 37 ℃ for 10 min, followed by addition of mpIgA-AE without washing and incubation for a further 5 min. RLU values were measured. Six strong positive samples and one negative sample were assigned to six distinct testing sets for measurement of RLU values using one- and two-step model. The P/N ratio was calculated and compared.

Cut-off value, diagnosis sensitivity and Specificity

A total of 120 serum samples with well-defined backgrounds were tested using the optimized S1-CLIA. Receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) analysis was conducted and interactive scatter plots were used to determine the optimal cut-off value, as well as the diagnostic specificity, and diagnostic sensitivity based on the Youden index. Once the cut-off value was established, the detection results were expressed as a cut-off index (COI), which was calculated as the ratio of the sample’s RLU value to the cut-off value. Samples were classified as positive (COI > 1.0) or negative (COI < 1.0) based on this index.

Performance of detection

Repeatability

Intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation (CVs) were determined to evaluate repeatability. Twenty-one PEDV serum samples (fourteen positive samples and seven negative samples) were tested. To determine the intra-assay CV, all samples were tested in triplicate using one batch of S1-MPs and mpIgA-AE. Three different batches of S1-MPs and and mpIgA-AE were used to confirm the inter-assay CV. The mean (M) and standard deviation (SD) of the COI values were calculated for each set of measurements, and the CV was calculated as (SD/M) × 100%.

Cross-reactivity

Cross-reactivity was assessed by testing positive sera against common porcine pathogens, including CSFV, PRRSV, PCV2, ASFV, PRV, PDCoV and TGEV.

Calibration curve and linearity range

One strong positive and one negative serum sample were serially diluted at the following ratios: 1:1, 1:2, 1:4, 1:8, 1;16, 1:32, 1:64, 1:128, 1:200, 1:400, 1:800, 1:1600, 1:3200, 1:6400, 1:12800, 1:25600, 1:51200, 1:102400, 1:204800, and 1:409600. All dilutions were tested in triplicate using both S1-CLIA and a commercial ELISA kit (IDEXX) to generate calibration curves. The IgA COI values obtained from serum samples at different dilution factors was analyzed using a four-parameter logistic (4PL) model. To analyze both S1-CLIA and ELISA data, a log10(Y) ~ log10(X) regression analysis was conducted. As optimal linear dynamic range was identified in a continuous range where log(Y) and log(X) form a straight line, with R² reached maximized, meaning signal is proportional to concentration (concentration ∝ 1/X). Log–log transformation was described in previous study [25].

Statistical analysis

We did not use any Artificial Intelligence Generated Content (AIGC) tools to develop any part of our manuscript. Data analysis was performed using excel, OriginPro 7.0 (Microcal Software Inc., Northhampton, MA, USA), and GraphPad Prism version 8.0 software (San Diego, CA, USA). R2 values represent correlation coefficients, and P values of < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Expression and purification of S1 protein

PEDV S protein is a type I transmembrane glycoprotein consisting of an N‑terminal signal peptide, a large ectodomain, a transmembrane domain, and a short cytoplasmic tail. It comprises two functional subunits, S1 and S2 [26]. In this study, the S protein sequence was obtained from the PEDV strain FJzz02, with the signal peptide spanning residues 1–20, and the S1/S2 cleavage region located between amino acids (aa) 763 and 782. In addition to the N-terminal domain (NTD), and C-terminal domain (CTD), two previously identified core epitopes, SS2 and SS6, were mapped to residues 761 − 758 and 767–774, respectively [27]. The S1 domain (residues 20–793) was Consequently expressed and purified using HEK-293 F cells (Fig. 2A). Three to four days after transient transfection, cells were harvested and analyzed by IB assay (Fig. 2B). Successful expression of recombinant S1 protein was confirmed using a mouse anti-S mAb. Purification was achieved using affinity beads followed by size-exclusion chromatography, yielding a highly pure S1 protein with a final concentration of 4.1 mg/mL, as shown in Fig. 2C. However, the apparent MW of the recombinant S1 protein exceeded 100 kDa, which was substantially higher than the theoretical MW of approximately 80–90 kDa. Subsequent proteomic analysis demonstrated a high peptide coverage rate (Fig. 2D), confirming the protein’s identity as PEDV S1. As PEDV S protein is known to be heavily glycosylated, a deglycosylation assay was performed. Treatment with PNGase F, an enzyme that removes N-linked glycans, reduced the apparent MW to a range of 70–100 kDa, bringing it closer to the predicted size (Fig. 2E). This indicated that N-linked glycosylation modifications contributed to the observed upward shift in SDS-PAGE mobility. Collectively, these results revealed the successful expression and purification of a glycosylated PEDV S1 antigen that is ready for use in downstream applications.

Dispersion stability test of S1-MPs

To further evaluate the dispersion stability of S1-MPs, both sedimentation assays and microscopic observations were performed. In the sedimentation test, no phase separation was observed for either S1-MPs or control MPs at a concentration of 10.0 mg/mL within 20 min. However, upon dilution to 5.0 mg/mL, gradual and uniform settling became apparent within 20 min, with a clear supernatant forming after 60 min and almost complete sedimentation occurring overnight incubation. Following overnight incubation, the resulting pellet could be easily resuspended by gentle agitation, indicating that aggregation remained reversible with no irreversible particle clustering occurring (Fig. 3A). Microscopic analysis corroborated these findings, revealing that S1-MPs exhibit small particle size, homogeneous distribution, and minimal aggregation—features comparable to those of the control MPs (Fig. 3B). Together, these data strongly suggest that the prepared S1-MPs possess robust dispersion stability, and also provides a method for evaluating the performance of coated MPs.

Optimization of working conditions

Through extensive research, we have determined that serum provides more convenient detection of PEDV IgA antibodies compared to milk. Therefore, this study first established an IgA antibody detection method using serum, followed by optimization of experimental conditions and performance analysis. Subsequently, milk processing steps and dilution ratios were defined.

Dilutions of S1-MPs and mpIgA-AE

Quality control results verified that both S1-MPs and mpIgA-AE were suitable for subsequent assays. As shown in Fig. 4A and B, the prepared S1-MPs yielded a strong chemiluminescent signal (4.0 × 106) at a 50-fold dilution, which was markedly higher than that of control MPs (3450.0 ± 103.6, < 4.0 × 103), thus confirming efficient protein conjugation. Similarly, mpIgA-AE exhibited a high labeling efficiency, generating RLU value exceeding 1.0 × 107 at a 100-fold dilution with negligible background signal (2498.7 ± 70.6, < 3.0 × 103).

Dilution of serum

To enhance accuracy for clinical detection of IgA antibodies in serum, sample dilution ratios were further optimized. Above positive-negative pair were detected under serially dilution. As presented in Fig. 4D, the optimal sample dilutions were determined to be 1:200 for serum, where its P/N value reached maximum of 2382.05 (mean value). Therefore, final recommended working dilutions were established as 1:200 for serum.

Dilution of milk sample

High abundance of antibodies and complex matrix often make it difficult to accurately detect target analytes in milk. To overcome these technical challenges, we first evaluated various sample processing protocols derived from commercial kits, including those supplied by IDEXX, Vazyme, as well as protocols from other studies. For routine assays, milk samples were subjected to a high-speed centrifugation, followed by a 2-fold dilution. One strongly positive milk sample and one negative milk sample were selected from a cohort of 97 clinical specimens for gradient dilution validation. As shown in Fig. 4E, a peak P/N ratio of 2088.65 (mean value) was achieved at a dilution of 300-fold. Based on this finding, a uniform 300-fold dilution was adopted for all milk samples in subsequent experimental procedures.

Detection procedure of S1-CLIA

Both one‑step and two‑step procedures exert substantial impacts not only on detection sensitivity but also on total assay duration. In contrast to conventional one‑step assays that rely on blocking or sandwich formats, our S1-CLIA was established on an indirect competitive principle and employs a delayed one‑step reaction format. Our results demonstrated that the entire workflow, from sample dilution to final result analysis, can be seamlessly performed on automated analyzers. In terms of detection time, one-step procedure generated the first result within 20 min, followed by subsequent results at 3–5 s intervals. By contrast, two-step procedure took 25 min to produce the initial result, with subsequent results produced at approximately 6 s interval. As illustrated in Fig. 4F, both methods yielded comparable P/N values across tested samples. Although two-step procedure achieved a modestly higher P/N ratio, large-scale evaluations of thousands of clinical samples confirmed that this difference had negligible clinical or analytical impact. Furthermore, the delayed one-step procedure showed superior efficiency. Considering these factors, this study adopted delayed one-step approach to achieve an optimal balance between operational efficiency and assay performance.

Cut-off values and cross-reactivity determination

Cut-off values

Under optimal conditions, a total of 120 serum samples with a clearly defined background were measured using the developed S1-CLIA. As shown in Fig. 5A and B, the cut-off value was found to be 32,134, resulting in a diagnostic sensitivity of 96.3% and a specificity of 98.48%. The area under the curve (AUC) was 0.982 with a p value of less than 0.0001. An AUC above 0.9 is indicative of excellent diagnostic performance [28]. These findings underscore the high diagnostic accuracy of the S1-CLIA in detecting PEDV IgA antibodies.

Cross-reactivity

Cross-reactivity was assessed by testing serum samples that were positive for CSFV, PRRSV, PCV2, ASFV, PRV, TGEV and PDCoV. As shown in Fig. 5C, only the PEDV positive serum sample yielded a COI value exceeding 1.0, while all others remained below this thershold, indicating that the S1-CLIA can specifically detect IgA to PEDV without cross-reactivity with antibodies directed toward other prevalent swine viruses.

Analytical sensitivity

To rapidly evaluate the analytical sensitivity of the newly developed CLIA, six PEDV positive serum samples from vaccinated sows were tested using both the S1-CLIA and commercial ELISA kit. As summarized in Table 1, samples #15 and #18 were consistently detected at dilutions of up to 1:12800 and 1:3200, respectively, by both methods. In contrast, samples #17, #22, #23, and #27 remained positive at higher dilutions with the S1-CLIA-specifically, 1:12,800, 1:102,400, 1:51,200, and 1:6,400, respectively-indicating a two fold improvement in detection limit compared to the ELISA. All samples exhibited COI values exceeding 1.0, confirming high specificity and further supporting the enhanced sensitivity of the S1-CLIA.

Standard curve and liner range

Under optimal experimental conditions, serially diluted PEDV positive serum samples were measured using both S1-CLIA and a commercial ELISA kit. Signal outputs were recorded across a dilution range from 4 to 409,600-fold. As shown in Fig. 6A, S1-CLIA signal values were significantly higher than ELISA (which uses a different unit of measurement), but both decreased with an increasing dilution factor. S1-CLIA exhibited a ‘plateau zone’ at high concentrations (dilution factor 4–128), where the signal changed little or fluctuated, suggesting a possible hook effect or detector saturation. ELISA exhibited a gradual signal change in signal (around 9–10) at low dilutions (8–800), suggesting that its absorbance readings may be approaching saturation. At higher dilution ratios (1600–51200), both methods entered a distinct decline zone, representing the quantitative linear range.

These data clearly demonstrate that, although both methods are fundamentally robust, S1-CLIA offers distinct advantages in terms of linearity, dynamic range, and ideal dose-response conformity. These factors are critical for the precise quantification of PEDV IgA antibodies.

Discussion

PEDV variants frequently emerge, leading to substantial economic losses [29–31]. An ideal PEDV vaccine would induce robust maternal immunity to confer passive protection to neonatal piglets, which was particularly susceptible to infection [32, 33]. This highlights a critical need for reliable diagnostics tools that can accurately monitor protective immune responses, particularly IgA, which is a key mediator of mucosal immunity [14]. Although ELISA is widely used in serological testing, its limitations, such as complex manual operations and a very narrow linear range, make it difficult into smart aquaculture [34]. In this study, we successfully developed a novel fully automated CLIA for the detection of PEDV specific IgA in both serum and milk. This new tool not only addresses shortcomings of conventional assays, but also offers a powerful support for herd immunity monitoring and management.

In the present study, the recombinant S1 protein was expressed in line with previous findings. Several ELISA-based methods have been developed using whole virus, S, M and N [35–38]. Of these, S1 subunit offers distinct advantages in the detection of PEDV IgG and IgA antibodies [39]. It harbors multiple neutralizing epitopes and functional domains, such as NTD, CTD, receptor-binding domain (RBD), core neutralizing epitope (COE), domain 0 (D0), making it a prime candidate for vaccine development [40, 41]. For example, S1-based nanoparticle vaccines have been shown to elicit protective immunity effectively pigs [42–44]. Maternal IgA, rather than IgG, has been consistently correlated with piglets survival rates upon PEDV, establishing IgA as a key correlate of protection [23, 33]. These findings have been independently validated by subsequent studies [14]. We also observed a significant impact of N-terminal glycosylation on S1 protein electrophoretic mobility. As N-glycosylation of the S protein contributes to PEDV infectivity and virulence [45, 46]. Further studies will investigate this effect of N-terminal glycosylation on antigen immunogenicity, particularly in diagnostic and vaccine design.

The established S1-CLIA represents a substantial improvement over conventional ELISA by combining high sensitivity with full automation. Similar CLIAs have been successfully applied for the detection of a broad range of pathogens, including toxoplasmosis, sheep and bovine brucellosis, ASFV, PCV3, and FMDV [19, 47–50]. Our S1-CLIA employs MPs as solid-phase carriers. Their large surface area facilitates efficient antigen immobilization and enables rapid magnetic separation, thereby improving assay kinetics and enhancing analytical sensitivity. As a result, the S1-CLIA achieved excellent diagnostic performance, with a sensitivity of 96.30% and a specificity of 98.48%, representing a twofold increase in analytical sensitivity. Another key advantage of our S1-CLIA is its operational efficiency: it delivered initial results within 20 min and can generate one result every 3–5 s. This speed is largely attributable to AE, a flash-type chemiluminescent compound that enables signal acquisition within 0.2 s [51]. AE has previously been utilized in our in-house CLIA for ASFV and in recently developed CLIA [18, 19], demonstrating its reliability and adaptability. In contrast, enzyme-based “glow”-type systems, such as those employing HRP or alkaline phosphatase (ALP), typically require longer incubation times of 5–8 min. Furthermore, in concordance to previous reports, our S1-CLIA exhibited low background signal, and a high signal-to-noise ratio [52]. The combination of MPs and AE has been widely adopted across various diagnostic fields, including Alzheimer’s disease research, cancer biomarker detection, and food safety analysis (e.g. cortisol measurement) [53–55].

Another major strength of the S1-CLIA is its broad linear range, which is approximately 35.48-fold wider than that of conventional ELISA. Such an extended dynamic range is crucial for accurately quantifying antibody levels across a wide spectrum, from early, low-level immune responses to high-titer immunity. This capability allows a more precise assessment of individual immune status and antibody potency, enabling a shift from binary positive/negative classification toward a nuanced, quantitative evaluation of herd immunity. Consequently, the established S1-CLIA shows strong potential for routine monitoring of immune status and milk quality, thereby guiding optimized vaccination strategies. Most importantly, the S1-CLIA demonstrated a significantly superior correlation between IgA levels and PEDV VNTs compared to ELISA. This superior correlation is attributable to its wide linear range, which enables precise differentiation of high-concentration antibodies. Future studies will investigate whether CLIA offers similar advantages over ELISA in neutralizing antibody detection for other significant swine pathogens, such as PCV2, CSFV, and PRV.

Conclusions

We have successfully developed and validated a novel, fully automated S1-CLIA for the detection of PEDV-specific IgA. Our S1-CLIA effectively overcomes the limitations of conventional ELISA, offering greater sensitivity and a broader dynamic range, as well as a strong correlation with VNTs. However, we still recommend using a relevant reference materials to calibrate this experiment. Additionally, a chemiluminescent multiplex immunoassay can be deigned to enable the simultaneous detection of multiple pathogens, facilitating the develop of a comprehensive health screening package for herd immunity [56]. This method can also provide technical support for smart farming. Cloud-based data management allows test results to be linked with production data and immunization schedules from farms for big data analysis, providing decision support for early disease warning and precision immunization. In conclusion, our S1-CLIA provides a powerful and reliable tool for monitoring large-scale immunity, evaluating vaccine effectively, and ultimately, improving PED control.
