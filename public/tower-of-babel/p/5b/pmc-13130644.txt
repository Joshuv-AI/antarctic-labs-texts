Engineered oncolytic virus armed with anti-PCSK9 scFv boosts long-term CD8+ T cell immunity via rewiring MHC-I antigen presentation
Authors: Huolun Feng, Yuhan Zhang, Zuda Huang, Jianlong Zhou, Yongfeng Liu, Xiao Xiao, Mingxi Chen, Xin Guo, Jiabin Zheng, Zejian Lyu, Weixian Hu, Deqing Wu, Yong Li, Fan Xing
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryOncolytic viruses (OVs) are widely studied for their ability to lyse cancer cells and prime immune responses; however, the immune consequences triggered by OVs remain incompletely understood. Here, we discover that oncolytic VSVΔ51 treatment suppresses the T cell receptor signaling of tumor-infiltrating T cells. Mechanistically, VSVΔ51-infected cancer cells upregulate PCSK9 secretion, which triggers lysosomal degradation of major histocompatibility complex (MHC)-I in bystander cells. PCSK9 inhibition synergizes with VSVΔ51 treatment to suppress tumor growth in multiple colorectal cancer models and induce complete regression in a microsatellite-stable (MSS) tumor model. This combination fosters stem-like CD8+ T cells and establishes anti-tumor memory. Engineered VSVΔ51 expressing anti-PCSK9 single-chain variable fragments improves intra-tumor viral replication, sustains anti-tumor CD8+ T cell memory, and enhances anti-PD-1 therapy efficacy. Our results identify the role of PCSK9 in the immunosuppressive feedback following viral infection and propose a strategy for engineered oncolytic virotherapy.

Introduction

Oncolytic viruses (OVs) selectively lyse cancer cells and initiate anti-tumor immunity, making them promising cancer therapeutics. Nevertheless, the immune consequences of OV therapy remain unclear.1 Clinically, OVs remain limited by inadequate activation of host anti-tumor immunity and restricted intra-tumoral dissemination.2,3,4 To potentiate therapeutic outcomes, OVs have been engineered to express immune modulators that amplify or reinvigorate anti-tumor immunity.5,6,7 Therefore, elucidating the impact of OVs on anti-tumor immunity is essential for developing next-generation engineered OVs.

In recent years, significant progress has been made in the study of proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9) inhibitors within the field of cancer therapy.8,9,10 Emerging evidence suggests that PCSK9 not only plays a role in lipid metabolism but also may serve as a potential therapeutic target in cancer by modulating immune regulation within the tumor microenvironment (TME).11,12 For instance, PCSK9 inhibitors have been shown to enhance the anti-tumor activity of CD8+ T cells by promoting low-density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR)-mediated T cell receptor (TCR) recycling and signaling.13 Furthermore, PCSK9 inhibition can upregulate major histocompatibility complex-I (MHC-I) expression on tumor cell surfaces, thereby activating infiltrating CD8+ T cells and exerting anti-tumor effects. This mechanism may also potentiate the efficacy of PD-1 immune checkpoint inhibitors.14 However, whether PCSK9 influences OV-mediated oncolysis or immunomodulation remains largely unknown.

Here, we investigated the impact of OV infection on T cell function and found that it curtails TCR-MHC-I recognition via PCSK9 upregulation in tumor cells. Targeting PCSK9 with anti-PCSK9 inhibitor or recombinant OVs encoding anti-PCSK9 single-chain variable antibody fragments (scFv) enhanced OVs’ anti-tumor efficacy and established durable T cell immunity. Engineered OVs carrying anti-PCSK9 scFv exhibited better viral replication kinetics and improved the efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy. Collectively, our data demonstrate that PCSK9 blockade potentiates OV therapy and offers a treatment strategy for colorectal cancer (CRC).

Results

OV infection impaired CD8+ T cell response via inducing MHC-I degradation

Although OVs can directly lyse tumor cells and prime anti-tumor immune responses, their broader immunological consequences remain incompletely characterized. Vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV) was introduced as a potent oncolytic candidate and engineered by deleting the methionine at residue 51 of the matrix protein (VSVΔ51) to reduce the potential neurotoxicity.15 To systematically profile the immune consequences induced by VSVΔ51 infection, we re-analyzed our published single-cell sequencing (scRNA-seq) data on both PBS-treated and VSVΔ51-injected MC38 tumors at a later time point post-treatment (Figure 1A).16 Unexpectedly, enrichment analysis of T cell cluster revealed a significant downregulation of antigen processing and presentation pathways, along with TCR signaling, in the VSVΔ51-infected group compared to the control group (Figures 1B, 1C, and S1A). To functionally validate these findings, we established a co-culture model of CD8+ T cells from OT1 mice and CT26 tumor cells expressing ovalbumin peptide (CT26-OVA) in the conditioned medium (CM) from cancer cells. The CM derived from VSVΔ51-infected cancer cells was collected and subsequently exposed to UV light to inactivate the virus (CM-VSVΔ51-UV). CM-VSVΔ51-UV significantly impaired the cytotoxicity of OT1 CD8+ T cells against CT26-OVA cells (Figure 1D). Next, we used a pseudotyped strain VSVΔ51ΔG containing a gene deletion encoding its glycoprotein for the co-culture assay, which limits the viral spread and direct oncolysis of VSVΔ51. The CM derived from VSVΔ51ΔG (CM-VSVΔ51ΔG) also decreased the cytotoxicity of OT1 CD8+ T cells (Figure 1E).

Given that VSVΔ51 preferentially infects and replicates in tumor cells, we further investigated the impact of VSVΔ51 infection on tumor-cell surface MHC-I expression. Flow cytometry analysis revealed a decrease in MHC-I expression on the surface of CT26-OVA cells upon the treatment with CM-VSVΔ51-UV or CM-VSVΔ51ΔG (Figures 1F and 1G). Furthermore, heat-inactivated CM failed to reduce the cytotoxicity of OT1 CD8+ T cells and membrane MHC-I expression (Figures S1B and S1C). The above results indicate that VSVΔ51 infection significantly downregulates the surface MHC-I in bystander cells via a paracrine manner.

Next, we determined whether this observed phenomenon could be generalized to other cancer cells or virus strains. We found that VSVΔ51 also reduced the surface MHC-I in both DLD1 and HCT116 human CRC cell lines (Figure 1H). Vaccinia virus (VV)-infected cells and herpes simplex virus (HSV)-infected cells consistently degraded the MHC-I membrane (Figure S1D) and the total protein levels of MHC-I (Figure S1E). These findings suggest that OV infection can induce MHC-I loss in non-infected bystander cells, which diminished the CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity and promotes immune escape.

OV infection induces the SREBP2-PCSK9 axis to degrade MHC-I through the lysosomal pathway

To investigate how OV infection downregulates MHC-I, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) of the control and VSVΔ51-infected DLD1 cells. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) confirmed that VSVΔ51 infection upregulates the pathway of “regulation of cholesterol metabolic process” (Figure 2A). We assessed intracellular cholesterol levels and found a significant reduction in the total and free cholesterol content in DLD1 and HCT116 cells following VSVΔ51 infection (Figures S2A and S2B). Previous studies have demonstrated that cholesterol metabolism regulator PCSK9 plays a role in lysosomal degradation of MHC-I,14,17,18 which drove us to explore the role of PCSK9 in VSVΔ51 infection-regulated MHC-I loss. We found that VSVΔ51 infection upregulated the transcriptional levels of PCSK9 and its upstream transcription factor SREBP2 (Figure 2B). Immunoblot analysis confirmed that SREBP2 and PCSK9 levels were markedly increased, while MHC-I levels were significantly decreased post-VSVΔ51 infection (Figure 2C). Next, we investigated whether the SREBP2-PCSK9 axis mediated the downregulation of MHC-I. Pharmacologic inhibition of SREBP2 with fatostatin abolished the upregulation of PCSK9 upon VSVΔ51 infection (Figures S2D and S2E), while exogenous treatment of IFNa2b did not influence the PCSK9 expression (Figures S2F and S2G), establishing that VSVΔ51 infection triggers PCSK9 expression via SREBP2 activation. Then we found that the approved PCSK9-neutralizing antibody alirocumab rescued VSVΔ51-induced downregulation of MHC-I, as demonstrated by immunoblot analysis (Figures 2E and S2I) and flow cytometry (Figures 2F and S2H). We generated the PCSK9-knockout HCT-116 cells using CRISPR-Cas9 technology and found that VSVΔ51 infection failed to reduce MHC-I expression in PCSK9-knockout cells (Figures 2G and 2H). Concordantly, both pharmacological inhibition of SREBP2 via fatostatin (Figures 2I and S2J) and genomic knockdown of SREBP2 using short hairpin RNA (Figures 2J and 2K) reversed the MHC-I downregulation induced by VSVΔ51 infection. GSEA analysis also revealed the upregulation of “processing and presentation of peptide antigen via MHC class I” upon VSVΔ51 infection (Figure 2L). Quantitative PCR analysis demonstrated increased transcriptional levels of HLA-A, HLA-B, HLA-C, B2M, TAPBP, and TAP1 (Figures 2M and S2K). The transcriptional increase of the MHC-I pathway suggests that VSVΔ51-mediated downregulation of MHC-I occurs post-transcriptionally. In cycloheximide (CHX) chase experiments, VSVΔ51 significantly enhanced the rate of MHC-I degradation (Figure 2N). To examine the detailed mechanism of MHC-I degradation, the proteasome inhibitor MG132 and the lysosome inhibitor bafilomycin A1 (Baf-A1) were used to block protein degradation pathways. The results showed that Baf-A1, but not MG132, rescued VSVΔ51-induced downregulation of MHC-I (Figures 2O and S2L). Collectively, these data demonstrate that VSVΔ51 infection activates the SREBP2-PCSK9 axis to induce lysosomal degradation of MHC-I.

PCSK9 inhibition and VSVΔ51 treatment combined to suppress multiple tumor models and reshape the tumor microenvironment

To determine whether PCSK9 inhibition could enhance the therapeutic potential of OVs, we evaluated the effect of the anti-PCSK9 antibody on viral replication and oncolysis in vitro. However, alirocumab treatment did not influence the viral spread (Figure S3A) or viral replication (Figures S3B and S3C). Treatment with alirocumab also did not amplify the direct oncolysis of VSVΔ51 (Figure S3D). We then assessed the combined effect of anti-PCSK9 antibody and VSVΔ51 in vivo. Mice bearing subcutaneous MC38 tumors (microsatellite instability [MSI]) were treated with (1) intravenous VSVΔ51, (2) intraperitoneal alirocumab, or (3) a combination of VSVΔ51 and alirocumab (Figure 3A). Patients with microsatellite-stable (MSS) or proficient mismatch repair CRC, which accounts for approximately 95% of all CRC cases, exhibit low response rates to the immunotherapy.19 Therefore, syngeneic CT26 and Colon26 (MSS) tumor models were used to assess the effects of the combined potential of VSVΔ51/alirocumab treatment. Consistently, alirocumab treatment also synergized with VSVΔ51 to control tumor growth and reduce tumor burden in the subcutaneous tumor models of Colon26 (Figures 3F–3H) and CT26 (Figures 3J–3L). Tumor growth curves showed that the VSVΔ51/alirocumab combination induced tumor regression in partial mice (20%), whereas no animals in the monotherapy group exhibited complete remission in the CT26 model (Figure S3E).

To systematically delineate the immune alterations by the VSVΔ51/PCSK9i combination, we performed scRNA-seq on CD45+ cells isolated from CT26 tumors. We classified these clusters into 11 cell types by canonical cell type-specific markers (Figure 3M). By quantifying the cell composition of the samples, we found that VSVΔ51 treatment increased the frequency of macrophages; unexpectedly, the frequency of T cells did not differ significantly among the four groups (Figure 3N).

Focusing on the cluster of T cells, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis revealed that TCR signaling pathway was upregulated in the combination group compared with the VSVΔ51 group (Figure 3O), indicating that PCSK9 inhibition alleviates the OV-induced suppression of T cell cytotoxic capacity. We then reanalyzed T cell clusters and obtained 6 functional subclusters (Figures 3P and 3Q). Notably, the cytotoxicity-associated CD8_Teff subset was abundant in the combination group (Figure 3R). The ratio of CD4_T helper in the combination group declined compared to VSVΔ51 monotherapy group (Figure 3R). Such results indicate that CD8+ T cells primarily mediated the anti-tumor activity. Moreover, pseudotime analysis of T cell clusters revealed that T cells from the combination group preferentially occupied at earlier differentiation states, compared to the VSVΔ51 group (Figure 3S).

We further profiled natural killer (NK) cells and mono/macrophage compartments to obtain a comprehensive view of combination-therapy-driven TME remodeling. Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) analysis of NK cells showed 7 distinct NK cell subsets (Figure S4A). The PCSK9i/VSVΔ51 combination-treated tumor displayed higher cytotoxic population (Gzma+) and chemokine population (Ccl5+) versus VSVΔ51-treated tumor (Figure S4B). We re-clustered the mono-/macrophage population and identified 9 distinct subsets (Figure S4C). Mono-/macrophage cells in the combination group contained higher neutrophil-recruiting subsets (Cxcl2+) and lower complement-activating populations (C1qa+) than the VSVΔ51 group (Figure S4D). We also observed that combination treatment reduced the M2 score20 of macrophage polarization compared to the VSVΔ51 group (Figure S4E). Thus, combination therapy reprograms the principal immune infiltrates from a VSVΔ51-induced state into a more activated, cytotoxic milieu.

The combination of VSVΔ51 and PCSK9 inhibition fosters the stem-like CD8+ T cells and established the anti-tumor memory

We further investigated which immune cells mediated the anti-tumor activity of VSVΔ51/alirocumab combination through in vivo depletion experiments using antibodies against CD4+ and CD8+ T cells. The depletion antibodies effectively eliminated CD4+ and CD8+ T cells (Figure 4A). Our data demonstrated that depletion of CD8+ T cells largely abrogated the inhibitory effect of VSVΔ51/alirocumab combination on tumor growth, but not CD4+ T cells (Figure 4B). These observations prompted us to focus on the CD8+ T cell immunity upon the VSVΔ51/alirocumab combination and systematically analyzed T cells infiltrating the tumors using flow cytometry in CT26 and MC38 models (Figure S5A). The VSVΔ51/alirocumab combination significantly increased the percentage of tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells and the expression of interferon (IFN)-γ or Granzyme B in CD8+ T cells compared to the single treatment or control group (Figures 4C, 4D, S5B, and S5C). To determine the durability of the therapeutic effect of the VSVΔ51/PCSK9i combination, we established a tumor rechallenge model. Tumor-free mice from VSVΔ51/alirocumab combination therapy were rechallenged with a 2-fold higher dose of autologous CT26 cells or heterologous 4T1 cells on the bilateral side on day 50 post first tumor inoculation. Age-matched naive mice were injected with the same number of CT26 or 4T1 cells as the control group (Figure 4E). Notably, all tumor-free mice rejected the rechallenged CT26 tumors, but not the 4T1 tumors (Figures 4F and 4G). Partial regression of 4T1 tumors was also observed in the tumor-free mice; however, all naive mice developed CT26 and 4T1 tumors after inoculation (Figures 4F and 4G). These findings indicate that the VSVΔ51/alirocumab combination could establish durable tumor-specific immunity. Immune profiling of the peripheral lymphoid organs was also analyzed on day 50 in tumor-free mice. The tumor-free mice induced by combination treatment exhibited higher percentages of CD4+ and CD8+ central memory T cells (TCM), whereas effector memory T cells (TEM) proportions remained unchanged in the spleen and draining lymph nodes (Figures 4H and S5D).

Furthermore, splenic CD3+ T cells were isolated from the above-mentioned mice and co-cultured with CT26 or 4T1 cells in vitro. Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release assays confirmed that splenic T cells derived from tumor-free mice demonstrated stronger cytotoxic activity against autologous CT26 cells than those from naive mice, whereas no such effect was observed against heterologous 4T1 cells (Figure 4I). Upon stimulation with CT26 cells, splenic T cells from tumor-free mice also displayed significantly increased proportions of CD107a+ CD8+ T cells, Ki67+ CD8+ T cells, and Ki67+ CD4+ T cells relative to other groups (Figure 4J). Collectively, these data demonstrate that the VSVΔ51/PCSK9i combination elicits robust memory T cell responses and durable tumor-specific immunity.

Then, we evaluated the replication capacity and oncolytic efficacy of rVSV-Ali and rVSV-Evo. The plaque assay revealed that rVSV-Ali and rVSV-Evo exhibited higher viral titers than VSVΔ51 especially in DLD1 and HCT116 cells (Figure 5F). Additionally, rVSV-Ali and rVSV-Evo exhibited higher oncolytic activity in vitro at low MOI than parental VSVΔ51 (Figure 5G).

Our data demonstrated that extracellular addition of the PCSK9 antibody did not influence the viral replication (Figures S3A–S3C). However, rVSV-Ali and rVSV-Evo exhibited higher kinetics of viral replication than parental VSVΔ51 (Figure 5F). Previous studies have suggested that intracellular PCSK9 can modulate type I IFN pathways.21,22 Therefore, we measured the expression of IFNB1 and IFN-stimulated genes (ISG) following viral infection. The rVSV-Ali and rVSV-Evo significantly restrained the transcription of IFNB1, ISG15, and CXCL10, compared with VSVΔ51 (Figure S6A). PCSK9 knockout also suppressed the enhancement of IFNB1, ISG15, and CXCL10 transcription induced by VSVΔ51 infection (Figure S6B). Consistently, PCSK9 knockout enhanced the viral replication of VSVΔ51 (Figures S6C and S6D), which was reversed by exogenous IFNa2b treatment (Figures S6C and S6D). These findings suggest that rVSV-Ali and rVSV-Evo inhibit the IFN pathway by neutralizing intracellular PCSK9, which promotes viral replication kinetics.

We further analyzed the viral replication and tumor tropism of rVSV-Ali/Evo in vivo. We established a CT26 subcutaneous tumor model and intravenously injected VSVΔ51, rVSV-Ali, or rVSV-Evo respectively. The detection of viral RNA revealed an increase of vesicular stomatitis virus G protein (VSV-G) expression in tumor tissues of the rVSV-Ali and rVSV-Evo groups compared with the VSVΔ51 group (Figure 5H). However, there were no significant changes in the normal tissues including kidney, liver, lung, heart, and spleen (Figure 5H). Immunoblotting analysis of tumor tissues revealed that VSVΔ51 treatment induced the upregulation of PCSK9 and SREBP2 and lowered the MHC-I and LDLR expression, whereas rVSV-Ali/Evo restored MHC-I expression and enhanced the viral protein VSV-G (Figure 5I). No significant changes in the expression of SREBP2, PCSK9, or LDLR were observed in the liver tissues (Figure 5J). Furthermore, rVSV-Ali treatment only significantly elevated the cholesterol level specifically in tumor tissues, but did not affect the hepatic or serum cholesterol (Figure 5K). In contrast, the VSVΔ51/alirocumab combination treatment exerted a systemic effect on cholesterol homeostasis, reducing serum cholesterol levels while increasing hepatic cholesterol levels (Figure 5K). No significant difference in tumor control was observed between rVSV-Ali and VSVΔ51/alirocumab combination therapy in the CT26 tumor model (Figures S6E–S6H). These results indicate that rVSV-Ali/Evo could reverse the VSVΔ51-induced MHC-I loss, enabling local delivery of the anti-PCSK9 scFV in TME and avoiding the potential hepatotoxicity.

To further elucidate the effects of rVSV-Ali and rVSV-Evo on tumor immune microenvironment, we analyzed the tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) in the CT26 subcutaneous tumor model (Figure S7A). The treatment with rVSV-Ali or rVSV-Evo significantly increased the percentage of tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells and the expression of IFN-γ and Granzyme B in CD8+ T cells (Figure 6M). The proportion of Tregs within CD4+ T cells was modestly increased post-rVSV-Ali or rVSV-Evo treatment; however, the Teff/Treg ratio remained significantly elevated compared to the VSVΔ51 group (Figure 6M). These data indicate that rVSV-Ali and rVSV-Evo could potentiate the in vivo therapeutic effect by enhancing CD8+ T cell immunity. Moreover, both rVSV-Ali and rVSV-Evo induced a higher proportion of CD127hi memory precursor cells than VSVΔ51 treatment (Figure S8A). Consistently, TCF7, a transcription factor critical for central memory CD8+ T cell formation, was also upregulated in rVSV-Ali and rVSV-Evo groups (Figure S8B). We observed that TCM was nearly undetectable in TILs, whereas CD4+ or CD8+ TEM significantly increased following treatment with VSVΔ51, rVSV-Ali, or rVSV-Evo (Figures S8C and S8D).

To further address whether rVSV-Ali and rVSV-Evo could activate the long-term immune memory development, we next analyzed the splenic lymphocytes. Consistent with the immunological profile observed in TILs, treatment with either rVSV-Ali or rVSV-Evo significantly increased the expression of Granzyme B in CD8+ T cells and enhanced the proportion of CD127hi or TCF7hi memory precursor cells (Figures 6N, S8E, and S8F). The proportion of TEM cells within both CD4+ and CD8+ T cell subsets was concomitantly elevated in all treatments (Figures 6O and S8G). Interestingly, VSVΔ51 treatment reduced the TCM fraction within CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, whereas rVSV-Ali and rVSV-Evo markedly restored TCM population (Figures 6O and S8G). The aforementioned findings indicated that rVSV-Ali/Evo not only activated robust anti-tumor immunity but also promoted the development of long-term immunological memory.

We concurrently profiled immune reprogramming in both tumor and spleen following engineered rVSV-Ali/Evo + anti-PD-1 combination therapy. In the analysis of CT26 subcutaneous model TILs, the treatment of rVSV-Ali + anti-PD-1 or rVSV-Evo + anti-PD-1 did not induce a significant increase in the infiltration and functional activity of CD8+ T cells, compared to VSVΔ51 + anti-PD-1 group (Figure S9A). An enhancement in the ratio of CD127hi and TCF7hi memory precursor CD8+ T cells was observed in rVSV-Ali + anti-PD-1 or rVSV-Evo + anti-PD-1 groups compared to VSVΔ51 + anti-PD-1 group (Figure S9B). However, there were no significant changes in the CD4+ and CD8+ TEM fractions within the TILs (Figure S9C). These results demonstrate that rVSV-Ali/Evo show greater potential in activating long-term immune memory than VSVΔ51 in the combination with anti-PD-1 therapy.

In the analysis of splenic lymphocytes, the treatment of rVSV-Ali + anti-PD-1 or rVSV-Evo + anti-PD-1 did not significantly enhance CD8+ T cell population or function (Figure S9D), but markedly increased CD127hi and TCF7hi memory precursor cells compared to VSVΔ51 + anti-PD-1 group (Figure 7D). Additionally, the treatments with rVSV-Ali + anti-PD-1 or rVSV-Evo + anti-PD-1 also mitigated the reduction of TCM induced by VSVΔ51 + anti-PD-1 group, but did not induce significant changes in the TEM compared to VSVΔ51 + anti-PD-1 group (Figure 7E). Additionally, tumor rechallenge experiments (Figure S9E) revealed that mice cured by combined treatment with anti-PCSK9 scFv-engineered VSV and anti-PD-1 antibody completely rejected the rechallenged CT26 tumors, but not 4T1 tumors (Figures S9F–S9K). Partial regression of 4T1 tumors was observed in tumor-free mice, whereas all naive mice developed CT26 and 4T1 tumors upon inoculation (Figures S9F–S9K).

To further explore the clinical potential of rVSV-Ali as combination therapy for anti-PD-1 treatment, we established a human peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC)-engrafted humanized mouse model by intravenously injecting 1 × 107 PBMCs into NOD-IL2Rγnull (NSG) mice; 1 week later, 5 × 106 HCT116 cells were injected subcutaneously (Figure 7F). Three weeks after PBMC infusion, flow cytometry of mouse peripheral blood showed a marked increase in human CD45+ cells, confirming successful engraftment (Figure S10A). Tumor-bearing mice were then subjected to the indicated treatments. Notably, the combination of rVSV-Ali and anti-PD-1 antibody achieved the most potent therapeutic efficacy, leading to marked tumor regression and an 80% complete response rate (4 of 5 mice; Figures 7G and 7H). Collectively, these findings highlight the promising translational potential of rVSV-Ali in combination with PD-1 checkpoint blockade.

Discussion

OVs can mediate direct tumor lysis and reshape the cold TME by enhancing anti-tumor immunity while diminishing immunosuppression. However, the anti-tumor immune responses to OVs are not sustained. The immune consequences of oncolytic therapy remain insufficiently understood.1 Herein, we identified the induction of PCSK9 post viral infection as a negative regulator of T cell immunity via degradation of the MHC-I on tumor cells. Our results demonstrate that targeting PCSK9 can potentiate oncolytic virotherapy by promoting the development of effector and memory CD8+ T cell.

MHC presentation is the primary mechanism by which the adaptive immune system supervises viral infections. Consequently, many viruses have evolved sophisticated immune evasion strategies that specifically target MHC pathways. Previous studies have shown that certain viruses—such as human immunodeficiency virus type 1 (HIV-1)27 and Kaposi’s sarcoma-associated herpesvirus28,29—can downregulate MHC-I expression on host cells. Inversely, oncolytic HSV has been reported to reprogram the TME and upregulate MHC-II on tumor cells in vivo, potentially contributing to polyfunctional CD4+ T cells for tumor control and recall responses.30 In this study, we demonstrate that OVs infection curtails TCR-MHC-I recognition, thereby enabling tumor cell immune evasion.

In our study, OV infection triggers the secretion of PCSK9, leading to lysosomal degradation of MHC-I. Consistent with previous reports, PCSK9 over-expression promotes lysosomal degradation of MHC-I.14 A key advantage of targeting PCSK9 is the availability of the Food and Drug Administration-approved PCSK9-neutralizing antibody alirocumab. Previous kinetic analyses revealed that evolocumab binds murine PCSK9 with a Kd of 17 nmol/L—approximately 1,000-fold lower affinity than for human PCSK9 (Kd = 16 pmol/L). Alirocumab retains modest cross-reactivity, exhibiting a 4.5-fold reduction in affinity for murine PCSK9 (Kd = 2.61 nmol/L) versus human PCSK9 (Kd = 0.58 nmol/L).13 Consequently, alirocumab was chosen as the preferred agent for in vivo murine studies. In both microsatellite unstable (MC38) and MSS (CT26 and Colon26) tumors, alirocumab markedly potentiated VSVΔ51 efficacy in vivo by eliciting anti-tumor CD8+ T cell responses. This specific anti-tumor immunity persisted long term in tumor-free mice. Intriguingly, combination therapy demonstrated superior efficacy in MSS tumors (CT26 and Colon26) compared to MSI-high tumors (MC38), an effect potentially attributable to divergent basal PCSK9 expression levels across these tumor types. Collectively, these data indicate that PCSK9 inhibition combined with VSVΔ51 constitutes an effective strategy for tumors refractory to conventional immunotherapy.

Both rVSV-Ali and rVSV-Evo exhibited markedly superior anti-tumor efficacy compared to VSVΔ51. Interestingly, rVSV-Ali exhibited superior therapeutic efficacy than rVSV-Evo across multiple tumor-bearing mouse models. This may be attributed to the higher affinity of alirocumab for murine PCSK9 than that of evolocumab.13 Unexpectedly, rVSV-Ali and rVSV-Evo demonstrated higher viral replication kinetics than VSVΔ51. This difference is likely due to the suppression of endogenous PCSK9 by the recombinant VSV. Consistent with our findings, recent studies have shown that intracellular PCSK9 can promote type I interferon responses in tumor cells.21,22

Engineering OVs to express anti-PCSK9 scFV offers several key advantages: (1) it enhances the durability of anti-tumor CD8+ T cell immunity by counteracting OV-mediated downregulation and restoring MHC-I antigen presentation, (2) it promotes viral replication kinetics and persistence within tumor cells through mitigation of the type I interferon response, (3) it enables efficient intra-tumoral delivery of the anti-PCSK9 agent while reducing systemic exposure and potential hepatotoxicity associated with conventional antibody therapy, and (4) it directly overcomes MHC-I degradation, a major mechanism of immune escape triggered by OV infection of tumor cells.

In summary, we demonstrate that OVs exert a negative regulatory effect on T cell immunity. Targeting PCSK9 markedly enhances the therapeutic efficacy of OVs against CRC, an effect attributable to PCSK9-antibody-mediated T cell immune reprogramming. Collectively, this study highlights the potential of PCSK9-targeted strategies to potentiate OV therapy and offers an approach for the treatment of CRC.

Limitations of the study

Our current study does present some limitations. First, the engineering of OVs in this study utilized the VSV platform. However, clinically approved OVs are predominantly DNA viruses. It is imperative to express the PCSK9-targeting scFv in oncolytic DNA viruses and evaluate the therapeutic efficacy and viral virulence. Second, intravenous OV remains default for accessing metastases, yet systemic neutralization limits efficacy.31 Prior nano-camouflage strategies have circumvented this limitation.32,33 While the previous study used naked virus to establish proof-of-concept, future iterations will integrate such delivery platforms to preserve systemic bioavailability without sacrificing safety. Third, we cannot completely exclude the possibility that PCSK9 inhibition augments antitumor immunity by remodeling cholesterol metabolism. The specific impact of cholesterol remodeling on immune cell function will be further investigated.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animals

Female C57BL/6J, BALB/c, and BALB/c nude mice (4–8 weeks old, female) were purchased from GDMLAC and Rise mice, then maintained under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions (25°C–27°C, 50% humidity, 12-h light/dark cycle). Mice were acclimated for at least 5 days before experimentation. Animal care and experiments were performed in strict accordance with the “Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals” and the “Principles for the Utilization and Care of Vertebrate Animals” and were approved by Ethics Review Committee of Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital (KY-N-2022-129-02).

Cell lines

All cell lines used in this study were obtained from ATCC, Procell, and Meisen, including a range of CRC cell lines such as DLD1 and HCT116, as well as the murine CRC cell lines CT26, Colon26, and MC38, and the murine mammary carcinoma cell line 4T1. Each cell line was maintained under standard culture conditions, which involved incubation in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 at a temperature of 37°C. They were cultured in either DMEM or RPMI 1640 medium, both sourced from Procell, supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, from Vazyme), and a cocktail of antibiotics comprising 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin. Cell lines were authenticated by the short tandem repeat (STR) method and tested negative for mycoplasma contaminations.

Method details

The generation of cytotoxic T lymphocytes from OT-1 mice

Splenocytes from OT-1 mice were collected and processed using aseptic methods. RBC Lysis Buffer was used to lyse red blood cells for 5 min at room temperature. Subsequently, splenocytes were centrifuged, resuspended at a concentration of 1 × 106 cells/mL in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% antibiotics, 2-mercaptoethanol, along with 10 nmol/L OVA peptide and 10 ng/mL human rIL-2, and incubated for 3 days. Following this, cells were further cultured in fresh medium with 10 ng/mL IL-2 for an additional 2 days in preparation for subsequent experiments.

UV-inactivated OVs

500 μL of the conditioned medium after infection with VSVΔ51 was placed in a 12-well plate on ice and exposed to 254 nm UV-C light (CL-1000 Ultraviolet Crosslinker, UVP) at 1 J cm-2 (≈10 min at 10 mW cm-2) with gentle agitation every 2 min to ensure uniform exposure. After irradiation, 10 μL was immediately added to a 12-well monolayer of BHK-21 cells and incubated for 48 h; absence of cytopathic effect under microscopy confirmed complete loss of infectious particles.

Gene Transfer and stable cell line construction

Transfected HEK293T cells with core vector, psPAX2, and pMD2.G (4:3:1 ratio) using 10 mg/mL polyethylenimine, with medium refreshed 8h later. After 48h, lentiviral supernatants were harvested, centrifuged (2000 rpm, 10 min), and filtered (0.45 μm). DLD1 and HCT116 cells were exposed to the virus for 8h, then selected with puromycin (DLD1: 2 μg/mL; HCT116: 1.5 μg/mL) for 7-10 days to form stable lines.

Construction of recombinant OVs

To construct rVSV-Ali and rVSV-Evo, the anti-PCSK9 scFv coding sequences (Table S1) were inserted into VSV backbone between the VSV glycoprotein (G protein) and polymerase protein (L protein) using NheI (Thermo Fisher, ER0972) and XhoI (Thermo Fisher, ER0691) restriction enzyme sites. The VSV backbone harbors an M51R substitution in the matrix (M) gene, attenuating the neural virulence of the original strain.

Then rVSV-Ali and rVSV-Evo were rescued from the pXN2 cDNA plasmid using the established reverse genetics system in BHK cells. In brief, BHK cells were infected with by recombinant vaccinia virus producing the T7 RNA polymerase (rVV-T7) at an MOI of 1. Cells were incubated at 37°C and 5% CO2. After 2 h, cells were transfected with pVSV-XN2 genomic VSV plasmid, pBluescript (pBS)-encoding VSV-N, pBS-encoding VSV-P, and pBS-encoding VSV L proteins using lipofectamine 2000 reagent. Cells were incubated at 37°C and 5% CO2 for 48 h. After 48 h, supernatant was collected and clarified by passing through a 0.2-μm filter. The rVSV-Ali and rVSV-Evo were then expanded and titrated in Vero cells, then the titer of the recombinant virus was determined using a plaque assay.

Animal models

Approval was secured from Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital’s Ethics Committee. CT26 cells (2 × 106 in 100 μL PBS) were injected subcutaneously into 6-week-old female BALB/c mice’s hind flanks. At day 6, mice were split into four groups and administered Alirocumab (10 mg/kg, 5 dose) via i. p. injection with or without VSVΔ51 (3 × 107pfu/mouse) via i. v. injection. For Colon26 (2 × 106 in 100 μL PBS) or MC38 cells (8 × 105 in 100 μL PBS), the procedure was repeated in 6-week-old female BALB/c or C57BL/6 mice. Tumor dimensions were measured, with volume calculated as (length × widthˆ2)/2.

Humanized PBMC mice were established by intravenous injection of 1 × 10⁷ freshly isolated human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) into 6–8-week-old NOD-scid IL2Rγnull (NSG) mice. Engraftment was confirmed by flow cytometry detection of human CD45+ cells in peripheral blood at week 3 post-transplantation.

Mouse bioluminescence imaging

Mice were implanted with CT26 expressing luciferase. Mice were injected intraperitoneally with a luciferin solution (15 mg/mL in DPBS, dose of 150 mg/kg). The bioluminescence images were acquired using the IVIS Lumina system and analyzed by Living Image software.

RNA sequencing and transcriptome data analysis

Total RNA was extracted from DLD1 cells using the TRIzol reagent (Life Technologies). Samples were sent to Shanghai Biotechnology Corporation for RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis. Functional analysis of differentially expressed genes was performed by gene ontology (GO) and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA).

Single-cell RNA sequencing analysis

Raw scRNA-seq data were analyzed in R using Seurat (v4.2). For each sample, Seurat objects were created from gene–cell count matrices and filtered to remove low-quality cells/doublets using consistent QC criteria (nFeature_RNA 200–6,000, nCount_RNA 500–50,000, percent.mt < 10%; genes expressed in <3 cells removed). Data were log-normalized (NormalizeData, LogNormalize, scale.factor = 10,000), variable genes were selected (FindVariableFeatures, vst, 2,000), scaled (ScaleData), and reduced by PCA (RunPCA), with the top 30 PCs used for downstream analyses. Multi-sample integration and batch correction were performed using Harmony (RunHarmony; batch = sample ID), followed by SNN construction (FindNeighbors, dims = 1:30), clustering (FindClusters, resolution = 0.5), and UMAP visualization (RunUMAP, dims = 1:30). Cell types were annotated based on canonical markers (FindAllMarkers) and curated references, with marker expression visualized using FeaturePlot/DotPlot and ggplot2. T/NK cells and macrophages were subset by lineage markers and reprocessed with the same workflow (including Harmony when applicable), then annotated into subtypes using established markers and polarization-related signatures. Macrophage M1/M2 polarization was quantified by AddModuleScore using published M1/M2 gene signatures, and scores were compared across subsets and experimental groups. Differential expression between groups (e.g., Comb vs. VSVΔ51) was performed within each annotated population using FindMarkers (Wilcoxon; min.pct = 0.1, logfc.threshold = 0.25) with Benjamini–Hochberg correction (adjusted p < 0.05), and significant DE genes (adjusted p < 0.05, |log2FC| ≥ 0.25) were used for KEGG enrichment with clusterProfiler (bitr for Entrez conversion; enrichKEGG with BH correction; adjusted p < 0.05), visualized by ggplot2. Pseudotime trajectories were inferred using Monocle on a random 20% subset of cells after filtering genes detected in <10 cells, using DDRTree and orderCells with a biologically defined root state, and visualized by pseudotime and group.

Plague assay

Briefly, after infection, supernatants or sera containing virus were collected and diluted to infect Vero cells plated on 24-well plates at 90% confluence. Two hours post infection, the supernatants were removed, the cells were washed several times with PBS, and methylcellulose (0.8%) was added. Forty-eight hours later, the cells were stained with crystal violet (0.2%) for 12 h. Plaques (pfu/mL) were counted.

After blocking with 5% non-fat milk in TBS for 60 min and incubating with primary antibodies for 90 min at RT, membranes were washed three times with TBS/T (1% Tween 20) and exposed to secondary antibodies (IRDye 800CW anti-Rabbit IgG and IRDye 680RD anti-Mouse IgG, both at 1:10,000, LI-COR) for 60 min at RT. Flow cytometry analysis

Gastrointestinal tumor samples underwent harvesting, mincing, and incubation using the Miltenyi Tumor Dissociation Kit (130–096–730). Post-trituration and filtration through a 70-μm screen, cells were resuspended in FACS buffer. A subset of 1 × 106 cells was stained using BioLegend’s fluorochrome-conjugated anti-mouse antibodies, alongside isotype controls. The Zombie Red Fixable Viability Kit distinguished non-viable cells. Surface and viability staining adhered to BioLegend’s “no-wash” protocol, with intracellular Foxp3 staining conducted accordingly. Cytoflex flow cytometry was used to analyzed samples, with Flowjo software processing the data.

ELISA experiment

The production and secretion of human PCSK9 in tumor cell supernatants after infection with different OVs was measured using a pre-coated ELISA kit.

Real time RT-PCR

Employing TRIzol (Life Technologies), total RNA was isolated. Utilizing 3 μg RNA, reverse transcription was executed with oligo(dT) and RevertAid Reverse Transcriptase (Thermo Scientific) in a 20 μL volume, adhering to the protocol. qPCR analysis was carried out with SuperReal PreMix SYBR Green (TIANGEN) on a CFX96 system (Bio-Rad) under conditions: initial 95°C for 3 min, then 40 cycles at 95°C for 30 s, 60°C for 30 s, 72°C for 1 min, ending with a 72°C extension for 10 min. GAPDH was used for normalization of gene expression.

Cell viability assays

DLD1 and HCT116 cells (initially 8000 cells), treated with escalating titers of VSVΔ51 with or without Alirocumab (400ug/ml) for 24 h, were seeded into 96-well plates. Cell viability was determined using a CCK-8 assay (CCK-8, ImmunoWay Biotechnology Company, Plano, TX, USA). Absorbance values were determined at OD450. The assay was repeated at least three times.

Measurement of the cytotoxicity of CTLs

CT26 cells (initially 8000 cells), treated with conditioned medium or not, were seeded into 96-well plates and then co-cultured with different amounts of OT1. Cytotoxicity was determined using an LDH assay. Absorbance values were determined at OD490. The assay was repeated at least three times.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Prism 10.3.1 (GraphPad) software, and data are generally expressed as the mean ± standard. two-way ANOVA was used for multiple comparisons in tumor growth delay experiments. Log-rank tests were used for mouse survival analyses. Other data were compared by one-way ANOVA and unpaired two-sided Student’s t test. A p value less than 0.05 was considered statistically signiffcant.
