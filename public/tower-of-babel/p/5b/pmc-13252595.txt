Effects of sub‐symptom threshold aerobic exercise on persistent postconcussion symptom burden and exercise intolerance: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Lars-Johan V Valaas, Helene L Soberg, Mari S Rasmussen, Sophie E Steenstrup, Cathrine Brunborg, Cecilie Røe, Ingerid Kleffelgård
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractImportancePersistent postconcussion symptoms (PCS) following mild traumatic brain injury (mTBI) are common and are often associated with exercise intolerance. The effects of aerobic exercise on PCS in adolescent athletes have been promising. However, randomized controlled trials targeting persistent PCS in the adult population are needed.ObjectiveThe objective of this study was to evaluate the effects of sub‐symptom threshold aerobic exercise (SSTAE) on symptom burden and exercise intolerance in adults with persistent PCS.DesignThis was a randomized (1:1) controlled single-blind, parallel-group trial with follow-up at 12 weeks and 6 months.SettingThe setting was a TBI outpatient clinic at Oslo University Hospital, Norway.ParticipantsThe participants were 81 adults who were 18 to 59 years old and had PCS and exercise intolerance 3 to 24 months after mTBI.InterventionsThe interventions were treatment-as-usual plus SSTAE versus treatment-as-usual including only general exercise information and advice.Main Outcomes and MeasuresThe primary outcome was symptom burden, measured with the Rivermead Post-Concussion Symptoms Questionnaire. The secondary outcome was exercise intolerance, measured as the symptom threshold and minutes to stop on the Buffalo Concussion Treadmill Test. Tertiary outcomes were measurements of health-related quality of life, depression, anxiety, fatigue, and level of physical activity.ResultsNo between-group differences were observed in the Rivermead Post-Concussion Symptoms Questionnaire at 12 weeks or 6 months. sub‐symptom threshold aerobic exercise significantly improved the Buffalo Concussion Treadmill Test symptom threshold at 12 weeks, with effects sustained at 6 months. Additionally, the SSTAE group was superior to the control group in minutes to stop at 12 weeks but not at 6 months. No adverse events occurred. No statistically significant treatment effects were observed for the tertiary outcomes.ConclusionsThe added benefit of SSTAE to treatment-as-usual did not lead to greater reduction in symptom burden compared to the control group. However, SSTAE significantly improved exercise intolerance and was safe and well tolerated. Both groups improved over time in symptom burden, health-related quality of life, depression, anxiety, and fatigue.RelevanceSymptom restricted aerobic exercise intervention, provided by physical therapist, improves exercise intolerance in patients with persistent postconcussion symptoms after mTBI.Keywords: Symptom burden on the Rivermead post-concussion symptoms questionnaire (RPQ), traumatic brain injuries, exercise intolerance, buffalo concussion treadmill test, brain concussion, craniocerebral trauma, head injuries: closed

Introduction

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a complex injury, resulting from an external force to the head, leading to altered brain function or other signs of brain pathology.1 Mild TBI (mTBI) accounts for 80% to 90% of all TBIs, with an estimated annual incidence of 3.8 million in the United States, and between 302 and 1153 per 100,000 people in Norway and Canada.2,3 Common causes include falls, traffic accidents, sports-related injuries, and violence.3–5 Between 35% and 46% of patients with mTBI report persistent postconcussion symptoms (PCS) 6 months after the injury.6,7 Symptom burden typically decreases slower beyond 3 months after injury and commonly persists for a year, or longer.6,8,9

Identifying effective treatments for persistent PCS remains a clinical challenge.6,7,10 Traditionally, prolonged rest until symptoms wane has been recommended; however, this may inadvertently contribute to ongoing symptoms and exercise intolerance,11,12 as shown in a recent comparison of early versus delayed initiation of physical therapy.13

Exercise intolerance, defined as the inability to exercise or do physical activity near age-predicted maximum heart rate (HRmax) due to exacerbation of PCS, has emerged as a promising therapeutic target.10,14–17

sub‐symptom threshold aerobic exercise (SSTAE),16 typically prescribed based on incremental exercise testing until symptom exacerbation, such as the Buffalo Concussion Treadmill Test (BCTT)18,19 has emerged as a potential intervention for patients with persistent PCS and exercise intolerance. A systematic review of nonpharmacological treatments highlights graded physical exercise as a recommended intervention for reducing symptom burden in this population, although the evidence is of low to moderate certainty.10 In addition, clinical practice guidelines emphasize the importance of assessing exercise intolerance and prescribing individualized, graded aerobic exercise to address persistent physiological dysfunction.20 SSTAE has demonstrated shorter time-to-play in youth athletes during the subacute phase,16,17 and more recently in adults with persistent PCS indicating improvements in overall symptom burden in a 12-week aerobic exercise intervention.21 However, few randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have examined the effects of SSTAE on symptom burden, exercise intolerance and psychological and physical functioning in adults in the persistent phase and optimal exercise parameters have yet to be established.22,23 This gap underscores the importance of investigating SSTAE in adults to determine whether structured aerobic exercise can reduce symptom burden and improve exercise tolerance.

This study aimed to evaluate the effects of SSTAE on symptom burden and exercise intolerance, compared with treatment as-usual, consisting of general advice of physical activity and exercise, in adult patients with persistent PCS.

We hypothesized that SSTAE would lead to a greater reduction in persistent PCS burden and normalization of exercise tolerance compared to treatment-as-usual. Additionally, we hypothesized that SSTAE would improve health-related quality of life (HRQOL), depression, anxiety, fatigue and contribute to an increase in physical activity.

Methods

This trial was a parallel, randomized (1:1) controlled, single-blind clinical trial conducted from March 2022 to May 2024. Patients were enrolled from the TBI outpatient clinic at the Department of Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation, Oslo University Hospital, which is the regional trauma referral center in Southeast Norway.24

The trial adhered to the 2025 Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines.25 This study was approved by the Southeast Regional Ethics Committee (REK #256109) and the Data Protection Office at Oslo University Hospital. The study was preregistered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05086419) on October 20, 2021. The protocol including a feasibility study has been published.26 All patients provided written informed consent.

A patient organization was involved in giving feedback on the trial, including revision of the informed consent. User involvement was further supported by engaging patients in planning and managing their individual exercise programs. In addition, the primary and tertiary outcomes were patient-reported outcome measures.

Patients

The inclusion criteria were adult patients at Oslo University Hospital who were 18 to 60 years old, patients with mTBI according to World Health Organization criteria,27 persistent PCS 3 to 24 months after injury, and self-reported exacerbation of symptoms during physical activity or exercise. The exclusion criteria were severe neurological or psychiatric conditions documented in the medical record, cardiovascular disease that contraindicated exercise testing,28 extremity injuries preventing physical exercise, ongoing substance abuse, insufficient proficiency in Norwegian to understand instructions or complete questionnaires, and negative BCTT result.

Concurrent care

Both groups received treatment as usual provided by the multidisciplinary team at the outpatient TBI clinic that consisted of an individualized treatment plan tailored to specific needs, including both one-on-one treatment and participation in educational groups. The individual treatment was delivered by specialist in physical medicine and rehabilitation, neuropsychologist, occupational therapist, social worker, and/or a physical therapist based on each patient’s individual needs and according to international clinical guidelines.29,30 The physical therapy primarily addresses dizziness, balance impairments, musculoskeletal conditions, and provides general advice on physical activity, consistent with information and advice given to the present control group.

S‌STAE group

Patients randomized to the intervention group received a 12-week SSTAE program aligned with other treatment protocols used in similar studies,16,17 in addition to treatment-as-usual described in the concurrent care. The program included an introductory session outlining the exercise prescription and rationale for SSTAE, followed by 2 supervised sessions at the hospital in which patients performed SSTAE on a treadmill with guidance from a physical therapist (L-J.V.V.). Thereafter, the BCTT was repeated every third week (weeks 3, 6, and 9) to update the exercise prescription on the basis of the symptom threshold, expressed as a percentage of the HRmax. The “Outcomes” section provides a description of the BCTT. Intervention adherence was predefined26 as satisfactory if more than 67% of the participants attended the introductory therapy sessions, maintained an exercise diary, completed at least 2 BCTTs, and adhered to a minimum of 3 weekly unsupervised exercise sessions.

Patients were instructed to exercise 3 to 5 times per week at 80% to 90% of their symptom threshold, using a heart rate (in beats per minute [bpm]) monitor (Polar H10), for ~35 minutes per session. Each session included a 5-minute warm-up phase, 20 minutes at the prescribed intensity, and a 5- to 10-minute cool-down phase. For patients with a symptom threshold below 110 bpm, the target intensity was set at 110 bpm to ensure intensities associated with aerobic exercise.31,32 Patients who opted to exercise more frequently than prescribed were allowed to do so.

Patients were allowed to choose their preferred type of aerobic activity and to exercise at home, near their residence, or elsewhere. In the event of symptom exacerbation during exercise, patients were advised to reduce intensity or rest until symptoms were alleviated. If symptoms persisted, they were instructed to stop the session and resume a later day. We aimed to prevent symptom exacerbations lasting beyond the following night, which is known to increase risk of activity withdrawal.33 For any uncertainties or challenges related to the SSTAE, patients were instructed to contact the physical therapist by phone. Issues beyond the scope of SSTAE were referred to the TBI team or the patient’s general practitioner.

At each follow-up BCTT session (weeks 3, 6, and 9), exercise intensity was reevaluated and adjusted. These sessions also included a debriefing on patient-reported experiences with SSTAE, including adherence and symptom responses.

Control group

In addition on receiving the treatment-as-usual described under concurrent care, patients randomized to the control group only received general advice on physical activity both orally and in writing, consistent with international and national recommendations.31,34 The advice emphasized that it is safe to begin light physical activity and to gradually increase frequency and duration, based on each patient’s perceived PCS tolerance. As part of the baseline assessment, patients also performed BCTT; however, they did not receive individualized exercise recommendations based on their test results.

Outcomes

Demographic and injury-related characteristics include sex (women/men), age (years), body mass index (kg/m2), education (more than upper-secondary school, yes/no), present sick leave (>50%, yes/no). Injury characteristics included: the months since injury at baseline, injury mechanisms (traffic/bike accidents, falls, miscellaneous, sports, violence), Glasgow Coma Scale score (range = 13–15) and neuroimaging result (negative or positive) indicating complicated mTBI.7,35

The primary outcome was the Rivermead Post-Concussion Symptoms Questionnaire (RPQ) sum score.36 RPQ is a commonly used symptom questionnaire in the mTBI population and includes 16 items (score range = 0–64, with higher scores indicating worse symptoms) that assess physical, cognitive, and emotional symptoms during the last week compared with before the TBI. Each item is rated on an ordinal scale from 0 to 4, where 0 = no problem and 4 = a severe problem. Only item scores of ≥2 are calculated, as 1 indicates a past problem.

The secondary outcome was the symptom threshold determined by the BCTT.18,37 The test procedure is described in detail in the protocol paper.26 Briefly, patients walked on a treadmill at a brisk pace (~5.8 km/h) with the incline increasing by 1% every minute. Once a minute, patients reported symptom types and intensity using a numeric rating scale (score range = 0–10) as well as the Borg Rating of Perceived Exertion (score range = 6–20). At the same time, the assessor recorded heart rate. The test was stopped when 1 of the following criteria was met: exercise tolerance, that is, a Borg Rating of Perceived Exertion of ≥18 or > 90% of estimated HRmax, without symptom exacerbation; or exercise intolerance, that is, symptom exacerbation, defined as an increase in PCS symptoms of ≥3 points on the numeric rating scale (and + 1 for new symptoms). For patients who were exercise intolerant, the symptom threshold was defined as the heart rate at test cessation and was adjusted relative to the age-estimated HRmax using the following formula: heart rate/[211 − (0.64 × age)]38 with the minute reached noted as minutes to stop.

Tertiary outcomes included the Quality of Life after Brain Injury–Overall Scale (score range = 0–100, with higher scores indicating better HRQOL,39 with scores under 52 is considered low HRQOL,40 the 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire (score range = 0–27; cutoffs: mild = 5–9, moderate = 10–14, severe = ≥15),41 the 7-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale (score range = 0–21; cutoffs: mild = 5–9, moderate = 10–14, severe = 15–21),42 and the 9-item Fatigue Severity Scale, score range: 9–63, with higher scores indicating greater fatigue.43 A score of ≥45 on the 9-item Fatigue Severity Scale is considered the cutoff for a high level of fatigue.44 The International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) was used to estimate weekly physical activity as total metabolic equivalents in minutes per week.45

Patients’ perceived barriers to adherence were assessed using subscales from the Problematic Experience of Therapy Scale.46 These subscales are scored from 1 (disagree strongly) to 5 (agree strongly). The subscales include aggravated symptoms related to the intervention, uncertainty about how to perform the intervention, doubts about efficacy, and practical obstacles to performing the intervention.

Procedures for recording potential harms related to the test and intervention were established and recorded by the therapist (L.-J.V.V.) and blinded assessors (S.E.S., I.K.).

Sample size

Sample size was calculated based on the primary outcome, RPQ, assuming a mean group difference of 5 points12 and an SD of 8.7 arising from a pilot study conducted at the clinic.47 To achieve 80% power and a 5% significance level, 96 patients were needed.

Randomization and blinding

Patients were randomized 1:1 to the intervention or control group using a computer-generated sequence with variable block sizes, prepared by an independent statistician. Allocation was concealed using sequentially numbered, opaque sealed envelopes prepared by 1 of the authors (H.L.S.). Another author (L.-J.V.V.) was responsible for enrollment, baseline testing, and delivery of the intervention and was blinded to group allocation at baseline assessment.

All baseline clinical assessments followed a standardized data collection sequence prior to group allocation, preserving patient blinding at baseline. Follow-up assessments at 12 weeks and 6 months were conducted by blinded assessors (S.E.S., L.N., I.K.). For data analysis, dummy ID codes were used to ensure that both the first author and the statistician remained blinded to group assignment during the primary and secondary analyses, including drafting the “Results” section.

Missing data

Missing data were handled according to prespecified procedures outlined in the study protocol. For missing injury characteristics, such as Glasgow Coma Scale scores, the physician (N.A.) estimated values on the basis of medical records. Patient-reported outcome measures were managed by instrument-specific imputation methods. Primary and tertiary endpoint analyses were conducted using maximum-likelihood estimation to account for missing values. For the secondary endpoint, BCTT symptom threshold, missing values were imputed using the last recorded value to preserve within-subject continuity as prespecified. As model imputation and attempts to apply multiple imputation resulted in BCTT values exceeding the 90% HRmax threshold, biased estimates, and disproportionate weighting of imputed data, this approach was not used in the final analysis.

Statistical methods

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline demographics, injury-related characteristics, and unadjusted baseline outcome measures. Normality testing was formally assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test.

Intention-to-treat analyses were conducted for all endpoints.48 Treatment effects for the primary and tertiary endpoints (RPQ, Quality of Life after Brain Injury–Overall Scale, 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire, 7-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale, 9-item Fatigue Severity Scale, and IPAQ) were estimated as between-group changes from baseline to 12 weeks and 6 months. Analyses were performed using repeated-measures linear mixed-effect models (LMMs) with maximum-likelihood estimation, accounting for intraindividual correlations over time.

For the secondary endpoint, symptom threshold tested by the BCTT, treatment effects were estimated by a repeated-measures Tobit LMM,49 adjusted for baseline values, sex, age, body mass index, and walking speed during testing as covariates. This model was selected for its ability to include both continuous symptom threshold values and censored data (ie, stop criterion of >90% HRmax). The analysis was amended from the trial protocol26 as repeated-measures Tobit LMMs were made available.

All models included fixed effects for time and a treatment × time interaction and a random intercept for individual patients. As each hypothesis was evaluated individually, alpha correction was not applied.50 Model output includes adjusted means (estimated marginal means, denoted as β), treatment effects (between-group difference in adjusted means, denoted as Δ), SEs, 95% CIs, and P values.

All endpoint models were adjusted for baseline values, in accordance with current US Food and Drug Administration recommendations, which enhance the precision of treatment effect estimates.51 As a result, baseline differences between groups were zero (Table 1).

Results

Between March 2022 and December 2023, 404 potential patients who had mTBI and were 18 to 60 years old were treated at the TBI outpatient clinic. Of these, 181 were screened, gave initial consent, and were referred by their treating physician. A total of 105 patients completed the baseline assessment. A higher-than-expected proportion of patients were exercise tolerant, leaving 81 patients included in the study, 41 in the intervention group and 40 in the control group.

For the primary outcome (RPQ), all 81 patients completed the baseline assessment. Sixty-five patients were available for testing at 12 weeks, and 63 were available for testing at the 6-month follow-up, resulting in an overall missing rate of 14.4%. For the secondary outcome (BCTT), there were no missing values at baseline; 63 patients completed testing at 12 weeks and 53 did so at 6 months, corresponding to a total missing rate of 18.1%. In addition to the loss of follow-up presented in the flow diagram (Figure 1), the BCTT had additional missing data: 2 patients refused to perform the BCTT at the follow-up assessments, 1 had acute lung disease, 1 refused to travel, and 1 had an unknown reason.

Primary endpoint: RPQ

SSTAE did not provide additional effects on symptom burden, measured by RPQ sum scores. No significant between-group differences in treatment effect measured were observed at 12 weeks (Δ = −0.46 [95% CI = −4.39 to 3.47]; P = .82) or at 6 months (Δ = 2.91 [95% CI = − 1.06 to 6.86]; P = .150) (Table 3).

Both groups showed significant within-group improvements, measured by RPQ sum scores, over the 3 time points. From baseline to 12 weeks, the adjusted mean RPQ sum score was reduced by β = −6.52 (95% CI = −9.34 to −3.70; P < .001) in the SSTAE group and β = −6.06 (95% CI = −9.03 to −3.09; P < .001) in the control group. At 6 months, further reductions in RPQ were observed for the SSTAE group (β = −8.51 [95% CI = −11.36 to −5.65]; P < .001) and for the control group (β = −11.41 [95% CI = −14.39 to −8.43]; P < .001).

An adjusted model of the primary endpoint controlling for confounding factors, including sex, age, and time since injury as covariates, resulted in nonsignificant between-group treatment effects at 12 weeks (Δ = −0.44 [SE = 1.99] [95% CI = −4.34 to 3.44]; P = .822) and at 6 months (Δ = 3.16 [SE = 2.00] [95% CI = −0.77 to7.10]; P = .115).

Secondary endpoints: BCTT

The between-group treatment effect at 12 weeks favored the SSTAE group (Δ = 5.92 [95% CI = 0.84 to 11.0]; P = .022). At 6 months, the effect was maintained, with a trend toward significance (Δ = 4.98 [95% CI = −0.20 to 10.15]; P = .059).

The adjusted mean symptom threshold at baseline was β = 71.20% HRmax (95% CI = 68.50 to 73.90). The within-group analyses showed that both groups significantly improved over time (P < .001). At 12 weeks, the SSTAE group improved by β = 11.52 percentage points (95% CI = 7.60 to 15.44; P < .001), while the control group improved by β = 5.65 percentage points (95% CI = 1.73 to 9.56; P = .005).

The adjusted mean BCTT minutes to stop at baseline was β = 8.16 minutes (95% CI = 7.00 to 9.32). At 12 weeks, the SSTAE group showed a significantly greater improvement than the control group (Δ = 1.95 [95% CI = 0.09 to 3.81]; P = .039). At 6 months, the between-group difference was no longer significant (Δ = 0.14 [95% CI = − 1.71 to 2.00]; P = .878).

Tertiary endpoints

No statistically significant between-group treatment effects were found for the Quality of Life after Brain Injury–Overall Scale, 7-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale, 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire, and 9-item Fatigue Severity Scale scores. However, significant within-group improvements were observed over time for these outcomes. For physical activity (IPAQ, as metabolic equivalents in minutes per week), no significant within-group or between-group differences were observed.

Concurrent care

Concurrent care at the clinic during the intervention period varied according to individual needs at the time of inclusion. The intervention and control groups received cumulatively 100 and 99 individual sessions, respectively, with individual frequencies ranging from 0 to 8 in the SSTAE group and 0 to 9 in the control group. Utilization of the different disciplines in the team between the groups was similar for physical therapists, occupational therapists, psychologists, and physicians, while more patients in the SSTAE group utilized a social worker (P = .02). Adherence

Intervention adherence was satisfactory with >67% for all adherence measures according to predefined criteria utilized in the published feasibility study26 covering attendance of introductory sessions (68%; n = 28); BCTT at weeks 3, 6, and 9 (76%; n = 31); exercise diary maintenance (78%; n = 32); and adherence to a minimum of 3 weekly unsupervised exercise sessions (68%; n = 28).

At 12 weeks, the intervention group reported less severe or aggravated symptoms related to the intervention on the Problematic Experience of Therapy Scale, approaching statistical significance (SSTAE: mean = 2.25 [SD = 1.14]; control: mean = 2.85 [SD = 1.20]; 95% CI = −0.01 to 1.20; P = .053). The other subscales did not result in significant between-group differences on t tests at week 12: uncertainty about how to perform the intervention (SSTAE: mean = 1.26 [SD = 0.66]; control: mean = 1.61 [SD = 0.99]; 95% CI = −0.08 to 0.77; P = .105), doubts about efficacy (SSTAE: mean = 1.23 [SD = 0.60]; control: mean = 1.50 [SD = 0.85]; 95% CI = −0.11 to 0.64; P = .158), or practical obstacles to performing the intervention (SSTAE: mean = 2.05 [SD = 1.10]; control: mean = 1.97 [SD = 0.96]; 95% CI = −0.61 to 0.45; P = .766).

Adverse outcomes

No adverse events were reported in relation to the BCTT or during the prescribed exercise sessions, whether performed at the hospital or at home. The only reported issues were expected transient PCS exacerbation during testing and physical activity.

Discussion

This RCT evaluated the added benefits of structured SSTAE compared to the standard care control group on symptom burden and exercise intolerance in adults with persistent PCS 3 to 24 months following mTBI.

Our findings indicate that SSTAE did not provide additional benefit in reducing symptom burden, as measured by the RPQ sum score. However, both groups demonstrated overall improvement across multiple domains, including symptom burden, HRQOL, anxiety, depression, and fatigue. Notably, SSTAE was superior to the control group in improving exercise intolerance measured by BCTT at 12 weeks, and this effect was sustained at 6 months.

Effects of SSTAE on symptom burden, symptoms, and quality of life

SSTAE did not result in statistically significant improvements in RPQ sum scores compared with the control group at either 12 weeks or 6 months, consistent with Mercier et al.21 This is in contrast to RCTs on adolescent athletes with improved time-to-symptom resolution in the SSTAE groups.16,17 Importantly, both groups in the present study, demonstrated clinical meaningful improvement in symptom burden, with adjusted mean improvements of −8.51 (95% CI = −11.36 to −5.65) in the SSTAE group and − 11.41 (95% CI = −14.39 to −8.43) in the control group, exceeding the minimal clinically important difference of 4.6 points.12 The within-group improvement, for both groups, is notable given that large cohort studies report only a 0.2-point monthly reduction in RPQ scores beyond 3 months after injury.6,52 In line with our study, Mercier et al reported a 7-point RPQ reduction after 6 weeks of SSTAE, suggesting that the intervention made clinical and functional differences. The within-group change estimated in the control group at 6 months exceeded twice the minimal clinically important difference, indicating that treatment as usual combined with BCTT and general advice on physical activity also was associated with clinically important improvement of symptom burden.

Because physical activity was part of standard care in our clinic at the initiation of the study, we considered it ethically inappropriate to restrict physical activity in the control group, narrowing the contrast between the 2 groups, compared to trials using low intensity or stretching-based controls.16,17,21,53 Furthermore, the individual sessions given as part of the concurrent care were similar across the 2 groups and cannot explain the absence of treatment effect on symptom burden. Thus, the absence of a treatment effect on symptom burden may reflect a conservative estimate of the treatment effect compared to studies with more sedentary control interventions.12,33,37

Prior studies have demonstrated faster improvement in physically active groups than in more passive controls.12,13,16,17,53 In this study, IPAQ scores were similar across groups and time, suggesting both groups were equally physically active, and that the control group were able to self-regulate their exercise regimen based on the BCTT and advice without exacerbating symptoms. As this was a study on physical exercising, we might have recruited patients that were more interested in being physically active than the general mTBI population which might have impacted on the activity level in the control group as well.

The optimal duration, frequency, and period of SSTAE needed to improve persistent symptoms and normalize exercise intolerance remain unclear and likely vary across individuals, underscoring the need for further research. This study used a 12-week protocol for adults with persistent PCS, which exceeded the durations of 2 positive adolescent subacute-phase studies16,17 and aligned with a comparable adult PCS protocol.54

While RPQ sum score is widely used in PCS research and enables cross-study comparisons, it has been criticized for limited sensitivity to change55 and the presence of symptom subscales56 that may obscure group analysis due to independent temporal fluctuation between the subscales and symptoms.52,57 In addition, symptoms like noise and light sensitivity may not respond to aerobic exercise, representing items of less relevance to exercise intolerance. Together with sample size limitation, this may partly explain the lack of between-group differences in the current study.

A similar pattern was observed for HRQOL, with both groups showing significant and clinically meaningful improvements from very poor baseline levels to between poor and borderline poor levels40 at 12 weeks and 6 months. Previous studies analyzing trajectories of HRQOL suggest that low levels of HRQOL, such as observed in our study, remain poor and stable for years after injury.58 In contrast to this, both the SSTAE and control group achieved HRQOL improvements comparable to those observed in the within-group analysis in Mercier et al.21 Similar results, as in the current RCT, were reported in the intention-to-treat analysis of Mercier et al,21 however, the per-protocol analysis found significant HRQOL improvements favoring SSTAE.

Anxiety, depression, and fatigue improved significantly in both groups, with no added benefit from SSTAE. At baseline, anxiety was mild, and depression reached the threshold for moderate severity.41,42 Improvements continued through the 6 months, although depression remained within the mild range. These outcomes were not directly targeted by the intervention but were addressed through concurrent multidisciplinary care at the TBI outpatient clinic. These patterns align with Mercier et al who also observed general psychological improvements across groups.

Overall, the results support that integrating structured exercise into multidisciplinary, evidence-based care contributes to reduced symptom burden and improved HRQOL following mTBI. The within-group improvements likely reflect the combined effects of physical activity and exercise emphasis in the trial33,37 and the individualized multidisciplinary approach provided by the TBI outpatient clinic. Importantly, SSTAE did not increase symptom burden, and fewer aggravated symptoms were reported on the Problematic Experience of Therapy Scale at 12 weeks, supporting the tolerability of the intervention.

Effects of SSTAE on exercise intolerance

The SSTAE group demonstrated a significant improvement in symptom thresholds on the BCTT at 12 weeks, with the treatment effect maintained but no longer statistically significant at 6 months. On average, the SSTAE group increased their symptom threshold from 136 to 158 bpm, allowing higher-intensity exercise before symptom exacerbation, while the control group only increased to 147 bpm. The gains may partly reflect familiarity with the BCTT rather than true physiological adaptation, though experience with symptom exacerbation timing may balance this effect.

Minutes to stop on the BCTT is frequently used as the primary BCTT outcome,16,21 yet this measure may not accurately capture exercise intolerance because it is influenced by factors such as body mass index, age, sex, and overall physical capacity. Symptom threshold, by contrast, aligns more closely with proposed physiological59 and is increasingly used in recent studies.60,61 In the present trial, symptom threshold outcomes indicated that exercise intolerance improved consistently across sexes and age groups.

Increased minutes to stop during an incremental test, such as BCTT, reflects improved aerobic capacity.62 In our study, minutes to stop increased by nearly 5 minutes at 12 weeks but plateaued at 6 months (mean increase of 8 seconds), despite continued gains in symptom threshold. The nonlinear relationship between symptom threshold and minutes to stop suggests that exercise intolerance may be driven more by symptom exacerbation than aerobic capacity, which is supported by recent trial investigating the relation between exercise intolerance and deconditioning.63 This may support extending or intensifying SSTAE beyond 12 weeks for patients with occupational or recreational aerobic capacity demands.

The improvements in minutes to stop in the intervention group were comparable to Mercier et al21 while our control group outperformed the stretching-based control group in the trial of Mercier et al, suggesting a possible dosage-response effect of aerobic exercise intensity. However, IPAQ data showed high within-group variability and limited precision, highlighting the need for objective physical activity monitoring in future studies.

Safety and tolerability

No adverse events were reported, apart from transient symptom exacerbation related to exercise intolerance during BCTT and intervention. These results suggest that both BCTT and the SSTAE are safe and well tolerated in this population, even among patients with high symptom burden and low baseline HRQOL.

Clinical implications

SSTAE proved to be a safe and well tolerated intervention for adult patients with persistent PCS. While SSTAE is effective in reducing exercise intolerance, a rigorous exercise program may not be necessary for all patients with PCS. Clinicians should adopt a tailored approach, considering symptom profiles, patient goals, physical activity requirements, and recovery trajectories. Information and safe exercise guidance should be an integral part of evidence-based management of PCS following mTBI to avoid the risk of symptom flair-ups that last for days. Even in the absence of additional short-term reductions in self-reported symptom burden, improving exercise tolerance has clinical value by enabling a safe, graded return to activity, reducing activity avoidance, and facilitating progression through broader rehabilitation programs. As such, targeting exercise intolerance may represent a meaningful intervention that supports recovery trajectories beyond symptom change alone.

The intervention requires a treadmill and heart rate monitor, making it feasible for implementation in most rehabilitation settings.

Importantly, our findings suggest that initiating exercise several months after the TBI can still yield benefits. However, the current evidence shows that treatment should opt for early symptom-based management to optimize recovery.10,13,16,17,22,58,64 Although the effects of SSTAE were sustained at 6 months in exercise intolerance, the rate of improvement slowed after the intervention ended, suggesting that extending SSTAE beyond 12 weeks in clinical practice may enhance outcomes further in individual patients.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the single-center design and use of a single physical therapist to deliver both interventions may limit the generalizability. It is important to be aware of the lack of a passive control group in this RCT and that both groups received the BCTT and were physically active during the intervention period. This makes the study unsuitable to estimate differences between a SSTAE and a physically inactive control group. The active control group may potentially engage different therapeutic mechanisms, other than SSTAE. Further, the results may not be generalizable to other populations such as children and older adults. Additionally, 23.8% of eligible patients did not respond to contact or declined participation prior to baseline, which may have introduced a selection bias of participants interested in physical activity and follow-up.

The sample size was smaller than estimated, due to exclusion of a higher-than-expected proportion of patients who were exercise tolerant at the baseline assessment (19 patients were excluded due to normal BCTT), increasing the risk of a type II error. However, the nonsignificant between-group difference of the primary endpoint was 2.1 points lower than the minimal clinically important difference and, thus, unlikely to exceed the minimal clinically important difference even if the sample size target were met. However, a significant effect on exercise intolerance was observed and the sample size was larger than those in comparable trials.16,21,53 While outcome assessors were blinded and dummy ID numbers were applied in the analysis, patient blinding was not feasible due to the nature of the intervention. Finally, as no established minimal clinically important difference exists for the BCTT symptom threshold, clinical interpretation and implementation should be made with caution and tailored to the individual.

Conclusions

In this RCT, adding SSTAE to treatment-as-usual did not lead to greater reduction in symptom burden compared to the control group. sub‐symptom threshold aerobic exercise was safe and well tolerated, and significantly improved exercise intolerance at 12 weeks, with the effects maintained at 6 months. Both the SSTAE group and the control group showed within-group improvements over time in symptom burden, HRQOL, depression, anxiety, and fatigue. These results show that SSTAE could be beneficial for improving exercise intolerance in adults with persistent PCS and thus support the inclusion of SSTAE in individualized PCS care aimed at reducing exercise intolerance.
