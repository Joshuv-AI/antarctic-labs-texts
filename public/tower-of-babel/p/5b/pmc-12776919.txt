Assessment of the Relationship between Crying Patterns, Dental Anxiety, and Behavior Guidance Techniques: A Behavioral Interventional Study
Authors: Namratha Tharay, Deepa Gurunathan, SVSG Nirmala, Sivakumar Nuvvula, Venkata N Bavikati
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe cry is considered the basic reflex action the child tries to communicate. Applying behavior guidance techniques (BGT) suitably allows the dentist to lessen fear and anxiety.AimThe study aims to correlate the crying patterns with behavior and dental anxiety, and select appropriate nonpharmacological BGT for a specific type of cry.Settings and designThe present study is designed to be a single-arm, behavioral interventional study.Materials and methodsA total of 50 children aged 5-11 years were included, out of which crying was observed in 39 children. Introduction

Pediatric dentistry is a unique specialty that pacts not only with children's oral health but also targets the alteration of their behavior and instills positivity toward dentistry in the future. In addition to fear and anxiety, any disturbance in this process leads to a stressful and unpleasant environment for the child, parent, as well as the dentist.

Behavior guidance techniques (BGT) help modify the child's behavior. The goals of behavior guidance are to establish communication, alleviate fear and anxiety, deliver quality dental care, build a trusting relationship between dentist and child, and promote a child's positive attitude toward oral/dental health care.1

The cry is the utmost emotion by which the child expresses fear and anxiety in general, as well as in the dental office, which impedes the delivery of quality oral care. The cry can be defined as a complex secretomotor phenomenon characterized by the shedding of tears from the lacrimal apparatus, without any irritation of the ocular structures, and often accompanied by alterations in the muscles of facial expression and vocalizations.2 This occurs predominantly in situations characterized by separation, helplessness, and being overwhelmed by strong emotions that may be negative or positive.3

The crying child can be managed by applying BGT appropriately to deliver qualitative treatment. So, the aim of the pediatric dentist should be to assess the behavioral changes of the child both in the preventive and treatment visits and try to modify the child's behavior by appropriate employment of BGT, which decreases anxiety and fear.

Various authors have assessed the effectiveness of BGT on dental anxiety, but none of the studies has evaluated the efficacy of these techniques for individual treatment procedures and crying patterns. Therefore, the present study aims to assess and associate crying patterns with behavior and different treatment procedures. The hypothesis is the null hypothesis, that is, the crying patterns do not influence dental anxiety or behavior of the child and vice versa. The primary outcome of the study is to assess the behavior of different crying patterns whereas the secondary outcome is to evaluate the influence of various BGT on dental anxiety related to different crying patterns in children.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Setting

The study was a single-arm, interventional study with pre- and poststudy design. The current study was carried out in the Department of Pediatric and Preventive Dentistry after the institutional ethical committee approval (NDC/PG-2013-14/EC/2014).

Sample Size Determination and Inclusion Criteria

The first 50 children aged 5-11 years attending the department for one specific month who fulfilled the inclusion criteria were carefully selected. Inclusion criteria were children with (1) an age range of 6-11 irrespective of gender, (2) complete physical and mental health, with no confounding medical history, (3) no history of post-traumatic stress disorders or specific phobia related to the dental settings, and (4) parents and children who were willing to participate in the study.

Sample Size Determination and Measurements

The sample size of 30 was estimated by taking 95% power and a 5% α error using G power software.

Parameters assessed were different types of cry as per Elsbach Classification4 (Table 1) that consist of obstinate cry (OC), frightened cry (FC), manipulative cry (MC), compensatory cry (CC), boredom cry (BC), and pain cry (PC). The therapeutic procedure (TP) employed in the study was the administration of local anesthesia (LA) during the biomechanical preparation of root canals in an endodontic procedure (EP), restorative procedures (RP), and radiovisiography (RVG). The child's behavior is evaluated using Frankl's behavior rating scale5 before the start of the treatment procedure. Dental anxiety was measured by the facial image scale (FIS), clinical anxiety rating scale (CARS), and pulse rate (PR) subjectively, objectively, and physiologically, respectively. The measures of dental anxiety via validated anxiety scales FIS6 and CARS7 were recorded before the elucidation of cry and after the application of BGT, whereas PR was recorded before T1, during T2, and after T3 the treatment procedure to assess the effectiveness of BGT through changes in the dental anxiety measurements.

Procedure

The child's cry was video-recorded during the specific treatment procedure anytime starting from the first contact between the child and the pediatric dentist. The recorded video was shown to two renowned pediatric dentists who were not involved in the study to assess the type of cry. One investigator applied each BGT for 10 minutes each in a particular manner as shown in Figure 1 until the child displayed the desired positive behavior and the appropriate technique was noted, that is, if the child's behavior is molded in the first technique itself, the application of BGT is stopped at that point and further BGT is not applied. Although HOME (hand-over-mouth exercise) was omitted from the American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry (AAPD), the present study also assessed the efficiency of the same as the last technique.

Statistical Analysis

The data were entered into the Microsoft Excel spreadsheet 2010. Crosstabs were used to evaluate the parameters, and the differences were assessed using the Chi-square test and one-way ANOVA. Interexaminer and intraexaminer reliability were evaluated using Cohen's Kappa analysis. Statistical significance was considered at p < 0.05.

Results

A total of 50 children were included during the study period, out of which 39 (N = 39, 78%) children unveiled crying at some point during their respective procedure progression starting from the first dental visit. In comparing crying patterns with the gender in the present study, girls (61.5%) conveyed more of their emotions through crying than boys (38.5%). The most common type of cry observed was FC (33.3%) followed by CC (28.2%), OC (17.9%), PC (15.4%), and MC (5.1%) (Table 2, Fig. 2).

Distribution of crying patterns

Cry vs Behavior

In the present study, 8 out of 39 showed Frankl's behavior rating 1, that is, definitely negative behavior, and displayed more OC and fewer FC. MC is more prevalent in children with Frankl's behavior rating of 2, followed by FC and CC, respectively. The children with Frankl's behavior rating 3 also showed more of CC, followed by PC and FC. Children with Frankl's behavior rating 4 also exhibited cry, of which PC was more followed by CC. Statistical significance exists when the cry is related to the behavior by Chi-square analysis (p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Cry vs Dental Anxiety

When the type of cry is related to dental anxiety subjectively by FIS and objectively by CAS (clinical anxiety scale), the children with OCs and FCs have the maximum dental anxiety, whereas children with PC, CC, and MC have the lowest scores with statistical significance (p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Cry vs Birth Order

When analyzing the birth order with the type of cry, children without siblings had OC, and those with siblings exhibited FC. Still, the two parameters had no statistical association (p = 0.089) (Table 2).

Cry vs Treatment Procedure

When the cry is correlated to TP, the maximum number of children displayed the behavior during the administration of LA followed by EP, RVG, and RP (31, 26, 22, and 21%), respectively. When treatment procedures are taken individually, FC (12.8%) was witnessed more in EP, followed by CC (10.2%) and OC (2.5%). During LA, FC, OC, and PC were observed in an equal number of children (10.2, 10.2, 10.25%), that is, CC and MC were not observed. In RP, all types of cries were observed in equal percentages (5.1, 5.1, 5.1, 5.1, and 5.1%). While taking RVG, most of the children exhibited FC (10.2%), followed by CC (7.6%). The association between the type of cry and the treatment procedures was statistically significant (p < 0.001).

Cry vs BGT

When BGT was assessed in particular with the cry, 100% of children who revealed PC responded to distraction. Whereas children who uttered in the form of OC, responded better to active physical restraint (71.4%), followed by parental absence, that is, omission (14.3%), and HOME (14.3%), respectively. In CC, most of the children responded to tell, show, do (TSD) (81.8%), followed by distraction (18.2%). Furthermore, both TSD (50%) and distraction (50%) were effective in MC. Among the children with FC, TSD (69.2%) is more appropriate, followed by distraction (15.4%) and physical restraint; omission is equivalent (7.7%). When the relation between cry and BGT was evaluated statistically, a very high statistical significance was found (p < 0.001) (Table 3, Fig. 3). Further, there was a significant reduction of dental anxiety after the application of appropriate BGT when assessed physiologically, that is, PR by one-way ANOVA (p < 0.001) (Table 4).

Discussion

Crying, like other emotional manifestations of human behavior, is an expression of the personality of an individual. Associated factors include a child's fear disposition, negative emotions, pain sensitivity, and coping style.4 Cry in the early part of life is a reflex reaction, but as the child matures, the cry turns to be a means of communication.

In the present study, girls conveyed more of their emotions through crying than boys, which specify gender preference. This may be attributed because the girls were brought up by expressing their feelings, whereas boys suppressed them. The most common type of cry seen was FC, followed by CC, OC, PC, and MC. BC was observed in none of the children. Contrary to this finding, Chunawalla et al.'s study has PC most frequently seen, followed by FC, OC, CC, MC, and BC.4

Frightened cry throughout EP, LA, and RVG might be owing to the fear generated by sight of needle-like instruments, that is, endodontic files, needles, and equipment of RVG, and also may be because of the subjective and objective fears.4 Whereas, CC was due to prolonged treatment procedure time and can also be the fears that are transmitted from the parents and peers, that is, subjective fear.7 PC is due to the causation of pain in the course of the procedures, such as penetration of needles, and excavation of caries.8

A feeling of insecurity in the dental office causes OCs as the child considers it a punishment and is interconnected to personality traits and parenting styles. In RP, crying might be due to dentinal sensitivity9 in case of deep dentinal caries, as well as to overcome the noises caused by the air rotors and suction.

Various studies have evaluated the efficacy of different BGT in modifying the behavior of the child,10–12 but to our knowledge, this was the first study to evaluate the appropriate BGT for the particular type of cry based on the variations of dental anxiety assessed through PR preoperatively and postoperatively. In the current study, TSD and distraction were shown to be the most potent BGT following the systematic review and meta-analysis by Dhar et al.13

The paucity of literature in dentistry is present regarding the practical knowledge gap associated with cry that can be used as a strong basis for the selection and utilization of BGT as it is the major communication means by which they display their emotions. Drawbacks of the present study include a smaller sample size; parenting styles are not associated with the crying pattern, and also not all the BGT were included in the study.

Conclusion

Cry can be considered a potential communicating tool for the children, and based on the type of cry, we can also select appropriate BGT for a particular child in particular TP. Therefore, the conclusions drawn from the study are as follows:

The cry can be considered a basic communication technique in children; the most common type in an dental setting is an FC.
