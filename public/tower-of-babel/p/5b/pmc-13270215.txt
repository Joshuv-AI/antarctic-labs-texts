Molecular Docking and Experimental Evaluation of Trolox and Neocuproine in Targeting Neuroinflammation in Neuropathic Pain Model
Authors: Selma Yaman, Güven Akcay, Fatma Yesilyurt, Metin Yildirim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study was carried out to evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of Neocuproine/Trolox treatment on chronic neuropathy induced by Chronic Constriction Injury (CCI). Rats (n = 40) were grouped as Sham, CCI, CCI+Trolox, and CCI+Neocuproine. Rats with proven neuropathy were given 80 mg/kg/day (i.p.) Trolox and 100 μM/day (i.p.) Neocuproine for 7 days. Open field and tail withdrawal tests were conducted at baseline and on days 7 and 14. ELISA test was employed in analyzing IL 1β, IL 10, TNF‐α, and TGF‐β levels in spinal cord and cortex tissues of rats. To explore potential mechanistic interactions, molecular docking was conducted with the key cytokine receptor. On day 14, a significant decrease in total path taken, number of frames entered, and delay values were observed in CCI group compared to the control, whereas significant increases were observed in total path taken, number of frames entered, and delay values in the CCI+Neocuproine/Trolox treatment groups compared to CCI group (p < 0.05 for all). Docking analyses were performed for Neocuproine/Trolox. In comparison to the sham group, IL‑1β and TNF‐α levels in spinal cord and cortex tissues were higher, while IL‑10 and TGF‐β levels were lower (p < 0.05). Neocuproine/Trolox treatments reduced proinflammatory cytokine levels and elevated anti‐inflammatory cytokine levels in CCI rats on day 14 (p < 0.05 for all). Considering the docking score of Neocuproine/Trolox, high scores were observed, especially with TNF‐α and TGF‐β receptor. The results suggest that Neocuproine/Trolox treatments induce thermal anti‐hyperalgesic behaviors in CCI‐induced pain, showing significant improvements in locomotor activity, and achieve these by reducing the production of proinflammatory cytokines and elevating that of anti‐inflammatory cytokines.Keywords: chronic constriction injury model, hyperalgesia, locomotor activity, Neocuproine, neuroinflammation, Trolox

Introduction

Neuropathic pain (NP) is a pathological condition that has not been completely resolved by the treatment methods currently available in the clinic and is currently the subject of both clinical and preclinical studies [1, 2]. Somatosensory system pathologies, such as nerve injury and disease, contribute to the development of neuropathy and lead to both morphological and functional impairments [3]. Responses such as hyperalgesic and allodynic responses to various nociceptive stimuli, in which nociceptive sensitivity is altered, are observed in cases of chronic NP [4].

Neuropathic and inflammatory markers are present in the CCI‐induced sciatic nerve injury model, accompanied by pro‐inflammatory and anti‐inflammatory cytokines [5]. Trolox is a water‐soluble analogue of α‐tocopherol (Vitamin E), an excellent lipid‐soluble antioxidant that protects the membrane from oxidative radicals [6]. In experimental diabetic neuropathy, Trolox has been shown to significantly improve the activity of antioxidant enzymes, nerve conduction velocity, and nerve blood flow; it has also been shown to inhibit lipid peroxidation and thermal hyperalgesia, and it has been reported that Trolox has beneficial effects in experimental diabetic neuropathy [7]. Neocuproine is a specific copper (Cu) chelator and also has antioxidant properties and anti‐inflammatory effects [8, 9, 10]. Major contributors to the persistence and progression of NP are oxidative stress and inflammation [11]. This study was planned with the prediction that Neocuproine and Trolox, which are known to have anti‐inflammatory and antioxidant effects, may have positive effects on CCI‐induced neuropathy, a peripheral neuropathy model.

In this study, it was predicted that Neocuproine/Trolox application may improve neuropathophysiological processes and exhibit antinociceptive effects in CCI‐applied rats. To illustrate Neocuproine/Trolox's effectiveness as a treatment for neuropathic pain, locomotor activities were tested using open field tests, and thermal hyperalgesia using tail withdrawal tests in rats with sciatic damage caused by CCI. The levels of pro‐inflammatory and anti‐inflammatory markers such as TNF‐α, TGF‐β, IL‐1β, and IL‐10 were investigated in the cerebral cortex (CC) and spinal cord (SC) preparations. Furthermore, molecular docking analysis was performed to examine binding interactions of Neocuproine/Trolox with crucial proteins in the CCI‐induced sciatic nerve injury model, including IL‐1β receptor, TNF‐α receptor, TGF‐β receptor, and IL‐10 receptor, and to clarify its potential binding affinities and targeted proteins. To explore potential mechanistic interactions, molecular docking was conducted with the key cytokine receptor.

Material and Methods

Experimental Animals and Groups

The experiments were conducted under conditions supporting the experimental protocols of the International Association for the Study of Pain Research and Ethical Issues Committee (IASP), and Bolu Izzet Baysal University's Animal Research Committee (2024/21‐07.08.2024). Wistar Albino (200–250 g) male rats were used in this study. For the sensitivity of the experiment, the rats were kept in standard cages with a 12 h light/dark cycle (06:00‐18:00), appropriate air‐conditioning (22°C–24°C), and no restrictions defined for feeding and water. During the experiments, care was taken to use a sufficient number of rats and to ensure that the rats suffered minimal pain during all the experiments due to Chronic Constriction Injury (CCI) ligation, which is an invasive procedure. NP was performed with the CCI model. CCI applications and experimental groups were determined by considering previous studies and pilot applications (n = 10). In light of this preliminary information, rats were grouped as (i) Sham (sciatic nerve exposure without ligation), (ii) CCI, (iii) CCI + Trolox, and (iv) CCI + Neocuproine. The rats were let contact the systems for about a week before the start of the experimental schedule to let them adapt to both the researcher and the systems. In experimental groups, 80 mg/kg/day Trolox (i.p.) and 100 μM/day Neocuproine (i.p.) were administered on the 7th day for 7 days to the animals [12]. Only animals that demonstrated a significant decrease in withdrawal latency (indicating confirmed neuropathy) were included in the treatment groups. On the experiment's 14th day, open field tests were conducted to assess the locomotor activity of the rats, and tail withdrawal tests were used to assess thermal hyperalgesia. The experimental schedule sacrificed the rats, and the levels of IL‐10, IL‐1β, TGF‐β, and TNF‐α in the SC and cortex tissues were analyzed using ELISA.

CCI Model

The CCI model was employed to elicit chronic NP through unilateral loose ligation of the rat sciatic nerve [13]. All surgical procedures were conducted under sterile conditions. The sciatic nerve was exposed at the mid‐thigh level in the CCI surgical ligation groups after the fascia between the rats' gluteus and biceps femoris muscles was loosened under isoflurane anesthesia (5% for induction, 2.5% for maintenance). Four ligatures (chromic gut 4.0, Ethicon, USA) were loosely tied at 1 mm intervals and 1 mm above the trifurcation of the sciatic nerve. Ligation only constricted one‐third to one‐fourth of the nerve diameter and caused a small and brief twitch in the muscle around the exposed area. The wound was then closed using two staples for the skin and 5‐0 absorbable sutures (Ethicon, USA) for the muscles, and then a povidone‐iodine solution was administered. Then, the animals were randomly placed in cages in groups of three and given a week to recover. In the sham group, however, the sciatic nerve was exposed but left intact because no ligation was performed [13, 14]. In the experimental groups, the rats were returned to their cages after surgery, and anesthesia. The rats' CC and SC tissues were removed on the 14th day after the completion of pain tests.

Experimental Design

All animals were allowed to contact the systems for 1 week to adapt to the researchers and the tests. As of the first day of the experimental calendar, their basal values were measured, and these values were recorded as pre‐CCI values. After the sciatic nerve ligation procedure, the presence of NP was evaluated by performing latency measurements determined from thermal hyperalgesia, and tail‐pull test responses were assessed 7 days after CCI in the experimental groups. These values were considered as pre‐CCI, and post‐CCI data in the statistical analyses. In the groups that developed NP, Neocuproine (Sigma) and Trolox (Sigma) were administered for 7 days by following the treatment protocols. Thermal hyperalgesia (tail‐pull tests) and locomotor activities (open field test) were evaluated after the treatments (Figure 1). Behavioral tests were repeated on the 7th and 14th days of the experimental calendar. The experiment was terminated by sacrificing the rats on the 14th day, and IL‐10, IL‐1β, TGF‐β, and TNF‐α levels in the CC and SC tissues were measured by using ELISA method.

Behavioral Experiments

Open Field (OF) Test

This test aims to measure the rats' locomotor activity at the beginning of the experiment and on the 7th and 14th days of the experiment. Locomotor activity was carried out in a setup with a base of 80 × 80 cm. Rats were located in the center of the area and monitored and recorded by the video camera for 5 min. Total distance (cm), and the number of squares entered (frequency) were calculated to evaluate locomotor activity [15].

Tail Flick Test

Tail pulling is one of the methods that allows the assessment of thermal hyperalgesia by exposing the rat's tail to a heat source (radial, hot water heat source, etc.). In the experiment groups, it allows for a nociceptive measurement that provides information about the measurement of the response that develops after each rat is exposed to the heat source. Thermal hyperalgesia develops when a thermal stimulus that produces a lower pain response than the pain intensity felt under normal conditions develops a more painful response. With the given heat stimulus, the rat's spontaneous lifting of its tail is evidence of hyperalgesia, and the pulling time is determined as thermal latency (sec). In this study, the duration of the rats' tail pulling was determined by immersing 2 cm of their distal tail in a hot (52.5 ± 0.2°C) water bath. The duration of each rat's tail lifting (sec) was calculated during the experiment. To prevent the device from causing extra tissue damage, the closing time was fixed at 20 s [15, 16, 17, 18, 19].

Biochemical Evaluation

Tissue Homogenization Process

SC and CC tissues were homogenized using a mechanical method with liquid nitrogen in a ceramic mortar. The brain tissue transferred to the Eppendorf tube was weighed and then combined with 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4, containing 1 mM EDTA) to 10 times its volume. After 60 s of homogenization using a tissue homogenizer, the homogenates were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm and +4°C for 10 min.

Measurement of Cytokines (TGF‐β, TNF‐α, IL‐10, and IL‐1β)

Cytokine levels (TGF‐β, TNF‐α, IL‐10, and IL‐1β) were quantified using commercially available rat‐specific ELISA kits (e.g., TGF‐β ELISA Kit, Cat. No. E‐EL‐0162, Elabscience; TNF‐α ELISA Kit, Cat. No. RE1060RG, ReedBiotech; IL‐1β ELISA Kit, Cat. No. RE1074RG, ReedBiotech; IL‐10 ELISA Kit, Cat. No. E‐EL‐R0016, Elabscience). All procedures were performed strictly according to the manufacturer's instructions. Before ELISA measurements, the total protein concentration of each tissue homogenate was determined using the Bradford assay (Bio‐Rad, USA). All samples were then normalized to equal protein concentrations (1 mg/mL) with phosphate buffer (0.1 M, pH 7.4) before being subjected to cytokine ELISA. This normalization ensured that measured cytokine levels reflected differences in expression rather than variations in protein content across samples.

Specific monoclonal antibodies targeting rat TGF‐β, TNF‐α, IL‐10, and IL‐1β were added to 96‐well plates along with standard solutions, as well as samples that were prepared in serial dilutions of decreasing concentrations. This technique relies on the binding between antigens and antibodies, allowing IL‐10 and IL‐1β molecules in the samples to attach to the antibodies. The incubation was performed at 37°C for 90 min within an incubator. After this period, 100 µl of diluted biotinylated detection (BD) was introduced to each well, the concentration was reduced to 1/100 by using a BD dilution solution, and then incubated for 1 h (37°C). Unbound molecules were removed by repeatedly washing the wells with a wash buffer solution prepared with 750 mL of distilled water. Then, 100 µL of the streptavidin‐bound peroxidase (HRP) conjugate was added, and incubation was continued for 30 min at 37°C. After this incubation, the substrate solution was introduced after 5 consecutive washes. The color changes in the samples indicated the concentrations of TGF‐β, TNF‐α, IL‐10, and IL‐1β; the reaction was terminated by adding 50 µl of stop solution to each well. In the final step, absorbance readings for each well were evaluated with a 450 nm microplate reader.

Molecular Docking

Molecular docking calculations were performed to investigate the binding interactions between the synthesized compounds and selected target proteins and to support the experimental findings. Docking studies were carried out using the Maestro 13.8 interface of the Schrödinger 2023‐3 software suite with minor modifications to previously reported protocols [20, 21].

The three‐dimensional crystal structures of the target proteins were retrieved from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (PDB), while ligand structures were obtained from PubChem. Protein–ligand interaction energy calculations were performed for compounds associated with four cytokine‐related proteins involved in inflammatory signaling pathways, namely interleukin‐1β (IL‐1β, PDB ID: 8C3U), tumor necrosis factor‐α (TNF‐α, PDB ID: 2AZ5), transforming growth factor‐β receptor I (TGF‐βRI, PDB ID: 1PY5), and interleukin‐10 (IL‐10, PDB ID: 2H24).

Prior to docking, protein structures were prepared using the Protein Preparation Wizard implemented in the Schrödinger suite. During this process, co‐crystallized ligands were removed from the protein structures, bond orders were assigned, hydrogen atoms were added, missing side chains were corrected, and the hydrogen‐bonding network was optimized. In addition, water molecules located more than 5 Å from the binding site were removed. The prepared proteins were then subjected to energy minimization using the OPLS‐2005 force field at pH 7.0 to obtain energetically favorable geometries.

Ligand structures were prepared using the LigPrep module, where possible ionization states were generated at pH 7.0 ± 2.0, followed by geometry optimization using the OPLS‐2005 force field. Potential binding pockets of the proteins were identified using the SiteMap module, and receptor grids were generated around the active sites based on the coordinates of the original co‐crystallized ligand. For all docking calculations, the grid box dimensions were set to 20 × 20 × 20 Å to adequately cover the binding region.

Finally, molecular docking simulations were performed using the Glide extra precision (XP) module, allowing the evaluation of binding poses, interaction patterns, and docking scores of the ligands within the active sites of the selected proteins. The docking results were subsequently analyzed to identify key intermolecular interactions, including hydrogen bonding, π–π stacking, and hydrophobic contacts between the ligands and amino acid residues located in the binding pocket.

Statistical Analysis

One‐way ANOVA was utilized to analyze data that fit into a normal distribution in the Shapiro‐Wilk test and the post‐hoc Tukey test for pairwise comparisons between groups. The Kruskal‐Wallis test was employed to analyze the non‐normally distributed data and the Mann‐Whitney U test for group comparisons conducted pairwise. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. The data was shown as Mean ± SEM. The “SPSS 20.0” was used to analyze the data.

Trolox demonstrated slightly higher docking scores in comparison to Neocuproine, with values ranging between −3.710 kcal/mol and −6.642 kcal/mol (Table 1). Discussion

This study provides new evidence that Neocuproine/Trolox treatments significantly reduce the amount of pro‐inflammatory cytokines, elevate anti‐inflammatory cytokines in the CC and SC tissues, and alleviate pain behaviors in a CCI‐induced chronic neuropathy model in rats.

NP has a negative impact on patient standards, and it is known that currently available alternative treatment options do not fully eliminate these negative impacts. Major contributors to the persistence and progression of NP are oxidative stress and inflammation [11]. Substances with antioxidant and anti‐inflammatory properties modulate pain pathways by affecting mechanisms such as oxidative stress and inflammation, which play a role in the pathophysiology of neuropathic pain [11, 22]. Increased TNF‐α, a proinflammatory cytokine, has been associated with the development of NP, and anti‐TNF‐α treatments have been reported as an immunotherapeutic approach to alleviate neuropathic pain [23].

There are few studies on the effects of Neocuproine/Trolox on pain models. In a study demonstrating the efficacy of Trolox on neuropathic pain, a common side effect of chemotherapy, it was shown that MitoVitE, which can penetrate mitochondria, had better results than non‐mitochondrial target Trolox against paclitaxel‐induced mitochondrial damage and paclitaxel‐induced mechanical hypersensitivity in rat dorsal root ganglion (DRG) cells [24]. The difference in correlation between the antihyperalgesic and antiallodynic efficacy of Neocuproine/Trolox in the CCI‐induced neuropathy model and the results in the paclitaxel‐induced cancer model obtained in our study may be due to various factors. Paclitaxel‐induced and CCI‐induced neuropathy are different neuropathy models and have different pathophysiological processes. Furthermore, molecular docking data revealed that Neocuproine/Trolox exhibited high binding affinity to specific protein targets such as TNF‐α and TGF‐β receptor, while other study [24] findings showed that Trolox, a non‐target antioxidant for mitochondria, was more likely to be found in cystosol and therefore ineffective in mitochondrial damage. This can be considered strong evidence suggesting that Neocuproine/Trolox may function not only as general antioxidants but also as agents regulating specific signaling pathways.

The possible effects of Neocuproine/Trolox treatments on central nervous system‐focused inflammatory markers were combined with the CCI model of neuropathic pain for the first time under in‐vivo conditions. Trolox is an antioxidant analog of vitamin E, and its antioxidant property is preferred as a positive sham group in many studies. It was reported in a study that incubation of glial cells with Trolox inhibited neuronal cell death through IL‐1β expression [25]. In a study on isoprostane in the rat CC, the treatment with the antioxidant Trolox was reported to eliminate isoprostane release in basal strategies [26]. In addition, previous studies reported the anti‐inflammatory and antioxidant activity of Trolox [27, 28]. Trolox was shown to be effective via neuroinflammation and oxidative stress in the Alzheimer's disease mouse model by Tahir et al. [29]. In a study incorporating Neocuproine, it was predicted that it would be effective in protecting motor neuron cells during oxidative stress, which underlies neurodegenerative diseases in this system [30].

Pain is an unpleasant sensation that negatively affects a person's standards, and chronic NP is one of the most common clinical pain conditions [31]. This study contributes to the literature by revealing that the use of Neocuproine/Trolox has effective anti‐hyperalgesic effect and locomotor activity through proinflammatory and anti‐inflammatory pathways in a CCI‐induced chronic NP model induced. Hyperalgesia, and allodynia, another abnormal pain response, are considered a marker of sensory dysfunction in many experimental animal models accompanying inflammation [14].

The CCI‐induced chronic experimental model was preferred to induce chronic NP. The CCI model is a reliable and reproducible model that has been replicated many times, and it provides clinical findings. Besides, the team members who carried out this study are experienced in this model. In the CCI‐induced chronic NP group, tenderness developed in sciatic nerve‐damaged feet after ligation; the rats were more careful when using these feet and tended to lick them. These behaviors in the ligated sciatic nerve experimental groups align with the clinical findings of chronic neuropathy [13, 14].

This study corroborates to the literature also through the findings indicating that the use of Neocuproine/Trolox in the CCI‐induced chronic NP model has an anti‐hyperalgesic effect and locomotor activity via pro‐inflammatory and anti‐inflammatory pathways.

Hyperalgesia and allodynia, another abnormal pain response, are considered indicators of sensory dysfunction that accompanies inflammation, and are accepted as indicators of sensory dysfunction in many experimental animal models [14]. The results achieved in this study revealed that Neocuproine/Trolox reduced inflammation in the CC or SC and exhibited anti‐hyperalgesic effects in thermal measurements of rats treated with CCI. It was reported that chronic NP was at the highest level on the 7th day after CCI application, and continued for 14 days [13, 14]. Given the tail‐pull test results, the decrease in thermal latency values in the CCI group in comparison to the sham group was an indicator of thermal hyperalgesia, and this finding aligns with studies proving the presence of neuropathy after CCI sciatic nerve ligation [14].

The locomotor activity behaviors with a tendency to decrease, as shown in this study, also align with the neuropathy behaviors [29]. The present study revealed that Neocuproine and Trolox exerted beneficial effects on behavioral and cytokine endpoints [31].

Previous studies reported that inflammatory cytokines are important factors in the NP treatment protocol. Immune system elements play an important role in neuropathic processes. Activated macrophages produce proinflammatory cytokines that play a role in the control of inflammatory reactions, and there are many findings indicating that proinflammatory cytokines such as IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNF‐α play a role in neuropathological pain [32]. Pro‐inflammatory cytokines IL‐1, IL‐6, and TNF‐α and anti‐inflammatory cytokine IL‐10 are among the mediators secreted by these cells that control the inflammatory process inside the tissue.

Pro‐inflammatory activity is implicated in the development and continuation of aberrant pain responses in experimental chronic non‐painful situations, as shown by ample amount of data [33]. Moreover, it was noted that proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF α or IL‐1β might induce neuropathic pain symptoms when injected intraneurally [34, 35]. One possible therapeutic target for neuropathy disorders is TNF‐α [36]. In CCI‐induced chronic NP rats, immune cells are active in the damaged nerve region in triggering NP pain, and proinflammatory cytokines such as IL‐1β, and IL‐6 released by non‐neuronal inflammatory cells in the damaged nerve region show a significant increase, causing abnormalities in pain responses [37]. In this context, the results achieved in this study support the nexus between the presence of thermal hyperalgesia and elevated proinflammatory cytokine levels, while Neocuproine/Trolox applications reduced the proinflammatory cytokine level. Again, in the experiment groups with CCI, it increased the level of another inflammatory cytokine, the anti‐inflammatory cytokine, IL‐10. Given the evidence, it was reported that it suppresses the synthesis of the anti‐inflammatory cytokine IL‐10 and mainly the pro‐inflammatory cytokines IL‐1β and IL‐6 [12, 14, 38, 39, 40]. The study data revealed that Neocuproine/Trolox, considering their docking scores, yielded high docking scores, especially with TNF‐α (PDB ID: 2AZ5). These results suggest that Neocuproine/Trolox may represent a potential molecular target, considering their docking scores, and they align with the biochemical data. Another binding affinity that attracted attention in the docking analysis was the scores for TGF‐β receptor (PDB ID: 1PY5).

The limitations of the study are as follows: First, the dose intervals were not tested in single applications to reveal the most effective one for Neocuproine/Trolox administration in NP models. However, this is a preliminary study to assess whether Neocuproine/Trolox has a place in neuropathic pain studies. Another limitation is that their effectiveness on NA when used together has not been shown. Determining the effective doses for Neocuproine/Trolox separately and showing what their effective dose together achieves in the treatment process will be the next step.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the results in this study revealed that Neocuproine/Trolox treatment applied to rats in the chronic NP model induced by CCI may have an anti‐nociceptive effect by improving thermal hyperalgesia and may be effective in neuroinflammation through pro‐inflammatory and anti‐inflammatory parameters. Molecular docking results also support these results with these binding scores. These findings support the potential of Neocuproine and Trolox as candidate pharmacological agents for modulating neuroinflammation in NP. However, further validation is required before considering clinical translation.
