Amyloid PET quantification with deep learning segmentation models without MRI
Authors: Sheng-Chieh Chiu, Yun-Chi Lin, Jonathan McConathy, Sheng-Yu Lin, Yu-Hua Dean Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo develop and validate deep learning (DL) segmentation models for accurate, MRI-less amyloid PET quantification through the Centiloid scale.MethodsIn this retrospective study, two DL models (PET/CT and PET-only) were trained using 2774 18F-FBP PET/MRI scan pairs from the ADNI dataset, where synthetic CT were generated from T1w MRI and used as training data. External validation was performed on 424 PET/CT scans from the OASIS-3 dataset. The performance of the DL models was compared to the MRI-based FreeSurfer reference method and the template-based rPOP method. Statistical analyses included the calculation of mean absolute error (MAE), R-squared (R²), equivalence testing, and diagnostic accuracy for amyloid positivity (Centiloid > 20), with a focus on the 0–40 Centiloid ‘gray zone’.ResultsThe PET/CT model demonstrated the highest accuracy, with a MAE of 5.79 Centiloid and a strong correlation (R² = 0.96). The PET-only and rPOP methods showed higher errors with MAEs of 10.8 and 14.45 Centiloid, respectively. The PET/CT model’s Centiloid values were shown to be equivalent to the MRI-derived values within a ± 5 margin, while the PET-only and rPOP methods were not. In the Centiloid gray zone subgroup (n = 93), the PET/CT model yielded the highest diagnostic accuracy of 90.3% and agreement (Cohen’s kappa = 0.79).ConclusionDL models, particularly the PET/CT model trained with synthetic CT, provide accurate and robust Centiloid quantification for 18F-FBP amyloid PET, suggesting their potential as a tool for MRI-less amyloid PET quantification.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40658-026-00854-8.

Introduction

Amyloid PET imaging is widely used to evaluate brain amyloid-β plaque deposition, one of the hallmark biomarkers of Alzheimer’s disease (AD), with amyloid tracers including 11C-PiB and several FDA-approved 18F-labeled tracers. One advantage of amyloid PET imaging is the capability to quantify the amyloid burden and its spatial distribution with distribution volume ratio, standard uptake value, or standard uptake value ratio (SUVR). With different tracers and quantification methods, a standardized and quantitative scale of amyloid deposition is desirable to facilitate a harmonized comparison between different protocols, tracers, and institutions. The Centiloid Project was developed to standardize the amyloid PET quantification through the Centiloid scale [1], which is useful for quantifying amyloid PET scans prescribed for disease-modifying therapies with anti-amyloid β monoclonal antibodies [2, 3] in the evaluation of the patient eligibility, post-treatment efficacy and prognosis [4–6]. A typical cutoff of 20 effectively identifies patients with clinically relevant amyloid burden who may qualify for amyloid-targeting therapies [7, 8]. MRI is regarded as the ideal modality to guide the brain image segmentation for PET quantification [9]. An alternative method for amyloid PET segmentation is the template-based approach which is often applied when there is no available MRI data [10, 11]. Template-based methods typically warp an individual’s PET volume to a PET template volume with non-linear co-registration and transformation algorithms to overlay the pre-defined region of interest (ROI) in the template space onto the warped PET images. Spatial normalization techniques, however, may not fully account for subject-specific anatomical variations, such as the individual gyral/sulcal patterns or brain atrophy present in diverse patient populations [9]. As Centiloid is computed with the cortical gray matter, which is a thin structure of 2 to 3 mm thickness [12], segmentation error may lead to biased or imprecise Centiloid measurements.

We hypothesize that deep learning-based (DL-based) segmentation methods may serve as an useful and reliable segmentation tool for amyloid PET or PET/CT because of their capabilities to recognize complex spatial patterns under individual variations of brain anatomy and PET uptakes, potentially overcoming the limitations of template-based methods. There have been a few previous attempts to use DL-based methods for segmenting PET or PET/CT data [13, 14]. Despite prior efforts, no public DL models have been developed for amyloid PET segmentation to our knowledge. One challenge may be the limited availability of well‑annotated amyloid PET and/or PET/CT datasets with accurate and reliable gold standard labels for model training. Cohort size could also be a challenge as DL models typically require a sufficiently large training dataset. Moreover, in subjects with profound amyloid deposition, the gray-white-matter contrast in the amyloid PET scans is considerably degraded [15], making precise delineation of the cortical gray matter a challenging task for DL models if only the PET images were used. When there is degraded gray-white contrast in amyloid PET images, the capability of DL-based models to incorporate CT images for providing anatomical guidance may help such models to achieve a more precise delineation of the desired cortical structures.

In this study, we developed DL models for quantifying amyloid PET or PET/CT studies when MRI are unavailable. We harnessed the advantage of a large-scale dataset (n ~ 2800) from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI, http://adni.loni.usc.edu/) study by using the MRI-segmented ROIs as the gold standard. A key aspect of our methodology is the use of synthetic CT images from MRI for training a PET/CT DL model, as CT images were not available from ADNI. This approach allows the model to learn and leverage the aforementioned stable structural hints from CT during training, even in the absence of paired PET/CT data. A PET/CT model and a PET-only model were trained to perform brain segmentation for Centiloid computation. We then used the publicly available Open Access Series of Imaging Studies‑3 (OASIS‑3) data [16], where PET, CT and MRI are all available for a smaller cohort (n ~ 400), to evaluate the performance of each model in Centiloid quantification and the Centiloid-based classification of amyloid positivity. To evaluate whether DL models provide a better performance in Centiloid quantification than the templated-based approaches, the robust PET-only processing (rPOP) toolbox was used as a representative template-based method for comparison [17].

Materials and methods

This study was conducted using publicly available, de-identified data from the ADNI and the OASIS-3 datasets. The original data collection for both ADNI and OASIS-3 was approved by their respective institutional review boards, and all participants provided written informed consent.

Training dataset

A training dataset was retrospectively collected and organized from the ADNI study. For each subject, we retrospectively collected pairs of 18F-Florbetapir (18F-FBP) PET and T1-weighted (T1w) scans that were acquired within a year from 2002 to 2024. CT images were unavailable. A total of 2802 scan pairs of PET and MRI were eligible from a total of 1240 subjects. Synthetic CT scans were used as the CT training data for the PET/CT DL model. This was achieved by generating synthetic CT scans from T1w scans using the open-source ‘mr-to-pct’ toolbox (v1.1) [18], which. T1w scans were performed with either a magnetization prepared-rapid gradient echo (MP-RAGE) or a spoiled gradient-recalled echo (SPGR) sequence. The injection dose for 18F-FBP PET in ADNI studies is 370 MBq (10 mCi). Static 18F-FBP PET scans were acquired from 50 to 70 min post-injection and reconstructed with ordered subset expectation maximization (OSEM). 18F-FBP PET were pre-processed by the ADNI PET Core [19] and harmonized to a 160 × 160 × 96 matrix with a uniform spatial resolution of 6 mm full width at half maximum (FWHM) and 1.5 mm isotropic pixel size. We downloaded and conformed all T1w images to 256 × 256 × 256 matrices with 1 mm isotropic pixel size. The 18F-FBP images were co-registered to the conformed T1w images using the ‘mri_coreg’ function from FreeSurfer with six degrees of freedom under a rigid transformation. All PET and synthetic CT images were skull-stripped with SynthStrip [20].

To prepare for the ground truth during the supervised learning, volumetric parcellation was performed for each T1w scan using FreeSurfer 7.3.2 [21].We followed the Centiloid workflow recommended by the ADNI/UC Berkeley group and summarized on the Laboratory of Neuro Imaging Image and Data Archive (LONI, https://ida.loni.usc.edu/). The reference ROI was the whole cerebellum, and the target ROI was a cortical composite region that includes the frontal cortex, anterior/posterior cingulate, lateral parietal cortex, and lateral temporal cortex from the FreeSurfer segmentation as the ground truth label for training. Eight scans failed to complete the FreeSurfer parcellation, and 20 scans were excluded due to unsatisfactory segmentation quality based on visual quality control.

DL models

The DL models used in this study were developed with the nnU-Net framework [22], and their architectures are illustrated in Fig. 1. Two modality-specific networks were trained: one with PET and CT as input channels and another one with the PET-only input. For each model, 3D full-resolution configurations via the nnU-Net pipeline were chosen with a five-level encoder–decoder U-Net architecture comprising two pairs of 3 × 3 × 3 convolutions, Z-score normalization, and Leaky-ReLU design. Channel width doubles after every pooling step, starting from 32 and ending at 320. For the dual-input PET/CT model, patches of 128 × 128 × 128 voxels were sampled, whereas the PET-only model utilized patches of 160 × 112 × 128 voxels. Both models were trained with a batch size of two. We adopted a 90:10 split for training and validation during each epoch. Data were augmented using random rotations, scaling, Gaussian noise, and intensity shifts. All networks were trained for 250 epochs with an NVIDIA A100 graphics processing unit (GPU) with the Adam optimizer (initial learning rate of 3 × 10⁻⁴ with polynomial decay). An unweighted sum of Dice loss and cross-entropy loss served as the objective function. The best checkpoint performance on the validation subset was selected for inference. All model training, inference, and data preprocessing steps involving neural networks were conducted using Python 3.10 with required libraries specified in ‘nnunetv2’ library version 2.1.1.

External validation

The external validation dataset was collected from the OASIS-3 project, a research initiative led by the Alzheimer’s Disease Research Center at Washington University, St. Louis. 18F-FBP PET, CT, and T1w MRI scans were retrospectively included if a subject has all three image volumes, and both the MRI and CT scans were acquired within one year of their corresponding PET scan. We identified 425 eligible scan sets where 18F-FBP PET, T1w MRI, and CT were all available from 371 subjects that included both cognitively normal and impaired subjects. ¹⁸F-FBP PET images were reconstructed with filtered back projection for the Biograph 40 PET/CT and OSEM for the BioGraph mMR PET/MR, as detailed in the OASIS-3 dataset. Static 18F-FBP PET scans were generated by summing the dynamic PET frames acquired from 50 to 70 min post-injection. Each T1w scan was conformed to an isotropic resolution of 1 mm with image size 256 × 256 × 256 and parcellated using FreeSurfer 7.3.2. The 18F-FBP images and the CT images were co-registered to the conformed T1w images using the ‘mri_coreg’ function with six degrees of freedom under a rigid transformation. One scan set failed during the co-registration step, leaving a final validation set of 424 scan sets. Skull stripping was performed using SynthStrip for both the PET and CT images.

PET template-based approach

To compare our proposed DL models to the template-based approaches, we chose the open-source rPOP toolbox [17] as the representative template-based MRI-less segmentation approach. The averaged tracer-independent template image volume included in the rPOP toolbox was used to perform spatial normalization with SPM12 ‘Old Normalization toolbox’ using non-linear transformation.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis of this study assumed that the MRI-based FreeSurfer segmentation is the optimal segmentation that is available for Centiloid calculation. Accordingly, CentiloidFS is regarded the gold standard of Centiloid measurement. Centiloid accuracy was evaluated through mean absolute error (MAE) as well as a correlation analysis between the estimated and reference Centiloid values. Equivalence tests were used to assess the agreement between Centiloid measurements obtained from MRI-less methods and those derived from the FreeSurfer-based method using the two one-sided tests (TOST) [23] with the equivalence margin at ± 5 Centiloid. This specific margin was chosen by considering the variability in the Centiloid cutoffs for determining the amyloid positivity reported in literature. While our current study used a commonly used cutoff of 20 Centiloid for amyloid positivity, other studies have reported cutoff values of 24 [24, 25] or 26 [8], suggesting that there is roughly a variation of 4 to 6 Centiloid across different methodologies and study cohorts. We therefore chose ± 5 Centiloid as an allowable measurement margin for the equivalence tests in this study.

Diagnostic performance for amyloid positivity was evaluated for the DL-based and template-based methods using CentiloidFS above 20 as the gold standard of amyloid positivity. Sensitivity, specificity, and overall accuracy were used as the performance metrics. We first calculated these metrics for the complete cohort, and the same metrics were calculated again for a ‘gray zone’ subgroup. We defined the gray zone as Centiloid between 0 and 40 [26]. The concordance between the MRI-based and the MRI-less methods was evaluated with the Cohen’s kappa coefficient [27]. Statistical analysis of the model outputs, including the calculation of SUVRs and their conversion to Centiloid values, was performed using Statistics and Machine Learning Toolbox in MATLAB R2024a.

Results

The demographic characteristics of the training and validation cohorts were summarized in Table 1. The Centiloid MAE computed with MRI-less methods was summarized in Table 2. Scatter plots and Bland-Altman plots were shown in Figs. 2 and 3, respectively. Representative qualitative segmentation results demonstrating the model’s performance across the amyloid spectrum were provided in the Supplementary Information. Within all subjects, the PET/CT achieved the lowest MAE (SD) of 5.79 (5.42) Centiloid, followed by the PET-only model of 10.80 (6.44) and the template-based rPOP approach of 14.45 (12.2) Centiloid. In the analyses stratified by amyloid positivity, the PET/CT model again yielded the lowest MAE among both the amyloid-negative and amyloid-positive subjects, outperforming the PET-only and rPOP methods. Beyond its advantage in quantification accuracy and consistency, the developed DL models were also characterized by high computational efficiency, requiring less than 3 s to infer a PET/CT volume with GPUs. Results from equivalence tests were summarized in Fig. 4. The PET/CT model showed a 95% confidence interval (CI) of 2.3 to 3.7 Centiloid units, which fell entirely within the ± 5 Centiloid equivalence margin, indicating that the measurement is equivalent to the FreeSurfer-based method. In contrast, the PET-only model showed a CI between 10 and 11.3 and the rPOP method had the CI between 9.7 and 12.7, both failing to pass the equivalence tests.

Discussion

In this study, we developed and validated DL models for MRI-less Centiloid quantification of amyloid PET scans. Leveraging the ADNI data, synthetic CT images, and the nnUnet pipeline, we were able to train two DL models with PET/CT and PET-only inputs for image segmentation and Centiloid calculation. Our PET/CT model demonstrated an accurate Centiloid quantification, achieving a low MAE and a high correlation to the MRI-based Centiloid. PET/CT CentiloidDL was found to be equivalent to CentiloidFS under the ± 5 Centiloid equivalence margin. When cutoff values were applied to determine the amyloid positivity, the PET/CT model also provided the best diagnostic performances, particularly within the gray zone subgroup. These findings suggest that the DL approach may reliably quantify amyloid burden for quantitative amyloid PET analysis when MRI is unavailable.

We evaluated the three MRI-less methods to estimate the Centiloid from the PET/CT or PET images through two different levels. First, we compared the Centiloid values from MRI-less methods to the gold standard CentiloidFS values. Second, we evaluated the diagnostic performances of the Centiloid values using predefined cutoffs to determine the amyloid positivity. For the direct comparison of Centiloid values, our developed DL models had a higher accuracy and precision compared to the rPOP method. Specifically, our PET/CT model achieved the lowest error and variability of Centiloid estimation with MAE (SD) of 5.79 (5.42) Centiloid, followed by the PET-only model with 10.8 (6.44) Centiloid and the rPOP method with 14.45 (12.2) Centiloid. The equivalence tests also showed that the Centiloid values from the PET/CT model and FreeSurfer were equivalent, whereas the PET-only and rPOP methods did not pass the equivalence tests. At the second level of analysis, we found that the PET/CT model had the leading performance among the three MRI-less methods in the whole validation cohort (Sens: 100%, Spec: 97.3%, Acc: 97.9%) and the gray zone subgroup (Sens: 100%, Spec: 85.7%, Acc: 90.3%). Those results suggest that the PET/CT DL model owns the potential to serve as a reliable tool for amyloid PET segmentation. We speculate that the robust performances of the developed DL-based models came from their capability to interpret and analyze individual anatomical structure and tracer distribution when a large and high-quality dataset was used for model training [28]. In contrast, it may be more challenging for template-based approaches to fully adapt to subject-specific anatomy for a precise cortical segmentation [29, 30]. The performance and adaptability demonstrated by the DL models suggest their potential utility for quantification with other PET tracers.

A key challenge in amyloid PET segmentation arises in amyloid-positive subjects, where reduced gray-white-matter contrast and non-specific white matter tracer binding complicate accurate cortical delineation [15, 31]. Without precise anatomical guidance, segmentation contours from PET-only methods may include white matter voxels, leading to over-estimated Centiloid that is often seen in such pipelines [9, 11, 32, 33]. Our PET/CT model better remedied this challenge. The structural information provided by the CT component, even when using the synthesized CT images for training, potentially enhances segmentation precision by providing anatomical guidance and information, thereby compensating for the limited resolution and disease-related loss of contrast in PET images [34, 35]. This improvement is qualitatively demonstrated in Supplementary Figs. 1–3, where the PET/CT model predicted cortical structures more accurately than the PET-only approach. This is particularly evident in high-uptake subjects where contrast is degraded, as well as in gray zone cases where precise cortical delineation is important for accurate amyloid classification. Our quantitative analysis further highlights the model’s superiority in PET quantification. Specifically, the PET/CT model preserved a proportional relationship with a slope of approximately one, whereas MRI-less methods lacking CT guidance exhibited slopes greater than 1.06. Furthermore, the PET/CT model demonstrated a smaller quantification bias, indicated by a lower intercept (8.65 Centiloid) compared to the PET-only (13.12 Centiloid) and rPOP (18.73 Centiloid) methods. These findings suggest that including structural information is beneficial for reducing quantification bias, particularly in these challenging cases with reduced cortical contrast.

Our MRI-less DL models for Centiloid quantification have several limitations. First, the external validation of our models was conducted on a dataset collected from a single institution and therefore may not fully capture the heterogeneity across different imaging sites, protocols or scanners. Future validation with multi-institutional datasets shall help to evaluate the developed models more comprehensively. Secondly, the PET/CT model was developed using synthetic CT due to the lack of large-scale, paired CT-MRI datasets. While our findings demonstrated the utility of synthetic CT, the inclusion of native CT data in future training may further improve segmentation accuracy. Lastly, the developed models were trained with 18F-FBP PET images. The generalizability of these models to other amyloid tracers—such as 11C-PiB, 18F- Flutemetamol, or 18F-Florbetaben—remains to be investigated. These tracer-specific patterns could potentially impact segmentation accuracy in PET-only models [36, 37]. However, we theorize that our PET/CT model might be more resilient to such variations since the CT component provides anatomical structural hints that are independent of tracer-specific contrast. The model trained with 18F-FBP images may also serve as a good foundational model for model re-training of other tracers to facilitate a faster and more efficient model expansion. Future research involving tracer-specific calibration or fine-tuning of the DL models will be needed to achieve optimal quantification across diverse radiotracers.

To sum up, we have developed DL models for amyloid PET image segmentation and quantification without MRI. Our data suggest that both models, especially the PET/CT model, provided robust image segmentation and reliable estimates of Centiloid values. These findings suggest the potential of DL-based PET/CT segmentation and quantification as a promising tool for PET quantification.
