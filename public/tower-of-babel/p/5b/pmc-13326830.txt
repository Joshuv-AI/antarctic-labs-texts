Surgical Outcomes of Sequential Robot‐Assisted Hepatobiliary–Pancreatic Surgery in a Single Operating Room: A Single‐Center Retrospective Analysis of a High‐Volume Center in Japan (TAKUMI‐6)
Authors: Tomokazu Fuji, Kosei Takagi, Kazuya Yasui, Takeyoshi Nishiyama, Yasuo Nagai, Naohiro Okada, Shohei Yokoyama, Toshiyoshi Fujiwara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimGiven the growing adoption of robot‐assisted hepatobiliary–pancreatic (HBP) surgery and the limited availability of robotic systems, high‐volume centers must optimize operating room time management. However, data on multiple robot‐assisted HBP surgeries performed on a single workday are limited. This study investigated the outcomes of sequential robot‐assisted HBP performed in a single operating room.MethodsThis single‐center retrospective study included 122 robot‐assisted HBP surgeries performed sequentially in a single operating room between September 2020 and September 2025. The outcomes and operating room timelines of the sequential procedures were analyzed, including the incidence of “day‐shift completion (defined as patient wheel‐out by 5:30 PM)”.ResultsThe procedures performed included robot‐assisted distal pancreatectomy (n = 33), liver resection (n = 85), and congenital biliary dilatation (n = 4). The overall operative time was 171 min (interquartile range [IQR], 129–203 min), and the median blood loss was 8 mL (IQR, 0–100 mL). The outcomes and operating room timelines were comparable between the first and second cases. The median turnover time was 49 min (IQR, 43–56 min). The success rate of day shift completion was 34.4%.ConclusionsThis study demonstrated the safety and feasibility of sequential robot‐assisted HBP surgery in a single operating room. Appropriate case selection may help successfully implement sequential robot‐assisted HBP surgeries.

Introduction

The adoption of robot‐assisted surgery has increased worldwide, driven by the demand for minimally invasive procedures, better patient outcomes, and technological advances. Within this context, robot‐assisted hepatobiliary–pancreatic (HBP) surgery is growing supported by increasing evidence [1, 2, 3]. However, the adoption of robot‐assisted HBP surgery has been slower compared with other surgical fields [4]. In Japan, national insurance coverage for robot‐assisted pancreatectomy, including robot‐assisted pancreatoduodenectomy (RPD) and robot‐assisted distal pancreatectomy (RDP), was introduced in 2020, followed by robot‐assisted liver resection (RLR) and robot‐assisted surgery for congenital biliary dilatation (RCBD) in 2022. Robot‐assisted HBP surgery has gradually become widespread, especially in high‐volume centers in Japan. Given the increasing trend in robot‐assisted HBP surgery and the limited availability of robotic systems, high‐volume centers should focus on operating room time management to perform procedures efficiently.

The feasibility of performing multiple robot‐assisted surgeries on a single workday has been reported [5, 6]. However, few studies have reported the safety and feasibility of multiple robot‐assisted HBP surgeries performed within a single workday. In 2022, our department initiated sequential robot‐assisted HBP surgeries in a single operating room to expand the program.

This study aimed to investigate the surgical outcomes of sequential robot‐assisted HBP surgeries performed in a single operating room to confirm their safety and feasibility, as part of a Training program in Okayama University for minimally invasive surgery (TAKUMI‐6).

Methods

Study Design

This single‐center retrospective cohort study included 406 robot‐assisted HBP surgeries, including RPD (n = 144), RDP (n = 93), RLR (n = 160), and RCBD (n = 9), performed at our institution between September 2020 and September 2025. In accordance with national insurance coverage, our department introduced RPD, RDP, RLR, and RCBD in 2020, 2021, 2022, and 2022, respectively. Among robot‐assisted HBP surgeries, sequential surgeries in a single dedicated operating room were included. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Okayama University Hospital (no. 2301‐023) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The requirement for written informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of this study.

Slot Allocation System for Robot‐Assisted Surgery

Our institution introduced a fixed‐slot allocation system for robot‐assisted surgery in each department. Our HBP department initially had one slot per week between 2020 and 2022, and two slots per week from 2023 onward.

Standardization of the Procedure and Dedicated Surgical Team

All procedures were performed using the da Vinci Si or Xi system (Intuitive Surgical, Sunnyvale, CA, USA). Our surgical protocol for robot‐assisted HBP surgery using a two‐surgeon technique has been previously reported [7, 8, 9, 10]. During the study period, all procedures were performed by experienced board‐certified HBP surgeons [11]. Moreover, a dedicated multidisciplinary team including HBP surgeons, anesthesiologists, scrub nurses, and medical engineers was established [7].

Data Collection

Clinical data were extracted from a prospectively collected database and included: age, sex, body mass index, American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status, primary disease, surgeon level (proctor [KT] or nonproctor [others]), anesthesia factor (epidural anesthesia, central venous catheter, transversus abdominis plane block), operative time, console time, estimated blood loss, conversion to open surgery, mortality, postoperative major complications (Clavien–Dindo grade ≥ 3) [12], postoperative pancreatic fistula (≥ Grade B) [13], postoperative bile leakage (≥ Grade B) [14], postoperative length of hospital stay, readmission within 1 month after surgery, and textbook outcome. Textbook outcomes were defined as the absence of the following adverse events: conversion to open surgery, mortality, major complications, pancreatic fistula, bile leakage, and readmission within 1 month after surgery [15, 16].

The surgical difficulty levels for RDP were classified as low, intermediate, or high, according to the modified Japanese difficulty scoring system [17]. The IWATE criteria were used to evaluate the surgical difficulty of RLR, including low, intermediate, advanced, and expert difficulties [18].

Definition of Operating Room Timeline

The operating room timeline was classified as follows: in‐room to anesthesia‐release time (IRAT), defined as the time from patient entry into the operating room to the completion of general anesthesia induction, including the duration for epidural anesthesia and central venous catheter insertion; anesthesia release to cut time (ARCT), measured from the completion of general anesthesia induction to the surgical time out, patient positioning, skin preparation, and equipment setup; cut‐to‐close time (CTCT), defined as the duration from surgical incision to surgical completion; close to wheels‐out time (CTWO), measured from surgical completion to anesthesia emergence and patient exit from the operating room; the total operating room time (TORT), spanned from patient entry into the operating room to patient wheel‐out from the operating room; and turnover time, defined as the time elapsed from the wheel‐out of the first case to the entry of the second case into the operating room. An overview of the operating room timeline for sequential surgeries in a single operating room is shown in Figure 1.

Operating room timeline of sequential surgeries in a single operating room. ARCT, anesthesia release to cut time; CTCT, cut to close time; CTWO, close to wheel‐out time; IRAT, in‐room to anesthesia‐release time.

At our institution, the patients were scheduled to enter the operating room at 8:30 ante meridiem (AM). As the day shift was regarded as between 8:30 AM and 5:30 post meridiem (PM), the success of the “day‐shift completion” was defined as the second procedure exiting the operating room by 5:30 PM in this study.

Statistical Analysis

Outcomes and operating room timelines were compared between the first and second cases in a single operating room. Procedure‐specific outcomes of sequential and nonsequential robot‐assisted HBP surgeries were also examined. Factors influencing day shift completion were investigated, focusing on surgical difficulty. Procedure‐specific TORT was calculated based on surgical difficulty. Thereafter, a risk score was assigned to each difficulty classification based on TORT, and the total risk scores of the first and second cases were calculated according to the difficulty estimates. Clinical variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous data and the chi‐square or Fisher's exact test for categorical data. Continuous variables are reported as median and interquartile range (IQR). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using JMP, version 14 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Results

The annual number of robot‐assisted HBP surgeries performed at our institution is shown in Figure 2A, showing a gradual annual increase. Among 406 robot‐assisted HBP surgeries performed between September 2020 and September 2025, 122 procedures performed sequentially in a single operating room were included. The percentages of sequential procedures performed in a single operating room were 0%, 17.2%, 15.4%, 42.6%, and 43.4% in 2021, 2022, 2023, 2024, and 2025, respectively (Figure 2B).

Patient Demographics

The baseline characteristics and perioperative outcomes of the 122 patients are summarized in Table 1. A total of 72 men and 50 women with a median age of 70 years (IQR, 57–76 years) were included in this study. The procedures included 33 RDP, 85 RLR, and 4 RCBD. Of the 122 procedures, 52 (42.6%) were performed by proctors and 70 (57.4%) by nonproctors. Regarding surgeon allocation on a single day, 29% of the procedures were performed by the same console surgeon, whereas 71% were performed by different console surgeons. The overall operative time was 171 min (IQR: 129–203 min), and the median blood loss was 8 mL (IQR: 0–100 mL). The incidence of mortality and major complications was 0% and 9.1%, respectively. The textbook outcome achievement rate was 88.5% (108 cases of 122).

The first and second groups had comparable operating room timelines, including IRAT, ARCT, CTCT, CTWO, and TORT (264 vs. 260 min, p = 0.61). The median turnover time was 49 min (IQR, 43–56 min). Turnover time significantly reduced over time compared to the early (2022–2024, n = 38) and late (2025, n = 24) periods (early vs. late, 50 [IQR, 44–58] vs. 48 [IQR, 41–51] min, p = 0.04; Figure 3). The day‐shift completion success rate was 34.4%.

Factors Influencing the Day‐Shift Completion

The procedure‐specific TORT stratified by surgical difficulty is shown in Figure 4A. The median TORT differed significantly between surgical difficulties: low vs. intermediate vs. high for RDP (243 vs. 273 vs. 321 min, p = 0.009) and low vs. intermediate vs. advanced vs. expert for RLR (202 vs. 238 vs. 288 vs. 330 min, p < 0.001). Considering an overall median TORT of 260 min (IQR: 219–293), we assigned a risk score to each difficulty classification as follows: low (1 point), intermediate (1), and high (2) for RDP; low (0), intermediate (0), advanced (1), expert (2) for RLR; and RCBD (1).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the surgical outcomes of sequential robot‐assisted HBP surgeries in a single dedicated operating room at a high‐volume center in Japan. We observed acceptable overall outcomes for sequential procedures and analyzed the detailed operating room timelines. Previous meta‐analyses have demonstrated that the short‐term outcomes of robot‐assisted HBP surgeries are comparable to those of conventional open and laparoscopic procedures [19, 20, 21]; however, the prolonged operative time of robot‐assisted surgeries remains a clinical concern. Therefore, a prolonged operative time with sequential procedures is not acceptable, and our novel concept of “day‐shift completion” offers a framework to evaluate efficacy.

At our institution, the number of robot‐assisted HBP surgeries has increased annually owing to expanded indications and procedures (Figure 2A). In contrast, the availability of robotic systems at our institution is limited to one slot per week between 2020 and 2022, and two slots per week between 2023 and 2025. Therefore, we face the challenge of increasing the number of procedures within a limited framework. To address this issue, we initiated a sequential robot‐assisted program in a single operating room in 2022. As the number of procedures increased, the percentage of sequential procedures also increased (Figure 2B).

Several key factors contributed to the successful implementation of the sequential robot‐assisted program. First, shortening the operative time, namely, CTCT, while ensuring patient safety is crucial. The selection of the procedure is important because RPD and complex RLR may be unsuitable for sequential surgeries. Standardization of surgical protocols is crucial. Moreover, our surgical protocol using a two‐surgeon technique helps reduce the operative time. At our institution, the operative times for RDP and RLR were significantly shorter than those for laparoscopic procedures [7, 8, 9, 10]. The two‐surgeon technique can also provide educational benefits for expanding the number of console surgeons. Approximately 70% of the sequential procedures were performed by different console surgeons. Second, a flexible scheduling system was required. We introduced a team‐based scheduling approach for improving patient access [22]. Using this approach, the timing and combination of surgeries were determined by considering robotic system availability and procedure complexity. Third, reducing the nonoperative time was a priority. Our results showed that approximately one‐third of robot‐assisted TORT procedures were nonoperative. Regarding the anesthetic protocol, epidural anesthesia and central venous catheter placement were waived because of lower blood loss and lower conversion rates in robot‐assisted surgeries, thereby reducing the IRAT. Rapid setup and preparation of a robotic system by a dedicated surgical team can minimize ARCT. Finally, turnover time is an important determinant of operating room utilization in sequential procedures. Our median turnover time of 49 min was shorter than that previously reported [23, 24]. Moreover, the institutional learning curve may have contributed to the shortening of turnover over time (Figure 3).

Further attention should be paid to the wheel‐out time from the operating room in the second case. Recent work style reforms in Japan have limited overtime for physicians in Japan [25]. Therefore, we defined “day‐shift completion defined as patient wheel‐out by 5:30 PM” to evaluate the quality of sequential procedures in this study. As shown in Figure 4A, surgical difficulty significantly influenced TORT. Therefore, appropriate case selection considering the surgical difficulty is necessary to achieve successful day shift completion. However, there is no global standard definition for overtime. Some studies have defined overtime as work beyond a specific clock time (ranging from 3:45 PM to 5:30 PM), while others have used continuous work duration thresholds (> 8 h) or the percentage of allocated block time [26, 27, 28, 29]. These variations in definitions across studies create challenges for direct comparison. Because overtime is a critical issue faced by many institutions worldwide, regardless of the healthcare system, further investigation is required.

This study has certain limitations. This was a single‐center, retrospective study with a relatively small sample size, and selection bias may have influenced patient selection. As we introduced sequential procedures only for robot‐assisted HBP surgery, data on the turnover time of open and laparoscopic HBP surgeries are lacking, limiting comparisons. Finally, this study focused on short‐term outcomes of sequential procedures. Therefore, future studies should investigate the effect of sequential procedures on long‐term outcomes.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated the safety and feasibility of sequential robot‐assisted HBP surgery in a single operating room. Appropriate case selection, considering procedure‐specific surgical difficulty, may help to successfully implement sequential robot‐assisted HBP surgery.
