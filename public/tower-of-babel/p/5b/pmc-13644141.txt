The role of an educational robot in advance decision learning among community-dwelling older adults
Authors: Chiu-Mieh Huang, Jia-Yi Yang, Su-Fei Huang, Hsiao-Pei Hsu, Yi-Chun Shieh, Li-Ting Lu, Jong-Long Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdvance decisions are a key component of advance care planning (ACP) and support individuals in communicating their preferences for future medical care. Despite policy efforts to promote ACP, public awareness and engagement in completing advance decisions remain limited in many settings. Educational robots, which provide interactive and engaging learning experiences, may represent a novel strategy for delivering ACP education in community settings.AimThis study aimed to examine older adults’ acceptance of an educational robot designed to facilitate learning about advance decisions. Specifically, we investigated factors associated with their intention to continue using the robot to learn about advance decisions and their intention to recommend the robot to others for advance decisions education.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among older adults recruited from community centers in northern Taiwan. After providing informed consent, participants attended a 90-minute robot-assisted educational session introducing key concepts of advance decisions and ACP. Following the session, participants completed a self-administered questionnaire assessing human–computer trust, decisional balance (perceived pros and cons of advance decisions), personal involvement, satisfaction with the robot-assisted learning experience, intention to continue using the educational robot, and intention to recommend the robot to others. Partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM) was used to test the proposed relationships among study variables.ResultsHuman–computer trust significantly increased older adults’ intention to continue using the educational robot, both directly and indirectly through satisfaction with the learning experience. Trust also influenced participants’ intention to recommend the robot to others indirectly via satisfaction. Perceived benefits of advance decisions were positively associated with recommendation intention, whereas perceived barriers showed no significant effects. Neither the perceived benefits nor barriers of advance decisions significantly influenced the intention to continue using the robot. Overall, satisfaction emerged as a key mediator linking trust to behavioral intentions related to robot-assisted ACP education.ConclusionsTrust in the technology and satisfaction with the learning experience play critical roles in shaping continued engagement and willingness to recommend such interventions. Integrating interactive technologies into ACP education may help expand public awareness and promote broader engagement in advance care planning.Keywords: Advance directives, Advance care planning, Older adults, Educational robots, Decisional balance, Technology acceptance

Introduction

Human life expectancy has significantly increased; however, the focus has shifted from mere longevity to “healthy longevity,” which refers to living longer while maintaining good health and functional independence. Taiwan is projected to become a super-aged society by 2025, when adults aged 65 and above will constitute 20% of the population [1]. Although average life expectancy is 79.84 years, Taiwanese adults spend an estimated 7.41 years in poor health [2, 3]. The burden of medical care resulting from severe disability not only creates immense physical and emotional strain on individuals but also imposes significant economic hardship on families and society. Given its potentially tragic consequences, an effective mechanism is required to alleviate this burden.

Advance care planning (ACP) and advance decisions (similar to advance directives) facilitate the expression of personal autonomy and reduce communication conflicts. ACP helps individuals understand the importance of advance decisions and the process of their completion. In Taiwan, an advance decision is a legally binding document that serves as strong evidence of a person’s medical preferences expressed in advance. Despite the crucial role of advance decisions in ensuring that the patients’ medical treatment preferences are respected, their implementation remains challenging. A scoping review of 13 articles revealed that although individuals recognize the importance of advance decisions and hold positive attitudes toward their implementation, the actual completion rate remains low [4]. Studies have shown that fewer than 50% of severely or terminally ill patients possess an advance decision [5], and only 1% of Taiwanese adults had completed an advance decision in 2024, five years after the enactment of the 2019 Patient Right to Autonomy Act [6]. Even when patients complete advance decisions, their chosen surrogates may still be unable to accurately predict their end-of-life wishes. Research shows poor agreement between patients and caregivers when caregivers act as surrogates in decision-making, especially if there has been no prior discussion or clarification about palliative care [7]. This highlights the difficulty in promoting the widespread adoption of ACP and completion of advance decisions, despite their critical importance in an aging society.

One major barrier to ACP implementation is the discomfort associated with discussing end-of-life issues, such as death and dying. In many Chinese cultural contexts, these topics are often considered taboo and are associated with bad luck or misfortune. A qualitative study involving nursing home residents in Taiwan identified several key barriers to signing advance decisions, including concerns about “bad omens,” traditional cultural beliefs, and uncertainty regarding the future [8]. These cultural and psychological barriers further complicate efforts to promote ACP among older adults.

Given these challenges, innovative and engaging strategies are needed to increase public awareness of advance decisions and enhance individuals’ willingness to engage in ACP. Educational robots, owing to their interactive and engaging nature, may offer digital solutions for addressing this challenge. Compared with desktop computers, tablets, and other devices, educational robots provide enhanced interactivity and engagement. Research has shown that learning with the assistance of robots can significantly improve health knowledge and literacy, as well as learning motivation. Robots have been employed for medical interviews [9], symptom monitoring and recording [10], medication reminders [11], guiding treatment tasks [12], and companionship [13]. Educational robots can offer an innovative approach for engaging older adults, particularly in community settings, by providing interactive learning experiences. It is essential to assess learners’ acceptance of this new form of learning to refine and improve the performance of educational robots. To understand how well the educational robot is received and its potential for broader adoption, acceptance could be represented by learners’ intention to continue using the robot, as well as their likelihood to recommend the robot to others for advance decisions learning. These behavioral intentions provide insight into the sustainability of robot-assisted educational interventions.

Satisfaction is a well-established determinant of the continued use and positive recommendations regarding innovative educational methods [14, 15]. As an innovative tool for learning advance decisions, the satisfaction with the experience of using the educational robot is likely a key factor influencing both the intention to continue using the robot and the likelihood of recommending it to others. In addition, learner involvement in the learning content or activities is an important factor for effective learning. When learners are actively engaged, they are more likely to comprehend the materials and effectively retain information, particularly when dealing with complex topics such as advance decisions. Higher levels of learner involvement improve both continued use and the likelihood of recommending the tool [16]. Learner involvement can be classified into two distinct types: cognitive and affective. Cognitive involvement pertains to an individual’s understanding and recognition of content or activities, as well as their functional appraisal. In contrast, affective involvement refers to emotional reactions, moods, and feelings elicited by content or activities, thereby enriching the overall learning experience.

Older adult individuals may have concerns about using technology or feel uncertain about the content and process of completing an advance decision. Therefore, this study incorporates human–computer trust and decisional balance as potential influencing factors. These factors may directly affect older adults’ intentions to continue using and to recommend the robot to others, as well as their level of involvement in or satisfaction with the learning experience. Moreover, their degree of involvement and satisfaction may subsequently influence their continued use of the robot and the likelihood of recommending it to others.

According to the revised Human–Computer Trust Model (HCTM) by Gulati et al. [17], human–computer trust comprises four dimensions—perceived risk, benevolence, competence, and reciprocity— and reflects users’ attitudes and their inclination to trust robots or intelligent machines. Moreover, trust between humans and machines can significantly influence people’s usage willingness and behaviors [18]. In addition, decisional balance, defined as the evaluation of the perceived advantages and disadvantages of adopting a behavior, is closely associated with behavioral intentions [19, 20]. Empirical evidence suggests that decisional balance not only predicts readiness for behavioral change but also correlates with actual behavioral outcomes such as engagement in health-related practices [19, 21]. Therefore, older adults’ evaluation of the benefits and barriers associated with completing an advance decision may influence their engagement with robot-assisted ACP education programs.

Study aims and hypotheses

However, despite the growing use of robotic technologies in health-related education [22], limited research has examined their application in advance care planning education for older adults, particularly in community settings. Little is known about whether older adults would accept educational robots as a tool for learning about advance decisions. In addition, the mechanisms underlying such acceptance remain unclear. Therefore, this study aims to evaluate older adults’ acceptance of an educational robot for advance decisions learning in community settings, focusing on (1) continuance usage intention and (2) intention to recommend the robot to others. Methods

Study design, participants, and sample estimation

A cross-sectional survey was conducted to evaluate the acceptance of using an educational robot designed to facilitate learning about advance decisions among community-dwelling older adults. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) aged 65 years or older, (2) possessed adequate visual and auditory functions, and (3) demonstrated a willingness to participate in the study. Individuals who were illiterate or unable to interact with the robot were excluded. This study examines learning about advance decisions among community-dwelling older adults. The study population is operationally defined as individuals aged 65 years and older, which aligns with the standard definition used in Taiwan’s Senior Citizens Welfare Act [23].

The minimum required sample size was determined using power analysis, based on a significance level of 0.05, a statistical power of 80%, and an R² value of at least 0.25. Accordingly, the recommended sample size was 75 for a model with a maximum of six paths directed toward a latent variable [24]. The final sample included 84 participants, exceeding the minimum requirement and ensuring sufficient statistical power for hypothesis testing.

Design of the educational robot

The educational content in this study is based on the illustrated handbook Patient Right to Autonomy Act: Advance Care Planning – Instruction Tools, developed by the Taiwan Ministry of Health and Welfare [25]. It introduces key concepts, including ACP and advance decisions, as well as available medical options (e.g., life-sustaining treatments and artificial nutrition), as well as the specific clinical conditions under which advance decisions can be implemented. At the end of the session, participants are informed that those who wish to complete an advance decision must do so at qualified healthcare institutions, where the document becomes legally binding. The design features of the robot include several elements aimed at enhancing the learning experience. First, it emphasizes social interaction by leveraging the robot’s ability to engage learners through multimodal communication, including self-introduction, facial expressions, movements, and attire. These interactive features help foster a dynamic learner-robot relationship, thereby creating a more engaging learning environment. Additionally, the robot uses varied facial expressions and vocal tones to assume different roles during case-based storytelling, which aids in contextualizing the material and improving its relatability. The design also integrates generative artificial intelligence (AI) for music creation to reinforce key concepts and enhance knowledge retention. The research team applied AI tools, including ChatGPT and Suno, to compose lyrics and melodies. Finally, the learning sessions incorporated interactive questioning activities to encourage active participation and assess learners’ understanding. Table 1 presents an overview of the learning content, key design elements, intended pedagogical effects, and photographic documentation of the educational activities. The educational robot was programmed to use Mandarin as the primary language which was also the common language used at the community centers. The learning activities were delivered through collaboration between the instructor and the robot. The instructor would provide Taiwanese translation based on participants’ feedback or questions as needed.

Data collection

Eligible participants were recruited from community centers in Northern Taiwan. Unit administrators were contacted via telephone, and the study’s purpose, procedures, and ethical safeguards were explained. After administrative approval was obtained, recruitment posters were distributed to invite potential participants. Approximately 10–20 older adults at each center expressed their willingness to participate. Individuals who agreed to participate provided written informed consent and attended a single 90-minute session (including one short break).

Measurements

Participants completed a self-reported questionnaire that captured sociodemographic information, including age, gender, marital status, educational level, religion, living arrangement, and health status, and the following scales.

Human–computer trust

Human–computer trust (HCT) was measured using the revised Human–Computer Trust Model by Gulati, Sousa, and Lamas [17], which comprises four dimensions: perceived risk, benevolence, competence, and reciprocity. The 11 items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 5 (Strongly Agree). Higher scores indicate a greater trust in the technology. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha values for the four dimensions ranged from 0.54 to 0.96. Due to the poor Cronbach’s alpha for perceived risk (0.54), this variable was excluded from the statistical analysis. Therefore, the HCT in this study was represented by benevolence, competence, and reciprocity using eight items, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.97.

Decision balance (pros and cons)

Perceived advantages (pros) and disadvantages (cons) of completing ACP or advance decisions were assessed with separate 5-item subscales. The pros included gaining control over medical decisions during a serious illness and reducing doubts and anxiety about end-of-life treatment. The cons included concerns such as discussing end-of-life care, which may be perceived as inviting bad luck, and finding the signing process complicated and burdensome. Respondents rated their agreement on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 5 (Strongly Agree). In this study, Cronbach’s alpha for the pros and cons subscales was 0.93 and 0.81, respectively.

Revised personal involvement inventory

Cognitive and affective involvement were measured using the 10-item revised Personal Involvement Inventory (RPII) [16]. Each item is scored on a 7-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating a higher level of personal involvement. Cronbach’s α coefficient in the current study ranged from 0.92 to 0.96.

Data analysis

To examine the proposed hypotheses, we conducted partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM) using the SmartPLS v3.0 software (SmartPLS GmbH). PLS-SEM is an appropriate method for testing exploratory models, particularly in research involving small sample sizes [26].

Convergent and discriminant validity were examined to verify that the latent variables were valid and reliable. Convergent validity was assessed through item loadings, Cronbach’s α, composite reliability (CR), and average variance extracted (AVE). An item’s loading and the Cronbach’s α for the measurement scale were considered reliable with values equal to or greater than 0.70 [27]. For the measurement model to meet the requirements of convergent validity and reliability, CR must exceed 0.70 (26) and AVE must be greater than 0.50 [26].

Discriminative validity was established using the Fornell-Larcker criterion and heterotrait-monotrait (HTMT) [27]. An HTMT value less than 0.9 establishes that the discriminant validity is sufficient [28]. According to the Fornell-Larcker criterion, the square root of the AVE for each variable should exceed the correlations between the latent variables [29].

The structural model was assessed based on the standardized root mean square residual (SRMR), which is used to evaluate the model fit. An SRMR value of less than 0.08 indicates an acceptable fit [30]. Path coefficients were tested with 5000-sample bootstrapping; t- and p-values determined statistical significance [26]. The coefficient of R2 was used to assess the proportion of variance explained in the model.

Results

Participants’ background information

Participants were 84 community-dwelling older adults (Table 2). The average age was 77.74 years (standard deviation 7.32). Approximately half were 80 years old or older (50.70%, n = 36). Most patients were women (78.6%, n = 66), 47.6% (n = 40) were married, 20.0% had attained college or higher education (n = 16), and 85.7% (n = 72) had religious beliefs. Most participants (81.0%) lived with their family or friends, and 32.1% reported a good or very good health status (n = 27) (Table 2).

Structure model assessment

Figure 2 shows the testing of PLS-SEM for the proposed model. The model exhibited adequate correspondence with the data (SRMR less than 0.08). The latent variables of human–computer trust, decisional balance (pros), and decisional balance (cons) explained 21.3%, 9.0%, and 52.8% of the variance in cognitive involvement, affective involvement, and satisfaction, respectively. Overall, the six latent variables collectively accounted for 66.9% of the variance in continuance usage intention and 79.3% of the variance in intention to recommend the robot to others.

In addition, indirect effects were observed from human–computer trust to continuance usage intention (indirect effect, path coefficient = 0.338, t = 3.632, p < .001) and intention to recommend via satisfaction (indirect effect, path coefficient = 0.350, t = 4.241, p < .001).

This study did not support the indirect effects of decisional balance. While the negative aspects of decisional balance (cons) significantly influenced satisfaction, there were no significant indirect effects via satisfaction on either continuance usage intention (indirect effect, path coefficient = -0.107, t = 1.714, p > .05) or intention to recommend (indirect effect, path coefficient = -0.111, t = 1.763, p > .05).

Discussion

This study examined older adults’ acceptance of an educational robot as an innovative approach to promoting awareness of advance decisions. Overall, the findings indicate a high level of acceptance of this technology-assisted learning approach among community-dwelling older adults. Participants reported relatively strong behavioral intentions, with mean scores of 4.02 and 4.07 on a 5-point scale for continuance usage intention and recommendation intention, respectively. These findings suggest that educational robots may represent a promising tool for delivering ACP education in community settings.

Human–computer trust demonstrated a significant direct effect on continuance usage intention and an indirect effect mediated through satisfaction. This finding aligns with previous research identifying trust as a critical determinant of technology adoption and sustained use [31]. Consistent with the Expectation–Confirmation Model (ECM), users’ experiences with a technology shape their intentions for continued use. When the educational robot met or exceeded users’ expectations, satisfaction increased, which in turn strengthened their intention to continue using the technology [32]. In contrast, human–computer trust did not exert a direct effect on recommendation intention; instead, its influence was mediated by satisfaction. This finding provides additional insight into older adults’ decision-making processes regarding technology recommendations. Specifically, older adults may rely more on their overall satisfaction with the learning experience rather than solely on their level of trust in the technology when deciding whether to recommend the educational robot to others.

Previous literature indicates that decisional balance is associated with behavioral intention, with pros and cons exerting different effects [20]. Studies based on the Transtheoretical Model highlight that when individuals perceive the benefits (pros) of a particular behavior to outweigh its drawbacks (cons), they are more likely to form positive intentions and move toward action [33]. Research on patients with advanced cancer has found that participants’ ACP practice behaviors were predicted by ACP-decisional balance, but not by their ACP attitudes [19]. The results of this study diverge from previous findings, as neither the participants’ perceived pros nor cons significantly influenced continuance usage intention. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in the selection of outcome behaviors. While previous studies often focused on the intention to complete an advance decision, the present study examined the intention to continue using an educational tool for learning about advance decisions. Therefore, decisional balance related to ACP may not directly influence how individuals choose to learn about advance decisions.

Perceived benefits (pros) significantly and directly increased recommendation intention in this study. Although decisional balance related to ACP and advance decisions did not appear to influence individuals’ preferences for learning about advance decisions (e.g., through an educational robot), older adults who perceived greater benefits of ACP and advance decisions were more likely to encourage others to use the educational robot for advance decisions education. This finding suggests that individuals’ positive perceptions of ACP may motivate them to promote educational opportunities related to advance decisions within their social networks.

In contrast, the perceived disadvantages (cons) of decisional balance did not show a significant effect on recommendation behavior, either directly or indirectly. This pattern is consistent with previous evidence suggesting that perceived benefits tend to exert stronger influences on behavioral intentions than perceived barriers [34]. Prior studies involving older adults have also indicated that decisional balance—particularly the perception of benefits—serves as a reliable predictor of technology adoption [35].

Contrary to previous studies that identified involvement as an important predictor of continuance usage intention, this study did not observe significant direct or mediating effects of involvement. Rather, satisfaction emerged as a key determinant of both continuance usage intention and recommendation intention for the educational robot. This finding is consistent with prior evidence indicating that user satisfaction positively influences continuance intention and e-loyalty [36].

Our findings suggest that educational robots may serve as a promising alternative tool for delivering ACP education in community settings. Based on field observations, many participants perceived the robots as novel and engaging. Some took photos and shared them with family members, expressing an intention to use these photos as a starting point for discussing the ACP-related content they had learned with their families. In addition, participants were observed informally discussing ACP topics with each another during break times. These observations indicate that introducing diverse educational media can help stimulate ACP-related discussions. However, this study did not assess behaviors toward ACP, and thus we were unable to quantify the impact of the educational intervention on subsequent actions. Future research could therefore consider incorporating measures of behaviors toward ACP, such as those used in a population-based study, including whether individuals have discussed their end-of-life wishes, completed advance decisions, and appointed a potential surrogate for medical decisions [37].

Limitations

This study had some limitations. First, the research was conducted in an urban setting, where participants may have familiarity with educational robots owing to increased opportunities for technology exposure, limiting generalizability to rural settings. Future studies should incorporate rural samples to develop a more comprehensive understanding of the adoption and use of educational robots. Second, our inclusion criterion of 65 years and above encompasses considerable heterogeneity within the older adult population. Given the mean age of 77.74 ± 7.32 years, our sample likely included participants with varying levels of cognitive function, and some may have experienced early cognitive decline that could affect learning outcomes and technology acceptance. The acceptance of robot-assisted education may differ across age sub-groups (young-old, old-old, and oldest-old adults). Caution is warranted when generalizing these results to specific age sub-groups. Third, the participants attended a 90-minute robot-assisted educational session (including one short break). Considering the relatively short attention time of older adults, the design of educational session may contribute to the discrepancy between their hypothesis and the findings. Fourth, the absence of a control group limits our ability to attribute the observed outcomes specifically to the robot intervention. While the findings are compelling, a comparative group would have provided more robust validation of the robot’s specific impact. Future research should employ controlled designs with comparison groups to better isolate the robot’s specific effects. Finally, the perceived risk dimension of human–computer trust was excluded from the data analysis due to low internal consistency (0.54); thus, trust was represented by only positive facets. Findings should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Conclusion

Community-dwelling older adults demonstrated favorable acceptance of a robot-assisted educational program for advance decisions, suggesting that educational robots may serve as a promising approach for promoting ACP and increasing advance decisions awareness in community settings. The PLS-SEM findings provide important insights for the design and refinement of robot-assisted educational interventions aimed at improving ACP education among older adults.

Human–computer trust influenced continuance intention both directly and indirectly through satisfaction, whereas its effect on recommendation intention occurred only through satisfaction. These findings suggest that when deciding whether to recommend the educational robot to others, older adults may place greater emphasis on their actual learning experience and level of satisfaction rather than relying solely on their trust in the technology. In addition, perceived benefits of ACP and advance decisions significantly increased recommendation intention, whereas perceived barriers showed no significant effect. Together, these results highlight the importance of enhancing user satisfaction and emphasizing the benefits of advance decisions when developing educational technologies to support ACP engagement among older adults.

Relevance to clinical practice

The findings of this study have several implications for clinical practice in ACP and palliative care education. First, the results suggest that educational robots may serve as a feasible and acceptable tool for delivering ACP education to older adults in community settings. Integrating interactive technologies into ACP education programs may help healthcare professionals engage older adults more effectively and increase awareness of advance decisions. Second, the study highlights the importance of fostering trust and satisfaction when implementing technology-assisted educational interventions. Healthcare providers should ensure that robot-assisted learning programs are user-friendly, trustworthy, and capable of providing positive learning experiences, as satisfaction was shown to be a key factor influencing both continued use and recommendation of the educational robot. Third, emphasizing the benefits of advance decisions during educational interventions may encourage older adults not only to participate in ACP-related learning but also to promote such educational opportunities within their social networks. By facilitating peer recommendations and community dissemination of ACP knowledge, innovative educational tools such as robots may contribute to broader public engagement in advance care planning. Finally, previous research has supported a significant association between end-of-life health literacy and individuals’ behaviors toward ACP. Specifically, among the three dimensions of end-of-life health literacy, interactive end-of-life health literacy has been found to be significantly associated with multiple ACP-related behaviors, whereas functional health literacy was not significantly associated [37]. In our study, the educational robot employed situational scenarios to stimulate question–answer interactions, suggesting that it may be an appropriate tool for enhancing interactive health literacy in the context of ACP and for helping to build interactive literacy in this domain.
