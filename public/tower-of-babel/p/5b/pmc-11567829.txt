The origins of ADNI
Authors: William Z. Potter, Neil S. Buckholtz, Steven M. Paul
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractA brief history of events surrounding the conceptualization and original implementation of the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) as a public–private partnership (PPP) is provided from the perspective of three individuals directly involved from the outset. Potential barriers and how they were addressed are summarized, especially the decision to make all data freely accessible in real‐time. Decisions made at the beginning of ADNI are revisited in light of what has been learned over the past 20 years, especially the importance of the investment in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and blood measures and the commitment to data sharing. The key elements of ADNI's success from the authors’ perspective are also summarized.Highlights Informal interactions among colleagues were the beginning of something big.An NIH Director's personal decision on open data sharing has had perhaps the greatest impact of any single decision in the past several decades in terms of advancing clinical biomarker research.After 20 years, blood‐based biomarkers of brain disease may soon take the place of brain imaging for purposes of diagnosis and drug development.

The breadth of scientific achievements arising from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) over the past 20 years speaks to the much greater success than anticipated of the first and largest Alzheimer's disease (AD) research collaboration ever undertaken among the government (National Institutes of Health [NIH]), pharmaceutical companies, and non‐profit organizations. How did this then‐unique public‐private partnership (PPP) come about, and what were the key elements contributing to its enormous success?

ADNI was designed to address an important challenge facing AD clinical trials as to whether a drug's potential benefits were disease‐modifying rather than purely of symptomatic benefit. In the latter part of the 1990s, the “amyloid hypothesis” of AD was formulated with several industry programs being initiated to find drugs that could reduce or prevent amyloid deposition in the brain. By the time these programs were advancing to the point of generating compounds to be tested in patients in the early 2000s, the limitations of the clinical and neuropsychological outcome measures available for use in clinical trials designed to study their disease modifying effects posed a major barrier. It was reasoned that that by directly assessing the pathological processes in the brain that are associated with AD, employing various neuroimaging modalities and fluid biomarkers, and correlating these with the standard clinical, neuropsychological, and neuropsychiatric tests, the best biomarker or combination of biomarkers of disease progression could be identified. If validated, these biomarkers could in turn be applied for both selection of patients for clinical trials to follow (and hopefully objectively quantify) the beneficial impact of drugs that would likely precede by months or even years convincing evidence of ultimate clinical benefit.

The background against which the full ADNI plan emerged can be summed up by comments made by Mike Weiner, who had become the principal investigator of ADNI, in an interview with Gina Kolata of the New York Times in 2010. 1 To quote, “The problem in the field was that you had many different scientists in many different universities doing their own research with their own patients and with their own methods. Different people using different methods on different subjects in different places were getting different results, which is not surprising. What was needed was to get everyone together and to get a common data set.”

It was with this idea in mind that several colleagues (former investigators in the Intramural Research Program of the National Institute of Mental Health with two of us [Steve Paul and Bill Potter] from the Lilly Research Labs and the other [Neil Buckholtz] from the National Institute on Aging [NIA]) raised the possibility of a collaborative effort which eventually became ADNI. It was in a car driving back to the airport following a visit about the status of AD clinical trials at Lilly that Neil and Bill decided the goal was sufficiently important enough that it warranted a large PPP to discover and qualify AD‐related biomarkers that could be used in clinical trials of potential disease‐modifying drugs. Shortly after presenting the idea of the NIA serving as an “honest broker” to bring together the necessary public and private sector stakeholders, Richard Hodes (Director of NIA) and Steve Paul (Lilly's R&D Leader) were on the phone with the latter committing to seek the intellectual and financial support of other biopharmaceutical companies.

At that time, a forum to discuss how such direct strategic discussions between NIH and industry which involved government funding, that is a so‐called “safe harbor” was necessary. The latter was provided by the NIA working together with the Foundation for the National Institutes of Health (FNIH) which was established by Congress in 1990 to “support the mission of NIH and to advance collaboration with the biomedical researchers from universities, industry and non‐profit organizations.” A series of small initial meetings was coordinated by the NIA and FNIH, to bring together leading investigators from around the world along with representatives from the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA), industry, and advocacy groups. These meetings focused on whether candidate biomarkers could be standardized, developed, and advanced by a naturalistic longitudinal study that would eventually support the design of informative clinical trials including those testing potential disease‐modifying agents. At that time, it was clear that a relatively large longitudinal study was required and that an initial 5‐year “pilot” effort could be supported with NIH funding as well as with the support of the private sector to provide sufficient data to decide whether to continue or not.

After 2 to 3 years of discussions, there was sufficient enthusiasm and alignment that biomarkers could potentially be developed to serve the stated goal and to support the issuance of a Request for Applications (RFA) for a 5‐year study funded primarily by the NIA along with funds provided by several other NIH Institutes and Centers as well as the private sector, both pharmaceutical companies and non‐profit organizations. What was notable about the requirements and structure of ADNI at the time were its governance structure and unprecedented requirement for data sharing in real‐time which encountered considerable initial pushback, a theme to which we will return. There was also a 2‐decade history of NIA leadership committing funds to large collaborative efforts or special programs that the Institute had supported separately from RO1 grants. Particularly relevant to ADNI was the NIA investment in the infrastructure required to sustain the Alzheimer's Disease Research Centers (ADRCs) dating back to 1984. 2 In several respects, ADNI represented a departure from how many in the broader field viewed the responsibilities of NIH and industry with naysayers among both groups questioning the value of such an undertaking. When finally launched in 2004, ADNI, with its substantial scale and scope, and unprecedented close and pre‐competitive collaboration between the public and private sectors, was met with understandable skepticism by many as to what would really be delivered.

As individuals involved from the beginning of ADNI, we will share our perspective and memory as to how potential barriers were overcome. The question of how to structure a novel 5‐year PPP was relatively easily addressed through a tailored approach between the NIA and FNIH. More challenging were concerns from academia that there would be little incentive for investigators to participate in such a collaborative effort as this form of “team science” would not allow for individual contributions to be recognized in order to advance academic careers. Over 5500 publications later based on ADNI data, the concerns about career advancement have been largely unfounded. Moreover, the almost 2 decades of ADRC programs had built a culture of working collaboratively across multiple academic institutions. It is a tribute to the flexibility of the NIH system that decisions could be made at an Institute level to find a mechanism to support what was clearly a unique need and opportunity. No other NIH brain institute had seriously considered so substantive a coordinated effort to develop biomarkers for drug development until much more recently.

The sheer scope of what would be required to generate results that might be sufficiently robust to inform drug development was well beyond any previous central nervous system (CNS) biomarker development efforts. The proposal that was ultimately funded 3 involved the recruitment of 200 cognitively normal controls, 400 mild cognitive impairment (MCI) subjects, and 200 mild AD individuals. These were to be followed every 6 months over a 2‐ to 3‐year period with standardized magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (FDG‐PET) along with blood and less frequent cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) sampling. To have any expectation of delivering on this sample size within the specified time frame over 50 sites were identified taking advantage of the aforementioned ADRC structure. This decision entailed a 6‐month “pilot” commitment to standardize the neuroimaging and other outcome measures in such a large multisite study. The sample size was powered so that another MRI measure of brain structure would show rates of change which, if slowed, would provide objective evidence of disease progression before the more subjective clinical findings. The explicit sample size powering assumptions went well beyond what was typical of clinical research studies at the time since the focus was not on hypothesis testing per se but establishing what would be required to detect evidence of change in a prespecified number of patients in a clinical trial.

The functional measure of FDG‐PET was viewed as the second‐best choice of a potential biomarker of disease progression with a majority of industry scientists, and a few academic investigators, including the late John Trojanowski, who were also enthusiastic about CSF biochemical measures. Blood sample collection and storage were included in the hope that informative analytes in this readily obtainable peripheral tissue compartment would eventually be found. We recall lively debates on whether it would be possible to obtain repeated annual CSF samples over a 3‐year or longer period in even 20% of participants, with the group finally agreeing on entry requirements that eventually led to obtaining CSF in over 50% of subjects during the first 5‐year period of ADNI! CSF findings proved of sufficient importance that, in the later stages of ADNI, the rate of collection reached close to 90%. Industry scientists and advocacy groups worked closely together to “sell” collecting CSF to skeptical investigators as well as providing tools to make this acceptable to study participants; an excellent example in our opinion of the special contributions that can be made by different PPP stakeholders all working together. Another path for pre‐competitive collaboration utilizing samples gathered by ADNI was provided in 2006 by the Biomarkers Consortium of the FNIH 4 which raised additional funds from industry and advocacy to support and manage projects focused on fluid biomarkers of special interest. As we all now appreciate, it was the ratio of βamyloid peptides (Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio) in CSF that was initially shown to be highly correlated with amyloid PET measurements, after it was incorporated somewhat later into ADNI, and that ultimately supported registration of disease‐modifying monoclonal antibodies that were capable of removing amyloid from the brain parenchyma and slowing, albeit modestly, disease progression. More recently, blood‐based biomarkers have proven to be incredibly powerful at quantifying both amyloid and tau pathology in AD patients.

The selection of the original neuroimaging and biochemical biomarkers required achieving alignment on highly focused hypotheses relevant to testing for drug effects and for which precedents for some component of drug development for other conditions could be found. Given the hope that one or more biomarker measures would be sufficiently powerful to use in clinical decision‐making, much attention was given to the acquisition of MRI brain structural data, curation, and sharing of the data and resources needed to explore optimal analytic approaches; that are, to obtain the level of sensitivity and reproducibility needed for multisite trials of agents aimed at altering disease progression. Industry partners were especially interested in the possibility of accessing any relevant data in real‐time that might be of help in designing clinical trials of novel potential disease‐modifying agents that were under development.

This seemingly simple principle of real‐time data sharing so soon after it was acquired was a radical departure in 2004 from the usual practice of sharing clinical data. Many academic scientists raised strong objections arguing for “ownership” of data by the investigators. This initial pushback by academic scientists was very shortly dealt with by Richard Hodes, the NIA Director, who insisted on open access and sharing of all ADNI data which was explicitly included in the RFA. It is difficult to overstate the importance of this precedent‐setting decision, especially in light of many subsequent forums discussing the potential benefits of data sharing with the practice only recently becoming more widely implemented for NIH and advocacy‐funded studies. None of those involved (including us) imagined what it would take to store and share all ADNI data nor how extensively the data would be analyzed and communicated by the broader scientific community.

Nonetheless, the data‐sharing commitment did raise some concerns in the industry. During the early stakeholder deliberations on ADNI, there was much discussion on the potential ownership of any intellectual property that might be generated. Could the funding of ADNI, in part by the private sector, create intellectual property that might provide a barrier to its eventual use or provide a competitive advantage for any given company? As a pre‐competitive partnership, ADNI wasn't designed to generate intellectual property but at the outset, there was a heavy emphasis on how the possibility of such intellectual property would be handled and who would own it. Fortunately, both the public and private sector sponsors of ADNI decided early on that all of the data would be expeditiously placed in the public domain, more or less pre‐empting its use for commercial purposes. Having said this, the ADNI data have actually proved to be pro ‐competitive in that multiple companies have already developed their proprietary diagnostic tests for AD based at least in part on the findings from ADNI. The latter includes both the development of PET tracers used for neuroimaging of amyloid and tau as well as the blood‐based biomarkers for both amyloid and tau. Moreover, data from ADNI CSF samples were pivotal in the FDA approval in 2023 of Αβ42/40, tau, and ptau181 for clinical use.

In hindsight, the key elements of ADNI's success were multifold. First, was the widely agreed upon need to validate and qualify objective biomarkers for measuring the longitudinal progression of AD from its earliest preclinical and asymptomatic phase to the prodrome MCI and then through the various stages of the disease itself (early, mid, and late‐stage AD). Such biomarkers were deemed important, if not necessary, for testing potential disease‐modifying agents. All of the eventual partners and stakeholders of ADNI recognized this critical need. Moreover, it was widely appreciated that neither the NIH nor any single company or organization was likely to take this on by themselves. Second, there was already enough good biomarker data available (and emerging) based on solid neuropathology (e.g. neurodegeneration, amyloid, and tau pathology) to ensure that definitive data were likely to be generated, especially from analytes in CSF. In many respects, ADNI served to validate and extend work previously reported by many groups but with much smaller underpowered datasets. Third, advances in the field of AD biomarkers, both PET imaging and blood‐based biomarkers which frankly couldn't have been anticipated when ADNI began, rapidly emerged following compelling CSF findings and could now be quickly studied using ADNI resources providing for their further validation and adoption. Fourth, the bold decision of making all of the data publicly available in real‐time, that is, to any qualified investigator, provided an incredible accelerant, resulting in literally thousands of scientific publications by investigators who otherwise might have never worked on AD. Conversely, this “open sourced” pre‐competitive PPP benefitted enormously from the diverse skillsets and expertise of these non‐AD investigators. Fifth, the considerable investments made by both the NIH and private sector on novel disease‐modifying therapeutics (both small and large molecule drugs) were being realized quickly and, thus, the prospect of developing the first disease‐modifying drugs for AD. Thus, the need for qualified biomarkers remains a major motivation among ADNI stakeholders.

While the first of these disease‐modifying drugs are now FDA‐approved, almost everyone appreciates that they have rather modest efficacy; thus, they represent the “end of the beginning, not the beginning of the end.” Finally, as a postscript, we would underscore that, while ADNI began primarily as a “neuroimaging initiative,” it appears that the blood‐based biomarkers (validated by the neuroimaging data) will prove the most useful for real‐world diagnoses and for monitoring disease progression as well as the effects of disease‐modifying treatments. We will end by dedicating this brief history of ADNI to the memory of John Trojanowski whose support of ADNI and especially the longitudinal collection of CSF and blood samples have proven especially prescient.
