Management of Cervical Spinal Fractures With Ankylosing Spondylitis by Single‐Anterior Surgery: A Case Series Report and Literature Review
Authors: Qizhao Tan, Fang Zhou, Hongquan Ji, Zhishan Zhang, Yun Tian, Bingchuan Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveSurgical management of cervical spinal fractures accompanying ankylosing spondylitis (ASCSF) is intractable in clinical practice. There is still debate about whether surgery by a single‐anterior approach is enough for treating ASCSF. The purpose of this study is to summarize and share relevant experience and lessons from both our team and the literature.MethodsPatients referred to our center for ASCSF following single‐anterior surgery (from January 2008 to December 2020) were distinguished and enrolled. In addition, literature published from 2000 to 2021 on PubMed and Web of Science databases was systematically reviewed.ResultsA total of 63 patients (7 from our center and 56 from the literature) who underwent single‐anterior surgery for treating ASCSF were brought into this study. The average follow‐up time of patients in our center is 44 months. The average age of all patients was 58.6. C6/7 was the most commonly injured level (22 patients, 34.9%), and 44 patients (69.8%) experienced neurological impairment at admission. Most ASCSF patients have lordotic cervical alignment and minimal displacement of the fracture. A total of 8 patients died at an early stage after surgery. Apart from these 8 cases, the incidence rates of general complications and surgical complications were relatively 10.9% and 20.0% respectively. The incidence analysis revealed pneumonia (5.45%) as the predominant general complication, contrasting with implant failure (14.55%), which emerged as the most common surgical complication. Among the 8 cases (14.55%) demonstrating implant failure, radiographic analysis revealed preserved cervical lordosis in 4 patients (50%), kyphotic deformity in 1 patient (12.5%), while cervical alignment data were unavailable for the remaining 3 cases (37.5%). Moreover, 29 patients (52.7%) achieved improvement, and 18 patients (37.5%) maintained stable neurological function.ConclusionsFor most ASCSF patients with preserved lordotic alignment and minimal displacement, a single anterior surgery can achieve significant neurological improvement and result in a relatively lower incidence of complications. This provides a good basis for orthopedic physicians to handle cervical spine trauma patients with ankylosing spondylitis.Keywords: ankylosing spondylitis, cervical spinal fractures, single‐anterior approach, surgical effects, surgical indications

Introduction

Ankylosing spondylitis (AS) is a chronic seronegative inflammatory arthritis mainly affecting the axial skeleton. Advanced AS is commonly characterized by ossification of the spinal joints, paraspinal ligaments, intervertebral disks, sacroiliac joints, and osteoporosis. These mixed factors can result in reduced joint mobility, stiffness, and deformity of the spine [1, 2]. Patients with AS are prone to fractures. The risk of vertebral fractures can reach 10%–14% in these populations [3], even under a low‐velocity accident.

Cervical spinal fracture (CSF) is one of the main components among AS‐related spinal fractures. As reported, the incidence of lower cervical fracture is approximately 50%–55% [4, 5]. Particularly, CSF is relatively more dangerous; impaired neurological function, paralysis, cardiopulmonary dysfunction, and even death may happen in severe cases. Management of patients suffering from CSF with ankylosing spondylitis (ASCSF) is particularly challenging due to high rates of mortality and morbidity. Hence, a prompt and reasonable strategy is vital to achieve better treatment effects for patients with ASCSF.

In recent years, surgical treatment has been employed more frequently for ASCSF patients. Surgery helps in restoring cervical realignment, improving cervical stability, correcting deformity, promoting intervertebral fusion, and creating favorable conditions for the recovery of impaired neurological function. Single‐anterior, single‐posterior, and combined anterior–posterior are alternative surgical approaches; however, which approach is better remains controversial. Many relevant studies have been reported on single‐posterior and combined anterior–posterior approaches [6, 7, 8]. But relatively few studies have reported on the single‐anterior approach due to the relatively narrow application scope and higher risk of fixation failure. According to the literature, the failure rate of initial anterior fixation was as high as 50% in treating ASCSF, causing many surgeons to abandon this approach [9, 10, 11].

In a previous study, we analyzed patients with ASCSF and found that surgery via single‐posterior and combined approaches could achieve similar treatment effects, while the combined approach was closely linked with a higher complication rate, surgical duration time, and surgical trauma [12]. In the present study, we continued the analysis related to the selection of the surgical approach for ASCSF. The purposes of the study are to (i) analyze if a single‐anterior approach is enough for treating ASCSF; (ii) summarize the cases from our center and the literature, including 63 patients with ASCSF cases whose initial surgery was completed through a single‐anterior approach; (iii) analyze and share related experience, emphasizing the meaningful lessons learned and surgical indications.

Materials and Methods

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

We retrospectively collected the clinical characteristics of patients, surgical technique, and outcomes from our hospital, specifically including age, sex, traumatic event, mechanism of injury, fracture level, cervical alignment, degree of fracture displacement, fracture classification, surgical skills, neurological status, bone fusion, and complications. Patients with ASCSF who underwent surgery via a single‐anterior approach from January 2008 to December 2020 were enrolled. The inclusion criteria are: (i) patients with a clear diagnosis of AS; (ii) confirmed by cervical spine CT and/or MRI as an acute cervical fracture; (iii) presence of a definite neurological deficit. The exclusion criteria are (i) fractures caused by malignant tumors, infection, or other definite non‐traumatic etiologies; (ii) old fractures where the time from injury exceeds 4 weeks; (iii) the presence of severe concomitant injuries, such as severe traumatic brain injury, thoracoabdominal injuries, or multiple trauma; (iv) a history of previous cervical internal fixation surgery at the fractured level. The fracture classification introduced by Caron T et al. [13] was used: type I, disc injury; type II, body injury; type III, anterior body or posterior disc injury; type IV, anterior disc or posterior body injury. American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA) grade was used to represent the neurological status of patients. The study was approved by the ethics committee of Peking University Third Hospital (approval number: M2017331). Consent to participate was obtained from the patients.

Search Strategy

Additionally, we systematically reviewed the relevant literature published in the PubMed and Web of Science databases from 2000 to 2021. The search strategy incorporated the following Boolean keyword combination: (“spinal fracture” OR “cervical spinal fracture” OR “vertebral fracture”) AND (“ankylosing spondylitis” OR “ankylosing spinal disorder”). Besides, we also searched some hand‐selected studies from references of classical publications on this topic. All eligible case reports and clinical series published about this topic were assembled. Inclusion criteria were: (i) adult patients (≥ 18 years) diagnosed with AS who sustained an acute traumatic cervical spine fracture; (ii) surgical intervention for the cervical spine fracture (e.g., anterior, posterior, or combined approach fixation and fusion); (iii) studies reporting on at least one of the following primary outcomes: neurological status, radiological outcomes (fusion, alignment), perioperative complications, or mortality; (iv) original research studies, systematic reviews, or meta‐analyses published in peer‐reviewed journals. Exclusion criteria included: (i) studies not directly related to ASCSF; (ii) editorials, conference abstracts, guidelines, or consensus statements; (iii) studies lacking extractable or analyzable data; and (iv) cases without complete demographic information or clinical data were excluded.

Implementation of the Revision Surgery

In cases when the implant failure or cervical fracture re‐dislocation occurs after the initial surgery via the single‐anterior approach, we usually choose the combined anterior–posterior revision surgery to reduce dislocation and enhance cervical stability. Combined with literature and our own clinical experience, if the re‐dislocation or implant failure occurs after the initial single‐anterior surgery, the revision option should be performed by the combined anterior–posterior approach as long as the patient's condition permits. The combined method is used to deal with dislocation reduction, stability reconstruction, and thorough decompression once for all, preventing re‐dislocation and one more revision surgery to the greatest extent. In a recent study, Mesbahi et al. [14] reported a patient suffering from C5/6 ASCSF, who underwent surgery four times to obtain a satisfactory reduction and stabilization, including the first initial C5‐6 ACDF surgery; the second revision surgery via anterior C4‐C7 fixation and C6 corpectomy with iliac graft placement; the third revision surgery via anterior C4‐T1 fixation and expandable cage implantation; and the fourth revision surgery via posterior C4‐T1 fixation with two rods. This particular case was of great learning and educational significance and emphasized the importance and effectiveness of performing a posterior revision surgery after the failure of the initial anterior surgery in treating ASCSF.

Summary of Literature Review

As illustrated in Figure 5, after duplicate checking, title and abstract screening, as well as full text assessment, 12 articles with 56 patients were ultimately included in the final analysis. The characteristics of the included patients are shown in Table 3. The average age of patients was 59.8 (range, 33–84) years, and their injured vertebral level included C2/3 (1 patient, 1.8%), C3/4 (1 patient, 1.8%), C4 (1 patient, 1.8%), C4/5 (4 patients, 7.1%), C5 (1 patient, 1.8%), C5/6 (18 patients, 32.1%), C6 (2 patients, 3.6%), C6/7 (20 patients, 35.7%), C7 (4 patients, 7.1%), and C7/T1 (4 patients, 7.1%). A total of 43 patients (76.8%) had neurological impairment on admission, and 10 of them (23.3%) had complete spinal cord injury (SCI) (ASIA grade = A grade or Frankel grade = A). Among the 56 cases, cervical spine alignment data were available for 38 patients, of whom 31 exhibited cervical lordosis (81.6%) and 7 (18.4%) demonstrated cervical kyphosis. Postoperatively, 8 patients (14.3%) died from fatal complications, 5 among the remaining patients (10.4%) developed other complications (including pneumonia, hoarse voice, urinary tract infection, bedsore), and 8 among the remaining patients (16.67%) experienced implant‐related complications (including implant failure and case sinkage). After surgery, except for the 8 deaths, the same neurological status was maintained stable in 18 patients (37.5%), improved neurological status was achieved in 28 patients (58.3%), and the neurological status worsened in 2 patients (4.2%).

Aggregation of the Cases From Both Our Hospital and Literature

In total, collecting the cases from both our hospital and the literature, 63 patients with ASCSF whose initial surgical approach was single‐anterior were enrolled in this study. Their average age was 58.6 years. As shown in Figure 6, C6/7 was the most popular cervical injured level (22 patients, 34.9%), and C5/6 took second place (19 patients, 30.2%). Before surgery, 44 patients (69.8%) experienced neurological impairment, including 10 (15.9%) complete SCI cases (ASIA grade = A grade or Frankel grade = A). According to the literature report, the rate of neurologic deficit following CSF in patients with AS could reach 43% [26], which was less than the result of our present review. A total of 8 patients (12.7%) died, while as reported, the postoperative mortality of CSF patients with AS was close to 10.2% [4], and the incidence of death in AS patients with spinal fractures was nearly 14.9% [5]. The mortality summarized by the present review lay between these two rates. After surgery, apart from the 8 dead patients, the incidence rates of general complications and surgical complications were 10.9% (6/55) and 20.0% (11/55), and 29 patients (52.7%) achieved improvement in neurological status. In terms of the postoperative complications, a novel study enrolled 60 patients with spinal fractures in ankylosing disorders; they concluded that surgical complications occurred in 18.3% of patients, and the general complications were pneumonia in 38.3% and urinary tract infection in 8.3% [27]. For comparison, the rate of postoperative surgical complications was higher in the present study, and the rate of postoperative general complications was relatively lower.

Discussion

Applying single‐anterior surgery to treat ASCSF is a double‐edged sword because it has apparent advantages and limitations. The choice of surgical approach depends on both patient characteristics and fracture pattern. Under existing conditions, single‐anterior is not the mainstream and optimal surgical approach for ASCSF. However, controversy still exists regarding the postoperative characteristics and logical selection of single‐anterior surgery in treating patients with ASCSF. Hence, in this study, we summarized the relevant experience based on the cases from our hospital and literature, with a view to provide beneficial reference to surgeons in clinical decision‐making. In the present study, we found that in most ASCSF patients presenting with preserved lordosis and minimal displacement, a single‐anterior procedure can yield favorable neurological outcomes while incurring a relatively lower risk of complications.

Advantages of Choosing Single‐Anterior Surgery to Treat ASCSF

Regarding anterior approach technology, due to the supine position, the risk of dislocation can be avoided during the positioning process and surgery. As mentioned, the spine of AS patients loses normal curvature and flexibility, and postural changes and moving patients can increase the risk of fracture displacement and even worsen SCI. The patient is prone to accidental rotation, flexion, and extension stress during the process of turning over and lying prone. It is easier and safer to be translated to the operating bed than the posture for posterior surgery. Besides, the deformity of spine kyphosis can also cause difficulty with prone positioning. Single‐anterior surgery only needs to support the neck and occiput in the natural cervical status, and it can be completed in the supine position, semi‐sitting position, or even sitting position.

Compared with single‐posterior or combined anterior–posterior surgery, other obvious advantages of the anterior‐alone approach include less trauma and surgical duration time, fewer soft tissue complications, direct decompression of anterior compression, and a high interbody fusion rate; several studies have reported many successful cases [6, 20, 21, 24].

Limitations of Choosing Single‐Anterior Surgery to Treat ASCSF

It is difficult for patients with AS to overextend their cervical spine during the anterior approach due to the loss of elasticity of the ankylosed cervical spine, which makes it hard to expose the surgical field, and the feasibility for long‐distance fixation is limited. In addition, the degree of cervical deformity also affects the application of anterior surgery. The cervical lordosis or neutral state is conducive to the full exposure of the surgical field. But if the distance between the mandibular chin and the sternum is too small, especially in the case of severe cervical kyphosis, the anterior approach becomes rather difficult. In addition, AS can result in vertebral osteoporosis, which reduces the strength of the fixation screws. As reported, the reduced bone density within the vertebral bodies typical of AS leads to a high rate of implant loosening of about 10%–15% [13, 28]. For fractures that have displaced before surgery, the single‐anterior titanium plate may not be sufficient to maintain the reduction state, and the fractures tend to displace again. Just as in the typical cases 2, 3, and 4 in this study, in which the internal fixation loosened early after the anterior surgery. Under these circumstances, the fixed segment can be appropriately extended to strengthen the fixation, and generally, it can be extended to 2 segments above and below the fracture end (as in our typical case 1). But if the biomechanical requirements for definitive fracture fixation are not met, a risk of internal fixation failure exists even after extended fixation, just like the typical case from the literature [14].

Unstable three‐column extension‐type injuries in the setting of ankylosing spondylitis are among the most challenging and stressful types of fractures to stabilize due to multiple factors. The appropriate non‐controversial treatment of these fractures is with long constructs that include multiple points of fixation above and below the fracture, usually achieved through dorsal fusions. It is worth noting that if an anterior surgery is conducted, it should be with a long anterior plate with multiple fixation points and never with a single‐level anterior fusion, as this is doomed to fail and failure in those patients can be catastrophic.

Feasibility and Indication Evaluation of the Single‐Anterior Approach

As mentioned above, an obvious advantage of the anterior surgery is relatively small surgical trauma. Therefore, for patients with medically tenuous conditions and difficult to tolerate long surgical time and large blood loss, a single‐anterior approach may be preferred as appropriate. While for patients whose physical conditions can tolerate posterior surgery, a single‐anterior approach should be selected based on the following factors:

Anterior surgery requires a full field to expose at least 1 to 2 vertebral bodies above and below the injured level. Before surgery, surgeons need to check the atlanto‐occipital motion of patients and measure the distance between the mandibular chin and manubrium sterni in the natural position. At the same time, patients' x‐ray and computed tomography (CT) images should be carefully analyzed to observe their cervical natural curvature, fracture characteristics, and displacement. Lordosis or neutral curvature increases the distance between the mandibular chin and the manubrium sterni to facilitate anterior exposure, as the cases reported from our hospital (Table 1), while kyphosis may cause huge troubles for anterior surgical exposure. For patients with chin‐on‐chest deformity, just as a case we reported earlier [29], anterior fixation can become particularly difficult.

A situation also occurs where the fracture line crosses the vertebral body; then the cage may sink or migrate during the process of postoperative recovery, thereby further affecting the bone fusion. When the fracture line is located at the junction of the upper–lower cervical spine or the cervical–thoracic region, exposing the surgical field and achieving steady internal stabilization will become difficult.

The stress distribution of the cervical spine may become more concentrated here in the flexion and extension movement due to the changes in local anatomy and biomechanical properties. As a result, the strength of single‐anterior fixation will suffer greater challenges. Moreover, for fractures with a severe displacement, which can reflect the local relatively larger stress to some extent, single‐anterior fixation has a higher risk of failure. In this difficult situation, it is often necessary to choose posterior multi‐segment fixation or combined anterior–posterior fixation. Although Kouyoumdjian et al. [19] reported their series of 14 patients with ASCSF treated with single‐anterior fixation and argued that this approach could be sufficient if the hardware is long enough, the extension of the fixation segment (2 segments above and below the fractured level) is not sufficient to maintain stability, just as in the typical case from our hospital (Case 4) and literature report [14], whose initial anterior stabilization also failed after an extended fixation segment.

Anterior‐only fixation may work effectively if the anterior plate and screw construct is long enough to minimize leverage induced by the long movement arms created by adjacent spine segments [30], but no accepted standard exists to determine the optimal fixation segments by single‐anterior surgery. ASCSF usually involves the anterior and posterior columns, which makes anterior‐alone fixation susceptible to failure. In a novel finite element model study reported by Liu et al. [31], the biomechanical influence of the instrumentation for the surgical treatment of ASCSF was investigated. When referring to the single‐anterior fixation, it was found that this approach provided little resistance to flexion, meaning fixation failure may occur during cervical flexion. Besides, the study also indicated that the longer the anterior fixation level, the smaller the range of motion change, suggesting higher stability in resisting long arms. Thus, for patients with ASCSF who can only undergo anterior fixation, they recommended that 3‐level fixation could be more reliable [31].

The incidence of SCI in patients with AS might be 11.4 times higher than that in the general population [32], and as reported, SCI rates after traumatic spine fractures in patients with AS ranged between 25.0% and 58% [27, 33, 34]. Besides, a new study found that SCI was associated with major complications during hospitalization (odds ratio, 5.65, 95% confidence interval 1.03–31.03, p = 0.046) [5]. For these reasons, the preoperative severity of SCI not only reflects the fracture degree and secondary injury to a certain extent, but also influences treatment decisions. For patients without or with mild neurological impairment, among whom posterior extensive decompression is not required, usually single‐anterior surgery is sufficient. Whereas, if the neurological function status of patients is significantly impaired, and the nerve and spinal cord are compressed seriously via imaging examinations, a posterior or combined anterior–posterior surgery might make more sense.

Overall, deliberate evaluation of the aforementioned factors before surgery can ensure a safe and successful surgery. If unfavorable factors are found, the posterior fixation should be supplemented in time or the initial surgery should be carried out via posterior approach to reduce the risk of internal fixation failure.

Prospect and Limitations

In the study, etiological analysis of the 8 documented mortality cases identified acute respiratory failure (n = 2), pulmonary failure (n = 1), and end‐stage renal failure (n = 1). The remaining four cases, which lacked detailed documentation in the source literature, are potentially attributed to either severe initial spinal cord injury or profound cervical kyphotic deformity. Importantly, the mortality analysis confirms no direct correlation between the anterior surgical approach and fatal outcomes. These findings underscore the necessity for: (1) developing more robust preoperative risk stratification protocols in future studies, and (2) implementing specialized evaluation criteria for patients with severe neurological compromise or significant spinal malalignment.

It should be noted that this study has a relatively small sample size and further multicenter, large‐scale studies are needed to better validate these conclusions. Secondly, although patients in our center were followed for an extended period, the follow‐up duration in the literature was varied, which limits the evaluation of long‐term clinical outcomes.

Conclusions

For most ASCSF patients demonstrating lordotic alignment and minimal displacement, the single‐anterior surgery can achieve effective anterior fracture reduction, neural decompression, anterior spinal stabilization, and ultimately promote intervertebral fusion while optimizing neurological recovery. After single‐anterior surgery, the incidence of complications is relatively lower. Due to the relatively small sample size, the absolute indications of single‐anterior surgery for treating ASCSF are relatively narrow. Therefore, surgeons should fully assess the physical condition of patients, fracture pattern, and neurological function before making a surgical decision, and select longer anterior fixation. Furthermore, if the initial single‐anterior surgery fails, combined anterior–posterior surgery is recommended to finish the revision, rather than another single‐anterior revision.
