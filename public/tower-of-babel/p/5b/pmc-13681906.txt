Clinical characteristics and outcomes of Candida auris in a tertiary intensive care unit: a four-year retrospective case series
Authors: Cevdet Furkan Köşker, Gürhan Taşkın, Mete Erdemir, Şerife Bozdaş, Ramazan Gümral, Serhat Duyan, Ömer Faruk Özdemir, Levent Yamanel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCandida auris is an emerging multidrug-resistant yeast associated with healthcare-associated transmission and severe outcomes in intensive care settings. Clinical ICU-based cohort data from Türkiye remain limited.MethodsWe conducted a single-center retrospective case series in a tertiary adult intensive care unit in Türkiye, including patients with C. auris isolated from blood and/or urine cultures between January 1, 2021, and January 1, 2025. Species identification was performed by matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization–time of flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS). Antifungal susceptibility testing (AST) for selected isolates was conducted using the Sensititre™ YeastOne™ colorimetric microdilution system. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (minimum–maximum) according to distribution, whereas categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Comparative statistical analyses were not performed because of the descriptive case-series design and limited sample size.ResultsAmong 5,778 ICU patients evaluated during the study period, C. auris was identified in 36 patients. No cases were detected in 2021–2022; all cases occurred between September 18, 2023, and December 23, 2024, representing 2.37% of 1,517 ICU admissions during that interval. Isolation was most frequent in urine cultures (77.8%), followed by blood cultures (33.3%), with simultaneous urine and blood positivity in 11.1% of patients. The mean length of hospitalization before the first C. auris-positive culture was 44.9 days. All patients had urinary and central venous catheters and had received broad-spectrum antibiotics before C. auris detection. In the preceding month, MDR bacterial bloodstream infections were identified in 16 patients. AST data were available for four blood culture isolates from three patients; fluconazole MIC values were > 256 µg/mL in all tested isolates, whereas micafungin showed the lowest arithmetic mean MIC value (0.188 µg/mL).ConclusionsC. auris emerged in our ICU after 2023 and was mainly detected in patients with prolonged hospitalization, invasive devices, broad-spectrum antimicrobial exposure, and frequent MDR bacterial co-occurrence. These single-center data from Türkiye highlight the importance of reliable species-level identification, infection control awareness, and antimicrobial stewardship. Larger multicenter prospective studies using standardized surveillance and reference-method-based AST protocols are needed to better define transmission dynamics, resistance patterns, and clinical outcomes.Keywords: Candida auris, Intensive care unit, Candidemia, Candiduria, Antifungal resistance, MALDI-TOF MS

Introduction

Candida auris, initially isolated from the external ear canal of a patient in Japan in 2009 was therefore named ‘auris’, was subsequently revealed through retrospective analyses to have been misidentified as Candida haemulonii in South Korea as early as 1996 [1, 2]. Following its first European detection in France in 2011 [3], the pathogen has now been isolated in 60 countries across all continents except Antarctica [4]. In recent assessments, C. auris has been classified as an “urgent threat” in the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) 2019 Antibiotic Resistance Threats Report and designated within the “critical priority” group of the World Health Organization (WHO) fungal priority pathogens list [5].

Unlike many other Candida species, C. auris is most characteristically associated with persistent skin colonization, particularly in the axillary and inguinal regions. Gastrointestinal colonization appears to be less consistently documented than for several other Candida species [6]. In colonized patients, C. auris primarily resides on the skin, with a predilection for the axillary and inguinal regions. This colonization can persist for months or even years, potentially leading to invasive infections long after initial exposure [7, 8]. C. auris exhibits a broad spectrum of clinical manifestations, including bloodstream infections (fungemia), myocarditis, urinary tract infections, surgical site infections, burn-related infections, catheter-associated infections, otitis, meningitis, and osteomyelitis [9].

As a significant nosocomial pathogen, C. auris spreads readily via contact in intensive care units (ICUs) and poses a severe threat to infection control due to its environmental resistance characteristics. Research has demonstrated that C. auris can maintain viability on hospital beds, high-touch surfaces, and various medical devices for up to four weeks [10]. In addition, C. auris can tolerate relatively high temperatures and high-salinity conditions, which may contribute to persistence in healthcare environments [11]. Furthermore, the capacity of C. auris to form biofilms, coupled with its multidrug resistance, adversely affects therapeutic success and contributes to high mortality rates.

The laboratory identification of C. auris is challenging, and misidentifications are frequently reported [12]. Guidelines from the ECDC and CDC recommend MALDI-TOF MS with updated databases as a preferred and reliable method for routine identification of C. auris, while molecular methods may be used for confirmation in selected settings [13, 14].

Clinical data on C. auris from Türkiye remain limited, particularly for ICU-based cohorts addressing candiduria, candidemia, antifungal susceptibility patterns, and concomitant multidrug-resistant bacterial infections. In this context, additional regional data may help clarify the clinical and microbiological profile of C. auris in high-risk intensive care populations. In this case series, we aimed to describe the clinical characteristics, isolation patterns, therapeutic approaches, and clinical outcomes of C. auris isolated in our intensive care unit. We also aimed to contribute single-center regional data regarding ICU epidemiology, colonization and infection patterns, antifungal susceptibility findings, and MDR bacterial co-occurrence.

Materials and methods

This study was designed as a single-center retrospective observational case series conducted in the adult intensive care unit (ICU) of a tertiary training and research hospital in Turkey. All consecutive patients admitted to the ICU between January 1, 2021, and January 1, 2025, were screened for C. auris isolation. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from Gulhane Training and Research Hospital’s Scientific Research Ethics Committee (Decision No: 2025 − 269, Date: May 6, 2025). Due to the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the ethics committee. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (2013).

All adult patients (≥ 18 years) with at least one positive culture yielding C. auris from blood or urine samples during the study period were included. Inclusion criteria: Age ≥ 18 years, ICU admission during study period, at least one microbiologically confirmed C. auris isolate, availability of clinical and microbiological records. Exclusion criteria: Patients with incomplete medical records.

C. auris strains isolated from the blood and urine cultures of patients monitored in the ICU were identified using matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization-time of flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS, Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany). Antifungal sensitivity testing for amphotericin B, fluconazole, voriconazole, itraconazole, posaconazole, caspofungin, micafungin, and anidulafungin was performed using the Sensititre™ YeastOne™ colorimetric microdilution system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cleveland, OH, USA / Remel Inc., Lenexa, KS, USA) method. Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) values were read according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The tested antifungal agents represented the major systemic antifungal classes used in clinical practice for Candida infections, including polyenes, azoles, and echinocandins. The results were not confirmed by CLSI or EUCAST reference broth microdilution methods; therefore, MIC values were interpreted descriptively.

C. auris candidemia was defined as isolation of C. auris from at least one blood culture and was classified as invasive infection. C. auris candiduria was defined as isolation of C. auris from urine culture. Urinary tract infection was considered only when candiduria was accompanied by compatible clinical findings, urinary tract-related clinical suspicion, pyuria, or clinician-directed antifungal treatment. In the absence of compatible clinical findings, urinary isolation was classified as colonization. Patients with C. auris growth in either urine or blood cultures were included in the case series. Clinical characteristics, culture results, antifungal therapies, and clinical outcomes of all cases were recorded.

Persistent culture positivity was defined as at least one follow-up blood or urine culture remaining positive for C. auris after the index positive culture, without any documented subsequent negative culture from the same site during the available follow-up period. Follow-up cultures were obtained according to clinical indications and were not performed according to a standardized surveillance protocol.

Demographic and clinical data, including age, gender, comorbidities, admission diagnoses, and APACHE-II scores measured within the first 24 h of ICU admission, were documented. Prior to the detection of C. auris in blood or urine cultures, the following parameters were recorded: length of stay, fungal and bacterial growths in blood and urine samples within the last month, presence and site of central venous catheters, presence of urinary catheters, antifungal treatments administered within the last 30 days (including start and end dates), and use of broad-spectrum antibiotics. Additionally, data regarding surgical history, gastrointestinal anastomosis or perforation, emergency GI/hepatobiliary surgery, acute kidney injury, need for renal replacement therapy, blood transfusion history, severe burns, HIV infection, hematological malignancies, solid tumors, history of transplantation, chemotherapy within the last month, corticosteroid use, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, tracheostomy, and PEG/PEJ presence were collected.

Broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure was defined as the receipt of systemic antibacterial therapy with activity against nosocomial Gram-negative pathogens and/or multidrug-resistant organisms within 30 days before the index C. auris positive culture. Prior antifungal exposure was defined as systemic antifungal treatment administered within 30 days before the index culture. Acute kidney injury was defined according to the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) criteria.

Following the detection of C. auris, 30-day mortality, all-cause mortality during the ICU stay, and specific antifungal treatments (including durations) were recorded. Thirty-day mortality was defined as death from any cause occurring within 30 days of the first positive C. auris culture. All-cause ICU mortality was defined as death from any cause during the same ICU admission following the index C. auris positive culture. All clinical, laboratory, and demographic data were retrospectively retrieved from the hospital’s electronic health records and laboratory information management system. One patient was excluded because of incomplete core clinical records that precluded reliable assessment of clinical classification, treatment course, or outcomes.

Several measures were undertaken to minimize potential sources of bias. Selection bias was minimized by including all consecutive eligible patients during the study period. Information bias was reduced through standardized data collection from electronic medical records. Misclassification bias was minimized by using MALDI-TOF MS with updated databases for organism identification.

Data were summarized using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation when they showed an approximately normal distribution and as median (minimum–maximum) when they were skewed. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages (%). Due to the descriptive nature of this case series and the limited sample size, comparative statistical analyses were not performed.

Results

In our case series, blood and urine culture results from 5,778 patients monitored in our clinic between January 1, 2021, and January 1, 2025, were analyzed. C. auris was identified in 36 patients, yielding an overall incidence of 0.62%. No cases of C. auris were detected in blood or urine cultures during 2021 and 2022. Between September 18, 2023, and December 23, 2024 (the period spanning the first and last detected cases) C. auris was identified in 2.37% (n = 36) of the 1,517 total patients admitted to the ICU. During this specific timeframe, C. auris was determined to be the causative agent in 20.3% (n = 12) of the 59 patients who developed candidemia.

Analysis of isolation sites revealed that 77.8% (n = 28) of patients had positive urine cultures, 33.3% (n = 12) had positive blood cultures, and 11.1% (n = 4) showed simultaneous positivity in both sites (Table 1). Additionally, other sites associated with positive blood cultures included endotracheal aspirate (n = 1, 2.8%) and catheter tips (n = 1, 2.8%); one patient (2.8%) presented with concurrent urine and wound culture positivity. Among patients with both positive blood and urine cultures (n = 4), 75% (n = 3) developed candidemia within an average of 7 days following the initial detection of urinary tract positivity. For analytical purposes, patients with simultaneous blood and urine culture positivity were categorized as blood culture positive.

The mean age of patients with positive urine cultures was 68.91 years (range: 31–85), with a mean APACHE-II score of 28.66 (range: 11–44). Regarding comorbidities in this group, malignancies were present in 45.83% (n = 11), acute kidney injury (AKI) in 58.33% (n = 14), chronic kidney disease (CKD) in 25% (n = 6), diabetes mellitus (DM) in 28.57% (n = 8), hypertension (HT) in 54.16% (n = 13), congestive heart failure (CHF) in 20.83% (n = 5), and central nervous system (CNS) diseases in 12.5% (n = 3). Only one patient in the urine culture group had no prior history of comorbid diseases (Table 1).

In patients with positive blood cultures, the mean age was 59 years (range: 27–90), and the mean APACHE-II score was 26.91 (range: 15–41). Within this group, coexisting malignancies were identified in 50% (n = 6) of the cases, AKI in 50% (n = 6), CKD in 8.3% (n = 1), DM in 25% (n = 3), and HT in 41.66% (n = 5). Two patients had no recorded history of comorbid diseases (Table 1).

When cases were analyzed in terms of 30-day mortality, the rate was 41.66% in patients with positive urine cultures and 16.6% in those with positive blood cultures (Table 2). Bacterial pathogens identified in blood cultures obtained within one month prior to C. auris positivity were also evaluated. Sixteen cases, in which Acinetobacter baumannii, Acinetobacter pittii, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa were detected, were classified as being infected with multidrug-resistant (MDR) microorganisms based on their antimicrobial susceptibility profiles. Among these cases, C. auris positivity was present in the urine cultures of 12 patients and the blood cultures of 4 patients (Fig. 1).

Among patients with C. auris detected in urine cultures, 62.5% (n = 15) had prior growth of different Candida species in their urine cultures within the preceding month. C. albicans was the most common species (66.7%, n = 10), followed by C. parapsilosis (13.3%, n = 2), C. glabrata (6.7%, n = 1), C. orthopsilosis (6.7%, n = 1), and Cyberlindnera fabianii (6.7%, n = 1). Evaluation of prior antifungal exposure within the last month showed that 37.5% (n = 9) of the urine-positive group had used antifungals, predominantly fluconazole (55.5%, n = 5), followed by micafungin (33.3%, n = 3) and caspofungin (11.1%, n = 1). In the blood-positive group, 75% (n = 6) had a history of antifungal use within the last month; micafungin was the most frequent agent (50%, n = 3), followed by caspofungin (33.3%, n = 2) and anidulafungin (16.7%, n = 1).

Regarding microbiological clearance in follow-up cultures, 27 patients remained positive without any negative samples, while 9 patients achieved culture negativity. Culture conversion was observed in 12.5% (n = 3) of the urine-positive group and 50% (n = 6) of the blood-positive group. The mean time to achieve culture negativity (from the last positive culture) was 15 days (range: 3–35) for urine cultures and 9 days (range: 2–16) for blood cultures. Of the six patients with blood culture clearance, four were receiving micafungin, one caspofungin, and one anidulafungin. Among the three patients with urine culture clearance, two were on micafungin and one on caspofungin treatment.

A notable finding in the preceding month’s data for the blood-positive group was that 33.3% (n = 4) of these patients had different Candida species in their urine cultures, while their concurrent blood cultures remained negative for yeast. Antifungal susceptibility testing (AST) was not performed for any of the urine-positive patients. However, AST was conducted for 3 patients in the blood-positive group across a total of 4 different blood culture samples. The lowest arithmetic mean MIC was observed for micafungin (0.188 µg/mL), whereas fluconazole MIC values were > 256 µg/mL in all tested isolates (Table 3).

Discussion

C. auris is a pathogen of increasing clinical significance due to its recent identification, diagnostic challenges, diverse clinical manifestations, high virulence, and association with substantial morbidity and mortality. Furthermore, its multidrug resistance (MDR), environmental persistence against common disinfectants, and increasing global recognition underscore the importance of continued clinical and epidemiological surveillance [9, 11, 12]. In this context, our study contributes single-center ICU-based data from Türkiye, where clinical reports on C. auris remain limited. By evaluating culture source distribution, colonization and infection patterns, MDR bacterial co-occurrence, persistent culture positivity, and antifungal susceptibility findings together, this case series provides a descriptive overview of the clinical and microbiological profile of C. auris in a high-risk intensive care population.

In our case series, the mean APACHE-II score was 28, which is consistent with previous studies in this field [15]. The mean APACHE-II score of 28 indicates a severely ill ICU population and may partly explain the high mortality, prolonged ICU stay, and susceptibility to candidemia or MDR bacterial co-infections observed in this cohort. Therefore, clinical outcomes should not be attributed solely to C. auris isolation but interpreted in the context of baseline disease severity, comorbidities, invasive devices, and antimicrobial exposure.

Reported isolation rates of C. auris among critically ill patients in intensive care units (ICUs) range from 1% to 4%; our finding of a 2.37% incidence rate aligns with these global estimates [16, 17]. For incidence calculation, the overall study period and the interval between the first and last detected C. auris cases were evaluated separately. The active case-detection interval was defined as the period from the first detected C. auris positive culture to the last detected case, between 18 September 2023 and 23 December 2024. During this interval, the denominator was the total number of ICU admissions, and the numerator was the number of patients with at least one microbiologically confirmed C. auris positive blood or urine culture.

According to the ECDC, MDR microorganisms are defined as pathogens exhibiting resistance to at least one agent in three or more antimicrobial classes [18]. A 2025 study by Akinosoglou et al. reported simultaneous MDR bacterial co-infections in nearly half (48.96%) of 96 patients with C. auris candidemia [18]. Our cohort exhibited a similar pattern, with 16 patients having MDR bacterial infections. Among these, five had concurrent C. auris candidemia, while 11 had C. auris isolated in urine cultures. However, this finding should be interpreted as a descriptive association rather than evidence of causality. Prolonged ICU stay, invasive devices, broad-spectrum antimicrobial exposure, severe underlying illness, and previous antifungal treatment may represent shared risk factors for both MDR bacterial infections and C. auris colonization or infection. Therefore, the co-occurrence of MDR bacteria and C. auris should be interpreted within the broader context of critical illness and antimicrobial pressure.

The literature frequently highlights difficulties in laboratory diagnosis, including species misidentification and confusion with other Candida species [12]. Our data supports this trend; within the month prior to C. auris isolation, various other fungal agents were identified in 22 urine cultures from 19 patients. Subsequently, 15 of these patients developed C. auris candiduria, and four developed candidemia. These observations suggest that initial isolation of different species may reflect diagnostic inaccuracies or limitations in laboratory-level species differentiation. Alternative explanations include true sequential colonization, changes in fungal ecology under antifungal or antibacterial selective pressure, mixed yeast populations, or dynamic microbiological shifts in critically ill ICU patients. Nevertheless, these observations highlight the importance of accurate species-level identification in high-risk settings. Therefore, laboratory standardization in high-risk ICU settings should prioritize species-level identification using MALDI-TOF MS with updated databases, while molecular methods may be considered for confirmation in uncertain cases. Misidentification of C. auris may delay appropriate antifungal treatment, contact precautions, and infection control interventions. In critically ill ICU patients, delayed recognition may increase transmission risk and complicate the timely management of invasive infection.

CDC guidelines report that patients can remain colonized for years—some for over four years—with test results often fluctuating between positive and negative [8]. In our study, follow-up cultures revealed that 27 patients (75%)—including 21 with urinary and 6 with blood isolates—maintained persistent C. auris growth without achieving culture negativity. Persistent culture positivity may support heightened infection control awareness in high-risk ICU settings. However, because this study did not evaluate the effectiveness or optimal duration of isolation precautions, no direct recommendation regarding prolonged isolation periods can be made.

It is well-established that the vast majority of C. auris-positive patients have been exposed to broad-spectrum antibiotics [9], a pattern also observed in our cohort. Strong evidence links broad-spectrum antibiotic use to both Candida colonization and invasive infection [9, 19, 20]. Broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure was observed in all patients; however, because this is common in critically ill ICU populations and no control group was available, it cannot be interpreted as an independent risk factor for C. auris colonization or infection in this study.

CDC assessments show that the mean fluconazole MIC for C. auris is often ≥ 256 µg/mL, while the MIC range for micafungin typically varies between 0.125 and 4 µg/mL [21]. Similarly, the 2022 SENTRY antimicrobial surveillance report identified high resistance to fluconazole but low resistance to echinocandins [22]. Our limited AST findings from blood culture isolates, showing low micafungin MIC values and uniformly elevated fluconazole MIC values, were broadly consistent with previously reported susceptibility patterns. Antifungal susceptibility results should be interpreted with caution, as testing was performed using the commercial Sensititre YeastOne® YO10 AST method and was not confirmed by CLSI or EUCAST reference broth microdilution. Therefore, MIC values—particularly elevated results—should be considered descriptive local data, and direct comparison with international datasets using reference methods may be limited. When testing the voriconazole MIC ranges of C. auris strains, it has been shown that there are isolates with very high MIC values, and in a significant portion of them, MIC values reach 8, 16, and even 32 µg/mL (pan-azole-resistant strains) [23]. Our findings are consistent with the literature. Although the Caspofungin MIC value is high (> 8), the other echinocandins anidulafungin (0.25) and micafungin (0.25) were at low values. This maybe due to known technical difficulties in caspofungin testing or may indicate a true FKS gene mutation.

Although echinocandin MIC values were generally low in our limited dataset, emerging echinocandin resistance in C. auris remains clinically important. Therefore, susceptibility results should not be interpreted solely on the basis of low mean MIC values, and potential MIC elevations or resistant isolates should be carefully monitored in future studies with larger isolate numbers and reference-method confirmation.

In our study, C. auris candiduria was defined as urine culture positivity, whereas urinary tract infection required compatible clinical findings or clinician-directed treatment. Therefore, candiduria was not considered equivalent to invasive infection and was interpreted according to clinical context. Griffith and Danziger (2020) noted that C. auris is relatively common in the urinary system, where distinguishing between colonization and true infection is difficult [12]. Urinary colonization may serve as a reservoir for invasive candidemia, and the unnecessary use of broad-spectrum antibiotics can enhance the pathogen’s capacity for adherence, proliferation, and biofilm formation [24]. The development of candidemia after prior C. auris candiduria in four of our cases is clinically noteworthy; however, this temporal association does not prove causal progression from urinary colonization to bloodstream infection. The mean length of stay in the intensive care unit for the four cases that developed candidemia following candiduria was 61.25 (23–137), slightly below the average (69.68); all cases had urinary catheters and all had central venous catheters; none had a history of neutropenia or chemotherapy; one patient was using fluconazole before candida overgrowth. Given CDC guidance that urinary isolates often represent colonization and do not routinely require antifungal therapy in non-invasive cases [8], clinicians must carefully evaluate urinary findings to avoid unnecessary treatment.

In Turkey, the existing literature on C. auris consists largely of case reports or laboratory-based studies; clinical cohorts addressing both colonization and infection in the ICU are scarce. We believe our study, spanning three years and evaluating colonization, infection, mortality, and resistance patterns, provides contribution to the national data pool. By highlighting issues such as misidentification, persistent colonization, and MDR co-infection, we provide epidemiological insights that align with regional and international trends. These data may also inform healthcare policies regarding laboratory diagnosis, infection control, and antimicrobial stewardship. Future multicenter prospective studies incorporating molecular epidemiology analyses and standardized, reference-method-based AST protocols are needed to better define transmission dynamics, resistance mechanisms, and clinical outcomes of C. auris in ICU settings.

Our study has certain limitations. It was conducted at a single center, and its retrospective, observational design precludes definitive causal inferences. The small sample size (n = 36) limited subgroup analyses. However, AST data were available only for four blood culture isolates obtained from three patients, which limits the generalizability of our susceptibility findings. Furthermore, antifungal susceptibility testing was performed using the commercial Sensititre YeastOne® YO10 AST method and was not confirmed by CLSI or EUCAST reference broth microdilution, which limits the interpretability and comparability of MIC results.

Conclusion

In conclusion, C. auris colonization and infection remain important concerns in ICU settings in ICUs, associated with prolonged hospitalization, broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure, invasive procedures, and MDR bacterial co-infections. Our findings provide single-center clinical data from Türkiye and are consistent with the emerging clinical profile of C. auris in high-risk ICU populations, including persistent culture positivity, MDR bacterial co-occurrence, diagnostic challenges, and limited antifungal susceptibility findings. These findings underscore the importance of standardized laboratory identification, rational antibiotic use, infection control awareness, and targeted screening of high-risk ICU patients, particularly those with prolonged hospitalization, invasive devices, broad-spectrum antimicrobial exposure, prior antifungal exposure, or concomitant MDR bacterial infections.
