Outer membrane vesicles-based nano-regulator for cancer immunotherapy by modulating neutrophils N1 polarization and hitchhiking delivery
Authors: Xiaoning Liu, Qun Niu, Haoxiang Yang, Tongyao Du, Yichen Liu, Hengqi Zhou, Shuqi Wang, Lingli Shen, Xuannian Wang, Gaiping Zhang, Hua He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Efficient eradication of cancer cells and enhanced tumor accumulation of drug via immune cells represent promising strategies for cancer treatment, yet they remain significant challenges. Inspired by neutrophils (NEs), we designed a novel nano-regulator (OMV@PCB NPs) to improve cancer therapy through polarizing NEs into anti-tumor N1 phenotype and photodynamic therapy (PDT)-assisted NEs hitchhiking delivery. OMV@PCB NPs were constructed by encapsulating Ce6 and SB525334 (TGF-β inhibitor) within PLGA nanoparticles, which were subsequently camouflaged with bacteria outer membrane vesicles (OMVs). Beyond inducing ROS generation under light irradiation to kill tumor cells, OMV@PCB NPs could effectively hitchhike NEs to enhance tumor accumulation based on PDT-assisted NEs recruitment. Encouragingly, SB525334, a TGF-β inhibitor in OMV@PCB NPs, could significantly polarize NEs into anti-tumor N1 phenotype, which could not only promote neutrophil elastase (ELANE) release to selectively eliminate cancer cells, but also elevate anticancer immunity by reversing the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, thereby achieving effective immuno-photodynamic therapy against tumor growth. This study offers a prospective avenue for enhancing cancer therapy.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-025-03658-7.

Introduction

Cell-based immunotherapy, which harnesses the intrinsic properties of immune cell (e.g., NK cell, T cell, macrophage, dendritic cell) to recognize and eliminate tumor cells [1, 2], has garnered significant attention as a promising therapeutic modality [3–5]. For instance, T cell-based immunotherapy, especially chimeric antigen receptor T cell (CAR-T) therapy, has been successfully approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for B-cell lymphoma therapy [6–9]. However, challenges such as T cell exhaustion, insufficient target antigen expression, inefficient T cell transportation to tumor site and inadequate immune response hinder the broader application of T cell immunotherapy [10–12]. Neutrophils (NEs), key components of innate immunity, can migrate to tumor site and further be polarized into either anti-cancer N1 phenotype or pro-tumor N2 phenotype [13–15]. Especially, N1 NEs-released neutrophil elastase (ELANE) can selectively kill various cancer cells via proteolytically liberating CD95 death domain [16], initiating apoptotic pathway and releasing cancer-specific antigen, thus eliciting adaptive immune response in vivo [17, 18]. However, transforming growth factor β (TGF-β), an immunosuppressive molecule in the tumor microenvironment (TME) [19], can cause NEs to polarize into pro-tumor N2 phenotype, potentially compromising the antitumor activity of T cells [20, 21]. Therefore, disrupting TGF-β signaling represents a potential strategy to promote NEs polarization into N1 phenotype.

Recently, cellular hitchhiking of therapeutics drugs shows great potential for target drug delivery and enhanced tumor accumulation [5, 22, 23]. For example, photodynamic therapy (PDT) is a high-efficiency cancer treatment strategy that can not only eliminate tumor cells through harnessing photosensitizer to generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) [24–26], but also enhance antitumor immunity by promoting T lymphocyte infiltration [27–29]. Moreover, PDT-induced acute inflammation can stimulate NEs proliferation and recruitment to inflammatory tumor sites [30], providing a NEs-based hitchhiking strategy for efficient tumor accumulation [31, 32]. The ex vivo hitchhiking strategy involves NEs separation, loading therapeutic into NEs ex vivo, and reinfusing NEs into blood [33–36]. However, the short lifetime of NEs (~ 7 h) [37], high cost and complex process restrict its wide application [38]. In comparison, the in situ hitchhiking strategy may be more conducive to practical application, which designs antibody-decorated cargos to target circulating NEs, thus overcoming the drawbacks of ex situ process [39, 40]. Nevertheless, developing a more universal and effective strategy for specifically hitchhiking circulating NEs remains a significant challenge.

Outer membrane vesicles (OMVs), derived from Gram-negative bacteria (e.g., E. coli) possess extensive pathogen-associated molecular pattern (PAMP) such as lipoprotein and lipopolysaccharide (LPS) [41], which can effectively activate innate and adaptive immunity response [42, 43]. Besides, LPS expressed on OMVs can be specifically recognized by toll-like receptor (TLR) on NEs [44, 45], thus improving the uptake of OMVs by NEs [46, 47]. Hence, OMVs-coating nanoplatform exhibits significant potential for PDT-assisted NEs hitchhiking delivery, thus achieving effective tumor accumulation.

Herein, we developed an OMVs-based nano-regulator for hitchhike delivery of cargos to polarize NEs into anti-tumor N1 phenotype, thereby inhibiting tumor growth (Fig. 1). Specifically, the photosensitizer (chlorin e6, Ce6) and TGF-β inhibitor (SB525334) were encapsulated in poly (lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) nanoparticles (NPs), followed by coating with OMVs to yield OMV@PCB NPs. Interestingly, OMV@PCB NPs inherited the biological characteristics of OMVs, which not only activated immune responses, but also could be recognized by NEs for NEs-based hitchhiking delivery. Upon light irradiation, PDT generated substantial ROS that effectively kill tumor cells, while simultaneously amplifying inflammatory factors at the tumor sites to stimulate NEs recruitment, thus enhancing the tumor accumulation of OMV@PCB NPs via hitchhiking NEs. In addition, the delivered SB525334 effectively disrupted TGF-β signaling to promote NEs polarization into anti-tumor N1 phenotype, which could not only selectively kill cancer cells via releasing ELANE, but also attenuate the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment to elevate anticancer immunity, resulting in effective immuno-photodynamic therapy to mitigate tumor growth. This study provides a promising paradigm for augmented tumor therapy.

Methods

Preparation of OMVs

E. coli (DH5α) was inoculated into LB medium and incubated at 37 ℃ with shaking at 220 rpm. Once the OD600 value reached 1.0, bacterial culture medium was collected by centrifugation (4000 g, 4 ℃, 15 min). The supernatant was then filtered using a 0.45 μm filter, and concentrated by ultrafiltration tubes (3500 Da). Subsequently, the concentrate was ultracentrifuged (100000 g, 3 h, 4 ℃) to obtain OMVs. The OMVs pellet was resuspended in PBS and stored at −80 ℃. The protein concentration of OMVs was determined using BCA protein kit. To assess the safety of OMVs, OMVs were co-incubated with 4T1 cells for 24 h, and viability was assessed via MTT assay. Besides, mice were treated with OMVs, and serum was collected at 12 h post-administration for cytokine secretion analysis.

Preparation and characterization of OMV@PCB

OMV@PCB NPs was prepared according to our previously reported method [31, 48]. Briefly, PLGA (20 mg), Ce6 (2 mg), SB525334 (2 mg) were dissolved in DCM, then added to sodium cholate solution (1%) and sonicated at 20,000 J for 80 s. The emulsion was subsequently mixed with sodium cholate solution (0.5%), stirred for 4 h, and centrifuged (13000 rpm, 4 ℃) for 30 min to obtain Ce6- and SB525334- co-loaded PLGA nanoparticles (PCB NPs). Similarly, Ce6-loaded PLGA NPs (PC NPs), SB525334-loaded PLGA NPs (PB NPs) were prepared using the same protocol. OMVs (0.8 mg/mL, 0.5 mL) were then mixed with PCB NPs (1 mg/mL, 2 mL), and extruded to obtain OMVs coated NPs (OMV@PCB NPs).

The particle size, zeta potential, morphology of OMVs, PCB NPs, OMV@PCB NPs were characterized by dynamic light scattering (DLS) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM), respectively. To assess stability, OMV@PCB NPs (2 mL) were stored in PBS at 4 ℃, and particle size was monitored at various time points. To further confirm successful coating of OMVs on PCB NPs, OMVs and OMV@PCB NPs were lysed in RIPA buffer for 30 min, and subjected to sodium dodecyl sulfate - polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) analysis after centrifugation (4 ℃, 13000 g, 10 min). Besides, FITC-labeled OMV@PCB NPs were prepared by extruding FITC-labeled OMVs (FITC-OMV) with PCB NPs, and subjected to confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM) observation.

PDT-induced inflammation and NEs recruitment

For PDT treatment of tumor, mice bearing 4T1 tumor (~ 300 mm3) were i.v. injected with PCB NPs or OMV@PCB NPs (3 mg Ce6/kg), and then irradiated (660 nm, 10 mW/cm2, 30 min) at 24 h post-injection. To assess the inflammatory response induced by PDT, mice were treated with OMV@PCB NPs and further received PDT treatment (n = 3). Twenty-four hours after irradiation, tumor tissues were harvested, and the cytokine level of IL-6 and TNF-α was determined by ELISA kit.

To assess NEs recruitment following PDT treatment, 4T1 tumor-bearing mice in OMV@PCB group received PDT treatment (n = 3). Tumor tissues were collected 12 and 48 h after irradiation, and processed into tumor-infiltrating leukocytes. After PE-anti-Ly6G and APC-anti-CD11b staining, cells were subjected to flow cytometry (FCM) analysis. Moreover, collected tumors were embedded, sectioned, and stained with PE-anti-Ly6G and DAPI before fluorescence microscope observation.

NEs targeting

To evaluate uptake of NPs by NEs, NEs (5 × 104 cells/well) seeded in 24-well plates were treated with PCB NPs or OMV@PCB NPs (5 µg Ce6/mL). After incubation for 4 h, NEs were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with DAPI and visualized by CLSM. Alternatively, neutrophils treated with PCB NPs or OMV@PCB NPs were collected, washed with PBS before FCM analysis.

OMV@PCB hitchhiked neutrophils in vivo

4T1 tumor-bearing mice were divided into PCB, OMV@PCB group, and received PDT treatment. Twenty-four hours later, mice received a second dose (3 mg Ce6/kg, n = 3). Blood was collected from heart 4 h after the second administration, lysed in red blood cell (RBC) lysis buffer, and centrifuged (1200 rpm, 4 ℃) for 10 min. Then, the obtained leukocyte was stained with PE-anti-Ly6G and subjected to FCM analysis. Besides, tumor tissues were harvested 8 h after the second administration, isolated into tumor-infiltrating leukocytes, and stained with PE-anti-Ly6G before FCM analysis.

To clarify the role of NEs in the delivery process, mice were pretreated with anti-Ly6G via intraperitoneal injection for 24 h to deplete neutrophil (20 µg/kg, n = 3). Subsequently, OMV@PCB NPs were administered as described above, and tumor accumulation was monitored at different time points via In vivo imaging system (IVIS).

In vivo biodistribution

4T1 tumor-bearing mice in OMV@PCB, PCB group were treated with PDT, and again administered different formulation 24 h later (3 mg Ce6/kg, n = 3). At different time points, in vivo fluorescence imaging was performed using IVIS. Moreover, major organs, including heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney and tumor were collected 24 h after the second administration, and imaged as described above.

Intercellular transportation in vitro and in vivo

4T1 cells (1 × 105 cells/well) were seeded in 24-well plates and incubated overnight. NEs (5 × 103 cells/mL) were treated with PCB NPs or OMV@PCB NPs (5 µg Ce6/mL) for 4 h, followed by co-incubation with cells under phorbol myristate acetate (PMA) (800 nM) for 8 h. After washing with PBS, cells were stained with DAPI before CLSM observation.

To assess neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) formation, NEs isolated from peripheral blood were incubated with PMA (800 nM) for 8 h and subjected to FCM analysis. To further evaluate the in vivo intracellular transportation, 4T1-bearing mice were treated with OMV@PCB NPs according to the established in vivo biodistribution protocol. Tumor tissues were collected at 24 h post-the second injection, and dissociated into single-cell suspension. After staining with various antibody, NEs (Ly6G+ Ce6+) and tumor cells (CD45- Ce6+) were analyzed by FCM.

Antitumor efficacy in vitro

4T1 cells seeded in 96-well plates (~ 80% confluence) were incubated with OMV@PC, OMV@PB, PCB and OMV@PCB NPs for 24 h. After irradiation (660 nm, 5 mW/cm2) for 30 min, cells were cultured for another 24 h, and viability was assessed by MTT.

To evaluate apoptosis of tumor cells, 4T1 cells seeded in 6-well plates (~ 80% confluence) were incubated with OMV@PC, OMV@PB, PCB, OMV@PCB NPs (5 µg Ce6/mL, 3.1 µg SB525334/mL) for 24 h. After irradiation (660 nm, 5 mW/cm2) for 0.5 h, cells were incubated for another 24 h, stained with Annexin V-FITC/Propyl iodide (PI) apoptosis detection kit before FCM analysis. To observe dead/live cells, cells were received the same treatment and stained with calcein-AM (1.5 µM) and PI (5 µM) before fluorescence microscopy observation.

Antitumor efficacy in vivo

4T1 tumor-bearing mice (~ 100 mm3) were randomly divided into five groups (n = 8), and i.v. injected with PBS, OMV@PC, OMV@PB, PCB and OMV@PCB (4 mg Ce6/kg, 2 mg SB525334/kg) on days 0, 1, 4, 5, 8, 9. Mice in OMV@PC, PCB and OMV@PCB group were subjected to PDT treatment at 24 h post-injection. Tumor volume and body weight were monitored every other day, with tumor volume calculated as length × (width)2 × 0.5. When the tumor volume was > 1500 mm3 or mice died, they were identified as dead. On day 26, tumor tissues were collected for hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) and terminal dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) staining analysis.

To evaluate the improvement in the tumor immune microenvironment, tumors tissues were harvested at day 11, and dissociated into single-cell suspension. After staining with various antibody, DCs (CD80+ CD11c+/CD86+ CD11c+), infiltrated T cells (CD3+ CD4+/CD3+ CD8+), Tregs (CD3+ CD4+ CD25+) and MDSCs (CD11b+ Gr1+) were analyzed by FCM. Besides, collected tumors were lysed and centrifuged (1000 g, 30 min), cytokine level (TNF-α, IFN-γ, IL-6, IL-12) in the supernatant was detected using ELISA kit.

To evaluate in vivo safety, heart, liver, spleen, lung and kidney were harvested at day 11 for H&E staining. Moreover, blood was collected to measure aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate transaminase (AST), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and creatinine (CRE) levels.

NEs polarization

4T1 tumor-bearing mice were treated according to the established in vivo anticancer treatment protocol. On day 14, the tumor tissues were collected, and homogenized in lysis buffer containing proteinase inhibitor, followed by centrifugation (3000 rpm, 4 ℃) for 15 min. The key protein expression level of TGF-β signaling was detected by Western blot as described previously [49, 50], with β-action serving as an internal control.

Alternatively, collected tumors were dissociated into single-cell suspension. After staining with APC-anti-CD11b, PE-anti-Ly6G, FITC-anti-CD54 and FITC-anti-CD95, cells were subjected to FCM analysis. To assess the secretion level of ELANE, the collected tumor tissues were embedded in paraffin and sectioned before H&E staining. Immunohistochemical staining was performed using anti-neutrophil elastase and anti-Histone H1.0. Besides, RNA was extracted from homogenized tumors using Trizol reagent. cDNA was reverse transcribed from RNA using HiScript II 1 st Strand cDNA synthesis kit. Relative mRNA levels were detected by quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) using AceQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix. The primers were shown in Table S1, and results were expressed relative to the internal control gene GAPDH.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of OMV@PCB

The OMV@PCB NPs were prepared as illustrated in Fig. 1. OMVs were isolated from E. coli (DH5α) using multiple centrifugation and ultracentrifugation following the reported method [44]. As shown in Fig. 2A and C, TEM images revealed the vesicle structure of OMVs, with a particle size of 80 nm as determined by DLS. To assess the safety of OMVs, OMVs were co-incubated with 4T1 cells for 24 h, and viability was assessed via MTT assay. As shown in Fig. S1A, cell viability remained > 90% at the OMV concentration of 40 µg/mL, indicating excellent in vitro safety. To further evaluate the in vivo safety, mice were treated with OMVs, and serum was collected at 12 h post-administration for cytokine secretion analysis. As demonstrated in Fig. S1B and C, the level of IL-6 and TNF-α in OMVs group did not significantly differ from that in the PBS control group, confirming the favorable biosafety of OMVs.

Ce6- and SB525334- co-loaded PLGA (PCB) NPs with a particle size of 97 nm and zeta potential of −12 mV were prepared according to our previously reported protocols (Fig. 2A and B) [31, 48]. The drug loading content (DLC) of Ce6 and SB525334 in PCB NPs was 13% and 6.6%, and the cumulative Ce6 release was > 80% when PCB NPs were incubated under acidic condition (pH 5.5) for 12 h (Fig. S2), as determined by fluorescence and UV-Vis spectroscopy, respectively (Table S2). Subsequently, OMVs were coated on the surface of PCB NPs by extrusion to formulate OMV@PCB NPs, and the abbreviations of different formulations were summarized in Table S3. To optimize OMV/PCB NPs weight ratio, the protein content in OMV@PCB NPs was quantified using BCA kit. The protein content reached a maximum value when the OMV/PCB NPs weight ratio was 1:5 (Table S4), so this optimal weight ratio was used in all studies. Compared with bare PCB NPs, the particle size increased from 97 nm to 110 nm after OMVs coating, TEM images further confirmed that OMV@PCB NPs displayed core-shell structure (Fig. 2C), implying successful OMV coating. Additionally, FITC-labeled OMVs were coated on PCB NPs and further visualized by CLSM. As shown in Fig. 2D, a significant overlapping of green and red fluorescence was observed, collectively suggesting successful formulation of OMV-coated NPs (OMV@PCB NPs).

To further identify whether the characteristic protein of OMVs was presented on OMV@PCB NPs, SDS-PAGE was subjected to evaluate protein expression. As demonstrated in Fig. 2E, the protein band patterns of OMV@PCB NPs were similar to those of pure OMVs, indicating that OMV@PCB NPs retained typical OMVs proteins. In addition, OMV@PCB NPs displayed excellent stability for at least 8 days when stored in PBS, as evidenced by consistent particle size and zeta potential (Fig. 2F). In sum, OMVs were successfully coated onto PCB NPs to formulate OMV@PCB NPs, thereby endowing NPs with the biological functionality of OMVs.

PDT-induced inflammation and NEs recruitment

Ce6, as a photosensitizer, can generate ROS under light irradiation, leading to acute inflammation at the tumor site and recruiting NEs [51–54]. To assess the level of PDT-induced inflammation, mice were administered OMV@PCB NPs followed by PDT. As shown in Fig. S3, the levels of IL-6 and TNF-α in tumors showed a remarkable increase after PDT treatment, indicating that PDT effectively induced a systemic inflammatory response. To further assess the effect of PDT on NEs recruitment, tumors were harvested at various time points, and tumor-infiltrating leukocytes were subjected to FCM analysis. Excitingly, the proportion of CD11b+ Ly6G+ NEs in OMV@PCB (w/L) group was 2.2-fold higher than that in OMV@PCB (w/o L) group (Fig. 3A). Moreover, a high level of CD11b+ Ly6G+ NEs proportion was still demonstrated in tumor harvested at 48 h post-irradiation (Fig. S4). Immunofluorescence staining images also revealed significantly accumulation of NEs in tumor treated with OMV@PCB (w/L), as evidenced by extensive red fluorescence within the tumor (Fig. 3B). Collectively, these results indicated that PDT-induced inflammation facilitated NEs recruitment to tumor sites, providing potential for NE-hitchhiking delivery.

NEs targeting

To evaluate the targeting efficiency of OMV@PCB NPs to NEs, NEs were isolated from peripheral blood of mice, achieving a purity of ~ 89.9% as determined by FCM (Fig. S5). After incubation with OMV@PCB NPs and PCB NPs for 4 h, NEs were observed by CLSM. Compared with bare PCB NPs, OMV@PCB NPs were notably internalized by NEs, as indicated by extensive intracellular red fluorescence (Fig. 3C). This enhanced uptake was attributed to NEs’ ablility to sense and ingest bacteria-secreted OMVs, thus improving cellular uptake and NEs-targeting of OMV@PCB NPs. In agreement with the aforementioned findings, FCM analysis further confirmed that the NEs uptake was 3.0-fold higher in OMV@PCB NPs than that in PCB NPs (Fig. 3D). Collectively, these results indicated a superior NEs-targeting capability of OMV@PCB NPs.

Tumor accumulation by NPs-hitchhiked NEs

To verify whether OMV@PCB NPs could hitchhike NEs to enhance tumor accumulation, PDT-treated mice received OMV@PCB NPs and PCB NPs treatment as described in Fig. 3E, blood and tumor were collected at the predetermined time for FCM analysis. Excitingly, the proportion of Ce6+ Ly6G+ NEs in OMV@PCB NPs was 1.84- and 1.44-fold higher than that in PCB NPs in serum and tumor, respectively (Fig. 3F and G), suggesting a significant ability of OMV-coating NPs to hitchhike NEs. Alternatively, the in vivo NEs depletion experiment was further performed. Mice were pretreated with anti-Ly6G via intraperitoneal injection for 24 h to deplete neutrophil. Subsequently, OMV@PCB NPs were administered following the protocol outlined in Fig. 3E, and tumor accumulation was monitored at different time points. As shown in Fig. S6, pretreatment with anti-Ly6G resulted in significantly reduced accumulation of OMV@PCB NPs in tumor tissues, as indicated by weak fluorescence signals within the tumor region. This phenomenon might be attributed to the antibody-induced depletion of NEs, which hindered the ability of OMV@PCB NPs to hitchhike NEs for tumor accumulation.

To further assess the tumor accumulation of NPs, mice were treated with different NPs as above, and monitored at various time points using in vivo imaging. Based on the remarkable effect of OMV-coating NPs hitchhiking NEs, the fluorescence intensity of tumor in OMV@PCB NPs (w/o L) was higher than that in PCB NPs (w/o L) (Fig. 3H and Fig. S7). Surprisingly, the fluorescence intensity of OMV@PCB NPs-treated tumor with light irradiation was significantly higher than without light irradiation, reaching a peak at 8 h post-second injection. This enhancement was attributed to PDT-induced inflammation promoting NEs recruitment to tumors, thus increasing tumor accumulation through NEs-hitchhiking delivery. Moreover, ex vivo imaging of tumors and major organs further supported these findings. In line with above results, extensive fluorescence intensity was demonstrated in tumor treated with OMV@PCB NPs (w/L) compared to other control groups (Fig. 3I and J), indicating that PDT could assist in the tumor accumulation of OMV@PCB NPs via NEs-hitchhiking delivery.

Intercellular transportation in vitro and in vivo

To investigate whether OMV@PCB NPs could be transferred from NEs to tumor cells, NEs containing NPs were co-cultured with 4T1 cells for 8 h under PMA condition prior to CLSM observation. As shown in Fig. 4A, OMV@PCB NPs remained predominantly within NEs rather than being internalized by 4T1 cells, as evidenced by the weak Ce6 fluorescence observed in 4T1 cells. In contrast, under PMA stimulation, PMA-induced inflammatory conditions significantly increased NETs generation compared to controls (Fig. S8). Moreover, OMV@PCB NPs were effectively transferred from NEs to 4T1 cells. This phenomenon might be attributed to PMA mimicking inflammatory factors and inducing the formation of NETs, leading to the disintegration of NE membrane to release NPs.

Alternatively, FCM analysis was used to assess whether NPs could co-localize within tumor cells after NEs infiltration into tumor tissues. As shown in Fig. 4B, the proportion of NEs containing OMV@PCB NPs decreased by 12.9%, suggesting that PDT induced effective cargo release from NEs. Antitumor efficacy in vitro

In order to further evaluate the antitumor effect and biosafety, the cytotoxicity of PNPs, OMV@PNPs was first assessed. After a 24-h incubation with PNPs and OMV@PNPs, cell viability remained > 90% at the OMV@PNPs concentration of 20 µg/mL (Fig. S9), indicating excellent in vitro safety. Additionally, 4T1 cells treated with different formulations were irradiated before antitumor evaluation. As shown in Fig. S10, siginificant green fluorescence was distributed in OMV@PCB NPs-treated cells under light irradiation, indicating extensive ROS generation. Additionally, the combination index (CI) between Ce6 and SB525334 was < 1, suggesting that OMV@PCB NPs had obvious synergistic antitumor efficacy (Fig. 4E and Table S5). To assess dead and live cells, 4T1 cells treated with NPs were stained with calcein-AM and PI. Notably, strong red fluorescence (PI) was appeared in OMV@PCB group but not in the other controls (Fig. 4F), suggesting the substantial therapeutic effect of OMV@PCB NPs. Consistent with these findings, cell apoptosis analysis further revealed that OMV@PCB NPs induced maximum level of cell apoptosis (37.3%), significantly higher than OMV@PC NPs (16.9%), OMV@PB NPs (19.5%), and PCB NPs (21.5%) (Fig. 4G). These results collectively indicated that OMV@PCB NPs had excellent in vitro antitumor efficacy.

Antitumor efficacy in vivo

To evaluate the in vivo antitumor effect of various NPs, 4T1 tumor-bearing mice were subjected to different treatment regimens as outlined in Fig. 5A. Tumor volume and body weight were measured every other day. As shown in Fig. 5B-D, OMV@PC, OMV@PB and PCB NPs exhibited only partial inhibition of tumor growth. In comparison, OMV@PCB NPs exhibited significant tumor regression for up to 30 days, with a 2.0-22.2 times higher tumor growth inhibition (TGI) rate compared to other groups. This enhanced efficacy was attributed to substantial tumor accumulation and synergistic PDT-immunotherapy effects. In addition, OMV@PCB NPs groups achieved the highest survival rate during 50-d observation period (Fig. 5E), and induced significantly more cell death and apoptosis than other groups, as evidenced by H&E and TUNEL staining (Fig. 5F). These finding collectively indicated a marked inhibition of tumor growth.

Mechanism of NEs polarization

SB525334, a TGF-β inhibitor, has been shown to shift NEs towards anti-tumor N1 phenotype, thereby restraining tumor growth (Fig. 6A) [13, 21]. To investigate whether OMV@PCB NPs could inhibit TGF-β signaling, tumors were collected on day 14 following the in vivo anticancer treatment protocol, and the expression of TGF-β pathway components was determined by Western blot. Compared with other control groups, OMV@PCB NPs significantly reduced the phosphorylation of SMAD2, a downstream target of TGF-β (Fig. 6B), suggesting that OMV@PCB NPs could effectively inhibit TGF-β expression.

To evaluate the polarization of NEs to anti-tumor N1 phenotype, the collected tumors were dissociated into single-cell suspension and subjected to FCM analysis. Excitingly, the proportion of CD54+ and CD95+ NEs in OMV@PCB-treated tumors was 7.1- and 4.7-fold higher, respectively than that in PBS-treated tumors (Fig. 6C and D), suggesting an increased presence of N1 phenotype NEs. Moreover, qRT-PCR analysis further revealed that OMV@PCB NPs showed a 2.8- to 2.2-fold increase in the expression level of N1 phenotype marker (Fas, CCL3), and a 14.7- to 24.8-fold decrease in the expression level of N2 phenotype marker (CCL2, CCL5) compared to PBS controls (Fig. 6E). These findings collectively demonstrated that OMV@PCB NPs effectively reversed NEs phenotype and promoted their polarization towards the anti-tumor N1 phenotype.

NEs-released ELANE, could not only proteolytically release the death domain of CD95 to interact with histone H1.0 isoforms, but also initiate the apoptotic pathway in the tumor cells, resulting in selective inhibition of tumor growth [16]. To further explore the expression level of ELANE and histone H1.0, tumors treated with different NPs were analyzed by immunohistochemical staining. As shown in Fig. 6F, a higher level of ELANE and histone H1.0 was found in OMV@PCB group but not in other groups, as evidenced by strong positive signals distributed throughout the tumor tissues. Consistent with these results, OMV@PCB NPs also induced maximum cell apoptosis compared to controls (Fig. 5F). Collectively, these results indicated that OMV@PCB NPs effectively mediated the polarization of NEs towards N1 phenotype by inhibiting TGF-β expression, consequently leading to the efficient elimination of tumor cells through ELANE release.

OMV@PCB NPs-enhanced anticancer immunity

In the TME enriched with TGF-β, NEs predominantly exhibit N2 phenotypes. These N2 NEs facilitate tumor progression through increased secretion of cytokines such as matrix metalloproteinase 9 (MMP9) and prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), while simultaneously suppressing immune responses by recruiting immunosuppressive cells like regulatory T (Treg) cells into the TME and upregulating immune checkpoints such as PD-L1 [55]. To evaluate the reversal of immunosuppression mediated by OMV@PCB NPs, 4T1 tumor-bearing mice were treated according to the protocol outlined in Fig. 5A. FCM analysis revealed that the proportion of CD11c+ CD80+ and CD11c+ CD86+ DCs in OMV@PCB groups was 8.0- and 3.5-fold higher, respectively, compared to the PBS group (Fig. 7A, B and S12). This suggested that OMV@PCB NPs contributed to DCs maturation, thus promoting T cells activation and CD8+ T cells infiltration. In addition, the proportion of CD3+ CD4+ T cells (12.6%) and CD3+ CD8+ T cells (40.4%) in OMV@PCB group was significantly elevated, being 3.3- and 16.2-fold higher than that in the PBS group, respectively (Fig. 7C, D and S13). This indicated that OMV@PCB NPs could enhance T cells infiltration, thus boosting immune responses within TME. Consistent with these findings, ELISA results demonstrated a significant increase in cytokine level of IL-6, IL-12, TNF-α and IFN-γ, ranging from 1.5- to 13.8-fold higher in OMV@PCB group compared to PBS group (Fig. 7E). Additionally, the proportion of immunosuppressive cells, including Treg cells, myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) were markedly reduced in OMV@PCB group compared to the PBS group (Fig. 7F, G, S14 and S15). Taken together, these results indicated that OMV@PCB NPs effectively mitigated the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, consequently amplifying immunotherapy effect.

Conclusion

In summary, we successfully developed a novel OMVs-based nano-regulator, designated as OMV@PCB NPs, which integrated OMVs-camouflaged PLGA NPs encapsulating the TGF-β inhibitor (SB525334) and photosensitizer (Ce6). This formulation enabled effective tumor accumulation and immuno-photodynamic therapy against tumor growth. Leveraging the inherent characteristics of OMVs, OMV@PCB NPs could be effectively recognized by NEs, facilitating NEs-mediated hitchhiking delivery while exhibiting adjuvant properties to stimulate immune activation. Moreover, OMV@PCB NPs not only eliminated cancer cells through ROS generation following PDT treatment, but also amplified inflammatory signals to recruit NEs towards tumor sites, thereby enhancing tumor accumulation via NEs hitchhiking. It was worth noting that the delivered SB525334 could significantly regulate NEs polarization towards anti-tumor N1 phenotype by inhibiting TGF-β signaling in tumor tissues. This dual action selectively killed tumor cells via ELANE generation, and reversed the immunosuppressive TME to boost anticancer immunity. Consistent in vitro and in vivo results demonstrated that OMV@PCB NPs possessed superior safety and immune-photodynamic therapy compared to other formulations for suppressing tumor growth. This research offers a potential approach for efficient cancer treatment.
