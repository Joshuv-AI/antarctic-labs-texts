Impact of twice‐daily budesonide foam administration on early clinical response and endoscopic remission in patients with ulcerative colitis: a post hoc analysis
Authors: Kenji Watanabe, Fumihito Hirai, Kiyonori Kobayashi, Ken Takeuchi, Shinsuke Kurosu, Katsutoshi Inagaki, Ken‐ichi Iwayama, Makoto Naganuma
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimEarly treatment response of ulcerative colitis (UC) symptom resolution is desirable. This post hoc analysis evaluated efficacy outcomes, including endoscopic remission, by responder status and the influence of once‐daily (QD) versus twice‐daily (BID) budesonide foam dosing in patients with UC.MethodsData were pooled from phase 2 and phase 3 clinical trials of budesonide rectal foam QD or BID or placebo for up to 12 weeks. Outcomes were evaluated by treatment and budesonide administration regimen and by responder group: early (rectal bleeding subscore [RBS] 0 from Week 2 through Week 6), delayed (RBS 0 at Week 6), and nonresponder (RBS > 0 at Week 6).ResultsThe main analysis set included 55 (QD) and 120 (BID) budesonide‐treated patients and 116 placebo‐treated patients. At Week 6, the trend in early response rate was significant among treatment groups (BID, 45.3%; QD, 32.1%; placebo, 12.8%; P < 0.0001). Among BID recipients, trends for complete endoscopic remission rate (Mayo endoscopic score [MES] = 0) and endoscopic remission rate (MES = 0 or 1) were significant among responder status groups (early responder, 67.4% and 95.4%, respectively; delayed responder, 48.1% and 85.2%; nonresponder, 24.0% and 64.0%; P < 0.001 for both). Regardless of the administration regimen, most early responders achieved endoscopic remission at Week 6. Among responder status groups, early responders' cumulative non‐relapse period was greatest (P = 0.07).ConclusionA BID budesonide administration regimen is preferred to increase the probability of early response and, following endoscopic remission, a better prognosis after stopping treatment.

Introduction

Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a nonspecific, chronic, immune‐mediated inflammatory disorder of the colonic mucosa. 1 , 2 , 3 UC follows a relapsing and remitting course; symptoms include mucous bloody stool and abdominal pain. In Northern Europe and North America, there are approximately 9–20 new cases of UC per 100 000 persons per year, and the prevalence is reported to be between 156 and 291 cases per 100 000 persons per year. 4 The incidence and prevalence of UC are reportedly increasing in Asia. 5 The initial goal of treatment is prompt symptom control in the active phase of the disease with the induction of a sustained and durable period of steroid‐free remission and the achievement of an adequate health‐related quality of life. 2 , 3 , 6 An ideal treatment goal is the achievement of complete endoscopic remission, which is indicated by a Mayo endoscopic subscore (MES) of 0, representing normal/inactive disease. 3 , 7 An understanding of the most effective treatment strategies is necessary to achieve this goal.

Current treatment guidelines recommend monotherapy or various combinations of rectal or oral 5‐acetylsalicylic acid (ASA), 5‐ASA enemas, oral or rectal steroids, biologics, or Janus kinase inhibitors, depending on the activity level and location of UC within the colon. 3 Patients who fail to respond to these treatments may be treated with alternative therapies. Topical treatment for UC (e.g. 5‐ASA or steroids) is highly effective and reduces the symptoms that may be experienced with oral treatment. 8

Budesonide foam is a topical synthetic steroid with a highly potent anti‐inflammatory effect at the administration site. 9 Systemic glucocorticoid‐related adverse events occur less frequently with budesonide foam versus other corticosteroids. 10 , 11 , 12 In 2017, budesonide foam was approved in Japan for treating patients with mild‐to‐moderate UC based on the findings from phase 2 13 and phase 3 14 studies conducted on Japanese patients. In the phase 2 trial, patients were treated with budesonide foam once daily (QD) or twice daily (BID). 13 Efficacy findings indicated that BID administration had a potential benefit over QD dosing for improving colonic inflammation. The ESCORT study, a post hoc analysis of phase 2 and phase 3 data, suggested a better median survival without relapse with BID versus QD dosing (1.03 vs 0.54 years); however, this difference was not statistically significant. 14

Some patients respond to budesonide foam treatment early in treatment, while others have a delayed response in daily clinical practice. This post hoc analysis aimed to evaluate efficacy outcomes by response group (early responders, delayed responders, and nonresponders) and the influence of QD versus BID dosing in patients with UC treated with budesonide foam.

Methods

Study design and patients

This post hoc analysis included pooled data from one phase 2 and one phase 3 study, the primary results of which have been previously reported. 13 , 15 The phase 2 (JapicCTI‐132294) and phase 3 (JapicCTI‐142704) studies were multicenter, randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled studies conducted in Japan that enrolled patients aged ≥16 years with active UC. A modified Mayo Disease Activity Index (MMDAI), 16 which omitted friability from the endoscopic subscore definition, was used to assess disease activity. Variables included in the MMDAI were bowel frequency, rectal bleeding, physician's global assessment, and colonoscopic findings. 17 Enrolled patients had an endoscopic subscore of 2, a rectal bleeding subscore (RBS) of 1 or 2, a stool frequency subscore of 0–2, and a confirmed UC diagnosis for ≥12 weeks. The following concomitant medications were permitted at stable doses: oral 5‐ASA agents and oral salazosulfapyridine agents. Salazosulfapyridine suppositories, 5‐ASA enema or suppositories, biologics including antitumor necrosis factor antibody (from 12 weeks before eligibility verification), cytapheresis (from 4 weeks before eligibility verification), systemic or topical corticosteroids (from 8 weeks before eligibility verification), immunomodulators (from 12 weeks before eligibility verification), and surgical treatment for UC were prohibited. Patients with irritable bowel syndrome, a history of colon resection, a plasma cortisol level <4.0 μg/dL (phase 2 study) or <6.2 μg/dL (phase 3 study), or an intolerance/allergic reaction to budesonide were excluded.

Patients were treated with budesonide foam QD (phase 2 study) or BID (phase 2 and phase 3 studies) or with placebo foam for 6 weeks, with continuation up to Week 12 in phase 3 trial participants with an endoscopic subscore of 1 at Week 6. In the QD group, placebo foam was administered in the morning, and budesonide foam (2 mg/25 mL) was administered in the evening. In the BID and placebo groups, budesonide foam (2 mg/25 mL) or placebo foam was administered in the morning and evening. Efficacy was evaluated at baseline (Week 0) and Weeks 2, 4, 6, and 12 (when data were available) or at study withdrawal. Colonoscopy and symptom diary data assessed the MMDAI score at Weeks 0 and 6 or 12. The other ESCORT study (UMIN 000031983) observed the time to relapse among patients who achieved remission in the phase 2 and 3 studies. 14

ESCORT study participants included 84 patients (QD, 29; BID, 55) who achieved remission induction from phase 2 and 3 studies (excluding placebo). Additionally, 24 patients (QD, 11; BID, 13) were excluded because data could not be collected from four non‐participating sites. Therefore, a post hoc non‐relapse analysis was performed by analyses of these 60 patients (QD, 18; BID, 42) (Fig. 1).

Post hoc analysis

All patients included in the statistical analysis for the phase 2 and phase 3 studies were included in this post hoc analysis. Data were pooled for up to 12 weeks and analyzed by responder category. Early responders were defined as patients who achieved an RBS of 0 at 2 weeks of treatment and whose RBS remained 0 throughout the 6 weeks. Delayed responders were defined as patients who achieved an RBS of 0 at 6 weeks (excluding early responders). Nonresponders were defined as patients who did not achieve an RBS of 0 at 6 weeks.

In this combined analysis, clinical remission was defined as a rectal bleeding subscore of 0, an endoscopic subscore ≤1, and a stool frequency subscore of 0 or a decrease of ≥1 in the stool frequency subscore from baseline. Relapse was defined as the onset of rectal bleeding, excluding hematochezia due to infectious enteritis, or the start of treatment with induction therapy for UC.

Responder rates by treatment and budesonide foam administration regimen (QD or BID; active treatment groups only) were evaluated, as were the rates of endoscopic improvement by responder category, treatment group, and budesonide foam administration regimen; non‐relapse by responder category for all patients in the active treatment group and according to budesonide foam administration; changes in fecal calprotectin levels by responder category for all patients in the active treatment group and according to budesonide foam administration; and the correlation of treatment duration and time to relapse. Patients were followed up for 2 years.

Statistical methods

The post hoc analysis set included all patients who participated in the study, provided informed consent, and had data during the observation period. The post hoc non‐relapse analysis set included all patients who met the inclusion criteria of this post hoc analysis and were not excluded (i.e. lack of participation in the study). Differences in baseline demographic and clinical characteristics among treatment groups were evaluated using the Kruskal–Wallis, chi‐square, or Fisher's exact tests. For responder rates and achievement rates, comparisons between groups were assessed using the chi‐square test or Student's t‐test, and trends among groups were examined using the Cochran–Armitage trend test. Time to relapse was analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method, and between‐group comparisons were made using a log‐rank test. Fecal calprotectin levels were log‐transformed and shown by group means of difference from baseline; trend among groups was assessed using the Jonckheere–Terpstra trend test. Correlation between time to relapse and treatment duration was represented by Pearson's correlation coefficient. The significance level (two‐sided) was set at 0.05. Statistical analyses were conducted using SAS Version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Patients

Overall, 55 patients were treated with budesonide foam QD, 120 with budesonide BID, and 116 with placebo (post hoc analysis set) (Fig. 1). As previously mentioned, of those treated with budesonide foam, 26 and 65 in the QD and BID groups, respectively, did not meet the inclusion criteria for the present analysis. A further 11 and 13 patients, respectively, were excluded from the non‐relapse analysis because of a lack of participation in the study. The non‐relapse analysis set included 18 patients in the QD group and 42 patients in the BID group. Baseline demographics and clinical characteristics were not significantly different among the treatment groups (post hoc analysis set) (Table 1). No patients were steroid‐refractory or dependent. The proportion of male and female patients was similar; most patients had no smoking history, and most had recurrent UC at the time of enrollment.

Early or delayed responder rates by administration regimen at Week 6

At Week 6, there was a significantly higher proportion of early responders with budesonide foam QD (32.1%; 17/53) or BID (45.3%; 43/95) versus placebo (12.8%; 12/94) (P = 0.005 or P < 0.001, respectively); the difference between the QD and BID groups was not significant (P = 0.117) (Fig. 2a). The trend among the three groups was significant (Cochran–Armitage, P < 0.0001).

At Week 6, the proportion of delayed responders was numerically higher or similar with budesonide foam QD (39.6%; 21/53) or BID (28.4%; 27/95), respectively, versus placebo (26.6%; 25/94), though the differences were not significant (P = 0.102 or P = 0.779, respectively); the proportion of delayed responders was numerically higher with QD versus BID administration (P = 0.163) (Fig. 2b).

In all patients treated with budesonide foam, rates of complete endoscopic remission and endoscopic remission at Week 6 were significantly higher for early responders versus nonresponders (65.0% [39/60] vs 20.0% [8/40], P < 0.0001, and 95.0% [57/60] vs 52.5% [21/40], P < 0.0001, respectively) (Fig. 3a,b). Among patients who received QD administration, rates of complete endoscopic remission (MES = 0) and endoscopic remission (MES = 0 or 1) at Week 6 were significantly higher for early responders versus nonresponders (58.8% [10/17] vs 13.3% [2/15], P < 0.01 and 94.1% [16/17] vs 33.3% [5/15], P < 0.001, respectively), and for endoscopic remission with delayed responders versus nonresponders (85.7% [18/21] vs 33.3% [5/15], P = 0.02) (Fig. 3c,d). Rates of complete endoscopic remission at Week 6 were numerically higher for early responders versus delayed responders (58.8% [10/17] vs 38.1% [8/21], P = 0.20) and for delayed responders versus nonresponders (38.1% [8/21] vs 13.3% [2/15], P = 0.10). The trend among the groups was significant for both complete endoscopic remission (Cochran–Armitage, P < 0.01) and endoscopic remission (Cochran–Armitage, P < 0.0001).

For patients in the BID group, rates of complete endoscopic remission and endoscopic remission at Week 6 were significantly higher for early responders versus nonresponders (67.4% [29/43] vs 24.0% [6/25], P < 0.001 and 95.3% [41/43] vs 64.0% [16/25], P < 0.001, respectively) (Fig. 3e,f). Numerically higher rates of complete endoscopic remission and endoscopic remission were observed for early responders versus delayed responders (67.4% [29/43] vs 48.1% [13/27], P = 0.11 and 95.3% [41/43] vs 85.2% [23/27], P = 0.14, respectively) and delayed responders versus nonresponders (48.1% [13/27] vs 24.0% [6/25], P = 0.07 and 85.2% [23/27] vs 64.0% [16/25], P = 0.08, respectively). The trend among the groups was significant for both complete endoscopic remission and endoscopic remission (Cochran–Armitage, P < 0.001 for both).

Regardless of the budesonide foam administration regimen (QD or BID), most early responders achieved endoscopic remission and over half achieved complete endoscopic remission at Week 6.

Cumulative relapse rate up to Week 12

The cumulative non‐relapse period was numerically greatest for early responders, followed by delayed responders and nonresponders (log‐rank test, P = 0.07) (Fig. 4a). A similar percentage of patients in each responder group relapsed immediately after stopping treatment; however, following recovery from the initial relapse, early responders were less likely to relapse than the other responder groups. The rate of patients without relapse at 1 year was 56.2% for early responders (median survival time without relapse, 450.0 [95% CI, 167.0–721.0] days) (n = 32), 36.1% for delayed responders (275.0 [95% CI 113.0–548.0] days) (n = 22), and 16.7% for nonresponders (222.5 [95% CI, 19.0–infinity] days) (n = 6). Non‐relapse rates were significantly greater for early versus delayed responders with QD dosing (P = 0.04) (Fig. 4b) and numerically greater with BID dosing (P = 0.09) (Fig. 4c). The rate of patients without relapse at 1 year was 66.7% for early responders (median survival time without relapse, 450.0 [95% CI, 42.0–infinity] days) (n = 9) and 13.0% for delayed responders (113.0 [95% CI, 20.0–196.0] days) (n = 9) for the QD group and 52.2% (554.0 [95% CI,105.0–721.0] days) (n = 23), 49.9% (335.0 [95% CI, 231.0–556.0] days) (n = 13), and 16.7% (222.5 [95% CI,19.0–infinity] days) (n = 6) for early, delayed, and nonresponders, respectively, for the BID group.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the efficacy and outcomes of budesonide foam treatment in patients with UC according to whether they were early or delayed responders. A BID administration regimen was most effective for achieving early responder status at Week 6; the trend among treatment groups was significant. Early responders were most likely to achieve both endoscopic remission and complete endoscopic remission at Week 6; trends with delayed responders and nonresponders were significant. Most early responders achieved endoscopic remission at Week 6 regardless of administration regimen. Additionally, following budesonide foam withdrawal, early responders exhibited the highest cumulative relapse‐free period compared with delayed responders and nonresponders. There was no correlation between treatment duration and time to relapse after withdrawing. Considering biomarkers, early responders had the greatest reduction in fecal calprotectin titer compared with delayed responders or nonresponders; the trend was significant. Incidentally, a recent post hoc analysis of the VARSITY study reported that vedolizumab early responders were more likely to achieve histoendoscopic mucosal improvement (MES ≤ 1 and Geboes score highest grade <3.2) at Week 52 compared with delayed responders; no difference was observed between early and delayed responders to adalimumab in patients with UC. 18 This highlights the importance of performing individual analyses for early and delayed responders with each drug.

Topical therapy is an important treatment option for patients with UC who experience insufficient efficacy with other treatments. Updated recommendations for treat‐to‐target strategies for patients with UC have identified a short‐term target of symptomatic relief, including bowel urgency. 19 Budesonide foam is effective for improving urgency. 20 While histological remission is an ideal goal, endoscopic remission is the long‐term treatment goal in daily clinical practice using a treat‐to‐target strategy. 19 The efficacy of BID topical budesonide foam for complete endoscopic remission has been demonstrated previously. 15

Another important short‐term target is earlier improvement of UC symptoms, including rectal bleeding. When treating with budesonide foam, a BID administration regimen should be used to achieve an earlier treatment response, fecal calprotectin reduction, and endoscopic remission. Hibi et al. reported that normal stool frequency at baseline, history of 5‐ASA topical treatment, and elimination of rectal bleeding after Week 2 are predictors of complete endoscopic remission at Week 6 with budesonide foam BID administration. 21

A concern with budesonide foam treatment is relapse following withdrawal. We previously reported that cumulative remission rates at 6 and 12 months were 64.5% and 45.5% after budesonide foam withdrawal. 14 In the present post hoc analysis, early responders were less likely to relapse following withdrawal compared with delayed responders. This result suggests that cases in which an early response was obtained with BID administration may be less likely to relapse even if budesonide foam is discontinued after 6 weeks. Although there was a significant difference in the duration of treatment among treatment regimens, there was no correlation between duration of treatment and time to relapse (Fig. 6). Therefore, we believe that the duration of treatment does not influence the higher achievement of early responders in the BID group.

Interestingly, there was a difference in the QD and BID administration regimens in endoscopic remission rates for nonresponders at Week 6 (33.3% vs 64.0%). The endoscopic remission rate was higher in the BID group than QD group even in patients who did not achieve the disappearance of bloody stools at 6 weeks, suggesting that BID administration of budesonide for active UC is meaningful in clinical practice.

This study was limited in that it was a post hoc analysis. Given that the statistical analysis was not hypothesis testing but rather an estimation, the results should be interpreted with caution. Additionally, this study only included Japanese patients, potentially limiting the generalizability of the findings. Histological assessment was lacking; however, a central reading of the endoscopic conclusions by three experts for UC and colonoscopy was performed to assess the endoscopic efficacy.

In conclusion, a BID budesonide foam administration regimen is preferred over QD administration to increase the probability of early response, fecal calprotectin reduction, endoscopic remission, and a more extended relapse‐free period following drug withdrawal. These data can aid clinicians in making treatment decisions when considering budesonide foam for patients with UC.
