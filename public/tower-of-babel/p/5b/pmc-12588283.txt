Charting the nanotopography of inner hair cell synapses using MINFLUX nanoscopy
Authors: Rohan Kapoor, Hyojin Kim, Evelyn Garlick, Maria Augusta do R. B. F. Lima, Klara Esch, Torben Ruhwedel, Wiebke Möbius, Fred Wolf, Tobias Moser
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The cochlea encodes sounds into neural signals at the synapses of inner hair cells (IHCs) and spiral ganglion neurons (SGNs) with remarkable fidelity. To achieve high rates of precise synaptic transmission over long periods, IHCs use ribbon-type active zones (AZs). To understand synaptic sound encoding, we need to decipher the underlying molecular topography of these synapses, which has remained challenging because of technological limitations. Here, we applied three-dimensional minimal flux optical nanoscopy to mouse IHC-SGN synapses to chart the positions of key pre- and postsynaptic proteins with single-digit nanometer resolution. We demonstrate that nanoclusters of ion channels and their interacting proteins govern the topography of AZs and postsynaptic densities (PSDs). We count synaptic proteins and their nanoclusters and determine their spatial organization, feeding into computational modeling of AZ function. In conclusion, this study reveals a nanocluster-based molecular AZ and PSD topography, likely serving as functional modules in synaptic sound encoding.

INTRODUCTION

Inner hair cells (IHCs) of the cochlea, analogous to retinal photoreceptors, form specialized synapses that are controlled by graded receptor potentials, providing greater bandwidth for relaying sensory information than achievable by all-or-none action potentials [for review, see (1)]. The hallmark of these synapses are the eponymous synaptic ribbons—electron dense structures that tether synaptic vesicles (SVs) in vicinity of the presynaptic active zone (AZ) thought to enable fast, precise, and tireless neurotransmission at these synapses. Synaptic ribbons represent the most elaborate specializations among other presynaptic electron-dense projections that tether SVs, which include the small presynaptic grids, found at vertebrate central nervous system (CNS) synapses and T bars in Drosophila [for review, see (2)]. IHC synaptic ribbons are typically ellipsoidal, round, or wedge-like in shape, ranging between 100 and 500 nm along their maximal extent, about three times smaller than in retinal rod photoreceptors (3). The protein RIBEYE is the core component of all ribbons (4–7) and has been proposed to self-assemble into a multilamellar structure, which constitutes the ribbon scaffold (8), along with the protein Piccolino (9–11).

Over the decades, electron microscopy (EM) and tomography have been extensively used to study the architecture of these specialized synapses, particularly shedding light on the abundance, arrangement, and tethering of SVs (12–17). On the other hand, light microscopy, particularly super-resolution stimulated emission depletion (STED) microscopy, has provided high-resolution insights into synaptic assemblies of key molecular components, including AZ proteins such as Bassoon (14, 18), Rab3 interacting molecule 2 (RIM2) (19), RIM-binding protein 2 (RIM-BP2 or RBP2) (20), and CaV1.3 (voltage-gated L-type calcium channel subunit α1D) Ca2+ channels (18, 21). CaV1.3 channels have been shown to cluster at the presynaptic AZ, forming, on average, a 400-nm-long narrow stripe at 60% of the synapses (6, 11, 18, 20).

However, methods used thus far could not answer key remaining questions: How does the arrangement of CaV1.3 channels relate to vesicular release sites? How is the topography of CaV1.3 channels and AZ components? Are the half-micrometer-long clusters of CaV1.3 channels and other AZ proteins (“microclusters”) further composed of smaller “nanoclusters”? These nanoclusters were shown by STORM (stochastic optical reconstruction microscopy) imaging for the multidomain AZ protein Munc13-1 (mammalian uncoordinated 13-1) at conventional CNS synapses and were interpreted as molecular correlates of SV release sites (22). Moreover, studies at CNS synapses have shown nanoclusters of presynaptic AZ components to be tightly aligned with postsynaptic scaffold proteins and receptor nanoclusters, suggesting transsynaptic “nanocolumns” that may contribute to synaptic efficiency at these synapses (23). Last, how RIBEYE and Piccolino assemble within the synaptic ribbon remains an important open question.

Several arrangements of CaV channels and vesicular release sites have been suggested for different synapses, including random positioning of Ca2+ channels and SVs (24), Ca2+ channels forming a ring around SVs (25), positioning of SVs at the perimeter of Ca2+ channel clusters (26, 27), a one-to-one stoichiometry of SV and CaV channel (28–33), or an exclusion zone model where Ca2+ channels do not cluster but are simply excluded from a 50-nm zone around docked SVs (34). A paradigmatic topography of Ca2+ channels and SV release sites supporting one-to-one stoichiometry is the longitudinal array of functional units suggested on the basis of studies of the neuromuscular junction (30) and the rod ribbon synapse (33). SV release at mature IHC ribbon synapses seems to be governed by one to three Ca2+ channels exerting a Ca2+ nanodomain–like control (14, 15, 21, 35–39). The effective coupling distance between Ca2+ channels and the Ca2+ sensor for SV exocytosis at the IHC AZ has been estimated to be within 17 nm (37). This results in a near linear dependence of release on the number of open CaV channels offering a wide dynamic range of the synaptic transfer function that tracks the open probability of CaV1.3 channels for optimal control of sound intensity coding by the IHC receptor potential [e.g., (38–40)]. Moreover, a tight channel-vesicle coupling places the low-affinity Ca2+ sensor of exocytosis (41), most likely otoferlin (42–44), into the saturating range, thereby reducing synaptic delay and its variance for fast and temporally precise neurotransmission (40). Biophysical modeling indicates that a subset of Ca2+ channels at the periphery of the typically stripe-like microcluster of the AZ exert a Ca2+ nanodomain control akin to “private channels” of the release site (21, 37). However, all this remains inference from indirect approaches, and the precise topography of Ca2+ channels and SV release sites remains to be elucidated.

Advances in minimal fluorescence photon fluxes (MINFLUX) nanoscopy (45, 46) have now made it possible to investigate the spatial arrangement of multiple proteins with fluorescence microscopy at a molecular-level spatial resolution in three dimensions, offering to bridge conventional electron and light microscopy techniques with molecular specificity. Despite this, routine application of MINFLUX has used cultured cells, and its utility has remained more limited for native tissues. This is primarily due to (i) high sensitivity of MINFLUX to photons emerging from background signal, which is far more pronounced in tissue samples owing to autofluorescence caused by chemical fixation, out-of-focus signal, and general “stickiness” of tissue samples to adsorb primary and secondary antibodies nonspecifically; and (ii) spherical aberrations and refractive index mismatch in the tissue, which progressively worsens imaging conditions if the structure of interest is away from the coverslip and deeper into the tissue. Here, we have established MINFLUX imaging of 1-μm semithin sections from Epon-embedded mouse cochleae, which were etched before immunostaining. This allowed for convenient, reproducible, and high-yielding tissue sample preparation for MINFLUX, with synaptic structures immobilized close to the coverslip allowing minimal spherical aberrations and fluorescence background.

Using MINFLUX imaging of synapses in these semithin sections, we have mapped the distribution of proteins of the synaptic ribbon (RIBEYE and Piccolino) and of the AZ proper (CaV1.3, RBP2, and RIM2) and the postsynapse of spiral ganglion neurons (SGNs; Homer1 and GluA2) to reveal their three-dimensional (3D) spatial arrangement. We further quantified the data to deduce “molecular counts” of synaptic proteins and observed that the spatial arrangement of most of these proteins follows a nanocluster-based molecular organization. Biophysical modeling indicates that such a nanocluster-based organization may support the high efficiency of synaptic transmission at IHC-SGN ribbon synapses.

RESULTS

Immunolabeled 1-μm sections for MINFLUX imaging of cochlear synapses

MINFLUX allows rapid imaging at molecular-level spatial resolution (1 to 3 nm) (33, 45). Tissues of varying thicknesses and complexities do not match well with the high sensitivity of single-molecule localizations due to background fluorescence and spherical aberrations. Recently, MINFLUX was used to study the nanoarchitecture of the photoreceptor ribbon synapse by developing heat-assisted rapid dehydration (HARD), which allows immobilization of a thin layer of retinal cells on the coverslip (33). However, the tissue complexity of the cochlea with its osseous labyrinth, spacious cavities, and delicate organ of Corti, the sensory epithelium, made it challenging for us to adapt HARD for studying hair cell synapses that were often at a distance of >3 μm from the coverslip (fig. S1, A to D). We also attempted using cochlear cryosections of 10 to 16 μm in thickness (fig. S1, E to I). Treating the samples using 0.1% NaBH4 solution or 0.1 M tris before blocking and immunostaining markedly quenched tissue autofluorescence and allowed us to obtain an improved signal-to-noise ratio of immunolabeled ribbon synapses. While we could perform preliminary MINFLUX imaging in these sections, the quality still remained inadequate because of high photon emissions from the tissue background. We refrained from using paraffin-embedded sections, which often have limited epitope availability for antibody binding.

Instead, several previous studies have shown how embedding tissue samples in epoxy resin followed by sectioning and etching the resin can help overcome this problem and allow nanometer sections with largely preserved labeling efficiency (47–49). We obtained sections of 0.3 to 1 μm in thickness from chemically fixed, decalcified cochleae, which were embedded in Epon (see Fig. 1A). After sectioning, we removed Epon before immunostaining by etching the samples with NaOH in absolute ethanol (50). These samples were highly appropriate for MINFLUX imaging, as the minimal thickness of the tissue ensures low background and high signal quality, and the synapses were close to the coverslip. One-micrometer-thick sections turned out to be most suitable with homogenous antibody penetration and full enclosure of synapses, whereas thinner sections of 300 and 600 nm often only featured partial synaptic structures. Advantages of this sample preparation approach are simplicity, high reproducibility, and yield of hundreds of sections from a single cochlea. We could obtain good-quality staining for a majority of IHC markers, AZ proteins, synaptic ribbon markers, and postsynaptic proteins, as summarized in fig. S2A, with the exception of AZ protein Bassoon and the putative Ca2+ sensor of SV fusion otoferlin, for which we could obtain only faint signals with substantial background. Staining IHCs with antibodies directed against calretinin or vesicular glutamate transporter 3 (VGLUT3) allowed easy identification of IHCs in tissue sections for imaging (Fig. 1B). We also compared the immunofluorescence intensity of synaptic ribbons in these samples with those in acute preparations of the organ of Corti stained with the same antibody concentrations and imaged in parallel with the same laser power. We did not find any reduction in immunofluorescence intensity, implying that the Epon embedding and etching likely do not contribute to loss of epitopes (fig. S2B). Expectedly (20, 35), staining for CaV1.3 Ca2+ channels and RBP2 was not optimal with 4% formalin fixation but improved considerably with glyoxal fixation (51) and could then be used for MINFLUX imaging. In summary, we propose that using semithin sections of Epon-embedded tissue can provide a simple and highly adaptable method for routinely performing MINFLUX imaging, potentially also on different tissue types.

3D MINFLUX reveals IHC ribbons packed with RIBEYE molecules

We started with 3D MINFLUX imaging of the synaptic ribbon. We first used a primary antibody labeling the N-terminal A-domain of RIBEYE, the core scaffold protein of the ribbon, in 1-μm cochlear sections from 2-week-old C57BL6/J mice, followed by a secondary antibody conjugated to Alexa Fluor 647. We found the raw localization precision of the imaging to be between 3.9 and 5.9 nm in all three dimensions. Synaptic ribbons were ellipsoidal in shape and, on average, about 400 nm in length, 150 nm in width, and 200 nm in height (measures of an exemplary ribbon depicted in Fig. 1C). A comparison of synapse dimensions estimated by MINFLUX versus other imaging modalities has been provided in table S1. The topography of RIBEYE from MINFLUX data did not seem to indicate any regular, lamellar pattern of arrangement as has been reported to occasionally occur in electron micrographs of mature synaptic ribbons (3, 52, 53). We also encountered double ribbons seemingly from the same synapse as shown in Fig. 1C, which appear as one entity using confocal imaging but could be resolved well into two discernible synaptic ribbons using MINFLUX. Three of the six imaged ribbons appeared to have a somewhat hollow center, potentially corresponding to the translucent cores in otherwise electron-dense IHC ribbons of adult mice, found in previous EM studies (11, 52, 54–56). Alternatively, the finding might reflect reduced penetration of the antibody into a densely packed ribbon.

For quantification of the MINFLUX data, we used a custom-written analysis pipeline using the density-based spatial clustering of applications with noise (DBSCAN) clustering algorithm. The localization of an emitting molecule is performed by the microscope several times by repetition of the last two MINFLUX iterations. Since these successive localizations belong to the same blinking event or localization “burst,” they are assigned the same trace ID (TID). We used MINFLUX images filtered for quality parameters (see Materials and Methods) and determined the centroid of each TID (for TIDs with at least five localizations) by averaging coordinates of all localizations per TID. We then implemented DBSCAN clustering with minimum number of points = 1 and radius ε of cluster defined by the square root of sum of 2σ in each dimension (~5 nm) to obtain molecular counts or “units” (57); see Fig. 1D for schematic representation. These molecular counts represent the number of emitting molecules underlying different blinking events that localize at a distance greater than ε between each other, while minimizing the contribution from repeated localizations. Synaptic ribbons appeared to be packed with, on average, 103 ± 19 RIBEYE molecular counts per synapse (SD = 47, range = 45 to 159, N = 6 synapses) and average density per unit volume = 21,458.3 molecular counts/μm3, which is substantially lower than prior estimates for retinal bipolar neurons of goldfish obtained from peptide labeling (~4000 RIBEYE molecules per ribbon) (58). This might be largely due to technical differences, as the ribbon dimensions are similar (1, 59).

We also performed 3D MINFLUX on synaptic ribbons immunolabeled with an anti-CtBP2 (C-terminal binding protein 2) primary antibody, targeting the C-terminal RIBEYE B-domain. The topography, molecular counts of RIBEYE within the ribbons, and the size of the ribbon appeared comparable and did not seem to depend on whether the A-domain (N-terminal) or the B-domain (C-terminal) of RIBEYE was targeted (fig. S3, A to E).

Piccolino forms an arch around the membrane-distal synaptic ribbon

Piccolino (9) is a short ribbon synapse–specific splice variant of the multidomain AZ protein Piccolo, found in the cochlea and the retina. Piccolino appears to be distributed exclusively at the ribbon (10, 11, 54, 60, 61) and has been shown to interact with RIBEYE directly (10). We immunostained 1-μm semithin cochlear sections from p15 C57BL6/J mice with VGLUT3 (Alexa Fluor 488, labeling IHCs), CtBP2/RIBEYE (Alexa Fluor 541, labeling synaptic ribbons), and Piccolino (Alexa Fluor 647), using an antibody that labels not only Piccolino but also full-length Piccolo. Our attempts to perform two-color MINFLUX for Piccolino and CtBP2/RIBEYE did not work out well, and spectral separation of signals from the two proteins was unclear. To distinguish Piccolino at afferent ribbon synapses from full-length Piccolo at efferent synapses, formed by olivocochlear neurons onto SGN boutons, we imaged only Piccolino immunofluorescent puncta that colocalized with CtBP2/RIBEYE immunofluorescence observed at confocal resolution. 3D MINFLUX revealed Piccolino to be distributed over the synaptic ribbon in the form of an arch, presumably enveloping the membrane-distal (apical) circumference of the ribbon (fig. S4, A and B) with approximate dimensions of 400, 120, and 200 nm in length, width, and height, respectively. This is also in agreement with previous immunogold EM and STED microscopy data that have shown Piccolino to be similarly distributed along the membrane-distal aspect of the IHC ribbon (11, 54).

Nanoclusters of RBP2 and CaV1.3 are arranged in a narrow stripe under the ribbon

As for Piccolino, we imaged only RBP2 immunofluorescence puncta that colocalized with synaptic ribbons (Fig. 2, A and B), as RBP2 also localizes at adjacent efferent synapses (20). 3D MINFLUX images reveal that RBP2 is organized in distinct stripe–like microclusters (on average, around 382 nm in length, 94 nm in width and 60 nm in height, N = 10 synapses) arranged linearly underneath the synaptic ribbon along its length (Fig. 2, C to E), in agreement with published STED microscopy data (20). MINFLUX further resolved these microclusters that appeared to be composed of discrete nanoclusters of RBP2, uncovering an additional level of organization not previously detectable with STED.

We also performed 3D MINFLUX imaging for the AZ protein RIM2, which also yielded a similar linear, stripe-like arrangement of nanoclusters underneath the ribbon, with some synapses showing a more complex organization (fig. S5). Unfortunately, our RIM2 staining gave results only occasionally, and, hence, these data were not used for quantitative analysis (N = 4 successfully imaged synapses).

CaV1.3 localizations, likewise, appeared to form nanoclusters distributed in narrow stripe–like microclusters (Fig. 2, F to K). The stripe-like microclusters are consistent with previous observations from STED imaging where CaV1.3 clusters took this shape in over 60% of AZs (6, 11, 14, 18, 20). En face views of CaV1.3 channels at 8 of the 18 synapses imaged resembled a more complex and wider arrangement of CaV1.3 channels, again reminiscent of previous STED results (18). However, on rotating, these typically also appeared linear (see Fig. 2H, another exemplary synapse shown in fig. S6A), likely representing a side view along the plasma membrane. In one particular synapse of these, we also encountered Ca2+ channel nanoclusters seemingly arranged in two stripe-like microclusters [akin to double line–shaped CaV1.3 clusters seen in STED images (18)], as shown in Fig. 2K. The distance between the two lines was around 160 nm from center points. On the other hand, another 8 of the 18 synapses imaged showed a more defined linear morphology (Fig. 2, I and J) in all perspectives, irrespective of the orientation in which they were observed (see fig. S6B). These linear stripe–like microclusters of channels, on average, appeared 422 nm long, 102 nm wide, and 47 nm high and presumably run underneath the synaptic ribbon along its length (~400 nm). The line appeared thicker along one side than the other in half of these synapses. The remaining 2 of the 18 synapses seemed reminiscent of “spot-like” CaV1.3 channel clusters reported previously (18) and appeared to have highly concentrated localizations in two to three “spots” with no seemingly defined microscale morphology (fig. S6C). A summary of all imaged CaV1.3 microclusters, their heterogenous morphologies, and calculated parameters has been provided in table S2.

We estimated between 9 and 70 CaV1.3 molecular counts per synapse (on average, 35 ± 4 counts per synapse, SD = 18, median = 34, N = 18 synapses). These estimates fall within limits of previous functional estimates (20 to 330 Ca2+ channels per AZ) but are lower than the median counts ranging 60 to 120 Ca2+ channels per AZ (18), indicating an underestimation by MINFLUX, e.g., due to incomplete labeling and/or detection. RBP2 interacts with CaV channels (62), and at the IHC ribbon synapse, it has been shown to play a role in promoting larger complements of CaV1.3 channels (20). Expectedly, RBP2 counts appear similar to counts of CaV1.3 channels on average (30 ± 3 counts per synapse, SD = 9, range = 20 to 43, N = 10 synapses). As above, we reason that it is likely that our estimates provide a lower bound of the number of RBP2 counts on account of not all epitopes being available for antibody binding.

Postsynaptic AMPA receptors are organized in a ring engulfing the presynapse

3D MINFLUX imaging of Homer1 shows that the postsynaptic density (PSD) of the afferent synapse formed by SGNs is packed with hundreds of Homer1 molecules (Fig. 3, A to D). The top view reveals dense localizations spread across the PSD, with intermediate regions of relatively sparse localizations that give the impression that the Homer1 scaffold may be arranged in flat, roundish nanoclusters. Side views of the 3D MINFLUX images show the characteristic flat, plate-like PSD (Fig. 3D). The PSD appeared to be elongated along one axis (~800 nm) and shorter (~300 nm) along the other axis and appeared relatively flat in its height (~100 nm). We also performed two-color 2D MINFLUX by labeling synaptic ribbons and postsynaptic Homer1. For two-color MINFLUX imaging, the fluorophores were excited with the same excitation wavelength, and two different spectral detectors were used to detect emissions that could be separated via spectral unmixing, as described in Materials and Methods. Figure 3E shows a side profile of a synaptic ribbon seated atop of a PSD with the synaptic cleft in between (distance = ~65 nm from the base of ribbon to the top of the PSD).

Next, we investigated the topography of postsynaptic AMPA receptors in SGN boutons. Postsynaptic currents in SGNs are primarily driven by AMPA receptors and not N-methyl-d-aspartate or Kainate receptors (63), and SGN boutons have been shown to express mostly GluA2, along with GluA3 and GluA4 subunits of AMPA receptors but not GluA1 (64). Using 3D MINFLUX, we imaged GluA2 localizations and found GluA2 in en face synapses (three of N = 8 synapses) to be arranged in a characteristic ring-like manner (Fig. 3F), as also shown previously with STED imaging (65, 66) and immunoelectron microscopy (64). In the remaining synapses, the ring-like arrangement of GluA2 was less clear as also shown before with STED imaging (65, 66). The peak-to-peak distance was, on average, ~790 nm, close to the long axis from Homer1 MINFLUX images. We report, on average, 51 ± 12 GluA2 units per synapse (SD = 34, range = 24 to 110, N = 8 synapses), which correspond well with estimates of AMPA receptors (~60 particles per PSD) from immunogold EM data of guinea pig IHC ribbon synapses (64). Next, we aimed to assess the labeling efficiency, acknowledging that absolute quantification is challenging in native tissue samples compared to previous estimates in samples with known protein stoichiometry in cultured cells [e.g., nuclear pore complexes (67) and Ryanodine Receptor, RyR2 complexes (68)]. To empirically arrive at labeling efficiency estimates, we expressed RIBEYE–green fluorescent protein (GFP) in human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293 cells and performed combined MINFLUX and DNA-PAINT (DNA point accumulation for imaging in nanoscale topography) imaging of the expressed RIBEYE-GFP clusters (4, 69). RIBEYE was labeled with a primary antibody, followed by a secondary nanobody, and GFP was directly labeled with a nanobody (see Materials and Methods). By correlating the number of RIBEYE localizations with GFP localizations, we estimated the relative labeling efficiency of the RIBEYE primary antibody compared to the GFP nanobody. To control for photobleaching effects, we randomized the imaging sequence: In some cells, RIBEYE was imaged first and GFP second, and vice versa in others. Irrespective of the order of imaging, we observed high variability in the relative labeling efficiency across RIBEYE-GFP clusters (fig. S7A), with some clusters even showing more RIBEYE primary antibody localizations than GFP nanobody localizations. Given the estimated size of RIBEYE (120 kDa) plus labeling contributions, we tested thresholds ranging from 15 to 35 nm to assign RIBEYE-GFP localization pairs. For a threshold of 25 nm, the average relative labeling efficiency was 67% (median = 70%, ranging from 36 to 113% across clusters; n = 9 RIBEYE-GFP clusters, N = 4 cells; fig. S7B). On the basis of estimates from previous studies suggesting 50 to 70% labeling efficiency for anti-GFP nanobodies themselves (67, 70, 71), the overall effective labeling efficiency should range roughly between 22 and 68%. Labeling efficiency likely varies across proteins, conditions, and tissue depths; thus, this range provides a reasonable approximation. A second approach to estimating labeling efficiency compared the number of CaV1.3 channel counts per synapse (median = 34) to previous functional estimates reporting medians of ~60 and ~120 channels per synapse, dependent on the analysis method (18). On this basis, we conservatively report an overall labeling efficiency of 28 to 57%. This is consistent with estimates from the approach above and observations from immunoelectron microscopy of AMPA receptors (64, 72). We consider this range to accommodate variations of labeling and detection efficiency across proteins, cultured cells versus tissue sections, and imaging conditions.

Two-color MINFLUX reveals distribution of VGLUT3-positive vesicular compartments around the synaptic ribbon

Next, we performed two-color MINFLUX imaging by labeling synaptic ribbons with an antibody against CtBP2/RIBEYE (Alexa Fluor 647–conjugated secondary) and SVs with VGLUT3 (CF660C-conjugated secondary antibody), the vesicular glutamate transporter isoform found in IHCs (73–75). We observed a very dense labeling for VGLUT3 around the synaptic ribbon and also throughout the IHC (Fig. 5, A and B; N = 4 MINFLUX images). The VGLUT3 signal appeared highly specific to IHCs with negligible signal outside the cell. IHCs have been shown to be densely packed with glutamatergic SVs as demonstrated by the strong VGLUT3 immunofluorescence and colocalized otoferlin immunofluorescence (76, 77). VGLUT3 localizations may therefore reflect putative SVs around the synaptic ribbon and other VGLUT3-positive compartments in the cell. We performed cluster analysis of VGLUT3 units using DBSCAN with minimum number of six VGLUT3 molecules (assuming ~55% labeling efficiency; estimates suggesting ~10 VGLUT1/2 per SV) (78) and ε = 20 nm (Fig. 5C). VGLUT3 units assigned into clusters were fitted with circles to estimate diameters of the putative vesicles, which was, on average, 44.30 ± 2.13 nm (SD = 17.42, median = 40.31 nm; Fig. 5D).

Nanoclusters of ion channels and their interacting proteins govern AZ and PSD topography

We next tested the above notion that several synaptic proteins show a higher-order organization at the AZ and the PSD. Specifically, we probed if the protein units form nanoclusters. To do so, we used Monte Carlo simulations to compare the cumulative distribution of the nearest-neighbor distances, known as G-function, of the measured unit positions with synthetic data of randomly arranged positions confined to the same measured volume and with the same number of units. The simulations were repeated 1000 times for each synapse to generate a confidence envelop. A G-function with a curve higher than the simulation envelop indicates clustering behavior, while one with values below (or to the right of) the simulation envelop indicates a dispersion pattern. When the measured G-function falls within the simulated envelop for random positions, this means that the measured pattern does not deviate from the null hypothesis, which is a random arrangement in our case. With the exception of RIBEYE, all the other synaptic proteins showed clustering behavior as evident from the deviation of their G-functions to the simulated envelops (Fig. 6, A and B). To derive a binary conclusion from the envelop graphs, we applied the Diggle-Cressie-Loosemore-Ford (DCLF) test (79) with probing distances from 10 to 18 nm (see Materials and Methods). The observed results indicated that in 77.8% of synapses, CaV1.3 units exhibit some degree of clustering (significance level α = 0.002; see Fig. 6C and fig. S8). Data for Piccolino, RBP2, and Homer1 also indicate clustering for 60, 70, and 90% of synapses, respectively, while GluA2 showed the most prominent spatial clustering, having a nonrandom behavior for all the imaged synapses. On the other hand, the spatial distribution of RIBEYE units within the synaptic ribbon appeared nonclustered and did not indicate any higher-order organization (Fig. 6, B and C, and fig. S8).

CaV1.3 nanoclusters serve efficient SV release of IHC ribbon synapses

We next used biophysical modeling to assess whether nanocluster-based organization of CaV1.3 would impart any functional advantages at the IHC ribbon synapse. We simulated Ca2+ binding to SV Ca2+ sensors (41) at the vesicular release site using the particle-based Monte Carlo method (MCell) (80). We assumed that a single fully Ca2+-occupied sensor triggers SV release and estimated the release probability by accounting for stochastic influx and diffusion of Ca2+ in the presence of buffers estimated for physiological conditions (18). First, we modeled a simple scenario where a single SV was coupled to a nanocluster consisting of 12 CaV channels. The SV diameter was set to 50 nm (17), and the coupling distance (d), defined as the distance between the SV center and the edge of the nanocluster, was fixed to 15 nm (see Fig. 7A). Six sensors were placed with a polar angle (θ) of 115° empirically, as direct experimental estimates of the number and location of the SV Ca2+ sensors are currently unavailable. We generated 10 different scenarios of Ca2+ channel distribution, and simulations were repeated 100 times. The local [Ca2+] was estimated with the hit rate per unit area, showing ~40 μM around the sensor index 1 (see Supplementary Text for biophysical modeling and fig. S9). For each sensor, we estimated the effective coupling distance, Rc (see Materials and Methods). As shown in Fig. 7B (top) when sensor indices are ordered according to the average distance between a sensor and channels, a closer proximity to the nanocluster corresponds to a lower Rc (black line) and a higher number of release events (gray line). The closest sensor (sensor index 1) exhibited an Rc of 23.2 nm, while the other sensors showed values of 32.1, 33.1, 50.6, 51.2, and 59.8 nm, respectively. Most release events were triggered by sensor index 1 (74.5%), followed by sensor index 3 (14.4%), sensor index 2 (10.9%), and sensor index 4 (0.2%), and others did not trigger any release events.

As shown in Fig. 7B (bottom), the release probability within 2 ms was 0.4 ± 0.1. Notably, progressive removal of Ca2+ channels, starting with those closest to the sensor index 1, led to a rapid decline in release probability. The removal of the two closest Ca2+ channels, “near to far,” reduced release probability by 55% (from 0.40 to 0.18), and the removal of five Ca2+ channels resulted in nearly zero release. Progressive removal of Ca2+ channels furthest from the sensor index 1, “far to near,” led to slower decline in release probability. In such a scenario, deleting seven Ca2+ channels resulted in a comparable decrease in the release probability as deleting two Ca2+ channels under the near-to-far condition. Furthermore, having only the two closest Ca2+ channels did not trigger any release events. This may indicate that in addition to the two to three “private” channels (21) closest to the sensor that are the primary drivers of release, surplus channels in a nanocluster might contribute, e.g., by building a [Ca2+] microdomain, thereby depleting buffer molecules and likely promoting Ca2+-dependent SV replenishment by private channels (81).

Next, we extended our simulations to physiologically relevant scenarios with multiple nanoclusters taking advantage of physiological channel counts (18) and morphological evidence for channel topography [this study and (18)]. To evaluate the functional efficiency of nanocluster organization, we estimated the fraction of the readily release pool (RRP) released within 2 ms in two topographical scenarios: (i) random Ca2+ channel distribution and (ii) Ca2+ channel nanoclusters. In both scenarios, we distributed 96 CaV1.3 Ca2+ channels within a rectangular AZ with dimensions of 410 nm by 135 nm. The minimum interchannel distance was set to 11 nm. For the nanocluster scenario, 12 channels were distributed within a 50-nm diameter, and eight nanoclusters were distributed as two lines, where each nanocluster was placed at the edge of the AZ. The nanocluster center was fixed at 42.5 and −42.5 nm along the y axis, while nanoclusters were randomly positioned along the x axis. We positioned 12 docked SVs around Ca2+ channel clusters (17) (Fig. 7, C and D). For each scenario, we further generated 10 possible configurations with different channel distributions and SV arrangements. Each configuration for a scenario was simulated 100 times (i.e., 1000 iteration for nanocluster arrangement with a d of 15 nm). We tested three coupling distances (d): 15, 20, and 25 nm. Other model parameters including buffer concentrations, Ca2+ channel kinetics, and Ca2+ binding kinetics of the Ca2+ sensor on the SV were based on previous physiological estimates (see Materials and Methods and table S3). To investigate the effect of spatial coupling between the SV Ca2+ sensors and Ca2+ channels on synaptic efficacy, we used the fraction of released RRP (FRRP within τ of RRP release) as a proxy of the release probability. The physiological estimates of FRRP at 2 ms range from 0.09 to 0.27 [see Materials and Methods and (82)]. Here, we found the FRRP for random arrangement of channels to be 0.25 ± 0.02, 0.17 ± 0.02, and 0.12 ± 0.02 (for d = 15, 20, and 25 nm, respectively). As expected, FRRP decreases with increasing coupling distance d. For the nanocluster scenarios, the predicted FRRP were 0.35 ± 0.04, 0.27 ± 0.04, and 0.2 ± 0.02 at d = 15, 20, and 25 nm, respectively. For a given coupling distance, our simulations predict that a nanocluster arrangement of Ca2+ channels provides more efficient SV release when compared with randomly distributed channels (***P < 0.001, t test; Fig. 7E).

To further investigate the relationship between Rc and release probability, we plotted histograms of Rc first for the single SV-nanocluster scenario, where only the coupling distance values associated with release events were included (Fig. 7F). This shows that SV release events were triggered when the coupling distance was within 50 nm (median of 31.4 nm). To better reflect the relation between the Rc and the number of release events, we additionally plotted a weighted histogram taking into account the number of release events (weighted median of 23.5 nm). This revealed two distinct groups of release events: one with shorter Rc around the weighted median from sensor index 1, corresponding to the majority of release events (74.5%), and another with relatively higher Rc from sensor indices 2 and 3 (25.3%), corresponding to the remaining release events.

Next, we analyzed histograms of Rc for the simulated nanocluster versus random arrangement of channels at the AZ (Fig. 7G). The nanocluster scenario exhibited shorter Rc than the random distribution; for instance, for d = 25 nm, the median Rc was 54 nm for nanoclusters versus 59 nm for random arrangement (***P < 0.001, Mood’s median test). In addition, generating weighted histograms accounting for the number of release events per bin (Fig. 7H) revealed that shorter Rc corresponded to higher release probabilities with median Rc of 51 nm for nanoclusters and 55 nm for random arrangement (***P < 0.001, t test). This highlights the functional advantage of a nanocluster-based organization and that in physiology, a difference of ~4 nm in Rc might significantly affect release dynamics.

Last, we exemplarily implemented a model of one imaged synapse (synapse shown in Fig. 6D with maximum CaV1.3 counts) and incorporated both Ca2+ channel nanoclusters and nonclustered Ca2+ channels. This model comprised seven nanoclusters with an average of 8.3 channels each, together with randomly distributed channels, summing to a total number of 70 channels per AZ and hypothetically placing 12 docked SVs positioned around the AZ boundary (fig. S10A). We simulated a matched control scenario with 70 channels distributed randomly within the same AZ dimensions. For both scenarios, we generated 10 possible configurations with varying SV arrangements (and channel distribution for the random case), running 100 iterations each, and testing three coupling distances (d): 15, 20, and 25 nm as above.

Consistent with the aforementioned findings, the nanocluster-based scenario exhibited significantly higher FRRP than the random scenario at all tested coupling distances (fig. S10, B and C; ***P < 0.001; t test for d = 15 nm; Mann-Whitney U test for d = 20 and 25 nm). Despite somewhat lower absolute FRRP values, likely reflecting the total channel number here compared to the model above, this confirms the robustness of nanocluster organization of CaV1.3 channels for enhancing SV release. Analysis of Rc further supported this conclusion, revealing shorter median and weighted median Rc values for the realistic channel nanocluster + random channel scenario compared to the purely random channel distribution across all coupling distances (***P < 0.001, Mood’s median test and t test; fig. S10, D and E), corroborating the functional significance of nanoclusters at the IHC ribbon synapse.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we used 3D MINFLUX to elucidate the molecular nanoanatomy of IHC ribbon synapses. We established immunolabeled semithin Epon-embedded cochlear sections that provide minimal background and allow immobilization of synapses on the coverslip for efficient single-molecule localizations with MINFLUX. 3D MINFLUX revealed the densely packed, nonclustered arrangement of RIBEYE inside the synaptic ribbon, with Piccolino distributed along the membrane-distal apex of the ribbon, enveloping it in the form of an arch. Using two-color MINFLUX imaging of VGLUT3 and synaptic ribbons, we visualized putative SVs and other VGLUT3-positive organelles/membranes around the ribbon which, thus far, were only accessible to EM. Our data revealed nanoclusters of CaV1.3 channels and the multidomain presynaptic AZ protein RBP2 that were typically arranged in a stripe-like microcluster along the long axis of ribbon. CaV1.3 nanoclusters comprise 3 to 26 channel counts, interspersed at an average distance of 10.8 nm from the nearest-neighboring channel. Mathematical modeling indicates that such a nanocluster-based organization of CaV1.3 enables a higher release probability by shorter effective coupling distance, contributing to the efficiency of synaptic sound encoding in the ear. Our MINFLUX data also shed light on the topography of postsynaptic Homer1 and AMPA receptors, with hundreds of Homer1 units arranged in densely packed, flat patches at the PSD, and nanoclusters of GluA2 receptor units assembled in a ring, presumably at the periphery of the PSD.

Molecular nanoanatomy of the IHC ribbon synapse

Our MINFLUX study supports and extends previous structural studies of the IHC ribbon synapses using STED imaging and/or EM. Our data agree with morphology and dimensions of microclusters of CaV1.3 channels (6, 11, 14, 18, 20), RBP2 (20), RIM2 (19), Piccolino (11, 54), RIBEYE (3, 21, 54, 65), and GluA2 (64–66). However, while RIBEYE has been previously proposed to assemble in a staggered array inside the ribbon scaffold (8) and, occasionally, a lamellar substructure has been observed in mature synaptic ribbons with EM (3, 52, 53), we did not observe any such lamellar or staggered organization of RIBEYE assembly inside ribbons of mature mice. Instead, we found RIBEYE to show a distribution statistically indistinguishable from spatial randomness inside the ribbon. We cannot exclude this finding to be specific to IHC ribbons studied here. Moreover, future cryogenic electron tomography and subtomogram averaging might help further addressing the molecular structure of this enigmatic nanomachine.

Previous MINFLUX imaging of ribbon synapses in retinal rod photoreceptors (33) indicated an arrangement of AZ proteins Bassoon, RIM2, CaV1.4 channels, and ubMunc13-2 in two parallel lines along each side of the rod ribbon. This was taken to suggest a regularly ordered repetition of a molecular and structural correlate of SV release sites in linear tracks on the two sides of the synaptic ribbon reminiscent of the classic “ribs and pegs” model of the AZ, derived from EM studies at the frog neuromuscular junction (30, 83). In contrast, MINFLUX imaging of RBP2, CaV1.3, and even RIM2 at IHC AZs showed a nanocluster topography forming single stripe–like microclusters. MINFLUX imaging also indicated more complex microclusters that can appear stripe like upon rotation. We note the possibility that two proximal lines of AZ proteins could not be parsed out because of sparse localizations and the small size of the IHC AZ compared to rod photoreceptor AZ. However, it seems more likely that the spatial organization of AZ proteins follows a different principle at IHC synapses: nanoscale coclustering of RBP2, RIM2, and CaV1.3 likely mediated by liquid-liquid phase separation and domain-domain interactions (Fig. 8).

Mapping SVs using light microscopy

The molecular machinery underlying IHC exocytosis is unconventional (1): The role of canonical neuronal SNARE [soluble N-ethylmaleimide–sensitive factor attachment protein (SNAP) receptors] proteins is controversial (84, 85); Synaptotagmin 1 (86, 87), Complexins (88), Munc13s (16), Munc18s (89), Synaptophysin 1, Synapsin 1, 2 (90, 91), and VGLUT1, 2 (73, 74) seem to be absent. Instead, IHC synapses rely on VGLUT3 (73, 74) and on otoferlin that is involved in SV tethering, docking, Ca2+ sensing for fusion and exocytosis-endocytosis coupling [e.g., review in (1, 92, 93)], and defective in hereditary autosomal recessive deafness 9 (DFNB9) (94). Otoferlin distributes broadly in IHCs (42, 76, 77) and, aside from SV cycling, seems also involved in constitutive membrane trafficking (77). The otoferlin labeling we achieved in the semithin section thus far did not lead to efficient MINFLUX imaging. Further optimization of staining or the use of mouse lines expressing tagged-otoferlin will serve future MINFLUX studies. Here, we embarked on detecting SVs by labeling for VGLUT3, which is a bona fide SV marker in IHCs (73, 74, 76, 77, 89). Our two-color MINFLUX data show the expected broad distribution of VGLUT3 in the synaptic IHC pole and depict putative SV localizations around the synaptic ribbon and near the membrane. Further validation by colabeling for other SV markers such as otoferlin will help make MINFLUX available as an approach to studying SV distributions at the AZ including at different states of activity, complementing electron tomographic studies (13, 17) with molecular information. This will be particularly meaningful for assessing the spatial relationship of SV release sites, SV Ca2+ sensors, and the CaV1.3 channels triggering SV release.

Determining molecular counts of synaptic components using MINFLUX

The first estimates of Ca2+ channels at IHC ribbon synapses came from a study in the frog that used nonstationary fluctuation analysis on whole-cell Ca2+ tail currents and reported, on average, 80 Ca2+ channels per AZ (36). A subsequent study in mouse IHCs reported a similar estimate of 80 Ca2+ channels per AZ, also using fluctuation analysis (35). Note that because of the scarcity of IHCs in the cochlea, validation using proteomic approaches to determine absolute copy numbers of IHC ribbon synapse proteins, for instance, as done for neuronal synapses (78, 95), has remained challenging. A later study used imaging of Ca2+ influx at single IHC AZs in combination with patch-clamp and performed (i) optical fluctuation analysis and (ii) correlation of Ca2+ influx at a single AZ and whole-cell Ca2+ influx, while locally depleting free extracellular Ca2+ via iontophoresis at that single AZ using the Ca2+ chelator EGTA (18). Through both approaches, a wide range of channels per AZ was reported: 20 to 200 channels per AZ (median of 60) for the first and 30 to 300 channels per AZ (median of 120) for the latter. Quantitative analysis of our MINFLUX data suggests between 9 and 70 (mean = 35 ± 4; median = 34) molecular counts of CaV1.3 per synapse in IHCs. While our estimates lie within range of previously published functional estimates in IHCs (closer to the estimates predicted by optical fluctuation analysis), they likely underestimate the number of CaV1.3 per synapse due to limiting labeling efficiency. Despite our efforts to approximate labeling efficiency in our samples (fig. S7), a limitation in the present study arises from the lack of established methods to determine absolute labeling efficiency in native tissue sample. Previous efforts in cultured cells have relied on protein complexes with known stoichiometry, such as the nuclear pore complex (67) or the RyR2 receptor (68). However, these strategies are not applicable here, as the stoichiometry of the protein complexes we analyzed is not yet known. Studying the absolute numbers and relative topographies of CaV1.3, RBP2, and Bassoon remains an important objective for future multicolor MINFLUX studies. Notably, the number of CaV1.3 channels, as well as the resultant size of CaV1.3 channel microclusters (or intensity of Ca2+ influx in the case of functional Ca2+ imaging studies), scales with size of the synaptic ribbon (11, 18, 96, 97). IHC AZs display a remarkable position-dependent heterogeneity in terms of size and properties [for review, see (98)], which seem causally related to the firing properties of corresponding SGNs (99).

Another limitation of the current study is the linkage error of ~20 nm when localizing proteins by indirect immunolabeling [for review, see (100)]. Although emitter localizations were performed with high optical precision, the linkage error compromises the accuracy of determining protein localizations themselves. Unfortunately, nanobodies, which could alleviate this problem to an extent, were largely unavailable for the targets of our study or generated weak labeling not supporting MINFLUX imaging. Moreover, our attempts using secondary nanobodies in combination with primary antibodies also resulted in weak labeling, which often did not support MINFLUX imaging. A recent study has used MINFLUX imaging to reveal that the linkage errors for nanobody, primary antibody, and primary-secondary antibody complex labeling show non-normal distributions, likely due to structural flexibility and randomness in antibody-epitope geometry (101). Therefore, we have refrained from applying an offset correction for linkage error, as it might not be suitable and will likely introduce actual structural deviations. A possible approach in the future may be to use a probabilistic model based on the distribution of linkage errors. However, to our knowledge, no current method allows for mapping each MINFLUX localization to a specific point on the linkage error distribution. Future work using mice expressing tagged proteins such as CaV1.3 with a HaloTag (102) will help to determine localizations and molecular counts more accurately. Moreover, future experiments tagging both N and C termini of large and spatially extended proteins such as Bassoon and RBP with small tags (e.g., HaloTag and SNAP) and performing dual-color MINFLUX imaging will inform their spatial organization at the AZ.

Nanotopography of Ca2+ channels using MINFLUX—What can we learn?

Our 3D MINFLUX data reveal that the CaV1.3 channels and RBP2 form discrete nanoclusters aligned along the synaptic ribbon, with their molecular counts and spatial topography showing notable concordance. This supports previous suggestions that RBP2 interacts with CaV1.3 (62) and positively regulates CaV1.3 abundance at the IHC ribbon synapse (20, 103). RBP2 has been shown to link CaV1.3 to Bassoon (14, 69, 104), potentially facilitating the spatial assembly of CaV1.3 nanoclusters into higher-order clusters. Disruption of Bassoon results in abnormal CaV1.3 microcluster morphology (14) and a widespread presynaptic Ca2+ influx (18), likely due to dispersal of CaV1.3 nanoclusters. In another recent work of ours, we have demonstrated that RBP2 coexpression suffices to drive the assembly of CaV1.3 nanodomains at synthetic ribbon–type AZs in a heterologous system (69). On the basis of these observations, we propose that RBP2 acts as a molecular scaffold, organizing CaV1.3 nanoclusters via multivalent interactions (Fig. 8). We hypothesize that beyond the established high-affinity domain-domain interactions (e.g., Src homology 3 domain–PXXP), organization of nanoclusters of CaV1.3, RBP2, and RIM2 may also be governed by low-affinity interactions between their intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs). These IDR-mediated interactions could enable the formation of liquid-like nanocondensates (105), which are likely dynamic interactions occurring in a constant state of flux based on synaptic activity and plasticity (106, 107).

While we cannot entirely rule out the possibility of clustering due to fixation, a recent study shows that the modulatory C-terminal domain of CaV1.3 promotes nanoscale channel clustering even in live induced pluripotent stem cell–derived cardiomyocytes (108). Moreover, the spatial arrangement of RIBEYE units inside the ribbons as seen in our MINFLUX data does not show such a nanoclustering also arguing against this possibility. Our data converge with an average of seven CaV1.3 units per nanocluster, at a mean distance of 10.8 nm from the nearest-neighboring channel within the same nanocluster similar to the nanoclusters reported (108). Moreover, a report from CNS synapses using EM on SDS-digested freeze-fracture replica labels also arrived at an average estimate of ~9 CaV2.1 channels per nanocluster, with each nanocluster tightly coupled in a one-to-one stoichiometry with a vesicular release site (28). IHCs are predicted to have 10 to 15 release sites per AZ, with approximately 80 to 120 channels per AZ (14, 18, 76, 109), which would give a similar figure of 8 channels per release site, of which 1 to 3 private CaV1.3 channels exert a Ca2+ nanodomain–like control of release with an effective coupling distance of ~17 nm (21, 37). However, our data report an average of five nanoclusters per synapse (range = 2 to 8) with additional nonclustered CaV1.3 channels (between one and eight per synapse). Therefore, we refine our prior suggestion of SV release sites lining the circumference of the CaV1.3 microcluster with an effective coupling distance of the of ~17 nm from private CaV1.3 channels in the periphery of the cluster (21, 37) and propose SV release sites to be positioned around the circumference of CaV1.3 nanoclusters. In such a scenario, we envision that a subset of channels within the nanocluster would be within the described effective coupling distance to the Ca2+ sensor of SV fusion. Such a perimeter model has also been recently proposed for the mature inhibitory basket cell–Purkinje cell synapse of the cerebellum, which also show a tight Ca2+ nanodomain control of exocytosis (27). The CaV1.3 nanoclusters and associated release sites at the IHC synapses represent modular implementation of maximal synaptic strength, which offers versatility. Not only IHCs likely vary in the number of these modules, but this way also might tune the coupling of Ca2+ influx with SV release. Paired pre- and postsynaptic patch-clamp recordings and combined imaging of presynaptic Ca2+ signals and glutamate release demonstrate variations of the prevailing Ca2+ nanodomain coupling across AZs (38, 39, 82). Future work might study the pre- and postsynaptic molecular topographies of IHC synapses formed with SGNs that differ in their spontaneous and evoked firing properties. Paired pre- and postsynaptic recordings (82) classified the synapses based on the rate of spontaneous excitatory postsynaptic currents, referred to as spontaneous rate of release (SR) and found (i) shorter latency of release, (ii) higher initial rate of release, and (iii) faster RRP depletion for high SR synapses. It will be interesting to elucidate the AZ topographies that best approximate the properties of “high-SR” and “low-SR” synapses.

Moreover, it will be exciting to compare the functional implications of the “CaV1.3 nanocluster + nonclustered CaV1.3 topography” described here for IHC AZ to those of the regular arrangement of CaV1.4 in parallel rows with <20-nm distance from the putative SV release sites at retinal rod ribbon synapses (33). We hypothesize that experiments and modeling will reveal a tighter Ca2+ nanodomain control at the rod AZ. Likewise, future work might study the CaV1.3 topography of IHC AZs before the onset of hearing, when Ca2+ microdomain–like control of exocytosis is prevalent (21), or using genetic models with perturbed RIBEYE, Bassoon, or Piccolino, which show disrupted Ca2+ channel clusters and/or diffuse Ca2+ influx (6, 11, 14, 18). Our data argue against the possibility of an exclusion zone (34) at the mature IHC ribbon synapse. Future experiments with multicolor MINFLUX to directly visualize CaV1.3 channel topography with respect to vesicular release sites/vesicular markers would add more clarity. However, this might be challenging as two-color MINFLUX becomes difficult when the abundance of fluorophores is considerably disproportionate for two different proteins and blinking events from one would merely dominate over the other (e.g., VGLUT3 over CaV1.3). Future studies combining DNA-PAINT and MINFLUX (110) could be used for multiplexing to investigate multiple synaptic structures in the same sample using MINFLUX.

Last, at CNS synapses, presynaptic AZ nanoclusters have been shown to be positioned in tandem with postsynaptic AMPA receptor clusters generating synaptic nanocolumns (23). We did not exclusively probe the existence of these transsynaptic nanocolumns at the IHC ribbon synapse using two-color MINFLUX. However, it seems likely that the IHC-SGN synapse has a different implementation, where a presynaptic AZ extending to just about 400 nm in length is engulfed by a “ring-like detector” of postsynaptic AMPA receptor clusters, which extend to even ~900 nm. This way, the distribution of AMPA receptor nanoclusters might maximize efficiency for detection of glutamate as proposed before (64, 66).

Key limitations of the study

Efficiency and localization accuracy of protein labeling are the key limitations of the present study. Further validation via genetic labeling strategies and improvement of labeling efficiency will be important objectives for future work to fully capitalize on the powerful MINFLUX approach to quantitatively assessing the molecular nanoanatomy of subcellular specializations such as pre- and postsynaptic compartments. Combinations with DNA-PAINT for multiplexing the imaging will be key to assess the structural organization of large proteins such as multidomain AZ proteins and their relative topography.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Tissue processing and sectioning

Animal handling complied with guidelines from the University of Göttingen Board for Animal Welfare and the Animal Welfare Office of the State of Lower Saxony. Cochleae from 2-week-old [postnatal day 14 (P14) to P21] C57BL6/J mice were dissected in ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and immediately fixed in 4% formalin (in PBS) on ice for 40 to 60 min. For samples fixed with glyoxal (51), fixation solution (3%, w/v) was prepared as follows (4 ml of solution mix): 2.835 ml of double-distilled H2O, 0.789 ml of ethanol, 0.313 ml of glyoxal (40% stock solution from Sigma-Aldrich), and 0.030 ml of acetic acid; solution was vortexed and brought to pH 4/5 by adding 1 M NaOH. Samples were then fixed for 30 min on ice and then 30 min at room temperature. Following fixation with either protocol, samples were thoroughly washed three times with PBS (5 to 10 min each wash) and then decalcified using Morse’s solution (10% sodium citrate + 22.5% formic acid) for 20 to 30 min. Cochleae were washed again three times (5 min each) with PBS.

After decalcification, the cochleae were embedded in Epon epoxy resin after dehydration using acetone and polymerized for 24 hours at 60°C. One-micrometer sections were cut using DIATOME histo diamond knife and a Leica UC7 microtome. About three to four such sections were placed on 18- to 24-mm-diameter coverslips (thickness = 1.5H, i.e., 170 ± 5 μm). The coverslips containing the sections were heated on a hot plate (60°C) for about 15 to 20 min to immobilize the sections on to them. These were then used for immunostaining or could be stored at room temperature until needed.

Immunohistochemistry

Before immunostaining, we removed the Epon resin by treating coverslips containing sections with a saturated solution of NaOH in absolute ethanol (50) for 15 min at room temperature. This was followed by thorough washing with PBS (three to four times). Blocking and permeabilization were performed in a humidified wet chamber with goat serum dilution buffer [GSDB; 16% normal goat serum, 450 mM NaCl, 0.3% Triton X-100, and 20 mM phosphate buffer (pH ∼ 7.4)] for 1 hour at room temperature. We then incubated the coverslips with primary antibodies (diluted in GSDB; refer to table S4) overnight at 4°C. Antibodies used for MINFLUX imaging of most synaptic proteins have undergone knock-out validation in previous studies: RIBEYE-A (111), CtBP2 (6), CaV1.3 (35), RBP2 (20), Piccolino (11), VGLUT3 (112), and RIM2 (19). After primary antibody incubation, the samples were washed three times for 10 min in a wash buffer [450 mM NaCl, 0.3% Triton X-100, and 20 mM phosphate buffer (pH ∼ 7.4)]. We then incubated the coverslips with respective secondary antibodies (diluted in GSDB; refer to table S5) for 1 hour in a light-protected humidified chamber at room temperature. Last, we washed the samples three times for 10 min in wash buffer and two more times with PBS. Samples could be left in PBS at 4 °C for around 1 to 2 weeks until MINFLUX imaging.

MINFLUX imaging

On the day of MINFLUX imaging, the coverslips were washed in PBS and incubated for 5 min with 150-nm gold nanoparticles (BBI solutions, EM.GC150), which serve as fiducials during MINFLUX image acquisition to enable sample stabilization and beamline monitoring. Excess of the gold nanoparticles was removed by washing the samples three times in PBS. We then added 10 mM MgCl2 for 5 min on to the coverslips, which enhances adhesion of gold nanoparticles to the coverslip and the sample, followed by three washes in PBS. Samples were mounted in freshly prepared GLOX buffer [50 mM tris-HCl, 10 mM NaCl, 10% (w/v) glucose, catalase (64 μg/ml), glucose oxidase (0.4 mg/ml), and 10 to 25 mM mercaptoethylamine (pH 8.0)] for imaging, and the coverslips were lastly sealed using twinsil (picodent). Imaging was performed on an Abberior 3D MINFLUX system (Abberior Instruments), as described before (113). Briefly, the microscope is built on a motorized inverted microscope body (Olympus, IX83) with CoolLED illumination for epifluorescent imaging via the eye piece. The system is equipped with 405-, 485-, 561-, and 640-nm laser lines, as well as a 980-nm infrared laser for the active stabilization system. Images were acquired with a 60× 1.42 numerical aperture (NA) UPLXAPO or a 100× 1.45 NA UPLXAPO oil objective (both Olympus). Detection was performed with two avalanche photodiodes in spectral windows Cy5 near (650 to 685 nm) and Cy5 far (685 to 720 nm), which allows for simultaneous two-color imaging. Sites of interest were identified with a 488-nm costain via the eyepiece, with ribbons located confocally either directly from the 647-nm stain or via a 546 nm costain. Regions of interest were selected for MINFLUX measurement and acquisition proceeded with the standard MINFLUX 2D and 3D imaging sequences. Key sequence parameters are listed in tables S6 and S7 for 2D and 3D acquisitions, respectively. Ultraviolet activation was applied as appropriate to maintain single-molecule blinking behavior.

MINFLUX data analysis and statistics

Data (drift corrected using beamline monitoring) were imported from iMSPECTOR (Abberior Instruments) as .npy files into pyMINFLUX (114), where we filtered for the quality parameters effective frequency at offset (EFO; rejecting > ~70,000) and center frequency ratio (CFR; rejecting > ~0.65). MINFLUX images displayed in the manuscript were created using ParaView software 5.11.2 (115). Dimensions of synaptic structures reported were measured using the ruler function on ParaView. Confocal images were processed using ImageJ and were adjusted for brightness and contrast. Numerical data were sorted using Microsoft Excel, and graphs were created using GraphPad Prism or IGOR Pro 7 (WaveMetrics). Figures were compiled using Adobe Illustrator.

For two-color MINFLUX, spectral separation of the two colors was performed on the basis of DCR (detector channel ratio) valuesDCR=countsCh1,offsetcountsCh1,offset+countsCh2,offset

The DCR values were weighted by the corresponding effective counts at offset values for each iteration beginning from the head start. This way, we accounted for any inhomogeneity in the background signal. Then, a two-component Gaussian mixture model was fitted to the resulting DCR values, and the assigned probability of belonging to distribution 1 or 2 was retrieved. This allowed us to assign a localization to one color if its probability was greater than 95%; otherwise, the localization was assigned to a “not defined” category. Afterward, all the localizations belonging to the same trace, i.e., with the same TID, were assigned to the category corresponding to the majority of the localizations in that trace. In this way, all the localization in one trace would correspond to a single color. Traces for which most of the localizations were not defined were not used for visualizing and analyzing the two-color MINFLUX data.

The localization precision along each dimension was also determined after preprocessing of data using pyMINFLUX and has been reported as the median ± SD of deviations derived from all localizations per TID. The localization precision was typically about 4 to 6 nm in all dimensions. Data from MINFLUX images with more than one synaptic structure were separated into individual data files for each synapse by sorting the coordinates on Microsoft Excel. Filtered data were exported from pyMINFLUX as .csv files and were ultimately analyzed using a custom written Python script. We visualized the raw data using Plotly Express (Plotly Technologies Inc. Collaborative Data Science, Montréal, QC, 2015; https://plotly.com/.) and discarded TIDs with ≤4 localizations. Next, we determined the centroid of each TID by averaging coordinates of all localizations per TID and then performed DBSCAN (116) using a package from scikit-learn (117), with the minimum number of points to form a cluster = 1. The radius ε of the cluster was calculated as follows (57)ε=√2σx+2σy+2σz

The ranges of ε were as follows: 5.5 to 5.6 nm for RIBEYE, 5.8 to 6.1 nm for Piccolino, 4.9 to 6.0 nm for RBP2, 4.4 to 6.4 nm for CaV1.3, 4.9 to 5.8 nm for Homer1, and 2.9 to 5.7 nm for GluA2. After removing centroids not pertaining to the synaptic structure and originating from background localizations, we determined the molecular counts (units) per synaptic structure and calculated the shortest neighbor distances between them. We used alphashape 1.3.1 toolbox to fit α shapes to the coordinates of units and determined volume estimates of the synaptic structures. The α parameter was optimized for complete fitting to structure of interest and was kept consistent for every protein: 0.01 for RIBEYE and Piccolino, 0.001 for RBP2, CaV1.3, and GluA2, and 0.003 for Homer1. For cluster analysis of putative vesicles, DBSCAN was implemented on VGLUT3 units with minimum number of points = 6 and ε = 20. Circle fits have radii equal to the Euclidean distance between the centroid of the individual VGLUT3 clusters and the most distant point in the cluster.

To assess the 3D spatial arrangement of the protein units, we performed Monte Carlo simulations of randomly distributed units confined to the convex hull of the measured protein units for each synapse. We applied the constraint that the random units could not be closer to each other with a distance smaller than the calculated localization precision. The number of random units was also constrained to be same as the number of measured units. In this way, we adapted our null hypothesis to a more realistic scenario than complete spatial randomness, considering the dimension of the measured proteins, their number, and the estimated volume that comprise them. The simulations were repeated 1000 times for each synapse, and their spatial behavior was described using the cumulative distribution of the nearest-neighbor distances, also known as the G-function. To extract a binary result out of the G-function graphs, i.e., random or nonrandom, we applied a custom Python implementation of the DCLF envelop test (79) for distances in an interval ranging from 10 to 18 nm. For analysis of nanoclusters, we further implemented DBSCAN on data containing protein units with the following parameters: minimum number of points to form a cluster = 3 and radius ε = mean of nearest-neighbor distance of all units per synapse + 2 SDs. Intercluster distances reported were calculated as the nearest-neighbor distances between centroids of each nanocluster

Estimation of relative labeling efficiency using DNA-PAINT

For the labeling efficiency experiment, we transfected HEK293 cells plated on poly-l-lysine–coated coverslips with a plasmid adeno associated virus (pAAV)-RIBEYE-GFP construct as described before (69), resulting in heterologous expression of RIBEYE containing a C-terminal GFP tag. The expressed RIBEYE formed large cytosolic clusters as previously described (4), which we targeted for 3D MINFLUX imaging. Eighteen to 24 hours after transfection, cells were fixed with chilled 99% methanol for 2 min on ice. Following fixation, the cells were washed three times with PBS and subsequently blocked in GSDB. The anti–RIBEYE-B (rabbit, Synaptic Systems, 192003) or the anti–RIBEYE-A (rabbit, Synaptic Systems, 192103) primary antibody was diluted 1:200 in GSDB and incubated with the cells for 1 hour. Afterward, the cells were washed three times with the washing buffer (as described in the “Immunohistochemistry” section) and stored in PBS at 4°C until imaging.

On the day of MINFLUX imaging, we incubated the cells with an anti-rabbit nanobody [Massive Photonics, single-domain antibody (sdAB) anti-rabbit immunoglobulin G (clone: 10E10) with docking site 2] and an anti-GFP nanobody [Massive Photonics, sdAB anti-GFP (clones: 1H1 and 1B2) with docking site 3], both diluted 1:200 in Massive Photonics antibody incubation buffer for 1 hour. After washing with the wash buffer provided in the kit, coverslips were incubated with gold fiducials (150-nm gold colloid, BBI Solutions, EM.GC150/4) for 5 min, followed by three brief washes in PBS and incubation with 10 mM MgCl2 in PBS for an additional 5 min. Coverslips were mounted in an open topped chamber, and the respective DNA-PAINT imagers (Imager 2 or Imager 3), at a concentration of 800 pM in Massive Photonics imaging buffer, were added to perform DNA-PAINT MINFLUX imaging. The systems’ beamline monitoring module was engaged. After the initial acquisition, the first imager was removed, the sample was washed once with Massive Photonics wash buffer, and the second imager was introduced. To control for photobleaching effects, we randomized the imaging sequence: In some cells, RIBEYE was imaged first and GFP second, and vice versa in others.

Correction of the data for drift was performed by fiducial bead–based registration using the iterative closest point (ICP) algorithm. Corresponding bead positions across datasets, used for MINFLUX beamline monitoring (MBM), were set as target and source before and after buffer change, respectively. Beads used for MBM correction and present in both datasets were selected. The ICP was implemented using a custom Python script: After target and source bead association, consecutive rigid transforms were applied until the root mean square error of bead displacements postalignment was less than half a nanometer. The reference matrices for performing the rigid transforms were then applied to the MINFLUX localizations for measurement alignment.

For data analysis, we used corrected coordinates after EFO and CFR filtering for both GFP and RIBEYE localizations, as described above. Localization centroids for GFP and RIBEYE were estimated by aggregating individual localizations based on their TIDs, as described above. We then plotted the distribution of RIBEYE-GFP centroid pairs across pairing distances ranging from 0 to 80 nm and compared it to a distribution of RIBEYE localizations paired with randomly simulated points (100 simulations) within the region of interest space, including an error envelope. Within the 15- to 35-nm range (test range), the two distributions showed significant difference. We calculated the effective labeling efficiency of the RIBEYE primary antibody relative to the GFP nanobody by taking the ratio of the number of RIBEYE-GFP centroid pairs to the total number of GFP centroids for pairing distance thresholds of 15, 20, 25, 30, and 35 nm.

Biophysical modeling of CaV1.3 nanoclusters

Estimation of the fraction of released RRP

On the basis of the depletion time constant of RRP of high- and low-SR fibers from a previous study (82), we estimated the fraction of RRP released within 2 ms (FRRP) using the following power-law equation. Assuming that the RRP depletion follows a power-lawRRP(t)=RRP0e−ktwhere RRP0 is the initial number of RRP, t represents time, and k is the rate constant defined as the inverse of time constant ( 1τ ). Using this model, the fraction of RRP released within 2 ms was calculated asFRRP=1−e−kt

For high SR, the depletion time constant (τ) was estimated to be 6.347 ± 1.096 ms, and for low SR, the time constant was 20.88 ± 5.063 ms (82). Accordingly, the range of FRRP at 2 ms is from 0.09 (low SR) to 0.27 (high SR).

Stochastic simulations of Ca2+ reaction diffusion and SV release

We implemented stochastic simulations of Ca2+ reaction diffusion dynamics and SV release using MCell 3.4 (80). A presynaptic AZ model was constructed on the basis of a rectangular stripe–shaped Ca2+ channel cluster (410 nm by 135 nm) with 96 Ca2+ channels and 12 SVs. Two coupling scenarios, random distribution and nanocluster distribution, were modeled to investigate spatial dependencies in SV release. Ca2+ channel gating was modeled with a three-state Markov chain with a parameter set from the previous IHC AZ experimental data (18). For Ca2+-triggered vesicle fusion kinetics, a seven-state Markov chain model was used with a five Ca2+ binding steps, followed by a vesicle fusion (41). The refilling rate was additionally set on the basis of the experimental data, showing the sustained release rate (76). The space of the simulated AZ was defined as a hemisphere with a diameter of 1 μm, considering that the mean of the nearest-neighbor distance of synapses is around 2 μm (65). Within the space, fixed and mobile buffers were included (see table S3). The fixed buffer concentration was set to 610 μM, and adenosine 5′-triphosphate was included with a concentration of 68 μM. EGTA and 1,2-bis(2-aminophenoxy)ethane-N,N,N′,N′-tetraacetic acid (BAPTA) were set to 800 and 400 μM, respectively, to reflect Ca2+-buffering condition in IHCs with both fast and slow binding kinetics (18). The bottom of the space was reflective to molecules, the spherical boundary of the space was absorptive for Ca2+ ions, and we clamped the concentration of mobile buffers BAPTA and EGTA at this surface to emulate the refilling of Ca2+-free buffers upon Ca2+ influx. The clamped concentration was set for both unbound and bound buffers where bound concentration was calculated by multiplying the buffer concentration with bound fraction [[Ca2+] · Kon / (Koff + [Ca2+] · Kon)], and unbound fraction was calculated as 1 − bound fraction. The single Ca2+ current was 0.137 pA (35, 118) with the open channel probability of 0.4, which is the half of the experimental data, considering the absence of Bay K8644 under the current simulation condition (18, 35). Each coupling condition was simulated with 10 different scenarios, and each scenario was repeated 100 times (total of 1000 simulation iterations for each scenario). Each simulation had a duration of 2 ms with a time step of 10 ns to ensure that the average diffusion distance of Ca2+ at every time step is within single digit nanometer (~3.37 nm computed via 24D∆t/π ). For each iteration, we counted each SV release event when one of SV Ca2+ sensors triggered release.

Statistical testing

All statistical analyses to test significant difference in FRRP were performed using Python packages. We first assessed the normality of each dataset using the Shapiro-Wilk test, given the small sample size, and the results indicated that each dataset followed a normal distribution. For comparisons between the random and nanocluster conditions at the same d value, we performed a two-sample t test with equal variance, which was tested with Levene’s test. For comparisons between three d values within the same condition, we first conducted a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), which was followed by pairwise t tests. For multiple comparisons, we applied the Bonferroni correction. For comparing the median of the effective coupling distance between random and nanocluster, we used a Mood’s median test. For comparing the weighted median of the effective coupling, we first performed a bootstrap resampling (N = 100) by weighting with the number of release events. Then, we compared effective coupling distances between two groups with a two-sample t test on the distributions of bootstrapped weighted medians.
