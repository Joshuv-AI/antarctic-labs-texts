The minor spliceosome component U4atac regulates JAK/STAT signaling to modulate hematopoiesis and immune responses in Drosophila melanogaster
Authors: Dania Shikara, Eden Bishop, Nathan Barton, Stephanie Makdissi, Senthilkumar Kailasam, Attila György, Daria E Siekhaus, Maria Carla Borroto, Philippe M Campeau, Linda Vong, Chaim M Roifman, Brendon D Parsons, Francesca Di Cara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe small nuclear RNA U4atac is a core component of the minor spliceosome. In humans, homozygous or compound heterozygous point mutations in U4atac cause rare developmental disorders, such as Roifman syndrome, characterized by growth restriction, brain anomalies, and immune deficiency. To better define the pathophysiological role of U4atac mutations, we here establish a model of minor spliceosome dysfunction by generating a Drosophila melanogaster CRISPR/Cas9-induced U4atac mutant in the highly conserved stem II region. U4atac homozygous mutants exhibit growth and neurodevelopmental defects, immunodeficiency, and gastrointestinal symptoms. Using bulk RNA-sequencing and functional assays, we reveal that mutations in U4atac affect the splicing of a large set of transcripts involved in innate immunity, hematopoiesis, and intestinal cell functions, including the Drosophila Janus kinase (JAK) homolog hopscotch (hop). Importantly, U4atac deficiency reduces Hop expression and causes Hop-related hematopoietic defects at the embryonic and larval stages. Notably, we also observe reduced expression of Jak1 and attenuated activation of downstream signaling in patients with Roifman syndrome. Thus, our work identifies alterations of Jak signaling as part of the pathogenesis of RNU4atac-opathy.Subject terms: Disease model, Gene expression, Haematopoiesis

Introduction

Splicing of precursor messenger RNA (pre-mRNA) is an evolutionarily conserved process in all eukaryotes that is necessary for RNA maturation and, consequently, gene expression1.

The process is catalyzed by the spliceosome, a large complex formed by five small nuclear RNA (snRNA) and more than 100 proteins that undergo multiple dynamic rearrangements2,3.

Most metazoan and a few fungi species have two parallel but analogous splicing machineries: the major spliceosome or U2-dependent spliceosome and the minor spliceosome or U12-dependent spliceosome4–7. The significant differences between these two spliceosomes lie in the snRNA components. While the major spliceosome relies on U1, U2, U4, U5, and U6 snRNA, the minor spliceosome uses U11, U12, U4atac, U6atac and the shared U5 snRNA. Generally, many proteins of the spliceosomes appear to be shared; however, the protein components of the minor spliceosome remain largely undetermined due to their cellular scarcity and instability8.

More than 99.5% of introns, the U2-type introns, are recognized and removed by the major spliceosome in metazoans9, while only a small fraction of introns, U12-type introns, are removed by the minor spliceosome4,10–13. U2- and U12-type introns can be distinguished by the sequence of their conserved recognition elements, including the 5’ splice site (5’ss), 3’ splice site (3’ss), and branch point sequence (BPS)12,14,15. Whereas U2-type introns exhibit relatively weak conservation at BPS, U12-type introns display highly conserved sequence motifs.

Intriguingly, some introns harbor either tandem or overlapping splice sites that enable intron recognition by both major and minor spliceosomes to produce alternative transcripts that can arise from collaboration or competition between the two complexes16–18. Global transcriptome analyses from invertebrates to plants19–21 with compromised minor spliceosome functions have led to the identification of U12-type intron retention events resulting from splicing defects. However, several studies have also demonstrated that the splicing defects are not limited to U12-type introns but can also spread to the flanking U2-type introns in a subset of mRNAs22–26. Thus, the effect of the minor spliceosome extends beyond the removal of minor introns, potentially leading to alternative splicing. This can result in the generation of new transcripts that produce truncated proteins or new products, leading to altered cellular function and pathology.

Indeed, defects in components of the minor spliceosome in humans cause the onset of complex developmental disorders and are linked to autoimmune diseases, neurodegeneration, and cancer27–30.

To date, six human rare diseases have been attributed to mutations in specific components of the minor spliceosome29. Three of these involve components of the U11/U12 di-snRNP complex, namely the U11/U12-65K protein22, U12 snRNA31, and Urp protein (ZRSR2)24; while three diseases known as RNU4atac-opathy are attributed to mutations in the U4atac snRNA20,32,33. Although the underlying pathophysiology of these diseases reflects shared disruptions to minor splicing, their clinical manifestations range from overlapping features to distinct syndrome-specific phenotypes29. Notably, even mutations affecting a different region of the same snRNA can lead to distinct diseases. For instance, homozygous or compound heterozygous mutations in the snRNA U4atac have been linked to the onset of three types of rare diseases: Taybi-Linder Syndrome/Microcephalic Osteodysplastic Primordial Dwarfism type 1 (TALS/MOPD1)20,33, Roifman syndrome (RS) and recently Lowry-Wood syndrome (LWS)34. In TALS/MOPD1, mutations are mostly in the 5’ Stem-Loop of snU4atac RNA, whereas in RS, they localize to highly conserved positions of the U4atac Stem II region35, or one variant is found in the Stem II region and the second variant is located in the 5′ stem-Loop, and in LWS, mutations occur mainly in the 3’ Stem-Loop. Clinically TALS/MOPD1 presents with severe developmental defects and early lethality33,36; RS is characterized by prenatal and postnatal growth retardation, humoral immune deficiencies37, neurologic development, post-/prenatal growth, and skeletal anomalies32 without typically resulting in early death. LWS is even less severe and produces a combination of intellectual disability with microcephaly, congenital nystagmus, and a skeletal dysplasia34,38. An open question is whether the defects observed in all these disorders are due to a large-scale reduction in the expression of U12-type intron-containing genes, an overall global impact on both U2-type and U12-type intron-containing genes caused by the cooperation between the 2 spliceosomes, or to tissue-specific effects of alteration in the expression of distinct U2-type and U12-type genes.

In this work, we use a CRISPR/Cas9-inducible Drosophila melanogaster line targeting the U4atac gene to demonstrate the impact of U4atac on the global embryonic transcriptome. We report 1334 genes and reveal two novel transcripts with retained introns, and seventy-five transcripts that are alternatively spliced in the U4atac Drosophila mutants. Some altered genes belong to functional categories previously unlinked to minor spliceosome function, such as innate immunity, global hematopoiesis, and digestive tract morphogenesis.

In addition, we identify JAK/STAT as a signaling pathway defective in the Drosophila U4atac mutant and demonstrate alterations in this pathway in RS patient samples. Thus, our study broadens the view of U4atac and the minor spliceosome’s impact on global splicing events and provides a plausible molecular lead for the hematopoietic and immune defects described in RNU4atac-opathy TALS/MOPD1 and RS patients, offering new insights to inform patient treatment.

Results

CRISPR/Cas9-induced point mutations in the promoter and stem II regions of the Drosophila melanogaster U4atac snRNA result in growth defects and developmental arrest

We wished to determine whether the predicted snRNA U4atac gene in Drosophila has a role in splicing and, thus, confirm a conserved function from invertebrates to vertebrates for this component of the minor spliceosome complex. We engineered a CRISPR/Cas9 inducible Drosophila strain to target the genomic region corresponding to the highly conserved U4atac stem II region, where homozygous mutations associated with RS pathology in humans are found (Fig. 1a). Drosophila and human U4atac sequences have over 55% sequence identity, with the stem II region exhibiting 93% identity (Fig. 1b)39,40. We initially triggered the mutation globally using a Cas9 expressed under the Actin5C promoter (Act5C-Cas9), which is active in all tissues and stages. We crossed Act5C-Cas9 virgin females to homozygous males carrying in their genome the recombinant transgene pCFD541 expressing the sgRNA targeting U4atac under the U6 tRNA promoter (strain indicated as v1). Crosses to males carrying the empty pCFD5 vector served as a control for potential genotoxic stress caused by Cas9 activation42.

The F1 was crossed to the background parental strain v- carrying the balancer TM3,sb to remove the Cas9 and potential off-target effects. Sanger sequencing identified flies with a defined mutation in the stem II region of U4atac. All isolated independent lines carried the same germline mutation, containing two-point mutations in the first 10 nucleotides of U4atac (nt + 1 and + 10), with one mutation (nt + 10) falling into the stem II region and eight-point mutations in the proximal promoter region of U4atac (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1a). We backcrossed the mutant lines to the background parental strain v- and maintained them as stocks with the mutated chromosome over a GFP marked balancer and crossed them to generate homozygous U4atac-/- progeny. QRT-PCR analysis revealed a 50% reduction in the U4atac transcript in stage 4 homozygous U4atac-/- embryos compared to the U4atac+/+ control obtained from the original control strain ActCas9 > pCFD5 backcrossed 5 times to v- (Supplementary Fig. 1b). We assessed the effects of these reductions in U4atac on development. While U4atac+/+ (obtained from the original control strain ActCas9 > pCDF5 backcrossed 5 times to v-) and U4atac-/+ embryos develop normally and reach the adult stage, homozygous mutant embryos (sorted as GFP-negative animals, Supplementary Fig. 1c, d) predominantly die during embryogenesis, with few animals surviving to larval and pupal stages, and no animal reaching the adult stage (Fig. 1c). QRT-PCR analyses showed that U4atac-/- embryos at stages 8 and 15 exhibit a progressively significant reduction in U4atac transcript compared to the U4atac−/+ embryo controls (Fig. 1d), similar to what was observed in patients affected by RNU4atac-opathies, such as MOPD1/TALS and RS43. QRT-PCR assays also determined that the few surviving U4atac-/- larvae (sorted as GFP-negative animals, Supplementary Fig. 1e) display U4atac transcript levels comparable to heterozygous larvae, potentially due to individual variability in the penetrance of the promoter mutations or the efficiency of the elimination of maternal RNA contribution (Fig. 1d–f). U4atac-/- mutants that reached the larval stage remained small compared to age-matched heterozygous larvae (Fig. 1e), with a few larvae prematurely attempting pupation and dying at this stage (Fig. 1e, g). Around 20% of these escaping U4atac-/- larvae survive to the pupal stage, but they fail to eclose as adults (Fig. 1g). Importantly, a similar developmental phenotype is seen reproducibly in independent U4atac-/- mutant lines (Supplementary Fig. 2a, b).

Expression of Drosophila U4atac (DmU4atac) under the Act5C-GAL4 ubiquitous promoter led to a complete rescue of the developmental arrest with male and female U4atac-/- animals eclosing as normal adults (Fig. 1h, i). Moreover, the U4atac-/- developmental arrest could also be rescued by ubiquitous expression of the human U4atac (HsU4atac) (Fig. 1h). QRT-PCR assays using species-specific primers detected high expression of DmU4atac or HsU4atac, respectively, in the rescued animals (Fig. 1j, k). Thus, our strategy isolated a molecularly defined U4atac snRNA mutant essential for the growth and survival of developing Drosophila melanogaster.

U4atac-/- exhibits neurodevelopmental and hematopoietic defects

In humans, mutations in the 5’ stem-loop region of U4atac snRNA reduce its function and also its stability, leading to a more severe form of RNU4atac-opathy, TALS/MOPD1, which is characterized by intrauterine and postnatal growth retardation, developmental defects in several organs, immunodeficiency, and death typically within 3 years after birth29,44–46; however, less severe developmental defects and/or survival to at least 12 years of age or even adulthood have been reported. On the other hand, homozygous or compound heterozygous mutations in the stem II region, or biallelic mutations in the stem II region and 5’stem-loop region of the U4atac gene, lead to RS characterized by poor pre- and post-natal growth, spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia, cognitive delay, and immunological defects29,32,39,44. Since our CRISPR-Cas9 Drosophila mutant carries an homozygous point mutations in the stem II region of U4atac (nt+10) that is linked to RS but also extends to its nt +1 (conserved in the human RNU4atac) and to the proximal promoter region, reducing U4atac amount, which occurs in TALS/MOPD132, we sought to determine if the phenotypes observed in the U4atac-/- embryos resemble RS and/or TALS/MOPD1. To probe whether our U4atac mutation leads to neurodevelopmental defects, we performed indirect immunofluorescence (IF) experiments on embryos at stages 12-15 of embryogenesis, when neurogenesis occurs. We evaluated the central nervous system (CNS) and peripheral nervous system (PNS) organization in U4atac-/- embryos compared to age-matched U4ata+/+ and U4atac-/+ control embryos using anti-Futsch, anti-embryonic lethal abnormal visual system (ELAV), and anti-neuroglian (Nrg) as markers for both CNS and PNS. We observed that both the PNS and the ventral nerve cord of the CNS were disorganized in U4atac-/- embryos (selected as GFP-negative, Supplementary Fig. 3a, b), consistent with the cognitive delays seen in RS but appearing developmentally more severe and therefore similar to TALS/MOPD1 (Fig. 2a, b).

RS patients present with defects in humoral immunity39,47 and TALS/MOPD1 patients can present with severe anemia or immunodeficiency40,48. Interestingly, U4atac-/- mutants that survive to the larval stage exhibit necrotic blood cells at II-III instar larva (Fig. 2c, d). Melanotic tissue and larger melanotic masses were visible in various parts of the larval body and around the gastrointestinal tract in 20% of the mutant wandering larvae (Fig. 2e, f). Of note, the formation of melanotic masses has been linked to the alteration of genes involved in hematopoiesis and immunity49. In conclusion, mutating U4atac in Drosophila causes developmental defects that include neurodevelopmental abnormalities of the CNS and PNS, as well as irregular hematopoiesis with accumulation of necrotic cells/tissue surrounding the intestinal tract, analogous to many of the phenotypes seen in both RS and TALS/MOPD1 patients. Our Drosophila mutants, therefore, represent a good model for defining the requirement of U4atac in animal developmental physiology and for initial mechanistic studies of RNU4atac-pathies.

RNAseq study and gene enrichment analysis suggest a requirement for U4atac in immune and intestinal epithelial cell function

We sought to identify genes and pathways altered by our mutation in the minor spliceosome snRNA U4atac and linked to the specific phenotypes observed in the Drosophila mutant. Minor spliceosome components are ubiquitously expressed, yet the restricted phenotypes observed in mutants in these components26,50,51 may reflect a higher requirement for regulation of the minor spliceosome-mediated transcripts in distinct tissues. Moreover, these phenotypes could be caused by errors in the removal of minor introns, as well as by defects in the generation of alternative splicing induced by the cooperation and competition between the major and minor spliceosomes on major, minor, major-like, and minor-like introns9,26,51. To define how splicing is affected in U4atac-/- and, more broadly, how such defects could impact the global transcriptome in developing embryos, we performed RNA sequencing analysis of stage 8–14 U4atac-/- and U4atac-/+. We read about 60 M reads per sample and mapped them to the Drosophila genome. Using STAR and edgeR, we identified 1334 significantly differentially expressed genes between U4atac-/- versus U4atac-/+ (p < 0.05) (Supplementary Data 1). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) and Cytoscape52 highlighted changes in cellular processes such as RNA metabolism, including RNA splicing, regulation of the mitotic cycle, organ homeostasis and cilium assembly, that were processes previously reported to be linked to mutations in genes encoding snRNA or protein of the minor spliceosome machinery in other organisms, including MOPD1/TALS and RS patients (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Data 2)26,44,51,53–55, supporting the reliability of our screen. The cluster enrichment analysis also identified neurogenesis and digestive tract development as significantly underrepresented processes and the regulation of innate immunity and hematopoiesis as significantly overrepresented processes. This was particularly interesting as several of these tissues were affected in the U4atac-/- mutant during development (Fig. 2).

To further validate the GSEA results, we induced a CRISPR-specific mutation of U4atac solely in immune cells in their filial generation 1 (F1). We crossed males expressing the sgRNA that targets U4atac with virgin flies (srpHemo-Cas9) expressing Cas9 specifically in hemocytes, the primary immune cells of Drosophila56,57. Both male and female F1 flies survived to adulthood and were as healthy as the age-matched control flies carrying srpHemo-Cas9 along with the pCFD5 empty vector used to create the v1 construct expressing U4atac sgRNA (v1) (Fig. 4a, b). However, upon systemic infection via thoracic injection with a natural bacterial pathogen of Drosophila, Erwinia corotorova corotorova (Ecc15), which triggers a potent immune response58, the srpHemo-Cas9; v1 flies displayed greater susceptibility compared to sex and age-matched controls (Fig. 4a, b). This suggests that the mutating U4atac exclusively in hemocytes reduces the ability of these immune cells to fight systemic bacterial infections.

To determine whether U4atac is also required to regulate genes involved in intestinal tract development and/or function, as GSEA indicates, we utilized an intestinal epithelial cell-specific driver, Mex-GAL459, to induce a Cas9 mutation in U4atac. Many Mex > Cas9; v1 and Mex > Cas9; pCFD5 (control) animals could develop to adulthood. However, 20% showed overgrowth and were arrested at the pupal stage (Fig. 4c, d). We tested gut physiology and measured bowel activity60 in adult male and female Mex > Cas9; v1 and age-matched control Mex > Cas9; pCFD5 adult flies by analyzing the color and area of their feces deposited on white plugs 6 h after they were fed a 24 h blue dye-supplemented diet. Densitometric analyses of the blue color area on the white plugs indicated that both male and female Mex > Cas9; v1, which carry a mutation in U4atac (Supplementary Fig. 3), expel blue dyes faster than age and sex-matched Mex > Cas9; pCFD5 and Mex > Cas9; pCFD5 treated with 2% SDS and used as a negative control, as their intestines burst and they do not defecate (Fig. 4e, f). These results suggest that mutating U4atac in the Drosophila gut can sometimes arrest development and speed gut content expulsion.

Changes in gut permeability can affect bowel movements, potentially leading to constipation or diarrhea61,62. To test if gut permeability was altered in male and female Mex > Cas9; v1 compared to Mex > Cas9; pCFD5 controls at 15 days of age, we used a well-established approach. In this Smurf assay63, a nonabsorbable blue food dye reveals leakage through the digestive tract into the body cavity, by turning the animal partially or fully blue after feeding, depending on the extent of gut permeability. Both male and female Mex > Cas9; v1 and Mex > Cas9; pCFD5 flies showed blue-colored food retained in the intestine (Fig. 4g, h). Mex > Cas9; pCFD5 flies fed for 24 h with sucrose in 2% SDS solution to trigger epithelial barrier damage were used as a positive control for increased permeability and exhibited the blue dye throughout their body (Fig. 4g, h). Thus, a mutation in U4atac in intestinal epithelial cells partially impacts animal growth and development, speeding gut content expulsion, but does not alter epithelial barrier permeability.

In conclusion, tissue-specific mutation of U4atac in immune cells or the intestinal epithelium revealed the importance of the minor spliceosome for cellular response to systemic infection and bowel motility, respectively. This confirms the findings of the GSEA analyses and indicates the importance of U4atac snRNA and the minor spliceosome for development and organ-specific physiological functions.

Identification of minor-spliceosome sensitive genes in U4atac-/- embryos

Following RNAseq analyses of mutated U4atac-/- embryos, we used the SpliceWiz64, SUPPA265, and IRfinder66 tools to identify intron retention events (IR) (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b). SpliceWiz and IRfinder predicted intron retention (IR) in 96 genes (Supplementary Data 3–7), a higher number than the 19 Drosophila melanogaster genes predicted to contain minor introns (MIGs) in recent studies (19 genes)19,50,51. Therefore, we used SUPPA2 with increased stringency and found genes that were previously identified as MIGs (e.g., prospero and nemo) and 29 genes with predicted IR events in U4atac-/- (Supplementary Data 5) and not previously classified as minor intron-containing genes (e.g., dila, Supplementary Fig. 5a, b) in Drosophila melanogaster67–70. Most of the 29 genes were predicted to have major-like or non-canonical types of introns rather than minor introns (Supplementary Data 5) according to the Minor Intron Database (MIDB), which identifies minor introns based on the nucleic acid sequence at their splice-sites using position-weight matrices51; this suggests that they might have nested intron types and that the IR event might be caused by failure in competition or cooperation between the minor and the major spliceosome9,26,51,69. However, six of these genes did have strong IR prediction scores, with four also identified as minor intron-containing genes by MIDB.

To verify the reliability of the retention events predicted to occur in four of the six new genes identified by SUPPA2 compared to validated minor-intron containing genes such as prospero19, we conducted RT-PCR-specific assays on total RNA extracted from U4atac-/- and U4atac-/+ embryos. Gel electrophoresis analysis showed that the transcript encoding Hsc70-1, a chaperone protein involved in protein refolding, retains intron 3 in U4atac-/- compared with U4atac-/+ embryos (Fig. 5a, b). In addition, the transcript of the gene dila, which encodes for a protein with a role in cilium/flagellum formation, retained intron 1 in U4atac-/- and did not in U4atac-/+ embryos (Fig. 5c). On the other hand, RT-PCR assays and gel electrophoresis analyses did not confirm the intron retention events predicted by SUPPA2 in Cdk2 and CG1569 transcripts (Fig. 5d, e). To demonstrate the efficacy of our assay design, we used the retention of intron 2 of prospero as a positive control, which has been shown to be a minor intron19 (Fig. 5f). We also used primers to detect intron splicing in the U2-spliceosome-dependent RpL23 transcript to normalize our assays (Fig. 5g). Altogether, our IR analyses identified and molecularly validated two new genes that depend on U4atac RNA for their correct splicing. Two predicted intron-containing transcripts were not validated; RNAseq is a powerful, high-throughput technique, but it also involves many technical and biological factors that can create variability71,72. As with any screen, hits must be validated with complementary molecular assays.

Given the recent finding that RS patients present defects in cilia formation44, our identification of dila validates our analysis pipeline and our capacity to identify new genes that require the minor spliceosome to produce a functional product.

Mutating U4atac leads to various alternative splicing events during embryogenesis

Alternative splicing events can be difficult to predict. Minor and major spliceosomes compete in genes that have both types of introns26,51; some introns even have no canonical sites for any spliceosome or have sequences that can be recognized by both spliceosomes (i.e., twintrons)73. Indeed, many minor-spliceosome-dependent genes in the human and mouse genomes were only identified in recent studies using more sophisticated prediction matrices51 after remaining hidden in earlier studies74,75. To complement these newer predictive approaches, we again utilized our RNAseq data from our minor spliceosome mutant to globally explore the alternative splicing events occurring in U4atac-/- and U4atac-/+ embryos; we determined differential exon usage based on exon counts of transcripts by SpliceWiz and DEXseq76.

SpliceWiz defines alternative splicing events (ASEs) apart from IRs as binary events between two possibilities, the included and excluded isoforms. This approach allows for the detection and measurement of alternative 5’/3’ splice site usage (A5SS / A3SS), alternate first / last exon usage (AFE / ALE), mutually-exclusive exons (MXE), and skipped (cassette) exons (SE), based on annotated U2 and U12 spliceosome target sequences (Supplementary Data 3, 4). SpliceWiz identified 1115 statistically significant ASEs in U4atac-/- versus U4atac-/+ embryos (Supplementary Data 3), including all types of ASEs listed above (Supplementary Figs. 4B, 5A, B). We identified alternative splicing events in genes that had already been reported in other studies51, as well as in genes previously not predicted to have minor introns51. DEXseq calculations of exon usage per transcript helped us identify alternative splicing and differential transcript usage (Supplementary Data 8, 9). We performed gene set enrichment analyses against the 74 transcripts we identified with this analysis as displaying significant differential exon usage in U4atac-/- versus U4atac-/+ embryos (Supplementary Data 10 and Supplementary Fig. 6). 22 of the 74 genes have roles in organogenesis throughout development (e.g., smog, nicastrin and StacI). All 74 genes are under-represented in U4atac-/- dataset, and belong to the categories of “developmental processes”, “cell differentiation”,”primary metabolism”, “gene expression”, “response to stimulus”, and “nitrogen metabolism”, indicating a downregulation of these processes in U4atac-/- embryos versus age-matched U4atac-/+ embryos because of defects in alternative splicing.

To validate the differential exon abundance in specific transcripts identified by the DEXseq analyses, we examined transcripts of genes linked to some of the major phenotypes observed in the U4atac-/- mutant, such as neurodevelopment, hematopoiesis, cell division, or organogenesis defects. We queried the abundance of the predicted affected exon relative to other exons predicted to be unaffected. In transcripts from two genes involved in neural function or development, we found differential effects. Exon 11 in stac1, which encodes a protein involved in calcium transport, was at higher abundance in the U4atac-/- mutant, while a control exon 10 was unaffected (Fig. 6a). Exon 5 in poe, which affects peripheral nervous system formation, was at lower levels in the U4atac-/- mutant, while control exon 1 remained the same (Fig. 6b). Thus, U4atac is required both to remove and utilize specific exons from transcripts in the nervous system.

In transcripts from two genes affecting development and immune function, U4atac-/- mutant exhibited a lower utilization of a specific exon compared to others in the same transcript. This occurred for exon 7 but not the control exon 11 in smog, which encodes a protein important for mesodermal development and gastrulation, and therefore for the healthy completion of embryogenesis and intestinal morphogenesis77 (Fig. 6c). This also occurred for exon 4 but not control exon 10 in the gene hop, which encodes the ortholog of the mammalian JAK1/2 genes, which are involved in hematopoiesis78, immune responses, and embryonic development79,80 (Fig. 6d). Notably, SpliceWiz analyses indicated the presence of A3SS in exon 4. Thus, U4atac and the minor spliceosome are also required for the production of transcripts of genes controlling organogenesis and immunity, such as smog and hop.

Only for one transcript, dj-1β, which encodes a protein required for synaptic plasticity and development81, the QRT-PCR analysis did not concur with DEXseq analysis’s predictions. These digital analyses had suggested that exon 2 is specifically absent in the dj-1β transcript in U4atac-/- embryos. SpliceWiz had also predicted A3SS in this exonic region (Supplementary Data 3). However, our QRT-PCR targeting exon 1-2 or exon 3-4 (control) showed that both exon amplicons were reduced in U4atac-/- compared with U4atac-/+embryos (Fig. 6e), suggesting that instead of dj-1β splicing being affected in the U4atac mutant, the entire gene is downregulated.

Finally, as a negative control, we also quantified the abundance of two exons of the gene eIF2α, which require the minor spliceosome in mice and humans but not in flies51,74, and confirmed no differences in eIF2α splicing in U4atac-/- compared with U4atac-/+embryos (Fig. 6f).

Together, these analyses argue that a mutation in the snRNA U4atac can lead to alternative splicing events that both include or exclude particular exons. The events identified by the bioinformatic platform need to be fully validated by alternative molecular approaches; however, our QRT-PCR of selected genes of interest confirmed the majority of our selected hits. Therefore, our RNAseq of the U4atac-/- and U4atac-/+ embryos represents a valuable resource to define the broad impact of the minor spliceosome beyond IR in Drosophila development.

Identification of candidate genes responsible for one or more phenotypes observed in U4atac-/-

We aimed to determine if splicing defects in some of the gene transcripts validated above could explain one or more of the phenotypes observed in the global, hemocyte-specific, or gut-specific U4atac-/-. Following our long-standing interest in immunity, we investigated the effects of altered hop splicing on U4atac-/- immune cell development and/or function. Mutations in hop were previously linked to developmental defects during embryogenesis and to the regulation of cell cycle, immune cell development and functions, and intestinal regeneration79,82–87. Moreover, hop mutants are linked to a lymphoma-like phenotype in Drosophila larvae, leading to the formation of melanotic masses (Drosophila blood tumors)78,88–90, a phenotype observed in U4atac-/- animals that survive to the larval stage. In addition, two human orthologues of hop (JAK1 and JAK2) are transcriptionally misregulated in PBMC from TALS/MOPD1 and RS patients26. In sum, hop has been previously linked to many of the phenotypes observed in Drosophila, and patients carrying mutations in U4atac-/- show alterations in hop ortholog expression.

Therefore, we focused on this gene in Drosophila, and depleted hop ubiquitously by double-stranded RNA interference (RNAi) using the Gal4/UAS system91 (Act5C > hop-i). Developmental survival analysis showed that the majority of Act5C > hop-i animals died at the embryonic stage (Fig. 6g); only 30% reached the larval stage, and a smaller percentage attempted pupation compared to control Act5C > GFP-i, similar to what was observed for U4atac-/- embryos. Moreover, the few Act5C > hop-i animals that reached larval stages of development exhibited melanotic masses at a similar proportion to that observed in U4atac-/- mutant larvae (Fig. 6h, i). Of the very few Act5C > hop-i animals that reached pupation, all exhibited melanotic masses (Fig. 6j, k). These results confirm previous findings discussed above that depletion of hop leads to phenotypes similar to those seen in U4atac-/- mutant.

These results suggest that a defect in alternative splicing that generates a hop transcript lacking exon 4 in U4atac-/- animals is linked to many of the phenotypes observed in U4atac-/-. To explore this, we ectopically expressed a previously validated hop wild-type cDNA88 and followed development. We found that the ectopic expression of wild-type hop cDNA (hopOE) containing exon 4 under the Actin5C ubiquitous promoter using the Act5C-GAL4/ UAS system in the U4atac-/- flies and observed partial rescue of development to the adult stage (Fig. 7a). To test if the decreased levels of hop transcript expression also observed in the U4atac-/- animals could be deleterious, we depleted hop by RNAi targeting exon 5 of the transcript in wild-type and U4atac-/- background. No animals developed to the adult stage upon hop depletion in a wild-type background, and no increases or decreases in developmental defects occurred when hop was depleted in U4atac-/- alone. These findings argue that the absence of the normal hop transcript that contains exon 4 (nt 1431-1750 of CDS) underlies much of the U4atac-/- phenotype. Interestingly, this exon encodes the atypical SH2 domain of Hop (residue 478-584), essential to mediate the signaling cascade78,92 and upstream of the two kinase motifs (Supplementary Fig. 6B). Thus, depletion of the function of the minor spliceosome leads either to a truncated Hop protein or a protein lacking the SH2 region, either of which would reduce normal JAK/STAT signal transduction. We probed the size of Hop-GFP recombinant protein expressed by a fosmid comprising the tagged gene and most of its regulatory information93 in U4atac-/- and U4atac-/+ genetic backgrounds. Western blotting analysis using anti-GFP antibody showed a strong band at around 160 kDa representing the Hop-GFP recombinant protein (133 kDa Hop +28 kDa GFP) in U4atac-/+; this band was remarkably reduced or absent in the U4atac-/- embryonic protein lysate, whereas the actin or tubulin loading controls showed no difference. This data suggests that the alternatively spliced hop transcript found prevalent in U4atac-/- embryos leads to reduced amounts of full-length Hop protein (Fig. 7b, c, and Source Data File for full-size blots and replicates).

Finally, to more directly assess the involvement of the minor spliceosome in splicing exon 4 from the hop transcript, we performed RNA-protein co-immunoprecipitation from wild-type Stages 8–14 embryos expressing an HA-tagged version of the Drosophila Small nuclear ribonucleoprotein U11/U12 subunit 65 (known as 65 kDa protein). This protein is predicted to enable U12 snRNA-binding activity and pre-mRNA intronic binding activity, and thus be involved in alternative mRNA splicing via the minor spliceosome. We conducted immunoprecipitation using an HA antibody to pull down the HA-65kDa-RNA complex, and verified with QRT-PCR that U12 snRNA was enriched in the immunoprecipitate, and Rpl23, a gene that is known to be spliced by the major spliceosome, was not (Fig. 7d, e). QRT-PCR against hop exon 4 detected hop transcript enrichment in the HA-65kDa immunoprecipitate but not in the IgG negative control (Fig. 7e).

Altogether, our data show that the minor spliceosome is required for proper splicing of the hop transcript, and that Hop expression is reduced in U4atac-/-, explaining the similarity in the phenotypes observed in U4atac-/- and Act5C>hop-i.

U4atac mutation leads to hop-linked blood defects

To investigate the connection between U4atac and Hop in the hemopoietic compartment, we selectively depleted these two genes in hemocytes (srpHemo-Cas9; v1). In extracts from adults, we compared the number of blood cells from srpHemo-Cas9; v1 to those from control strains and flies with hop specifically RNAi depleted or ectopically expressed in hemocytes using Hml-GAL4 (Fig. 7f). Hemocyte number was lower in 7-day-old adult males or females from srpHemo-Cas9; v1, hml > hop-i and hml > hopOE adults than from srpHemo-Cas9; pCFD5 or hml-GAL4;+ control strains (Fig. 7f). The reduction in hemocyte numbers caused by the selective deletion of U4atac in the srpHemo-Cas9 > v1 strain could be rescued through hemocyte-specific ectopic expression of hop cDNA (srpHemo-Cas9; v1/hmlGal4 > hopOE), arguing that U4atac’s critical function in these cells is to produce a tuned amount of hop cDNA containing exon 4. This data suggests that both mutating the snRNA U4atac or altering hop expression leads to hematopoietic defects due to altered Hop signaling.

We sought to determine if the reduction in hemocyte numbers in the srpHemo-Cas9; v1 strain affects cell-mediated immune responses to systemic infection (Fig. 7g). We pricked the thoraxes of flies with gram-negative bacteria Ecc15 and compared the survival of srpHemo-Cas9 > v1 flies that have hemocyte-specific U4atac deletion to the control strain, srpHemo-Cas9 > pCFD5. Flies lacking U4atac in hemocytes were susceptible to Ecc15 infection, and this susceptibility could be rescued to control levels by ectopic hop expression in hemocytes (srpHemo-Cas9 > v1; hml > hopOE) (Fig. 7g). Notably, colony-forming unit analyses (C.F.U.) of the bacteria extracted 24 hours after infection from the flies of each genotype revealed no significant change in the bacterial loads (Fig. 7h). This result is consistent with previously reported data showing that Hop depletion does not affect bacterial clearance in C.F.U. assay as JAK/STAT signaling controls tolerance more than clearance in adult Drosophila melanogaster90.

Together, our data indicate that snRNA U4atac is required for normal hematopoiesis in Drosophila during the embryonic and larval stages, thereby increasing the number of circulating immune cells. U4atac additionally strengthens the cellular immune response to systemic infection in adults. Much of this effect appears due to a requirement for U4atac to differentially splice out exon 4 from the Drosophila JAK/STAT component hop’s transcript to produce a functional protein in hemocytes.

Jak1 protein is affected in the of an RS patient

Mutations in hop have been linked to defects in immune cell development and/or function in Drosophila78,85,86,89,94 and mammals84,95. Patients affected by mutations in snRNA or proteins of the minor spliceosome, including snRNA U4atac, present hematopoietic defects that range from deficiency in humoral immunity to myelopathies24,39,96–99. Yet how mutations in U4atac alter blood cell development and function in RNU4atac-opathy is unexplored.

JAK1 and JAK2/STAT5 signaling are the closest orthologs to Hop, the only Jak (Hop)/STAT in Drosophila100, and JAK/STAT signaling is a master regulator of human immune cells’ development and function. We therefore hypothesized that U4atac mutations in patients may lead to changes in the splicing and thus the sizes of JAK/STAT proteins. To assess this, we conducted Western blots for JAK1 and JAK2 proteins in PBMC from 3-5 RS patients, compared to PBMC from a healthy male and a healthy female donor. We detected a single band for JAK1 at the predicted size of about 130 kDa in healthy PBMC extracts (Fig. 7i, j); JAK1 was markedly reduced in PBMC protein extracts from all analyzed RS patients, with lower-molecular-weight bands appearing. In contrast, one band was detected for JAK2 at the expected size of around 130 kDa in patient and healthy control PBMC (Fig. 7k, l). Jak1-mediated signaling in human PBMC leads to the activation of STAT1, 3, or 592. To assess Phosphorylated (activated) STAT5, we performed Western blot analyses on protein lysates from PBMC cultured in media supplemented with IL-2. We studied an RS patient and the patient’s heterozygous healthy carrier father, as well as another RS patient and an unrelated healthy donor. Our analyses revealed a stronger P-STAT5 band in the PBMC of the healthy heterozygous donors than in RS patients (Fig. 7m, n). Non-phosphorylated STAT5 levels are comparable in the PBMCs from the patients and healthy donors (Fig. 7m, n). These results support the hypothesis that U4atac impacts JAK1 protein size and amount in human PBMC, thereby reducing STAT5 activation and JAK1 downstream signaling. Alteration of this signaling in RNU4atac-opathy could cause defects in lymphocyte survival, maturation, and function, harming the patients’ immune defense.

Discussion

The removal of intronic regions from pre-mRNAs to produce mature transcripts is a primary, conserved process of post-transcriptional gene regulation and the major source of complexity in eukaryotes. Many eukaryotic cells have two types of introns in the pre-mRNA: the major introns that are removed by the major spliceosome complex, also known as the U2-dependent spliceosome, and the minor introns processed by the minor spliceosome complex or U12-dependent splicing machinery69. Generally, in metazoans, 99.5% of introns are removed by the major spliceosome, and only 0.5% (around 800 genes in humans) are spliced by the minor spliceosome9. In recent years, multiple studies have revealed that the interactions between the major and the minor spliceosome are more complex than they appear26,51,55, supporting the idea that minor and major spliceosomes often collaborate or compete, promoting alternative splicing9,26,51,73,101. The spliceosome is far from being fully understood, and the minor spliceosome and its effects on health are even less so.

The generation of mutant model organisms for various components is essential to shed light on the processes controlled by this spliceosome, their tissue-specific requirements in developmental and physiological processes, and connections to disease. Drosophila melanogaster has been a useful model to study splicing events and the requirement for specific components of the spliceosome102, and has been recently used to define analogies and differences in the transcriptomes and phenotypes between organisms with global mutations in U6atac and U12 snRNA50,103,104, however their studies mainly focused on IR events.

We generated an inducible CRISPR/Cas9 mutant targeting the snRNA U4atac to probe the functional conservation of the predicted U4atac snRNA in Drosophila, to reveal its contribution to minor spliceosome-dependent gene expression, and to establish a model to dissect the mechanisms underlying disease in individuals carrying U4atac mutations. To date, there are three human diseases linked to homozygous or compound heterozygous mutations in U4atac: LWS, TALS/MOPD1, and RS29,34. Individual single-nucleotide point mutations that fall mainly in the 5’ stem-loop or 3’ loop of the RNA at ten different positions within the U4atac snRNA have been associated with TALS/MOPD1. RS patients carry at least one mutation in stem II39, and LWS patients display more dispersed individual single-nucleotide mutations than in the other conditions34. Despite the fact that Drosophila and human U4atac snRNA genomic sequences share only 55% identity, our data showed that one-point mutations in U4atac lead to developmental growth arrest similar to RS and TALS/MOPD1, with an early lethality that resembles TALS/MOPD1 that can be rescued by the human RNU4atac. Our mutant contains a single point mutation in the stem II region, which interacts with U6atac RNA, potentially reducing the minor spliceosome assembly and function. However, the mutant additionally contains several point mutations in the proximal promoter region that alter the proximal promoter consensus, which is essential to bind RNA Pol II and drive snRNA transcription in Drosophila melanogaster105; likely leading to the reduced amount of U4atac transcript; such a phenotype is pronounced in TALS/MOPD132,43. Moreover, JASPAR-mediated analyses106 of the promoter region harboring the point mutations predicted the presence of consensus sequences for 30 transcription factors involved in developmental processes. Thus, the larger number of point mutations might cause the more severe developmental defects our mutant exhibits compared with what is described for RS patients.

This mutant also displayed hematopoietic deficiencies, which are present in both TALS/MOPD1 and in RS patients24,39,96–99. Thus, our U4atac Drosophila mutant likely represents a combination of the molecular defects in U4atac seen in the individual diseases, leading to more profound effects on splicing than seen alone in either.

Transcriptomic analyses of U4atac-/- embryos revealed multiple changes in gene expression and splicing events compared with the control, including intron retention events. We identified and confirmed two novel transcripts that retain introns in U4atac-/-, but these are classified as major-like introns in available databases such as D2UB and MIDB26.

The other genes whose splicing was affected participate in biological processes such as mitosis, metabolism, RNA processing, microtubule organization, and cilia assembly, all functions previously reported to be affected in minor spliceosome-defective cells or organisms51,4,4, and in RS PBMC26. These newly identified IR events are in genes such as dila, which is involved in ciliogenesis, a process affected in RS patients44 and whose human ortholog encoding the centrosomal protein 131107 was found transcriptionally misregulated in RS PBMC26,44. Thus, our studies support the recently published observations that the minor introns are probably far from being completely defined26.

We identified significant changes in the expression of genes involved in immunity, hematopoiesis, and intestinal organogenesis, which have not been molecularly characterized in patients. We found that intestinal epithelium-specific depletion of U4atac-/- causes gut motility problems in adult flies, while U4atac-/- larvae exhibit necrotic spots in the intestinal tract. These phenotypes resemble RNU4atac-opathy, in which severe atrophy of the gastrointestinal epithelium, leading to impaired nutrient absorption, has recently been reported108. We probed the effect of tissue-specific depletion of U4atac only in the immune cells of Drosophila, called hemocytes; we observed a profound loss of blood cells and reduced survival of the animals following systemic bacterial infection compared with age-matched controls. Defects in splicing in humans, including minor spliceosome-dependent splicing, are linked phenotypically with chronic lymphocytic leukemia24,98 and myelodysplasia97.

Our study defined a molecular special function for the minor spliceosome in immunity and hematopoiesis. We identified the transcript of a JAK ortholog, Hop, as a significantly differentially spliced gene in our U4atac-/- mutant. The closest human orthologs of Drosophila Hop and Stat proteins are JAK1 or 2 and STAT582. We found the Jak transcript complexed with the minor spliceosome for physiological processing of the transcript and expression. Jak1 and Jak2 gene expression were downregulated in the PBMC of RS patients26,44; however, no further analyses were performed. We identify no statistically significant change in Jak2 protein amounts in five RS patients, while finding reductions in Jak1 full-size protein levels in RS patients. Finally, our study revealed that altered JAK1 protein size and reductions in JAK1/STAT5 signaling are part of the mechanisms underlying U4atac-dependent diseases such as RS. Such a conclusion is supported by studies showing that germline biallelic STAT5 loss-of-function variants in humans cause severe growth hormone insensitivity with immunodeficiency, autoimmunity, and dysmorphism109. Lymphopenia and defects in immunoglobulins are features of RS patients39 and MOPDI patients40, arguing that alterations in STAT5 signaling may underlie aspects of disease in these patients.

Given that we were able to rescue the hematopoietic defects in our U4atac-/-, by expressing the normal hop transcript, our studies suggest that the JAK/STAT pathway warrants investigation as a potential therapeutic target, to control defects in TALS/MOPD1 and RS patients, and potentially other diseases affected by mutation in the minor spliceosome components.

Overall, our study provides a unique tool and important insight into snRNA U4atac function and into the poorly known minor spliceosome.

Methods

Drosophila melanogaster strains and husbandry

All fly stocks were maintained at either 18 °C or 25 °C on standard Bloomington cornmeal medium. The Cornmeal diet (CMD) food was prepared following the Bloomington stock center recipe (https://bdsc.indiana.edu/information/recipes/bloomfood.html). The liquid food was then poured into a DrosoFiller for mass dispensing into polypropylene 46 mL housing vials.

Female and male flies of the required genotypes were crossed in vials for 2 days and then moved to an embryo collection mini cage with 35 mm Petri dishes filled with sucrose-agar (0.012 g of methyl-4-hydroxy benzoate, 0.2 g of sucrose, 0.18 g of agar, and 11 mL of autoclaved distilled water) with a fresh dollop of activated yeast for 4 h. The Petri dishes with the collected eggs were incubated at 25 °C for 3 h, 8 h,12 h, or 16 h to collect embryos at stage 4, 7-8, 12, 14, 15 for analyses. Alternatively, eggs collected on agar Petri dishes were left to develop into adults, and phenotypic analyses were performed every 24 h until eclosion to assess developmental progress, growth, and survival.

Embryos were sorted based on GFP intensity and molecularly tested by QRT-PCR for GFP expression prior to further analyses.

The initial transcriptomic screen and QRT-PCR experiments to validate the RNAseq results were performed on mixed male and female embryos at stage 8–14 (100 ng total RNA for RNAseq and μg total RNA for QRT-PCR). U4atac transcript abundance was measured at embryo stage 4, 7-8, and again at stage 14-15 to monitor its stability over time. QRT-PCR experiments were also performed on U4atac-/- animals that survived to larval stages (collection at 5 days after egg lay, AEL) and U4atac-/+ (GFP + ) at 2-6 hs after L2-L3 molt.

The fluorescence microscopy experiments were performed on pooled male and female embryos, stage 12–15, as reported in the respective figure legends. Anti-GFP staining was used to confirm the identity of mutant (GFP-/-) or heterozygous (GFP+/-) embryos pre-screened before dechorionation and fixation at the fluorescent scope.

Survival assay, Smurf Assay, and gut motility assay were conducted using male and female flies. Survival to infection was assessed in male and female flies infected at 5 days post-eclosion. The sex of flies for each experiment is reported in the figures. The Smurf Assay and gut motility assay were performed on 15-day-old flies that reached adulthood.

For rescue experiments, the parental crosses were set to generate: flies carrying one construct needed to promote RNA-mediated depletion or overexpression of a gene of interest in U4atac-/-, together with the ubiquitous driver Act5C-GAL4 or Tub-GAL4 (or hml-Gal4 for hemocyte-specific rescue), flies carrying individual recombinant constructs in a control background to manipulate only the gene of interest, and the flies only carrying the driver. Embryos, larvae, pupae, and animals from each of these groups were compared in each biological replicate. Flies of the correct genotype were selected at the adult stage for the absence of markers of the balancer chromosomes. For all the other crosses, experimental F1 was compared to the F1 of control crosses that were individually set. For hemocyte-specific RNA silencing, we used the driver hml-GAL4. Our team generated the U4atac (pCFD5-V1) strain and control (pCFD5) strain for this study. We also generated pAWG-DmU4atac and pAWG-HsU4atac for the rescue experiments (see section below). A full list of the Drosophila melanogaster strains used in this study is reported in Supplementary Table 1,.

Generation of pCFD5-V1 recombinant line

The recombinant sgRNA targeting U4atac was designed using chopchop (https://chopchop.cbu.uib.no). The sgRNA containing insert was made by first generating the sgRNA sequence using the primers v1-Forward 5′-TGCACATTTCTGCTCCCCAAGACAAGG and v1-Reverse 5′- AAACCCTTGTCTTGGGGAGCAGAAATG. The annealed sgRNA fragment was cloned into the pCFD5 plasmid, digested using the BbsI type-IIS, following the protocol published by the inventor (http://crisprflydesign.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/pCFD5cloningprotocol.pdf)41.

The recombinant DNA v1 and the empty pCFD5 vectors were sent to Rainbow Transgenic Flies Inc., California, United States, to generate transgenic flies. To generate the ubiquitous U4atac-/- strain used in the study, we crossed Act5C-Cas9 virgin female flies to v1 males. The F1 was crossed to the background parental strain with balancer y[1] sc[1] v[1]; Dr[1] e[1]/TM3, Sb[1]. Individual flies from the F1 progeny were screened for indels in the StemII domain by PCR amplification and Sanger sequencing of the amplified U4atac genomic region using the primers U4atac-F 5’ TATGAGACGAATCCGATTAGGATCCG and U4atac-R 5’GTGTGCCTGTGAGAGCACATAAA. Isolated heterozygous flies with mutations in the target region and pCFD5 control were backcrossed 4 more times to the v- parental strain and then again 3 times to the TM3,Ser, Act5C-GFP strain. The isogenized U4atac-/TM3, Ser,GFP heterozygous flies were crossed to perform developmental studies and RNA-seq analyses.

Heterozygous flies were used as controls for most experiments.

To separate GFP-/- from GFP+/- and GFP+/+ embryos in the F1 progeny of U4atac-/-/TM3, Ser,Act5c-GFP intercross, we sorted embryos based on GFP intensity using a Zeiss Discovery stereomicroscope with an LED lamp and a GFP filter. For the developmental studies, embryos were manually collected and sorted prior to dechorionation to minimize dehydration under the microscope light. Manual sorting was validated by QRT-PCR assay measuring GFP transcript expression. Heterozygous and homozygous larvae were also selected based on GFP intensity using a Zeiss Discovery stereomicroscope with an LED lamp and a GFP filter. QRT-PCR was performed on total RNA extracted from 3-4 larvae to test GFP expression and U4atac transcript levels.

Construction of pAWG-DmU4atac and pAWG-hsU4atac

DmU4atac and hsU4atac sequences were amplified from genomic DNA samples extracted from stage 14 embryos and human monocytes, respectively, using the primer pair (Dm U4atac-F 5’-CACCACCTTCCTTGTCTTGGG and Dm U4atac-R 5’-CATTCAAGAGGCGGCTCCCT or hsU4atac-F 5’-CACCAACCATCCTTTTCTTGGGGTT and hsU4atac-R ATTTTTCCAAAAATTGCACC). Amplified fragments were purified using the PCR Purification and cloned into the pENTER Directional Topo vector, following the manufacturer’s instructions. The pENTR/D-TOPO donor recombinant clones were used to transfer the fragments to the destination plasmid pAWG using the LR Clonase II Enzyme mix. The final recombinant vectors, pAWG-DmU4atac and pAWG-hsU4atac, were sent to Genome Prolab (Sherbrooke, QC, Canada) for the generation of transgenic flies with inserts on the second chromosome by C31-mediated integration.

Construction of srpHemo-Cas9 line

A 4.3 kb fragment containing the srp promoter was amplified from plasmid srpHemoA (a gift from K. Brückner)56 by PCR with the following primers:

5′CGAGGTCGACTCTAGAAAATTTTGATGTTTTTAAATAGTCTTATCAGCAATGGCAA-3′.

5′CGAGGTCGACTCTAGAAAATTTTGATGTTTTTAAATAGTCTTATCAGCAATGGCAA-3′.

5′-ACGAAGCTTCTCTAGATATGGGATCCGTGCTGGGGTAGTGC-3′.

5′-ACGAAGCTTCTCTAGATATGGGATCCGTGCTGGGGTAGTGC-3′.

This fragment was cloned at the XhoI site by Infusion cloning into w+attB, plasmid, to generate DSPL244 (SrpHemo in w+attB) (XhoI).

In order to increase the number of cloning sites, the 165 bp MCS sequence was amplified from pBluescript SK(+) and cloned downstream of the srpHemo promoter site at the AscI site by Infusion cloning to create DSPL337 (SrpHemo + MCS in w+attB). Next, a 4.2 kb (4179 bp) fragment, which contains Cas9, was cloned from pAc-sgRNA-Cas9 [a gift from Ji-Long Liu] into the multiple cloning site of DSPL337 (SRP + MCS in W+attB) at the NotI site, by Infusion cloning to create DSPL422 (srpHemo-NLS-Cas9-NLS).

After sequencing, this construct was injected into syncytial blastoderm stage embryos of the M{3xP3-RFP.attP}ZH-86Fb line (a gift from Peter Duchek of IMBA) to generate inserts on the third chromosome by C31-mediated integration110.

For hemocyte-specific mutation, srpHemo-Cas9 virgin females were crossed to v1 or pCFD5 males. The srpHemo-Cas9; pCFD5 strain was used as a control to account for genotoxic stress caused by expression of Cas9. For intestinal epithelial cell-specific mutation, Mex-Gal4; UAS-Cas9 virgin females were crossed to v1 or pCFD5 males. All the strains used in this study are reported in the Supplementary Table 1.

Human peripheral blood mononuclear cells

Fresh Peripheral Blood Mononuclear Cells (PBMC) were isolated from the blood of a 6-month-old male with Roifman syndrome with two mutations in trans at n.17 G > A and n.116 A > G, and from his unaffected carrier father. Pelleted PBMCs were from the blood of a 15-year-old female homozygous for a mutation at n. 16 G > A, a 29-year-old male with mutations in trans at n. 13 C > G and n. 29 T > C, a 29-year-old male with mutations in trans at n. 13 C > T and n. 48 G > A, a 27-year-old female with mutations in trans at n.13 C > T and n.46 G > A, and a 24-year-old healthy heterozygous male and a 28-year old healthy heterozygous female. For human PBMC culture, frozen cell stocks were thawed and grown overnight in RPMI medium supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (HI FBS), 2 mM L-glutamine (Gibco), 50 mM 2-mercaptoethanol, 50 U penicillin/mL, 50 μg streptomycin sulfate/mL, and 50 ng/mL IL-2. Cells were then harvested and processed for protein extraction. Cell pellets were lysed for protein extraction and Western blot analysis (see below). The protocols utilized in this study were approved by the Institutional Review Board committee.

Protein analysis and western blotting

PBMCs or Drosophila embryos were lysed in cold Ephrussi–Beadle Ringer’s buffer diluted 1:2 in Laemmli Sample Buffer and supplemented with PhosSTOP, 10 mM DTT, and protease inhibitor cocktail cOmplete; a volume of 50 µL was used for each 24-well insert. Samples were then sonicated and incubated for 10 min at 100 °C, then stored at − 80°. The protein concentration in the lysates was determined using the Qubit Protein Assay Kit, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The samples (15 µg) were run on a 10% or 12% SDS-PAGE gel. At the end of the electrophoresis, the proteins were transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane, the membrane blocked according to the antibodies’ data sheet instructions for 1 hour in 3% BSA or 5% skimmed milk in TBST (150 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 0.05% Tween 20), then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibody at 1:1000 dilution (rabbit P-Stat5-Tyr694, Stat5, JAK1 or JAK2) and mouse Tubulin or rabbit, rabbit anti-GFP or rat anti-HA, see supplementary Table 1 for cat. numbers). The following day, the membrane was washed in TBST and incubated with the secondary antibody for 1 h. After three washes, the membrane was developed with a ChemiDOC Imaging System, using the Clarity Western ECL Substrate, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Image quantification was done in ImageJ as described in the Supplementary Information file.

RNA immunoprecipitation protocol

One gram of Drosophila embryos stage 8–14 was homogenized on ice in 1000 µL (for mock and IP) RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) Buffer (20 mM Hepes-KOH pH 7.9, 10 mM KCl, 1.5 mM MgCl2, 1% Triton X-100, 0.5% NP-40, 0.5 mM DTT, 1 × Roche Complete protease inhibitor, 0.5 U/μl recombinant RNA inhibitor, and 1 x Roche Phospho-Stop) using a Dounce homogenizer with 15–20 strokes. Lysates were collected in a separate tube, and precipitated pellets were then resuspended in 600 mM KCl and further homogenized. Supernatants from the above two steps were combined, and KCl concentration was adjusted to 100 mM. Membrane and debris were pelleted by centrifugation at 13,000-16,000 x g for 10 min at 4 °C, supernatants were collected in clean tubes, protein concentration was measured using a Qubit Fluorometric assay and an aliquot of 40 µL from each lysate was frozen for western blot analyses of the input before applying ANTI-HA High Affinity Agarose beads (concentration 2 µg of antibody to the supernatant 6 mg) or IgG-conjugated agarose beads and incubate for an overnight at 4 °C with gentle rotation. The day after, the beads were pelleted according to the manufacturing instructions (30” at 13,000 × g) and washed 3 times in RIP buffer. An aliquot of resuspended beads (50 µL) was saved at the second wash for western blot analyses. After the last wash, co-purified proteins and RNA were resuspended in TRIzol reagent for RNA extraction and QRT-PCR. RIP analyses by QRT-PCR were performed following the data analyses reported in ref. 111. The average Ct was calculated for each gene and sample, and for each sample (Input, IgG control and HA sample) we calculated the ΔCt = Ct gene in IP – Ct gene in IgG control IP). From the ΔCt we calculated the ΔΔCt = ΔCt in IP - ΔCt (IgG). We calculated fold enrichment = 2- ΔΔct, which informs how many folds more RNA is in the target sample compared to the IgG background. Values are normalized relative to a housekeeping gene that is spliced by the major spliceosome.

Immunofluorescence on Drosophila embryos

Embryos were collected from sucrose-agar plates, sorted at the GFP-SteREO Discovery V20 microscope, and dechorionated in 50% bleach for 2 min. and rinsed in water. After fixation in a 1:1 solution of 4% formaldehyde, PBS:heptane for 20 min in a 37 °C shaker, embryos were devitellinized by a 30s vortex in a 1:1 heptane:methanol solution. Embryos were permeabilized in PT (1 × PBS, 0.1% Triton X-100) for 30 min, blocked for 1 hour in PT-NGS (0.1% bovine serum albumin, 5% normal goat serum in PT), and incubated overnight with primary antibodies in PT-NGS. Embryos were washed 3 × 5 minutes in PT, 3 × 15 minin PT with 300 mm NaCl, rinsed 5 times in PT, and then incubated for 30 minutes in PT-NGS. Embryos were incubated with preabsorbed secondary antibodies for 1 h. Before mounting, embryos were washed 3 × 5 min, then 4 × 15 min in PT and 1 min in PBS. Embryos were mounted in ProLong Gold Antifade Mountant. The following primary antibodies were used: Antibodies to Futsch, raised by Prof. Benzer (California Institute of Technology), to Elav, raised by Prof. Rubin (Janelia Farm Research / HHMI) and to Neuroglian, raised by Prof. Goodman (University of California, Berkeley), raised by Corey Goodman (University of California), and rabbit anti- Green Fluorescent Protein (GFP). All primary antibodies were diluted 1:20, and Alexa Fluor 555 donkey anti-mouse secondary antibody or Alexa Fluor 488 donkey anti-rabbit secondary antibody was diluted 1:1000. Anti-GFP was used to select mutant (GFP-negative) from heterozygous embryos (GFP-positive). Images were collected using a 20X (NA = 0.5) objective mounted onto the LSM 880 Confocal Microscope (Zeiss) and analyzed with ImageJ as described in the Supplementary Information file. Each individual whole-embryo image was acquired from 3 or 4 overlapping 20 × objective fields and compiled using Photoshop 2025 (Adobe). All figures were assembled with Photoshop 2025 and Illustrator 2025 (Adobe).

Larvae and pupae imaging

Larvae or pupae of the desired genotype and age were imaged with a 12.4X magnification on a SteREO Discovery V20 Microscope, equipped with an Axiocam 208 color camera (Zeiss). Image analysis was performed in ImageJ. Larvae were kept at -20 °C for 20 min before imaging.

Smurf assay

15-day-old female and male flies were moved from regular cornmeal food to vials containing a vial plug saturated with a solution of 5% sucrose in PBS plus blue food coloring. After 48 h the flies were imaged with a 12.4X magnification on a SteREO Discovery V20 Microscope, equipped with an Axiocam 208 color camera (Zeiss). Image analysis was performed in ImageJ as described in the Supplementary Information file.

Gut motility assay

15-day-old female and male flies were moved from regular cornmeal food to vials containing a vial plug saturated with a solution of 5% sucrose in PBS plus blue food coloring and moved in a vial containing a white vial plug for 6 h. The plugs were imaged with a 12.4X magnification on a SteREO Discovery V20 Microscope, equipped with an Axiocam 208 color camera (Zeiss). Image analysis was performed in ImageJ as detailed in the Supplementary Information file.

Total RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis and QRT-PCR

Embryos stage 8–14 were collected, sorted, and transferred to TRIzol reagent, and snap-frozen in dry ice. Total RNA was extracted using the Direct-zol RNA Microprep kit following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA was reverse transcribed using the High-Capacity Script cDNA Synthesis kit, and the synthesized cDNA was amplified by QPCR using the SYBR-Green PCR master mix using a QuantStudio 6 Flex qPCR machine (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Samples were normalized to Rpl23 gene expression, the relative quantification methods, and primer efficiency. Primer sequences used in real-time qPCR are presented in the Supplementary Table 2. In some experiments, data were represented as % of relative expression compared to the control condition.

RT-PCR to test intron retention

Total RNA was isolated from U4atac-/- and U4atac-/TM3,ser Act-GFP embryos. Total RNA was isolated using the Direct-zol RNA Microprep kit, and two μg of RNA was reverse transcribed using the High-Capacity Script cDNA Synthesis kit. The synthesized cDNA was amplified by PCR using the 2X Q5 master mix. The PCR products were detected on 2% agarose gel by electrophoresis. The band’s intensity ratio indicated the amount of the specific amplicon for the gene of interest relative to the Rpl23 band that was used as a loading control. The bands’ intensities were calculated using the open-source ImageJ software package (refer to the Supplementary Information file) and plotted as relative intensity normalized to the 200 bp or 500 bp reference bands of the 100 bp Ladder (see below). We analyzed at least 3 biological replicates of RNA extracted from 100 embryos.

RNA isolation and RNA sequencing library preparation

RNA was isolated from the embryos collected using a Direct-zol RNA Microprep kit, followed by library preparation using the Illumina Stranded Total RNA with RiboZero plus with the Drosophila oligo pool for ribodepletion (1pmol/μL), separately purchased from IDT. We used 100 ng of total RNA. Libraries were quantified using the QIAseq library Quant Assay kit and analyzed on a bioanalyzer (Agilent). Libraries were sent to Center d’expertise et de services Génome Québec. Libraries were quantified using the KAPA Library Quantification Kits - Complete kit (Universal). Average fragment size was determined using a Fragment Analyzer 5300 (Agilent) instrument. The libraries were normalized and pooled, then denatured in 0.02 N NaOH and neutralized with HT1 buffer. The pool was loaded at 175pM on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 S4 lane using Xp protocol as per the manufacturer’s recommendations using the Nextera adapter primers of the v1.5 kits. The run was performed for 2 × 100 cycles (paired-end mode). A phiX library was used as a control and mixed with libraries at 1% level. Base calling was performed with RTA v3. Program bcl2fastq2 v2.20 was then used to demultiplex samples and generate fastq reads.

RNA-seq Analyses

Gene expression profile changes between U4atac-/- and U4atac-/TM3, GFP, Ser were analyzed using Gene Set Enrichment Analysis to identify biological processes, cellular components, and KEGG pathways that were differentially regulated in U4atac-/-. Data from the Gene Set Enrichment Analysis were then visualized using GeneMANIA Force-Directed Layout of the Enrichment Map plugin in Cytoscape to generate the gene interaction network52. The resulting network map was curated to remove redundant and uninformative nodes, resulting in a simplified network. The maps for each comparison were combined.

Alternative splicing analysis

RNA sequencing data focused on alternative splicing events were processed using the nf-core/rnasplice pipeline (v1.0.4)112. Total RNA was extracted from Drosophila melanogaster U4atac-/+ (U4atac-+ 1–3, n = 3) and U4atac-/- (U4atac-/- 1–3, n = 3) samples. U4atac-/- n. 1 was excluded due to poor sequencing quality. Libraries were prepared for paired-end sequencing on an Illumina platform. Reads were quality-filtered and aligned to the BDGP6 reference genome (Ensembl) using the splice-aware aligner STAR (v2.7.1a)113. Aligned reads were coordinate-sorted and indexed using SAMtools (v1.12).

Alternative splicing events were identified and quantified with SpliceWiz (v1.4.0)64. The reference database was built from the Ensembl BDGP6 GTF annotation. BAM files were processed using the collateData function (IRMode = “SpliceMax”, novelSplicing=TRUE, lowMemoryMode = FALSE)65. High-confidence splicing events were retained by applying SpliceWiz’s built-in filters (applyFilters), resulting in a final NxtSE object containing quantified inclusion and exclusion counts. The intron retention module of SpliceWiz employs an algorithm highly similar to IRFinder114, enhancing the detection of retained introns alongside other splicing event types.

Differential alternative splicing analysis was performed using edgeR (v3.44.0), limma (v3.58.1), DESeq2 (v1.42.0), and DoubleExpSeq (v1.1). Splicing events were considered significantly differential if they met thresholds of FDR < 0.05, |log2FC | > 1, and |ΔPSI | > 0.24.

Differential splicing results were visualized using volcano plots (log2FC vs. -log10 FDR), PSI correlation plots, and hierarchical clustering heatmaps generated with pheatmap (v1.0.12). Coverage plots for selected events were created using SpliceWiz’s integrated visualization functions.

Survival rate of adult flies

To generate survival curves to infection, adult flies were anesthetized with CO2and infected by being pricked in the thorax with a thin needle that had been dipped into a bacterial (Ecc15) working solution of O.D. 200. Survival experiments were done at 29 °C with 20–30 flies tested for each group. Surviving flies were transferred daily to fresh vials and counted115.

Extraction of hemocytes from adult Drosophila

For the extraction of plasmatocytes from adults, 10 anesthetized female and male flies were moved into a Mobicol F column with a fixed outlet plug and a 35 μm filter. The flies were covered in glass beads. The outlet was unplugged, and the column was inserted into a 1.5 μL centrifuge tube. The tube was centrifuged in a benchtop refrigerated minicentrifuge at 8,000 g for 20 min at 4 °C. The eluate was resuspended 50 μL of SFM4 Insect Cell Culture Medium containing a protease inhibitor cocktail to avoid coagulation. The cell suspension was mixed with 50 μL of Trypan blue stain and counted at the hemocytometer. The number of total cells in the suspension was divided by the total number of flies to calculate the number of hemocytes/fly.

CFU Counting

To measure the bacterial burden during infection in adult flies, an individual fly from each vial infected with Ecc15 (O.D. 200) was picked every 24 hr, dipped into 70% ethanol and air-dried. Individual flies were mashed in 100 μL of sterile microorganism culture medium, and the mash was subjected to centrifugation at 8000 × g for 5 min to remove debris. The resultant supernatant was serially diluted, plated onto solid nutrient medium, and incubated at 29 °C until there were visible colonies, which were counted. Ecc15 was a kind gift of Dr. Edan Foley, University of Alberta. The bacteria were grown in LB broth at 29 °C for 24 h.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were done in Prism (Graph-Pad 11.0.0). All data were assessed for normality using Shapiro–Wilk checks before running statistical tests. Statistical significance was determined using a two-tailed Student’s t test, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), two-way ANOVA, or using the Kaplan–Meier log rank for survival assays. Significance is shown in figures as ns; ∗p ≤ 0.05; ∗∗p ≤ 0.01; ∗∗∗p ≤ 0.001; ∗∗∗∗p ≤ 0.0001. The statistical details of experiments can be found, including the statistical tests used, the exact values of N and n, and dispersion and precision measures are reported in the figure legends.
