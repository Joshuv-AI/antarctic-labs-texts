DNA methylation-mediated silencing of STAT5A drives breast cancer metastasis via dual regulation of EMT and immunosuppressive microenvironment
Authors: Xue Yang, Yuhang Su, Yushi Ying, Youyun Peng, Ruyuan Wang, Shaojie Xu, Yuxi Zhao, Xin Shu, Qiyue Peng, Xingrui Li, Yaying Du
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSignal transducer and activator of transcription 5 A (STAT5A) modulates breast cancer cell proliferation and metastasis, yet its epigenetic regulatory mechanisms and impacts on the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) remain unclear. Here, we explored STAT5A’s epigenetic traits, metastatic roles, and effects on tumor-associated immune cells via two mouse models (immunodeficient vs. immunocompetent) with metastasis established by tail vein injection or orthotopic tumorigenesis, flow cytometry to characterize immune cell subsets, cell co-cultures, clinical tissue analyses, and dCas9-TET1CD-mediated STAT5A promoter methylation intervention. Low STAT5A expression reduced tumor cell proliferation but enhanced migration/invasion, with in vivo metastatic capacity dependent on TIME. Tumors with low STAT5A expression grew slower and metastasized less in immunodeficient mice, but exhibited opposite phenotypes in immunocompetent cohorts. Mechanistically, STAT5A downregulation was associated with epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), cytokine pathway remodeling, impaired T-cell cytotoxicity, and macrophage-associated immunosuppressive remodeling. Macrophage-derived LIF activated STAT5A phosphorylation via LIFR. Clinical samples showed sequentially increasing STAT5A promoter methylation and decreasing protein expression from normal tissues to primary/metastatic tumors. dCas9-TET1CD-mediated targeted demethylation restored STAT5A expression and reduced cell migration. These findings suggest that epigenetic silencing of STAT5A contributes to breast cancer metastasis through coordinated regulation of EMT-like plasticity and the TIME, and support further investigation of STAT5A reactivation or LIF-LIFR modulation as potential anti-metastatic strategies.Subject terms: Breast cancer, Immunosurveillance

Introduction

Breast cancer ranks as one of the most prevalent cancers globally [1]. According to the National Cancer Center of China, breast cancer was the second most common cancer in women and the fifth leading cause of cancer-related deaths [2]. Worldwide, cancer metastasis accounts for over 90% of cancer mortality, with approximately 10%-15% of the 18 million annually diagnosed patients developing metastatic disease [3]. The progression from localized tumors to disseminated metastases involves a complex multistep process. Cancer cells first invade through the local basement membrane [4], enter the circulation (intravasation), survive in bloodstream [5]. Successful extravasation allows them to reach distant tissues, where colonization and the formation of secondary tumors [6]. This process is highly inefficient, with <0.1% of tumor cells succeeding in establishing metastases [6]. Cellular plasticity is indispensable for cancer cells to adapt to dynamic microenvironmental cues throughout this process [5]. Many researches have emphasized driver gene mutations (e.g., BRCA1/2 [7], PIK3CA [8]) that sustain proliferative signaling and initiate metastasis, but the mutation alone is insufficient to explain the rapid adaptive changes observed during metastasis [9, 10]. Studies have demonstrated that genetic and epigenetic mechanisms synergistically drive tumor cell reprogramming during metastasis [11], with epigenetic mechanisms provide dynamic and rapid systems that respond quickly to the local environment [12].

Epigenetics defines a series of DNA-based regulatory mechanisms that modulate gene expression without altering the nucleotide sequence [13]. Strong evidence supports epigenetic reprogramming as a driver of dynamic transcriptomic heterogeneity in cancer, with common regulators include DNA methylation, histone modifications, and non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) [14–16]. Among these, DNA methylation is the most extensively studied [17, 18]. Hypermethylation of promoter-associated CpG islands is a hallmark of cancer cells and usually inhibits gene expression [19, 20]. Alterations in DNA methylation patterns regulate tumor progression and metastasis [18, 21]. Unlike genetic mutations, DNA methylation exhibits greater plasticity, faster response, and enhanced adaptability to environmental alterations [21], making it critical to identify methylation-regulated genes in breast cancer and decipher their roles in metastasis.

This study identified STAT5A as a key methylation-regulated gene in breast cancer, with high methylation and low expression predicting poor prognosis. STAT5A, a member of the Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription (STAT) protein family, exhibits context-dependent roles in malignancy. It acts as an oncogenic driver in hematological malignancies [22] and ovarian cancer [23], and undergoes METTL3-mediated m6A modification to promote gastric cancer progression [24], but suppresses proliferation and invasion in hepatocellular carcinoma via the cGAS-STING pathway [25]. In mammary tissue, STAT5A mediates normal lactogenic function by regulating prolactin-induced differentiation and casein production [26, 27]. In malignancy, however, the role of STAT5A appears to be more complex and context-dependent, varying by tumor type and biological setting. In breast cancer, previous studies have suggested associations between STAT5A-related signaling and differentiation status, hormone response, and clinical behavior [28, 29]. Given its unique physiological role in mammary tissue, elucidating STAT5A’s mechanistic contributions to breast cancer malignancy is essential.

In this study, we combined integrative bioinformatic analyses, clinical specimen validation, mechanistic cell-based experiments, co-culture systems, transcriptomic and chromatin profiling, and mouse metastasis models to define the role of STAT5A in breast cancer progression. We found that STAT5A undergoes progressive promoter hypermethylation and expression loss during progression from normal breast tissue to primary tumors and metastatic lesions. Functionally, STAT5A downregulation suppressed tumor cell proliferation but enhanced migration, invasion, and EMT-like changes, while also promoting an immunosuppressive phenotype characterized by impaired T-cell cytotoxicity and macrophage-associated remodeling. Mechanistically, these effects were linked to cytokine-related signaling and were partially reversible through targeted demethylation or activation of the LIF-LIFR-STAT5A axis. Together, these findings support a model in which DNA methylation-mediated silencing of STAT5A contributes to breast cancer metastasis through coordinated regulation of EMT-like plasticity and the tumor immune microenvironment.

Methods

Bioinformatics analysis

DNA methylation, transcriptomic, and clinical data for breast cancer were obtained from TCGA-BRCA. Samples with matched methylation and expression profiles were retained for integrative analyses. Methylome-driven genes were screened based on inverse promoter methylation-expression correlation and differential methylation between tumor and normal tissues. Prognostic analyses were further performed in TCGA-BRCA and externally validated in GSE25066. Subtype-stratified analyses and immune/purity-related analyses were conducted as described in the Results and Supplementary Methods. Public online resources were additionally used for validation and visualization of STAT5A methylation, expression, and subtype-related features.

Cell culture and stable line generation

Human breast cancer cell lines (MDA-MB-231 and T47D) and murine 4T1 cells underwent STR authentication prior to experimentation. To establish stable lines, lentiviral transduction was used to generate two models [1] STAT5A-knockdown variants (STAT5A-SH-puro) and scramble control (STAT5A-NC-puro) [2] dCas9-TET1CD-blasticidin (BSD) co-expressed with sgRNA-puro constructs. After 48-h transduction, cells were selected with puromycin and/or blasticidin. Successful integration was confirmed via qPCR and immunoblotting.

Cell proliferation, migration, and invasion assays

Cell proliferation was assessed by CCK-8 assay. Cell migration was evaluated by wound-healing and Transwell migration assays, and cell invasion was assessed using Matrigel-coated Transwell chambers. Quantification was performed by microscopy-based image analysis. Detailed plating densities, incubation times, serum conditions, and quantification procedures are described in the Supplementary Methods.

Quantitative PCR

Total RNA was isolated from cultured cells and clinical specimens and reverse-transcribed into cDNA. Quantitative PCR was performed using SYBR Green chemistry on a QuantStudio 5 system. Relative mRNA expression was calculated using the 2^-ΔΔCt method with GAPDH as the internal control. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Immunoblotting

Whole-cell protein lysates were prepared using RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against STAT5A, pSTAT5A, EMT-related proteins, and GAPDH, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence. Detailed antibody information is provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Histology, immunohistochemistry, and immunofluorescence

Tumor and lung tissues were fixed, paraffin-embedded, sectioned, and subjected to histological evaluation. H&E staining was used to assess tissue morphology and metastatic lesions in the lungs. Immunohistochemistry was performed on FFPE sections to detect CD8a and F4/80. Immunofluorescence was used to assess subcellular localization of pSTAT5A in cultured cells. Histological and image-based quantification was performed in a blinded manner. Detailed staining procedures, antigen retrieval conditions, and image acquisition parameters are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Clinical samples and tissue-based analyses

Human breast cancer tissue specimens were obtained from Tongji Hospital with institutional ethics approval and written informed consent. Tissue-based analyses, including qPCR and histological/immunostaining validation, were performed according to standard protocols. Where applicable, tissue microarray or histological scoring procedures were conducted using predefined pathological criteria.

Animal experiments and in vivo imaging

Female BALB/c and NSG mice were used to establish orthotopic mammary tumor and tail-vein injection metastasis models. Mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups before tumor implantation and, where applicable, before subsequent treatment allocation. Tumor burden and metastatic progression were monitored using luciferase-based bioluminescence imaging after D-luciferin administration. In orthotopic models, tumor growth was also measured longitudinally. At the endpoint, lungs and livers were collected for ex vivo imaging and histological analyses. Mice were monitored every other day for body weight and tumor size, and metastatic progression was evaluated weekly by bioluminescence imaging. Animals that developed poor general condition or reached predefined humane endpoints were euthanized by CO₂ inhalation before the scheduled endpoint, in accordance with institutional ethical guidelines.

T-cell isolation, activation, and co-culture assays

Murine splenic T cells were isolated by magnetic negative selection and activated with anti-CD3/CD28 stimulation. Tumor–T-cell co-culture assays were established to assess immune cytotoxicity and tumor cell killing. Cytotoxic activity was quantified using luciferase-expressing target cells at different effector-to-target ratios. Supernatants from co-culture systems were further analyzed for cytokine secretion by ELISA.

Bone marrow-derived macrophages and flow cytometry

Bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were generated from murine bone marrow cells cultured in macrophage induction medium. Flow cytometry was used to analyze immune cell populations in peripheral blood, tumors, and lungs, as well as apoptosis in co-culture systems. Samples were processed into single-cell suspensions, subjected to erythrocyte lysis and Fc receptor blockade, stained with fluorescence-conjugated antibodies, and analyzed on a Beckman CytoFLEX instrument. Gating was performed in FlowJo.

RNA-seq and CUT&Tag

RNA-seq was performed on T47D cells with high or low STAT5A expression (n = 3 biological replicates per group). Total RNA was extracted, quality-controlled, and used for poly(A)-selected library construction followed by paired-end sequencing. Differential expression analysis was conducted using DESeq2, followed by pathway enrichment and network analyses. CUT&Tag was performed to profile STAT5A-associated chromatin binding. Library construction, sequencing quality control, peak calling, annotation, and downstream enrichment analyses were performed using standard bioinformatic workflows.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism and R. Data distribution was assessed before selection of parametric or nonparametric methods. Two-group comparisons were analyzed using Student’s t test or Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. Multiple-group comparisons were performed using ANOVA- or Kruskal-Wallis-based methods with multiple-comparison correction where applicable. Longitudinal tumor growth curves were analyzed using two-way ANOVA or related repeated-measures approaches as appropriate. Survival analyses were performed using Kaplan-Meier methods and Cox regression models. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data are presented as mean ± SD unless otherwise indicated.

Supplementary methods

Detailed protocols for cell functional assays, lentiviral packaging, flow cytometry, apoptosis assays, cytotoxicity assays, RNA-seq, immunohistochemistry, immunofluorescence, immunoblotting, quantitative PCR, online analytical tools, and full antibody/primer information are provided in the Supplementary Information.

Results

STAT5A is identified as a methylation-regulated candidate associated with breast cancer progression

Integrating TCGA methylation and transcriptome data, we identified differentially methylated genes (DMGs) and differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between normal and tumor tissues, then screened for methylation-silenced genes (methylation-expression correlation coefficient < -0.3), yielding 41 candidates (Supplementary Fig. 1A–B). Univariate and multivariate Cox regression established a three-gene prognostic signature (CX3CL1, ZNF154, STAT5A). Risk stratification showed differential expression of these genes between high- and low-risk groups (Fig. 1A), with the low-risk cohort exhibiting superior overall survival in both TCGA and GSE25066 datasets (Fig. 1B, Supplementary Fig. 1C–E) and fewer deaths (Fig. 1C). GSEA revealed high-risk tumors enriched for cell cycle regulation and DNA replication, while low-risk tumors showed activation of chemokine signaling, cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions, and T-cell receptor signaling (Supplementary Fig. 1F–G).

We next focused on STAT5A itself. In the overall cohort, lower STAT5A expression was associated with worse overall survival, whereas higher STAT5A methylation was associated with poorer survival outcome (Fig. 1D, E). STAT5A methylation showed a bimodal distribution in breast tumors. One peak (β ≈ 0.4) matched normal tissues, and the hypermethylated peak (β ≈ 0.75) indicated methylation gain during malignant transformation (Fig. 1F). STAT5A expression was inversely correlated with methylation (r = -0.67, p = 1.58e-114; Fig. 1G), consistent with methylation-associated transcriptional repression.

To further determine whether these patterns were uniform across breast cancer subtypes, we performed subtype-stratified analyses. STAT5A expression and methylation both differed significantly among molecular subtypes (Fig. 1H–I). Expression was significantly lower in multiple tumor subtypes relative to normal breast tissues, whereas methylation patterns showed subtype-dependent heterogeneity rather than a uniform shift across all subtypes. In external KM-Plotter analyses, the association of STAT5A with clinical outcome was also subtype-dependent, while higher STAT5A expression was associated with more favorable overall survival in the overall cohort, subtype-specific effects were variable and not uniformly preserved across all subtypes (Supplementary Fig. 2A, B, E–L). These findings indicate that the clinical relevance of STAT5A is context-dependent rather than that of a uniform pan-BRCA prognostic marker.

Because STAT5A is expressed in multiple stromal and immune compartments, we further assessed whether bulk STAT5A expression might mainly reflect tissue composition. In public breast cancer single-cell data [30], STAT5A expression was detected across multiple cell populations, including epithelial, immune, and stromal compartments (Fig. 1J, Supplementary Fig. 2C–D). In bulk TCGA-BRCA data, STAT5A expression was positively correlated with ImmuneScore, StromalScore, and ESTIMATEScore, and negatively correlated with tumor purity (Fig. 1K–L, Supplementary Fig. 2Q–R), indicating that bulk STAT5A signal is influenced by tissue admixture. However, the inverse association between STAT5A methylation and expression remained robust in subtype-aware and purity-related analyses (Supplementary Fig. 2S), supporting the interpretation that STAT5A repression is not explained solely by immune infiltration but is also linked to tumor-associated epigenetic dysregulation.

STAT5A deficiency enhances migratory behavior and EMT-like reprogramming

We next examined the functional consequences of STAT5A loss in breast cancer cells. Efficient STAT5A knockdown (mimicking methylation-induced silencing) was confirmed at both mRNA and protein levels in T47D, MDA-MB-231, and 4T1 cells (Fig. 2A). STAT5A knockdown inhibited proliferation (Fig. 2B) but enhanced migration and invasion capacity of cancer cells (Fig. 2C–D). To gain mechanistic insight, we performed pathway enrichment analysis and chromatin-binding evaluation. KEGG analysis implicated cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, JAK-STAT signaling, cell adhesion molecules, and pluripotency-related pathways among the enriched programs associated with STAT5A perturbation (Fig. 2E). CUT&Tag and integrative analyses further showed STAT5A-associated chromatin occupancy near the CDH1/E-cadherin and CDH2/N-cadherin loci (Fig. 2F), supporting a potential link between STAT5A and EMT-related transcriptional regulation. Consistent with this, STAT5A knockdown induced EMT-like molecular reprogramming. In T47D cells, loss of STAT5A reduced epithelial markers and increased mesenchymal-associated genes, together with upregulation of several EMT-related transcription factors (Figs. 2G, H). Similar changes were observed in 4T1 cells (Figs. 2I, J). EMT is a complex process in tumor progression, defined by tumor cells’ gradual loss of epithelial markers, gain of mesenchymal markers, and acquisition of mesenchymal-like migratory traits. Post-EMT tumor cells further display enhanced stemness, diminished proliferation, and strengthened migratory capacity [31]. Together, these results indicate that STAT5A deficiency suppresses baseline proliferation but enhances migratory and invasive properties through EMT-like reprogramming.

Host immune context shapes the in vivo metastatic phenotype associated with Stat5a Loss

To determine how Stat5a loss influences metastatic progression in vivo, we used both immunodeficient NSG and immunocompetent BALB/c mouse models, including tail-vein injection and orthotopic mammary tumor implantation (Fig. 3; Supplementary Fig. 3). In the NSG intravenous model, quantitative bioluminescence analysis showed that the overall tumor-associated signal was significantly higher in the NC group than in the SH group (Supplementary Fig. 3A). H&E-stained lung sections and corresponding quantitative analysis further showed a significantly larger pulmonary tumor area in the NC group (Fig. 3A, B). In addition, immunohistochemical analysis of pulmonary lesions in the NSG intravenous model showed reduced E-cadherin and increased Vimentin staining in Stat5a-deficient lesions (Supplementary Fig. 3C), indicating that the EMT-like phenotype associated with Stat5a loss was maintained in vivo.

In the NSG orthotopic model, in vivo imaging likewise showed a significantly higher overall tumor-associated signal in the NC group (Supplementary Fig. 3B). However, histological quantification of lung sections did not show a significant difference in the number of pulmonary tumor foci between the NC and SH groups (Fig. 3C, D). Therefore, although the NSG models support differences in overall tumor burden, these data do not justify a strong conclusion that Stat5a deficiency independently increased pulmonary metastatic burden in the orthotopic immunodeficient setting. Rather, they suggest that in the absence of an intact immune system, reduced Stat5a expression was associated with an EMT-like phenotype but not with a clear increase in orthotopic lung metastatic burden, and differences in overall tumor burden may have contributed to the observed pulmonary findings.

In the BALB/c intravenous model, serial bioluminescence imaging and quantitative analysis showed significantly greater pulmonary tumor burden in the SH group than in the NC group (Fig. 3E, F), while body weight remained comparable between groups (Fig. 3G). Representative H&E-stained lung sections further supported the presence of more extensive pulmonary lesions in the SH group (Supplementary Fig. 2E). These findings indicate that under intact host immunity, loss of Stat5a promotes pulmonary colonization and outgrowth after tail-vein injection.

In the BALB/c orthotopic model, Stat5a-deficient tumors exhibited accelerated growth during the later phase of progression, resulting in larger tumors at endpoint (Fig. 3H–I). Consistent with this, ex vivo imaging showed significantly greater metastatic involvement of both the lungs and livers in the SH group (Fig. 3J–M). Representative H&E-stained lung and liver sections from this model further supported the increased metastatic burden in the SH group (Supplementary Fig. 3F). Taken together, these results suggest that the prometastatic consequence of Stat5a loss becomes much more evident in the presence of an intact immune system. To examine whether the in vivo metastatic phenotype remained linked to EMT-like reprogramming and immune remodeling, we further analyzed orthotopic BALB/c tumors. Immunohistochemical staining showed lower E-cadherin and higher Vimentin expression in the SH group (Supplementary Fig. 2G), consistent with maintenance of an EMT-like state in vivo. In parallel, tumors with low Stat5a expression showed reduced infiltration of CD8a⁺ T cells and increased F4/80⁺ macrophage infiltration (Supplementary Fig. 2H). These findings support the interpretation that Stat5a deficiency is associated not only with EMT-like plasticity but also with a more immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, which may help explain why the metastatic phenotype is more pronounced in immunocompetent than in immunodeficient models.

To further assess whether the in vivo phenotype associated with Stat5a loss was accompanied by systemic and local immune remodeling, we analyzed immune cell populations by flow cytometry in the BALB/c models (Fig. 3N–Q). In the intravenous model, dynamic analysis of peripheral blood showed that the SH group displayed reduced proportions of CD3⁺ T cells together with increased CD11b⁺ myeloid cells during metastatic progression (Fig. 3N). Similar changes were observed in peripheral blood from mice bearing orthotopic tumors (Fig. 3O). Within orthotopic tumors and in lung tissues, the SH group exhibited reduced CD3⁺ T-cell infiltration and increased myeloid/macrophage-lineage populations (Fig. 3P–Q). Together, these results indicate that Stat5a deficiency is associated with coordinated systemic and local immune remodeling in immunocompetent hosts, characterized by reduced T-cell representation and increased myeloid/macrophage-lineage accumulation, thereby providing an in vivo immunological context for the more pronounced metastatic phenotype observed in BALB/c mice.

Overall, these in vivo data indicate that the biological consequence of Stat5a loss is strongly host-context-dependent. In immunodeficient NSG mice, Stat5a deficiency retained EMT-like features but did not clearly increase orthotopic pulmonary metastatic burden, whereas in immunocompetent BALB/c mice it was associated with increased tumor growth, higher lung and liver metastatic burden, and immune microenvironment remodeling. This pattern suggests that the prometastatic effect of low Stat5a expression is not explained by tumor-cell plasticity alone, but is substantially shaped by interactions with the host immune system.

Stat5a deficiency promotes an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and macrophage-dependent tumor progression

Given the marked difference between immunodeficient and immunocompetent models, we next examined how Stat5a loss influences anti-tumor immunity. In co-culture systems, 4T1 cells with low Stat5a expression showed reduced apoptosis after exposure to T cells (Fig. 4A), and T cells co-cultured with the SH group displayed markedly reduced production of Il2, Ifng, Gzmb, and Tnfa (Fig. 4B–E), indicating impaired cytotoxic activity. T cells had lower lysis efficiency against SH group cells across four E:T ratios (Supplementary Fig. 4A). When macrophages were incorporated into the co-culture system, tumor-cell protection became even more apparent. In the presence of BMDMs, the apoptotic fraction of Stat5a-deficient tumor cells remained lower than that of control cells, and the overall cytolytic effect of T cells was further blunted (Fig. 4F). At the same time, cytokine expression profiling showed that BMDMs exposed to Stat5a-low tumor cells upregulated Ccl2 and Ccl5 (Fig. 4G). Functionally, this was accompanied by an increase in a CD206-enriched macrophage population after co-culture with tumor cells and T cells (Supplementary Fig. 4C), supporting macrophage remodeling toward an immune-suppressive state. The tumor cells themselves also displayed coordinated immune-regulatory changes. In the presence of BMDMs, SH tumor cells showed increased expression of Ccl2, Ccl5, Csf1, Il6, and Pdl1, with reduced Cxcl10 (Supplementary Fig. 4D), whereas T cells co-cultured with the SH group upregulated inhibitory molecules including Ctla4, Foxp3, Lag3, and Pd1 (Supplementary Fig. 4E). When tumor cell migration was induced with 30% conditioned medium from macrophage, the SH group showed a greater number of migrating tumor cells (Fig. 4H, Supplementary Fig. 4F), consistent with reciprocal tumor-macrophage-T-cell crosstalk that may favor metastatic progression.

To test whether macrophages functionally contributed to the in vivo phenotype, we depleted macrophages using clodronate liposomes. Macrophage depletion reduced orthotopic tumor growth and markedly decreased lung and liver metastatic burden, particularly in the SH group (Fig. 4I–L). These results identify macrophages as a key amplifier of the prometastatic phenotype associated with low Stat5a expression. To place these observations in a broader immune-state framework, we analyzed public single-cell and spatial datasets of breast cancer lung metastasis [32]. Myeloid populations consisted of multiple transcriptionally distinct states (Fig. 4M). At the sample level, tumor-cell Stat5a expression was associated with relative enrichment of specific myeloid states, including C1qc-high resident/APC-like macrophages, whereas inflammatory S100a8/9/Il1b-associated neighborhoods showed weaker or inverse trends (Fig. 4N–O). These public-data analyses are supportive rather than definitive, but they reinforce the idea that Stat5a-related biology in metastatic lesions is better understood as context-dependent myeloid remodeling.

LIF-LIFR: the upstream activator of STAT5A phosphorylation

We analyzed the cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction pathway, and among the immune cell chemokines (such as CCL2, CCL5, and CXCL10), LIFR (a component of the JAK-STAT signaling pathway) drew our attention. Protein interaction analysis revealed that there might be an interaction between LIFR and STAT5A (Fig. 5A). Moreover, LIFR has been reported to be a metastasis-suppressing gene in breast cancer [33, 34]. When T47D and 4T1 cells were stimulated with LIF (the ligand of LIFR), we observed the accumulation of phosphorylated STAT5A (pSTAT5A) in the cell nucleus (Fig. 5B). LIF stimulation increased tumor cell epithelial markers (E-Cadherin, EPCAM), decreased mesenchymal markers (N-Cadherin, VIM), reduced macrophage chemokines (CCL2, CCL5, CSF1), elevated T cell chemokine CXCL10, and lowered PD-L1 (Fig. 5C–F). These findings indicate that LIF-LIFR signaling can function upstream of STAT5A phosphorylation and can partially attenuate EMT-like and immune-suppressive tumor-cell programs.

Leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF) is predominantly produced by various cell types, including activated T cells, monocytes, and glial cells [35]. Among BMDMs and T cells co-cultured with tumor cells, BMDMs exhibited the most substantial increase in Lif production (Fig. 5G), suggesting that macrophages may serve as a local source of Lif in this context. When macrophages were examined under distinct in vitro activation conditions, Lif expression differed substantially across conditions, with the strongest induction observed in the pro-inflammatory activation condition (Fig. 5H). Consistent with these observations, conditioned media from differently activated macrophage populations differentially modulated tumor-cell pStat5a, E-cadherin, and Vimentin (Fig. 5I), and membrane-associated E-cadherin staining was most evident after treatment with conditioned medium from the activation condition with the highest Lif output (Fig. 5J). Together, these data support a model in which macrophage-derived Lif contributes to restoration of Stat5a activity via Lif-Lifr signaling.

Progressive STAT5A silencing in clinical specimens and targeted demethylation partially reverses metastatic phenotypes

We validated our hypothesis using clinical samples. The methylation level of CpG sites near the STAT5A promoter was the lowest in normal breast tissues, increased in primary tumors, and reached the highest in metastatic lesions (Fig. 6A). Correspondingly, immunohistochemical staining of STAT5A was performed on a tissue microarray containing para-tumor tissues, ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS), invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC), positive lymph nodes, and distant metastatic lesions. The results showed that STAT5A expression was the highest in para-tumor tissues, while the lowest expression was observed in tumor tissues of positive lymph nodes and distant metastases (Fig. 6B–D). We collected 100 cases of breast carcinoma with axillary lymph node metastasis and 101 cases without. In carcinoma in situ with axillary lymph node metastasis, the expressions of STAT5A and E-Cadherin were lower, while the expressions of VIM and CCL2 were higher (Fig. 6E). Correlation analysis further showed that STAT5A expression was positively associated with E-cadherin and negatively associated with Vimentin and CCL2 (Fig. 6F), linking low STAT5A expression to both EMT-like and macrophage-associated immune-regulatory phenotypes in clinical samples.

To reverse epigenetic silencing, we used a dCas9-TET1CD system (catalytically inactive dCas9 fused to TET1 demethylase) with 6 sgRNAs targeting the STAT5A promoter (Fig. 6G). Targeted demethylation restored STAT5A expression in T47D and 4T1 cells. sgRNA5 was optimal for T47D, and sgRNA3/5 for 4T1 (Figs. 6H, J). The MS2 system, recruiting multiple TET1CD via MS2 coat protein, further enhanced STAT5A expression (Figs. 6I, K). Functionally, STAT5A reactivation reduced wound closure and migration (Supplementary Fig. 5A–C), restored epithelial markers, reduced mesenchymal-associated markers (Supplementary Fig. 5D), and suppressed immune-regulatory cytokines in both T47D and 4T1 cells (Supplementary Fig. 5E–F). In co-culture systems, STAT5A reactivation also increased tumor-cell apoptosis and reduced the expansion of CD206-enriched macrophage populations (Supplementary Fig. 5G–H). Together, these results support a functional link between methylation-associated STAT5A silencing and metastatic phenotypes and show that targeted reactivation of STAT5A can partially reverse both EMT-like and immune-suppressive features.

Proposed mechanism

We propose that promoter hypermethylation-mediated silencing of STAT5A contributes to breast cancer metastatic progression through coordinated regulation of tumor-cell plasticity and the immune microenvironment. Reduced STAT5A expression and phosphorylation are associated with EMT-like reprogramming, enhanced migration/invasion, impaired T-cell cytotoxicity, and macrophage-associated immunosuppressive remodeling. Reactivation of STAT5A by targeted demethylation or stimulation of the LIF-LIFR pathway partially attenuated these phenotypes, supporting a functional connection between epigenetic STAT5A silencing and metastatic behavior.

Discussion

STAT5A exhibits tissue-specific functions, oncogenic in hematological malignancies and ovarian cancer, tumor-suppressive in hepatocellular carcinoma [22–25], and here, a metastasis suppressor in breast cancer. In mammary tissue, STAT5A mediates lactogenic differentiation [36, 37], and its downregulation in breast cancer drives distant metastasis. DNA methylation-mediated gene silencing plays a crucial role in tumor metastasis [38]. We show that STAT5A undergoes progressive methylation-associated silencing during breast cancer progression, with reduced STAT5A expression observed from primary lesions to metastatic sites. Functionally, loss of STAT5A suppressed baseline tumor-cell proliferation but promoted migration, invasion, and EMT-like molecular changes, indicating that STAT5A deficiency shifts tumor cells toward a more dissemination-prone phenotype rather than simply increasing proliferative output.

Our chromatin profiling analyses further suggest that STAT5A participates in EMT-related transcriptional regulation. In particular, CUT&Tag analysis revealed STAT5A occupancy near the CDH1 and CDH2 loci. However, occupancy at both loci should not be overinterpreted as proof of identical transcriptional effects. Because CDH1 and CDH2 are oppositely regulated in our system, the current data do not by themselves establish whether STAT5A directly activates one locus while repressing the other, or whether one or both effects are indirect. Locus-specific transcriptional output may depend on chromatin context, co-regulators, and broader network interactions [39–41]. Thus, our current data support a role for STAT5A in EMT-related transcriptional control, while the precise directionality of regulation at individual loci remains to be clarified.

Breast cancer metastasis arises from coordinated interactions between tumor-cell plasticity and the tumor microenvironment. In this study, our findings support STAT5A as an important molecular node linking these two processes. A major observation of this study is that the prometastatic phenotype associated with low STAT5A expression is strongly host-context-dependent. In immunodeficient NSG mice, STAT5A-deficient tumor cells retained EMT-like features in vivo, but did not show a clear increase in orthotopic pulmonary metastatic burden. By contrast, in immunocompetent BALB/c models, low STAT5A expression was associated with greater pulmonary and hepatic metastatic burden, indicating that tumor-cell plasticity alone is insufficient to explain the full in vivo phenotype. Instead, the metastatic consequence of STAT5A loss appears to be substantially shaped by interactions with the host immune system. Consistent with this interpretation, our immune analyses showed that low STAT5A expression was associated with reduced T-cell cytotoxicity, increased macrophage recruitment-related cytokines, and macrophage-enriched immunosuppressive remodeling. Our data are more consistent with context-dependent macrophage activation states and macrophage-associated immunosuppressive phenotypes. Public single-cell and spatial analyses further support this view by showing that metastatic lung myeloid populations consist of multiple transcriptionally distinct states, and that tumor-cell-associated Stat5a expression is differentially linked to selected myeloid/macrophage programs. These public-data analyses reinforce the interpretation that STAT5A-related biology in metastatic lesions is better understood as context-dependent myeloid remodeling.

Another important finding is that LIF-LIFR signaling functions upstream of STAT5A phosphorylation. LIF stimulation promoted nuclear accumulation of pSTAT5A and was accompanied by molecular changes consistent with attenuation of EMT-like and immune-suppressive tumor-cell programs. In our co-culture system, macrophages represented an important local source of LIF, and conditioned media derived from differently activated macrophage populations differentially affected STAT5A phosphorylation and epithelial marker expression in tumor cells. While LIFR-STAT3 signaling is well-documented [42–44], our study expands LIFR’s metastasis-suppressive function to STAT5A, highlighting pathway crosstalk. Together, these findings support a model in which macrophage-derived LIF contributes to restoration of STAT5A activity within the tumor microenvironment. When STAT5A is epigenetically silenced, this protective signaling axis appears to be weakened, thereby favoring both tumor-cell plasticity and immunosuppressive microenvironmental remodeling.

Reduced pSTAT5A correlates with anti-estrogen therapy resistance [45], suggesting STAT5A may predict treatment response. The expression level and promoter methylation status of STAT5A can be quantitatively detected. By combining with molecules such as E-Cadherin and VIM, a combined prediction model might be constructed, which could be expected to further improve the accuracy of breast cancer metastasis risk stratification. In terms of therapeutic targets, the LIF-LIFR-STAT5A axis revealed in this study provides a new direction for the intervention of breast cancer metastasis. On one hand, demethylating drugs targeting the methylation of the STAT5A promoter (such as azacitidine) might restore STAT5A expression, reverse the EMT phenotype of tumor cells and reshape the anti-tumor immune microenvironment; on the other hand, LIF or LIFR agonists might directly activate the phosphorylation of STAT5A and enhance anti-tumor effects. However, the clinical associations and translational applications of STAT5A should be interpreted cautiously. Subtype-stratified analyses indicate that the relationship of STAT5A with expression, methylation, and outcome is subtype-dependent rather than uniform across all breast cancers.

Limitations

The study has some limitations. Firstly, although the clinical samples cover various types of lesion tissues, they are still single-center data with a relatively small sample size, and further validation of the prognostic value of STAT5A is needed through multi-center large-sample cohorts. Secondly, the specific regulatory mechanism of STAT5A promoter methylation has not been clarified, and the role of DNA methyltransferases (such as DNMT1, DNMT3B) in it needs to be further explored. Finally, the efficacy and safety of targeted drug intervention for STAT5A methylation still need to be further studied.

Future directions

Future research can focus on the following aspects. First, analyze the upstream regulatory mechanism of STAT5A promoter methylation and identify the key signaling pathways that induce its methylation during tumor progression. Second, conduct preclinical studies on targeted intervention of STAT5A to evaluate the anti-metastasis effects of demethylating drugs or LIFR agonists. Third, explore the synergistic effect of STAT5A with immune checkpoint inhibitors (such as PD-1 antibodies) to provide new combined strategies for breast cancer immunotherapy.

In summary, our findings support a model in which DNA methylation-mediated silencing of STAT5A contributes to breast cancer metastatic progression through coordinated regulation of EMT-like reprogramming and immune microenvironment remodeling. These results identify STAT5A-related signaling as a biologically relevant pathway for further mechanistic investigation and as a potential direction for future therapeutic exploration.
