Model-based pharmacoeconomic analysis of anti-VEGF strategies for neovascular age-related macular degeneration: a value-based comparison of real-world administration approaches
Authors: Saturnino Manuel Gismero Moreno, Francisco Jódar Sánchez, Nuria García-Agua Soler, Francisco Rivas Ruiz, Antonio J Garcia-Ruiz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo evaluate the cost-effectiveness of anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) treatments for neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD) using a value-based model that considers drug durability, dosing regimens and real-world administration strategies, including safe vial fractionation.Design and settingModel-based pharmacoeconomic analysis using data from randomised clinical trials and network meta-analyses. Analysis conducted from the payer perspective using cost data from the Spanish National Health System.MethodsA model-based analysis compared five anti-VEGF agents—innovator and biosimilar ranibizumab, aflibercept 2 mg, brolucizumab and faricimab—across three dosing regimens: fixed, Pro Re Nata and Treat-and-Extend (TAE). Administration formats included single-use vials, prefilled syringes and vial fractionation (VF), with or without dead-space-free (DSF) syringes to minimise waste. The primary outcome was cost per optimal responder, defined as a patient gaining ≥15 Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) letters, with and without adverse events. Cost-effectiveness was evaluated using Number Needed to Treat (NNT), Net Efficacy Adjusted for Risk-NNT (adjusted for safety) and incremental cost-effectiveness ratios. Secondary outcomes included the number of treated patients and optimal responders achievable within a fixed €1 000 000 budget.ResultsThe most cost-effective strategy was aflibercept 2 mg under a TAE regimen using DSF VF, with a total cost of €6214 per patient and a cost per optimal responder of €27 155. Under a fixed budget of €1 000 000, this approach allowed treatment of 160 patients, yielding 36 optimal responders. Faricimab with DSF VF ranked second, with a total cost of €5847 and a cost per optimal responder of €28 652, treating 171 patients and achieving 34 responders. In contrast, single-use vials without VF led to substantially higher total costs (eg, €11 305 for aflibercept TAE) and lower treatment capacity (eg, 88 patients treated).ConclusionsThis model demonstrates that combining durable agents, extended dosing intervals and optimised delivery strategies (eg, prefilled syringes and DSF VF) can substantially improve the cost-effectiveness and sustainability of anti-VEGF therapy in public health systems.Keywords: Clinical Decision-Making, Drug Therapy, Health Care Costs, HEALTH ECONOMICS, Medical ophthalmology, Medical retina

Introduction

Previous pharmacoeconomic studies often assess direct drug costs without fully integrating treatment durability and administration methods. Existing literature highlights clinical and economic benefits of early intervention and potential cost savings from less frequent dosing.1,4

Increasing clinical demand and budget constraints necessitate strategies optimising both effectiveness and resource use. The value-based healthcare (VBHC) framework emphasises maximising patient-centred health outcomes per unit cost. This study evaluates the cost-effectiveness of anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) treatments, comparing single-use vials, prefilled syringes (PFS) and vial fractionation (VF), particularly with dead-space-free (DSF) syringes. Using metrics such as Number Needed to Treat (NNT)5 and Net Efficacy Adjusted for Risk (NEAR-NNT),6 we estimate cost per meaningful outcome (Vision gain of ≥15 letters on the Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study -ETDRS- chart) and budget impact, guiding providers and policymakers toward optimal, sustainable treatment choices.

Neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD) is a leading cause of blindness in the elderly,7 significantly impacting quality of life and healthcare costs. Anti-VEGF therapies have revolutionised treatment, but their high cost challenges sustainability in publicly funded healthcare systems.

Approved anti-VEGF agents for nAMD in Spain include innovator ranibizumab (IN-RBZ), aflibercept 2 mg (AFL), brolucizumab (BRL), biosimilar ranibizumab (BS-RBZ), faricimab (FAR) and aflibercept 8 mg (AFL8).

These agents show comparable efficacy in randomised controlled trials, making durability, safety and cost critical in treatment selection.8,11 Bevacizumab was excluded due to its off-label status, and AFL8 due to insufficient long-term data.12

Previous pharmacoeconomic studies have assessed clinical and economic benefits of early intervention (Brown et al),1 2 cost-effectiveness in different settings (Ferrante et al),3 cost savings from less frequent dosing (Quist et al),4 and reduced resource utilisation with extended fixed dosing schedules using longer-lasting drugs such as BRL (Cabrera et al).5 However, these analyses usually omit comprehensive real-world administration strategies.

Growing clinical demand and budget constraints necessitate strategies optimising both clinical effectiveness and resource utilisation. The VBHC framework emphasises maximising patient-centred health outcomes per unit cost. This study assesses the cost-effectiveness of anti-VEGF treatments, comparing single-use vials, PFS and VF, particularly using DSF syringes. Employing metrics such as NNT6 and NEAR-NNT,13 we estimate how many patients must be treated to achieve a meaningful clinical outcome (≥15 ETDRS letters gained), along with their cost and budget impact, providing guidance for healthcare providers and policymakers towards sustainable and optimal treatment decisions.

Methods

Study design

A cost-effectiveness analysis was chosen over cost-minimisation analysis due to differences in anti-VEGF durability and administration frequency. The incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) was compared across Treat-and-Extend (TAE), Pro Re Nata (PRN) and fixed extended regimens followed by personalised treatment intervals (PTI). The study included AFL, BRL, FAR, IN-RBZ and BS-RBZ, following clinical guideline dosing regimens, where q8w, q12w and q16w indicate administration every 8, 12 and 16 weeks, respectively.

Cost analysis

This pharmacoeconomic evaluation assessed no-VF, PFS and VF with and without DSF syringes. Costs were calculated from a payer perspective, applying a 3% discount rate per WHO CHOICE guidelines. Direct costs included drug acquisition (adjusted for hospital pricing and market variations), administration (intravitreal injection procedures) and monitoring (outpatient visits, optical coherence tomography (OCT) imaging).

Efficacy measures

Efficacy estimates were derived from Tricco et al and Finger et al.14 15 NNT, Number Needed to Harm (NNH) and NEAR-NNT were calculated using Bucher’s method and Bayesian models,16 incorporating real-world administration strategies like VF for improved practical relevance. NEAR-NNT combined efficacy and safety by assessing optimal responders without adverse events (AEs).

Sensitivity analysis

We conducted deterministic univariate sensitivity analyses to evaluate the impact of delivery strategies (single-use vials, PFS and VF using either conventional or DSF syringes—Zero Residual, 0.2 mL silicone oil-free syringe, SJJ Solutions B.V.—to minimise drug waste) and dosing regimens (fixed, PRN and TAE) on cost-effectiveness. Analytical approach and cost data

Costs were based on the Spanish National Health System and expressed in euros for European comparability. The methodology followed Spanish Society of Hospital Pharmacy guidelines.

The Spanish Laboratory Selling Price (LSP) was used as the reference drug cost per intravitreal injection (IVI), adjusted for Royal Decree-Law 8/2010 and 4% Value Added Tax (VAT).

To consider direct costs, we must account for those associated with the administration procedure and the monitoring expenses incurred during follow-up visits at the outpatient clinic and for OCT imaging, that are listed in the public price list of the Andalusian Health Service as €54.58 (according to article III.2.3.2) and €119.90 (according to article III.3.1.4.29), respectively. The cost of the IVI procedure, not including the drug, is not specified in the mentioned list. Therefore, we obtained this information from the public price list of the Community of Madrid Health Service (E 03.1.2.7.1.1), which amounts to €225.

Total cost per injection visit was €399.48, applicable to monthly, bimonthly, TAE and PTI regimens. For PRN regimens, monthly monitoring visits (€174.48) were counted separately, while injection frequency determined total costs.

Monitoring and injection frequency

Injection and monitoring frequency estimates were derived from Finger et al (2021) Network Meta-Analysis (NMA), except for FAR, which was based on TENAYA and LUCERNE trials.

BRL: in HAWK (55.6%) and HARRIER (51%) trials, most patients required one extra monitoring visit beyond injections, leading to 11.96 visits over 2 years.

FAR: after a four-dose loading phase, 21.2%, 33.4% and 45.3% of patients followed q8w, q12w and q16w dosing, averaging 10.76 injections and 12.01 monitoring visits over 2 years.

Drug presentation and cost variability

Vials and PFS were considered single-use formats. In contrast, VF allowed multiple doses to be extracted from a single vial—typically 0.1 mL per dose with conventional syringes and 0.05 mL with DSF syringes. Drug cost per injection was calculated by dividing the adjusted LSP, including 4% VAT and mandatory discounts under Spanish Royal Decree-Law 8/2010, by the number of extractable doses. Data analysis

The primary outcome was cost per additional OptR, calculated using evidence-based medicine indexes and expressed as ICER. Treatments were compared with placebo, assuming zero cost/effectiveness for placebo. Although Quality-Adjusted Life Year (QALY)-based thresholds are not applicable without utility measures, we used a threshold of £20 000 per optimal responder (≥15 ETDRS letters gained without AEs). This is supported by studies linking such gains to 0.9–1.1 QALYs over 2 years,21 aligning our ICER benchmark with National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE)22 and Spanish value-based criteria.23 We considered strategies cost-effective when their ICER fell below £20 000 (€23 775.55). We applied the official exchange rate published by the Spanish Official State Gazette (BOE-A-2024–17650) on 30 August 2024 (£1=€1.00/0.84120), resulting in a 2-year threshold of €47 551.11 per responder. This corresponds to an annualised ICER of €23 775.55, in line with commonly accepted value-for-money benchmarks in the UK and Spain.

Model assumptions

This model assumed standard dosing regimens and injection frequencies based on pivotal trials and NMAs. Four administration formats were evaluated: single-use vials, PFS, conventional VF and VF using DSF syringes, each with corresponding cost structures. Drug prices reflected Spanish LSP adjusted for VAT and legislation; DSF syringes added €30 per dose. Monitoring and procedure costs followed public tariffs. All cost inputs were from the most recent sources available (2023–2024), with no additional inflation adjustment applied beyond the 3% discount rate. No relevant differences in safety across agents were assumed based on pooled AE data. Cost-effectiveness outcomes were based on visual gain thresholds (≥15 ETDRS letters), using NNT and NEAR-NNT metrics.

Patient and public involvement

None.

When considering drug acquisition costs alone, BRL-PFS, AFL-PFS-TAE and IN-RBZ-PRN were the most cost-effective, followed by FAR vials and BS-RBZ-PRN. Cost-effectiveness rankings were influenced by treatment durability, with BRL-PFS achieving the lowest total cost due to its quarterly fixed regimen and PFS format. Cost per optimal responder

Cost-effectiveness was assessed using the cost per OptR (NNT for ≥15 ETDRS letters gained) and OptR without AEs. BRL-PFS was the most cost-effective, followed by AFL-PFS-TAE, while IN-RBZ-PFS-PRN surpassed FAR vials depending on total costs. Among vial-based options, FAR was the most efficient due to its extended durability but remained less cost-effective than lower-priced PFS options. As expected, fixed regimens incurred higher costs due to more frequent injections.

Budget-constrained analysis (€1 000 000)

A €1 000 000 threshold was selected as a pragmatic reference point commonly used in budget impact models to reflect a high-cost therapy population within regional health systems (see table 4). BRL-PFS treated the most patients (125), followed by AFL-PFS-TAE (121) and FAR vials (107), outperforming RBZ-based regimens. Among RBZ options, IN-RBZ-PFS-PRN was the most efficient (89 patients treated). In OptRs, BRL-PFS and AFL-PFS-TAE achieved 31 and 28, respectively, followed by IN-RBZ-PFS-PRN (24), BS-RBZ-PRN and FAR vials (22 each).

The most cost-effective approach was AFL in a TAE regimen combined with DSF VF. Although not the most durable agent, its larger vial volume allows more efficient fractionation and lower per-dose costs, offsetting its moderate injection frequency. Among DSF VF RBZ-based strategies, PRN regimens showed the greatest cost reduction, as fewer injections combined with lower drug waste maximised efficiency.

Overall cost-effectiveness ranking

The most cost-effective option was AFL-TAE with DSF VF, followed by FAR with DSF VF. In third place, AFL-TAE and FAR with conventional VF were tied, followed by BS-RBZ-PRN with DSF VF in fourth. IN-RBZ-PRN with DSF VF and BRL-PFS ranked jointly fifth, with BRL-PFS representing the most efficient strategy when VF is not employed, followed by AFL TAE (no VF) and FAR (no VF).

As expected, under a €1 000 000 budget with VF, the ranking remained consistent.

Discussion

The sustainability of anti-VEGF therapy depends not only on drug selection but also on optimised delivery strategies that reduce costs while maintaining clinical outcomes. Sensitivity analyses highlighted the impact of DSF VF in improving cost-effectiveness. Fractionated AFL, due to its larger vial volume and moderate durability, achieved optimal efficiency, rivalling the performance of PFS strategies such as BRL-PFS. AFL and durable agents like FAR, especially with DSF delivery, consistently maximised both patient access and treatment outcomes—reinforcing the need for resource-conscious approaches in cost-sensitive healthcare systems.

A key finding was the substantial variation in injection burden across regimens over 2 years. AFL in a TAE regimen required fewer injections than ranibizumab in a PRN strategy, reducing hospital visits and costs while potentially improving adherence.24 FAR showed a comparable injection burden to AFL-TAE, positioning it as a viable alternative for reducing healthcare resource use and a strong candidate for broader application in TAE protocols.

Economic impact of vials versus PFS

Among agents available in both vial and PFS formats, we observed significant cost differences. While PFS offers advantages in sterility and handling time, they entail higher acquisition costs.25 When fractionation is not feasible, vials become less efficient due to drug waste. This highlights the importance of assessing at what price the additional convenience of PFS justifies its higher cost, particularly in high-volume settings.

VF as a cost-optimisation strategy

VF substantially reduces per-injection costs without compromising safety or efficacy.26,28 These findings are consistent with bevacizumab repackaging practices adopted in several countries.29

However, VF should only be implemented under strict aseptic conditions, including International Organization for Standardization (ISO)-classified cleanrooms, silicone oil-free syringes and handling by trained pharmacy staff following Good Manufacturing Practices (GMP). In public healthcare systems, where staff turnover may compromise continuity, the uninterrupted availability of qualified personnel is essential. Otherwise, VF should be avoided due to potential risks to patient safety and procedural reliability. When performed under GMP conditions in hospital pharmacies, VF may offer greater microbiological safety than in-clinic preparation from single-use vials immediately prior to injection, although PFS remains the safest option.30 Commercial PFSs remain the preferred option due to their safety, ease of use and dosing accuracy. Nevertheless, to encourage wider adoption, pricing policies should reflect the efficiency gains achievable through safe and standardised VF.

Implications for policy and clinical decision-making

The threshold price analysis incorporating VF strategies provides a pragmatic tool for policymakers, identifying drug price points at which alternatives become cost-effective versus the most efficient strategy. This supports VBHC principles, where decisions weigh both outcomes and economic value. Though scenario-dependent and based on efficacy/safety assumptions, these thresholds guide price negotiations, reimbursement and procurement—especially for PFS, biosimilars and costly retinal therapies.

Although not a replacement for QALY-based thresholds, our ICER benchmark enables value-based evaluation using established ophthalmic outcomes when utility data are lacking. It offers a practical tool for decision-making where QALYs are hard to estimate or fail to reflect full patient benefit.

From a healthcare system perspective, incorporating pharmacoeconomic evidence into clinical guidelines and reimbursement frameworks could enhance equity of access by enabling more patients to receive optimal treatment within existing budget constraints.

Future research should assess the long-term clinical and economic impact of VF strategies in real-world settings, as well as the potential role of emerging biosimilars in improving the sustainability of retinal care. These findings may also support the allocation of dedicated resources to hospital pharmacies to ensure GMP-compliant VF conditions and provide robust data to inform pricing negotiations for PFS.

The budget impact analysis offers a population-level perspective aligned with VBHC, showing that efficient strategies enable more patients to achieve meaningful visual improvement safely within budget limits.

Limitations

This study has certain limitations. While both fixed and PRN regimens were analysed from a pharmacoeconomic perspective, real-world practice often differs, with undertreatment leading to lower effectiveness than clinical trial data suggest. The exclusion of AFL8 due to insufficient 2-year follow-up data in network meta-analyses limits the scope of the study; however, findings with AFL suggest that the higher-dose formulation may be a competitive option—alongside FAR under TAE regimens. Additionally, as the cost analysis is based on the Spanish healthcare system, its generalisability to other European settings may be limited.

Future research should incorporate TAE regimens with newer agents—such as FAR and AFL8—into network meta-analyses, and explore the cost-saving potential of upcoming AFL biosimilars, while also addressing real-world safety concerns, including BRL-associated occlusive vasculitis.31 Further investigation into sustained-release devices, such as the Port Delivery System,32 33 may offer additional insights into reducing treatment burden, patient visits and overall healthcare costs.

Conclusion

This study highlights the importance of considering not only the choice of anti-VEGF agent and treatment intervals in the management of nAMD, but also the strategy of administration. Our findings demonstrate that drug durability and the ability to extend injection intervals significantly influence cost-effectiveness when compared with traditional fixed regimens.

Furthermore, although PFS are clearly the preferred option, real-world practices such as safe and efficient VF may offer a practical opportunity to enhance economic sustainability without compromising clinical outcomes. These practices also provide a valuable benchmark for the pricing of commercial PFS, encouraging pharmaceutical manufacturers to align their cost with the efficiency already achieved through optimised compounding. This would help ensure that the clear safety and convenience advantages of PFS are matched by economic value, thereby facilitating their broader and more sustainable adoption.

The analysis focuses on cost per meaningful clinical result, rather than cost per patient treated, reflecting a value-based approach to therapeutic evaluation. The findings also underscore the potential role of future AFL biosimilars in further reducing treatment costs. This data-driven framework enhances our understanding of the effectiveness, safety and efficiency of available treatment options, while providing a basis for value-based comparisons that can inform pricing negotiations and support the responsible allocation of healthcare resources.
