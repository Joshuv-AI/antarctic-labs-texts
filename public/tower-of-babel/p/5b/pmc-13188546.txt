Functions of dendrobine in a zebrafish model of diabetic retinopathy
Authors: Zilu Zhu, Xinyu Li, Songtao Wang, Hui Luo, Jiaolong Huang, Peng Duan, Chunhui Hu, Meijuan Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDendrobine (DEN), an alkaloid isolated from Dendrobium species, is widely used in diabetes management. However, its therapeutic potential against high-glucose–induced diabetic retinopathy (DR) has not been explored. This study used zebrafish (Danio rerio) as a model to investigate the protective effects of DEN on high-glucose–induced DR and to elucidate its underlying mechanisms.MethodsThe effects of DEN were evaluated using transgenic zebrafish lines, with hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining of ocular tissues to assess retinal morphology. Behavioral tracking quantified locomotor function. RNA sequencing was performed to identify co-differentially expressed genes (DEGs), characterize common signaling pathways, and construct protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks. Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) was used for validation.ResultsDEN significantly alleviated high-glucose–induced retinal damage, improved physiological development, and restored locomotor function. Transcriptomic analysis of zebrafish treated with 20 mg/L and 40 mg/L DEN + glucose identified 80 commonly reversed DEGs, significantly enriched in retinol metabolism, steroid hormone biosynthesis, porphyrin metabolism, and PPAR signaling pathway. Notably, shared genes such as ugt1a1, ugt2a2, ugt2b1, and ugt5a2 were downregulated across the retinol metabolism, steroid hormone biosynthesis, and porphyrin metabolism pathways. ConclusionsThis study provides novel morphological and transcriptomic evidence that DEN mitigates high-glucose–induced DR in a zebrafish model. The findings highlight DEN’s therapeutic potential and offer new insights into the application of traditional Chinese medicine for DR treatment.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12967-026-08113-1.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a chronic metabolic disorder characterized by persistently elevated blood glucose levels resulting from insulin deficiency or dysfunction, leading to multisystem damage [1]. Chronic hyperglycemia can precipitate microvascular complications across multiple organs, with DR being a primary manifestation of ocular injury. It also affects other target organs such as the heart and kidneys [2]. Previous research indicates that DR currently affects more than 100 million individuals worldwide and is the leading cause of acquired visual impairment and blindness among the working-age population. This poses a serious threat to human health and has become a major global public health challenge [3, 4].

The core mechanism of DR stems from chronic hyperglycemia, which triggers a cascade of metabolic, vascular, and inflammatory events [5–7]. Central to this process is the disruption of metabolic pathways caused by sustained hyperglycemia. In particular, it activates the polyol pathway within the retina, leading to intracellular accumulation of sorbitol and advanced glycation end products. This buildup increases cellular osmotic pressure, ultimately resulting in cell damage and dysfunction. Critically, the cascade also engages the Bax/Bcl-2 apoptotic pathway, undermining the integrity of capillary walls [8]. At the same time, hyperglycemia raises the expression of retinal vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), which increases vascular permeability and disrupts the blood–retinal barrier—changes that can cause diabetic macular edema and vision loss [9, 10]. Furthermore, hyperglycemia provokes retinal inflammation, releasing mediators like IL-1, IL-6, and TNF-α [11]. Over time, this inflammatory response—effectively a cytokine storm—interacts with metabolic toxic byproducts to create a complex injury network, driving progressive disruption of retinal architecture and ultimately leading to functional deterioration.

Current standard therapies for DR, including anti-VEGF biologics, laser photocoagulation, and microinvasive vitrectomy, have shown efficacy in delaying disease progression [12]. However, each comes with notable limitations. Anti-VEGF treatments, while effective at reducing macular edema, are hindered by drug resistance and the need for frequent injections, which can reduce patient compliance and increase healthcare costs [13]. Likewise, although laser photocoagulation can help prevent vision loss, it may cause retinal damage, leading to peripheral vision loss [14]. Vitrectomy, despite its therapeutic benefits, is invasive and carries risks such as cataract formation and retinal detachment [15]. Given these challenges, the development of minimally invasive traditional Chinese medicine extracts—offering synergistic, multi-target effects and potentially improving treatment compliance—represents a promising direction in DR management.

The zebrafish is an ideal model for studying vertebrate genetics and disease because of its genetic similarity to humans, low maintenance costs, high reproductive rate, and transparent embryos. Notably, the zebrafish retina reaches maturity by 72 h post-fertilization (hpf), exhibiting structural and functional similarities to the human retina [16], making it especially valuable for ophthalmic research. Zebrafish have become a robust in vivo model for investigating retinal pathologies [17]. They are also valuable in metabolic disease research because of their conserved pancreatic structure, expression of key glucose-regulating proteins, and measurable glucose levels at all life stages. Importantly, zebrafish display hyperglycemia-induced retinal microvascular damage, closely resembling early DR pathology [18, 19]. Their unique β-cell regenerative capacity further supports studies into metabolic recovery mechanisms. Collectively, these attributes make zebrafish a powerful, high-throughput platform for drug screening targeting both glucose dysregulation and DR [20].

Dendrobium officinale, a traditional Chinese medicinal herb, is widely used in clinical settings for the treatment of diabetes. DEN, a pyrrolizidine derivative alkaloid isolated from Dendrobium officinale [21], exhibits diverse biological activities, including regulation of blood glucose levels, anti-inflammatory effects, analgesia, hypotensive action, hypothermic effects, and neuroprotection [22, 23]. Recent studies further suggest that DEN can improve glucose and lipid metabolism disorders, reduce memory deficits, and enhance neuronal function while preserving synaptic structural integrity [24]. Notably, DEN has been identified as a natural Nrf2 agonist, offering retinal protection against oxidative stress via activation of the Nrf2 pathway [25]. Despite these promising findings, its potential role and underlying mechanisms in alleviating DR remain unclear. Therefore, this study was performed to investigate the therapeutic effects of DEN on DR and to elucidate its mechanisms, with the aim of contributing to safer treatment options for patients.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

DEN was purchased from Chengdu Efa Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Chengdu, China). Glucose was obtained from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), and 4% paraformaldehyde was obtained from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). The Glucose Assay Kit was purchased from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China).

Zebrafish husbandry and embryo collection

AB wild-type zebrafish (Danio rerio) were obtained from the Key Laboratory of Zebrafish Modeling and Drug Screening for Human Diseases in Xiangyang City. Transgenic zebrafish Tg(flk1:EGFP) were purchased from the China Zebrafish Resource Center. Adult zebrafish aged 5–6 months were maintained in 40-L glass tanks under standardized conditions (28.0 °C ± 0.5 °C, 14-hour light/10-hour dark cycle) and fed twice daily with freshly hatched feed. For embryo production, healthy adults (2 males:1 female) were placed in spawning tanks separated by transparent barriers overnight. At light onset, the barriers were removed to initiate natural mating. Fertilized eggs were collected within 4 hpf, rinsed three times with embryo medium to remove debris and unfertilized eggs, and maintained in medium refreshed every 24 h to prevent microbial contamination.

Establishment of DR model

To determine the optimal glucose concentration for inducing DR in zebrafish, glucose gradients of 90, 110, 130, and 150 mmol/L were established. Twenty viable embryos per group were randomly allocated into six-well plates (three biological replicates per concentration). Glucose was dissolved in an appropriate volume of 0.5% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) solution and then brought to 50 mL with E3 medium to prepare the respective glucose solutions. The control group received E3 medium containing 0.5% DMSO. For each concentration, zebrafish mortality was recorded. In addition, Tg(flk1:EGFP) transgenic zebrafish were used for retinal vascular imaging. Retinal vasculature was imaged, and vessel diameters were quantified using ImageJ software v1.53k (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). Based on mortality rates and changes in vascular diameter, a glucose concentration of 130 mmol/L was selected as the modeling condition. This group was designated as the Glu group (high-glucose).

Experimental procedures

To determine the safe experimental concentration of DEN, gradients of 20, 40, 60, 80, 100, 120, and 140 mg/L were established under a background of 130 mmol/L glucose. DEN was dissolved in 0.5% DMSO to prepare a 2000-mg/L stock solution, which was then added to E3 medium containing 130 mmol/L glucose and diluted with 0.5% DMSO to the target concentrations. Zebrafish larvae were exposed to the different DEN solutions, and mortality and malformation rates were recorded. Based on these results, 20 mg/L and 40 mg/L were selected as experimental concentrations and designated as follows: 20 mg/L DEN + Glu group (20 mg/L DEN + 130 mmol/L glucose) and 40 mg/L DEN + Glu group (40 mg/L DEN + 130 mmol/L glucose).

At 3 days post-fertilization (dpf), zebrafish larvae were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 20 larvae/group) and treated for 3 days (to 6 dpf) with the following solutions: Control group, Glu group, 20 mg/L DEN + Glu group, and 40 mg/L DEN + Glu group. All treatments were performed in six-well plates, with 8 mL of treatment solution per well. During the treatment period, half of the solution volume (4 mL) was replaced daily to maintain drug and glucose concentrations. At 6 dpf, larvae were anesthetized with MS-222 solution until unconscious, then euthanized and collected for subsequent analysis.

Measurement of glucose levels

Glucose levels in whole-body lysates were quantified using a commercial Glucose Assay Kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China). Fifty zebrafish larvae per group were homogenized in 200 µL ice-cold physiological saline using a mechanical homogenizer on ice. The homogenate was centrifuged at 2,500 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected for analysis. A standard curve was generated according to the manufacturer’s protocol by serial dilution of the provided glucose standard solution. For each sample, 2 mL of assay reagent was added, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 10 min. Fluorescence intensity was measured at an emission wavelength of 505 nm using a SuperMax 3100 Microplate Reader (Shanghai Flash Spectrum Biological Technology Co., Ltd., China). Glucose concentrations were calculated by referencing the standard curve.

Larvae locomotor behavior

The locomotor behavior of 6 dpf zebrafish larvae was monitored and recorded using the Danio Vision Tracking System (Viewpoint Information Technology). Larvae were individually housed in 24-well plates (1 larva per well; n = 15 total, with 3 independent replicates of 5 larvae each) and acclimated for 10 min under dark conditions at 28 °C. Behavioral recordings were performed using ViewPoint Application Manager software (ViewPoint, France) during a 30-minute continuous session, with alternating 5-minute light/dark phases. Total distance traveled (mm) was automatically calculated from movement trajectories.

Lens isolation and measurement of hyaloid-retinal vessel diameter

At 6 dpf, Tg(flk1:EGFP) transgenic zebrafish larvae were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Beyotime Biotechnology) for 24 h at 4 °C. After fixation, samples were rinsed three times with phosphate-buffered saline for 10 min each. Retinal vascular networks were enzymatically isolated by incubating larvae in 3% trypsin solution (Tris-HCl, pH 7.8, 37 °C) for 80 min, followed by three 5-minute phosphate-buffered saline washes to terminate enzymatic activity.

Vascular morphology (vessel diameter, optic disc) was imaged in situ using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Leica Microsystems GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany). Image analysis was performed on maximum-intensity projections via ImageJ v1.53k (National Institutes of Health), with triplicate measurements per sample averaged across three biological replicates.

Histopathological analysis of ocular tissues

At 6 dpf, the larvae were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Serial Sect. (3 μm thickness) through the eyes were obtained using a rotary microtome and stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Whole-slide digital imaging was performed using a Pannoramic 250 high-throughput slide scanner (3D Histech, Budapest, Hungary) for comprehensive analysis of histoarchitectural features.

Oxidative stress analysis

We measured reactive oxygen species (ROS) production with 2′,7′–dichlorofluorescin diacetate (DCFH–DA). We exposed the larvae to DMSO, DEN + Glu, and Glu for three days. After the exposure, we added a DCFH–DA solution at a 1:1000 dilution to the treated larvae. We then incubated the samples in the dark at 28.5 °C for 40 min. After staining, we rinsed the larvae three times with MilliQ water. We observed the stained larvae using a inverted fluorescence microscope (Leica Microsystems GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany). We captured images and analyzed them with Image J software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). Each concentration had three replicates, with six larvae per replicate.

At 6 dpf, we randomly collected zebrafish after DEN and Glu exposure and homogenized them. We homogenized approximately 50 larvae in 400 µL of 0.9% NaCl. We then centrifuged the homogenate at 4000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. We collected the supernatant to measure malondialdehyde (MDA), Superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT) levels. We used assay kits from AIDISHENG Institute of Biotechnology (Jiangsu, China) and followed the manufacturer’s instructions. We determined the protein concentration in the supernatant using a BCA protein quantification kit (Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China).

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis

At 6 dpf, larvae (n = 3 biological replicates per group) were collected, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and transported on dry ice to Shenzhen BGI Co., Ltd. for RNA extraction and purification. RNA sequencing was conducted using the Dr. Tom data visualization system (Shenzhen, China).

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the Dr. Tom Multi-omics Data Mining system (https://biosys.bgi.com) with the criteria log2|fold change| ≥ 1 and P ≤ 0.05. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses were performed to determine the biological processes and pathways associated with the DEGs, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks were analyzed using the STRING database (https://string-db.org/) and visualized with Cytoscape software version 3.5.1.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis

To validate the RNA-seq results, key DEGs were analyzed by qRT-PCR. Total RNA was extracted from 30 larvae (n = 3 per group) using TRIzol reagent (Beyotime Biotechnology) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Only RNA samples with A260/A280 ratios between 1.8 and 2.2 were used to ensure high quality. RNA was reverse-transcribed to cDNA using the HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR (+ gDNA wiper) (Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China).

All qRT-PCR reactions were performed in triplicate. Primer sequences are provided in Table S1. GAPDH was used as the housekeeping gene for normalization, and gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−△△Ct method.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software version 16.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), and graphs were generated with GraphPad Prism version 8.0. Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation. Between-group comparisons were conducted using one-way analysis of variance, followed by post hoc multiple comparisons with the least significant difference method. A P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Development of DR model under high-glucose conditions

To determine the optimal glucose concentration for establishing a zebrafish DR model, larvae were exposed to glucose concentrations of 90, 110, 130, and 150 mM for 3 days, from 3 to 6 dpf. At 6 dpf, retinal blood vessel diameter and whole-body blood glucose levels were measured. Compared with the control group, larvae exposed to 90, 110, 130, and 150 mM glucose showed an increased retinal vessel diameter, with a clear concentration-dependent trend (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1A, C). Blood glucose levels also increased in a dose-dependent manner (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1B). Mortality rates rose significantly with increasing glucose concentration (P < 0.05), although no significant differences were observed among the different glucose concentration groups (P > 0.05). Mortality also increased with longer exposure duration (P < 0.05) (Fig. S1A). Balancing treatment efficacy with reduced mortality, 130 mM glucose was selected as the optimal concentration for establishing a reliable DR model.

Effects of DEN on zebrafish larval development

The embryonic developmental toxicity of DEN was assessed in zebrafish larvae exposed from 3 to 6 dpf. Both mortality and malformation rates increased in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. S1B, C). Based on these results, two optimal combinations—20 mg/L DEN with 130 mM glucose and 40 mg/L DEN with 130 mM glucose—were selected for subsequent experiments. In the glucose-treated group, heart rate was significantly elevated compared with the control group (P < 0.05), and body length was reduced (P < 0.05). These adverse effects were alleviated in larvae co-treated with 20 mg/L or 40 mg/L DEN (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2B, C). No significant differences were observed in eye area, eye length, or the eye-to-body length ratio among the control, glucose, and DEN groups (P > 0.05) (Fig. 2D–F). These results suggest that glucose primarily impacts the internal tissue structures of zebrafish rather than their external morphological characteristics.

Effect of DEN on retinal vessels and blood glucose in zebrafish larvae

Blood glucose levels and retinal vessel diameter are key indicators for the early diagnosis of DR. In our study, treatment with 40 mg/L DEN significantly reduced Glu-induced retinal vessel enlargement (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3B). By contrast, 20 mg/L DEN showed a trend toward reduction, but the change was not statistically significant (P > 0.05) (Fig. 3B).

A similar pattern was observed for blood glucose levels: 40 mg/L DEN significantly normalized glucose levels in Glu-exposed zebrafish (P < 0.05), whereas 20 mg/L DEN produced a non-significant improvement (P > 0.05) (Fig. 3C). These findings indicate that DEN confers beneficial effects on both retinal vasculature and blood glucose regulation in zebrafish larvae.

Effects of DEN on zebrafish visual functions

To investigate the neuroprotective effects of DEN, we evaluated the behavioral responses of zebrafish larvae to light–dark cycles at 6 dpf in the control, Glu, and Glu + DEN groups. Behavioral analysis showed that larvae in the Glu group traveled significantly greater total distances than controls during both light and dark cycles (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4C, D). By contrast, larvae treated with 40 mg/L DEN exhibited a significant reduction in swimming distance compared with the Glu group (P < 0.05). Larvae treated with 20 mg/L DEN also showed reduced swimming distances, although the change was not statistically significant (P > 0.05) (Fig. 4C, D).

HE staining was used to assess tissue morphological changes associated with the treatments. Compared with the control group, the Glu group showed marked thickening of the inner nuclear layer and thinning of the outer nuclear layer (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4E, F). Treatment with 40 mg/L DEN attenuated these morphological alterations, suggesting that DEN may improve visual and behavioral function by counteracting the adverse effects of elevated blood glucose.

Effects of DEN on oxidative stress in zebrafish larvae

We evaluated the level of ROS in the zebrafish larvae at 6 dpf. Juvenile zebrafish treated with 130 mmol/L Glu exhibited significantly higher ROS accumulation than the control group (P < 0.05) (Fig. 5A and B). In addition, Glu treatment significantly increased the SOD activity and content of MDA and reduced the CAT activity in the zebrafish larvae (P < 0.05) (Fig. 5C-E). However, co-treatment with 20 and 40 mg/L DEN significantly reduced ROS level compared to the 130 mmol/L Glu group (P < 0.05) (Fig. 5A and B). In addition, CAT activity in the 40 mg/L DEN group was significantly higher than that in the Glu group. The SOD activity and MDA content in this group were also significantly lower (P < 0.05) (Fig. 5C-E). The 20 mg/L DEN group exhibited a similar trend to the 40 mg/L DEN group. But, the difference between the 20 mg/L DEN group and the Glu group was not statistically significant (P > 0.05) (Fig. 5C-E). These findings suggest that DEN can attenuate the high glucose-induced disorder of the antioxidant system in zebrafish larvae.

Transcriptomic analysis

Transcriptomic profiling was performed on whole zebrafish at 6 dpf from the control group, Glu group, and DEN + Glu co-treatment groups to investigate the molecular mechanisms by which DEN mitigates Glu-induced DR. Between the Glu and control groups, 1,107 DEGs were identified, including 629 upregulated and 478 downregulated genes (Fig. 6A). In comparison with the Glu group, the 20 mg/L DEN + Glu group showed 462 DEGs (149 upregulated, 313 downregulated) (Fig. 6B), while the 40 mg/L DEN + Glu group showed 387 DEGs (128 upregulated, 259 downregulated) (Fig. 6C). The PCA plot (Fig. S1D) revealed clear separation among the four groups, indicating substantial transcriptional differences induced by the treatments.

GO enrichment analysis indicated that compared with the control group, DEGs in the Glu group were significantly enriched in lipid metabolic processes and compound eye photoreceptor cell differentiation (Fig. 6D). In comparison with the Glu group, both the 20 mg/L and 40 mg/L DEN + Glu groups showed significant enrichment in functions related to lipid metabolism, retinol metabolism, eye development, retinal metabolism, and compound eye photoreceptor cell differentiation (Figs. 6F, S2A).

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that DEGs in the Glu group, relative to controls, were significantly associated with the PPAR signaling pathway, steroid hormone biosynthesis, retinol metabolism, and type II diabetes mellitus (Fig. 6E). Relative to the Glu group, both DEN treatment concentrations enriched overlapping pathways, including retinol metabolism, steroid hormone biosynthesis, porphyrin metabolism, the PPAR signaling pathway, and type II diabetes mellitus (Figs. 6G, S2B).

Identification of treatment-reversed DEGs

Following treatment with 20 mg/L and 40 mg/L DEN, 126 and 133 DEGs, respectively, showed expression reversal compared with the Glu group. In the 20 mg/L DEN group, 23 reversed DEGs were upregulated and 103 were downregulated (Fig. 7A). In the 40 mg/L DEN group, 29 reversed DEGs were upregulated and 104 were downregulated (Fig. 7B). A heatmap illustrates the expression patterns of these DEGs (Fig. S2C, D).

Functional enrichment analyses, including GO and KEGG, were performed on the 126 and 133 reversed DEGs to characterize DEN-mediated transcriptional regulation in zebrafish larvae and assess their biological significance. GO analysis revealed enrichment in cholesterol biosynthetic process, steroid biosynthetic process, lipid metabolic processes, and compound eye photoreceptor cell differentiation at both DEN concentrations (Fig. 7C). Notably, the 40 mg/L DEN group also showed enrichment in the retinol metabolic process (Fig. 7E). KEGG enrichment analysis indicated that both DEN concentrations significantly enriched the same pathways, including steroid hormone biosynthesis, retinol metabolism, porphyrin metabolism, and PPAR signaling (Fig. 7D, F).

Transcriptomic analysis of commonly reversed DEGs

Venn diagram analysis identified 80 DEGs that were commonly reversed in response to both 20 mg/L and 40 mg/L DEN treatments (Fig. 8A). Expression heatmaps showed distinct clustering patterns among the Glu, 20 mg/L DEN, 40 mg/L DEN, and control groups (Fig. 8B). To identify core therapeutic targets of DEN, functional enrichment analyses (GO and KEGG) were performed on the 80 commonly reversed DEGs. GO analysis revealed significant enrichment in cholesterol biosynthetic process, steroid biosynthetic process, lipid metabolic processes, and compound eye photoreceptor cell differentiation (Fig. 8C). KEGG analysis indicated significant enrichment in steroid hormone biosynthesis, retinol metabolism, porphyrin metabolism, and PPAR signaling pathways (Fig. 8D). PPI network analysis highlighted notable gene interactions, with lss and hsd17b7 emerging as central nodes (Fig. 8E). Pathway integration analysis demonstrated functional interplay among eye development, steroid hormone biosynthesis, lipid metabolism, porphyrin homeostasis, and PPAR signaling, underscoring the interconnected mechanisms engaged by DEN treatment (Fig. 8F).

Validation of transcriptomic sequencing by qRT-PCR

To validate the transcriptomic results, qRT-PCR was performed on 20 key genes associated with retinol metabolism, embryonic camera-type eye morphogenesis, lipid metabolism, and PPAR signaling pathways (Fig. 9A, B). Although minor variations in expression levels were observed, the overall expression trends from qRT-PCR were consistent with those from RNA-seq for all transcripts, confirming the reliability of the RNA-seq data (Fig. 9B). Linear regression analysis further demonstrated a positive correlation (R2 = 0.8173) between RNA-seq and qRT-PCR fold changes for these DEGs (Fig. 9C).

Discussion

DR is one of the most common and severe microvascular complications of diabetes [26]. Its prevalence continues to rise in parallel with the global increase in diabetes incidence and patient numbers [27]. In this study, larval zebrafish were exposed to various glucose concentrations. At 130 mmol/L glucose—previously identified as optimal for inducing hyperglycemia-associated retinopathy [28, 29]— we observed significantly elevated systemic blood glucose levels and increased fundus vascular diameter, without significant developmental toxicity (Figs. 1, S1). Prolonged hyperglycemia is well established as a major etiological factor in diabetes [3], triggering a cascade of biochemical and physiological changes that ultimately lead to microvascular injury and retinal dysfunction [30, 31]. Compared with adult zebrafish models requiring long-term high-glucose exposure, the larval model can more rapidly reflect early retinal injury induced by acute hyperglycemia, simplified ocular procedures, and strong potential for broader application.

Our RNA-seq analysis revealed that DEGs induced by high-glucose treatment were primarily associated with visual perception, suggesting that zebrafish may experience visual impairment due to both retinal neuronal dysfunction—particularly affecting photoreceptors—and microvascular damage. These results are consistent with findings by Parravano et al. [32]. The DEGs were also enriched in pathways related to steroid hormone biosynthesis, retinol metabolism, and PPAR signaling. We presume that high-glucose disrupts PPARγ-mediated lipid metabolism regulation, leading to lipid metabolic disorders [33], and simultaneously interferes with steroid and retinoid metabolism. This combined disruption amplifies damage signals contributing to DR pathogenesis [34, 35] and is linked to stress responses triggered by short-term high-glucose exposure during early zebrafish development [36]. The high-glucose provides abundant precursors for lipid biosynthesis, stimulating lipid metabolism but ultimately causing metabolic imbalance [37]. In parallel, it significantly alters osmotic conditions, disturbing larval cellular homeostasis and impairing metabolic and signaling processes [38]. These molecular findings align with our phenotypic observations: thickening of the retinal inner nuclear layer and inner plexiform layer, along with increased locomotor activity in the glucose group, are consistent with a stress-response state (Fig. 4).

DEN, a prominent natural alkaloid, has emerged as a focus of research for the development of novel therapeutic agents with anti-inflammatory, neuroprotective, and anti-diabetic properties [39]. Zebrafish are widely recognized as a powerful platform for drug discovery because of their high-throughput screening capacity, strong conservation of vertebrate physiology and pathology, and the ability to visualize live phenotypes [40]. Leveraging these advantages, this study employed a zebrafish DR model and integrated morphological, transcriptomic, and molecular approaches to comprehensively evaluate the therapeutic potential of DEN and elucidate its underlying mechanisms. Our results demonstrate that DEN, at concentrations of 20 mg/L and 40 mg/L, exerts significant protective effects against high-glucose–induced early ocular developmental abnormalities. Co-treatment with DEN led to concentration-dependent improvements in key parameters, including retinal vascular diameter, retinal layer thickness, body length, heart rate, and locomotor activity, offering new insights into the potential application of this traditional Chinese medicine in early DR therapy.

Our analysis of eye-specific phenotypes and RNA-seq data revealed rapid improvements in retinal morphology and function. These changes occurred alongside only minor fluctuations in systemic blood glucose levels. The improvements appeared before any major systemic metabolic shifts took place. This timing points to the involvement of a local mechanism. Furthermore, previous studies have shown that DEN can provide direct neuroprotection independent of its effects on glucose regulation [41]. Although DEN significantly improved retinal morphology and visual behavior in high-glucose-exposed zebrafish larvae, these beneficial effects may be attributable, at least in part, to its systemic glucose-lowering action.

Notably, GO enrichment analysis revealed that the 80 genes commonly reversed by 20 mg/L and 40 mg/L DEN treatments were predominantly associated with compound eye photoreceptor cell differentiation. We believe that DEN not only mitigates early acute DR condition but also promotes photoreceptor development—a process impaired under high-glucose exposure. Supporting this, DEN markedly upregulated key factors such as six7, which are essential for photoreceptor differentiation and are otherwise downregulated under high-glucose conditions [42, 43]. Mechanistically, these effects align with previous evidence that DEN alleviates diabetes-induced retinal dysplasia by modulating photoreceptor developmental pathways, specifically through suppression inflammation and activation antioxidant signaling [44, 45]. Collectively, based on these findings, we assume that DEN plays a dual role in rescuing visual function by preserving photoreceptor integrity and counteracting early hyperglycemic damage at the molecular level.

Moreover, the 80 commonly reversed genes in response to DEN treatment were significantly associated with lipid biosynthesis, and the cholesterol biosynthesis pathway may play a key role. This pathway supplies essential precursors for steroid hormone production as well as structural components necessary for lipid metabolism. Mechanistically, DEN appears to exert a dual regulatory effect on lipid metabolism. First, it inhibits the regulators of lipogenesis-related enzymes, thereby reducing excessive synthesis of triglycerides and cholesterol in the bloodstream [46]. Second, it mitigates the hyperactivation of lipid metabolism induced by elevated glucose, enhancing lipid utilization and fatty acid oxidation in the liver and other tissues [47]. Together, these actions improve lipid profiles by lowering total cholesterol, triglycerides, and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, while increasing high-density lipoprotein cholesterol [48]. Additionally, DEN alleviates lipid overload, reducing lipotoxicity to photoreceptor precursor cells, and promotes retinal progenitor cell differentiation into photoreceptors, thereby enhancing visual signal transmission [49, 50]. This mechanism underscores the critical role of lipid metabolism homeostasis in photoreceptor regeneration and highlights DEN’s potential to strategically reverse DR progression.

Our KEGG enrichment analysis further indicated that the commonly reversed DEGs were mainly enriched in four signaling pathways: retinol metabolism, steroid hormone biosynthesis, porphyrin metabolism, and the PPAR signaling pathway. The retinol metabolic pathway is essential for maintaining physiological homeostasis [51], and its dysregulation can lead to excessive conversion of retinol to retinoic acid [52]. Notably, the transcriptomic results of this study demonstrate that DEN effectively alleviates the retinol metabolic disorder induced by high glucose. We speculate that this effect is associated with mitigating the adverse effects of excessive retinoic acid on the retina. This observation offers new insights for further investigation of the mechanism by which DEN protects retinal function. These results are consistent with previous reports linking abnormal activation of the retinol pathway to DR [53, 54]. In parallel, DEN rebalanced the PPAR signaling pathway by downregulating fat-producing proteins such as PPARγ, which are upregulated under high-glucose conditions [55]. This downregulation reduced lipid accumulation and mitigated retinal damage from lipid toxicity [56], consistent with previous findings that restoring PPAR signaling can alleviate high-glucose–induced retinal injury [57]. In this study, we also found that the retinol metabolic pathway and PPAR signaling intersect functionally, jointly regulating adipocyte differentiation, lipid metabolism, and inflammation [58] through heterodimerization of retinoid X receptor and PPARγ—an interaction well documented in the literature [59].

Our findings suggest that DEN can inhibit steroid hormone biosynthesis, thereby reducing retinal developmental abnormalities and metabolic disorders associated with excess steroid hormones—an effect consistent with previous studies on high-glucose–induced retinopathy [60, 61]. In addition, DEN may contribute to the restoration of heme homeostasis [62], counteracting high-glucose–induced disruptions in porphyrin metabolism, primarily via activation of the HO-1/Nrf2 axis [63–65]. Notably, steroid hormone biosynthesis and porphyrin metabolism both utilize cholesterol as a precursor and often respond in concert under stress conditions [66, 67]. Our PPI network analysis revealed that these four signaling pathways interact, with the PPAR pathway functioning as a central metabolic integrator, coordinating retinol metabolism, steroid hormone biosynthesis, and porphyrin metabolism. The PPAR pathway directly regulates the expression of genes involved in retinol metabolism, thereby influencing the production of active vitamin A derivatives [68]. It also contributes to steroid hormone biosynthesis by modulating the expression of rate-limiting enzymes and shares key components with porphyrin metabolism [69, 70]. Furthermore, retinol metabolites exert feedback regulation on PPAR activity [71], while steroid hormones modulate PPAR target genes through crosstalk mechanisms [72]. This intricate interactive network positions the PPAR pathway as a central hub for metabolic integration, collectively supporting essential physiological processes such as lipid homeostasis, energy allocation, and oxidative stress responses [73, 74].

This study assessed the protective effects of DEN on DR using a zebrafish larval model subjected to short-term high-glucose exposure. As whole-larvae RNA-seq analysis was employed and experiments designed to dissociate systemic metabolic effects from direct retinal actions were not included, we cannot conclude that DEN acts directly on retinal tissue. Future studies will be necessary to determine whether DEN exerts independent retinal protective activity. To further clarify the precise mechanisms by which DEN alleviates DR, future investigations should include in vitro experiments and western blot validation. Such approaches will help strengthen the conclusions. In addition, diabetes is a chronic metabolic condition. Therefore, subsequent research should employ long-term exposure models and more complex systems. This will allow a better assessment of the therapeutic effects of DEN. These efforts will provide more substantial evidence for its potential clinical application.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study refines the molecular foundation for developing the early acute high-glucose–induced DR model and demonstrates that DEN confers protective effects in this larval zebrafish model. Of note, DEN partially mitigates high-glucose–induced retinal developmental defects and behavioral impairments, with RNA-seq analysis further indicating its involvement in regulating compound eye photoreceptor differentiation and lipid metabolic processes. Furthermore, DEN effectively alleviates acute DR through coordinated modulation of retinol metabolism, steroid hormone biosynthesis, porphyrin metabolism, and PPAR signaling pathways. This multi-target action underscores its therapeutic potential, identifies precise molecular targets, and offers novel insights into traditional Chinese medicine–based DR treatment. Future research using more complex mammalian models is warranted to further elucidate DEN’s specific molecular mechanisms and clinical applicability.
