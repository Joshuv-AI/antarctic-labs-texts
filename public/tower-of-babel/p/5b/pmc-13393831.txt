Clinical 2D MRI radiomics for infant brain age and trajectory modeling
Authors: Huanyu Luo, Huanjie Li, Siting Xiong, Rui Wang, Ziyang Li, Jia Wang, Di Hu, Hang Li, Sami Kari-Pekka Äyrämö, Hao Huang, Xin Fan, Yun Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryClinical 2D magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is the cornerstone for assessing early brain development, yet the lack of a systematic methodology renders the fine-grained and quantitative evaluation of early brain growth underdeveloped. We address this gap using a data-driven radiomics framework that integrates automatic registration, segmentation, and feature extraction for the analysis of 2,893 routine T1-weighted and T2-weighted scans from children aged 0–2 years. The framework yields accurate brain age prediction (mean absolute error = 1.19 months) and pinpoints the “corpus callosum median” (CC-median) as the most critical, biologically relevant predictor. We used the generalized additive model for location, scale, and shape (GAMLSS) to model the normative trajectory of CC-median. This standardized curve robustly differentiates developmental delays from typical peers, achieving an accuracy of 0.96 (for ages 6–24 months). This work establishes a clinically compatible framework, transforming conventional 2D MRI data into a powerful, objective tool for early neurodevelopmental assessment.

Introduction

The human brain undergoes remarkable growth during the first two years of life, a period with profound, lasting impacts on cognitive, emotional, and social development.1,2,3 To date, investigating this critical period has largely been advanced by high-spatial resolution 3D structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). These “research-quality” scans have been employed to quantify morphological changes in gray matter (GM), white matter (WM), and the cerebral cortex, establishing foundational knowledge and normative growth trajectories for early development.1,4,5,6,7 Nonetheless, this 3D-centric research approach faces significant practical limitations in this specific population. The prolonged duration of these examinations makes motion artifacts inevitable when scanning neonates and infants, resulting in a diminished success rate for 3D analysis techniques.8 Consequently, in clinical practice, conventional 2D MRI remains the most common imaging modality for assessing brain development in children aged 0–2 years and serves as the primary initial screening tool for suspected neurodevelopmental disorders.

The most widely used clinical criteria for assessing brain developmental status within the first two years are specific myelination milestones based on conventional 2D T1-weighted (T1w) and T2-weighted (T2w) MRI images.9,10 However, this assessment largely relies on the subjective opinion and experience of radiologists and may exhibit substantial variability and inconsistency.11,12,13 In fact, even though delayed myelination is the predominant feature of developmental delays, nearly half of children with actual developmental delays were reported to have an apparently normal myelination appearance upon visual inspection of T1w/T2w MR images.14,15,16 Therefore, there is a clear need to develop objective approaches to analyzing conventional 2D MR data and to establish comprehensive criteria for early diagnosis of developmental delays. However, research methodologies tailored for 3D data analysis cannot be directly applied to 2D data due to fundamental differences in data dimensionality and associated characteristics. Although some studies have made attempts to explore 2D data,17,18 none of them have proposed a fully automated and systematic method for analyzing it. Therefore, developing integrative methodologies tailored for mining standardized quantitative brain information from 2D MRI data is essential to allow for in-depth research of early childhood brain development.

To standardize the application of clinical 2D MRI and validate its potential for brain age prediction and developmental trajectory construction, we analyzed a large cohort of 2,893 children aged 0–2 years (Figure 1; Table 1), utilizing their combined T1w and T2w 2D MR images (see Figures S1 and S2 for the construction pipeline and illustrative samples, respectively). To address the inherent challenges of low-resolution 2D data, we developed and deployed a complete, radiomics-based processing pipeline (Figure 2). We systematically addressed these aims through three major investigations. First, to validate the generalizability of 2D measures, we established a standardized 2D MR image processing workflow and performed a cross-modality comparison of radiomic features and brain networks between paired 2D and 3D T1w images (Figure 3). Second, to construct an accurate brain age prediction model, we used the extracted 2D radiomic features to benchmark the predictive performance of the traditional Gaussian process regression (GPR) model against a deep learning (DL) convolutional neural network (CNN) model. Finally, to build standardized brain developmental trajectories, we leveraged the most significant predictor identified in the brain age analysis—“corpus callosum median” (CC-median)—to model its course using generalized additive model for location, scale, and shape (GAMLSS). These resulting normative trajectories were then utilized to objectively identify and differentiate typical development from developmental delays via percentile thresholds. Results

Clinical and demographic participant characteristics

A total of 2,982 participants who underwent brain MRI examinations at Beijing Children’s Hospital, Capital Medical University, were included in this study, comprising two datasets acquired for clinical purposes.

Dataset 1 consisted of 2,948 participants aged 0–24 months, each with available 2D T1w and T2w images. After excluding radiomic feature outliers (n = 55), 2,893 participants remained for analysis. These were categorized into typically developing children (TDC, n = 2,204), children with clinically diagnosed developmental delay (DD-C, n = 539), and children with both clinical and MRI-based developmental delay (DD-CM, n = 150). For brain age prediction, the TDC cohort was randomly divided into an 80% training set (n = 1,764) and a 20% testing set (n = 440), with all DD-C and DD-CM participants included in the testing set.

Dataset 2 included 34 participants aged 9–20 months, each with both 2D and 3D T1w images for a comparative analysis. After excluding three cases that failed preprocessing, 31 participants were retained for further evaluation.

The clinical and demographic characteristics are presented in Table 1, and the age distribution across groups in Dataset 1 is shown in Figure 1.

2D-3D consistency of radiomic feature matrices and structural similarity

We evaluated the correspondence of radiomics features (Tables S2 and S3) extracted from 18 neuroanatomical regions (Table S4) in 31 subjects by using 2D and 3D MRI images. Pearson’s correlation coefficients were calculated for two types of data representations: feature matrices, which contain the quantitative radiomics features, and brain structural similarity matrices, which describe inter-regional similarity based on those features. The correlations showed moderate agreement for the feature matrices (average Pearson’s correlation coefficient = 0.40, standard deviation [SD] = 0.09) and higher concordance for the structural similarity matrices (average = 0.70, SD = 0.07) (Figure 3). Although both data representations were assessed for consistency, the quantitative radiomic features were selected for subsequent brain age modeling due to their greater sensitivity to microstructural developmental changes.

Feature selection and stability for brain age modeling

To identify stable predictors of brain age, we applied GPR with automatic relevance determination (GPR-ARD) to regional radiomic features obtained from 2D structural MRI in the TDC training set (n = 1,764). Feature selection was performed using a 10-repetition, 10-fold cross-validation framework. We identified 34 region-radiomic pairs as consistently stable predictors, which were selected in at least six of the ten cross-validation runs (indicated by black boxes in the heatmap, Figure 4). Table S5 lists the 10 most significant features. This analysis further characterized the composition and distribution of the 34 stable features. Specifically, the most predominant metrics observed were “median,” which accounted for 23.5% of the total features, followed by “cluster shade” (20.6%) and “contrast” (11.8%). Concurrently, feature origination showed a clear spatial bias, with the left frontal lobe (L-FL) contributing the highest proportion (20.6%), followed by the right occipital lobe (R-OL, 14.7%) and the right frontal lobe (R-FL, 11.8%) as the next most frequent source regions.

Comparative brain age prediction using radiomic features and image-level models

To evaluate the predictive capacity of the selected radiomic features for estimating brain age, we used the 34 region-radiomic pairs identified in the previous step as inputs to the GPR-ARD. In parallel, a ResNet18-based CNN was implemented as an image-level benchmark to compare feature-based and end-to-end prediction performance. 10-fold cross-validation was first performed on the training dataset (n = 1,764; TDC cohort) to assess model accuracy and generalizability. The GPR-ARD model achieved a mean absolute error (MAE) of 1.16 ± 0.08 months, while the ResNet18 model yielded a comparable MAE of 1.21 ± 0.12 months. Both models were then retrained using the full training dataset and applied to three independent test cohorts (TDC, DD-C, and DD-CM) for external validation.

As summarized in Table 2, both models (GPR and ResNet18) yielded high accuracy in the TDC test set, with MAE values ranging from 1.13 to 1.19 months and R2 values exceeding 0.94, confirming the robustness of brain age estimation from radiomic features. Figure 5 presents the differences between the predicted and chronological ages among the TDC, DD-C, and DD-CM groups for the GPR model (Figure S3 shows the corresponding ResNet18 results). Compared with the TDC group, the predicted brain ages were smaller in the DD-C and DD-CM groups at equivalent chronological ages (Figures 5A and C). Prediction errors (Figure 5B) were largest in the DD-CM group, with an overall MAE of 2.17 months, followed by the DD-C group (1.6 months). The distributions of predicted age difference (PAD) values differed among the three groups (Figure 5D), with mean PADs of 0.06, −0.56, and −2.09 months for TDC, DD-C, and DD-CM, respectively. Further age-bin analysis revealed that the PAD in the DD-CM group was significantly lower than in TDC across all age bins (Mann-Whitney U test; see Table S6 for exact p values and sample sizes). Age-related trajectory modeling of a key radiomic feature using GAMLSS

The preceding brain age prediction model confirmed a significant overall developmental deviation in the delayed groups. Given the need to translate this group-level finding into a tool for individual classification and biomarker refinement, we focused our subsequent analysis on the CC-median, which was identified as the top-ranked predictor through feature importance analysis. We then used the GAMLSS framework to establish a precise, age-specific reference standard.

Preliminary analyses justified the use of GAMLSS. The CC-median data significantly deviated from normality (Anderson-Darling test, A = 59.0, p < 0.01), necessitating a flexible distributional framework. Furthermore, the CC-median values did not differ significantly between sexes (Mann-Whitney U test, W = 394, 193, p = 0.21), with a negligible effect size (δ = 0.03). This supported a sex-independent model specification. We selected the Box-Cox Cole and Green (BCCG) distribution for model fitting, as it minimized the Akaike information criterion (AIC) and generalized AIC (GAIC3) among candidates (Table 3). Model adequacy was confirmed both formally and visually. A non-significant goodness-of-fit (GOF) chi-square test (p > 0.05) indicated a robust fit, confirming that the model’s predicted distribution was statistically consistent with the observed data. Finally, inspection of diagnostic plots (Figure S4), particularly the normal quantile-quantile (Q-Q) plot in (D), confirmed model adequacy. Discussion

Accurate evaluation of brain developmental status during the first two years of life is critical for the early detection and intervention of neurodevelopmental disorders.19 While 2D MRI is the most common imaging modality in pediatric neuroimaging, the lack of objective, quantitative indicators that can be directly applied in routine practice remains a significant clinical challenge. Consequently, the potential of these conventional 2D MRI data for automated developmental assessment has remained largely unexplored.20,21 Here, our study proposes the clinically compatible framework designed for systematic quantitative feature extraction from routine 2D MRI scans. We demonstrate that these features can be effectively used to predict brain age and characterize developmental trajectories in children aged 0–2 years. This work establishes a foundation for future research leveraging these ubiquitous clinical datasets. Moreover, it provides a set of objective indicators to support the clinical assessment of early brain development.

Given that 2D structural MRI is the predominant modality in clinical pediatric imaging, establishing its quantitative reliability is essential. Although 2D scans are rarely used for automated feature extraction, verifying their consistency with 3D-derived information provides a necessary validation step. Therefore, we validated the 2D-3D correspondence by comparing radiomic feature matrices and brain structural similarity matrices. Our correlations revealed a modest alignment for 2D-derived versus 3D-derived radiomic features. This weaker correspondence may be attributed to factors inherent in 2D acquisition, such as the loss of precise 3D spatial context, increased sensitivity to local texture variations, and severe partial volume effects resulting from thick slices.22,23,24 Conversely, the structural similarity matrices exhibited higher consistency, as these matrices quantify the inter-regional patterns of radiomic features and are, therefore, inherently less susceptible to the random, uncorrelated noise introduced by variations at the slice level. Unlike the individual feature values, this approach suggests the preservation of crucial global anatomical patterns.25 Although the network-level matrices exhibited higher cross-dimensional consistency, we ultimately used radiomic features for age prediction modeling. This decision was made because radiomic features are more sensitive to the age-related microstructural changes central to our study.26,27 Furthermore, they represent a practical, clinically adaptable solution given that whole-brain parcellation is often infeasible in routine 2D MRI.

In our 2D-MRI analysis pipeline, feature importance analysis quantifies the contribution of each feature to the prediction results, facilitating interpretation of the mode’s decision logic. Based on 2D MR images, the macroscopic features of early brain development are reflected in signals highly sensitive to myelination—characterized by gradually increasing T1w WM intensity, decreasing T2w intensity, and clearer GM-WM boundaries.28,29 Among the top-ranked features, “median,” “cluster shade,” and “contrast” could capture complementary aspects of brain tissue organization. “Median” represents a first-order intensity measure reflecting the central tendency of voxel gray-level distribution, whereas “cluster shade” and “contrast” describe gray-level asymmetry and local variation, reflecting tissue heterogeneity and textural complexity.30,31 The prominence of these features suggests that age-related alterations in brain MRI are driven not only by global intensity changes but also by shifts in spatial texture organization, consistent with prior radiomics findings.32,33 Meanwhile, our brain region importance analysis revealed that the frontal lobe ranked as one of the most predictive regions, which indicates that it is a key brain region for predicting brain age. Prior studies have revealed the frontal lobe’s central position in brain development during the 0–2 year period, as it lays the physiological foundation for infants’ cognitive, behavioral, and social abilities and promotes overall brain development via neural connectivity.34,35,36,37 Our study further corroborates the crucial role of the frontal lobe.

Utilizing the radiomic features identified and validated in the preceding parts, our brain age prediction analysis achieved robust predictive efficacy for children aged 0–2 years, reporting an MAE of 1.19 months. This result is highly comparable to the MAE of 1.13 months demonstrated by the ResNet18 trained on GWCE images, thereby confirming the robustness of the extracted imaging signal irrespective of the modeling architecture. Crucially, biological interpretability is the core advantage of the feature-based framework, fundamentally distinguishing it from prevalent DL approaches.38,39 These DL models typically operate as black boxes, failing to link the prediction mechanism to the underlying biological processes driving early development. Furthermore, their dependence on large and homogeneous datasets severely limits their applicability in small, heterogeneous pediatric cohorts. In fact, prior DL-based works40,41,42,43,44 on early childhood brain age prediction illustrate this challenge, with studies primarily adopting CNNs trained on limited pediatric MRI datasets. For instance, Hong et al.41 achieved an MAE of 0.9 months, using a 3D CNN on routine 2D T1w MRIs (220 children aged 0–5 years), and Chen et al.43 reported an MAE of 2.1 months on T1/T2w scans (518 infants aged 0–25 months). Although they have established the feasibility of achieving high predictive accuracy using raw neuroimaging data, they did not provide a biological interpretation of their findings in the context of early brain development. Therefore, in a data-limited pediatric MRI setting, traditional machine learning remains a more efficient, interpretable, and stable alternative to DL, offering clearer insights into the imaging correlates of brain maturation while maintaining competitive predictive accuracy.

In addition, our brain age prediction model provides a robust, global measure of deviation (i.e., the PAD score) that is highly valuable for confirming a general developmental delay at the group level. Our finding of lower mean PADs in our DD-C and DD-CM groups align with prior works45,46 suggesting that developmental disorders are characterized by delayed brain age. However, this global PAD score only confirms that a deviation exists and cannot pinpoint which specific biological processes are affected, nor does it provide a granular metric for individual classification. To translate our findings into a clinically actionable tool, we must, therefore, refine the brain age framework by moving beyond this global summary score. This requires establishing a standard trajectory for the specific, interpretable biomarkers that drive the prediction. To identify the most promising biomarker for this task, we leveraged our feature importance analysis, which pinpointed CC-median as the most crucial feature for predicting brain age. Therefore, we modeled the normative trajectory of CC-median using the GAMLSS framework to establish a quantitative, age-adjusted reference. We prioritized CC-median for this analysis to establish a proof-of-concept. This single-feature prototype validates our GAMLSS framework’s ability to model complex, biologically relevant growth from 2D data, thus establishing a clear foundation for expansion into a multi-feature panel. The resulting percentile curves, spanning the full 0–24 months range, clearly demonstrated a highly non-linear growth trajectory: a modest initial phase (0–3 months), followed by a critical period of accelerated increase (3–6 months post-birth). This growth pattern is not only consistent with the temporal milestones of CC myelination assessed by clinical 2D-MRI9,10 but also aligns with findings from advanced structural47,48 and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) studies.49,50 This strong agreement with known WM growth trajectories further substantiates the biological validity and translational relevance of our imaging biomarker.

Furthermore, we performed a preliminary diagnostic classification task to explore the clinical utility of the CC-median biomarker. Specifically, using the p3 percentile as a threshold, our model effectively distinguished between TDC and DD-CM in the test set among children aged 6–24 months. This finding underscores the clinical relevance of CC-median as a simple, objective biomarker of developmental delay. However, the sensitivity of classification results was lower for the 0-24-month age group. This is likely attributed to the relatively flat growth of CC-median from 0 to 3 months and the significant difference in developmental rates between the neonatal period (0–3 months) and infancy (3–12 months). When modeling across age ranges with large spans and significant differences in developmental rates (i.e., 0–24 months), the model tends to fit the overall trend at the expense of capturing details during rapidly changing phases.51,52 Therefore, while CC-median holds promise as an imaging biomarker for early detection of developmental delays, further exploration with refined age intervals and specialized classification models is necessary to elevate the clinical impact.

Conclusion

Our study presents a comprehensive 2D MRI data analysis framework for assessing early brain development in children under 2 years, confirming that valuable developmental information is embedded within routine clinical data modalities. This data-driven approach successfully extracts pertinent quantitative measures and utilizes them to construct a robust brain age prediction model exhibiting excellent predictive performance. Notably, the CC-median emerges as the most critical and interpretable imaging biomarker, demonstrating significant potential for both accurate brain age prediction and the early identification of developmental delays in clinical settings. Our findings establish a foundational framework for leveraging conventional 2D MRI to monitor pediatric brain development, with potential applications in investigating neurodevelopmental disease pathogenesis and facilitating clinical translation.

Limitations of the study

Our study has several key limitations. First, the single-center design limits generalizability, necessitating future multi-center, multi-vendor external validation. Second, the technical constraints of clinical 2D MRI (e.g., large slice thickness) precluded the use of standard 3D segmentation and registration algorithms and limited motion correction. Although we mitigated motion artifacts using QC metrics, specialized 2.5D (pseudo-3D) methodologies are required. Third, our binary classification (“typical” vs. “delayed”) is insufficient to capture clinical heterogeneity. Future work must expand phenotyping to explore disorder subtypes and integrate multi-modal data to refine these models.

Experimental model and study participant details

Participants

This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Beijing Children’s Hospital, Capital Medical University (Protocol number: [2022]-E-241-Y), with a waiver of informed consent. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the relevant regulatory standards and the Declaration of Helsinki. A total of 2,982 Chinese infants and young children (aged 0–24 months) who underwent brain magnetic resonance examinations for clinical purposes at our center were enrolled from January 2015 to December 2022.

Dataset 1 comprised magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans from 2948 participants acquired on a Philips Gyroscan Intera 1.5 T scanner at the hospital. For each participant, T1-weighted (T1w) and T2-weighted (T2w) images were acquired. Scans of newborns (defined here as infants in the early postnatal period, corresponding to the initial acquisition protocol) were imaged using a T1w spin-echo sequence (repetition time [TR] = 500 ms, echo time [TE] = 12 ms, flip angle [FA] = 70°, 15 slices, slice thickness = 5 mm, field of view [FOV] = 146 × 127 mm, voxel dimensions = 1.2×1.2 × 5 mm) and T2w Turbo spin-echo sequence (TR = 3903 ms, TE = 125 ms, FA = 90°, 15 slices, slice thickness = 5 mm, FOV = 146 × 129 mm, voxel dimensions = 0.6 × 0.6 × 5 mm). Scans of infants older than 28 days were also imaged using a T1w spin-echo sequence (TR = 500 ms, TE = 12 ms, FA = 70°, 18 slices, slice thickness = 5 mm, FOV = 182 × 158 mm, voxel dimensions = 1.0 × 1.0 × 5 mm) and a T2w Turbo spin-echo sequence (TR = 3128 ms, TE = 100 ms, FA = 90°, 18 slices, slice thickness = 5 mm, FOV = 182 × 161 mm, voxel dimensions = 0.6 × 0.6 × 5 mm).

Dataset 2 included MRI scans from 34 participants (8 females, 26 males; age range: 9–20 months), each imaged on a Philips Achieva 3.0 T scanner. Both 2D and 3D T1-weighted sequences were acquired. The 2D T1-weighted images were acquired using the following parameters: TR = 2200 ms, TE = 12 ms, flip angle = 90°, 18 slices, slice thickness = 5 mm, FOV = 180 × 150 mm, and voxel size = 0.75 × 0.95 × 5 mm. The 3D T1-weighted images were acquired with TR = 8.3 ms, TE = 3.8 ms, flip angle = 12°, 160 slices, slice thickness = 1 mm, FOV = 180 × 200 mm, and voxel size = 1.0 × 1.1 × 1.0 mm.

Clinical assessments and data screening

Based on clinical evaluations and MRI findings, participants were categorized into three groups:

Typically developing children (TDC): Infants born full-term (gestational age ≥37 weeks), with normal developmental milestones evaluated by pediatric neurologists, and no abnormalities on brain MRI jointly interpreted by three board-certified neuroradiologists.

Children with clinically diagnosed developmental delay (DD-C): Full-term infants diagnosed with developmental delay by pediatric neurologists according to developmental milestones or the Gesell Developmental Scale, and showing no abnormal brain MRI findings.

Children with both clinical and MRI-based developmental delay (DD-CM): Infants with delayed myelination as determined by three board-certified neuroradiologists using the Barkovich criteria,9,53 accompanied by clinical signs of developmental delay. In addition, preterm infants were defined as those born before 37 weeks of gestation, according to birth records.

For subjects with both 2D T1w and T2w images, outlier (n = 55) detection was performed separately within each group (TDC, DD-C, and DD-CM) using an Isolation Forest algorithm54 applied to the full set of extracted radiomic features to identify and remove potential anomalies. For subjects with both 2D and 3D T1w images, three participants were excluded due to preprocessing failures.

Method details

Image quality metrics

To quantitatively characterize image quality in the clinical 2D MRI dataset, two reference-free image quality metrics—Average Edge Strength (AES) and Tenengrad (TG)—were computed, following prior work demonstrating their strong correlations with radiological evaluations across MR sequences.55 Both AES and TG are gradient-based sharpness measures that quantify edge intensity and overall image clarity.

The Tenengrad (TG) metric56 evaluates image sharpness by computing the mean squared gradient magnitude of the Sobel-filtered image:(Equation 1)TG=1N∑i=1NGx,i2+Gy,i2,where Gx,i and Gy,i are the horizontal and vertical gradient components at pixel i, and N is the total number of pixels.

The Average Edge Strength (AES)57 quantifies the mean gradient magnitude across detected edges:(Equation 2)AES=1M∑j=1MGx,j2+Gy,j2,where M denotes the number of edge pixels identified by gradient thresholding. Higher AES and TG values indicate sharper images with more distinct structural boundaries.

For 2D clinical MRI data, both metrics were calculated on a slice-by-slice basis, and the final quality index for each scan was obtained by averaging the metric values across all slices. This approach ensured consistent quantification across datasets with varying slice numbers and orientations.

Construction of the 2D brain template and segmentation map

To achieve stable spatial normalization across the 0-24-month range, we used a 24-month-old template, as brain structures at this age are more mature and provide clearer tissue contrast for reliable registration. Given the relatively low resolution of our clinical 2D MRI data, we followed practices in developmental functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) studies58,59 and adopted a single 24-month-old template to ensure consistent alignment across subjects.

We used previously described methods60 to construct an infant average template (Figure 2A). First, a total of 25 high-quality 2D T1w images with dimensions of 384 × 84 × 18 were selected from 24-month-old individuals to construct the average template (A-Template). These images underwent pre-processing for skull stripping using FSL’s brain extraction tool (BET)61 with a fractional intensity threshold of 0.55. Finally, the A-Template was constructed based on the script buildtemplate.sh from Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs).62

To delineate clinically significant brain regions during early brain development, we generated brain segmentation maps corresponding to the A-Template (Figure 2B). To alleviate the manual annotation workload, we employed an automated segmentation approach instead of manual delineation. This segmentation map was derived using a previous template (F-Template) and atlas (F-Atlas)63 through the following pipeline. First, registration was performed using the F-Template as the moving image and the A-Template as the fixed image. To closely match the resolution of the moving image atlas, the A-Template was upsampled to a size of 384 × 384×180 before registration. Next, the regions from the F-Atlas were fused into 18 clinically pertinent brain regions (F-Atlas-18, see Table S4 for more details). Subsequently, the deformation field resulting from the initial registration was applied to the F-Atlas-18 to achieve a coarse registration result: wF-Atlas-18. In addition, similarity features were computed to identify layers in the registered F-Template closely resembling the raw A-Template, which were then incorporated into the wF-Atlas-18. The final segmentation map of the A-Template was obtained by aligning the layers of the registered F-Template to the raw A-Template via 2D-2D registration, followed by applying the deformation field to the layers of the wF-Atlas-18.

Construction of contrast-enhanced combination images

Due to the low resolution of clinical 2D imaging and the diminished gray-white matter contrast in infant MRI, we aimed to enhance image quality by increasing tissue separability. Following,64 we generated a composite image from T1w and T2w images using the contrast index (CI),(Equation 3)sT1w/T2w=T1w−sT2wT1w+sT2w,sT2w=MG_T1MG_T2×T2w.where MG_T1 and MG_T2 denote the median intensities of gray matter (GM) derived from the probability maps of GM T1w and T2w, respectively.

The CI calculation involves three key steps: (1) scaling the T2w image to compensate for arbitrary MR intensity units, (2) subtracting the scaled T2w from T1w to provide the main component of image contrast, taking advantage of the opposite contrast directions of T1w and T2w images across brain tissues, and (3) normalizing T1w + sT2w to mitigate intensity inhomogeneity. Crucially, the normalization factor T1w + sT2w is derived using the median intensity value within the GM region of interest. The median is chosen for its robustness against noise/outliers, and the GM is used as a stable anatomical reference to effectively scale the contrast measures across the heterogeneous developmental range.65

For our data, the preprocessing pipeline (Figure S1) comprised T1w-to-T2w alignment using ANTsPy (ANTs in Python), tissue classification and bias correction using Statistical Parametric Mapping 12 (SPM12, https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/software/spm12/), and calculation of the sT1w/T2w image. The T2w image was skull-stripped using the Data Processing Assistant for Resting-State fMRI (DPARSF, http://rfmri.org/DPARSF), and the resulting binary mask was applied to the corrected T1w and sT1w/T2w images. The corrected T1w image was then registered to the A-template via ANTsPy, and the same transformation was applied to the sT1w/T2w image for spatial normalization (see Figure S2 for illustration of the normalization effects). To focus on GM and white matter (WM), cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) voxels were removed using SPM12, producing the final GM-WM contrast-enhanced (GWCE) image for feature extraction. Binary masks of each brain region were derived from a previously constructed segmentation map to segment each individual’s GWCE image. Due to 2D alignment limitations, only the central and most reliable 3–16 layers of the GWCE images (those containing complete ROI coverage and optimal visual quality) were used for feature extraction.

Feature extraction

Obtaining conventional brain structural features such as cortical thickness from clinical 2D images presents challenges. Radiomics provides a robust alternative by extracting quantitative features that capture subtle variations in tissue intensity and texture. This approach has demonstrated strong clinical applicability in pediatric neuroimaging, not only by revealing biologically meaningful morphological and network-level properties in developing brains25 but also by providing accurate prediction of adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes in high-risk cohorts, such as preterm infants66,67,68 and children with congenital heart disease.69 Therefore, a radiomics-based approach was adopted to characterize structural variations during early brain development. Following the Image Biomarker Standardization Initiative (IBSI)31 guidelines, and leveraging the robust methodology previously validated by Zhao et al.25 in pediatric cohorts, we selected 46 radiomic features for extraction from each of the 18 brain regions. The set includes 14 intensity-based features derived from histograms and 32 texture-based features derived from gray-level co-occurrence matrices (GLCMs) and gray-level run-length matrices (GLRLMs). The definitions and detailed descriptions of these features are available in previous publications25,70 and in Tables S1 and S2.

To ensure methodological clarity, we briefly outline the computation of GLCM- and GLRLM-based texture features and the corresponding parameter settings used in this study. A GLCM of size Ng × Ng (where Ng is the number of discrete intensity levels) characterizes the joint probability distribution of voxel intensity pairs separated by a distance δ and angle θ within a defined mask.30 Each element P(i, j|δ, θ) represents the frequency of co-occurring gray levels i and j. A GLRLM quantifies the number of consecutive voxels (runs) sharing the same gray level, where each matrix element P(i, j|θ) denotes the count of runs with gray level i and length j along direction θ.71 Before calculating texture features, the GWCE image intensity was normalized to the range [−0.5, 0.5] and discretized into 32 gray levels. To minimize methodological differences between 2D and 3D image feature extraction, parameters such as direction and inter-voxel distance were kept consistent. GLCM and GLRLM features were computed in four directions (0°, 45°, 90°, and 135°) for each image layer, and their values were averaged across layers within each brain region. The inter-voxel distance δ was set to 11 voxels for most regions, except for the internal capsule, where it δ = 1 was used (due to its distinctly smaller size and fine fiber structure, requiring a minimal distance setting to capture local texture).

Redundant features, defined as those highly correlated with others (R > 0.9), were removed before subsequent analyses. Features were first normalized using a min-max approach within each subject across brain regions. Redundant features were identified using a greedy correlation-based method: a feature was removed if its absolute correlation with any previously retained feature exceeded 0.9. After redundancy removal, 19 non-redundant features per brain region were retained for subsequent analyses (see Table S3).

Feature selection

To identify the most informative radiomic features associated with chronological age, we employed Gaussian Process Regression (GPR) with an automatic relevance determination (ARD) kernel (GPR-ARD), implemented in the GPML toolbox.72 ARD-GPR was chosen for its strong predictive accuracy and inherent ability to quantify the relative importance of input variables through the learned length-scale parameters.73,74 This approach enables simultaneous nonlinear regression and feature relevance estimation without requiring separate feature selection procedures.

Model hyperparameters were initialized to default values and subsequently optimized during training by minimizing the negative log marginal likelihood over 120 function evaluations. For the squared exponential covariance function, the initial length scale for each feature was set to 30, the signal variance to 1, and the likelihood variance to -1 for the Gaussian likelihood function.

Feature selection was performed within the TDC group. For each of 10 random train-test splits (80%/20%), a 10-fold cross-validation was conducted within the TDC training set. In each fold, ARD-GPR models with squared exponential kernels were optimized, and the learned length-scale parameters were used to rank feature relevance (smaller values indicating higher importance). The top 5–20% of features were iteratively evaluated, and the subset yielding the lowest average mean absolute error (MAE) on validation folds was retained. Features that appeared in at least 6-folds within a repetition were considered fold-stable, and those recurring in at least six of ten repetitions were defined as globally stable. Feature occurrence frequency and average rank were summarized in a heatmap (Figure 4), highlighting the most stable and informative features across brain regions and radiomic domains.

Brain age prediction

To further validate the relationship between the selected radiomic features and chronological age, we constructed brain age prediction models using the ARD-GPR approach described above and a convolutional neural network (CNN) based on the ResNet-18 architecture.75 The ResNet-18 model served as a deep learning benchmark for comparison. Participant grouping and dataset composition followed those described in the ∗Clinical and demographic participant characteristics∗ and ∗Clinical assessments and data screening∗ sections.

For both GPR and ResNet-18 models, performance was first estimated by 10-fold cross-validation on the training set to assess model accuracy and stability. Subsequently, each model was retrained using the full training dataset and evaluated on the independent test set for each group (TDC, DD-C, and DD-CM).

For the GPR-based model, all hyperparameters were identical to those used in the feature selection procedure. The model input consisted of the 34 globally stable radiomic features identified through the ARD-GPR feature selection process.

For the ResNet-18 model, the GWCE images (before CSF removal) were resampled 128 × 128 × 18 and used as inputs. The network was trained with mean squared error (MSE) as the loss function, using a mini-batch size of 16 and 100 epochs. The Adam optimizer was applied with an initial learning rate of 0.0001, β1 = 0.9, and β2 = 0.999. An early stopping criterion was employed to prevent overfitting, whereby training was halted if the validation MAE did not improve for 10 consecutive epochs. The checkpoint with the lowest validation MAE was retained for subsequent evaluation, and the final output layer produced predicted brain age in months.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Brain age prediction and group-wise statistical analysis

The performance of the brain age prediction model was evaluated using 10-fold cross-validation, with the MAE reported as the average ±standard deviation across folds. For the final model, evaluation metrics on the test set included MAE, predicted age difference (PAD), and the coefficient of determination (R2). MAE quantified the overall prediction error, PAD reflected individual deviations in brain maturation, and R2 measured the proportion of variance in chronological age explained by the model. Group differences in PAD between TDC and developmental delay groups (DD-C and DD-CM) were assessed using the Mann-Whitney U test, which was selected due to the nonnormal distribution and unequal group sizes. This analysis aimed to determine whether significant developmental deviations existed across age intervals (0–3, 4–6, 7–9, 10–12, 13–15, 16–18, 19–21, 22–24, and 0–24 months), with p-values summarized in Table S6.

GAMLSS modeling and normative curve construction

To establish normative developmental trajectories for CC-Median values, the Generalized Additive Models for Location, Scale, and Shape (GAMLSS) framework was adopted. This framework allows for flexible modeling of the entire data distribution, enabling the generation of age-specific percentile curves. As an initial step, normality was assessed using the Anderson-Darling test (significance level α = 0.05), and variance homogeneity between sexes was tested using Levene’s test. Owing to the anticipated non-normality, sex differences were further examined using the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test, and the magnitude of any potential difference was quantified using Cliff’s delta (δ) as an effect size measure, in which small, moderate, and large effects were defined as 0.01-0.06, 0.06-0.14, and ≥0.14, respectively.76 Based on the preliminary assessments, and specifically due to the confirmation of non-normality and the finding of negligible sex differences, the initial GAMLSS model specification proceeded with a sex-independent approach (i.e., sex was not included as a covariate).

Subsequently, several candidate distributions were evaluated to identify the most appropriate family for modeling age-related changes. The tested distributions included the Box-Cox Cole and Green (BCCG77), Box-Cox t (BCT78), Box-Cox power exponential (BCPE79), and Gamma (GA80) families. As a necessary preprocessing step, all values for the feature CC-Median were shifted by +1 to ensure positivity, a requirement for these distribution families. These distributions differ in their flexibility to accommodate skewness and kurtosis in empirical data.81 Model fitting and comparison were performed using the gamlss and gamlss.dist packages in R (https://www.r-project.org). Following Cole et al.,81 cubic splines were employed to smooth the age-dependent parameter curve for μ, while other parameters were initially held constant.

Model adequacy was assessed using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and the Generalized AIC with penalty k = 3 (GAIC3), defined as:(Equation 4)AIC=−2⁡ln(L)+2p,GAICk=−2⁡ln(L)+kp,where L denotes the maximized likelihood and p the effective degrees of freedom. Both criteria evaluate the trade-off between model fit and complexity, with lower values indicating better performance. Consistent with standard GAMLSS practice, GAIC3 was used as the primary criterion, and the distribution yielding the minimum GAIC3 was retained for subsequent model optimization (Table 3).

Because the association between chronological age and imaging features is highly nonlinear during early infancy, an age power transformation was applied to stretch the age scale near zero and improve model flexibility. This transformation allows spline terms to capture rapid developmental changes in early months without overfitting older ages.82 The optimal power parameter (λ) was identified by repeatedly fitting GAMLSS models with BCCG across a grid of λ values ranging from 0.05 to 1.00 in 0.05 increments. The global deviance (GD),(Equation 5)GD=−2×ℓ(θˆ),where ℓ(θˆ) is the maximized log likelihood, was used to evaluate fit quality, with smaller GD indicating superior performance. The λ corresponding to the minimum GD was chosen for subsequent modeling (Table S7).

Finally, to refine model specification, two standardized image-quality covariates—AESs and TGs—were systematically incorporated into the location (μ) and scale (σ) parameters in all possible combinations under the BCCG distribution. Each model included cubic spline smoothers for age (using the optimal power transformation), and models were compared using AIC and GAIC3. The optimal configuration, which minimized GAIC3, indicated that AESs best explained variation in σ, whereas TGs was most appropriately modeled in μ. Detailed results are provided in Table S8.

After identifying the optimal distribution, age transformation, and covariate configuration, model flexibility was optimized by tuning the degrees of freedom (df) for the spline terms. A grid search was conducted over dfμ (5–15) and dfσ (2–10), with AIC and GAIC3 used for model comparison. The combination yielding the lowest GAIC3 was selected (dfμ = 10, dfσ = 4 for CC-Median). Next, the df of the skewness parameter (ν) was varied from 2 to 8 while fixing dfμ and dfσ. GAIC3 again guided selection, with dfν = 2 providing the best fit. The final BCCG model thus incorporated smooth age functions for μ, σ, and ν, with AESs modeled in σ and TGs in μ. The fitted BCCG-based GAMLSS model can be expressed as follows:(Equation 6)Yi∼BCCG(μi,σi,νi),withg1(μi)=β0μ+fμ(ageλi,dfμ=10)+γ1μTGs,g2(σi)=β0σ+fσ(ageλi,dfσ=4)+γ1σAESs,g3(νi)=β0ν+fν(ageλi,dfν=2),where Yi denotes the observed CC-Median value for subject i, ageλi is the Box-Cox transformed age with exponent λ = 0.45 (Table S7), and f(⋅) represents cubic smoothing spline functions.

Model adequacy was further evaluated using standard GAMLSS diagnostic plots (plot(fit) in R). These plots (Figure S4) visualize the residual distribution, fitted values, and quantile residuals against theoretical expectations, providing a comprehensive assessment of model fit, variance structure, and potential deviations from distributional assumptions.

After confirming the final GAMLSS configuration, percentile curves were generated based on the fitted BCCG model. A dense sequence of ages was used to predict model parameters (μ, σ, ν) and to compute corresponding centile values (3rd, 10th, 50th, 90th, and 97th percentiles) using the model-specific quantile function. These smoothed reference curves represent the age-dependent normative distribution of the feature.

Goodness-of-fit (GOF) was assessed by a chi-square test, which compared the observed and expected frequencies of subjects within predefined percentile bands (e.g., 0–25%, 25–50%, 50–75%, 75–100%). A non-significant result indicated that the empirical distribution was consistent with the theoretical model predictions.

To further visualize the model-derived growth patterns, the age trajectories of the fitted mean (μ) and coefficient of variation (σ/μ) were plotted. Finally, the normative centile curves were overlaid with the test cohort’s scatter points to illustrate model fit and variability across age (Figure 6).

Classification based on GAMLSS-derived centiles

To evaluate the clinical applicability of the constructed normative model, centile-based classification was performed on the independent test set. For each subject, the feature value was converted into an age-adjusted centile using the fitted GAMLSS model. A threshold of the 3rd percentile was applied to identify abnormal values: subjects with feature values below the 3rd percentile were classified as DD-CM, whereas those at or above the 3rd percentile were classified as TDC.

Classification performance was evaluated using six commonly used metrics: accuracy (ACC), sensitivity (Sen), specificity (Spe), precision (Pre), balanced accuracy (BAcc), and F1 score. Let TP, TN, FP, and FN denote the numbers of true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives, respectively. The metrics were computed as follows:(Equation 7)ACC=TP+TNTP+TN+FP+FN,Sen=TPTP+FN,Spe=TNTN+FP,Pre=TPTP+FP,BAcc=Sen+Spe2,F1=2×Pre×SenPre+Sen.

Accuracy reflects the overall proportion of correctly classified subjects. Sensitivity (also known as recall) measures the model’s ability to correctly identify DD-CM cases, while specificity quantifies its ability to correctly recognize TDC subjects. Precision assesses the reliability of DD-CM predictions. Balanced accuracy provides an unbiased measure when class sizes are unequal by averaging sensitivity and specificity. The F1 score represents the harmonic mean of precision and sensitivity, summarizing the trade-off between detection completeness and precision.
