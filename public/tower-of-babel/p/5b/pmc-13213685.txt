The relationship between patient-reported hunger and gastric contents in adult patients with acute surgical abdominal pathology: exploratory prospective observational study
Authors: Fraser Morton, Emma Ballard, Peter Van de Putte, Nathan Peters, Christine Woods, Victoria Eley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatient-reported hunger has been used to infer an empty stomach preoperatively. We hypothesised that hungry patients with acute abdominal pathology would be considered ‘low risk’ of aspiration when assessed by gastric ultrasound. The primary aim was to determine whether patient-reported hunger was associated with gastric ultrasound findings of aspiration risk in patients with acute abdominal pathology.MethodsIn this exploratory prospective observational study, we recruited adults awaiting emergency abdominal surgery without additional risk factors for gastroparesis. Participants self-completed a questionnaire, and then a blinded operator performed a standardised gastric ultrasound. Hunger was assessed using three question formats (binary, ordinal, or numerical rating scale). A ‘high risk’ stomach was defined as the presence of gastric solids, Perlas grade 2, cross-sectional area >9.9 cm2, or gastric volume (clear fluid) >1.5 ml kg−1.ResultsOne hundred patients were enrolled (median age=32 years, 61% female, mean BMI=28 kg m-2) across two hospitals in Brisbane, Australia from April 2022 to May 2024. Fifty-nine (59%) had appendicitis, and 35 (35%) had cholecystitis. The antrum was visualised in 96 patients, of whom 33 (34%) were considered high aspiration risk. Forty-four (70%) of the ‘low risk’ patients were hungry compared with 22 (67%) of the ‘high risk’ patients (P=0.75). For the primary outcome, there was a mean hunger rating of 4.6 (95% confidence interval [CI]=4.1–5.2) and no significant difference between aspiration risk groups (mean difference = −0.75 [95% CI= −1.96 to 0.45]). For secondary outcomes, we demonstrated a weak negative correlation between gastric volume per kilogram and rating of hunger (rs = −0.137 [95% CI= −0.402 to 0.128]) with no discernible trend.ConclusionsIn this exploratory study, no meaningful relationship between hunger and aspiration risk using gastric ultrasound could be identified. Our results require confirmation.Keywords: acute abdomen, fasting, gastric emptying, hunger, respiratory aspiration, ultrasonography

Pulmonary aspiration remains a leading cause of anaesthesia-related morbidity and mortality, and inaccurate preoperative risk assessment is a common contributing factor.1,2 Gastric ultrasound is a well-validated point-of-care tool to assess gastric contents that improves aspiration risk stratification in patients undergoing anaesthesia.3 It is a noninvasive, fast, and easy-performed procedure with a short learning curve.4 There is strong correlation between ultrasound-derived gastric volumes and those measured through ingestion,5 direct aspiration,6 magnetic resonance imaging,7 and endoscopic8 evaluation.

Emergency surgical patients are at particularly high risk of aspiration, with up to 56% demonstrating a ‘full stomach’ on ultrasound assessment.9 The reason is likely multifactorial, related to delayed emptying from trauma, pain, opioids, bowel obstruction, and inadequate fasting.10 Anecdotally, anaesthetists often use subjective symptoms such as hunger, satiety, or nausea to support their anaesthetic decision-making in these patients with moderate levels of agreement between clinicians, despite limited supporting evidence.11 The best method to assess hunger is unknown (and as a subjective feeling, it is impractical to validate). Previously, studies using visual analogue or numerical rating scales (NRS)12,13 demonstrated a poor ability to predict residual gastric contents, although the individual trend was useful to assess the emptying process.14 The minimum volume of gastric fluid that increases the risk of aspiration in patients is unknown; however, it is generally accepted that baseline gastric secretions up to the 95th percentile of healthy fasted patients is safe (1.5 ml kg−1), with an ‘uncommon’ risk of aspiration of 1:943 elective anaesthetics.1

Point-of-care gastric ultrasound provides two types of information: qualitative information and a quantitative assessment of gastric fluid volume. Total gastric fluid volume can be measured with a validated mathematical model that estimates volume on the basis of the measured antral cross-sectional area (CSA) with the patient in the right lateral decubitus position.8 The Perlas grading system is a semiquantitative antral assessment in supine and right lateral decubitus position. A grade 0 indicates an empty antrum in both positions. A grade 1 corresponds to an empty antrum in supine but with the presence of fluid in right lateral decubitus. A grade 2 indicates the presence of fluid in both positions. Finally, a grade 3 denotes the presence of solid contents in any position.8 Gastric ultrasound results can be used to stratify patients into ‘low risk’ or ‘high risk’ stomach, depending of the presence of fluid or solid food in the antrum in a fasted patient. A ‘high risk’ stomach is either defined by the presence of solid contents, a Perlas grade 2, CSA > 9.9 cm2, or total gastric volume >1.5 ml kg−1.

This exploratory prospective observational study aimed to assess whether patient-reported hunger is associated with aspiration risk assessed using gastric ultrasound in adults undergoing emergency abdominal surgery. The primary objective was to examine three question formats (binary, ordinal, or NRS) for assessing hunger and to determine whether patient-reported hunger was associated with gastric ultrasound findings of aspiration risk in patients with acute abdominal pathology. Secondary objectives included identifying the most appropriate method for assessing hunger and determining which ultrasound parameters—Perlas grade or calculated volume—showed the strongest association with patient-reported hunger.

Methods

Study design and setting

This exploratory prospective observational study was conducted across two hospitals in Brisbane, Australia from April 2022 to May 2024. The Royal Brisbane and Women’s Hospital is a 929-bed quaternary referral hospital, and Redcliffe Hospital is a 250-bed regional hospital that provides general and other surgical services with an onsite intensive care unit.

Participants

Participants were screened from the emergency surgery bookings list, and written informed consent was obtained. Patients were eligible if they were aged >18 years, had capacity to consent, were awaiting emergency abdominal surgery, and were fasting from food for more than 6 hours (consistent with Australia and New Zealand College of Anaesthetists fasting guideline). Exclusion criteria were previous surgery on the stomach or oesophagus, conditions known to delay gastric emptying (Parkinson’s disease; multiple sclerosis; achalasia or other swallowing disorders; BMI >40 kg m−2; pregnancy; poorly controlled diabetes [glycated haemoglobin A1c>8%]; or severe renal, hepatic, or neurological disorders), gynaecological pathology requiring surgery, or inability to self-position in right lateral decubitus position. Participant characteristics were recorded, including age, weight, height, BMI, urgency of the procedure (immediate, within 1 h; critical, 4 h; priority, 24 h; or non-critical, 10 days), scheduled operation, fasting times, comorbidities, and opioid and antiemetic medications type and dose administered in previous 24 h.

Ethics approval and reporting standards

The study received ethics approval (HREC/2022/QRBW/65816) from the Metro North Human Research Ethics Committee. The reporting of this paper complies with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines.15

Study procedures

Participants self-completed a questionnaire assessing their hunger, thirst, nausea, and pain (Supplementary Material 1). The questionnaire was developed by the authors, using clinical expertise, to provide measures of hunger in a binary (yes/no), ordinal (none/mild/moderate/severe), or continuous (0–10 NRS) variable format. A single ultrasound operator, blinded to the questionnaire results, performed all ultrasound examinations both in the supine and right lateral decubitus position. The operator had gastric ultrasound course certification and had previously completed over 100 scans. A Sonosite X-PORTE ultrasound (model P19220-77, serial number 04V3KR with an equipment loan from Fujifilm Sonosite Australasia Pty Ltd) with a 2–5 MHz curved probe was used to identify the gastric antrum (if possible) between the left lobe of the liver and abdominal aorta. If clear fluids were identified in the right lateral decubitus position, three measurements of the CSA were obtained and averaged. Images were recorded for subsequent analysis. The total gastric volume was calculated on the basis of the following validated formula: volume (ml)=27.0+(14.6×CSA)−(1.28×age).7 The Perlas grade (a 3-point ultrasound system used to assess stomach content and volume in patients. If there was uncertainty with image interpretation, clarification was sought from a second highly experienced operator.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was aspiration risk status based on ultrasound findings, with high risk defined as evidence of antral solids, Perlas grade 2, CSA > 9.9 cm2, or calculated gastric volume (clear fluid) >1.5 ml kg−1. The primary explanatory variable of interest was hunger measured on a 0–10 NRS, with 0 being the least hungry and 10 being the most hungry. Potential patient and clinical reported predictors of aspiration risk status were examined. Perlas grade and estimated total gastric volume per kilogram body weight were also compared using each of the three hunger method formats.

Statistical analyses

Categorical variables were summarised as frequencies and proportions and examined using Pearson chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test where more than 20% of the expected values were less than 5. The distribution of continuous variables within groups was examined visually (box plots) and with descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation [sd], median, 25th and 75th percentile, minimum, maximum, skewness, and kurtosis). Continuous variables were summarised as mean and sd or median and interquartile ranges (IQRs) and examined using the Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test. A linear-by-linear test for trend was used to examine the ordinal scale for hunger, thirst, and nausea. A scatter plot was used to visualise the relationship between gastric volume per kilogram body weight and hunger (NRS and ordinal scale). Owing to the presence of a non-linear relationship (shown by locally weighted scatterplot smoothing) and significant outliers, a Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient, with a bootstrapped 95% confidence interval (CI) on 10 000 samples, was used to describe the monotonic relationships between the two variables. To account for multiple comparisons, a Bonferroni correction was applied. Given that there were three hunger method formats being assessed, a two-sided P-value of 0.017 was considered statistically significant.

Multivariable Firth logistic regression was used to explore potential confounders and their effect on hunger as measured on an NRS for the prediction of a ‘high-risk’ aspiration status. The nine potential confounders included BMI, patient diagnosis, time since last clear fluid and food intake, pain level, thirst, nausea, opioid use in the past 24 h, and antiemetic use in the past 24 h. Each model contained a single confounder and hunger measured on a 0–10 NRS as fixed effects. Odds ratios and 95% CIs are reported. This analysis is exploratory and beyond the primary estimand. Data were analysed using STATA, version 18.5 (StataCorp, College Station, TX).

Sample size calculation

At the time of planning, there were no available data concerning gastric volumes in patients with abdominal pathology. The targeted sample size of 100 participants was one of convenience aiming to provide reasonable estimates of the difference between groups for both the categorical or continuous outcomes.16 The sample size was not calculated to detect clinically meaningful associations, and thus, non-significant P-values should not be interpreted as a lack of evidence that an association exists.

Data availability statement

The deidentified data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author, FM, upon reasonable request.

Results

A total of 100 patients were enrolled in the study and were examined by gastric ultrasound from 261 screened (see Fig. 1 and Supplementary Tables S3, as well as S4 Supplementary Tables S1 and S2 for exclusion details). Seventy-eight (78%) were from the Royal Brisbane and Women’s Hospital, and 22 (22%) were from Redcliffe Hospital. The median age was 32 (IQR=26–45), 61 (61%) were female, and the mean (sd) BMI was 28 (6) kg m−2. Fifty-nine (59%) had a diagnosis of appendicitis, and 35 (35%) had cholecystitis. Sixty-eight (68%) patients reported being hungry, and 32 (32%) reported not being hungry. Of the 96 patients with a visible gastric antrum, 33 (34%) were considered ‘high risk,’ and 63 (66%) were considered ‘low risk’, as shown in Table 2. Forty-four (70%) of the ‘low-risk’ patients were hungry compared with 22 (67%) of the ‘high-risk’ patients; thus, no association was found between a ‘low-risk’ stomach and hunger status (P=0.75). Evidence of an association was found between the planned surgery and risk status, with 15 (26%) of appendicectomy, 18 (50%) of cholecystectomy, and zero (0%) of other surgery patients being ‘high risk’; however, it was not significant after adjusting for multiple comparisons (P=0.026). Forty-six (48%) described moderate or severe hunger, and the mean hunger rating was 4.6 (95% CI=4.1–5.2). For the primary outcome, there was no significant difference in mean hunger rating between aspiration risk groups (mean difference= −0.75 [95% CI= −1.96 to 0.45]); P=0.22). Moderate or severe thirst was reported in 62 (65%) patients, and 50 (52%) complained of nausea, with no significant differences between groups (P=0.24 and P=0.47). The median time since last solid intake was 15 hours (IQR=12–20), and time since last clear fluid was 8 hours (IQR=3–13), with no significant difference between groups (P=0.27 and P=0.35).

Supplementary Table S2 and Supplementary Table S4 show the estimates for Perlas grade and estimated total gastric volume per kilogram of body weight for each of the hunger method formats. Fifty-five (69%) hungry patients had a Perlas grade of 0–1 compared with 11 (69%) of those with a Perlas grade of 2–3. The mean difference in hunger scores on a 0–10 scale between Perlas grades 0–1 and 2–3 was 0.70 (95% CI= −0.84 to 2.24). There was no association between the ordinal hunger categories and Perlas grade (P=0.41). Estimated total gastric volume per kilogram of body weight was not found to be significant with any of the methods. Although all three question formats showed similar relationships with Perlas grade and gastric volume, hunger measured on an NRS had the highest level of precision and provided the most accurate estimates for group differences. Eight patients with solids (Perlas grade 3) on ultrasound reported being hungry, of whom six were awaiting cholecystectomy (15% of cholecystectomy patients). No association was found between the planned surgery and hunger status, with 17 (29%) of appendicectomy, 13 (33%) of cholecystectomy, and two (67%) of other surgery patients being not hungry (P=0.37).

Gastric volume was calculated in 52 patients. The median average CSA was 8 cm2 (IQR=6–10), and 40 (77%) had a CSA less than 9.9 cm2, with no significant differences between groups (P=0.73). Eleven (61%) of the not hungry patients had a volume less than 1.5 ml kg−1 compared with 20 (59%) of the hungry patients (P=1.00).

Fig. 2 shows the estimated total gastric volume per kilogram of body weight over the range of hunger scores for the 52 patients with clear fluid visible on gastric ultrasound. No discernible trend was observed, and small volumes were present across the range, which is reflected in a weak negative correlation between estimated total gastric volume per kilogram of body weight and rating of hunger (rs= −0.137 [95% CI= −0.402 to 0.128]). Discussion

Our study found no clear relationship between patient-reported hunger and gastric ultrasound findings of stomach contents and aspiration risk in this sample. Regarding the primary outcome, mean hunger rating was not significantly different between high and low aspiration risk groups. For the secondary outcomes, hunger measured on an NRS was shown to have improved precision and accuracy compared with the binary and ordinal formats; however, the size of the difference between groups was not clinically significant. The presence of hunger was not predictive of a ‘low-risk’ stomach, defined by gastric ultrasound in adults with acute abdominal pathology awaiting surgery. These results are consistent with previous research demonstrating only very weak correlations between hunger and gastric volume in well-fasted participants.14 The findings of this exploratory study do not support the use of patient-reported hunger as a tool to assist in aspiration risk stratification or justification to modify precautions against aspiration.

In total, 35% of patients had a ‘high-risk’ stomach on gastric ultrasound, consistent with the elevated aspiration risk typically reported in emergency surgical populations.9 Those awaiting cholecystectomy were more likely to have a ‘high-risk’ stomach identified on ultrasound, although this did not reach statistical significance after accounting for multiple comparisons. Gallstone disease is a known cause of delayed gastric emptying, potentially because of H. pylori infection and chronic inflammation.17,18 These findings may suggest a differing risk profile between those having laparoscopic cholecystectomy and appendicectomy.

The use of point-of-care gastric ultrasound has increased markedly over the previous decade, with numerous publications supporting its utility in clinical practice.3 However, because ultrasound is not universally available, an important role of research in this area is to determine how clinical assessments (such as patient-reported hunger) relate to objective findings on gastric ultrasound. This is even more relevant with the increasing rates of obesity and expanding use of glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP1) agonists that are known to slow gastric emptying.19 In such contexts, evidence-based screening tools are needed to guide aspiration risk assessment, especially in low-resource or time-critical environments.

This study has several limitations. The relatively small sample size and inclusion of only two centres limit generalisability to other institutions or surgical populations. The exploratory study was not powered to detect a clinically significant effect because it used a convenience sampling approach. However, even with the limited sample, there was no strong evidence of a relationship between hunger and aspiration risk status. All estimates should be interpreted with caution and replicated in a larger sample. As an example, using the estimates in Table 2 for mean NRS for hunger, a total sample size of 388 194 in each group would be required to detect a mean difference of −0.8 assuming a pooled sd of 2.8, 80% power, and alpha of 5% using a two-sided t-test (PASS, version 20.0.10; NCSS LLC, East Kaysville, UT). Consideration should be given to whether this is a clinically significant difference, and it should be noted that there is potential measurement error because of our use of non-validated hunger assessments. Gastric contents represent only one component of aspiration risk, and we did not account for all known risk factors. A relative strength of this study was the use of a single experienced ultrasound operator; however, similar results may not be obtained outside the research setting. Our results are only applicable to the population studied, which excluded those with abnormal gastric anatomy or other causes of delayed emptying. There may be a selection bias because severely unwell patients were unable to be studied owing to the time-critical nature of their surgery. We did not control for GLP1-agonist usage, because at the time of study design, the association with delayed gastric emptying was still emerging. This is unlikely to have influenced our results, because patients with poorly controlled diabetes were excluded, and GLP1-agonist formulations were not indicated or scarce during the recruitment period.

Patient-reported hunger has traditionally been used by some anaesthetists as an indicator of gastric emptiness when assessing fasting status and deciding on an airway management strategy. The results of this exploratory study suggest that there may be no meaningful association between hunger and objective gastric ultrasound findings in patients with acute abdominal pathology. Our findings require validation in a larger sample.
