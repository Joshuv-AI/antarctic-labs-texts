The prevalence, nature and trajectory of oropharyngeal dysphagia within the first year following curative open resection for esophageal cancer
Authors: Michelle Hayes, Anna Gillman, Jessie A Elliott, Claire L Donohoe, John V Reynolds, Julie Regan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryIn the absence of a mechanical stricture, oropharyngeal dysphagia following esophageal cancer surgery, and swallowing physiology more broadly, is poorly understood. This study investigated oropharyngeal dysphagia within the first year following curative open esophageal resection. A prospective cohort study was conducted (January 2022 to January 2024) at the National Esophageal Cancer Centre in Ireland. Participants were recruited between 6 to 12 months post-esophagectomy. A standardized videofluoroscopy was completed. Outcome measures included the Dynamic Imaging Grade of Swallowing Toxicity (DIGESTv2), Modified Barium Swallow Impairment Profile (MBSImP), and Penetration-Aspiration Scale (PAS). Functional Oral Intake Scale (FOIS) was used to identify oral intake status. To evaluate the trajectory of oropharyngeal dysphagia, long-term data were compared to previously published acute data findings. Seventeen participants (12 males; mean age 65 years, range 46–80) were included. The cohort comprised transthoracic (2-stage n = 7, 3-stage n = 3) and transhiatal (n = 7) resections. 11 participants (65%) had persistent oropharyngeal dysphagia (DIGESTv2), and 2 (12%) continued to aspirate greater than 6-months, both of whom had a transhiatal resection. 10 (59%) continued to modify their diet (abnormal FOIS <7). MBSImP revealed impaired initiation of swallow (82%), anterior hyoid excursion (82%), tongue base retraction (100%), pharyngeal residue (100%) and neo-esophageal clearance (82%). When comparing acute to longer term data, there was a statistically significant difference in aspiration based on PAS (P = 0.016), but not dysphagia based on the DIGESTv2 (P = 0.500). Oropharyngeal dysphagia is prevalent within the first year following open esophageal cancer surgery. The study informs the need for structured guidelines and a swallowing care pathway.

INTRODUCTION

Esophageal cancer is an aggressive malignancy. 1,2 Surgery is central to curative approaches, and notwithstanding advances in surgical techniques in the modern era, this highly complex operation remains associated with a significant risk of major morbidity.3,4 Respiratory complications, anastomotic leak, and atrial fibrillation are the most common major complications following resection.5 With advances in enhanced recovery after surgery programs and clinical research, including work focused on prevention, early diagnosis, and targeted interventions for these index complications, outcomes for many patients continue to improve.6

In comparison, the study of esophageal physiology and associated clinical problems including oropharyngeal dysphagia has received little research attention. Abnormal swallowing function may affect quality of life (QOL) and increase the risk of aspiration pneumonia, malnutrition, eating ability, and extended hospital stay.7–9 One-year following surgery, patients have reported physical challenges such as dry mouth, altered food aversion, eating restrictions, and swallowing difficulties.10 A recent qualitative study conducted by this research team examined the long-term impact of aerodigestive symptoms greater than one-year following esophagectomy, and reported physiological symptoms leading to isolation, fear of choking and dysphagia, and avoidance of social situations involving food.11 The combination of physical, social, and emotional issues may contribute to a broader decline in overall QOL where maintaining optimal nutrition is compromised.12,13

To date, there have been few studies examining oropharyngeal dysphagia including both transthoracic (2-stage, 3-stage) and transhiatal esophagectomy in the first year following surgery. Investigation is needed to gain a comprehensive understanding of the prevalence, nature and trajectory of oropharyngeal dysphagia within the first year of survivorship. The findings from this study are intended to inform clinical practice and gain a greater understanding of oropharyngeal dysphagia, with identification of patients most at risk, to prompt intervention and follow-up, and enhance long-term outcomes. We hypothesized that the prevalence of oropharyngeal dysphagia would be higher in the immediate post-operative phase, in older participants, and with those undergoing a cervical anastomosis, with a gradual decline over time as individuals recover. There were two aims: first, to study the prevalence and nature of oropharyngeal dysphagia across surgical subgroups following resection at a long-term phase (greater than 6-months and less than 1-year) following surgery; and second, to examine the trajectory of oropharyngeal dysphagia between long-term and the acute phase (post-operative day 4 or 5) within the same participant group.

METHODS

Study design

A prospective observational cohort study was conducted in a single acute hospital setting, over a 24-month period from January 2022 to January 2024. The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology statement14 were adhered to (see Appendix A). Ethical approval was granted from the St. James’s Hospital/Tallaght University Hospital Joint Research Ethics Committee Project, ID 0029. The primary author created a Patient and Public Involvement group, which included esophageal cancer survivors (> 5 years post esophagectomy) to review the research study design across the research timepoints and assist with the selection of appropriate assessments and outcome measures.

Participant recruitment

Eligibility criteria

Participants were recruited at the National Esophageal Centre (NEC) in Ireland. Adults (aged >18 years) with a diagnosis of curative esophageal cancer who had undergone an esophagectomy were invited to participate in this research and were assessed 6-months following resection. Informed written and verbal consent was obtained prior to surgery. All participants who had a diagnosis of esophageal cancer, had completed neoadjuvant or adjuvant chemotherapy (either trimodality therapy (CROSS regime)15 or perioperative chemotherapy (FLOT/FOLFOX)16,17), and undergone surgery for either a transthoracic (2-stage or 3-stage) or a transhiatal surgical approach were invited to participate. Exclusion criteria included patients with known metastatic disease, or history of oropharyngeal dysphagia caused by an acute or progressive neurological condition or head and neck cancer.

Surgical approach

Eligible participants had undergone open transthoracic (two-or three-stage en bloc) or a transhiatal resection. A two-stage (Ivor Lewis) approach included abdominal and chest (right thoracotomy with one-lung ventilation) incisions for lower third and esophageal gastric junctional tumours (typically adenocarcinoma).18 A standard two- field lymphadenectomy was carried out in all cases. A three-stage (McKeown) approach included abdominal incision, chest (right thoracotomy with one-lung ventilation), and neck incision for middle and upper esophageal tumours (primarily squamous cell carcinoma).19 A transhiatal esophagectomy involves incisions in the abdomen and neck but avoids opening the chest for participants of advancing age or with underlying respiratory disease.20

Study protocol

Each participant underwent a standardized videofluoroscopy (VFS) assessment. Validated swallowing-related outcome measures were collected using VFS and oral intake status was rated on the functional oral intake scale (FOIS).21

VFS protocol

A standardized VFS protocol was completed (see Appendix B) by the first author (MH; an experienced speech and language therapist) and a radiographer. VFS was recorded on TIMS DICOM (Foresight Imaging LCC) using continuous fluoroscopy capturing 25 frames per second as per European recommendations.22 Bolus volumes and consistencies were prepared according to the International Dysphagia Diet Standardization Initiative (IDDSI) framework using a water-soluble contrast Niopam 61.2% w/v Iopamidol equivalent to 300 mg iodine/ml.23,24 The exact VFS protocol was used at the long-term phase as the acute phase VFS.

Clinical/surgical outcomes & VFS data analysis

Clinical and surgical data were obtained from the electronic hospital records across the research timepoints. Clinical data (age, neoadjuvant therapy, pneumonia, recurrent lower respiratory tract infections and FOIS) and surgical factors (site of anastomosis and esophageal stricture), were collected to determine trends in aspiration and oropharyngeal dysphagia rates. The FOIS was used to capture the level of oral intake. The FOIS is a 7-point ordinal scale where 1–3 indicates a feeding tube requirement, 4–6 modified/avoidance of food/fluid consistencies, and 7 no food/fluid restriction. Three validated tools were used to analyse swallow safety (Penetration-Aspiration Scale (PAS)),25 swallow efficiency (The Dynamic Imaging Grade of Swallowing Toxicity (DIGESTv2)),26 and swallow physiology (Modified Barium Swallow Impairment Profile (MBSImP))27 based on VFS studies (see Appendix C for detail on validated tools used during the study). Participants who were presenting with aspiration were defined as those who were rated as 6–8 across bolus volumes and consistencies on the PAS, whereas participants who did not experience aspiration were defined as those who were rated as 1–5 on the PAS.28 The presence of pharyngeal dysphagia was defined as a rating of grade 1 or more on the DIGESTv2, and those who rated 0 were categorized as not having dysphagia. The validated VFS tools collectively offer a comprehensive approach to assessing and managing swallowing impairment, each focusing on different aspects of swallowing physiology, and the FOIS measures tube reliance and food modification or avoidance across the different research timepoints. To evaluate inter-rater reliability of VFS data, a separate author independently re-rated 25% of VFS studies (PAS, MSBImP and DIGESTv2 tools) using a two-way random model assessment on SPSS.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to illustrate participant characteristics and reported as means with standard deviations (SD) and median with interquartile range (IQR). Subgroup analysis examined the median differences in aspiration, oropharyngeal dysphagia and food avoidance/modification using the PAS, DIGESTv2, MBSImP, and FOIS validated tools across the 3 surgical subgroups. To identify differences in swallowing across the 3 surgical approaches, visual review of the data and descriptive analysis determined trends in clinical and surgical factors in participants presenting with aspiration or oropharyngeal dysphagia at the long-term phase. To examine differences in swallowing across the 3 surgical approaches, a mean rank Kruskal-Wallis H-test was utilized as the non-parametric equivalent of a one-way ANOVA. A non-parametric McNemar test was used to determine median differences in VFS data between the long-term phase and previously published acute-phase data29 from the same participants who presented with aspiration versus no aspiration and dysphagia versus no dysphagia. Statistical analysis was carried out using SPSS 27 (SPSS, Inc, Chicago, IL, USA). For all statistical tests, a two-sided α of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Long-term phase recruitment

Seventeen participants (12 males), mean age (range) of 65 (46–80y), completed the swallowing outcome measures and VFS between 6 to 12 months post-esophagectomy. See Figure 1 for participants recruited between 6 to 12 months post resection. 8 (47%) participants were over the age of 65 years, and 9 (53%) were under 65 years. 41% (7) had undergone a two-stage, 18% (3) a three-stage, and 41% (7) a transhiatal esophagectomy. The median time since surgery was 7.1 months. Following surgical resection, 10 (58%) participants, 3 (2-stage), 2 (3-stage), and 5 (transhiatal) had experienced an esophageal stricture and required esophageal dilatation. 12 (70%) participants reported experiencing a lower respiratory tract infection (LRTI) following discharge home and were treated with antibiotics, with 2 (12%) participants re-admitted to hospital for intravenous antibiotics for LRTI. Long-term Phase Dysphagia, Aspiration and Oral Intake Status results

Long-term phase nature of oropharyngeal dysphagia

Six (35%) participants presented as having no dysphagia based on the DIGESTv2 (score 0), 8 (47%) had a mild dysphagia (grade 1), 1 (6%) had a moderate dysphagia (grade 2), and 2 (12%) had a severe dysphagia (grade 3). No surgical subgroup scored with a profound pharyngeal impairment (grade 4) at this phase of their recovery. In total, 11 (65%) participants presented with grade 1 or more on the DIGESTv2 and categorized as having pharyngeal dysphagia greater than 6-months following esophageal resection. When examining oropharyngeal dysphagia across surgical subgroups, all participants in the transhiatal group presented with dysphagia, 5 (29%) with a mild impairment (grade 1), 1 (6%) with a moderate impairment (grade 2) and 1 (6%) with a severe impairment (grade 3). 1 (6%) 3-stage presented with a severe impairment (grade 3). The 2-stage surgical subgroup scored highest with 5 (29%) having no pharyngeal impairment (grade 0) and 2 (12%) with a mild impairment (grade 1). There was no statistically significant difference observed in those with dysphagia across surgical groups P = 0.297 or surgical approaches P = 0.121. Long-term phase aspiration

Participants were classified into 2 groups, those who had presented with aspiration or no aspiration. 2 (12%) participants were placed in the aspiration group and 15 (88%) participants experienced no aspiration. 10 (59%) participants did not demonstrate signs of airway penetration or aspiration on the PAS, 1 (6%) participant had uncleared penetration, 4 (23%) participants effectively cleared penetration, and 2 (12%) participants had uncleared aspiration. The cohort was then further divided into three surgical subgroups: 2-stage, 3-stage, and transhiatal. In the 2-stage group, 7 (100%) participants did not experience aspiration. 3 (100%) participants in the 3-stage group also did not experience aspiration. Finally, in the transhiatal group 2 (28.5%) participants exhibited aspiration scores while 5 (71.5%) did not aspirate. There was no statistically significant difference in those aspirating across surgical groups (P = 0.218) based on the PAS, and no statistically significant difference across anastomotic location (transthoracic vs cervical) (P = 0.222) based on the PAS. Long-term phase swallow physiology

All participants presented with multiple impairments on the MBSImP with the highest impairment in oral residue (100%), swallow initiation (82%), reduced anterior hyoid excursion (82%), reduced tongue base retraction (100%), pharyngeal residue (100%), and neo-esophageal clearance (82%). The components whose median (IQR) results were impaired (>0) during oral swallow included tongue control 2 (1,2), oral residue 2 (1.65,1), and initiation of swallow 3(1,3). The component whose median (IQR) results were impaired (>0) during neo-esophageal clearance 2 (1,2).

The trajectory of aspiration and oropharyngeal dysphagia

Acute to long-term phase recruitment

These data was compared to VFS findings from the same participants in the acute phase (n = 30). 43% (13/30) were lost between the acute phase to the long-term phase due to (i) psychological distress and wished to be withdrawn from the research (n = 3), (ii) reduced social supports/lack of transport to attend VFS clinic (n = 2), (iii) complex/prolonged hospital admission (n = 1), (iv) re-admitted to intensive care, intubated, ventilated and tracheostomized (n = 1), (v) metastatic disease and did not wish to participate further in the study (n = 3), and (vi) died of the disease (n = 3).

Acute phase aspiration and oropharyngeal dysphagia

Findings from this study determined that 53% (9/17) of participants presented with aspiration (6–8 on PAS) and 47% (8/17) had no evidence of aspiration on VFS imaging (1–5 on PAS). 82% (14/17) of participants presented with grade 1 or more on the DIGESTv2 and categorized with dysphagia, and 18% (3/17) rated 0 therefore categorized as not having dysphagia. At the acute phase, all participants presented with multiple impairments on the MBSImP with the highest impairment in bolus transfer (100%), oral residue (96%), reduced laryngeal elevation (90%), reduced anterior hyoid excursion (90%), reduced tongue base retraction (82%), pharyngeal residue (100%), and neo-esophageal clearance (100%).

Trajectory of aspiration and dysphagia

There was a statistically significant difference in aspiration between the acute and long-term phase based on PAS (P = 0.016). There was no significant difference in dysphagia based on the DIGESTv2 (P = 0.500). Table 4 highlights the trajectory of aspiration across surgical subgroup. Trajectory of oral intake status

In terms of eating patterns, (FOIS <7), the mean pre-operative oral intake status was 5.3 (i.e., modifying or avoiding food textures), which altered to 1 (no oral intake/tube reliance via enteral or parenteral means) in the acute post-operative phase. DISCUSSION

This prospective observational study explored the prevalence, nature and trajectory of oropharyngeal dysphagia within the first year following open curative esophagectomy, identifying late emerging and ongoing swallowing problems. The key findings was that 11 (65%) participants continued to experience oropharyngeal dysphagia beyond 6-months, which remained prevalent across all surgical subgroups. Participants who had underwent a transhiatal surgical resection, experienced the most disruptive impairment in swallowing safety and efficiency in this study, however those undergoing a 3-stage resection also presented with swallowing dysfunction, thus highlighting the relevance of the cervical anastomosis. At 6-months, no participants presented with grade 4/profound pharyngeal dysphagia. However, ongoing impairment in swallow pathophysiology was evident with all participants presenting with at least one impairment on the MBSImP. The highest impaired components included increased oral and pharyngeal residue, delayed swallow initiation, reduced anterior hyoid excursion, tongue base retraction and neo-esophageal clearance. Furthermore, 2 (12%) participants were experiencing aspiration, both had undergone a transhiatal surgical approach and presented with aspiration at the acute phase. In contrast, no participant who had undergone a 2-stage or 3-stage esophagectomy demonstrated evidence of aspiration on imaging. The cervical anastomosis in the transhiatal resection is no different to that of a 3-stage resection so it is possible that the age profile or participants are deemed a higher operative risk from a respiratory perspective and may be an important contributing factor in terms of aspiration. Given only two participants aspirated, a larger study would be beneficial to determine if the surgical approach is relevant.

In the early post-operative phase, the findings demonstrated a high incidence of acute post-operative aspiration in up to 57% of participants, and 82% having oropharyngeal dysphagia. To swallow safely requires the precise integration of sensory information from the oropharyngeal receptors to execute a motor plan without the risk of laryngeal penetration or aspiration.30,31 The high rate of post-operative aspiration may be a result of multiple confounding factors including the surgical removal of the carcinoma and reconstruction of a neo-esophagus, diminished respiratory capacity (including one-lung ventilation carried out during surgery), cognitive disturbances from medications including sedatives and narcotics, trauma from oral intubation, disease related sarcopenia or post-operative related stress, pain and fatigue.32–34 The study noted a significant (P = 0.016) difference in aspiration from the early post-operative phase to the long-term phase. The trajectory and recovery of the swallowing mechanism was noted on the MSBImP, with a marked improvement across timepoints in airway protection involving improvement in pharyngeal contraction, epiglottic movement, laryngeal vestibular closure and pharyngo-esophageal-segment opening. Some researchers have suggested that the esophagus has the ability to modulate the motor pattern of the oropharyngeal swallow, which may explain the disruption in swallow safety and efficiency seen at the acute phase, however further research is warranted to investigate this intricate relationship.35 Other studies have suggested that swallowing impairments such as hyoid displacement, geniohyoid muscle impairment and decreased swallowing pressures are recognized to persist following esophagectomy, with pharyngeal residue identified as a critical factor in the development of post-operative pneumonia.36–38 A reduced hyolaryngeal movement may be impacted as a consequence of esophageal shortening and gastric pull-up. Results from this research are similar to findings in a recent cross-sectional study conducted by Gillman et al. (2024),39 where the long-term outcomes of oropharyngeal dysphagia were evaluated, examining its nature, severity and impact on QOL one-year or more after participants underwent curative resection for esophageal cancer. Findings from this study determined that 10% of the patients experienced persistent aspiration during VFS, and up to 35% of the participants experienced chronic oropharyngeal dysphagia, greater than one-year into survivorship.

We acknowledge some limitations in this research. Longitudinal studies are known to be challenging in this population. In this study, retention loss from the acute phase to the long-term phase was 43%. Non-participation in the current study was mainly related to post-operative complications, severe illness, participant withdrawal, cancer recurrence and disease-related death. This resulted in the study having a relatively small sample size, and future larger studies are warranted to examine subgroup analysis greater than 6-months. We cannot moreover be certain if some participants had pre-existing oropharyngeal dysphagia prior to resection relating to the tumour itself or neoadjuvant chemotherapy or chemoradiotherapy. Future studies would be strengthened by including pre-surgery swallowing function and the impact of neoadjuvant treatment on the swallow pathophysiology. Furthermore, patients had open surgery, so whether minimally invasive or robotic approaches result in similar outcomes is unclear and merits testing, although intuitively the impact on esophageal physiology is likely to be similar. A larger sample size across multiple sites is warranted to facilitate directions for future research.

In conclusion, this is the first prospective study investigating the prevalence, nature and trajectory of oropharyngeal dysphagia following open curative esophagectomy from the acute post-operative setting up to 1-year following surgery. The high incidence of oropharyngeal dysfunction should inform clinical multidisciplinary teams with respect to progression of oral intake and aspiration risks. We advise based on this data that best practice should include early swallow screening, timely instrumental swallow examination, dietary modification and exercise-based swallow rehabilitation for acute patients following resection and to continue follow-up post hospital discharge. This study suggests the need for dysphagia specialists working alongside the esophageal multidisciplinary team to develop a framework of care with a tailored protocol for patients following esophagectomy within the first year and into survivorship.
