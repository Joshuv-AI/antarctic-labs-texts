Deep learning approaches for time series prediction of renal recovery in medical critically Ill patients with acute kidney injury: LSTM, GRU, and transformer models
Authors: Anawat Ratchatorn, Natdanai Ketdao, Suphachoke Sonsilphong, Donlaporn Triamwichanon, Anupol Panitchote
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRenal recovery after acute kidney injury (AKI) significantly influences prognosis, clinical management, and resource allocation. We aimed to evaluate deep learning architectures using high-resolution intensive care unit (ICU) time-series data with explicit missingness handling to predict renal recovery within 7 days after acute kidney injury (AKI).MethodsWe conducted an ambispective cohort study of adult medical ICU patients with AKI admitted between December 2022 and February 2025, with temporal validation from March to September 2025. Hourly physiological, laboratory, ventilator, and medication data were extracted from the ICU electronic medical record. Long short-term memory (LSTM), gated recurrent unit (GRU), and Transformer were trained to predict 7-day renal recovery. Missing data were handled using last observation carried forward (LOCF), LOCF with time-gap encoding (LOCF-TG), and LOCF with time-gap encoding and masking (LOCD-TG-M). Model performance was assessed.ResultsAmong 1,493 ICU admissions, 438 patients with AKI were included, of whom 162 (37%) experienced renal recovery. The Transformer with LOCF-TG-M achieved the highest performance (AUROC 0.97; F1 score 0.89; accuracy 0.91), followed by the GRU with LOCF-TG-M (AUROC 0.96). Background

Acute kidney injury (AKI) affects approximately 30–60% of critically ill intensive care unit (ICU) patients. AKI is associated with acute respiratory failure, fluid overload, prolonged mechanical ventilation, and increased need for renal replacement therapy (RRT), resulting in worse outcomes and increased in-hospital mortality [1–4].

Renal outcomes range from complete renal recovery to progression to chronic kidney disease (CKD) or persistent renal dysfunction requiring long-term RRT. The prevalence of renal recovery varies widely (57-92.5%), reflecting heterogeneity in populations and definitions [3, 5, 6]. These outcomes are influenced by age, comorbidities, pre-existing CKD, etiologies, and severity of AKI, nephrotoxin exposure, and RRT [6–9]. In critically ill patients, concomitant cardiopulmonary dysfunction can adversely affect renal function, highlighting cardio-pulmonary-renal interactions [10, 11]. Predicting renal recovery could improve management of ICU patients by optimizing resource allocation and facilitating timely interventions and targeted care bundles to prevent progression and promote recovery [12]. For instance, patients at high probability for non-recovery allows for the early initiation of kidney-sparing bundles including nephrotoxic avoidance, and optimize heart-lung-kidney interactions.

Renal recovery remains an active research area because of limited data, heterogeneity, and lack of a standardized definition. Few studies have investigated predictors of renal recovery in critically ill patients [13–17]. Most have relied on cross-sectional data, which lack temporal dynamics and organ interactions. ICU time-series data (vital signs, ventilator parameters, fluid balance) can capture temporal patterns, monitor real-time physiological changes, and provide forecasting, which are suitable for predictive analytics [18].

Analytical approaches for time-series data must address the complexity of the ICU dataset: interdependent variables, non-linear relationships, irregular sampling, and missingness. Deep learning architectures, especially long short-term memory (LSTM), gated recurrent units (GRU), and Transformer, are particularly well-suited to long sequential dependencies and high-dimensional time-series [19–21].

The objective is to develop deep learning models using ICU time-series physiological data to predict renal recovery and compare model performance.

Methods

We conducted an ambispective cohort study from December 1, 2022, to February 28, 2025. Model development used medical ICU admissions between December 1, 2022, and July 20, 2023 (retrospective), and July 21, 2023, to February 28, 2025 (prospective). Temporal validation used an independent dataset from the same hospital during March 1 and September 30, 2025.

Eligible participants were adults (> 18 years) admitted to the medical ICU with AKI or developed AKI during ICU admission. The AKI was defined by the Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) 2012. We excluded AKI occurring before ICU admission, after ICU discharge, lacking data in the ICU electronic medical record (EMR), or patients with chronic kidney disease stage 5 with or without RRT. The Khon Kaen University Ethics Committee approved the protocol (HE664004) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained for all prospective participants.

Data collection and definition

AKI and staging followed KDIGO 2012 [22] criteria using serum creatinine (SCr) and urine output. Baseline SCr was the mean value 7-365 days pre-hospitalization. If unavailable, baseline Scr was back-calculated using the Modification of Diet in Renal Disease (MDRD) equation assuming eGFR 75 mL/min/1.73 m2 [23].

The primary outcome was complete renal recovery within 7 days after AKI, per Kellum et al. [24]. and the Acute Disease Quality Initiative (ADQI) 16 Workgroup [7]: alive, free of RRT, SCr < 1.5 times the baseline SCr, and urine output > 0.5 mL/kg/hour for > 6 h. The trajectory of renal recovery was monitored until day 28 or hospital discharge, whatever occured first.

Data was extracted from: (1) the ICU electronic medical record (ICU-EMR) provided high-frequency time-series data (vital signs, ventilator parameters, hourly urine output), infusion medications, and dialysis records; (2) hospital information system (HIS) included time-stamped laboratory results; (3) the ICU patient registry managed using Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap), contributed demographics, severity scores (APACHE-III, SAPS II, non-renal SOFA score), medical history (including diuretic/nephrotoxin use, cardiac arrest), baseline creatinine, detailed fluid balance, CKD status, and AKI onset/recovery date.

Data pre-processing

We visualized the missing data patterns for laboratory parameters and identified an inflection point (“elbow”) in the distribution. Features were retained if they were recorded at least once in > 93% (elbow threshold) or if they were deemed clinically relevance. Plausibility checks were applied: tidal volume was validated against minute ventilation and respiratory rate. If the measured tidal volume < 100 mL, or < 50% of minute ventilation/respiratory rate, it was replaced by the calculated value. For PaO₂, only arterial samples with PaO₂ > 40 mmHg or with SaO2-SpO2 difference < 10% were accepted. Two patients with missing height were imputed to the sex-specific mean.

Derived features included: predicted body weight; SpO₂/FiO₂ ratio; tidal volume/PBW; shock index (heart rate/systolic blood pressure); diastolic shock index (heart rate/diastolic blood pressure); neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio; platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio; lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio; and albumin-corrected anion gap.

Time-stamped data were prepared hourly and censored to 7-days post-AKI onset, resulting uniform sequences of 192 hourly steps per patient. We defined the prediction trigger as the point of AKI onset. The deep learning architectures were trained using an observation window of 8 days (Day 0 to Day 7). Specifically, the models integrated time-series data from the initial AKI onset through the subsequent 7 days to predict the clinical outcome of renal recovery. This approach ensures the models capture the dynamic physiological trajectory during the acute phase of injury before generating a prognosis.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized by 7-day renal recovery status. Continuous variables are expressed as median (IQR) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test, while categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages and compared using the Chi-square test. A two-sided p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Missingness was quantified per variable: day-level across ICU stay for laboratories and hour-level for high-frequency monitoring variables.

Missing data handling strategies

Preprocessed sequences still contained gaps. We evaluated three parallel strategies, each starting with backward fill followed by last-observation-carried-forward (LOCF):

LOCF: models received only the imputed values.

LOCF: models received only the imputed values.

LOCF with time-gap encoding (LOCF-TG): each variable included the imputed value plus elapsed time since the last observed values [25–27].

LOCF with time-gap encoding (LOCF-TG): each variable included the imputed value plus elapsed time since the last observed values [25–27].

LOCF with time-gap encoding and masking (LOCF-TG-M): each variable included the imputed value, time-delta, and binary missingness mask (1 = observed; 0 = imputed) [25].

LOCF with time-gap encoding and masking (LOCF-TG-M): each variable included the imputed value, time-delta, and binary missingness mask (1 = observed; 0 = imputed) [25].

Model training and evaluation

We compared LSTM, GRU, and Transformer architectures [28]. Data was initially split into training (80%) and test (20%) sets, we further subdivided the training partition (80:20) to create an internal validation set for hyperparameter tuning. Continuous features were standardized to a zero mean and unit variance. Each architecture was trained using the three missing-data handling strategies (LOCF, LOCF-TG, and LOCF-TG-M). Hyperparameters were optimized using randomized search (Scikit-learn).

We performed a hybrid feature selection process. First, candidate features were prioritized based on clinical relevance derived from a consensus of senior intensivists and data avalilability. Second, we applied the elbow method to the mean absolute SHAP values computed on the test set for each combination of model and missing data handling strategy, yielding nine feature subsets. The union of these subsets was then used to retrain all models for final evaluation and temporal validation.

Model performance was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC), accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score. 95% confidence intervals were obtained by bootstrap resampling. Model interpretability was examined using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), where mean absolute SHAP values were used to quantify global feature importance.

Model calibration was evaluated in the test and temporal validation cohorts using calibration plots. Overall calibration accuracy was quantified using the Brier score. Clinical utility was assessed using decision curve analysis (DCA), which estimates net benefit across a range of threshold probabilities for predicting 7-day renal recovery.

Hardware and software

The programming environment was Python 3.10.13 with NumPy 1.26.4, pandas 2.2.3, Matplotlib 3.10.0, Seaborn 0.13.2, Scikit-learn 1.5.2, TensorFlow 2.10.0, Keras 2.10.0, and SHAP 0.46.0.

Result

Study cohort and clinical characteristics

Of 1,493 ICU admissions, 438 patients met the inclusion criteria for model development. Of these, 910 were excluded due to the absence of AKI, and 145 developed AKI outside the ICU. Renal recovery occurred in 162 patients (37%) (Fig. 1). AKI severity was: stage 1, 125 (28.5%); stage 2, 110 (25.1%); stage 3, 203 (46.3%). Baseline SCr was unavailable for 29.2%.

Laboratory, non-hourly by nature, had the highest missingness, even the most frequently recorded test was absent in 69.7% of patients. Among ventilatory and vital sign parameters, dynamic ventilator metrics and temperature had high missingness (Table 1). All feature characteristics are shown in Table S1 (Supplementary file).

Patients without renal recovery had higher illness severity. They had higher AKI stages, were more likely to received RRT (41.3 vs. 9.9%; p < 0.001), had late-onset AKI, lower hourly urine output, higher cumulative fluid balance, and greater nephrotoxin exposure. Hemodynamic parameters showed higher heart rate, respiratory rate, and shock indices, while body temperature, oxygen saturation, and SpO₂/FiO₂ were lower. Respiratory compromise was reflected by longer duration of mechanical ventilation, higher airway pressures, lower dynamic compliance, and higher FiO₂ requirements. Laboratory abnormalities included higher blood urea nitrogen, phosphorus, magnesium, and bilirubin, with lower serum protein, chloride, and hemoglobin.

Model training & performance evaluation

We evaluated LSTM, GRU, and Transformer models to predict renal recovery within 7 days using hourly ICU data. Each model was trained on 192-time steps anchored to the time of AKI diagnosis (t0 through t192). The initial candidate set included 94 features. After feature selection, 37 features were retained and subsequently applied to each missing data strategy, resulting in final input of 37 features for LOCF, 60 for LOCF-TG, and 83 for LOCF-TG-M.

Transformer

With LOCF-TG-M imputed data demonstrated strong predictive performance with AUROC 0.97 (95% CI 0.94–0.99), AUPRC 0.96 (95%CI 0.91 − 0.9), accuracy 0.91 (95%CI 0.85–0.95), precision 0.91 (95%CI 0.84–0.98), recall 0.88 (95%CI 0.77–0.96), F1 score 0.89 (95%CI 0.83–0.95). Incorporation of time-gap encoding further improved discrimination compared with LOCF alone. Moreover, the incorporation of both time-gap encoding and masking yielded the highest discriminative performance (Table 2).

LSTM

Best performance was observed with LOCF-TG-M. The discrimination was AUROC 0.95 (95%CI 0.90–0.98), AUPRC 0.94 (95%CI 0.88–0.98), accuracy 0.88 (95%CI 0.82–0.94), precision 0.86 (95%CI 0.77–0.94), recall 0.88 (95%CI 0.77–0.96), F1 score 0.87 (95%CI 0.80–0.93) (Table 2).

Temporal validation of the model

The temporal validation cohort included 108 patients, of whom 61 (56.5%) acheived complete renal recovery. Baseline characteristics of the temporal validation cohort are shown in Table S2 (Supplementary file). Across models and missingness strategy, AUROC ranged from 0.87 to 0.97 (Table 2). In temporal validation, the Transformer with LOCF-TG-M yielded the highest performance (AUROC 0.97, 95% CI 0.94–0.99), followed by GRU (AUROC 0.96, 95%CI 0.92–0.99) and LSTM (AUROC 0.95, 95% CI 0.90–0.98). AUROC and AUPRC comparisons for LOCF-TG-M models are shown in Fig. 2. ROC and precision-recall curves of each model are in Fig. S1 and Fig. S2 (Supplementary file). LOCF-TG-M and LOCF-TG outperformed LOCF alone in temporal validation, consistent with internal validation, supporting the utility of time-gap encoding and masking for ICU time-series missingness.

Model explanation with SHAP

We applied SHAP analysis to quantify feature contributions to the model predictions. The top 20 predictors ranked by SHAP beeswarm plots and mean absolute SHAP values are shown in Fig. 3. For the Transformer model with LOCF-TG-M in the temporal validation cohort, the top 10 strongest predictors of renal recovery within 7 days included lower AKI stage, sepsis-associated AKI, lower mean airway pressure, a higher SpO2/FiO2 ratio, absence of mean arterial pressure measurement, lower baseline eGFR, lower serum potassium, a lower shock index, higher hourly urine output, and a lower Charlson comorbidity index. Feature-importance profiles based on SHAP plots and mean SHAP values for the remaining models are provided in Fig. 3.

Feature importance for the Transformer model with LOCF-TG-M (A) SHAP values and (B) Mean absolute SHAP values

For the GRU model with LOCF-TG-M, the pattern of important features differed substantially from that of Transformer model; however, additional key features included a longer time elapsed since the last dialysis session, higher serum chloride, higher hemoglobin, higher dynamic respiratory compliance, presence of cardiorenal syndrome, lower BUN, and positive furosemide stress test response. Additional important predictors are presented in Fig. S3. SHAP plots and mean SHAP values for all additional models are presented in Fig. S5 – S11.

The temporal evolution of model learning was analyzed by plotting the mean signed SHAP values of the top 20 features across time for the two best models (Fig. S4). GRU with LOCF-TG-M showed minimal early-to-mid sequence signal, with a pronounced influence late in the window, suggesting reliance on information closer to the outcome. In contrast, the Transformer with LOCF-TG-M showed changing feature importance throughout the entire sequence.

Model calibration and decision curve analysis

Calibration plots for the Transformer model with LOCF-TG-M showed good agreement in the temporal validation cohort (Fig. 4). The calibration curve is close to the perfect line (45-degree line); however, there are minor deviations at extreme probabilities. Quantitative assessment yielded a Brier score of 0.13 in the test set, which further improved to 0.078 in the temporal validation cohort, indicating excellent probabilistic accuracy and model stability over time. The DCA demonstrated that the Transformer model with LOCF-TG-M provided greater net benefit than default strategies across a broad range of threshold probabilities (Fig. 4).

Discussion

We developed and compared deep learning models using 192-hour time-series data to predict 7-day renal recovery. Models with time-gap encoding and masking performed best. Transformer with LOCF-TG-M achieved the highest discrimination (AUROC 0.97), followed by the GRU with LOCF-TG-M (AUROC 0.96). SHAP analyses identified clinically plausible predictors: lower AKI stage, sepsis-associated AKI, lower mean airway pressure, a higher SpO2/FiO2 ratio, absence of mean arterial pressure measurement, lower baseline eGFR, lower serum potassium, a lower shock index, higher hourly urine output, fewer comorbidities, a longer time elapsed since the last dialysis session, higher serum chloride, higher hemoglobin, higher dynamic respiratory compliance, presence of cardiorenal syndrome, lower BUN, and positive furosemide stress test response. Together, these findings highlight the combined role of AKI severity and potentially modifiable, treatment-related in renal recovery among critically ill patients.

Prior studies predicting renal recovery in critically ill population often used conventional machine learning and achieved moderate performance. Luo et al. [29] used traditional machine learning methods to discriminate between transient and persistent AKI in septic adults with AUROCs of 0.74–0.76, with the artificial neural network and logistic regression showing the highest performance. The most important features were urine output and SCr. Consistent with our findings, increased urine output ranked among the top 20 important features and strongly indicated renal recovery. Zhao et al. [14] reported a random forest AUROC of 0.86 but limited predictors to the first 24 h of ICU. Huang et al. [30] used LASSO to predict renal recovery in patients with stage 3 AKI with AUROCs of 0.53–0.61. By leveraging multi-day, high-frequency time-series data including ventilator mechanics and detailed medication exposures, our models outperformed these approaches and were not restricted to narrow AKI subgroups.

Most influential features in our models were consistent with established determinants of renal recovery [9]. Renal repair after tubular injury depends on epithelial regeneration and resolution of inflammation, whereas maladaptive repair relates to injury severity, duration, and etiology [8]. Our top predictors correspond to biologically plausible mechanisms: greater AKI severity; sepsis-associated AKI and cardiorenal syndrome as distinct injury phenotypes; preserved oxygenation and better lung mechanics; a lower shock index and higher hemoglobin level, reflecting improved oxygen delivery. These findings align with the concept of cardio-pulmonary-renal crosstalk.

We explicitly addressed irregular sampling and informative missingness using time-gap encoding and masking rather than relying on imputation alone. Across strategies, LOCF-TG-M consistently improved performance, particularly for GRU and Transformer architectures. GRU performance increased from an AUROC of 0.90 with LOCF to 0.96 with LOCF-TG-M, while the Transformer similarly improved from 0.88 to 0.97. Notably, our findings also indicated that Transformer model benefited from this information, whereas performance gains in LSTM were comparatively modest, suggesting relative limitations in handling sparse and irregular time-series data compared with GRU and Transformer. Finally, temporal validation using a non-overlapping cohort from a later time period demonstrated moderate-to-high predictive accuracy, supporting the robustness and generalizability of our models.

Despite differences in measurement frequency across data sources, the ICU-EMR contained high-frequency physiological data, while HIS and ICU-patient registry provided lower-frequency information. LOCF-only achieved reasonable predictive performance. However, incorporating time-gap encoding with or without missingness masks further enhanced performance by explicitly capturing the irregularity of measurement intervals and the informative nature of missing values. The superior performance of GRU and Transformer with explicit missingness handling supports their use for ICU prediction tasks characterized by complex, irregular time series.

Learning dynamics also diverged by architecture. GRU predictions were mainly influenced by later time steps, consistent with the fading influence of earlier data and possible vanishing-gradient effects in long sequences. In contrast, the Transformer’s self-attention mechanism enabled it to capture relationships across the entire time series, leading to a more balanced use of temporal information. Consequently, the feature importance profiles derived via SHAP exhibit architectural divergence. These discrepancies do not suggest model instability; instead, they illustrate how distinct deep learning frameworks prioritize different physiologic signals within the same clinical trajectory to achieve comparable predictive performance.

An ethical consideration in real-world deployment is the risk of automation bias, where a prediction of impending renal recovery might inadvertently lead to reduced clinical vigilance. Importantly, the model is intended to support, rather than replace, clinical judgment. A prediction of recovery should prompt the initiation of ‘renal recovery bundles’ to ensure the predicted positive outcome is realized.

DCA suggested that model-guided decision-making can provide greater benefit than treating all or none at a range of clinically relevant thresholds, particularly in scenarios with intermediate predicted probability or renal recovery. In this setting, model-guided may support decisions to continue RRT, defer escalation, or delay routine nephrology consultation. Prognostic information also enables shared decision-making and clearer communication regarding prognosis and treatment goals.

The strengths of our study include: (1) the incorporation of high-frequency 192-hour ICU time-series data covering physiological, laboratory, and renal parameters; (2) a systematic comparison of multiple deep learning architectures; (3) the integration of SHAP-based explainability, connecting model behavior to biologically plausible mechanisms; and (4) strong predictive performance in the temporal validation cohort demonstrating good generalizability despite the methodological complexity and high-dimensional input structure. However, several limitations must be considered. First, this single-center design and the sample size was relatively small may limit the generalizability. Second, our cohort was medical ICU patients that differ significantly from the baseline characteristics, hemodynamic and physiological profiles of postoperative or surgical ICU. Third, in the Thai context, external validation data were not available; however, external validation is needed to confirm model stability across treatment. Fourth, novel biomarkers with prognostic value such as neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL), cystatin C, tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinase-2 insulin-like growth factor-binding protein 7 (TIMP-2 x IGFBP7) were not included in our models due to unavailability. Finally, our prediction horizon was fixed at 7 days, alternative windows, particularly over longer periods, may show different performance and patterns, providing additional insight into AKI trajectories.

Conclusion

In a medical ICU, Transformer combined with LOCF and time-gap encoding with masking, achieved superior performance in predicting renal recovery among critically ill medical patients with AKI. Model explainability showed less severe AKI, hemodynamic and respiratory stability, a lower comorbidities and favorable diuretic responsiveness, as key predictors of renal recovery.
