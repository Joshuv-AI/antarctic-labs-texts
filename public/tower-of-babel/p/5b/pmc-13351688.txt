Retroperitoneal laparoscopic pyelolithotomy as salvage therapy for refractory infectious renal calculi: A case report
Authors: Jiahe Zhou, Yang Dong, Lin Liu, Guangyuan Zhu, Liang Xue, Yufeng Sun, Weiming Ma, Conghui Han, Lili Xiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Refractory infectious renal calculi with severe pyonephrosis pose a clinical dilemma due to ineffective drainage and contraindicated percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL). We report a 56-year-old male with diabetes and paraplegia who developed left renal pelvic calculi (2.5 cm) with refractory infection after failed stenting/nephrostomy. Retroperitoneal laparoscopic pyelolithotomy was performed, achieving complete stone removal and pus drainage. The patient recovered uneventfully with no infection recurrence at 3-month follow-up. This procedure is a safe, effective minimally invasive salvage therapy for PCNL-ineligible patients with refractory infectious renal calculi, with high clinical application value.

Introduction

Renal calculi are a prevalent urological disorder with a high incidence in China, ranging from 1% to 5% nationwide and 5% to 10% in southern regions, and the incidence rate has risen steadily over the past two decades.1 Clinical treatments include open surgery, percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL), extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy (ESWL), retroperitoneal laparoscopic pyelolithotomy, and flexible ureteroscopic lithotripsy, each with specific indications based on calculus characteristics and patient status.

However, renal calculi complicated by refractory urinary tract infection pose a critical clinical dilemma, especially in patients with large calculi, poor physical condition from comorbidities, and ineffective anti-infective therapy. Standard staged surgery—percutaneous renal drainage or double-J stent placement for infection control, followed by PCNL for stone removal—often achieves unsatisfactory drainage outcomes in cases with severe infection and highly viscous pyonephrosis, as incomplete pus drainage may hinder subsequent core stone removal procedures. Progressive infection may lead to life-threatening complications such as septic shock and multiple organ failure, imposing severe physical and economic burdens on patients.

This study retrospectively analyzed a 2025 December case of renal calculi with severe infection unresponsive to percutaneous puncture, catheter drainage, and sensitive antibiotics. Integrating domestic and international literature, we explored the safety, efficacy, and application value of retroperitoneal laparoscopic pyelolithotomy for refractory infectious renal calculi, aiming to provide novel clinical references.

Case report

A 56-year-old male was admitted to a local hospital with persistent left lumbago and acute fever for 1 day, with a 20-year history of high paraplegia (thoracic trauma-induced motor impairment below the chest, extremity contracture, limited lumbar movement) and 10-year poorly controlled type 2 diabetes (metformin + acarbose; fasting blood glucose ∼7 mmol/L, 2h postprandial ∼11 mmol/L).

Urinary system CT revealed a solitary left renal pelvic calculus and moderate hydronephrosis with obstructive infection. Ureteroscopic Fr 5 double-J stent implantation + empirical antibiotics failed to relieve lumbago, and the patient developed periodic fever (peak 39.5°C). Follow-up CT confirmed proper stent positioning but unimproved hydronephrosis. Three days later, percutaneous nephrostomy with a Fr 12 tube was performed, yielding only 30 mL of purulent fluid, followed by cessation of drainage; CT confirmed the proper position of the nephrostomy tube, but normal saline irrigation was ineffective, suggesting obstruction due to viscous purulent fluid.

The patient was transferred to our hospital due to persistent lumbago, recurrent high fever, rising white blood cell count, and falling hemoglobin. Physical examination showed poor mental status, emaciation, extremity contracture, left renal percussion tenderness (++), and an indwelling nephrostomy tube with no surrounding redness/exudation. Laboratory tests indicated moderate anemia (hemoglobin 79 g/L), severe bacterial infection (white blood cell 16.8 × 109/L, Neut% 90.7%, PCT 16 ng/ml), significant urinary tract infection (1067 WBC/HP, 2750 RBC/HP), hypoalbuminemia (28 g/L), mild renal impairment (creatinine 123 μmol/L, cystatin C 1.35 mg/L), hypokalemia (3.45 mmol/L), hyponatremia (128 mmol/L), and poor glycemic control (6.8 mmol/L). Urine culture identified Escherichia coli sensitive to piperacillin. Non-contrast CT confirmed a 2.5 cm left renal pelvic calculus, moderate hydronephrosis, and proper positioning of the nephrostomy tube and double-J stent.

Upon admission, we administered piperacillin-tazobactam (2.5 g iv q8h) + symptomatic support (albumin infusion, electrolyte supplementation, insulin-based glycemic control, enteral nutrition). Lumbago was slightly relieved, but recurrent fever (38.1–39.5°C) persisted, indicating ineffective infection control.

A multidisciplinary assessment concluded left renal pelvic calculus obstruction and viscous pyonephrosis with ineffective drainage as the core causes of progressive infection. PCNL was excluded due to high risk of bacterial seeding and septic shock from intraoperative pressurized irrigation. Retroperitoneal laparoscopic pyelolithotomy with drainage and stone removal was selected to relieve obstruction, remove calculi, and drain pus under minimally invasive conditions while maintaining safe renal pelvic pressure.

Surgical procedure

Under general anesthesia, the patient was placed in the right lateral decubitus position. A laparoscope sheath was inserted 2 fingerbreadths above the anterior superior iliac spine, with 10 mm Trocars placed below the 11th costal margin (anterior axillary line) and 12th costal margin (posterior axillary line) to establish a retroperitoneal channel. Retroperitoneal adipose tissue was dissected, Gerota's fascia incised 2 cm from the psoas major muscle, and renal sinus fat exposed by reflecting dorsal renal fat inferiorly. The dilated renal pelvis was visualized after dissecting adherent renal sinus fat, incised longitudinally, and a 2.5 cm calculus was removed intact with foreign body forceps. A large amount of thick purulent fluid was aspirated immediately, and the surgical field was irrigated 3 times with normal saline (Fig. 1). A ventricular drainage tube was placed for low-pressure irrigation, and a F5 double-J stent for internal drainage. The renal pelvic incision was sutured with 3-0 absorbable sutures, perirenal fat repositioned to prevent urinary leakage, and incisions closed sequentially. Postoperative management and recovery

Postoperatively, the patient received sensitive antibiotics, nutritional support, glycemic control, and electrolyte maintenance. Lumbago was completely relieved, fever resolved, and body temperature normalized. On postoperative day 3, infection-related indicators decreased significantly; the drainage tube was removed with good wound healing and no urinary leakage. The urethral catheter was removed on day 7 with normal spontaneous micturition. The double-J stent was removed via cystoscopy 1 month postoperatively, and the patient was discharged fully recovered (Fig. 2). Discussion

PCNL, first reported in 1976,2 is the first-line treatment for renal calculi ≥2 cm,3 with advantages of minimal invasiveness, high stone clearance, rapid recovery, and short hospitalization. It is also indicated for ESWL failure, lower pole calculi, and impacted upper ureteral calculi, serving as the mainstream for complex renal calculi.

However, PCNL has inherent limitations: percutaneous renal channel establishment, intraoperative pressurized saline irrigation, and calculus fragmentation increase risks of bleeding and infection. Severe infections may progress to SIRS and urosepsis, potentially leading to septic shock and MODS with high mortality.

Domestic and international studies identified key risk factors for PCNL-related infectious complications: Draga RO et al.4 found 11.2% of PCNL patients developed SIRS, with paraplegia and diabetes as independent risk factors (OR = 10.7). Tang et al.5 reported 12.8% SIRS and 5.5% severe urosepsis in 758 PCNL patients, with diabetes, positive urine culture, and S.T.O.N.E. score as risk factors—higher stone burden and infection severity correlated with increased complications. Staghorn calculi carry a 68% postoperative infection rate, with urosepsis mortality of 66–80%6,7 due to large stone burden, prolonged lithotripsy, and multiple percutaneous accesses causing severe mucosal injury.

Preoperative sensitive antibiotic therapy (≥7 days for positive urine culture) reduces PCNL infection risk, but 27.8% of such patients still develop postoperative SIRS,8,9 indicating routine pretreatment cannot eliminate infection risk in all infectious renal calculi cases.

Drainage is fundamental for infection control in calculi with pyonephrosis; percutaneous nephrostomy and double-J stent implantation are first-line for obstructive pyelonephritis.10,11 Zhang Z et al.12 analyzed 172 patients with obstructive upper urinary tract calculi and severe infection, finding that a small subset had ineffective drainage due to viscous pus, ureteral stenosis, or severe obstruction despite minimally invasive drainage.

For refractory patients with comorbidities, severe obstruction, and viscous pyonephrosis, anti-infective therapy + drainage fails to relieve obstruction, leading to progressive infection. PCNL is contraindicated due to high septic shock risk, resulting in a clinical dilemma of "ineffective drainage and no feasible stone removal".11,12

The case patient had multiple PCNL risk factors (uncontrolled diabetes, paraplegia)4 and progressive infection unresponsive to standard therapy, precluding PCNL. The core clinical goal was to rapidly relieve obstruction, remove calculi, and drain pus to eliminate the infection source while ensuring patient safety.

Molina Escudero R et al.13 confirmed laparoscopic surgery's efficacy for complex calculi such as staghorn calculi. Given the favorable clinical outcome observed in this single patient with refractory infectious renal calculi unsuitable for standard PCNL, retroperitoneal laparoscopic pyelolithotomy may serve as a viable alternative approach in comparable complicated infectious stone cases, with several distinct clinical merits worth further exploration in larger cohorts:1.No renal parenchymal injury: No percutaneous channel is needed; renal pelvis incision for stone removal preserves renal function and reduces bleeding (150 ml in this case, no bleeding complications).2.Reduced infection risk: No pressurized irrigation and intact stone removal avoid bacterial dissemination, critical for uncontrolled infection.3.Immediate obstruction relief: Direct aspiration of viscous pus rapidly reduces renal pelvic pressure, a more effective approach than indirect drainage.4.Peritoneal integrity: Retroperitoneal surgery prevents pus from entering the abdominal cavity, avoiding peritoneal infection and peritonitis.5.Combination flexibility: Intraoperative ureteroscopy (rigid/flexible) improves stone clearance for combined upper ureteral or multiple small pelvic calculi, with efficacy comparable to PCNL and open surgery.

This case confirmed the surgery's safety and efficacy: infection was rapidly controlled, no SIRS, urosepsis, bleeding, or urinary leakage occurred, and long-term follow-up showed no recurrence, consistent with existing literature.14, 15, 16

Notably, this surgery demands advanced laparoscopic skills, with renal pelvis exposure and precise suturing as core challenges—adherent pelvic tissue increases renal pedicle vessel injury risk. We summarize key operative points for clinical reference:1.Renal pelvis exposure: Create an oblique incision through perirenal fat at the mid-pole of the kidney, then reflect the fat inferiorly to fully expose the renal pelvis and proximal upper ureter. Dissection near the upper renal pole should be avoided to reduce the risk of renal vascular injury.2.Management of adhesive perirenal tissues: Dissect adherent tissues gently without brute force. Locate calculi using suction probes and perform a direct small incision on the renal pelvis to limit tissue trauma and shorten operative duration.3.Stone extraction and pelvic closure: Rotate large calculi gently for atraumatic enucleation to prevent laceration of pelvic mucosal lining. All purulent fluid is aspirated first, followed by low-pressure normal saline irrigation. For proximal pelvic incisions, tight primary suturing of the pelvic wall is unnecessary; urinary drainage via indwelling double-J stent and coverage of the incision with perirenal fat are sufficient to prevent postoperative urine leakage.4.Surgical field management: Irrigate the surgical cavity with normal saline no fewer than three times to clear residual pus and tissue debris, lowering postoperative infection risks.

Strict adherence to surgical indications is essential to avoid overapplication. We propose the following indications13,14,17: ① Refractory infectious renal calculi unresponsive to standard anti-infective therapy + drainage; ② PCNL high-risk patients (paraplegia, diabetes, solitary kidney with renal insufficiency); ③ Pyonephrosis with ineffective drainage from viscous pus, ureteral stenosis, or impacted calculi. The core principle is to rapidly and safely relieve obstruction, remove calculi, and drain pus under minimally invasive conditions to control infection and save lives.

Conclusion

Retroperitoneal laparoscopic pyelolithotomy is a safe and effective treatment for refractory infectious renal calculi, especially for PCNL-ineligible high-risk patients, providing a novel minimally invasive option.14,17,18 Intraoperative combination with ureteroscopy improves stone clearance and reduces residual calculi. This surgery rapidly relieves obstruction via minimal invasion, avoids bacterial dissemination from calculus fragmentation, and significantly reduces perioperative infectious complications,14,16 with minimal trauma and rapid recovery, making it a valuable clinical alternative with high promotion potential.

This study has limitations as a small-sample retrospective case report. The long-term efficacy, stone recurrence rate, and renal function impact of this surgery require verification via large-sample, multicenter, prospective long-term follow-up studies. Additionally, surgical techniques need further standardization to reduce difficulty, enabling mastery by primary hospital urologists and benefiting more patients with refractory infectious renal calculi.
