Effect of chronic kidney disease on adverse drug reactions to anti-tubercular treatment: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Divya Datta, Indu Ramachandra Rao, Attur Ravindra Prabhu, Shankar Prasad Nagaraju, Girish Thunga, Rahul Magazine, Shivashankar Kaniyoor Nagri, Raghavendra Shetty, Nisha Abdul Khader, Dharshan Rangaswamy, Srinivas Vinayak Shenoy, Mohan V. Bhojaraja, Asha Kamath
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPatients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) are at increased risk of developing tuberculosis (TB). These patients may also be at higher risk of developing antitubercular treatment (ATT)-associated adverse drug reactions (ADRs). Although dose modification has been recommended, data regarding the impact of impaired kidney function on ATT-associated ADRs is sparse. We studied the incidence and profile of ATT-associated ADRs in patients with CKD and compared them with those with normal kidney function.MethodologyThis retrospective study analyzed all patients initiated on ATT from January 2016 to August 2019. Patients were grouped into CKD and normal kidney function based on their eGFR. Data on ATT-associated ADRs were collected from medical records. Predictors of ADRs were assessed using univariable and multivariable logistic regression. Additionally, Propensity score matching and analysis were done for CKD and normal kidney function in 1:3 ratio.ResultsOf 1815 patients on ATT, 75 (4.1%) had CKD. ADRs were more frequent [36/75 (48.0%) vs. 239/1740 (13.7%), p ≤ 0.0001] and more severe [15/46 (32.6%) vs. 43/283 (15.1%), p = 0.010] in CKD than those with normal kidney function. The most common ADRs were hepatobiliary [23/75 (30.6%) vs. 156/1740 (8.9%), p ≤ 0.0001], neuropsychiatric [8/75(10.6%) vs. 21/1740(1.2%), p ≤ 0.0001], renal [4/75(5.3%) vs. 8/1740(0.4%), p = 0.001], and gastrointestinal [5/75(6.6%) vs. 34/1740 (1.9%), p = 0.020]. CKD was an independent predictor for ADRs (OR −4.96, 95% CI: 2.79–8.82; p ≤ 0.0001). The matched cohort showed similar results.ConclusionATT-associated ADRs were more common and severe in patients with CKD, despite drug dose modifications. Optimal dosing of ATT in CKD needs to be further evaluated.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a public health concern globally, with an estimated 10.6 million new cases and 1.3 million deaths in 2022 [1]. Anti-tubercular drugs are highly effective, with an estimated success rate of 86% for first-line treatment; however, an important determinant of treatment outcome is adherence to treatment. Adverse drug reactions (ADRs) are common in patients on anti-tubercular therapy (ATT), with a reported incidence of 8% to 85% with first-line drugs and 69% to 96% with second-line drugs [2]. This often leads to non-adherence to the planned treatment regimen and consequently, suboptimal treatment outcomes, drug resistance and mortality from the infection. ADRs are also associated with significant morbidity and in extreme cases, can even directly lead to death. Finally, they result in increased healthcare expenditure to the patient due to need for hospitalizations and/or a switch to more expensive second-line drugs.

Evidence suggests that patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) have a greater risk of TB, with a relative risk of 10 to 25.3 for active infection, compared to the general population [3,4]. Cho et al. reported an increase in TB risk by 5.1% with every 10 mL/min/1.73 m2 reduction in the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) [5]. Despite this, there is a lack of high-quality evidence regarding the optimal choice of drugs and their dosing in CKD. The kidneys play a major role in the clearance of aminoglycosides, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol. Therefore, modification of ATT is warranted in patients with CKD to avoid drug toxicity. Given that the anti-tubercular activity of these medications is concentration-dependent and that lowering daily dosages may result in decreased therapeutic efficacy, it is advised that dosing intervals be increased (rather than decreasing daily dosage) in patients with advanced kidney impairment (eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73 m2) or those receiving dialysis [6–8].

Despite modifications in drug dosing, patients with CKD remain vulnerable to ADRs, with approximately one in two patients with CKD on ATT developing ADRs as per reported literature [9,10]. This could be due to additional predisposing factors such as malnutrition, presence of other comorbid conditions [11]. Yet, there is little data on ADRs in the CKD population, particularly in those with pre-dialysis CKD. We studied the incidence, spectrum, and severity of ATT-associated ADRs in patients with CKD and compared them to those with normal kidney function.

Materials and methods

This was a retrospective cohort study conducted at a tertiary care hospital in South India. Consecutive patients newly diagnosed with active tuberculosis based on standard methods (microbiological evidence) and initiated on ATT between 1 January 2016 and 31 August 2019 were included. Those with no follow-up data at 6 months were excluded. The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee (IEC-845/2019). The institutional ethics committee approved a waiver of consent, as this was a retrospective study.

Demographic and laboratory data at baseline and at follow-up were recorded. The presence of comorbidities, site of TB, details of ATT including start and completion date were noted. Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation. CKD was defined as an eGFR of <60 mL/min/1.73 m2 on two consecutive measurements at least 3 months apart and was staged, as per the 2012 Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines [12]. Daily drug dosage for first-line drugs in patients with normal kidney function was as follows: isoniazid 5 mg/kg with a maximum of 300 mg, rifampicin 10 mg/kg with a maximum of 600 mg, ethambutol 15 mg/kg, and pyrazinamide 25 mg/kg. In those with CKD and an eGFR of <30mL/min/1.73m2, pyrazinamide and ethambutol were given on alternate days. The modification in drug regimen for other medications was according to the Programmatic Management of Drug Resistant Tuberculosis in India guidelines (Table 1) [13]. The outcomes studied were frequency and type of ADRs. Medical records were screened and physician-reported ADRs were recorded, along the details of their management. The causal relationship of ATT with ADRs was assessed using the Naranjo scale and ADRs whose causal relationship with ATT were deemed to be ‘uncertain’ were excluded from analysis [14]. ADRs severity was assessed using the Hartwig–Seigel scale and classified into mild, moderate, and severe categories [15]. Any death that was directly or indirectly related to ATT was considered to be an ADR-associated mortality.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 29. Continuous data were represented as mean and median, whereas categorical data were represented as percentages. To compare baseline characteristics between the two groups (patients with CKD vs. those with normal kidney function), the chi-square test was employed for categorical data and the t-test (or Mann–Whitney U test) for continuous variables. The predictors of adverse drug reactions were assessed using univariable and multivariable logistic regression, and the findings were provided as odds ratios (OR) with respective 95% confidence intervals (CI). Co-variates included were age, gender, diabetes, hypertension, CKD, alcohol, smoking, HIV, BMI and serum albumin. A p-value of <0.05 was considered to be significant. Propensity score matching (PSM) was performed using Python. Propensity scores were calculated using logistic regression using covariates viz. age, gender, diabetes mellitus (DM), chronic liver disease (CLD), alcohol use and serum albumin. Patients with and without CKD were matched 1:3 with a caliper width of 0.1 times the standard deviations of the logit of the propensity scores using nearest neighbour matching without replacement. Between-group balance before and after matching was assessed using standardized mean difference (SMD). SMD of <0.1 was considered to be good balance, with values of >0.2 suggestive of serious imbalance.

Results

A total of 2016 patients on ATT were identified during the study period and screened for eligibility (Figure 1). We included 1815 patients with follow-up data at 6 months for analysis, of which 75 (4.1%) had CKD. Of the 75 with CKD, 12 (16%), 23 (30.7%), 24 (32%), 7 (9.3%), and 9 (12%) were in stages 3A, 3B, 4, 5, and 5D, respectively, and six (8%) of them had received kidney transplant and were staged in accordance with KDIGO guidelines. Diabetes mellitus [45/75 (60%) vs. 502/1740 (28.8%), p < 0.0001] and hypertension [59/75 (78.6%) vs. 217/1740 (12.4%), p < 0.0001] were more common in the CKD group compared to those with normal kidney function, and the mean age was higher (53.5 ± 11.9 vs. 43.4 ± 14.5, p < 0.0001) in the CKD group.

ATT-associated adverse drug reactions

A total of 329 ADRs were noted in 275 patients. In those with ADRs, the most common reactions observed were drug-induced hepatitis [179/1815 (9.8%)], gastrointestinal (GI) [39/1815 (2.1%)], dermatological [32/1815 (1.7%)] and neuropsychiatric manifestations [29/1815 (1.5%)] (Table 3). On the Naranjo scale, assessment of causality of ADRs were found to be definite in 184/329 (55.9%) and probable in 145/329 (44.1%) of included cases (Table 4).

The severity of ADRs was classified as mild in 75/329 (22.7%), moderate in 196/329 (59.5%) and severe in 58/329 (17.6%) of cases on the Hartwig–Seigel scale (Table 4). The suspected drugs were temporarily discontinued in 198 (72.2%), withdrawn in 43 (15.6%) and dose reduced in 33 (12.0%). Symptomatic treatment was given to 62 (22.6%), 165 (60.2%) required hospitalization and 40 (14.5%) intensive care. Reasons for ICU admissions included sepsis in 11 (27.5%), multiorgan dysfunction in 4 (10%), metabolic acidosis in 5 (12.5%), respiratory distress in 9 (22.5%), encephalopathy in 4 (10%), and hypotension in 7 (17.5%). In 7 (2.5%), the ATT regimen remained unchanged and no treatment for ADRs was given. ADR-related deaths occurred in 11 (0.6%) of cases.

Impact of CKD on ATT-associated ADRs

ADRs were significantly more common among patients with CKD, compared to those with normal kidney function [36/75 (48.0%) vs. 239/1740 (13.7%), p < 0.0001]. ADRs occurred in 8/12 (66.6%) with stage 3A, 12/23 (52.1%) with stage 3B, 8/24 (33.3%), with stage 4, 4/7 (57.1%) with stage 5 and 4/9 (44.4%) patients with CKD stage 5D.

Compared to those with normal kidney function, drug-induced hepatitis [23/75 (30.6%) vs 156/1740 (8.9%), p < 0.0001], gastrointestinal ADRs [5/75 (6.6%) vs. 34/1740 (1.9%), p = 0.020], neuropsychiatric ADRs [8/75 (10.6%) vs. 21/1740 (1.2%), p < 0.0001], and acute kidney injury [4/75 (5.3%) vs. 8/1740 (0.4%), p = 0.001] were more common in CKD. Patients on hemodialysis (HD) had significantly higher neurological/neuropsychiatric adverse effects when compared to those with pre-dialysis CKD [3/9 (33.3%) vs 5/66 (7.5%), p < 0.0001).

CKD was significantly associated with occurrence of ADRs on both univariable (OR 5.79, 95% CI: 3.61–9.30; p < 0.0001) and multivariable logistic regression analysis (OR 4.96, 95% CI: 2.79–8.82; p < 0.0001). HIV co-infection (OR 1.66, 95% CI: 1.03–2.68; p = 0.035) and serum albumin (OR 0.66, 95% CI: 0.54–0.80; p < 0.0001) were also independent predictors of occurrence of ADRs (Table 5).

The severity of ADRs was also significantly different between the CKD group and those with normal kidney function (p = 0.010) (Table 3). Patients with CKD were more likely to need a change in ATT regimen [31/75 (41.3%) vs. 167/1740 (9.5%), p = 0.047]. At the end of a median follow-up of 6 months following ATT initiation, all-cause mortality was significantly higher in CKD, compared to those with normal kidney function [9/75 (12%) vs. 51/1740 (2.9%), p = 0.001]. The causes of mortality included sepsis with septic shock in 12 (20%), multiorgan dysfunction in 17 (28.3%), cardiac arrest in 21 (35%) and cerebrovascular accident in 10 (16.6%).

Propensity score matching

We matched 75 CKD patients with 218 patients with normal kidney function using PSM (1:3). Following matching, all covariates were well balanced, except for alcohol intake which showed a SMD of 0.15 (Supplementary Table and Figure 1). ADRs were more common in the CKD group (36/75 (48%) vs. 43/218 (19.7%), p ≤ 0.0001). All-cause mortality was also higher (9/75 (12%) vs. 12/218 (5.5%), p = 0.071), although the difference was not statistically significant. In consonance with the overall analysis association of CKD with ADR remained consistent in the matched cohort (OR 3.16, 95% CI: 1.61–6.22, p = 0.001) (Table 6).

Discussion

This study highlights the fact that treatment of tuberculosis in patients with kidney disease is complex and challenging. Isoniazid and rifampicin are mainly metabolized in the liver, and the contribution of the kidney to drug clearance is insignificant at standard doses. However, ethambutol, pyrazinamide and aminoglycosides are predominantly cleared by the kidneys [16]. The anti-tubercular activity of these drugs is concentration-dependent and, therefore, extending the dosing interval (rather than dose reduction) is preferred in those with advanced kidney impairment (eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73m2) or on dialysis, as it maintains peak concentrations (Cmax) and optimizes efficacy while preventing drug accumulation and toxicity [6, 17]. This approach suits drugs with a wide therapeutic window and long plasma half-life. Whereas, reducing the single dose is more appropriate for time-dependent antibiotics such as beta-lactams, which require stable blood concentrations and have narrow therapeutic windows and short halflives [18].

Despite these dosing adjustments, ADRs were more common in patients with CKD, compared to those with normal kidney function [36/75 (48.0%) vs. 239/1740 (13.7%), p < 0.0001]. These findings are in line with previous results of Xaio et al. who reported that ADRs occurred in 51% of patients with pre-dialysis CKD, in 53% of patients on hemodialysis, compared to 30% of those with normal kidney function [10]. We also found that a significantly higher number of severe ADRs occurred in the CKD group, compared to those with normal kidney function [15/46 (32.6%) vs. 43/283 (15.1%), p = 0.010]. Mortality rates were also significantly higher in CKD [9/75 (12%) vs. 51/1740 (2.9%), p = 0.001].

ADRs occurred in 66.6% in stage 3A, 52.1% in stage 3B, 33.3% in stage 4, 57.1% in stage 5 and 44.4% of patients with CKD stage 5D. Notably, 57.1% of patients with an eGFR between 30 and 59 mL/min/1.73m2 developed one or more ADRs. Similarly, Saito et al. too found that ATT-associated ADRs occurred in 34.9% of patients with an eGFR between 30 and 59 mL/min/1.73m2, while 20.4% and 20.7% of patients with an eGFR of 60–89 mL/min/1.73m2 and >90 mL/min/1.73m2, respectively, had ADRs [4]. We observed a higher incidence of ADRs in CKD stage 3 compared to other stages. Even though this could be a chance finding due to small numbers in subgroups we wish to highlight that present guidelines do not recommend ATT dose modification in patients with eGFR between 30 and 59 mL/min/1.73m2, and this could be another reason for higher ADRs. This raises the question of whether dose and/or dose interval adjustments should be considered in CKD stage 3; however, there is a need for further studies to confirm these findings.

Patients with CKD had a higher incidence of drug-induced hepatitis [23/75 (30.6%) vs. 156/1740 (8.9%), p < 0.0001], neurological or neuropsychiatric manifestations [8/75 (10.6%) vs. 21/1740 (1.2%), p < 0.0001], AKI [4/75 (5.3%) vs. 8/1740 (0.4%), p < 0.001] and gastrointestinal ADRs [5/75 (6.6%) vs. 34/1740 (1.9%), p = 0.020], compared to those with normal kidney function. There was a high incidence of neurological/neuropsychiatric disturbances in patients on HD 3/9 (33.3%), compared to 5/66 (7.5%) in those with pre-dialysis CKD and 21/1740 (1.2%) of patients with normal kidney function (p < 0.0001). This is consistent with the findings of Quantrill et al. who reported that among patients with CKD, neuropsychiatric manifestations (attributable to isoniazid) occurred almost exclusively in those on HD [9]. A possible explanation for this is an inhibition of pyridoxine conversion to pyridoxal phosphate due to uremia, coupled with a loss of pyridoxine during the hemodialysis procedure [19–21]. The resultant pyridoxine deficiency may lead to an increased propensity for neurotoxicity. Siskind et al. recommended at least 100 mg/day of pyridoxine supplementation for patients on HD; however, major guidelines do not specify pyridoxine dosing in CKD [22]. The 2016 Indian technical and operational guidelines for TB control recommend 50 to 100 mg of pyridoxine per day in multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB) (no specific guidance for drug-susceptible TB), whereas the 2017 Revised National Tuberculosis Control Programme (RNTCP) guidance document for nutritional care and support for patients with TB recommends a dose of 10 mg/day. In our study, the dosage of pyridoxine prescribed varied from 20–40 mg/day [23, 24]. Isoniazid inhibits the conversion of pyridoxine to its active form by inhibiting the enzyme pyridoxine phosphokinase and can also bind to pyridoxal phosphate to form inactive hydrazone compounds, leading to pyridoxine deficiency and consequently, depletion of the inhibitory neurotransmitter gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) [25]. Inter-individual differences in isoniazid metabolism due to genetic polymorphisms can, therefore, predispose to neurotoxicity. Fast acetylators metabolize isoniazid quickly, while slow acetylators do not and, therefore, have higher plasma isoniazid levels and longer half-lives. Therefore, slow acetylators are more prone to neurological toxicity due to pyridoxine deficiency [26, 27]. Prolongation of plasma half-life of isoniazid has indeed been described among hemodialysis patients who are slow acetylators, and a reduction of isoniazid dose in these patients was recommended by Gurumurthy et al. [28]. However, there is a concern that lower doses may lead to suboptimal drug efficacy [17]. There is a need for further studies to evaluate the utility of pharmacogenetic testing to determine acetylator status and guide isoniazid dosing in CKD.

The presence of CKD was associated with five-fold higher odds (3-fold higher odds in matched cohort) of ATT-associated ADR, even when adjusted for age, diabetes mellitus, hypoalbuminemia, HIV co-infection and other known risk factors. Impaired clearance by the kidneys, altered pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics, higher number of potential drug–drug interactions due to polypharmacy, and comorbid illnesses may all lead to an increased vulnerability to ADRs in CKD. These factors underline the need for heightened physician awareness, a multidisciplinary approach to management (with involvement of nephrologists, clinical pharmacists and infectious disease specialists), improved patient education and need for closer monitoring. A subgroup analysis of drug-induced hepatitis and CKD in our study revealed that CKD is an independent predictor of drug-induced hepatitis (OR 3.33,95% CI: 0.42–0.67, p < 0.0001). This contradicts a previous study by Baghaei et al. which concluded that chronic renal failure is not an independent risk factor for drug-induced hepatitis (OR 1.91, 95% CI: 0.90–4.10; p = 0.092) [29]. The differences in results between the two studies may be explained by distinct baseline demographic characteristics of the study populations. For example, the mean age of our study population was 43.9 ± 14.5 years, whereas in the study by Baghaei et al., it was 65.92 ± 13.38 years. It is noteworthy though that we found a consistent association of CKD with drug-induced hepatitis even in the propensity-score matched cohort (OR 2.88, 95% CI: 1.28–6.45, p = 0.010).

This is the first study, to the best of our knowledge, to systematically evaluate the incidence, probability of causation, and severity of ATT-associated ADRs in the CKD population. The limitation of this study is its retrospective nature, which can introduce inherent biases. This being a medical record-based study, it is possible that milder ADRs may not have been captured. Furthermore, some patients initiated on ATT at our hospital might have sought treatment for ADRs elsewhere, and thus, the number of ADRs may have been underestimated. Finally, our findings may not be generalizable since this was a single-center study.

Despite these shortcomings, our study sheds light on the existing knowledge gaps and lack of high-quality evidence in the management of TB in the CKD population. The 2010 British Thoracic Society guidelines offer the most comprehensive guidance for management of TB in CKD; however, its recommendations pertaining to drug dosing in active TB are graded ‘D’. To date, there are no randomized trials or well-designed prospective studies to guide optimal management of TB in CKD. Further, pharmacokinetic studies of first-line drugs and second-line drugs in CKD are sparse, with most primarily focusing on patients on hemodialysis. It is imperative that well-designed prospective studies, incorporating pharmacokinetic data, be conducted to provide insights on optimal drug dosing, so that ADRs can be minimized, without compromising on drug efficacy. In this regard, the role of therapeutic drug monitoring in the CKD population also needs to be explored.

Conclusions

The odds of developing ATT-associated ADRs are five-fold higher (three-fold higher odds in matched cohort) in patients with CKD, compared to those with normal kidney function. Neurological and neuropsychiatric ADRs occur in a third of patients on HD. A high proportion of patients with stage 3 CKD develop ADRs; hence, the present recommendation of modification of ATT only for advanced CKD may need to be reconsidered.
