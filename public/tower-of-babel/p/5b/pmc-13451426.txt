Growth regulator-mediated modulation of antioxidant and secondary metabolism in coriander under exposure to high temperature
Authors: Yamini Tak, Preeti Verma, Manpreet Kaur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHigh temperature poses a serious constraint to coriander (Coriandrum sativum L.) limiting growth, essential oil yield and antioxidant potential. This study provides a novel evaluation of the combined effects of plant growth regulators (salicylic acid, thiosalicylic acid, and thiourea) on antioxidant systems and secondary metabolism across contrasting coriander genotypes (RKD-18 and RCr-728) under variable field temperature conditions. Thiosalicylic acid (TSA) treatment significantly enhanced essential oil content (0.58% in RKD-18) under normal sowing conditions. Among the treatments, foliar application of (TSA, 100 ppm) proved most effective, significantly enhancing osmolyte accumulation (proline: 15.01 µg/g FW), and secondary metabolites, including total phenolics (60.9 mg GAE/g) and flavonoids (18.89 mg QE/g). TSA also markedly enhanced antioxidant enzyme activities, with superoxide dismutase reaching 0.35 µmol min− 1 g− 1 FW, catalase up to 0.45 µmol min− 1 g− 1 FW, and peroxidase up to 0.37 µmol min− 1 g− 1 FW under normal sowing conditions, while reducing lipid peroxidation and improving redox balance. Enhanced antioxidant capacity was further supported by higher ferric reducing antioxidant power (4.54 mg TE/g) and 2,2′-azinobis-(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (56.1 µmol TE/g) values, particularly in RKD-18. In contrast, thiourea at higher concentration (500 ppm) exhibited inhibitory effects, indicating possible phytotoxicity. Across treatments, RKD-18 consistently outperformed RCr-728, demonstrating superior biochemical and enzymatic resilience. Overall, the study is significant in identifying thiosalicylic acid as an effective and practical foliar elicitor that enhances antioxidant defense and secondary metabolism, thereby improving coriander performance, yield, and quality under variable high-temperature field conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53256-2.Keywords: Antioxidant enzymes, Coriander, Essential oil, Genotypic variation, High temperature, Thiosalicylic acidSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biotechnology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Coriander (Coriandrum sativum L.) is an imperative aromatic and medicinal herb extensively cultivated for its culinary and therapeutic properties. It is valued for both its leaves and seeds, which are used as flavouring agents in various cuisines worldwide. It is enriched in essential nutrients, vitamins (A, C, and K), minerals (calcium, potassium, and iron) and bioactive compounds such as flavonoids, polyphenols and essential oils, coriander exhibits significant antioxidant, anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial properties1. As Export Import Data2 India stands as the world’s largest producer of coriander, contributing approximately 68.58% to global production with an output of 1.89 billion metric ton. According to Report Linker Insights3, within India, the total area devoted to coriander cultivation is projected to be around 1.985 million hectares in 2024, with expectations of reaching 2.2719 million hectares by 2028. In 2024, the area devoted to coriander cultivation in Rajasthan experienced a notable decline. According to data from the Department of Agriculture & Farmers Welfare 2025, the cultivated area decreased to 49,425 hectares, down from 94,423 hectares in 2023. Factors contributing to this decline may include unfavourable climatic conditions and market dynamics. Despite this, Rajasthan remains a significant player in India’s coriander production, with regions like Ramganj Mandi, known as the “Coriander City,” hosting one of Asia’s largest coriander markets.

Coriander growth and its productivity are significantly affected by numerous environmental stresses, particularly high temperature stress. Coriander is a cool-season crop and exposure to high temperature, especially above 30–35 °C, disrupts its physiological and biochemical processes4. One of the most critical effects is premature bolting, where the plant shifts to flowering earlier than expected, leading to reduced leaf yield and poor marketability. High temperature induce oxidative stress in plants by increasing the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which can lead to cellular damage, reduced photosynthetic efficiency and overall decline in plant health. As a result, essential oil content and seed quality decline, reducing the crop’s economic and medicinal value5,6. Among the strategies to mitigate such impacts, plant growth regulators (PGRs) had gained attention as key modulators of stress adaptation. Recent studies have highlighted the role of signalling molecules such as salicylic acid (SA) and its derivatives in enhancing stress tolerance by regulating antioxidant defense systems, osmolyte accumulation, and secondary metabolism. These compounds influence both enzymatic antioxidants, including superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT) and peroxidase (POD), and non-enzymatic components such as phenolics and flavonoids, thereby maintaining redox balance under stress conditions7,8. In addition, PGRs are known to modulate key metabolic pathways, including phenylpropanoid and terpenoid biosynthesis, which are directly linked to antioxidant potential and essential oil production. Despite these advances, important gaps remain in our understanding of PGR-mediated stress responses in coriander. In particular, there is limited information on (i) the combined effects of different PGRs, such as salicylic acid (SA), thiosalicylic acid (TSA) and thiourea (TU) on antioxidant systems and secondary metabolism and (ii) genotype-specific responses under variable field temperature conditions. Most previous studies have focused on individual regulators or controlled environments, with relatively few addressing integrated responses under realistic field conditions.

In this context, the present study investigates the role of SA, TSA, and TU, individually and in combination, in modulating antioxidant defense system and secondary metabolite accumulation in contrasting coriander genotypes under normal and late-sown (exposure of high temperature) field conditions. The study specifically aims to: (i) evaluate the influence of PGRs on enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidant systems, (ii) examine their role in osmolyte accumulation, oxidative damage regulation, and secondary metabolite biosynthesis, and (iii) identify effective PGR strategies for enhancing coriander adaptions to high temperature while sustaining essential oil yield and quality. By integrating genotype-specific responses with field-level stress conditions, this work provides novel insights into PGR-mediated regulation of metabolic and antioxidant pathways, and highlights their potential application for improving coriander resilience under changing climatic scenarios.

Materials and methods

Experimental design and crop management

A field experiment was conducted at the Agricultural Research Station, Ummedganj, Kota, Rajasthan, during the rabi season of 2023-24. Throughout the crop growth period, the maximum and minimum temperature recorded were 35.5 °C and 6.5 °C, respectively, with a total rainfall of 74 mm. During the reproductive phase (flowering to seed filling) crops under normal sowing experienced a mean maximum and minimum temperature of 26.2 °C and 11.2 °C, respectively, whereas late sown crops were exposed to substantially higher temperature, with mean maximum and minimum values of 38.6 °C and 14.2 °C, respectively, thereby confirming the imposition of high temperature stress during this critical developmental stage. The experiment involved two contrasting coriander varieties, RCr 728 and RKD-18, sown on two different dates to impose high temperature-normal sowing in the first week of November and late sowing in the third week of November. Late sowing exposed the reproductive stages of the crop to higher temperature, particularly during flowering to seed development, which is highly sensitive phase in coriander. RCr-728 is a late-maturing coriander variety, while RKD-18 is an early-maturing variety. Seeds were sown in four-meter-long rows, spaced 30 cm apart, using a seed rate of 20 kg/ha. Sowing was carried out behind a hand plough at a depth of 2–3 cm. The treatments included a control (water spray), foliar application of salicylic acid (SA) at 100 ppm, thiosalicylic acid (TSA) at 100 ppm, thiourea (TU) at 500 ppm, and a combination of SA (100 ppm) and TSA (100 ppm), applied at 70–80 days after sowing (DAS) following all the recommended agricultural practices. Each treatment was replicated three times in a factorial randomized block design (RBD). During the crop-growing season, two irrigations were applied: the first at the pre-flowering stage and the second at the flowering/seed formation stage. The seeds were harvested on March 22, 2024 and used for the estimation of biochemical constituents.

Biochemical analysis

Essential oil content

Essential oil was extracted from 100 g of cleaned seeds from each treatment using the hydro-distillation method given by Clevenger9. Prior to distillation, the chilled seeds were finely ground and placed in 500 ml of distilled water. The distillation process was carried out continuously for 4–5 h. The essential oil layer was carefully separated using a micropipette. Essential oil, light yellow in colour, was weighed and the yield was expressed as a percentage (w/w).

Proline content

Proline content in coriander seed samples was determined using the method described Bates Bates et al.10,. The samples were homogenized in 3% sulfosalicylic acid and the resulting homogenate was filtered. To 2 ml of the filtrate, 2 ml of acid ninhydrin reagent and 2 ml of glacial acetic acid were added. The reaction mixture was incubated in a water bath at 100 °C for 1 h, then immediately cooled on ice for 20 min. Subsequently, 4 ml of toluene was added to extract the chromophore. The absorbance of the upper toluene layer was measured at 520 nm using a spectrophotometer and proline concentration was calculated based on a standard calibration curve.

Lipid peroxidation activity

Lipid peroxidation was estimated by quantifying malondialdehyde (MDA) content following the method described by Cakmak and Horst11, with slight modifications. The reaction mixture (3 ml total volume) consisted of 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.8) and 0.5% (w/v) 2-thiobarbituric acid (TBA). The extract was incubated in a boiling water bath at 100 °C for 10 min and then rapidly cooled on ice. The mixture was centrifuged at 3,000 × g for 10 min, and the absorbance of the supernatant was recorded at 532 nm. To correct for non-specific turbidity, the absorbance at 600 nm was subtracted. MDA concentration was calculated using the extinction coefficient of 155 mM⁻¹ cm⁻¹ as described by Cakmak and Horst11.

Total phenolic content

Total phenolic content was estimated spectrophotometrically using the Folin-Ciocalteu method as described by Sánchez-Rangel et al.12,. The reaction was carried out in volumetric flasks by sequentially adding 100 µL of diluted extract, 5 ml of distilled water, 250 µL of Folin-Ciocalteu reagent, and 750 µL of 20% sodium carbonate solution. The volume was adjusted to 10 ml with distilled water. The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 30 min, after which the absorbance was recorded at 700 nm using a spectrophotometer. Gallic acid was used as the standard for calibration, and the results were expressed as micrograms of gallic acid equivalent mg GAE/g of sample.

Total flavonoid content

Total flavonoid content was determined using the aluminium chloride colorimetric method, as described by Zhishen et al.13,. A 200 µL aliquot of appropriately diluted extract was added to a volumetric flask containing 2 ml of distilled water and 150 µL of 5% sodium nitrite. After 5 min of incubation, 150 µL of 10% aluminium chloride was added and mixed thoroughly. After an additional 8 min, 1 ml of 1 M sodium hydroxide and 1.5 ml of distilled water were added, and the solution was mixed well. The absorbance was measured at 510 nm against a water blank. Catechin was used as the standard for calibration, and the total flavonoid content was expressed as micrograms of catechin equivalent mg CE/g of sample.

Determination of antioxidant capacity

2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) assay

The DPPH radical scavenging activity was determined following the method of Blois14, with minor modifications. A total of 3 ml of the sample solution was mixed with 0.5 ml of DPPH (2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl) radical solution. The mixture was incubated in the dark at room temperature for 40–45 min, after the absorbance was measured at 515 nm using a spectrophotometer. A standard calibration curve was constructed using different concentrations of Trolox ranging from 30 to 230 µg/ml. 2,2′-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS) assay

The ABTS assay was performed according to the method described by Re et al.15, with slight modifications. The ABTS⁺• radical cation was generated by mixing 7 mM ABTS (2,2′-azinobis-(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) with 2.45 mM potassium persulfate. The mixture was incubated in the dark at 20–25 °C for 12–14 h to allow the formation of a stable radical solution. Prior to use, the solution was diluted with phosphate-buffered saline to obtain an absorbance of approximately 0.90 ± 0.02 at 734 nm. For the assay, 2 ml of the diluted ABTS⁺• solution was mixed with 1 ml of the appropriately diluted sample. The reaction mixture was incubated in the dark at room temperature for 10–12 min, and the absorbance was then measured at 734 nm using a spectrophotometer. A standard curve was prepared using Trolox concentrations ranging from 15 to 40 µmol TE/g16.

Ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) assay

The FRAP assay was performed following the method described by Benzie and Strain17 with minor modifications. The FRAP reagent was freshly prepared before each measurement by mixing 300 mM acetate buffer (pH 3.6), 10 mM TPTZ (2,4,6-tripyridyl-s-triazine) dissolved in 40 mM HCl, and 20 mM FeCl₃·6 H₂O in distilled water in a volume ratio of 10:1:1 (v/v/v). The reagent mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 10 min prior to use. For the assay, 3 ml of the freshly prepared FRAP reagent was mixed with 0.1 ml of the sample solution. The mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 10 min, and the absorbance was recorded at 593 nm using a spectrophotometer. A standard curve was generated using Trolox concentrations ranging from 40 to 190 mg TE/g.

Antioxidant enzymes

Antioxidant enzyme extraction and activity assays were performed at 4 °C to preserve enzyme integrity. A 200 mg of finely ground coriander seed was homogenized in a pre-chilled mortar using 3 ml of ice-cold 0.1 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.8) containing 1 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF), and 0.5% (w/v) polyvinylpolypyrrolidone (PVP). The homogenate was filtered through two layers of muslin cloth, and the filtrate was centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was collected and stored at −20 °C for subsequent enzyme assays.

Superoxide dismutase (SOD)

Superoxide dismutase activity was determined by measuring the inhibition of photochemical reduction of nitro blue tetrazolium (NBT) as described by Dhindsa et al.18,. The reaction mixture (3 ml) contained 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.8), 13 mM methionine, 75 µM NBT, 0.1 mM EDTA, 2 µM riboflavin and an appropriate aliquot of enzyme extract. The reaction was initiated by placing the tubes under fluorescent light (approximately 4000 lx) for 10–15 min, while a non-illuminated tube served as the blank. The absorbance was recorded at 560 nm, and SOD activity was expressed as units per gram fresh weight (µmol/min/g FW). One unit of SOD activity was defined as the amount of enzyme required to cause 50% inhibition of NBT reduction under assay conditions.

Catalase (CAT)

The reaction mixture contained 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.5) and 20 mM H₂O₂ in a final volume of 3 ml. The reaction was initiated by adding the enzyme extract, and the decrease in absorbance due to the decomposition of H₂O₂ was recorded at 240 nm for 1–2 min using a spectrophotometer as described by Aebi19. Catalase activity was calculated from the rate of decline in absorbance and expressed as units per gram fresh weight (µmol/min/g FW). One unit of CAT activity was defined as the amount of enzyme required to decompose 1 µmol of H₂O₂ per minute under assay conditions.

Peroxidase (POD)

Peroxidase activity was determined following the method of Castillo et al.20, based on the oxidation of Guaiacol in the presence of hydrogen peroxide. The reaction mixture (3 ml final volume) contained 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 5.5), 1 mM guaiacol, 0.5 mM H₂O₂ and an appropriate aliquot of enzyme extract. The reaction was initiated by the addition of H₂O₂, and the increase in absorbance due to the formation of tetraguaiacol was recorded at 470 nm for 1–2 min. Peroxidase activity was expressed as units per gram fresh weight (µmol/min/g FW), based on the rate of change in absorbance per minute under assay conditions.

Statistical analysis

The data obtained were analysed by analysis of variance (ANOVA) using RStudio version 4.2.0 (RStudio, Inc., Boston, MA). The differences between mean values were compared using Tukey’s multiple comparison tests with a significance level of p ≤ 0.05. The correlation analysis was done by using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. Different letters presented in the figures as superscripts illustrate significant differences among the treatment among varieties at p ≤ 0.05.

Results

Essential oil content

The essential oil (EO) content in coriander seeds varied significantly among the genotypes, sowing times, and growth regulator treatments (Fig. 1a). Under normal sowing conditions, RKD-18 recorded the highest EO content across all treatments, with a peak value of 0.58% at 100 ppm TSA, which was significantly higher than other treatments (p < 0.05). In contrast, under late sowing conditions, both genotypes exhibited a marked decrease in EO content. The essential oil content for the RCr-728 genotype rose from the control to 100 ppm SA and peaked at 100 ppm TSA (0.5% under normal sowing and 0.32% with late sowing). However, a decline was observed at 500 ppm TU in both sowing conditions. A similar trend was seen in RKD-18, though the decline at 500 ppm TU was more pronounced under late sowing. Among all treatments, 100 ppm TSA resulted in the highest EO content for both genotypes in normal conditions, while 500 ppm TU had the lowest EO levels, particularly in the late sown RCr-728 genotype (0.25%).

Total phenols and flavonoids

The total phenolic content varied significantly (p < 0.05) among genotypes, treatments, and sowing conditions. The highest phenol content was observed in late sown RKD-18 under 100 ppm TSA treatment (60.9 mg GAE/g), followed by normal sown RKD-18 (60.4 mg GAE/g). The lowest phenol level was recorded in late sown RCr-728 under control (54.2 mg GAE/g). Thiosalicylic acid treatment significantly enhanced phenolic accumulation compared to control and other treatments, while 500 ppm TU showed a slight decline in most cases (Fig. 1b). In terms of flavonoid content, the trend closely paralleled phenol accumulation. The highest flavonoid content was noted in late sown RKD-18 treated with 100 ppm TSA (18.89 mg QE/g), followed by SA treatment (18.28 mg QE/g). The lowest flavonoid level was observed in normal sown RCr-728 under control (14.35 mg QE/g). As with phenols, TSA (100 ppm) significantly enhanced flavonoid content across all treatments, while thiourea (500 ppm) had a moderate or no enhancing effect (Fig. 1c). Statistical analysis indicated that mean values sharing different letters are significantly different at p < 0.05, confirming the effectiveness of TSA in boosting antioxidant metabolites, particularly in the Late RKD-18 genotype.

Proline and lipid peroxidation activity

Maximum proline content was observed in late sown RKD-18 under 100 ppm TSA treatment (15.01 µg/g FW), followed closely by late sown RCr-728 (14.88 µg/g FW) under the same treatment. The lowest proline content was recorded in normal sown RKD-18 under control conditions (12.06 µg/g FW) (Fig. 1d). Thiosalicylic acid (100 ppm) significantly enhanced proline levels in all genotypes and sowing conditions, while 500 ppm TU resulted in a moderate decline. In terms of lipid peroxidation, measured as MDA content, the highest level was found in late sown RKD-18 under control (0.36 µmol min−1 g−1 FW), indicating severe oxidative stress. In contrast, the lowest MDA level was observed in normal sown RCr-728 under 100 ppm TSA (0.10 µmol min−1 g−1 FW), reflecting enhanced adaptations against high temperature. Application of TSA (100 ppm) significantly reduced MDA content across all treatments, while TU (500 ppm) showed only moderate improvement compared to the control (Fig. 1e).

Antioxidant activity

DPPH radical scavenging activity

Under normal sowing conditions, DPPH IC₅₀ values were lowest with 100 ppm TSA (47.98 µg/ml in RCr-728 and 48.43 µg/ml in RKD-18), indicating higher antioxidant activity compared to control. Under late sowing conditions, control plants of both varieties exhibited higher IC₅₀ values (52.03–53.21 µg/ml), reflecting reduced antioxidant potential under high temperature stress. Thiosalicylic acid treatment maintained relatively lower IC₅₀ values even under late sown conditions (51.25–51.35 µg/ml), suggesting better radical scavenging efficiency compared to other treatments (Fig. 1f).

ABTS radical scavenging capacity

The ABTS assay showed that 100 ppm TSA significantly enhanced antioxidant capacity in all genotypes. The maximum ABTS value was recorded in normal sown RKD-18 treated with TSA (56.9 µmol TE/g), followed by late sown RKD-18 (56.1 µmol TE/g) and normal sown RCr-728 (55.3 µmol TE/g). The lowest ABTS activity was recorded in late sown RCr-728 under control (52.9 µmol TE/g) (Fig. 1g). Statistical groupings indicate significant improvement in ABTS activity with TSA and SA compared to control and TU.

Ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP)

The FRAP values followed a similar pattern, with the highest ferric reducing capacity in normal sown RKD-18 with 100 ppm TSA (5.89 mg TE/g), statistically higher than other treatments (p < 0.05). Late-sown RCr-728 consistently exhibited the lowest FRAP values across treatments, with the lowest under control (3.11 mg TE/g) (Fig. 1h). Thiosalicylic acid significantly increased FRAP in all conditions, while TU showed either marginal or no improvement.

Antioxidative enzymes

The activities of antioxidative enzymes SOD, CAT, and POD were quantitatively evaluated in RCr-728 and RKD-18 genotypes under normal and late sowing conditions. Statistical analysis revealed significant differences (p < 0.05) among treatments and genotypes for all three enzymes. Under normal conditions, enzyme activities were markedly enhanced by growth regulator treatments. Superoxide dismutase activity was highest in RKD-18 with 100 ppm TSA (0.41 ± 0.01 µmol min−1 g−1 FW), significantly exceeding its control (0.28 ± 0.01 µmol min−1 g−1 FW) and RCr-728 (Fig. 1i). Catalase activity peaked under 100 ppm TSA, reaching 0.45 ± 0.005 µmol min−1 g−1 FW in RCr-728 and 0.42 ± 0.01 µmol min−1 g−1 FW in RKD-18 (Fig. 1j), while POD activity was similarly maximized with TSA (0.3 ± 0.01 and 0.29 ± 0.01 µmol min−1 g−1 FW in RCr-728 and RKD-18, respectively). Overall, RKD-18 exhibited a stronger antioxidative response, particularly in terms of SOD activity, while TSA proved more effective for CAT and POD enhancement.

In contrast, late sowing significantly reduced (p < 0.05) all enzymatic activities, reflecting the adverse effects of high temperature exposure on the antioxidative defense system. Superoxide dismutase activity in RKD-18 declined to 0.20 ± 0.01 µmol min−1 g−1 FW under control conditions and increased only up to 0.35 ± 0.01 µmol min−1 g−1 FW with TSA, remaining lower than normal conditions. Similarly, CAT activity under TSA reached only 0.45 ± 0.01 µmol min−1 g−1 FW in RCr-728 and 0.41 ± 0.01 µmol min−1 g−1 FW in RKD-18. Under late sowing, POD activity peaked with 100 ppm TSA (0.36 and 0.37 µmol min−1 g−1 FW in RCr-728 and RKD-18), while control showed the lowest values (Fig. 1k). TU treatment resulted in comparatively lower activity. These results suggest that although SA and TSA partially mitigate oxidative damage, their effectiveness is constrained under higher temperature stress. Furthermore, application of 500 ppm TU resulted in a consistent reduction in enzyme activities across both genotypes and conditions, indicating possible phytotoxic effects or disruption of redox balance at higher concentrations.

Correlation analysis

The correlation matrix provided valuable insights into the intricate biochemical relationships governing coriander performance under high temperature. Essential oil content (EO), a key economic trait, demonstrated a strong positive association with overall antioxidant capacity, as reflected by its significant correlations with FRAP (r = 0.80), ABTS (r = 0.67), and the antioxidant enzyme POD (r = 0.52). Conversely, EO was negatively correlated with malondialdehyde (MDA; r = − 0.63), a marker of lipid peroxidation. This negative association highlights that reduced oxidative membrane damage, coupled with elevated antioxidant activity, creates a biochemical environment favorable for EO biosynthesis and accumulation. Non-enzymatic antioxidants, particularly total phenolics, emerged as central contributors to heat stress mitigation. Their strong correlation with FRAP (r = 0.96) and ABTS (r = 0.79) underscores their pivotal role in neutralizing reactive oxygen species (ROS) and maintaining redox balance (Fig. 2). Similarly, enzymatic antioxidants, SOD, CAT, and POD, showed positive interrelationships, reflecting their coordinated function in detoxifying superoxide radicals and hydrogen peroxide. Their negative correlations with MDA further emphasized their role in safeguarding cellular structures from oxidative injury. These findings suggest that the interplay between enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidant systems not only reduces oxidative stress but also indirectly enhances essential oil biosynthesis. Thiosalicylic acid application, particularly in the heat-resilient genotype RKD-18, likely strengthened these synergistic relationships. This biochemical reinforcement translated into greater antioxidant resilience and higher EO production, positioning TSA as a potent regulator of coriander adaptive responses under high-temperature stress.

Discussion

This study provides compelling evidence that genotype, sowing environment, and the exogenous application of PGRs collectively shape EO biosynthesis and antioxidant defense strategies in coriander. The consistent superiority of RKD-18 over RCr-728, particularly under late-sown conditions, highlights its inherent genetic capacity to sustain secondary metabolism during thermal stress. The observed increase in essential oil content, particularly from 0.25% under late sown conditions to 0.40% with TSA application in RKD-18, is agronomically significant, as essential oil content is a key quality trait directly influencing the market value and commercial utility of coriander. Such genotypic variability in EO accumulation echoes earlier reports on differential metabolic resilience of coriander genotypes exposed to abiotic stress21,22, underscoring the importance of genotype selection for stress-prone agroclimates. The superior performance of RKD-18 may be partly attributed to its earlier maturity, which likely enables the plant to escape high temperature stress by completing critical developmental stages before the onset of high temperature.

Among the PGRs tested, TSA at 100 ppm was particularly effective, markedly enhancing EO yield across both sowing environments. This enhancement may be attributed to TSA’s capacity to stimulate terpenoid biosynthetic pathways, potentially by modulating key enzymatic steps in monoterpene and sesquiterpene formation23. Kurmi et al.24, found that foliar application of GA₃ (50 ppm) recorded maximum essential oil content (~ 0.51%) in coriander seeds and higher concentration of thiourea (500 ppm) reduced essential oil content in coriander compared to lower doses, indicating a concentration-dependent inhibitory effect25. At elevated levels, thiourea may disrupt metabolic balance and divert assimilates away from secondary metabolite pathways, thereby lowering essential oil biosynthesis. Kaya et al.26,. These contrasting outcomes reinforce the principle that PGR efficacy is concentration-dependent and context-specific, requiring precise optimization for agronomic use. The contrasting responses between the genotypes indicate inherent differences in metabolic efficiency, antioxidant capacity, and responsiveness to PGR treatments. Heat stress triggered by late sowing profoundly disturbed redox homeostasis, as evidenced by elevated MDA and proline levels. MDA accumulation, particularly pronounced in the untreated late-sown RCr-728 plants, reflected severe lipid peroxidation and membrane destabilization. Thiosalicylic acid application significantly mitigated this effect by reducing MDA accumulation across genotypes, indicating improved oxidative balance and stronger membrane protection. Pazoki et al., 2022 found that foliar application of salicylic acid (100–200 ppm) significantly increased proline accumulation and reduced MDA content compared to control under drought stress, indicating improved oxidative stress tolerance. These findings align with previous reports of salicylic acid derivatives enhancing antioxidant capacity and limiting lipid peroxidation under abiotic stress27,28. Proline accumulation, another hallmark of stress adaptation, increased significantly under TSA treatment, with RKD-18 showing the most pronounced response in late sowing. The concurrent reduction in MDA levels and elevation in proline suggests that TSA not only promotes proline biosynthesis, possibly via upregulation of Δ¹-pyrroline-5-carboxylate synthetase (P5CS), but may also suppress its catabolism29. This dual action enhances osmotic adjustment, stabilizes cellular proteins and membranes, and contributes to ROS detoxification, further supporting the role of TSA in strengthening cellular defense mechanisms under heat stress.

Thiosalicylic acid markedly promoted the accumulation of non-enzymatic antioxidants, including phenolics and flavonoids, particularly in RKD-18 under high-temperature stress. These compounds, synthesized via the phenylpropanoid pathway, play a crucial role in ROS scavenging and cellular redox homeostasis. The enhanced accumulation under TSA treatment suggests activation of phenylalanine ammonia-lyase, a key regulatory enzyme of this pathway, which is commonly induced by salicylic acid and its analogs30. This response indicates a significant involvement of the phenylpropanoid pathway in conferring stress tolerance. Furthermore, as a structural analog of salicylic acid, thiosalicylic acid likely promotes phenolic and flavonoid biosynthesis through similar regulatory mechanisms, consistent with reports of increased accumulation at 100–200 ppm salicylic acid in coriander31. In parallel, the observed increase in essential oil content further suggests stimulation of terpenoid biosynthesis, highlighting the coordinated regulation of secondary metabolic pathways under TSA treatment. The observed 30–40% increase in total phenolics and flavonoids in TSA-treated plants, compared to control and TU treatments, confirms TSA potency in strengthening non-enzymatic antioxidant defense. Antioxidant activity assays further validated these biochemical improvements. Lower DPPH IC₅₀ values and higher ABTS and FRAP activities under TSA treatment indicate improved radical scavenging and reducing power. In contrast, increased IC₅₀ values under late sowing confirm that high temperature impairs antioxidant potential due to enhanced oxidative stress. This finding is consistent with Ali et al.32,, reported that salicylic acid enhances antioxidant capacity in coriander by improving DPPH radical scavenging and overall reducing power. By contrast, DPPH activity declined under TU, especially in late-sown RCr-728, indicating impaired hydrogen-donating capacity and weakened ROS neutralization33. This response likely reflects the assay-specific nature of DPPH, which detects hydrogen-donating antioxidants such as phenolics and flavonoids, suggesting a shift in antioxidant composition rather than a reduction in overall antioxidant capacity.

Thiosalicylic acid significantly enhanced the activities of key antioxidative enzymes, including SOD, CAT and POD under normal conditions. These enzymes constitute the primary defense against ROS, where SOD catalyses the dismutation of superoxide radicals into hydrogen peroxide, which is subsequently detoxified by CAT and POD34,35. The higher enzyme activities observed under TSA treatment indicate an efficient detoxification system and improved cellular protection. The comparatively stronger SOD response in RKD-18 further supports its superior oxidative stress tolerance. Salicylic acid is known to regulate antioxidant gene expression36, and TSA likely exerts similar or even stronger regulatory effects, thereby playing a critical role in regulating antioxidant enzymes and maintaining redox homeostasis under high temperature stress. Similar enhancement in antioxidant defense and reduction in oxidative damage has been reported in canola following exogenous application of signalling molecules such as melatonin and ascorbic acid under salt stress conditions37. However, late sowing conditions resulted in a marked decline in enzymatic activities across all treatments, highlighting the detrimental impact of high temperature stress on the antioxidative machinery. Although TSA and salicylic acid treatments partially alleviated this decline, enzyme activities remained lower than those observed under normal conditions, suggesting that severe or prolonged heat stress may overwhelm the defense system38. Interestingly, TU at 500 ppm consistently reduced both antioxidant activity and enzyme levels, may disrupt cellular redox balance or interfere with metabolic processes, leading to reduced efficiency of both enzymatic and non-enzymatic defense systems.

The superior performance of RKD-18 indicates its potential as inherent capacity to withstand the high temperature conditions, while TSA application emerges as an effective strategy to mitigate high temperature induced oxidative damage through coordinated regulation of antioxidant systems. Thus, combining genotype like RKD-18 with targeted PGR application represents a practical strategy to sustain coriander productivity and quality under elevated temperature conditions. While the present study offers valuable insights into the role of TSA in regulating antioxidant responses and secondary metabolism under heat stress, some limitations should be considered. The results are based on a single location and growing season, and the lack of controlled environment and yield-related data may limit broader applicability. Despite this, the consistent improvement in antioxidant capacity and essential oil accumulation suggests that TSA could serve as a practical field-level strategy for managing exposure of high temperature in coriander. Future work should focus on validation across multiple environments, along with controlled studies and yield assessments, to better establish its agronomic relevance and potential for integration into crop improvement programs.

Conclusion

Genotype, sowing environment, and plant growth regulator application jointly modulate metabolic resilience in coriander under high-temperature conditions. RKD-18 outperformed RCr-728, indicating a stronger genetic capacity to sustain essential oil biosynthesis and antioxidant defense. Application of thiosalicylic acid at 100 ppm was most effective in improving essential oil yield, osmolyte accumulation, and antioxidant activity, thereby maintaining redox balance and limiting membrane damage, whereas a higher concentration of thiourea 500 ppm was detrimental. The positive association between essential oil yield and antioxidant systems highlights the importance of an efficient antioxidative defense mechanism under exposure of high temperature. These findings support the use of optimized growth regulator strategies to improve coriander performance in heat-prone environments and provide a basis for enhancing crop resilience under changing climatic conditions.

Future perspectives

Future research should prioritize multi-location and multi-season validation to ensure the broader applicability of the findings under diverse agro-climatic conditions. Further investigations are needed to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying TSA-mediated responses, particularly the regulation of key genes involved in terpenoid and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis as well as antioxidant defense pathways. Additionally, integrating biochemical traits with agronomic performance, including yield and quality parameters, will provide a more comprehensive understanding of stress adaptation. The incorporation of TSA-based strategies into breeding and crop improvement programs may facilitate the development of climate-resilient coriander cultivars suited for high-temperature environments.
