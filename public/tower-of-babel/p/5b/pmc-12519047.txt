Experiences and barriers to alcohol use disorder treatment among adults with and without self‐reported executive functioning challenges: A mixed‐methods study
Authors: Cayley Russell, Jessica L Wiese, Anthony C Ruocco, Nikki Bozinoff, Raul Gonzalez, Sheila Jennings, Marco Leyton, Yuliya S Nikolova, Josh Orson, Marc N Potenza, Lena C Quilty, Kevin D Shield, Antonio Verdejo‐Garcia, Samantha Wells, Shannon Lange, The CDiA Program Study Group
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExecutive functioning (EF)—the ability to plan, organize, and complete goal‐directed tasks—plays a critical role in the onset and course of alcohol use disorder (AUD). Individuals with AUD often report challenges in EF domains, such as initiating, planning, and performing key tasks, including seeking, engaging in, and adhering to treatment. While AUD treatment efficacy is well‐established, little is known about individuals' lived experiences with AUD treatment, especially among those with EF challenges. To date, no studies have explored how self‐reported EF challenges shape treatment‐seeking experiences in people with AUD using a mixed‐methods approach. This study addresses that gap.MethodsWe conducted a mixed‐methods study involving 30 adults seeking AUD treatment between June 2022 and June 2023. This work was part of a broader research program examining cognitive functioning in addictions within a mental health and addictions hospital in Toronto, Canada. Data collection included semistructured qualitative interviews and a standardized self‐administered EF questionnaire. Data were integrated, analyzed thematically, and narratively synthesized.ResultsApproximately half of participants (53%) met the threshold for EF challenges. Many described multiple prior treatment attempts, a delayed recognition of problematic alcohol use, and an incongruence between treatment expectations and experiences. Those with EF challenges described distinct barriers to care and expressed a need for additional supports, including access to psychotherapy, clearer treatment pathways and timelines, and more proactive communication and follow‐up from healthcare providers.ConclusionsIndividuals with AUD often experience co‐occurring mental health and EF challenges that may affect their motivation to seek treatment and shape treatment experiences and trajectories. Tailored treatment approaches that address EF challenges through enhanced support, structure, and provider communication may improve treatment engagement and outcomes among this population.Keywords: alcohol treatment, alcohol use disorder, executive functioning, impulsive behavior

INTRODUCTION

Treatment uptake for alcohol use disorder (AUD) remains exceptionally low. In the United States, less than 5% of adults with past‐year AUD accessed treatment in 2021 (National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism [NIAAA], 2023; World Health Organization [WHO], 2024). Treatment rates are similarly low in Canada (Canadian Centre on Substance Use and Addiction [CCSA], 2021). Notably, pharmacotherapy for AUD is particularly underutilized in both countries (Konrad et al., 2021; Tourchian et al., 2024).

While the efficacy of both pharmacological and behavioral interventions for improving AUD treatment outcomes is well‐established, limited research has explored individuals' experiences with these treatment approaches, including how such experiences influence treatment uptake, engagement, and success (Agabio et al., 2024; Flanagan et al., 2018). Traditional AUD treatment research has primarily focused on quantitative measures, such as relapse rates, treatment retention, and the effectiveness of pharmacological interventions (Kranzler & Soyka, 2018; Maisel et al., 2013). Although such quantitative approaches provide valuable information, they often fail to capture the lived experiences of individuals navigating treatment for AUD. This limitation is particularly consequential for individuals with executive functioning (EF) impairments, who may face distinct challenges in accessing, engaging with, and benefiting from treatment for AUD.

EF refers to a set of cognitive processes that support goal‐directed behavior, including the ability to plan, organize, initiate, and sustain actions toward achieving future outcomes (Miyake et al., 2000). Differences in these abilities affect one's capacity to manage daily life tasks and adapt to changing circumstances (Ramey & Regier, 2019). For individuals with EF impairments, these cognitive challenges can interfere with key aspects of treatment engagement and recovery for AUD, compounding other structural and psychosocial barriers they may already face.

Research estimates that between 30% and 80% of individuals seeking treatment for AUD display clinically significant EF impairments upon treatment entry (Bruijnen et al., 2019). These impairments are thought to play an important role in both the onset and trajectory of AUD (Carvalho et al., 2019; Domínguez‐Salas et al., 2016), influencing alcohol use initiation and increasing the risk of advancing to problematic drinking patterns and related harms (Belin et al., 2008; Kozak et al., 2019). Individuals with EF challenges are more likely than those without such challenges to initiate alcohol use earlier and develop AUD (Spychala et al., 2024). Moreover, chronic alcohol use can cause physiological and structural changes to the brain, further compromising EF domains such as impulse control, cognitive flexibility (e.g., set‐shifting, reversal learning), and other neuropsychological functions, including spatial processing, perceptual speed, selective attention, response inhibition, and information updating (Maharjan et al., 2022; Stephan et al., 2017). EF challenges are also associated with poorer AUD prognosis, including a higher likelihood of co‐occurring impulse control disorders (Koudys & Ruocco, 2020; Volkow et al., 2019). These cognitive challenges can make it more difficult for individuals to seek treatment, navigate complex health systems, and engage in treatment protocols and tasks (Fama et al., 2021). Participating in treatment often requires learning new skills, processing unfamiliar information, and performing cognitively demanding tasks such as initiating steps toward recovery, sustaining attention, regulating behavior, and updating working memory (Copersino et al., 2012).

Empirical evidence links EF challenges to worse AUD treatment outcomes, including lower adherence and increased relapse risk (Andersson et al., 2019; Domínguez‐Salas et al., 2016). In particular, deficits related to impulsivity, such as difficulties delaying gratification, are strongly associated with both relapse and greater alcohol‐related harm (Courtney et al., 2012; Reyes‐Huerta et al., 2018; Sliedrecht et al., 2020). Moreover, EF challenges can diminish insight and self‐awareness, making individuals less likely to recognize the severity of their alcohol use problems, and consequently, less likely to seek or engage in treatment (Bruijnen et al., 2019; Mojtabai et al., 2002; Priester et al., 2016; Probst et al., 2015).

To date, no studies have explored how self‐reported EF difficulties intersect with lived experiences of accessing AUD treatment using a mixed‐method approach (Government of Canada, 2021; Wilcox et al., 2014). This study combined qualitative interviews with a standardized EF self‐report survey among a sample of treatment‐seeking adults with AUD to provide a more comprehensive understanding of how EF challenges influence treatment engagement and barriers. The findings aim to inform the development of more effective interventions and improved treatment systems. To achieve this, this study had three primary objectives: (1) to explore general treatment experiences among individuals seeking treatment for AUD; (2) to identify specific barriers and challenges faced by those with AUD and EF challenges; and (3) to generate recommendations for improving AUD treatment for individuals with EF challenges.

METHODS

Study design and objectives

This study is part of a longitudinal research program entitled Cognitive Dysfunction in the Addictions (CDiA), consisting of several interrelated projects examining cognitive dysfunction in people seeking substance use treatment at the Centre for Addiction and Mental Health (CAMH) in Toronto, Ontario (Nikolova et al., 2025). This research program includes a Lived Experience Research Advisory Committee (LERA) comprised of people with experience with substance use who share expertise and collaborate on research projects. Members of the LERA were involved in the development of this study's qualitative interview guide, the interpretation of the results, and drafting the article.

The current substudy employed a pragmatic theoretical framework, which posits that social problems are best understood and defined by those who experience them directly (Kaushik & Walsh, 2019). Guided by this perspective, this study centers the lived experiences of individuals with AUD and EF challenges in accessing and engaging with treatment services, ensuring our recommendations are grounded in real‐world experiences.

Setting

CAMH offers a broad range of low‐barrier substance use and mental health treatment services delivered by multidisciplinary teams that include addiction physicians, nurse practitioners, psychiatrists, psychologists, pharmacists, and social workers. Services are accessible via self‐referral or referral from another healthcare provider.

For individuals with AUD, care begins with an initial assessment, which usually includes evidence‐based pharmacotherapy, internal referrals to group‐based psychotherapy (e.g., cognitive behavioral therapy), and/or linkage to external healthcare or community supports, based on client needs and preferences. Unlike many community‐based or rural/remote settings, where access to prescribers may be limited and treatment may emphasize psychoeducation or psychological interventions, CAMH offers integrated, specialist‐led care pathways that combine pharmacological and psychological interventions.

Participant recruitment and eligibility

Participants were recruited from the parent CDiA study through two main sources: (1) a CAMH research repository containing contact information for individuals who had expressed interest in research opportunities, and (2) direct referral from the individual's CAMH addiction physician or clinician.

Eligibility for the parent CDiA study included adults aged 18–60 years who met criteria for past‐year AUD or substance use disorder (SUD) and who were either seeking or receiving addiction treatment. Individuals over the age of 60 were excluded due to the increased likelihood of age‐related cognitive impairments, such as dementia, which may confound the relationship between EF and AUD treatment experiences.

All CDiA participants completed a comprehensive baseline assessment including clinical, cognitive, and biological measures, with a follow‐up one year later. For this study, we included only those participants from the parent study who met criteria for past‐year AUD.

Data collection and measures

From June 1, 2022, to June 22, 2022, we conducted in‐depth, semistructured interviews by phone, virtually, or in person. Prior to the interview, all participants provided informed consent, conducted through an interviewer‐administered process using Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap), a secure web‐based data collection software (Harris et al., 2019). Following the consent process, participants immediately completed an interviewer‐administered survey, also conducted through REDCap, collecting sociodemographic information including age, sex, gender, race, ethnicity, highest level of education, and current housing status. Additional variables on AUD severity, concurrent SUDs, past two‐month substance use, and lifetime mental health diagnoses were ascertained from the parent CDiA study at the reporting stage (Nikolova et al., 2025). Each interview lasted approximately 45–60 min and participants received a $50 CAD electronic gift card of their choice. Data collection continued until data saturation was reached and no new information emerged.

Qualitative interview

The qualitative interview guide was developed in collaboration with the LERA committee and a clinical psychologist specialized in neuropsychology. It included open‐ended questions exploring participants' experiences seeking AUD treatment—both at CAMH and more broadly over their lifetime. The guide also addressed perceived facilitators and barriers to treatment, as well as suggestions for improvements. EF difficulties were assessed post‐interview, thus blinding the interviewer to this knowledge and minimizing interviewer bias.

EF assessment

Following the interview, participants were sent a follow‐up email to complete the Behavior Rating Inventory for Executive Functioning in Adults (BRIEF‐A), a validated, self‐report measure of EF (Roth & Gioia, 2005), which has been endorsed for clinical assessment of individuals with AUD (Hagen et al., 2016).

The BRIEF‐A is comprised of 75 3‐point Likert‐scale items asking about personality and behavior within nine mutually exclusive scales measuring EF, as well as two composite index scales, (Behavioral Regulation [BRI] and Metacognition [MI]) (Roth et al., 2022; Roth & Gioia, 2005). BRI measures an individual's overall tendency to regulate behavior and emotional responses, and includes the following scales: inhibition, shift, emotional control, and self‐monitoring. MI describes the individual's tendency to solve problems efficiently by planning and organizing, and includes the following scales: initiation, working memory, plan/organize, task monitoring, and organization of materials. The overall score from every scale is combined and averaged into one total summary scale measuring overall EF (Global Executive Composite [GEC]). Raw scores on each scale are transformed into T scores (M = 50, SD = 10), which provide information about an individual's scores relative to the scores of respondents in a standardized sample, used to interpret the individual's level of EF. The normative standardization sample included US‐based men and women aged 18–90 years from a wide range of ethnic and educational backgrounds and geographic regions, matched to 2002 US census data. Percentiles represent the percentage of adults in the standardization sample who fall below a given raw score. Higher scores indicate greater EF challenges, with T‐scores at ≥65 considered clinically significant (Roth & Gioia, 2005).

The online BRIEF‐A self‐assessment was administered through Psychological Assessment Resources (PAR) Inc. (2020) remote administration service, PARiConnect. For each participant, the online system produces an interpretative summary report, raw scores, and an overall GEC summary score.

Data management and analysis

Data from the sociodemographic survey and BRIEF‐A were exported and analyzed using descriptive statistics. All interviews were audio‐recorded and transcribed by a third‐party service. Following transcription, all personally identifying information was removed, and audio recordings were destroyed.

Interview transcripts were analyzed using NVivo qualitative software (QSR International Pty Ltd., 2018). A hybrid thematic analysis approach was employed, combining both deductive and inductive coding, in accordance with established methods (Braun & Clarke, 2013; Xu & Zammit, 2020). The first author (CR) developed an initial codebook based on the study objectives, interview guide, and analytic memos, which included broad “parent codes” (e.g., reasons for seeking treatment). CR then applied the coding framework iteratively to each transcript, revising codes as necessary and identifying key phrases, words, emotions, and sentiments (e.g., “hitting rock bottom”) following an inductive process. This allowed for the development of more detailed “child” codes nested within parent codes, further contextualizing emerging themes. Codes and themes were discussed regularly within the research team, and the final codebook was applied across all transcripts. To increase credibility, a second coder (JLW) independently coded a subsample of transcripts. Discrepancies were discussed and resolved collaboratively. Transcripts were reviewed and themes refined until they sufficiently reflected the dataset (LaDonna et al., 2021).

Next, transcripts were stratified by participants' GEC scores, dividing them into two groups based on whether their scores were above or below the T‐score cut‐off of 65, indicating the presence or absence of self‐reported EF challenges. Both CR and JLW independently conducted crosstab queries within NVivo using this stratification to examine themes related to treatment challenges and barriers among participants with and without EF challenges.

To further enhance the credibility and validity of our findings, we conducted a member checking exercise with the LERA committee. We presented our preliminary findings to assess how well they aligned with the committee's lived experiences and to identify any potential “negative cases” or instances where our findings differed from their experiences (McKim, 2023). The LERA committee provided feedback that validated our results and provided additional insights for consideration, which were incorporated into the discussion.

Ethics and qualitative reporting checklist

The CDiA program and present study were both approved by CAMH Research Ethics Board (REB) (#2021/108).

RESULTS

Participant characteristics

Seventy‐nine participants were referred from the CDiA program to the current study. Of these, 20 were excluded because they were receiving treatment for substances other than alcohol, and 29 either declined to participate or could not be reached. This resulted in a final sample of 30 participants. Most interviews were conducted over the phone (n = 26; 87%), with the remainder occurring virtually (n = 2; 7%) or in person (n = 2; 7%).

Sociodemographic characteristics

Participants ranged in age from 24 to 59 years (M = 44.4 years; SD ± 9.82) and the majority self‐identified as men (57%). Most identified as straight/heterosexual (87%) and White (77%). Over half (62%) had completed college or university, and most (53%) resided in a rental property on most days within the past 30 days.

The majority of participants (83%) met criteria for severe AUD, with smaller proportions classified as moderate (10%) or mild (7%). Concurrent SUDs were present in 33% of participants and included cannabis use disorder (17%), cocaine use disorder (10%), stimulant use disorder (3%), and sedative use disorder (3%). In the past two months, half of participants (50%) reported cannabis use, followed by sedatives (16%) and stimulants (13%).

Mental health concerns were common among the sample, with 57% reporting a lifetime diagnosis of major depressive disorder, 40% reporting generalized anxiety disorder, and 30% having a current diagnosis of major depressive disorder. Posttraumatic stress disorder was also reported by 18%, and fewer participants had been diagnosed with bipolar I (7%) or bipolar II disorder (3%). See Table 1 for full sample characteristics.

Qualitative results

Our qualitative findings are organized into three overarching categories aligned with our research objectives, each containing specific subthemes: (1) Treatment Experiences among Individuals Seeking Treatment for AUD; (2) Treatment Experiences and Barriers among Individuals with EF Challenges Seeking Treatment for AUD; and (3) Treatment Needs: Recommendations to Support Individuals with EF Challenges Seeking Treatment for AUD.

Treatment experiences among individuals seeking treatment for AUD

Dual diagnoses: difficulties with treatment initiation and the role of concurrent mental health challenges

Most participants reported an early onset of problematic drinking behaviors, with many beginning in their early teenage years. Over time, their drinking frequency and volume increased, eventually leading them to seek treatment. Many described a cyclical pattern of abstinence and relapse, while others noted a recent intensification in their drinking habits or the experience of “hitting rock bottom,” which prompted them to seek treatment:

From the first time I was able to legally drink, there was a problem, from day one. Basically, if I start drinking and I have a quantity of alcohol in front of me, it's difficult for me not to just compulsively go through all of it. I didn't sort of hit a rock bottom moment, until I got to 23 or 24. And that's when my drinking was heaviest. (Participant 087; GEC 65)

Many participants described a personal lack of recognition of their drinking behavior as problematic, often discounting the severity of their drinking and indicating that they had been in denial for a long time. For instance, when asked why they had not sought treatment before, one participant described the following:

I guess I was sort of caught between roles for a while, you know? Like not wanting to admit that I had a problem with alcohol, but simultaneously being aware that I was losing control more and more each time I drank. So I guess, initially […]I wasn't ready to sort of face it yet. (Participant 087; GEC 65)

Additionally, most participants reported having concurrent mental health diagnoses, including lifetime and current major depressive disorder, generalized anxiety disorder, bipolar disorder, post‐traumatic stress disorder, and attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder. Several also disclosed experiences of suicidal ideation. Participants described a complex relationship between their mental health and alcohol use, often using alcohol to cope with symptoms of anxiety or depression. However, this self‐medication often led to a negative feedback loop in which increased alcohol consumption ultimately worsened their mental health: “It's the chicken and the egg, right? Did mental health problems cause the alcoholism, or did the alcoholism cause the mental health problems?” (Participant 108; GEC 75).

For many participants, mental health treatment needs commonly superseded drinking concerns, and it was not until they presented to treatment for their mental health that they ended up being diagnosed with co‐occurring AUD. As a result, alcohol use was often identified and addressed only later in their treatment journey:

I went to CAMH for depression and anxiety a couple of years ago, and it wasn't for addiction, but I was prescribed a medication by a doctor there that would help me with addiction. So I didn't go there on that path, but I guess it kind of morphed into that, but that wasn't the reason for me being there. (Participant 019; GEC 64)

For this reason, participants often experienced lengthy and disjointed treatment trajectories as they had to navigate several referrals to various departments and/or clinicians before receiving appropriate treatment for both diagnoses:

My big problem was it took a lot to get a grip on, and to understand [the dual diagnoses], and there was a big runaround for me. It took, three different psychiatrists, lots of different referrals. I was doing all the thinking, all the figuring out, of what my problem was, what I do next, what my next steps are. It was very difficult and it took, like, two years to figure out that I had a problem and to advocate for myself and just get bounced along and have different interactions with multiple psychiatrists. (Participant 092; GEC 78)

In many cases, participants did not initially recognize their drinking as problematic, as it was often masked or overshadowed by more immediate or distressing mental health symptoms. This tendency to prioritize mental health treatment may have influenced the direction, timing, and coordination of their overall care.

Expecting psychotherapy, receiving pharmacotherapy: misalignment between expectations and experiences

The combined impact of participants' co‐occurring mental health and AUD diagnoses often led to a misalignment between the type and level of treatment that was offered and received, versus what they desired or expected. Indeed, many participants expressed a desire for psychotherapy, and some directly communicated this preference to their clinicians. However, participants noted that pharmacotherapy was often emphasized as the first‐line treatment. While some acknowledged that medication was beneficial, many felt that it was offered at the expense of other treatment options:

The first doctor was kind of pushing [medication] on me and the second one was kind of questioning why I wouldn't want to go that route. It's like they didn't take me seriously… they just kept sort of selling me those options of medication…even after the basic diagnoses that they gave me […] that wasn't necessarily what I was expecting as a resource to be offered. (Participant 147; GEC 81)

Despite pharmacotherapy being an evidence‐based treatment, participants largely perceived the lack of equal emphasis on psychotherapy as a push for medication. In some cases, it was also perceived as an invalidation of their concerns or a misunderstanding of the type of support they wanted:

With this one doctor, I feel like it's just constantly medication talk, it's not really about me and how I'm feeling and how I'm doing. It's just like, “Well this med is working” and whatnot…I feel like I'm a number, not like I mattered…I've been asking for a couple of years now to have someone to talk to weekly kind of thing…I guess for the doctors to take their time with me and really listen to what I need. (Participant 182; GEC 84)

For many participants, pharmacotherapy addressed only one aspect of their concurrent diagnoses. As a result, some considered the medications ineffective or even disruptive, particularly when they experienced adverse effects. These experiences led some to cycle through multiple medications or eventually discontinue them, which deterred further treatment seeking:

I had some reactions when I was taking naltrexone. I tried that a number of times and every time I tried it, I had side effects. Mostly headache, nausea type of thing, you know. It wasn't working; every time I tried it, I was getting sick. It wasn't helping me at all […] Eventually, I kind of stopped going. (Participant 077; GEC 47)

Although some participants found medications helpful, most strongly expressed a desire for access to psychotherapy. They felt they would have significantly benefited from any form of therapy, whether one‐on‐one or group, and were open to various formats, including virtual, in‐person, or hybrid:

I just feel like in general, I can use more access to in‐person support. Just like all kinds of talk therapy, whether in individual situations or group scenarios […] Counselling. I think that's missing there […] Like I guess I realized that after the first time I went, and then we discussed meds, and all that's great. And then I was kind of like, oh, and then that was it. So it's like, okay, I thought I was coming here for like some sort of therapy‐based discussions and stuff. (Participant 087; GEC 65)

Many participants emphasized that addressing the root causes of their AUD and mental health concerns required more frequent and longer‐term psychotherapy, alongside a more patient‐centered approach. This need for more comprehensive care was especially crucial for those with co‐occurring diagnoses, as it was closely tied to the treatment challenges and barriers they faced.

Treatment experiences and barriers among individuals with EF challenges seeking treatment for AUD

Delays and disruptions: how EF challenges impeded treatment initiation and success

Delays in initiating treatment were especially pronounced among participants with EF challenges. For instance, waitlists were identified as a significant obstacle, with some participants reporting delays of up to a year. Those with EF challenges emphasized how such delays were especially detrimental, in some instances describing waitlists as a matter of life or death:

I think this is generally well known, that waitlists are an issue…I've been to [publicly‐funded residential] treatment several times, it's always a six‐to‐twelve months waiting list. And I mean I had to go in immediately… so that was very challenging…If I need treatment right away, you know, I can't wait a year…I may not see that day…And it's pretty well‐known that it's very difficult to get into psychiatry, even if you're like a standing patient. Like at times I've had to wait three months for an appointment when in my opinion, it was like, near crisis level. (Participant 127; GEC 66)

Once engaged in treatment, many participants described having to wait extended periods between appointments. These delays were often compounded by perceived miscommunication and a lack of follow‐up. Those with EF challenges commonly described instances of being lost‐to‐follow‐up after initiating treatment. For example, one participant recounted:

So the first time…I gave them my number and they said that they would follow up and they never did…and the second time, they were like, oh, we see that you were brought in, like, a year or two ago, and then they said the problem was that they lost my number or something and that's why they never called me…And then the second time they were taking a long time to get back to me and I actually was really anxious about that […] they said they'd connect with me within a week and, like, two weeks go by, three weeks go by. So then finally I just called them myself. And then someone called me back and they were like, we're so sorry. Like, thank you for calling us. We're having difficulty. (Participant 082; GEC 68)

Participants with EF challenges frequently described attempting to access multiple types of treatment, only to be placed on waitlists at each, and then never contacted again. This lack of follow‐up left many feeling discouraged, hopeless, and confused. Several participants noted that it was often unclear who was responsible for initiating next steps, and when their efforts went unanswered, they tended to internalize the experience as a personal failure. One participant with EF challenges described the difficulty of navigating the system, even when actively advocating for themselves:

[System fragmentation] is a bit of an issue, and again, I don't know if it's me, or the institution? I don't know if the groups and that stuff is very limited? And I think it is, or if not, I'm just not somehow getting connected to it? Maybe I'm missing something? Or I'm not doing something? And I always, you know, I always try to be accountable and advocate for myself… but there seems to be something happening there, that I can't figure out… I find that's been challenging experience. (Participant 002; GEC 80)

Similarly, other participants with EF challenges expressed frustration with what they perceived as insufficient care, often attributed to a lack of provider follow‐up. Many reported difficulties navigating the treatment system and described feeling as though they had “fallen through the cracks.” Long wait times and poor follow‐up prolonged the process of initiating treatment—challenges that were further compounded by EF‐related difficulties, particularly involving time management. Participants with EF challenges suggested they struggle to prioritize treatment over other pressing concerns, even when recognizing its importance, often procrastinating and avoiding this task. These issues made it harder to initiate and follow through with treatment plans, attend appointments, or remain engaged in care. When asked whether their EF challenges influenced their treatment experiences, one participant stated:

I would say so because even getting to start treatment in general as a task, I'd put it off for a long time. And certain sort of things, like the waitlist, and having to go through different counsellors to talk about my experiences, they kind of like, you know, slowed me down and otherwise let me stop myself. (Participant 147; GEC 81)

Navigating the treatment system: the role of self‐advocacy among those with EF challenges

Participants with EF challenges often emphasized that navigating the treatment system required a high degree of self‐advocacy—a task with which they struggled due to difficulties initiating action, sustaining motivation, and following through. These cognitive barriers contributed to a disconnect between the treatment they expected versus what they received. For instance, one participant reflected on this discrepancy, suggesting it may have stemmed from challenges asserting their needs:

I suppose [there is a discrepancy]. I mean, I guess there have been times where I felt like maybe I needed to advocate for myself. You know, a little more strongly than I'm used to? In order to sort of get the attention that I wanted. (Participant 087; GEC 65)

Other participants with EF challenges described difficulties with attention, emotional regulation, and adapting to changes in treatment plans—all of which contributed to frustration and disengagement when expectations were unmet. Many associated these challenges with their mental health diagnoses, highlighting the interplay between mental health symptoms and cognitive functioning. Some explicitly linked their difficulties initiating tasks or advocating for themselves to negative treatment experiences. For instance, one participant described how EF challenges became especially pronounced during relapse, creating major barriers to reengaging in care:

Yes [my EF challenges have been a barrier]. Typically, like when I'm in a relapse phase, I have in the past, gone to the point where I can't even pick up the phone. I can't even like self‐initiate any work and treatment and I just get worse and worse. (Participant 127; GEC 66)

For others, challenges voicing their concerns or needs resulted in missed opportunities for appropriate care. Some participants reported difficulty seeking treatment, even after deciding they wanted help and feeling motivated to seek it:

Procrastinating [is definitely an issue], I guess knowing that after treatment had originally ended, even being ready to jump into treatment again, knowing that it's going to benefit me and knowing that it's something that I actually want to do, I'm still just putting it off. (Participant 147; GEC 81)

These accounts highlight the added burden placed on individuals with clinically significant EF challenges to self‐navigate a system that assumes a level of initiative and self‐advocacy many are unable to meet, ultimately resulting in gaps in care and unaddressed needs.

Treatment needs: recommendations to support individuals with EF challenges seeking treatment for AUD

Bridging the gaps: enhancing clarity, communication, and patient‐centered approaches

While many participants experienced challenges that impeded their ability to seek and benefit from treatment, most expressed overall positive treatment experiences. However, many also emphasized that improvements could be made to better meet their needs. Key suggestions focused on enhancing clarity and communication and providing more patient‐centered care, including long‐term and follow‐up support, as well as seamless connections between programs. One of the most common suggestions was the need for clearer communication: “We just need more resources and more accountability and more communication. Definitely more communication with follow‐up. Because I feel like people don't follow up as much as they should” (Participant 082; GEC 68).

Another frequent recommendation was the need to provide information about the types of treatment available, eligibility, and expected benefits, along with realistic timelines. For instance, one participant described how providing a tentative waitlist timeline could alleviate anxiety and improve engagement:

Just like having a timeline would be super helpful. Like ‘it's going to be a year’. Like, just knowing. I find for myself personally, it gives me more peace of mind. Even if that wait is going to be long. Having some sort of ballpark range of when I might expect to get in. (Participant 072; GEC 69)

Participants with EF challenges also suggested that frequent reminders and follow‐up communication would enable them to remember appointments, stay motivated, and remain engaged in treatment. For instance, one participant described how treatment reminders were especially helpful for managing time and staying on track:

Then I either get a text reminder or an email reminder so not only am I able to make sure I have it in my calendar, right away in that moment, but then I also get a reminder, or two reminders sometimes, depending on how far away the appointment is. So I found that really useful as well and a really effective way of making sure my addict brain, which isn't really at its top cognitive level, is able to have that reminder so that I stay on top of it. (Participant 213; GEC 65)

Overall, participants with EF challenges emphasized the need for a more hands‐on, patient‐centered approach to treatment that accounts for their difficulties in initiating tasks and managing time. One participant summarized this need, suggesting practical ways to better support individuals with EF challenges during the treatment process:

I wish that there was a way to sort of really, really simply, maybe, sentence by sentence, you get one reminder per day. Or maybe even sort of knowing where I physically stand in terms of, like, the waiting list. If there was a way to even know how many people or what the general timeline is. I know that might frustrate some people if they know that they're like, months away. But also at the same time, just, like, knowing where I lie between the groups. (Participant 147; GEC 81)

DISCUSSION

To our knowledge, this is the first study to employ a mixed‐methods approach to examine experiences and barriers among individuals seeking treatment for AUD in general, as well as the unique barriers faced by those with self‐reported EF challenges. Our findings indicate that individuals seeking treatment for AUD often present with multiple co‐occurring mental health disorders and needs. Notably, participants with EF challenges reported heightened difficulties navigating the treatment system, including difficulties with time management, procrastination, and self‐advocacy, suggesting that EF impairments may compound existing structural and systemic barriers to treatment.

The impacts of high mental health co‐morbidity among individuals with AUD

Nearly all participants, regardless of cognitive functioning, reported a co‐occurring mental health disorder. In many cases, participants initially sought treatment for mental health concerns prior to or instead of AUD, which may have contributed to fragmented and nonlinear treatment trajectories. Mental health issues were often prioritized over AUD, leading to delays in seeking and engaging in treatment for AUD. As several participants described, this pattern may be linked to the “masking” or “overshadowing” of AUD symptoms by more prominent or distressing psychiatric conditions (Bruijnen et al., 2019; Probst et al., 2015).

It may also reflect other factors—both internalized and systemic. Internalized self‐stigma, structural stigma within healthcare settings, and broader societal stigma related to both mental health and substance use often manifest as barriers to treatment (Hammarlund et al., 2018). AUD has consistently ranked among the most stigmatized health conditions globally; individuals with AUD are more likely to be blamed for their condition and to encounter structural discrimination than those with mental health disorders (Crapanzano et al., 2018; Schomerus et al., 2011). This is particularly evident within healthcare settings, where individuals with AUD frequently face overt and covert forms of prejudice and discrimination from providers (Kilian et al., 2021). Stigmatizing attitudes reinforce feelings of guilt and shame, which not only deter individuals from seeking treatment but also compromise treatment engagement and outcomes (Snoek et al., 2021). In comparison, mental illness is increasingly recognized as a legitimate health condition deserving of medical care and public support, while addictions—particularly AUD—are still frequently viewed through a moralistic lens (Morris & Schomerus, 2023). This disparity in societal perceptions may help explain why many participants in our study expressed greater comfort seeking treatment for mental health concerns rather than for AUD.

The disjointed treatment pathways experienced by participants may also reflect systemic limitations within mental health services in recognizing, managing, and treating co‐occurring AUD (Priester et al., 2016). Many clinicians feel underprepared to support individuals with complex, co‐occurring conditions due to insufficient addiction‐specific training and limited access to supervision or advice from experts with AUD clinical expertise (Howard & Holmshaw, 2010; Rose, 2019). As a result, individuals are frequently initiated on pharmacological treatment plans without—or with limited—concurrent psychosocial support. This treatment experience may reflect service treatment delivery models (e.g., referral to evidence‐based psychosocial treatments following physician assessment in specialized settings) and/or provider awareness and availability of resources (e.g., particularly in nonspecialized settings). This may partially explain participants' experiences of unmet expectations and delays in accessing psychotherapy, which they frequently described as their preferred or expected modality of care (Howard & Holmshaw, 2010; Rose, 2019).

Such service gaps are likely compounded by an overburdened health system, which remains inadequately resourced and ill‐equipped to deliver the intensity and diversity of support that patients may require, desire, or expect to receive. For instance, despite growing demand for more evidence‐based psychotherapy, many healthcare providers face increasing workloads leading to extended waitlists and heightened burnout, limiting the quality and availability of care (Canadian Mental Health Association [CMHA], 2022). These issues may reinforce the perception that healthcare providers are unable to devote sufficient time to patients and that treatment is emphasizing medication management more than multidisciplinary therapeutic models. Moreover, there are also important constraints within the publicly funded mental health system, such as limited provision of individual psychotherapy, which further restrict treatment options, and individuals' definitions and expectations of “treatment” may diverge significantly. While a detailed discussion of these definitional and systemic limitations is beyond the scope of this paper, they represent important contextual factors that shape treatment engagement and experiences.

Specific treatment experiences and barriers among people with AUD and EF challenges

In addition to the complexity of participants' co‐occurring mental health and AUD concerns, results from the BRIEF‐A indicated that a substantial proportion of participants reported clinically elevated EF difficulties. Our qualitative findings further demonstrate that individuals with AUD and EF challenges encounter distinct treatment experiences and barriers. These difficulties include navigating long wait times, limited provider follow‐up, persistent struggles with time management, and the need for ongoing self‐advocacy. Specific EF‐related impairments, particularly difficulties with task initiation and working memory, appear to negatively impact treatment‐seeking behaviors, engagement, and retention. Once participants with EF challenges did access treatment, several described deriving some benefit; however, many also identified significant barriers that hindered their ability to fully engage with and benefit from services (Mojtabai et al., 2002; Priester et al., 2016). A central barrier was difficulty initiating tasks, with several participants reporting low motivation or an inability to take the first step toward seeking help. Even after entering treatment, maintaining engagement and following through with appointments and self‐directed activities proved difficult. These issues were often compounded by broader systemic barriers such as limited communication from providers, extensive waitlists, and fragmented care pathways—factors reinforced by existing literature (Farhoudian et al., 2022; Priester et al., 2016).

Key recommendations for improving treatment for people with AUD and EF challenges

Participants with EF challenges provided several concrete suggestions to improve treatment accessibility and engagement for individuals with similar cognitive challenges. Many recommendations focused on addressing structural and programmatic barriers, particularly those related to communication with healthcare providers. A recurring suggestion was the implementation of multimodal appointment reminder systems, such as text messages, emails, and phone calls, which could help individuals manage their schedules more effectively. The use of text messaging is a growing practice that has been shown to improve treatment adherence and appointment attendance, while also promoting self‐efficacy and motivation – factors that are integral for individuals with EF limitations (Tofighi et al., 2017). Further, participants emphasized the need for more frequent and proactive communication from providers, including clear, step‐by‐step instructions outlining treatment processes and associated timelines. This approach may be especially useful for individuals facing multiple structural vulnerabilities, such as precarious housing or inconsistent access to phones or transportation, who may struggle to maintain contact or navigate fragmented systems.

Another key theme was the burden of self‐advocacy. Many participants reported that navigating the treatment system independently, without adequate support, was particularly difficult for those with EF challenges and often served as a barrier to sustained engagement. Increasing health literacy and promoting patient self‐efficacy may help reduce reliance on self‐advocacy, particularly among individuals with cognitive issues (Rolova et al., 2021). For instance, one potential strategy is the development of plain language materials and infographics that clearly describe and explain the nature of AUD and co‐occurring mental health conditions, the range of available treatment options, how to access services, and expected wait times. Participants also suggested greater access to intensive case management and the integration of peer navigators who have lived experience with treatment and can provide guidance, encouragement, and support throughout the treatment process (Eddie et al., 2019).

Participants further emphasized the need to reduce systemic barriers such as long wait times, fragmented service delivery, and limited access to long‐term psychotherapy. Addressing these issues will require sustained investment in mental health and addiction services, including increased funding and resources. A more patient‐centered treatment approach that is responsive to individuals' motivations, stages of change, and personal goals is also recommended. Such an approach would foster shared decision‐making, strengthen therapeutic alliances, and allow for flexible, self‐directed timelines that reflect clients' readiness to engage in care (Marchand et al., 2019; Mueser, 2004). Finally, participants called for a broader range of treatment modalities beyond pharmacotherapy. Healthcare providers should be equipped to offer diverse, evidence‐based interventions tailored to the unique constellation of substance use, mental health, and cognitive needs of each individual (Barrio & Gual, 2016).

EF challenges are commonly observed in individuals with AUD and co‐occurring mental health disorders, and their presence has been associated with poorer treatment responses and longer‐term outcomes (Boyle et al., 2023). This situation highlights the importance of developing and implementing tailored treatment approaches that address not only mental health symptoms but also underlying cognitive issues (Mojtabai et al., 2002). Evidence suggests that this group may benefit from specialized interventions that directly target EF difficulties in the context of treatment. One promising approach involves the integration of goal‐management training, which employs interactive tasks, strategy learning, and real‐life examples to strengthen individuals' ability to plan, organize, and inhibit impulsive responses. These interventions have demonstrated efficacy in reducing EF challenges and decision‐making deficits among individuals in addiction treatment settings (Verdejo‐Garcia, 2020; Verdejo‐García et al., 2019). Moreover, the development of integrated treatment settings staffed with clinicians specially trained to address intersecting cognitive, psychiatric, and substance use needs of this population may enhance care delivery and improve outcomes (Mueser, 2004). Integrated care models have shown particular utility in supporting individuals with complex comorbidities, offering a coordinated approach that addresses the full spectrum of mental health, addiction, and cognitive functioning concerns (Farhoudian et al., 2022; Mueser, 2004; Priester et al., 2016).

Limitations

Several study limitations should be acknowledged. Participants were at different stages of treatment and recovery, which may have impacted their responses. Individuals further along in recovery and posttreatment often reported better management of EF domains such as task initiation, time management, impulsivity, and memory. Thus, differences in treatment experiences and challenges between those actively drinking and those managing their alcohol use may have existed but were not explicitly explored in this study. Similarly, although all participants were seeking treatment for AUD, many had co‐occurring SUDs and used other substances. This may have complicated interpretations of whether their responses reflected experiences specific to alcohol versus other substances more broadly, or how their use of other substances may have impacted their treatment experiences.

As with all qualitative research, the potential for inherent biases in self‐report data, such as social desirability and recall biases, warrants consideration. The findings are also not generalizable beyond the context in which they were collected. Participants were recruited from CAMH, a leading mental health and addictions hospital that offers a patient‐centered, multidisciplinary model of care for AUD. CAMH's approach reflects a medicalized model of care that aligns with national AUD treatment guidelines, advocating for the combined use of pharmacotherapy and psychosocial interventions (Wood et al., 2023). CAMH's client‐centered model accommodates a range of treatment goals, including abstinence, reduction, or management of alcohol use, which may not be consistently supported across more resource‐limited or abstinence‐oriented treatment settings in other parts of Canada. As such, this program reflects a high‐resource, urban model of care that may differ substantially from lower‐resource or geographically remote treatment contexts in terms of accessibility, intensity, and clinical specialization. While this model may not be representative of all treatment models, particularly in regions or institutions with limited resources or differing healthcare infrastructures, such as community‐based and rural and remote treatment contexts, it is consistent with the services offered by other specialized services across Ontario. Participants also described experiences with other institutions; however, the CAMH setting may have influenced their experiences in ways not representative of less‐specialized care environments or pathways.

The sample also lacked diversity in several sociodemographic domains. On average, participants were highly educated, heterosexual, White, and stably housed. These characteristics limit the broader generalizability of our findings. EF challenges were also assessed using a self‐report measure. While such tools are validated and widely used, individuals may not be able to accuratly estimate their own cognitive abilities. Behavioral measures could have complemented our self‐report measures.

Lastly, we did not assess diagnosed or undiagnosed learning disorders, which fall outside the scope of this paper, but may have impacted EF and treatment experiences, given the known overlap between cognitive function and learning disorders (Brandenburg et al., 2021). Future research should aim to disentangle the interactions between EF challenges, mental health concerns, and AUD, with a particular focus on how these factors shape treatment motivation and trajectories. Additional exploration of sex, gender, and cultural influences would also be valuable. Including provider perspectives could further enrich our understanding of the barriers and facilitators to treatment engagement.

CONCLUSIONS

Adults seeking treatment for AUD often present with co‐occurring mental health and EF challenges, which influence their decisions to initiate treatment and shape their subsequent treatment trajectories and experiences. Despite a desire or expectation for counseling or psychotherapy, many reported that treatment was primarily focused on pharmacotherapy. Individuals with clinically significant EF impairments described encountering specific barriers to care, including long waitlists, limited follow‐up, fragmented services, and requirements to self‐advocate. Additional issues included difficulties with motivation, time management, and navigating a complex treatment system. Participants with EF challenges offered concrete recommendations for improving health system delivery, such as clearer and more direct communication from providers, the provision of structured timelines, and consistent appointment reminders. Implementing these strategies may enhance treatment accessibility and outcomes for individuals with EF challenges, thereby improving the overall effectiveness of AUD treatment.
