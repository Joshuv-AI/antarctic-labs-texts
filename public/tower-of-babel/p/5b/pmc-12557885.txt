Diagnostic performance of adenosine deaminase activity in pleural fluid in tuberculous pleural effusion and relevant factors: a single-center experience with 1,491 consecutive patients
Authors: Chi Yang, Xiang Shi, Shaojun Zhang, Mengting Xiong, Haili Hu, Xiaohui Hao, Wei Sha
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveAdenosine deaminase (ADA) is a widely-used diagnostic test for tuberculous pleural effusion (TPE), but its accuracy, optimal diagnostic threshold and relevant factors are controversial or unclear. We aimed to reassess these diagnostic variables in a large single center series.MethodThis retrospective study included 1,491 TPE patients and 597 malignant pleural effusion patients seen between 2012 and 2019. Receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) and multivariate logistic regression analysis were performed for the diagnostic performance and relevant factors of ADA.ResultsThe AUC for ADA was 0.952 (95%CI: 0.942—0.961; p = 0.000) in discrimination of TPE from malignant pleural effusion, with an optimal cut-off value of 26.6 IU/L. While levels > 35 IU/L showed high specificity (97.0%), the significant 20–35 IU/L overlap underscores the need for integrated diagnostic approaches in this gray zone. The accuracy of ADA was significantly correlated with age (OR = 0.97; 95% CI: 0.95–0.99; p = 0.0066), pleural monocyte ratio (OR = 1.02; 95% CI: 1.00–1.04; p = 0.0346) and CD45+ relative count (OR = 0.942; 95% CI: 0.90–0.99; p = 0.015).ConclusionPleural ADA had good accuracy in the diagnosis of TPE. Age, pleural monocyte ratio and CD45+ relative count may affect pleural fluid ADA accuracy, therefore, ADA results should be interpreted with caution in patients aged > 80 years or with a pleural monocyte ratio < 60%. Moreover, for pleural effusions with ADA 20–35 IU/L, combined diagnostic approaches are superior to fixed cutoffs.Keywords: Adenosine Deaminase, Tuberculous Pleural Effusion, Relevant Factors, Accuracy

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a major cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide [1]. It is estimated that 2 to 3 billion people worldwide are infected with Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MT), of which 5–15% will develop active TB during their lifetime [1]; reportedly about 10 million 400 thousand in 2015, according to The World Health Organization (WHO) [1]. A tuberculous pleural effusion (TPE) is one of the most common clinical manifestations of pulmonary TB [2]. TPE often presents a diagnostic challenge because of the low microbiological confirmation rate in pleural fluid and the practicalities of an invasive pleural biopsy [2, 3]. Early diagnosis and treatment are extremely important, however, since delay may increase morbidity and worsen prognosis [4]. Markers in pleural fluid have therefore, attracted a lot of attention as potential alternatives to pleural biopsy because of their rapid results and convenience [5].

Adenosine deaminase (ADA) is an enzyme produced by lymphocytes which participates in purine metabolism [6]. Pleural fluid ADA concentration is considered to be an important diagnostic tool for evaluating exudative pleural effusion, as raised levels are typical of TPE, although, high levels are sometimes seen in pleural fluid in malignancy, rheumatoid pleurisy or in patients with empyema[7]. ADA, as a biochemical index in pleural fluid, was first advocated in 1978 [8] and has been widely used in TPE, because it is a simple, cheap, rapid and minimally invasive method, which can be carried out in most laboratories [2]. Although widely used, hundreds of previous studies have reported different diagnostic accuracies and different threshold values for ADA [9–12], however, because of substantial heterogeneity and high risk of bias meta-analysis failed to draw any firm inference of relative clinical utility of different diagnostic thresholds [13]. In addition, there are few studies on the factors affecting pleural ADA level, which may be of great relevance in its clinical application [14].

It is therefore of great importance to reassess the accuracy, optimal threshold and relevant factors affecting ADA levels in a large series of patients with TPE. Our study aimed to: a) reassess the ADA test accuracy in a large series of samples from a single center b) establish the optimal ADA cutoff value for TPE diagnosis, and c) determine whether demographic characteristics, pleural fluid and serum biochemistry, peripheral blood cell count, particular immune status variables and some chronic disease factors affect the ADA concentration in pleural fluid.

Materials and methods

Patient population

This retrospective study analyzed the clinical data of all consecutive patients diagnosed with TPE in Shanghai Lung Hospital Affiliated to Medical College of Shanghai Tongji University from December 2012 to August 2019. Over the same period, from the same institution, patients with malignant pleural effusion without a TB history were selected as controls. The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Shanghai Pulmonary Hospital (No.: K25-501), with strict adherence to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. A written informed consent was obtained from the patients.

Patient’s age, sex, and pleural chloride, lactate dehydrogenase, triglyceride, monocyte ratio (monocytes/total white cell count), CD45+ and CD8+ relative counts, CD4+/CD8+, CD3+ CD8+/CD3+, CD16 +/CD56 +, and blood hemoglobin, absolute lymphocyte count, basophil count, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), prealbumin, lipoprotein, albumin and globulin, all measured in routine hospital laboratories by standardized techniques, were recorded. History of diabetes and hypertension were included in the analysis. The ADA activity in pleural fluid was measured by colorimetry with an Olympus 640 biochemical analyzer (Tokyo, Japan) using the methods of Giusti and Galanti [15].

Classification and diagnosis

The diagnosis of TPE had to meet the following three criteria: (a) preliminary diagnosis of TPE: patients with pleural effusion who had microbiological evidence (positive smear, culture or polymerase chain reaction of MT in pleural effusion or sputum or other biopsy samples) or pathological evidence (histopathological or cytological evidence of MT on pleural biopsy, (b) positive radiological improvement after anti TB treatment. Exclusion criteria: No histopathological or cytological evidence of malignancy; no clinical diagnosis of heart failure; renal failure; nephrotic syndrome; liver cirrhosis; and no transudate effusion.

Malignant pleural effusion was confirmed conventionally, by positive pleural cytology or biopsy histology. Exclusion criteria: Patients with evidence of TB or a history of TB were excluded from the control group.

Statistical analysis

Medical records data were presented as mean + standard deviations (SD) or median and interquartile range (IQR)where appropriate. Age, gender, pleural chloride, pleural lactate dehydrogenase and pleural triglyceride concentrations, pleural monocyte ratio, CD45+, CD8+, CD4+/CD8+, CD3+CD8+/CD3+ relative counts, and blood measurements of hemoglobin, absolute lymphocyte count, basophil count, ESR, albumin, globulin, and diagnoses of diabetes and hypertension were all examined by univariate logistic regression analysis. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was then performed on variables in univariate analysis with P values < 0.2 to calculate their odds ratios (OR) and P values. Factors with P value < 0.05 were considered as relevant factors relating to sensitivity and specificity. In addition, in order to avoid collinearity between variables in multivariate analysis, we determined their variance inflation factor (VIF), and factors with VIF > 5 were considered collinear.

The area under the ROC curve (AUC) together with confidence intervals (CI) were calculated for pleural ADA; in general, the higher the AUC, the greater the reliability, and the better the discrimination of positive and negative diagnoses. An AUC above 0.9 indicates high accuracy, 0.7–0.9 suggests medium accuracy, and 0.5–0.7 only low accuracy. However, variables with an AUC greater than 0.7 may be accepted as valuable in diagnosis [16]. A threshold value was determined to maximize the sum of sensitivity and specificity. Test sensitivity, specificity, negative likelihood ratio (LR +), negative likelihood ratio (LR -) and diagnostic odds ratio (DOR) were calculated. ADA value distributions were analyzed with 1-U/L binned histograms and intergroup proportion comparisons. All statistical analyses were performed using Medcalc software (v9.0.1.1; Medcalc Software, Mariakerke, Belgium).

ADA distribution patterns in TPE versus malignant pleural effusion

The comparative analysis revealed markedly distinct ADA distribution patterns between tuberculous pleural effusion (TPE) and malignant pleural effusion (Fig. 3). Notably, TPE and malignant pleural effusion demonstrated significant overlap in their ADA value distributions, particularly in the intermediate range of 20–35 IU/L. The malignant pleural effusion cohort demonstrated a strongly right-skewed distribution, with 93.8% of values falling below 35 IU/L, while the TPE group exhibited a right-skewed unimodal distribution with substantially elevated values and a pronounced heavy tail extending beyond 35 IU/L. Notably, ADA levels above 35 IU/L showed exceptional specificity for TPE, comprising 97.0% of cases in this range with particularly robust diagnostic performance in the 50–200 U/L range where TPE cases accounted for 97.9% of observations. The analysis identified three clinically relevant diagnostic zones: the 26.6–35 IU/L transition zone demonstrated strong TPE predominance (88.7% vs 11.3%, ratio 7.9:1), the 20–26 IU/L intermediate zone—representing the most significant overlap region—showed comparable representation (TPE: 56.0%, malignant: 44.0%), while values below 20 IU/L were overwhelmingly malignant cases (91.7%) with TPE comprising only 8.3% of observations.

Discussion

We included 1491 consecutive patients, from a single center, with TPE and compared them with a control group of 597 patients with a malignant pleural effusion, in our retrospective study. We showed an AUC for pleural ADA of 0.95 (95% CI 0.94–0.96) showing very good discrimination of TPE from malignant pleural effusions. These represent the main differential diagnoses since other causes of effusions including parapneumonic effusions and empyema which may have a raised ADA are readily distinguished by their neutrophilic cytology [17] and CT scan appearances.

Hundreds of studies have evaluated the test accuracy of pleural fluid ADA in the diagnosis of TPE, nearly all with a sample size of less than 100 [13]. To our knowledge, a 2013 study had the largest sample size [18], though this was only 548. While meta-analysis may include many publications with large sample sizes, heterogeneity (e.g., different ADA measurement methods or diagnostic thresholds; and publication bias) may limit the reliability of estimates of test accuracy [19].

However, over the past two decades, multiple meta-analyses have demonstrated high test accuracy of pleural ADA in differentiating TB. In 2003, Goto et al. collected 40 studies (including 1,857 patients with TPE) and found that the sensitivity, specificity and area under the summary ROC (SROC) curve of ADA in pleural fluid were 92%, 92% and 0.92, respectively [10]. In 2008, Liang et al. summarized 63 studies (2,796 patients with TPE) and reported a sensitivity of 0.92, a specificity of 0.90, a DOR of 110.08 and an area under SROC curve of 0.96 [11]. In 2019, Aggarwal et al. collected 174 papers involving 10,696 patients with TPE and summarized sensitivity, specificity and DOR as 0.92, 0.90 and 97.42 respectively. Our single center results were consistent; (sensitivity: 0.92; specificity: 0.90; DOR: 117.83). We found an ADA threshold for the diagnosis of TPE of (26.6 U/L) but various thresholds have been employed, even interfering with meta-analysis [9]. The diagnostic thresholds for pleural fluid ADA varied across studies with different cohort sizes. Blakiston et al. analyzed 1,637 pleural effusion episodes (57 tuberculous) in a low-TB incidence setting (Auckland, New Zealand), demonstrating that ADA cutoffs of ≥ 15 U/L and ≥ 30 U/L yielded sensitivities of 100% and 93%, with specificities of 62.7% and 87.3%, respectively [20]. Porcel et al. evaluated 2,104 consecutive pleural effusions (221 tuberculous) and proposed an optimal ADA threshold > 35 U/L, achieving 93% sensitivity and 90% specificity for tuberculous pleurisy diagnosis [17]. In Garcia-Zamalloa et al.'s study of 472 effusions (73 tuberculous), combining ADA ≥ 40 U/L with lymphocyte proportion ≥ 50% (LP (50)) significantly enhanced specificity (98.3%) and positive predictive value (90%) [21]. Liu et al. incorporated ADA into a decision tree model derived from 1,435 pleural effusion patients, establishing its role in differentiating malignant from tuberculous effusions [22]. Shimoda et al.'s diagnostic algorithm, developed from 958 patients (174 tuberculous pleurisy), identified ADA ≥ 40 IU/L as a key marker for tuberculous effusion [23]. Aggarwal et al. summarized the thresholds in 174 studies [12]; the commonest threshold was 40 IU/L (in 43 studies with 2718 TPE patients). They divided all thresholds into 4 groups (< 36, 36–44, 45–65 and > 65 IU/L) and recommended conducting a detailed evaluation for non-tuberculous diseases in patients with low pleural ADA (below 36—44 IU/L). The reason for our lower ADA cutoff value compared to previous studies remains unclear, though potential contributing factors may include differences in selection criteria, the prevalence of tuberculous pleurisy (TPE), and sample size. Notably, the relatively low ADA threshold observed in our study could also be partially attributed to the lower mean pleural fluid ADA levels in our cohort (mean 58.0 IU/L, median 53.8 IU/L). Importantly, our study included a higher proportion of culture-negative tuberculosis patients (73%), who typically exhibit lower pleural fluid ADA levels. Although the difference did not reach statistical significance, culture-positive TPE cases tended to have slightly higher mean ADA levels than culture-negative cases (60.5 IU/L vs. 57.5 IU/L, p = 0.1688). Thus, the overrepresentation of culture-negative patients may have contributed to the lower threshold observed in our study compared to others. Therefore, the proposed cut-off value should be considered as preliminary reference data, with its diagnostic utility strictly confined to discriminating between tuberculous and neoplastic pleural effusions. The optimal critical value of ADA still needs further study.

Our distribution pattern analysis reveals a clinically significant diagnostic challenge, demonstrating substantial overlap in ADA values between TPE and malignant pleural effusion within the 20–35 U/L interval, where ADA's discriminative power markedly declines. These findings argue against using absolute ADA cutoff values as definitive diagnostic thresholds and instead support establishing a 20–35 U/L "gray zone" requiring cautious interpretation, where clinicians should maintain high suspicion for TPE. This aligns with prior evidence from Kim et al. [24], who identified similar diagnostic complexities in intermediate ADA ranges. Given these findings, we recommend adopting the integrated diagnostic approaches proposed by Liu et al. and Lee et al. [25, 26] when evaluating pleural effusions in this gray zone, which emphasize combining multiple parameters (e.g., biochemical markers including CEA and IFN-γ, radiographic features, and clinical predictors) through comprehensive scoring systems rather than relying on single biomarkers. This multidimensional strategy has been shown to significantly improve differential diagnostic accuracy for challenging effusions. Future validation studies should further refine these integrated models specifically for the 20–35 U/L ADA range to optimize clinical decision-making.

We found a significant correlation between the accuracy of ADA and age (OR = 0.97, p = 0.0066). Accuracy of ADA testing was acceptable for patients under 80 years old, but ADA results need careful interpretation in older patients. Pleural ADA has previously been shown to be significantly negatively correlated with age [13, 14, 27]. Korczynski et al. reported that TPE was more likely with increasing age, while ADA levels were lower in the elderly [28] but direct evidence that decline of ADA affects the accuracy of TPE discrimination was not provided.

We demonstrated a significant correlation between high pleural monocyte ratio and a true ADA positive test (p = 0.0003 by univariate analysis and p = 0.035 by multivariate analysis). A pleural monocyte ratio above 60%, was associated with reliable ADA accuracy (> 0.9, Fig. 2B). Improved accuracy is likely related to the high activity of ADA2 in monocytes [29] raising ADA in TPE. In a study of 192 Korean TPE patients, low pleural ADA was correlated with low pleural lymphocyte percentage but also with organ failure [24]. For the first time, we investigated a possible relationship between pleural ADA test accuracy and blood CD45+ relative count, however, we do not recommend routine measurement of blood CD45 + relative count in patients with pleurisy for two reasons: first, due to the additional cost burden, and second, the impact of CD45 + relative counts on accuracy may not be significant, as even the lowest value remained acceptable (0.91).

We need to acknowledge the limitations of our work. First, this was a retrospective study of patients from a single center and we cannot exclude selection bias. Our country has a high incidence of TB, and our findings may not be as relevant in countries with a low disease incidence. More large, ideally prospective, intercontinental studies are needed to verify its generalizability. Second, although this study had a large sample size, the comparisons were limited to malignant pleural effusions and did not adequately consider clinical differential diagnoses such as pleural mesothelioma, malignant lymphoma, autoimmune diseases, borderline empyema or nontuberculous mycobacterial infections due to the rarity of these diseases or the absence of readily available diagnostic gold standards. Future studies should consider these factors and combine in-depth multicenter studies to further enhance the generalizability and clinical applicability of the findings. Thrid, a definitive diagnosis of TPE on pleural biopsy was lacking in our study. Our diagnostic criteria were mainly based on clinical features and response to anti-TB treatment, patients who died due to TB progression without showing radiological improvement would be excluded. Therefore, it is in this context that we confirm the useful role of pleural ADA. We further acknowledge that other potentially useful markers (eg ADA isoenzymes ie ADA2 or interferon gamma etc. exist [17]). Fourth, the limited diversity of disease types in our control and experimental groups restricts the generalizability of the derived cut-off value. Therefore, we do not recommend adopting this as a new definitive cut-off value. However, this lower threshold still suggests that tuberculous pleurisy should not be readily excluded in patients with ADA levels between 26–35 IU/L. For future studies, we recommend enrolling more diverse case types to compare their ADA levels, which would provide more reliable cut-off values.

Conclusions

In summary, in a large, single center, consecutive, series of TPE patients, we found that age, pleural monocyte ratio and blood CD45+ relative count may affect the test accuracy. Higher age and lower pleural monocyte ratio reduce the accuracy of ADA in TPE diagnosis; particularly for age > 80 years or pleural monocyte ratio < 60%, careful interpretation of ADA results is required. Although increased blood CD45+ relative count, had a statistically significant effect, ADA test accuracy remained relatively high at (> 0.9). While our data suggest a potential diagnostic cutoff of 26.6 IU/L for distinguishing TPE from malignant effusions, significant overlap between groups precludes adopting this as an absolute threshold. Instead, we recommend that effusions in the 20–35 IU/L gray zone require comprehensive evaluation integrating ancillary biomarkers and clinical assessment rather than relying solely on ADA levels.
