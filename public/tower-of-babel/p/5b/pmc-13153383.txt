CD177⁺ neutrophil-platelet aggregates contribute to thromboinflammation via NETs in necrotizing enterocolitis
Authors: Chaoting Lan, Bowen Tian, Yongyan Shi, Yuling Yang, Yan Tian, Chun Yan, LongLong Hou, Lin Li, Li Yuan, Lianwei Lu, Junjian Lv, Weizhe Wu, Qiuming He, Huimin Xia, Wenhao Zhou, Wei Zhong, Yufeng Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNecrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) is a severe gastrointestinal disease affecting premature infants, yet its precise pathogenic mechanisms remain unclear. While immunothrombosis, an interplay between inflammation and coagulation, is well recognized in systemic inflammatory diseases, its role in NEC pathogenesis and local intestinal regulation has been incompletely characterized. Here, we combine multi-omics profiling, histopathological analyses, and functional studies using clinical NEC samples and neonatal mouse models. We demonstrate prominent intestinal thromboinflammation in NEC, histologically characterized by immunothrombosis with infiltrating CD177⁺ neutrophils, activated platelets, and fibrin deposition. We show that extensive formation of CD177⁺ neutrophil–platelet aggregates (NPAs) amplifies intestinal injury through neutrophil extracellular trap (NET)-mediated thromboinflammation. Blocking CD177⁺ NPAs or inhibiting NET formation significantly alleviates NEC severity in mouse models. Clinically, elevated CD177⁺ NPA levels correlate with NEC severity and increased thrombotic biomarkers, highlighting their potential as diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers. Importantly, prophylactic administration of low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) disrupts NPA formation, reduces NET burden, and improves survival in experimental NEC. Collectively, these findings establish CD177⁺ NPAs as central drivers of NEC pathogenesis, support a thromboinflammatory framework for NEC, and highlight LMWH therapy and CD177⁺ NPA monitoring as possible clinically translatable strategies for neonatal care.Subject terms: Gastrointestinal diseases, Mucosal immunology, Paediatric research, Neutrophils, Coagulation system

Introduction

Necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC), a life-threatening gastrointestinal emergency that predominantly affects preterm infants, is characterized by overwhelming intestinal inflammation, ischemic necrosis, and mortality rates exceeding 30% in very low birth weight neonates. Survivors often suffer serious long-term sequelae—such as short bowel syndrome and neurodevelopmental impairment—underscoring that the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms of NEC remain poorly understood1–3. Although NEC has traditionally been viewed as an inflammatory disorder, emerging clinical observations reveal a coexistence of systemic hyperinflammation and coagulation dysfunction, with severe cases progressing to disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)4–7. This duality raises a fundamental but unresolved question: How do the immune and coagulation systems interact within the immature intestinal microenvironment to drive NEC progression?

Immunothrombosis—a pathological interplay between innate immunity and coagulation—has emerged as a key amplifier of tissue injury in systemic inflammatory diseases such as sepsis and COVID-198–10. Neutrophils orchestrate this process through the release of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) and the formation of neutrophil–platelet aggregates (NPAs), which promote microvascular occlusion, intensify thromboinflammation, and correlate with disease severity11. Under physiological conditions, immunothrombosis functions as a host-defense mechanism, spatially restricting pathogens through localized clot formation12. However, in disease states, dysregulation transforms this protective response into a self-perpetuating cycle of thromboinflammation, whereby clot-driven ischemia and inflammatory mediator release exacerbate tissue damage13,14. The preterm intestine—with its immature vascular regulation, diminished ischemic tolerance, and unique microbial exposures—may be particularly vulnerable to these pathological cascades15, yet mechanistic insights are limited.

Here, we show CD177⁺ neutrophil–platelet aggregates (CD177⁺ NPAs) form important mediators of NEC pathogenesis through integrated multi-omics, histopathological, and functional studies. We provide evidence of intestinal immunothrombosis in NEC, characterized by CD177⁺ neutrophil infiltration, platelet accumulation, and fibrinogen deposition. CD177⁺ neutrophils, expanded in NEC, interact with activated platelets to form hyperactive NPAs that amplify thromboinflammation in a NETosis-dependent manner. Clinically, CD177⁺ NPA levels correlate with NEC severity (Bell staging), thrombotic biomarkers (D-dimer, PF4), and mortality risk, establishing their utility both as diagnostic indicators and therapeutic targets. Therapeutically, prophylactic administration of low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH), a multifunctional anticoagulant with anti-inflammatory properties, disrupted CD177⁺ NPA formation, reduced NET burden, and improved survival in preclinical models. Collectively, these findings further define NEC as a thromboinflammatory disorder and position LMWH prophylaxis, coupled with CD177⁺ NPA monitoring, as a promising strategy for neonatal critical care. By bridging intestinal immunopathology with systemic coagulation cascades, this work advances precision diagnostics and targeted therapies, and implicating a path foward for mitigating NEC’s substantial clinical burden.

Results

NEC exhibits pathological features of thromboinflammation

To characterize inflammatory and coagulation phenotypes in NEC, we analyzed clinical data and found prolonged prothrombin time (PT) and activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), along with elevated fibrinogen (FIB), in NEC versus controls (Fig. 1A). ELISA of intestinal tissue homogenates and plasma showed increased D-dimer, FIB, and platelet factor 4 (PF4), consistent with coagulation dysfunction (Fig. 1B and Supplementary Fig. 1A). Systemic inflammation was evident from neutrophilia (Fig. 1C). Thrombocytopenia (Fig. 1D) coincided with increased platelet CD62P expression, indicating excessive platelet activation as a likely driver of platelet consumption (Fig. 1E). Using the Japanese Ministry of Health and Welfare disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) score16, we compiled complete coagulation parameters for 18 NEC infants (Supplementary Fig. 1B and Supplementary data 1) and stratified them by survival status. Spearman correlation analysis showed that higher peripheral neutrophil counts and higher DIC scores were associated with mortality, whereas platelet counts inversely correlated with death (Fig. 1F). Collectively, these data link systemic inflammation (neutrophilia) and coagulopathy (thrombocytopenia, elevated DIC score) to adverse outcomes in NEC.

To dissect the interplay between inflammation and coagulation in NEC, we conducted a systematic pathological analysis of the intestine. NEC specimens consistently exhibited two histologically distinct zones: (i) lesional zones, showing marked, transmural congestion and edema with dense immune-cell infiltration; and (ii) necrotic areas, displaying transmural necrosis with extensive cellular debris (Fig. 1G). Within small- and medium-sized vessels in lesional zones, we frequently observed luminal thrombi enriched in neutrophils that occluded the vascular lumen (Fig. 1G). Immunofluorescence staining demonstrated that these thrombi comprised neutrophils (MPO), platelets (CD41), and fibrin (FIB), identifying them as neutrophil-driven immunothrombi (Fig. 1H). Detailed histopathological assessment also revealed hyaline (“transparent”) thrombi in some small vessels within lesional zones, a morphology characteristic of DIC-associated thrombosis (Fig. 1I). Analysis of serial sections delineated a dynamic morphological spectrum of immunothrombosis in NEC: an early stage, marked by perivascular neutrophil accumulation and intraluminal cellular aggregates; a progressive stage, in which mature immunothrombi formed and led to complete vascular occlusion; and a late stage, where residual, partially degraded immunothrombi persisted within debris-rich necrotic regions (Fig. 1J).

Together, these findings provide direct histopathological evidence of thromboinflammation in NEC intestinal lesions, characterized by neutrophil infiltration, platelet aggregation, and fibrin deposition.

Single-cell dissection of neutrophil-driven thromboinflammation in NEC

We performed single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) on intestinal tissue and peripheral blood (post-erythrocyte lysis) from NEC patients and matched controls (Fig. 2A). Dimensionality reduction and transcriptional profiling revealed higher thrombotic gene-set scores in NEC across both compartments, indicating systemic and local coagulation dysregulation (Fig. 2B). Immune-landscape analysis showed a marked expansion of neutrophils in NEC in blood and intestine (Fig. 2C, D and Supplementary Fig. 2A, B), corroborated by flow cytometry in patients and experimental NEC models (Supplementary Fig. 2C, D). Within the scRNA-seq data, neutrophils emerged as the principal drivers of coagulation abnormalities and platelet activation, with more pronounced effects in NEC than controls (Fig. 2E, F). KEGG pathway analysis further implicated neutrophil programs in platelet activation, extending their role beyond classical host defense (Supplementary Fig. 2E). Mechanistically, platelet Toll-like receptor activation by pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) promotes neutrophil–platelet aggregate (NPA) formation, amplifying crosstalk between inflammation and coagulation17. Consistently, NPAs were significantly elevated in NEC patient blood (Fig. 2G). In a mouse NEC model, neutrophil depletion with anti-Ly6G reduced neutrophils and NPAs (Fig. 2H–J) and alleviated intestinal injury, lowering NEC severity scores (Fig. 2K).

CD177⁺ neutrophils drive thromboinflammatory responses in NEC

Neutrophil subsets exhibit functional heterogeneity in coordinating innate immune responses, including cytokine/chemokine release and tissue factor–dependent procoagulant activity. However, their contribution to NEC-associated thromboinflammation remains unclear. Aggregating peripheral-blood neutrophils from NEC and control neonates, UMAP embedding revealed a distinct CD177⁺ neutrophil population (Fig. 3A). CD177—a marker of neutrophil activation, adhesion, and migration18—was notably upregulated in NEC. When stratified by diagnosis, the proportion of CD177⁺ neutrophils were significantly increased in NEC, indicating expansion of this subset (Fig. 3B). KEGG pathway enrichment further showed that the CD177⁺ subset was associated with leukocyte transendothelial migration, platelet activation, NET formation, and other immune-response pathways (Fig. 3C). Genes related to platelet activation and complement/coagulation cascades were upregulated in CD177⁺ neutrophils from NEC patients (Fig. 3D), linking this subset to thromboinflammatory mechanisms.

In intestinal tissue, detailed neutrophil subsetting identified three major transcriptional states—CD177_NEU, IL1B_NEU, and FOS_NEU (Fig. 3E and Supplementary Fig. 4A). CD177⁺ neutrophils were the most abundant and were significantly enriched in NEC versus controls (Fig. 3F). Consistent with the blood findings, KEGG analysis linked the intestinal CD177⁺ subset to leukocyte transendothelial migration, platelet activation, NET formation, and additional immune pathways (Fig. 3G), with enrichment for platelet activation and complement/coagulation cascades (Fig. 3H).

To further validate these observations, we performed scRNA-seq on intestinal tissues from NEC and control mice (Fig. 3I and Supplementary Fig. 3B, C). UMAP analysis identified two major neutrophil clusters, Il1b _neu and Cd177_neu (Fig. 3J and Supplementary Fig. 3D). Cd177_neu was significantly expanded in NEC (Fig. 3K). Functionally, the Cd177_neu subset exhibited significant upregulation of cytoskeletal effector genes (e.g., Tpm4, Actn1, Wdr1, Myh9) implicated in platelet activation/immunothrombosis and actomyosin-driven clot retraction, relative to Il1b _neu, suggesting a specialized pro-thromboinflammatory phenotype (Fig. 3L).

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that CD177⁺ neutrophils are key mediators of thromboinflammatory responses in the NEC intestine.

CD177⁺NPAs drive thromboinflammation in NEC

To define how CD177⁺ neutrophils contribute to thromboinflammation in NEC, we profiled peripheral blood from NEC infants and intestinal tissues from a mouse NEC model by flow cytometry. In NEC infants, CD177⁺ neutrophils (CD45⁺CD66b⁺CD177⁺) were significantly increased versus controls (Fig. 4A). Concordantly, intestinal CD177⁺Ly6G⁺ neutrophils were elevated in NEC mice (Fig. 4B). CD177⁺ neutrophil–platelet aggregates (NPAs) were enriched in NEC: the frequency of CD41a⁺ events within CD177⁺ cells among CD45⁺ leukocytes were higher in patients than controls (Fig. 4C), with a parallel rise in intestinal CD177⁺CD41⁺ NPAs in mice (Fig. 4D). Platelets within CD177⁺ NPAs showed increased activation, evidenced by higher CD62P mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) in NEC (Fig. 4E). Both the proportion of CD177⁺ neutrophils and the frequency of CD177⁺ NPAs scaled with clinical and histologic NEC severity (Fig. 4F) and positively correlated with circulating D-dimer and PF4 levels (Fig. 4G). Immunofluorescence localized CD177⁺ neutrophils together with platelet aggregates and microthrombi in the inflamed NEC intestine (Fig. 4H).

Targeted modulation of CD177⁺ NPAs alleviates NEC

To delineate the mechanistic role of CD177⁺ neutrophil–platelet aggregates (NPAs) in NEC-associated thromboinflammation, we used complementary gain- and loss-of-function approaches. Adoptive transfer of CD177⁺ neutrophils from NEC mice into NOG recipients (NOD/Shi-scid/IL-2Rγnull) induced pronounced CD177⁺ neutrophil infiltration in the ileal lamina propria (Fig. 5A and Supplementary Fig. 4A) and exacerbated disease, as evidenced by higher histopathology scores (Fig. 5B) and increased CD177⁺ NPA formation (Fig. 5C). Conversely, CD177⁻/⁻ mice exhibited attenuated NEC pathology, including lower intestinal inflammation scores (Fig. 5D), improved survival (Fig. 5E), and reduced plasma IL-6 (Fig. 5F). In these mice, Ly6G⁺ neutrophils and CD41⁺Ly6G⁺ NPAs were also significantly decreased (Fig. 5H). Consistently, neutrophil-specific CD177 conditional knockout (cKO; CD177fl/fl; MRP8-Cre) mice showed reduced inflammation scores (Fig. 5I), improved survival (Fig. 5J), and lower inflammatory markers (Fig. 5K) relative to littermate CD177 fl/fl controls, accompanied by diminished intestinal CD41⁺Ly6G⁺ NPAs (Fig. 5L and Supplementary Fig. 4B).

CD177⁺ NPAs drive thromboinflammation via NETs in NEC

To elucidate how CD177⁺ NPAs exacerbate thromboinflammatory pathology in NEC, we integrated omics profiling with functional validation. Transcriptomic analysis of CD177⁺ neutrophils isolated from NEC patients revealed pronounced activation of neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation programs (Fig. 6A, B), with upregulation of genes involved in NETosis and chromatin remodeling, including PAD4, multiple core histone genes (H2AC6, H2BC4, H2BC21, H3-3A, H3-3B), and the coagulation factor F5. KEGG pathway enrichment further substantiated these findings, demonstrating coordinated upregulation of NET-related genes, platelet activation markers, and complement/coagulation components (Fig. 6C). Multiplex immunofluorescence demonstrated the colocalization of CD177⁺ neutrophils with MPO⁺/CitH3⁺ NETs in NEC intestinal lesions (Fig. 6D), while flow cytometry confirmed elevated NET release by CD177⁺ NPAs from NEC patients (Fig. 6E). Notably, CD177⁺ neutrophils from NEC patients exhibited abnormally high baseline NET activity, comparable to PMA-stimulated control neutrophils (Supplementary Fig. 4C). In the NEC mouse model, intestinal NET density was increased (Fig. 6F), consistent with KEGG analyses indicating NET pathway activation (Fig. 6G). By contrast, CD177⁻/⁻ mice displayed a substantial reduction in NET formation (Fig. 6H), with residual Ly6G⁺ neutrophils showing significantly lower NET release and fewer NPA-associated NETs (Fig. 6I, J). Furthermore, platelet-derived mediators from NEC patient platelet-rich plasma (PRP) induced CD177⁺ neutrophil expansion and NETosis in healthy donor PBMCs, highlighting a reciprocal platelet–neutrophil activation loop (Fig. 6K, L).

Intervening in NETs and coagulation alleviates NEC via CD177⁺ NPAs

We confirmed that adoptive transfer of CD177⁺ neutrophils increased CD177⁺ neutrophil–platelet aggregates (NPAs) and NETosis in NEC mice (Fig. 7A, B). To evaluate therapeutic strategies, we inhibited NET formation using DNase I and a PAD4 inhibitor; both interventions suppressed NETs and ameliorated intestinal injury (Fig. 7C, D). These treatments also significantly reduced CD177⁺ NPA formation and NETosis (Fig. 7E), underscoring the central role of NET-driven thromboinflammation in NEC pathogenesis. Given the established clinical use of low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) for thrombosis and disseminated intravascular coagulation—owing to favorable pharmacokinetics, lower bleeding risk, and anti-inflammatory, antiplatelet, and endothelial-protective properties19—we next tested LMWH in neonatal NEC models. Prophylactic LMWH significantly improved intestinal pathology (Fig. 7F, G), reduced CD177⁺ NPAs (Fig. 7H), diminished NET release (Fig. 7I), and lowered circulating pro-inflammatory cytokines (Fig. 7J). Collectively, these data provide preclinical evidence that targeted modulation of thromboinflammatory pathways is a promising strategy for NEC prevention.

Clinical utility of monitoring CD177⁺ NPAs in NEC diagnosis and treatment

The lack of reliable biomarkers for early detection and real-time monitoring remains a major challenge in NEC. To assess the clinical relevance of CD177⁺ neutrophil–platelet aggregates (NPAs), we conducted a longitudinal study measuring CD177⁺ neutrophils and CD177⁺ NPAs at diagnosis and at a predefined post-treatment time point (Fig. 8A). Both CD177⁺ neutrophils (Fig. 8B) and CD177⁺ NPAs (Fig. 8C) decreased significantly after therapy, approaching control levels and coinciding with clinical improvement. In parallel, plasma NET-associated markers—DNA–elastase complexes, CitH3, and MPO–DNA complexes—were assessed at control, diagnosis, and post-treatment time points (Fig. 8D) and were elevated at diagnosis but declined following treatment (Fig. 8E). Correlation analyses showed that NET markers, particularly elastase and MPO–DNA complexes, positively associated with CD177⁺ neutrophil frequencies (Fig. 8F), supporting their value for dynamic disease monitoring and response assessment. Mechanistically, pronounced neutrophil–platelet interactions were observed in NEC: CD177⁺ neutrophils formed aggregates with activated platelets (CD177⁺ NPAs) that readily underwent NETosis, generating CD177⁺ NPA–derived NETs tightly interlaced with platelets and fibrin to form thromboinflammatory microthrombi that may exacerbate intestinal injury. Clinically, circulating CD177⁺ NPA–derived NETs, together with routine clinical features, demonstrated potential for early diagnosis, perioperative evaluation, and recurrence monitoring in NEC (Fig. 8G).

Discussion

This study identifies CD177⁺ neutrophil–platelet aggregates as key cellular mediators linking neutrophil activation to thromboinflammation in NEC. By integrating histopathological, molecular and clinical data, our findings support the view that NEC has a prominent thromboinflammatory component and provide a framework for exploring targeted anti-thromboinflammatory strategies in this disease.

Immunothrombosis as a core pathomechanism in NEC

Our histopathological analysis of NEC intestine, integrated with multi-omics data, identifies neutrophil-rich immunothrombi as a recurrent lesion pattern, extending beyond the canonical focus on dysregulated inflammation20. These thromboinflammatory foci exhibit a triad—CD177⁺ neutrophil infiltration, CD41⁺ platelet aggregation, and fibrin(ogen)-rich deposition—underscoring tight coupling between inflammatory and coagulation pathways. Morphology supports a dynamic, multistage model of immunothrombosis: a primary stage with perivascular cuffs of neutrophils around largely patent vessels; a mature stage with occlusive, intraluminal thrombi enriched in neutrophils and platelets; and a lytic stage with residual thrombotic structures within necrotic areas (Fig. 1G–J). These stages frequently coexist within the same specimen, reflecting regional asynchrony rather than a linear sequence. The concurrent presence of intramural thrombosis and intestinal hemorrhage further indicates intense neutrophil–coagulation crosstalk, supporting dysregulated immunothrombosis as a core driver of NEC pathophysiology.

CD177⁺ NPAs as a nexus of thromboinflammation

We provide histopathological evidence of immunothrombosis in human NEC lesions, marked by microvascular occlusion, fibrin deposition, and pronounced NET release mediated by CD177⁺ neutrophils (Figs. 1–3). Prior work has implicated neutrophil–platelet aggregates (NPAs) in immunothrombotic dysfunction across inflammatory disorders21,22. Here, we show that the infiltrate in NEC is enriched for the CD177⁺ neutrophil subset; these cells activate platelets via NET formation, giving rise to CD177⁺ NPAs (Fig. 4). This mechanism differs from the more common neutrophil population forming platelet complexes23. By resolving neutrophil heterogeneity, we identify CD177⁺ cells as key mediators of NEC immunothrombosis and as a mechanistic basis for targeted cell therapies.

This thromboinflammatory axis exacerbates local hypoxia and ischemia, fueling coagulation imbalance and amplifying tissue injury. Quantitatively, CD177⁺ cells, NPAs, and NETs track with NEC severity, and CD177⁺ NPAs correlate with coagulopathy (Fig. 4). Thus, CD177⁺ NPAs are both informative biomarkers of coagulopathy and central drivers of NEC pathophysiology.

Building on these observations, we tested therapeutic strategies targeting CD177⁺ NPAs and NET formation in mouse NEC. Blockade of CD177⁺ NPAs attenuated disease progression, reduced immunothrombotic burden, and lessened intestinal injury. Likewise, NET-disrupting interventions (DNase I and PAD4 inhibition) decreased NET release and NPA formation, mitigating tissue damage and improving survival (Figs. 5, 6). Collectively, these data establish the CD177⁺ subset and NET-mediated immunothrombosis as pivotal in NEC and highlight targeted thromboinflammatory interventions as a promising therapeutic avenue.

Clinical Translation: From Mechanisms to Bedside

Potential diagnostic biomarker identification

These findings nominate early-warning indicators for disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) in NEC. Before conventional DIC criteria are met, elevated CD177⁺ neutrophils, NETs, and CD177⁺ neutrophil–platelet aggregates (NPAs) may serve as early biomarkers. After treatment, CD177⁺ NPA levels decline, supporting their potential utility for early warning, diagnosis, and prognostic prediction (Fig. 8). Further work should clarify how CD177⁺ NPAs reflect disease severity and hypercoagulability, and how best to integrate them with laboratory measures of coagulation and inflammation, intestinal pathology, and clinical/imaging data. Developing a multiparametric predictive model or risk score could enable timely intervention before overt DIC in NEC, reducing complications and mortality.

Innovation in intraoperative decision-making

Immunothrombosis reflects a coupled inflammatory–coagulant state driven by a self-perpetuating thromboinflammatory cycle24. Without timely therapy or surgery, affected bowel segments can progressively enlarge and undergo necrosis25, underscoring the need for precise delineation of diseased tissue. In NEC surgery, margin selection for resection, stoma creation, or anastomosis depends on accurate assessment of the involved zone (Supplementary Fig. 6)26. Suboptimal margins may contribute to poorer postoperative outcomes27. Building on our clinical and experimental data showing that treatment improves immunothrombotic dysfunction, we hypothesize that the burden of immunothrombosis—indexed by CD177⁺ NPA infiltration—is a significant, under-recognized determinant of prognosis.

Intraoperative indocyanine green (ICG) angiography provides a preliminary assessment of perfusion but is limited in visualizing the microvasculature and does not capture inflammatory infiltration, microthrombosis, or metabolic status28. We therefore propose integrating rapid intraoperative histopathological assessment, guided by the degree of inflammatory infiltration and immunothrombosis, to inform margin selection and surgical strategy. This combined physiological–pathological approach could enhance surgical accuracy and improve outcomes in NEC.

Exploring targeted anti-thromboinflammatory strategies

We propose that CD177⁺ neutrophil–platelet aggregates (NPAs) are candidate clinical biomarkers of therapeutic response in necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC). Based on our data, we hypothesize that prophylactic low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) mitigates immunothrombotic dysfunction, attenuates NEC-associated intestinal injury, and helps delineate underlying mechanisms29. Experimentally, LMWH reduced CD177⁺ NPA formation, interrupting the feed-forward thromboinflammatory cycle and preserving compromised intestinal tissue (Fig. 7). In high-risk neonates and preterm infants without contraindications, these findings support evaluating prophylactic LMWH to lower CD177⁺ NPA burden and potentially decrease NEC incidence. We have initiated a clinical study to validate these observations. Prior literature further supports prophylactic LMWH in preventing severe immunothrombotic states such as disseminated intravascular coagulation and sepsis30,31, reinforcing the rationale for this strategy in NEC prevention and treatment.

By positioning CD177⁺ NPAs at the intersection of intestinal ischemia, systemic inflammation, and coagulation, our study reframes NEC as a thromboinflammatory disorder. This framework links clinical features to outcomes via an immunothrombotic-dysfunction cycle and points to integrated immune–coagulation interventions as potential therapeutics. It may prompt re-evaluation of current NEC management and inspire analogous investigations in other neonatal inflammatory diseases.

Limitations and future directions

Our human data are predominantly derived from surgically resected, advanced NEC, emphasizing terminal thromboinflammatory phenotypes and limiting causal inference. Interventions focused on neutrophils, NETs, and CD177⁺ NPAs; other immune and stromal compartments were not systematically interrogated. Although LMWH and other antithrombotics emerge as promising preventive/therapeutic options, optimal dosing, timing, safety, and long-term effects in high-risk neonates remain uncertain. Future work should include longitudinal cohorts, multi-omic profiling, and prospective interventional trials to (i) define triggers of thromboinflammation, (ii) map cellular networks and crosstalk, (iii) validate CD177⁺ NPAs and NETs as biomarkers, and (iv) enable rational clinical translation of targeted anti-thromboinflammatory therapies in NEC.

Methods

Patient samples

Peripheral blood and tissue samples from patients were obtained from the Department of Neonatal Surgery at Guangzhou Women and Children’s Medical Center. All research was conducted in accordance with relevant ethical regulations, and the study protocol was approved by the Scientific Research Ethics Committee of Guangzhou Women and Children’s Medical Center (Approval No. 2023307B01). Written informed consent was obtained from the legal guardians of all participants. Peripheral blood samples were used for flow cytometry, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA), and single-cell sequencing. Tissue specimens collected from the same institution were used for immunofluorescence, histochemistry, single-cell sequencing, and ELISA. Detailed descriptions of the clinical samples are provided in Supplementary data 2.

Inclusion criteria: Control group: Peripheral blood samples were collected from infants matched to NEC patients by chronological age or corrected gestational age. Tissue samples were obtained from surgical cases of non-NEC intestinal diseases (e.g., congenital intestinal atresia, intestinal duplication, meconium ileus, or spontaneous intestinal perforation) with corrected gestational ages matched to those of NEC patients. Exclusion criteria: Infants with major congenital anomalies (e.g., chromosomal abnormalities or severe cardiac malformations) or those with an anticipated postnatal demise were excluded32,33.

Animals and NEC model

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with relevant ethical regulations and guidelines, and the study reporting complies with the ARRIVE guidelines. The animal protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Guangdong Huawei Testing Co., Ltd. (protocol no. 202312005) and by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Guangdong Huawei Testing Co., Ltd. Pregnant dams were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle, at an ambient temperature of 22 ± 1 °C and relative humidity of 50 ± 5%, with ad libitum access to sterilized food and water. C57BL/6 J mice were purchased from Zhuhai BesTest Biotechnology Co., Ltd. CD177⁻/⁻ mice were provided by Dr. Ruizhong Zhang (Guangzhou Women and Children’s Medical Center). CD177 conditional knockout mice (CD177-cKO) were generated by crossing CD177fl/fl mice with MRP8-Cre mice. NOG (NOD/Shi-scid/IL-2Rγnull) mice were obtained from Beijing Vital River Laboratory. Unless otherwise stated for individual experiments, mice were used at the ages indicated in each experiment. For the neonatal NEC model, 5–7-day-old neonatal mice were used; sex was not determined or considered in the study design and analysis because sex cannot be reliably ascertained at this age and is not expected to influence outcomes at this developmental stage. Therefore, data were not disaggregated by sex for the NEC experiments.

A neonatal mouse NEC model was established using 7-day-old pups, which were randomly allocated to either a dam-fed control (CTRL) group or an NEC induction group. The NEC induction protocol was conducted over four consecutive days. Each day, pups in the NEC group received the following treatments: oral gavage of lipopolysaccharide (LPS; 30 mg/kg; L2630, Sigma-Aldrich) at 8:00 AM after a fasting period, followed by artificial formula feeding (15 µL/g body weight) at 9:00 AM. Hypoxic–ischemic and cold stress were applied at 11:00 AM and 4:30 PM. Each stress cycle consisted of 10 min of hypoxia (5% O₂/95% N₂), 10 min of normoxic recovery, and 10 min of cold exposure at 4 °C. Additional formula feedings (15 µL/g) were administered at 3:00 PM and 9:00 PM, along with a second LPS gavage at 8:00 PM34.

Mouse intestinal cells preparation

The mouse intestine was excised, cleaned, opened longitudinally, and sequentially processed with EDTA, collagenase Ⅳ (Biotopped), and deoxyribonuclease Ⅱ (Biotopped) to obtain a single-cell suspension35.

Histopathology

Mice were euthanized with an isoflurane overdose on the fourth day after NEC induction. Following abdominal dissection, small intestinal tissue was collected. A 1 cm segment near the terminal ileum was isolated, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and subjected to standard hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. Intestinal injury was evaluated according to established NEC histopathological scoring criteria, which include five grades: grade 0, no injury; grade 1, mild separation of the lamina propria; grade 2, moderate separation of the submucosa; grade 3, severe separation and/or edema in the submucosa; and grade 4, transmural necrosis36.

Treatment administration

NEC mice received daily injections of the following interventions: a neutrophil-neutralizing antibody (1A8, 1 mg/kg), with an IgG isotype antibody as the corresponding control; DNase I (a NET-degrading enzyme, 15,000 units/kg), with 0.9% NaCl as the vehicle control; a PAD4 inhibitor (GSK484, 4 mg/kg), with 3% DMSO in PBS as the vehicle control; and low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH, 4 mg/kg), with 0.9% NaCl as the vehicle control.

Cell isolation and flow cytometry

Mouse. Mouse intestines were dissected and digested with EDTA, collagenase IV, and DNase II to obtain single-cell suspensions. For mouse flow cytometry, cells were stained with Ly6G, CD11b, CD45, CD177, and CD41 to identify CD177⁺ neutrophil–platelet aggregates (NPAs)37,38; cells were then permeabilized and stained intracellularly for citrullinated histone H3 (CitH3) to assess NETs38,39.

Human. Peripheral blood (0.5 mL) was collected from neonates with NEC and matched controls. For flow cytometry, whole blood was subjected to red blood cell lysis, followed by washing. The remaining cells were Fc-blocked and stained with antibodies against CD177, CD66b, and CD41a to detect CD177⁺ neutrophil–thrombotic complexes17,40. For platelet activation assays, whole blood was directly stained with anti-CD41 and anti-CD62P.

Flow cytometry data were acquired on a BD LSRFortessa and analyzed with FlowJo software. All fluorescence-minus-one (FMO) controls and gating strategies are provided in Supplementary Fig. 6.

Adoptive transfer of CD177+ neutrophils

CD177+ neutrophils were isolated from the spleens of mice using fluorescence-activated cell sorting, and 10⁶ cells were adoptively transferred into NOG mice on days 1 and 3 of NEC induction41. Control mice were injected with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Mice were sacrificed on day 4 post-induction for analyses.

Immunofluorescence

Paraffin sections (5 µm) were incubated at 65 °C for 2 h, deparaffinized in xylene, and rehydrated through graded ethanol solutions. Slides were treated in a 95 °C water bath for 20 min with Tris-EDTA antigen-retrieval solution (pH 9.0; Beyotime). After cooling for 30 min at room temperature, slides were washed three times with PBS for 5 min each. Nonspecific staining was blocked with 5% blocking solution (for goat-derived antibodies) for 1 h at room temperature. Sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the appropriate primary antibodies. The next day, sections were incubated with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-labeled secondary antibody for 50 min at room temperature in the dark, followed by incubation with 435-labeled tyramides for 10 min at room temperature in the dark. Slides were washed in PBS with 0.1% Tween-80 and underwent antigen retrieval using 1 × citrate antigen-retrieval solution (pH 6.0) in a microwave (high heat for 6 minutes, medium heat for 15 min), then cooled to room temperature to remove previously bound primary and secondary antibodies. Slides were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the second primary antibody, followed by HRP-labeled secondary antibody and Alexa Fluor Plus 647-labeled tyramides. The third primary antibody was labeled similarly, with the secondary antibody replaced by an Alexa Fluor™ 594-labeled secondary antibody for 10 minutes at room temperature in the dark. Nuclear counterstaining was performed with DAPI for 10 min at room temperature in the dark. Sections were imaged using a ZEN 2 (Blue Edition) laser-scanning confocal fluorescence microscope.

NETs staining

CD177⁺ neutrophils from the spleens of CTRL and NEC mice were isolated and activated with phorbol myristate acetate. NET formation was visualized by DAPI staining, and images were captured using a confocal fluorescence microscope37.

ELISA measurement protocol

Analytes were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA). Plasma (20 μl) and intestinal tissue samples were collected and centrifuged to remove cellular components; the resulting plasma or tissue homogenates were stored at − 80 °C until analysis. Commercial human ELISA kits were used according to manufacturers’ instructions to quantify D-dimer, fibrinogen, platelet factor 4, elastase, DNA–elastase complexes, and citrullinated histone H3 (CitH3). Mouse samples were assayed for IL-6, IL-1β, TNF, MPO–DNA complexes, and CitH3 according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All assays were conducted following standardized procedures to ensure accuracy and reproducibility.

Data processing and analysis

scRNA-seq data from mouse neutrophils were processed with Cell Ranger (v5.1.0) and analyzed with Seurat (v4.3.0). Principal component analysis and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) were used for dimensionality reduction, and differential gene-expression analysis was performed using the Wilcoxon test. Gene clusters were annotated based on canonical marker genes.

Availability of sequencing data in the National Genomics Data Center (CNCB)

All data have been uploaded to the National Genomics Data Center (CNCB). The human single-cell data are identified by accession numbers OMIX012457, OMIX012413, and OMIX008000. The mouse data are identified by accession number GSA: CRA018983.

Statistical analysis

Results are presented as individual data points with medians. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Normality was assessed for all datasets. Two-group comparisons used an unpaired t test or the Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. Multiple-group comparisons used one-way analysis of variance with Holm–Sidak post hoc testing or the Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn’s post hoc test. Correlations were assessed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient or Spearman’s rank-correlation coefficient for parameters within the NEC patient group.
