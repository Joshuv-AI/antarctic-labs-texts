Management of sudden severe artery stenosis during transcatheter aortic valve replacement: a case report of rare complications caused by failed suture of severely tortuous femoral artery
Authors: Qi Cheng, Long Zhao, Xiaomei Li, Yitong Ma, Zixiang Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVascular closure devices are commonly used instruments in TAVR, and severe vascular stenosis or occlusion is a serious complication. Therefore, the correct use of vascular suturing devices and the timely management of complications are particularly important.Case summaryWe report a case of severe femoral artery stenosis that occurred after the use of a vascular closure device during transfemoral transcatheter aortic valve replacement. This is due to the severe curvature of the patient's blood vessels and the incorrect tactile feedback when using ProGlide, which in turn led to iatrogenic severe vascular stenosis. The cause of this complication is rare. By implanting a lower limb vascular stent, the blood flow in the lower limb was quickly restored.DiscussionOur experience reminds operators to be more cautious when using ProGlide in patients with severely tortuous blood vessels. Once severe vascular stenosis or acute vascular occlusion occurs, vascular interventional therapy should be carried out as early and as soon as possible.

Introduction

The femoral artery is the preferred access route for transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR), while vascular closure devices (VCDs) are utilized in the standard haemostasis technique.1 ProGlide is a commonly used VCD in TAVR that reduces blood loss and shortens vascular compression time. Severe stenosis/occlusion of the femoral artery is one of the serious complications associated with the use of this device.2 The occurrence of vascular complications significantly increases patient hospitalization duration and mortality.3 To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of severe tortuous blood vessel stenosis caused by the utilization of a vascular suture device that was followed by successful stenosis relief.

Case presentation

A 79-year-old male patient was admitted to the hospital with the chief complaint of ‘intermittent chest tightness for 15 years that was aggravated by chest pain for one month’. The patient was previously diagnosed with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and presented with acute exacerbation at this time. Oxygen inhalation and anti-infection treatment were administered during the hospitalization. The patient also had a history of primary hypertension and coronary atherosclerotic heart disease. He was diagnosed with severe aortic valve stenosis based on a transthoracic echocardiogram that was completed upon admission. The patient’s EuroSCORE II of 2.35% indicated moderate surgical risk. With consideration of the patient's personal preference, the cardiac valve team recommended that he undergo TAVR.

A 23-mm prosthetic valve (Edwards Lifesciences, Irvine, CA, USA) was used. The transaortic valve gradient decreased to 6 mmHg immediately after the procedure. Angiography showed no paravalvular leak or valve regurgitation.

The 14F right femoral artery sheath was withdrawn after valve implantation. The pre-implanted Perclose ProGlide (Abbott Vascular, CA, USA) was intended for knotting to achieve haemostasis. However, three sequential attempts with the suture device failed to achieve successful closure. Significant resistance was encountered during device withdrawal. Repeat angiography revealed severe stenosis in the tortuous segment of the right femoral artery (Figure 1A).

A crossover sheath was subsequently used to navigate via the left femoral artery to the proximal end of the right occluded site. Bidirectional femoral artery angiography showed extensive contrast extravasation at the right femoral artery puncture site and severe stenosis at the tortuous segment of the femoral artery (Figure 1B). It was hypothesized that the ProGlide closure system incorrectly sutured at the tortuous site, leading to iatrogenic stenosis at the tortuous vascular segment with persistent vascular breach at the access site.

A pre-implanted forward guidewire was then used as an intravascular guide. A reverse-controlled retrograde technique was used to facilitate guidewire passage through the severe stenosis (Figure 1C). The retrograde guidewire eventually crossed the stenosis, and a Fluency Plus Vascular Stent Graft (8.0 × 80 mm; Becton Dickinson, Karlsruhe, Germany) was deployed at the stenotic site (Figure 2A). Repeat angiography revealed significant improvement in the right femoral artery stenosis and no contrast extravasation at the femoral artery breach (Figure 2B).

Discussion

Severe femoral artery stenosis/occlusion during TAVR may be caused by thrombosis, arterial dissection, or arterial embolism. However, the present case differed from these aetiologies in the following aspects: (i) a comprehensive aorto-arterial computed tomography angiography (CTA) was performed in preoperative evaluation to exclude femoral artery thrombosis, while cardiac ultrasound and electrocardiogram also eliminated embolic sources, and (ii) based on angiography findings, thrombosis typically showed irregular filling defects along the vessel axis with possible contrast retention. However, the present case revealed only localized annular stenosis at the severely tortuous segment of the right femoral artery, matching the ProGlide suture site. No classic thrombosis sign (filling defects/retention), arterial dissection (intimal flap/double lumen), or embolism (sudden interruption) was observed, distinguishing the present case from these conditions, and (iii) the patient’s activated clotting time was maintained at 290 s during the procedure.

The design limitations of ProGlide and biomechanical alterations in tortuous vessels may have contributed to this iatrogenic femoral artery stenosis. Severe vascular tortuosity can lead to axial deviation of the vessel, radial wall stress inhomogeneity, and ProGlide sheath tip deformation.4 These then resulted in misalignment between the ProGlide needle and the puncture site, as well as distorted tactile feedback (where resistance caused by vascular tortuosity was misinterpreted as needle contact with the vessel wall), ultimately leading to mechanical folding of the vessel wall. Bradley et al.5 identified arterial diameter, vascular calcification, insufficient operator experience, improper puncture (puncture angle of <45°), obesity, and anatomical abnormalities as risk factors for ProGlide suture failure. However, they did not elaborate on the specific mechanisms by which anatomical abnormalities contribute to suture failure. The present case confirmed that severe tortuosity was not an ambiguous anatomical risk, but rather a quantifiable biomechanical challenge. Furthermore, distinct aetiologies for complications associated with VCDs were identified. Unlike the thrombosis caused by collagen leakage documented by Liu et al.4, the observed mechanical stenosis highlighted the incompatibility between device design and anatomical structures.

Acute severe femoral stenosis occurred immediately after failed ProGlide suturing, risking limb ischaemia. Angiography confirmed localized stenosis solely at the tortuous right femoral segment without calcification and occlusion, providing anatomical basis for stenting. Femoral stent implantation effectively restored perfusion and minimized surgical risk. Grossman et al.6 followed 56 patients who received iliofemoral covered stents during TAVR for 2 months to 5 years. None of the patients required reintervention for the affected limb, and no one developed claudication. This suggests that iliofemoral stent implantation is a safe and effective treatment for vascular access sites and access-related complications during transfemoral TAVR.

Due to the anatomical characteristics of the inguinal ligament and joint mobility, the risk of rupture of the femoral artery stent in the inguinal region was undeniable. It has been our practice to always instruct the patients to avoid squatting at large angles. A lower extremity ultrasound follow-up on the femoral artery stenosis stent performed at 30 days postoperatively revealed a patent vascular stent with no significant restenosis in the present case. The patient had no claudication.

Conclusions

The present case report for the first time identified severe femoral artery tortuosity as the direct cause of iatrogenic stenosis associated with Perclose ProGlide. A novel mechanism was also elucidated, where the pin fixation design of the device and reliance on tactile feedback were incompatible with the biomechanical changes in severely tortuous vessels, leading to suture displacement at the tortuous segment rather than the puncture site, ultimately resulting in severe complications. The recommendations for such patients are as follows: (i) preoperative total aortic CTA should be performed to quantify the degree of vascular tortuosity to stratify the risk of ProGlide use; (ii) intraoperative resistance during ProGlide manipulation in tortuous vessels necessitates immediate angiography to rule out suture displacement; and (iii) future ProGlide optimization may involve the use of radiopaque markers on the pins, adjustable needle angles, and other methods to enhance device safety in complex vascular anatomies. The present case redefined the understanding of VCD failure in tortuous vessels and provided evidence-based guidance for TAVR access management in patients with complex vascular anatomy.
