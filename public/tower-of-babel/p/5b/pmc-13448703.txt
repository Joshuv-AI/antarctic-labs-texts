Superhydrophobic PTFE/MWCNT coatings fabricated by supercritical fluid processing with controlled wettability
Authors: S. Rajiv, S. Murugesh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Superhydrophobic PTFE/MWCNT composite coatings were fabricated via supercritical fluid processing followed by air-spray deposition to control hierarchical surface architectures. The composites were prepared at PTFE: MWCNT mass ratios (w/w) of 10:1, 10:2, and 10:3 (corresponding to ~ 9.1–23.1 wt% MWCNT) to conduct a more systematic evaluation of the influence of nanotube loading on surface morphology and wettability. Structural and chemical analyses (Raman, FTIR, XRD) confirmed the successful incorporation of MWCNTs without compromising the integrity of the PTFE matrix. Morphological characterization (FESEM, HRTEM, and AFM) confirmed the formation of interconnected micro–nano hierarchical features governed by nanotube dispersion and concentration. All coatings demonstrated superhydrophobic characteristics, with the highest water contact angle measuring 156.3° observed at the optimal 10:2 (w/w) composition. This performance is attributed to an optimal balance between surface roughness, uniform nanotube dispersion, and effective air entrapment within the hierarchical structure. The modulation of wettability was extended to the number of spray passes, with the second coating cycle being the most effective in surface architecture. Such observed behavior is supported by a structure–wettability correlation based on a hierarchical morphology and air fraction. These results demonstrate that supercritical fluid processing enables a coherent control of the nanotube dispersion, scalable fabrication of PTFE-based superhydrophobic coatings, and a robust processing–structure–property paradigm for designing advanced water-repellent surfaces.

Introduction

Carbon nanostructures for controlling wettability on the surface have long been an active area of interest for the scientific community because of their applications in self-cleaning, water-repellent, anticorrosion, and functional surfaces1–13. Among different approaches for modifying the properties of a surface, the development of a superhydrophobic coating based on the integration of a low-surface-energy materials component with a hierarchical roughness component on the surface has emerged as a successful method14,15. Carbon nanomaterials with unique morphology, stability, and modifiable surfaces play an important role in the formation of such hierarchical structures on the surface.

Polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) has long been at the forefront of fluoropolymer research and applications owing to its extremely low surface energy, along with chemical inertness and thermal stability16–19. While PTFE is intrinsically hydrophobic, a WCA of about 110° for flat PTFE surfaces is not high enough to achieve superhydrophobicity20. The absence of appropriate surface roughness and micro/nano-scale hierarchical structure restricts the wettability performances of pristine PTFE coatings. Incorporating nanoscale fillers into the PTFE matrix thus represents an effective approach to enhance surface roughness and wettability without sacrificing the intrinsic low surface energy of the polymer21,22.

Multi-walled Carbon Nanotubes (MWCNTs) are extremely valuable because of their high aspect ratio, very large specific surface area, excellent mechanical strength, and the fact that they are made of graphitic carbon23–25. They can be used as structural scaffolds when they are in a polymer matrix. MWCNTs promote and support the creation of interconnected networks of nanotubes as well as hierarchical surface features. Previous research indicates that Carbon Nanotube-Polymer composites have greater surface roughness and better interfacial interaction and have tunable wettability properties than pure polymeric materials26. However, homogeneous dispersion of MWCNTs in polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) represents a major challenge since nanotube agglomeration will severely impact the wettability of the composite and the surface morphology27–29.

Several fabrication strategies for PTFE–MWCNT composite coatings have been reported, including solution mixing, melt blending, and supercritical fluid processing30,31. Among these, supercritical fluid processing has emerged as a highly effective approach for improving nanotube dispersion and interfacial interactions while avoiding the limitations associated with conventional solvent- or melt-based techniques. The distinct transport properties of supercritical fluids allow for the improvement of wetting, penetration, and deagglomeration of nanofillers into the polymer matrix, which makes this method especially attractive for the fabrication of composite coatings with uniform and reproducible properties11. Thus, the investigation of MWCNT loading on surface morphology, microstructure, and wettability is particularly important in conditions of supercritical fluid fabrication32–34.

The MWCNT content does not linearly affect the wettability of the MWCNT-reinforced polymer composites. At low MWCNT loadings, inadequate surface roughness hinders water repellency. At an optimal concentration, MWCNTs promote the formation of micro-/nano-hierarchical structures that stabilize the Cassie–Baxter wetting state, resulting in a significant increase in water contact angle35–37. In contrast, excessive nanotube loading leads to agglomeration, exposure of higher-surface-energy carbon domains, and surface nonuniformity, which collectively degrade wettability performance. Establishing this structure–property relationship is essential for the rational design of PTFE-based superhydrophobic coatings38.

In this work, PTFE/MWCNT composite coatings are prepared by a supercritical fluid–assisted processing route followed by air-spray deposition. The primary focus is to explain how MWCNT loading influences surface morphology, microstructural features, and wettability behavior of PTFE-based coatings prepared under supercritical conditions. The research was conducted in a systematic way using PTFE: MWCNT weight ratios such as 10:1, 10:2, and 10:3. The structural and morphological characteristics of the coatings were characterized using Raman spectroscopy, FT-IR, XRD, FESEM, HRTEM, and AFM. Moreover, the wettability performance was assessed by static water contact angle measurement by critically focusing on the role of MWCNT loading and number of air-spray coating passes. This work presents practical aspects to optimize PTFE/MWCNT composite coatings for superhydrophobic applications using a scalable supercritical fluid processing route.

Materials and methods

Materials and preparation of PTFE/MWCNT composite coatings

PTFE powder, with an average particle size of approximately 100 μm, and multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs) were procured from Sigma-Aldrich, India. FRP sheets supplied by Swasthick Scientific Corporation, Karaikudi, India, were used as substrates for coating deposition. Analytical-grade isopropanol and acetone, used as dispersion solvents and cleaning agents, were procured from Merck, India, and used as received without further purification.

The FRP substrates were mechanically polished in orthogonal directions using fine-grade silicon carbide abrasive sheets to obtain a uniform surface texture. The polished substrates were ultrasonically cleaned in distilled water for 5 min to remove residual particulates and contaminants, followed by drying under ambient conditions.

PTFE/MWCNT composite formulations have been prepared employing a supercritical fluid processing approach combined with homogenous dispersion. The compositions were defined as weight ratios (w/w) of PTFE to MWCNT, such as 10:1, 10:2, and 10:3 (corresponding to approximately 9.1, 16.7, and 23.1 wt% MWCNT, respectively – calculated based on total composite mass). For each formulation, proper amounts of PTFE and MWCNTs were dispersed in isopropanol and subjected to ultrasonication at room temperature to ensure homogeneous distribution of nanotubes in the polymer matrix.

The slurry obtained was transferred to a 35 mL stainless steel high-pressure reactor vessel and sealed. The reactor was then put in a 300 °C preheated vertical tubular furnace and exposed to supercritical conditions for one hour. The processing was done at a temperature and pressure above that of critical isopropanol (Tc ≈ 235 °C, Pc ≈ 4.8 MPa), and the internal pressure of the sealed reactor was said to be approximately ~ 8–12 MPa given that pressure generation was autogenous, hence ensuring a supercritical environment. Upon completion of the treatment, the reactor was quenched in an ice–water bath to stop further processing and ‘freeze’ the composite’s structure. The suspension, as recovered from this process, was subjected to centrifugation at 2000 rotations per minute for 15 min to isolate the solid phase, which contained solidified PTFE–MWCNT composites.

The collected composite material was subsequently redispersed and applied onto pretreated FRP substrates using a spray-coating system equipped with a pneumatic nozzle. Spray deposition was carried out at a fixed nozzle-to-substrate distance under constant pressure and flow conditions, with multiple passes to ensure complete surface coverage. The coated substrates were allowed to dry at ambient temperature to remove residual solvent and were then cured at 100 °C for 2 h to improve film consolidation and adhesion to the substrate.

Characterization techniques

The surface morphology and microstructural features of the prepared PTFE/MWCNT composite coatings were examined using field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM, Carl Zeiss AG, Supra 55VP). High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM, Tecnai G2 TF20) was employed to investigate the dispersion state of MWCNTs within the PTFE matrix and to analyze interfacial interactions at the nanoscale.

For HRTEM analysis, a small amount of the composite material was redispersed in isopropanol and sonicated until a well-dispersed suspension was obtained. The concentrated suspension was deposited on a carbon-coated copper grid and dried at room temperature. Samples were prepared to obtain regions thin enough to be electron transparent (~ 100 nm and below), limiting nanotube agglomeration and suitable for high-resolution imaging.

Surface topography and roughness characteristics were quantified using atomic force microscopy (AFM, Agilent 5500) operated in non-contact mode. Three-dimensional surface profiles were obtained, and average roughness (Ra) values were calculated to evaluate the influence of MWCNT loading on surface hierarchy.

Raman spectroscopic analysis was performed using a Renishaw Raman microscope to confirm the presence of MWCNTs within the composite coatings and to examine interactions between the nanotubes and the PTFE matrix. The characteristic functional groups of PTFE were identified using FT-IR spectroscopy (TENSOR 27 spectrometer), which was also used to assess the surface functionalities of MWCNTs and the chemical integrity of the composite coatings. X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements were carried out using a Bruker D8 ADVANCE diffractometer to investigate the crystalline structure of PTFE and the incorporation of MWCNTs into the composite.

Characterization of surface wettability was done by static water contact angle (WCA) measurements, using Data Physics OCA-35 contact angle analyzer. The contact angle was then measured at room temperature using video recording. In each sample, measurements were made at different points, with at least three distinct measurements per sample. Reported WCA values represent the mean ± standard deviation (statistical significance). Variation in WCA with MWCNT content was analyzed to construct a structure–wettability relationship of the composite coatings.

Results and discussion

Raman spectroscopy analysis

Figure 1 presents the Raman spectra of (a) PTFE, (b) MWCNT, and (c) PTFE/MWCNT composite coating. The Raman spectrum of pure PTFE (Fig. 1a) shows characteristic vibrational bands at ~ 732 cm−1, ~ 1215 cm−1, and ~ 1305 cm−1, which are attributed to the symmetric and asymmetric stretching vibrations of CF₂ groups, confirming the polymer’s chemical structure39.

The Raman spectrum of MWCNTs (Fig. 1b) exhibits two prominent bands: the D band at ~ 1350 cm−1, associated with defect-induced disorder in sp² carbon structures, and the G band at ~ 1580 cm−1, corresponding to the in-plane stretching vibration of graphitic sp² carbon. A weak shoulder around ~ 1610 cm−1 (D′ band) and the presence of the 2D (G′) band near ~ 2700 cm−1 further confirm the graphitic nature and multi-walled structure of the carbon nanotubes40.

Figure 1c shows the Raman spectra of the PTFE/MWCNT composite coating. From these spectra, features belonging to both PTFE and MWCNTs appear. The coappearance of the CF2-related PTFE bands and characteristic D, G, and 2D bands of MWCNTs ensures the presence of carbon nanotubes in the PTFE matrix. No noticeable shift or significant broadening of the D and G bands is observed after composite formation, indicating preservation of the structural integrity of MWCNTs during fabrication. The effective dispersion achieved through supercritical fluid processing can be attributed to the high diffusivity and penetration capability of the supercritical medium, which assists in separating nanotube agglomerates. The dispersed nanotubes form an interconnected network within the PTFE matrix, contributing to the development of hierarchical surface roughness.

FTIR spectroscopy analysis

The FT-IR spectra of three samples, including raw PTFE (a), raw MWCNTs (b) and the PTFE/MWCNT composite coating (c), are presented in Fig. 2. A broad range of absorption (1100–1200 cm −1) characteristic of stretching C-F bonds is observed in the raw PTFE, which validates the existence of the chains of fluoropolymers41. Additionally, a broad band appears at 3400 cm− 1 because of O-H stretching, and at 1700 cm− 1 in the MWCNT instance, linked to C-O stretching. This indicates the presence of oxygen-containing surface groups on the carbon nanotubes42. These oxygen-containing functional groups (such as –OH and –COOH) play an important role in enhancing the interfacial interaction between MWCNTs and the PTFE matrix. Although no new chemical bonding is observed, these groups improve interfacial compatibility by promoting better wetting and adhesion of PTFE onto the nanotube surface, resulting in improved dispersion and mechanical interlocking within the composite.

The FT-IR spectrum of the PTFE/MWCNT composite coating shows characteristic bands of both PTFE and MWCNTs, confirming the successful incorporation of nanotubes into the PTFE matrix. The persistence of C–F stretching bands indicates that the chemical structure of PTFE remains unchanged after composite formation, and no new absorption bands are observed, confirming the absence of chemical reactions between PTFE and MWCNTs. The chemical structure does not change, but the addition of MWCNTs can physically alter PTFE molecular chains. For the moderate MWCNT loading, heterogeneous nucleation sites stimulate better alignment of molecular chains CF2, which lowers surface energy slightly. The higher multi-walled carbon nanotube (MWCNT) loading leads to more crowding, which disrupts the chain packing and introduces a degree of local disorder in the chain molecular arrangement, resulting in insignificant changes to surface energy. Although oxygen-containing functional groups may slightly increase the intrinsic surface energy of MWCNTs, their influence on wettability is minimized by the dominant low-surface-energy PTFE matrix. These results confirm that the PTFE/MWCNT composite can maintain chemical integrity, while allowing for beneficial interfacial interaction and structural organization, which are able to support the associated wettability performance.

XRD analysis

The XRD patterns of (a) PTFE, (b) MWCNT and (c) PTFE/MWCNT composite coating are presented in Fig. 3. This pattern of the diffraction of pure PTFE (a) clearly shows a sharp and strong peak at approximately 18°, which is the (100) crystallographic plane, thus making it semi-crystalline43. A typical diffraction peak of the MWCNT sample (b) is observed at a position of 26 ° which is attributed to the plane (002) of graphitic carbon, which proves the graphitic structure of the nanotubes44.

The XRD pattern of the PTFE/MWCNT composite coating (Fig. 3c) shows the diffraction peaks of both phases clearly, confirming the presence of PTFE and MWCNTs in their crystalline forms within the composite. The presence of the PTFE (100) reflection together with the peak of the MWCNT (002) shows that the incorporation of nanotubes does not change the crystalline structure of the PTFE matrix. No additional diffraction peaks are observed, implying no secondary phases and thus confirming the physical nature of PTFE–MWCNT interaction. The presence of MWCNT (002) diffraction peaks also ensures that the graphitic structure of the nanotubes is maintained, along with its crystallinity during the process of composite fabrication. The graphitization degree of MWCNTs plays a critical role in governing surface morphology, as highly crystalline nanotubes maintain their rigidity and high aspect ratio, facilitating the formation of stable micro–nano surface asperities. This contributes to improving surface roughness, which is instrumental to enhanced air entrapment under Cassie–Baxter wetting regime. At moderate loading, MWCNTs can induce heterogeneous nucleation of polymer chains thus encouraging localized ordering. On the other hand, at higher nanotube loading, limited chain mobility and nanotube crowding may restrict crystal growth leading to slight non-uniformity in crystallinity. Hence, the retained graphitic structure of MWCNTs not only ensures structural integrity but also contributes to the formation of hierarchical surface features associated with superhydrophobic performance.

HRTEM analysis

Figure 4 shows the HRTEM images of the PTFE/MWCNT composite coating at various magnifications. The low-magnification image in Fig. 4a demonstrates an interconnected network of entangled MWCNTs that forms a continuous conductive and reinforcing framework within the composite. These nanotubes exhibit a high aspect ratio, and their relatively homogeneous dispersion at moderate loading leads to the formation of a hierarchical micro–nano structure. This interconnected network also acts as a three-dimensional scaffold during spray film formation, guiding the deposition and organization of PTFE and thereby regulating the development of hierarchical surface features.

In contrast to the polymer phase surrounding the carbon tubular structures, a high-resolution image in Fig. 4b distinctly verifies the presence of MWCNTs integrated into the PTFE matrix. Thus, there is a conformal coating of PTFE around the nanotubes resulting in a core–shell like structure with intimate interfacial contact. This coating imposes steric hindrance, increasing the interparticle separation distance, D, with van der Waals attractive forces, F ∝ 1/D2, suppressed resulting in the mitigation of nanotube agglomeration upon spray deposition. There is no evidence for MWCNT wall damage or collapse, verifying the structural integrity of MWCNTs.

At the optimal loading (10:2), sufficient PTFE coverage ensures effective steric stabilization and uniform dispersion. The nanotube network is well interconnected and evenly distributed, enabling the formation of consistent micro–nano asperities that promote efficient air entrapment at the solid–liquid interface. In contrast, at lower loading (10:1), the network is sparse and insufficient to support continuous hierarchical structure formation, resulting in limited roughness and reduced air trapping capability.

At higher loading (10:3), the available PTFE per nanotube decreases, leading to incomplete surface coverage and reduced steric protection. This limitation, combined with increased nanotube collision frequency (∝ φ², where φ is nanotube volume fraction), promotes localized re-agglomeration during spray-induced shear and solvent evaporation. Consequently, excessive network densification and agglomeration disrupt uniform feature formation and reduce effective pore connectivity.

These results demonstrate that while the PTFE-coated core–shell structure effectively suppresses agglomeration at moderate loading, its stabilizing effect becomes insufficient at higher nanotube concentrations due to reduced polymer coverage and increased interparticle interactions. The optimal balance between dispersion and network connectivity at the 10:2 composition enables stable hierarchical structure formation and enhanced air entrapment, which directly contributes to the observed superhydrophobic and anti-icing performance.

FESEM analysis

The FESEM micrographs of the PTFE/MWCNT composite coatings with varying MWCNT loadings are presented in Fig. 5. For the nanotube loading of 10:1 (Fig. 5a), the surface is dominated by a loosely connected nanotube network, with relatively large spaces or voids visible, indicating that at this loading of MWCNTs, there is incomplete coverage of the PTFE matrix by MWCNTs. When the nanotube loading is increased from 10:1 to 10:2 (Fig. 5b), the nanotube network becomes more continuous and denser. The MWCNTs appear to be uniformly distributed, resulting in increased surface roughness and the formation of interconnected hierarchical surface features. These observations indicate improved interfacial contact between PTFE and MWCNTs.

At the highest MWCNT loading of 10:3 (Fig. 5c), the surface is characterised by a dense interweaving of nanotubes, leading to a considerably rough and porous architecture. Whereas the nanotube network is well developed, a localized agglomeration can be seen in certain regions, indicating partial nanotube clustering at higher loading. These agglomerates occupy a limited surface fraction and therefore do not dominate the overall morphology, while the surrounding regions still maintain interconnected rough surface features favorable for air retention.

A comparison of the various FESEM images shows the dispersion efficiency at different levels of MWCNT loading. At 10:1, nanotubes are rather well separated but form a relatively sparse network with few interconnections. However, at 10:2, a relatively uniform and interconnected nanotube network is observed with limited agglomeration, indicating improved dispersion within the PTFE matrix. Conversely, at 10:3, more frequent collisions are observed due to higher nanotube numbers, accompanied by fewer polymers per nanotube, suggesting localized agglomeration and a collapse in dispersion homogeneity. These observations suggest that supercritical fluid processing improves nanotube dispersion at intermediate loading conditions.

To quantify the observed morphology, FESEM images were analyzed using image processing (threshold-based segmentation) to estimate surface porosity and corresponding solid fraction. The 10:2 PTFE/MWCNT coating exhibited an optimal combination of interconnected nanotube network and uniformly distributed pores, whereas the 10:1 composition showed limited porosity and the 10:3 composition exhibited localized agglomeration leading to non-uniform pore distribution. These semi-quantitative observations further support that dispersion efficiency is maximized at the 10:2 composition, where both pore uniformity and network continuity are optimized.

To further relate morphology with wettability, the estimated surface porosity was correlated with air entrapment at the solid–liquid interface. The interconnected nanotube morphology observed at the 10:2 composition is favorable for partial air retention at the solid–liquid interface. In contrast, insufficient porosity at 10:1 and non-uniform pore distribution at 10:3 reduce the efficiency of air trapping. These observations indicate a correlation between nanotube network morphology, surface porosity, and wettability behavior.

AFM analysis

Figure 6 shows AFM surface topography of PTFE/MWCNT composite coatings prepared at three different MWCNT loadings. For the coating with a 10:1 ratio (Fig. 6a), there is a relatively low roughness shown on the surface with limited height difference. The surface topographies are rare and show that the quantity of MWCNTs is not enough to alter the PTFE surface morphology to a great extent. Its corresponding average roughness (Ra) value is relatively low, which limits air entrapment and corresponds to comparatively lower WCA.

In the case of the 10:2 ratio, Fig. 6b shows a notably increased surface roughness factor with a more homogeneous distribution of features on the surface. The AFM images show well-dispersed asperities throughout the scanned area, indicating improved incorporation of MWCNTs within the PTFE matrix. The increase in Ra at this composition reflects the formation of a relatively uniform hierarchical surface structure, which enhances air trapping at the solid–liquid interface and leads to the highest WCA observed. This composition presents a more consistent surface texture relative to the 10:1 coating.

The surface topography for the highest MWCNT loading of 10:3 (Fig. 6c) reveals a significant height variation and closely spaced peaks and valleys. The surface demonstrates lower uniformity despite the anticipated roughness at higher nanotube ratios. The reduced uniformity is attributed to localized nanotube agglomeration at higher loading. Therefore, although the Ra value remains high or may slightly increase, the roughness becomes non-uniform, which reduces the effectiveness of air pocket formation. Therefore, the increase in roughness at 10:3 is not reflected in a proportional increase in WCA. Overall, comparative AFM analysis demonstrates that the 10:2 PTFE/MWCNT coating offers a better balance of the degree of surface roughness and uniformity. This indicates that both the magnitude of roughness (Ra) and its uniformity are critical to further stabilising the Cassie–Baxter state for maximum superhydrophobic performance.

Wettability analysis

Figure 7a–c presents the WCA images of PTFE/MWCNT composite coatings prepared using different loadings of MWCNT. A WCA as high as 154.5° is observed for the coating with a 10:1 ratio, which indicates superhydrophobicity arising from the synergistic effects of low surface energy of PTFE and the surface roughness introduced by MWCNT addition. Despite the relatively loose MWCNT network at this composition, the surface is still capable of sustaining a Cassie–Baxter wetting state. The presence of micro-scale roughness and large voids within the network facilitates air entrapment at the solid–liquid interface, thereby reducing the effective solid–liquid contact area and enabling superhydrophobic behavior even at lower nanotube loading.

An increase in the amount of MWCNT to a 10:2 ratio leads to a further increase in the WCA to 156.3°, the highest among the three analyzed compositions. This is due to the establishment of a more appropriate hierarchical structure that gives rise to enhanced air entrapment at the solid–liquid interface. However, the increase in WCA from 10:1 to 10:2 is relatively small (~ 1.8°), indicating that once the Cassie–Baxter state is established, further improvements in roughness or nanotube content result in only marginal enhancement in wettability due to a saturation effect in air fraction.

The WCA slightly drops to 155.2° for the coating with the highest MWCNT loading (10:3, Fig. 7c), but the surface remains superhydrophobic. Localized nanotube aggregation is likely responsible for the slight decrease in WCA; however, the reduction is not significant because the overall surface still maintains a hierarchical micro–nano structure that supports the Cassie–Baxter wetting state. The influence of these agglomerates can be quantitatively understood in terms of their surface coverage fraction. Although agglomerates are present at the 10:3 composition, their area fraction remains relatively low compared to the total surface, and therefore the effective solid fraction (fₛ) is still governed by the surrounding hierarchical structure. While larger agglomerates may locally reduce air trapping efficiency, their low distribution density prevents significant disruption of the overall air fraction. Since the wettability is governed by the area-averaged surface structure, most of the surface still supports stable air pocket formation, preserving the Cassie–Baxter state. The contact angle thus only marginally decreases, and the coating remains superhydrophobic.

Supercritical fluid processing allows for the uniform dispersion of MWCNTs and the controlled development of hierarchical roughness to explain the invariance in wettability behavior across compositions. The best balance between surface roughness and nanotube distribution is observed for the 10:2 PTFE/MWCNT coating, resulting in improved superhydrophobic performance. The quantification provided by the Cassie–Baxter model further substantiates the improved wettability at the 10:2 composition, suggesting that the interplay between the solid fraction and surface porosity optimizes entrapment of air and stabilizes the superhydrophobic state.

Surface hydrophobicity analysis

Figure 8 shows the variation of water contact angle (WCA) with the number of air-spray coatings for PTFE/MWCNT composite coatings at different compositions (10:1, 10:2, and 10:3 (w/w)). Each value is the average along with a standard deviation obtained at several locations of each sample. All coatings attain a WCA value of over 145° (Fig. 8), confirming the superhydrophobicity of all the coatings.

With the 10:1 (w/w) composition, WCA values decrease slightly with the increasing spray passes, indicating a limited development of hierarchical roughness at low nanotubes content. This is attributed to PTFE matrix leveling, where excess polymer fills surface voids and smoothens asperities, effectively reducing roughness and the entrapment of air.

For the 10:2 (w/w) composition, WCA values increase between the first and second spray passes but slightly decreases on the third pass. The first coating creates a micro-structured template, while the second spray pass introduces nanoscale surface variation, resulting in a hierarchical structure favorable for air entrapment. Additional coating passes result in partial smoothing of surface features and WCA slightly drops.

In the case of the 10:3 (w/w) composition, the hierarchical structure is already sufficiently developed, with additional spray passes simply supporting already existing roughness, which is reflected in relatively stable WCA values with minimum variation. Those results show that wettability depends on both MWCNT content and the number of spray coatings. However, 10:2 (w/w) composition after the second spray pass provides the best-suited surface condition for superhydrophobic performance.

Structure–wettability correlation

To establish a quantitative relationship between surface morphology and wettability, the Cassie–Baxter model was applied, where apparent contact angle depends on solid fraction (fₛ) and the trapped air at the interface. Based on FESEM image analysis, the solid–liquid contact fraction (f1) for the 10:2 PTFE/MWCNT coating was estimated to be in the range of ~ 0.55–0.65. Hence, the solid–air fraction (f₂ = 1 − f₁) is found to be ~ 0.35–0.45, suggesting a significant amount of trapped air at the interface. The apparent contact angle of the Cassie theory was then estimated using an intrinsic contact angle for PTFE of about 110° to be in the range of ~ 125–135° which was less than the experimental observation of 156.3°. This balance of f₁ and f₂ is critical to stabilize the Cassie–Baxter state, minimizing liquid–solid contact while providing sufficient structural support for the droplet to maintain a high apparent contact angle.

The formation of this air-trapping structure is highly controlled by the interconnected MWCNT network that serves as a three-dimensional scaffold during spray deposition. This network controls the spatial distribution of PTFE and nanotubes, which promotes the formation of hierarchical micro–nano features required to stabilize the Cassie–Baxter state. At 10:2 composition, the network is at its best in connectivity and uniformity of distribution, thus creating relatively uniform surface asperities and interconnected pores favorable for air retention. Below this concentration, the network is disconnected and inadequate to sustain a continuous form of hierarchical structures, while the 10:3 composition is characterized by network overdensification and localized agglomeration leading to nonuniform pore connectivity with depleted air trapping efficiency.

Therefore, a direct correlation between surface roughness and wettability can be made when the AFM results are considered; the parameter of average roughness (Ra) increases from the 10:1 to 10:2 composition, which corresponds to an increase in WCA. This signifies that an increase in roughness aids the entrapment of air, hence stabilizing the Cassie–Baxter state. However, at higher MWCNT loading (10:3), though Ra still remained high or slightly higher, the roughness was no longer uniform due to localized agglomeration, reducing the pockets’ effectiveness. This indicates that not only roughness but also uniform distribution of roughness plays a key role in optimizing wettability.

In addition to surface morphology, the degree of crystallinity of the PTFE matrix can also influence wettability through its effect on surface energy. Higher crystallinity generally results in more ordered CF₂ chain alignment, which slightly reduces surface energy. However, in the present system, the contribution of crystallinity to wettability is secondary compared to the dominant effect of surface roughness and air entrapment. At the 10:2 composition, the combined effect of relatively uniform crystallinity and optimized hierarchical structure promotes stable air pocket formation, leading to enhanced superhydrophobic behavior. In contrast, at higher loading (10:3), localized agglomeration and possible non-uniform crystallinity distribution may lead to slight variations in surface energy, contributing to the observed marginal decrease in contact angle.

The enhanced superhydrophobicity is associated with the combined effect of hierarchical surface roughness and partial air entrapment. In addition, porosity estimation from FESEM is based on two-dimensional images and may underestimate the actual three-dimensional air fraction. The interconnected nanotube network generates irregular porous surface features that may assist in stabilizing the Cassie wetting state.

The thermodynamic stability of the superhydrophobic state is determined by the ability of the surface to sustain the Cassie–Baxter regime over the Wenzel regime. In the present system, the combination of low surface energy of PTFE and the hierarchical structure increases the energy barrier for liquid penetration into surface asperities. The optimized balance between network connectivity and the uniformity of pores at the 10:2 composition lowers the free energy of the Cassie state making it thermodynamically favorable and resistant to wetting transition.

Superhydrophobicity is retained under changing temperature and humidity conditions owing to chemical inertness and hydrophobic nature of PTFE, as well as interfacial adhesion between PTFE and MWCNTs. The interconnected porous surface features may help resist liquid infiltration and delay wetting transition. Although a rise in temperature or humidity leads to a slight loss in air retention, the hierarchical structure ensures that enough energy barriers prevent a complete transition to the Wenzel state.

Conclusion

This work presents a scalable approach for the fabrication of superhydrophobic PTFE/MWCNT composite coatings using supercritical fluid processing combined with spray deposition. The process enables uniform dispersion of MWCNTs within the PTFE matrix while preserving their structural integrity, leading to the formation of hierarchical micro–nano surface architectures. An optimal balance between surface roughness and morphological uniformity is achieved at the 10:2 (w/w) PTFE/MWCNT ratio, resulting in a maximum water contact angle of 156.3°. The findings show that wettability is governed not only by surface roughness but also by the uniform distribution and interconnectivity of the hierarchical structure, which promotes efficient air entrapment.

Furthermore, surface architecture is significantly influenced by the number of spray passes, with the second spray cycle producing the most favorable configuration for superhydrophobic performance. The established structure–wettability relationship provides a clearer understanding of the interplay between processing, morphology, and wetting behavior. This study demonstrates the effectiveness of supercritical fluid processing as a versatile route for designing high-performance superhydrophobic coatings with potential applications in anti-icing, self-cleaning, and corrosion protection.
