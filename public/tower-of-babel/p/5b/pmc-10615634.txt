The Impact of the COVID-19 Outbreak on Emergency Medical Service: An Analysis of Patient Transportations and Time Intervals
Authors: Yong Joo Park, Kyoung Jun Song, Ki Jeong Hong, Jeong Ho Park, Tae Han Kim, Young Su Kim, Seung Hyo Lee
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to investigate the impact of the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) outbreak on the Emergency Medical Service (EMS) system in South Korea. The study focused on the differences in EMS time intervals following the COVID-19 outbreak, particularly for patients with fever.MethodsA retrospective analysis of EMS patient transportation data from 2017 to 2022 was conducted using the national EMS database.ResultsStarting from the year 2020, coinciding with the COVID-19 outbreak, all EMS time intervals experienced an increase. For the years 2017 to 2022, the mean response time interval values were 8.6, 8.6, 8.6, 10.2, 12.8, and 11.4 minutes, and the mean scene time interval values were 7.1, 7.2, 7.4, 9.0, 9.8, and 10.9 minutes. The mean transport time interval (TTI) values were 12.1, 12.3, 12.4, 14.2, 16.9, and 16.2 minutes, and the mean turnaround time interval values were 27.6, 27.9, 28.7, 35.2, 42.0, and 43.1 minutes. Fever (≥ 37.5°C) patients experienced more pronounced prolongations in EMS time intervals compared to non-fever patients and had a higher probability of being non-transported. The mean differences in TTI between fever and non-fever patients were 0.8, 0.8, 0.8, 4.3, 4.8, and 3.2 minutes, respectively, from 2017 to 2022. Furthermore, the odds ratios for fever patients being transported to the emergency department were 2.7, 2.9, 2.8, 1.1, 0.8, and 0.7, respectively, from 2017 to 2022.ConclusionThe study findings highlight the significant impact of the COVID-19 outbreak on the EMS system and emphasize the importance of ongoing monitoring to evaluate the burden on the EMS system.Keywords: COVID-19, Emergency Medical Service, Time Interval

INTRODUCTION

The outbreak of coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) in South Korea had a sudden impact on the Emergency Medical Service (EMS) system. On January 20, 2020, the first confirmed case of COVID-19 was reported in South Korea. Initially, the number of confirmed cases was relatively small (around 30 cases) for about a month. However, from February to April 2020, a large number of cases (approximately 10,000) were found to be concentrated in a specific area, Daegu city in Gyeongbuk province, marking the onset of the “1st wave.” The “2nd wave” occurred from August to October 2020, with approximately 10,000 cases, starting from the Seoul city and spreading to other regions of the country.1,2

Since the initial outbreak, many ambulances have experienced delays due to uncertainty about the destination of patients and a lack of isolation units in emergency department (ED). The third, fourth, and fifth waves emerged successively from late 2020 to mid-2022. In each wave period, the number of daily confirmed cases increased rapidly, leading to a nationwide crisis. In May 2022, South Korea recorded its highest number of daily cases, exceeding 600,000. As the outbreak continues to escalate, delays in transporting EMS patients have become more severe, prompting concern from EMS staff and media outlets.3

Several studies have reported on the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on EMS patient characteristics and transport, such as a decreased EMS transport to EDs during the early period of the outbreak,4 an increased in EMS patient access time,5 and longer EMS time intervals and decreased transport particularly for patients with fever.6 However, there is a lack of studies that describe the nationwide impact of COVID-19 on EMS time intervals through long-term time-series visualizations.

This study aims to investigate the impact of the COVID-19 outbreak on the EMS system in South Korea. The study focuses on the challenges faced by EMS staff in transporting patients to EDs in a timely manner and the impact of COVID-19 on EMS time intervals.

METHODS

Study setting

In South Korea, almost all emergency patients are transported by the public EMS system, known as “119 EMS,” which is operated by the National Fire Agency (NFA). The 119 EMS operates around 1,600 ambulances with 19 dispatch centers, and transports approximately 1.8 million patients each year.7 Since the outbreak of COVID-19, the 119 EMS has been transporting all patients with confirmed or suspected disease, as well as individuals who require quarantine. As the scale of the crisis continues to grow, some non-emergent COVID-19 patients are being transported by private EMS or public health centers, but all emergent patients continue to be transported by the 119 EMS.3

Study design and data source

This study is a retrospective observational study conducted nationwide using the national database of 119 EMS run sheets maintained by the NFA of South Korea. The database contains detailed records of patient demographics, medical conditions, field triage, mode of transport, and operational times. Data collection and analysis were approved by the NFA.

Study population

The study population included all cases (run sheets) in the national database of 119 EMS from January 1, 2017 to December 31, 2022. The air transport cases were excluded because they were considered to have significantly different EMS time intervals compared to ground transport. Each run sheet has a unique identification number, so if a duplicated number was found, it was considered a technically duplicated case and excluded to ensure uniqueness. To analyze EMS time interval values, we additionally excluded cases that were not related to patient transport runs and cases with missing time interval values.

Variables

The categories of transportation were classified into four: transported, co-transported, non-transported, and non-EMS activity. Transported cases referred to patients who were transported to a destination, such as ED. Co-transported cases referred to patients who received multiple responses and were transported by another EMS team. Non-transported cases included patients who could not be transported due to cancellation, refusal, treatment on the scene, or the absence of a patient. Non-EMS activity referred to runs for support missions of firefighting, training missions, etc.

Patient variables such as age, sex, field triage, whether they were injured or not, presented place, and body temperature were documented in the run sheet. Field triage consisted of five levels: emergency, sub-emergency, non-emergency, deceased and not-a-case. “Emergency” triage was selected for patients showing signs and symptoms requiring immediate medical management within minutes. “Sub-emergency” was for patients requiring urgent medical management, but not within minutes. “Non-emergency” was for the patient need to transport to ED but not on an urgent basis. “Not-a-case” was selected if it is a non-time-dependent transport mission such as visiting outpatient clinic, pre-reserved inter-hospital transportation, etc. Injured case was defined as a patient injured by external forces and environments, such as road accidents, falls, collisions, cut/piercing, burns, poisoning, drowning, etc. The presented place was classified as private and public. Private places included home, residence, or dormitory and public places included road/street, public building, workplace, sports/recreational institution, educational institution, healthcare facility, and so on. Body temperature of 37.5°C or above was considered as fever, according to the COVID-19 response guideline of Disease Control and Prevention Agency of South Korea.8

The EMS operational times such as call time, run time, scene-arrival time, scene-departure time, and hospital-arrival time, were documented in the run sheet. Response time interval (RTI) was defined as the time interval from the incoming call taken by 119 EMS dispatcher to the time the ambulance stopped at a point closest to the patient’s location. Scene time interval (STI) was defined as the time interval from when the ambulance stopped to the time when the ambulance departed the scene. Transport time interval (TTI) was defined as the time interval from when the ambulance departed from the scene to the time it arrived at the ED. These definitions were based on those used in previous studies.9,10

Turnaround time interval (TAI) was defined as the time interval from the arrival of the ambulance at the ED to its return to the base. Total EMS time interval (TEI) was defined as the total time interval from when the emergency call was taken to the time the ambulance returned to the base (TEI = RTI + STI + TTI + TAI).

Outcomes

The primary outcome of this study is the annual change of mean EMS time intervals from the beginning to the end of the study period. The secondary outcome is focused on subgroups of patients with and without fever (≥ 37.5°C), and includes the annual change in EMS time intervals, as well as the probability of being transported to the ED.

Statistical analysis

To provide more representative results for cases with significantly increased EMS time intervals, we opted to calculate the mean EMS time intervals (RTI, STI, TTI, TAI, and TEI) for each year instead of using medians. Logistic regression analysis was used to compare the probability of patients being transported to the ED, reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).11 The locally estimated scatterplot smoothing (LOESS) technique was used to visualize trends in EMS time intervals.11 For the statistical analysis of all variables and visualization of the EMS time intervals as a time series, we used the statistical and plotting packages of the Julia programming language (version 1.8.5).12

DISCUSSION

The outbreak of COVID-19 has had a significant impact on the EMS system. One of the primary ways of this impact is through changes in the trend of patient transportation. During the 1st wave of the COVID-19 outbreak in early 2020, there was a sudden decrease in the number of EMS runs and patient transportation, as seen in Fig. 1.

This decrease can be attributed to the fear of COVID-19 transmission. Many patients avoided visiting the emergency department during the early stages of the outbreak, particularly those who were considered sub or non-emergent cases. This avoidance could lead to a decline in the number of runs and transported cases for EMS. However, this decrease in demand was short-lived, and as the outbreak continued to spread, the demand for EMS services increased.

In the early to mid-2021, there was a steep increase in EMS runs, which could have been influenced by the vaccination campaigns. During this time, public awareness of adverse vaccine effects was rising, and public advertisements encouraging people to call 119 if they experienced severe symptoms after vaccination.13,14 Moreover, the 4th wave of COVID-19 followed in mid-2021, which contributed to the increase in the number of EMS runs. It seems that the fear of patients about adverse vaccine effects and infection must have led to more public EMS calling. As seen in Fig. 1, the situation worsened as the 5th wave of COVID-19 brought explosively more confirmed cases. Notably, the number of non-transported cases increased steeply, reaching its highest peak.

In terms of the EMS burden, the EMS time intervals could be an indicator. As seen in Fig. 3, the increasing of EMS time intervals abruptly appeared with 1st wave of COVID-19 and repeated at every wave of COVID-19. The difference in EMS time intervals between the fever and non-fever subgroups was surprisingly severe and steeper, as shown in Fig. 4. These sudden changes in trends were never seen before the outbreak. Considering the rapid deterioration of EDs capacity since the COVID-19 outbreak, it is likely that the reduced acceptance of EDs has made a significant contribution to this phenomenon.

The decreasing acceptance of fever patients by EDs seems to result in a lower possibility of transporting them. During the COVID-19 period, the probability of being transported to ED of fever patients was much lower than before, as shown in Table 6. These results suggest that many patients may have abandoned visiting EDs due to even worsened ED acceptance, which was particularly crucial for patients with fever.

In the mid to late 2022, there was an improvement in these trends of EMS time intervals, even during the sixth wave. The reason for the improvement may be that the sixth wave was not as severe as the fifth wave, and after experiencing repeated waves, the isolation policies of EDs become less strict. And the government’s policy regarding COVID-19 has also been relaxed, emphasizing home treatment based on out-patient clinics.15,16,17

Considering the delays in EMS time intervals during COVID-19, it is important to consider that efforts to respond to the outbreak can lead to additional delays at every step of EMS activity. These steps include the time required for wearing personal protective equipment, managing an infectious patient, and decontaminating the ambulance for the next run. Meanwhile, another significant factor contributing to the extended EMS time intervals before reaching the hospital could be the delayed handover of patients from ambulances to the ED. This delay may occur due to limited medical resources, including beds, staff, and medical instruments. This delay is commonly known as “ambulance offload delay” (AOD).18,19,20,21,22

With the scale of the COVID-19 outbreak increasing in South Korea, the trend of AOD seems to be extending. The generally spoken reason behind prolonged AOD was the lack of patient isolation units. Many EMS staff commonly reported that patients with fever were denied entry to the ED, regardless of whether the medical staff had assessed the patient to determine the need for isolation. This trend appears to contribute to the prolongation of EMS time intervals and the widening differences between the fever and non-fever subgroups nationwide.

In terms of ED acceptance, the AOD can be considered as a specific indicator of ED overcrowding or a shortage of emergency medical resources. However, measuring AOD directly can be challenging. Therefore, the TAI is often used as a reliable proxy for AOD.23,24 But, during COVID-19 outbreak, there were instances where patients were denied entry to the ED, resulting in EMS staff and patients waiting on the street or in front of the ED. In some cases, patients even had to remain at home until their destination was determined. As a result, the severity of the AOD during COVID-19 can be primarily represented by the TTI and partially by the STI. Furthermore, it can potentially increase the RTI as it impacts the overall responsiveness of the EMS. On the other hand, the prolongation of the TAI appears to be more influenced by post-load factors of patient transportation during the COVID-19 period, such as decontamination processes. To capture all aspects of the additional workload during the outbreak, the EMS transportation burden can be reflected by considering the TEI as an indicator.

To accurately observe and evaluate the burden on EMS, appropriate indicators are essential. EMS time intervals can serve as fundamental indicators of EMS burden. To assess the proportion of the burden imposed by EDs on EMS, the AOD can be used as a specific indicator, as it reflects the lack of resources and acceptability of ED. However, due to the challenges in determining handover time, it is necessary to establish criteria for AOD and adopt a standardized reporting method, preferably utilizing automated recording. In the 119 EMS system, ED staff are required to provide a digital signature upon patient handover, making it possible to generate a time stamp automatically for a relatively accurate record of AOD.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, it was unable to determine whether the cause of the delay or abandonment in transport was due to low acceptance of the ED or other factors, such as patient’s intention. Secondly, during COVID-19 outbreak, 119 EMS transported a small number of hospital-to-hospital transfers (pre-scheduled long-distance transport), which may have influenced the trends of EMS time intervals. However, we believe that this factor was minimal and did not significantly affect the overall scale and trend.

In conclusion, The COVID-19 outbreak has placed a significant burden on EMS systems. These trends could be observed more promptly and accurately using time-series visualization, and it suggests the primary cause could be the lower acceptance of ED during the outbreak. As prolonged AOD has always been a problem due to shortfalls in the medical system, the lower acceptance of EDs was not a sudden phenomenon, the outbreak has brought this issue to light. Policies should be established to expand resources and improve the efficiency of the EMS, and the impact of these policies should be evaluated using indicators such as EMS time intervals, including AOD. It is essential to address the burden on EMS imposed by EDs by developing standardized reporting methods, defining criteria for AOD, and implementing automated recording.
