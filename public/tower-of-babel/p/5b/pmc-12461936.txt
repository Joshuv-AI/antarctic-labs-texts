One-Time Network Rearrangement for Homogenization of Epoxy Resin Structures
Authors: Kazuma Kumamoto, Atsuomi Shundo, Satoru Yamamoto, Keiji Tanaka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Epoxy resins, which form highly cross-linked and permanent network structures by curing reactions between epoxy and amine compounds, are typically in a glassy state at room temperature. They are thus inherently brittle, a property that severely limits their broader practical application. To address this issue, the structural heterogeneity of the cured network, which is closely related to fracture behavior, must be precisely controlled, even after curing. Herein, we propose an epoxy resin system featuring a one-time rearrangeable network designed to reduce heterogeneity after curing. Disulfide bonds were introduced into the epoxy-amine network to enable disulfide exchange reactions, which promote network homogenization. Following this rearrangement, the resulting less-heterogeneous network was subsequently fixed via a chemical reaction between the disulfide bonds and terminal carbon–carbon double bonds on dangling chains. The resin was synthesized by curing a mixture of a bifunctional epoxy compound, a monofunctional epoxy compound containing a terminal double bond, and a bifunctional amine compound bearing disulfide linkages. Thermal annealing was used as a stimulus to activate both the disulfide exchange reactions and the subsequent fixing reaction. Upon annealing, the resin exhibited an increased glass transition temperature and a reduction in dynamic heterogeneity. Compared to a reference epoxy resin lacking disulfide bonds, the annealed resin showed a narrower distribution of relaxation times and higher fracture energy. These findings offer a valuable insight into the design of thermosetting resins, demonstrating that network heterogeneity can be reduced even after curing, while maintaining thermal and dimensional stability.

Introduction

Epoxy resins, typically formed through curing reactions between epoxides and amines, represent a critical class of thermosetting polymers due to their ability to form densely cross-linked, permanent three-dimensional networks. − Their outstanding mechanical strength, chemical resistance, and adhesive properties make them indispensable in applications such as structural materials, − adhesives, − composites, − and protective coatings. , These performance attributes are not easily replicated by conventional thermoplastics. Owing to their high glass transition temperatures, epoxy resins usually exist in a glassy state under ambient conditions, − which imparts dimensional stability and stiffness but also introduces brittleness and limited resistance to crack propagation. , While this brittleness may be acceptable in coatings or electronic encapsulants, it remains a major limitation in load-bearing or impact-prone applications. Improving fracture toughness is thus essential and can be achieved by modifying the network architecture, introducing flexible segments, or incorporating toughening agents such as nanoparticles or secondary phases. −

To this end, many studies have explored the relationship between cross-linking density and mechanical performance. − Although increased cross-linking density is generally expected to improve toughness, , conflicting results , suggest that this relationship is not yet fully understood. Recent research indicates that spatial heterogeneity in cross-linking density, which arises during curing, plays a critical role in determining fracture behavior. − Highly reacted domains form alongside less reacted regions, producing networks with spatial variation in density. − This heterogeneity, influenced by curing temperature, ,,, greatly affects mechanical performance as networks with more homogeneous cross-linking exhibit higher fracture strain and energy, even when the average cross-linking density remains constant. Solvent-crack testing further supports this, showing longer resistance to failure in more homogeneously cured samples. These findings underscore the importance of not only average cross-linking density but also its spatial uniformity for achieving improved toughness.

Once the network is fully formed, this heterogeneity is effectively locked in, as thermally or solvent-driven rearrangement is not possible in chemically cross-linked systems. − The covalent network topology is fixed and cannot self-correct post-curing, unlike in many thermoplastics. If network heterogeneity could be reduced after curing, it would present a powerful strategy for enhancing toughness. One promising approach is to incorporate reversible or exchangeable covalent bonds, such as disulfide or ester linkages, − that allow bond exchange reactions under specific stimuli. , These reactions can induce topological rearrangement via associative or dissociative mechanisms, enabling local reconfiguration. In heterogeneous networks, the clustering of exchangeable bonds in densely cross-linked regions may promote more localized bond exchanges, potentially homogenizing the network. − However, the ongoing exchange reactions that facilitate this also compromise thermal and dimensional stability, posing limitations for long-term use. − This trade-off should be the case even for vitrimers, which have seen major advancements in recent years. ,

In this study, we propose a novel epoxy resin system that allows a one-time, post-cure topological rearrangement to reduce network heterogeneity while preserving long-term stability. Figure schematically shows the concept. Disulfide (S–S) bonds are introduced into the epoxy-amine network, enabling dynamic bond exchange to locally rearrange the network structure. , This homogenization step is followed by a chemical “lock-in” reaction between disulfide groups and terminal carbon–carbon double bonds (CC) on dangling chains, thereby stabilizing the network through covalent C–S fixation. This second step, achievable via thiol–ene chemistry or radical-mediated coupling, permanently arrests the bond exchange activity, because the reaction is irreversible and the C–S bonds are energetically stable, distinguishing this system from conventional vitrimers. By decoupling the rearrangement phase from the service phase, this strategy retains the benefits of dynamic bond-assisted network homogenization while ensuring dimensional and thermal stability. The approach offers a promising path toward tougher, more reliable epoxy thermosets without sacrificing their inherent advantages.

Experimental Section

Materials

Figure shows the chemical structures of bis-[4-(2,3-epoxipropoxi)­phenyl]­propane (bisphenol A diglycidyl ether) (DGEBA), 9-decenylglycidyl ether (DCGE), dithioaniline (DTA), and 4,4′-diaminodiphenylmethane (DDM). DCGE was synthesized from 9-decen-1-ol by reaction with 2-(chloromethyl)­oxirane (epichlorohydrin) in the presence of BF3–OEt2 as a catalyst (see Supporting Information). DGEBA, DTA, DDM, and all reagents used for DCGE synthesis were purchased from Tokyo Chemical Industry Co., Ltd. Organic solvents for DCGE purification were obtained from FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Co. Deuterated chloroform for proton-nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) spectroscopy was supplied by Cambridge Isotope Laboratories, Inc. All reagents and solvents were used as received.

Chemical structures of (a) DGEBA, (b) DCGE, (c) DTA, and (d) DDM.

Sample Preparation

DGEBA and DCGE were thoroughly mixed, followed by the addition of DTA or DDM. The molar ratio of DGEBA:DCGE:DTA/DDM was 3:2:2, corresponding to stoichiometric ratios of epoxy to amino groups (2:1) and disulfide to double bonds (1:1). Assuming full conversion, three DGEBA molecules and one DCGE molecule would ideally be connected to a single DTA/DDM molecule. The mixtures were pre-cured at 363 K for 6 h, followed by post-curing at 413 K for 6 h to obtain the epoxy products, which were used without further purification. The post-cured products derived from DGEBA-DCGE-DTA and DGEBA-DCGE-DDM are denoted as ER-SS and ER, respectively. ER-SS was then thermally annealed at 453 K for 12 h to induce a reaction between S–S and CC bonds. As a reference, ER was also annealed under the same conditions.

Fourier-Transform Infrared Spectroscopy

The curing reactions and the subsequent reaction between S–S and CC bonds was monitored by Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy. Spectra were recorded using an FT/IR-6700 spectrometer (JASCO Co.) equipped with a triglycine sulfate (TGS) detector. The wavenumber range for monitoring the curing process was 4,400–5,200 cm–1, while 1,550–1,700 cm–1 was used to detect reactions involving S–S and CC bonds. Each reaction mixture, DGEBA-DCGE-DTA and DGEBA-DCGE-DDM, was preheated at 363 K for 10 min and sandwiched between two quartz glass substrates with a 0.5 mm thick silicon rubber spacer. The mixtures were pre-cured and then post-cured under the same curing conditions described in Section For reactions involving S–S and CC bonds, calcium fluoride substrates were used instead of quartz. All spectra were recorded in transmittance mode at a resolution of 4 cm–1 over 32 scans under a dry nitrogen purge.

Characterization

Each reaction mixture, preheated at 363 K, was poured into a silicon rubber mold placed on an aluminum substrate, then pre-cured, and thermally annealed as described above. Glass transition temperatures (T g) were determined by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC6220, SII Nanotechnology Inc.). Samples were heated to 433 K at a rate of 10 K·min–1 under dry nitrogen, cooled to 303 K, and then reheated. The T g was obtained from the third heating scan. Mass density (ρ) was examined by a gas pycnometer (ULTRAPYC 1200e, Quantachrome Instruments Inc.), with the helium target pressure set to 117 kPa and the measurements repeated 150 times to take an average.

Dynamic Mechanical Analysis

Dynamic mechanical analysis (DMA) was performed using a Rheovibron DDV-01GP (A&D Co., Ltd.). Specimens with a thickness of 300 μm, a width of 3 mm, and a length of 30 mm were prepared following the same procedure described in Section . A sinusoidal strain of 0.033%, within the linear response regime, was imposed on specimens under a static force of 49 mN. Measurements were taken at a heating rate of 2 K·min–1 under a dry nitrogen purge. The frequency was varied from 0.1 to 110 Hz.

Tensile and Stress Relaxation Tests

Tensile testing was performed using a TENSILON RTF-1310 (A&D Co., Ltd.). Specimens were prepared according to Japanese Industrial Standards (JIS) K6251–7, with a gauge length of 12 mm and a width of 2 mm. Temperature and crosshead speed were room temperature and 1 mm·min–1, respectively. Stress relaxation behavior was examined using the same setup. To accelerate relaxation, tests were conducted at (T g – 10) K. A small strain (ε) of 5.0 × 10–3, within the linear response regime, was applied and the resulting stress (σ) was recorded as a function of time (t) for 2 × 105 s.

Results and Discussion

Curing Reactions

DGEBA and DCGE were used as an epoxy base and a precursor of dangling chains, respectively. As curing agents, DTA and DDM were used, whose only structural difference was the presence or absence of an S–S bond at the para position of the phenyl ring in the amine moiety. The mixtures of DGEBA, DCGE and either DTA or DDM were prepared in a molar ratio of 3:2:2. This ratio corresponds to a 2:1 stoichiometric balance of epoxy to amino groups, and a 1:1 ratio of S–S and CC bonds. Each mixture, DGEBA-DCGE-DTA and DGEBA-DCGE-DDM, was pre-cured at 363 K for 6 h, followed by post-curing at 413 K for 6 h. The curing reactions were monitored by FT-IR spectroscopy. Panels (a) and (b) of Figure show partial FT-IR spectra of the DTA and DDM systems, in the initial and post-cured states in the range of 4,400–5,200 cm–1. In the initial state of the DTA system, two characteristic absorption bands were observed at 4,528 and 5,059 cm–1. The former is assigned to the combination of the stretching and bending vibrations of epoxy groups, and the latter to the corresponding combination for primary amino groups. − Both bands disappeared after the post-curing, which followed pre-curing, as evidenced by the spectrum of the post-cured state. The same behavior was observed for the DDM system. These results indicate that the epoxy–amine reactions were nearly, or at least apparently, complete after the post-curing process. The post-cured epoxy resins obtained from the DTA and DDM systems are hereafter referred to as ER-SS and ER, respectively, where the suffix “-SS” denotes the incorporation of disulfide bonds.

FT-IR spectra of (a) DGEBA-DCGE-DTA and (b) DGEBA-DCGE-DDM in their initial and post-cured states in the range of 4,400–5,200 cm–1. FT-IR spectra of (c) ER-SS and (d) ER in their initial and post-cured states in the range of 1,550–1,700 cm–1.

It is well-known that aliphatic S–S bonds can undergo reactions with CC bonds at room temperature in the presence of suitable catalysts. − However, diphenyl disulfide, as used in DTA in this study, exhibits poor reactivity under ambient conditions. Previous studies have shown that this reaction becomes feasible at elevated temperatures. Based on this, ER-SS and ER (post-cured samples) were further thermally annealed at 453 K for 12 h, a temperature higher than the post-curing temperature, to promote the reaction between the S–S and CC bonds. Panel (c) of Figure shows partial FT-IR spectra of ER-SS before and after thermal annealing in the range of 1,550–1,700 cm–1. In the untreated ER-SS, an absorption peak appeared at 1,640 cm–1, adjacent to an intense peak with a shoulder, both arising from the stretching vibration of phenyl rings. This 1,640 cm–1 band is assigned to the stretching vibration of terminal CC bonds in dangling chains. , After thermal annealing, this CC peak disappeared, indicating its consumption. As such a disappearance could also be due to thermal decomposition, we examined this possibility by analyzing the FT-IR spectra of ER, which contained no S–S bonds. Panel (d) of Figure shows FT-IR spectra of ER before and after thermal annealing. The CC peak remained even after thermal annealing, indicating the persistence of the CC bonds. Since the disappearance of the CC peak was observed only in the presence of S–S bonds, it is reasonable to conclude that in ER-SS, the CC bonds reacted with the S–S bonds during annealing. It should be noted that the reaction between the S–S and the CC bonds possibly occurs at molar ratios of 1:1 or 1:2, as illustrated in Scheme S1 in the Supporting Information. − ,− If the former occurs, the effective cross-linking density of the network is expected to increase. This implication will be further discussed in Section .

Glass Transition Temperature

Table summarizes the characterization data for the T g and ρ at room temperature for ER-SS and ER, both of which were subjected to thermal annealing. The T g value was defined as the midpoint of the baseline shift on the DSC thermogram. The annealed ER-SS exhibited a higher T g than the annealed ER, indicating that the segmental motion in ER-SS was more restricted. ,, Typically, T g correlates with the cross-linking density of the polymer network. ,, To further evaluate the cross-linking density, we attempted both solvent swelling and DMA measurements. However, these attempts were unsuccessful or inconclusive. In the solvent swelling test, both ER-SS and ER exhibited good solubility, making it difficult to obtain meaningful swelling data. In the DMA measurements, ER-SS showed a marked decrease in the rubbery plateau modulus after thermal annealing due to S–S bond exchange reactions, preventing accurate estimation of cross-linking density (see Figure S2). −

As shown in Table , the ρ value of annealed ER-SS was higher than that of annealed ER. This difference may be partially attributed to the presence of sulfur atoms in ER-SS, which are heavier than the corresponding carbon and hydrogen atoms in ER. To assess the extent of this mass-related contribution, the molecular weights of the repeating units in ER-SS and ER were compared. ER-SS was found to be 1.054 times heavier than ER. If this difference alone accounted for the density increase, the expected ρ for annealed ER-SS would be approximately 1.181 g·cm–3. The experimentally obtained value for annealed ER-SS was 1.201 g·cm–3, markedly higher than the mass-based estimate. This discrepancy suggests that the increased density in annealed ER-SS reflects not only compositional differences but also a denser glassy structure, likely due to reduced free volume. This structural densification is a plausible explanation for the higher T g observed in annealed ER-SS.

Dynamic Heterogeneity

The segmental dynamics in the epoxy resins were examined by DMA. Panel (a) of Figure shows the temperature dependence of storage modulus (E′) and loss modulus (E″) for the annealed ER-SS at various frequencies ranging from 0.1 to 110 Hz. An E″ peak was observed slightly above the T g, accompanied by a pronounced drop in E′. This E″ peak is attributed to the α-relaxation process associated with segmental motion. ,, A similar behavior was observed for ER (see Figure S3). Panel (b) of Figure shows semilogarithmic plots of relaxation time (τ) versus the inverse of the peak temperature (T –1), normalized by the dynamic T g (T g DMA), defined as the temperature at which τ = 100 s. ,, Here, τ was simply given by (2πf)−1, where f is the measurement frequency. Table summarizes the VFT fitting parameters together with the T g DMA and fragility index (m), which corresponds to the apparent activation energy for the α-relaxation process at near the glass transition. The m value, estimated from the VFT fitting parameters, was substantially higher for annealed ER-SS than for annealed ER. In prior studies, the magnitude of m has been interpreted in terms of dynamic heterogeneity, which reflects a transient spatial fluctuation in the segmental mobility near the glass transition. ,, A lower m value corresponds to a higher degree of dynamic heterogeneity. Therefore, these results suggest that the annealed ER-SS exhibits reduced dynamic heterogeneity in comparison with the annealed ER. This conclusion is also supported by the narrower temperature width of the E″ absorption and tan δ (=E″/E′) peaks observed for ER-SS (see Figure S3). , Given that segmental motion is governed by the local cross-linking environment, dynamic heterogeneity can be regarded as a manifestation of structural heterogeneity in the network, arising from regions with varying cross-linking densities. , Thus, the reduced dynamic heterogeneity in annealed ER-SS is consistent with a more spatially uniform network structure than that in annealed ER.

Mechanical Properties

Figure shows representative stress–strain curves obtained from tensile tests conducted on annealed ER-SS and annealed ER. For both samples, the stress increased with strain, followed by yielding and subsequent fracture. Table summarizes the mechanical properties averaged over 15 specimens per sample, including Young’s modulus (E), yield stress (σy), fracture strain (ε f), and fracture energy (G f). The G f value was calculated as the area under the stress–strain curve and used as an index of material toughness. , The E values for all samples were comparable within the margin of experimental error. Notably, σy, ε f, and thereby G f for annealed ER-SS were all higher than those for annealed ER. Considering that annealed ER-SS exhibited lower dynamic heterogeneity, and hence network heterogeneity, than annealed ER, these findings make it clear that as the network becomes less heterogeneous, the toughness increases. This can be rationalized by assuming that once the heterogeneous network vitrifies, regions with higher and low cross-linking densities translate into densely and loosely packed glassy regions, respectively. , The less-dense glassy domains are mechanically weaker and thus more prone to stress concentration during deformation, which can initiate cracks and lead to failure. − Therefore, the reduced network heterogeneity in annealed ER-SS results in fewer mechanically weak zones, thereby improving overall toughness.

Stress Relaxation Process

To discuss the mechanical response over longer time scales, stress relaxation behavior was examined. A small ε of 5.0 × 10–3, confirmed to be within the linear viscoelastic regime based on tensile testing, was imposed on the specimens, and the resulting σ was recorded as a function of t at a temperature of (T g – 10) K. Panels (a) and (b) of Figure show the normalized stress (σ/σ0) versus t for annealed ER-SS and annealed ER, respectively. Open symbols denote experimental data, while solid curves are the best-fits based on the Kohlrausch–Williams–Watts (KWW) equation − 2 σσ0=exp{−(tτKWW)β}+σ∞σ0 where τKWW, β, and σ∞ are the characteristic relaxation time, the stretched exponential parameter, and the equilibrium stress, respectively. Table summarizes the fitted parameters for each sample. The τKWW for annealed ER-SS was longer than that for annealed ER. This result is consistent with the general understanding that stress relaxation is slower in more highly cross-linked systems. − As discussed earlier, thermal annealing promotes additional cross-linking in ER-SS via reactions between the S–S and the CC bonds, which does not occur in ER due to the absence of S–S bonds. Thus, the increased cross-linking density in annealed ER likely accounts for the observed longer relaxation time. It should also be noted that in the annealed ER-SS sample, the network rearrangement has already been completed so stress relaxation during the measurement does not involve further rearrangement of the network. Importantly, the β value for annealed ER-SS was greater than that for annealed ER. This reflects the breadth of the relaxation time distribution: a β of 1 corresponds to a single relaxation process, while lower values indicate broader distributions. − Thus, a greater β value for annealed ER-SS suggests a narrower distribution of relaxation times. Given that relaxation time is sensitive to local cross-linking density, this observation implies that the network structure in annealed ER-SS is more uniform than that in annealed ER. This conclusion is in good accordance with the results obtained from the DMA.

Finally, we turn to the discussion of when the disulfide exchange reaction occurs. As confirmed by FT-IR spectroscopy, the peak due to the CC bonds was observed for ER-SS even after post-curing. This would not be expected if the CC bonds were completely consumed by the reaction with the S–S bonds. In other words, a portion of the S–S bonds remained during the post-curing process. This raises the possibility that the disulfide exchange reaction may take place either during post-curing or during subsequent thermal annealing. To determine the dominant stage at which the exchange occurs, we evaluated the stress relaxation behavior for ER-SS after post-curing but before thermal annealing and compared it with that for annealed ER-SS. If the exchange reaction predominantly occurred during post-curing, the β value in the stress relaxation curve would remain unchanged after thermal annealing. Conversely, if the exchange reaction primarily occurred during the thermal annealing, β would be expected to increase due to network homogenization. As shown in Figure and Table , the β value for the non-annealed ER-SS was substantially smaller than that for the annealed ER-SS. This result indicates that the disulfide exchange reaction primarily occurred during thermal annealing. That is, the S–S bonds underwent dynamic exchange during thermal annealing, promoting network homogenization, while simultaneously reacting with CC bonds to topologically fix the structure.

Conclusions

We proposed a one-time rearrangeable network in an epoxy resin to reduce the extent of the heterogeneity even after curing. The epoxy resin, referred to as ER-SS, was prepared by pre- and post-curing a mixture of DGEBA, DCGE, and DTA. As a reference, an epoxy resin, ER, was prepared using DGEBA, DCGE, and DDM instead of DTA. Upon thermal annealing, the reaction between the S–S and CC bonds in ER-SS proceeded, as confirmed by FT-IR spectroscopy. DSC revealed that the T g of annealed ER-SS was higher than that of annealed ER. This increase may be attributed to a higher mass density, indicating a denser glassy state with reduced free volume. DMA showed that the m value for annealed ER-SS was markedly larger than that for annealed ER, suggesting that annealed ER-SS possessed a more uniform network structure and, consequently, a more homogeneous dynamic environment. This homogenization led to enhanced fracture energy, as demonstrated by tensile testing, where annealed ER-SS exhibited a higher fracture energy than annealed ER. Stress relaxation measurements further supported these findings: compared with annealed ER, annealed ER-SS exhibited a longer relaxation time and a narrower relaxation time distribution, indicative of a less heterogeneous network structure with relatively high cross-linking density. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate the enhancement of thermal and mechanical properties of epoxy resins after curing based on the concept of a one-time rearrangeable network. Although the S–S bond was used for exchangeable covalent bonds and the CC group for fixation in this study, other combinations, including ester bonds and amino groups, should in principle be possible. Thus, the concept proposed here may be valuable for practical applications such as structural adhesives and composite materials, where the cured network tends to be heterogeneous, especially along the direction normal to the solid interface. ,,
