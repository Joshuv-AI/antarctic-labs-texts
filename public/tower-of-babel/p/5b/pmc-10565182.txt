Cross-sectional study investigating the association between inflammatory biomarkers and neuropathy in adolescents with type 1 diabetes
Authors: Vinni Faber Rasmussen, Verena Hirschberg Jensen, Mathilde Thrysøe, Esben Thyssen Vestergaard, Joachim Størling, Kurt Kristensen
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe aims of this study were to investigate circulating levels of inflammatory markers in adolescents with type 1 diabetes with and without different types of neuropathies and evaluate the association between inflammatory biomarkers, nerve function and clinical parameters.DesignCross-sectional study.SettingHospitals and Steno Diabetes Center in Denmark.ParticipantsAdolescents with more than 5 years of diabetes duration were investigated for large fibre, small fibre and autonomic neuropathy as a part of the T1DANES study. Blood samples from the participants were analysed for inflammatory biomarkers by Meso Scale Discovery multiplexing technology.Primary and secondary outcome measuresInflammatory biomarkers and results of diagnostic nerve tests.ResultsFifty-six adolescents with type 1 diabetes and 23 healthy controls were included. The adolescents with diabetes had significantly higher interferon-gamma, tumour necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-a), interleukin (IL)-10 and soluble urokinase plasminogen activator receptor (suPAR) compared with healthy controls (p values<0.05). TNF-a was higher in the adolescents with large fibre neuropathy (LFN) (p=0.03) compared with those without LFN in the group with diabetes. A negative correlation was seen between TNF-a and conduction velocity in nervus tibialis (p=0.04), and higher TNF-a and IL-6 were associated with higher gastric motility index (TNF-a, p value=0.03; IL-6, p value=0.02). There were no significant associations between inflammatory markers and expressed symptoms, haemoglobin A1c, diabetes duration or body mass index standard derivation score (p values>0.05). The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves for the inflammatory markers suggested them as poor screening methods for all types of neuropathies with an area under the curve between 0.47 and 0.67.ConclusionOur results confirm increased low-grade inflammation in adolescents with type 1 diabetes. TNF-a was higher in adolescents with LFN and correlated negatively with nervus tibialis conduction velocity. The other inflammatory biomarkers fail to support differences in those with and without different types of diabetic neuropathies. However, TNF-a and IL-6 were positively correlated to gastric motility index.Keywords: adolescent, diabetic neuropathy, risk factors

Introduction

Neuropathy is common in adolescents with type 1 diabetes,1 and it can have serious consequences such as reduced quality of life, increased risk for cardiovascular events, amputations and death.2 International guidelines for screening for neuropathy exist,3 however, the lack of good screening methods remains a problem.

The pathogenesis behind neuropathy in diabetes is complex and involves a combination of metabolic, vascular and neurotrophic factors.4 Evidence suggests that cytokines, particularly pro-inflammatory cytokines caused by metabolic changes associated with hyperglycaemia, play a role as facilitators of neurotoxicity.5 In children with type 1 diabetes systemic levels of interleukins have been found to correlate with stimulated C peptide levels supporting the immunemodulatory role, and its contributing role in beta-cell damage in type 1 diabetes.6 Protein kinase C as well as the polyol and hexosamine pathways contribute to oxidative stress and chronic low-grade inflammation.4

Studies in humans have reported elevated levels of inflammatory markers in people with diabetic neuropathy compared with those without, with a correlation to the severity of the condition.7–10 Soluble urokinase plasminogen activator receptor (suPAR) is a protein involved in inflammation and immune function that is elevated in several chronic diseases, including diabetes and its complications.11 12 A few studies have investigated the relationship between suPAR and diabetic neuropathy and an association with an increased risk of diabetic neuropathy has been described.12

The abovementioned leads to the hypothesis that inflammatory cytokines and suPAR are possible biomarkers for early neuropathy in young people with diabetes. The aims of this study were (1) to investigate circulating inflammatory markers and suPAR in adolescents at early stages of different types of diabetic neuropathies and in healthy controls, (2) to associate inflammatory biomarkers with findings on nerve tests and clinical parameters and (3) to evaluate inflammatory markers as a possible screening tool for neuropathy.

Methods

The methods for the T1DANES study are published separately.13 14 The main features are repeated below.

Research design and methods

Cross-sectional study.

Study population

Adolescents aged 15–18 years with a history of type 1 diabetes of at least 5 years were recruited from outpatient clinics at Danish hospitals in Randers, Aarhus and Aalborg and Steno Diabetes Centers Aarhus, and North Denmark, between August 2020 and December 2021. Participants were excluded if they were taking any medication or had diseases that could impact the central or peripheral nervous system. However, the presence of associated well-treated autoimmune disorders (such as thyroid disease, and coeliac disease) or complications to diabetes (such as microalbuminuria) was allowed. Healthy adolescent controls were enrolled via advertisement at a boarding school and through social media.

Information regarding diabetes duration, total and basal daily insulin dose, time-in-range the last 14 days, glucose-monitoring system, haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) the last 5 years, events of severe hypoglycaemia and ketoacidosis in the past year and the most recent test results for retinopathy and nephropathy (albumin/creatinine ratio) was obtained from the patient’s electronic clinical records.

Data storage

Data were safely stored in Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) administrated by Aarhus University (REDCap 12.0.30 - 2023 Vanderbilt University), which is a secure web application for online surveys and databases.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design of the study. All adolescents who were treated at the involved hospitals and diabetes centres got the opportunity to participate in the study. All participants received written information about the results of nerve tests if they wanted.

Data collection

All participants underwent an examination day at Aarhus University Hospital. The adolescents with type 1 diabetes continued with their normal insulin treatment on the examination day without any adjustments. During the test day, blood glucose was within normal range or as close as possible and in case of hypoglycaemia, blood glucose was corrected by fast-acting carbohydrates in fruit juice. All available clinical and biochemical data were extracted from their patient records. In cases of missing data, supplementary data were extracted on the day of the tests. A fasting blood sample was taken from all participants for later analysis, after which they were given 200 mL of water. The blood samples were stored at −80°C.

The weight and height of each participant were measured, and their body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight divided by the square of height. Hip and waist measurements were taken, and blood pressure (BP) and heart rate (HR) were recorded using an automatic BP monitor. The stage of puberty was determined through self-assessment using illustrations of different Tanner stages. Activity levels, alcohol consumption and smoking status were obtained through self-reporting.

Symptoms of possible neuropathy were collected by asking and using the Composite Autonomic Symptom Score (COMPASS)-31 and Gastrointestinal Symptom Rating Scale (GSRS).15 16

Biochemical tests

Cytokines

The concentrations of interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), tumour necrosis factor-alpha (TNF)-α, interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, IL-12p70 and IL-13 in plasma samples were measured with multiplex ELISA technology on a MESO QuickPlex SQ 120 instrument by Meso Scale Diagnostics (MSD). The method is previously used and described in the field of diabetes and children.6

Fasting blood samples were collected from the cubital vein of study participants. Subsequently, samples were centrifuged, and isolated plasma was aliquoted in smaller volumes. Aliquots were stored at −80°C and thawed just prior to analysis. Cytokine concentrations were analysed in duplicate using the Proinflammatory Panel 1 (human) (MSD, Cat. No. K15049D, Meso Scale Diagnostics, Rockville, Maryland, USA) according to the instructions of the manufacturer. Readings below the detection limit of the assay were replaced with values equal to the lower detection limit divided by the square root of 2. Readings with a value of 0 or with a coefficient of variation (CV) above 25% between duplicates were excluded from the data set.

suPAR was quantified in plasma with validated latex particle-enhanced turbidimetric immunoassay on c702 Roche Cobas 8000 systems.11

Definition and diagnostic tests for neuropathy

A detailed description of the nerve tests was previously published.13 14 The following definitions and diagnostic criteria were used:

Large fibre neuropathy (LFN): two or more abnormal results from nerve conduction studies (NCS) of five tested nerves. Confirmed LFN if abnormal results of NCS and abnormal bilateral findings on neurological examinations indicating LFN. Subclinical LFN if only abnormal NCS.

Large fibre neuropathy (LFN): two or more abnormal results from nerve conduction studies (NCS) of five tested nerves. Confirmed LFN if abnormal results of NCS and abnormal bilateral findings on neurological examinations indicating LFN. Subclinical LFN if only abnormal NCS.

Small fibre neuropathy (SFN): reduced intraepidermal nerve fibre density (IENFD) measured in a skin biopsy. Confirmed SFN if reduced IENFD and abnormal bilateral findings on neurological examinations indicating SFN. Subclinical SFN if only reduced IENFD.

Small fibre neuropathy (SFN): reduced intraepidermal nerve fibre density (IENFD) measured in a skin biopsy. Confirmed SFN if reduced IENFD and abnormal bilateral findings on neurological examinations indicating SFN. Subclinical SFN if only reduced IENFD.

Autonomic neuropathy (AN): two or more abnormal results from cardiovascular reflex tests (CARTs: deep breathing test, Valsalva test and lying-to-standing test) or abnormal sweat response measured by quantitative sudomotor axon reflex test (QSART)

Autonomic neuropathy (AN): two or more abnormal results from cardiovascular reflex tests (CARTs: deep breathing test, Valsalva test and lying-to-standing test) or abnormal sweat response measured by quantitative sudomotor axon reflex test (QSART)

Gastrointestinal/enteric neuropathy (GI/EN): abnormal transit time or abnormal motility index (motility index=Ln (sum of amplitudes×the number of contractions+1)) obtained by wireless motility capsule.

Gastrointestinal/enteric neuropathy (GI/EN): abnormal transit time or abnormal motility index (motility index=Ln (sum of amplitudes×the number of contractions+1)) obtained by wireless motility capsule.

The cut-off for abnormality of the used confirmatory nerve tests was below the fifth percentile of results obtained from included healthy adolescents.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were carried out using the software program R (R Core Team (2022), Vienna, Austria). The distribution of the variables listed in table 1 was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test and Q–Q plot. Descriptive data are presented as mean (SD) for variables with normal distribution, median (range) for continuous variables without normal distribution and number (percentage) for categorical variables. Differences between groups were compared using Student’s t-test for normally distributed continuous variables, Wilcoxon rank-sum test for non-parametric continuous variables and Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. A p value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant, and the sample size was determined as part of the previously published study investigating confirmatory, bedside test and risk factors for neuropathy.13 Missing data were excluded from the analysis. Linear regression in R was used to analyse the associations between parameters.

Comparing adolescents with confirmed LFN versus adolescents with subclinical LFN or normal results of NCS, no significant differences in inflammatory biomarkers were found (all p values>0.05).

Associations between inflammatory markers and confirmatory diagnostic tests for neuropathy

Applying linear regression analysis, we found that higher TNF-a was associated with lower conduction velocity in nervus tibialis (motoric) in adolescents with type 1 diabetes (p=0.04). There were no associations between the other inflammatory markers and other test results indicating peripheral sensorimotor neuropathy: NCS and IENFD (data not shown, all p value>0.05).

Similarly, there were no associations between inflammatory markers and test results from CARTs (deep breathing, Valsalva and lying-standing test) and QSART. However, we found that higher TNF-a and IL-6 were associated with higher gastric motility index (TNF-a, p value=0.03; IL-6, p value=0.02). There were no associations between inflammatory markers and the motility index in the small bowel and colon, as well as the transit times through the GI tract (data not shown, all p values>0.05).

Inflammatory markers and clinical characteristics

There were no significant associations between the included inflammatory markers and current HbA1c, mean HbA1c last 5 years, time in range, diabetes duration, body mass index standard derivation score (BMI-SDS), total daily insulin dose, cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein (LDL), high-density lipoprotein (HDL) and triglycerides (data not shown, all p values>0.05). A higher level of suPAR was associated with lower systolic BP (p value<0.01).

Six of the participants with type 1 diabetes defined themselves as smokers. The smokers did not have higher inflammatory markers compared with non-smokers in the group with type 1 diabetes (all p values>0.05).

Three of the six smokers had between two or three inflammatory markers (TNF-a, IL-6, IL-8 and/or IL-10) above the third quartile of the data set. These three adolescents with higher levels of inflammatory markers had all abnormal NCS (one confirmed LFN, two subclinical LFN) and one of them had also reduced IENFD.

In the control group, there were no differences in inflammatory markers depending on self-reported puberty stage (all p values>0.05). In the group with diabetes, the adolescents with self-rated Tanner stage 5 had higher IL-6 than those with Tanner stage 4 (median IL-6; 0.91 vs 0.66, p=0.03). This difference was also observed in women (median IL-6; 0.95 vs 0.61, p=0.02), but not in men (median IL-6; 0.67 vs 0.68, p=0.77).

Inflammatory markers and symptoms

None of the participants with diabetes reported symptoms in the length-dependent area, but in the questionnaires, some adolescents expressed symptoms that could be a sign of autonomic dysfunction. The number of participants with a total COMPASS-31 score above 24 (third quantile of the data set) was 12 adolescents with diabetes.

There were no associations between the total score of COMPASS-31 or GSRS and the level of inflammatory markers (data not shown, all p values>0.05). We observed trends of negative associations between diarrhoea (expressed in GSRS) and IL-6 (p=0.07), as well as between indigestion and TNF-a (p=0.08).

However, different clinical presentations were observed. For example, in two cases with both GI neuropathy and four elevated inflammatory markers (IFN-y, TNF-a, IL-6 and suPAR) above third quantile, one adolescent expressed GI symptoms in the questionnaires and the other did not.

Inflammatory biomarkers as screening methods for early neuropathy

Applying ROC analysis, we found that serum concentrations of all the included inflammatory markers were poor quality screening methods for all types of neuropathies (data not shown, all AUC between 0.47 and 0.67).

Discussion

In this study, we found that serum concentrations of IFN-y, TNF-a, IL-10 and suPAR were significantly higher in adolescents with a type 1 diabetes duration for at least 5 years, compared with healthy controls. TNF-a was higher in adolescents with LFN and correlated negatively with nervus tibialis conduction velocity. The remaining inflammatory biomarkers did not provide evidence of distinctions between individuals with various forms of diabetic neuropathies and those without. However, higher TNF-a and IL-6 serum levels were associated with higher gastric motility index. ROC-analysis demonstrated poor quality of the inflammatory markers as a screening method for the tested types of neuropathies. No significant associations between inflammatory markers and expressed symptoms, diabetes duration, HbA1c, BMI-SDS and lipid profile were observed, but lower systolic BP was associated with a higher plasma level of suPAR.

In this study, we set out to measure 11 inflammatory markers, but could only detect 6 of them in our study population. Four out of these six markers had elevated levels in adolescents with type 1 diabetes, supporting the presence of low-grade inflammation in this group. Our findings of elevated suPAR levels in adolescents with diabetes, as compared with those without, are consistent with previous research conducted in children.17

Cytokines produced by immune and pancreatic islet cells are known to play diverse roles in type 1 diabetes. These cytokines can influence the development of the disease in both progressive and preventive ways.18 Cytokines can be classified into two types (1) anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-4 and IL-10, and (2) pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-a, IFN-y, IL-4 and IL-1β. However, it is worth noting that many of these cytokines seem to be capable of exerting dual effects in people with diabetes.18 During the first month after diagnosis of diabetes, changes in the cytokine profile of children have been observed,6 19 suggesting that the duration of time since diagnosis should be taken into account when investigating inflammatory markers. Dogan et al demonstrated higher levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines in 24 newly diagnosed children compared with control subjects, and the level of TNF-a and IL-6 were even higher in the subgroup of participants with a mean diabetes duration of 2.9 years.20 This contradicts with findings of Erbağci et al, who observed that the elevation of IL-6 and TNF-a was limited to cases with a diabetes duration below 1 year, and only IL-8 was elevated in children with a mean diabetes duration of 3.9 years compared with controls.21 Romano et al supported that TNF-a and C reactive protein were higher in children and adolescents with a diabetes duration below 1 year compared with those with a longer diabetes duration.22 Overgaard et al found that the concentrations of TNF-a and IL-10 at baseline (2.45±0.65 months after diagnosis) predicted stimulated C peptide level at 6 months.6 The SEARCH study investigated 553 youth with type 1 diabetes with a median duration of 2.7 years, and they showed persistent excess inflammation due to higher IL-6 and fibrinogen levels.23 In our study, where the participants had a diabetes duration of at least 5 years, we did not find elevated IL-6 levels. However, our findings support a status of chronic low-grade inflammation independent of diabetes duration.

The above findings illustrate the complexity of understanding the multiple factors that influence inflammation and its consequences in young people with diabetes, including the risk of developing neuropathy.

While a relationship between the presence of diabetic neuropathy and circulating pro-inflammatory cytokines is well documented in adults, only a few studies on this relationship have been conducted in paediatric populations to the best of our knowledge. Aulich et al found no difference in high sensitivity C reactive protein and sE-selectin between 134 youth with type 1 diabetes and control subjects. However, they did find associations between inflammatory markers and microvascular complications (including neuropathy) as well as HR variability,24 indicating a possible link to peripheral sensorimotor and cardiovascular AN.

The inflammatory pathway from hyperglycaemia to diabetic peripheral neuropathy has been previously illustrated.5 We found a significant association between TNF-a and LFN, as evidenced by the negative correlation with the conduction velocity in one motoric nerve. Additionally, we observed higher TNF-a in the group with abnormal NCS compared with those with normal NCS. In adults, TNF-a levels are shown to be significantly higher in people with diabetic peripheral neuropathy compared with those without, and higher in those with painful versus painless neuropathy.7 Magrinelli et al found that inflammatory markers were associated with large nerve fibre damage but not with small fibre function in a study population of 32 subjects,25 which we also observed, however, studies with a greater population are needed to investigate this further.

Regarding AN, our results of differences in the level of inflammatory markers were not significant compared with previous findings. Theilade et al found suPAR to be associated with autonomic dysfunction,12 and Wegeberg et al discovered that circulating inflammatory markers were inversely associated with both parasympathetic and sympathetic HR variability in adults with type 1 diabetes.26 These findings suggest that inflammation may play a role in the pathogenesis of AN.

Both cytokines, like TNF-a and IL-6 and high blood glucose levels could potentially impact various physiological processes, including those related to peristalsis and metabolism. In our cohort, we have shown that a low colonic motility index was inversely associated with ‘time in target range’ for blood glucose,14 however, drawing a direct link between the association of TNF-a, IL-6 and GI motility as a surrogate for high blood glucose effects would require further research and investigation.

Notably, we found that higher TNF-a and IL-6 were associated with higher gastric motility index, and we observed trends indicating associations between these inflammatory markers and reported symptoms.

Inflammation has been shown to both increase and decrease GI motility, however, the published studies on IL-6 and TNF-a show conflicting results, as reported in the review of Docsa et al.27 Most animal and human studies show that TNF-a suppresses contractile activity in the small bowel and colon. However, there are discrepancies, especially in the gastric segment. Interestingly, TNF-a has been found higher in diarrhoea-predominant irritable bowel syndrome and was associated with increased contractile activity in the oesophagus.27

Whether inflammation is directly linked to early detectable neuropathy remains unclear, but complex interactions between the immune system and nervous system exist. The nervous system reflexively regulates the inflammatory response. In the inflammatory reflex, the vagal nerve plays an important role in regulating immune function and inhibiting excessive pro-inflammatory cytokine function.28

The associations between inflammation and nerve function and reflexes support the potential role of inflammatory biomarkers as a contributing screening tool for diabetic neuropathy. However, more evidence is needed before it becomes a useful supplement to nerve tests and interventions against risk factors.

Increased glucose variability and clinical factors such as higher BMI, higher HbA1c and diastolic BP have been shown to be associated with increased inflammation in adolescents with type 1 diabetes.24 29 In our study, BMI-SDS, alcohol consumption, diastolic BP and level of triglycerides were higher in the group with diabetes than in the control group. These factors might potentially have overestimated our results of higher levels of inflammation in adolescents with diabetes. Aulich et al found that BMI was a particularly strong independent explanatory variable of inflammation.24 We did not observe similar associations, possibly because our study group had a lower BMI and none were obese with BMI-SDS above 2. We found that higher levels of suPAR were associated with lower systolic BP, which is inconsistent with previous literature where suPAR has been linked to increased risk for cardiovascular diseases,30 including in children with diabetes.17

In healthy adults, male sex, increased BMI and smoking are shown to be associated with increased levels of TNF-a and IL-6.31 In our control group, there was a higher proportion of women compared with men, whereas an equal gender distribution was seen in the type 1 diabetes group. The women might have lowered the mean values of TNF-a in the control group and thereby increased the differences between the gender-mixed groups. When comparing men with and without diabetes, the significant difference between inflammatory markers disappeared, which might most likely be due to the small number of men in the control group.

Remarkably, sex hormones, such as estrogen and testosterone, are known to play a role in immune modulation and can affect cytokine production. The hormonal changes that occur during puberty can contribute to variations in levels of inflammatory markers between boys and girls.32 Estrogen, for instance, is known to have inhibitory effects on IL-6 production.33 Somewhat contradictorily, we observed the highest levels of IL-6 in adolescents in the final stage of puberty, where we would expect the highest levels of estrogen. The above highlights that including information about sex hormone levels and the females’ menstrual stage34 would have been relevant in a study like this.

Our study has a number of limitations. The cross-sectional study design is not useful in order to predict the causality between low-grade inflammation and the development of neuropathy, and a single measurement may not accurately reflect long-term levels. Additionally, an individual’s metabolic status can vary in diabetes, and markers such as time-in-range (3, 9–10 mM), time-in-target (4–8 mM) and the percentage coefficient of variation for glucose may be better than HbA1c for evaluating metabolic status at a given time. These variables, along with lipids and BP, may be favourable for long-term monitoring in relation to markers for low-grade inflammation.

An important limitation is our small number of participants, which does not allow us to adjust for factors in the statistical analysis, and a larger group and sample size calculation specific to this study would have provided greater statistical power. In addition, our small control group set the cut-off for abnormality in the diagnostic nerve test, which could result in an incorrect distribution of the groups with and without neuropathy. However, comparing our prevalence of LFN and cardiovascular neuropathy corresponds well with previously published findings (LFN: 30–57%35–38; cardiovascular neuropathy: 14%39). Overall, we believe that our analyses are informative exploratory, because of our use of the most accepted confirmatory nerve tests and validated methods for biomarker quantification.

In conclusion, we observed low-grade inflammation in adolescents with type 1 diabetes, and that TNF-a and IL-6 were associated with test results indicating neuropathy supporting neuroimmune interaction in the pathogenesis of neuropathy. TNF-a was higher in adolescents with LFN but the other inflammatory biomarkers fail to support differences in those with and without different types of diabetic neuropathies. Circulating inflammatory biomarkers as a complement to existing screening methods for diabetic polyneuropathy may be relevant, however, further research on larger cohorts is needed.
