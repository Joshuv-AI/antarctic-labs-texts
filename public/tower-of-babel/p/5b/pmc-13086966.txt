Amplifying performance, combustion, and emission characteristics of a CRDI engine using diesel-WCO methyl ester-dyglyme ternary fuel blends with carbon nanotubes
Authors: Sarojini Jajimoggala, Malathi Narra, Shabana Shabana, P Uma Maheswara Rao, Debabrata Barik, Milon Selvam Dennison, R Srinivasan, Ayyar Dinesh, Saravanan Rajendran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe present study investigates the performance, combustion, and emission characteristics of various fuel formulations, including neat diesel (D100), a biodiesel blend (WCO20: 20% waste cooking oil methyl ester and 80% diesel), a Ternary Fuel (TF) blend composed of 20% WCOME, 70% diesel, and 10% diglyme (DGM), and TF blends doped with Carbon Nanotube (CNT) nanoparticles at concentrations of 25, 50, and 75 mg/L. All fuel samples were evaluated using a twin-cylinder, four-stroke, Common Rail Direct Injection (CRDI) compression-ignition engine. Biodiesel is prepared from WCO using the well-known transesterification process. The NPs included in TF samples are found to be stable and confirmed by characterization (FESEM, HRTEM, FTIR, and UV Spectroscopy). The performance parameters, such as Brake Thermal Efficiency (BTE), are increased by 15.15%, and Brake Specific Fuel Consumption (BSFC) is decreased by 16.5%. Moreover, the combustion parameters, including Cylinder Pressure (CP), Net Heat Release Rate (NHRR), Mass Fraction Burning (MFB), and Cumulative Heat Release Rate (CHRR), are improved due to catalytic activity, higher evaporation capacity of DGM, improved rate of reaction, and oxygen content in the fuel blend. The emission characteristics, including Carbon monoxide (CO), Carbon dioxide (CO2), Hydrocarbons (HC), and Nitrogen Oxides (NOx), are reduced by 17.5, 22.27, 22.09, and 25.8% at higher load for the TF+CNT50 mix, respectively. The outcomes show the feasibility of DGM and CNT NPs as suitable additives in the WCO20 sample for lower emission parameters without compromising performance and combustion characteristics.Keywords: Diglyme, Carbon Nanotubes, Ternary Fuel, Compression Ignition, Performance, EmissionSubject terms: Chemistry, Energy science and technology, Engineering, Environmental sciences, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

Energy sources are being depleted at a terrifying pace as a result of the sharp rise in automobile usage worldwide. The global temperature rises as a result of excessive fossil fuel consumption. The serious problem of today’s world is global warming, which is the extensive utilization of fossil fuels and their pollution. Biodiesel is receiving increasing attention because of its ability to both fulfil energy demand and decrease emissions1. Since biodiesel is environmentally beneficial and renewable, it can be termed a sustainable fuel for diesel engines. The biodiesel is made from non-edible oils, which are abundantly available in the environment, and apart from this, they do not compete with human food resources. Biodiesel is most commonly suitable to substitute for CI engines and enhances the sustainability over traditional diesel. The biodiesel is considered carbon neutral and emits fewer net carbon emissions when burnt2. Biodiesel is not suitable for use on CI engines directly because of its poor performance and poor cold flow properties, which clogs the engine components with fuel gel in cold climate conditions. Hence, it is mixed up to 20% vol of biodiesel in 80% vol of diesel to form a B20 blend as per ASTM D7467. This combination is a widely accepted fuel blend ratio to test on diesel engines without any engine modifications3,4. A thorough analysis of the impact of biodiesel fuels on diesel engine emissions was carried out, resulting in decreasing HC, CO, smoke, and particle emissions in the majority of the experimentation5. On the other hand, NOx emissions slightly increased due to oxygen content in the blend and elevated exhaust gas temperature. Balasubramania et al.6 explored that 10% Exhaust Gas Recirculation (EGR) rate reduced NOx emissions by up to 16.34% while having no effect on HC, CO, smoke, and CO2 emissions.

In recent studies, many researchers are using alcohol additives due to various advantages such as lower viscosity, greater volatility, higher cetane number, and greater oxygen level. Alcohols are classified as either high-level or low-level. The high-level alcohols have greater energy content, higher volatility, and cetane number (CN), and lower volatility, which enables better mixing with diesel and maintaining stability over time, and improves evaporation and atomization. Thus, resulting in an improved rate of reaction in the premixed zone and expediting the combustion process. Further, the alcohol addition improves the physicochemical properties of fuel samples. Besides, the lower level alcohols have a relatively low cetane number and energy density that limits their solubility with standard diesel7. In an article, Jogarao et al. (2021)8 studied the dispersion of ethanol in Niger oil methyl ester and diesel at different concentrations (up to 15% of ethanol) and showed enhanced BTE and decreased BSFC, CO, and HC emissions at higher injection pressures. While the NOx emission is decreased slightly at higher loads. In a study, Thakkar et al. (2021)9 investigated the inclusion of n-butanol in castor oil methyl ester-diesel mixtures on a CI engine to investigate performance and emission parameters. The results stated that the BTE is reduced by 1–3% for the B30 sample. Further, the ternary fuel (B15Bu15) reduced NOx emissions by 20–60% to the B30 blend. Moreover, the performance and combustion parameters are improved, and the emission characteristics are decreased for B10Bu20. In another study, Singh & Kumar (2022)10 studied the performance, combustion, and emission parameters of a CI engine fueled with biodiesel-diesel, biodiesel-n-butanol, and biodiesel-diesel-n-butanol blends. The results proved that inferior performance and better combustion and emission characteristics were obtained with the inclusion of n-butanol up to 20% in biodiesel-diesel mix. In another article, Behera & Hotta (2024)11 investigated the feasibility of n-butanol-biodiesel-diesel blends on a CI Engine. The addition of n-butanol (10%) in methyl ester-diesel mixtures was used to determine overall performance and emission parameters of the CI engine. The biodiesel is made from palm oil and blended with diesel at concentrations of 10, 15, and 20%. Among these samples, the blend comprising 10% of n-butanol, 10% of biodiesel, and 80% of diesel outperformed in performance (2% higher BTE and a 1.33% lower BSFC), and reduced emissions (CO, CO2, HC, NOx, and smoke opacity). In another article, Yage et al. (2022)4 experimented with the inclusion of DGM (at a dosage of 2–10% at a variation of 2%) in a low sulfur diesel-biodiesel blend and tested on a 4-cylinder, DI, CI engine. The results show that the combustion parameters resulted in lower peak values than the diesel due to the poor heating value. Besides, the brake-specific emissions and smoke are decreased due to the benefits of biodiesel and DGM oxygenated alcohol.

Diglyme (DGM), also known as diethylene glycol dimethyl ether, is a substance having a high oxygen level, strong diesel fuel solubility, a greater cetane value, a low susceptibility to soot, and appropriate physical-chemical characteristics for use in modern CI engines12. The oxygenated alcohol fuels like DGM have been considered as a potential substitute for diesel engines. Its combustion and emission characteristics have been the subject of several published investigations13. The combustion parameters, such as diffusive heat release during the premixed stage, and maximum mean gas temperature (MGT) are observed to increase with increased DGM content in diesel fuel. While the Ignition Delay (ID), diffusive and comprehensive combustion duration are decreased14. Nevertheless, the emission parameters, including CO, HC, NOx, Soot, and particle matter, are decreased15. In an article, Babu et al. (2024)16 stated that the inclusion of n-pentanol (at a dosage of 10, 15, 20, and 25% vol.), and DGM (at a concentration of 5, 10, 15, and 20% vol.) in diesel (at 70% vol.) and tested on a 1-cylinder, diesel engine. The inclusion of 20% DGM and 10% n-pentanol in diesel resulted in decreased CO and NOx emissions without affecting performance characteristics at all loads when compared to diesel fuel. In another article, Nabi et al. (2024)17 reported that the addition of DGM, Di-n-Butyl Ether (DBE), and Tripropylene-Glycol Monomethyl ether (TPGM) in diesel at a dosage of 10, 15, 25, and 30% vol. in diesel and used these oxygenated alcohols directly in a diesel engine. The inclusion of 30% of these additives in diesel resulted in improved performance and combustion parameters. While the emission characteristics, including CO and HC, are decreased compared to the diesel fuel. But, NOx emission is slightly raised. An experimental investigation by Miyamoto et al. (2000)18 revealed low NOx, higher BTE, and smokeless diesel combustion when the CI engine was tested with DGM as the primary fuel. As per the study, fuel-bound oxygen decreased all significant diesel pollutants, including NOx emissions. DGM alcohol is moderately biodegradable. It has effects on humans when exposed directly, such as Skin absorption hazard, causes eye and respiratory irritation, can affect the nervous system, Significant reproductive and developmental toxicity, and Potential liver/kidney toxicity with chronic exposure. Further, DGM is moderately toxic to aquatic life, can pollute water and soil, and persists in the environment19. Due to the above challenges, a limited quantity of DGM alcohol is blended in biodiesel-diesel blends to prepare the TF fuel sample, which provides a few advantages, including lower CO, HC, and NOx, lower viscosity, improved volatility, enhanced evaporation and atomization, and higher energy density.

On the other hand, many scholars are actively working on the addition of NPs to biofuels, which is the most innovative technique to improve engine performance and combustion efficiency20. According to a study by Bidir et al. (2021)21, the inclusion of NPs reduces CO and PM emissions while enhancing BSFC by 23% and BP by 2.5%. In another investigation, Ferrao et al. (2021)22 conducted an experiment in which they added aluminium NPs to hydro-processed vegetable oil at concentrations of 40 and 70 nm and tested it on the diesel engine. The findings showed that the 40 nm size of NPs boosted the reaction rate by 42.5%. Purushothaman et al. (2021)23 investigated in another research how adding Al2O3 and TiO2 NPs to mahua oil methyl ester can improve performance and drastically lower emissions. Jabraeili et al. (2021)24 showed in another work that the hybrid mode of Al2O3-SiO2 nano composite particles is mixed with WCO-based bodiesel at dosages of 30, 60, 90, and 100 ppm and evaluated on a diesel engine. The findings resulted in improving BTE by 1.44% and Torque by 1.64% when correlated to diesel. In another study, Hassan et al. (2023)25 studied the addition of Al and Ni NPs at a dosage of 100 mg/l in caster methyl ester-diesel-n-butanol samples to find the performance and emissions of a diesel engine. The blend D80B10Bu10 enhanced BP, and BTE are increased by 14.99% and 8.09% respectively. Further, BTE is improved by 3.81% and 6.49% when Al and Ni NPs are included. Whereas the addition of n-butanol contributed to reducing CO and NOx majorly.

On the other side, many researchers have emerged on using carbon-based NPs, such as carbon nanotube (CNT), graphene platelet (GNPs), and Graphene Quantum Dots (GQDs) NPs, since they are non-metallic, with a superior surface area to volume of NPs, sophisticated thermal, optical, and electrical capabilities, which have a major impact on the chemical reaction during combustion over metal or metal oxide NPs26. The physicochemical characteristics of biofuels are improved by the dispersion of these NPs27. This promotes cumulative combustion properties due to the rate of reaction, improved thermal diffusivity, and micro explosion which improves overall performance of the CI engine and lowers emissions28,29. In a study, Hoseini et al.30 determined the influence of graphene oxide (GO) NPs on Ailanthus altissima methyl ester mixtures. The advanced thermal properties of GO and oxygen levels in the fuel blends improved BP and reduced SFC, CO, and HC emissions. While raising NOx due to increased combustion temperature. Researchers examined incorporating GO additions into biodiesel from a variety of feedstocks, including Oenothera Lamarckiana, Ailanthus altissima, Camelina sativa, and rice bran oil. Similar trends in engine performance and emissions were seen31,32. In another article, Ettefaghi et al. (2021)33 looked at the usage of GDQs as biodegradable NPs in a diesel-biodiesel-water mix. The produced mix improved BP, reduced SFC, and NOx is mitigated by 3.8%. In another article, Chacko et al. (2020)34 investigated GO and Graphene nanoplatelets (GNPs) additives into WCO-based biodiesel and benchmarked it against petroleum diesel and Karanja-based biodiesel. GNPs outperformed GO additives in reducing NO, CO, and HC emissions, which concluded that GO and GNPs are promising fuel additives for better emission control without significant effects on engine parts such as injector wear. In another investigation, Bikkavolu et al. (2024)35 concluded that the addition of CNTs (at a dosage of 25, 50, and 75 mg/l) in Yellow Oleander-based methyl ester-diesel blends resulted in improved performance parameters, such as BTE is enhanced by 15.5%, and BSFC is lowered by 20.57% over the B20 sample. Moreover, the combustion parameters including CP, HRR, and rate of pressure raise, are improved, which is due to improved evaporation rate, oxygen content, and catalytic effect of NPs. Nevertheless, the emission characteristics such as CO, HC, and NOx are mitigated by 26.8, 35.7, and 13.2% respectively at maximum BP. In another article, Rao Bikkavolu et al. (2025)36 reported that the inclusion of Al2O3, GO, and CNTs (at a fixed dosage of 50 mg/l) in the B20 sample to determine the Energy, Exergy, and sustainability index using 2nd law of thermodynamics along with engine performance characteristics. The outcomes of the study revealed that BTE is improved by 15.94%, and BSFC is decreased by 20.5% at higher Brake Power (BP). While, the Energy, and exergy efficiencies, and sustainability index of the B20CNT50 blend are improved by 33.6, 23.6, and 7.14% respectively. In the current study, CNT NPs are used due to several benefits over other carbon-based NPs such as High mechanical strength, High aspect ratio, Electrical and thermal pathways, Mechanical reinforcement, Strong catalytic support, and nano-fuel and nanolubricant performance. Most of the investigations are carried out by including NPs up to 100 mg/l since the higher concentration of NPs results in agglomeration and sedimentation. In the current investigation, the NPs are dispersed at a dosage of 25, 50, and 75 mg/l in the biodiesel-diesel blends.

Novelty of the work

The above literature states that the addition of carbon-based NPs improved the performance, combustion, and emission characteristics. Whereas, the inclusion of alcohols reduced emission characteristics, including NOx. The current investigation is aimed at determining the performance, combustion, and emission parameters of a 4-stroke, 2-cylinder, CRDI diesel engine employing TF (20% of WCOME-70% of diesel- 10% of DGM alcohol) with CNT NPs under a higher IP of 500 bar. A limited investigation is carried out with TF containing DGM in B20 blend and CNTs NPs in the B20 sample. But, the combination of TF sample including DGM, and CNT NPs are not blended in the B20 sample individually. Hence, the current investigation aims to determine performance, combustion, and emission characteristics using the TF sample with DGM alcohol and CNT NPs and tested on the CRDI diesel engine at an IP of 500 bar. This systemic strategy not only aids in understanding of NPs and DGM-assisted biodiesel-diesel mixtures on performance, combustion, and emission parameters, but it also optimizes the fuel blend, improving overall performance with alternative fuels using DGM and CNTs. Ultimately, this study contributes to the development of more sustainable and efficient fuel samples for existing diesel engine technology, paving the way for long-term usage.

Materials and methodology

The deep-frying oils from various edible feedstock such as palm, sunflower, corn, soybean, safflower, etc., are polymerized, thermally degraded, hepatic dysfunction due to repeated heating, and hydrated due to moisture in them. Hence, this oil is degraded and not suitable for cooking again and again. The WCO is used in the current investigation, which contains 87.44% of free fatty acid content37,38. The WCO is collected from the local restaurants in Visakhapatnam, India. The chemicals used in the current study, such as hexane (99%), methanol (99.5%), DGM (99%), KOH pellets (85%), and surfactant QPAN 80, are provided by Molekula BioKemix Limited, Hyderabad, India.

Biodiesel and fuel samples preparation

The collected oil is heated to 120℃ to eliminate moisture in the oil. Then, the oil is filtered to eliminate dust and sediment particles. In biodiesel preparation, 1.5% KOH is mixed with 200 g of methanol in a round-neck container, heated to 65℃, and swirled at a continuous speed of 200 rpm. After 15 min of stirring, 885 g of pure WCO oil is combined with methanol at a molar ratio of 1:6 and placed on a heating mantle while being stirred at 200 rpm. During this process, the produced KoH and methanol solution is slowly added, and the entire mixture is vigorously agitated for 2 h. During this process, long-chain fatty acids are converted into short-chain esters. After 2 h, the solution is transferred in a separation funnel and left for 24 h. Finally, the solution is found to be in 2 layers1. The lower layer is observed to be murky and thick brown colour and is removed from the bottom knob, and the upper layer is washed and distilled. Results and discussions

The current investigation determines the engine performance, combustion, and emission characteristics of a CRDI, CI engine using D100, WCO20, TF, TF+CNT25, TF+CNT50, and TF+CNT75 samples at an IP of 500 bar. Initially, the engine is run with diesel for 30 min. During this period, the engine parts reach a steady state temperature and tested for performance, combustion and emission characteristics which are treated as reference data. Then, the tested fuel mixtures are tested on the diesel engine. Each sample is tested 5 times to reduce the errors in measurements. The overall engine performance is observed to be enhanced, and emissions are noticed to be mitigated at higher BP. The inferences are presented in the below discussions.

Performance characteristics

The effect of BTE and BSFC with reference to the BP is illustrated in Fig. 7. It is observed from the results that the BTE is improved as the BP is raised, and consequently, the BSFC results are decreased with increasing BP. The improved BTE and Decreased BSFC are owing to enhanced physicochemical properties majorly. Further, these findings are improved due to fuel and air interaction with reduced viscosity which leads to better evaporation and combustion. The BTE results are noticed to be 30.92, 29.7, 30.68, 34.2, and 32.78% similarly, the BSFC values are found to be 0.314, 0.344, 0.322, 0.287, and 0.303 for D100, WCO20, TF+CNT25, TF+CNT50, and TF+CNT75 respectively at higher BP. The maximum BTE is increased by 15.15% and BSFC is decreased by 16.5% for the TF+CNT50 sample at higher BP. Whereas, the higher concentration of NPs in the TF sample resulted in agglomeration and sedimentation which causes stability issues. The performance parameters are increased due to the inclusion of DGM which increases energy content in the fuel blend, enhanced cetane number and reduced viscosity4. Thereby, when the fuel is injected, the fuel particles are spread finely throughout the cylinder and evaporated and atomized rapidly which reduces the ID, and leads to comprehensive combustion. Since the diesel engines are self-governing engines, whenever the power is released from the fuel spontaneously, the fuel injection is cut off as the piston starts expanding. Thus decreasing the BSFC. Nevertheless, the more complete combustion is carried out due to the increased energy level in the fuel samples and the catalytic activity of NPs. In a paper, Suresh and Babu (2025)39 explored that the performance characteristics are enhanced (BTE is enhanced by 8.02%, and BSFC is decreased by 15.62%) when a microalgae-based methyl ester-diesel-bioethanol sample is used with CNTs NPs on a 1-cylinder, CRDI, CI engine at maximum load. In another article, Nabi & Chowdhury (2006)40 explored that DGM and Cerium are blended in diesel as an additive at a dosage of 0 to 100 Vol. % at a variation of 25%. The investigation reported that the addition of DGM reduced Brake Specific Energy Consumption (BSEC) and emissions, while BTE is improved. In a recent study, Menda et al. (2025)41 reported that the inclusion of CNTs and Di-Ethyl Ether in Spirulina platensis methyle ester-diesel blends resulted in improved BTE by 15%, and reduced BSFC by 13.5%.

Combustion parameters

Cylinder pressure (CP)

The variation of CP vs. Crank angle is shown for various tested fuel mixtures in Fig. 8(a). The combustion of fuel mainly depends on the physicochemical properties of the fuel sample. The addition of NPs improves rate of reaction because of catalytic effect, large surface-to-volume ratio, and better thermal diffusion. Nevertheless, the addition of DGM enhances the evaporation rate and atomization due to improved cetane number and reduced viscosity which tends to enhance the combustion efficiency. Ultimately, as the combustion process is rapid, the CP also reaches to peak value29. The CP values are found to be 66.54, 62.44, 63.86, 69.42, and 67.83 bar respectively for D100, WCO20, TF+CNT25, TF+CNT50, and TF+CNT75 at maximum load. The highest peak pressure is achieved by 11.17% for the TF + CNT50 mix at maximum load. In a study, Kumar et al. (2019)42 revealed that the inclusion of CeO2 NPs in B20 mix enhanced CP which is owing to superior combustion characteristics and shorter ID. The NPs enhanced heat diffusion, and the rate of reaction which leads to rapid combustion. In an article, Pullagura et al. (2024)1 stated that the inclusion of 60 mg/l of SiO2 NPs and 10% of iso-Butanol in B20 mix improved CP by 10.09% which is attributed to improved Cetane number, lower viscosity, and higher oxygen level in the fuel blend.

Net Heat Release Rate (NHRR)

The cetane number, calorific value, rate of reaction, and shorter ID effect the improved NHRR. The influence of TF with inclusion of NPs on HRR vs. Crank angle (J/℃A) is depicted in Fig. 8(b). It is noticed from the results that the HRR values are poor for the WCO20 blend which is owing to poor calorific value, improper air-fuel interaction in the pre-mixed zone, low cetane number causing poor ignition quality, and higher viscosity leading to injection and evaporation issues which resulting in increased ID. On the other side, the HRR findings are improved at higher loads for nano nano-assisted TF sample. The combined addition of DGM and CNTs enhances atomization and combustion through complementary physicochemical mechanisms. DGM increases the volatility and intrinsic oxygen availability of the fuel blend, thereby facilitating early-stage vaporization and promoting low-temperature oxidation pathways. Simultaneously, CNTs provide high-conductivity thermal pathways within droplets and heterogeneous catalytic surfaces that accelerate surface-mediated oxidation of fuel fragments. The interaction of these effects yields reduced droplet size, accelerated evaporation rates, an intensified radical pool, and more complete high-temperature oxidation of intermediates, collectively improving overall combustion efficiency8. The NHRR values are found to be 66.82, 63.68, 66.36, 69.84, and 67.24 J/℃A for D100, WCO20, TF+CNT25, TF+CNT50, and TF+CNT75 at maximum load. The maximum NHRR is increased by 9.6% for the TF+CNT50 sample at maximum BP. In a recent study, Rao et al. (2024)29 reported that the addition of CNTs in biodiesel-diesel samples enhanced HRR by 15.72% at a higher load which is due to heat transportation capability and the catalytic effect of NPs.

Mass Fraction Burning (MFB)

The MFB of fuel samples with reference to crank angle (℃A) is shown in Fig. 9(a). It is clear from the results that the combustion products’ weight gradually increases with crank angle. As the fuel samples possess easy evaporation and atomization due to the addition of DGM and the NPs. This facilitated comprehensive combustion which is owing to better heat diffusion, heat transfer capabilities, and catalytic activity. Thereby, more fuel is burned as compared to the diesel and WCO20 samples. The MFB is found to be close to 100% for the TF+CNT50 sample. In a study, Kumar et al. (2019)42 investigated that the MFB value is enhanced with the inclusion of CeO2 NPs in the B20 sample due to improved rate of reaction with increased heat diffusion. Another study conducted by El. Seesy et al. (2017)43 stated that the dispersion of MWCNTs in jatropha-based methyl ester - diesel blends enhanced air fuel mixing, evaporation, and atomization, decreased ID, and expedited combustion due to the addition of NPs.

Cumulative Heat Release Rate (CHRR)

The CHRR refers to the total amount of heat liberated in the combustion process within the cylinder. The amount of heat released during combustion relies on the crank angle at which the actual combustion starts, the physicochemical characteristics of fuel samples, and the peak pressure achieved. Figure 9(b) depicts the variation of CHRR vs. Crank angle (℃A) for prepared fuel blends at higher BP. Among the tested fuel blends, the nano-included TF sample proved to improve the CHRR. The dispersion of NPs in TF mix is found to be stable up to 50 mg/l and revealed sorter ID, and effective combustion which leads to liberating maximum CHRR. On the other side, the higher concentration of NPs showed agglomeration and sedimentation. The NPs and DGM accelerate the evaporation characteristics due to less viscosity of DGM, and improved cetane number, and higher thermal conductivity, and heat diffusion capabilities of NPs. In a study, Rao et al. (2024)29 concluded that the addition of CNT NPs (at a dosage of 60 mg/l) in a yellow oleander-based B20 sample enhanced CHRR. The improved CHRR is owing to better atomization, thermal diffusion of NPs, greater surface-to-volume ratio, and decreased ID.

Emission characteristics

Carbon monoxide (CO)

The CO gases are claimed due to partial combustion. The variation of CO gases with reference to the BP for various fuel blends is depicted in Fig. 10(a). It is observed from the results that the CO gases are increased for D100 fuel and WCO20 samples at higher BP which is attributed to greater viscosity, density, poor air fuel intermingling and spray characteristics, and improper combustion in the local zones. Moreover, the addition of DGM decreased viscosity, improved evaporation and atomization, and oxygen content. Nevertheless, the NP’s inclusion in the TF sample decreased ID and enhanced combustion reaction. The CO results are observed to be 4.9, 4.68, 4.56, 4.04, and 4.42 g/kWh for D100, WCO20, TF+CNT25, TF+CNT50, and TF+CNT75 respectively at higher BP. The maximum CO gases are lowered by 17.55% for the TF+CNT50 blend as compared to D100. In a similar article, Akkoli et al. (2022)44 concluded that the dispersion of Al2O3 NPs in fish oil-based biodiesel-diesel mixtures reduced CO gases by 17.51% due to the improved rate of reaction due to NPs dispersion.

Carbon dioxide (CO2)

The variation of CO gases vs. BP for prepared blends is illustrated in Fig. 10(b). It is noticed from the graph that the addition of NPs in TF fuel mixtures improved CO2 gases which is owing to complete combustion with the higher content of oxygen, and improved physicochemical characteristics of WCO20 blend dispersed with DGM, and CNT additives. The CO2 gases are observed to be 3.86, 4.12, 4.34, 4.72, and 4.46 g/kWh for D100, WCO20, TF+CNT25, TF+CNT50, and TF+CNT75 respectively at higher BP. The enhancement of CO2 gas is observed to be 22.27% for the TF+CNT50 mix at maximum BP. In an experimental study, Joga Rao et al. (2021)8 stated that the addition of ethanol in methyl ester-diesel mixtures improved CO2 emissions at higher load due to higher content of oxygen and lower viscosity.

Hydrocarbon (HC)

The UHC gases are emitted because of partially burnt fuel particles due to insufficient oxygen in the combustion. The effect of HC gases with respect to BP is shown in Fig. 11(a). The inclusion of DGM in the TF mix reduced viscosity, improved evaporation characteristics, and shortened ID. Hence, comprehensive combustion is achieved. Further, the addition of NPs in the TF sample further accelerated the combustion due to improved rate of reaction, enhanced heat transfer capacity, and higher surface-to-volume ratio. The measured HC emissions are 0.172, 0.166, 0.158, 0.134, and 0.151 g/kWh for D100, WCO20, TF+CNT25, TF+CNT50, and TF+CNT75 respectively at higher BP. In a similar article, Pullagura et al. (2024)1 showed that the inclusion of SiO2 NPs at a dosage of 50 ppm in the TF sample decreased HC gases by 13.9% at a higher load which is attributed to better air-fuel interaction, catalytic effect, evaporation rate, and oxygen level in the blend.

Nitrogen oxide (NOx)

The combustion parameters such as air fuel mixture and structure, residence time, and combustion temperature, are the major factors that emit NOx from the exhaust tailpipe. The variation of NOx vs. BP at different loads is illustrated in Fig. 11(b). It is evident from the graph that as the BP increases, NOx values also increase, and at full load conditions, diesel fuel shows the highest NOx emissions compared to nano-assisted TF samples due to effective combustion and rising temperature45. The emissions are found to be 6.2, 5.8, 5.1, 4.6, and 4.9 g/kWh for D100, WCO20, TF+CNT25, TF+CNT50, and TF+CNT75 respectively at higher BP. The reduction in NOx is primarily due to lower peak flame temperatures resulting from DGM’s quenching effect and CNTs’ thermal conductivity enhancement, combined with a more uniform heat-release profile and controlled radical-mediated oxidation. This combination effectively suppresses the Zeldovich thermal NOx pathway, which dominates NOx formation in diesel engines. In a study, Gavhane et al. (2020)46 reported that the addition of copper-coated zinc oxide NPs in soybean methy ester-diesel blends lowered NOx emissions, which is due to greater heat transfer capabilities of NPs.

Conclusions

The present study investigates the effect of diesel, WCO20, and TF with NPs inclusion at a quantity of 25, 50, and 75 mg/l on a twin cylinder, 4-stroke, CRDI, diesel engine to evaluate the performance, combustion, and emission parameters. The performance, combustion, and emission parameters are found to be improved, and the inferences are given from the above discussions:

The WCOME-diesel was blended at a 20:80 ratio to prepare the WCO20 sample. Further, the TF samples are prepared using WCO20 and 10% of DGM. Then, the fictionalized NPs are dispersed at a concentration of 25, 50, and 75 mg/l in the TF mix using a water bath and probe sonicator. The prepared samples are observed to be stable and consistent, as presented in characterization (FESEM, HRTEM, FTIR, and UV Spectroscopy). Further, the thermal conductivity of all blends was tested, and the nano-included TF samples are found to have the highest thermal conductivity at 50℃.

The synergistic effect of DGM and NP additives in TF samples successfully improved the performance characteristics including BTE (Improved by 15.15%), and BSFC (Lowered by 16.5%) than the WCO20 blend at maximum BP.
