Equity in health service utilisation among middle-aged and elderly people with multiple chronic conditions in China: evidence from longitudinal data of 2011–2018 CHARLS
Authors: Tianjiao Zhang, Min Su, Dongxu Li, Weile Zhang, Fan Yang, Wenhui Li
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesEquity in health service utilisation is a central objective for health systems. Middle-aged and elderly patients with multiple chronic conditions (MCCs) are particularly vulnerable to healthcare inequity. This study aimed to update the information on the trends in the incidence and equity of outpatient health service utilisation (OHSU) and inpatient health service utilisation (IHSU) for middle-aged and elderly MCCs patients in China, identify socioeconomic determinants that may contribute to inequity, and suggest optimisation strategies to mitigate this disparity.MethodsPanel data obtained from four waves of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) were used to determine the trends in OHSU and IHSU. The inequity in OHSU and IHSU was measured by the Concentration Index (CI) and Horizontal Inequity Index (HI), which is a valid measure of health service utilisation equity. The decomposition model of the CI was set up to explore the contribution of various determinants of overall equity.ResultsThe annual rate of OHSU gradually decreased from 29.32% in 2011 to 27.27% in 2018. The HI remained positive and decreased from 0.0803 in 2011 to 0.0662 in 2018, indicating the existence of pro-rich inequity. The annual rate of IHSU gradually increased from 13.31% in 2011 to 19.89% in 2018. The HI remained positive and showed a declining trend from 0.2363 in 2011 to 0.0574 in 2018, evidencing pro-rich inequity; however, a trend towards the easing of inequity was observed.ConclusionsPro-rich inequity was present in both OHSU and IHSU among middle-aged and elderly MCCs patients in China. Economic status, area, education and age were the main contributors to pro-rich inequity. Concerted efforts are needed to allocate resources for mitigating health service utilisation inequity in middle-aged and elderly people with MCCs.Keywords: Health Equity, HEALTH ECONOMICS, HEALTH SERVICES ADMINISTRATION & MANAGEMENT, Health Services Accessibility

Introduction

With the global intensification of ageing, the prevalence of multiple chronic conditions (MCCs) has become a major public health issue worldwide, posing a great challenge to limited health resources.1 2 MCCs are defined as the coexistence of more than one chronic condition, which significantly increases the complexity of therapeutic management for both patients and health systems.3–5 MCCs often impose a heavy financial burden on middle-aged and elderly individuals and low-income patients and exacerbate inequity in health service utilisation.6 A previous study found that as of 2014, approximately 42% of American adults had more than one chronic condition. Americans with one or more chronic conditions accounted for 90% of total health expenditure.7 Another study showed that the prevalence of MCCs was 41%–43% from 2010 to 2020 in China.8 According to the Healthy China initiative (2019–2030) statistics, the economic burden of chronic diseases in China has reached 76% of the total health burden.9 Compared with only one chronic disease, MCCs posed a greater threat to patient safety and quality of life, leading to increased health service rates, complicated outpatient consultations, longer hospital stays and increased disease burden, which had a negative impact on health expenditure and the equitable utilisation of health service resources.10–12

In recent years, China has carried out in-depth reform and development to reduce inequity in health service utilisation, but inequity in outpatient and inpatient service utilisation among MCCs patients persists.13 China’s social health insurance schemes, apart from the Urban Employee Basic Medical Insurance, also include the Urban and Rural Residents Basic Medical Insurance. The latter was launched in China in 2009 as an integrated programme combining different social health insurance programmes for rural residents (the New Cooperative Medical Scheme) and urban residents (the Urban Resident Basic Medical Insurance), which were established to ensure the financial protection of different populations in a medical context.14 Although most Chinese people are covered by different types of medical insurance programmes, large disparities embedded in different administrative regions’ economic development levels and different types of medical insurance programmes have induced non-ignorable inequity in access to inpatient and outpatient health services. This is partly due to the diversity in coverage items and reimbursement rates among various health insurance plans, which provide limited protection in terms of equitable health services for MCCs patients at different income levels.15 16 A study showed that out-of-pocket expenses for older adults with MCCs were twice as high as for those without MCCs, and differentially insured seniors and low-income families were disproportionately affected.17 18 Moreover, China has not yet developed MCCs prevention and treatment healthcare system with professional treatment guidelines and primary healthcare, making it difficult for middle-aged and elderly MCCs patients to access universal and equitable health services.19–21 Previous studies showed that MCCs patients had difficulty accessing effective consultation and treatment in primary care.22 They were also more likely to forgo necessary health services and fall into medical poverty.23 Focusing on the utilisation of health service related to chronic diseases is at the centre of the global health and development agenda.24 MCCs pose an even greater challenge to the provision of equitable access to health services, which is closely linked to patient well-being, the allocation of healthcare resources and the stable development of societies, where equitable access to health services can mitigate the life-consuming and economically threatening effects of MCCs.25

In recent decades, the equity of health service utilisation has been a hot topic. First, regarding health service utilisation, a large body of literature has focused on the reform process of health service systems, influencing factors of health service utilisation inequity, and the connection between MCCs and health service utilisation, cost and mortality.13 26–28 Second, in terms of vertical equity in health service utilisation, few studies have longitudinally measured and summarised health service demand and utilisation equity among MCCs patients, mostly using cross-sectional data or an overview of the influencing factors and severity of MCCs.4 29 30 Third, in terms of horizontal equity in health service utilisation, abundant research has considered objective differences, such as gender and age, while placing greater emphasis on the inequity stemming from controllable socioeconomic factors. However, due to the limited collection of aggregated data, many of these studies focused on health service utilisation inequity within given regions (urban31 or rural32 33 or specific provinces34 35), specific populations (elderly16 28 or patients with a single chronic disease23 36) or a particular service (inpatient services27). Thus, few studies have explored and provided information on the overall equity of healthcare utilisation in MCCs patients, and their findings may not fully address the equity of middle-aged and older MCCs patients in China. Moreover, few studies have identified and analysed the socioeconomic determinants that may contribute to inequity in the MCCs population based on panel data.37

Therefore, this study aimed to (1) update the information on the trends in the annual incidence and equity of outpatient health service utilisation (OHSU) and inpatient health service utilisation (IHSU) among middle-aged and elderly MCCs patients, (2) identify the socioeconomic determinants associated with inequity in OHSU and IHSU and (3) provide scientific evidence and recommendations for improving governmental health policies and alleviating the economics related inequity in health service utilisation. This study will be of great reference significance to sustainably and effectively understanding and alleviating inequity in health service utilisation among middle-aged and elderly MCCs patients in China.

Methods

Data

The China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) conducted in 2011, 2013, 2015 and 2018. The CHARLS was an ongoing nationally large-scale representative cohort study targeting the population aged 45 years and above in China, followed up with participants every 2 years, which included 150 counties in 28 provinces (Available online at: https://charls.charlsdata.com/pages/data/ 111/zhcn.html.) (dataset).38 The data inclusion criteria for this study were as follows: (1) aged ≥45 years, (2) complete information on age, MCCs prevalence, outpatient and inpatient status. The final number of the sample included in this study after screening was 3966. Among those, rural respondents were 2924 (73.73%), and urban respondents were 1042 (26.27%). Measures

CHARLS included detailed information on respondents and their living spouses. The main questionnaire included information on basic demographics, family, health status, healthcare and health insurance, and individual and household economic status.

For respondents, the question ‘Do you have a chronic disease diagnosed by a doctor?’ in the CHARLS survey was used to determine whether the respondents had a chronic disease. If the patient answered ‘yes’, they were considered to have a corresponding disease. The same respondent was considered to suffer from MCCs if they reported having two or more of 14 chronic diseases, including hypertension, hyperlipidaemia, diabetes, cancer, chronic lung disease, liver disease, heart disease, stroke, kidney disease, digestive system disease, emotional and mental disorders, arthritis, and asthma.

Regarding the outcome variable, statistical analyses of OHSU and IHSU in the postinclusion sample of MCCs patients were conducted. (1) OHSU was based on respondents’ reports from the 2011–2018 waves on the following item: ‘Have you received outpatient care in the past year?’ This was a binary variable, where 0 denoted ‘no’ and 1 denoted ‘yes.’ (2) IHSU was based on respondents’ reports from the 2011–2018 waves on the following item: ‘Have you received inpatient care in the past year?’ This was a binary variable, where 0 denoted ‘no’ and 1 denoted ‘yes.’

The control variables included respondents’ area, education level, insurance status, marital status, smoking status, alcohol consumption and economic status. The need variable included respondents’ gender and age, which were objectively present factors obtained from the CHARLS.27 Age was divided into four groups. Respondents’ education was coded into two categories, namely lower than elementary school and high school and above. Marital status was classified as married or other. The smoking status and alcohol consumption were assessed as binary variables, with No coded as 0 and Yes coded as 1. Economic status was measured by self-reported per capita household consumption expenditure, and further divided into three groups by the third percentile method, which better reflects household co-payment’s ability. Per capita household consumption expenditure was a continuous variable. Research has shown that self-reported consumption expenditure is less biased than self-reported income due to factors such as personal honesty and presents a more realistic picture of actual household disposable income.6 39 The living place was divided into rural and urban groups. Considering the huge socioeconomic gaps and differences in the distribution of medical resources and services between urban and rural areas, the rural and urban samples were analysed separately.

The equity of health service utilisation

Concentration Index

The Concentration Index (CI) is recommended by the World Bank to measure health service utilisation and health equity under socioeconomic conditions.33 40 CI ranges from –1 to 1, and 0 represents no economic-related inequity.41 42 The Equation 1 was used to calculate the CI:

C=2μcov(Y,R) (1)

Where C represents the CI, cov is the covariance, Y represents OHSU and IHSU, μ represents the mean of Y, and R represents the fractional rank of per capita household consumption expenditure distribution (low, medium and high).43 The positive score of the CI indicates pro-rich inequity in health service utilisation, and the negative score of indicates pro-poor inequity.27

Decomposition methods

Wagstaff proposed the concentrated index decomposition method in 2003.44 The result of the centralised exponential decomposition of the dependent variable Y is as follows: The income-related inequity of OHSU and IHSU could be explained by decomposing the CI into the contributing factors based on a probit model, using Equation 2.45 As the probit model is nonlinear, the linear approximation to the non-linear model was conducted by estimating the partial effects evaluated at the covariate means.

Y=αm+∑jβjmxj+∑kγkmZk+ε (2)

Where Y represents OHSU and IHSU; xj is the required variable, zk is the control variable; βjm and γkm are marginal effects, namely dy/dxj and dy/dzk; ε stands for the residual term. The result of the concentrated exponential decomposition of y is as follows Equation 3:

C=∑j(βjmxj−μ)Cj+∑k(γkmzk−μ)Ck+GCεμ (3)

Where C is the CI of Y, μ is the mean of y; Cj and Ck are the CI of xj and zk; GCμ is the CI of the residual item; xj¯ and zk¯ are the mean of xj and zk, respectively. Equation 3 states that the CI of OHSU and IHSU is equal to the weighted sum of the CI of the need variable and the control variable, and the product of the CI of each variable and its weight is the contribution to inequity. After eliminating the contribution of demand variables to inequity, the horizontal equity can be calculated.

Horizontal Inequity Index

Horizontal Inequity Index (HI) is the CI that is need-standardised health service utilisation. It reflects the level of inequity in health service use among people with equal needs. The HI is derived from the CI minus the contribution of the need variables (gender, age), as follows:

HI=C-∑k(γkmzk-μ)Ck (4）

HI ranges between 0 and 1, which indicates that the disproportionate distribution of health service utilisation is more concentrated among the individuals with higher per capita household consumption expenditure and vice versa.

Analytical strategy

Logistic regression was employed to analyse the adjusted ORs after controlling for a number of confounding factors at baseline for separately test whether the rates of OHSU and IHSU were statistically significant. And since the outcome variable was binary, we decomposed the CI for OHSU and IHSU by the probit model to explore the contribution of various influential determinants to inequity. All statistical analyses were performed using STATA statistical software V.15.0 (StataCorp).

Patient and public involvement

No patient involved.

Results

Descriptive statistics

The sociodemographic characteristics of the participants are presented in table 1. A total of 3966 respondents were enrolled in this study. According to the descriptive statistics, 43.47% of the middle-aged and elderly individuals were male and 56.53% were female. Of the respondents, 54.77% were aged between 45 and 60 years, while 45.23% were aged over 60 years. A total of 71.21% for respondents had education below the elementary school level, and most respondents had insurance (95.74%). Furthermore, nearly 9/10 (88.48%) of respondents were married. Of the respondents, 37.75% smoked, while 29.48% consumed alcohol. Statistical differences existed between urban and rural populations in terms of sociodemographic characteristics such as education, insurance and economic status. Trends in OHSU and IHSU rates

Figure 2 presents the rates of health service utilisation of outpatients (A) and inpatients (B) from 2011 to 2018. Approximately 29.32% of respondents reported OHSU in 2011; this value slightly decreased to 29.07% in 2013, 27.22% in 2015, and 27.27% in 2018. Overall, the proportion of OHSU was higher in rural respondents than in urban respondents. Around 13.31% of respondents reported IHSU in 2011; this value slightly increased to 18.58% in 2013, 19.67% in 2015, and 19.89% in 2018. In contrast to OHSU, the proportion of IHSU was higher in urban respondents than in rural respondents.

Analysis of influencing factors

Table 2 shows the logistic regression analysis of influencing factors of OHSU and IHSU. The rates of OHSU were lower in 2018 (OR 0.77, 95% CI 0.68 to 0.88) than in 2011. Compared with rural areas, living in urban areas increased the OHSU rate by approximately 0.27 times (95% CI 1.08 to 1.50). Respondents who consumed alcohol were 0.25 (95% CI 0.66 to 0.86) times less likely to use outpatient health services than those who did not. Regarding economic status, the medium-status and high-status groups, respectively, were 0.16 (95% CI 1.01 to 1.32) and 0.34 (95% CI 1.18 to 1.53) times more likely to use outpatient health services than the low-status group. The rates of IHSU were higher in 2018 (OR 1.57, 95% CI 1.33 to 1.85) than in 2011. The results revealed that respondents older than 70 years were 1.23 (95% CI 1.61 to 3.11) times more likely to use inpatient health services than those aged 45–50 years. Respondents who consumed alcohol were 0.44 (95% CI 0.47 to 0.66) times less likely to use inpatient health services than those who did not. Compared with the low-status group, the medium-status and high-status groups, respectively, were 0.42 (95% CI 1.20 to 1.69) times and 0.54 (95% CI 1.30 to 1.82) times more likely to report IHSU.

Discussion

This study longitudinally explored and updated the overall trends of health service utilisation and its equity among middle-aged and elderly MCCs patients in China from 2011 to 2018 in three ways. First, we evaluated the trends of OHSU and IHSU using hierarchically and nationally representative data from the CHARLS. Second, we used panel data to estimate the equity of OHSU and IHSU. Third, we provided a detailed decomposition analysis of the CI for middle-aged and elderly MCCs patients’ OHSU and IHSU, facilitating the identification of effective strategies to reduce their inequity.

Regarding the overall trends of OHSU and IHSU, the OHSU rate of middle-aged and elderly MCCs patients declined and the IHSU rate increased in China from 2011 to 2018. There are two possible reasons for this phenomenon. First, due to the complex characteristics of the long-term periodic diagnosis and treatment of MCCs, fragmented and simplified outpatient services may no longer be able to meet the health needs of MCCs patients to the greatest extent, and middle-aged and elderly MCCs patients with IHSU may be more than those with OHSU.4 16 Second, the higher hospitalisation rate may also be caused by the higher reimbursement rate provided by medical insurance for hospitalisation and the lower out-of-pocket medical expenses.46 Notably, rural MCC patients used more outpatient services than urban ones, and urban inpatient services were used more than rural ones. One possible reason for this result is that rural MCCs patients with poorer economic status have some affordability for outpatient services, whereas hospitalisation has higher financial and time costs. The medical needs of poor rural MCCs patients may require more outpatient self-care to maintain the status quo and prevent disease progression rather than seeking the high-cost eradication of inpatient services and health management.47 In addition, rural MCCs patients may not have accessible health services due to imbalances in health institution establishment and health resource distribution that led to inconvenient transportation, an inadequate supply of healthcare professionals, and a lack of inpatient departments, making outpatient settings more convenient than inpatient ones in rural areas compared with urban areas.48 49

Regarding the equity of health service utilisation among middle-aged and elderly MCCs patients, significant inequity in OHSU and IHSU among middle-aged and elderly MCCs patients was present in China. In terms of OHSU, overall inequity trends fluctuated and declined, but the variation range was small. In terms of IHSU, we identified pro-rich inequity both in rural and urban settings. However, in general, the degree of inequity has been reduced, which indicates that the optimisation of health service policies and the active response to healthy ageing has been effective in China.27 50 Our findings suggest that inequity was greater in IHSU than in OHSU, whether in rural or urban areas, and that the abundant needs and equitable utilisation of health services of middle-aged and older MCCs patients of lower economic status were of greater concern, especially in rural areas. MCCs is particularly complex among older women, minority cultural groups, those of lower socioeconomic status or rural residents.51 5216,27,52 Significant inequity is present in access to health services for patients with a single chronic non-communicable disease, particularly prominent for IHSU.23 53 Studies have also found that the annual out‐of‐pocket expenditure on medications associated with 0–1, 2 and more than 3 chronic conditions increased by 2.7 times, 5.2 times and 10.1 times, respectively.4 MCCs add to the burden of equitable access to health services.

Further, regarding the decomposition analysis of inequity in health service utilisation among middle-aged and elderly MCCs patients, we would like to discuss four key points. First, we found that economic status made a pure percentage contribution to CI, which had the most significant association with inequity in health service utilisation. This finding was consistent with previous studies.16 23 26 33 Pro-rich middle-aged and elderly MCCs patients were more likely to have OHSU and IHSU. One possible explanation is that the financial burden of health service utilisation for MCCs patients is still relatively heavy.54 Previous studies showed that patients with MCCs had higher health service utilisation rates and costs.11 However, the current operation of the social health insurance system has failed to comprehensively reduce the gap between urban and rural MCCs patients regarding the out-of-pocket cost burden in health service utilisation.55 Middle-aged and elderly MCCs patients who are better off may have greater control over health costs and be willing to spend more to obtain higher-quality health services, while poorer MCCs patients often do not have equitable opportunities. Second, area made a notable pure percentage contribution to CI, which indicated that different urban and rural areas were significantly associated with equity of health service utilisation. One potential cause for this result is the large gap between urban and rural settings in resources for health service utilisation for MCCs.56 MCCs control requires well-equipped medical facilities, scientific treatment protocols and intensive medical care by specialised health personnel such as general practitioners.11 22 Due to the current imbalance in the allocation of human, material and financial resources between urban and rural areas in China, the poor quality of health services in primary care institutions, and the lack of comprehensive coverage of family general practitioners, it is difficult to provide equitable, accessible, professional and life-cycle health services to middle-aged and elderly MCCs patients of pro-poor economic status and rural areas. Third, we found that most middle-aged and elderly MCCs patients with higher education levels virtually used more health services. The explanation may be that middle-aged and elderly MCCs patients with higher levels of education have a stronger awareness of interventions, a superior medical knowledge base, and the ability to self-manage their medical conditions and health behaviours.57 58 Education had a significant impact on reducing and even eliminating inequity in health service utilisation.59 Fourth, elderly MCCs patients were significantly associated with the inequity occurring in health service utilisation. Ageing has gradually intensified in China. The threat of disease and mortality risk among middle-aged and elderly MCCs patients increases substantially with increasing age; thus, these patients have greater eligibility and demand for diverse health insurance coverage for health service utilisation to ensure more equitable access to prevention, treatment and care.

This study has several limitations. First, the data were self-reported and therefore subject to potential recall bias. Moreover, the CHARLS mainly collected information on 14 self-reported chronic diseases and ignored some common chronic diseases (such as osteoporosis), which may have affected the inclusion of MCCs groups. Second, the analysis was up to 2018, and the data may not be comprehensive enough to account for new factors such as the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic. Third, many potential unobserved confounding factors remained, and the availability of measured determinants was limited by the prespecified questions in this survey. For example, the utilisation of prognosis and rehabilitation services for MCCs patients, level and nature of medical institutions were not discussed in our study.

Conclusions

In summary, this study provided updated information about the trends, influencing factors and distinctive inequity of OHSU and IHSU among middle-aged and elderly MCCs patients in China from 2011 to 2018. We found a pro-rich inequity in both OHSU and IHSU for middle-aged and elderly MCCs patients in China, with a greater focus on the lower-economic-status population. The inequity in IHSU was more severe compared with that in OHSU both in rural and urban settings. Economic status, area, education and age made the greatest contributions to pro-rich inequity. We propose the following solutions: (1) Narrow the gap between the rich and the poor, promote common prosperity, and improve the assistance and payment systems related to health service utilisation for key middle-aged and elderly populations with a high prevalence of MCCs. Additionally, reform the payment method and pay for diseases according to the combination of the high prevalence of clinical MCCs to reduce the out-of-pocket ratio and financial burden of OHSU and IHSU; (2) Integrate the distribution of health service resources and lessen urban–rural disparities, strengthen the construction of primary health institutions in urban and rural areas, and improve the health service model and capacity for integrated MCCs; (3) Improve access to education and vigorously reinforce MCC-related knowledge to effectively control the development of MCCs and (4) Supply and optimise universal, comprehensive and multilevel insurance for MCCs for elderly MCCs populations.
