High need, low use: separating multidimensional need from formal healthcare use among older adults in China using CHARLS 2020
Authors: Lan Mi, Qiufeng Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEvidence on healthcare utilization among older adults in China has grown rapidly, but less is known about whether recent formal healthcare contact is aligned with multidimensional need. Because high need and low service use represent distinct processes, this study separated need status from service-use status and examined low formal healthcare use among older adults classified as high need.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional secondary analysis of CHARLS Wave 5 (2020), restricted to respondents aged 60 years or older. High need was defined strictly as at least two of four observed need indicators: poor self-rated health, any activities-of-daily-living (ADL) limitation, multimorbidity, and high depressive symptoms (CES-D-10 > = 10). Low formal healthcare use was defined as no outpatient service in the past month and no hospitalization in the past year. We first described a four-cell classification of need status by use status and then fitted the primary model for low use among high-need respondents. Models used logistic regression with community-clustered robust standard errors. We report odds ratios, average marginal effects, predicted probabilities, individual-weighted descriptive estimates, and sensitivity analyses including modified Poisson regression, individual-weighted regression, categorical education, inverse probability weighting, multiple imputation, and an IADL-based threshold validation.ResultsAmong 11,473 age-eligible respondents, 9,103 had complete information for the strict need-use classification. The high-need/low-use screening-proxy group comprised 2,516 respondents, representing 27.6% of the strict outcome-observed sample and 26.5% after applying the individual response-adjusted CHARLS weight. Among 4,434 high-need respondents, 2,693 had complete covariate data for the primary model; 1,549 (57.5%) reported low formal healthcare use. In the primary model, rural residence was not associated with low use among high-need respondents (adjusted OR 1.02, 95% CI 0.84–1.23; average marginal effect + 0.4% points). Male sex (OR 1.00, 95% CI 0.85–1.17), cognition (OR 1.02 per point, 95% CI 0.99–1.05), education, income, marital status, and surviving children also showed no strong association with low use in the high-need subgroup. By contrast, a sequential model showed that rural residence, lower income, lower cognition, older age, female sex, and not being married/cohabiting were associated with being classified as high need. The need-score threshold of > = 2 provided a pragmatic balance of sensitivity (0.78) and specificity (0.61) against IADL limitation as an external vulnerability marker; the AUC of the strict need score for IADL limitation was 0.745.ConclusionsA substantial subgroup of older adults in CHARLS 2020 had multidimensional need and no recent outpatient or inpatient use. However, once the analysis was restricted to high-need respondents, rural residence, sex, and cognition were not strong predictors of low formal use. Background

China is ageing rapidly. The Seventh National Population Census reported 264.02 million people aged 60 years or older in 2020, accounting for 18.70% of the national population [1]. The health system faces growing pressure from multimorbidity, functional decline, depressive symptoms, and cognitive vulnerability among older adults. CHARLS provides a national platform for studying ageing, health, family support, and healthcare use among community-dwelling adults aged 45 years or older and their spouses [2–4]. Recent comparative evidence from six Asia-Pacific countries, including China, shows that ADL, IADL, and mobility limitations remain key indicators of ageing-related functional vulnerability, with trends and social gradients varying across national contexts [5]. Recent national CHARLS analyses found that outpatient use in the past month and hospitalization in the past year remained limited among older adults [6], and depressive symptoms have also been associated with healthcare utilization patterns in CHARLS-based analyses [7]. Direct studies of self-reported unmet healthcare need further suggest that unmet needs are concentrated among adults with poor self-rated health, ADL assistance needs, multiple chronic conditions, and depressive symptoms [8]. Utilization counts are important, but they do not by themselves show whether formal service use is aligned with need. A policy-relevant question is whether older adults with layered need have recent contact with the formal medical system that is plausibly commensurate with their need profile.

The conceptual challenge is that high need and low use are distinct processes. If a composite outcome, such as high need combined with low use, is modeled in the full sample, the reference group contains several heterogeneous groups: lower-need respondents with some use, lower-need respondents with low use, and high-need respondents with some use. Such a model can identify correlates of a composite category, but it cannot show whether associations reflect determinants of need, determinants of service use among high-need respondents, or both. Earlier studies in Chinese older adults with multimorbidity, functional impairment, limited mobility, or home- and community-based care needs likewise suggest the importance of distinguishing healthcare need from observed service use [9, 10]. Therefore, this study first classifies respondents by need status and service-use status and then models low formal healthcare use within the high-need subgroup.

We intentionally frame the outcome as a screening proxy for possible need-use misalignment rather than as direct evidence of unmet need. CHARLS does not capture every relevant form of care, including informal family support, self-management, traditional Chinese medicine outside formal service-use items, village-doctor or family-doctor contacts, preventive public-health services, medication refills, or home- and community-based support [10–13]. A respondent with high need and no recent outpatient or inpatient use may or may not have had actual unmet need. Nevertheless, this classification can help identify a policy-relevant subgroup whose recent formal medical contact appears low relative to a multidimensional need profile.

Guided by Andersen’s behavioral model of health service use [14], we organized respondent characteristics into predisposing, enabling, and need-related domains. The primary estimand was low formal healthcare use among respondents classified as high need. We also present a four-cell need-by-use classification, use CHARLS individual weights for descriptive estimates, apply community-clustered robust standard errors, report absolute marginal effects, and conduct missing-data, threshold, and model-specification analyses.

Methods

Study design and data source

This was a cross-sectional secondary analysis of CHARLS Wave 5 (2020). CHARLS is a national ageing survey using multistage probability sampling, with respondents followed across survey waves [2–4]. We restricted the analysis to Wave 5 respondents aged 60 years or older. The age-eligible analytic source file contained 11,473 respondents. Respondent identifiers were harmonized as 12-digit strings to merge the official Wave 5 individual weights; 10,706 age-eligible respondents had an individual response-adjusted weight available.

Ethics

The present secondary analysis was approved by the Ethics Committee of Jiangsu Nursing College (approval number JSCN-ME-2026041311). The original CHARLS study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Peking University (IRB00001052-11015), and all participants provided informed consent [2]. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Need and use definitions

Need score was constructed from four binary indicators. Poor self-rated health was coded 1 for poor or very poor self-rated health on the CHARLS scale. ADL limitation was coded 1 if the respondent reported any difficulty with dressing, bathing, eating, getting into or out of bed, using the toilet, or controlling urination/defecation. Multimorbidity was coded 1 for two or more chronic conditions among 14 conditions: hypertension, diabetes, cancer, chronic lung disease, heart disease, stroke, psychiatric or emotional disorder, arthritis or rheumatism, dyslipidemia, liver disease, kidney disease, digestive disease, asthma, and memory-related disease. High depressive symptoms were coded 1 for CES-D-10 > = 10 [15]. In the primary analysis, the need score was calculated only when all four components were observed. The score ranged from 0 to 4, and high need was defined as a score of 2 or higher.

Low formal healthcare use was defined as reporting no outpatient service in the past month and no inpatient service or hospitalization in the past year. The high-need/low-use category was treated descriptively as a screening proxy for possible need-use misalignment. The primary regression estimand was low formal use among respondents classified as high need, rather than the composite high-need/low-use category in the full sample.

Covariates

The core model included age, sex, marital status, rural residence, education level, log household income, number of surviving children, and cognition score. Education was modeled as a four-level ordinal variable in the primary model, with higher values indicating higher attainment; a categorical specification was used as a sensitivity analysis. Household income was recoded to nonnegative values before applying log(1 + income). Cognition was measured using the CHARLS total cognition score, ranging from 0 to 21, with higher values indicating better cognition. An expanded Andersen-framework sensitivity model additionally included medical insurance, pension insurance, and retirement status.

Threshold validation and sensitivity analyses

Because no high-need threshold is intrinsically definitive, we evaluated thresholds of need score > = 1, >=2, and > = 3 against IADL limitation as an external vulnerability marker. This does not validate unmet need directly, but it tests whether the multidomain need score aligns with an independent functional-severity indicator. We report sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and the AUC of the strict need score for IADL limitation.

Missing data and statistical analysis

We summarized missingness in the strict need-use classification, household income, cognition, and weights. We compared respondents with and without observed strict need-use classification, and compared primary complete cases with respondents excluded from the primary high-need model. The primary model used complete cases because the model required observed high-need status, low-use outcome, and all core covariates. To assess the influence of missing covariates, we conducted inverse probability weighting for complete-case inclusion and multiple imputation for missing income and cognition among high-need respondents with observed low-use status. The imputation model included the outcome, model covariates, need score and components, IADL, insurance, pension, and retirement status; 30 imputed datasets were pooled using Rubin’s rules [16, 17].

For descriptive population-oriented estimates, we used the CHARLS individual response-adjusted weight. Primary regression inference used community-clustered robust standard errors, and individual-weighted logistic regression with community clustering was reported as a sensitivity analysis. We also fitted modified Poisson regression with robust variance to estimate risk ratios, a categorical education model, an expanded Andersen model, and a full-sample composite-outcome comparison model. Analyses were performed in Python 3.13 using pandas 2.2.3, statsmodels 0.14.6, scikit-learn 1.8.0, SciPy 1.17.0, and NumPy 2.3.5. Reporting followed STROBE guidance [18]. Additional sample-flow, missing-data, threshold-validation, and sensitivity-analysis results are provided in Supplementary Tables S1-S22.

Results

Sample flow and four-cell need-use classification

Among 11,473 Wave 5 respondents aged 60 years or older, 9,103 had complete information for the strict four-component need-use classification. Of these, 4,434 were classified as high need. The primary high-need complete-case model included 2,693 respondents, of whom 1,549 reported low formal use and 1,144 reported some outpatient or inpatient use (Fig. 1).

Primary model: low formal use among high-need respondents

In the primary model (Table 3; Fig. 2), rural residence was not associated with low use among high-need respondents (OR 1.02, 95% CI 0.84–1.23; p = 0.850; AME + 0.4% points). Male sex was also not associated with low use (OR 1.00, 95% CI 0.85–1.17; p = 0.986; AME + 0.0% points). Cognition showed a small positive but statistically uncertain association (OR 1.02 per point, 95% CI 0.99–1.05; p = 0.120; AME + 0.5% points). Education showed a modest, borderline inverse association (OR 0.94 per category, 95% CI 0.87–1.01; p = 0.096; AME − 1.6% points). Predicted probabilities were substantively similar for rural versus urban respondents (57.7% vs. 57.3%) and for men versus women (57.5% vs. 57.5%).

Sequential and sensitivity analyses

Sequential modeling clarified why a full-sample composite-outcome comparison model can produce different findings. In a model for high-need status among strict complete cases, rural residence was positively associated with high need (OR 1.22, 95% CI 1.06–1.40), while male sex (OR 0.65, 95% CI 0.58–0.73), higher income (OR 0.95 per log unit, 95% CI 0.93–0.97), and higher cognition (OR 0.94 per point, 95% CI 0.93–0.96) were inversely associated with high need. In contrast, those variables were not strong predictors of low formal use once the analysis was restricted to high-need respondents. This supports the methodological concern that composite-outcome models can mix determinants of need with determinants of service use.

Sensitivity analyses supported the interpretation. The individual-weighted high-need model yielded similar conclusions: rural residence OR 1.03 (95% CI 0.78–1.36), male sex OR 1.04 (95% CI 0.84–1.30), and cognition OR 1.03 per point (95% CI 1.00-1.07). Modified Poisson regression similarly showed no strong association for rural residence (RR 1.01, 95% CI 0.93–1.09), male sex (RR 1.00, 95% CI 0.94–1.07), or cognition (RR 1.01, 95% CI 1.00-1.02). The IPW and multiple-imputation models did not change the substantive interpretation. Treating education as categorical produced no clear non-linear pattern, although the highest category tended to have lower low-use probability.

Threshold validation

The strict need score aligned with IADL limitation, with an AUC of 0.745. A threshold of need score > = 1 was highly sensitive but nonspecific, whereas a threshold of > = 3 was highly specific but identified a smaller, more severe group. The primary threshold of > = 2 provided a pragmatic balance between sensitivity (0.779), specificity (0.605), face validity, and sample size. We therefore retained it for the primary analysis without describing it as a definitive or formally validated unmet-need threshold (Supplementary Tables S9 and S10).

Missing data

Missingness was not random. The strict need-use classification was missing for 2,370 respondents, largely because depressive symptoms or self-rated health were missing. Compared with respondents with observed strict outcome classification, respondents missing the classification were older, less likely to be married/cohabiting, less educated, had more surviving children, and were more likely to have ADL and IADL limitations. Among respondents with observed strict need-use classification, complete cases for the primary covariate set were healthier and more socioeconomically advantaged than excluded respondents. These patterns support cautious interpretation and justify the IPW and multiple-imputation sensitivity analyses.

Discussion

Decomposing need status and service-use status changes the interpretation that would be drawn from a full-sample composite-outcome model. The high-need/low-use category remains descriptively important: more than one quarter of older adults with complete strict need-use data had multidimensional need and no recent outpatient or inpatient use, and the weighted estimate was similar. However, the primary model shows that, among respondents already classified as high need, rural residence, sex, and cognition were not strong predictors of low formal use. The associations observed in the full-sample composite-outcome comparison model were largely attributable to predictors of being classified as high need, not predictors of low use within the high-need subgroup.

This distinction is methodologically important for health-services research. A composite outcome can be useful as a transparent descriptive proxy, but it should not be interpreted as showing determinants of low use among those with high need unless the analysis is restricted to the high-need subgroup or otherwise decomposes the composite. The four-cell classification and sequential models make this distinction explicit. They show that rural residence, lower income, lower cognition, older age, and female sex are associated with multidimensional need, whereas recent formal use among those with high need appears less clearly patterned by these individual-level characteristics.

The results should also be interpreted in light of the 2020 context. Healthcare-seeking in 2020 was disrupted by the COVID-19 pandemic, and CHARLS 2020 included pandemic-related delayed-care questions in the broader health module [19, 20]. The analytic extract available for the present analysis did not contain those delayed-care variables, so we could not directly distinguish voluntary postponement, fear of infection, institutional disruption, or reduced clinical need. This limitation is especially relevant because our low-use definition depends on recent outpatient and inpatient contact.

The threshold analysis supports the use of the > = 2 need-score threshold as a pragmatic, not definitive, decision rule. It required need in at least two domains and performed reasonably against IADL limitation as an external vulnerability marker. Still, IADL limitation is not a direct measure of unmet healthcare need. The proxy therefore identifies possible need-use misalignment rather than inadequate care. Some high-need/low-use respondents may have received effective support through informal care, self-management, family-doctor or village-doctor contacts, preventive services, medication refills, traditional Chinese medicine, or home- and community-based services not captured by the outpatient and inpatient items.

The findings have practical implications. First, studies of access and equity should avoid treating high need plus low use as a simple binary determinant model without decomposing need and use. Second, the descriptive high-need/low-use group remains useful for screening and targeting: it identifies older adults with multidimensional vulnerability whose recent formal medical contact is limited. Third, improving continuity and responsiveness of primary care, family-doctor teams, rural health services, and community-based follow-up may still be important [11–13], but the present analysis does not show that rural residence independently predicts low use among high-need respondents after conditioning on observed covariates.

Limitations

Several limitations remain. First, the high-need/low-use category is a screening proxy and should not be interpreted as direct evidence of unmet need, inappropriate care, or poor care quality. Second, formal use was measured only through outpatient use in the past month and hospitalization in the past year; the data do not fully capture chronic disease follow-up, medication refills, public-health services, traditional Chinese medicine, family-doctor or village-doctor contacts, home-based care, or informal support [10–13]. Third, the cross-sectional design precludes causal interpretation. Fourth, missingness in depression, income, and cognition was substantial and selective, although IPW and multiple-imputation analyses were used to assess robustness. Fifth, individual weights were available and used for descriptive estimates and a weighted sensitivity model, but full stratified survey-design variance estimation was limited by the design variables available in the analytic extract. Finally, the 2020 pandemic context may limit generalizability to non-pandemic years [19, 20].

Conclusions

A sizeable subgroup of older adults in CHARLS 2020 had multidimensional need and no recent outpatient or inpatient use. After decomposing need and use, however, rural residence, sex, and cognition were not strong predictors of low formal use among high-need respondents. The results support using the high-need/low-use category as a transparent screening proxy for possible need-use misalignment, while emphasizing that full-sample composite models can conflate determinants of need with determinants of service use. Future work should incorporate direct unmet-need measures, delayed-care variables, community service supply, longitudinal patterns of care, and full survey-design information to better distinguish unmet need from appropriate non-use or care outside the measured formal services.
