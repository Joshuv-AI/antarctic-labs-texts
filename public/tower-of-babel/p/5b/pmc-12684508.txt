Plasma Phenylacetylglutamine and Cognitive Impairment After Ischemic Stroke
Authors: Jiawen Fei, Xinyue Chang, Wenjing Yang, Yu He, Yi Liu, Mengyao Shi, Yang Liu, Xiaoxiao Wang, Hao Peng, Xiaoqing Bu, Tan Xu, Aili Wang, Tian Xu, Yonghong Zhang, Zhengbao Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPhenylacetylglutamine was reported to be associated with ischemic stroke and cognitive performance, but its association with poststroke cognitive impairment (PSCI) remained unclear. We aimed to prospectively investigate the association between plasma phenylacetylglutamine levels and PSCI at 3 months in a multicenter cohort study.MethodsA total of 617 patients with ischemic stroke were included on the basis of a preplanned ancillary study from CATIS (China Antihypertensive Trial in Acute Ischemic Stroke). PSCI was evaluated using the Mini‐Mental State Examination scale and Montreal Cognitive Assessment scale. Logistic regression analyses were performed to evaluate the association between plasma phenylacetylglutamine and the risk of 3‐month PSCI.ResultsAccording to the Mini‐Mental State Examination score, a total of 376 participants developed PSCI at 3 months. After adjustment for age, sex, education, and other important risk factors, the multivariable‐adjusted odds ratio of PSCI for the highest tertile of phenylacetylglutamine was 2.16 (95% CI, 1.32–3.54; P trend=0.002) compared with the lowest tertile. A multiple‐adjusted spline regression model showed a positive linear association of plasma phenylacetylglutamine levels with PSCI at 3 months (P for linearity<0.001). Adding plasma phenylacetylglutamine to conventional risk factors significantly improved the risk reclassification of PSCI (net reclassification improvement: 21.35%, P=0.019; integrated discrimination index: 1.78%, P=0.003). Similar significant findings were observed when PSCI was defined by the Montreal Cognitive Assessment score.ConclusionsHigh plasma phenylacetylglutamine levels were associated with increased odds of PSCI at 3 months, suggesting that plasma phenylacetylglutamine might be a potential predictive biomarker for PSCI among patients with ischemic stroke.Keywords: biomarker, cognitive impairment, ischemic stroke, phenylacetylglutamine, prognosisSubject Categories: Ischemic Stroke, Cerebrovascular Disease/Stroke, Cognitive Impairment

Stroke is the third‐leading cause of death and disability combined globally, 1 and ischemic stroke is the most common type of stroke. 2 Poststroke cognitive impairment (PSCI) is a major complication of stroke, 3 affecting nearly 80% of stroke survivors. 4 Several clinical studies have reported that PSCI is associated with a variety of adverse clinical outcomes after stroke, including stroke recurrence, death, and functional disability. 5 , 6 , 7 Therefore, it is necessary to explore novel and reliable biomarkers to identify patients at high risk of PSCI and provide clinical evidence for better prevention.

Gut microbiota plays a critical role in the occurrence and development of ischemic stroke, 8 and microbiota‐related metabolites can modulate brain function by penetrating the gut mucosa layer into the bloodstream. 9 Phenylacetylglutamine, a newly discovered gut microbiota‐dependent metabolite, 10 has been shown to be associated with major adverse cardiovascular events (eg, myocardial infarction, stroke, and cardiovascular death). 10 , 11 Beyond its vascular effects, phenylacetylglutamine is also neurotoxic and can impair neuronal function, 12 and elevated phenylacetylglutamine levels are associated with severe neurological deficits. 13 Our recent study reported that elevated plasma phenylacetylglutamine was associated with increased risks of disability and death after ischemic stroke. 14 In addition, previous studies found that phenylacetylglutamine was also implicated in poor cognitive performance (eg, impairments in executive function and semantic memory) 15 , 16 and neurodegenerative diseases (eg, Alzheimer disease and Parkinson disease). 17 , 18 , 19 However, no studies have investigated the relationship between plasma phenylacetylglutamine levels and cognitive decline among patients with ischemic stroke, and the predictive value of phenylacetylglutamine on PSCI remains unclear.

Given the crucial role of phenylacetylglutamine in ischemic stroke 20 and cognitive function, 15 phenylacetylglutamine appears to be a promising biomarker for PSCI. Therefore, we prospectively investigated the association between plasma phenylacetylglutamine levels and the odds of PSCI among patients with ischemic stroke in a multicenter study based on CATIS (China Antihypertensive Trial in Acute Ischemic Stroke).

Methods

Data Availability

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

Study Design and Participants

CATIS was a multicenter, single‐blind, blinded end points randomized controlled clinical trial conducted in 26 hospitals across China to test whether early antihypertensive intervention after acute ischemic stroke would reduce death or major disability. Detailed methods of CATIS have been described previously. 21 In brief, from August 2009 to May 2013, CATIS recruited 4071 patients aged >22 years who had computed tomography– or magnetic resonance imaging–confirmed ischemic stroke within 48 hours of onset and who had an elevated systolic blood pressure (BP) between 140 and 220 mm Hg. Patients with a systolic BP ≥220 mm Hg or diastolic BP ≥120 mm Hg, who had severe heart failure, acute myocardial infarction or unstable angina, atrial fibrillation, aortic dissection, cerebrovascular stenosis, resistant hypertension, or deep coma, or were receiving intravenous thrombolytic therapy were excluded from CATIS.

The present multicenter study was based on a preplanned cognitive substudy before the recruitment of participants of the CATIS trial, which was designed to test whether early antihypertensive treatment could reduce cognitive impairment in patients with acute ischemic stroke. 22 All CATIS participating hospitals participated in a special training session on the cognitive assessment, and satisfactory performance during a written test on knowledge of cognitive assessment were required for certification as a participating hospital for cognitive substudy. After a special training session, 7 hospitals among the 26 participating hospitals of the CATIS trial were qualified to assess cognitive function. The exclusion criteria for the cognitive substudy were visual or hearing impairment substantial enough to hinder performance on cognitive testing. 22 In each of the 7 participating hospitals, the first 80 to 100 eligible patients consecutively enrolled in the main CATIS study were invited to participate in the cognitive substudy. From August 2009 to November 2012, a total of 660 patients were recruited in this cognitive substudy of the CATIS Trial. A total of 21 patients were excluded due to a lack of blood samples at baseline, 15 patients were lost to follow‐up, and 7 patients were deceased within 3 months after stroke onset. Therefore, a total of 617 patients were finally included in the present analysis (Figure S1).

This study was approved by the ethical committees at Soochow University in China and the participating hospitals. Written consent was obtained from all study participants or their immediate family members.

Data Collection

Baseline data on demographic characteristics, clinical features, and medical history were collected by trained interviewers using a standard questionnaire at admission. The National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale was used to evaluate stroke severity by trained neurologists at baseline. According to the symptoms and imaging data of the patients, ischemic stroke was classified as large‐artery atherosclerosis (thrombotic), cardiac embolism (embolic), and small‐artery occlusion lacunae (lacunar) on the basis of the Trial of Org 10172 in Acute Stroke Treatment criteria by an adjudicating committee. 23 Three BP measurements were obtained at admission by trained nurses when the patient was in the supine position according to the procedures recommended by the American Heart Association.

Plasma Phenylacetylglutamine Measurement

Fasting blood samples were collected after at least 8 hours of fasting within 24 hours of hospital admission. All plasma samples were separated at the clinical laboratories of the participating hospitals and immediately frozen at −80°C until laboratory testing. Plasma phenylacetylglutamine was quantified on an ultra‐performance liquid chromatography coupled with triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (ACQUITY UPLC I‐Class/Xevo TQ‐S, Waters Corp., Milford, MA), using phenylacetylglutamine (catalog no. P306500‐10MG, TRC) as the internal standard. The metabolites were chromatographically eluted with 0.1% formic acid water and 0.1% formic acid acetonitrile and separated on a C18 column (2.1×50 mm, 1.6 μm; Waters Corp.) at 45 °C. The chromatographic gradient ramped from 1% weak elution to 99% in 4.5 minutes, setting flow rate at 0.5 mL/min. In multiple reaction monitoring mode, the main parameters of mass spectrometer were optimized to desolvation gas flow 1000 L/h, source temperature 150 °C, desolvation temperature 550 °C, and capillary voltage 2 kV. Quality control samples and blank samples were interspersed in the whole detection procession. The intra‐ and interassay coefficients of variation for the phenylacetylglutamine measurements were <2.18% and 3.96%, respectively. The raw measurement files were processed using MassLynx software version 4.1 (Waters Corp.) to perform peak integration, calibration, and quantitation for phenylacetylglutamine. Laboratory technicians who performed these measurements were blinded to the clinical characteristics and outcomes of patients.

Outcome Assessment

The primary outcome was cognitive impairment at 3 months after ischemic stroke assessed by trained neurologists using the Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) scale and the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) scale in Chinese. The MMSE is an 11‐item scale that tests 5 areas of cognitive function: orientation, registration, attention and calculation, recall, and language, with a maximum score of 30. 24 The MoCA evaluates visuospatial/executive functions, naming, memory, attention, language, abstraction, and orientation, and also has a maximum score of 30. 25 We defined PSCI using the following age‐ and education‐adjusted thresholds of the MMSE and MoCA: MMSE score <26.5 or MoCA score <19.5 for individuals aged ≤75 years with education ≤6 years; MMSE score <22.5 or MoCA score <15.5 for individuals aged >75 years with education ≤6 years; MMSE score <28.5 or MoCA score <24.5 for individuals aged ≤75 years with education >6 years; and MMSE score <26.5 or MoCA score <24.5 for individuals aged >75 years with education >6 years. 26

Statistical Analysis

All participants were divided into 3 groups according to the tertiles of plasma phenylacetylglutamine levels. Plasma phenylacetylglutamine levels in this study were right‐skewed due to a small number of extremely high values (Shapiro–Wilk test, P<0.001). To normalize the distribution and enable more valid and interpretable statistical modeling, we applied a natural log transformation to improve the normality of plasma phenylacetylglutamine levels (Shapiro–Wilk test, P=0.281). Multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to calculate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% CIs of PSCI for upper tertiles of phenylacetylglutamine with the lowest tertile as a reference. Potential covariates for PSCI were selected on the basis of prior knowledge. 27 , 28 Covariates included in the multivariable models were age (<75, ≥75 years), sex (men, women), education (years), current cigarette smoking (yes, no), current alcohol drinking (yes, no), time from onset to randomization (hours), baseline National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score (<4, ≥4), body mass index (<24, ≥24 kg/m2), total cholesterol (mmol/L), platelet count (109/L), white blood cell count (109/L), high‐sensitivity C‐reactive protein (mg/L), diabetes (yes, no), hypertension (yes, no), history of coronary heart disease (yes, no), and family history of stroke (yes, no). In the present study, the percentages of missing data were as follows: 0.32% for time from onset to randomization, 4.38% for white blood cell counts, 2.43% for blood glucose, 6.32% for high‐sensitivity C‐reactive protein, 8.43% for total cholesterol, 4.38% for platelet count, and 6.32% for body mass index. Given that the proportion of missing values was relatively low for each covariate (<10%), we conducted a complete case analysis without imputation in this study. To evaluate the robustness of the association between plasma phenylacetylglutamine and PSCI, we performed sensitivity analyses by further adjusting randomized treatment or use of antihypertensive medications or lipid‐lowering drugs before stroke onset in the multivariable models. To further address the potential between‐center variability, we performed an additional sensitivity analysis incorporating study center as a random effect in mixed‐effects model for the association between plasma phenylacetylglutamine and PSCI.

Cubic spline analysis fits restricted cubic splines within a logistic regression model to allow for nonlinear associations, with P values derived from likelihood ratio tests assessing both linear and nonlinear components. 29 We applied this method to further evaluate the pattern of the association between plasma phenylacetylglutamine and PSCI, with 4 knots placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of the phenylacetylglutamine distribution. In addition, net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) are 2 indexes that assess the improvement in model performance accomplished by adding new markers. 30 We constructed a conventional model that included only conventional risk factors and then developed an extended model by adding plasma phenylacetylglutamine to the conventional risk factors. To assess whether adding plasma phenylacetylglutamine to the conventional risk factors could improve the predictive ability for PSCI, we calculated NRI and IDI by comparing these 2 models. A 2‐tailed P<0.05 was considered to be statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using SAS statistical software version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

Results

Association Between Plasma Phenylacetylglutamine and PSCI

There were 617 patients (432 men and 185 women) included in the current study, and the mean±SD age was 60.0±10.5 years (Table 1). According to the MMSE score at 3 months, 376 participants had cognitive impairment. After adjustment for age, sex, education, and other potential confounders, the OR of PSCI associated with the highest tertile of plasma phenylacetylglutamine was 2.16 (95% CI, 1.32–3.54; P trend=0.002; Table 2) compared with the lowest tertile. On continuous analysis, each SD increase of log‐transformed phenylacetylglutamine was associated with a 45% (95% CI, 18%–77%) increased risk of MMSE‐defined PSCI (Table 2). According to the MoCA score at 3 months, a total of 344 patients developed cognitive impairment. As shown in Table 2, the multivariable‐adjusted OR of PSCI for the highest versus the lowest tertile of plasma phenylacetylglutamine was 2.03 (95% CI, 1.25–3.28; P trend=0.006). In addition, each SD increase of log‐transformed phenylacetylglutamine was associated with a 29% (95% CI, 7%–57%) increased risk of MoCA‐defined PSCI (Table 2).

Incremental Predictive Value of Phenylacetylglutamine for PSCI

We further assessed whether plasma phenylacetylglutamine could improve the risk prediction of PSCI at 3 months beyond conventional risk factors. As shown in Table 4, adding plasma phenylacetylglutamine to the conventional risk factors could significantly improve the risk reclassification for MMSE‐defined PSCI (NRI, 21.35% [95% CI, 3.62%–39.08%]; P=0.019; IDI, 1.78% [95% CI, 0.63%–2.94%]; P=0.003) and MoCA‐defined PSCI (NRI, 18.53% [95% CI, 1.20%–35.86%]; P=0.037; IDI, 1.36% [95% CI, 0.35%–2.38%]; P=0.008) at 3 months.

Discussion

In the present multicenter study, we found that high phenylacetylglutamine levels were associated with increased odds of cognitive decline among patients with ischemic stroke, and there were positive linear dose–response associations between plasma phenylacetylglutamine and PSCI. These associations were independent of conventional risk factors for PSCI, and sensitivity analyses further confirmed these findings. In addition, plasma phenylacetylglutamine could significantly improve the risk reclassification for PSCI beyond conventional risk factors, as evidenced by NRI and IDI. Our findings provided potential evidence for the association of plasma phenylacetylglutamine with PSCI and suggested that plasma phenylacetylglutamine might be a valuable predictive biomarker for cognitive decline among patients with ischemic stroke.

As a gut microbiome–dependent metabolite derived from the essential amino acid phenylalanine, phenylacetylglutamine has a direct neurotoxic effect by crossing the blood–brain barrier and contacting neurons in the cortical layers. 12 Recently, high phenylacetylglutamine levels were observed to be associated with increased risks of stroke recurrence and poor functional prognosis of ischemic stroke. 13 , 20 In addition, previous studies also found that the alterations of gut microbiota and metabolites played important roles in the development and progression of cognitive impairment. 31 , 32 It had been reported that elevated phenylacetylglutamine levels were related to poor cognitive performance, including language, memory, and executive functioning. 15 , 16 Taken together, these findings suggested that microbiota‐derived phenylacetylglutamine might be associated with cognitive function among patients with ischemic stroke.

To date, there were no clinical studies investigating the association between plasma phenylacetylglutamine and PSCI, but several studies had explored the potential relationships between phenylacetylglutamine and neurodegenerative diseases. 17 , 18 , 19 For example, in an analysis based on 29 patients with Alzheimer disease and 59 cognitively normal individuals, serum phenylacetylglutamine levels were significantly higher in patients with Alzheimer disease compared with cognitively normal individuals. 33 In addition, Shao et al. showed that plasma phenylacetylglutamine levels were elevated in patients with Parkinson disease, and the plasma phenylacetylglutamine levels were higher in patients with advanced‐stage Parkinson disease than those in early‐stage Parkinson disease. 34 Notably, a recent study reported a potential protective role of phenylacetylglutamine in reducing cancer risk, 35 highlighting its pleiotropic and context‐specific biological effects. Therefore, further investigation into the role of phenylacetylglutamine in different pathological conditions is warranted. In this multicenter cohort study, we further extended the current knowledge to the patients with ischemic stroke and found that high plasma phenylacetylglutamine levels were significantly associated with cognitive decline after ischemic stroke. In addition, adding plasma phenylacetylglutamine to conventional risk factors could also improve the risk reclassification for PSCI, suggesting that plasma phenylacetylglutamine might be a potential biomarker for PSCI.

Given the high incidence and adverse impacts of PSCI among patients with ischemic stroke, 4 novel and accurate biomarkers are needed for better prevention and intervention of PSCI. From our findings, patients with ischemic stroke with high plasma phenylacetylglutamine levels might be at a high risk of PSCI and should receive aggressive monitoring and therapeutic interventions to prevent subsequent cognitive impairment. Of note, carvedilol, a commonly used β blocker in clinical practice, has been reported to reverse the detrimental effects of phenylacetylglutamine by attenuating phenylacetylglutamine‐induced platelet hyperreactivity and thrombus formation through blockade of adrenergic receptors. 11 Therefore, further clinical studies are needed to evaluate whether β blockers can prevent cognitive decline in patients with ischemic stroke with high plasma phenylacetylglutamine levels.

The mechanisms underlying the observed associations between plasma phenylacetylglutamine and PSCI remain unclear, but several potential mechanisms may explain these relationships. Previous studies have found that high phenylacetylglutamine levels were associated with moderate to severe white matter hyperintensities, 36 which was associated with an increased risk of cognitive impairment. 37 , 38 In addition, phenylacetylglutamine is implicated in inflammatory responses by upregulating the expression of proinflammatory cytokines, 39 thereby promoting the progression of cognitive impairment. 40 Furthermore, phenylacetylglutamine can induce the production of reactive oxygen species, 41 , 42 which may induce vascular dysfunction and ultimately result in vascular cognitive impairment. 43 Further studies are needed to assess the detailed mechanisms of the associations between plasma phenylacetylglutamine and PSCI.

Our study is the first to investigate the association between plasma phenylacetylglutamine levels and PSCI based on a multicenter cohort study, which used standardized protocols and strict quality control procedures to collect data and assess outcomes. In addition, comprehensive information on potential confounders was collected and controlled in the multivariable models, so our study could provide a valid appraisal of the association between plasma phenylacetylglutamine and PSCI. However, the present study has several limitations. First, our study was based on the CATIS trial, which excluded patients with atrial fibrillation, severe cerebrovascular stenosis, and BP ≥220/120 mm Hg at admission, so there might be a selection bias in our study. Second, plasma phenylacetylglutamine levels were measured only once in the acute phase of ischemic stroke, so we were unable to evaluate the associations between phenylacetylglutamine changes and the cognitive function among patients with ischemic stroke. Third, phenylacetylglutamine can be influenced by diet and gut microbiota, but there are no data on the dietary habits of our participants. Therefore, there might be a confounding bias in our study. Finally, all patients in our study are Chinese, so our findings should be applied to other populations cautiously. Further studies conducted among different populations are needed to confirm our findings.

Conclusions

Elevated plasma phenylacetylglutamine levels were associated with increased odds of PSCI at 3 months, suggesting that plasma phenylacetylglutamine might be a potential predictive biomarker for PSCI. Further studies from various populations are needed to verify our findings and clarify the potential mechanisms.
