Identifying delayed human response to external risks: an econometric analysis of mobility change during a pandemic
Authors: Gaofei Zhang, Ann Osi, Navid Ghaffarzadegan, Hazhir Rahmandad, Ran Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHuman behavioral responses to changes in risks are often delayed. Methods for estimating these delayed responses either rely on rigid assumptions about the delay distribution (e.g., Erlang distribution), producing a poor fit, or yield period-specific estimates (e.g., estimates from the Autoregressive Distributed Lag (ARDL) model) that are difficult to integrate into simulation models. We propose a hybrid ARDL–Erlang approach that yields an interpretable summary of behavioral responses suitable for incorporation into simulation models.MethodWe apply the ARDL–Erlang approach to estimate the effect of COVID-19 deaths on mobility across US counties from October 2020 to July 2021. A standard panel autoregressive distributed lag (ARDL) model first estimates the effect of past deaths and past mobility on current mobility. The ARDL model is then transformed into an Infinite Distributed Lag (IDL) model consisting of only past deaths. The coefficients of the past deaths are aggregated into an overall effect and fit to an Erlang distribution, summarized by average delay length and shape parameter.ResultsOur results show that on the national level, a one-standard-deviation permanent increase in weekly deaths per 100,000 population (log-transformed) is associated with a 0.46-standard-deviation decrease in human mobility in the long run, where the delay distribution follows a first-order Erlang distribution, and the average delay length is about 3.2 weeks. However, there is much heterogeneity across states, with first- to third-order Erlang delays and 2 to 18 weeks of average delay providing a theoretically cogent summary of how mobility followed changes in deaths during the first year and a half of the pandemic.ConclusionThis study provides a novel approach to estimating delayed human responses to health risks using a hybrid ARDL-Erlang model. Our findings highlight significant variability in the impact and timing of responses across states, underscoring the need for tailored public health policies. This study can also serve as guidelines and an example for identifying delayed human behavior in other settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12874-025-02696-x.Keywords: Human behavior, Causality, Delay, COVID-19

Background

Understanding societal responses to various changes in social and environmental systems, like epidemics, natural disasters, or significant policy changes, is a topic of interest in many research fields [1, 2]. Quantifying the strength of these responses is particularly challenging when the events unfold gradually and persist over time, resulting in delayed and diffuse public responses. For instance, disruptive events such as ongoing climate change (e.g., rising sea levels, changes in weather patterns) or the emergence of a novel infectious disease (e.g., the COVID-19 pandemic) are realized by the public as experts and media report on those, and people start to see their impact in their day-to-day lives. Upon receiving the information, the public perceives the risks or rewards of the focal event, which later trigger changes in thoughts, behaviors, and policies. This process introduces delays between the event’s onset and the observable societal responses [3]. As the intensity of events evolves through time, public perception of risks also evolves, leading to dynamic changes in behavioral responses. This delay in behavioral responses is a common feature of social and environmental systems.

Unlike processes where delays between stimuli and response can be readily measured (e.g., average evacuation time during drills or service time in a supermarket) or estimated theoretically (e.g., chemical reaction rates), quantifying delays in behavioral responses presents a challenge. The significant variability of societal reactions makes the average duration and distribution of delays unknown and not directly observable. Consequently, different studies make simplifying assumptions. Some studies estimate only immediate behavioral changes [4–6], while others assume a fixed delay without estimating its length or distribution [7]. These approaches fail to capture the nuanced dynamics of behavioral responses through time and may lead to biased estimates. In reality, delays are not fixed but follow probabilistic distributions [3, 8]. Ignoring these delays can lead to unreliable and inconsistent estimates for critical concepts such as the responsiveness of individuals and communities to risk [9].

Econometric methods offer greater flexibility in representing delayed behavior. One of the most general is the autoregressive distributed lag model (ARDL), which captures the influence of both past values of the dependent variable (autoregressive component) and past and current values of the explanatory variables (distributed lag component) on the current value of the dependent variable [9]. It allows for estimating short-run effects (immediate impact), cumulative effects (through a lagged dependent variable), and delayed effects with different delay lengths. ARDL allows for incorporating different lags for both dependent and independent variables and imposes few constraints on the coefficients [10]. The resulting flexibility of the method often enables great (in-sample) data fit, and the estimated parameters provide detailed information about behavioral change dynamics [11].

However, this flexibility and close connection to empirical data come with some costs. First, the number of estimated parameters is determined by the lagged effects included and can proliferate, complicating theoretical interpretation [10]. Second, without constraints on parameters, the risk of overfitting the model to past data increases. As a result, out-of-sample predictions using the estimates may underperform [12, 13]. Third, including lagged dependent variables complicates both projection and interpretation: some of the effects of (lagged) independent variables are embedded in (lagged) dependent variables, so a pure measurement of the causal effects of independent variables on the dependent variable is not straightforward [14]. Moreover, projections become infeasible if data on dependent variables are unavailable until the previous period, which is often the case for longer-term predictions [15]. These limitations also make it difficult to directly integrate ARDL estimates into dynamic simulation models, which typically require interpretable and straightforward parameters.

To address these issues, some alternative methods constrain the structure of the delayed effects [16–18]. Many dynamic modeling communities use Erlang distributions for continuous-time models or Pascal distributions for discrete-time models, which are modeled as serially coupled first-order exponential lags [3, 8]. This approach significantly reduces the number of estimated parameters to a delay length, reporting the average lag between the change in the independent and dependent variables, and a shape parameter, informing the distribution of that lag within a distribution family. While this approach is simple and easily interpretable, its reliance on a priori qualitative assumptions about delay characteristics can misrepresent the actual dynamic effects, unlike econometric approaches, where the data inform the delay structure.

Hence, there is a clear gap in the literature in bridging the econometrics and simulation communities when modeling behavioral dynamics. Simulation communities often fit the data to a prespecified distribution, which can misrepresent real-world behavior and limit empirical validity [3, 19, 20]. Econometrics communities use more flexible models, yet their outputs can result in overfitting, are difficult to interpret, and are challenging to integrate into simulation frameworks [10]. In this study, we provide a hybrid ARDL-Erlang approach that bridges these two limiting cases to estimate delays in behavioral responses to social and environmental disruptions. The motivation is to offer a method that benefits from the empirical rigor of ARDL while also offering more interpretable results that can readily be incorporated into standard modeling approaches for simulating and projecting long-term dynamics.

We apply this method in the context of behavioral responses to COVID-19 risks. Our process involves three steps. First, we estimate the dynamic relationship between COVID-19 deaths and mobility (a measure of behavioral reactions) using the ARDL model. Next, we derive the total and long-run impact of a change in deaths on mobility responses. Finally, we approximate the model using an Erlang distribution, which provides concise estimates for the length and shape of the delay distribution.

We use the COVID-19 pandemic as a case study to apply this method empirically. Several studies have noted the importance of estimating the reciprocal relationship between the state of a pandemic and human behavior [21–24]. While many have examined the effect of changes in mobility on the spread of the disease, the reverse direction, from risk indicators (such as cases and deaths) to human mobility, is less explored (for an exception, see [25]). Here, we specifically examine the impact of reported deaths on behavior, measured by county-level mobility in the United States. The COVID-19 pandemic serves as an ideal case study for several reasons. The rapid growth in cases and mortality triggered unprecedented behavioral changes in the public, including social distancing, mask-wearing, and changes in mobility patterns [26]. Compared to other disruptive events, there are publicly available data on both the spread of the disease and corresponding behavioral responses [27]. Moreover, this methodology has yet to be implemented in the existing literature. A limited number of studies have empirically assessed the pandemic’s impact on human behavior, and they often do not consider delays in behavioral response (e.g [5, 6, 28–30]).,. By applying the proposed method to our case study, we measure heterogeneity in behavioral responses across different regions in the United States using only a length and a shape parameter. The method applied in this paper can be easily used to estimate delays in behavioral responses in other settings.

Methods

Data

Weekly data on COVID-19 deaths per 100,000 population for all counties in the United States were obtained from the Centers for Disease Control COVID-19 data tracker [31]. The death data were log-transformed to normalize the distribution and meet the assumptions of linear regression models. Mobility data were retrieved from the Google Community Mobility Reports [32]. A mobility measure was constructed by averaging the percentage change in movement compared to a pre-pandemic baseline period for four categories: retail and recreation, grocery stores and pharmacies, transit stations, and workplaces. Both measures were aggregated to the weekly level and standardized (mean zero and standard deviation of one) before the analysis to facilitate interpretation.

This analysis is limited to the United States from October 1, 2020, to July 31, 2021. This period was chosen to start after severe underreporting and data quality issues during earlier months of the pandemic. It ends before the emergence of new variants, extensive vaccination, and other factors that affect mobility in later periods and may fall outside the scope of this paper.

General approach

Broadly, our goal is to provide a method for reliably estimating the lagged effects of a changing risk on human responses in interpretable and simple formats. We also want to assess the usability of this method and the value it may generate compared to alternative methods using a concrete example. Our analysis uses the xtdpdgmm package in Stata to estimate the ARDL model, Python to transform the ARDL model into an IDL model, and R to fit the IDL coefficients to the Erlang Distribution. Using the above methods, we quantify how weekly mobility patterns respond to changes in weekly COVID-19 deaths and discuss two primary outcomes. First, we report the total magnitude of impact at national and state levels, defined as the cumulative effect of past weekly deaths on the human mobility index. Second, we report the average delay length and shape parameters at national and state levels, allowing one to summarize the delay distribution in two simple parameters.

ARDL model specification

We specify the ARDL model using current mobility in each county as the outcome, with lagged COVID-19 deaths and lagged mobility as the key independent variables. We include dummy variables representing a 4-week cyclic pattern in mobility (confirmed through descriptive statistics analysis) and seasonal effects (dummy variables for fall, spring, and summer) as control variables. As a robustness check, we also include the % of the population that received at least one dose of a vaccination in each county as an additional covariate in a separate specification. Panel unit root tests were performed on all variables to ensure all of the time series were stationary [33].

Given that OLS estimates will produce biased and inconsistent estimates from this model, we employ the Generalized Method of Moments (GMM) to estimate this dynamic panel model, with 1 st to 20th lags of deaths and mobility as instrumental variables [34]. During the estimation process, the model was first differenced (yit-yit−1) to remove the county-specific fixed effects. In the transformed (first-differenced) model, the transformed independent variables (e.g., first-differenced lagged deaths) are correlated with the transformed error terms, under the sequential exogeneity assumption, past deaths and mobility are not associated with the error terms. They thus can be used as instrumental variables for the transformed independent variables to achieve consistent estimation. This method has been shown to produce consistent estimates and be robust against the biases caused by endogeneity, especially when the number of cross-sectional units is large [35]. To select the optimal number of lags for deaths (l) and mobility (p), we iterate over all possible combinations of lags of 1 to 8 weeks of mobility and lags of 1 to 4 weeks of death to determine the best combination that minimizes Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) values.

At the national level, a one standard deviation permanent increase in weekly deaths per 100,000 population (log-transformed) is associated with a 0.46 standard deviation decrease in human mobility in the long run, where the delay distribution follows a 1 st order Erlang distribution, and the average delay length is about 3 weeks. It is also worth mentioning that our proposed approach results in a much better model fit (the medians for within-county R-squares values across states are 0.52, 0.19, and 0.17 for ARDL, unconstrained IDL, and constrained IDL models) and significantly different estimates of the total impact and average delay length compared with the two IDL models in most states. The unconstrained IDL models result in highly fluctuating IDL coefficients and an overall much weaker median total impact (−0.08) across states. The constrained IDL models result in more substantial median total impact (−0.39) and a similar median average delay length (5.18 weeks) compared to those obtained from the ARDL model across states. However, the estimates from the constrained models are more extreme for certain states and have a much wider range (e.g., IQR for the total impact is 3 times that from the ARDL models across states) overall (see details in the appendix). In addition, inclusion of cumulative vaccination as an additional covariate in the ARDL model results in a weaker overall impact of death on mobility (median total impact of −0.11), but all results remain consistent (see details in the appendix).

At the state level, there is significant variability across states in both the total impact and average delay lengths. Most states exhibit a first-order delay (k = 1), indicating an immediate yet gradually diminishing response.

Discussion

This study addresses the challenge of estimating societal responses to external shocks, such as changes in public compliance with health guidelines during a pandemic. A fundamental difficulty in these situations is the delay in human reactions, which complicates the accurate estimation of effects — especially when both the extent and structure of these delays are unknown. To address this challenge, we propose the use of ARDL models to estimate lagged relationships between two variables, followed by translating these estimates into theoretically transparent Erlang lag distributions characterized by a length and shape parameter. Using data from the COVID-19 pandemic, we estimated how changes in weekly deaths influenced county-level mobility in the USA, while accounting for delays that could arise from documenting and reporting deaths, information dissemination, shifts in public risk perception, and subsequent behavioral changes.

Our analysis revealed a few insights. First, the impact of COVID-19 deaths on mobility is substantial and varies significantly across states. At the national level, mobility declines as COVID-19 deaths increase, with a total impact of −0.46. Across states, the total median effect is −0.18 with an interquartile range (IQR) of 0.36. This variability reflects the diverse public health responses and demographic characteristics among different states. Second, the average delay length at the national level is approximately 3.2 weeks, with states exhibiting a wide range of delay lengths—from as short as 1–2 weeks in states like New Mexico and Delaware to over 15 weeks in states like Oregon and Arizona. These differences indicate that states experience the peak effects of COVID-19 deaths on mobility at varying times. Third, most states exhibit a first-order delay distribution, indicating immediate responses that diminish over time. However, some states show more complex patterns with higher-order delay distributions, suggesting more sustained responses to COVID-19 mortality. Comparisons with unconstrained and constrained IDL models indicate that this approach provides much better model fit and offers more flexible and robust results.

The ARDL-Erlang method proposed in this paper fills the gaps in the literature by improving on three approaches: statistical approaches that only consider immediate effects or simplistic delay structures [4–7], econometric studies that estimate dynamic effects but produce outputs that are difficult to incorporate into simulation models [9, 36], and simulation models that impose delay structures without empirical support [27]. By fitting ARDL estimates into Erlang distributions, our method provides compact and data-driven length and shape parameters that are straightforward to incorporate into dynamic simulation frameworks for policy analysis and long-term forecasting. Furthermore, the study contributes to epidemiological modeling by providing an approach to estimate societal response to changes in the state of a pandemic [1]. Specifically, it resonates with calls for incorporating human response dynamics into epidemic models [21, 23], a concern that has received increasing attention after the COVID-19 pandemic [5]. Our findings provide evidence of considerable heterogeneities in human mobility responses to COVID-19 risks across different US regions. Moreover, it shows how these responses can be parameterized using a length and shape parameter to improve simulation and projection models.

This study has several implications for policy-making. The heterogeneity in response dynamics underscores the importance of tailored public health policies. States with longer delay lengths and more significant impacts may require prolonged and intensified public health interventions to manage mobility and mitigate the spread of COVID-19 effectively. Conversely, states with shorter delays may benefit from more immediate, but less prolonged, measures.

We acknowledge several limitations of this study. The analysis is limited to the second wave of the COVID-19 pandemic due to data quality issues in the first wave and the emergence of new factors in later waves, such as vaccinations and new variants. In addition, here we assume the effect of death on mobility is symmetric. In practice, an increase vs. a decrease in deaths can have different impacts on mobility. While some studies have demonstrated the existence and consequences of such ‘asymmetric delay’ using simpler formulations [24, 37], their incorporation into ARDL represents an interesting direction for future research. Furthermore, we assume that all counties within a state have the same delay parameter, which overlooks possible intra-state variability. Future research could explore more granular approaches to capture county-level differences. While the methodology is robust, the findings are context-specific to the United States during the COVID-19 pandemic. Application to other settings may require adjustments to account for different socio-economic and demographic factors.

Conclusions

Overall, this study provides a novel approach to estimating delayed human responses to health risks using a hybrid ARDL-Erlang model. Our findings highlight significant variability in the impact and timing of responses across states, underscoring the need for tailored public health policies. Future research should continue to refine these methods and expand their application to other contexts to enhance our understanding of human behavioral dynamics in response to various social and environmental disruptions.
