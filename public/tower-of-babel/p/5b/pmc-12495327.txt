Improvement of guar gum on fermentation characteristics and protein physicochemical properties of dough under freeze-thaw cycles
Authors: Jiaxin Zheng, Dongdong Xie, Xing Li, Yuyang Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFrozen dough often faces freeze-thaw cycles (FT) caused by temperature fluctuations during production, transportation, and processing. The mechanism of guar gum's effect on dough improvement is explored by the freeze-thaw dough model in this study. The reducing sugar content was increased at 0.3 %, 0.6 %, and 0.9 % in FT0 and FT8 treatment, while decreased at 1.2 % guar gum, the content decreased at 0.9 and 1.2 % guar gum in FT4; the production of glycerol was increased at 0.6 % to 1.2 % groups in FT0, increased at 0.3 % to 1.2 % groups in FT4, increased at 0.3 % and 0.9 % groups in FT8, the content of glutathione in the dough was reduced in groups with guar gum. The maximum fermentation height, the dough's total CO2 production, and holding gas production were at 0.6 % guar gum in FT0 and FT8. The highest content of gluten was at 0.6 % in FT4 and FT8. Guar gum addition inhibited the depolymerization of proteins, prevented the protein structure from transforming to a disordered structure, improved the structural integrity of proteins, and increased the development of disulfide bonds and glutenin macropolymers. In conclusion, 0.6 % performed best within the tested conditions.Keywords: Guar gum, Freeze-thaw cycle, Fermented dough, Yeast fermentation, Protein properties

Introduction

The frozen fermented dough has been widely used in steamed breads, buns, breads, and dumplings production due to centralized production, standardization of product quality, distribution networks, and low costs (Lou et al., 2023). The frozen dough's quality is declined by multiple freeze-thaw cycles because of temperature fluctuations during production, storage, transportation, and process of finished product processing (Jiang et al., 2020). The freeze-thaw cycles have been recognized as an accelerated process of frozen dough quality degradation (Wang, Wang, et al., 2022). Under the freeze-thaw process, the formation and melt of ice crystals are constantly repeated, and the small ice crystals recrystallize into larger ice crystals due to moisture flow when the temperature decreases (Wang, Wang, et al., 2022). Because of the ice crystal's generation and recrystallization, the cells of yeast lose their activity, the gluten protein is continuously destroyed, and the frozen fermented dough quality deteriorates, resulting in the texture and appearance of the product buns being damaged (Omedi et al., 2019).

Therefore, various measures have been taken to improve the frozen dough's quality under freeze-thaw cycling, such as the utilization of polysaccharides, enzymes, and emulsifiers into frozen dough. Guar gum is a strongly hydrophilic natural polysaccharide consisting of one galactose and two mannoses linked by α-1,6 glycosidic and β-1,4 and bonds. Guar gum's hydrophilic groups are tightly linked to water molecules via hydrogen bonding, which elevates the internal viscosity in the dough system, restricts water migration, and inhibits large ice crystals production (Linlaud et al., 2011). The 0.5 % guar gum to vegetable pellets improved the dense structure and physical stability of the products (Wang, Zeng, et al., 2022); the addition of 0.4 % and 0.8 % guar gum was able to increase the specific volume and decrease the hardness of bread after 15 days of frozen storage (Hejrani et al., 2017). However, few researchers have explored and elucidated guar gum's effect on fermented dough's quality under freeze-thaw cycles.

Under freeze-thaw cycles, metabolites such as ethanol, carbon dioxide and glutathione, succinic acid, acetic acid, and glycerol are present in fermented dough. Metabolites in fermented dough also have an effect on the tensile and dough's rheological properties (Jayaram, Cuyvers, Verstrepen, et al., 2013). Glutathione has a softening effect on dough after leaking from dead yeast cells into the dough matrix (Verheyen et al., 2014); when yeast metabolites (glycerol, ethanol, and glutathione) are added to unfermented doughs, and ethanol and glutathione altered the dough gluten network's structure (Meerts et al., 2018). Most studies have emphasized only the fermentation properties of doughs after freezing or freeze-thawing cycles, but few studies have been able to systematically elucidate the influence of guar gum on the metabolites of the fermented dough under the freeze-thaw process.

Gluten affects dough's rheological properties by intermolecular covalent and noncovalent bonds (hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, and disulfide bonding). During freeze-thaw cycling, gluten proteins depolymerize because of the destruction and recrystallization of ice crystals, and the air-holding capacity decreases, resulting in decrease in product quality (Cheng et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2014). The gluten network was destroyed in dough freezing, the dough strength decreased and the air-holding capacity became weaker, which ultimately led to steamed bread's poorer quality (Wang et al., 2016). Freezing decreased the α-helix content of gluten protein (Lu, Xing, Yang, et al., 2020). The surface hydrophobicity of gluten proteins increased after freezing and thawing (Wang, Zeng, et al., 2022). Pig skin gelatin, as a hydrophilic colloid, could make the glutenin macropolymer (GMP) depolymerization degree and sulfhydryl content of gluten proteins decrease, and the gluten strength increase (Yu et al., 2020). However, there are few studies that can systematically elucidate the influence of guar gum on the physicochemical properties of gluten in systems of fermented dough under the freeze-thaw cycle.

Therefore, the yeast metabolite content in the dough, dough physicochemical properties (wet gluten content, tensile properties, fermentation rheological properties, intermolecular forces, and dough microstructure), protein physicochemical properties (protein secondary structure, surface hydrophobicity, free sulfhydryl, disulfide bonds, and GMP content) of fermented doughs under freeze-thaw process were explored in this study, it is hoped that the study could reveal the influence of guar gum on the quality of frozen thawed fermented dough, provide a better understanding of a better understanding of metabolite-protein-water mobility view across FT cycles, this is of great significance for guiding the improvement of frozen fermented dough quality and the development of the frozen dough industry.

Methods and measures

Materials

Wheat flour (moisture 13 %, protein content 16.13 % (dry basis, determined by Kjeldahl definition nitrogen method), ash (0.6 %, dry basis), 21.6 % damaged starch (dry basis)) was bought in ChenKou flagship store (Shandong, China). Guar gum was from Beijing Guarun Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Yeast was from Angel Yeast Co., Ltd. (Hubei, China).

Dough preparation and freeze-thaw processing

The basic formula of dough consists of 1 g high active dry yeast, 100 g wheat flour, and 55 g distilled water. The water absorption and mixing time of the dough was determined by Mixograph (National Manufacturing Co., Lincoln, NE, USA) according to AACCI method 50–40.02. The amount of water was 80 % of the optimum water absorption determined by 110 Brabender farinograph. The guar gum replaced flour at 0 %、0.3 %、0.6 %、0.9 % and 1.2 % of wheat flour (dry basis) was determined in accordance with GB 2760–2024. After all the ingredients were put into the mixer, mixed for 2 min at low speed until the basic shape was formed, and for 5 min at high speed, wrapped in plastic wrap to relax for 10 min, and then divided into 50 g of small dough and rolled out for shaping.

Freezing and thawing process: the dough was frozen at −33 °C for 2 h when the center temperature of the dough reached −18 °C, and then transferred to the refrigerator at −18 °C for 24 h, and then thawed for 2 h in a constant temperature and humidity box (Shanghai CIMO Medical instrument Manufacturing Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) (25 °C, 85 % RH) when the center temperature of the dough was 25 °C, and the dough at this time was the control group (FT0), the center temperature of dough was monitored by a MIK-R9600 recorder (Asmik Technology, Hangzhou, China) equipped with temperature probes at the center of the dough. The frozen dough was put back into the −18 °C refrigerator for 24 h, and thawed for 2 h until the temperature of the center of the dough was 25 °C, at this time, it was the fermented dough determined as FT1. The frozen dough was repeated for freeze-thaw cycles and thawed (25 °C, 85 % RH) at the fourth and eighth freeze-thaw cycles, determined as FT4 and FT8. The control, 4 times freeze-thawed and 8 times freeze-thawed doughs were fermented for 50 min (30 °C, 85 % RH) and subjected to subsequent test operations.

Gluten samples were prepared by hand washing. The dough obtained in the previous step was washed with distilled water until the distilled water could no longer be seen to contain starch or other soluble substances, thus obtaining gluten protein samples. The gluten protein was then freeze-dried. Specifically, dough frozen for 24 h was placed in a freeze dryer (FD-80, Boyikang Laboratory Equipment Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) and dehydrated for 48 h at a vacuum of 15 Pa and a temperature of −80 °C. The freeze-dried samples were then manually ground and stored in sealed bags at room temperature.

Determination of yeast metabolites during dough fermentation

Dough (10 g) from each group was distributed in distilled water (90 mL), the suspension was centrifuged (5000 g) for 10 min, and then the supernatant was collected as yeast metabolites, and the content of glutathione was calculated (Xie et al., 2023). Ethanol content was measured according to the method (Ruz et al., 1986). Reducing sugar was measured (Lamounier et al., 2020). Glycerol was measured (Xie et al., 2024). Metabolites standard curves were shown in Supplementary Fig. 2.

Measurement of total acidity (TTA) and pH value

The pH was determined using a digital pH meter (FB20, Ohaus Instruments Co., Ltd. Changzhou, China) following a standardized protocol. Firstly, the dough (10 g) sample was homogenized in deionized water (90 mL), adopting the methodology described by Katina et al. (Katina et al., 2006). After thorough mixing, the suspension was settled, and 30 mL of the resulting supernatant was carefully collected. To measure the dough's acidity, sodium hydroxide (NaOH) (0.1 M) solution was used to titrate the sample when the pH arrived at 8.6. The volume of NAOH used was used to calculate the total acidity of the dough sample.

Dough stretching determination

The dough specimens were prepared by molding the mixture into standardized cubic strips (0.125 cm3) using olive oil-coated molds to prevent adhesion. The mechanical properties were evaluated by a texture analyzer (Stable Micro Systems, London, UK) with an A/KIE tensile probe. The testing parameters: a constant 3.3 mm/s is crosshead speed, 50 mm is the predetermined extension distance, and 5 g is the initial trigger force threshold. During the tensile testing, two primary rheological parameters were recorded: the peak resistance force (indicating dough strength) and the maximum elongation (reflecting extensibility) (Li et al., 2017).

Dough's fermentation characteristics

The samples were investigated by an F3 Rheofermentometer (Chopin Technologies, Villeneuve-la-Garenne, France) following a standardized protocol. Precisely weighed 315 g aliquots of dough were transferred into the instrument's fermentation chamber and subjected to controlled fermentation conditions at 30 ± 0.5 °C for a duration of 180 min. During the fermentation process, a constant pressure of 2000 g was maintained to simulate industrial baking conditions. The equipment automatically recorded the temporal changes in dough development and carbon dioxide production, generating characteristic fermentation curves that represent the dough's expansion dynamics and gas retention capacity throughout the fermentation period (Guan et al., 2022). These samples are dedicated ones.

Measurement of the intermolecular force of dough

The intermolecular force of gluten protein was measured using the method described by Xie et al. (Xie et al., 2023). Buffer solution: 0.05 M NaCl (S1), 0.6 M NaCl (S2), 0.6 M NaCl +1.5 M urea (S3), 0.6 M NaCl +8 M urea (S4). A 50 mg lyophilized powder was dissolved in 5 mL of the above buffer and extracted at 25 °C for 1 h. The dough suspension was centrifuged at 4000 ×g for 10 mins. The protein concentration in the supernatant was determined by Coomassie brilliant blue method. The supernatant (1 mL) and 5 mL of coomassie brilliant blue solution were mixed and reacted for 2 mins, and then the absorbance was measured at 595 nm. Protein content was calculated based on the protein standard curve (y = 174.89× + 2.8269, R2 = 0.9933). The ionic bond was the difference between the S2 and the S1, the hydrogen bond was the difference between the S3 and the S2, and the hydrophobic interaction was the difference between the S4 and the S3.

The microstructure of the dough

The dough's microstructure was determined by a scanning electron microscope (Quanta250FEG, FEI Company, Hillsboro, USA). The sample was treated with gold spraying, and the images were observed at 20 kV accelerating voltage at a magnification of 2000 times (Duan et al., 2024).

SDS-page

The sample solution was obtained by mixing the sample (5 mg) well with 5 mL of sample buffer containing β-mercaptoethanol, boiled for 5 min, and centrifuged at 10, 000 ×g for 20 min. Standard protein marker (10 μL) was injected into the leftmost gel, and then 10 μL of sample solution was injected sequentially, and the electrophoresis apparatus was operated at a constant voltage of 100 V. The gel consisted of 12 % separator gel (pH 8.8) and 5 % concentrate gel (pH 6.8) (Binle et al., 2021).

Measurement of secondary structure in gluten protein

The secondary structure of proteins was characterized through Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy by the method by Xie et al. (Xie et al., 2023). Spectra acquisition was performed using a high-resolution FTIR spectrometer (PerkinElmer Enterprise Management Co., Ltd., Massachusetts, USA) with a 400–4000 cm−1 spectral range. The 1600–1700 cm−1 spectral range was given particular attention, which provides critical information about protein secondary structures. Spectral data processing was conducted via Peak Fit V4.12 software (SeaSolve Software Inc., USA), incorporating sequential steps of baseline adjustment, Gaussian deconvolution, and second derivative analysis.

Measurement of protein surface hydrophobicity

The surface hydrophobicity of protein was quantified following an adapted protocol based on Jiao et al. (Jiao et al., 2019), and 1-anilinonaphthalene-8-sulfonic acid (ANS) was used as the fluorescent probe. Initially, gluten powder freeze-dried (50 mg) was hydrated in 10 mL of 50 mM acetate buffer (pH 4.0) and allowed to hydrate at ambient temperature (25 ± 1 °C) for 60 min. The protein suspension was then clarified by centrifugation at 10,000 ×g for 15 min at 4 °C. Serial dilutions of the supernatant were prepared, and 4 mL aliquots of each dilution were mixed with 20 μL ANS solution (8 mM). Measurements were conducted via a spectrofluorometer (FL970 Plus, Shanghai Techcomp Instrument Ltd.), excitation is 390 nm, the emission is 470 nm, and both slit widths were set at 5 nm. The relative fluorescence intensity values were recorded and normalized against protein concentration to determine the surface hydrophobicity index.

Sulfhydryl and disulfide bond contents determination

The sulfhydryl group analysis was conducted following a standardized protocol. For sample preparation, freeze-dried dough powder (75 mg) was homogenized in Tris-Glycine buffer (1 mL, 90 mM glycine, 86 mM Tris, 4 mM EDTA, 4.7 g guanidine hydrochloride, pH 8.0), deionized water was used to adjust the final volume to 10 mL.

The content of free sulfhydryl was measured by mixing the protein solution (1 mL) with denaturing buffer (4 mL, 5 M guanidine hydrochloride, and 8 M urea) and, then was mixed with Ellman's reagent (50 μL, 4 mg/mL DTNB in Tris buffer). The reaction mixture was incubated for 15 min at room temperature, and measurement was carried out at the absorbance of 412 nm via a spectrophotometer.

For total sulfhydryl quantification, protein solution (1 mL) was treated with denaturing buffer (4 mL) and β-mercaptoethanol (50 μL). After 1 h incubation at 25 °C, 12 % (w/v) trichloroacetic acid (10 mL) was added to precipitate proteins, followed by centrifugation (5000 ×g, 10 min). 12 % trichloroacetic acid was used to wash the pellet twice and then the pellet was distributed in 10 mL urea solution (8 M). The sulfhydryl content was then determined by adding DTNB solution (50 μL) and measurement was carried out at 412 nm absorbance (Xie et al., 2023). The calculation formula is as follows:

μmol SH/g = 73.53 × A412 × D/C.

SH: sulfhydryl; A412 is the absorbance value at 412 nm; D is the dilution coefficient, D = 5.02 (sulfhydryl) and 15 (sulfhydryl and reduced disulfide bond); C is the final protein concentration of the sample in mg/mL.

μmol SS/g = (SHtotal-SHfree)/2.

SS: Disulfide bond.

Determination of gluten macromer content

The dough was vacuum-dried and ground. The lyophilized sample (2 g) was taken in sodium lauryl sulfate (28 mL, 1.5 % (w/v)) and extracted (120 rpm, 12 h) at 37 °C, followed by centrifugation (15,000 g, 20 min, 4 °C). The gelatinous layer of the precipitate was accumulated and weighed (Lu & Zhu, 2023).

Low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF NMR)

The fluidity and distribution of moisture in the fermented dough were determined by the Niumag NMR analyzer (Shanghai Newmax Electronic Technology Co., LTD, Shanghai, China). The optimal pulse parameters were: TD = 185,606, NECH = 4000, TE = 200 μs, TR = 4000, NS = 4. Use T2-FitFrm software to analyze the data to calculate the T2 value.

Determination of protein digestion of steamed bread in vitro

Protein digestion was Referring to Chen (Chen et al., 2021) et al. with some modifications:10 mL of simulated gastric fluid (SGF) (2000 U/ml pepsin and 0.02 % (w/v) NaCl (pH 1.20)) was taken and maintained in a 37 °C water bath for 5 min, then 10 mL of steamed bun homogenate was added and digested at 37 °C for (0, 30, 60, and 120) minutes. Subsequently, simulated intestinal fluid (SIF) containing 2500 U/ml trypsin and 0.7 % (w/v) KH2PO4 pH 7.50 was added to the residual SGF suspension containing digested proteins and digested for 0, 30, and 120 min respectively. The enzymatic action of pepsin and trypsin was stopped immediately at the collection time points, respectively, the pH was adjusted to 7.50, and the solution was boiled. Protein digestibility (PD) =100 %-(the percentage of undigested protein content/ the total protein content in the supernatant). The protein content of the supernatant and the total protein content were assessed by the Bradford method. The digested samples were centrifuged at 25 °C for 10 min at 2310 g to determine the protein content of the supernatant.

Statistical analysis

To ensure data reliability, triplicate measurements were performed for each experimental condition. Mean ± standard deviation was used to present data. Groups were compared by analysis of variance (ANOVA) via SPSS 21.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, USA), and significance analysis (p < 0.05) was carried out via the Duncan test. The Origin 8.5 (Origin Lab Corp., Northampton, MA, USA) was used to plot data.

Results and discussion

Content of yeast metabolites, pH and total acidity in fermented doughs

Reducing sugars such as glucose and fructose can be directly utilized by yeast in glycolysis and the tricarboxylic acid cycle, and as downstream products of yeast metabolism, glycerol and ethanol are accumulated when the cell is stressed (Rezaei et al., 2014). In Table 1, the reduced sugar content in the dough decreased after four freeze-thaw cycles, this may be because the yeast required more energy and produced stress-resistant substances such as glycerol to cope with the stress and thus consumed higher reducing sugars (Shi et al., 2014). Compared to the control group, the elevated reducing sugar content of the dough with 8 freeze-thaw cycles may be attributed to yeast's reduced activity after several freeze-thaw cycles, which decreased its ability to utilize reducing sugars. As for the control group, the reducing sugar content in the doughs of the FT-0.3 % and FT-0.6 % groups were reduced compared to FT-0; at 8 freeze-thawing cycles, doughs' reducing sugar content of the FT-0.0 %, FT-0.3 %, and FT-0.6 % groups was lower. This suggests that the appropriate addition of guar gum could improve the ability of yeast to utilize reducing sugars, thus facilitating good fermentation of frozen dough after thawing. Compared with the control group, the ethanol content increased at 4 freeze-thaw cycles, and the yeast could enhance the ethanol metabolic pathway for rapid energy supply in response to 4 freeze-thaw stress. At 8 freeze-thaw cycles, the ethanol content decreased, which could be attributed to the activity decrease in yeast because of the effects of multiple freeze-thaw processes. The decrease in ethanol content may also be due to ethanol evaporation during the freezing and thawing process of the dough (Shi et al., 2014). The content of ethanol was reduced after adding guar gum compared to FT0–0.0 % in the control groups. Glycerol content was elevated in the dough with the freeze-thawing process to the control. This may be because the yeast in the dough undergoing freeze-thaw stress would adjust the carbon metabolism pathway under stress to produce more glycerol. It is one of the major carbon metabolites of yeast, maintains intracellular osmotic pressure balance, and prevents the diffusion of water within the dough and the yeast cells (Zhang & Chen, 2008). Compared with FT-0.0 %, at the same freeze-thaw level, glycerol content was elevated in the doughs, except for the doughs of groups FT0–0.3 %, FT8–0.6 %, and FT8–1.2 %. Guar gum might improve the fermentation environment of the yeast, strengthen the metabolic pathways of the yeast, and promote glycerol production. At the same time, guar gum may increase the relative concentration of glycerol by reducing the free water content. Glutathione (GSH) content had a trend to increase as the freeze-thaw cycles increased, this may be because the yeast in the dough increases the synthesis of the antioxidant, glutathione, in response to freeze-thawing stress, while repeated freeze-thaw cycles cause damage to yeast cells, causing glutathione to leak into the dough (Yang et al., 2017). With the same freeze-thaw cycle, the GSH content was reduced in doughs supplemented with guar gum compared to FT-0.0 %, which further suggests that guar gum improves the activity of yeast cells. GSH also depolymerizes the glutenin macropolymer (GMP) through a reduction reaction, which ultimately presents a firmer bread (Guo et al., 2020; Lu, Xing, Yang, et al., 2020). Further research is needed to investigate whether guar gum may have a protective effect on cell membranes, inhibit glutathione synthesis and cell leakage in yeast, improve GMP stability, and enhance dough and bread quality. As presented in Table 1, the pH of the dough increased and the total acidity decreased after freezing and thawing compared with the control group. The change in pH was might because of the organic acids produced by yeast (Chen et al., 2021).

The fermentation rheological properties of fermented dough under freeze-thaw cycle

Fermentation rheological properties of freeze-fermented doughs are shown in Table 2 and Fig. 1. Compared with the control, the maximum height and total gas release in the doughs decreased and retention coefficients increased after the freeze-thaw cycle. This may be because of the reduced yeast cell viability during freeze-thaw cycling caused by the osmotic pressure, which was the result of migration of intracellular water and the formation of extracellular ice crystals, the decrease of carbon dioxide production, the decrease of dough leavening power, and the decrease of dough expansion height (Barrera et al., 2016; Shehzad et al., 2010; Soveral et al., 2008). Freezing and thawing break the disulfide bonds, leading to destruction of the gluten network, strength loss of the dough, and inability to retain gas and a reduce in the dough fermentation volume, and the increase in dough retention coefficient after freezing and thawing may be because the reduce in the dough's fermentation volume outweighs the decrease in of the gas volume (Wang, Yang, et al., 2017). Under the same freeze-thaw level, the maximum height and retention coefficient of doughs with guar gum were significantly higher compared to FT-0. As shown in Fig. 1, doughs with 0.6 % guar gum had the highest total CO2 production, holding gas production and dough height in the control group and dough in 8 freeze-thaws, and doughs with guar gum showed higher retention coefficients than FT-0 with the freeze-thaw cycles. The Vr assesses the ability of the dough to stretch into a film and retain gas, which is determined by the quality of the gluten network (Zhang et al., 2024). Guar gum, as a hydrocolloid, increases the strength of the dough's gas chambers, which enhances the dough's ability to retain CO2 gas and improves the dough's leavening rheological properties (Hejrani et al., 2017).

Wet gluten content and tensile properties of fermented dough under freeze-thaw cycle

In Table 2, wet gluten content and tensile force of the doughs reduced as the freeze-thaw cycle increased in FT-0.0 % groups, it suggested that freezing and thawing broke the network structure of the doughs and the gluten formation, and the doughs' strength decreased as well (Guo et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2023). This might be explained that the GSH content was increased as the freeze-thaw cycle increased, this resulted in a decrease in the number of sulfur bonds between gluten structures. After adding guar gum, the gluten content increased significantly under the same freeze-thaw conditions, which is consistent with the decreasing trend of glutathione content in the dough. The elevated dough tensile displacement after freeze-thaw may be related to the elevated dough viscosity (Guo et al., 2020). Under the same freeze-thaw cycles, the tensile force of doughs with guar gum significantly increased compared with FT-0. This may be because guar gum consists of many hydrophilic groups, and these groups could interact with proteins through hydrogen bonding to strengthen the dough structure (Jiang et al., 2020).

The intermolecular forces in fermented dough under freeze-thaw cycles

The amino acid residues have small amounts of carboxyl groups and free amino, and ionic bonds were formed by these charged groups (Li, Liu, et al., 2021). Hydrogen and hydrophobic are responses to changes in the microenvironment of the nonpolar amino acid side chains of gluten proteins (Li, Liu, et al., 2021). As shown in Table 3, the ionic bonding, hydrogen bonding, and hydrophobic forces within the dough were elevated after the freeze-thaw cycle in FT-0.0 %. This could be attributed to the increased concentration of dispersed ions in the liquid state due to ice formation and water migration, as well as enhanced ionic bonding due to ice crystals leading to partial denaturation and aggregation of proteins and enhanced interactions between exposed charged groups (Li, Liu, et al., 2021). In addition, the exposure of hydrophobic amino acid residues of proteins resulted in aggregation of hydrophobic regions and enhanced hydrophobicity (Liu et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2018). Under the freeze-thaw process, the starch molecular structure is disrupted, and water molecules penetrate more easily into the starch and form hydrogen bonds with more aggregates of starch molecules (Wang, Wang, et al., 2022). In the control group, guar gum addition enhanced hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions and ionic bonding compared to FT0, which helped to enhance the structural stability and viscoelasticity of the three-dimensional network inside the dough. At 4 freeze-thaw cycles, the addition of guar gum strengthened hydrogen bonds and reduced hydrophobic interactions. The addition of 0.6 % and 0.9 % guar gum weakened ionic bonds. This may be because guar gum's strong hydrophilicity causes the dough to tend to form hydrogen bonds internally. Additionally, guar gum interacts with proteins to slow down protein denaturation and aggregation, allowing the dough to hydrate more effectively and thereby reducing the exposure of hydrophobic groups. The addition of guar gum enhanced ionic bonding and weakened hydrogen bonds under 8 freeze-thaw cycles, and 0.3 % guar gum weakened hydrophobic interactions. This may be because, after multiple freeze-thaw cycles, the melting and recrystallization of ice crystals cause uneven distribution of moisture within the dough. At the same time, the protein and starch structures are severely damaged, exposing more charged groups and hydrophobic groups. This alters the interaction process between guar gum and various components in the dough, leading to the breaking and weakening of hydrogen bonds, as well as the strengthening of ionic bonds and hydrophobic forces (Li, Liu, et al., 2021). The continuity and elasticity of the dough depended on the non-covalent interactions within and between the wheat gluten chains, whose hydrogen bonding was the main factor (Li & Hu, 2020), and the incorporation of guar gum favored the enhancement of hydrogen bonding within the dough to raise the internal continuity and elasticity of the dough.

The microstructure of fermented dough under freeze-thaw cycle

Under the freeze-thaw cycle, carbon dioxide produced by yeast expands the fermented dough, and the gluten-starch three-dimensional network accommodates carbon dioxide to develop a porous internal structure (Rezaei et al., 2016). In Fig. 2, the microstructure of dough is embedded with starch granules in the control group. After freezing and thawing, there were obvious fractures and lamellae inside the dough, which could be because of the aggregation of ice crystals in the amorphous regions inside the dough, which disrupted the gluten-starch network and resulted in the rupture of the internal structure (Fu et al., 2015), yeast metabolism also affects the microstructure of protein networks (Jayaram, Cuyvers, Lagrain, et al., 2013; Wang, Yang, et al., 2017). After adding guar gum, the hole numbers inside the dough decreased significantly, the overall structure became dense and consecutive, and the starch granules were firmly wrapped. This may be because guar gum can fill the three-dimensional structure of the dough and promote a closer bond between protein, starch, and water molecules (Li, Qu, et al., 2020). At the same time, guar gum as a hydrophilic colloid can ensure the full hydration of gluten and starch with water molecules, promote gluten aggregation, and enhance the fixity of the gluten-starch three-dimensional network (Cheng et al., 2024). However, when guar gum is added in excess, it competes with starch and protein for moisture, which damaged the internal three-dimensional network structure of the dough (Li, Lv, et al., 2020).

The molecular weight of proteins under freeze-thaw cycles

The distribution of different protein molecular weights is shown in Fig. 3. Compared with the FT0 group at the same guar gum level, the intensity of protein bands of the FT4 and FT8 groups increased after freeze-thaw cycles in the low molecular weight region, this indicated that depolymerization of the gluten protein matrix occurred during the freeze-thaw cycles (Binle et al., 2021; Cheng et al., 2024). At 4 freeze-thaw cycles, the intensity of gluten protein bands was decreased in the group with guar gum added compared to FT4–0.0 % in macromolecular proteins; at 8 freeze-thaw cycles, the intensity of gluten protein bands was weakened in the groups with 0.6 %, 0.9 % and 1.2 % guar gum compared to FT8–0.0 % in macromolecular proteins, the strength of gluten bands with the addition of guar gum was weakened compared to FT-0.0 % in the low molecular weight region. This suggests that the addition of guar gum could inhibit the depolymerization of glutenin subunits under freeze-thaw cycles and slow down the destruction of macromolecular gluten (Jiao et al., 2019).

Proteins secondary structure in fermented dough under freeze-thaw cycles

β-Turns and α-helices belong to ordered structures, random curls belong to disordered structures, and α-helices and β-sheet are relatively stable (Choi & Ma, 2007). As presented in Table 3, compared with the control group, the α-helix content of the proteins did change insignificantly after freezing and thawing, the β-sheet content showed a reduction, the random coil content raised significantly, and the content of β-turn in proteins with no addition of guar gum was reduced after freezing and thawing. This may be because the α-helical structure is more stable than the β-sheet and is not easily damaged by freezing and thawing, whereas the β-sheet showed a tendency to transform into irregular curls, indicating that the protein structure within the dough began to become discrete and disordered after freezing and thawing. At 4 freeze-thaws, the addition of guar gum favored the increasement of β-sheet content and the reduction of irregular curl content, and the α-helix content of guar gum added at 0.6 % was significantly higher. At 8 freeze-thaws, the addition of guar gum favored a decrease in the irregular curl content, and the α-helix content of the proteins with 0.6 % guar gum was highest. The formation of α-helixes was associated with enhanced hydrogen bonding (Wang, Zhang, et al., 2022). Guar gum caused a transformation between the secondary structure of proteins within the freeze-thawed dough, which may be because guar gum strengthened its hydrogen-bonding interactions with gluten proteins, reduced the flexibleness of the polypeptide chains, and enhanced the structural stability of gluten proteins. At the same time, guar gum could prevent the breakage of disulfide bonds, decrease the GMP depolymerization, and stabilize the steadiness of the α-helical structure.

Protein surface hydrophobicity and free sulfhydryl and disulfide bond content in fermented dough under freeze-thaw cycles

As presented in Table 4, compared to the control group, the surface hydrophobicity of proteins in the dough increased after freeze-thawing, this might be because of ice crystals disrupting the protein structure, denaturing the proteins and exposing more hydrophobic groups (Zhu et al., 2019). The surface hydrophobicity of the proteins had a trend to decrease with the increase of guar gum content in the control group; the surface hydrophobicity of the proteins decreased significantly with 0.9 % and 1.2 % guar gum levels at 4 freeze-thaw cycles. There was no significant difference in protein surface hydrophobicity between the treatment groups at 8 freeze-thaw cycles. This may be because the anionic groups in guar gum interact electrostatically with the amino groups of proteins, which can change the hydrophobicity of proteins and improve the structural integrity of gluten, thus, it may be subject to the same freeze-thaw stress as FT8–0.0 % protein (Jiao et al., 2019).

Disulfide bonds (S—S) and free sulfhydryl (SH) had a negative relationship and could reflect dough properties (Chen et al., 2018; Zhu et al., 2022). The disulfide promotes the structural stability of gluten and strengthens the gluten polymerization process (Cui et al., 2022; Wang, Yang, et al., 2017). As presented in Table 4, disulfide bond content was reduced and free sulfhydryl content was elevated in the freezing and thawing group compared to the control. This may be because ice crystal formation and water migration aggravated gluten unfolding and disrupted disulfide bonds. Under the same freeze-thaw process, the addition of guar gum promoted disulfide bonding and enhanced gluten structural stability compared to FT-0.0 %. This may be because guar gum effectively decreases the migration of water (Wang, Yang, et al., 2017). In addition, the conversion reaction between free sulfhydryl groups and disulfide bonds is a redox reaction, in which free sulfhydryl groups are formed by breaking intramolecular or intermolecular disulfide bonds via glutathione. The addition of guar gum can reduce the content of reduced glutathione in the dough by leakage from yeast cells, thus reducing the destruction of disulfide bonds by reduced glutathione.

The internal gluten macromer content and water migration of dough under freeze-thaw cycles

Glutenin macropolymer (GMP) content reflects the aggregation properties of gluten, and GMP is formed from high-molecular-weight and low-molecular-weight wheat gluten subunits linked by intermolecular and intramolecular disulfide bonds (Jekle & Becker, 2015). As presented in Table 4, compared with the control, the gluten macromer content of the dough decreased significantly after freeze-thawing, because of the decrease of disulfide bonds within the dough. In addition, it has been shown that yeast metabolites reduced gluten aggregation during freeze-thaw cycles, which may originate from the reduction of disulfide bonds by glutathione produced by yeast metabolism within the dough after freeze-thawing (Lu, Xing, Guo, et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2016). At 4 freeze-thaw cycles, 0.9 % guar gum increased the GMP content compared with FT4–0.0 %; at 8 freeze-thaw cycles, guar gum significantly improved the GMP content compared with FT8–0.0 %. This may be related to the fact that guar gum can reduce the accumulation of reduced glutathione in the dough and promote disulfide bond formation, it is a key functional group for the formation of GMP, and an increase in disulfide bond content can significantly increase the GMP content.

As shown in Fig. 1 (in supplementary material) and Table 4, as the freeze-thaw cycles increased, the free water (A23) content inside the dough increased significantly compared to the control group, which could be caused by the decrease in the water-holding capacity of the dough and the disruption of the gluten network caused by freeze-thaw process (Duan et al., 2024; He et al., 2023). Guar gum decreased the water-binding strength of the dough in both the control and the four freeze-thaw cycle groups. The hydrophobicity of the protein surface increased and the water-binding strength decreased during the freeze-thaw cycle (Jiang et al., 2020). In the control group and the 4 freeze-thaw cycles group, the addition of guar gum decreased free water amount compared with FT-0.0 %; guar gum increased the amount of semi-bound (A22) water inside the dough compared with FT-0.0 % in the control group; and in the group with 8 freeze-thaw cycles, the 0.6 % and 0.9 % guar gum increased the amount of semi-bound water inside the dough. Guar gum significantly reduced the dough's free water content, which may be that the hydrophilic groups in guar gum can be tightly connected with water molecules via hydrogen bonding, which limits water migration and reduces the formation of large ice crystals (Linlaud et al., 2011).

In vitro protein digestibility of steamed bread under freeze-thaw cycle

As reflected in Fig. 4, the addition of guar gum reduced the protein digestibility in the bread compared to that without guar gum during the gastric enzyme digestion in the gastric. There were more undigested proteins in the bread, which may be attributed to the fact that guar gum can promote the aggregation of proteins inside the dough during the process of freezing and thawing and the formation of disulfide bonding, which makes the protein structure more solid and it is not easy to be degraded and absorbed in the undigested stage (Angioloni & Collar, 2012; Li, Lv, et al., 2020). During the trypsin digestion in the intestines, in the control group and the group with 4 times of freezing and thawing, the protein digestibility of buns with 0.3 % guar gum was the highest, and the protein digestion of bread was more complete; in the group with 8 times of freezing and thawing, the protein digestibility of buns with 0.6 % guar gum was the highest, and the protein digestion of buns bread more complete. This may be due to the change of the digestive environment from the gastric digestion stage to the intestinal digestion stage, the presence of guar gum increased the specific volume of the bread, and the internal structure of the bread was more fluffy, which improved the digestive enzymes' digestive action; at the same time, guar gum enhanced the stability of the gluten network and also made the proteins more uniformly distributed in the steaming, which improved the accessibility of digestive enzymes to the proteins (Gong et al., 2022).

Conclusion

Under the freezing-thawing process, guar gum improves the yeast's metabolism and enhances the dough's fermentation rheological properties. Meanwhile, guar gum strengthens the dough's internal structure, and guar gum could inhibit the degradation of protein's secondary and GMP in dough by freezing and thawing and promoting the gluten's formation and strength. As a space-filling agent, guar gum can inhibit water migration and develop a stable structure with protein, starch, and water molecules to protect the structural and functional stability of fermented dough under freezing and thawing cycles. In conclusion, 0.6 % performed best for fermented dough under a freeze-thaw cycle within the tested conditions.
