Teratomas are neoplasms derived from the 3 embryonic germ layers. Since their first description, fewer than 100 cases of intrapulmonary teratomas have been reported in the literature, highlighting the diagnostic challenges associated with this rare entity.

Teratomas are classified as mature (benign) or immature (potentially malignant).1 Their most frequent location is the gonadal region, whereas extragonadal presentations are uncommon. When present, they predominantly involve the mediastinum, retroperitoneum, and sacrococcygeal region.1,2 The intrapulmonary localization is exceptional.

Clinically, intrapulmonary teratomas may be asymptomatic or present with nonspecific manifestations, most commonly in young adults. Typical symptoms include chest pain, fever, productive cough, and hemoptysis. Trichoptysis, although infrequent, is considered pathognomonic and reflects rupture of tumor contents into the bronchial tree.

Radiologically, these tumors appear as heterogeneous lesions with cystic or solid components and focal calcifications, often associated with atelectasis. Computed tomography (CT) is the diagnostic gold standard, allowing the identification of fat, fluid, soft tissue, and calcified densities, as well as precise anatomical delineation. Complete surgical resection is indicated because of the risks of infection, bleeding, and bronchial rupture and is usually achieved by lobectomy or pneumonectomy.3,4

Clinical Case

A 41-year-old woman with no relevant medical history, in the late postpartum period after cesarean delivery complicated by surgical wound infection, was referred for evaluation of 2 weeks of persistent fever, progressive dyspnea, productive cough with hemoptysis, and left-sided pleuritic chest pain. Informed written consent was obtained from the patient to include her information in this publication. Institutional review board approval was not required.

On admission, blood pressure was 110/70 mmHg, heart rate 112 bpm, respiratory rate 22 breaths per minute, oxygen saturation 95% on room air, and temperature 38.5 °C. Findings of the physical examination revealed signs of consolidation over the left hemithorax and grade I digital clubbing.

Laboratory test results showed leukocytosis (15,200/μL, 88% neutrophils), C-reactive protein 95 mg/L, and erythrocyte sedimentation rate 60 mm/h. Alpha-fetoprotein and beta-human chorionic gonadotropin as well as D-dimer levels were within normal limits.

Given the patient's puerperal status, pulmonary embolism, pneumonia, and pulmonary edema were initially considered. Radiography of the chest demonstrated near-total opacification of the left lung with mild contralateral mediastinal shift. Thoracic CT revealed a large heterogeneous mass with mixed densities, suggestive of lung abscess versus intrapulmonary teratoma (Figure 1). Flexible bronchoscopy showed edematous and erythematous mucosa without focal lesions. Bronchial lavage cultures for aerobic, anaerobic, mycobacterial, and fungal pathogens were negative.

Because of the lack of clinical improvement and the exclusion of alternative diagnoses, a multidisciplinary team recommended surgical intervention. A left upper lobectomy was planned via video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery. However, conversion to posterolateral thoracotomy was required because of dense adhesions involving the lung, pleura, mediastinum, and pulmonary vessels. An ovoid mass containing sebaceous material and hair was identified intraoperatively.

Thermal injury to the phrenic nerve occurred during dissection. Postoperatively, the patient developed dysphonia and elevation of the left hemidiaphragm. Complete resection required careful manipulation of the recurrent laryngeal and phrenic nerve regions.

The patient was discharged on postoperative day 8 with referral to pulmonary and speech rehabilitation. At 6-month follow-up, her dysphonia had resolved, although left hemidiaphragm elevation persisted (Figure E1). Pulmonary function test results were normal limits.

Discussion

Intrapulmonary teratomas primarily affect young adults and most frequently involve the left upper lobe.3 Their clinical presentation is usually nonspecific and often mistaken for pneumonia, lung abscess, tuberculosis, or other inflammatory conditions, leading to delayed diagnosis.

CT is essential for diagnostic suspicion, because it typically demonstrates fatty, cystic, and calcified components.5 However, these features may overlap with infectious or necrotic lesions, particularly in complex clinical settings. Surgical resection remains the treatment of choice, and complete excision is curative in mature teratomas. Dense pleuropulmonary adhesions frequently necessitate thoracotomy, as observed in our patient.3

Presentation during the late puerperium represents an exceptional scenario. In this context, the differential diagnosis includes pulmonary embolism, postpartum cardiomyopathy with pulmonary edema, bacterial pneumonia, lung abscess, and atypical infections. Extensive evaluation is therefore required before considering surgical management.

Pregnancy and the puerperium are associated with significant physiological changes that may obscure underlying respiratory disease. Increased oxygen consumption, reduced functional residual capacity, diaphragmatic elevation, and physiological dyspnea are common and may persist postpartum. As a result, symptoms such as fatigue and mild dyspnea are often interpreted as normal adaptations.

Physical examination may be limited by thoracic restriction, and imaging of the chest is frequently deferred during pregnancy because of concerns regarding fetal radiation exposure. Even when respiratory symptoms persist, delayed imaging may contribute to late diagnosis.

In the present case, these factors likely masked disease progression, allowing the tumor to reach a considerable size before detection. This underscores the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion in patients who are pregnant and postpartum with persistent or progressive respiratory symptoms unresponsive to conventional therapy.

This report highlights the value of multidisciplinary collaboration, timely imaging, and early surgical intervention. Awareness of this rare entity in the peripartum period may facilitate earlier diagnosis, prevent complications, and improve clinical outcomes.
