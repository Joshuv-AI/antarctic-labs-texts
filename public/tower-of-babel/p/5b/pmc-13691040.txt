Intergenerational inheritance of paternal metabolic disorder: miR-29a/c-3p/glucose-6-phosphatase 1 axis in glucose intolerance propagation
Authors: Chenxuan Zhao, Muxue Lu, Ting Xu, Lei He, Fengying Deng, Meihua Zhang, Qinqin Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe escalating global burden of obesity and diabetes underscores the need to elucidate generational effects of parental metabolic health. This study investigates the generational inheritance of paternal obesity and glucose dysregulation in offspring, along with its underlying epigenetic mechanisms. Four-week-old male C57BL/6 J mice were randomly assigned to either a control diet (CON) or high-glucose/high-fat (HGF) group. After 4 weeks, these males were mated with healthy females to produce F1 male offspring, with the same breeding strategy applied to generate F2 males. Metabolic phenotyping revealed impaired glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity in HGF-fed F0 males and F1/F2 male offspring, demonstrating intergenerational transmission of paternal HGF-induced metabolic dysfunction. Liver transcriptomic profiling identified elevated expression of the gluconeogenic enzyme glucose-6-phosphatase 1 (G6pc1) and reduced levels of miR-29a/c-3p in HGF males across three generations. Luciferase assay and molecular studies confirmed that G6pc1 is a direct target of miR-29a/c-3p, and miR-29a/c-3p negatively regulates G6pc1 expression. Functional analyses showed that hepatic miR-29a-3p knockdown in wild-type mice increased G6pc1 expression, inducing glucose intolerance and insulin resistance. Conversely, miR-29a-3p overexpression in HGF offspring suppressed G6pc1 and restored glucose homeostasis. Notably, sperm miR-29a/c-3p levels were consistently reduced in HGF-fed males, suggesting its role as an epigenetic carrier mediating intergenerational metabolic dysregulation. This study uncovers a paternal intergenerational inheritance mechanism whereby HGF-induced glucose dysregulation propagates intergenerationally through the miR-29a/c-3p-G6pc1 axis. Introduction

The global rise in obesity and diabetes represents a critical public health challenge of the twenty-first century. Recent estimates from the Global Burden of Disease Study (GBD 2021) indicate that 45.1% of adults aged ≥ 25 years (over 2.11 billion individuals), were overweight or obese in 2021 [1]. Concurrently, diabetes prevalence reached 529 million cases, with projections suggesting a surge to 1.31 billion by 2050 [2]. These trends are driven largely by widespread consumption of high-sugar, high-fat diets, which promote obesity-associated metabolic dysregulation, including insulin resistance and impaired glucose homeostasis—key contributors to type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular diseases. While maternal metabolic health has been extensively studied in developmental programming, the role of paternal factors has received less attention. Emerging evidence from the Paternal Origins of Health and Disease (POHaD) hypothesis demonstrates that paternal obesity, poor diet, and metabolic dysfunction can disrupt offspring metabolic function, with potential transgenerational consequences [3–5]. However, the molecular mechanisms, key regulatory targets, and patterns of intergenerational inheritance remain poorly defined.

Mounting evidence demonstrates that environmental stressors and nutritional imbalances can induce phenotypic alterations in the parental (F0) generation, some of which termed "acquired" traits, are transmitted to offspring (F1) and subsequent generations (F2 +) [4–6]. This intergenerational inheritance underscores the heritability of environmentally induced traits, mediated largely by epigenetic mechanisms. Environmental stressors, such as nutritional imbalance, remodel DNA methylation, histone modifications, and non-coding RNAs in F0 germ cells, thereby influencing metabolic phenotypes in F1 and F2 generations [4, 7–9]. Notably, sperm small RNAs (particularly microRNAs (miRNAs)), due to their sensitivity to dietary changes and regulatory roles in embryonic development, are recognized as key mediators of paternal acquired trait transmission [7, 10, 11]. For instance, paternal high-fat diets markedly modify sperm miRNA profiles, thereby perturbing offspring metabolic programming via fertilization [7, 10].

Glucose metabolism is central to systemic energy homeostasis, with its dysregulation underlying metabolic disorders such as obesity and diabetes. Hepatic glucose metabolism is tightly controlled to maintain blood glucose homeostasis. Upon uptake by hepatocytes, glucose is phosphorylated by glucokinase (GCK) to yield glucose-6-phosphate (G6P), whereas glucose-6-phosphatase (G6pc, key gluconeogenic enzyme) catalyzes the reverse reaction, hydrolyzing G6P back to glucose. This glucose-G6P cycle is essential for metabolic stability [12, 13]. G6pc comprises three isoforms (G6pc1, G6pc2, and G6pc3), and its dysregulation is strongly linked to metabolic diseases, particularly type 2 diabetes and insulin resistance [12]. Moreover, miRNAs, key post-transcriptional regulators, modulate glucose metabolism-related signaling pathways [14, 15], playing a pivotal role in glucose homeostasis and the pathogenesis of insulin resistance. Emerging evidence suggests that G6pc, as a critical metabolic enzyme, may be finely regulated by miRNAs [16, 17]. Although it remains unclear whether miRNA-mediated regulation of key glucose metabolism genes (e.g., G6pc) contributes to the generational inheritance of paternal metabolic disorders, elucidating the mechanistic interplay between miRNAs and metabolic genes could deepen our understanding of disease pathogenesis and pave the way for miRNA-based precision therapeutics.

This study establishes a paternal diet-induced obesity and glucose dysregulation animal model to investigate: (1) the mechanistic link between paternal metabolic disorders and offspring hepatic glucose metabolism impairment; (2) the role of miRNAs in mediating generational inheritance through key gene regulation (e.g., G6pc); and (3) the persistence of such metabolic disorders across generations. These findings will advance the POHaD theory by elucidating generational transmission mechanisms and identifying novel targets for early metabolic disorder intervention.

Material and methods

Experimental animals

Male C57BL/6 J mice (4 weeks old) from Soochow University’s Animal Experiment Center were randomly allocated into two groups (N = 40 per group): control (CON; standard rodent chow: 52% carbohydrates, 22.1% protein, 5.3% fat, 1.0% sodium, 1.0% calcium, 0.9% phosphorus, 0.5% potassium, 0.2% magnesium) and high-glucose/high-fat (HGF; 42% fat-derived calories + 20% glucose water). High-fat feed (#D12451) was purchased from Shanghai Ruian Biological Company, and the specific formula was shown in Table 1. Mice were maintained at 22 ± 1 °C under a 10:14-h light–dark cycle. After 4 weeks (8-week-old), F0 males from both groups were mated with normal-diet females (1:2 ratio). Gestational day 1 (GD1) was confirmed by vaginal plug observation. Pregnant dams received standard chow and water; offspring were designated as F1. F1 males were weaned at postnatal day 21, maintained on standard diet until 8 weeks, and bred identically to produce F2. One male offspring was randomly selected from each litter in F1 and F2 generations for subsequent experiments. Body weights were monitored weekly from 4 to 12 weeks in F0-F2 males. At 12 weeks, mice were euthanized under anesthesia, and heart, kidney, and liver weights were recorded as percentages of body weight. The white (perirenal, perigenital, and inguinal) and brown adipose tissue from F0-F2 male mice were also dissected and recorded post-euthanasia. All procedures complied with ARRIVE guidelines, were approved by the Institutional Animal Welfare Committee and adhered to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Ethics approval was obtained from Soochow University Medical Center (Ethics Approval #2022488).

Glucose metabolism-related tests

For intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test (IPGTT), pyruvate tolerance test (IPPTT) and glucagon tolerance test (IPGcTT), 12-week-old F0–F2 males were fasted overnight (16 h; water ad libitum), weighed, and labeled. After baseline (0 min) tail-vein blood glucose measurement, glucose, pyruvate and glucagon at the indicated dosage (1.5 g/kg body weight) were respectively administered intraperitoneally. Blood glucose levels were monitored at 15, 30, 60, and 120 min post-injection[18, 19]. For insulin tolerance test (IPITT), mice were fasted for 4 h (water ad libitum), weighed, and injected intraperitoneally with insulin (0.4 U/kg body weight in saline) following baseline (0 min) blood glucose measurement. Glucose levels were assessed at 15, 30, 60, and 120 min. To prevent hypoglycemia, glucose (1.0 g/kg) was administered intraperitoneally at endpoint [9]. Blood glucose levels were measured by using the blood glucose meter (OneTouch, Johnson & Johnson), and drawn blood glucose-time curve.

Plasma insulin and HOMA-IR analysis

Blood samples were collected from 12-week-old F0 − F2 generation mice for plasma insulin quantification via Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay as the manufacturer's protocol. Insulin resistance was evaluated using the homeostasis model assessment (HOMA-IR), and calculated as: HOMA − IR = (Fasting insulin × fasting glucose)/22.5.

Cell culture and treatments

Primary hepatocytes were isolated from liver tissues of 4-week-old male F1 and F2 generation mice. Briefly, mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection, followed by liver perfusion with phosphate buffer (PBS) containing 2% penicillin–streptomycin (PS) through the inferior vena cava. The excised liver was minced into a homogenate in PBS with 2% PS and digested with 3 mg/mL collagenase type II (dissolved in serum- and antibiotic-free medium) at a 1:2 (w/v) ratio for 30 min at 37 °C. After enzymatic digestion, the suspension was centrifuged at 7,000 rpm for 3 min, and the middle/lower layer was collected. The pelleted cells were resuspended in DMEM complete medium supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin, then cultured at 37 °C under 5% CO₂ [20, 21].

Primary hepatocytes were seeded in 10 cm2 dishes and passaged every 2–3 days using standard protocols; cells at passages 3–5 were used for experiments. To investigate the regulatory relationship between G6pc1 and miRNA, miRNA inhibitor (knockdown) or mimics (overexpression) were transfected using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen). Cells were harvested 48 h post-transfection for subsequent miRNA, mRNA, and protein analyses. All custom miRNA sequences (Tables 2 and 3) were synthesized by Genepharma (China).

Immunofluorescence staining

Immunofluorescence staining was performed to assess G6pc1 expression in offspring liver tissues. Fresh liver specimens from F0–F2 generation mice were dissected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 1 h. The tissues were then embedded in optimal cutting temperature compound (OCT; Sakura, Tokyo, Japan), and 2–4 μm sections were prepared using a cryostat (Leica CM1850). After fixation, liver sections on slides were permeabilized with 1% PBST (PBS containing 1% Triton X-100) on ice for 30 min, followed by blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin (Beyotime Biotechnology) at 37 °C for 1 h. The sections were sequentially incubated with anti-G6pc1 primary antibody (1:1000; SAB2106669; Sigma) and corresponding fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibody (1:500; Proteintech). Nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; Invitrogen). Images were acquired and analyzed using a Nikon E600 fluorescence microscope equipped with a DMX1200 digital imaging system.

Tail-vein injection

To investigate the roles of miR-29a/c-3p in paternal diet-induced intergenerational metabolic dysfunction, we functionally modulated miR-29a/c-3p expression in mice via hydrodynamic tail vein injection. Male CON-F2 mice were randomly assigned to either a control group (injected with empty vector) or an miR-29a/c-3p (miR-29a-3p + miR-29c-3p) inhibitor group. Similarly, male HGF-F2 mice were divided into a control group (empty vector) or an miR-29a/c-3p mimic group. Plasmids for miR-29a-3p and miR-29c-3p inhibition (LV3(H1/GFP&Puro)-miR-29a/c-3p inhibitor) or overexpression (LV3(H1/GFP&Puro)-miR-29a/c-3p mimics) were synthesized by Genepharma (Shanghai, China). For hydrodynamic tail vein injection, mice received 20 μg of inhibitor or mimic plasmid (10 mg each for miR-29a-3p and miR-29c-3p) suspended in saline at a volume equivalent to 10% of body weight (1.2–1.8 mL) delivered within 5–10 s. Control animals were injected with an equal volume of empty vector (LV3-shNC) [22, 23]. Four weeks post-injection, fasting blood glucose was measured, followed by IPGTT and IPITT. Mice were subsequently anesthetized and euthanized for liver tissue collection and subsequent analyses.

Quantitative real‑time PCR and western blot

Total RNA was isolated from liver tissues or primary hepatocytes using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen), followed by quantification of RNA concentration and purity (NanoDrop 2000 C; Thermo Scientific). Reverse transcription of 1 μg RNA was performed using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara). qRT-PCR was conducted on an iQ5 system (Bio-Rad) with SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems) in 20μL reactions. Gene expression was normalized to GAPDH and calculated via the ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are listed in Tables 2 and 3. For miRNA analysis, miRNAs were extracted using the miRcute kit (Tiangen), reverse-transcribed with miRcute Plus reagents (Tiangen), and normalized to U6 snRNA. All experiments included three biological and technical replicates.

Liver tissues or hepatocytes were lysed in RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1% SDS, 10 mM EDTA, pH 7.4) supplemented with protease inhibitors (sc-29130; Santa Cruz Biotechnology). Protein concentrations were determined by BCA assay, and 20 μg lysates were resolved by 10% SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes, and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (ST023; Beyotime). Membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against G6pc1 (1:1000; SAB2106669; Sigma) or β-actin (1:3000; A5441; Sigma), followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h. Signals were detected using an EC3 imaging system and quantified via band intensity ratios (G6pc1/β-actin) in ImageJ. Experiments were repeated 3–5 times with independent samples, as previously described [24, 25].

Luciferase reporter assay

To validate the direct regulatory interaction between miR-29a/c-3p and the 3'UTR of G6pc1, we performed dual-luciferase reporter assays. The experimental procedure involved cloning either the wild-type (G6pc1 wt) or mutant (G6pc1 mut) 3'UTR binding sequences into the PmirGLO dual-luciferase reporter vector (Promega, USA). Hepatic cells were co-transfected using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, USA) with the following constructs: (1) PmirGLO-G6pc1 wt + miR-29a/c-3p mimic; (2) PmirGLO-G6pc1 mut + miR-29a/c-3p mimic; (3) empty PmirGLO vector + negative control miRNA (NC). After 48 h, cells were lysed and luciferase activity was determined using a dual-luciferase reporter detection kit (#RG027, Beyotime, China). The relative luciferase activity was determined with the average activity of the firefly luciferase normalized to the renilla luciferase activity [26].

Transcriptome sequencing and analysis

Liver tissue samples from F2 male offspring of CON and HGF groups (three biological replicates per group) were homogenized for total RNA extraction. RNA concentration and purity were measured using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and integrity was assessed (RIN ≥ 7.0) on an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer system using the RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit (Agilent Technologies). During library construction, we employed strand-specific library preparation, specifically enriching mRNA from total RNA using Oligo dT magnetic beads. Following fragmentation, the first strand of cDNA was synthesized using random hexametric primers. Subsequently, the second strand of cDNA was synthesized using dUTP instead of dTTP. Following end repair, A-tailing, adapter ligation, fragment selection, amplification, and purification, the strand-specific library was prepared. Upon completion of library preparation, preliminary quantification was performed using a Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer. The library was diluted to 1.5 ng/μl, followed by insert size analysis via Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Once the insert size met expectations, qRT-PCR was employed for precise quantification of the library's effective concentration (> 1.5 nM), thereby ensuring library quality. Following successful library validation, different libraries were pooled according to effective concentration and target read-through requirements prior to Illumina sequencing. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina platform using sequencing-by-synthesis chemistry. The flow cell was loaded with fluorescently labeled dNTPs, DNA polymerase, and adapter primers. During cluster amplification, incorporation of each dNTP released a corresponding fluorescent signal, which was captured and computationally converted into sequencing reads [26, 27]. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 R package (v1.16.1), and functional annotation of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was conducted by Tiangen Biotech (Beijing, China). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of DEGs was implemented via the topGO R package, with correction for gene length bias. GO terms with adjusted P < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched. Additionally, protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis of DEGs was constructed using the STRING database to identify potential functional regulatory modules.

Bioinformatics analysis

Candidate miRNAs targeting G6pc1 were predicted using miRDB (https://mirdb.org/), a widely adopted database for miRNA target prediction and functional annotation. By entering the murine species and gene name in the "Search by gene target" option, miRDB generated a ranked list of potential regulatory miRNAs based on target prediction scores. Seven top-scoring candidates (miR-185-5p, miR-451a, miR-29a-3p, miR-29b-3p, miR-29c-3p, miR-200b-3p, and miR-200c-3p; Table 3) were selected for further experimental validation.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Time-dependent responses were analyzed by two-way ANOVA with Bonferroni post hoc tests (GraphPad Prism 8, USA). Other comparisons were performed using Student’s t-tests or one-way ANOVA, as specified in figure legends. A significance threshold of P < 0.05 was applied.

Results

Intergenerational inheritance of glucose metabolic dysfunction induced by paternal HGF exposure

A paternal high-glucose/fat-diet exposure model was established as outlined in Fig. 1A. Four-week-old male C57BL/6 J mice (F0) were fed an HGF diet (42% fat-derived calories supplemented with 20% glucose water) for four weeks. F0 males were then mated with age-matched females reared on standard chow to generate F1 offspring. F1 males were maintained on standard chow for eight weeks before mating with control-diet females to produce F2 offspring. Body weight analysis revealed significant increases in HGF-F0 to HGF-F2 males compared to CON (Supplemental Fig. 1A-C). We also examined adipocyte tissue weight in male offspring, finding significant differences correlating with body weight changes. Compared to CON males, HGF males across F0 to F2 generations had increased brown adipose tissue and white adipose tissue weights (Supplemental Fig. 2). Organ-to-body weight ratios demonstrated reduced liver/body weight ratios in HGF-F0 to HGF-F2 mice (Supplemental Fig. 3), suggesting impaired hepatic development in HGF males.

Glucose tolerance tests showed elevated fasting blood glucose in HGF-F0 versus CON-F0 mice (Fig. 1B (a)). HGF-F0 exhibited glucose intolerance, with significantly higher area under the curve (AUC) (Fig. 1B (b)), and reduced insulin sensitivity (increased insulin tolerance test AUC; Fig. 1B (c, d)). These metabolic disturbances persisted in HGF-F1 and HGF-F2 generations (Fig. 1C and D). We also performed pyruvate tolerance test and glucagon tolerance test in F1 and F2 offspring. It was found that both the pyruvate and glucagon tolerance responses were slightly abnormal in the F1 and F2 generation (Supplemental Fig. 4). The above experiments demonstrate that males on a high-sugar, high-fat diet across F0 to F2 generations exhibit increased weight, glucose intolerance, and insulin insensitivity. This also indicates that the impaired glucose metabolic-related pathological phenotypes caused by a high-glucose/fat-diet in the F0 generation can be intergenerational inheritance.

Alterations in hepatic glucose metabolic-related gene expression profiles in HGF offspring

To investigate whether insulin secretion was altered in HGF offspring, we measured plasma insulin levels across generations. Compared to CON-F0, HGF-F0 mice exhibited significantly elevated plasma insulin, whereas no significant differences were observed in F1 or F2 generations (Fig. 2A). However, the insulin resistance index was markedly increased in all HGF offspring generations (F0–F2), with the most pronounced effect in F0 (Fig. 2B). These results indicate that paternal HGF diet does not disrupt insulin levels in F1 and F2 offspring, but that insulin resistance persists across generations.

To identify key pathways and target genes underlying hepatic glucose dysregulation in HGF offspring, we performed liver transcriptome sequencing in HGF-F2 males. Compared to CON-F2, 451 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified in HGF-F2 livers, including 48 significant DEGs (32 upregulated, 16 downregulated) (Fig. 2C). GO and pathway enrichment analyses revealed predominant involvement in starch/sucrose metabolism, insulin signaling, insulin resistance, and glycolysis/gluconeogenesis (Fig. 2D). Notably, G6pc1, a key enzyme in gluconeogenesis, glycogenolysis, and the glucagon glycemic response, was significantly upregulated. Protein–protein interaction network analysis further highlighted G6pc1 as a hub gene (Fig. 2E and F), suggesting its pivotal role in paternal HGF-induced intergenerational metabolic dysfunction.

Altered G6pc1 expression in the livers of HGF-exposed mice

Hepatic glucose metabolism is primarily regulated through several key pathways: glycolysis, gluconeogenesis, and glycogen metabolism. Based on liver transcriptome sequencing and protein–protein interaction network analyses, we identified 14 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) associated with glucose metabolism, including G6pc1/2/3, GCK/GCKr, Gpi1, Gys1/2, GSK3a/3b, Pygl, Pck1/2, and Pcx. Expression analysis of these genes across three generations (F0–F2) of HGF-exposed mice revealed distinct generational patterns. While certain genes (GCK, Gys1, Pygl, and Pck2) exhibited altered expression exclusively in the HGF-F2 generation compared to CON, GSK3a/3b showed changes only in the HGF-F0 generation (Fig. 3A–D). Notably, G6pc1 displayed significantly elevated mRNA and protein levels not only in HGF-F2 but also in HGF-F0 and HGF-F1 (Fig. 3D–G; Supplemental Fig. 5). These findings suggest that paternal HGF exposure may upregulate G6pc1, potentially contributing to hyperglycemia and metabolic dysfunction.

Upregulation of G6pc1 expression is associated with miR-29a/c-3p

Our study revealed elevated G6pc1 mRNA levels in the HGF group, suggesting transcriptional activation as the underlying mechanism. Since DNA methylation within gene promotor represents a key epigenetic regulator of gene transcription, and prior study has established epigenetic role in modulating G6pc1 expression [27], we first investigated whether DNA methylation mediated this transcriptional activation. Bioinformatics analysis identified one CpG island (positioned 963–1063 bp upstream of the transcription start site) containing five CpG sites in the G6pc1 promoter region (Supplemental Fig. 6A and B). Bisulfite sequencing revealed no significant differences in promoter DNA methylation between HGF and CON groups (Supplemental Fig. 6C). Hepatocytes were treated with DNA methyltransferase inhibitor (5-AZA-2’-deoxycytidine) to evaluate changes in G6pc1 expression upon inhibition of DNA methylation. We found no significant changes in G6pc1 mRNA levels (Supplemental Fig. 6D). These data indirectly suggest that G6pc1 transcriptional activation in the HGF group occurs independently of DNA methylation.

Small RNAs, particularly miRNAs, are sensitive to nutritional changes and serve as key mediators of paternal acquired trait transmission across generations [7, 10]. Emerging evidence suggests that G6pc1 expression may be regulated by miRNAs [16, 17]. Using the miRDB database and integrating previous studies [28–30], we identified seven candidate miRNAs (miR-185-5p, miR-451a, miR-29a/b/c-3p, and miR-200b/c-3p) potentially involved in G6pc1 transcriptional regulation. In F2 offspring liver tissues, miR-185-5p expression was significantly elevated in the HGF-F2 group compared to CON-F2, whereas miR-29a/b/c-3p levels were markedly reduced (Fig. 4A). Further analysis across three generations (F0–F2) revealed that only miR-29a/c-3p exhibited consistently low expression in HGF-exposed mice (Fig. 4B). These findings not only indicate that paternal HGF exposure induces sustained generational suppression of miR-29a/c-3p expression in offspring livers, but also suggest a potential mechanistic link between reduced miR-29a/c-3p levels and G6pc1 transcriptional activation.

To investigate the regulatory role of miR-29a/c-3p on G6pc1 expression, we transfected CON-F2 primary hepatocytes with miR-29a/c-3p inhibitors (Fig. 4C). RT-qPCR and western blot analyses revealed a significant upregulation of G6pc1 upon miR-29a/c-3p suppression (Fig. 4D and E). Conversely, transfection with miR-29a/c-3p mimics (Fig. 4F) markedly downregulated G6pc1 expression (Fig. 4G and H). These results demonstrate that the elevated G6pc1 levels in HGF-exposed offspring livers are associated with the reduced miR-29a/c-3p expression.

miR-29a/c-3p directly targets G6pc1

To validate whether miR-29a/c-3p directly regulates G6pc1 expression, we performed dual-luciferase reporter assays. We constructed reporter vectors containing either the wild-type (G6pc1-wt) or mutant (G6pc1-mut) 3′UTR of G6pc1 (Fig. 5a). Hepatic cells were co-transfected with these reporter plasmids and either miR-29a/c-3p mimics or negative control oligonucleotides (NC). Luciferase assays revealed that miR-29a/c-3p mimics significantly suppressed firefly luciferase activity when co-transfected with the wild-type G6pc1 3′UTR reporter, whereas this suppression was abolished with the mutant reporter (Fig. 5b). These results demonstrate that G6pc1 is a direct target of miR-29a/c-3p.

Analysis of miR-29a/c-3p in F0-F1 sperm revealed significantly lower expression in the HGF group than in controls across generations, which was similar to the liver experiment results (Fig. 6E). These data suggest that paternal HGF exposure induces glucose metabolic dysfunction in F0 mice, which is transmitted to offspring, potentially via altered sperm miR-29a/c-3p expression. The sustained downregulation of miR-29a/c-3p in HGF-exposed sperm further supports its role as an epigenetic mediator in the intergenerational inheritance of metabolic phenotypes. In summary, our findings demonstrate that paternal HGF exposure disrupts miR-29a/c-3p expression in both liver and sperm across F0-F2 generations. Given miR-29a/c-3p’s direct targeting of G6pc1, its suppression in hepatic tissue elevates G6pc1 expression, promoting gluconeogenic conversion of G6P to glucose. This mechanism culminates in hyperglycemia and impaired glucose homeostasis (Fig. 6F).

Discussion

This study demonstrates that paternal HGF exposure induces intergenerational transmission of glucose metabolic disorders. Mechanistically, we indicate that: (1) persistent hepatic G6pc1 upregulation in offspring is associated with impaired glucose homeostasis; (2) G6pc1 dysregulation is directly targeted by miR-29a/c-3p, which exhibits sustained downregulation in both paternal/offspring sperm and liver; (3) functional rescue experiments confirm that modulating miR-29a/c-3p normalizes G6pc1 expression and ameliorates metabolic dysfunction, establishing causality for the miR-29a/c-3p–G6pc1 axis; and (4) the intergenerational suppression of sperm miR-29a/c-3p suggests that it may be an epigenetic carrier. Together, our findings reveal a paternal nutrition-induced intergenerational inheritance mechanism associated with the miR-29a/c-3p–G6pc1 regulatory axis, which advances understanding of the origins of metabolic diseases and informs potential miRNA-targeted interventions.

The escalating global prevalence of obesity and diabetes has positioned intergenerational inheritance as a pivotal focus in metabolic disease research. Clinical cohort studies demonstrate that paternal obesity significantly elevates risks of fetal developmental abnormalities [31] and childhood type 1 diabetes [32], whereas paternal adherence to healthy lifestyles mitigates offspring obesity [33], underscoring the critical role of paternal factors in metabolic disease transmission. Emerging evidence reveals that paternal dietary insults similarly drive transgenerational phenotypes: high-fat diets impair F1 offspring skeletal muscle lipid homeostasis [34], promote hepatic steatosis [35], and reduce sperm quality [36], implicating germline epigenetic modifications. Notably, these effects persist multigenerationally, with grandpaternal high-fat diets inducing persistent hepatic transcriptomic alterations in F2 offspring [37], while paternal high-sugar diets perpetuate triglyceride dysregulation across generations [38]. Overnutrition, a prevalent health concern in modern societies, primarily stems from excessive consumption of high-fat-high-sucrose diets, a hallmark of Western dietary patterns. However, existing studies on the intergenerational effects of overnutrition remain limited, as most focus solely on single-nutrient (e.g., high-fat or high-sugar) parental exposures while neglecting the generational consequences of composite dietary patterns. Using a paternal HGF-induced obesity mice model, we systematically demonstrate that paternal HGF exposure induces metabolic disturbances, including glucose intolerance, and reduced insulin sensitivity across F0-F2 generations. These findings establish that adverse paternal dietary patterns can compromise offspring metabolic health via intergenerational inheritance.

Paternal HGF exposure across F0–F2 generations induced glucose intolerance, reduced insulin sensitivity, and elevated insulin resistance, without significant changes in plasma insulin levels—collectively suggesting hepatic-centered metabolic dysfunction. Hepatic glucose synthesis is central to systemic glucose homeostasis and is tightly regulated through several key pathways: glycolysis, gluconeogenesis, and glycogen metabolism. To elucidate the cause of abnormal glucose metabolism in HGF offspring, we performed liver transcriptome analysis coupled with molecular validation of key glucose metabolism regulators. These approaches revealed that the dysregulated glucose metabolism in HGF offspring is associated with persistent upregulation of G6pc1, a rate-limiting enzyme that governs hepatic glucose output during fasting by mediating the final steps of gluconeogenesis and glycogenolysis [39]. The increase in G6pc1 expression explains the reason why the area under the glucose curve of the HGF males increased in all glucose, pyruvate and glucagon tolerance tests. Consistent elevation of G6pc1 across generations also suggests that paternal HGF-induced intergenerational glucose metabolism dysfunction is related to aberrant G6pc1 expression. Although our data establish a central role for hepatic G6pc1-upregulated in the observed phenotype, we acknowledge that whole-body insulin resistance likely involves contributions from peripheral tissues such as skeletal muscle and adipose. Future studies assessing tissue-specific insulin sensitivity, muscular glucose uptake, and adipokine profiles will help delineate the multi-tissue mechanisms underlying this intergenerational metabolic phenotype.

DNA methylation is a major epigenetic modification that regulates gene expression. Although the transcriptional activity of genes is more closely related to DNA methylation of gene promoter CpG islands, DNA methylation of enhancers and gene bodies also play important and complex regulatory roles. In this present study, we found that there was no significant change in the DNA methylation level of the G6pc1 gene promoter in the HGF offspring livers. Meanwhile, G6pc1 mRNA levels were not significantly altered when DNA methylation was inhibited in hepatocytes. These data indirectly suggest that G6pc1 transcriptional activation in the HGF offspring livers is independent of DNA methylation. Published evidence highlights the critical involvement of miRNAs in regulating hepatic G6pc1 expression [16, 17]. For example, miR-451 ameliorates gluconeogenesis-associated metabolic disorders by targeting G6pc1 [39], while miR-185-5p directly suppresses G6pc1 expression via binding to its 3′UTR [28]. Leveraging the miRDB database and prior studies [28–30], we identified seven candidate miRNAs (miR-185-5p, miR-451a, miR-29a/b/c-3p, and miR-200b/c-3p) as putative G6pc1 regulators. We found only miR-29a/c-3p were consistently downregulated in the third-generation male offspring livers from HGF fathers, with dual-luciferase assays confirming G6pc1 as their direct target. MiR-29 family members are well-documented regulators of metabolic disorders [29, 40]. Our functional studies further demonstrate that liver-specific knockdown of miR-29a/c-3p upregulated G6pc1, inducing glucose intolerance and insulin resistance in wild-type mice. Conversely, miR-29a/c-3p overexpression in HGF offspring suppressed G6pc1 and restored glucose homeostasis. Collectively, these findings reveal that paternal HGF exposure disrupt the miR-29a/c-3p–G6pc1 axis, thereby impairing glycemic control in the offspring.

Recent studies indicates that sperm miRNAs, owing to their high sensitivity to nutritional changes, function as key epigenetic mediator in the intergenerational transmission of paternally acquired metabolic disorders [7, 10, 11]. Among these, the miR-29 family exhibits broad expression in germ cells [41, 42], with its levels readily modulated by external stimuli. Although previous studies have reported the regulatory relationship between miR-29 and G6pc1, the mechanistic innovation of this study is not to rediscover this relationship, but to establish it as a key downstream effector pathway in paternal diet-induced intergenerational metabolic programming. It is currently unclear whether this specific regulatory axis can lead to the intergenerational transmission of glucose metabolism disorders in offspring through paternal germ cells. This study fills this gap through the following evidence: (1) Paternal HGF exposure can stably reduce the expression of miR-29a/c-3p in offspring sperm and liver, thereby establishing a direct link between paternal diet and offspring specific miRNA expression changes; (2) Downregulation of this miRNA is functionally critical, as it directly leads to offspring glucose homeostasis imbalance by upregulating G6pc1 expression. This study reinterprets known metabolic pathways within an intergenerational genetic framework. It not only goes beyond phenomenon correlation, but also proposes a possible cascading mechanism: “paternal HGF exposure downregulation of sperm and offspring liver miR-29a/c-3p → upregulation of G6pc1 expression → offspring hyperglycemia”. Our study not only highlight the dietary sensitivity of miR-29a/c-3p, but also indicate that it may be a key epigenetic mediator in the intergenerational transmission of paternal metabolic abnormalities, providing new experimental basis for miRNA-mediated transgenerational inheritance of acquired metabolic characteristics, thus promoting the development of the field of intergenerational inheritance.

Although this study indicates the intergenerational role of miR-29a/c-3p and its regulation of G6pc1, the upstream mechanisms driving its persistent down-regulation remain to be fully elucidated. Existing literatures reveal that miR-29 expression is tightly controlled by transcription factors such as c-Myc and TGF-β1, which respond to metabolic, inflammatory, and fibrotic signals [43–45], as well as by epigenetic mechanisms including DNA methylation and histone modifications [43, 46]. In the context of paternal HGF exposure, the intergenerational transmission of metabolic stress may alter the epigenetic landscape of the miR-29a/c-3p locus or modulate relevant transcription factor activity, leading to sustained repression in offspring livers. Furthermore, the reduced levels of miR-29a/c-3p in paternal sperm could itself act as an epigenetic signal, contributing to its heritable expression pattern in the next generation. Future studies integrating transcriptional profiling and epigenetic editing will be valuable in delineating the precise regulatory mechanism underlying the intergenerational suppression of miR-29a/c-3p.

Non-coding small RNAs—such as miRNAs, tsRNAs, piRNAs, and rsRNAs—have emerged as key mediators in the intergenerational transmission of paternal diet-acquired metabolic traits [4, 8, 47]. Previous studies systematically profiling gametic small RNAs, particularly tsRNAs and piRNAs, have indicated their role in propagating high fat diet-induced metabolic disturbances across F1 to F3 generations [4, 8, 47]. In this present study, rather than broadly profiling small RNA alterations in sperm across generations under paternal HGF exposure, we focused on G6pc1 as an entry point. We demonstrate that miR-29a/c-3p regulates G6pc1 expression and observe its dysregulation in sperm from multiple generations of HGF-exposed mice. Consequently, a genome-wide evaluation of sperm small RNA expression was not performed in this study. Nevertheless, comprehensively mapping the sperm small RNA landscape across generations and linking it to other intergenerationally transmitted phenotypes represents an important future direction.

In summary, our study elucidates that paternal high-fat/high-sugar diet induces intergenerational transmission of glucose metabolic disorders in male offspring, through the miR-29a/c-3p/G6pc1 signaling axis. However, several limitations warrant consideration. Firstly, this study only focuses on the phenotypic characteristics of male offspring and failed to comprehensively evaluate the intergenerational impact of paternal metabolic abnormalities on female offspring and its potential sex-specific regulatory mechanisms. The impact of paternal diet on male and female offspring and the differences in underlying mechanisms are important directions that need to be systematically explored in the future. Secondly, the upstream regulatory mechanisms of abnormal miR-29a/c-3p expression have yet to be elucidated, which will be a key focus for future mechanism studies. Thirdly, although this study quantified the expression of miR-29a/c-3p in sperm, the causal relationship between it and the intergenerational transmission of abnormal glucose metabolism in offspring has not been further confirmed by in vitro modification of the expression of this miRNA in sperm and combined with in vitro fertilization experiments. Furthermore, while there has been extensive research on maternal environmental factors, studies on the intergenerational effects of paternal nutritional stress are still insufficient. Future research should focus on the following areas: 1) validating animal experiment findings through multicenter clinical cohorts; 2) systematically analyzing the synergistic effects of both parental nutritional factors; 3) using new technologies such as single-cell sequencing to elucidate the spatiotemporal dynamics of gamete epigenetic reprogramming, thereby establishing a more comprehensive intergenerational risk assessment system for metabolic diseases.
