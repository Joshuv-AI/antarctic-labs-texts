Determinants of equitable public health human resource allocation in China: a multidimensional analysis using RIF-I-OLS decomposition
Authors: Hao Wang, Guoliang Ma, Hui Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRapid economic development and urbanization in China have improved population health outcomes, but exacerbated inequalities in the allocation of public health human resources (PHHR). Existing studies largely rely on static measures and offer limited insights into the mechanisms driving these disparities. This study systematically identifies and quantifies the determinants influencing public health workforce allocation in China, aiming to provide empirical evidence to guide policy interventions.MethodsThis study analyzed the allocation of PHHR across 31 Chinese provinces from 2018 to 2022, employing four inequality indices: Gini coefficients (Gini), concentration index (CI), absolute Gini (AGini), and absolute concentration index (ACI). Two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and bivariate correlation analyses were used to assess temporal and regional variations. The recentered influence function-index-ordinary least squares (RIF-I-OLS) method was applied to decompose these inequality indices. This approach quantified the contributions of key factors, including the illiteracy rate among the population aged 15 years and above, government health expenditure, number of professional public health institutions (PPHI), mortality rate from Class A and B infectious diseases (IDs), and life expectancy (LE), while distinguishing between characteristic effects and coefficient effects.ResultsBetween 2018 and 2019, inequity in PHHR allocation increased (Gini: 0.3792–0.3844; CI 0.0215–0.0495). In contrast, from 2019 to 2022, allocation equity improved (Gini: 0.3715; CI 0.0279). A greater number of PPHIs, a lower mortality rate of class A and B IDs, and longer LE helped mitigate inequalities, whereas a lower illiteracy rate among the population aged ≥ 15 years and increased governmental health expenditure exacerbated disparities. Inequality in PHHR allocation during 2018–2019 was primarily driven by characteristic effects, whereas in 2021–2022, coefficient effects became dominant, underscoring the role of institutional and systemic factors.ConclusionsWhile the equity of PHHR allocation in China has shown modest improvement, structural and institutional factors remain key determinants of lingering inequities. This highlights the need for targeted policies to optimize the distribution of the public health workforce.

Introduction

Ensuring an adequate and equitable distribution of public health human resources (PHHR) is fundamental to the effectiveness of health systems. Public health professionals are critical for disease prevention, health promotion, and preparedness for public health emergencies. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends a minimum density of “44.5 health workers per 10,000 population” to achieve universal health coverage. Nevertheless, many regions worldwide, including rural and western provinces of China, remain below this threshold, underscoring persistent inequities in access to essential health services [1, 2].

Over the past decades, China’s rapid economic growth and urbanization have substantially improved population health outcomes; however, these advances have been accompanied by pronounced disparities in the allocation of PHHR. Data from the China Health Statistical Yearbooks reveal a disproportionate concentration of public health personnel in economically developed regions (e.g., Beijing, Shanghai, and Jiangsu province), whereas inland and western provinces (e.g., Guizhou province, Gansu province, and Tibet) continue to lag far behind both national and international benchmarks [3]. In 2021, for example, the density of public health workers in Beijing exceeded 10 per 10,000 population, whereas several underdeveloped provinces reported fewer than half this figure. Moreover, numerous county-level Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDCs) fail to meet staffing standards established by the National Health Commission [4].

The underlying drivers of these disparities have been examined through both theoretical frameworks and empirical investigations. From a structural perspective, the distribution of human resources for health is shaped by broader social determinants, including fiscal capacity, educational attainment, urbanization, and the infrastructure of health systems [5, 6]. For instance, health labor market theory posits that the allocation of health workers is influenced by both demand-side factors (e.g., population health needs and disease burden) and supply-side factors (e.g., remuneration, career prospects, and living conditions) [7, 8]. Meanwhile, spatial equity theory highlights geographic and socioeconomic disparities, suggesting that resource allocation often mirrors regional economic inequalities unless actively mitigated through policy interventions [9]. Collectively, these frameworks underscore the multifactorial nature of PHHR inequities.

Empirical evidence on PHHR allocation, both in China and globally, has grown significantly in recent years. In China, studies have shown that regional disparities are closely linked to population density, governmental health expenditures, local disease burden, and urbanization levels [3, 10–13]. For example, a 2019 nationwide decomposition analysis of county-level CDCs revealed that inequalities in public health workforce distribution were predominantly concentrated within counties and provinces, with factors such as infrastructure per employee and local gross domestic product (GDP) exerting significant influence [12]. Longitudinal analyses spanning 1985–2011 further indicated that reforms in urbanization, education, and employment substantially shaped the uneven distribution of health workers between urban and rural areas [14]. Internationally, evidence from sub-Saharan Africa highlights that inequitable workforce allocation is exacerbated by limited training capacity, weak governance, high migration of health personnel, and inadequate retention mechanisms [15]. Despite contextual differences, these findings suggest that the structural determinants of PHHR inequities are broadly consistent across settings.

However, most existing studies rely heavily on static inequality metrics, such as the Gini coefficient (Gini) and Theil index, which offer limited insight into the underlying mechanisms driving inequities. Few studies have employed longitudinal approaches or examined the dynamic interactions among socioeconomic policies, public health emergencies, and workforce distribution [16–18]. The COVID-19 pandemic further exposed structural vulnerabilities in China’s public health workforce, particularly in under-resourced regions where personnel shortages compromised emergency response capacity [19]. Although policy measures (e.g., targeted investments and talent exchange programs) have been implemented to promote a more equitable distribution of PHHR, systematic evaluations of their effectiveness remain scarce.

This study addresses these gaps by applying a multidimensional framework to assess the equity of PHHR allocation across China’s 31 provinces (Beijing, Tianjin, Hebei, Shanxi, Inner Mongolia, Liaoning, Jilin, Heilongjiang, Shanghai, Jiangsu, Zhejiang, Anhui, Fujian, Jiangxi, Shandong, Henan, Hubei, Hunan, Guangdong, Guangxi, Hainan, Chongqing, Sichuan, Guizhou, Yunnan, Tibet, Shaanxi, Gansu, Qinghai, Ningxia, and Xinjiang) from 2018 to 2022. We employ advanced methodologies, including the recentered influence function-index-ordinary least squares (RIF-I-OLS) approach [14], alongside complementary inequality indices, including Gini, concentration index (CI), absolute Gini (AGini), and absolute concentration index (ACI), to quantify the contributions of key determinants such as governmental health expenditure, population size, and disease burden. The RIF-I-OLS method transforms the inequality indices into interpretable micro-level variables using the “Recentered Influence Function (RIF)” and then estimates the marginal effect of each factor via ordinary least squares (OLS), thereby disaggregating the contribution of each determinant to overall inequality [20]. This approach outperforms traditional descriptive metrics by elucidating the drivers of inequity in contexts where economic, demographic, and policy factors interact. When combined with AGini and ACI, the RIF-I-OLS framework enables the simultaneous analysis of relative inequality (differences in resource shares) and absolute inequality (disparities in PHHR numbers per 10,000 population), a dual perspective critical for addressing workforce shortages in less developed regions, which manifest not only as relative underrepresentation but also as absolute deficits (e.g., 50 fewer PHHR per 10,000 population compared to eastern provinces), often overlooked in conventional studies. By integrating these methodological enhancements, the RIF-I-OLS approach provides comprehensive insights, addressing not only the question “how unequal is PHHR distribution in China?” but also “which factors drive these inequalities?”.

Methods

Variables

Annual data were obtained from official publications, including the China Health Statistics Yearbook (2018–2023) [21–26], the China Statistical Yearbook (2018–2023) [27–31]. Provincial geographic area data were derived from annual statistical administrative division information released by the Ministry of Civil Affairs of the People’s Republic of China [31]. Standardized disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) due to non-communicable chronic diseases (DALY-NCDs) were extracted from published literature [32]. All datasets were integrated at the provincial level for subsequent analyses.

Definition of PHHR

This study focuses on health personnel employed in professional public health institutions (PPHI), which are official or formal organizations tasked with public health responsibilities and performing critical functions for population health maintenance and promotion. These institutions include CDCs, specialized disease control organizations, maternal and child health care facilities, health education institutions, emergency centers (stations), blood collection and supply centers, health supervision agencies, and family planning technical service institutions at all levels. Health personnel in this study include licensed (assistant) physicians, registered nurses, pharmacists, other technicians, administrative staff, and grassroots technicians employed by these institutions.

Variable selection

Consistent with prior research, the primary outcome for assessing PHHR allocation equity was the number of public health personnel. To evaluate the spatial aggregation of resources, explanatory variables were categorized into two dimensions: (1) public health service needs: population, area of regions, life expectancy (LE), morbidity and mortality of class A (including plague and cholera) and class B (including pulmonary tuberculosis, schistosomiasis, filariasis, echinococcosis, leprosy, influenza, epidemic parotitis, rubella, neonatal tetanus, acute hemorrhagic conjunctivitis, and infectious diarrhea other than cholera, dysentery, typhoid and paratyphoid) infectious diseases (IDs), DALY-NCDs, and number of PPHI; (2) socioeconomic development and resource level: GDP per capita, illiteracy rate among the population aged ≥ 15 years, urban population proportion, and governmental health expenditure. These variables were selected based on prior empirical studies and public health frameworks that highlight the importance of socioeconomic, demographic, and health system-related factors in shaping health outcomes and disease burden across regions [33–37]. GDP per capita and government health expenditure are widely recognized as determinants of regional health disparities and health service coverage [33, 34]. Urban population proportion and LE reflect population distribution and overall health status, which influence exposure to and response to infectious diseases [35]. The number of public health personnel and institutions represents health system capacity, essential for disease prevention and control [36]. The adult illiteracy rate correlates with health knowledge and behaviors, which impact disease incidence [37].

Evaluation method

Two-way ANOVA for temporal variations

To examine temporal differences in key indicators, a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted. In this model, “year” was specified as the independent variable to capture temporal variation, while “province” was treated as a control variable to account for fixed regional characteristics. The two-way ANOVA was applied to each continuous variable of interest (e.g., GDP per capita, government health expenditure) to assess the main effects of the year. When significant differences were identified, Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) test was performed for pairwise comparisons to determine specific differences in mean values across years. The analyzed indicators included the number of PHHR, population, area of regions, LE, incidence rate of class A and B IDs, mortality rate of class A and B IDs, DALY-NCDs, number of PPHI, GDP per capita, illiteracy rate among the population aged ≥ 15 years, urban population proportion, and governmental health expenditure. Violin plots were employed to visualize the distributional differences.

Inequality indices

Gini: quantifies income distribution equity and is commonly applied in health economics to assess fairness in health resource allocation, ranging from 0 (perfect equality) to 1 (perfect inequality) [38]. It is calculated using the cumulative percentage of the population and health resources across regions, with values categorized as follows: high equity (< 0.2), relative equity (0.2–0.3), moderate equity (0.3–0.4), relative inequity (0.4–0.5), and extreme inequity (> 0.5) [39–41].

AGini: reflects inequality by comparing individual differences in resource distribution. Derived from the Lorenz curve (plotting cumulative shares of resources against regions), it is calculated as the ratio of the area between the Lorenz curve and the 45° line of perfect equality [42]. Since it uses the 45° line (representing absolute equity) as a benchmark, it is also referred to as “the absolute Gini”.

CI: evaluates the distribution of health resources relative to economic status, ranging from −1 (perfect pro-poor inequality) to +1 (perfect pro-rich inequality). A positive CI indicates concentration in wealthier regions, while a negative CI suggests concentration in poorer regions [43, 44].

ACI: incorporates the mean value of the indicator combining the dual sensitivities of relative ranking and absolute volume changes. It reflects distributional equity while capturing changes in total resource volume. Unlike CI (which remains constant when individual resource levels change), ACI adjusts for such changes, providing a more comprehensive assessment of health resource allocation [45, 46].

RIF-I-OLS analysis

Correlation and multicollinearity tests: firstly, a correlation analysis was conducted with the number of PHHR as the dependent variable and the aforementioned explanatory variables as independent variables to identify significantly correlated variables. A variance inflation factor (VIF) test was then performed to avoid multicollinearity. Variables with VIF > 5 were excluded: population was removed (VIF = 9.91) to ensure stable regression coefficients, while all remaining variables had VIF values below 5.

RIF-I-OLS regression: four regression models (using Gini, AGini, CI, and ACI as dependent variables) were constructed to examine the relationship between PHHR inequality and independent variables (e.g., area of regions, mortality rate of class A and B IDs, DALY-NCDs, number of PPHI, illiteracy rate among the population aged ≥ 15 years, governmental health expenditure). These models comprehensively assessed multidimensional PHHR distribution inequality. Gini quantified overall inequality; AGini refined the contribution of each independent variable; CI indicated PHHR concentration across population groups; and ACI adjusted for health needs to identify unexplained disparities. This integrated approach elucidated key influences on PHHR allocation, guiding policy to reduce disparities and enhance equity.

Decomposition of inequality indices: using the COVID-19 outbreak in 2020 as the dividing line, we decomposed changes in PHHR allocation equity during the pre-pandemic period (2018–2019) and the post-pandemic period (2021–2022). The objective is to compare and elucidate the evolving patterns and determinants of PHHR distribution before and after the pandemic.

The decomposition of inequality indices was conducted by incorporating the relevant explanatory variables into regression models to quantify their relationships with observed disparities. This decomposition produces two main components: the coefficient effect (comprising the pure coefficient effect and weight adjustment error) and the characteristic effect (comprising the pure characteristic effect and model specification error) [47, 48]. The characteristic effect represents the portion of inequality attributable to differences in observed explanatory variables, such as governmental health expenditure. In contrast, the coefficient effect captures the contribution of unobserved or unexplainable factors, including systemic biases, resource allocation preferences, and variations in local policy implementation.

In practical terms, the inequality in PHHR distribution is partitioned into two segments: the explainable component and the unexplainable component. The explainable component reflects the portion of differences in PHHR allocation across years that can be attributed to variations in observed characteristics or endowments. Conversely, the unexplainable component represents differences arising from unobserved factors, such as divergent policy responses or institutional arrangements.

Building on this framework, we applied the Oaxaca–Blinder decomposition to evaluate the extent to which temporal changes in PHHR equity could be attributed to variations in observable structural characteristics, including the number of public health institutions, governmental health expenditure, and adult illiteracy rates. This analysis emphasizes the contribution of measurable structural determinants to changes in distributional equity, thereby providing insight into the drivers of PHHR inequality over time.

Results

Inequality indices of PHHR allocation in China (2018–2022)

The Gini and CI, key indicators of PHHR inequity, displayed temporal fluctuations between 2018 and 2022 (Table 1). From 2018 to 2019, the Gini increased from 0.3792 to 0.3844, indicating a decline in PHHR allocation equity. Concurrently, the CI rose from 0.0215 to 0.0495, signaling a growing concentration of PHHR in economically developed regions. From 2019 to 2021, however, the Gini declined from 0.3844 to 0.3720, and the CI gradually decreased from 0.0495 to 0.0333, indicating improved equity. By 2022, the Gini declined slightly further to 0.3715, and the CI dropped to 0.0279, demonstrating continued progress in the equity of PHHR allocation and a further reduction in resource imbalances. During 2018–2019, inequities in PHHR distribution were predominantly driven by the characteristic effect, whose contribution substantially exceeded that of the coefficient effect. This pattern indicates that regional disparities in observable factors, such as governmental health expenditure and the number of PPHI, were the primary determinants of inequality during the pre-pandemic period.

By contrast, in 2021–2022, the contribution of the characteristic effect declined markedly. Decomposition analyses of the Gini, CI, and ACI revealed that the coefficient effect had become the dominant source of inequality, surpassing the characteristic effect. Discussion

This study systematically assessed the equity of PHHR allocation across China from 2018 to 2022 and identified the key determinants of this equity, addressing gaps in prior research by employing longitudinal data and multidimensional analytical methods. Results from the two-way ANOVA revealed no significant changes in China’s total population, mortality rates of class A and B IDs, DALY-NCDs, or area of regions. This stability points to a relatively balanced population structure, with fertility policies and natural demographic shifts remaining largely aligned, and low birth rates offset by similarly low mortality rates. The steady trends in mortality and DALY-NCDs further underscore the ongoing effectiveness of China’s disease prevention and control measures, indicating that healthcare service coverage and system capacity remained largely uninterrupted during this period. Similarly, the unchanged geographic area (including both administrative boundaries and habitable regions) highlights sustained economic development without large-scale population displacement or regional deprivation.

In contrast, several public health and socioeconomic indicators in China exhibited notable changes between 2018 and 2022. The simultaneous growth in professional public health personnel and decrease in the number of PPHI signals a strategic shift toward resource consolidation and efficiency enhancement [50]. This trend reflects national policies prioritizing the quality of service delivery over mere expansion of institutional infrastructure. Concurrent improvements in LE, alongside reductions in the incidence of class A and B IDs, further underscore the strengthening of public health systems and the effectiveness of targeted disease prevention strategies.

Economic development also exerted a substantial influence on public health outcomes. Rising GDP per capita, expanded governmental health expenditure, and ongoing urbanization were positively associated with enhancements in public health infrastructure and workforce capacity. Nevertheless, inequities in PHHR distribution persisted. Although the Gini for PHHR allocation declined slightly (stabilizing between 0.3 and 0.4, indicating modest progress toward equity), the CI rose steadily over the same period. This pattern confirms that PHHR remained disproportionately concentrated in economically advanced regions, consistent with prior observations by Yin et al. [51] and Anand [52]. Such disparities suggest that economic growth alone does not guarantee equitable resource distribution; targeted policies are needed to redirect resources to underserved areas.

Bivariate correlation analyses (2018–2022) uncovered systematic associations between PHHR and regional socioeconomic status, health investment, and disease burden. First, PHHR was strongly correlated with population (r = 0.937, P < 0.01), governmental health expenditure (r = 0.863, P < 0.01), and the number of PPHI (r = 0.745, P < 0.01). These findings suggest that regions with larger populations and greater economic capacity are more successful in attracting and retaining health personnel. This aligns with the “resource absorption capacity hypothesis” proposed by Yip et al. [53], which argues that regions with larger populations and stronger fiscal capacity are more effective at attracting and retaining health personnel, thereby potentially exacerbating pre-existing inequalities by concentrating resources in already advantaged areas.

Second, PHHR was negatively correlated with the illiteracy rate among the population aged ≥ 15 years (r = − 0.270, P < 0.01) and DALY-NCDs (r = − 0.302, P < 0.01). Illiteracy reflects regional educational attainment, and its inverse association with PHHR indicates that areas with lower education levels face challenges in recruiting and retaining health workers [54]. Similarly, regions with higher NCD burdens tend to have fewer health personnel, illustrating a “high demand-low resource” mismatch. This finding is consistent with Ashraful et al. [55], who reported that inadequate human resources substantially constrain NCD service delivery. Collectively, these results underscore that both inadequate PHHR supply and structural imbalances in PHHR allocation persist.

Notably, a weak positive correlation was observed between PHHR and mortality from class A and B IDs (r = 0.190, p = 0.018), which may reflect the emergency deployment of personnel and targeted policy interventions in high-burden regions during public health crises [56]. Similarly, LE was positively, though marginally, correlated with PHHR (r = 0.145, p = 0.072), suggesting that the relationship between health outcomes and workforce distribution is confounded by other factors (e.g., disease control quality) and requires further investigation with stricter covariate control. In contrast, regional land area was negatively associated with PHHR (r = − 0.218, P < 0.01), highlighting that geographic accessibility remains a critical barrier to equitable workforce distribution, particularly in remote and sparsely populated areas. This observation is consistent with Bai et al. [57], who reported that inequalities in workforce allocation are more pronounced when measured by land area than by population size, which underscores that merely increasing the total number of health workers without optimizing spatial distribution is insufficient to achieve substantive equity.

Using the RIF-I-OLS decomposition method and after controlling for multicollinearity, this study systematically examined the key determinants of inequality in PHHR allocation. The results revealed substantial heterogeneity in both the direction and magnitude of associations across different inequality indices, reflecting structural characteristics of PHHR distribution in China as well as regional policy response patterns.

Regression analyses of the CI indicated that adult illiteracy was negatively associated with CI. This finding aligns with Chen et al. [58], who reported positive spatial autocorrelation between medical education and health workforce allocation, characterized by “high-high” and “low-low” clustering. Similarly, LE correlated negatively with CI, underscoring the fundamental role of improved population health in fostering more equitable resource distribution, consistent with empirical observations in central and western China by Wang et al. [59]. In contrast, governmental health expenditure was positively associated with CI, suggesting that fiscal inputs are disproportionately directed toward economically developed regions. This result is consistent with the perspective of Yip et al. [53], who argued that government health subsidies in China have predominantly benefited higher-income areas.

From the perspective of the Gini, the number of PPHI was negatively associated with inequality, indicating that the expansion of institutional networks enhances service accessibility, particularly in resource-constrained regions. This mechanism aligns with Anand’s assertion that equitable infrastructure distribution is a prerequisite for fair allocation of health resources [60]. Additionally, mortality from category A and B IDs was negatively associated with the Gini, suggesting that high disease burden may trigger emergency responses and resource reallocation, thereby directing additional workforce support to high-burden regions. This observation is consistent with Meng et al. [61], who emphasized the role of emergency-driven workforce redistribution during public health crises.

It is also noteworthy that the effects of different factors varied across inequality measures. While governmental health expenditure was associated with increased inequality as reflected by the CI, its relationship with the overall Gini was not statistically significant. This suggests that although fiscal investment has expanded the total volume of health resources, it has not effectively reduced relative disparities across regions. This finding supports Zhao et al. [62], who highlighted the trade-off between efficiency and equity in China’s health expenditure.

Decomposition of the RIF regression further revealed stage-specific shifts in the drivers of PHHR equity between 2018 and 2022. The outbreak of the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020 represented a critical historical juncture, exerting a profound influence on the mechanisms of workforce distribution.

During the 2018–2019 period, inequities in PHHR allocation were primarily driven by the characteristic effect, reflecting inter-regional differences in observable variables such as governmental health expenditure and the number of PPHI. This observation aligns with prior studies indicating that disparities in initial resource endowments substantially contribute to inequities in health resource allocation [63, 64]. For instance, Dong et al. reported that in Shanghai, both the volume and growth rate of health resources were consistently higher in central urban districts than in suburban areas, and the observed downward trend in inequality was largely shaped by differences in initial resource distribution [65].

The outbreak of the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020 substantially altered the logic and priorities of PHHR allocation. During 2021–2022, the coefficient effect surpassed the characteristic effect as the dominant source of inequality. This shift indicates that in the post-pandemic period, unobservable structural and institutional factors, such as emergency response capacity, policy implementation efficiency, and institutional flexibility, exerted an increasing influence on resource allocation outcomes. The pandemic functioned as a comprehensive stress test, revealing pronounced disparities in regions’ abilities to mobilize resources, deploy personnel, and coordinate organizations during major public health emergencies. Even with comparable levels of resource investment, variations in institutional responsiveness led to significant differences in PHHR allocation efficiency, thereby amplifying substantive inequities [66–69].

Furthermore, individual variable contributions lack significance, even though the overall explained effect is significant. This situation suggests that inequality is driven by the combined influence of multiple factors rather than any single dominant variable. This finding underscores the utility of RIF decomposition in uncovering latent, aggregate dynamics that are not readily apparent through conventional regression analyses.

From a policy perspective, our results reaffirm the importance of governmental health investment and institutional coverage, such as PPHI density, as effective levers for reducing inequality. The observed post-pandemic shift toward coefficient effects emphasizes the need for flexible and responsive allocation strategies that can adapt to evolving public health challenges. These findings are consistent with prior research highlighting the pandemic’s transformative impact on public resource distribution [68, 70]. Importantly, our study provides novel evidence that the pandemic not only intensified service demand, but also restructured the fundamental relationships between demographic factors and workforce deployment, suggesting a potential long-term shift in China’s public health planning paradigm.

Limitations and future directions

The analysis primarily relies on aggregated data at the provincial level, which may mask important intra-provincial disparities in resource distribution, particularly between urban and rural areas. Future studies using more granular, county- or township-level data could provide a more precise understanding of local inequities.

Key factors influencing PHHR equity were not included due to data unavailability, including: (1) PHHR quality dimensions (e.g., professional background, technical skills, and training experience), as equivalent PHHR numbers may translate to vastly different service capacities if personnel skill levels vary; (2) workforce mobility dynamics (e.g., temporary inter-regional deployment during emergencies or long-term brain drain from rural to urban areas), which may have distorted the observed “allocation equity” in cross-sectional analyses; (3) institutional governance quality (e.g., local CDC management efficiency and policy implementation fidelity), which could moderate the relationship between resource input and equitable distribution.

While the Gini, CI, and ACI offer robust quantitative measures of distributional inequality, they do not fully capture need-based equity (e.g., whether regions with higher disease burden or aging populations receive proportionally more PHHR) or outcome-based equity (e.g., whether PHHR allocation correlates with improved health outcomes like reduced infectious disease mortality). Incorporating need-adjusted metrics (e.g., PHHR-to-DALY ratio) or health outcome indicators could enhance the comprehensiveness of equity evaluations.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that while China’s PHHR allocation equity showed modest, non-uniform improvement between 2018 and 2022 as a result of policy interventions and crisis-induced resource adjustments, PHHR remained relatively concentrated in economically advanced regions, with regional disparities persisting to varying degrees. Structural factors, including population size, governmental health expenditure, and the number of public health institutions, were positively correlated with PHHR distribution in the observed sample, whereas areas with lower educational attainment and higher non-communicable disease burdens faced persistent resource shortages, reflecting a potential “high demand-low resource” mismatch in these specific contexts. The COVID-19 pandemic further highlighted the influence of institutional and systemic factors, with coefficient effects becoming more prominent than characteristic effects in 2021–2022 (the post-pandemic period in this study). Future policies may benefit from systemic, flexible, and responsive resource allocation strategies, as well as more granular data and qualitative indicators, to gradually improve the fairness and efficiency of public health workforce distribution.
