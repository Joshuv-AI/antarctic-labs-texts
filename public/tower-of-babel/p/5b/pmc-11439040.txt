Impact of artificial intelligence assistance on pulmonary nodule detection and localization in chest CT: a comparative study among radiologists of varying experience levels
Authors: Alan Arthur Peters, Nina Wiescholek, Martin Müller, Jeremias Klaus, Felix Strodka, Ana Macek, Elias Primetis, Dionysios Drakopulos, Adrian Thomas Huber, Verena Carola Obmann, Thomas Daniel Ruder, Justus Erasmus Roos, Johannes Thomas Heverhagen, Andreas Christe, Lukas Ebner
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The study aimed to evaluate the impact of AI assistance on pulmonary nodule detection rates among radiology residents and senior radiologists, along with assessing the effectiveness of two different commercialy available AI software systems in improving detection rates and LungRADS classification in chest CT. The study cohort included 198 participants with 221 pulmonary nodules. Residents’ mean detection rate increased significantly from 64 to 77% with AI assist, while seniors’ detection rate remained largely unchanged (85% vs. 86%). Residents showed significant improvement in segmental nodule localization with AI assistance, seniors did not. Software 2 slightly outperformed software 1 in increasing detection rates (67–77% vs. 80–86%), but neither significantly affected LungRADS classification. The study suggests that clinical experience mitigates the need for additional AI software, with the combination of CAD with residents being the most beneficial approach. Both software systems performed similarly, with software 2 showing a slightly higher but non-significant increase in detection rates.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-73435-3.

Introduction

Lung cancer still is one of the cancers with the highest incidence and remains the leading cause of cancer related deaths worldwide1. In 2018, the Global Cancer Observatory (GLOBOCAN) estimated that lung cancer globally accounted for more than 2 million new diagnoses and more than 1.7 million deaths2. Additionally, incidence of lung cancer are estimated to further increase within the next decade3. Therefore, lung cancer will remain a highly significant clinical issue in the future.

Screening with low-dose CT can detect lung cancer at an early stage and has been shown to significantly reduce cancer-related mortality in a high-risk group4. Critical for the effectiveness of the screening is the identification and correct characterization of pulmonary nodules. Especially the detection of small and subsolid nodules remains a challenge for radiologists5,6. In this context, a report based on the International Early Lung Cancer Action Program (I-ELCAP) study showed that in 75% of confirmed cancer patients, the corresponding small nodules were retrospectively detectable in the previous CT scan7.

According to a study of Torres et al., up to 35% of small nodules were missed in a single read by a radiologist8. These findings are pertinent, especially in view of the large number of lung cancer screening programs, which result in an increasing number of chest CT scans for radiologists to read.

One solution to tackle this problem are computer assisted diagnosis (CAD) systems. In the late 1980s, the first CAD system was developed to assist radiologists in pulmonary nodule detection9. To date, various studies have shown that the utilization of CAD systems, especially when used as second reader devices, can significantly improve nodule detection rates and they have since been endorsed by the European Society of Thoracic Imaging (ESTI)10–12. In recent years, new technologies such as deep-learning (DL)- based CAD have come to the focus of research. In contrast to conventional CAD, DL-based CAD learn autonomously13. DL-based CAD have even been shown to be superior regarding detection rates and pulmonary nodule volumetry compared to conventional machine learning based CAD14,15. There even have been attempts to improve the prediction of malignancy risk of newly detected pulmonary nodules by DL-based CAD systems16,17.

One of the main limitations of DL-CAD systems is their tendency to yield higher rates of false positive results than human readers9,18.

While there is a growing body of literature on DL-CAD systems for nodule detection, there is a limited number of large cohort studies comparing the potential to improve the performance of radiologists18,19.

The aims of this study were (1) to evaluate the performance of two different DL-based CAD systems for lung nodule detection and classification using a large clinical cohort in a second reader setup and (2) to assess the impact on readers with different levels of experience.

Materials/methods

This study was approved by the local ethics committee and conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (including the later amendments). All included participants provided written informed consent.

Primary cohort

This retrospective exploratory study consisted of 205 eligible subjects with 228 index nodules (subsolid, n = 66; solid, n = 162).

This cohort consisted of 122 patients provided by the local lung cancer center containing patients with histologically proven tumor stage 1 (T1) tumors of the lung. As a tertiary care lung cancer center, the main institution serves as a local reference center; thus retrieving CT scans from various institutions.

The included CT scans were acquired at 20 different institutions in the period from December 2010 to December 2020.

To expand the study cohort to a sufficient size, we reviewed chest CT scans acquired over the last two years at the local institution. Given the underrepresentation of micronodules < 6 mm and subsolid nodules, the cohort was extended by 53 patients with a total of 68 nodules (subsolid, n = 17; solid < 6 mm, n = 51). For cases with pulmonary nodules, the earliest CT scan depicting the nodule was selected. The CT scans were evenly distributed across Lung imaging reporting and data system (LungRADS) scores, with 53% of nodules classified as LungRADS 2/3 and 47% as LungRADS 4 A/4B. Here, the minimal nodule size was 3 mm.

Finally, 30 patients without any pulmonary nodules were included as a control group.

Inclusion criteria were age > 18 years, signed general consent and an available chest CT scan. In cases with pulmonary tumors, the first available CT scan depicting the nodule was selected. Exclusion criteria were missing general consent and technical problems with the AI analysis.

CT scan protocol

The CT examinations from the Lung Cancer Center originated from over 20 different sites all over the country with 4 different CT vendors (Siemens, n = 127; Philips, n = 34; GE, n = 18; Toshiba, n = 26). All examinations were performed with standard settings, reconstruction parameters were in accordance with the national parameters for chest CT, with dose values remaining within the respective National Diagnostic Reference Levels20.

The acquired minimum slice thicknesses varied from 0.5 to 4 mm, with 94% of all examinations having a minimum slice thickness of ≤ 2 mm and 70% of all examinations ≤ 1 mm. Reconstruction algorithms contained filtered-back projection as well as iterative reconstruction (IR). Scan volumes varied from whole-body examinations to chest-only acquisitions based on clinical symptoms and indication. Out of the 205 CT scans, 137 (67%) were contrast enhanced. The settings of the institutional CT scanner, on which 62% of all scans were performed, can be found in the supplements.

DL-based CAD systems

Software 1 is an advanced commercially available machine learning software capable of lung segmentation, nodule size and density measurement. It isolates pulmonary nodules from vessels, bronchi and fissures (ClearRead-CT, Riverain Technologies, Miamisburg, Ohio, USA). By subtraction of those structures, it produces a new CT dataset highlighting pulmonary consolidations and nodules, the so-called vessel suppressed-CT.

Software 2 is a fully automated artificial intelligence (AI) convolutional neural network (CNN), which resembles a multi-layered machine learning algorithm containing multiple hidden layers and sequential output patterns (AI-Rad Companion, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany).

Both systems have been broadly validated in several studies21–24.

Readout

Five radiologists with different levels of experience (two board certified, subspecialized chest radiologists with 15 and seven years of experience and three trainees with three, two, and one, years of experience, respectively) evaluated all CT scans independently and blinded to the number of nodules. Two readers used the results provided by software 1 and three readers used the results of software 2. The readout was performed on dedicated Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS)-workstations (Sectra PACS IDS7, Sectra) using dedicated monitors (BARCO Coronis Fusion 6MP LED). The software results were displayed as additional series in the PACS.

The readout was performed in two steps: first, each rater independently read all CT scans and document number, size group (categories based on LungRADS classification), density group (solid, part-solid, ground-glass) and location (side and segment) of all pulmonary nodules on a standardized readout form. After a one-month hiatus, each rater performed a second – AI aided – read-out of the CT scans with an overlay of the AI findings in the DICOM data. Readers were not allowed to alter the results of the stand-alone readout.

Two board certified radiologists with eight and fifteen years of experience in chest radiology defined the standard of reference independently and, in case of differences in a consensus rating, with access to the results of the CAD systems. The LungRADS category of each nodule was calculated based on its size and density following LungRADS v202225.

The nodule detection rate represents the proportion of correctly identified nodules on scan-level. A nodule was considered correctly detected if the reported lobe and density matched the actual nodule characteristics; otherwise, the reported finding was classified as a false positive.

Statistics

All statistical analyses apart from the multilevel mixed-effects regression (see below) were performed using SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (IBM Corp. 2020) and GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, Inc., version 8). Continuous parameters are reported as mean and standard deviation (SD) and categorical variables as absolute frequencies with relative percentages. The impact of the use of AI software at different experience levels was performed by pairwise comparisons using McNemar tests. Fleiss kappa was used to assess for interreader agreement. Kappa values of 0 to 0.20 were interpreted as slight, 0.21 to 0.40 as fair, 0.41 to 0.60 as moderate and 0.61 to 0.80 as substantial, while values between 0.81 and 1.0 were interpreted as excellent agreement based on the publication by Landis and Koch26.

As an additional sensitivity analysis, a multilevel mixed effects logistic regression model was performed to assess the interaction of AI software use and experience regarding the detection rate. A detailed description of the model is provided in the supplements.

Results

Final cohort

From the eligible subjects of the primary cohort, seven were not analyzed by the software due to technical reasons such as missing CT slices in the acquired image stack and were excluded from further analysis (Fig. 1). The final cohort consisted of 198 participants with 221 pulmonary nodules. The mean age (SD) of the final cohort was 63.1 (12.8) years, 40% of the subjects were females (n = 79, Table 1).

Comparison of the DL-based CAD systems

In order to compare the software performance, a pooled analysis of the respective readers was performed regarding detection rate and correct LungRADS classification for each software. Both systems led to a significant increase regarding the detection rates with software 2 leading to a slightly higher increase (software 1, 80.3–85.9% and software 2, 67.1–76.9%; both p < 0.001) and showed no significant effect on the correct LungRADS classification (software 1, 68.4% vs. 69.4%; p = 0.754 and software 2, 60.4% vs. 57.3%; p = 0.190). Regarding the segmental localization accuracy of the nodules, both systems led to a significant increase (software 1, 70.7–78.3%; software 2, 71.3–78.4%; both p < 0.001). However, there was no significant effect on correct size or attenuation categorization for both systems (all p > 0.05).

The false positive rates (FPR) were not significantly affected by the CAD systems (Table 2).

Experience dependent analysis

Across all readers and systems there was fair agreement regarding pulmonary nodule detection for the stand-alone readouts (Κ = 0.29) and it remained fair for the AI assisted readouts (Κ = 0.23).

The individual detection rates of each reader are depicted in Fig. 4.

In the experience dependent analysis the residents’ mean detection rate increased significantly after using the respective software as a second reader device (64% vs. 77%; p < 0.001, Fig. 4; Table 3). The senior radiologists’ mean detection rate did not improve by using the software (85% vs. 86%; p = 0.25).

Regarding the correct localization of the nodules, the rates of the residents for segmental localization improved significantly (64% vs. 68%; p < 0.001). The senior radiologists on the other hand showed no significant benefit by using the software (78% vs. 80%; p = 0.243).

Categorization of nodule size and attenuation were not affected by using the systems for any of the readers (Table 3).

Consecutively, the correct LungRADS categorization was not affected by using the software for most readers. The only exception was reader 2, who significantly improved from 76 to 82% (p = 0.019).

The results of the multilevel mixed effects logistic regression model confirmed the achieved results by showing a significant impact of AI assistance for residents, but no impact on the senior readers (Fig. S1).

Discussion

The current study aimed to compare two different DL-based CAD systems regarding pulmonary nodule detection and classification. Both systems exhibited comparable performance; however, software 2 demonstrated a slightly higher increase in detection rates compared to software 1.

Nonetheless, neither system showed a significant effect on the LungRADS classification of nodules, suggesting no consequent impact on patient management.

This finding contrasts with previous studies; for instance, Yuan et al. reported that CAD systems influenced patient management, attributing this mainly to the detection of different nodules compared to the radiologists27. The discrepancy between the current study and previous research could be explained by the absence of a second reader setup in the mentioned study, contrasting with the current setup. A recent study by Park et al. reported that the majority of LungRADS categories remained unchanged between a reading session with and without CAD systems, aligning with the present results28.

A possible explanation for the difference between the systems might be that the senior radiologist, who used software 1, had the highest detection rate among all while software 2 was used by two residents instead of only one using software 1.

The experience-based analysis indicated that particularly inexperienced readers can benefit from DL-based CAD systems in terms of detection and localization of pulmonary nodules.

This finding is generally consistent with the literature, as various studies have shown the advantage of a second reading by CAD systems29,30. A recently published study by Wataya et al. showed that particularly young radiologists with an experience level of less than five years benefited from using a DL-based CAD system regarding characterization and diagnosis of pulmonary nodules analyzing a cohort consisting of 101 nodules31. In contrast to the current study, the authors evaluated specific image characteristics such as tumor margin, calcification or pleural contact, which were automatically generated by the used CAD system.

Both systems led to a more precise nodule localization of the three residents but had no significant effect on the senior radiologists in this regard. This could be attributed to the cohort including patients with extensive alterations of lung parenchyma by background pathologies such as small airway disease, emphysema, fibrosis with architectural distortion and several postoperative cases, in which the correct localization of nodules requires experience and practice.

Size and attenuation of the nodules were not significantly affected by the DL CAD systems; however, the rates of the correct size and density categories were relatively low throughout the readout. This can be explained by approximately one-third of the nodules being micronodules < 6 mm, where minor volumetric differences can lead to alteration of the size category and precise density measurements are more challenging. In the study by Li et al., the authors reported a higher sensitivity (90.3%) and specificity (100%) for a DL-CAD system regarding the correct density classification of solid pulmonary nodules9. However, they excluded nodules smaller than 5 mm from their analysis, which are among the most challenging ones regarding precise nodule volumetry15.

One of the major concerns regarding the use of DL-CAD systems in daily clinical practice is the high FPR. In the current study, the FPR was mainly dependent on the human reader and were not significantly influenced by either of the CAD systems. The rates of 1.2 (software 1) and 0.6 (software 2) are comparable to the rate of 1.5 reported by Li et al. and in the range between 0.3 and 15 per scan reported by Al Mohammad et al. in a recent review on the role of CAD in the lung cancer screening context9,32.

This study has limitations. First, the number of readers was not balanced between the two CAD systems. Initially, there were three readers planned for each CAD system, but due to technical problems with displaying the CAD results on an external site, the third reader of software 1 was unable to perform the readout correctly. Second, the used cohort comprises a variety of CT scanner vendors, scan protocols and reconstruction settings, which made the readout very challenging for both human readers and software. Nevertheless, this diversity could also be considered a strength, as the results are expected to be robust and generalizable. Third, the cohort used in this study is not a screening cohort but consists of patients with incidentalomas. As a result, these findings should be interpreted with caution and should not be directly extrapolated to screening cohorts. Fourth, the control group size was relatively small compared to the overall cohort. This was due to the inclusion of micronodules as positive findings in the study, which made it challenging to find CT scans without (micro-) nodules33. Fifth, the relatively small number of readers per software contributed to less robust results. Recruitment was challenging, as readers had to perform the readouts alongside their daily clinical responsibilities. Nonetheless, several studies in this field have used a comparable number of readers23,28. Furthermore, the raters of the two CAD systems were not identical, limiting the software comparison. Sixth, the reference standard was partly based on the results from the CAD systems, which may have introduced bias by influencing the expert radiologists in favor of CAD-detectable nodules.

In conclusion, this study showed a comparable performance of the two DL-based CAD systems and demonstrated that inexperienced readers, in particular, can benefit from AI assistance by increasing their detection rates and accurately localizing pulmonary nodules.
