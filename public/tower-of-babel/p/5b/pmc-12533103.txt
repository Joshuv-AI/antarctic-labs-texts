Stability analysis of a tunnel face reinforced with bolts considering the intermediate principal stress component
Authors: Kai Xu, Xiang Chen, Gaofeng Luo, Xuebing Hu, Yong Li, Feng Dai, Junchao Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The stability of the tunnel face during excavation is a critical issue in tunnel construction. Traditional methods have offered various approaches for analyzing tunnel face stability but often neglect complex factors such as the intermediate principal stress. The mechanical behavior of rocks, however, is strongly influenced by the intermediate principal stress. This paper investigates the effectiveness of bolt reinforcement and pre-grouting techniques in enhancing tunnel face stability. A comprehensive 3D model is developed to incorporate the intermediate principal stress with Mogi Coulomb criterion to assess the impact of these reinforcement methods. Our findings suggest that the critical bolt reinforcement length ranges from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:0.3\text{D}$$\end{document} (tunnel diameter) to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:1.0\text{D}$$\end{document}, significantly reducing extrusion deformation and enhancing tunnel stability. Pre-grouting improves the cohesion and strength of the surrounding rock, effectively preventing seepage and collapse. The safety factor obtained from the model calculations shows that SF increases with the increase of the intermediate principal stress coefficient, reaching its maximum value when the coefficient is 0.5, and then decreases. The reliability and practical applicability of our proposed model are validated through a comparative analysis with classical models. This research could provide essential guidelines for optimizing tunnel face reinforcement strategies, ensuring safer and more efficient tunneling operations.

Introduction

Maintaining the stability of the tunnel face during excavation is a fundamental requirement. Apart from the shield method, conventional tunneling techniques do not directly account for the supporting forces acting on the tunnel face. Therefore, it is necessary to preemptively reinforce the tunnel face to prevent instability.

Methods such as bolt reinforcement, pre-grouting, and pipe-roof support have been widely recognized for their practicality and effectiveness in stabilizing the tunnel face1. These methods have held significant practical value in construction. Researchers, both domestically and internationally, have extensively studied the mechanisms and design methods of bolt reinforcement and pre-grouting. Kamata and Mashimo2 studied the impact of bolts on tunnel face stability and identified a critical reinforcement length for the bolt rods. They found that when the bolt length exceeded \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:0.5\text{D}$$\end{document} (tunnel diameter), the stabilization effect on the tunnel face was optimal, but further increases in bolt length had a minimal effect on stability. Additionally, they determined that reinforcing the upper half of the tunnel face was more effective than reinforcing the lower half. Some scholars3,4 combined numerical simulations and experimental models to investigate the effects of tunnel depth, rock mass mechanical parameters, and bolt length, stiffness, and density on tunnel face stability. Their research demonstrated that the effectiveness of bolts depends on their length, stiffness, and density. The critical reinforcement length of bolts ranged from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:0.3\text{D}$$\end{document} to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:1.0\text{D}$$\end{document} and was associated with the friction angle of the surrounding rock, but not with the tunnel depth, bolt length, stiffness, or density.

Numerous studies5–8 have also demonstrated that pre-reinforcing the tunnel face with bolts could reduce the instability range and extrusion deformation. Studies9–18 showed that reinforcing the tunnel face with bolts, through the interaction between bolts and the surrounding rock, effectively reduced extrusion deformation, instability range, plastic zone extent, and surface settlement, thus significantly enhancing tunnel face stability. The effectiveness of bolts increased with their length, stiffness, and density up to a critical reinforcement length; beyond this length, additional length had a minimal effect on stability. This critical length was related to the mechanical properties of the surrounding rock. Perazzelli and Anagnostou19found that the mesh size of the rock around the “bolt” elements significantly affected the calculation results for the tunnel face, requiring careful selection. The effectiveness of bolt reinforcement was influenced by the cohesion of the surrounding rock, decreasing as cohesion increased. Paternesi et al20., using the finite element strength reduction method and limit equilibrium method on silty clay layers, demonstrated that bolt reinforcement effectively controlled extrusion deformation and surface settlement, and its effectiveness was similar to an increase in the cohesion of the surrounding rock. Horn21 introduced the “prism-wedge” model, which consisted of a wedge in front of the tunnel face and a prism above it. Based on Horn’s model, stability analysis models for tunnel faces reinforced with bolts was developed22,23. However, their computational accuracy was lower compared to numerical simulations. These models assumed a planar failure surface in front of the tunnel face, but numerical simulations indicated that the failure surface was more akin to a logarithmic spiral.

Pre-grouting reinforcement of the tunnel face enhanced the cohesion of the surrounding rock and effectively improved its strength and integrity. This approach aimed to reinforce the strata, prevent seepage, and avert collapse. In recent years, researchers have extensively discussed grouting diffusion theory and tunnel face reinforcement, conducting numerous model tests2,24–28 and numerical simulations29–34. These studies provided valuable guidance on the design of grouting parameters and the effectiveness of tunnel face reinforcement. However, current research mainly focused on the mechanical parameters of grouted reinforcement, with limited investigation into pre-grouting reinforcement and its effectiveness on tunnel faces.

Many scholars studied tunnel face stability using the Mohr-Coulomb or Hoek-Brown Yield criteria without considering the effects of intermediate principal stress. Incorporating this stress helps reflect the stress conditions of deep tunnels within the strata. To address this, we propose a 3D model that includes the impact of intermediate principal stress on the tunnel face. This model assesses the stability of the tunnel face under bolt reinforcement and pre-grouting conditions (Fig. 1). Additionally, we introduce a calculation model that combines the limit equilibrium method and strength reduction method to analyze the stability coefficient of the tunnel face. Comparing our results with classical models confirms the reliability of our proposed model. Its practicality is further validated through engineering case studies, accompanied by parameter analysis and discussions.

Outline

The traditional ‘prism-wedge’ model has been optimized to account for intermediate principal stresses on the tunnel face. The failure surface in front of the tunnel face has been enhanced from a plane to a logarithmic spiral. Additionally, the tunnel depth and the mechanical properties of the surrounding rock are taken into account when determining the instability height above the tunnel face. To simplify the derivation of theoretical equations, the failure region above the tunnel face is approximated from an ellipsoid to a prismatic model, while also considering the length of the unsupported span during tunneling.

Bolt reinforcement model

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{M}_{\text{a}}$$\end{document} is the bolt support moment at the tunnel face. Figure 6 presents the stability model of the tunnel face under bolt reinforcement. The anchoring system comprises three components: surrounding rock, grouting model, and bolt rods. There are five theoretical damage modes for the bolt rods: (1) Damage mode I, tensile failure of the bolt rods; (2) Damage mode II, shear failure at the interface between peripheral rock in the instability zone and the grouting; (3) Damage mode III, shear failure at the interface between peripheral rock in the stability zone and the grouting; (4) Damage mode IV, shear failure at the interface of the bolt rod in the instability zone and the grouting; and (5) Damage mode V, shear failure at the interface of the bolt rod in the stability zone and the grouting. However, due to the short effective anchorage length of tunnel face bolts and their typical use in weak surrounding rocks, damage modes IV and V are typically not considered. Therefore, this paper focuses on damage modes I, II, and III.

Discussion

This paper optimizes the classical “prism-wedge” model by incorporating the effects of intermediate principal stresses on tunnel face stability. The proposed model integrates a logarithmic spiral failure surface with the Mogi-Coulomb strength criterion. This model accounts for tunnel depth and the mechanical properties of the surrounding rock, affecting the height of the instability region above the tunnel face. It simplifies theoretical derivations by approximating the failure region as a prismatic model. The model also considers the length of the unsupported span during excavation. The study shows that pre-grouting reinforcement and bolt support greatly enhance tunnel face stability. Pre-grouting increases cohesion and shear strength at the tunnel face. Some comparisons with existing models reveal that when the pre-grouting range surpasses the instability range, the safety factor increases significantly. For bolt support, the total support moment is the sum of the moments from each bolt, showing that bolt length and density greatly affect stability. The model’s reliability is validated by comparing it with four other models. The safety factor increases with friction angle and normalized cohesion, with minimal errors compared to other models. The model’s accuracy is further validated by analyzing safety factor variations with different bolt lengths, widths, and surface loads.

Conclusion

This paper introduces an enhanced computational model for tunnel face stability assessment. By refining the classical “prism-wedge” model with a logarithmic spiral failure surface and the Mogi-Coulomb criterion, the model considers intermediate principal stresses. The proposed model’s reliability is confirmed by comparisons with four other models, showing consistent results across various friction angles and cohesion values, with minimal errors.
