Oxidative stress and metabolic dysfunction in oral lichen planus pathogenesis
Authors: Hyo-Jin Song, Kun-Hwa Kang, Jin-Seok Byun, Do-Yeon Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTOral lichen planus (OLP) is a chronic, immune-mediated inflammatory disease of the oral mucosa that can cause persistent pain and has a recognized, though relatively low, risk of malignant transformation. Recent studies have identified oxidative and nitrosative stress as key contributors to the pathophysiology of OLP. Excessive production of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species, combined with reduced antioxidant defenses, leads to cellular damage, including lipid peroxidation, protein modification, and DNA injury. This oxidative imbalance drives keratinocyte apoptosis and sustains inflammatory signaling, creating a pro-inflammatory tissue environment. In parallel, metabolic dysfunction is increasingly recognized in OLP, characterized by altered energy production and nutrient utilization that disrupt tissue homeostasis. These two processes are closely linked, as oxidative stress impairs metabolic pathways, while metabolic alterations further increase oxidative burden, forming a self-reinforcing cycle that perpetuates mucosal injury and chronic inflammation. Clinically, markers of oxidative stress and metabolic imbalance, particularly those detectable in saliva, show promise for non-invasive diagnosis and risk assessment. Therapeutic strategies aimed at restoring redox balance and improving metabolic regulation represent emerging avenues beyond conventional symptom management. Understanding the crosstalk between oxidative stress and metabolism offers new opportunities for precision medicine approaches to improve the diagnosis, treatment, and long-term outcomes of patients with OLP.KEYWORDS: Oral lichen planus, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, redox imbalance, malignant transformation, biomarkers

Introduction

Oral lichen planus (OLP) is a chronic, immune-mediated inflammatory disease of the oral mucosa, characterized histologically by degeneration of basal keratinocytes and subepithelial infiltration of lymphocytes. Clinically, OLP manifests as reticular, erosive, or atrophic lesions that are frequently accompanied by discomfort or burning sensations. Although the incidence is low, OLP has the potential of developing into oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) (Roberts et al. 2024). The etiopathogenesis of OLP is multifactorial, involving genetic predisposition, immune dysregulation, and environmental triggers, yet the precise molecular mechanisms driving disease persistence and progression remain incompletely understood (Kim et al. 2023).

Emerging evidence implicates oxidative stress that is defined as an imbalance skewed toward reactive oxygen/nitrogen species (ROS/RNS) versus antioxidant defenses in the pathophysiology of OLP. A comprehensive meta-analysis of 28 studies (849 OLP patients, 1,052 controls) showed significantly elevated levels of malondialdehyde (MDA) and nitric oxide (NO), along with decreased total antioxidant capacity and uric acid in both saliva and serum/plasma of individuals with OLP (Wang et al. 2021). Additional studies have reported increased 8-hydroxy-deoxyguanosine (8-OH-dG), advanced oxidation protein products, and disrupted glutathione and antioxidant enzyme profiles, including glutathione peroxidase (GPx) and superoxide dismutase (SOD), in the saliva or serum of OLP patients (Shirzaiy et al. 2022). Oxidative stress not only contributes to keratinocyte apoptosis but also amplifies pro-inflammatory signaling pathways, including NF-κB and MAPKs, perpetuating the chronic inflammatory loop that characterizes OLP lesions (Shin et al. 2020).

Oxidative stress in OLP is intimately linked to metabolic dysfunction at the cellular level. For instance, OLP-associated mitochondrial respiratory impairment, particularly reductions in the succinate dehydrogenase B (SDHB) subunit (complex II), leads to compromised oxidative phosphorylation, accumulation of succinate, activation of HIF-1α signaling, and a metabolic shift toward glycolysis in the mucosal tissue (Zhang et al. 2022). Energy-sensing pathways, such as AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) and mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR), are redox-sensitive and can shift cellular metabolism between glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation in response to stress signals. In OLP lesions, miR-122 and miR-199 have been shown to modulate autophagy and inflammatory responses via targeting the Akt/mTOR pathway (Wang et al. 2019). Additionally, lipid peroxidation products such as 4-hydroxynonenal can form adducts with metabolic enzymes, further disrupting metabolic homeostasis (Panchal et al. 2015). Such metabolic alterations may exacerbate oxidative damage, creating a vicious cycle that sustains tissue injury and inflammation.

The combined burden of chronic oxidative stress and metabolic derangement in OLP may also have implications for malignant transformation. Excessive oxidative and metabolic stress can induce persistent DNA damage, mitochondrial genome instability, and epigenetic modifications that favor carcinogenesis (Rizvi et al. 2021). Understanding this crosstalk at the molecular level could open new avenues for biomarker discovery and therapeutic intervention, moving beyond symptom control toward targeted modulation of redox and metabolic pathways.

Given these insights, this review aims to consolidate current knowledge on the molecular mechanisms linking oxidative stress and metabolic dysfunction in OLP, with placing emphasis on mitochondrial perturbations, redox-sensitive signaling, and metabolic reprogramming. We will also discuss their potential as diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets in the clinical management of OLP.

Oxidative/nitrosative stress in OLP pathophysiology

Previous studies consistently showed that OLP lesions have higher lipid, protein, and DNA oxidation, including salivary/serum MDA, advanced oxidation protein products (AOPP), 8-hydroxy-2'-deoxyguanosine (8-oxo-dG), with reduced total antioxidant capacity and small-molecule antioxidants such as uric acid, indicating a shift toward oxidants (Chaiyarit et al. 2005). Enzymatic antioxidant defenses are frequently blunted in OLP such as lower salivary GPx and altered SOD, supporting insufficient detoxification of ROS/RNS (Shirzaiy et al. 2022). This oxidative milieu interacts with inflammation and cell-death programs also in oral mucosal environment (Ene et al. 2021).

Critical genes producing ROS/RNS in OLP

The NOS2 gene encodes inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), one of three nitric oxide synthase isoforms (the others are endothelial NOS/eNOS and neuronal NOS/nNOS). Unlike eNOS and nNOS, which are constitutively expressed and calcium-dependent, iNOS is transcriptionally induced by inflammatory stimuli such as cytokines (IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-1β) or microbial products (LPS) (Hussain et al. 2003). Once expressed, iNOS generates large, sustained amounts of nitric oxide (NO) from L-arginine, NADPH, and O₂, independent of calcium flux. This high-output NO production can have antimicrobial, antiviral, and tumoricidal effects, but also causes host-tissue damage when excessive or chronic. Immunohistochemistry in OLP biopsies shows colocalization of iNOS with 8-nitroguanine (nitrative DNA lesion) in oral epithelium and inflammatory cells, indicating iNOS-driven RNS generation within the lesion microenvironment (Chaiyarit et al. 2005). This study also demonstrated co-accumulation of 3-nitrotyrosine and 8-nitroguanine in basal/suprabasal epithelial layers, mapping nitrative injury to the histologic interface where keratinocyte damage is most prominent. Clinically, total nitrite/nitrate levels are elevated in serum/saliva of OLP patients versus controls, supporting systemic/locoregional activation of nitric-oxide pathways in OLP (Shiva et al. 2020). Considering that 8-nitroguanine is formed at the locations of inflammation-related carcinogenesis (Pinlaor et al. 2004), iNOS may be a significant factor in the malignant transformation of OLP.

SDHB is one of four nuclear-encoded subunits of succinate dehydrogenase, which uniquely functions in both the TCA cycle and the electron transport chain. SDHB specifically contains iron–sulfur clusters critical for electron transfer. Loss or mutation of SDHB disrupts electron flow, leading to succinate accumulation, impaired oxidative phosphorylation, and mitochondrial ROS production (Guzy et al. 2008). Previous study identified reduced SDHB expression in OLP lesions, which caused succinate accumulation, HIF-1α stabilization, impaired oxidative phosphorylation and metabolic reprogramming toward glycolysis, and increased mitochondrial stress (Zhang et al. 2022). Because electron leakage from dysfunctional Complex II is a canonical source of superoxide, SDHB loss links mitochondrial metabolism to ROS generation in OLP keratinocytes. Furthermore, given that succinate accumulation may serve as an oncometabolite (Eijkelenkamp et al. 2020; Kim et al. 2024), SDHB alteration may be another important cause of tumorigenesis from OLP.

NADPH oxidase (NOX) enzymes are specialized membrane-bound complexes whose primary function is the generation of ROS, specifically the one-electron reduction of oxygen to superoxide (O₂−), using NADPH as an electron donor (Kumar et al. 2017). Human skin (HaCaT) and gingival mucosal (GM16) keratinocytes harbor a constitutively expressed NADPH oxidase–like system that generates superoxide, establishing the presence of non-phagocytic NOX activity (Chamulitrat et al. 2004). Among NOX isoforms, NOX4 is broadly expressed in epithelia and can produce H₂O₂ under basal conditions, providing a steady ROS source that is upregulated by inflammatory/hypoxic signaling in other tissues (Diebold et al. 2010). Direct isoform-level profiling in OLP lesions (e.g. NOX1/NOX4/dual oxidase (DUOX) 1/2) has not yet been consistently reported. However, lichen planus skin lesions show high DUOX1 expression, and DUOX enzymes are recognized epithelial ROS producers, together supporting a testable hypothesis that NOX/DUOX contribute to ROS burden in OLP mucosa (Ashtiwi et al. 2021).

Myeloperoxidase (MPO) is a heme-containing enzyme predominantly stored in the azurophilic granules of neutrophils and released during degranulation to generate hypochlorous acid (HOCl), a highly reactive oxidant central to microbial killing and innate immunity. MPO has been known as a potent ROS/RNS-amplifying protein in oral inflammation, but OLP-specific salivary peroxidase/MPO readouts are inconsistent, with at least one study finding no significant increase in salivary peroxidase in OLP versus controls (Boras 2004). Broader oral-disease meta-evidence shows that salivary MPO can be altered in inflammatory conditions, underscoring plausibility yet highlighting the need for OLP-focused MPO quantification with cell-type resolution (Nijakowski et al. 2023).

Oxidative/nitrosative damage to biomolecules

Accumulating clinical evidence indicates that patients with OLP exhibit enhanced lipid peroxidation. Salivary MDA, typically measured as thiobarbituric acid-reactive substances (TBARS), is frequently elevated in OLP compared with healthy controls, supporting the notion of intensified oxidative membrane injury in this condition (Singh et al. 2022). A larger clinical series further demonstrated significantly higher TBARS and lower antioxidant capacity in saliva from patients with reticular and erosive OLP, linking oxidative injury with disease activity (Darczuk et al. 2016). Nevertheless, some controlled studies reported no significant differences in salivary oxidative markers between patients and controls, highlighting variability due to methodological differences and underscoring the need for standardized biomarker assays (Gonzaga et al. 2023). Importantly, a recent meta-analysis concluded that OLP is characterized by an overall oxidant–antioxidant imbalance, reflected by increased MDA and nitric oxide levels and reduced total antioxidant capacity and uric acid (Wang et al. 2021).

Protein oxidative damage has also been documented in oral potentially malignant disorders, including OLP. Elevated salivary protein carbonyls have been reported in affected patients compared with controls, indicating systemic protein modification through redox stress (Vlkova et al. 2012). In clinical practice, AOPP, primarily generated through MPO-mediated HOCl reactions, serve as sensitive indicators of neutrophil-mediated protein oxidation in the oral cavity (Tothova et al. 2015; Lettrichova et al. 2016). However, salivary MPO levels in OLP have yielded mixed results across studies, suggesting that protein oxidation markers should be interpreted in conjunction with disease severity and other redox profiles (Nijakowski et al. 2023).

DNA is a critical target of oxidative and nitrative stress in OLP. Lesional biopsies demonstrate strong immunoreactivity for 8-oxo-dG and 8-nitroguanine in basal and suprabasal epithelial layers, regions most affected by keratinocyte degeneration (Chaiyarit et al. 2005) (Figure 1). The co-localization of these lesions highlights a genotoxic milieu that may contribute to the malignant potential of OLP.

Metabolic dysfunction in OLP

Mitochondrial dysfunction

Recent evidence has highlighted the critical role of mitochondrial dysfunction in the pathogenesis of OLP. In particular, the expression of SDHB, a critical component of mitochondrial complex II, is significantly reduced in OLP tissues (Zhang et al. 2022). As SDHB is required for electron transfer within the TCA cycle and oxidative phosphorylation, its downregulation disrupts normal mitochondrial respiration. This impairment leads to the accumulation of succinate which stabilizes HIF-1α and thereby promotes a pseudohypoxic response even under normoxic conditions. A change toward glycolytic reprogramming and decreased oxidative phosphorylation are the outcomes, exhibiting a metabolic profile akin to the Warburg effect. Beyond these metabolic alterations, SDHB deficiency contributes directly to excessive mitochondrial ROS production, since impaired electron transport increases electron leakage and superoxide formation. Elevated mitochondrial ROS not only exacerbate oxidative damage within epithelial cells but also act as signaling molecules that amplify pro-inflammatory pathways such as NF-κB and NLRP3 inflammasome activation. This creates a feed-forward cycle of redox imbalance, metabolic stress, and inflammation within the oral mucosa (Figure 2).

Importantly, the dual role of succinate as both a metabolic intermediate and signaling molecule provides further mechanistic insight. Accumulated succinate can be released extracellularly, where it engages the succinate receptor (SUCNR1/GPR91) on immune cells, enhancing pro-inflammatory cytokine production and immune cell recruitment (Rubic et al. 2008). Thus, loss of SDHB function in OLP keratinocytes may not only disturb intracellular metabolism but also reshape the inflammatory microenvironment, reinforcing chronicity and potentially contributing to malignant transformation risk.

Energy metabolism alterations

Emerging evidence suggests that immune and epithelial cells within OLP lesions undergo profound metabolic reprogramming. One key observation is the activation of the Akt/mTOR-autophagy pathway in T lymphocytes infiltrating OLP mucosa, indicating a shift toward altered immune cell metabolism under chronic inflammatory stress. The mTOR pathway serves as a central hub that integrates signals from growth factors, nutrients, and oxygen availability, driving anabolic processes such as protein and lipid synthesis, and favoring glycolysis over oxidative phosphorylation when activated (Simcox and Lamming 2022; Liu et al. 2025). In the OLP microenvironment, persistent mTOR activation may sustain T-cell proliferation and pro-inflammatory cytokine release, thereby perpetuating disease chronicity. In contrast, AMPK pathway functions as a critical energy sensor that becomes activated under conditions of energy stress. AMPK promotes catabolic pathways, enhances mitochondrial biogenesis, and inhibits mTORC1 activity, thereby restoring energy balance and promoting cellular survival (Herzig and Shaw 2018). Given that dysregulation of the AMPK–mTOR axis has been described in multiple chronic inflammatory conditions (Jeon 2016), excessive mTOR signaling and insufficient AMPK activity drive aberrant immune activation, metabolic stress, and tissue injury.

Although direct studies in OLP remain limited, parallels from other autoimmune and inflammatory mucosal disorders suggest that hyperactive mTOR signaling, coupled with impaired AMPK-mediated metabolic restraint, could underlie the persistent immune activation and epithelial damage observed in OLP (Lyu et al. 2024). This hypothesis is supported by the observation that mTOR and Akt signaling pathways are intimately linked to T-cell differentiation, promoting Th1 and Th17 responses that are also implicated in OLP pathogenesis (Xie et al. 2012). Moreover, chronic mTOR activation reduces autophagic clearance of damaged mitochondria, thereby exacerbating mitochondrial ROS accumulation and redox imbalance (Bartolome et al. 2017), further amplifying inflammatory signaling cascades.

Metabolic disturbances in lipid, amino acid, and one-carbon pathways

Metabolic remodeling in OLP is not confined to energy pathways but extends to lipid and amino acid metabolism, both of which influence redox balance and immune function. Recent metabolomic analyses have revealed alterations in lipid species during the progression from OLP toward OSCC, including changes in diacylglycerols, triacylglycerols, and phosphatidylcholine derivatives (Yao et al. 2025). These lipid perturbations are clinically relevant because diacylglycerols and lysophospholipids can act as bioactive signaling molecules, activating protein kinase C (PKC) and other pathways that drive inflammatory signaling and keratinocyte stress responses (Wright and McMaster 2002; Kolczynska et al. 2020). Moreover, imbalances in membrane phospholipids can compromise epithelial barrier integrity and promote susceptibility to oxidative damage, potentially contributing to the chronicity and malignant potential of OLP lesions.

In parallel, amino acid and one-carbon metabolism play critical roles in maintaining cellular antioxidant defense and epigenetic regulation (Ducker and Rabinowitz 2017). Glutathione (GSH), the most abundant intracellular antioxidant, is synthesized from glutamate, cysteine, and glycine. Perturbations in the availability of these precursors can limit GSH biosynthesis, thereby weakening redox buffering capacity and leaving keratinocytes vulnerable to oxidative/nitrosative stress. Inflammatory microenvironments, such as those in OLP, are also characterized by increased consumption of amino acids for immune cell proliferation, further straining epithelial antioxidant defenses (Ruth and Field 2013; Li et al. 2024).

The methionine cycle and associated one-carbon pathways contribute not only to nucleotide biosynthesis but also to the production of S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), a universal methyl donor for DNA and histone methylation. Disruptions in this cycle can therefore alter epigenetic regulation of inflammatory and stress-responsive genes (Jasiulionis 2018). Such changes are increasingly recognized in chronic inflammatory disorders and could play a role in the persistence of OLP lesions and their predisposition to malignant transformation (Abbenhardt et al. 2014). In addition, one-carbon metabolism supports the folate and transsulfuration pathways, both of which intersect with GSH metabolism, linking amino acid flux directly to antioxidant capacity.

While lipidomic data provide early evidence of metabolic shifts in OLP, direct investigations of amino acid and methylation pathways remain scarce. Nonetheless, previous pertinent studies suggest that lipid remodeling and amino acid/one-carbon dysregulation converge to amplify oxidative stress, inflammation, and epigenetic instability in OLP.

Differential metabolic fingerprints of reticular versus erosive/atrophic OLP

Clinically, OLP is heterogeneous, and the reticular and erosive/atrophic variants differ not only in symptom burden and malignant potential but also in their underlying metabolic fingerprints. Previous metabolic study demonstrated that erosive OLP exhibits a distinct metabolic profile compared with reticular lesions, with significantly lower levels of cyclohexanamine, glycine, mannitol/sorbitol, methyl palmitate, and trehalose in erosive mucosa (Cruz et al. 2019). The relative depletion of glycine in erosive lesions is notable, given its role as a precursor for glutathione synthesis and collagen remodeling, and may reflect impaired cytoprotective and antioxidant buffering capacity in clinically more aggressive lesions. Reduced mannitol/sorbitol and trehalose suggests altered handling of osmolytes and carbohydrates, potentially compromising epithelial stress tolerance and barrier stability under chronic inflammatory insult. In parallel, lower methyl palmitate is consistent with perturbed fatty-acid and membrane-lipid metabolism, which could translate into differences in membrane integrity, lipid signaling, and susceptibility to peroxidation between the two clinical forms.⁣

These tissue-level differences align with functional data indicating that erosive OLP is characterized by a higher oxidative load and more profound redox failure than reticular subtype. In paired lesion analyses, erosive OLP showed significantly higher MDA and nitrite levels, greater depletion of glutathione and GSH-regenerating enzymes, and a compensatory rise in SOD and catalase activity, together indicating an overwhelmed antioxidant network and a high-stress redox phenotype (Jana et al. 2021). Comet assays further revealed greater DNA strand breaks in erosive than reticular lesions, corroborating the concept that erosive/atrophic OLP exists at the edge of homeostatic collapse, whereas reticular OLP maintains a more elastic redox and metabolic set point. These observations support a model in which reticular lesions retain relatively preserved glycine-/trehalose-linked cytoprotection and a more balanced energy metabolism, while erosive/atrophic lesions shift toward glycolysis-biased, lipid-peroxidation-prone, and antioxidant-depleted states.

Integrating multi-biospecimen data from lesion tissue, serum, saliva, and urine, the differential metabolic fingerprints of reticular and erosive OLP delineate a coherent biological trajectory. The reticular form reflects a compensated inflammatory state, characterized by moderate oxidative stress counterbalanced by increased apoptosis within the inflammatory infiltrate, a mechanism that likely limits the excessive accumulation of tissue-destructive T cells. Local tissues retain relatively higher levels of cytoprotective metabolites, including trehalose and glycine. Salivary profiles are marked by elevated acetate and methylamine (Kashyap et al. 2024), while systemic metabolic features are significantly associated with hypertension and diabetes (Toader et al. 2025), collectively indicating a stable yet metabolically stress-susceptible condition. In contrast, the erosive form represents a decompensated metabolic state. Its metabolic signature is defined by profound exhaustion of antioxidant defenses and extensive lipid peroxidation. Local tissues exhibit marked depletion of cytoprotective trehalose and the anti-inflammatory lipid methyl palmitate (Cruz et al. 2019). At the systemic level, erosive OLP is associated with increased cardiometabolic risk and a profoundly dysregulated sphingolipid profile (Toader et al. 2025).

Clinically, this framework supports the rationale for subtype-stratified biomarker strategies. For example, panels combining oxidative stress indices, antioxidant enzyme activity, and tissue markers of oxidative/nitrative DNA damage may provide improved discrimination of erosive form activity, enable longitudinal monitoring, and assist in risk-enriched follow-up. Furthermore, these differential metabolic fingerprints illuminate the molecular pathways that drive the clinical progression of OLP.

Crosstalk between oxidative stress and metabolic dysfunction

ROS/RNS-mediated modulation of metabolic enzymes

In OLP, chronic inflammation and persistent immune activation form a highly oxidative/nitrosative microenvironment within the oral mucosa (Wang et al. 2021). ROS and RNS generated by infiltrating immune cells, such as activated T lymphocytes and macrophages, as well as stressed epithelial cells, are not merely toxic byproducts of inflammation. At low to moderate levels, these reactive molecules act as signaling intermediates, modulating epithelial cell survival, immune responses, and wound-healing pathways (Hong et al. 2024). However, sustained oxidative stress, which is common in erosive OLP lesions, shifts this balance, leading to pathological changes in metabolism and cellular redox homeostasis (Jana et al. 2021).

One of the primary mechanisms through which ROS and RNS exert their effects is via post-translational modifications (PTMs) of metabolic enzymes. These include S-glutathionylation, S-nitrosylation, and carbonylation, which alter the activity, structure, or stability of target proteins (Kalinina and Novichkova 2023). For instance, S-glutathionylation, the reversible attachment of GSH to cysteine residues, helps protect enzymes in oral keratinocytes from irreversible oxidative damage during acute inflammatory flares. Conversely, chronic oxidative stress depletes intracellular GSH and promotes irreversible carbonylation, leading to the permanent inactivation of enzymes involved in antioxidant defense and energy metabolism (Vrettou and Wirth 2022). Mitochondrial proteins are particularly vulnerable to these redox-based PTMs. When epithelial cells are exposed to sustained cytokine signaling and ROS, mitochondrial enzymes such as succinate dehydrogenase (SDH, complex II) and ATP synthase can undergo oxidative modifications (Mailloux and Willmore 2014). These changes lead to a self-amplifying cycle of mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress. Such alterations may explain clinical findings of reduced SDHB expression in OLP lesions (Zhang et al. 2022).

Importantly, under moderate oxidative stress, these PTMs are reversible, allowing cells to dynamically regulate enzyme activity and respond to environmental changes. This reversible regulation may help explain why reticular OLP lesions often exhibit periods of remission (Thorn et al. 1988; Rode and Kogoj-Rode 2002). However, when oxidative stress is severe or prolonged, these modifications become irreversible, leading to permanent enzyme inactivation, loss of mitochondrial function, and sustained tissue damage. This may also increase the risk of malignant transformation, as mitochondrial dysfunction and ROS-driven DNA damage accumulate over time (Kuo et al. 2022) (Figure 3).

Mitochondria–inflammation–ROS feedback loop

Mitochondria is not only the primary source of intracellular ATP but also central regulators of innate immune responses and oxidative balance (West et al. 2011). In OLP, persistent T-cell–mediated immune activation and epithelial stress converge on the mitochondria, resulting in mitochondrial dysfunction, increased ROS generation, and sustained tissue inflammation. A key molecular pathway linking mitochondrial dysfunction to chronic inflammation is the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome. This multiprotein complex plays a crucial role in producing interleukin (IL)−1β and IL-18, two cytokines implicated in the progression of OLP (Thi Do et al. 2018; Doss et al. 2024). Several metabolic signals can drive NLRP3 activation, including succinate accumulation, which stabilizes HIF-1α, and increased glycolytic flux, which fuels inflammatory cell activation (Yu et al. 2022). These signals are intimately related to ROS since mitochondrial oxidative stress supplies the priming and activation signals necessary for inflammasome assembly (Harijith et al. 2014). In OLP, succinate buildup may be a critical factor amplifying inflammation by enhancing HIF-1α–dependent IL-1β production (Zhang et al. 2022).

Another pivotal molecule in this feedback loop is high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), a damage-associated molecular pattern released by stressed or necrotic cells. While direct data from OLP are limited, elevated HMGB1 expression has been observed in cutaneous lichen planus, where it colocalizes with TLR4 in lesional tissue, supporting a pathogenic role as a danger signal in epithelial inflammation (de Carvalho et al. 2018). Oxidative stress promotes HMGB1 translocation to the extracellular space, enabling its DAMP activity (Chen et al. 2022), and additionally modulates its pro-inflammatory potential by altering the redox state of HMGB1 (Venereau et al. 2012). Extracellular HMGB1 binds to receptor for advanced glycation end-products (RAGE) and TLR2/4 on keratinocytes and immune cells, triggering NF-κB and MAPK signaling pathways (Lin et al. 2022; Ren et al. 2023). This cascade drives the production of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-17, all of which are central to OLP pathogenesis (Salem et al. 2018; Yang et al. 2020; Agha-Hosseini et al. 2023).

The interplay between mitochondrial dysfunction, inflammasome activation, and DAMP signaling creates a self-reinforcing loop of oxidative stress and inflammation. This vicious cycle eventually leads to diminished antioxidant defenses, chronic epithelium damage, and a setting that is favorable for malignant transformation. This mechanistic link is supported by the observation of increased oxidative DNA lesions such as 8-oxo-dG and 8-nitroguanine in OLP tissues, particularly in patients with a history of longstanding erosive lesions (Chaiyarit et al. 2005).

Persistent oxidative and nitrative stress in OLP does not merely inflict nonspecific DNA damage. Instead, it generates distinct DNA lesions and metabolic signals that are capable of driving stepwise malignant transformation. A representative example is 8-nitroguanine, a nitrative guanine lesion formed under iNOS-driven RNS excess and detected at high levels in basal and suprabasal epithelial cells in OLP and OSCC, but not in normal mucosa (Chaiyarit et al. 2005). 8-Nitroguanine is intrinsically unstable and undergoes spontaneous depurination, resulting in apurinic sites. During error-prone translesion DNA synthesis, these sites or the 8-nitroguanine lesion itself are frequently bypassed through adenine insertion, leading to guanine-to-thymine transversions and the progressive accumulation of point mutations in oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes (Hiraku 2010). In the context of OLP, sustained formation of 8-nitroguanine and 8-oxo-dG within the proliferative basal layer likely seeds a mutational burden in keratinocyte stem/progenitor populations, thereby creating a cancer-prone epithelial field over years of chronic disease.

Concurrently, SDHB loss and succinate accumulation in OLP lesions introduce an oncometabolite axis that couples metabolic rewiring to epigenetic silencing. Succinate is a structural analog of α-ketoglutarate and at high concentrations inhibits α-KG-dependent dioxygenases, including prolyl hydroxylases that normally target HIF-1α for degradation and TET family DNA demethylases that catalyze 5-methylcytosine oxidation. Inhibition of prolyl hydroxylases stabilizes HIF-1α even under normoxia, driving a pseudohypoxic transcriptional program that promotes glycolysis, angiogenesis, and survival signaling, which is consistently associated with malignant progression (Luo et al. 2023). At the same time, suppression of TET activity and related α-KG-dependent epigenetic enzymes favors DNA hypermethylation at CpG islands in promoter regions, a well-described mechanism of stable epigenetic silencing of tumor-suppressor and DNA-repair genes in cancer (Tycko 2000). Thus, succinate accumulation may simultaneously reinforce HIF-1α-driven metabolic reprogramming and lock in epigenetic repression of protective genes, shifting the mucosa from a reversible inflammatory state toward an epigenetically fixed, transformation-prone phenotype.

Energy stress, redox signaling, and immune response

Energy metabolism and redox signaling are intimately connected, especially under chronic inflammatory conditions such as OLP. Persistent immune activation in OLP leads to high metabolic demand in both epithelial cells and infiltrating immune cells, creating a state of energy stress and oxidative imbalance (Li and Wang 2022). When activated by energy depletion, AMPK shifts cellular metabolism toward catabolic pathways such as fatty acid oxidation and autophagy while suppressing biosynthetic processes. Importantly, AMPK enhances Nrf2 activity, promoting the expression of antioxidant genes including HO-1, GCL, and NQO1, which neutralize ROS and protect tissues from oxidative damage (Mo et al. 2014; Xu et al. 2020). In OLP, particularly in the erosive form, salivary levels of key enzymes such as glutathione reductase and catalase are significantly reduced compared to controls, reflecting impaired GSH recycling and antioxidant capacity (Shirzaiy et al. 2022; Rezazadeh et al. 2023). This depletion of antioxidant defenses suggests that AMPK–Nrf2 signaling seems to be insufficient to counterbalance rising ROS.

Conversely, mTOR, which drives anabolic metabolism and pro-inflammatory responses, is highly phosphorylated in OLP patients (Zhang et al. 2017; Wang et al. 2020). Hyperactive mTOR signaling in OLP supports glycolysis and immune cell proliferation, particularly in Th1 and Th17 lymphocytes, which are central to OLP pathogenesis (Xie et al. 2012). Moreover, mTORC1 has been linked to NLRP3 inflammasome activation, increasing IL-1β production and amplifying local inflammation (Yang et al. 2019). In OLP, weak AMPK–Nrf2 activity combined with hyperactive mTORC1 signaling likely drives persistent lesions and aggressive clinical behavior. Treatment options for OLP may include medications that inhibit mTORC1 (rapamycin analogs) or activate AMPK (metformin, resveratrol), which may aid in restoring metabolic and redox balance.

Potential clinical implications

Biomarkers for diagnosis and monitoring

A growing body of evidence highlights the utility of oxidative stress markers in the diagnosis and monitoring of OLP. Salivary and blood-based oxidative stress readouts, including elevated MDA and NO levels, coupled with reduced total antioxidant capacity, are consistently altered in OLP. These markers offer a non-invasive approach for both early detection and longitudinal assessment of disease progression (Wang et al. 2021). In particular, enzymatic antioxidants are markedly diminished in OLP. Patients with OLP, especially those with erosive subtypes, have much decreased salivary glutathione reductase and catalase activity, which suggests impaired GSH recycling and weakened antioxidant defense systems (Rezazadeh et al. 2023). These findings suggest GSH-related enzymes as trackable biomarker candidates for monitoring redox imbalance in OLP. At the tissue level, oxidative/nitrative DNA damage markers are significantly increased in OLP lesions. Elevated levels of 8-oxo-dG and 8-nitroguanine in both the epithelial and subepithelial inflammatory compartments reflect persistent oxidative damage to the oral mucosa. These markers may serve as risk-enriched pathology indicators, particularly in patients at higher risk for malignant transformation (Chaiyarit et al. 2005).

Mitochondrial-metabolic indicators, including reduced SDHB with succinate accumulation and HIF-1α activation, are detectable in OLP tissue and may serve as metabolic biomarkers indicating mitochondrial dysfunction and a pseudohypoxic tissue environment. These metabolic alterations may hold prognostic relevance and help stratify patients by disease severity or progression risk (Zhang et al. 2022). Multi-parameter diagnostic panels are also under development. Combining oxidative stress indices, salivary miRNAs, and metabolomic profiling has demonstrated superior diagnostic accuracy compared to single-marker approaches (Fatfat et al. 2025). For instance, a recent meta-analysis of salivary miRNAs in OLP reported a pooled AUC of 0.93, underscoring their potential as reliable, non-invasive biomarkers. In parallel, salivary metabolomics has identified distinct metabolites, such tyrosine, that may be leveraged for screening and disease classification (Kashyap et al. 2024; Koopaie et al. 2024).

Therapeutic targeting

Therapeutic approaches for OLP are increasingly exploring strategies that address oxidative stress and metabolic dysfunction, in addition to traditional anti-inflammatory treatments. A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials demonstrated that antioxidant therapies, including curcumin, lycopene, aloe vera, vitamin E, and coenzyme Q10, significantly improved clinical symptoms such as pain, burning sensation, and lesion severity, with favorable safety profiles (Bao et al. 2022). Among these agents, curcumin has been the most extensively studied. A meta-analysis reported that curcumin supplementation led to significant reductions in erythema, lesion size, and pain, supporting its use as an adjunct to conventional topical corticosteroids in improving patient outcomes (Moayeri et al. 2024). These findings highlight the potential of curcumin as a low-risk therapeutic option targeting oxidative and inflammatory pathways in OLP.

Despite these promising results, a lack of standardized study designs and outcome measures remains a challenge. A separate meta-analysis on oxidative stress markers in OLP emphasized the necessity of harmonized clinical endpoints and subtype stratification to ensure reliable interpretation of therapeutic efficacy across different patient populations (Wang et al. 2021). This is particularly important, considering the heterogeneity of OLP, where erosive lesions may require different treatment approaches compared to reticular forms.

Beyond antioxidants, targeting core metabolic pathways has emerged as a novel therapeutic strategy. Mechanistic studies have shown that rapamycin suppressed OLP T-cell proliferation and shifts the Th17/Treg balance toward a more regulatory phenotype (Wang et al. 2020). Similarly, pharmacologic glycolysis inhibition with 2-deoxy-D-glucose reduced effector T-cell function and dampened inflammatory responses in OLP (Wang et al. 2021). Several clinical studies are currently investigating adjunctive and mechanism-based therapeutic strategies for OLP. In particular, JAK inhibitors have emerged as promising candidates. Upadacitinib has been used off-label in three case reports, demonstrating marked clinical improvement in patients with erosive or non-erosive OLP, including successful treatment with 15 mg daily as monotherapy or in combination with methotrexate (Kooybaran et al. 2021; Didona et al. 2025). Baricitinib 3.4 mg twice daily has been reported as successful in a non-erosive OLP patient (Moussa et al. 2022). Deucravacitinib 6 mg daily produced a good clinical response in three OLP patients, improving reticular lesions and reducing erosions, although complete resolution was not achieved after 12 weeks (Gowda et al. 2024). A randomized clinical trial (NCT07131813) is currently evaluating oral tofacitinib, a selective JAK1/JAK3 inhibitor, as an adjunct to topical triamcinolone in patients with moderate-to-severe OLP who are refractory to corticosteroid monotherapy. This study specifically targets patients with moderate to severe OLP who may be refractory to standard steroid monotherapy. In parallel, improved topical delivery platforms are being explored. NCT06233591 evaluates LP-310, a liposomal tacrolimus oral rinse (Phase 2a), aiming to enhance local mucosal exposure while limiting systemic burden and improving practicality for chronic disease control (Brennan et al. 2026). In addition, NCT06060301 examines topical sulfasalazine as an adjunct to topical corticosteroids, representing a repurposing strategy to broaden steroid-sparing or add-on options in routine practice.

Conclusion

OLP is a complex chronic inflammatory disorder in which oxidative stress and metabolic dysfunction are central to disease pathogenesis and progression. Evidence consistently demonstrates a profound imbalance between ROS/RNS and antioxidant defenses in OLP, leading to lipid, protein, and DNA damage within epithelial tissues. These oxidative injuries not only compromise epithelial cell integrity but also perpetuate inflammatory signaling pathways. A self-sustaining loop of inflammation and oxidative damage is the cumulative effect that propels the persistence of chronic lesions.

Recent findings have highlighted a critical role for mitochondrial dysfunction and altered energy metabolism in OLP. Mitochondrial respiratory defects are linked to redox imbalance, pseudohypoxia, and aberrant glycolytic reprogramming. Dysregulation of the AMPK–mTOR axis further contributes to uncontrolled immune cell activation and impaired autophagy, amplifying ROS generation and metabolic stress. These intertwined mechanisms suggest that OLP is not merely an inflammatory condition but also a disease of disrupted cellular bioenergetics.

Clinically, these molecular insights open new opportunities for biomarker discovery and precision diagnostics. Salivary oxidative stress markers, mitochondrial proteins, and multi-omics panels may offer promise for non-invasive detection and risk stratification. Moreover, understanding the interplay between oxidative stress and metabolism provides a rationale for targeted therapeutic strategies. Antioxidant interventions can show meaningful clinical benefit, while experimental approaches targeting mTOR signaling or glycolytic flux hold potential to modulate the underlying disease processes.

Moving forward, standardized clinical trials and mechanistic studies are essential to validate these emerging biomarkers and therapeutic targets. By integrating molecular insights with clinical practice, future management of OLP can evolve beyond symptom control toward precision medicine approaches that mitigate oxidative stress, restore metabolic balance, and ultimately reduce the risk of malignant transformation.
