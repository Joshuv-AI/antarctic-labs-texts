Electrophysiological outcomes of radiofrequency ablation for supraventricular tachycardias in patients with congenitally corrected transposition of the great arteries
Authors: Toshihiro Nakamura, Yoshiaki Kato, Heima Sakaguchi, Kenzaburo Nakajima, Aki Mori, Kenichi Kurosaki, Hideo Ohuchi, Kengo Kusano
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with congenitally corrected transposition of the great arteries (cc-TGA) are predisposed to supraventricular tachycardias; however, data regarding the outcomes of catheter ablation (CA) remain limited.ObjectiveThis study aimed to assess the clinical impact of CA for supraventricular tachycardias in patients with cc-TGA.MethodsThis retrospective, single-center cohort study evaluated 29 of 112 cc-TGA patients (26%) who underwent CA. Patients were stratified into 3 groups based on surgical history: anatomical repair (n = 10/43, 23%), physiologic repair (n = 14/32, 44%), and no prior surgery (n = 5/37, 14%). Clinical characteristics and procedural outcomes were analyzed.ResultsOf the 29 patients who underwent CA, 10 belonged to the anatomical repair group, 14 to the physiologic repair group, and 5 to the non-surgical group. Median age at ablation was significantly lower in the anatomical repair group (25.6 years), compared with physiologic (40.6 years, P = .005) and non-surgical patients (43.8 years, P = .01). The arrhythmia types included intra-atrial reentrant tachycardia (n = 20), focal atrial tachycardia (n = 3), paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia (n = 2), and atrial fibrillation (n = 6). Cavomitral isthmus ablation was frequently required across all groups. Over a median follow-up of 5.6 years, 3 patients required repeat ablation; all experienced new arrhythmia mechanisms distinct from the index procedure.ConclusionSupraventricular tachycardia patterns varied by surgical background, but cavomitral isthmus ablation was commonly indicated. CA is a key therapeutic strategy in the long-term rhythm management of cc-TGA patients.Keywords: Atrial fibrillation, Atrial tachycardia, Congenitally corrected transposition of the great arteries, Double-switch operation, Radiofrequency catheter ablation, Supraventricular tachycardia

Key Findings

▪Catheter ablation for supraventricular tachycardias in patients with congenitally corrected transposition of the great arteries (cc-TGA) was technically feasible and yielded favorable acute procedural success. Despite the complexity of congenital anatomy and surgical alterations, ablation provided meaningful arrhythmia control in most cases.

Introduction

Congenitally corrected transposition of the great arteries (cc-TGA) is a rare congenital heart defect characterized by discordant atrioventricular (AV) and ventriculoarterial (VA) connections, accounting for approximately 0.5% of all congenital heart diseases.1 In the absence of intracardiac malformations, patients with cc-TGA may remain asymptomatic and be diagnosed later in life. However, associated anomalies such as severe tricuspid regurgitation (TR), ventricular septal defect (VSD), and pulmonary stenosis often lead to earlier clinical presentation. Individuals with a systemic right ventricle (RV), as seen in cc-TGA, are at risk for progressive RV enlargement and dysfunction, which may result in significant long-term sequelae. The optimal timing of surgical intervention in cc-TGA subgroups remains a subject of debate. Anatomical repair, physiologic repair, and palliative procedures offer potential survival benefits, yet long-term outcomes remain uncertain.2 Prior studies have demonstrated that catheter ablation (CA) of supraventricular tachycardia plays a critical role in the long-term management of arrhythmias in congenital heart disease.3, 4, 5, 6 However, owing to the rarity of cc-TGA, the efficacy and outcomes of CA across different surgical subgroups remain largely unknown. This study aims to evaluate the clinical characteristics and outcomes of CA in patients with various forms of cc-TGA.

Methods

Study design and patient selection

This retrospective, single-center cohort study examined CA outcomes in patients with supraventricular tachycardia and cc-TGA. 29 patients who underwent CA were selected from a cohort of 112 individuals diagnosed with cc-TGA at the National Cerebral and Cardiovascular Center between January 2010 and December 2023. The diagnosis of cc-TGA was based on AV and VA discordance. Consequently, in cases with atrial situs solitus, the right atrium (RA) is connected to the morphologic left ventricle (LV) via the mitral valve, whereas the left atrium (LA) is connected to the morphologic RV via the tricuspid valve (TV). Patients with AV discordance and a double-outlet RV pattern were excluded. According to a previous report of the assessment of Ebstein anomaly using echocardiography,7 we considered apical displacement of the septal TV leaflet from the insertion of the anterior leaflet of the mitral valve greater than 8 mm/m2 in the 4-chamber view of echocardiography as an Ebstein anomaly in cc-TGA. The cohort was classified based on the treatment approach into anatomical repair, physiologic repair, and non-surgical management. In terms of anatomical arrangement, (S, L, L) was defined as situs solitus with L-looping of the ventricles and the aorta positioned anterior and rightward of the pulmonary artery, (I, D, D) was defined as situs inversus with D-looping of the ventricles and the aorta anterior and leftward. In this study, anatomical repair was defined as surgical reconstruction of both the AV connection and the VA connection to the normal configuration, thereby assigning the morphologic LV to support the systemic circulation. Physiologic repair was defined as preservation of the existing ventricular–great artery connections, with correction of associated lesions such as VSD, pulmonary stenosis, or TR. Supraventricular tachycardia included intra-atrial reentrant tachycardia (IART), AV nodal reentrant tachycardia (AVNRT), AV reentrant tachycardia (AVRT) mediated by an AV accessory pathway (AP), focal atrial tachycardia, and atrial fibrillation (AF).

The study assessed patient characteristics and the impact of CA on supraventricular tachycardia in cc-TGA. Institutional approval was obtained from the Research Ethics Board of the National Cerebral and Cardiovascular Center, Suita, Japan (M29-039-4), and the study followed the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Electrophysiological study, mapping, and ablation

The indications for CA in this study cohort included (1) patients with tachyarrhythmia documented via a 12-lead electrocardiogram who were refractory to or intolerant of medical therapy, and (2) patients experiencing arrhythmia-related symptoms, such as frequent palpitations or syncope. Linear ablation was employed to interrupt conduction through the critical isthmus, which was identified with 3-dimensional (3D) activation map or entrainment pacing, specifically the cavomitral isthmus (CMI) or lateral incisional isthmus. In the anatomical repair group, CMI ablation was performed in both the LA and RA using retroaortic access through the rerouted VSD. Bidirectional conduction block was typically confirmed by pacing on one side of the ablation line and assessing the earliest activation site on the opposite side. For focal atrial tachycardia, ablation was targeted at the site of the earliest atrial activation. In cases of AF originating from the LA, pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) was performed, with additional linear ablation or defragmentation as required. Defragmentation was performed to identify regions exhibiting complex fractionated atrial electrograms defined same as in previous study,8 which were targeted for radiofrequency (RF) energy delivered. For AVNRT or AVRT, either slow pathway modification or AP elimination was selected based on the specific arrhythmic mechanism. When feasible, activation mapping of supraventricular tachycardia was conducted under the guidance of either the CARTO or Ensite system. As the onset of the P wave could not always be reliably identified, the local electrogram at the anatomical left atrial appendage in the anatomical repair group and at the anatomical right atrial appendage in physiologic repair or non-surgical group or coronary sinus was used as a reference to determine local activation time. Mapping of the RA was performed first. A transseptal approach was utilized when left atrial access was required. RF ablation was performed with the maximum temperature set at 43 °C. Power settings were adjusted up to 35–40 W with a flow rate of 15 or 30 mL/min. Acute procedural success was defined as the non-inducibility of supraventricular tachycardia on rapid atrial pacing combined with the administration of isoproterenol.

Follow-up

At least one follow-up was conducted annually, even for patients who remained symptom-free after the procedure. However, some patients continued follow-up at other hospitals. If tachycardia recurrence occurred and was refractory to or intolerant of medical therapy, a repeat CA was recommended. We performed a Kaplan-Meier time-to-event analysis to assess freedom from any supraventricular tachycardia following CA.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, whereas variables with evidence of non-normal distribution were presented as median (quartile range). Differences in continuous variables between groups were assessed using an unpaired t test or the Mann-Whitney test. All P values were 2-sided, with statistical significance defined as P < .05. Statistical analyses were conducted using EZR in R Commander version 1.36 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Baseline clinical characteristics

Among the 112 patients with cc-TGA (43 who underwent anatomical repair, 32 who underwent physiologic repair, and 37 who did not undergo surgical intervention), we evaluated 29 patients (26%) who underwent CA for supraventricular tachycardia, including IART, AVNRT, AVRT, focal atrial tachycardia and AF. A flow-diagram of all 112 cc-TGA patients was shown in Supplemental Figure 1.” Among these 29 patients, none had concomitant cc-TGA and Ebstein anomaly of the TV. Additionally, no patients had undergone anatomical repair involving a combined atrial switch and arterial switch operation. Among them, 10 of 43 (23%) were in the anatomical repair group (median age: 35.2 years; median follow-up after CA: 4.7 years), 14 of 32 (44%) were in the physiologic repair group (median age: 45.0 years; median follow-up: 5.7 years), and 5 of 37 (14%) were in the non-surgical group (median age: 56.7 years; median follow-up: 6.3 years) (Figure 1). Notably, CMI ablation was frequently required in both the anatomical and physiologic repair groups. Clinical outcome after catheter ablation

At a median follow-up of 5.6 years, 7 patients (24%) experienced tachyarrhythmia recurrence. Among these, 3 patients (1 in the anatomical repair group and 2 in the physiologic repair group) underwent a second ablation procedure. Defragmentation of the atrial substrate was performed for AF and focal atrial tachycardia in 2 patients, whereas linear ablation was added for IART in the remaining 1 patient. The tachyarrhythmias targeted during the second ablation session were distinct from those ablated during the first session. 4 patients did not undergo second ablation procedure: 1 owing to a LA thrombus and the other because of asymptomatic arrhythmia recurrence.

3 patients required pacemaker implantation because of sinus node dysfunction or AV block during the follow-up period. Additionally, 3 patients died because of cerebral hemorrhage or infection, both of which were unrelated to tachyarrhythmia and the CA procedure. Discussion

Main findings

This study documents the electrophysiological characteristics and CA outcomes of supraventricular tachycardia in patients with cc-TGA. To the best of our knowledge, this is the largest study to date detailing the procedural characteristics and outcomes of CA in this patient population. Over a 14-year period, a total of 29 patients underwent CA at our institution, with CMI ablation being necessary in many cases. Among these, only 4 patients required a second ablation procedure.

The characteristics of supraventricular tachycardia in cc-TGA

Reports of CA for atrial flutter in patients with cc-TGA are scarce. CMI-dependent circuits, as well as IARTs occurring around atriotomy incisions, have been described.9,10 In the present study, IART was identified as the predominant mechanism of supraventricular tachycardia, with most cases exhibiting CMI dependence, aligning with prior findings. Furthermore, patients with cc-TGA frequently experience significant TR associated with systemic RV dysfunction, often necessitating multiple reinterventions on the left-sided TV. Chronic TR contributes to progressive LA dilation, predisposing individuals to LA arrhythmias.10,11 This may explain the success of PVI in some cases of AF within the present cohort. Additionally, focal atrial tachycardia near the suture line of the baffle appears mechanistically similar to arrhythmias observed following atrial switch procedures in TGA.12,13 In patients with cc-TGA and situs solitus, the pulmonary outflow tract rather than the aortic root, as seen in normal hearts, is deeply situated between the atria. Recently, successful CA of atrial tachycardia has been reported in the pulmonary sinus.4 Although no such cases were observed in this study, this technique may be a viable approach in selected cases of supraventricular tachycardia in cc-TGA.

AVNRT was identified in only 1 patient without a history of surgical intervention. In patients with cc-TGA, the location of the cardiac conduction system depends on atrial arrangement,14 and accessing the coronary vein may be challenging owing to anatomical variation.15 In this case, slow pathway modification was performed caudal to the His bundle region near the ostium of the coronary vein, a site that did not significantly differ from its location in a normal heart.

The impact of surgical treatment strategies on supraventricular tachycardia

Traditionally, surgical scarring, atrial enlargement, ventricular dysfunction, and myocardial fibrosis have been recognized as key substrates for supraventricular tachycardias in adult patients with congenital heart disease. In particular, atrial switch operations (Senning or Mustard), which are no longer considered the primary surgical approach, have been associated with a high incidence of supraventricular tachycardia during long-term follow-up in individuals with transposition of the great arteries (TGA).13,16, 17, 18, 19, 20 Prior ablation studies in congenital heart disease cohorts, including those with TGA who underwent atrial switch procedures, have demonstrated a high prevalence of peri-tricuspid flutter. Additionally, recurrent peri-tricuspid flutter has been attributed to the absence of a bidirectional isthmus line block, underscoring the importance of complete conduction blockade in preventing arrhythmia recurrence. These cohort results reaffirm that ablation of the CMI, with the demonstration of the bidirectional block, is paramount for preventing the recurrence of this specific arrhythmia. This finding aligns with observations in our cohort of patients who have undergone anatomical repair. In cases of peri-tricuspid re-entry in TGA, CA is typically performed in both the LA and RA, which corresponds to CMI-dependent re-entry in cc-TGA. However, in our cohort, the transaortic approach proved to be technically challenging because of the need for catheter placement over the mitral valve via a rerouted VSD, necessitated by the presence of a ventricular switch (Rastelli procedure) rather than an arterial switch.

Furthermore, the surgical incision in anatomical repair is wider than the typical lateral incision and necessitates baffle suturing, which may increase the likelihood of focal atrial tachycardia along the baffle suture line. Prior studies have demonstrated that successful ablation sites for focal atrial tachycardia following atrial switch procedures in individuals with TGA were located immediately adjacent to the baffle suture lines.12,13,17 Given these findings, it is plausible that focal atrial tachycardia following anatomical repair in cc-TGA may share a similar underlying arrhythmogenic mechanism.

The efficacy of catheter ablation on supraventricular tachycardia in cc-TGA

According to recent ablation registry data in patients with congenital heart disease, CA for supraventricular tachycardia substrates has a reported acute success rate of 71%–81%, with less favorable outcomes observed in patients with multiple arrhythmogenic targets, non-cavotricuspid isthmus–dependent substrates, or a history of Fontan or atrial baffle procedures.21 Although our cohort consisted exclusively of patients with cc-TGA and the underlying etiologies differed from those in the registry, the success rate in our study was comparable. IART associated with cc-TGA may be a CMI-dependent atrial tachycardia, even in cases where it exhibits an atypical waveform that differs from the classic sawtooth pattern. At least 1 lifetime ablation procedure is considered a fundamental component of the therapeutic strategy, although careful procedural planning is required in post-atrial switch cases. Although integrating pharmacological therapy and device-based interventions,22 as well as surgical approaches such as the Maze procedures23,24 may be necessary, CA remains a valuable treatment modality for the long-term management of supraventricular tachycardia in cc-TGA. We acknowledge that long-term follow-up is one of the strengths of our study; however, it may also represent a limitation in terms of the potential influence of temporal changes (time period effect). Advances in ablation technology, including the introduction of contact force-sensing catheters, high-power irrigated ablation, and upgraded versions of 3D electro-anatomical mapping systems, have substantially improved the safety, efficacy, and precision of CA procedures.

Limitations

This study is limited by its single-center retrospective design, which may affect generalizability. Although 8 patients had a pacemaker or cardiac resynchronization therapy with defibrillator implanted, arrhythmia monitoring was conducted primarily on an outpatient and symptom-driven basis; therefore, asymptomatic AF or IART may have been missed. Additionally, the small number of patients with specific arrhythmias and changes in ablation technology over the observation period may have influenced outcomes. However, cc-TGA is a rare condition, and this study represents one of the largest series on supraventricular tachycardia in cc-TGA. Accumulating such case series is essential for clarifying the efficacy and safety of CA in rare congenital heart diseases.

Conclusion

The onset timing of supraventricular tachycardia in cc-TGA may vary depending on the treatment pathway. In most cases, CA is an effective strategy for controlling supraventricular tachycardia in cc-TGA, regardless of surgical history.
