The potential impact of nonsuicidal self-injury disorder: Insights from individuals with lived experience
Authors: Gregory J. Lengel, Brooke A. Ammerman, Kerri-Anne Bell, Jason J. Washburn
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Nonsuicidal self-injury disorder (NSSI-D) is presently a condition for further study in the Diagnostic & Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5-TR) (American Psychiatric Association, 2022). While previous studies focused on NSSI-D through the lens of experts, there is a shortage of research that explores the potential impact of NSSI-D from the perspective of those most directly affected - individuals with NSSI lived experience. The present study aimed to expand this limited literature and provide a more enhanced and nuanced understanding of the potential implications and consequences of NSSI-D from lived experience viewpoints. Adults with lifetime NSSI history (N = 38) completed a semi-structured interview that surveyed perspectives on NSSI-D, including the meaning of officially recognizing NSSI-D, potential impacts of receiving an NSSI-D diagnosis, and the impact of NSSI-D on one’s decision to speak with a clinician. Results from our thematic analysis of the interview data suggested opinions about NSSI-D and its potential impact were generally positive (e.g., increased awareness, understanding, and validation, increased comfort with disclosing NSSI behavior, positive impacts on treatment, and improved functioning). Participants also highlighted potential concerns about the potential consequences of NSSI-D (e.g., negative self-perception, increased stigma, and concerns about the necessity and utility of NSSI-D), and some expressed neutral/indifferent opinions about NSSI-D. Overall, results provide valuable insights regarding potential implications and consequences of official recognition and diagnosis of NSSI-D and have relevant implications for client-clinician interactions. Results also highlight the importance and value of amplifying lived experience perspectives.

Introduction

Nonsuicidal self-injury (NSSI) is the deliberate, self-inflicted damage to body tissue without suicidal intent (International Society for the Study of Self-Injury, 2023). NSSI represents a significant public health concern, with relatively high prevalence rates among adults (4–6%; Swannell et al., 2014), emerging adults (13.4%; Swannell et al., 2014) and adolescents (12–23%; Claes et al., 2014; Swannell et al., 2014), as well as associations with negative health outcomes and problems in living. For example, NSSI is associated with physical health concerns (e.g., infection, tissue damage) and negative psychological sequelae e.g., depressive, anxiety, eating, personality and substance use disorders; Bentley et al., 2015; Glenn & Klonsky, 2013; Kerr et al., 2010; Klonsky, 2011; Wilkinson, 2013), as well as suicide ideation, attempts, and death by suicide (Joiner, 2005; Nock et al., 2006; Klonsky et al., 2013; Ribeiro et al., 2016). Moreover, NSSI contributes to distress and functional difficulties in personal, social, and occupational domains and can contribute to treatment interfering behavior (Fliege et al., 2009).

Despite its clinical and public significance, until the publication of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, fifth edition (DSM-5; American Psychiatric Association, 2013), NSSI’s only official recognition within the diagnostic manual was as a criterion for borderline personality disorder (BPD). While there is a clear relationship between NSSI and BPD, the two constructs are distinct (e.g., Glenn & Klonsky, 2013; Gratz et al., 2015; Turner et al., 2015). This limited incorporation of NSSI within the diagnostic nosology has contributed to the conflation of NSSI with BPD and has perpetuated misunderstandings about its relationship with other forms of self-harm (e.g., suicidal self-injury, excoriation, trichotillomania, and self-harm associated with developmental disabilities). Collectively, these challenges with NSSI’s current limited diagnostic representation highlight the need for broader and more accurate inclusion of NSSI within the DSM.

To meet the need for recognition and inclusion of NSSI within the diagnostic manual, multiple calls for a standalone NSSI diagnosis have been proposed (e.g., Muehlenkamp, 2005; Shaffer & Jacobson, 2009). Advocates for a nonsuicidal self-injury disorder (NSSI-D) contend that an independent NSSI diagnosis could help promote a clearer operationalization of NSSI, improve differentiation between NSSI and suicidal self-injury, and facilitate the development of new and improved assessment measures and interventions. NSSI-D could also decrease the misdiagnosis of BPD, inappropriate treatment referrals, unnecessary hospitalization, and misallocation of resources. Meanwhile, critics and skeptics of a standalone NSSI-D have argued that NSSI-D is unnecessary and inappropriate, may increase stigma, and contributes to potential unnecessary expansion of the number of mental health diagnoses (i.e., diagnostic bloat) and that NSSI is better conceptualized as a symptom or specifier, rather than an independent condition (Lengel et al., 2023).

Resulting from increasing dialogue and advocacy for a standalone NSSI diagnosis, NSSI-D was officially proposed and considered for inclusion in the development of the DSM-5 (APA, 2013; Schaffer & Jacobson, 2009). However, field trials were unsuccessful, largely due to recruitment failure and poor inter-rater reliability (Regier et al., 2013). Consequently, NSSI-D was not adopted as an official diagnosis, but rather was relegated to Section III of DSM-5 (APA, 2013) as a condition for further study where it continues to reside in the DSM-5, text revision (DSM-5-TR; APA, 2022). Subsequent research, however, has indicated mixed support for the reliability, validity, and utility of the proposed NSSI-D criteria (see Lengel et al., 2023 for a review of this literature), highlighting the need for additional research to further clarify and support NSSI-D.

Examination of NSSI-D through a professional lens has undoubtably led to valuable insights into its validity, utility, and potential consequences. For example, research in this area has highlighted the potential perceived value of a diagnosis, but also concerns and suggested modifications regarding specific aspects of the proposed criteria (i.e., Ammerman et al., 2021; Lengel & Mullins-Sweatt, 2013). However, limiting the focus to professionals excludes the perspectives of many individuals who might be most directly impacted by the adoption of NSSI-D. Importantly, we recognize that many professionals also have personal NSSI lived experience that offers meaningful perspectives beyond one’s professional expertise (see Victor et al., 2022). However, individuals with lived experience who are outside of academia and clinical practice are largely underrepresented in NSSI research and can offer unique and nuanced insights that complement and enrich the field’s understanding of the complexities surrounding NSSI-D, including the potential implications of NSSI-D (e.g., impact on daily life, stigma, willingness to disclose, relationships, mental health, treatment and recovery, and identity) (Hasking et al., 2023). By amplifying these perspectives, the field can acquire more comprehensive and diverse viewpoints, as well as empower those most impacted to contribute to, and shape, discussions about NSSI and NSSI-D, ultimately improving the construct of NSSI-D.

Although there is growing demand to include lived experience perspectives in research, to our knowledge, Lewis et al. (2017) is the only study that has examined opinions about NSSI-D and its implications from a lived experience perspective. Specifically, Lewis and colleagues (2017) surveyed 76 individuals with NSSI lived experience about their attitudes towards NSSI-D. Using free-response self-report items, participants were asked to discuss what they believed to be the advantages and disadvantages of NSSI becoming a disorder, whether they believed that NSSI should be treated by a professional, and if they would pursue treatment should NSSI-D become an official diagnosis. Thematic analysis of the participants’ survey responses identified perceived advantages of NSSI-D (i.e., enhanced understanding of NSSI, validation of the NSSI experience, facilitation of NSSI treatment, encouragement of NSSI-help-seeking, and reduction of NSSI stigma) and potential disadvantages (i.e., exacerbation of NSSI stigma and diminishment of underlying concerns).

Present study

While Lewis et al.’s (2017) noteworthy contribution to the literature provided valuable insights into lived experience perspectives on NSSI-D, opportunities for additional exploration and expansion remain. Specifically, Lewis et al. (2017) utilized a survey-based approach which did not allow for interactive dialogue with participants, potentially limiting elaboration of responses and opportunities for follow-up. Further, although Lewis et al.’s (2017) sample featured distinctive and diverse perspectives (e.g., 44.74% non-heterosexual), the sample’s overall representativeness is unclear, as no information about race/ethnicity was provided, potentially limiting the interpretability of the specific contexts and perspectives represented. Additionally, because thematic analysis involves interpretation, Lewis et al.’s (2017) findings contribute valuable insights that inherently are contextual and subjective. Additional research could expand on and complement these findings through additional perspectives. Finally, since the study was published in 2017, it is plausible that there has been an evolution in viewpoints regarding NSSI and NSSI-D. Accordingly, the present study aimed to build upon Lewis et al.’s (2017) findings to provide more enhanced and nuanced understandings of the potential validity, utility, and implications of NSSI-D from individuals with lived experience. To do so, we pursued a diverse sample of individuals with NSSI history and utilized a semi-structured interview to expand upon and provide further insights and clarification into lived experience perspectives about NSSI-D.

Materials and Methods

Researcher positionality

The research team consisted of three licensed psychologists with Ph.D.s in clinical psychology and a clinical psychology doctoral student, all with backgrounds and expertise in treating and researching NSSI, including expertise in diagnosis and diagnostic classification. The overall team represents diverse personal and cultural perspectives, although all reside, and were trained, in the United States. Together, these factors impacted data interpretation, based on our cultural understanding and definition of NSSI, our familiarity with the healthcare system as it exists in the U.S., and our ability to relate with the perspectives across individuals with lived experience. The authors engaged in discussions that allowed for reflection on the data and the space to challenge any assumptions that may have been present.

Participants

We recruited adults (aged 18 years and above), that were fluent in reading and speaking English and had a lifetime history of at least one NSSI episode to complete a self-report survey that was part of a larger study, with an option to express interest in participating in a follow-up semi-structured interview. We recruited potential participants online from social media platforms (e.g., NSSI-themed Reddit communities and Facebook groups) along with snowball sampling via social media. Participant recruitment involved posting invitations to participate in the study on these forums, as well as encouraging professional organizations (e.g., International Society for the Study of Self-Injury) and professionals in the field to share and re-share the recruitment advertisement on social media accounts (e.g., Facebook, Twitter/X). Specifically, 442 participants completed the larger study, while a subset (n = 38) expressed interest and completed the semi-structured interview. The subset of 38 participants with lived experience is the focus of the present study.

Materials and procedure

Participants initially completed online measures assessing demographic information, NSSI history, and NSSI-D (part of the larger data collection) and expressed interest in the follow-up semi-structured interview. They were subsequently contacted by the research team who provided additional details about the interview process and arranged a one-hour interview via Zoom, a secure video communication platform. Interviews were conducted with or without video; however, all interview audio was recorded. Audio from each interview was later transcribed verbatim using Happy Scribe and reviewed by trained members of the research team for completeness. Participants provided informed consent prior to the initial survey and the start of the interview; all procedures were approved by the second author’s Institutional Review Board. Participants completing the initial self-report survey were compensated separately, with participants of the follow-up qualitative interview receiving a US$20 gift card. Participant well-being was prioritized by the trained interviewers who assessed participant distress at the start and end of each interview, identified coping strategies, offered mood improvement activities, and provided a list of national resources. Additionally, a licensed psychologist or trained doctoral student was available for consultation.

Demographics

Demographic information, including age, sex, gender, sexual orientation, race, ethnicity, description of area in which one lives (i.e., rural, small city, suburban, urban), education level, and treatment history, was collected.

NSSI History

Participants were asked to report their history of engaging in NSSI behaviors from a list of 66 NSSI behaviors adapted from a list utilized in Lengel et al. (2022), along with an option to report additional behaviors not listed. Additionally, we surveyed first onset of NSSI behavior, age of most recent NSSI behavior, and whether they engaged in any NSSI behaviors in the past three months.

Qualitative semi-structured zoom interview

Participants completed a one-hour semi-structured qualitative interview that asked about participants’ personal NSSI history and experiences and opinions about a clinical diagnosis of NSSI (see Table 1).

Data analytic strategy

We conducted an inductive thematic analysis of participants’ interview transcripts to explore lived experience opinions regarding the meaning and potential consequences of NSSI being officially adopted as a DSM disorder, as well as potentially being diagnosed with NSSI-D. Thematic analysis was chosen for its flexibility and capacity to capture rich, detailed accounts and in-depth understanding of the participants’ diverse and complex lived experience perspectives, including the contextual nuances. This approach aligned with the study’s aim to examine the potential implications and consequences of NSSI-D from the perspectives of individuals with NSSI lived experience and was ideal for identifying and interpreting patterns and themes within the qualitative data while ensuring that participants’ unique viewpoints were emphasized. We utilized the six reflexive thematic analysis phases recommended by Braun and Clarke (2006) in an iterative fashion to guide our analysis. We used NVivo 12 software (QSR International, 2018) to facilitate the thematic analysis of the interview transcripts.

Phase 1: Familiarization with the Data

In this initial stage, the first author and multiple research assistants actively and repeatedly read each transcript, exploring and recording ideas related to potential meaning and trends within responses. Multiple reviewers were utilized to ensure a diversity of ideas. Care was taken to approach this organically and without presupposition of potential themes identified by extant literature.

Phase 2: Initial code generation

Next, the first author created preliminary codes to assist in the later generation of themes, transcripts were reviewed again, and statements from each were organized by these codings.

Phase 3: Searching for themes

The first author then met with the co-authors to begin exploring, deliberating, arranging, and actively interpreting codes into possible themes, considering the entire dataset.

Phase 4: Reviewing themes

The authors then continued to deliberate, explore, and discuss the preliminary themes as well as to generate potential thematic maps to better identify relations between themes, and the codes and dataset as a whole. We also began to explore combining overlapping themes and considering whether broader themes could be broken into separate themes.

Phase 5: Defining and naming themes

All authors further reviewed, deliberated, revised, and identified sub-themes, being mindful of clarity and coherence of the themes and their structure. We eventually agreed upon the final thematic structure reported in the present study, agreed to labels that best describe each theme and subtheme, and then identified exemplar quotes to better communicate each theme and sub-theme.

Phase 6: Producing the report

All authors collaborated to report and represent the results of the thematic analysis.

Following the guidance of Braun and Clarke (2006; 2020), we utilized a reflexive process and worked through each step non-linearly and recursively. We approached the identification of themes based on their significance and potential relevance to the overall research question, as opposed to frequency, and intentionally aimed to remain flexible as well as to avoid arbitrary rigidity and rules, use of an explicit coding framework or codebooks, and overprioritization of inter-rater reliability, which could potentially impose unnecessary constraints on the analytic process. We acknowledge that less frequently occurring responses may not be indicative of the majority within the sample. However, they may offer valuable insights into the viewpoints and perspectives of a subset of individuals within the population. There are several factors that can influence participant responses (e.g., use of follow-up questions, participants’ motivation, and rapport between interviewer and respondent). Thus, a low frequency response does not necessarily discount the possibility that similar opinions are held by others if prompted differently. It is important to note that even themes that were modestly represented in the sample may represent a subpopulation, underscoring the importance of adopting an inclusive approach and considering diverse perspectives within our data.

Results

Participant demographics

Of the 38 participants in the present qualitative study, we note that demographic data were available for 33 due to an inability to link survey and interview data.1 Of the 33 participants with survey data, participant ages ranged from 18 to 38 years (M = 27.9; SD = 5.4). Twenty-two participants described themselves as cisgender male (66.7%), 21 as heterosexual (63.64%), 17 as African-American/Black (51.52%), and 26 as non-Hispanic/Latine (78.8%). The majority (93.9%) reported living in the United States at the time of data collection, while one participant was living in Canada, and one was residing in Spain. Twenty-two (66.7%) participants described their living area as “urban,” and 17 (51.5%) reported a college degree as their highest level of education. Supplementary Table 1 displays full demographic information.

Participant treatment history

All 33 participants that successfully provided demographic, treatment history, and NSSI history information on the initial survey reported a history of care for emotional, psychiatric, alcohol, and/or drug difficulties, with 26 (78.8%) participants reported that NSSI was, at some point, the focus of their care. Twenty-one (63.6%) reported receiving treatment at the time of data collection. Additional treatment history information can be found in Table 2 and Supplementary Table 2.

Participant NSSI history

Notably, of the 66 NSSI behaviors surveyed, each behavior with the exception of whipping oneself with a belt, cord, or other object causing a wound (i.e., bleeding, bruising, or scarring), was endorsed by at least one participant, with skin cutting being the most endorsed method (n = 30; 90.9%), followed by punching, hitting, slapping self (n = 25; 75.8%), pinching skin with fingers (n = 24; 72.7%), biting (n = 23; 69.7%), and burning skin with fire (n = 22; 66.7%). The age of NSSI onset ranged from 5 to 35 years old (M = 16.1; SD = 6.0). A majority (n = 28; 84.8%) also reported that they had engaged in at least one form of NSSI in the past three months. Additional participant NSSI history and characteristics are detailed in Supplementary Table 3.

Thematic analysis results

Perceived NSSI-D Positives

Our analysis of participant semi-structured interview transcripts (n = 38) found that participants made several statements that reflected positive sentiments towards NSSI-D, including the potential benefits and positive consequences of NSSI-D being officially recognized or being diagnosed with NSSI-D (see Figure 1). Thus, we identified Perceived NSSI-D Positives as a primary theme, within which we identified five subthemes.

Awareness, understanding, and validation

Several participant comments indicated belief that NSSI-D could significantly improve awareness, understanding, and validation of NSSI, both personally and societally. Additionally, several participants expressed that adoption of NSSI-D would provide validation. For example, Participant 1016 expressed, “I guess, I guess it would be some kind of validation that I’m not just like, out here on my own, you know.” This sentiment was also exemplified by the following comment:

And so, when I was properly diagnosed, that was, like, a very validating experience. Like, Oh, I’m not the only one, there is a term for this. I don’t just like…that’s not to say everyone fits in a box, but it was very validating for me. So, I imagine it could be validating for someone to be told, like, oh, you’re not crazy, this is, like, a disorder that you’re struggling with. (Participant 1019)

Additionally, some participants discussed how NSSI-D could validate NSSI as a condition and could prompt more research and professional understanding of NSSI. For instance, Participant 1017 noted:

I think on one hand, [NSSI-D] would add a lot of understanding to people, because if it’s its own separate diagnosis, I think it prompts more research on it, more people might get diagnosed with it.

Another participant compared the potential validating impact of NSSI-D to that of a substance use disorder:

I think the only thing would…that the only difference would be to validate the experience of it as an addiction, as opposed to just, like, a symptom. Kind of, like, substance use disorder, where it’s, like, validating that this has become, like, a biologically or psychologically hardwired behavior. (Participant 1019)

Results also indicated a belief that NSSI-D could enhance understanding of the nature of NSSI. For instance, Participant 1061 expressed:

It’s going to make you to know how to carry yourself around other persons. Going to make you realize what you’re passing through. Going to make you to realize that you have a problem that needs to be solved. Going to give you a broader view of what the whole NSSI is all about.

Moreover, a few participants notably emphasized that NSSI-D could enhance awareness and understanding of NSSI acknowledging its occurrence beyond BPD and communicate the importance and utility of differentiating NSSI from BPD. For example, Participant 1070 stated:

Like I said, just validating people who are struggling, who might think that if someone says, “Oh, only people who have borderline personality disorder self-harm.” Like, if someone who’s young and who’s trying to figure out what’s wrong with them, what’s going on, they might say, “Oh, only people who do who have borderline personality disorder. That’s what the DSM says. Only those people self-harm. Is that what I have?” Like, they might get confused. They might feel like I have this larger thing. I mean, I’m not saying no one would have borderline, but it’s like you get confused, you don’t know what’s up. And having that diagnosis would probably help people kind of, I don’t know, identify themselves more, or it would, I don’t know, be less confusing. I think it would help people identify their issue and then stop them from kind of catastrophizing, “What else do I have?” if it’s like its own thing.

Increased comfort with discussing and disclosing NSSI

Over half of the participants (n = 25) responded affirmatively when asked if including NSSI-D in the DSM would positively impact their willingness to speak with a professional. Similarly, several participants reported that the official inclusion of NSSI-D in DSM or being diagnosed with NSSI-D would have a beneficial impact on their comfort and confidence in discussing and disclosing NSSI to family, friends, and coworkers, as well as professionals. For example, Participant 1073 stated, “…it will make it a little bit easier for me to express myself,” while another expressed:

Yes, and know how to address people in the society and people also address me in NSSI because there are some ways that you behave people run away from you. If actually I’m being officially diagnosed, maybe in the workplace, maybe in schools, I would actually identify myself as someone with NSSI with official diagnosis, you understand? (Participant 1080)

Expanding on this point, several participants noted that NSSI-D would increase confidence in disclosing NSSI behavior due to the belief that professionals will be more informed about NSSI. For example, Participant 1061 explained:

I would say it will change personally, I will know I will have some confidence in this professional I’m talking to, because if someone that is into the system, he knows or the person knows how to handle it, everything I say to the person will be kept confidential. So I’ll have confidence in that person, and I’ll know it’s been approved. I can’t just see anyone on the road and start talking up to the person or start talking to the person. So you said something DSM, something is legalized or is approved. So I’ll have some level of confidence and trust that everything that is said to this person will be confidential and the person can help me. To some extent, the person can be of help.

Treatment-related benefits

Participants expressed that NSSI-D might have a positive impact on intervention and treatment-seeking behavior. Several participants noted that NSSI-D could increase recognition that one’s NSSI behavior is severe enough to warrant needing help and increase motivation to seek treatment. For example, Participant 1021 stated, “If I am diagnosed, it means I need help, so all I would do is try to find help.” Adding more context, Participant 1072 noted that a diagnosis of NSSI-D would help them see their NSSI as a problem: “Well, the only impact I think it might have is maybe me starting to see it as a problem. Maybe starting to see it as a problem, turning to…therapy, going in for classes.” Further, Participant 1087 explained, “[NSSI-D becoming an official diagnosis] would increase the chances of one looking for treatment.”

In addition to motivating treatment-seeking behavior, several participants made statements that conveyed belief that NSSI-D could increase access to care, as exemplified by Participant 1020’s statement, “Yeah, especially for people who don’t have a super clear diagnosis, but need treatment. I think that would lead to more access, so I think that’s good.” Similarly, one participant notably expressed hope that NSSI-D would also improve insurance coverage:

So, the impact of considering it as a mental disorder, really goes a long way to affect many person. Because when it affects things, maybe as I said earlier, the insurance or maybe afterwards we are special then given other special mental care and the cost has to be on the Medicare also, so it’s going to really affect a lot financial wise, health wise, or maybe psychological wise as well…You get better insurance coverage even though it might take you longer time. (Participant 1072)

Many participants also communicated that NSSI-D would help facilitate treatment and lead to improvements in interventions, as demonstrated by the following responses:

I think treatment for it would probably be quite a bit better because right now, I feel like it’s almost this broad thing where it’s like people are just like, stop cutting yourself. Go for a walk or something. But I feel like if it was an actual diagnosis, people would be like, what are ways we can treat it? And then you would probably kind of get more of a, I guess, structure for how to treat it, like, a normal way to treat it and stuff like that. That could be really helpful for people who do self-harm. (Participant 1006)

I think it will be of a great help, and it will help medical professionals to know the direction they are going to be looking in terms of effective treatment. So I think it would be a great development. (Participant 1058)

Some participants also indicated that the diagnosis could lead to better training and knowledge for providers. For example:

Especially, like, professionals, because I don’t think all mental health professionals, all psychiatrists and therapists, not all of them are probably properly trained on how to deal with NSSI and self-harm, just in general. Maybe they’re not really, they don’t understand it. So I think having a specific diagnosis helps them to become educated and to learn more about it. And then once someone shows up in their office engaging in NSSI, they say, “Oh, well, here is a diagnostic criteria”, or “Here’s a label I can give you at the very least to help ground you, treat you,” whatever. So I think professionals will help a lot. (Participant 1070)

Furthermore, some participants also made statements that communicated increased hope and optimism in the effectiveness of treatment or that there would be a solution to their difficulties. For instance, when responding to a question about what it would mean to be diagnosed with NSSI-D, Participant 1064 expressed, “…maybe I know that my problem will be solved;” and Participant 1086 stated, “I got the hope that I’ll get cured someday.”

Stigma reduction

In addition to providing validation, understanding, and awareness regarding NSSI, several participants communicated that they anticipate NSSI-D will affect public perception and help reduce NSSI-related stigma. For example, Participant 1070 noted:

I think…it’s important to destigmatize it and to help people feel comfortable talking about it too, I think, because I think if with the stigma surrounding it right now, kids teenagers or older teenagers and adults who are going through it might feel too ashamed or too embarrassed to talk about it and seek treatment. So, I think generally recognizing it as a disorder and also just destigmatizing it would be really important to help people feel more comfortable and seek treatment.

Some participants framed stigma reduction in terms of a reduction in inappropriate stereotyping of NSSI and individuals who engage in NSSI, as demonstrated by Participant 1006’s comment:

[Stigma will] decrease, because I think if there was a diagnosis, I think a lot more people who are suffering would get diagnosed and not just the vocal portion, if that makes sense. So, I feel like the vocal portion or the vocal portion would be, like, the stereotypical teenage girl. And then people are like, well, it’s teenage girls looking for attention. But if you go online, there are a lot of teenage girls, but it’s also people of different races, people of different genders, people of different orientations, people from all walks of life are doing this. And I feel like if more people got diagnosed, it could show, like, hey, look, it’s not just the stigma of these teenage girls. It’s everybody. It’s a lot of people.

Participants also reported that NSSI-D could help in reducing negative judgement from others. For example, in reference to the impact of NSSI-D and professional judgement, Participant 1088 stated:

Positive in the sense that maybe doctors can talk about it without the feeling that they are judging you. For example, people now don’t just do for having depression. Maybe ten years ago you could say that you have depression, and the stigma was bigger. Maybe, I think that being diagnosed with non-suicide behaviors as a diagnosis, it will mean that that person is not having those ideas on purpose in a healthy mind, but rather that person needs help. So, in the eyes of a professional, it will… I think in my opinion, it will help a lot to the professional to not judge the patient, but rather try to help.

Improvement in functioning

Participants also articulated that an NSSI-D could potentially contribute positive outcomes related to their social, occupational, and academic functioning, as well as decreased personal distress and increased positive affect. Socially, participants suggested that NSSI-D would help facilitate improvement in interpersonal relationships by increasing trust as well as help repair relationships. For example, Participant 1046 noted, “I think I’ll be more social instead of being introvert. And I think I can get trust, especially for my family, and I can also advise others;” and Participant 1047 expressed, “Like bringing back, even if not all, I feel bringing back a lot of things that I’ve actually lost, like family, friends...”

Further, a few participants noted potential improvement in occupational and academic domains. For example, Participant 1047 followed up their aforementioned statement with, “…[bringing back] at least a very good job, I can actually still go back to school and other stuff like that.” Moreover, a few participants expressed that they would experience joy, have improved mood, and reduced distress. For example:

It should be recognized right now because I would feel so happy. I would just jump on and I would just shout for rain, at least I’ve been part of something big. I would feel so happy and I would feel a little bit fulfilled if I should use that word. (Participant 1051)

Additionally, Participant 1064 stated, “I think I’ll feel relief.”

Perceived NSSI-D Negatives

Despite the anticipated benefits and positives noted above, along with several participants expressing being unable to identify potential negative impacts of NSSI-D, some participants communicated contrasting opinions. Accordingly, we also recognized a primary theme of perceived negatives or concerns related to the potential DSM recognition, or diagnosis, of NSSI-D, as represented in the following three sub-themes.

Negative self-perception

Some participants expressed statements that suggested NSSI-D might contribute to a negative self-perception. For example, one participant exemplified the concern of overidentifying and becoming attached to the label if given a diagnosis, as opposed to seeing NSSI as a temporary problem:

Yeah, I mean guess when you give people label, they get attached to it. They see it as like a fundamental part of themselves. Like I don’t know I feel like I already see that in people.... I guess it’s just like I could see people getting attached to it or thinking it’s like if you give a label, some people might think this is like a fundamental part of who they are rather than just like a temporary problem. (Participant 1014)

Additional ways participants expressed how NSSI-D could potentially contribute to negative self-perception included the diagnosis leading to feeling like something is wrong with them, as demonstrated by the following comment:

I think for some people, they might be like, oh, there’s something wrong with me, or if I have this, then I’m sick, or potentially there’s that, or maybe the fear of having to deal with this for a large portion of your life. I think that’s a big thing. (Participant 1017)

Similarly, a few participants suggested that NSSI-D could lead to feelings of shame. For example, Participant 1059 noted:

I will feel ashamed, this type of disease is similar to someone who is being diagnosed or who is being treated by a psychiatrist. It’s a mental problem, someone who is mentally deranged.

Negative social perception/increased stigma

In addition to a negative self-perception, the interviewees also reported that an NSSI-D could potentially contribute to negative public perceptions and stigma.

Stigma/misunderstanding

Some participants made comments that suggested NSSI-D could contribute to stigmatization and misunderstanding of those diagnosed. For instance, Participant 1017 expressed:

… I feel like maybe it might add, like, a bit of a stigma around it just because it’s like, oh, it’s like its own diagnosis. So, if you get diagnosed, there must be something wrong with you. … if it’s its own thing, then, yeah, there might be more stigma around it where people would be like, oh, there must be something wrong with you, or you’re sick.

Concerns about public records and misdiagnosis

Like concerns about stigma and judgement, a few participants expressed concerns related to the negative impact caused by having an NSSI-D on one’s medical record. For example, one participant expressed apprehension about their diagnosis being made public, leading to feelings of shame or embarrassment, and highlighting sensitivity to the potential negative societal perception and stigma associated with NSSI:

That’s the only negative impact I might be seeing is if it’s too public or something. I don’t know, but if people’s information are being broadcast around so people kind of like seeing it as being ashamed or something, so that’s the only negative impact I might be seeing of apart from that, it’s a very good opinion. (Participant 1013)

Similarly, Participant 1016 voiced concern about one’s diagnostic status being documented in some form of record that is accessible to others prior to personally meeting them: “I guess the thought of it like being in some kind of record that someone could see before they even meet me is a little bit stressful, you know.” Furthermore, one participant noted the potential harm that could come from misdiagnosis and drew parallels between NSSI-D and other potentially stigmatizing diagnoses, highlighting concern that providers might treat one differently based on one’s diagnosis:

Yes. And I mean, it’s kind of the same with, like, bipolar disorder. Bipolar disorder, it can have a very negative impact if you’re misdiagnosed. It can stay on your mental health records forever. And doctors treat you differently. Even if you go to the doctor for any other thing, they see that you have a bipolar diagnosis, they treat you differently. So if someone were to be diagnosed with an NSSI disorder, I can see how it could have those sort of issues that bipolar disorder has in a way where it’s, like, this person is going to do this behavior because they have this diagnosis. So it might cause some problems, like, when someone goes to see doctors that aren’t mental health professionals…. In the same way, it’s like stigmatizing just to have a label. Or it could be. (Participant 1070)

Collectively, there appeared to be concerns about the societal consequences and potential stigmatization associated with mental health labels.

Questioning the necessity, validity, and utility of NSSI-D

A few participants made comments that brought into question the validity, necessity, and potential clinical utility of an NSSI-D in the DSM. For example, Participant 1012 expressed, “The inclusion of NSSI, for me, is uncalled for.” Also exemplifying this theme, Participant 1020 commented:

For me, I get why NSSI being a classified disorder would be useful for research sake or for people who maybe don’t meet criteria for other things. I think generally, though, it’s not something in and of itself, totally. I think a lot of people who suffer from depression or PTSD or anxiety or whatever, they also self-harm as a way of coping. I think of it more as coping than, like, a disorder. And yes, it’s like, I don’t know, dysfunctional, but I think it comes from something else.

Further, another participant discussed how NSSI-D might be useful for research, but questioned whether NSSI standalone exists outside of other psychopathology to warrant its own diagnostic classification, questioning the necessity of NSSI-D:

…I’m curious if there are clinical instances where NSSI cannot be…is not related to other disorders, because from what I understand, you know, NSSI could happen a lot of people. But commonly it’s found in people who have depression, people who have borderline personality disorder, and people who have PTSD and/or dissociative disorders. So, I guess my question…like, you can have NSSI and have panic disorder or have anxiety, but my question is just what is the population that is not meeting for any of these disorder criteria, but is also having NSSI?... I guess what I’m trying to get out is, like, what is the diagnostical significance or people who have this NSSI disorder who also don’t fall into, like, MDD or BPD or something?...It’s just, like, there doesn’t have to be a diagnosis for every symptom, necessarily. (Participant 1011)

Relatedly, one participant commented on the challenges of defining NSSI behavior for an NSSI-D:

I question, what would you like to include as a self-harm behavior? For example, alcoholism and drug addiction will perfectly fit the system of self-harm, non-suicidal, I think. Not only cutting etc.... Also, I must add that in order to focus in conversations or thinking, sometimes I self-harm, like picking my nails with the other and even bleeding causing blood on my fingernails, just because that harm and that finger play keeps me focused. Would you include that in a non-suicide behavior? (Participant 1088)

Finally, a few participants expressed concerns about NSSI-D’s utility in informing treatment planning For example, Participant 1019 noted,

…once you have a diagnosis that should inform your course of treatment. So, I don’t think it helps to have just a new diagnosis or new disorder without, like, you should go to DBT or something.

and Participant 1014 expressed belief that the diagnostic label itself would not be relevant:

I mean, I feel like it doesn’t really matter what you’re diagnosed with, it matters whether or not the help you’re receiving helps you to develop better habits, so like the label or the diagnosis don’t really matter.

Collectively, results suggest that some individuals with lived experience may view the diagnosis as unnecessary or unhelpful.

Neutral/indifferent opinions about NSSI-D

In addition to several statements that fit the themes of positive and negative consequences of NSSI-D, we found that several participants expressed neutral and indifferent opinions regarding the potential impact of NSSI on their lives. For example, many participants suggested that NSSI-D being officially adopted or receiving a diagnosis of NSSI-D would have little or no impact on one’s life, perspectives about NSSI, behaviors, or treatment, or oneself in general. For instance, participants made statements such as “Nothing going to change about it [how one feels about NSSI]” (Participant 1060); “I don’t think it would really impact it that much at all. I think it wouldn’t really change anything for me” (Participant 1006), and:

I mean, I feel like it doesn’t really matter what you’re diagnosed with, it matters whether or not the help you’re receiving helps you to develop better habits, so like the label or the diagnosis don’t really matter. (Participant 1014)

Also of note, regarding the potential impact on stigma, one participant stated:

I don’t know that it would affect the general population because it’s so stigmatized, and just most media portrayals are what they are, but I would like to think it would change things in the medical community. I guess. So, I don’t know. (Participant 1016)

This statement suggests that NSSI-D might not have a large impact on changing peoples’ perspectives on NSSI in general. Another notable comment highlighted mixed feelings about NSSI-D, suggesting that the timing of the diagnosis might be relevant:

I don’t think it would change a lot because I’m actually very self-aware. Maybe in the past it would have helped me to know that I was self-harming.... Maybe at that point it would have served me in order to cope with the things better. But nowadays, I’m more aware of everything. (Participant 1088)

Collectively, this suggests that some individuals with NSSI lived experience might be indifferent or ambivalent about NSSI-D and believe that NSSI-D might not have a significant impact on their lives.

Discussion

Debate about the validity, utility, and necessity of a standalone NSSI diagnosis has occurred for decades and has only increased with the inclusion of NSSI-D in Section III of the DSM-5 (APA, 2013). Recently, several studies have empirically examined the validity and utility of NSSI-D and its proposed criteria as well as explored potential ways to improve and revise the diagnosis for future adoption. However, relatively few studies have explored the potential impact of NSSI-D from the unique perspective of those most directly impacted by the potential inclusion of NSSI-D in DSM—individuals with NSSI lived experience. The present study aimed to expand and incrementally add to this emerging literature (i.e., Lewis et al., 2017) to provide a more enhanced and nuanced understanding of the potential validity, utility, and implications of NSSI-D from the perspective of those with lived experience.

Our thematic analysis provided valuable insights into the potential impact of officially including NSSI-D in a future DSM revision from the perspective of individuals with NSSI lived experience. Overall, participants expressed predominantly positive sentiments, suggesting that NSSI-D could increase awareness, understanding, and validation of their experiences with NSSI, increase comfort with NSSI-related disclosure and communication, foster and benefit treatment and intervention, and reduce stigma, as well as improve functioning across multiple domains of living and increase positive affect. However, participants also raised concerns about potential negative impacts, such as promoting and exacerbating negative self and public perceptions and stigma, as well as fostering overidentification with the diagnosis. Some participants questioned the validity and necessity of the diagnosis. Finally, a subset of participants expressed neutral or indifferent sentiments towards NSSI-D, suggesting that not all individuals with NSSI lived experience have strong opinions about NSSI-D or expect it to significantly impact their lives.

Notably, several of our identified themes were consistent with those identified by Lewis et al. (2017). Like Lewis et al. (2017), our most prominent themes also could be organized as advantages and disadvantages of NSSI-D. Regarding advantages, both thematic analyses identified that NSSI-D could have the benefits of contributing to increased understanding of NSSI, validation of NSSI, encouragement of treatment-seeking behavior, increased disclosure, facilitation of NSSI treatment, and reduction in NSSI stigma. The present study expanded on these themes by identifying opinions about NSSI-D’s potential to improve in personal, occupational, academic, and social functioning and further differentiate NSSI from BPD. Regarding disadvantages/negatives, both Lewis et al. (2017) and the present study identified potential concern for increased stigma. Notably, Lewis et al. (2017) identified a theme of NSSI-D leading to a diminishment of underlying concerns. While this was not a major theme in the present study, we note that one participant from the present study expressed a similar concern:

I would worry that if a person was engaging in self-injury and they just got, without further analysis, the diagnosis of, you know, self-injury disorder.... I would worry that that would kind of be the end of investigation for a therapist. Like, oh, okay, we found our problem, but maybe this problem is actually because of depression, or if it’s more related to body dysmorphic, like body dysmorphia, I would worry that other symptoms would get missed because you’ve now put this one symptom in its own box. (Participant 1019)

Although generalization is not the aim of qualitative investigation, this overlap between Lewis et al. (2017) and the present study suggests that these possible benefits and downsides of NSSI-D may be relevant across a diverse range of individuals with NSSI lived experience, potentially reinforcing their relevance. Further, the present study was able to build upon Lewis et al. (2017) with the use of a diverse sample with relatively severe NSSI history. The use of semi-structured interviews in the present study also allowed for additional enrichment and elaboration in responses, which expanded the depth of insight on these themes.

Implications

Collectively, results help clarify and incrementally expand the present literature, offering meaningful and unique insights about the potential implications of NSSI-D from the perspectives of individuals with NSSI lived experience. Moreover, the results underscore the importance of research being inclusive of lived experience perspectives and can inform clinicians on how to best interact with individuals with NSSI history. Further, results can inform continued discussions about whether to officially include NSSI-D in a future revision as they help illuminate potential consequences an NSSI-D might have on stakeholders as well as the field and the public at large.

One major takeaway from the present study is that the majority of participant sentiments about NSSI-D appeared to be positive and/or neutral. This implies that NSSI-D may not widely be seen as something that will have a negative or harmful impact on those diagnosed, further supporting official inclusion in DSM. That said, it is important to validate and be mindful of the concerns raised by even a minority of participants and work to mitigate these concerns should APA move forward with officially including NSSI-D in the DSM. To this end, our results suggest that future discussions about NSSI-D’s inclusion in DSM and revisions to NSSI-D should be mindful of potential stigma (e.g., language) and highlight that NSSI-D should not be considered a “permanent” condition. Additionally, results suggest that some individuals may have expectations about the impact of an NSSI-D for their clinical care (e.g., NSSI-D would mean that professionals will be more informed and know how to treat NSSI), that if these expectations are unmet, could lead to frustration, dissatisfaction, and decreased trust in services and providers. Professionals in the field should aim to take an active role in mitigating NSSI stigma, misinformation, and other potential harms that might come from an NSSI diagnostic classification as well as communicate accurate information about the diagnosis and intervention.

The findings also suggest clinicians and researchers would benefit from inviting those with NSSI lived experience to share their opinions about the possible inclusion of NSSI-D as well as discuss the possible consequences, positive and negative, with them. The field will benefit from the unique and diverse perspectives of this population in informing research and clinical directions. Results also suggest that clinicians should be mindful that there might be reluctance to seek help and disclose NSSI behavior due to the concern of negative judgment and stigma or that the provider lacks awareness, understanding, and knowledge about NSSI. Thus, we encourage professionals to educate themselves on NSSI, NSSI assessment, media and reporting guidelines for communicating about NSSI and the most effective way to talk with patients about NSSI.

The importance of personal and public validation associated with NSSI-D was evident for several of the participants in this study, underscoring the need for researchers, clinicians, families, and the public to validate and better understand the experiences of individuals who engage in or have a history of NSSI. Further, participants noted that NSSI-D could help contribute to a sense of identity in both positive and negative ways. Professionals should be aware of these impacts and work to help individuals with NSSI to foster an adaptive and balanced understanding of how NSSI is incorporated into one’s understanding of self.

Limitations and future directions

While the present study offers notable strengths (e.g., inclusion of lived experience perspectives, use of semi-structured interviews, and a diverse sample) that help expand the extant literature, there are notable limitations. For example, while the use of semi-structured interviews offers several advantages over free response items in a self-report survey (see Lewis et al., 2017), the use of videoconferencing across multiple interviewers with differing computing systems can lead to inconsistencies across participants (e.g., audio and connection problems, variation and inconsistency in prompting and follow up follow-up questions, and differences in rapport and ability to elicit elaborate responses). Similarly, our choice of interview questions and how they were asked might have impacted or not have elicited the full range of participant opinions and insights about NSSI-D. Moreover, participants in the present study were a subset of a larger sample who self-selected to take part in follow-up interviews. As such, this sample may have overrepresented attributes of NSSI that are likely to be more prevalent among a self-selected group (e.g., willingness to disclose NSSI, motivation, trust, and level of insight).

Additionally, while not necessarily a limitation, it is noted that the themes identified in the present study reflect the perspectives of a single, unique sample, and thus, might reflect only a small segment of the population and are sample specific. For example, our sample contained a relatively large proportion of participants who identified as men, which may not represent the NSSI-related experiences of individuals who identify with other gender identities (Bresin & Schoenleber, 2015). Accordingly, our results are not intended to be interpreted as reflective of the perspectives of all who engage in NSSI and/or might be impacted by an NSSI-D diagnosis. Similarly, themes that were developed by the research team may not fully represent the data. Thus, researchers should aim to further explore this topic with additional, diverse samples, which may provide more insight into, and possibly expand upon, our identified themes and broader lived experience perspectives on NSSI-D.

Future studies should aim to expand upon the present study. For example, given that one’s opinions and beliefs about NSSI-D might be dynamic, it would be beneficial to track opinions, perceptions, and experiences about and with NSSI-D over time, especially following the inclusion of NSSI-D in DSM should that become a reality. It might also be helpful to explore how factors such as help-seeking behaviors, treatment outcomes, and interpersonal interactions influence opinions about NSSI-D over time. Additionally, the present study highlighted the benefits of utilizing a lived experience sample. Given the concern from participants about the impact of NSSI-D on the opinions (both positive and negative) of others with no NSSI history, it could be beneficial to investigate the reactions, attitudes, and opinions about NSSI and individuals who engage in NSSI among those without an NSSI history.

Furthermore, while our study included participants from diverse backgrounds, future research could more explicitly investigate potential cultural variations in, and how intersecting identifies might influence, perceptions, and beliefs about NSSI-D. Such work could provide insight into potential social implications with officially recognizing NSSI-D. Additionally, while our sample included individuals with significant NSSI histories, we did not specifically recruit individuals who meet the current proposed criteria for NSSI-D. To further validate our findings, as well as better understand how NSSI-D might impact those who may receive the diagnosis, researchers should consider replicating the present study using a sample of individuals who meet NSSI-D criteria.

Finally, it would be helpful for future studies to investigate some of the identified themes in more depth. For example, similar methodology could be utilized to further examine factors that contribute to a reluctance to disclose NSSI history and factors that increase help-seeking behavior. Additionally, researchers could explore the resources and strategies that might help people with lived experience increase their understanding, awareness, and validation of NSSI.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the present study highlights viewpoints and insights of individuals with NSSI lived experience regarding the potential impact and implications of NSSI-D. Our participants offered diverse perspectives, ranging from hopeful expectation of potential positives of NSSI-D to concerns and reservations about potential negatives, as well as neutral and indifferent opinions. The findings illustrate the nuance and complexities of an NSSI-D diagnosis, emphasizing the importance and benefit of including the perspectives of those with lived experience in research and discussions regarding NSSI-D. As discussion and debate about NSSI-D continues, it is imperative for the field to be inclusive of and to consider the thoughts, ideas, and concerns of individuals with lived experience.
