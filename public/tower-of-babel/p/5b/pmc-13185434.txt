Omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acid exposure and cardiovascular outcomes in dialysis: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Arveen Shokravi, Sohat Sharma, Rishav Singh, Jayant Seth, GB John Mancini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPatients with dialysis-dependent chronic kidney disease (CKD) have a high cardiovascular burden, prompting interest in fish oils or long-chain omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (n-3 PUFAs) as potential risk-reducing therapies in this population.MethodsWe conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of studies in adults receiving dialysis that assessed associations between n-3 PUFA supplementation, baseline levels, or dietary intake and CV outcomes, or all-cause mortality. Hazard ratios (HRs) were pooled using random-effects models.ResultsTwelve studies met inclusion criteria. In hemodialysis-dependent CKD, fish oil supplementation lowered cardiovascular events by 44% (HR 0.56; 95% CI 0.46–0.68) and myocardial infarction by 48% (HR 0.52; 95% CI 0.34–0.78). Higher baseline n-3 PUFA levels were associated with a 31% reduction in all-cause mortality (HR 0.69; 95% CI 0.54–0.88). Higher dietary n-3 PUFA intake showed a non-significant trend toward lower all-cause mortality (HR 0.92; 95% CI 0.79–1.08).ConclusionIn dialysis-dependent CKD, higher n-3 PUFA exposure through fish oil supplementation or higher baseline levels was associated with fewer cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality. Appropriately dosed n-3 PUFA supplementation represents a promising cardiovascular risk reduction strategy in dialysis-dependent CKD, although confirmatory randomized trials are warranted.KEYWORDS: Fish oil, omega 3 fatty acid, dialysis, hemodialysis, cardiovascular outcomes, systematic review, PISCES trial, omega 3

Introduction

Patients with dialysis-dependent chronic kidney disease (CKD) have an extremely high cardiovascular (CV) burden, with CV mortality rates 10 to 20 times higher than the general population [1,2]. Although statins are the cornerstone of lipid-lowering therapy in the general population and pre-dialysis CKD, large randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have demonstrated that statin initiation after the onset of hemodialysis does not significantly reduce atherosclerotic CV disease (ASCVD) events [3,4]. This has led to increasing interest in other therapies, including long-chain omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (n-3 PUFAs).

Long-chain n-3 PUFAs, particularly eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), are a subclass of PUFAs with established cardioprotective effects. EPA and DHA lower circulating triglycerides by reducing hepatic production of very-low-density lipoprotein and enhancing clearance of triglyceride-rich chylomicrons [5,6]. EPA and DHA are incorporated into cell membranes, altering membrane fatty acid composition, and decreasing inflammatory milieu [7]. n-3 PUFAs additionally stabilize myocardial membranes, reducing the susceptibility to arrhythmias [8]. These combined actions provide a biologic rationale for evaluating n-3 PUFAs as potential cardioprotective therapies in dialysis populations.

This was explored in the recently published PISCES trial, which showed that high-dose fish oil supplementation significantly reduced the risk of serious CV events compared to placebo in patients receiving hemodialysis [9]. However, when viewed alongside the broader literature, evidence from supplementation trials, biomarker studies, and dietary intake cohorts remains fragmented. To address this, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis across three domains of n-3 PUFA exposure: 1) supplementation, 2) baseline levels, and 3) dietary intake patterns, assessing their associations with CV outcomes and all-cause mortality in dialysis-dependent CKD.

Methods

This review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses 2020 guidelines.

A search of MEDLINE and Embase from database inception to 12 November 2025 was completed with keywords including “omega-3 fatty acid” or “poly unsaturated fatty acid” or “docosahexaenoic acid” or “eicosapentaenoic acid” or “fish oil” or “icosapent ethyl” AND “dialysis” or “hemodialysis” or “peritoneal dialysis” or “end stage renal disease.” To supplement electronic database searching, we conducted gray literature and citation-based searching, including screening the reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews to identify additional eligible texts. The full search strategies are located in supplementary tables S1-S4. Search results were uploaded to Covidence where title and abstract screening and subsequently full-text review were completed independently by two reviewers, with discrepancies resolved through consensus.

Studies of interest included RCTs and observational studies that: (1) enrolled adults receiving maintenance dialysis (hemodialysis and/or peritoneal dialysis), or reported dialysis-specific data when mixed kidney populations were included; (2) evaluated omega-3 fatty acid exposure through supplementation or reported baseline omega-3 levels or dietary intake patterns; and (3) reported all-cause mortality and/or cardiovascular outcomes (including cardiovascular mortality, myocardial infarction, stroke, heart failure outcomes, or composite cardiovascular events). To enable consistent pooling, we included only studies reporting hazard ratios (HRs) (or providing sufficient data to derive an HR). We excluded studies limited to pediatric populations, non-dialysis populations without separable dialysis data, and reviews, editorials, case reports, or conference abstracts.

Of note, because exposure definitions and measurement approaches varied across studies, pooled estimates for these exposure domains should be interpreted primarily as reflecting the direction and statistical significance of the association rather than precise effect magnitudes.

Using the meta package (v8.2–1) in R (v4.5.2) within RStudio, random-effects models with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) were used to generate pooled HRs with 95% confidence intervals [10]. Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q test (α = 0.05). Heterogeneity was quantified with the I2 statistic (I2 < 25% = low, 25–49% = moderate, ≥50% = substantial heterogeneity). Tau-squared (τ2) was calculated to display in between the studied variances. Further exploration of sources of heterogeneity was not performed because the number of studies contributing to each pooled analysis was insufficient to permit reliable investigation.

Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool for RCTs and the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale for observational trials [11,12].

Results

After screening and full-text review, 12 studies met the inclusion criteria and did not meet exclusion criteria [9,13–23]. Of note, three observational studies were excluded because outcomes were not reported as HRs [24–26]. The study selection process is illustrated in the PRISMA diagram (Figure 1). Summary of the baseline information of the included trials can be found in Supplementary Table S5.

Risk of bias

Methodological quality was high overall, with most studies judged to be at low risk of bias. Nearly all randomized controlled trials demonstrated low risk across all Cochrane RoB 2 domains, aside from one trial with some concerns related to selective reporting. Observational studies were of high quality, with Newcastle-Ottawa Scale scores between 7 and 9 out of 9, reflecting appropriate cohort selection, comparability, and outcome assessment. Full risk of bias results are provided in Supplementary Tables S6 and S7.

N-3 PUFA supplementation

Of the four trials that examined the effect of n-3 PUFA supplementation in patients receiving hemodialysis, three were RCTs and one was an observational study. The PISCES trial by Lok et al. (2025) randomized adults on maintenance hemodialysis to receive 4 g/day of fish oil (1.6 g of EPA and 0.8 g of DHA) compared to a corn oil placebo. The RCT by Svensson et al. (2006) compared 1.7 g/day of fish oil (45% EPA and 37.5% DHA) to olive oil placebo for secondary CV prevention in patients on chronic hemodialysis. Lok et al. (2012) enrolled patients with new synthetic arteriovenous hemodialysis grafts and compared fish oil (4 g per day, containing a minimum of 1.6 g EPA and 0.8 g DHA) to a corn oil placebo. The observational cohort study by Inoue et al. (2015) evaluated the association between EPA (majority of patients receiving 1.8 g/day) and survival in patients receiving maintenance hemodialysis.

Two RCTs evaluated the effect of fish oil supplementation on CV events and found a statistically significant 44% reduction compared with control (pooled HR 0.56; 95% CI 0.46–0.68; I2 = 0%) (Figure 2, Panel A). Lok et al. (2025) defined a CV event as the composite of CV mortality, nonfatal MI, peripheral vascular disease leading to amputation, or nonfatal stroke, while Lok et al. (2012) defined a CV event as CV mortality, stroke, peripheral vascular disease, myocardial infarction, or congestive heart failure. Given differences in composite outcome definitions across trials, this pooled estimate should be interpreted cautiously, primarily as reflecting the directionality and general magnitude of the effect rather than a precise quantitative estimate.

Estimates were derived using a random-effects model; hazard ratios less than 1 favor fish oil. Horizontal bars represent 95% confidence intervals.

Two RCTs evaluated the effect of fish oil supplementation on myocardial infarction and found a statistically significant 48% reduction compared with control (pooled HR 0.52; 95% CI 0.34–0.78; I2 = 9.7%) (Figure 2, Panel B).

Two RCTs evaluated the effect of fish oil supplementation on stroke and found no significant difference compared with control (pooled HR 0.83; 95% CI 0.14–4.76; I2 = 81%) (Figure 2, Panel C). Svensson et al. (2006) reported a non-significant effect with wide confidence intervals (HR 2.23; 95% CI 0.58–8.64), while Lok et al. (2025) demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in strokes compared to placebo (HR 0.37; 95% CI 0.18–0.76).

Two RCTs evaluated the effect of fish oil supplementation on all-cause mortality and found no significant difference compared to placebo (pooled HR 0.91; 95% CI 0.78–1.06; I2 = 0%) (Figure 2, Panel D). Lok et al. (2025) showed a non-significant trend toward lower mortality and nearly reached statistical significance (HR 0.89; 95% CI 0.73–1.01), whereas Svensson et al. (2006) showed a non-significant trend toward higher mortality with wide confidence intervals (HR 1.12; 95% CI 0.69–1.83). Only one observational cohort study by Inoue et al. (2015) met the inclusion criteria. In the fully adjusted, post-matching model, EPA supplementation was associated with a lower risk of all-cause mortality (HR 0.29, 95% CI 0.09–0.81).

N-3 PUFA level

Five observational studies evaluated the association between relatively high compared to low n-3 PUFA levels and all-cause mortality in patients receiving dialysis. Two studies assessed erythrocyte DHA levels [17,20], one study evaluated total long-chain n-3 PUFA levels in erythrocytes [16], and two studies analyzed plasma or serum long-chain n-3 PUFA levels [18,19]. Across studies, higher n-3 PUFA levels were consistently associated with lower mortality risk, with three of the five studies reporting statistically significant associations. In a pooled random-effects model, relatively higher levels were associated with a significant 31% reduction in all-cause mortality (pooled HR 0.69; 95% CI 0.54–0.88; I2 = 44.9%) (Figure 3). Definitions of high versus low n-3 PUFA levels varied between studies and are summarized in Supplementary Table S5. Given the between-study differences in exposure measurements and definitions of high versus low PUFA levels, the pooled estimate should be interpreted as reflecting the direction of association rather than the precise effect magnitude.

N-3 PUFA dietary intake

Three observational studies evaluated the association between higher versus lower dietary n-3 PUFA intake and all-cause mortality in patients receiving dialysis [21–23]. Zhang et al. (2023) studied patients receiving peritoneal dialysis and compared the highest tertile of n-3 PUFA intake assessed by 3-day dietary records with the lowest tertile, observing a non-significant trend toward decreased all-cause mortality (HR 0.85, 95% CI 0.65–1.12). Noori et al. (2011) compared the highest versus the lowest quartile of dietary n-3 PUFA intake assessed by 3-day food records with dietitian interviews in patients receiving hemodialysis and reported a similar nonsignificant direction of effect (HR 0.76, 95% CI 0.52–1.11). Saglimbene et al. (2019) compared the highest versus the lowest tertile of dietary n-3 PUFA intake assessed by the GA2LEN Food Frequency Questionnaire and found no difference in mortality across dietary intake categories (HR 1.00, 95% CI 0.88–1.13). When pooled using each study’s highest versus lowest intake categories, higher n-3 PUFA intake trended toward lower mortality but did not reach statistical significance (pooled HR 0.92, 95% CI 0.79–1.08; I2 = 23.6%) (Figure 4). Given the between-study differences in PUFA intake scales and definitions of high versus low intake, the pooled estimate should be interpreted as reflecting the direction of association rather than the precise effect magnitude.

Discussion

In this systematic review and meta-analysis of 12 studies, n-3 PUFAs were associated with favorable cardiovascular profiles in adult patients receiving dialysis across three complementary domains of exposure: supplementation, baseline levels, and dietary intake. n-3 PUFA supplementation in hemodialysis patients significantly reduced the composite of CV events and myocardial infarction, higher baseline n-3 PUFA levels were associated with lower all-cause mortality, and higher dietary intake of n-3 PUFAs showed a statistically non-significant trend toward lower mortality. Importantly, these evidence domains are biologically and methodologically distinct, which may contribute to differences in effect size and statistical significance. Causal inference is most appropriate for the supplementation of randomized trials, whereas baseline-level and dietary analyses are observational and should be interpreted as associative signals rather than evidence of equivalent strength or direct treatment effects. Nonetheless, the overall direction of findings across domains suggests a consistent signal toward potential benefit.

While statins are highly effective for primary and secondary prevention in the general population and are recommended in earlier stages of CKD, their benefits have not translated when therapy is initiated after progression to dialysis [3,4]. CV events in dialysis are often driven by nonatherosclerotic mechanisms such as arrhythmias or heart failure rather than classic plaque rupture [27], with acute myocardial infarction only contributing to 5% of mortality [28]. The predominance of risk factors beyond LDL-carrying cholesterol particles, such as mineral and bone metabolism disorders and volume overload, may also blunt the relative benefit of statins in this population [27,29]. Hyperphosphatemia may blunt the efficacy of statins by promoting endogenous cholesterol synthesis via HMG-CoA reductase activation, further limiting statin benefit [30]. Consistent with the mechanistic and RCT data, KDIGO guidelines now suggest that statins or statin-ezetimibe combinations should not be initiated de novo in dialysis-dependent CKD [31].

Against the limited data from earlier n-3 PUFA trials in hemodialysis, PISCES is one of the first RCTs that was clearly powered to detect a reduction in CV outcomes in this population. Prior RCTs were constrained by small sample sizes and shorter follow-up periods, with many evaluating surrogate outcomes rather than definite clinical endpoints. In contrast, PISCES randomized 1228 patients at 26 sites to a high dose of daily fish oil and followed participants for 3.5 years. Notably, trials showing the most favorable cardiovascular signals used higher-dose supplementation (4 g/day of n-3 PUFA) [9,14], whereas the earlier trial used lower doses [13], which may partly explain differences in observed effects across trials. PISCES demonstrated statistically significant and robust reductions in CV events, including all components of the primary endpoint: CV mortality, myocardial infarction, stroke, and peripheral vascular disease requiring amputation. Notably, the magnitude of the benefit was similar regardless of prior CV history, with an HR of 0.50 for the primary outcome in those with previous events compared to 0.55 in those without [14]. Beyond definite clinical endpoints, n-3 PUFA supplementation has been shown to reduce protein-energy wasting, improve quality of life and several physical and biochemical parameters [32]. Although the PISCES results are promising, its primary endpoint analysis may limit confidence in the magnitude of the benefit. The trial used a recurrent-events model that counted multiple CV events per patient, yielding a 43% reduction in the primary endpoint (HR 0.57). In contrast, the more conventional time-to-first-event analysis for CV events or all-cause mortality showed a smaller 27% reduction (HR 0.73). This discrepancy raises the possibility that repeat events among high-risk individuals may have contributed to the apparent treatment effect in the primary analysis. Ultimately, because PISCES contributes the majority of the statistical weight across the meta-analytic RCT outcomes, the overall signal of benefit should be interpreted as largely PISCES-driven, underscoring the need for confirmatory, adequately powered RCTs with harmonized cardiovascular endpoints.

Interestingly, across the general population, RCTs have produced inconsistent results regarding the CV benefit of n-3 PUFAs. In REDUCE-IT, high-dose purified EPA (icosapent ethyl 4 g/day) led to a marked reduction in major CV events [33], whereas the STRENGTH trial, using an EPA/DHA carboxylic acid formulation at an equivalent dose in a comparable statin-treated population, showed no benefit and was stopped for futility [34]. Other large prevention trials using lower-dose EPA/DHA supplements, such as ASCEND, also reported neutral results [35]. Taken together, these findings suggest that n-3 PUFA efficacy in the general population is highly dependent on formulation, dose, and background therapy, rather than a uniform effect. Patients receiving dialysis often have intrinsically lower baseline EPA and DHA levels than those in the general population [9]. This may be driven by a decreased dietary intake due to restrictive renal diets and poor appetite [36,37]. Supplementation may therefore shift patients from a deficiency to a more physiologic range in which antiarrhythmic, anti-inflammatory, and triglyceride-lowering effects are more likely to translate into clinical benefit. In contrast, other non-dialysis populations may have higher baseline n-3 PUFA status, where additional supplementation provides a smaller marginal benefit, which may partly explain why benefits outside the dialysis setting have been more variable.

Limitations

This study has to be interpreted in the context of its limitations. First, although searches of MEDLINE and Embase, as well as supplementary gray literature and citation-based searching, were performed, standalone searches in additional citation-index databases were not performed; therefore, additional eligible studies may have been missed. Similarly, only studies reporting HRs were included in this analysis, which led to the exclusion of relevant evidence using other effect measures. Next, the review protocol was not prospectively registered, which may increase the risk of selective reporting compared to prospectively registered protocols. Next, the evidence base is modest, with only three RCTs and nine observational studies, many of which enrolled relatively small cohorts of patients. The limited number of studies precluded formal assessment of publication bias. Additionally, differences in population characteristics, fish oil formulations and doses, and outcome definitions contribute to significant heterogeneity. Next, the baseline level and dietary intake analyses are observational and therefore susceptible to residual confounding, survivor bias, and measurement error; in particular, dietary intake was assessed using participant-reported food frequency questionnaires which has known limitations, and high versus low baseline n-3 PUFA levels did not have equivalent thresholds across studies. Lastly, most trials were conducted in hemodialysis populations, which limits generalizability to peritoneal dialysis patients.

Future directions

Future studies should explore why n-3 PUFA supplementation is particularly beneficial in dialysis-dependent CKD. Mechanistic and translational studies are needed to define baseline n-3 PUFA thresholds at which supplementation confers benefit. Larger observational cohorts that stratify by baseline n-3 PUFA status, dialysis modality, and comorbidity burden would help identify subgroups most likely to benefit from this therapy. Finally, taken together with our meta-analytic signal, the robust cardiovascular benefits observed in PISCES support consideration of adequately dosed n-3 PUFA as a cardiovascular and mortality risk reduction strategy in dialysis-dependent CKD, and may justify future guideline updates to include formal recommendations in selected patients, while acknowledging that confirmatory data remains limited.

Conclusion

In this systematic review and meta-analysis of dialysis-dependent CKD, higher n-3 PUFA exposure through supplementation and baseline circulating levels was associated with fewer cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality, respectively, whereas higher dietary intake showed a trend toward decreased mortality that did not reach statistical significance. Taken together, these findings suggest a potential role for appropriately dosed n-3 PUFA supplementation in cardiovascular and mortality risk reduction among patients receiving hemodialysis. However, given heterogeneity across studies and the predominance of observational data, further randomized trials are required to confirm the benefits and to define the optimal formulation and dose.
