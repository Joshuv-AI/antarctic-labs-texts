Optilume drug-coated balloon dilation for treatment of post-prostate cancer ablative therapy urethral stricture: a case series
Authors: Alexandria Hertz, Emily Bochner, Daniel Segal, Daniel N Costa, Xiaosong Meng, Maia VanDyke
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOptilume-drug coated balloon as emerged as a less invasive management of recurrent bulbar urethral strictures. Data is still emerging in terms of its efficacy with regards to other stricture types. Additionally, ablative therapies (whether focal or whole gland) are also becoming more common for management of prostate cancer. As these treatments progress, we, as a specialty, will see more complications from these to include urethral strictures. This series demonstrates the use of the Optilume drug-coated balloon (DCB) dilation system for the treatment of urethral stricture disease after prostate ablative therapy for treatment of prostate cancer.Case DescriptionTwo patients involved men who had focal therapy with transurethral ultrasound ablation (TULSA) for treatment of localized prostate cancer who developed intraprostatic or membranous urethral stricture disease after treatment. The other two patients in our case series had ablative therapy with irreversible electroporation (IRE) for management of their localized prostate cancer and then subsequently developed posterior urethral strictures. All were treated with paclitaxel-coated (Optilume) balloon dilation with good response and no recurrence at up to 14 months after treatment.ConclusionsWith new therapies emerging with potential urethral complications, defining potential treatment methods is important. Given the novelty of both treatment modalities, this appears to offer a feasible treatment solution for an uncommon complication.Keywords: Urethral stricture, prostate cancer ablation, Optilume dilation, transurethral ultrasound ablation (TULSA), case series

Highlight box

Key findings

• Ablative prostate cancer treatments such as transurethral ultrasound ablation (TULSA) and irreversible electroporation (IRE) may have urethral stricture complications.

• Optilume drug-coated dilation offers a treatment option for strictures after ablation therapy that is durable out to 14 months.

• No significant complications were seen after stricture management with Optilume drug-coated balloon.

What is known and what is new?

• Optilume has been shown to help recurrent, bulbar urethral strictures. Ablative therapies have been used to treat localized prostate cancers as both focal and whole gland therapy. These ablative therapies may have urethral strictures post-treatment.

• We found good results in men with the use of Optilume dilation of urethral strictures after prostate cancer ablation.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Optilume shows promising benefits in use outside of recurrent, bulbar urethral strictures.

• Endoscopic management of post-ablation strictures is feasible.

• Future research is needed for long-term durability and comparison to urethroplasty.

Introduction

Localized prostate cancer has traditionally been managed with active surveillance or radical therapy (radical prostatectomy or radiation therapy). The well-documented side effects from both treatment modalities have subsequently given rise to a growing physician and patient interest in prostate-ablative therapies (PATs) in the treatment of localized prostate cancer (1,2). Recent technological advances have offered men an alternative treatment with imaging-guided PAT such as transurethral ultrasound ablation (TULSA) and irreversible electroporation (IRE) (3,4). The goal of these therapies is to offer good oncologic control while also reducing the risk of unfavorable urinary and sexual side effects which can significantly affect the patient’s quality of life (5-7). Urethral stenosis/stricture can have a significant quality of life impact, and known risk of both radiation and radical prostatectomy for management of prostate cancer (8,9). While rare, urethral stricture disease after both TULSA and IRE has also been documented (3,10,11). Treatment of urethral stricture disease can be complex in these post-treatment settings, and for vesicourethral anastomotic stenosis, may require multiple treatments (8). Optilume® drug-coated balloon (DCB) dilation has emerged as a treatment option for men with recurrent bulbar urethral strictures, with as many as 71.7% of patients remaining free from re-intervention at 5 years, while enjoying a low incidence of significant adverse events (12,13). A recent case study also reported successful use of Optilume® DCB in treatment of bladder neck stenosis after transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP) (14). In this case series we describe the short-term outcomes of Optilume® DCB in the treatment of post-PAT urethral stricture disease managed at a tertiary referral center. We present this article in accordance with the AME Case Series reporting checklist (available at https://tau.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tau-2025-404/rc).

Case presentation

All procedures performed in this study were in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee(s), and with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The patients included in this case series are part of an institutional review board (IRB) database, University of Texas Southwestern Medical Center (No. STU-2019-1454), where informed consent was waived as all information is retrospective and deidentified in nature.

Case 1

Patient is a 60-year-old male initially diagnosed with grade group (GG) 1 prostate cancer (10% of a single core) in 2020; he initially elected for active surveillance. He underwent repeat biopsy in 2 years later with upgrade of disease to GG3 and with new bilateral involvement. He elected to undergo treatment with TULSA with near whole-gland ablation in October 2022. Post-treatment, he had initial mild stress urinary incontinence (SUI), but over the next 6 months, he developed increasing obstructive symptoms. Cystoscopy demonstrated residual prostatic tissue and urodynamics confirmed obstruction. He underwent a TURP, 1-year post-PAT, but 2 months thereafter developed a bladder neck contracture requiring dilation with Heyman dilators and suprapubic tube (SPT) placement. Voiding cystourethrogram (VCUG) and retrograde urethrogram were performed redemonstrating the area of stenosis (Figure 1).

Voiding cystourethrogram and retrograde urethrogram for patient 1 demonstrating intra-prostatic stricture.

Four months later he underwent cystoscopy and urethral dilation with Optilume® DCB in the operating room using a 24 Fr non-DCB for pre-dilation and a 30 Fr DCB for drug delivery. This combination of 24 Fr non-drug-coated, and 30 Fr DCBs, were used for all patients in the series. His SPT was removed 6 weeks post-operatively after good emptying parameters were confirmed. He had severe incontinence using 5–7 soaked pads per day. On cystoscopy 4 months post-operatively he was seen to have an approximately 16 Fr lumen. Patient elected to proceed with artificial urinary sphincter placement and is now doing well 14 months after sphincter placement, using 1–2 damp pads per day and with no evidence of stricture recurrence.

Case 2

Patient is a 73-year-old male diagnosed with GG3 prostate cancer with disease located on the left lobe posterior and lateral at the base. He underwent 5-probe left hemigland IRE in October 2022. On surveillance magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) 9 months after treatment an out-of-field Prostate Imaging Reporting and Data System (PI-RADS) 4 lesion (in the midline) was noted, and repeat biopsy revealed GG2 disease. He underwent repeat IRE 11 months after his initial treatment, but subsequently developed urinary retention for which he underwent TURP to remove necrotic tissue.

Persistent slow stream post-operatively prompted evaluation with cystoscopy which confirmed a 6 Fr bladder neck contracture 11 months after his second IRE. He underwent Optilume DCB 2 weeks later, which was performed with a 24 Fr pre-dilation balloon and a 30 Fr DCB. Patient has been doing well since the time of the procedure with good emptying and no significant incontinence.

Case 3

Patient is a 76-year-old male with remote history of testicular seminoma with previous left radical orchiectomy, diagnosed with GG2 prostate cancer in December 2021. He received near-whole-gland ablation with TULSA in June 2022 (Figure 2). After his treatment, he initially reported progressive improvement in urinary flow until his 18-month follow-up appointment, when he reported significant slowing of his stream. Cystoscopy at that time demonstrated a stricture in the membranous urethra. Heyman dilators were used for in-office dilation of the stricture to 18 Fr.

TULSA-PRO MRI-guided focal ablation showing axial progression from base to apex with treatment planning (row 1), real-time thermometry (row 2), thermal dose mapping (row 3), and post-treatment contrast-enhanced imaging (row 4). MRI, magnetic resonance imaging; TULSA-PRO, transurethral ultrasound ablation for prostate.

Patient was found to have recurrence of his urethral stricture within one month after this initial dilation. He then underwent Optilume® DCB dilation 2 months later, and cystoscopy at 4 months post-operatively demonstrated patent urethra (Figure 3). He remains without reintervention 8 months after dilation and is continent.

Post-treatment voiding cystourethrogram for patient 3.

His 1- and 2-year post-treatment prostate MRI and biopsies demonstrated no evidence of recurrent disease.

Case 4

Patient is a 68-year-old male diagnosed with GG1 prostate cancer in 2020, initially managed with active surveillance. He had a subsequent rise in prostate specific antigen (PSA) and had repeat prostate biopsy with upgrading to GG2. He was deemed to not be a candidate for TULSA due to calcifications near one of his lesions (calcifications may lead to energy attenuation or buildup of heat by the calcification during TULSA that inhibit definitive ablation of the lesion of interest), and subsequently underwent 6-probe hemigland IRE in June 2024 (Video 1). Given the location of the lesion, his IRE ablation zone included a portion of the urethra. He developed urinary retention post-operatively requiring intermittent catheterization for 1 week. He developed difficulty voiding 5 months after treatment and attempted self-catheterization but was unable to pass the catheter.

Video of irreversible electroporation treatment for patient 4.

Cystoscopy 5 months after treatment demonstrated a 5 Fr stricture in the membranous urethra, which was sequentially dilated using Heyman dilators. He quickly had recurrence of the stricture and underwent Optilume® DCB dilation 1 month later without complication. He is currently 6 months out from surgery and is voiding well with no obstructive urinary symptoms or incontinence.

Discussion

Patients that are now opting for the various PAT therapies on the market including IRE, TULSA, high-intensity focused ultrasound (HIFU), etc., continues to increase (15-17). As this cohort grows, the incidence of even rare complications like urethral stenosis can be expected to rise. The optimal treatment modality for management of post-PAT stricture has yet to be defined. Over this short-term follow-up (6–14 months), we report four cases with successful management with the use of Optilume® DCB in this challenging population.

PAT modalities at this time report relatively low urethral stricture rates (1–5%) depending on treatment modality and technique (3-6,18-23). However, given that these therapies have not been in use for extended periods of time we may not yet know the true impact of these therapies on urethral stricture disease. It is possible that similar to radiotherapy or cryotherapy, urethral stricture disease presentation may be delayed as it is compared to prostatectomy (9,24).

Post-prostate cancer treatment related urethral strictures have been associated with high risk of recurrence and potential complications (8). The anti-proliferative effects of Optilume® delivered directly to the area of concern might represent a more durable solution for these patients (12). Further studies in this space, of course, will be needed to confirm or deny this hypothesis.

Currently exact patient drivers, and which patients are best suited for PAT, have yet to be delineated. However, there has been an increasing trend in patients performing their own research in making their prostate cancer treatment decision (25). Given the long-term sexual and urinary effects of both radical prostatectomy and radiation therapy, many men are looking to focal and PAT to minimize these long-term effects (15-17,26). Given this driver to minimize long-term symptoms, these patients may be interested in a minimally-invasive approach to urethral stenosis, making Optilume DCB a good option for these patients.

Optilume® DCB has been shown to have good success in the management of bulbar urethral strictures at 5 years, with more than 70% of patients remaining free from retreatment (12). This has allowed for some men to avoid more invasive treatment of urethral stricture disease such as formal urethroplasty. While the initial ROBUST trials only included men with recurrent, short-segment (≤3 cm) anterior urethral strictures, it is increasingly being used off-label for other indications (14,27-29). As many men who pursue PAT do so with the goal of minimizing quality-of-life changes after therapy, the Optilume® DCB may provide reasonable success with minimal lifestyle impacts for those who experience post-focal ablation urethral stricture disease (26).

We describe four patients with post-PAT urethral strictures who were successfully managed in the short term with the Optilume® DCB system. As both therapies are relatively new, and as stricture after focal disease is relatively uncommon, long-term follow-up and evaluation of higher patient volume will be needed to assess the true efficacy. Given the challenges and potential morbidity associated with management of post-prostate cancer treatment urethral stricture disease, a minimally invasive management option is very desirable (8,9). More formal investigation is needed to fully evaluate the efficacy Optilume® in this space.

Conclusions

Urethral dilation with Optilume® DCB following focal ablative therapy for localized prostate cancer seems to have good short-term results and this offers a potential treatment alternative for those patients hoping to avoid more radical surgery.
