A B3 Domain Transcription Factor NtMAB1 Regulates the Release and Outgrowth of Axillary Buds in Tobacco
Authors: Luyao Xue, Fei Xia, Wenting Song, Xinru Wu, Xiaoshan Jin, Qun Zhong, Tingting Sun, Changbo Dai, Guanshan Liu, Yuhe Sun, Chunming Liu, Shengjun Li, Xuefeng Yao, Dawei Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe release and outgrowth of axillary buds are essential parts of the process of shoot branching in plants, but knowledge of the underlying regulatory mechanisms remains limited. Analysis of mab1‐D, a tobacco mutant with a greater number of axillary branches, showed that the mutant phenotype resulted from positive modulation of axillary buds release and outgrowth. TAIL‐PCR and co‐separation analyses suggested that NtMAB1‐S1, a member of the Related to ABI3/VP1 (RAV) subfamily that was highly expressed in the base of axillary buds, was associated with the mutant phenotype. Phenotypic analyses of mab1‐D overexpression, knockdown, knockout and complementation lines demonstrated that NtMAB1‐S1 was a positive regulator of axillary buds release and outgrowth. NtMAB1‐S1 accelerated these processes through transcriptional repression via the B3 repression domain. RNA‐sequencing analysis revealed that NtMAB1‐S1 was involved in the trehalose‐6‐phosphate and gibberellin metabolism pathways. Moreover, NtMAB1‐S1 enhanced the release and outgrowth of axillary buds by directly repressing transcription of NtTPPF, a trehalose‐6‐phosphate phosphatase, and NtGA2ox8, a gibberellin oxidase. NtMAB1‐S1 thus plays a pivotal role in regulating the release and outgrowth of axillary buds and is a potential target for genetic interventions aimed at shaping ideal plant architecture.Keywords: gibberellins (GAs), NtGA2ox8, NtMAB1‐S1, NtTPPF, trehalose‐6‐phosphate (Tre6p)

Introduction

Shoot branching is a major component of shoot architecture that has a strong impact on crop yield (Cao et al. 2023). The shoot branching process can be divided into two stages, axillary meristem formation and axillary bud growth (Yan et al. 2023), which are affected by many factors, including gene transcription, hormone homeostasis and environmental conditions (Yang et al. 2023; Zhu and Wagner 2020). The growth of axillary buds, including their release and outgrowth, plays a crucial role in determining the final number of shoot branches (Zhu and Wagner 2020). Previous research has demonstrated that a regulatory network involving three key pathways, the sugar, hormone and transcription factor regulatory pathways, is involved in the release and outgrowth of axillary buds (Beveridge et al. 2023; Cao et al. 2023; Yan et al. 2020; Zhu and Wagner 2020). The interactions between various factors and the complex hormone regulatory network have made it difficult to analyse the mechanism underlying the release and outgrowth of axillary buds (Yan et al. 2020).

Sugar is a crucial factor that significantly influences the release and outgrowth of axillary buds (Wang et al. 2021; Wen et al. 2024; Xiong et al. 2024). Since the theory of apical dominance was first proposed in the 1930s, auxin has been considered the main mechanism by which the shoot tip inhibits the release and outgrowth of axillary buds over an extended period (Kebrom 2017). More recently, evidence has emerged that sugar metabolism, transport and signalling are the initial regulators of dormancy breaking in axillary buds (Mason et al. 2014; Sheikh et al. 2022). Sucrose, the primary form of sugar transported in plants (Liu et al. 2025), most likely functions as a signal for the release of axillary buds from dormancy (Barbier et al. 2019). Trehalose‐6‐phosphate (Tre6P) functions both as a signal and as a homeostatic regulator of sucrose levels (Fichtner and Lunn 2021), and Tre6P levels correlate well with the release and outgrowth of axillary buds. Tre6P plays a vital role in axillary bud development in a variety of plant species. It is involved in regulating axillary bud release and outgrowth in Arabidopsis ( Arabidopsis thaliana ) (Fichtner et al. 2021), promotes shoot branching in rice ( Oryza sativa ) (Fichtner et al. 2024) and is a key regulator of axillary bud release and outgrowth in pea ( Pisum sativum ) (Fichtner et al. 2017) and sorghum ( Sorghum bicolor ) (Kebrom and Mullet 2016).

Various hormones have distinct functions in the processes of axillary bud release and outgrowth (Barbier et al. 2019; Luo et al. 2021). According to the apical dominance theory, auxin is synthesised in the growing shoot tips and transported downward, indirectly inhibiting the release of axillary buds from dormancy (Kebrom 2017). Auxin represses cytokinin (CK) biosynthesis, thereby limiting the availability of CK in axillary buds and inhibiting their release and outgrowth (Müller and Leyser 2011; Qiu et al. 2019). Strigolactone (SL) acts downstream of auxin to inhibit the release and outgrowth of axillary buds (Brewer et al. 2009; Fichtner et al. 2024). A growing body of evidence suggests that gibberellin (GA) may play a dual role in the outgrowth of axillary buds (Katyayini et al. 2020). GAs negatively regulate axillary bud outgrowth in rice and Arabidopsis (Lin et al. 2020; Zhang et al. 2020) but promote outgrowth of axillary buds in pea ( P. sativum ), Jatropha curcas and Populus (Beveridge et al. 2023; Cao et al. 2023; Ni et al. 2015; Rinne et al. 2011). Moreover, the differential effects of GAs on axillary bud outgrowth in different plant species may depend upon whether the buds have entered the release stage (Han et al. 2024; O'Neill et al. 2019).

Transcription factors (TFs) are also involved in the regulation of bud release and outgrowth. SL and CK control early axillary bud release by regulating the expression of BRANCHED1 (BRC1), a central inhibitor of axillary buds release and outgrowth (Barbier et al. 2019; van Es et al. 2024). In addition, the roles of other TFs involved in shoot branching have been elucidated. Related to ABI3/VP1 (RAV) TFs contain a B3 domain in their C‐terminal region and/or an AP2 domain in their N‐terminal region (Wang et al. 2020). Heterologous expression of GmRAV in tobacco promotes shoot branching (Zhao et al. 2012). Furthermore, heterogenetic overexpression of CsRAV1 in poplar causes enhanced shoot branching (Moreno‐Cortés et al. 2012). Previous studies suggest that RAV TFs enhance shoot branching by regulating the formation of axillary meristems (Nicolas et al. 2022; Yuan et al. 2024), but their role in the release and outgrowth of axillary buds remains poorly understood.

In the current study, screening of an activation‐tagged mutant library (Liu et al. 2015; Wang et al. 2017) led to the identification of mab1‐D, a tobacco mutant with a greater number of axillary branches. We subsequently demonstrated that the mutant phenotype resulted from overexpression of NtMAB1‐S1, a gene belonging to the RAV TF subfamily. NtMAB1‐S1 proved to be a positive regulator of shoot branching in tobacco as it increased the levels of Tre6P and GAs, thus positively regulating the release and outgrowth of axillary buds. Our findings offer novel insight into the regulatory mechanisms through which RAV TFs regulate the release and outgrowth of axillary buds in plants.

Results

Phenotypic Characterisation of the mab1‐D Mutant

We previously used an activation‐tagged mutant library to identify several mutants that produced a greater number of axillary branches than wild‐type (WT) tobacco plants (Liu et al. 2015). The axillary buds of the mab1‐D mutant exhibited sustained outgrowth and produced more branches (Figure 1a, Figure S1). The lengths of the axillary buds/branches (ABs) at each leaf position differed significantly between mab1‐D and WT plants at the rosette stage on the 25th day after transplantation (Figure 1a–d). By the 50th day after transplantation at the prosperously growing stage, ABs were clearly visible at each leaf position in the mab1‐D mutant and AB length was significantly greater than in the WT plants (Figure 1e–g). mab1‐D exhibited sustained AB outgrowth across all developmental stages, indicating the partial loss of apical dominance and partial activation of axillary bud outgrowth in the mutant.

We constructed an F1 generation and an F2 population to determine the inheritance pattern of mab1‐D. WT plants were crossed with mab1‐D to produce the F1 generation, which was allowed to self‐pollinate to produce the F2 population. In F1 plants, the ABs in each leaf position had an intermediate length phenotype, being longer than those of WT but shorter than those of mab1‐D (Figure 1h,i). mab1‐D showed a semi‐dominant inheritance pattern with a phenotypic segregation ratio of 80:27 (χ 2 = 0.95549) between the mab1‐D and WT phenotypes in the F2 population. These observations suggested that mab1‐D was a semi‐dominant mutation that enhanced axillary bud activity.

Given that BRC1 plays a negative role in regulating shoot branching (van Es et al. 2024), we analysed the relative expression levels of NtBRC1 (Table S1). NtBRC1 expression levels were highest in WT plants, lowest in mab1‐D mutants and intermediate in F1 hybrids. This demonstrated that the expression of NtBRC1 in both the upper and lower ABs was suppressed in a gradient across the WT, F1 and mab1‐D plants (Figure 1j; Table S1). This result was consistent with earlier studies that showed rapid downregulation of NtBRC1 following axillary bud activation.

Identification and Isolation of NtMAB1‐S1

To investigate factors determining the outgrowth of axillary buds in mab1‐D, we performed high‐efficiency thermal asymmetric interlaced PCR (hiTAIL‐PCR) (Liu and Chen 2007). We identified two activation‐tagged T‐DNA insertions, designated C110 and C1130, based on the two corresponding flanking sequences, and performed a co‐separation analysis of genotypes and phenotypes in the segregating F2 population. All plants homozygous or heterozygous for the C110 T‐DNA insertion exhibited larger axillary buds and more branches, whereas plants lacking the C110 T‐DNA insertion had a WT phenotype (Figure 2a; Table S1). No co‐segregation correlation between genotype and phenotype was observed for the C1130 insertion (Figure S2). These results suggested that mab1‐D was a gain‐of‐function mutant whose phenotype resulted from the C110 T‐DNA insertion.

We identified six flanking genes within the genomic region 200 kb upstream and downstream of the C110 insertion site (Figure 2b). To identify the gene(s) responsible for the mab1‐D phenotype, we analysed the expression of all six genes using quantitative reverse‐transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT‐PCR). Expression of the flanking gene D1 was significantly upregulated in the root (16.62‐fold), stem (31.63‐fold), leaf (44.96‐fold) and axillary buds/branches (35.57‐fold) of mab1‐D, relative to its expression in WT plants, but the expression levels of the other five genes were the same or similar in both genotypes (Figure 2c–f). This indicated that overexpression of D1, henceforth designated NtMAB1, was responsible for the mab1‐D phenotype.

Common tobacco ( Nicotiana tabacum ) is an allotetraploid and thus NtMAB1 is likely to be present in two homologous copies deriving from its wild ancestors N. sylvestris and N. tomentosiformis. A homology analysis revealed one copy of NtMAB1, renamed NtMAB1‐S1, originating from N. sylvestris ; a second gene NtMAB1‐T1, originating from N. tomentosiformis, was also cloned but was found to be a pseudogene containing a premature termination codon prior to the B3 domain. The 1002‐bp open reading frame of NtMAB1‐S1 encoded a protein of 333 amino acids belonging to the RAV transcription factor family of the B3 superfamily. NtMAB1‐S1 contained one conserved B3 domain and one highly conserved B3 repression domain (BRD) with the key sequence RLFGV (Figure 3a). An analysis of the evolutionary relationships between RAV TFs in Arabidopsis ( A. thaliana ), tomato ( Solanum lycopersicum ) and rice ( O. sativa ) suggested that NtMAB1‐S1 was most closely related to SlRAV4 (Figure 3b). The genomic sequence of NtMAB1‐S1 contained two exons and one intron. Its genomic structure resembled that of its Arabidopsis homologue AtNGAL2 (Figure 3c).

NtMAB1‐S1 Is Highly Expressed in the Base of Axillary Buds

To further explore the expression pattern of NtMAB1‐S1, we analysed the cis‐elements in its promoter region. The NtMAB1‐S1 promoter contained many hormone‐responsive elements, including indole‐3‐acetic acid (IAA)‐responsive (AuxRR‐core and TGA‐element), methyl jasmonate (MeJA)‐responsive (CGTCA‐motif and TGACG‐motif), gibberellic acid (GA)‐responsive (TATC‐box) and abscisic acid (ABA)‐responsive elements (ABREs) (Figure S3). IAA and GAs are likely to be involved in the regulation of shoot branching (Zhang et al. 2020, 2021; Zou et al. 2024), whereas ABA and Me‐JA may be associated with other physiological processes. We investigated whether application of either GA or IAA induced expression of NtMAB1‐S1. Levels of NtMAB1‐S1 expression increased under GA and IAA treatments by almost 0.02‐fold at 6 h and 2.96‐fold at 48 h (Figure 4a,b; Table S1), leading us to speculate that NtMAB1‐S1 regulated the release and outgrowth of axillary buds via GA and IAA signalling pathways.

To monitor the spatial and temporal expression patterns of NtMAB1‐S1 during axillary buds development, we used qRT‐PCR to measure the expression levels of NtMAB1‐S1 in various organs of WT plants, including the root, stem, leaf and axillary buds. NtMAB1‐S1 was highly expressed in leaf during the seedling stage and in the base of ABs during the prosperously growing stage (Figure 4c; Table S1). Topping promotes the release and growth of axillary buds in tobacco (Wang et al. 2018). To study NtMAB1‐S1 expression dynamics across the distinct stages of axillary buds development, we analysed NtMAB1‐S1 expression levels in response to topping. Compared with intact plants, the expression levels of NtMAB1‐S1 in axillary buds were upregulated almost 5.27‐fold at 3 h and 2.46‐fold at 24 h after topping and then gradually decreased (Figure 4d). This result indicated that NtMAB1‐S1 played a crucial role in the release and growth of axillary buds.

To further determine the temporal and spatial expression patterns of NtMAB1‐S1, we developed transgenic tobacco plants containing the GUS reporter gene driven by the NtMAB1‐S1 promoter. Analysis at the seeding stage revealed significantly higher GUS activity in the axillary meristem than in other tissues (Figure 4e,f). Next, we examined GUS staining in axillary buds at various leaf positions during the prosperously growing stage and found that NtMAB1‐S1 was expressed in the base of axillary buds at all leaf positions (Figure 4g). These results were consistent with a role for NtMAB1‐S1 in axillary buds development. We also examined the subcellular localisation of NtMAB1‐S1 using PEG‐mediated protoplast transformation and found that NtMAB1‐S1 was localised in the nucleus (Figure S4). To confirm this result, we observed fluorescence in the leaves of 35S::GFP and 35S::NtMAB1‐S1‐GFP transgenic plants and again found that NtMAB1‐S1 was localised in the nucleus (Figure 4h).

NtMAB1‐S1 Promotes Axillary Buds Release and Outgrowth

To confirm whether NtMAB1‐S1 could facilitate the release and outgrowth of axillary buds, we generated 13 overexpression lines (designated OE‐1 to OE‐13), eight knockdown lines (designated KD‐1 to KD‐8) and 10 mab1‐D complementation lines (designated as mKD‐1 to mKD‐10). In addition, we used the CRISPR/Cas9 system to produce NtMAB1‐S1‐knockout mutants. This process generated two independent homozygous NtMAB1‐S1 knockout lines, KO‐1, which contained an ‘A’ deletion, and KO‐2, which contained a 14‐bp insertion (Figure S5a,b; Table S1). The lines OE‐1, OE‐2, KD‐3, KD‐6, mKD‐1, mKD‐2, KO‐1 and KO‐2 were selected for use in subsequent experiments as they showed the greatest differences in NtMAB1‐S1 expression between WT, mab1‐D and transgenic plants (Figure S5c–e; Table S1).

In order to explore the phenotypes of transgenic plants, WT, mab1‐D and transgenic plants were grown in pots in a greenhouse. Compared with the WT, the overexpression lines (OE‐1 and OE‐2) exhibited larger axillary buds and more branches in most leaf positions; these differences were consistent with the mab1‐D phenotype (Figure 5a,b). The release and outgrowth of axillary buds were suppressed or partially suppressed in the NtMAB1‐S1 knockdown and knockout lines (Figure 5c,e; Figure S6a). The axillary buds were smaller in the knockdown lines (KD‐3 and KD‐6) and knockout lines (KO‐1 and KO‐2) than in WT plants (Figure 5d,f). These results suggested that NtMAB1‐S1 was a positive regulator of axillary buds release and outgrowth.

To determine whether the mab1‐D phenotype could be rescued by inhibiting NtMAB1‐S1 expression, we introduced RNAi silencing binary vectors targeting NtMAB1‐S1 into mab1‐D mutants via transformation. The mab1‐D phenotype was rescued in the T2 RNAi transgenic lines mKD‐1 and mKD‐2, which showed similar phenotypes to WT plants (Figure 5g; Figure S6b). The mKD‐1 and mKD‐2 lines had shorter axillary buds than WT plants at all leaf positions, indicating the mab1‐D phenotype could be rescued by reducing NtMAB1‐S1 expression (Figure 5h).

To study the phenotypes of the WT, mab1‐D and transgenic plants further, we cultivated WT and all transgenic plants in the field and examined their axillary buds/branches at the rosette and prosperously growing stages. The phenotypes of transgenic plants grown in the field were consistent with those of transgenic plants cultivated in pots in the greenhouse (Figures S7 and S8).

NtMAB1‐S1 Functions as a Transcriptional Repressor

Given that NtMAB1‐S1 contained a BRD domain with the key sequence RLFGV (Liu et al. 2018), we investigated whether NtMAB1‐S1 functioned as a transcriptional suppressor. The VP16 element is frequently used to enhance the transcriptional activation capacity of a transcription factor as it reveals the function of transcriptional repressors (Fujiwara et al. 2014; He et al. 2024; Trojanowski et al. 2022). The NtMAB1‐S1 sequence encoding amino acids 239 to 243 corresponded with the BRD domain and included the five conserved residues (Figure 6a). To test whether NtMAB1‐S1 was a transcriptional repressor, we synthesised nucleotide sequences of NtMAB1‐S1 fused with VP16 with and without the BRD (ΔBRD) domain (Figure 6b). We separately inserted the NtMAB1‐S1 and NtMAB1‐S1‐VP16 proteins into the pGBT9 vector and transformed yeast cells with the fusion constructs. Yeast colonies harbouring pGBT9 (negative control) or VP16 (positive control) were white or blue, respectively, on synthetic defined (SD) medium lacking Trp and containing X‐gal (Figure 6c). By contrast, yeast colonies containing the NtMAB1‐S1 were white, demonstrating that NtMAB1‐S1 was likely to be a transcriptional repressor (Figure 6c). Yeast colonies containing NtMAB1‐S1‐VP16 turned a lighter blue colour than those harbouring VP16, suggesting that the transcriptional repression activity of NtMAB1‐S1 partially counteracted activation by the VP16 element (Figure 6c).

To investigate the role of the BRD domain in axillary buds release and outgrowth further, we inserted the NtMAB1S1‐ΔBRD construct under the control of the 35S promoter into the pCAMBIA1300 vector. Under growth conditions where WT plants exhibited no branching, plants of the 35S::NtMAB1‐S1‐ΔBRD‐VP16 line also showed no branching, whereas those of 35S::NtMAB1‐S1‐VP16 line demonstrated significantly increased branching (Figure 6d,e). These results indicated that NtMAB1‐S1 accelerated the release and outgrowth of axillary buds through transcriptional repression based on the BRD domain. In addition, the maximum length of the axillary buds of the 35S::NtMAB1‐S1 (OE‐1) plants was significantly higher than that of the 35S::NtMAB1S1‐VP16 plants; however, the axillary buds minimum length did not differ significantly between these lines (Figure 6e). These results revealed that VP16 could partially rescue the phenotype of OE‐1.

NtMAB1‐S1 Is Involved in Tre6P and GAs Metabolism Pathway

We performed an RNA‐sequencing (RNA‐seq) analysis of WT and mab1‐D plants to understand better how NtMAB1‐S1 regulated the release and outgrowth of axillary buds. Figure S9a–d shows an evaluation of the RNA quality of the samples and the results of a high‐throughput sequencing analysis. The Q30 of the cleaned tags was above 95%, indicating high sequencing quality (Figure S9a). The correlation coefficients between WT axillary buds (WIU) and mab1‐D axillary buds (MIU) samples were all greater than 0.98 (Figure S9b). The distribution of the RFKM values for genes was consistent across all samples, suggesting that levels of gene expression were comparable (Figure S9c). We selected several genes from the transcriptomic dataset and confirmed their expression levels using qRT‐PCR. The resulting expression patterns were consistent with the trends observed in the transcriptomic analysis (Figure S9d; Table S1). These results indicated that the samples exhibited excellent repeatability and were suitable for subsequent gene expression analysis.

We detected 3035 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between WT and mab1‐D plants; 1697 of these DEGs were upregulated and 1338 were downregulated in mab1‐D (Figure 7a). Since NtMAB1‐S1 functioned as a transcriptional repressor, we focused on the 1338 DEGs downregulated in the mutant. We functionally annotated and classified all 1338 downregulated DEGs using Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) significant enrichment analysis. Recent research indicates sugar and hormones play essential roles in the release and outgrowth of axillary buds (Moreno 2023) and we therefore specifically considered sugar and hormone metabolic pathways. An analysis of the DEGs associated with sugar or hormones indicated that 21 were classified into the ‘sugar synthesis and metabolism’ category and 64 were assigned to the ‘hormone synthesis and metabolism’ group. Notably, the largest groups consisted of genes involved in the Tre6P and GA synthesis and metabolism pathways, which accounted for 42.86% and 25% of the total, respectively (Figure 7b; Figure S9e).

Most forms of GA are inactive in plants, with only a limited number being bioactive, including GA1, GA3, GA4 and GA7 (Xie and Chen 2020). To investigate the efficacy of different forms of GA in facilitating axillary buds release and outgrowth, we quantified the levels of GA1, GA3, GA4 and GA7 in WT and mab1‐D plants. GA1 and GA4 showed significant increases in mab1‐D, relative to WT plants, whereas no changes in GA3 and GA7 levels were observed (Figure S9f). We also measured GA1, GA4 and Tre6P levels in WT and all lines of transgenic plants. Levels of GA1, GA4 and Tre6P showed significant increases in the OE lines but were reduced in the KO and KD lines. Additionally, levels of GA1, GA4 and Tre6P were lower in the mKD lines than in mab1‐D (Figure 7c,d). It is notable that we found a significant negative correlation between sucrose levels and AB length (Figure S9g).

Since GA1, GA4 and Tre6P levels increased significantly in mab1‐D, we conducted a comprehensive search of the DEGs involved in GAs and Tre6P synthesis and metabolism for genes associated with metabolism. We identified three DEGs that were involved in Tre6P metabolism and four DEGs associated with GA1 and GA4 metabolism (Figure 7e–h). These observations suggested that NtMAB1‐S1 regulated the release and outgrowth of axillary buds via the Tre6p and GA metabolism pathways.

NtMAB1‐S1 Directly Targets NtTPPF and NtGA2ox8

Previous studies have demonstrated that, in plants, proteins in the B3 superfamily specifically bind to the CACCTG motif (Kagaya et al. 1999). To investigate whether NtMAB1‐S1 was a direct regulator of genes involved in Tre6p and GAs metabolism, we determined whether the CACCTG motif was present in the promoters of the TREHALOSE‐6‐PHOSPHATE PHOSPHATASES (NtTPPs) and GA2‐OXIDASES (NtGA2oxs) previously identified as DEGs. These genes encode proteins related to Tre6P metabolism and GA metabolism, respectively. Only NtTPPF (Nta16g05010) and NtGA2ox8 (Nta03g29610) contained the CACCTG box in their promoters (Figure 8a,b).

We performed electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSAs) to determine whether NtMAB1‐S1 was capable of binding to the NtTPPF and NtGA2ox8 promoters. These assays showed NtMAB1‐S1 directly targeted NtTPPF and NtGA2ox8 by binding to the CACCTG boxes in their promoters (Figure 8c,d). Next, we generated 35S::GFP and 35S::NtMAB1‐S1‐GFP lines and conducted chromatin immunoprecipitation quantitative PCR (ChIP‐qPCR) analyses to investigate whether NtMAB1‐S1 could directly bind to and regulate NtTPPF and NtGA2ox8 in vivo (Figure S10). These analyses showed that NtMAB1‐S1 directly bound to the promoters of NtTPPF and NtGA2ox8 (Figure 8e). We therefore conducted transient dual‐luciferase (Dual‐LUC) assays in tobacco plants to investigate the effect of NtMAB1‐S1 on expression of NtTPPF and NtGA2ox8. The activities of the NtTPPF and NtGA2ox8 promoters were significantly suppressed by NtMAB1‐S1 (Figure 8f). Furthermore, expression of both NtTPPF and NtGA2ox8 was significantly downregulated in mab1‐D and the OE lines, compared with WT plants, but significantly upregulated in the KO and KD lines (Figure 8g,h; Figure S10).

Discussion

The timing of elevated endogenous Tre6P levels correlates with the onset of bud release (Fichtner et al. 2017). Expression of a heterologous trehalose‐6‐phosphate phosphatase (TPP) to reduce Tre6P levels in axillary buds significantly delays bud outgrowth in Arabidopsis (Fichtner et al. 2021). An elevation in endogenous Tre6P levels may therefore facilitate the release and outgrowth of axillary buds, with GAs promoting sustained outgrowth in buds that have already entered the release stage (Beveridge et al. 2023; Cao et al. 2023; O'Neill et al. 2019; Zhang et al. 2021). We hypothesised that NtMAB1‐S1 enhanced Tre6P content by downregulating NtTPPF expression, thereby promoting the release of axillary buds. Once the axillary buds had entered the release stage, NtMAB1‐S1 increased Tre6P and GA levels further by suppressing expression of NtTPPF and NtGA2ox8, thus facilitating the sustained outgrowth of the axillary buds (Figure 9).

Proposed model of the role of NtMAB1‐S1 in the release and outgrowth of axillary buds in tobacco. NtMAB1‐S1 reduces the levels of trehalose‐6‐phosphate (Tre6P) and gibberellin (GAs) metabolism by downregulating the expression of NtTPPF and NtGA2ox8, thus increasing the levels of Tre6P and bioactive GAs in the axillary buds. This accumulation of Tre6P and bioactive GAs promotes the release and outgrowth of axillary buds. Blue circles: Bioactive GAs; grey circles: Inactive GAs; yellow circles: Tre6P; black circles: Trehalose; black arrows: Promotion; black blunt ends: Suppression.

The release and outgrowth of axillary buds are crucial factors influencing shoot branching (Beveridge et al. 2023; Cao et al. 2023). The complex regulatory network makes it difficult to analyse the mechanism underlying the release and outgrowth of axillary buds. We used a tobacco mutant, mab1‐D, that had a greater number of axillary branches to facilitate our investigation into axillary buds development. Our analysis identified NtMAB1‐S1 as a positive regulator of shoot branching in tobacco (Figures 1 and 2). Tissue‐specific expression and GUS staining analyses indicated that NtMAB1‐S1 was highly expressed in the base of axillary buds (Figure 4c–f). These results showed that NtMAB1‐S1 played vital roles in axillary buds development.

TFs are proteins that bind to specific DNA sequences and thereby enhance or inhibit the recruitment of RNA polymerases, thus regulating gene expression (He et al. 2023). TFs typically consist of one or more DNA‐binding domains and a transcription activation and/or repressor domain; these domains determine their functional diversity (Roberts 2000). NtMAB1‐S1 is a RAV transcription factor belonging to the B3 superfamily. It contains one conserved B3 domain and a highly conserved B3 repression domain (BRD) with the key sequence of RLFGV (Figure 6a). Our Y1H analysis showed that NtMAB1‐S1 regulated shoot branching by functioning as a transcriptional repressor (Figure 6b,c), as 35S::NtMAB1‐S1‐ΔBRD‐VP16 plants exhibited no branching, whereas 35S::NtMAB1‐S1‐VP16 plants displayed significantly enhanced branching (Figure 6d,e). These findings suggested that NtMAB1‐S1 promoted the release and outgrowth of axillary buds via the BRD domain.

RNA sequencing identified 1338 genes that were downregulated in mab1‐D (Figure 7a). A KEGG analysis assigned 21 and 64 of these 1338 genes to the sugar and hormone synthesis and metabolism groups, respectively. Notably, genes involved in Tre6P and GAs synthesis and metabolism accounted for 42.86% and 25% of the total, respectively. We identified NtTPPF and NtGA2ox8 as downstream targets of NtNAB1‐S1. EMSA, ChIP‐qPCR and Dual‐LUC assays indicated that NtNAB1‐S1 directly targeted NtTPPF and NtGA2ox8 and suppressed their expression by binding directly to CACCTG boxes in their promoters (Figure 8). Although the specific functions of NtTPPF and NtGA2ox8 have yet to be experimentally validated in tobacco plants, TPP and GA2ox are involved in plant bud development in other species (Fichtner et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2022).

Our study revealed a previously unknown mechanism by which NtMAB1‐S1 repressed Tre6P and GA metabolism to enhance the release and outgrowth of axillary buds in tobacco. Levels of Tre6P and sucrose showed opposite trends in WT and transgenic plants (Figure 7c; Figure S9g). Consistent with our findings, Tre6P promotes shoot branching in Arabidopsis, although there is a significant negative correlation between sucrose levels and shoot branching in this species (Fichtner et al. 2021). In addition, levels of Tre6P and sucrose in rice leaves exhibit an opposite trend (Li et al. 2022). Tre6P promotes the transport of sucrose by sugars will eventually be exported transporters (SWEETs), thereby causing changes in sucrose content (Fichtner et al. 2021; Fichtner and Lunn 2021). The mechanism by which Tre6P facilitates axillary buds development will be the focus of our future research.

The main mechanism by which the shoot tip inhibits the release and outgrowth of axillary buds over an extended period is the release of auxins, including IAA (Kebrom 2017). The NtMAB1‐S1 promoter contained IAA‐related elements (Figure S3) and NtMAB1‐S1 expression was significantly downregulated by IAA treatment within 6 h (Figure 4a). We speculate that the repression of NtMAB1‐S1 by IAA was the major reason why the growth rate of axillary buds of untopped mab1‐D mutants was intermediate between that of WT plants and the mab1‐D mutant after topping.

GAs may play a dual role in the outgrowth of axillary buds in different species (Katyayini et al. 2020); for instance, GAs inhibit the outgrowth of axillary buds in rice ( O. sativa ) and Arabidopsis but promote it in pea ( P. sativum ), J. curcas and Populus (Beveridge et al. 2023; Cao et al. 2023; Lin et al. 2020; Ni et al. 2015; Rinne et al. 2011; Zhang et al. 2020). The different effects of GAs on axillary buds outgrowth in different plant species may depend on whether the buds have entered the release stage (O'Neill et al. 2019). In the current study, NtMAB1‐S1 promoted Tre6P accumulation in tobacco, thus enhancing the release of axillary buds, which in turn facilitated the sustained outgrowth of axillary buds via GAs (Figure 9).

The primary bioactive forms of GA include GA1, GA3, GA4 and GA7 (He et al. 2020; Xie and Chen 2020). GA3 induces the abscission of axillary buds, whereas GA4 promotes outgrowth in hybrid aspen (Katyayini et al. 2020). Although GA3 and GA4 application both promote the release of bud dormancy in tree peonies, GA3 treatment results in a more rapid bud burst and more shoots per plant (Zhang et al. 2021). The distributions of active GA1 and GA4 are closely associated with peach ( Prunus persica ) annual shoot growth (Li et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2022). Different bioactive forms of GA may therefore play different roles in regulating bud growth among different species. In this study, significant increases in GA1 and GA4, but not in GA3 and GA7, were observed in mab1‐D (Figure S9f). This suggested that GA1 and GA4 played crucial roles in the regulation of axillary buds outgrowth in tobacco.

NtMAB1‐S1 is a member of the B3 superfamily of RAV TFs (Figure 3). RAV TFs enhance shoot branching by regulating the formation of axillary meristems in plants (Nicolas et al. 2022; Yuan et al. 2024), but the role played by these TFs in axillary buds release and outgrowth remains poorly understood. We found that NtMAB1‐S1 functioned as a transcriptional repressor in tobacco. NtMAB1‐S1 increased levels of Tre6P and GAs by directly repressing NtTPPF and NtGA2ox8, thereby enhancing the release and outgrowth of axillary buds (Figures 8 and 9). This pathway may act in parallel with the RAV‐mediated axillary meristem formation pathway to regulate the process of shoot branching in tobacco.

In conclusion, we showed that the RAV transcription factor NtMAB1‐S1 was a positive regulator of shoot branching in tobacco. NtMAB1‐S1 functioned as a transcriptional repressor, promoting the release and outgrowth of axillary buds. NtMAB1‐S1 enhanced the accumulation of Tre6P and GAs in axillary buds by directly targeting and suppressing the expression of NtTPPF and NtGA2ox8, genes involved in Tre6P and GA metabolism, respectively, thus promoting axillary bud release and outgrowth (Figure 9). Our data provide novel insight into the mechanism controlling the release and outgrowth of axillary buds in tobacco and identify NtMAB1‐S1 as a potential target gene for future studies aimed at shaping ideal plant architecture.

Materials and Methods

Plant Materials and Growth Conditions

Plants of the tobacco ( Nicotiana tabacum L.) cultivar Honghuadajinyuan were employed as the WT genotype in the cloning and functional characterisation of NtMAB1‐S1. The mab1‐D mutant was identified in a population generated by T‐DNA activation‐tag mutagenesis in the WT background. All tobacco plants were grown in a substrate composed of an equal mixture of peat soil and vermiculite and cultivated in the greenhouse facilities of the Tobacco Research Institute at the Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences, Qingdao, China. Environmental conditions in the greenhouse consisted of a photoperiod of 16 h of light (light intensity 400 μmol m−2 s−1) and 8 h of darkness, a temperature regimen of 25°C during the light period and 23°C during the dark period and a relative humidity of 65%.

Sequence Analysis

Genomic DNA and total RNA were extracted from WT, mab1‐D and transgenic plants using suitable kits (TaKaRa). Multiple sequence alignments were performed using Clustal Omega (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/jdispatcher/msa/clustalo?order=input). Conserved domains were identified using InterPro (http://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/). Phylogenetic analysis was carried out using the neighbour‐joining method implemented in MEGA11.0, with 1000 bootstrap replicates (Tamura et al. 2021). The exon–intron structure of genes was analysed using the Splign program (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sutils/splign) and cis‐elements within promoter regions were identified using PlantCARE (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/).

Expression Analysis

Tobacco leaves at the four‐leaf stage were sampled 0, 0.5, 1, 3, 6, 12, 24 and 48 h after spraying with 0.1 mM GA4 or IAA in half‐strength Hoagland solution (Song et al. 2025). Root, stem, leaf and axillary bud/branch tissues of WT and transgenic plants were sampled at the rosette and prosperously growing stages for tissue‐specific expression analysis. The samples were collected from the basal region of three axillary buds located below the terminal bud of the topped plants at time points of 0 (untopped), 3, 6, 12, 24 and 48 h post‐topping. Three axillary buds/branches below the terminal bud of WT, mab1‐D and transgenic plants at the prosperously growing stage were collected for analysis of NtTPPF and NtGA2ox8 expression. Total RNA was extracted using the TaKaRa MiniBEST Plant RNA Extraction Kit. Gene expression levels were measured using quantitative reverse‐transcription PCR (qRT‐PCR). TOBACCO UBIQUITIN‐CONJUGATING ENZYME E2 was used as an internal reference (Wu et al. 2019).

GUS Staining Analysis

The NtMAB1‐S1 promoter sequence was inserted into the pCAMBIA3301‐GUS vector. Tobacco plants were transformed with the resulting plasmid using Agrobacterium‐mediated transformation, as described previously (Xue et al. 2022). Samples of root, stem, leaf and axillary bud/branch of transgenic plants were collected for β‐Glucuronidase (GUS) staining at the one‐leaf, four‐leaf and prosperously growing stages of development.

Subcellular Localisation Analysis

The constructs 35S::NtMAB1‐S1‐GFP and 35S::GFP‐NtMAB1‐S1 were cloned into the pCAMBIA1300‐GFP vector. Tobacco mesophyll protoplasts were co‐transformed with the resulting plasmids together with the nucleus marker NLS‐mCherry via PEG‐mediated protoplast transformation (Cat# PPT201‐10 T‐B; Solarbio, Beijing, China). In addition, 35S::GFP and 35S::NtMAB1‐S1‐GFP transgenic tobacco plants were used to determine subcellular localisation in vivo. Fluorescent signals were detected using a confocal laser scanning microscope (TCS SP8; Leica).

Tobacco Transformation and Phenotypic Analysis

The vectors pCAMBIA1300 and pUCCRNAi were used to construct overexpression and RNAi silencing binary vectors, respectively. sgRNA was cloned into the pHSE401 vector for CRISPR genome editing (Xing et al. 2014) (Table S1). The Agrobacterium strain EHA105 was transfected with each of the resulting constructs and used to transform WT and mab1‐D plants. The stable transformation of tobacco and subsequent detection of insertion sites were performed as previously described (Li et al. 2025).

WT and transgenic plants were cultivated in the greenhouse and in the field. The length of the axillary bud/branch at each leaf position was measured at both the rosette stage (25 days after transplanting) and the prosperously growing stage (50 days after transplanting) (Figure S12). Tre6P and GA levels in the base of the three axillary buds/branches below the terminal bud were measured in WT and transgenic plants at the prosperously growing stage using appropriate assay kits (Jingmei Biotechnology Co. Ltd).

Transcriptional Activation Assay

The full‐length cDNA of NtMAB1‐S1 was inserted into the pGBT9 vector, which was used in transcriptional activation assays. The yeast strain Y1H was transformed with the empty vector (negative control) and pGBT9‐VP16 vector (positive control) according to the Yeast Protocols Handbook (Clontech). The transformed colonies were cultured on SD/−Trp medium for 2 days and then streaked onto SD/−Trp/+X‐gal medium. The constructs 35S::NtMAB1‐S1‐ΔBRD‐VP16 and 35S::NtMAB1‐S1‐VP16 were inserted into the pCAMBIA1300 vector.

RNA‐Seq Analysis

Three axillary buds/branches below the terminal bud of WT and mab1‐D plants at the prosperously growing stage were used in RNA‐seq analyses by BGI (Shenzhen, China). Sets of two independent biological replicates were collected and analysed. The cleaned data were aligned with the Nicotiana tabacum assembled genome NtaSR1_genome_1.0 (Wang et al. 2024). Fragments per kilobase of transcript sequence per million mapped reads (FPKM) were used to quantify transcription levels. A false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05, as determined by DESeq2, was set as the threshold for identifying DEGs. Genes with a fold change ≥ 2 were classified as significantly differentially expressed.

Electrophoretic Mobility Shift Assay (EMSA)

EMSAs were performed as described previously (Xue et al. 2024) with minor modifications. Competent Escherichia coli Transetta (DE3) cells were transformed with the pCold‐NtMAB1‐S1 construct to express the protein 6His‐NtMAB1‐S1. The expressed protein was purified using assay kits (Cat No. 20503ES10; Yeasen, Shanghai, China). Probes labelled with biotin at the 5′ end and unlabelled probes were used as binding or competitive probes.

Chromatin Immunoprecipitation (ChIP) Assay

The vectors pCAMBIA1300‐GFP and pCAMBIA1300‐NtMAB1‐S1‐GFP were utilised for ChIP‐qPCR assays. The protein–DNA complex was immunoprecipitated with anti‐GFP antibodies (1:5000 dilution, BE2002, EASYBIO) and the precipitated DNA recovered. An equivalent amount of chromatin without antibody treatment served as the input control. ChIP DNA was analysed using qPCR and the results were normalised against the corresponding input values.

Dual‐Luciferase Assay

The full‐length coding sequence of NtMAB‐S1 was inserted into the pGreenII 62‐SK vector under the control of the CaMV 35S promoter. The NtTPPF and NtGA2ox8 promoter sequences were cloned into the pGreenII 0800‐LUC vector. Tobacco leaves were transiently transfected with the resulting constructs in dual‐luciferase assays. The activities of firefly luciferase (LUC) and Renilla luciferase (REN) were quantified using the Dual‐Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Infinite M200Pro). Three independent biological replicates were performed.

Accession Numbers

Sequencing data from this article can be found in the tobacco database (http://lifenglab.hzau.edu.cn/Nicomics/) under the accession numbers Nta23g14120 (NtMAB1‐S1), Nta16g05010 (NtTPPF) and Nta03g29610 (NtGA2ox8).

Data Analysis

All data were analysed using one‐way ANOVA followed by a post‐hoc Tukey's test or Student's t‐test with SPSS 26.0. Data are shown as mean ± standard deviation.
