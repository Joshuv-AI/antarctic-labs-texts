Novel strategies to FIND people living with genetic dyslipidemias: The family heart foundation flag, identify, network, and deliver (FIND) familial hypercholesterolemia collaborative learning network

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFamilial Hypercholesterolemia (FH) is among the most common genetic disorders. However, most people with FH are undiagnosed and many experience preventable premature cardiovascular disease. To improve identification of FH, the Family Heart Foundation established the Flag Identify Network Deliver™ Collaborative Learning Network (FIND FH™ CLN). This multi-year quality improvement initiative involves five healthcare systems, individuals with FH, and quality improvement/implementation scientists. This manuscript describes the methods and results of the FIND FH CLN.MethodsThe FIND FH CLN leveraged a machine learning model (MLM) run on de-identified data from each healthcare system, coupled with implementation/quality improvement methods to enhance FH diagnosis. Healthcare systems were supported in identifying care gaps, engaging patients in diagnostic assessment, locating improvement opportunities, and implementing feasible interventions. Tracked outcomes included outreach volume, completed appointments, and new diagnoses of FH. Improvement approaches, care process changes, and challenges/lessons learned were recorded.ResultsAcross sites, 4476 individuals were flagged by the MLM; 847 patients were contacted following output review, 209 appointments were completed, and 175 diagnoses of definite, probable, or possible FH resulted. Background

Familial Hypercholesterolemia (FH) is a common (1/250 to 1/300) autosomal dominant genetic disease of cholesterol metabolism that is associated with the development and progression of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) [1]. FH has been classified by the Center for Disease Control as a Tier 1 genomic condition, meaning diagnosis and treatment are associated with significant potential for improved public health based on evidence-based guidelines and recommendations [2]. Without identification and appropriate treatment, FH leads to marked elevation of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) with associated risks for ASCVD events and shortened lifespan, with an average age of 47 years for a cardiovascular event [[3], [4], [5]]. Despite the availability of affordable and effective lipid lowering therapies, recent estimates suggest that 70 % of individuals with FH remain undiagnosed and many remain under-treated [6,7].

Recognizing the significant preventable harm associated with missed or delayed diagnosis, there is a need to leverage existing improvement/implementation science, develop new strategies, and use of state-of-the-art methods to improve FH detection with the goal of facilitating appropriate treatment. Accordingly, the Family Heart Foundation - an organization whose mission is to save generations of families from heart disease through timely identification and improved care of people living with genetic dyslipidemias – convened Flag- Identify-Network-Deliver™ (FIND, a multi-year, multi-institutional, quality improvement initiative). FIND FH™, was launched in 2016. In 2022, a collaborative learning network (CLN) component was added. The FIND FH Program involved the development of an artificial intelligence-based machine learning model (MLM) that utilized structured medical record data to identify undiagnosed individuals likely to have FH. MLMs offer a scalable approach toward identifying individuals that have not yet been diagnosed with various conditions by ingesting and analyzing large amounts of data to identify features that collectively may point to conditions that could be missed in clinical practice [8].

Prior research suggests that quality improvement collaboratives that incorporate both inter-and intra-organizational learning, including shared learning sessions, collaborative faculty, and small tests of change support performance improvements [9,10]. The FIND FH CLN focused on the development of novel strategies to integrate the MLM output into clinical practice using an implementation science approach, with learnings shared throughout the collaborative. This manuscript reports on the CLN, outreach and awareness approaches, as well as outcomes of the FIND FH CLN through March 2025.

Methods

The FIND FH program

The FIND FH MLM was developed using healthcare system encounter data of individuals with and without FH at four healthcare systems, with initial performance metrics suggesting high levels of precision and recall [11,12]. The FIND FH MLM is driven largely by prescription-based, diagnosis-based, and procedure-based features, as well as lab results, but also takes into account relationships between variables (e.g. LDL-C in the context of lipid-lowering medications) [11]. One organization deployed the model and performed patient outreach to those flagged as “at risk” of having FH; this initial organizational effort predated the CLN [13].

Collaborative learning network

The FIND FH CLN used a mixed methods approach anchored in the Institute for Healthcare Improvement breakthrough collaborative framework [14]. Given gaps in baseline care delivery were significant, and evidence based best practices were lacking, novel interventions were developed and tested. The CLN used an implementation science approach to better understand barriers and facilitators to practice change, engage diverse stakeholders including individuals living with FH to “co-produce” outreach materials and develop processes using human centered design principles, and identify core goals and strategies to enable scale-up and spread [[15], [16], [17], [18]]. Throughout the course of the collaboration the sites and a team of individuals living with FH met individually with quality improvement/implementation science faculty and coaches from the Family Heart Foundation for guidance and collectively convened for shared learning sessions. The faculty and coaches had nationally recognized expertise in quality improvement, implementation science, behavioral economics, and human-centered design. Sites shared details about their care process improvement efforts and outcomes with the CLN coaches and typically met every two weeks over the course of their improvement work for updates and consultation. All sites participated in expert faculty learning sessions to introduce tools and methods for practice improvement.

Five healthcare systems participated in the FIND FH CLN – including two from the initial MLM development and three new sites [11]. Sites were selected by the Family Heart Foundation based upon set criteria: a) proven clinical champion collaborator; b) organizational support; c) information technology support and capability to pull, de-identify and structure data; and d) legal review and execution of a data use agreement and master collaborative agreement. Each site worked with their information technology teams to extract de-identified data from the population served by their health system per an input specification (consisting of demographics, ICD diagnostic and procedure codes, medications, and laboratory results). Data were from structured fields and did not include free text notes. Sites restricted the data gathered to enrich their population for those with ASCVD risk factors, with subsequent quality assurance review prior to the data files being entered into the MLM.

Sites received a model output consisting of a list of patients flagged as possible FH. The flag was binary, representing the patients who met the cut-off score classification threshold for possibly having FH. Upon return of the model output, sites re-identified individuals and developed strategies for chart review, patient outreach, and diagnostic assessment. Sites were encouraged to use modified Simon Broome/Dutch Lipid Network criteria during chart review [19,20]. Strict criteria for determination of “Unlikely FH” vs “Appropriate for Outreach and Assessment” were not standardized across sites. Sites also encouraged cascade screening to identify family members with undiagnosed FH and facilitated connections with supportive resources for newly diagnosed individuals (See Fig. 1 for a High-level Process Flow). As shown in Fig. 1, FIND FH recognizes that the work to reduce poor health outcomes continues after diagnosis. However, since specific care outcomes were beyond the scope of this quality improvement effort, treatment details for newly identified individuals were not collected.

Sites conducted a baseline assessment that represented the initial groundwork for the planned process of re-identification, chart review, primary care and patient outreach, as well as assessment and referral to supportive services. Sites were encouraged to utilize small cycle tests of change/ Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) to identify gaps in care, opportunities for improvement, and clinical practice interventions that were feasible given contextual factors in their local environment [21]. One site structured their workflow as an implementation science research project formally comparing several different outreach approaches. As a quality improvement initiative, no IRB approval was required for this overall initiative; several sites completed local IRB QI determinations confirming their work was non-human subjects research and the aforementioned site formally conducting implementation science research received human subjects research approval. Given the QI nature of this work formal informed consent was not obtained, individuals simply expressed interest in proceeding with an FH evaluation if they were interested in doing so. While several sites continue to actively reach out to patients from their output lists, this manuscript reports on outcomes as of March 31, 2025.

Analysis

Quantitative and qualitative data were collected and used to evaluate the outcomes of the FIND FH CLN, including patient outreach, education and awareness activities, and clinic evaluations. Quantitative data include the MLM output, exclusions, outreach volume, success in completing appointments and identifying new diagnoses of FH. Data ranges and percentages were used to categorize process metrics and variation across sites in their patient outreach activities. Qualitative data regarding clinical team processes and experiences were also collected and analyzed to better understand approaches utilized, challenges experienced, and lessons learned. Qualitative data were collected through debriefing interviews with key personnel at each site. The Family Heart Foundation team reviewed all data, and queried sites for additional details.

Results

Outreach approaches

Upon receiving their MLM output file, each site conducted chart reviews to exclude individuals not appropriate for outreach. While the MLM output was designed to help identify possible FH, the chart review process enabled teams to confirm their outreach was appropriate. Some exclusion criteria were applied across sites (e.g. individuals who were deceased since the time the model was run or had an FH diagnosis in their chart – either a new diagnosis since data extraction or discovered in free text); sites also applied individualized criteria regarding who was appropriate for outreach (See Table 1 for exclusionary factors at each site). Members of the FIND FH CLN teams (clinicians, medical students, pharmacists) reported that chart reviews typically took between 5–15 min per patient. Chart review enabled teams to both reduce subsequent workload and increase the likelihood that ensuing efforts would be high yield. Lengthier reviews were generally due to factors such as the need to review various scanned media files outside of defined fields for LDL-C values, data entry for site-based evaluation, and onboarding additional personnel. Many patients were excluded because the information in their chart was too sparse for the team to ascertain a potential FH diagnosis and feel comfortable engaging patients, e.g. they were missing LDL-C values, either in entirety or just pre-treatment, or untreated LDL-C values were low without apparent evidence to suspect FH.

Sites also varied in their outreach approaches (See Table 1). Sites engaged primary care practitioners (PCPs) in differing ways: prior to or simultaneous with patient outreach, explicitly encouraging clinicians to decline outreach if deemed inappropriate, and/or reach out to their patient to encourage evaluation. Options also included PCP referral for subspecialty consultation vs. providing the PCP with guidance to conduct the FH evaluation independently. Sites also varied in both the modes and extent of patient outreach, using a variety of strategies (phone calls, EHR messages, secure texts, letters, and secure email). Personnel conducting the outreach was also variable-ranging from the cardiologist and lipidologist who would provide the subsequent evaluations, to a clinic coordinator, advanced practice provider, research coordinator, medical students, a student intern, and a care navigator.

Sites engaged in a variety of activities to develop awareness of FH and of the FIND FH program within their healthcare system. These efforts included meetings with medical directors/primary care leadership, grand rounds presentations for primary care audiences, brief presentations during departmental meetings, and email communications to primary care and subspecialty departments. One health system engaged an individual with FH in their presentations to highlight the personal importance of diagnosis. In addition, several sites developed educational websites for patients and members of their healthcare team, including links to further resources.

Several sites made modifications to their EHR to either provide clinical decision support or patient education. For instance, two sites introduced a lab result notification that included text regarding the possibility of FH for LDL values>190 mg/dL, as well as links for further information about FH. Three sites introduced a SmartSet to guide FH management. One site tested the use of best-practice alerts, but to better align with clinical workflows pivoted to a pended order for referral for the FH evaluation visit—PCPs could accept or decline a referral for their patient, with non-response defaulting to a referral. One site implemented a dot phrase which incorporated a drop-down list from which PCPs could list all relevant FH criteria met by the patient and recommendations for further screening. Finally, another site created an embedded care path tracking mechanism within the EHR.

Assessment approaches

Assessment practices varied across sites. Most visits for FH evaluation were in person and included a clinician from the site’s FIND team (typically a lipid specialist or preventive cardiologist). All sites offered expedited appointments (e.g. slots reserved for patients identified through FIND). Most sites offered appointments during regular clinic hours; one site also offered appointment options in the evening (See Table 1).

Across sites, a total of 4476 individuals were flagged by the MLM (range per site 370–1583) (See Table 2). After chart review, sites excluded between 14 % to 76 % of their output list from outreach efforts. Of note, the three sites with the lowest exclusion rates continue to actively review their patients and are still conducting outreach; accordingly, these rates are likely to increase. Differences will likely remain, however, due to variation in exclusionary factors across sites. For one of the sites with outreach efforts still pending, the remaining efforts are focused on patients deemed “unlikely FH” by chart review.

Site outreach outcomes.

To date, two sites have completed outreach to patients deemed appropriate (24 % and 32 % of initial patients flagged), with three sites continuing to make determinations about which patients warrant outreach. These efforts resulted in a total of 209 completed patient visits across the sites (range 14–77), which represented an average of 24.7 % of individuals for whom outreach was initiated (range 14.2 % to 51.0 %). Across sites, a total of 175 patients were diagnosed with possible, probable, or definite FH (83.7 % of all patients evaluated, range 78.1 %−100 %). Fig. 2 provides a summary of this process. Based on the 4476 individuals flagged, these 175 diagnoses represent a 3.9 % identification rate compared to the expected population-level FH prevalence of roughly 1/250 or 0.4 %. When limited to the two sites that have completed all outreach efforts, there were a total of 72 new diagnoses out of 839 flagged individuals, representing an 8.6 % identification rate.

Discussion

The FIND FH CLN enabled a novel previously validated MLM to be deployed and integrated into practice in five healthcare systems. This resulted in the identification and diagnosis of 92 individuals with definite or probable FH, and an additional 83 individuals with possible FH, representing 44 % and 40 % of all those completing a diagnostic evaluation, respectively. In aggregate, this represents 21 % of individuals in whom outreach was initiated. This is a significant diagnostic yield from this ongoing initiative. Moreover, accelerating the identification of individuals with FH in partnership with their healthcare teams provides opportunity to mitigate risk and improve health outcomes. Cascade screening to identify family members also at risk may lead to additional FH diagnoses and appropriate treatment. Further, while not systematically assessed across site, one site interviewed patients identified through FIND and local clinicians (primary care and cardiology) and found that both groups perceived the direct to patient FH outreach favorably and recognized an impact beyond the individuals directly identified [22].

Despite variation across sites, this average yield of 21 % diagnosed with FH of those receiving outreach is a 10-fold improvement over the 2 % from the initial deployment of this MLM at a single center [13]. The diagnostic yield observed in this initiative is still below that predicted in prior validation studies [11,12]. One possible explanation for this difference may be that the prior validation studies used a stricter definition of ASCVD, resulting in a population with an expected higher prevalence of FH. Regardless, the real-world application of this novel FIND strategy illustrates the complexities in screening as well as the challenges of connecting with and motivating patients to pursue a potential diagnosis for a genetically based condition. The co-production of outreach materials including a team of individuals living with FH enabled carefully thought-out strategies and messaging that may explain, in-part, the improved outreach effectiveness compared to the prior study. Despite these improvements, there are several points in the process with drop-offs that reduced the overall number of patients diagnosed with FH, each of which provide important learnings.

The first drop-off point in the outreach process, 38 % of the initial output, was due to the application of exclusion criteria following chart review. While the FIND FH MLM did not require LDL-C values for its determination, sites typically were uncomfortable reaching out without this information. Lack of complete data is a known limitation of using EHR datasets [23,24]. Further, the challenges of identifying LDL-C values is consistent with prior research that suggests roughly a quarter to a third of adults for whom lipid evaluation is recommended have not been screened within the last five years [25,26], and the percentage of children not receiving recommended lipid screening is even higher [27]. This highlights both the need for more regular LDL-C testing in adults and children, as well as better EHR functionality for incorporating outside labs. Information about family history of ASCVD, including age of onset, was also sparse, hindering the identification of an FH diagnosis with established criteria [28]. These gaps in documented patient history are consistent with prior studies demonstrating knowledge gaps among both PCPs and specialists regarding FH, manifesting in subpar documentation of family history and relevant physical exam findings [[29], [30], [31]]. These findings suggest a need for more robust clinician-facing FH education in parallel to more regular lipid testing and enhanced EHR functionality.

Several sites experienced a second drop off point between individuals deemed appropriate for outreach and outreach initiation due to workforce limitations/time constraints. Healthcare labor shortages are widespread and have intensified since the COVID-19 pandemic, impacting patient care and time availability for novel initiatives [32]. The proactive, specialty preventive outreach approach required by this initiative is outside the realm of standard health system care delivery. The resource-intensive nature of the outreach and assessments demanded substantial time investment from study team members.

Despite the improved rate of clinical evaluations completed compared to a prior study, there remains a significant drop-off of 75 % between outreach initiation and completed appointments. This highlights the challenges of connecting to patients and successfully conveying the importance of diagnostic assessment amidst patients’ competing life demands and financial constraints. This drop-off may also reflect patient skepticism when contacted by someone with whom they do not already have a trusting relationship. Accordingly, some patients preferred to defer to their PCP or existing cardiologist before participating in further screening. Studies have suggested that direct communication about FH diagnosis from PCPs is preferred and can also result in more family members being diagnosed per individual initially diagnosed [33,34]. A further source of skepticism may have arisen from utilizing outreach methods considered non-traditional (i.e., texts and email) in a healthcare context. To exacerbate this, while some sites were able to generate institutional email addresses and phone lines associated with their institution, others were unable to do so, leading to additional patient concern and/or lack of attention when communication stemmed from an unknown source. Finally, lack of awareness of FH and genetic dyslipidemias may contribute to reticence about the need to address this issue [30]. Reservation to engage with the outreach teams may also have been hindered by references to the MLM, with some patients noting concern over how their EHR data was used.

Importantly, the learnings and challenges identified by the FIND FH CLN served as a catalyst to develop health system adaptations that may help with sustaining improvements in FH identification and care. Several sites introduced sustainable EHR features to support FH identification, education, and care. These included embedding FH and Family Heart information into lab reports for individuals with high LDL-C values, smartphrases that provide FH information to both patients and other members of the healthcare team, as well as pended referrals to facilitate patients connecting to appropriate care. These strategies help overcome some of the limitations of healthcare delivery—where clinician capacity is limited, visit time is constrained, and patient supports are sparse. These automated tools, integrated into the EHR, can expedite screening in real-time. Existing research suggests that primary care clinicians are generally supportive of user-friendly clinical decision support systems (CDS) to aid in diagnosing FH [35]. While clinicians often appreciate the assistance of CDS tools, implementation of such tools requires a sensitivity to alert fatigue, overall workload, and preferences for specialty vs. primary care management [36,37]. By collaborating with clinical informaticists at their local institutions, sites can tailor CDS builds to optimize uptake through changes such as limiting accessibility to relevant specialties, including pre-checked orders based on clinical indications, monitoring EHR usage analytics, or conducting formal usability testing.

As sites transition from a focused effort to sustained improvement, they may shift their approach from high-effort, lower reach strategies to low-effort broad reach tactics that may be better incorporated into existing care pathways. These varied approaches each have unique challenges and limitations. For instance, while an LDL-C cut-off of 190 can serve as a simple indicator, the anticipated yield is only 7 % [38] (compared to the present study’s 21 % of those receiving outreach); further, a recent deep-dive by one of the participating site’s found that many of the individuals identified through the FIND FH MLM would have been overlooked by a simple LDL-C 190 cutoff [39]. Accordingly, implementing several approaches may ultimately produce a synergy that reduces gaps in FH identification.

Of note, while not systematically recorded, several sites reported a significant number of new referrals for FH evaluation during their FIND work, likely, at least in part due to healthcare system education and awareness efforts. Further, the individuals excluded from outreach due to an existing FH diagnosis, may have in some instances been individuals that were identified between the time data was submitted and the model being subsequently run and outreach commencing. Accordingly, these individuals similarly may have been identified through education and awareness efforts. In addition, some patients who were contacted as part of the outreach process may have declined to come in for an evaluation but may have subsequently spoken to other members of their healthcare team, potentially resulting in additional diagnoses down the line that would not be captured in the current numbers. While there have been efforts to quantify learnings [30], additional efforts to study the impact of FIND education and awareness strategies on clinical practice are needed.

While outreach efforts may not always lead to a definitive ICD-10 coded FH diagnosis, prior work has demonstrated that the mere recorded mention of clinical suspicion for FH in the EHR is associated with more rigorous treatment modalities, such as more intense therapeutics for lipid management, frequent testing, and advanced screening for coronary artery disease [28]. This suggests that even when outreach efforts did not result in an FH diagnosis, they may still result in measurable benefit in identifying high-risk individuals not currently receiving guideline-recommended levels of therapy and optimal preventive focused care.

Limitations

The variation in site processes and tracking mechanisms limit comparison and generalizability across sites. In addition, site differences in approaches for documenting exclusionary factors preclude the possibility of clearly tallying specific reasons for exclusions. However, site variability also served as an opportunity for learning. Sites varied in the tradeoffs made, each finding a balance that worked with their team’s capacity. Sites balanced the desire to be thorough and work through the entirety of output vs. conserve outreach resources and focus on individuals with the highest likelihood of FH. In addition, sites balanced a focus on the immediate successes of diagnosing individuals from their MLM output lists with FH vs. long-term sustainable approaches for identification. This natural tailoring of approaches helps demonstrate how core functional needs can be met through a variety of strategies, suggesting opportunities for adaptations in the process of scaling and spreading best practices identified [17]. As noted, several sites reported an increase in referrals beyond the individuals identified from their MLM output list, however, this increase was not systematically measured. Further, while the impact on cascade testing was not assessed, one patient newly diagnosed through FIND shared, “You've helped me understand my father and brother's early deaths. I hope this information and medications can change things for me and my sons."

The FIND FH MLM was trained on data from US healthcare systems and applied among a US population at 5 healthcare systems [11,12]. While the overall methodology and potential for EMR-based approaches to FH identification would likely translate outside the US, the model would likely need to be retrained and refined to perform well in other countries. In addition, the implementation learnings and approaches would also likely need to be adapted to local cultures, both regionally and internationally, for the impact to be fully realized.

Conclusions and future directions

This initiative highlighted that health system change is complex and benefits from an ecosystem approach - support of multiple stakeholders, engagement of diverse teams, and building and reinforcing awareness throughout the health system [30]. Future work should explore opportunities to spread and scale learnings to the related genetic dyslipidemia (elevated Lp(a)) and to additional health systems. Future MLMs may be developed to facilitate precision screening for both conditions. Utilizing existing networks, e.g. the Cascade Registry [40], and sharing concise best-practice/implementation tips may be one strategy to extend the reach and impact of this work. Further, previous work suggests that this MLM based approach to FH detection has the potential to be a cost-effective strategy [12]. While the FIND FH CLN focused on developing, implementing, and learning from effective outreach and engagement approaches, as systems shift towards more sustainable approaches, future work should explore the return on investments of such strategies.

Additional work is needed to optimize the use of EHR data to improve FH detection. Developing strategies to integrate MLMs and real-time CDS tools into clinical workflows may enable prospective, continuous screening. This real-time functionality is currently being explored in other contexts, e.g. to predict risk for cardiorespiratory arrest [41]. For FH, the potential to incorporate lipid panels, ICD codes, family history, and clinician free-text notes–as they are entered, could enable real-time identification of individuals benefiting from further clinical review. Efforts to explain these approaches so that both patients and clinicians understand and trust them will likely improve confidence in their output and increase the likelihood that they lead to improvements in diagnosis and help to close care gaps.

While appropriate treatment was the ultimate goal of the FIND FH CLN, additional work is needed to explore how this initiative impacted care of the patients identified and family screening. Further, exploring how improvements in clinician awareness translate to future patient identification and management of FH may offer insight into how FIND’s impact extends beyond the patients directly identified in this initiative.

As healthcare systems continue to explore how artificial intelligence can improve patient care and reduce clinician burden, the learnings from the FIND FH CLN may inform efforts for other conditions. The Family Heart Foundation recently launched a similar initiative to find individuals living with elevated lipoprotein(a), FIND Lp(a) ™, with strategy incorporating insights from the FIND FH CLN.
