Vedolizumab Versus Other Biologics and the Risk of Venous Thromboembolism in Patients with Pediatric-Onset Inflammatory Bowel Diseases: A Target Trial Emulation Study
Authors: Serena Yun-Chen Tsai, Ashwin N Ananthakrishnan, Harland S Winter, Kevin Sheng-Kai Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPediatric-onset inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD) are associated with an elevated risk of venous thromboembolism (VTE). However, data on the VTE risk in these patients treated with novel biologics, particularly vedolizumab, remain limited.AimsTo evaluate the association between vedolizumab and the risk of VTE compared with other biologics in patients with pediatric-onset Crohn’s disease (CD) or ulcerative colitis (UC).MethodsWe emulated a target trial using electronic health records from the TriNetX research network. The study included patients with CD or UC diagnosed before the age of 18 years who were treated with vedolizumab, ustekinumab, or anti-tumor necrosis factor (TNF) agents. Comparative groups were generated using 1:1 propensity-score matching based on relevant confounders. The primary outcome was the occurrence of any VTE within 12 months of follow-up. Cox proportional hazards models were used to compare the risk of VTE between vedolizumab-treated patients and those treated with anti-TNF agents and ustekinumab.ResultsA total of 1806 matched patient pairs were analyzed across four comparisons: patients treated with vedolizumab versus anti-TNF agents (CD = 407 pairs; UC = 443 pairs), and vedolizumab versus ustekinumab (CD = 563 pairs; UC = 393 pairs), respectively. Patients with CD receiving vedolizumab had a significantly higher risk of VTE compared with those receiving anti-TNF therapy (hazard ratio [HR] 8.63; 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.08–69.10; p = 0.014). A higher VTE risk was also observed in vedolizumab-treated patients with CD compared with those treated with ustekinumab (HR 4.64; 95% CI 1.35–15.97; p = 0.007). In contrast, no significant difference in VTE risk was found between vedolizumab and either comparator group in patients with UC.ConclusionsTreatment with vedolizumab was associated with a higher incidence of VTE than anti-TNF agents or ustekinumab among individuals with CD. Prospective cohort studies are warranted to validate the safety of biologic therapy for pediatric patients with CD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40272-025-00717-2.

Key Points

A higher venous thromboembolism risk was observed in children with Crohn’s disease treated with vedolizumab compared with those on anti-TNF agents or ustekinumab; the heightened risk reached statistical significance only in Crohn’s disease, not in ulcerative colitis.This association may reflect a pharmacologic effect of the medication or a consequence of residual inflammation resulting from an insufficient therapeutic response.

Introduction

The advent of biologic therapies such as ustekinumab and vedolizumab has revolutionized the treatment of inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD). Despite limited data and its off-label status, vedolizumab is now frequently used in children with IBD [1]. While many studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of these biologics in achieving disease remission and established their overall favorable safety profile in Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC) [2–4], few studies have explored their impact on comorbidities in patients with pediatric-onset IBD.

The disease not only exhibits a bimodal age distribution influenced by various genetic and environmental factors but it also manifests differently across age groups [5, 6]. For instance, patients with pediatric-onset IBD often face more extensive intestinal involvement, faster disease progression, and higher risks of systemic complications compared with individuals with adult-onset IBD [7]. Beyond the intestinal involvement, extraintestinal comorbidities pose another major challenge, often complicating the already complex management of IBD [8]. Among these, cardiovascular events are particularly consequential owing to their substantial clinical burden [9]. Patients with IBD carry a greater risk for venous thromboembolism (VTE), contributing to significant morbidity and mortality [10]. The predisposition to a prothrombotic state is largely driven by disease-related pathophysiology, such as activation of the coagulation cascade and impairment of the intestinal barrier function. VTE in IBD also has unique characteristics, such as an earlier onset, higher rates in both active and remission phases, frequent recurrence, and unfavorable outcomes [11]. The risk of VTE in IBD is not confined to older populations but is also relevant to younger patients [12]. Data from the PIBD-SETQuality Safety Registry of 30,193 children with IBD showed that VTE was the most common adverse event, occurring at a rate of 5.50 per 10,000 person-years, followed by renal failure, opportunistic infection, and cancer [13]. A recent study found that children with IBD faced a substantially increased risk of VTE near the time of diagnosis (relative risk 51.8, 95% confidence interval [CI] 41.8–64.3) compared with those without IBD, with the incidence rate being higher in UC than in CD (12.4 versus 5.6 per 10,000 patient-years) [14].

In addition, certain treatments, prolonged hospital stays, pregnancy, and comorbid conditions such as metabolic syndrome may elevate the risk of VTE in IBD. Studies report that patients with IBD receiving systemic corticosteroids have a two-fold increased risk of VTE [15]. An opposite association was observed in patients treated with biologic agents alone, primarily anti-tumor necrosis factor (TNF) agents, with a five-fold lower risk of VTE compared with those receiving corticosteroid therapy [16]. Nevertheless, evidence regarding the risk of VTE in patients treated with newer biologics, such as vedolizumab and ustekinumab, remains limited and is predominantly derived from adult studies [10, 17].

To address this knowledge gap, we conducted a retrospective cohort study to examine the association between various biologics and the subsequent risk of VTE among patients with pediatric-onset IBD. We assessed whether the risk of VTE differed between patients treated with vedolizumab and those receiving other biologics, including anti-TNF agents and ustekinumab.

Methods

Study Design and Data Source

In this retrospective cohort study, data were obtained from the TriNetX research network (Cambridge, Massachusetts, USA), which collects real-time electronic health records from its collaborative healthcare organizations in the USA. The data retrieval period ranged from 1 October 2015 to 30 June 2024. As the study did not involve identified data, the institutional review board exempted the review. To strengthen causality and enhance the reliability of our findings, the study employed a target trial emulation framework, an approach that enables the use of observational data to simulate a hypothetical randomized controlled trial (RCT) [18, 19]. The method is considered valuable in IBD research, especially when conducting traditional RCTs is impractical or unfeasible [20]. The study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines.

Eligibility Criteria

The diagnoses were obtained on the basis of International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) codes (Supplementary Table 1). Patients with either CD or UC diagnosed under 18 years of age were eligible for the study. Patients with a history of coagulation disorders and malignancy were excluded, as they are known to be strong risk factors for VTE [21, 22]. Cohorts were established according to the type of biologics patients received from prescription records, including anti-TNF agents and their biosimilars (i.e., adalimumab, certolizumab, golimumab, and infliximab), vedolizumab, and ustekinumab. Owing to the limited number of patients receiving risankizumab in the database, a cohort for this agent was not established. Only first-time recipients of the biologics were included. The index date was defined as the date of the first prescription of the assigned drug. To ensure the temporal relationship between diagnosis and treatment, the diagnosis must have been recorded within 1 year prior to the index date. To mitigate the crossover effect, we excluded patients who had previously received the comparator medication. For example, in the analysis comparing vedolizumab and ustekinumab, individuals in the vedolizumab group were not treated with ustekinumab prior to the index date, and vice versa. Considering that vedolizumab is typically used as second-line medication for IBD, we restricted the vedolizumab group to biologic-naïve patients when comparing them with those treated with anti-TNF agents.

Baseline Characteristics and Study Outcomes

To address potential confounders that may affect the relationship between drug exposure and the outcome being studied, we controlled for the following variables through matching: demographic factors (age, sex, and race/ethnicity), smoking, obesity, prior medication use (systemic corticosteroids, immunosuppressants, and anti-TNF agents), prior surgical history, and laboratory data (hemoglobin, albumin, C-reactive protein, and fecal calprotectin). Data on these covariates were collected from records available before the index date.

The primary endpoint was the de novo occurrence of VTE. Patients with a VTE event that occurred before the index date were excluded from the analysis. Selected effectiveness outcomes including subsequent use of steroids, surgical interventions, and fecal calprotectin < 150 µg/g were assessed. We evaluated the risk of VTE among patients with pediatric-onset CD/UC treated with vedolizumab as compared with those patients receiving anti-TNF agents and ustekinumab, respectively. The outcomes were assessed following the intention-to-treat principle, with all patients being analyzed on the basis of their initial treatment allocation. All patients were monitored from the day following the index date until the occurrence of one of the following censoring events: the prespecified study outcome, death, loss to follow-up, or conclusion of the observation window (1 year from the index date), whichever occurred first.

Statistical Analysis

Using logistic regression, we estimated propensity scores that represent the conditional probability of treatment assignment on the basis of the previously described baseline characteristics. [23]. The propensity scores were then used in the following 1:1 matching using a nearest-neighbor algorithm with a caliper width of 0.1 pooled standard deviations (SD). Hazard ratios and 95% CIs were derived through the Cox proportional hazards model. The proportional hazard assumption was tested using the generalized Schoenfeld approach. A log-rank test p value < 0.05 is considered as statistically significant. As treatment persistence could serve as a proxy for clinical or endoscopic response or remission, we additionally conducted a within-vedolizumab-group sensitivity analysis to assess the risk of VTE among patients who had a continuous prescription of vedolizumab at least 6 months and 1 year from the index date, exploring whether remission status could have influenced the risk of VTE. All statistical analyses were conducted using TriNetX Advanced Analytics and RStudio (version 2023.12.1+402). Graphs were generated using the GraphPad Prism 10.4.2.

Results

Vedolizumab Versus Anti-TNF Agents in Biologic-Naïve Patients

Crohn’s Disease

After 1:1 propensity-score matching, the study analyzed 407 pairs of patients with pediatric-onset CD receiving vedolizumab or anti-TNF agents. The mean (SD) age of patients treated with vedolizumab and anti-TNF agents was 14.7 (3.5) and 14.9 (3.2) years, respectively. Females accounted for 49.1% of the vedolizumab group and 50.1% of the anti-TNF group, while the majority of patients in both cohorts were white (73% and 74%, respectively). In addition, more than 70% of patients in each group had a history of systemic corticosteroid use (Table 1).

Ulcerative Colitis

A total of 443 matched pairs of patients with pediatric-onset UC receiving vedolizumab (mean [SD] age: 14.9 [3.5] years, 48.3% females, and 67.0% white) or anti-TNF agents (mean [SD] age: 15.1 [3.1] years, 49.4% females, and 67.0% white) were included (Table 1). In contrast to results in the CD cohort, patients with UC treated with vedolizumab did not show a higher risk of VTE or differences in most selected effectiveness outcomes (use of steroids and fecal calprotectin < 150 µg/g) compared with the recipients of anti-TNF agents, whereas the risk of surgical interventions remained higher in the vedolizumab group (Table 2).

Ustekinumab Versus Anti-TNF Agents in Biologic-Naïve Patients

Overall, patients treated with ustekinumab did not demonstrate a higher risk of VTE or a difference in the incidence of selected effectiveness outcomes than patients with CD or UC treated with anti-TNF agents (Supplementary Table 2).

Approximately 80% of patients had received systemic corticosteroids and 66% had been treated with anti-TNF agents prior to initiating vedolizumab or ustekinumab (Table 3).

Discussion

Our study found that patients with pediatric-onset CD receiving vedolizumab had a significantly higher risk of VTE compared with children receiving anti-TNF agents or ustekinumab. While the direct effect of vedolizumab on VTE risk remains unclear, a few studies have explored this association, without reaching definitive conclusions. A higher rate of thromboembolic status in adult patients with IBD receiving vedolizumab compared with those receiving anti-TNF agents (1.0% versus 0.4%; proportional reporting ratio, 2.5 [95% CI 1.0–6.0]) was reported on the basis of the Food and Drug Administration Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) data [24]. In a conference abstract using the MarketScan claims database, adult patients with CD receiving vedolizumab were nearly three times more likely to develop venous thrombosis compared with those on anti-TNF therapy (incidence rate ratio [IRR] 2.67; 95% CI 1.32–5.41) [25]. As noted by other investigators, the results from the study by Cross et al. [25] may be biased given the higher proportion of corticosteroid use among patients receiving vedolizumab [26]. The anti-TNF agent infliximab is reported to reduce the IBD-attributable risk for VTE by normalizing clot lysis profiles and decreasing the rate of clot formation [27]. Vedolizumab concentrations are inversely correlated with plasma fibronectin levels, a protein essential for stabilizing platelet thrombi. That said, remitters usually have a drop in fibronectin concentration; however, inadequate response to vedolizumab or failure to achieve therapeutic levels may result in persistently elevated fibronectin levels and, by extension, a higher risk of VTE. At the same time, vedolizumab treatment is associated with a diminution of the clot lysis profile and accelerated clot formation, indicating a possible shift toward a hypercoagulable state despite its therapeutic effects [28, 29].

Few studies have explored the risk of VTE in patients with IBD treated with ustekinumab [30]. Among 1450 patients with CD receiving ustekinumab in a meta-analysis, only one episode of deep venous thrombosis was documented [31, 32]. Ustekinumab may either not harbor an increased thrombotic risk or, as suggested by our findings, may have an effect in reducing clot formation comparable to that of anti-TNF therapy. The potential antithrombotic effect might be related to its targeting of interleukin (IL)-12, as both IL-12 and TNF-α are pro-inflammatory cytokines involved in the coagulation cascades [33–35].

The concomitant use of steroids and surgical intervention could increase the risk of VTE in IBD [36]. Post-surgical VTE rates were higher in patients with IBD, with most occurring after discharge [37]. In our study, we accounted for baseline corticosteroid use through matching and compared the rates of steroid use and surgical interventions during the follow-up. However, neither steroid use nor surgery could fully explain the higher VTE risk observed in the vedolizumab group.

The differing results between the CD and UC cohorts may reflect differences in the efficacy of biologics between these two IBD subtypes. Evidence suggests that vedolizumab is effective for both CD and UC, but studies have shown that it tends to be more effective in UC than in CD, particularly with respect to clinical remission [38, 39]. The relatively lower efficacy in CD may result in inflammation being less controlled resulting in increased VTE events. In contrast, the absence of a similar VTE signal in patients with UC may reflect the more effective disease suppression achieved by vedolizumab in this population.

Our within-group sensitivity analyses provide further insight into the potential source of VTE risk in vedolizumab-treated patients. Although treatment persistence with biologics is generally associated with clinical remission in IBD [40], we observed that prolonged exposure to vedolizumab did not correspond to a reduction in VTE risk. When considering all available evidence, it remains unclear whether the elevated VTE risk observed is attributable to vedolizumab itself, assuming adequate disease control, or to ongoing inflammation in case of suboptimal treatment. Further evidence is needed to elucidate this relationship.

Strengths of this study include its large sample size and rigorous adjusting for confounders through matching. The study has several limitations, including its observational methodology and dependence on ICD-10 coding for diagnosis. Key clinical variables, such as disease activity, phenotype, location, and endoscopic/histologic findings of CD or UC were unavailable, as were relevant laboratory parameters. Data on treatment timing metrics and potential switching between biologics were also lacking. Furthermore, important risk factors for VTE, such as immobilization and hormonal contraception use, could not be captured using ICD-10 coding. Lastly, the limited ethnic diversity of the study population may restrict the generalizability of the findings.

Conclusions

In this target trial emulation study focusing on pediatric-onset IBD, we found that patients with CD, but not UC, treated with vedolizumab had a higher risk of VTE compared with those receiving anti-TNF agents or ustekinumab. Despite its substantial limitations, the study raises important safety considerations regarding vedolizumab use in this population. Future studies are warranted to confirm these associations, explore potential mechanisms, and guide biologic selection in clinical practice.
