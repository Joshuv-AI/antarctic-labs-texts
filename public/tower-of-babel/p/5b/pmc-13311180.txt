Predictors of treatment engagement in a group-based, telehealth smoking cessation program
Authors: Sarah M. Noone, Emily A. Atkinson, Andrea C. King, Daniel J. Fridberg, Emma I. Brett
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTobacco-related health disparities persist across age and socioeconomic status. Individual-level factors appear to influence engagement in smoking cessation treatment; however, findings have been mixed, with limited focus on telehealth treatment. The present study examined sociodemographic predictors of engagement in a telehealth smoking cessation program.MethodsPatients were primarily Black (78%), female (72%), and middle-aged or older (mean age = 58.2, range 21–92 years) adults who participated in a telehealth group smoking cessation program from 2020 to 2024 (N = 538). Engagement was defined as total sessions attended, treatment completion, nicotine replacement therapy (NRT) requests, and video vs. dial-in audio-only participation.ResultsPatients were more likely to complete treatment if they were older (p < .001) or White (p = .006). Patients were more likely to engage with telehealth treatment via video vs. dial-in audio-only if they were White (p = .003), employed (p = .02), and completed some college (p = .01).ConclusionsThis study shows differences in treatment engagement based on individual-level factors; older age, White race, and having higher education was predictive of increased engagement. Increased treatment accessibility may improve engagement among those most at risk for smoking-related harms. Findings highlight the program's strength in engaging older patients in telehealth.

Introduction

Cigarette smoking remains the leading preventable cause of death and disability worldwide, contributing to smoking-related diseases such as cancer, heart disease, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (CDC, 2024; CDC, 2025; Cornelius, 2023) and an estimated 7 million smoking-related deaths annually (Tobacco, 2026). Combustible tobacco use also places a significant burden on the healthcare system, with annual expenses reported at $225 billion in the United States alone (Xu et al., 2021). While global rates of smoking have declined (from 22.3% in 2007 to 16.4% in 2023 (World Health Organization, 2025)), quit rates differ across sociodemographic groups. For example, in the United States, smoking prevalence has remained stagnant in older adults (8.7% to 9.4% from 2011 to 2022) compared with substantial declines in younger adults (19.2% to 4.9% from 2011 to 2022 (Meza et al., 2023)). Also, studies and national data have consistently reported lower quit rates among Black relative to White adults who smoke (e.g., 4.9% of Black adults successfully quit compared to 7.1% of White adults) despite high quit intentions among Black adults (Leventhal et al., 2022; Jones et al., 2023; Avila et al., 2022; Babb, 2017). Further, tobacco-related morbidity and mortality is higher among Black versus White adults who smoke (U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, 2024; Bacio et al., 2014). Smoking rates are also higher among economically disadvantaged groups such that 20% of individuals with the lowest socioeconomic status (SES; <$35,000/year) report past month smoking compared with only 6% of those with the highest SES (≥$100,000/year) (U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, 2024; Garrett, 2019). These data highlight the need for effective targeted treatments to better engage those most at risk for smoking-related harms.

The “gold standard” for evidence-based smoking cessation treatment is FDA-approved medication (e.g., varenicline, bupropion, and/or nicotine replacement therapy [NRT]) combined with behavioral counseling (e.g., individual, group sessions, or tobacco quit lines) (Patnode et al., 2015; Hughes et al., 2011; Yingst et al., 2023). Treatment engagement, typically defined as treatment attendance and/or completion, has been linked to positive treatment outcomes (e.g., smoking cessation) across treatment modalities and populations (Mena et al., 2017; Graham et al., 2018; Brett et al., 2025; Paz Castro et al., 2017). Prior studies show that smoking cessation treatment engagement may be related to individual-level sociodemographic factors such as age, sex, education, and race/ethnicity (Ogunsanya et al., 2024; Figueiró et al., 2017; Siemer et al., 2020; Coffee et al., 2024; Jagielo et al., 2024). However, findings have been mixed. For example, while some work found that older age is associated with greater engagement and adherence for treatments ranging from counseling + NRT (Ogunsanya et al., 2024) to behavioral interventions alone (Figueiró et al., 2017; Siemer et al., 2020), other studies did not replicate this finding (Coffee et al., 2024). Additionally, studies investigating treatment engagement predictors have commonly focused on in-person behavioral counseling or randomized trials, and there has not been much emphasis on combined counseling + NRT programs for telehealth modalities.

Though telehealth smoking cessation treatment existed prior to the COVID-19 pandemic (e.g., quit lines and online resources), interest in identifying predictors of telehealth tobacco treatment engagement has grown in recent years given the significantly increased utilization of telehealth broadly in healthcare (Wosik et al., 2020). While the effectiveness of telehealth and in-person medical care is largely comparable (Siemer et al., 2020), factors that predict engagement specifically for telehealth treatment are unknown. Despite the touted benefit of telehealth for increasing accessibility, it remains uncertain whether these programs increase accessibility for everyone, or if some groups are “left behind” due to individual or structural barriers to engagement. For instance, studies across healthcare specialties found that younger age and higher education are associated with greater engagement in telehealth treatment (Jagielo et al., 2024; Lotito et al., 2023). This suggests that the link between older age and increased treatment engagement (Ogunsanya et al., 2024; Figueiró et al., 2017; Siemer et al., 2020) may not extend to telehealth treatments; further, different strategies may be necessary to promote engagement across different groups. Failing to understand and address these potential differences may result in a widening of existing tobacco-related health disparities.

To better understand factors that influence participation in telehealth cessation treatment, the present study aimed to evaluate individual-level predictors of engagement in a comprehensive group-based, rolling enrollment (i.e., continuous enrollment with no waiting period) stop-smoking program: Courage to Quit-Rolling Virtual (CTQ-RV). Early work supported the acceptability and feasibility of in-person CTQ across a range of community settings (Asvat et al., 2014) and within adults with lower income (Brett et al., 2023). Subsequent work by Brett and colleagues (2025) (Brett et al., 2025) found that CTQ-RV yielded better attendance, treatment completion, NRT use, and smoking cessation (33% vs. 16%) outcomes compared to the in-person group. Within the telehealth program, those who were able to join the treatment sessions with video-on (as opposed to dial-in audio-only) were more likely to report using NRT and reducing their smoking (Brett et al., 2025), suggesting video participation may be an additional marker of treatment engagement. Notably, in this sample, smoking abstinence was associated with number of sessions attended and treatment completion (Brett et al., 2025). However, factors that drive engagement in telehealth stop-smoking programs remain understudied.

The purpose of the present study was to investigate if telehealth-based smoking cessation program (CTQ-RV) engagement differed as a function of sociodemographic factors (i.e., age, sex, race, etc.). Identifying differences in telehealth smoking cessation engagement is crucial for the creation and refinement of targeted efforts to increase treatment engagement and retention and improve outcomes for those most at risk for smoking-related harms. Therefore, we examined the influence of age, sex, race, education level, and employment status on several aspects of treatment engagement, including the total number of sessions attended, treatment completion, requests for pharmacotherapy (NRT), and attendance mode (video vs. dial-in audio-only).

Methods

Study design and population

Enrollees were 538 adult patients who enrolled in the videoconference-based CTQ-RV program from 04/2020–12/2024. Patients were referred to the program by their medical provider from multiple clinics, including oncology, pulmonary medicine, cardiology, surgery, and primary care.

The program consisted of four core sessions (A, B, C, and D) delivered on a rolling basis each week by a tobacco treatment specialist (e.g., master's level social worker or doctoral level psychologist; 80% female, 70% Non-Hispanic White). Each CTQ-RV session was one hour long, took place on a secure videoconferencing platform during typical business hours, and focused on increasing motivation to quit smoking and developing cognitive and behavioral skills to cope with cravings and manage triggers (Fig. 1). See Brett et al. 2025 (Brett et al., 2025) for a detailed description of the program's telehealth adaptation protocols. Patients in the program were provided detailed instructions on how to join the program via videoconference or telephone dial-in audio, and study staff were available to troubleshoot technology problems with patients prior to sessions. Medically eligible patients (about 98% of those who expressed interest) had the option to receive no-cost NRT (nicotine patches, lozenges, and starting in 2022, nicotine gum) sent directly to their home address. Before receiving NRT, patients completed a safety form consenting to appropriate usage, e.g., using the product as instructed by the manufacturer on the packaging. NRT use was encouraged (for those eligible) at program intake and during the sessions as relevant topics were introduced (e.g., managing withdrawal). At each session, study staff recorded each patient's attendance, self-reported average cigarettes smoked per day during the past week, whether they requested NRT, and how they attended the group (i.e., via video or dial-in audio only). Measures

Baseline variables. Prior to their first session, patients completed a brief survey assessing demographic information (including age, sex assigned at birth, race, employment status, and education), smoking history (including number of smoking days per week, average number of cigarettes smoked per day, and cigarette type [menthol vs. non-menthol]), and readiness to change via the Smoking Contemplation Ladder (Biener and Abrams, 1991).

Engagement. Treatment engagement was defined by the total number of sessions attended, treatment completion (binary yes/no, i.e., attending at least four sessions), whether the patient requested the offered no-cost NRT (binary yes/no), and attendance mode (i.e., attending all or some of the sessions by video).

Statistical analyses

All analyses were exploratory as published findings on engagement broadly are either mixed or were not based upon data from a clinical, non-RCT, rolling enrollment group cessation program. Descriptive statistics were calculated for specific sociodemographic variables including age, sex, education, employment status, and smoking-related variables (described above). A series of multiple linear regression models for continuous outcomes and logistic regression models for binary outcomes examined the effect of specific sociodemographic characteristics on indicators of treatment engagement including total sessions attended, treatment completion, NRT use, and video vs. audio-only attendance. Sociodemographic predictor variables were entered into the models hierarchically to assess their individual and combined effects. For each model, age, race, and sex were entered at step 1, and education and employment were entered at step 2. Analyses were conducted using complete-case data and participants with missing data on variables included in a given model were excluded from that analysis.

We calculated R2 values for linear regression models and Nagelkerke pseudo-R2 values for logistic regression models. Model improvement was evaluated using F-change tests for linear regression and likelihood ratio tests for logistic regression. Variance inflation factors were calculated to assess multicollinearity between sociodemographic variables; all values were significantly below 5 (range: 1.01–1.20) indicating no significant multicollinearity (O'Brien, 2007). Unstandardized coefficients are reported for linear regressions, and odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) are reported for logistic regression models (Table 2). Analyses were conducted using RStudio version 4.4.1.

Results

Patients were age 58.20 ± 12.10 SD years (range: 21–92 years), 72% female, and 76% Black (22% White, and 2% non-White, non-Black). Most patients (69%) reported attending at least some college, while 31% reported having attended at least some high school or having a high school diploma or GED. Most were not currently employed, with approximately 33% of patients being retired, 24% disabled, and 9% unemployed. Patients reported smoking an average of 10.16 ± 7.89 SD cigarettes per day at enrollment and reported their readiness to quit as 6.67 ± 0.95 SD (i.e., the contemplation stage) on a 1–10 scale with 10 indicating the highest level of readiness. Regarding treatment engagement, 34% of patients attended only one session, and 42% completed treatment (i.e., attended at least four sessions). Patients attended an average of 6.03 ± 9.52 SD sessions with total number attended ranging from 1 to 72 sessions. Additionally, participants reported that they reduced their smoking by an average of 4.24 ± 6.67 SD cigarettes per day and approximately 31% reported total abstinence from smoking at their last session. Treatment completion was examined using a model which included age, sex, race, education, and employment (Table 2). As above, education and employment, entered at Step 2, did not significantly improve model fit but were retained (Δχ2(2) = 0.96, p = .62). Results showed that older age was associated with an increased likelihood of completing treatment (OR = 1.05, p < .01). Additionally, race was a significant predictor such that White patients had 2.01 times odds of completing treatment compared with Black patients (OR = 2.01, p = .01). Patient education and employment status was not associated with likelihood of completing treatment (ps = 0.36 and 0.35, respectively).

Age, sex, race, education, and employment were used to predict whether patients requested NRT during treatment (Table 2). Education and employment at Step 2 did not provide additional predictive value but were retained in the final model consistent with our a priori hypotheses (Δχ2(2) = 1.47, p = .69). Analyses found no significant effects of age, sex, race, employment, or education on the odds of requesting NRT over the course of treatment (ps = 0.46–0.99).

Finally, a model including age, sex, race, education, and employment best predicted whether patients attended via video or audio-only (Δχ2(2) = 15.78, p < .01; Table 2). Analyses showed that White patients had significantly increased odds of attending via video compared to non-White patients (OR = 2.64, p < .01). Additionally, odds of connecting via video increased significantly for patients who had at least some college education, compared to those with a high school education or less (OR = 1.81, p = .01), and for those who were employed, versus unemployed, retired, or disabled (OR = 1.95, p = .02). Patient age and sex were not significantly associated with odds of video use.

Discussion

The present study is the first to assess individual-level predictors of engagement within a group-based, rolling-enrollment, telehealth smoking cessation program. Several sociodemographic factors predicted different aspects of engagement in this sample of primarily Black, middle-aged to older adults.

Results showed that patient age significantly predicted several measures of engagement. For example, older age was modestly associated with a greater number of sessions attended and increased likelihood of treatment completion. Given that smoking rates among older adults have decreased more slowly than those for middle-aged and young adults (Meza et al., 2023), these results are encouraging. In addition to facilitating treatment attendance and completion, it is worth noting that age did not hinder patients from engaging in this telehealth treatment program in adjusted models. These results are encouraging since previous work has linked older age to decreased telehealth engagement (Jagielo et al., 2024; Lotito et al., 2023), possibly due to barriers such as difficulties using technology and hearing or vision problems (Mao et al., 2022). Additionally, in the multivariate model age did not predict attendance via video, which is promising given the link between video engagement and better treatment outcomes (Brett et al., 2025). Increasing engagement in telehealth smoking cessation treatment is vital for preventing the compound negative health effects of smoking in older adults, an area in tobacco prevention that has been historically understudied (Kleykamp and Kulak, 2023). The program's successful facilitation of older patients' engagement in telehealth was likely facilitated by technical support provided by staff prior to sessions and in-the-moment during sessions. On the other hand, the positive relationship between older age and treatment engagement may highlight a need for efforts to improve engagement among middle-aged or younger adults, such as adding sessions outside of regular working hours, to accommodate those who may experience attendance barriers such as work schedules or childcare responsibilities.

Patient race was also a predictor of treatment engagement such that White patients were more likely than Black patients to complete treatment (i.e., attend at least four sessions). Further, in adjusted models, Black patients were significantly less likely to attend the telehealth sessions via video compared to their White counterparts. As mentioned in Section 1, Black individuals, particularly those at the intersection of race and low SES, experience a disproportionate burden of tobacco-related disease and less success when attempting to quit smoking (Leventhal et al., 2022; Jones et al., 2023; Avila et al., 2022; Babb, 2017; U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, 2024; Bacio et al., 2014). Thus, programs may consider implementing strategies, such as providing patients with the option to borrow program-issued WiFi-enabled electronic devices, to increase engagement and improve patient smoking outcomes.

We also found that both education level and employment were significant predictors of treatment engagement. Specifically, patients who completed at least some college were more likely to complete treatment than those with a high school education or less. This finding is in line with previous work showing that less education is associated with lower telehealth engagement (Jagielo et al., 2024; Lotito et al., 2023). Our results also showed that those who were currently employed, at least part time, were more likely to engage. Future research should focus on innovative ways to reach those who may have less education and/or lower SES. Having adjuncts to telehealth or more flexible, low-cost, in-person options may be helpful for reaching these groups.

Patient likelihood to request NRT was not predicted by age, sex, race, education, or employment in adjusted models. This is favorable, as Black patients and those from other historically minoritized groups consistently report underuse of NRT (Leventhal et al., 2022; Carpenter et al., 2011). A major strength of the CTQ-RV program is that patients were provided with no-cost NRT sent to them by mail. Providing NRT at free or reduced costs, with frequent opportunities to consult with treatment providers regarding medication use, may be one avenue by which smoking cessation treatment programs can increase engagement and improve quit rates among those who shoulder a disproportionate burden of tobacco-related harms.

The present study featured several strengths, including evaluation of a gold standard behavioral counseling + NRT telehealth treatment within a fully operational clinical program, a large sample with a broad age range, and assessment of several aspects of treatment engagement. However, the results should be interpreted considering some limitations. First, data were collected as part of routine clinical care, rather than within a controlled study. Given the inherent lack of control in a usual care setting, it is possible that factors outside of those examined (such as guidance from additional healthcare providers or use of other, unreported medications) may have influenced treatment engagement. Second, groups did not have a defined start and end point; rather, data were collected from each participant for as long as they attended the program. As a result, the total number of sessions attended ranged widely between patients, and data were weighted equally for each patient regardless of how many sessions they attended. Third, this group-based telehealth smoking cessation intervention may not appeal to all individuals, and the counselors' sex and race/ethnicity did not necessarily match the characteristics of the patients. Thus, while telehealth overcomes transportation, mobility, and time barriers, and the counselors are trained on cultural competency, the present results may not apply to other treatment modalities and patient groups.

Conclusions

The present study identified sociodemographic factors predicting engagement in an innovative, rolling-enrollment, group-based telehealth smoking cessation program. Results showed that older, White, college-educated, and employed patients had the highest rates of treatment engagement, demonstrating a need for continued efforts to increase engagement among patients from marginalized or socioeconomically disadvantaged groups. Notably, the present study highlights the success of the CTQ-RV program in terms of engaging older adults in telehealth tobacco-cessation treatment. Future work should focus on examining the effectiveness of additional treatment support efforts, such as co-designing material with people with lived experience, offering sessions outside traditional work hours, and increasing targeted outreach and accessibility of cost-free tobacco treatment. Further, to mitigate existing barriers to treatment, programs may increase technical support to help patients engage with telehealth modalities and work to provide resources or devices for individuals who may not have consistent internet and/or a place to privately participate in treatment. Though a given treatment program may be established as effective, evaluating individual-level predictors of engagement may reveal opportunities to address disparities in tobacco treatment engagement, propelling programs to develop novel ways to engage all patients.
