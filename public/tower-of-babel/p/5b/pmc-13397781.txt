Multidrug-resistant Acinetobacter spp. bloodstream infections in hematologic patients: clinical characteristics, genomic features, and eravacycline-based combination therapy
Authors: Wenjing Guo, Jia Li, Nuobing Yang, Sisi Zhen, Tingting Zhang, Qingsong Lin, Yigeng Cao, Wenbin Cao, Aiming Pang, Donglin Yang, Xin Chen, Rongli Zhang, Jialin Wei, Qiaoling Ma, Weihua Zhai, Yi He, Yizhou Zheng, Erlie Jiang, Mingzhe Han, Sizhou Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMultidrug-resistant (MDR) Acinetobacter spp. bloodstream infection (BSI) is concerning in hematologic patients, but integrated clinical and genomic analyses are scarce.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 62 hematologic patients with Acinetobacter spp. BSI over 13 years, including 30 MDR and 32 non-MDR cases. Whole-genome sequence (WGS) was conducted on 23 MDR isolates. Checkerboard assays evaluated the in vitro activity of eravacycline combined with sulbactam, meropenem, cefoperazone–sulbactam, polymyxin B, and levofloxacin.ResultsFemale (66.67% vs. 34.38%, P = 0.022) and adult (≥ 14 years; 80.00% vs. 53.12%, P = 0.049) patients were more common in the MDR group. Prior carbapenem exposure, prolonged neutropenia, and respiratory tract colonization were associated with MDR-BSI. The majority of MDR cases did not receive appropriate empirical therapy within 24 h (40.00% vs. 90.62%, P < 0.001). The overall 30-day mortality was 38.71%, significantly higher in MDR than non-MDR patients (53.33% vs. 25.00%, P = 0.043). Among MDR subgroup, 30-day non-survivors had more profound cytopenias and higher inflammatory markers at BSI onset (P < 0.05). WGS revealed diverse sequence types and capsular loci across the isolates, including 14 non-A.baumannii and 9 A.baumannii strains. Among 20 carbapenemase-positive isolates, blaOXA−23 (n = 9) predominated in A.baumannii, while blaOXA−58 (n = 7) and blaNDM−1 (n = 7) were restricted to non-A.baumannii isolates. Eravacycline demonstrated potent in vitro activity (MIC50/90=0.125/0.5 mg/L) and showed synergy with sulbactam (52.17%), meropenem (43.48%), and cefoperazone–sulbactam (39.13%), while combinations with polymyxin B (86.96%) and levofloxacin (69.57%) were primarily additive.ConclusionsEravacycline exhibited strong in vitro activity and favorable synergy with sulbactam, supporting its potential role in treating MDR Acinetobacter spp. infections in this high-risk population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13653-4.

Introduction

Acinetobacter spp. is a complex genus that includes several clinically relevant species such as A. baumannii, A. calcoaceticus, A. pittii, A. nosocomialis. While A. baumannii accounts for 60–90% of clinical isolates and is the primary focus of research, non-baumannii Acinetobacter also cause a considerable proportion of infections with comparable mortality [1]. In recent years, multidrug-resistant (MDR) Acinetobacter spp. have disseminated globally, with MDR rates exceeding 50% and approaching 85% in some regions [2, 3], largely driven by extensive antibiotic use and horizontal gene transfer [4]. These pathogens commonly employ three major resistance mechanisms, including production of β-lactamases, overexpression of efflux pumps, and target site modifications [5], which collectively contribute to multidrug resistance and therapeutic challenges.

Eravacycline, a novel fluorocycline structurally related to tigecycline, inhibits bacterial protein synthesis by binding to the 30 S ribosomal subunit [6]. It evades common tetracycline resistance mechanisms, including Tet(A)/Tet(B) efflux pumps and ribosomal protection proteins Tet(M). Importantly, eravacycline achieves higher plasma and pulmonary exposure than tigecycline [7] and exhibits 2–8 fold greater potency against Gram-negative bacilli, including carbapenemase-producing strains [6]. Although eravacycline breakpoints for Acinetobacter spp. have not yet been established, a surveillance study of 1274 MDR A. baumannii isolates reported minimal inhibitory concentration (MIC) 50/90 values of 0.5/1 mg/L [8], indicating strong in vitro activity. Nevertheless, eravacycline is a bacteriostatic agent, and its MIC may increase in strains producing certain carbapenemases, overexpressing efflux pumps, or exhibiting tigecycline resistance [9]. Combination therapy is therefore often recommended to enhance efficacy and prevent resistance emergence, as endorsed by the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) [10] and European Society of Clinical Microbiology and Infectious Diseases (ESCMID) [11] for high-risk MDR Acinetobacter spp. infections [12].

MDR Acinetobacter spp. bloodstream infections (BSI) are life-threatening, with reported 30-day mortality ranging from 53.3%-69.4% [2, 3, 13]. This burden is even more pronounced in patients with hematologic diseases, who are predisposed to severe infections due to prolonged neutropenia, extended hospitalization, frequent broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure, and invasive interventions [14, 15]. Despite the clinical importance, data on eravacycline against MDR Acinetobacter spp., particularly bloodstream isolates from hematologic patients, remain limited. Furthermore, existing studies predominantly focus on A. baumannii, with few providing integrated clinical characteristics, microbiological and genomic analyses.

To address these gaps, this study aimed to: (i) characterize the clinical and microbiological features of MDR Acinetobacter spp. BSI in hematologic patients; (ii) elucidate antimicrobial resistance genes and virulence profiles using whole-genome sequencing; and (iii) evaluate the synergistic potential of eravacycline-based combination regimens to inform optimized therapeutic strategies.

Methods

Study population

A total of 62 patients with hematologic diseases developed Acinetobacter spp. BSI in our hospital between January 2013 and October 2025. Demographic, clinical and outcome data were collected from electronic medical records and telephone follow-up. Bloodstream isolates from these patients were identified using the VITEK 2 Compact system (bioMérieux) or matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI TOF MS; Bruker Daltonics), which classified most isolates as A. baumannii or members of the A. calcoaceticus–baumannii complex. Among these isolates, 30 were MDR, and 23 of them were available and preserved at − 80 °C for subsequent whole-genome sequencing and combination susceptibility testing. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Institute of Hematology and Blood Diseases Hospital.

Definitions

Isolates non-sensitive to ≥ 3 antibiotic categories were classified as MDR [16]. BSI onset was defined as the date of the first positive blood culture was collected, and laboratory parameters were obtained within 24 h. Respiratory tract colonization was identified when Acinetobacter spp. were isolated from respiratory specimens, including sputum, nasopharyngeal swabs, or oropharyngeal swabs, without clinical symptoms. Unresolved neutropenia referred to an absolute neutrophil count < 0.5 × 109/L lasting > 14 consecutive days before or after BSI or until death [17]. Polymicrobial BSI was diagnosed when ≥ 2 clinically significant pathogens were isolated from the same blood culture set. Prior antibiotic exposure was defined as receipt of any antimicrobial agent for at least 48 h during the 30 days preceding BSI onset. Routine antimicrobial prophylaxis is not implemented in our hospital. Empirical therapy was considered appropriate when at least one agent with in vitro activity against Acinetobacter spp. was administered at an adequate dose within 24 h of blood culture collection.

Antimicrobial susceptibility and synergy testing

Antibiotic susceptibility testing of all 62 isolates was initially conducted in the hospital microbiology laboratory using the VITEK 2 Compact automated system. Among the 30 MDR isolates, 23 stored strains underwent further minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) determination by broth microdilution for eravacycline, sulbactam (SUL), meropenem (MEM), cefoperazone–sulbactam (2:1, CSL), polymyxin B and levofloxacin (LVX). Eravacycline MICs were additionally assessed using MIC test strip (MTS; Liofilchem). Susceptibility interpretations followed Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines, with Federal Drug Administration (FDA) breakpoints applied for tigecycline (susceptible 2 mg/L, resistant 8 mg/L) [18]. Because no CLSI or European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) clinical breakpoints are currently available for sulbactam, CSL or eravacycline against Acinetobacter spp., MIC values for these agents were reported descriptively without susceptibility categorization.

The checkerboard microdilution panel method was used to assess the efficacy of eravacycline in combination with the five selected antimicrobial agents. Tested concentration ranges were as follows: eravacycline, 0.004-4 mg/L; SUL, 1–64 mg/L; MEM, 1–64 mg/L; polymyxin B, 0.125-8 mg/L; CSL, 2/1-128/64 mg/L; and LVX, 0.25-16 mg/L. The fractional inhibitory concentration index (FICI) was calculated using the formula: (MIC of Drug A in combination/MIC of Drug A alone) + (MIC of Drug B in combination/MIC of Drug B alone). Based on the lowest FICI, interactions were categorized as synergistic (FICI ≤ 0.5), additive (0.5 < FICI ≤ 1), indifferent (1 < FICI ≤ 4), and antagonistic (FICI > 4) [19]. Quality control strains included Escherichia coli ATCC 25,922, Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 27,853, Enterococcus faecalis ATCC 29,212, Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 29,213, and Klebsiella pneumoniae ATCC 700,603 (Table S1).

Sequencing analysis

Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) of the 23 isolates was performed using paired-end 150 bp reads on the Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform. Raw reads were quality-checked and filtered using fastp and draft genomes were assembled using SOAP (version 2.04), SPAdes (version 3.10.0), ABySS (version 1.5.2), and CISA (version 1.3). Sequence types (STs) were determined according to the Pasteur multilocus sequence typing (MLST) scheme, based on the allelic profiles of seven housekeeping genes (recA, gltA, rpoB, cpn60, rplB, pyrG, and fusA). Capsular polysaccharide locus (KL) and lipooligosaccharide outer core locus (OCL) were defined using Kaptive [20]. The antibiotic resistance and virulence factor genes were predicted using Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) searches against the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD) (https://card.mcmaster.ca/) [21] and the Virulence Factor Database (VFDB) (https://www.mgc.ac.cn/VFs/) [22], using thresholds of identity > 84%, coverage > 90% and e-value <1 e-10.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as the median and interquartile range (IQR), and compared using Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test. Survival curves were estimated by the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyzes were performed using R software (version 4.3.2).

Results

Clinical characteristics

Among 62 patients with Acinetobacter spp. BSI, 30 (48.39%) isolates were classified as MDR. The clinical characteristics stratified by MDR status are summarized in Table 1. The prevalence of MDR isolates did not differ between 2013 and 2019 and 2020–2025 (41.38% vs. 54.55%, P = 0.435) (Table S2). Female patients (66.67% vs. 34.38%, P = 0.022) and those aged ≥ 14 years (80.00% vs. 53.12%, P = 0.049) were more common in the MDR group. The median length of hospitalization before BSI was 18.00 [8.50, 36.50] days for the entire cohort, with no significant difference between the two groups (P = 0.289). Regarding underlying hematologic diseases, very severe aplastic anemia (VSAA) tended to predominate in the MDR group (33.33% vs. 9.68%, P = 0.052), while acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) was relatively more common in the non-MDR group (35.48% vs. 13.33%, P = 0.087).

The duration of neutropenia within 30 days before BSI onset was markedly longer in MDR group (12.00 [6.00, 30.00] vs. 3.00 [0.00, 15.00] days, P = 0.015), and respiratory tract colonization with Acinetobacter spp. was markedly more frequent (63.33% vs. 21.88%, P = 0.002). At the onset of BSI, patients in MDR-BSI exhibited more severe cytopenias (HGB: 71.00 vs. 78.00 g/L, P = 0.074; PLT: 19.00 vs. 39.00 × 109/L, P = 0.006; neutropenia: 90.00% vs. 66.67%, P = 0.060; severe neutropenia: 80.00% vs. 56.67%, P = 0.096) and higher inflammatory markers (PCT: 1.10 vs. 0.18 µg/L, P = 0.019; CRP: 88.35 vs. 11.28 mg/L, P = 0.002). Polymicrobial BSI was marginally more frequent in the MDR group (40.00% vs. 16.67%, P = 0.086).

Resistance profile and clinical outcomes

Almost all MDR patients had received antibiotics within 30 days before BSI (100.00% vs. 74.19%, P = 0.005), particularly carbapenems (86.67% vs. 22.58%, P < 0.001), cephalosporins (28.57% vs. 0.00%, P = 0.001), and tigecycline/eravacycline (22.22% vs. 3.23%, P = 0.042) (Table 1, S2). Notably, 80.00% of MDR-BSI occurred while patients were already on antimicrobial therapy (non-MDR: 34.38%, P < 0.001).

As shown in Fig. 1 and Table S3, all MDR bloodstream isolates were resistant to carbapenems and exhibited high resistance rates (> 60%) to nearly all tested antibiotics, with the exceptions of tetracycline (39.29%), tigecycline (17.86%), and polymyxins (colistin, 4.17%). Consequently, most MDR cases failed to receive appropriate empirical therapy within 24 h (40.00% vs. 90.62%, P < 0.001) and definitive treatment more often required combination regimens (81.48% vs. 33.33%, P < 0.001). The 30-day mortality rate was also markedly higher among MDR patients (53.33% vs. 25.00%, P = 0.043) (Table 1; Fig. 2A). This trend remained consistent in both pediatric (< 14 years) and adult (≥ 14 years) subgroups, although statistical significance was not reached, likely owing to the limited sample size (Fig. 2B-C).

Clinical characteristics associated with 30-day mortality in MDR subgroup

The comparison of survivors and non-survivors in MDR subgroup is presented in Table 2. Within the MDR subgroup, 30-day non-survivors exhibited even more severe conditions at BSI onset, characterized by profound cytopenias (HGB: 63.00 vs. 72.50 g/L, P = 0.050; PLT: 8.50 vs. 29.50 × 109/L, P = 0.006; neutropenia: 100.00% vs. 78.57%, P = 0.090) and a markedly heightened inflammatory response (PCT: 2.45 vs. 0.18 µg/L, P = 0.016; CRP: 191.17 vs. 24.60 mg/L, P < 0.001) (Fig. 3). The duration of neutropenia before BSI also tended to be longer in non-survivors (15.50 vs. 8.00 days, P = 0.089).

Inappropriate empirical therapy occurred more frequently among non-survivors (75.00% vs. 42.86%, P = 0.156), though this did not reach statistical significance. Notably, resistance to tetracycline was significantly higher in non-survivors (60.00% vs. 15.38%, P = 0.043), while resistance rates to other antibiotics, including fluoroquinolones (81.25% vs. 53.85%, P = 0.226), aminoglycosides (87.50% vs. 53.85%, P = 0.110), ampicillin-sulbactam (90.00% vs. 77.78%, P = 0.721), and tigecycline (26.67% vs. 7.69%, P = 0.655), showed similar trends without reaching statistical significance. These findings indicate that Acinetobacter spp. isolates from non-survivors may harbor broader or more complex resistance mechanisms, which could limit effective antibiotic options and adversely affect clinical outcomes.

Genomic features of MDR strains

Whole-genome sequencing was performed on 23 MDR isolates, comprising 13 A. pittii, 9 A. baumannii, and 1 A. nosocomialis. Among A. baumannii, ST2 was predominant (6/9, 66.67%), while the KL were highly diverse. In contrast, Acinetobacter non-baumannii (ANB) species exhibited substantial heterogeneity in both STs and KLs. Most A. baumannii isolates belonged to OCL1 (7/9, 77.78%), whereas OCL6 predominated among ANB isolates (13/14, 92.86%) (Fig. 4, Table S3-S4).

All isolates carried the class C β-lactamase gene blaADC−2. Acquired carbapenemase genes were detected in 20 isolates (86.96%). blaOXA−23 was the most prevalent (9/23, 39.13%), with 4 isolates also carrying the class A β-lactamase gene blaTEM−1. Among the blaOXA−23–positive isolates, eight were A. baumannii and one was A. pittii. In contrast, blaOXA−58 and the metallo-β-lactamase gene blaNDM−1 were detected exclusively in A. pittii and A. nosocomialis. Seven isolates (30.43%) carried the blaNDM−1, including 3 that co-carried blaOXA−58. Another 4 isolates harbored blaOXA−58 alone. blaKPC, blaVIM, blaIMPand mcr genes were not detected in any of the isolates (Table S4).

Efflux pump genes were also widespread, with all isolates carrying the multidrug and toxic compound extrusion (MATE)–family gene abeM and the bacterial integral membrane protein (BIMP)–family gene abeS, as well as genes of the AdeFGH/IJK systems. The AdeABC resistance–nodulation–division (RND) efflux system (adeA, adeC) and its two-component regulator adeRS were present in all A. baumannii and A. nosocomialis isolates but absent from A. pittii. The tetracycline efflux gene tet(B) was detected in five A. baumannii isolates (Table S4).

Additionally, virulence-associated genes were broadly distributed, including biofilm relation (pgaABC, csuABCDE, bfmRS), iron uptake (barAB, basABCDFGH, entE, bauABCDE), lipooligosaccharide biosynthesis (lpsB, lpxA, lpxL, lpxM), quorum-sensing (abaI, abaR), ompA, and pbpG (Fig. 4).

In vitro activity of eravacycline in combination with other antimicrobials

The MIC of eravacycline was determined by both MTS and broth microdilution, yielding consistent high potency with MIC50/90 values of 0.125/0.75 mg/L (range, 0.032-1 mg/L) by MTS and 0.125/0.5 mg/L (range, 0.064-0.5 mg/L) by broth microdilution. In contrast, broth microdilution MICs for the other agents showed limited single-agent activity: SUL and MEM exhibited MIC50/90 values of > 64/>64 mg/L, LVX 8/16 mg/L, and CSL 128 − 64/>128 − 64 mg/L, whereas polymyxin B was the only drug with notable activity (MIC50/90, 1/1 mg/L) (Table 3, S3).

Synergy was most frequently observed with eravacycline plus SUL (12/23, 52.17%) followed by eravacycline-MEM (10/23, 43.48%) and eravacycline-CSL (9/23, 39.13%). Synergy with eravacycline–polymyxin B was rare (1/23, 4.35%), although additive effects predominated in this combination (20/23, 86.96%), as well as in the eravacycline–LVX group (16/23, 69.57%). Indifference was observed in only 0–3 isolates for any combination and no antagonism was detected (Table 3; Fig. 5).

Consistent with the synergy and additivity patterns, combination with eravacycline markedly reduced the MICs of partner drugs. For eravacycline-SUL, the SUL MIC50/90 decreased from > 64/>64 to 8/32 mg/L, accompanied by a reduction in eravacycline MICs to 0.032/0.125 mg/L. Similar reductions were observed in the other 4 combinations.

Discussion

In this study, we retrospectively analyzed one of the largest cohorts of Acinetobacter spp. BSI among hematologic patients to date. By integrating clinical characteristics, antimicrobial resistance profiles, WGS, and checkerboard test of eravacycline with five additional antibiotics, we established a multidimensional framework for understanding MDR-BSI in this high-risk population. This comprehensive approach enabled us to elucidate the clinical and microbiological features of MDR-BSI, and to explore potential therapeutic combinations that may provide new perspectives for managing MDR Acinetobacter infections in this highly vulnerable population.

Over the 13-year study period, 62 hematologic patients developed Acinetobacter spp. BSI, among whom 48.39% were caused by MDR strains. The 30-day mortality of MDR-BSI was strikingly higher than that of that of non-MDR BSI (53.33% vs. 25.00%, P = 0.043). Previous studies from China have reported higher proportions of MDR Acinetobacter spp. BSI (81.07%, 274/338), and likewise showed substantially higher 28-day mortality in MDR compared with non-MDR cases (58.8% vs. 12.5%, P < 0.001) [2, 3]. Consistent with prior reports, well-established risk factors for MDR, including respiratory tract colonization and prolonged neutropenia [14, 15], were also associated with the development of MDR Acinetobacter spp. BSI in our cohort. MDR-BSI patients showed significantly higher rates of prior carbapenems exposure and concurrent broad-spectrum antibiotic using, further supporting the selective pressure model that extensive antimicrobial exposure drives both MDR acquisition [2, 3, 23] and breakthrough BSI during ongoing therapy. Additionally, while sex and age did not significantly affect the prognosis of MDR-BSI, we observed a higher proportion of MDR isolates among female and adult (≥ 14 years) patients. The increased MDR rate in adults may reflect greater cumulative antibiotic exposure over time [24]. However, evidence regarding sex-related susceptibility to MDR Acinetobacter remains inconsistent across studies [24–26], indicating these differences may vary by populations and clinical settings.

Compared with non-MDR BSI, patients with MDR-BSI had more profound cytopenias and markedly elevated inflammatory markers at onset, a pattern that became even more striking among 30-day non-survivors within the MDR subgroup. This intensified systemic inflammatory response is plausibly explained by delays in effective empirical therapy due to the limited therapeutic options for MDR strains. In fact, consistent with previous reports [27, 28], only 12 (40.0%) of 30 MDR-BSI patients received appropriate empirical therapy within the first 24 h, and merely 4 among 30-day non-survivors. In addition, MDR strain-level features such as virulence and serum resistance may also contribute to the observed severity [13], though these mechanisms warrant further investigation.

WGS of the 23 MDR isolates revealed clear genomic distinctions between A. baumannii and ANB species. A. baumannii isolates were dominated by ST2, a globally prevalent epidemic clone, whereas ANB isolates exhibited a highly heterogeneous clonal background [29]. Correspondingly, carbapenem resistance in A. baumannii was mainly driven by blaOXA−23, whereas blaOXA−58 and blaNDM−1 were exclusively identified in ANB isolates, consistent with previous reports [30]. Globally, blaNDM−1 and blaOXA−58 remain uncommon in carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (CRAB) [31, 32], except in South Asia, where prevalence rates of 25.78–53.45% have been reported [33, 34]. These findings indicate different resistance dissemination strategies between the two groups. In A. baumannii, blaOXA−23 is commonly chromosomally encoded and disseminated primarily through clonal expansion of prevalent lineages. In contrast, blaNDM−1 and blaOXA−58 are typically plasmid-borne, facilitating horizontal gene transfer across genetically diverse ANB species [35, 36]. Moreover, we observed a higher rate (50.00%) of blaNDM−1 among ANB isolates than previously reported (21.43%) [37], suggesting the role of ANB species as important reservoirs for the ongoing dissemination of metallo-β-lactamase genes. In addition to carbapenemase genes, efflux pump genes were widespread across both groups but differed in composition, and together contributed to the MDR phenotypes observed in Acinetobacter spp.

The MDR Acinetobacter spp. isolates in this study were resistant to nearly all tested antibiotics, with relatively lower resistance rates for tetracycline, tigecycline and polymyxin. Previous studies have shown that tigecycline combined with CSL can help attenuate inflammatory response and improve outcomes in MDR A. baumannii pneumonia [38]. However, the clinical utility of tigecycline for BSI is limited by its low plasma concentrations, whereas eravacycline achieves higher plasma and pulmonary exposure [7]. In our study, eravacycline exhibited the most potent in vitro activity, with uniformly low MICs (0.032-1 mg/L) across 23 MDR isolates by both MTS and broth microdilution, superior to tigecycline (MIC50/90 ≤0.5/2 mg/L; range, ≤ 0.5-4 mg/L). Consistent with our findings, a multicenter real-world study including 796 hematologic patients receiving eravacycline therapy reported that Acinetobacter spp. was the predominant pathogen (30.5%), with a susceptibility rate of 95.8% at the China Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (ChinaCAST) [39] breakpoint of 1 mg/L and an overall microbiological response rate of 90.7% [40]. Additionally, polymyxin B showed relatively low MICs (MIC50/90 = 1 mg/L; range, 0.5->8 mg/L), which may be associated with the significant fitness cost required for Acinetobacter spp. to acquire polymyxin resistance, limiting its emergence in clinical settings [41].

Synergy was most frequently observed in eravacycline–β-lactam combinations (SUL, MEM, and CSL), while eravacycline-polymyxin B regimens exhibited mainly additive rather than synergistic effects. MEM and cefoperazone are classical β-lactams, while SUL, a class A β-lactamase inhibitor, also exhibits whole-cell activity against Acinetobacter spp [42]. by targeting penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs) [43], and enhancing bacterial susceptibility to phagocytosis and polymorphonuclear leukocyte killing [44]. The mechanistic rationale for the observed β-lactam synergy may be explained by complementary actions in which β-lactams disrupt cell wall integrity, facilitating increased intracellular accumulation of eravacycline, while eravacycline suppresses protein synthesis.

However, the synergistic effect of CSL was less pronounced than that of SUL alone. Although a study reported that CSL combined with tigecycline exhibited better bactericidal activity than equivalent-dose SUL–tigecycline combinations in two of three XDR A. baumannii isolates [45], this inconsistency may reflect differences in β-lactamase profiles among strains. All isolates in our cohort carried blaADC−2, encoding a class C β-lactamase capable of hydrolyzing first- to third-generation cephalosporins but not carbapenems [4]. Since SUL only partially inhibits ADC enzymes, the addition of cefoperazone did not confer a significant advantage.

Despite the notable synergy and MIC reduction achieved by eravacycline–β-lactam combinations, the post-combination MICs of β-lactams remained relatively high (SUL: MIC50/90 = 8/32 mg/L; MEM: MIC50/90 = 16/32 mg/L), indicating challenges in achieving bactericidal concentrations with standard dosing. Given that SUL is a time-dependent agent, previous pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) studies have shown that A. baumannii isolates with SUL MIC of ≥ 8 mg/L may still reach target exposures with dose-intensified and prolonged-infusion regimens [46]. In contrast, combinations of eravacycline with polymyxin B or LVX showed mainly additive rather than synergistic effects, yet these regimens effectively lowered MICs into more readily attainable ranges (polymyxin B: MIC50/90 = 0.25/0.5 mg/L; LVX: MIC50/90 = 1/8 mg/L). Clinically, in a cohort of 179 patients with CRAB pneumonia [47], high-dose SUL-based therapy demonstrated significantly lower mortality (20.0% vs. 60.7%, P < 0.001) and reduced nephrotoxicity compared with polymyxin-based regimens. Similarly, a network meta-analysis [48] involving 18 studies and 1835 patients with MDR/XDR A. baumannii infections reported comparable mortality but higher clinical cure rates and less kidney injury with SUL. Collectively, these data support eravacycline–SUL as a potentially favorable regimen balancing efficacy and safety, though rigorous PK/PD investigations and prospective clinical trials are urgently needed to define optimal dosing and confirm clinical benefits against MDR-BSI.

This study has several limitations. First, although this is one of the largest single-center cohorts of Acinetobacter spp. BSI in hematologic patients, the sample size remains limited and the retrospective design may introduce inherent bias. Second, the study was conducted over an extended period, which may introduce potential temporal heterogeneity. While no major discrepancies were observed between the two periods (2013–2019 vs. 2020–2025), evolving clinical practices and management strategies may have influenced the results. Third, WGS was performed only on a subset of MDR isolates, and the exclusion of non-MDR strains limits comprehensive comparative genomic analyses across the full resistance spectrum and the assessment of links between specific genomic features and clinical outcomes. Finally, our findings on antimicrobial synergy are based solely on in vitro experiments. These results should therefore be interpreted with caution and require future clinical validation before translation into clinical practice.

In conclusion, hematologic patients with MDR Acinetobacter spp. BSI have limited therapeutic options and experience higher levels of inflammatory markers and increased 30-day mortality. Eravacycline demonstrated strong in vitro activity across all MDR isolates and exhibited potent synergistic effects when combined with SUL, while combinations with polymyxin B were primarily additive. These findings provide a rationale for further clinical evaluation of eravacycline-based regimens for MDR Acinetobacter spp. infections. Future prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings and establish optimized treatment strategies in hematologic populations.
