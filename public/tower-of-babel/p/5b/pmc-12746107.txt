The CCR4–NOT complex: a multifaceted sensor of molecular signals instructing eukaryotic mRNA translation and stability
Authors: Guillaume Caulier, Joseph Siblini, Lina Sène, Fabienne Mauxion, Bertrand Séraphin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe CCR4–NOT complex is a multi-subunit assembly found in all eukaryotic cells. Yet, its composition varies across organisms, with a universally conserved core enriched by lineage-specific subunits. Further, heterogeneity results from the occurrence of paralogous proteins, substoichiometric subunits, transient partners, and protein isoforms. Altogether, multiple CCR4–NOT complexes exist, and some even coexist within a single cell. The CCR4–NOT complex is an essential actor of gene expression through its roles in messenger RNA (mRNA) deadenylation, decay, and translation. Over time, support for the originally proposed role of the CCR4–NOT complex in transcription has been waning. Consistent with a role in post-transcriptional regulation, ribosomes appear to be major partners of the CCR4–NOT complex to coordinate translation and mRNA decay. Further, the CCR4–NOT complex is at the center of a network involving RNA-binding proteins and ubiquitin ligases, as well as factors of currently unknown function. Structural and functional analyses indicate that the CCR4–NOT complex integrates different levels of information present in mRNAs to control their stability and translation, thereby contributing to diverse functions including intricate processes such as human brain or pancreas development. It is thus not surprising that genetic alteration of this essential cellular machine, or impairment of its activity by pathogens, contributes to human diseases.

Introduction

In eukaryotes, the multistep gene expression pathway, leading from nuclear-encoded genes to protein production, minimally comprises transcription, pre-messenger RNA (mRNA) processing, mRNA export from the nucleus, translation and mRNA decay. Regulatory mechanisms and quality control processes impacting each of these steps enable cells to adapt to their environment and, in multicellular organisms, contribute to development. Deficiencies in these mechanisms can lead to severe diseases, including neurodevelopmental disorders and pancreatic agenesis.

In recent years, analyses of mRNA decay have provided increasing evidence for the central contribution of this process to gene expression and regulation [1]. In the cytoplasm, most eukaryotic mRNAs are protected from spurious exonucleolytic degradation by a 5′ m7G cap and a 3′ poly(A) tail covered by cytoplasmic poly(A)-binding proteins (PABPC). Generally, mRNA decay is initiated by the gradual removal of the poly(A) tail by deadenylases until 12–25 A residues remain [2–6]. This initial step is followed by cap cleavage mediated by the decapping enzyme that, at least in yeast, has been proposed to be recruited through an interaction with Pat1 and Lsm1-7 complex [7]. The removal of the two protective marks allows the main 5′-3′ exonuclease to quickly destroy the mRNA body, at least for a fraction of the mRNAs, co-translationally. In addition to this main pathway, a minor alternative mRNA turnover process implicates the exosome to digest the deadenylated mRNA. In parallel, several quality control pathways actively degrade a small subset of mRNAs bearing distinctive defaults such as a premature termination codon, absence of a termination codon, or damage preventing efficient translation elongation [8].

Because deadenylation initiates mRNA decay, identifying enzymes catalyzing this process has been of prime interest. Two complexes mediating cytoplasmic mRNA deadenylation have been described in most eukaryotes: the PAN2–PAN3 complex and the CCR4–NOT complex [9]. The former has been suggested to catalyze a first phase of poly(A) shortening [10] and acts in a PABPC-dependent manner [11]. Because its deletion does not result in strong growth phenotypes in unicellular model organisms [11, 12], its contribution to overall deadenylation appears limited. Data from studies in mice [13] also indicate that both subunits are dispensable for viability in mammals (even if partial lethality is observed for deletion of Pan3). Absence of the PAN2/PAN3 complex in some phylogenetic branches of the eukaryotic tree further questions its importance [14]. In contrast, since its first description over three decades ago, the CCR4–NOT complex has emerged as an important component of the eukaryotic gene expression pathway, with inactivation of most subunits resulting in severe growth phenotypes (reviewed in [15]). Although initially linked to RNA polymerase II transcription regulation, the CCR4–NOT complex is now primarily characterized as the main deadenylase initiating mRNA decay by shortening poly(A) tails [9]. More recently, it has also been found to contribute to translational control processes [16–20]. Thus, the CCR4–NOT complex has emerged as a central actor in the maintenance of proteostasis, from control of mRNA levels available for protein production to regulation of the translation mechanisms themselves. Here, we review several aspects of the CCR4–NOT complex with an evolutionary perspective, covering from its composition to its impact on diseases, to illustrate the fascinating multiple facets of this primordial eukaryotic protein complex.

Is there “one” CCR4–NOT complex? Composition, heterogeneity, and inter-species variations

The CCR4–NOT complex was originally identified in the yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae [21–24] and progressively shown to be composed of nine subunits: Not1, Not2, Not3, Not4, Not5, Caf1, Ccr4, Caf40, and Caf130 in this organism (Table 1). The presence of sequence orthologs of various subunits in other species indicated their evolutionary conservation [25–27], this assembly soon emerging as a distinctive feature of the eukaryotic phylum. The existence and precise composition of the complex were later corroborated in a few model species through biochemical purification and direct protein identification. With increasing available information, intra- and inter-species variability in the composition of this entity became apparent and it is now important to recognize that the term “the CCR4–NOT complex” refers to an ensemble of heterogeneous protein assemblies. The use of different nomenclatures for subunit names in different organisms and the existence of species-specific paralogs increase the difficulties for non-aficionados to apprehend the subtlety of this intricate machine. Here, we will use generic names primarily based on the human system as a paradigm to provide a general framework for the description of the CCR4–NOT complex. Differences and variations observed in other species will then be highlighted.

Multiple human CCR4–NOT complexes

Characterization of human CCR4–NOT complexes was initiated through the affinity purification of several tagged orthologs of yeast CCR4–NOT subunits, followed by mass spectrometry analyses [28]. This pioneering study was followed by similar analyses [29–33] and collectively they identified 11 recurrently co-purifying proteins: CNOT1, CNOT2, CNOT3, CNOT6, CNOT6L, CNOT7, CNOT8, CNOT9, CNOT10, CNOT11, and TNKS1BP1 (Table 1). These proteins appear to be phylogenetically conserved in chordates, suggesting that the human complex is a suitable model for this lineage. Surprisingly, human CNOT4 was not found to co-purify with human CCR4–NOT complexes even though its homolog, Not4, is constitutively present in yeast entities. CNOT4 was shown to transiently interact with the human complex [28] revealing that a mandatory subunit in a given species may only be a transient partner in another one.

CNOT7 and CNOT8 are paralogous proteins often ambiguously referred to as CAF1. Interestingly, either CNOT7 or CNOT8, but not both, appeared to be present in a given complex [28], indicating mutually exclusive association [34]. The same situation applies to CNOT6 and CNOT6L [28], collectively denominated CCR4 (Table 1). Consistently, a single ortholog of these two proteins is present in many species. Mass spectrometry analysis of human CNOT1 [29] and RNA interference followed by western blotting [35] revealed that CNOT1 exists as two isoforms of unknown origin (alternative transcription or translation initiation, or alternative splicing). The longer isoform contains at least 235 additional N-terminal amino acids compared to the shorter one. Since CNOT10 and CNOT11 dock on this extension [29, 36–38], this indicates that CCR4–NOT complexes with or without the CNOT10/CNOT11 module coexist in a given cell. Altogether, the confirmed existence of two CNOT1 isoforms and two paralogs each for CAF1 and CCR4 indicates that minimally eight different human CCR4–NOT complexes may (co-)exist.

Additional variability in complex composition may occur as a result of alternative splicing or the existence of uncharacterized variants. While different complexes may coexist in a given cell, some variants may be tissue- or development-specific owing to the regulated expression of distinct protein isoforms. Finally, although each subunit is maximally present at one copy per complex, the exact stoichiometry of the different subunits in complexes assembled in vivo was never carefully analyzed and some assemblies could lack some peripheral subunits, as suggested by a recent study [39]. Thus, additional complex heterogeneity may result from unequal subunit levels. Further, their activity could be modulated by post-translational modifications. Whether the different CCR4–NOT assemblies fulfill different roles is currently unknown, but this parameter should certainly be considered in future studies.

TNKS1BP1 is a peculiar protein, originally not considered as a subunit of the CCR4–NOT complex even though it was repeatedly and abundantly found by mass spectrometry analyses of the human assembly [28–33]. Moreover, the different subunits of the CCR4–NOT complex were recovered in TNKS1BP1 immunoprecipitation [30]. These reciprocal observations indicate that it represents a bona fide subunit of the human CCR4–NOT complex, and we propose renaming it CNOT12. Little is known about this protein and, in particular, its mode of interaction with the other subunits remains to be elucidated.

Phylogenetic variability

The composition of the CCR4–NOT complex at the protein level has been characterized in different model organisms, namely Drosophila melanogaster [40], Saccharomyces cerevisiae [24], Schizosaccharomyces pombe [41], and Trypanosoma brucei [42]. These studies revealed that five subunits are invariably found: CNOT1, CNOT2, CNOT3, CAF1, and CNOT9 (the generic nomenclature is used here for subunit description, species-specific terminology is listed in Table 1).

Certain subunits are present in the complex in some species but conspicuously absent in others. This applies, for example, to CNOT4, which is a constitutive subunit in the two yeast complexes but is absent from their human, drosophila, and trypanosome counterparts. Although CNOT4 homologs exist in human and drosophila and interact transiently with the complex, no clear homolog of this protein is encoded by the trypanosomal genome [42]. Thus, across the evolutionary spectrum, a given subunit may be a permanent member of the complex, a transient partner, or constitutively absent. The human CNOT10 and CNOT11 proteins have clear homologs in complexes from all species except yeasts [29], whereas CCR4 is absent only from the complex purified from trypanosomes. In the latter case, no ortholog is encoded in the trypanosoma genome, indicating that its absence is not the result of purification artifacts [42]. Proteins with no clear counterpart in the human complexes have been identified in other species. This includes Caf130 from S. cerevisiae that, until now, has no reported homologs in the other organisms. A similar situation is observed in humans: as indicated above, TNKSIBP1/CNOT12 appears to be a subunit of the human complex, and likely of all chordate complexes, as TNKS1BP1 homologs are found in this lineage. However, obvious homologs are absent from the genomes of the other species analyzed. In S. pombe, the RNA-binding YTH domain protein Mmi1 co-purifies with the complex [43, 44]. While distant YTH domain relatives of Mmi1 are found in different organisms, some of them have been shown to only associate transiently with the corresponding CCR4–NOT complexes [45].

Besides the examples observed in human, paralogous subunits have also been identified in the S. cerevisiae complex. In this case, Not3 or Not5 are interchangeably present [15, 46, 47] with their expression potentially varying under different conditions. Further, as in humans, two Not1 isoforms appear to coexist in S. cerevisiae [48], suggesting that this might be a common feature of CNOT1 in many eukaryotes. Considering protein isoforms and paralogs suggests that minimally four different CCR4–NOT complexes could be formed in S. cerevisiae. Strikingly, two genes encoding divergent Not1 isoforms have been identified in Plasmodium. The two isoforms are implicated in different stages of the parasite life cycle [49] and, given their sequences, they are likely to contact different subsets of partners. Thus, the presence of heterogeneous complexes is not limited to human and may be evolutionarily prevalent.

While orthologous sequences to many subunits of the CCR4–NOT complex have been identified in plant genomes [50], the complex has not been biochemically characterized in detail. Thus, its precise composition remains fuzzy and would warrant dedicated studies.

In conclusion, even though a limited number of species have been analyzed, the CCR4–NOT complex appears highly polymorphic within and between species boundaries. Overall, it is present in all eukaryotes and consists of a conserved core with peripheral subunits that are species- or lineage-specific. In several instances, duplications of genes encoding some subunits have occurred during evolution, leading to the presence of redundant alternative paralogous subunits in some organisms. The resulting compositional heterogeneity may have benefited adaptation to specific conditions and/or developmental regulations.

Organization and structure of the CCR4–NOT complex

The organization of the CCR4–NOT complex has been determined through biochemical, interactomic, and structural approaches. It is centered around the CNOT1 protein that provides a linear scaffold for the different modules of this assembly [36] (Fig. 1). In humans, the extreme N-terminus of CNOT1 associates with CNOT10 and CNOT11 to form a module, the presence of which is optional as it is only assembled on the long CNOT1 isoform (Fig. 1A). A similar situation probably occurs for the presence of Caf130 in the yeast complex, again due to Not1 N-terminal heterogeneity [48]. Of note, while the Not1 protein is essential in yeast, its N-terminal region is dispensable arguing that the critical function(s) of the CCR4–NOT complex are accomplished in this species by C-terminally located modules [48]. Along the N- to C-CNOT1 axis, the next module contains an internal region of CNOT1 bound to CAF1 that itself binds CCR4 (Fig. 1). This module, often referred to as the deadenylase module, harbors the two nuclease active sites required to catalyze poly(A) tail shortening. CCR4 is the only subunit of the complex that does not contact CNOT1 [48, 51, 52]. This structural peculiarity may contribute to explain why CCR4 may have been gained or lost during evolution as exemplified by the difference observed between trypanosomes and the other eukaryotic species. Further along the CNOT1 N-C axis, CNOT9 binds directly to CNOT1 and forms an independent module. Finally, CNOT2 and CNOT3 bind near the C-terminus of CNOT1 to form the NOT module (Fig. 1). The various modules are connected by structured and/or disordered regions of CNOT1 that act as linkers.

The overall three-dimensional structure of the CCR4–NOT complex has been determined through negative-stain electron microscopy and cryogenic-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) analyses from the two yeast species [43, 55]. Basically, the structure appears centered around Not1 and adopts a flat L shape with the deadenylase module positioned roughly at the elbow. The shorter arm represents the Not1 N-terminus and bound factors, while the longer branch would encompass the Not1 C-terminus and associated modules. However, only limited resolutions were achieved, possibly due to intrinsic flexibility within the complex. To circumvent this issue, crosslinking strategies and electron paramagnetic resonance spectroscopy have been applied to get insights into the distance between the different modules of the complex [52, 56]. In parallel, structural data for individual modules, submodules or protein domains, originating from different species, have been obtained. Together, they cover most regions of the CCR4–NOT complex (Fig. 1B). As these have been reviewed elsewhere [41, 57–61], they are only summarized here. Briefly, HEAT repeats, characterized by successive sets of two antiparallel α helices, are present all along CNOT1 [37, 47, 48, 52, 62–68] except in the region binding CNOT9 that adopts a specific structure originally named DUF3819 domain (Domain of Unknown Function 3819) and later renamed CN9BD for CNOT9 Binding Domain [64, 65] (Fig. 1). Some CNOT1 HEAT repeats belong to a subset of the large family of HEAT repeats that are named MIF4G domains. Those, originally identified in the middle region of translation initiation factor 4G (hence the name MIF4G), usually contain five to seven helix-turn-helix repeats. The CN9BD domain being composed of α-helical bundle, CNOT1 is almost exclusively made of α helices with only rare exceptions (Fig. 1B).

Other CCR4–NOT subunits adopt specific folds with, in many cases, structural similarities to other proteins. CNOT10 contains 13 tetratricopeptide repeats that wrap around the middle region of CNOT11 and contact CNOT1 [37, 38]. The N-terminal region of CNOT11 adopts a unique fold and contacts CNOT1. The C-terminal domain of CNOT11 is also a MIF4G domain (Fig. 1) that acts as a flexible connected antenna to recruit various partners [37]. In the deadenylase module, CAF1 is a typical member of the DDED 3′-5′ exonuclease family [51, 69], while the catalytic domain of CCR4 belongs to a large family of phosphoesterases including phosphatases and nucleases named EEP [51, 70, 71]. CCR4 connects to CAF1 through leucine-rich repeats [48, 51, 52]. CNOT9 is formed of armadillo repeats [72], while CNOT2 and CNOT3 C-terminal domains adopt Sm-like folds that dimerize and interact, together with part of the preceding nonglobular regions, with the C-terminal CNOT1 HEAT repeat [47, 67, 68]. Finally, the N-terminal domain of CNOT3 adopts a specific structure (Fig. 1B) that has been shown to bind ribosomes [17, 18, 73]. How TNKS1BP1/CNOT12 folds and binds to the rest of the complex remains a mystery.

While not being a member of the metazoan CCR4–NOT complex, we mention the organization of CNOT4 here as it is a stable subunit of the complex in yeast species. CNOT4 contains a C4C4 RING domain that possesses E3 ubiquitin ligase activity [74], an RNA recognition motif, and a C3H1 zinc finger motif, the latter two involved in binding RNA [26]. The remaining less-conserved C-terminal region of CNOT4 harbors motifs involved in interactions with the CCR4–NOT complex, but the modality of which differs between species. An alpha helix and adjacent residues are involved in animals and yeast, but those are divergent and they interact with CNOT9 in the former group of species and with Not1 in the latter case [75, 76]. Thus, during evolution, rewiring occurred, allowing CNOT4 to be permanently or temporarily incorporated into the CCR4–NOT complex in different organisms.

The CCR4–NOT complex as an inviting landing pad for auxiliary partners

To perform its functions, the CCR4–NOT complex interacts transiently with numerous auxiliary partners. Among the many protein partners, some have been well established, including at the structural level providing further insights into the functions of the CCR4–NOT complex. The detailed functions of some partners will be discussed in a following paragraph; here, we will focus on a general description on the biological activities of these factors. Indeed, different studies have revealed that the CCR4–NOT complex interactome is enriched in factors with specific functions and that those bind to different interaction hubs (Fig. 2).

Among the well-characterized CCR4–NOT partners in humans and drosophila are found many RNA-binding proteins including Nanos [68, 77], Smaug [78], members of the Pumilio family [79], Unkempt [80], Bicaudal-C [81], DND1 [82], Roquin [83, 84], and ZFP36, also named tristetraprolin [62, 85]. (We use hereafter ZFP36 to collectively designate this protein as well as related members of this family.) Proteins containing various types of RNA-binding domains share the ability to interact with the CCR4–NOT complex and to repress gene expression through RNA destabilization and/or translation control.

Interestingly, proteins lacking RNA-binding activity but competitively antagonizing the recruitment of the CCR4–NOT complex by specific RNA-binding proteins have been recently identified. These proteins stabilize mRNAs by interfering with their decay. One such factor is RESIST (Regulated Stimulator of Interferon via Stabilization of Transcript, previously annotated as Annexin 2 receptor), which was identified as a positive regulator that contributes to the tight control of interferon β production by stabilizing its mRNA [86]. RESIST proteins interact with several regions of the CCR4–NOT complex, including a binding site that overlaps with that of ZFP36 family members, thereby preventing these RNA-binding proteins from promoting mRNA decay.

RNA helicases DDX6 (named Dhh1 in yeast) [64, 65] and HELZ [87], although not considered as RNA-binding proteins, also belong to the CCR4–NOT interactome and lead to RNA destabilization and/or translation repression. YTH-domain-containing factors, many of which interact with modified N6-methyladenosine (m6A) residues, provide another means of recruitment of the CCR4–NOT complex to repress target mRNAs [45]. Mmi1 from S. pombe, a constitutive member of the complex in this species, is an extreme representative of this group even though it has lost the ability to recognize m6A [44]. Other factors binding to the CCR4–NOT complex indirectly bridge it to mRNAs. These include the TNRC6 proteins (also named GW182) involved in the microRNAs (miRNAs)-mediated silencing pathway [64, 65] and the BTG/Tob factors that associate with poly(A)-binding proteins [88–92]. Bag-of-marble (Bam), a protein found in a subset of insects, is a clear partner of the CCR4–NOT complex that may also fit in this category [84]. Indeed, recent evidence suggest that it links the Tut and Bgnc RNA-binding proteins to the CCR4–NOT complex [93]. It is noteworthy that several of the CCR4–NOT partners possess ubiquitin ligase activity, including RNF219 [31, 32, 94], CNOT4 [75], and Roquin [83, 84]. The latter two are particularly interesting as they host both RNA-binding and ubiquitin ligase activities. Additional factors binding to CCR4–NOT include mouse MARTRE proteins that have been reported to inhibit the CCR4–NOT activity [95]. Finally, among the recently identified partners of the CNOT10/CNOT11 module [20, 37], SCAPER appears to contribute to the regulation of the levels of mRNAs encoding tubulins as a function of the availability of free tubulin heterodimers [20], while GGNBP2 has been linked to the immune response to double-stranded RNAs [96]. If many partners of the CCR4–NOT complex are conserved in eukaryotes, some are found in only a subset of species. Those include the yeast Cal4 protein that interacts with the yeast-specific Caf130 subunit [19].

Finally, a major partner of the CCR4–NOT complex is the ribosome (Fig. 2), which also serves as an indirect link with mRNAs. As detailed below and in a recent review [8], the CCR4–NOT complex interacts directly or through partners with different regions of the ribosome, including internal transfer RNA (tRNA)-binding sites and specific surface regions through CNOT4 or partners such as SCAPER.

Overall, it is striking that partners of the CCR4–NOT complex are numerous and diverse, and that this population may differ from one species to the next. Yet, they collectively form a consistent network and the well-established partners of the CCR4–NOT complex connect this assembly to a limited set of cellular functions, including RNA decay and translation control. Interestingly, under appropriate conditions, a number of partners of the CCR4–NOT complex has been shown to localize in dedicated cytoplasmic structures related to mRNA translation and decay, including P-bodies and stress granules [97].

Networking the CCR4–NOT complex with its transient partners

The mode of interaction of numerous auxiliary partners with the CCR4–NOT complex has been deciphered, including, in some cases, with structural details, revealing the presence of interaction hubs in the latter (Fig. 2). A major interaction hub is the CNOT9 protein. Its Armadillo repeats provide two specific docking surfaces, one on its convex side that presents tryptophan (W)-binding pockets and one on its concave side presenting a groove anchoring α-helices. Interestingly, this situation elicits the possibility of generating peptides inhibiting CCR4–NOT recruitment through CNOT9 to suppress specific mRNA deadenylation [98]. CNOT9 interacts with TNRC6/GW182 [64, 65], ZFP36 [85, 99], Roquin [84], RNF219 [31] (but note that alternative data have suggested that RNF219 binds directly to CNOT1 at its CN9BD domain independently of CNOT9 [33]), CNOT4 [75], Unkempt [80], and Bam [93, 100]. Surprisingly, if CNOT9 is a major interaction hub of the metazoan complex, its counterpart in S. cerevisiae still lacks direct interaction partners.

The scaffold CNOT1 protein itself acts as an interaction hub allowing the binding of transient partners in addition to being the backbone of the complex (Fig. 2). Hence, it binds directly to ZFP36 through its HEAT repeat located between the CNOT10/CNOT11 and the deadenylase modules [62]. This binding can be outcompeted by RESIST proteins that interact with the same region of CNOT1 [86]. Intriguingly, ZFP36 appeared to interact with the CCR4–NOT complex through contacts with CNOT1, CNOT9, and CNOT3 [62, 85, 99]. The ZFP36 binding region of CNOT1 also interacts with Pumilio proteins [101]. The latter also contacts the C-terminal module composed of CNOT2–CNOT3 bound to the C-terminus of CNOT1 [101]. CNOT1 also recruits the DDX6 RNA helicase through the MIF4G domain of the deadenylase module [64, 65]. Given that DDX6 interacts with many partners involved in RNA decay and translational repression (EDC3, LSM14, PAT1, and 4E-T), it may bridge some of those factors to the CCR4–NOT complex [102].

The intricate NOT module constituted by the C-terminus of CNOT1, CNOT2, and CNOT3 forms another important interacting hub (Fig. 2), binding to the RNA-binding proteins ZFP36 [99], Nanos [68, 77], Smaug [78], Pumilio factors [79], Unkempt [80], Bicaudal-C [81], and the ubiquitin ligases CNOT4 (interaction conserved for Not4 in yeast [76]) and Roquin [84]. These factors contact the CNOT1 C-terminal region and/or CNOT2/CNOT3, as is the case for Nanos, sometimes in redundancy with the CNOT9 hub as mentioned above.

Another recently discovered interaction hub of the CCR4–NOT complex is the C-terminal domain of CNOT11 that constitutes the antenna of the CNOT10/CNOT11 module (Fig. 2). It has been shown to interact with GGNBP2 [37] and SCAPER [20] in humans.

Besides these major interaction hubs, other CCR4–NOT subunits also have dedicated partners. Hence, in metazoans, CAF1 binds the members of the BTG/Tob protein family [103–105] as well as the PieF factor from L. pneumophila (described in detail below [39, 106]).

As mentioned above, it is noteworthy that multiple contact sites have been recurrently found for CCR4–NOT auxiliary factors (e.g. ZFP36, Unkempt, and Pumilio) and binding often involves short linear motifs (SLiM), each providing low-affinity binding, but whose combination may allow tighter interaction and increased specificity. Indeed, in the case of ZFP36, multivalent binding to the CCR4–NOT complex was shown to be important for full deadenylation activation [99]. The use of SLiM may facilitate fine-tuning protein–protein contacts to adapt protein association to biological needs, enabling rapid evolution. Thus, not surprisingly, the CCR4–NOT interaction network differs between species. For example, if CNOT4 in metazoans transiently interacts with CNOT9 or the NOT modules [75], in S. cerevisiae, a longer region with three interacting segments directly contacts Not1 upstream of the region involved in binding Not2/Not3 [76]. Thus, some contacts may be rewired at a fast pace, probably under selective pressure.

Biochemical functions of the CCR4–NOT complex

Is CCR4–NOT involved in transcription?

The first subunits of the CCR4–NOT complex were identified in S. cerevisiae genetic screens for deregulated expression of reporter genes, namely Caf1 (then called Pop2 [107]) and Ccr4 [108]. Four Not genes (NOT1-4) were soon after described as global negative transcription regulators differentially affecting the HIS3 promoters [21, 109]. In parallel, Not4 (then called Mot2) was identified as a negative regulator of pheromone-responsive gene expression [110, 111]. Most of these early publications concluded that these factors repressed transcription. This suggestion was reinforced when it was observed that these proteins form a complex [112] possibly localized in the nucleus [109] and further strengthened by downstream interaction and functional studies [113–120].

The unanticipated discovery that subunits of the CCR4–NOT complex affected deadenylation and cytoplasmic mRNA decay, and that some were endowed with poly(A) nuclease activity, questioned the earlier conclusions [121, 122]. Although the possibility that the complex may have a dual function in transcription and mRNA decay was raised [123], several observations question this prospect. For example, the differential effect of Not proteins on different promoters driving expression of the His3 protein, initially taken as a solid argument for a transcriptional function, may simply reflect the concomitant decay of mRNAs that were not synthesized simultaneously. Further, several genetic interactions between CCR4–NOT subunits and transcription factors could be explained by a partial restoration of the cellular RNA balance resulting from altered mRNA decay, a process now identified as transcript buffering [124]. If some published data are more difficult to reconcile with the absence of a direct implication of the CCR4–NOT complex in transcription, after over 30 years, direct evidence for a role of the purified complex in transcription initiation or elongation is still elusive as are structural data demonstrating direct and specific interactions between transcription factors and the CCR4–NOT complex. This contrasts with the growing observations directly linking the CCR4–NOT complex to cytoplasmic mRNA decay and translation (reviewed in particular in [58]) and unbiased evidence supporting its predominant localization in the cytoplasm [125]. Strikingly, recent efforts to identify transcription factors associated with the yeast CCR4–NOT complex using the latest TurboID technique failed to provide support for a functional connection between the CCR4–NOT complex with the nuclear mRNA production machinery, but rather argued for its cytoplasmic role in mRNA decay and translational control [126]. Overall, even if the S. cerevisiae CCR4–NOT complex has been widely described as a transcription factor, its implication in this process remains uncertain, while many of its effects on the transcriptome may be explained through its direct or indirect role in controlling mRNA degradation.

In many other species, including humans, implication of the CCR4–NOT complex in transcription remains questionable: altering CCR4–NOT functions often has a major impact on the transcriptome, but whether this is due to altered mRNA decay, indirect consequences of the latter, and/or a direct effect on transcription is unknown. In humans, it is noteworthy that, nowadays, all subunits of the CCR4–NOT complex have been functionally linked to mRNA decay and translation, or are required for the integrity of the complex itself. Thus, there is no evidence for subunits with dedicated activity in transcription. In S. pombe, however, regulation of the mitotic versus meiotic cell state is accompanied by changes in chromatin states at specific loci, a process that involves dedicated long noncoding RNAs as well as nascent RNAs and requires the CCR4–NOT complex, including its fission yeast-specific Mmi1 subunit [127]. Both the nuclease and ubiquitin ligase activities of the CCR4–NOT complex are implicated in this process [128]. In this system, the CCR4–NOT complex appears to have a rather direct role in controlling transcription by modulating chromatin states. However, this regulation requires the ability of the CCR4–NOT complex to degrade RNAs. It is unclear whether this activity happens both in the nucleus and in the cytoplasm or in only one of these compartments. One could even envisage that the CCR4–NOT complex gain access to chromatin associated RNAs during mitosis, when the nuclear envelope breaks, allowing the content of both compartments to mix.

Altogether, while transcriptional control was the first proposed molecular function of the CCR4–NOT complex, current evidence for a direct involvement is still lacking and many observations may be reconciled with an indirect impact of its other functions.

Deadenylation

In vitro and in vivo data demonstrate that the CCR4–NOT complex is involved in deadenylation. First, besides their structural similarities with nucleases, CCR4 and CAF1 exert deadenylase activities in vitro, releasing AMP in a stepwise manner from the substrate RNA [71, 129–132]. These biochemical assays were substantiated by structural analyses that elucidated how poly(A) tails bind in the catalytic site of these enzymes [69, 71, 132]. Further, deleting or knocking down these factors reduces deadenylation in vivo in yeast, mammalian, and trypanosoma cells [5, 42, 121, 122, 133]. It is noteworthy, however, that the yeast Caf1 nuclease domain appears to diverge from the consensus at key residues of the catalytic site and that in this species most if not all the deadenylation activity is carried out by the CCR4 subunit [129, 130]. Hence, in various species, catalysis of deadenylation originates in different subunits: in Ccr4 in S. cerevisiae, in CAF1 and/or CCR4 in humans [5, 10, 134] and drosophila [40], and only in CAF1 in trypanosomes (CCR4 being absent from this organism). Interestingly, in human cells, over-expression of a catalytic dominant CAF1 mutant protein is sufficient to significantly reduce deadenylation in vivo suggesting that, in contrast to yeast, in the human case CAF1 subunits provide most of the deadenylating activity [134]. Importantly, integration of the deadenylase module in the CCR4–NOT complex is required for efficient deadenylation in vivo. Indeed, wild-type catalytically competent Caf1 and Ccr4 unable to interact with the rest of the complex as a result of mutations in Not1 generate a severe deadenylation defect in yeast [48]. Reciprocally, regulated deadenylation can be achieved in vitro by targeting the reconstituted CCR4–NOT complex to specific substrate RNAs through RNA-binding proteins [44, 135, 136]. The latter connect the body of the CCR4–NOT complex to the targeted RNA, thereby recruiting the deadenylases. Recruitment of CCR4–NOT thus appears to switch the enzyme activity from a distributive mode to a more processive mode of action. However, deadenylation does not appear to be fully processive in vivo [10, 121, 122]. Hence, in pulse chase experiments in human cultured cells, poly(A) tail sizes were initially broadly distributed, containing between roughly 100 and 250 A residues. Their shortening generated a more homogeneous population peaking at 30–50 A residues after ~100 min, after which poly(A) shortening was slower for the next 80 min [4]. The reduced kinetic of deadenylation during the second phase was attributed to inhibition of the CCR4–CAF1 deadenylase activities by association of PABPC with LARP1. It is still unclear whether dissociation of the final PABPC, uridylation and binding of the Lsm1-7 complex to the short oligo(A) tail, and/or other events trigger the end of deadenylation and activation of decapping. However, artificially fusing the deadenylase to an RNA-binding domain results in rapid degradation of mRNAs containing the cognate binding site, but surprisingly, 3′-5′ degradation may continue in this case past the poly(A) tail into the mRNA body [137]. The latter observation is consistent with recruitment of the Ccr4 deadenylase being limiting but also indicates that deadenylases do not have an absolute specificity for A residues. In vitro observations using yeast and human enzymes are consistent with this conclusion: deadenylase will slow down at non-A residues, G being the least preferred substrate [138, 139]. Hence, deadenylation in vivo may be modulated when non-A residues are incorporated in the poly(A) tail [139]. The role of PABPC in protecting the poly(A) tail and its interplay with the CCR4–NOT complex remains ambiguous. It has indeed been suggested that CAF1 nuclease activity is blocked by PABPC whose release from the poly(A) tail would be accomplished by CCR4 based on in vitro assays with purified S. pombe CCR4–NOT complex and in vivo studies both in S. cerevisiae and mammalian cells [5, 16]. However, contradictory in vitro observations have been reported [90, 136]. Further, this is difficult to reconcile with the fact that double knock-out of Cnot6 and Cnot6l, the two CCR4 paralogs in mammals, has no impact on the viability of primary mouse embryonic fibroblasts to the contrary of single knock-outs of the CAF1 paralogs, Cnot8 or Cnot7 [140]. Preliminary structural insights into the degradation of PABP-bound poly(A) by the CCR4–NOT complex have been obtained [141, 142] but more detailed studies would be useful to decipher fine mechanisms.

Further regulation of deadenylation rates can occur through post-translational modifications of the CAF1 nuclease. Hence, in humans, CNOT7 has been found to be ubiquitinated by MEX-3C, a protein possessing RNA-binding and ubiquitin ligase domains. Interestingly, ubiquitination of CNOT7 promoted its deadenylase activity and the decay of MEX-3C-bound transcripts [143]. Also, CNOT7 has been found to be acetylated on the same lysine (K) residues by the histone acetyltransferases p300 and CBP. Inhibition of the histone deacetylases HDAC1 and HDAC2 increased global mRNA decay rates suggesting that acetylation of CNOT7 increased its nuclease activity [144]. Alternatively, inhibition of HDACs reduced the amount of RNF219, a partner of CCR4–NOT that has been shown to inhibit the deadenylase activity of the latter [31, 32, 94]. Therefore, increased deadenylation rates upon inhibition of HDAC could be the result of either or both CNOT7 acetylation and decreased levels of RNF219.

Overall, available data unequivocally demonstrate that the CCR4–NOT complex is an active deadenylase involved in mRNA poly(A) tail shortening thereby triggering mRNA turnover. Yet, some details of the mechanisms involved remain to be elucidated.

Ubiquitination

CNOT4 is a constitutive subunit of the CCR4–NOT complex in yeast and a transient partner of the complex in other eukaryotes. It contains a RING domain with E3 ubiquitin ligase activity [145] that transfers ubiquitin from an E2 enzyme to target proteins, leading to their degradation and/or altering their function or localization. Consistently, the structure of the S. cerevisiae Not4 RING domain with the E2 enzyme Ubc4 has been solved [76]. In parallel, the NMR structure of the human CNOT4 RING finger has been solved and its interaction with the homologous UBE2D2 E2 enzyme has been mapped and the complex modeled [145]. In vitro assays confirmed that human and S. cerevisiae CNOT4 homologues are active enzymes [145, 146]. In humans, CNOT4 has been identified as a translation surveillance factor, functioning through the ubiquitination of stalled ribosomes during elongation [18]. Consistently, in S. cerevisiae, ubiquitination of the ribosomal protein eS7 by Not4 has been suggested to be necessary for efficient translation [147, 148] and a prerequisite to further poly-ubiquitination by surveillance ubiquitin ligases when translation is impaired, leading to ribosome arrest and/or collision [149]. Additional factors associated with the ribosomes have been reported to be ubiquitinated by CNOT4, including the translation termination factor ABCE1 in humans [150] and the major nascent-protein associated complex formed by Egd1 and Egd2 in S. cerevisiae [151, 152]. Further, S. cerevisiae Not4 ubiquitin ligase activity was implicated in the degradation of the nascent protein induced by translational arrests [153]. Finally, in S. pombe, the CNOT4 homolog Mot2 was shown to contribute to the destruction and/or inhibition of the meiotic regulator Mei2 during vegetative growth through its ubiquitin ligase activity [128]. Many additional proteins, including RNA-binding proteins [154], histone H2B [155], the H3K4 demethylase Jhd2 [156], or even RNA polymerase II [157], have been reported to be modified by CNOT4, or in a manner depending on CNOT4, affecting their stability and fate.

It is noteworthy that in metazoans, other E3 ubiquitin ligases, namely Roquin and RNF219, have been reported to bind transiently to the CNOT9 subunit of the CCR4–NOT complex and constitute an alternative source of ubiquitination activity that may modify other targets [31, 32, 75, 83, 84, 94]. Interestingly, helices of Roquin and CNOT4 compete for the same site on the concave surface of CNOT9 to bind to the CCR4–NOT complex [75]. Altogether, ubiquitination appears to be an important activity of the CCR4–NOT complex with the ribosome constituting a prominent target.

Translational control

The CCR4–NOT complex impacts protein production through several processes, some direct while others may be secondary consequences of other functions. Hence, as mentioned above, in some instances the CCR4–NOT complex may directly modify translating ribosomes by ubiquitination. Alternatively, downregulation of protein synthesis may result from poly(A) tail shortening, which could reduce translation initiation by impacting PABPC interaction with the translation initiation factor eIF4G. A direct control of translation by the CCR4–NOT complex is likely to occur through the DDX6 RNA helicase that has been shown to bind directly CNOT1 and reported to repress translation and recruit decapping factors [158]. Beyond basic translational control, the CCR4–NOT complex has been shown to act in specific translational control mechanisms as detailed in the following paragraph. It has also been suggested to play a role in the co-translational assembly of protein complexes [159] but further work will be required to clarify the molecular mechanisms involved.

The CCR4–NOT complex at the cellular level: controlling mRNA fates by integrating mRNA signals

In cells, each mRNA has its individual decay rate. Moreover, those are not fixed and for a given mRNA, its decay may be modulated according to the conditions. mRNA decay may be controlled at many steps, but in several instances, deadenylation is rate limiting in cells, and deadenylation rate will dictate the kinetics of mRNA turnover. Thus, the CCR4–NOT complex plays a central role in cells by controlling gene expression at the post-transcriptional level. A striking example occurs in trypanosomatida, organisms in which polycistronic transcription produces long pre-mRNAs that are split into newly made individual mRNAs, thus originally present in equimolar quantities [160]. Post-transcriptional regulation through mRNA deadenylation (and decay) allows trypanosomal cells to adjust the level of each mRNA to a specific level [161]. Because poly(A) tails eliminated by deadenylases are homogeneous and essentially invariant from one mRNA to the next, the rate of deadenylation of individual transcripts is imposed by specific features present in the mRNA body. These include RNA sequences recognized by different factors, the presence of mRNA modifications, but also the capacity of the mRNA to be more or less efficiently translated, and even the propensity of the nascent protein to associate with dedicated partners. Through its ability to decipher sequence and structural features present in mRNAs, the CCR4–NOT complex appears as a complex molecular machine capable of integrating this information to shape mRNA translation and decay (Fig. 3).

Bridging target mRNAs and the CCR4–NOT complex through RNA-binding proteins

As detailed above, several RNA-binding proteins interact directly with the CCR4–NOT complex. By recognizing binding sites in specific mRNAs, these factors bring deadenylases close to the target mRNA 3′ end and speed up their deadenylation (Fig. 3). A typical example involves the human RNA-binding protein ZFP36 that directly binds the CCR4–NOT complex through multiple contacts (Fig. 2). ZFP36 recognizes AU-rich elements (AREs) located in the sequence of the 3′ untranslated region (3′UTR) of target mRNAs, inducing their turnover [62, 162] (Fig. 3). This destabilization activity was indeed instrumental in the identification of ZFP36 as a factor that prevents synthesis of excessive levels of TNFα by destabilizing its mRNA under normal conditions [163]. More globally, ZFP36 safeguards cells from prolonged inflammatory responses by being induced in the process and promoting the rapid mRNA destruction of pro-inflammatory genes [163]. Interestingly, the activity of ZFP36 is controlled at several levels. For example, phosphorylation by the p90 ribosomal S6 kinase 1 (RSK1) and p38-activated protein kinase (MK2) will prevent its interaction with the CCR4–NOT complex, thereby stabilizing ARE-containing mRNAs [164, 165]. Binding of ZFP36 protein family members to the CCR4–NOT complex may also be prevented by competitive binding of RESIST factors, a process involved in the regulation of interferon ß production [86]. The mRNA destabilizing activity of ZFP36 can further be counteracted by the competitive binding of HuR to mRNA ARE elements, in agreement with the pro-inflammatory role of this factor critical for the initial phase of this response [165, 166].

Another important group of RNA-binding proteins that recruit the CCR4–NOT complex to target mRNAs is the Pumilio family, also designated as the PUF family in reference to their founding members, Pumilio in Drosophila melanogaster and FBF in Caenorhabditis elegans. They are largely found in all eukaryotes and have been studied in different model systems including H. sapiens, D. melanogaster, C. elegans, and S. cerevisiae [167, 168]. PUF proteins share a characteristic RNA-binding domain formed by tandem repeats, each interacting with one nucleotide [169]. They recognize short specific sequences generally located in the 3′UTR of target mRNAs and collectively designated as Pumilio-response elements [167, 168]. In most cases, their interaction with mRNAs induces the degradation of the latter [167, 168], although some Pumilio proteins also participate in mRNA transport and translational repression [170]. In several organisms, PUF have been found to bind to the CCR4–NOT complex [168] and the mode of interaction has recently been deciphered for the human PUM1 and PUM2 proteins [79]. Interestingly, transcriptomic analyses revealed that several hundreds of transcripts are regulated jointly by the PUM1/2 proteins and the CCR4–NOT complex in humans [79]. PUF proteins impact several biological processes at the cellular level, including ribosome biogenesis and mitochondrial functions, and have been shown to play important functions in development and in particular in stem cell maintenance [167, 168].

Many additional RNA-binding proteins have been shown to promote deadenylation by the direct or indirect recruitment of the CCR4–NOT complex to target mRNAs in different species. Those include for example Smaug, Nanos, Unkempt, and Roquin [68, 78, 80, 83, 84]. It is likely that, with time, additional factors mediating the enrollment of the CCR4–NOT complex to deadenylate bound mRNAs will be identified. Further, as many RNA-binding proteins coexist in cells, it will be of interest to study how they combine, possibly synergizing or antagonizing each other, to regulate deadenylation.

Using microRNAs to recognize target mRNAs

Another way to recruit the CCR4–NOT complex to target mRNAs involves miRNAs, which are small noncoding RNAs encoded in the genome of metazoans. They bind to partially complementary sites in the 3′UTR of mRNAs and induce translational repression and mRNA degradation. During their maturation, they are loaded into the miRNA-induced silencing complex containing Argonaute and GW182 proteins, the latter also known as TNRC6 in humans [171]. TNRC6 proteins have been shown to be necessary to induce deadenylation and decay of bound mRNAs [172, 173]. They do so by direct recruitment of the two cellular deadenylases PAN2–PAN3 and CCR4–NOT [172, 174, 175]. The mode of interaction with the CCR4–NOT complex involves conserved GW-repeats that bind to the W-binding pockets of CNOT9 [64, 65] and additionally to CNOT1 [172]. The TNRC6-dependent recruitment of the CCR4–NOT complex initiates translational repression that appears to involve DDX6 binding to the complex, and degradation of the targeted mRNAs [64, 65] (Fig. 3). The importance of miRNAs in the regulation of metazoan gene expression is thought to be consequent because thousands of miRNAs are encoded in the human transcriptome and because one miRNA can target several mRNAs [171]. Further, as metazoan mRNA 3′UTRs can contain both motifs bound by RNA-binding proteins and miRNAs, potential additive, synergistic, or competitive regulations may exist between miRNAs and RNA-binding proteins [171].

Sensing RNA modifications

mRNA fate may also be determined by the presence of post-transcriptionally added m6A in the transcript. This modification is recognized by various reader proteins. Among them, proteins containing a YTH domain have been shown to impact mRNA stability. Hence, the human YTHDF2 factor binds m6A marks and directly recruits the CCR4–NOT complex [45], establishing a direct link between m6A and mRNA turnover [45] (Fig. 3). As m6A has widely been observed to shorten mRNA half-lives, similar mechanisms of recruitment of the CCR4–NOT complex by m6A readers may occur in different species.

Generic detection of substrate poly(A) tails

In an unanticipated twist, the presence of poly(A)-binding proteins on mRNA poly(A) tails may also, in some conditions, promote the recruitment of deadenylases. Indeed, in metazoans, factors of the BTG/Tob family have been shown to bridge poly(A)-binding proteins and the CAF1 deadenylase subunit of the CCR4–NOT complex, thereby stimulating deadenylation [88–92, 141, 142] (Fig. 3). While this process may seem counterintuitive, as all mRNAs will be targeted, it allows cells to transiently downregulate the whole transcriptome, an advantageous situation when it has to be quickly reprogrammed for differentiation or in response to stresses. Accordingly, BTG1 and BTG2 have been shown to mediate global mRNA decay to maintain naïve T cells in a quiescent state [176]. Also, BTG4 has been found to mediate massive decay of the maternal transcriptome during early embryogenesis through interaction with either the embryonic-specific poly(A)-binding protein PAPBC1L or PABPN1L [91, 92].

However, Tob proteins may not mediate global mRNA decay, as their recruitment of the CCR4–NOT complex has been shown to require not only interaction with PABPC1 but also the association with CPEB proteins bound to CPE motifs present in the 3′UTR of mRNAs [177, 178], thereby targeting specific transcripts.

Monitoring the translating ribosome

Cryo-EM studies using factors from S. cerevisiae and humans have revealed that the N-terminal domain of CNOT3 (homologous to both Not3 and Not5 in yeast) interacts with stalled ribosomes [17, 18]. In detail, for yeast, the highly conserved N-terminal domain of Not5 binds to the empty E site of the ribosome when the A site is simultaneously not occupied by a tRNA (Fig. 3). This unusual situation arises when a rare codon is present in the ribosome A site, delaying tRNA recruitment, and thus allowing the E site tRNA to leave before the A site is filled. Thus, a unique conformation of the translating ribosome triggers the recruitment of the CCR4–NOT complex and initiates the degradation of inefficiently translated mRNAs [17, 18]. While the basic features of this mechanism are conserved in humans [17, 18], detailed analyses have revealed that in addition to docking in the ribosome E site when the A site is empty, CNOT3 recognizes specific features of the P site tRNA. These multiple recognitions restrict the recruitment of the CCR4–NOT complex to ribosomes decoding specific sites [73]. Altogether, these observations provide a molecular mechanism to explain how codon usage modulates mRNA stability, a feature observed in several organisms, including yeast and humans [179–181]. Beyond its contribution to the biological regulation of gene expression, these features are important to optimize protein production, for example, in the design of efficient mRNA vaccines [182].

Eliminating mRNAs encoding potentially deleterious proteins

In addition to targeting cellular mRNAs containing rare codons, the CCR4–NOT complex is involved in the degradation of mRNAs encoding proteins that fail to associate with important partners with possible toxic consequences. In humans, evidence for such a mechanism was observed for the synthesis of tubulin subunits. Specifically, nascent α- and β-tubulin proteins contain a motif at their N-terminus recognized by the protein TTC5 when excess unpolymerized tubulin dimers are present. TTC5 is crucial for the autoregulation of the levels of mRNA encoding these proteins [183]. Using Turbo ID, SCAPER was identified as a partner of TTC5 that also interacts with the CCR4–NOT complex [20]. Structural modeling suggested that SCAPER interacts with CNOT11, and consistently knockdown of CNOT1 and inactivation of CNOT11 stabilized α- and β-tubulin mRNA levels. Based on these observations, it is believed that, when an excess of tubulin dimers is present, TTC5 binds to nascent tubulin proteins at the ribosome exit tunnel, inducing the recruitment of the CCR4–NOT complex through a CNOT11–SCAPER interaction (Fig. 3). This process enables the deadenylation and degradation of mRNAs encoding α- and β-tubulins, allowing the fine adjustment of the levels of the mRNAs encoding these critical factors. A similar mechanism was detected in yeast. In this case, the slow growth of strains lacking the Acl4 chaperone, a protein specifically involved in escorting of protein Rpl4 from its site of synthesis to the nucleolus, is suppressed by mutations in the Caf130 subunit of the CCR4–NOT complex as well as in its partners Cal4, Btt1, and Egd2 [19]. This unexpected observation was explained by the fact that the CCR4–NOT complex initiates the deadenylation and the degradation of the Rpl4 mRNA when the nascent protein is unable to associate with its specific chaperone Acl4. Preventing the downregulation of the Rpl4 mRNA when Acl4 is absent by removing Caf130 and its partners, results in the synthesis of a higher level of the Rpl4 protein, which partly compensates from the defect caused by the absence of the chaperone.

Overall, through a complex network of interactions, the CCR4–NOT complex is able to decode several signals, present in mRNAs or in nascent proteins, to enforce a specific deadenylation rate that will dictate the tempo of the transcript disappearance. As the factors involved in this decoding can themselves be regulated, either through timely synthesis or degradation, or by post-translational modifications, the decay of individual mRNAs can be finely tuned. In this vein, it is possible that additional partners of the CCR4–NOT complex will contribute to the control of mRNA deadenylation.

The CCR4–NOT complex at the level of the organism: viability, development, and diseases

The essential nature of the CCR4–NOT complex is supported by several facts. Early on, the yeast Not1 protein was shown to be essential for viability. It is surprising that this is the only subunit of the CCR4–NOT complex that has this property in S. cerevisiae, suggesting that its main role as a scaffold is to balance the activities of the different CCR4–NOT modules [184]. If not essential for life, removal of most other yeast CCR4–NOT subunits (except Caf130 and Caf40/CNOT9), including the Ccr4 and Caf1 deadenylases, induces severe growth phenotypes revealing their important roles in cellular physiology. By contrast, analyses of CCR4–NOT complex subunit null mutants in multicellular organisms, including D. melanogaster, C. elegans, and Mus musculus, have shown embryonic lethality for most of them (e.g. Cnot1, Cnot3, Cnot4, Cnot9, and Cnot10 in mice) demonstrating the importance of the complex functions for harmonious development ([185, 186–189] reviewed in [185,190,191]). Of note, homozygous deletions of the nuclease subunits in mice result in diverse phenotypes. Hence, deletion of Cnot8 is embryonic lethal [140], while Cnot7 mutants are male sterile due to oligo-astheno-teratozoospermia [192, 193]. Surprisingly, inactivation of either or both paralogs of the CCR4 subunit, Cnot6 or Cnot6l, results in viable offsprings [188, 194]. Cnot6 KO mice, born at the predicted Mendelian frequency, exhibited no obvious abnormalities and were fertile [140]. However, female Cnot6l KO mice were sub-fertile [194] and displayed reduced inosine mRNA modifications in oocytes due to the clearance of inosine-modified mRNA. This indicated the existence of a previously unidentified mRNA mechanism to eliminate specific mRNAs during oocyte maturation [195]. The importance of CCR4 for female fertility has also been observed in D. melanogaster and C. elegans [196, 197].

In humans, high-throughput sequencing analyses have led to the identification of variants in the CNOT1 and CNOT3 subunits that are linked to rare human genetic diseases. Hence, variants in the scaffold subunit CNOT1 have been found to cause the Vissers-Bodmer syndrome (OMIM #61903), a neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by global developmental delay, intellectual disability, hypotonia, and autistic features with a highly variable phenotype [198–200]. Another specific variants of CNOT1, p.(Arg535Cys), is responsible for holoprosencephaly, a defect frequently associated with pancreatic agenesis or late-onset diabetes (OMIM #618500) [201–204]. Concerning CNOT3, variants have been observed to exert pleiotropic effects across a range of physiological systems. For example, they are implicated in a developmental disorder named Intellectual Developmental Disorder with Speech Delay, Autism, and Dysmorphic Facies (IDDSADF) (OMIM #618672) [205–208]. Importantly, many mutations affecting CNOT1 and CNOT3 are de novo mutations and patients are heterozygous. This underlines the importance of the complex as haploinsufficiency generates by itself a strong pathophysiological outcome. In most cases, mutations of CNOT subunits appear to result in loss of function. Indeed, many alleles result from frameshift or splice-site mutations or creation of a stop codon. It is thus likely that missense mutations with similar clinical characteristics also result in nonfunctional proteins, possibly inducing destabilization of the mutant factor. The location of most missense mutations provides therefore limited information. Yet, in the case of CNOT3, missense mutations and in-frame deletions cluster in the two globular domains located at the N- and C-termini of the protein involved in binding the ribosome and CNOT1, respectively. In contrast, the middle region of CNOT3, likely to be intrinsically disordered, is site of truncating mutations but with no or only few missense mutations, suggesting that it is highly tolerant to sequence changes [208]. In contrast, for CNOT1, that does not contain extensive intrinsically disordered regions, missense mutations are spread over different functional domains [198]. As they result in similar phenotypes and mimic loss-of-function alleles, the latter are all likely to abrogate CNOT1 activity to some extent. If most mutations observed in patients provide little information about the function of the CCR4–NOT complex, the observation that a recurrent de novo substitution of arginine 535 to cysteine in seven independent patients with holoprosencephaly associated or not with pancreatic agenesis [202–204, 209] questions how this unique substitution specifically result in this clinical outcome, especially when other mutations of CNOT1 result in the Vissers-Bodmer syndrome with different characteristics [198]. As the mutated residue at position 535 is not known to contact identified CCR4–NOT partners, its mechanism of action remains unexplained. Other subunits of the CCR4–NOT complex have also been linked to various human diseases, but the number of reported associations varies between subunits (for a review, see [191]).

Studies of cancer patients with deep-sequencing also uncovered variants of CCR4–NOT subunits such as mutations of CNOT3 in adenomatous polyposis adenomas [210] and acute lymphoblastic leukemias [211] or mutations of CNOT9 in cutaneous melanomas [212]. Some recurrent mutations found in cancer patients may provide some functional insights. For example, substitution of glutamate by arginine at position 20 of CNOT3 has been reported to be an activating mutation in adenomatous polyposis adenomas [210] that may result from increased interaction of the mutant CNOT3 with ribosomal RNA when the corresponding domain occupies the E-site of the ribosome [73] (Fig. 3). However, mutations observed in cancer cells are highly heterogenous with some loss-of-function mutants, activating mutations, or even amplification found in patients with similar types of cancers. Given this heterogeneity, often mingled with additional mutations, it is difficult to assess the causal contribution of the altered genes to specific form of cancer and to determine how the mutated protein contributes to these pathologies.

Beyond genetic alterations of CCR4–NOT subunits, several reports have implicated this complex in diverse aspects of pathogen infection dynamics. For example, during adenovirus infection, the levels of TNKS1BP1/CNOT12, and to a lesser extent of CNOT3 and of CNOT7, were reduced. Accordingly, depletion of CNOT1 resulted in an increase of viral early proteins. By contrast, depletion of CNOT1, CNOT10 and CNOT11 was found to favor reduced HIV infection in primary T cells by upregulating the innate immune system [35]. In detail, reduction of the levels of CNOT10, CNOT11, or of the long form of CNOT1 upregulated expression of the IRF7 transcription factor that itself stimulates expression of interferon induced genes. Thus, in the absence of the corresponding module of the CCR4–NOT complex, innate immunity pathways were constitutively activated, thus providing protection against HIV infection [35]. More recently, a mechanism was identified in which, during infection of human lung alveolar macrophages, the bacterial pathogen L. pneumophila modulates the deadenylase activity of the CCR4–NOT complex. When present in a vacuole inside the host cell, these bacteria secrete in the cytoplasm effector proteins to counteract cellular defenses and reorganize cellular functions to facilitate their growth. Among the numerous effector proteins involved in this process, PieF was shown to interact with the deadenylase module of the human CCR4–NOT complex preventing CNOT6 or CNOT6L to integrate the CCR4–NOT complex and inhibiting the deadenylase activity of CNOT7 and CNOT8 [39, 106]. Moreover, PieF overexpressed in yeast phenocopied a CAF1 mutant in that species, demonstrating that its inhibitory activity can target evolutionarily distant proteins [106]. While PieF collaborates with hundreds of other effector proteins secreted by L. pneumophila in human cells to induce Legionnaires’ diseases, its presence reveals that parasites have acquired the ability to modulate host mRNA deadenylation and thereby reprogram the host cell gene expression to their advantage.

Altogether, the importance of the post-transcriptional regulation of gene expression mediated by the CCR4–NOT complex is exemplified by its impact on viability and diseases. Therefore, the CCR4–NOT complex is emerging as a potential target for drug development [98].

Future perspectives

Over the past decades, research on the CCR4–NOT complex has shifted from viewing it as a mysterious player in transcription to recognizing it as a central cytoplasmic machinery that regulates mRNA degradation in close coordination with the ribosome. Numerous studies have revealed multiple pathways through which the CCR4–NOT complex is recruited to repress translation and promote mRNA decay—including mechanisms mediated by RNA-binding proteins, miRNA-associated RISC complexes, RNA modifications, ribosome pausing, and nascent polypeptides. Additional modes of recruitment will likely emerge as this field continues to evolve. Also, mechanistic details, e.g. regulations by post-translational modifications, in particular ubiquitination, need further investigations.

Given the essential role of the CCR4–NOT complex in cellular homeostasis, dissecting its precise contributions to mRNA turnover and translational control remains a major objective. Moreover, recent findings have challenged the long-held view that deadenylation is the primary trigger of mRNA decay [213, 214]. Future studies will be required to clarify the transition between deadenylation and the induction of decapping that ultimately leads to mRNA decay. Beyond mRNA decay, the CCR4–NOT complex is also clearly connected to translational control, but given the multiplicity of the molecular mechanisms involved, deciphering its role in this process is a complex task that should however bring exciting developments.

From a structural point of view, it will be important to determine whether the CCR4–NOT complex adopts a defined conformation or remains inherently flexible and dynamic. Resolving its structure, for example during deadenylation of a PABPC1-coated poly(A) tail, alone or associated with partners, would provide valuable insights. Beyond mechanistic understanding, the field now stands at a stage where existing knowledge can be leveraged to design small molecules that selectively disrupt CCR4–NOT interactions with specific partners, thereby enabling functional modulation with potential therapeutic applications.
