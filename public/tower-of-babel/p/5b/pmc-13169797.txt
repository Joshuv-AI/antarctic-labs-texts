Amikacin pharmacokinetics after nebulization among intubated patients
Authors: Nicolas Gregoire, Sandrine Marchand, François Barbier, Jean-Pierre Quenot, Jean-Etienne Herbrecht, Damien Roux, Mickaël Landais, David Schnell, Anne Veinstein, Claire Dahyot-Fizelier, Nathalie Heuzé Vourc’h, Renaud Respaud, Elsa Tavernier, Stephan Ehrmann, Reva network, CRICS-TRIGGERSEP F-CRIN research network
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAmikacin nebulization may be used in mechanically ventilated patients for pneumonia prevention and treatment. Available drug delivery and pharmacokinetic knowledge mainly rely on in vitro data and studies of a limited number of patients.MethodsTo evaluate pulmonary and systemic pharmacokinetics of nebulized amikacin in mechanically ventilated patients and assess the influence of ventilation and nebulization conditions on drug concentrations in the tracheal aspirate and plasma, this ancillary study of a published randomized trial performed population pharmacokinetic modelling on 498 tracheal aspirates and 602 plasma concentrations following 20 mg/kg amikacin vibrating mesh nebulization among 261 patients.ResultsTracheal aspirate amikacin concentrations were very high but variable between patients (median peak concentration 7269 µg/g, interquartile range 4935–9846 µg/g). The tracheal aspirate concentration declined with a median terminal half-life of 7.6 h (interquartile range 5.3–44.2 h), and no accumulation was observed over three doses. Systemic absorption was marginal (median peak plasma concentration 1.9 mg/L, interquartile range 1.3-3.0 mg/L). Despite differences in ventilation mode, circuit type, humidification, inspiratory flow rate, respiratory rate, and tidal volume, no significant impact on tracheal aspirate and plasma amikacin concentrations was observed.ConclusionsNebulized amikacin achieved high pulmonary concentrations with minimal systemic exposure. Tracheal aspirate and plasma drug concentration were not significantly influenced by ventilation or nebulization conditions. These findings suggest that strict optimization of nebulization parameters may not be necessary when aiming for tracheobronchial antibiotic inhaled delivery such as to prevent ventilator associated pneumonia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13054-026-05965-3.Keywords: Aerosols, Drug delivery systems, Mechanical ventilation, Ventilator associated lower respiratory tract infection

Background

Inhaled administration of antibiotics is theoretically of interest in the treatment of pulmonary infections, as it offers high local concentrations and low systemic concentrations, and therefore few systemic side effects. The antibiotics for which this route of administration seems most appropriate are those with a sufficiently long residence time in the lungs after nebulization, such as aminoglycosides, which are positively charged and not very lipophilic [1–3].

Historically, it was thought that aerosol therapies could not be used in mechanically ventilated patients because the dose fractions actually delivered were low [2, 4]. For example, in the 80’s McIntyre et al. showed that the fraction of delivered dose from radiolabelled aerosols was 3% for patients undergoing invasive mechanical ventilation, compared with 12% for healthy, non-intubated individuals [5]. A better understanding of the factors influencing drug delivery [6–8] has led to the development of new, more efficient systems and implementation modalities, and recent studies report deposited dose fractions of the order of 10 to 15% and higher [9]. This has led to renewed interest in inhaled therapy for mechanically ventilated patients [10].

Amikacin is an antibiotic of interest in the treatment of ventilator-associated pneumonia, often caused by Gram-negative aerobic bacilli [11]. While some recent clinical trials have shown disappointing results from treatment with inhaled amikacin [12, 13], others have shown promising results [14, 15].

Most of the aforementioned optimization of inhaled drug delivery through ventilator circuits has been derived from in vitro bench studies. Clinical data on plasma and pulmonary pharmacokinetic data following amikacin nebulization are scarce [16–20]. Available concentrations measured in the tracheal aspirates or epithelial lining fluid are very high and highly variable, while plasma concentrations are low, justifying the interest of nebulizing amikacin in these patients to obtain high local concentrations and low systemic toxicity. However, these clinical studies were carried out on a very small number of patients precluding evaluation of the impact of ventilation and nebulization conditions and their optimization on in vivo drug concentrations.

The AMIKINHAL trial showed a significant reduction in ventilator-associated pneumonia after preventive administration of 3 doses of 20 mg/kg amikacin administered 24 h apart by inhalation among 850 intubated patients. The present pharmacokinetic analysis is an ancillary study of this trial, involving a subgroup of 261 patients. The aim of the current study was to evaluate the pharmacokinetics of amikacin in the tracheal aspirate and plasma after nebulized delivery in a large-scale sample of mechanically ventilated patients, and to estimate the influence of mechanical ventilation and nebulization conditions on these pharmacokinetics. The primary outcomes were the maximum (Cmax) and trough (H24) concentrations in tracheal aspirates and plasma. The secondary outcomes were the changes in these concentrations according to the different mechanical ventilation and nebulization conditions. We hypothesized that amikacin inhalation would induce high tracheal aspirate and low plasma amikacin concentrations and that nebulization conditions would significantly impact tracheal aspirate concentrations.

Methods

Clinical trial

The pharmacokinetic data in the present study are derived from an ancillary study of the AMIKINHAL trial described elsewhere (NCT03149640; EUDRA-CT 2016 -001054-17) [14, 21]. In brief, the ancillary study was conducted in a subset of 9 centers in France. Intensive care unit (ICU) patients were eligible if they had been on invasive mechanical ventilation for at least 72 h. Patients were not eligible in case of invasive mechanical ventilation for more than 96 h, suspected or confirmed ventilator-associated pneumonia, chronic renal disease, acute renal failure without renal replacement therapy, tracheostomy, systemic aminoglycosides therapy. Patients receiving placebo or renal replacement therapy were not included in this ancillary study.

Nebulization (20 mg/Kg of ideal body weight, maximum 2 g, as per protocol) were performed over 3 consecutive days using a vibrating mesh nebulizer (Aerogen Solo, Aerogen, Gallway Irland). The nebulizer was placed upstream in the inspiratory limb of the ventilator, and the choice of ventilator circuit (either the use of an optimized circuit placing the nebulizer 30 cm upstream of a streamlined Y piece in a non-humidified circuit or the use of a standard heated humidified circuit placing the nebulizer immediately upstream of the Y-piece) was left to the discretion of the clinician (see Figure S1). Ventilation settings, sedation and muscle relaxation, interruption or not of the heated humidifier were left to the discretion of the clinician.

Sampling and bioanalytical assay

Over the three days of amikacin aerosols treatment, patients had one tracheal aspiration and 1 blood sample taken per day, with sampling times left to the discretion of the investigators. Tracheal aspiration was performed according to standard practice for routine airway suctioning, without any prior liquid instillation. Amikacin concentrations in tracheal aspirates and plasma were determined by 2 validated Liquid Chromatography coupled to tandem Mass Spectrometry (LC/MS-MS) techniques (see Additional File 1 for detail).

Population pharmacokinetic model development

The development of the population pharmacokinetic model is described in detail in Additional File 1. In brief, the population analysis was carried out using the Stochastic Approximation Expectation-Maximization (SAEM) algorithm implemented in the Monolix software (Monolix 2023R1, Lixoft SAS, a Simulations Plus company). Concentration data in tracheal aspirates and plasma were analyzed separately.

Various structural models were tested, both for concentrations in tracheal aspirates and in plasma. Inter-individual and inter-occasion variabilities were estimated and assumed to follow a lognormal distribution. Additive, proportional and combined error models were tested.

The effects of the following covariates were evaluated on the different pharmacokinetic parameters of the structural model: patients’ age, sex, weight, serum creatinine concentration at inclusion; at the time of nebulization, pulsed oxygen saturation (SpO2), fraction of inspires oxygen (FiO2), arterial partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2), intravenous sedation, muscle relaxation, ventilator mode and settings that were left at the discretion of the attending physician, (volume control – assist control ventilation, VC-AC ; or pression support ventilation, PSV), inspiratory flow rate, peak pressure, plateau pressure, positive expiratory pressure, tidal volume, respiratory rate, type of ventilator circuit (optimized dry circuit or heated humidified circuit); combining these variables, an optimal nebulization setup (respiratory rate ≤ 20 + tidal volume ≥ 450 mL + optimized dry circuit + inspiratory flow rate ≤ 40 L/min (in case of VC-AC) was compared to “non-optimal” conditions (respiratory rate > 20 + tidal volume < 450 mL + non-optimized heated humidified circuit + inspiratory flow rate > 40 L/min (if VC-AC) or intermediate conditions in-between those extreme; occurrence of side effects during nebulization (hypoxemia or hypotension). Missing covariate values were replaced by the median patient value or median value of all patients (Table S1, Additional File 1).

Final model evaluation included graphical analysis of goodness of fit plots, relative standard errors, visual predictive checks (VPCs), normalized prediction distribution errors (NPDE), and evaluation of shrinkage.

Inter- and intra-individual variability in K12 and K21 was very high, but the precision of the estimation of this variability was good (RSE < 25%) and there was no significant shrinkage (< 4%) (Table S3 and Figures S2 to S7). Discussion

A total of 498 concentrations in tracheal aspirates and 602 concentrations in plasma were measured in 261 patients at various sampling times determined by clinicians. This sampling scheme enabled a comprehensive characterization of the pharmacokinetic profiles in both matrices over multiple dosing intervals. It should be noted that, while the population parameter estimates of the final model obtained with this sampling scheme are robust, the individual estimates are model-derived. After amikacin nebulization (dose range 625 to 2500 mg placed in a vibrating mesh nebulizer), concentrations measured in the tracheal aspirates reached very high levels (median Cmax of the order of 7300 µg/g) and declined rapidly, with a terminal half-life of around 8 h and predicted median residual concentrations (H24) of around 100 µg/g. In previous studies, after aerosolization of amikacin in mechanically ventilated patients, pulmonary concentrations were also very high and highly variable [16, 17, 20, 22]. Montgomery et al. reported maximal tracheal aspirate concentrations of approximately 12,400 ± 4000 µg/g following nebulization of a 300 mg dose of amikacin [17]. In separate studies, Niederman et al. achieved mean maximal amikacin concentrations of about 6000 ± 3500 mg/L with 400 mg nebulization every 24 h [20] and 16,400 ± 41,600 mg/L with 400 mg nebulization every 12 h [22]. Notably, in our study, we administered considerably higher doses than those reported in previous investigations (median of 1625 mg versus 300 mg and 400 mg, respectively), yet we observed no corresponding increase in tracheal aspirate concentrations. This discrepancy is likely attributable to the different nebulization modalities used across studies: breath-synchronized nebulization in Niederman’s studies versus continuous nebulization in our protocol. Between studies pre-analytical matrix preparation differences may also have come into play as amikacin concentration measurement in mucus is not routine practice. Overall, our results are in agreement with previous studies, showing in a much higher number of patients that tracheal concentrations after amikacin aerosolization in mechanically ventilated patients are very high and very variable.

Under the conditions of the study, we observed no accumulation of amikacin concentrations in tracheal aspirates between the first and third administration. It can be noticed that according to our model, after a single dose of 1625 mg, the typical time required for the concentration to fall below the limit of quantification (10 µg/g) was 52 h. No accumulation in plasma was either observed. Petitcolin et al. also found no accumulation in plasma after 3 nebulization of 60 mg/kg amikacin [18]. However, in a previous study, peak concentrations in tracheal aspirates were higher at day 3 than at day 1 (16,000 vs. 12,000 µg/mL), suggesting an accumulation of amikacin in the lungs over the course of administrations [20]. Because of these contradictory results, for aerosol treatments lasting longer than 3 days, this accumulation should be assessed.

Concentrations in tracheal aspirates are probably a poor reflection of concentrations at the infection site in case of pneumonia compared to concentrations in the epithelial lining fluid [23]. However, Niederman et al. proposed a target maximum concentration to be achieved in tracheal aspirates of 6400 µg/mL to be effective in the treatment of Gram-negative pneumonia for minimum inhibitory concentrations of up to 256 µg/mL [20]. It may be noted that in our study 72% of patients achieved at least one maximum concentrations in tracheal aspirates above this very challenging target, which may be linked to the clinical gains in terms of pneumonia prevention demonstrated in AMIKINHAL [14]. Furthermore, in the AMIKINHAL study amikacin was used for the prophylaxis, not treatment, of ventilator-associated pneumonia. In this context the concentrations in tracheal aspirates may be relevant, since decontamination of the upper airways and tracheal tube is the target of prophylaxis. However, the tracheobronchial pharmacokinetic study result may not be extrapolated to potential alveolar amikacin concentrations when aiming for pneumonia therapy. For the same reasons, distal pathophysiological modifications such as airway obstruction or lung collapse seen in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease or the acute respiratory distress syndrome were not investigated in the current work.

Mechanical ventilation conditions can influence the fraction of dose delivered by nebulization. For example, the fraction of dose delivered has been reported to be greater in VC-AC rather than PSV (15.1% vs. 10.5%) [9]. Alternatively, in vitro models show that the use of a heated humidifier results in an increase in particle size and a decrease of around 40% in the fraction of the dose reaching the lungs [6]. However, clinical studies did not find any significant differences depending on whether humidifiers were used or not [24], and stopping the humidifier during nebulization was not effective [10]. Dugernier et al. showed that the specific optimized dry circuit used in the present study with the nebulizer placed in the inspiratory limb 30 cm from a streamlined Y-piece, compared with a conventional heated humidified circuit, increased the fraction of the dose delivered to the lungs [8]. It has also been shown that a decrease in inspiratory flow rate, and a continuous rather than variable flow rate, leads to an increase in the fraction of the dose delivered [8, 9]. The main limitation of most of these studies is their relatively limited sample size.

The large patient population of the present study represented a unique opportunity to evaluate in vivo the effect of nebulization conditions on drug delivery as evaluated through tracheal aspirate pharmacokinetics; however, feasibility considerations precluded broncho-alveolar lavage implementation in such a large study population, thus results extrapolation to alveolar drug concentration are limited.

Unexpectedly, no link was observed between nebulization conditions and tracheal aspirate amikacin concentrations. In particular, we found no difference between (i) VC-AC ventilation and PSV or (ii) types of ventilator circuit (optimized dry vs standard heated humidified circuit), (iii) low and high inspiratory flow setting in VC-AC, (iv) higher and lower pressure support level in PSV, (v) higher and lower respiratory rate, or (vi) higher or lower tidal volume. Even combining these variables to distinguish theoretical “optimal” nebulization conditions compared to “non-optimal” conditions, yielded no difference. Thus, the impact of those variables in vivo in a large-scale study may not be as relevant as in smaller studies specifically targeting evaluation of these variables. Similarly, at the systemic level no link was observed between nebulization conditions and plasma amikacin pharmacokinetics.

While these results do not contradict previous smaller sized in vivo and bench studies it may indicate that the impact of nebulization conditions, in the range evaluated in this study, is minor compared to the high intra-patient and inter-patient pharmacokinetic variability observed in the present large clinical data set. In other words, “non-optimal” conditions, as implemented in this trial, may be implemented without jeopardizing efficacy in terms of tracheal aspirate concentrations and represent a simplification of everyday clinical practice. In line with this interpretation, it appears not necessary to adapt the dosage of inhaled amikacin according to ventilation and nebulization conditions when targeting tracheobronchial deposition. One may hypothesize that large individual differences in clinical factors not evaluated in the present study such as quantity and quality of mucus, mucociliary clearance, conformation of the upper and lower airways, may play an important, so far underestimated role. Amikacin accumulation in the proximal tracheobronchial tree through drug trickling from the tracheal tube, as reported in a scintigraphy study may also have come into play [8]. One cannot rule out other factors such as the vibrating mesh nebulization device used in the present study compared to numerous previous works using jet nebulizers or that the very high dose used in the present study, through a mass effect, would render small delivery efficiency changes irrelevant. Furthermore, the range of variations of the various covariables may have been insufficient to show a statistically significant impact on delivery which represents a limitation of the current study. We did not observe a link between concentrations in the tracheal aspirates and plasma pharmacokinetic, thus data were modelled in 2 separate analyses. These results are in line with results from Rubino et al., who measured concentrations in both tracheal aspirates and plasma, and also separated the 2 analyses due to their independence [25]. This suggests that concentrations in the tracheal aspirates poorly reflect the concentrations that pass to the systemic level and that a large part of the amikacin found in the tracheal aspirates is eliminated by mucociliary clearance.

Regarding the systemic pharmacokinetics of amikacin observed after nebulization, the volume of systemic distribution of patients increased with weight and decreased with age, and the rate of elimination was lower in patients with high plasma creatinine at inclusion, as expected and already reported [26–28].

According to our model, the median peak plasma concentration was 2.5 mg/L, and was reached 1.2 h after the start of nebulization (which lasted on average 49 min). This compares with a median Cmax of 3.1 mg/L, observed 2.2 h after the start of amikacin nebulization, reported by Petitcollin et al. delivering a higher dose and using a different device [18]. The median terminal elimination half-life was estimated at 7.3 h in the blood, which is higher than that reported in ICU patients after intravenous administration (1.6 h for patients with renal function comparable to that of our patients) [18]. This suggests that the absorption of amikacin from the lungs to the blood is slower than its systemic elimination. Absorption to the blood is therefore a factor limiting systemic elimination, and the blood half-life we observed corresponded to an absorption half-life from the lung and not an elimination half-life. After 3 nebulization every 24 h of 1625 mg amikacin, the model predicted that less than 1% of patients would have a residual concentration of amikacin greater than 10 mg/L, a plasma concentration generally considered as the threshold for systemic toxicity29, thus documenting the systemic and in particular renal safety of inhaled amikacin for nearly all patients [16, 28]. This confirms, from a pharmacokinetic point of view, the very few systemic adverse effects that have been observed clinically in the AMIKINHAL trial [14]. Nevertheless, in settings of higher lung inflammation such as established ventilator associated pneumonia or the acute respiratory distress syndrome alveolo-capillary membrane alteration may lead to higher systemic drug passage.

Conclusions

When delivering inhaled amikacin at a daily dose of 20 mg/kg for 3 consecutive days in mechanically ventilated patients, concentrations in tracheal aspirates were very high and highly variable between patients. These concentrations did not appear to be significantly modified by ventilation and nebulization conditions in the range evaluated in this study and appeared to be sufficient when targeting the tracheobronchial tree to prevent pneumonia with little systemic side-effects.
