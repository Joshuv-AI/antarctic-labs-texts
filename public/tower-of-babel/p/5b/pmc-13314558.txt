The ‘Co‐Qual’: Real‐time assessment of prostate biopsy core quality using fluorescence confocal microscopy

Prostate biopsy remains fundamental to prostate cancer (PCa) diagnosis and risk stratification, yet inadequate tissue sampling is an ongoing concern that may result in missed diagnoses, disease underestimation, or the need for repeat procedures [1, 2]. In current practice, biopsy core adequacy is determined only after routine histopathological processing, typically several days post‐procedure, leaving no opportunity for intraoperative feedback or immediate re‐sampling [3, 4]. Fluorescence confocal microscopy (FCM) generates high‐resolution digital images of freshly acquired tissue in near real time, without compromising specimen integrity, and has demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy and concordance with standard histopathology for PCa detection in multiple single‐centre series [5, 6]. However, existing studies have focused primarily on FCM's diagnostic performance rather than its potential to evaluate sampling adequacy. We introduce the ‘Co‐Qual’, a structured, real‐time classification system for prostate biopsy core quality using FCM.

A prospective Institutional Review Board‐approved study was conducted between August and September 2025 including patients undergoing transperineal MRI‐targeted prostate biopsy. Patients with prior prostate treatment and those undergoing systematic biopsy only were excluded. Only targeted biopsy cores were included in the analysis. Of 81 enrolled patients, 50 met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis. Target lesions were identified on pre‐biopsy multiparametric MRI and sampled using MRI–TRUS fusion guidance via a transperineal approach. Immediately after acquisition, each core was immersed in acridine orange solution (Histolog® Dip, SamanTree Medical SA, Lausanne, Switzerland) for ~10 s, then placed on the FCM scanning window (Histolog® Scanner, SamanTree Medical) for image acquisition at 488 nm wavelength, with a tissue imaging depth of ~2 μm, completed within ~60 s per core.

The Co‐Qual scoring was performed in real‐time by a urology trainee following a brief standardised orientation to FCM image interpretation; no formal pathology training was required. The Co‐Qual incorporates three structural components reflecting biopsy core adequacy. Core length was assessed based on the continuous length of intact tissue visualised on FCM imaging and scored as <1, 1–1.5, or >1.5 cm. Fragmentation was assessed by gross inspection at the time of specimen handling and defined as discontinuity of the biopsy core into multiple non‐contiguous segments. The proportion of extraprostatic tissue was estimated relative to the total imaged core surface area based on characteristic FCM appearances of adipose tissue, skeletal muscle fibres, and peripheral nerve structures, which are visually distinct from prostatic glandular and stromal architecture. Representative FCM appearances of intra‐ and extraprostatic tissue components and Co‐Qual scoring examples are shown in Fig. 1. Component scores were summed to generate a total Co‐Qual score ranging from 0 to 5, with higher scores indicating superior biopsy core quality. The Co‐Qual assessment was performed independently of malignancy detection. Patient‐level variables recorded included maximum, minimum, and median Co‐Qual score, and the number of cores achieving the maximum score of 5. All specimens subsequently underwent routine haematoxylin and eosin histopathological evaluation, which served as the reference standard. Clinically significant PCa (csPCa) was defined as Grade Group ≥2. Logistic regression was used to assess associations between Co‐Qual scores and PCa detection, csPCa, and FCM–histopathology concordance; a P < 0.05 was considered significant.

Among the 50 included patients, the median (interquartile range [IQR]) age was 68.5 (61–73) years, the PSA level was 6.79 (4.68–10.10) ng/mL, and prostate volume was 46 (35–65) mL. Prostate Imaging‐Reporting and Data System (PI‐RADS) scores were 3 in 26%, 4 in 44%, and 5 in 30% of patients. The median (IQR) number of targeted cores per patient was 3 (2–3), with a median (IQR) core length of 1.9 (1.6–2.2) cm. At least one core achieving the maximum Co‐Qual score of 5 was obtained in 34 patients (68%). PCa was identified on final histopathology in 33 patients (66%), and csPCa in 17 patients (34%).

On logistic regression analysis, higher median Co‐Qual score was independently associated with PCa detection (odds ratio [OR] 2.38, 95% CI 1.13–6.42; P = 0.047). For csPCa, a greater number of cores achieving a Co‐Qual score of 5 was the strongest independent predictor (OR 4.17, 95% CI 1.41–8.26; P = 0.049). Overall FCM‐histopathology concordance was 90%; the Co‐Qual scores were not significantly associated with concordant detection of PCa (OR 1.56, 95% CI 0.55–4.02; P = 0.334) or csPCa (OR 0.35, 95% CI 0.09–1.99; P = 0.090).

In this study, we introduce a novel classification system designed to assess prostate biopsy core adequacy and quality in real‐time using FCM. Higher Co‐Qual scores were associated with improved detection of PCa, supporting the concept that core quality directly influences diagnostic yield in targeted biopsy procedures. The association between Co‐Qual scores and cancer detection is best interpreted in the setting of targeted transperineal biopsy, where lesions are selected based on pre‐biopsy MRI and the pre‐test probability of malignancy is relatively high. In this context, negative biopsy results more often reflect non‐representative or off‐target sampling rather than true absence of disease [7]. Biopsy cores that are longer, intact, and composed predominantly of intraprostatic tissue are therefore more likely to traverse the intended MRI target rather than adjacent perilesional or extraprostatic structures, and core length has been independently proposed as a quality indicator for transperineal biopsy [8]. The absence of an association between Co‐Qual scores and FCM–histopathology concordance is expected and consistent with Co‐Qual's intended role as a sampling adequacy metric rather than a diagnostic tool: once tissue is acquired, concordance reflects FCM interpretation rather than core physical attributes, and the already high baseline agreement between FCM and histopathology leaves limited room for further improvement. A key clinical application of Co‐Qual lies in identifying cores with substantial extraprostatic tissue, consistent with off‐target sampling, thereby enabling immediate re‐sampling before the patient leaves the procedural setting. The system was intentionally designed to be interpretable by non‐expert readers based on readily identifiable structural features, a design that supports scalability and integration into standard biopsy workflows without specialist pathology input.

This study has important limitations: it is a single‐centre experience with a modest sample size, Co‐Qual scoring was performed by a single reader precluding interobserver assessment, and the system was not prospectively used to alter sampling decisions. Validation in larger, multi‐institutional cohorts, and studies evaluating the real‐time clinical impact of the Co‐Qual on biopsy efficiency and outcomes are warranted.

In summary, the Co‐Qual provides a simple, scalable framework for real‐time prostate biopsy core quality assessment using FCM. Higher Co‐Qual scores are associated with increased detection of PCa and csPCa, supporting its potential role as an intraoperative procedural quality metric that could reduce inadequate sampling and the need for repeat biopsies.
