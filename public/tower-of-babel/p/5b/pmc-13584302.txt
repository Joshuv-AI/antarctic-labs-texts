An AI-augmented review of childhood atopic dermatitis biomarkers across genetic, immune, microbial, and metabolic domains
Authors: Jia Wei Lee, Evelyn X L Loo, Samuel S Chong, Kenneth H K Ban, Caroline G Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAtopic dermatitis (AD) is a prevalent inflammatory skin disease and a major source of disease burden in children. Biomarker studies in childhood AD span genetic, immune, microbial and metabolic domains, but prior reviews have often focused on single molecular layers, specific sample sites or clinical classification. As a result, the field lacks an integrated, systems-level synthesis that compares and contextualizes biomarkers across domains while clearly distinguishing evidence strength. The rapid growth of literature in this field also poses practical challenges for traditional manual review workflows.Main bodyTo address these gaps, we conducted an AI-augmented, multi-domain review of childhood AD biomarkers. ASReview supported title and abstract screening, while ChatGPT assisted structured data extraction with human validation. Across 526 studies, we identified 141 genome, 95 immunome, 57 microbiome and 75 metabolome childhood AD biomarkers. The most frequently reported biomarkers included Filaggrin, IgE, CCL17, Staphylococcus, Bifidobacterium and vitamin D. Using a structured evidence-grading framework, eight biomarkers were categorized as having strong evidence: IgE, CCL17, CCL27, eosinophil cationic protein, eosinophil, IL-18, IL-31 and Escherichia. By synthesizing evidence across biomarker domains, we developed a systems-level, conceptual AD model in which barrier defects, Th2 inflammation, microbial dysbiosis and metabolic imbalance drive a self-perpetuating cycle of inflammation and barrier dysfunction. We also developed a web app for exploration of the biomarker findings: https://leejw.shinyapps.io/eczema_review_526/.ConclusionThis review provides a broad synthesis of childhood AD biomarkers and frames the evidence within an integrated, multi-domain conceptual model. The findings support the rationale for approaches that consider multiple biological nodes, including barrier repair, immune modulation, microbiome-directed strategies and metabolic factors, while underscoring the need for further validation before clinical implementation. Background: childhood AD biomarkers

Atopic dermatitis (AD), or eczema, is a common allergic condition affecting over 170 million individuals globally (Faye et al. 2024), with a significant burden in childhood (Ali et al. 2020). Characterized by chronic inflammation and skin barrier dysfunction (Kim et al. 2019), AD impairs quality of life through chronic itch, psychological stress and sleep disturbances (Fasseeh et al. 2022), and is associated with comorbidities such as asthma, food allergy, anxiety and obesity (Paller et al. 2018; Silverberg 2019). Its multifactorial etiology encompasses genetic predispositions, immune dysregulation, microbial imbalance and metabolic disturbances. Advances in omics technologies and biomarker profiling have uncovered childhood AD biomarkers across these domains, offering the potential for risk identification, monitoring, and precision therapy (Broderick et al. 2023; Bieber et al. 2017).

Previous reviews have synthesized valuable insights on AD biomarkers, but most were limited to a single biomolecular domain—such as metabolomics, skin microbiome or microRNA (Yu et al. 2020; Niemeyer‐van der et al 2018; Martin et al. 2020; Upadhyay et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2023; Vaughn et al. 2019), or restricted to specific sample sites, such as serum and plasma biomarkers (Thijs et al. 2015) or skin biomarkers (Hui-Beckman et al. 2025). Broader reviews have also summarized AD biomarkers from multiple omics-related domains (Park et al. 2024; Indolfi et al. 2025; Yu and Li 2022; Thijs et al. 2017; Renert-Yuval et al. 2021; Bakker et al. 2023; Libon et al. 2024), but they primarily organize biomarkers by clinical application, such as diagnosis, prognosis, or severity. In contrast, this review adopts a multi-domain perspective, comparing biomarker evidence across genetic, immune, microbial and metabolic layers to identify convergent pathways and broader biological insights.

To our knowledge, this study is among the largest multi-domain synthesis of childhood AD biomarkers, drawing on evidence from 526 studies. We mapped biomarkers across multiple molecular domains and sample sites, incorporated standardized genomic annotations to evaluate variant effects and functional relevance and applied structured evidence grading to distinguish stronger from more exploratory findings. To manage the volume of literature, we employed an AI-augmented review pipeline that combined active learning (ASReview) with large language model-assisted extraction (ChatGPT), followed by human validation. Importantly, we synthesize biomarker evidence across multiple domains into an evidence-informed AD model that highlights biological pathways and clinically relevant insights.

Methodology: AI-assisted review pipeline

Overview of methodology

We leveraged a hybrid AI-human pipeline to conduct the review, while adhering to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines for study selection (Page et al. 2021). The overall methodology is summarized in Fig. 1.

Overall methodology of our review study. Firstly, screening of papers was conducted, which included systematic searches on PubMed and Web of Science, title and abstract screening using ASReview, and manual full-text screening. Next, data extraction was carried out, which comprised iterative prompt engineering, data extraction of the 526 included studies using ChatGPT, and manual validation and re-extraction of ChatGPT results. Subsequently, variant annotation was conducted, followed by functional analysis of biomarkers using ConsensusPathDB, MicrobiomeAnalyst and MetaboAnalyst. A structured evidence grading framework was implemented, where biomarkers were categorized as having strong, moderate or limited evidence. Lastly, an R Shiny app was developed to facilitate the querying of relevant biomarkers

Literature search

Systematic literature searches were conducted on PubMed and Web of Science to identify studies of omics-related biomarkers in childhood AD from database inception to 11 November 2024. Search terms relating to atopic dermatitis, childhood and omics-based biomarkers, including wildcard searches were included (Additional File 2: Text S1). The PubMed search yielded 9737 records, while 5164 records were obtained from the Web of Science.

Title and abstract screening

Title and abstract screening of the articles was conducted using ASReview, a machine learning (ML) tool that leverages active learning to prioritize relevant studies, allowing reviews to be conducted efficiently and transparently (Schoot et al. 2021). Other studies have also utilized ASReview to conduct screening (van Dijk et al. 2024; Ito et al. 2024; Warren and Moustafa 2023).

Following deduplication which identified 3,050 unique records from the Web of Science, the PubMed and Web of Science records were downloaded into ASReview. As prior knowledge for the ML model, a few randomly selected papers were screened to generate the preliminary rankings. As more papers were screened manually, the rankings of the papers were updated accordingly. Default model parameters were used: Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency Feature Extraction, Naive Bayes Classifier, Maximum Query Strategy and Dynamic Resampling Balance Strategy. In this review, we focused on biomarkers associated with AD onset, presence, or severity in childhood.

Following the criteria for title and abstract screening (Additional File 2: Text S2), the papers were screened in ASReview until the snowball threshold of ten consecutive irrelevant articles was reached. This pragmatic stopping heuristic of ten was predetermined before screening and also adopted by another study (Ito et al. 2024). Screening using ASReview identified 566 relevant records from PubMed and 243 Web of Science records after reaching the snowball threshold of 10. This threshold was considered pragmatic because more than 10% of the papers (1132 out of 9737 for PubMed and 443 out of 3050 for Web of Science) have been manually screened when it was reached. Previous work reported that ASReview identified at least 70% of relevant papers after manual screening of 10% of the records (Schoot et al. 2021). In this study, ASReview was used to improve screening efficiency relative to conventional manual screening (Additional File 1: Figure S1), while final inclusion decisions remain reviewer-led.

Full-text screening

Subsequently, manual full-text screening was conducted following the inclusion criteria as detailed in Additional File 2: Text S2. Full-text screening resulted in a final set of 526 included papers. The PRISMA diagram is shown in Fig. 2.

Validation of study inclusion

To assess whether key publications were captured, a reference set of 10 well-established studies on childhood atopic dermatitis biomarkers was compiled (Additional File 3: Table S1). These studies were identified from recent high-quality review articles and highly cited primary studies based on Google Scholar searches. All 10 studies were present among the final 526 included studies, increasing confidence that landmark studies were retained, although this does not rule out missing eligible studies.

Data extraction

From the 526 studies, data extraction was conducted using ChatGPT. An initial ChatGPT prompt was drafted following best practices for prompt engineering such as role prompting, few-shot prompting, and adopting a clear five-part structure (role, goal, task, context and output). Next, iterative prompt engineering was carried out by iterating and improving on the initial ChatGPT prompt by testing on five representative papers, which were selected from the pool of relevant studies. The steps of the iterative prompt engineering process are illustrated in Additional File 1: Figure S2.

During the process of iterative prompt engineering, some of the problems included multiple biomarkers displayed in one row and that not all biomarkers were extracted. To address these issues, we edited the ChatGPT prompt to specify our requirements more explicitly. The examples of specific problems encountered with the AI-assisted data extraction and prompt modifications to address them are detailed in Additional File 3: Table S2.

After multiple iterations, the best ChatGPT output obtained is shown in Additional File 3: Table S3, which compared the results of ChatGPT data extraction with that obtained from a human reviewer (JWL) as the ground truth. Table S4 (Additional File 3) illustrates that using ChatGPT for data extraction for the five studies yielded reasonable accuracy. As these were the best results obtained, the prompt used for that iteration was determined as the final prompt to be used for data extraction (Additional File 2: Text S3). For data extraction, the papers were uploaded to ChatGPT ten at a time using the prompt finalized in the previous step. If there are obvious errors, such as more than one biomarker fields in a row, or incomplete data extraction, a specific follow-up prompt was given to correct the error.

After data extraction was completed, ten additional studies were validated by a human reviewer (JWL) (Additional File 3: Table S5), and the ChatGPT results were reasonably accurate overall (Additional File 3: Table S6). However, the effect size and relationship with AD showed lower accuracies (58·8% and 64·7% respectively). Therefore, we manually extracted and validated the direction of association and effect size fields for the ChatGPT-extracted biomarkers for all 526 studies to improve reliability of the final dataset. To facilitate downstream data visualization and analysis, the data extraction results of the 526 studies were cleaned and recoded, yielding the final table of results (Additional File 3: Table S7).

Additionally, to ensure accuracy of data extraction, we performed double manual extraction on a representative sample of 40 biomarker entries. Data extraction was conducted independently by two reviewers (JWL and CL), and their extracted results were shown to be fully concordant (Additional File 3: Table S8). This verification supports the accuracy of our reported biomarker findings. Overall, this AI-augmented review pipeline combining iterative prompt engineering with human validation facilitated efficient data extraction while improving the transparency and reliability of the synthesized outputs.

Variant annotation and pathway analyses

To extend the review beyond a catalogue of reported biomarkers, Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor (VEP) (McLaren et al. 2016) and RegulomeDB (Dong et al. 2023) were used for variant annotation, enabling a standardized assessment of variant impact and potential functionalities of AD-associated genomic biomarkers across 526 studies. For immunome, microbiome and metabolome biomarkers, pathway analyses and targeted literature searches were performed to contextualize their functional roles in AD.

Structured evidence grading

To assess quality of evidence, a structured evidence-grading framework was implemented to evaluate the strength of evidence for each biomarker. Biomarkers were evaluated based on the study design, number of supporting studies and consistency of findings, and were categorized as having strong, moderate, or limited evidence. The criteria for evidence grading are presented in Additional File 3: Table S9. Each of the three domains was scored from 0 to 2, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 6. Based on the cumulative score, biomarkers were categorized as having strong (6), moderate (3–5), or limited (0–2) evidence.

Immunome biomarkers

A total of 95 distinct AD-related immunome biomarkers were identified (Additional File 4: Table 1), underscoring the central role of immune dysregulation in AD (Boguniewicz and Leung 2011). The immunome biomarkers identified include soluble immune mediators such as IL-4, IL-5, IL-13, and CCL17 as well as immune cells such as eosinophils, neutrophils, and T lymphocytes. The structured evidence grading results are presented in Additional File 3: Table S10, with seven biomarkers categorized as demonstrating strong evidence: IgE, CCL17, CCL27, eosinophil cationic protein, eosinophil, IL-18 and IL-31. Functional mapping based on literature search revealed that the biomarkers can be broadly categorized into Th2 immune response, immune regulation, chronicity or barrier dysfunction, pruritus or neuro-immune crosstalk, and disease initiation.

Among the 95 distinct immunome biomarkers, close to half (46·3%) represented Th2 immune response, reinforcing the canonical view of Th2 dominance as the immunologic hallmark of AD (Brandt et al. 2011). From Fig. 4a, Table 1 and Table S10, IgE and CCL17 are the most frequently reported immunome biomarkers often elevated in AD and are categorized as having strong evidence. They are both involved in Th2 immune response, reinforcing that AD is often characterized by the dysregulation of a Th2-skewed immune response that drives and maintains disease activity (Brandt et al. 2011; Nakazato et al. 2008). These biomarkers are potentially clinically relevant, as IgE is commonly used for AD assessment and monitoring (Carr et al. 2024; Jeskey et al. 2024), while CCL17 is the target of emerging biologics such as dupilumab and GBR 830 (Qi and Li 2021; Beck et al. 2025; Guttman-Yassky et al. 2019).

Apart from Th2 immune response, markers of immune regulation comprised 25·3% of the total, encompassing IL-16, CCL25 and IgA, illustrating the involvement of immune tolerance and regulation in AD progression. 16·8% of biomarkers were associated with AD chronicity, epidermal hyperplasia, and barrier dysfunction, such as IL-22, IL-17 and interferons. This highlights that chronic AD extends beyond Th2 immune response to involve skin barrier remodeling and persistent epithelial-immune interactions.

Our review also identified emerging biomarkers present in smaller proportions. 7·37% of the biomarkers reflect pruritus or neuro-immune crosstalk, including IL-31, periostin and BDNF. Although less frequently reported, IL-31 is assessed to have strong evidence. These molecules could be relevant to chronic itch and sleep disturbances in AD, warranting further study as an underexplored dimension of pediatric AD. Another 4·21% of biomarkers relate to disease initiation, comprising TSLP, IL-33, and IL-25, which are pivotal in triggering Th2 activation and sensitization.

To gain deeper insights into biological pathways of genes associated with the immunome biomarkers, over-representation analysis was conducted using ConsensusPathDB (Kamburov et al 2011). Eligible biomarkers were mapped to corresponding HUGO Gene Nomenclature Committee (HGNC) entries. The enriched pathways (q-value < 0.0001) clustered around immune activation and response, cell signaling, immune cell migration and chemotaxis, response to stimuli, and development, regulation and cell fate (Fig. 4b). These pathways are biologically relevant, as AD is characterized by altered immune responses (David Boothe et al. 2017) and dysregulated cell signaling (Timms et al. 2022). Collectively, these findings broaden the classical Th2-dominant paradigm, revealing a multi-dimensional immunological landscape in childhood AD, where Th2-driven immune response intersects with neuroimmune and barrier-associated pathways.

Genome biomarkers

A total of 141 distinct genomic biomarkers were identified, with functional annotations retrieved from Ensembl VEP and RegulomeDB (Additional File 4: Table 2). The strength of evidence of the biomarkers ranged from moderate to limited (Additional File 3: Table S11). Filaggrin (FLG) is the most reported genomic biomarker, specifically the variants R501X, 2282del4, R2447X and S3247X (Fig. 5a and Additional File 4: Table 2) and categorized to have moderate evidence. Filaggrin is not only most frequently reported in the literature but also one of the most functionally significant. Among the 59 Filaggrin variants (FLG/FLG2), all but one are located within exon regions. A substantial proportion of these variants are predicted to have deleterious effects, including premature termination of translation (49·2%), frameshift mutations (15·3%) and missense mutations (45·8%), leading to disruptions in protein function. Notably, among variants with available VEP clinical significance information, many are further classified as pathogenic or likely pathogenic (66·7%), supporting the functional relevance of Filaggrin variants in AD. These findings are consistent with other studies showing that Filaggrin loss-of-function mutations are critical risk factors for AD (O'Regan et al. 2008; Cabanillas and Novak 2016; Drislane and Irvine 2020), likely through impaired skin barrier integrity, facilitating allergen and microbial penetration (Kim et al. 2019; Irvine et al. 2011).

Beyond the canonical FLG and FLG2 loci, our review (Additional File 4: Table 2) also highlighted less-studied genes such as IL10, IL13 and TSLP, which are assessed to have moderate evidence. Among non-Filaggrin genetic variants, none of them were predicted to result in premature termination of translation or frameshift mutations, with only 17·1% classified as missense mutations and 1 variant categorized as likely pathogenic. This indicates that Filaggrin variants could play a central role in AD, while other genes may exert more modulatory effects. This interpretation is supported by RegulomeDB data: among non-Filaggrin variants with transcriptional binding information, 74·3% of the variants overlapped with ChIPseq peaks, indicating their localization within transcription factor binding sites, and 84·3% of them were situated within chromatin accessibility peaks, suggesting their presence in open chromatin regions. These variants may therefore influence gene regulation and warrant future investigation.

Similarly, we performed over-representation analysis on the genomic biomarkers, identifying enriched pathways (q-value < 0.0001) related to immune activation and response, response to stimuli, and development, cell fate and homeostasis (Fig. 5b). Notably, genome and immunome over-representation networks revealed striking convergence on AD-related pathways such as immune response (Eyerich and Novak 2013; Hülpüsch et al. 2021) and response to stimuli (Eyerich and Novak 2013; Tokura et al. 2025). This convergence supports the biological relevance of the identified biomarkers and suggests shared pathways across genetic and immune domains.

Microbiome biomarkers

A total of 57 different microbiome biomarkers were identified (Additional File 4: Table 3), highlighting the emerging relevance of the skin and gut microbiome in modulating pediatric AD. Based on the structured evidence grading, the strength of evidence of most microbiome biomarkers ranged from moderate to limited, with only one biomarker (Escherichia) categorized as having strong evidence (Additional File 3: Table S12). The identified taxa were categorized into three functional groups: potentially protective (associated with barrier integrity or immune regulation), potentially pathogenic (associated with inflammation or barrier disruption), and taxa whose relevance to AD remains unclear or context-dependent.

The potentially pathogenic microbial group (21·1%) comprised taxa such as Staphylococcus, Escherichia and Streptococcus. These taxa have been linked to barrier disruption, inflammation and increased AD severity. Staphylococcus is the most reported microbiome biomarker (Fig. 6a, Additional File 4: Table 3), consistent with established evidence implicating Staphylococcus aureus in AD. Through the production of virulence factors, Staphylococcus aureus exacerbates inflammation and disrupts skin barrier integrity, aggravating disease severity (Kim et al. 2019). Escherichia is categorized as having strong evidence, and this is corroborated with reports suggesting how Escherichia coli could exacerbate immune dysregulation in AD and increase intestinal permeability, thus facilitating the entry of antigens and subsequent allergic sensitization (Penders et al. 2007).

The potentially protective microbial group (38·6%) comprised genera such as Bifidobacterium, Ruminococcus, Akkermansia and Lactobacillus, which were also assessed as having moderate evidence. These taxa include commensals with barrier-enhancing and immunomodulatory functions and are generally associated with reduced AD severity. Among them, Bifidobacterium is the most frequently studied. The prominence of Bifidobacterium stems from its capacity to exert anti-inflammatory effects and enhance skin barrier integrity, thereby alleviating AD symptoms (Kim et al. 2022; Zhao et al. 2025). Recent findings further highlight Bifidobacterium’s promising potential as a probiotic for the treatment of AD (Kang et al. 2024; Andrade et al. 2022), offering new avenues for improved prevention and management of the disease.

A third group (unclear role in AD, 40·4%) included taxa such as Clostridium, Bacteroides and Prevotella, which demonstrate moderate evidence and are variably associated with AD based on current literature. Although the literature presents seemingly contradictory findings, these taxa may in fact function in an opportunistic or context-dependent manner. For instance, in an experiment where Corynebacterium was applied to the skin of mice, the treatment elicited noninflammatory effects in controls, whereas in mice fed a high-fat diet, it triggered IL-23-dependent inflammation and genetic upregulation (Ridaura et al. 2018). Such evidence supports a contextual model of microbiome dysbiosis, where shifts in host metabolic status and immune states influence whether specific taxa exert protective or pathogenic effects.

To uncover potential associations between microbiome biomarkers and host-intrinsic factors, we performed Taxon Set Analysis using MicrobiomeAnalyst (Lu et al. 2023). The analysis revealed associations between the identified microbiome biomarkers and a range of diseases, including allergies, gastrointestinal disorders and metabolic diseases (Fig. 6b). These findings align with existing research linking allergic diseases with gastrointestinal (Powell et al. 2007; Sjölund et al. 2021) and metabolic conditions (Hersoug and Linneberg 2007; Kusunoki et al. 2008; Klamt et al. 2015). The overlap of these diseases provides biological context for the microbiome findings and supports further investigation of microbiome-mediated immune and metabolic pathways in AD.

Overall, the pediatric AD microbiome is characterized by the reduction of commensals, expansion of pathogenic taxa, and alteration of context-dependent biomarkers. Given the key role of microbial dysbiosis in AD, these findings support the further investigation of microbiome-targeted interventions, including bacteriotherapy or probiotics, which are entering clinical trials (Andrade et al. 2022; Nakatsuji et al. 2021).

Metabolome biomarkers

A total of 75 distinct metabolome biomarkers were identified (Additional File 4: Table 4), with evidence strength ranging from moderate to limited (Additional File 3: Table S13). These biomarkers were grouped into four interlinked biochemical categories: nutrients with immunomodulatory or antioxidant roles, lipids/metabolites involved in inflammation and immune response, lipids involved in skin barrier function and environmental exposures. Together, these categories suggest that metabolic and environmental factors may collectively influence AD onset and persistence.

The nutrients category (30·7%) encompasses biomarkers such as vitamin D, retinol and vitamin E, which can modulate oxidative balance and immune tolerance, suggesting that antioxidant sufficiency could mitigate immune dysregulation and barrier dysfunction in AD (Kvedariene et al. 2025). Vitamin D emerged as the most consistently reported AD metabolome biomarker (Fig. 7a and Additional File 4: Table 4) and was categorized as having moderate evidence. Existing literature indicates that 25-hydroxyvitamin D3 can attenuate Th17-mediated inflammation and promote the restoration of skin barrier integrity (Li and Chan 2025). Vitamin D supplementation has also been evaluated as a potential therapeutic strategy for AD (Li and Chan 2025; Nielsen et al. 2024).

Another key category comprises lipids or metabolites involved in inflammation and immune response (30·7%), such as arachidonic acid and docosapentaenoic acid (both showing moderate evidence), underscoring a systemic pro-inflammatory milieu in pediatric AD. Complementing this, 9·33% of biomarkers were categorized as lipids involved in skin barrier function, such as gamma-linolenic acid and linoleic acid. Environmental exposures such as perfluorooctanoic acid and cotinine were also identified (29·3%), highlighting the contribution of the exposome in modulating AD.

To explore the functional implications of these metabolome biomarkers, we conducted enrichment analysis using MetaboAnalyst (Pang et al 2024). The top enriched pathways included lactose synthesis, degradation of superoxides and alpha linolenic acid and linoleic acid metabolism (Fig. 7b). These findings are consistent with existing literature highlighting the key role that oxidative stress plays in atopic dermatitis, particularly in terms of increased oxidative stress and decreased antioxidant defenses during AD exacerbations, worsening barrier dysfunction and immune dysregulation (Ji and Li 2016). Previous studies have also reported altered linoleic acid concentrations in the blood, milk and adipose tissue of eczema patients, suggesting that impaired linoleic acid metabolism may increase eczema risk (Horrobin 2000). This underscores the importance of lipid metabolic pathways in the disease process and highlights how metabolic dysregulation contributes to AD.

Collectively, our findings suggest that antioxidants, lipids, metabolites and the exposome interact with pathways regulating skin barrier integrity and inflammation, consistent with literature linking systemic metabolism to immune regulation and skin barrier function (Grieco et al. 2025; Lu et al. 2023; Olejnik et al. 2023). Thus, multidimensional profiling of the metabolome may help inform future risk prediction, disease monitoring and precision treatment strategies in childhood AD, pending further validation.

Biomarkers across sample sites

A stratified analysis of the identified biomarkers was conducted based on sample type. Immunome, genome, microbiome and metabolome biomarkers from the three most common sample sites (blood, skin and stool) were compared (Additional File 3: Table S14). Blood was the most common sample site, with blood-derived markers predominantly originating from the immunome (n = 481), followed by the genome (n = 192) and the metabolome (n = 99). No microbiome biomarkers were identified in blood samples. In contrast, skin samples were characterized primarily by microbiome biomarkers (n = 68), alongside a smaller number of immunome (n = 25), metabolome (n = 24) and genome (n = 11) markers. For stool samples, biomarkers were predominantly microbiome-based (n = 117), which is consistent with the widespread use of stool for studying the gut microbiome. Only a small number of metabolome biomarkers (n = 2) were identified, with no genome or immunome markers reported. Overall, the distribution of omics-based biomarkers differed substantially across sample sites (Fig. 8), suggesting that future cohort studies may benefit from collecting multiple sample types for more complete biomarker profiling.

Integrated systems-level AD model

Synthesizing key biomarkers across domains, we propose an evidence-informed, systems-level conceptual model of AD in which barrier defects, Th2 inflammation, microbial dysbiosis, and metabolic imbalance may reinforce one another (Fig. 9). The AD model is based on the aggregation and interpretation of our biomarker findings, together with established concepts such as the “inside-out” and “outside-in” frameworks (Elias and Steinhoff 2008; Patrick et al. 2021), previously-described feedback loops between skin barrier dysfunction and inflammation (Tsakok et al. 2019; Nakajima et al. 2024), and prior multi-layered omics perspectives (Ghosh et al. 2018). It should be interpreted as a conceptual synthesis rather than a causal model or formal multi-omics analysis. By synthesizing insights across immune, genetic, microbial, and metabolic domains, we propose that key biomarkers across omics reinforce one another, driving a self-perpetuating cycle of inflammation and barrier dysfunction.

Barrier defect initiation

An inherited filaggrin (FLG) loss-of-function mutation or other barrier defect can increase penetration of allergens and microbes, which interacts with antigen-presenting cells (Osawa et al. 2011). This “outside-in” disruption can initiate aberrant immune sensing, priming Th2-skewed inflammation.

Th2 immune activation

Allergen exposure in a genetically predisposed individual can trigger immune cells to induce a Th2-dominated inflammatory response in part through the production of CCL17 (Lamiable et al. 2020; Salazar and Ghaemmaghami 2013; Vestergaard et al. 2004; Lupancu 2023). IL-4 and IL-13 released from Th2 cells promote IgE production (Metwally et al. 2004) and impair the epidermal barrier by suppressing filaggrin expression (Torres et al. 2025). This “inside-out” provides a mechanism by which immune activation may exacerbate the original skin barrier defect.

Microbial amplification

A weakened skin barrier and Th2-dominant environment can promote colonization by Staphylococcus aureus (Alexander et al. 2020). Its cytolytic toxins, superantigens and virulence factors enhance T cells cytokine secretion and degrade junctional adhesion molecules, thereby intensifying both inflammation and skin barrier breakdown (Humeau et al. 2022).

Gut-skin axis

Gut microbiome dysbiosis dominated by S. aureus has been associated with increased intestinal permeability, which leads to more microorganisms and toxins entering circulation, thus aggravating the skin (Sadowsky et al. 2023; Kim et al. 2019). In addition, altered levels of butyrate-producing gut bacteria such as Bifidobacterium may influence the proliferation and activation of Treg cells, enhancing skin inflammation and providing a plausible gut-skin axis through which microbial imbalance could contribute to chronic AD (Sadowsky et al. 2023; Coppola et al. 2022).

Metabolic modulation

Gut dysbiosis may alter metabolite levels, such as affecting the expression of vitamin D receptor (Akimbekov et al. 2020). Nutrient deficiencies and fatty acid imbalances can in turn modulate immune function and barrier resilience. For instance, low vitamin D may impair antimicrobial peptide production which aggravates S. aureus skin infections (Youssef et al. 2011), while excess arachidonic acid may promote pro-inflammatory eicosanoid synthesis (Wang et al. 2021). These metabolic modulators act as exacerbating factors that worsen the core genetic-immune-microbial feedback loop in AD.

The result of these interconnected processes is the characteristic phenotype of AD: a patient with dry, cracked skin colonized by S. aureus, heightened Th2 immunity with high IgE, and chronic itch. Inflammation further erodes the skin barrier, while scratching (provoked by itch) causes mechanical injury that promotes microbial entry and allergen exposure, releasing alarmins that enhance inflammation (Beck et al. 2022). This reinforcing loop offers a plausible explanation for why AD can become self-perpetuating without adequate control. In this model, AD arises from interacting immune, genetic, microbial and metabolic disturbances rather than a single linear pathway. A filaggrin-impaired barrier invites allergen and microbe intrusion; the ensuing Th2 immune response damages the barrier further and accommodates microbial persistence; dysbiosis and nutrient imbalances intensify the immune deviation.

Understanding the multidimensional mechanisms of AD suggests that a holistic approach could be required for effective intervention. Addressing barrier defects could include emollient use and wet wrap therapy to repair the skin barrier and reduce microbe entry (Simpson et al. 2014; Nicol et al. 2014), while targeted biologic drugs such as anti-IL-4Rα monoclonal antibodies could reduce Th2 inflammation and ameliorate AD symptoms (Haddad et al. 2022). There is also a potential to tackle microbial dysbiosis through using probiotics or bacteriotherapy to increase beneficial flora (Andrade et al. 2022; Nakatsuji et al. 2021), and address metabolic imbalances through the consumption of nutraceuticals (Nielsen et al. 2024; Rios-Carlos et al. 2024). This integrated perspective suggests that durable disease control may require multi-pronged interventions combining barrier repair, immune modulation, microbiome restoration, and metabolic correction, providing a conceptual roadmap for designing next-generation therapeutic strategies and precision medicine approaches in AD.

Functional insights: variant annotation and web tool

A key methodological innovation in this study was the systematic variant annotation and functional classification. Because AD biomarker studies often report variants using heterogeneous nomenclature, cross-study synthesis can be difficult. By annotating reported variants based on VEP and RegulomeDB, we conducted a standardized assessment of variant impact and potential functionalities of AD-associated variants across 526 studies, revealing deeper insights into the functional roles of these AD biomarkers. For other biomarker domains, pathway analyses and targeted literature searches were performed to glean insights into the functional characteristics of the identified AD biomarkers. Together, these steps reveal deeper insights into the functional effects of these biomarkers and their roles in AD.

To bridge discovery with usability, we have developed a publicly accessible web app to query AD biomarker findings. It can be accessed via: https://leejw.shinyapps.io/eczema_review_526/. This tool demonstrates the concept of an interactive review, with the potential to be extended in future to incorporate dynamic updates as new findings emerge.

Scalable approach: AI-augmented reviews

Our review also illustrates the practical use of AI tools in conducting large-scale evidence synthesis. By combining ASReview and ChatGPT with human oversight, we were able to complete a large-scale review of more than 500 studies efficiently, while retaining reviewer control over study inclusion and key data fields. For screening, ASReview was used to prioritize relevant studies, but ultimately, it is the reviewer who decides on the study to be included, ensuring that all our included studies have been manually verified. For data extraction, ChatGPT was used to extract data from the included papers, but this was accompanied by random manual validation, as well as full manual extraction and validation of the direction of association and effect size fields by a human reviewer, with a subset of studies independently validated by a second human reviewer. The process also illustrates the importance of detailed prompt engineering when using large language models (LLMs). This workflow may serve as a framework for other research teams seeking to use AI to augment human-led reviews.

Limitations and future work

While the findings of our study are promising, limitations remain. Firstly, our search was restricted to PubMed and the Web of Science, which may have resulted in missed studies indexed exclusively in databases such as Embase or Scopus. Thus, future reviews should incorporate multi-database search strategies to ensure broader coverage.

Furthermore, the stopping criterion of 10 used for ASReview screening in this study may not guarantee complete recall. For future work, screening could be guided by a formal stopping criterion based on estimated recall rather than a fixed heuristic threshold, and this could be supplemented by backward and forward citation tracking to strengthen confidence in study inclusion.

Our AI-augmented review pipeline complements conventional evidence synthesis by assisting with prioritization and extraction, but it still requires human validation for critical fields. Manual validation confirmed that there are still notable flaws in ChatGPT’s accuracy in data extraction, especially in terms of the effect size, which is an area for improvement for future AI tools. While the critical variables in the biomarker table such as the effect size and direction of association have been manually re-extracted and validated, some inaccuracies may remain in other AI-extracted fields, and there could be other issues such as duplicate entries or missing biomarkers not extracted. Thus, future work could explore ensemble extraction workflows across multiple LLMs to improve accuracy.

Another limitation is that we did not formally benchmark the efficiency and accuracy of the AI-assisted workflow against conventional systematic review methods. Future studies could compare AI-assisted and traditional workflows using standardized metrics such as recall, precision, screening time and error rates. Future efforts should also explore federated data integration to enable real-time evidence updates, allowing the review pipeline to automatically incorporate newly published studies as they emerge.

In this review, the metabolome coverage was less detailed as compared to other biomarker domains. This partly reflects the complexity of lipid biomarkers, including diverse structures, inconsistent nomenclature, and variable annotation and reporting practices across studies. As a result, extraction and synthesis of lipidomic data were more challenging. Future studies should prioritize standardized and granular reporting of lipidomic data to enable a more complete synthesis of metabolomic biomarker evidence for childhood AD.

Furthermore, there is substantial heterogeneity across included studies, including differences in study design, population characteristics, diagnostic criteria (e.g. SCORAD versus ISAAC), sample types, and analytical methods. These variations could contribute to the inconsistencies in reported biomarker findings and limit comparability across studies. Thus, future studies incorporating standardized reporting would be valuable to enable more detailed comparative analyses.

Although this review spans multiple domains, it does not perform formal multi-omics integration because there are limited studies which have analyzed multiple biological layers in the same cohort. This fragmentation is a central limitation of the current evidence base. Future longitudinal cohort studies with matched genome, immunome, microbiome and metabolome data will be needed to move beyond associative synthesis towards systems-level and predictive models. Additionally, our evidence-informed AD model remains largely conceptual and will require validation through network-based analyses, causal inference and multimodal ML algorithms, enabling the identification of key molecular interactions across omics.

Overall, most identified biomarkers showed moderate to limited evidence. These biomarker findings remain largely exploratory, and substantial challenges remain in translating these findings into clinical practice. Key barriers include lack of assay standardization, heterogeneity across study populations, limited independent validation, insufficient reporting of diagnostic performance metrics and regulatory considerations. Translating biomarker discoveries into clinical applications such as diagnostic panels would require experimental validation, assessment across diverse cohorts, harmonized reporting of biomarker findings, and integration into clinical workflows.

Conclusion

Overall, our study presents a large-scale review of childhood AD biomarkers across genetic, immune, microbial and metabolic domains. Eight biomarkers were categorized as having strong evidence: IgE, CCL17, CCL27, eosinophil cationic protein, eosinophil, IL-18, IL-31 and Escherichia. Synthesizing findings across biomolecular layers, we propose an evidence-informed conceptual model of AD in which genetic barrier defects, Th2 inflammation, microbial dysbiosis, and metabolic imbalance may reinforce a self-perpetuating loop of inflammation and barrier dysfunction. Beyond summarizing AD biomarkers, the study demonstrates how AI tools can support large-scale evidence synthesis when paired with human validation. As the scale of biomedical literature continues to expand exponentially, such hybrid workflows may help to generate timely, reproducible and clinically relevant syntheses. Overall, this review elucidates the multi-domain biomolecular insights underlying childhood AD. The resulting biomarker landscape and conceptual model provide a foundation for future validation studies and the development of more precise, multi-node approaches to childhood AD.
