Comparative effects of unilateral versus bilateral training on performance adaptations: a systematic review and multilevel meta-analysis
Authors: Xianyang Xin, Qinlong Liu, Yongqing Guo, Pengfei Wang, Xiupeng Li, Zhongxu Hu, Bitai Wu, Qinzheng Liu, Zhihao Qi, Jiu Chen, Zhe Kong, Li Che, Jun Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study systematically compared the effects of unilateral training and bilateral training on strength, jump performance, muscle hypertrophy, sprint performance, and change-of-direction performance, and further explored potential moderators. A systematic search was conducted in PubMed, Web of Science Core Collection, the Cochrane Library, Embase, and EBSCO. Hedges’ g was used as the effect size, and a three-level meta-analysis was performed under random-effects models. Subgroup analyses, meta-regression, and sensitivity analyses were conducted to explore potential moderators and examine the robustness of the findings. A total of 24 randomized controlled trials were included. Compared with bilateral training, unilateral training showed significant advantages for unilateral strength (g = 0.42, 95% CI 0.23 to 0.60, p < 0.001) and unilateral jump performance (g = 0.35, 95% CI 0.14 to 0.56, p = 0.002). In contrast, bilateral training showed a small but significant advantage for bilateral strength (g = -0.28, 95% CI -0.54 to -0.02, p = 0.034). No significant between-group differences were observed for muscle hypertrophy, bilateral jump performance, sprint performance, or change-of-direction performance. Subgroup analyses did not identify significant between-subgroup differences for unilateral jump performance or change-of-direction performance. Meta-regression did not identify any significant moderators. Unilateral training appears to be more effective for improving unilateral strength and unilateral jump performance, whereas bilateral training may be more beneficial for enhancing bilateral strength. The choice between unilateral training and bilateral training should therefore be guided by specific training goals and sport-specific movement characteristics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13102-026-01834-2.

Introduction

Existing evidence generally suggests that, to maximize athletic performance, athletes should be exposed to training stimuli that not only comply with the principle of specificity but also resemble the physiological and mechanical demands of competition [1–3]. For example, movement velocity and force direction are regarded as key variables that coaches should consider when designing training programs [4, 5]. In recent years, another variable—the unilateral or bilateral mode of exercise execution—has also attracted considerable attention regarding its relative effects on athletic performance enhancement [6–8]. In this review, unilateral training refers to exercises performed predominantly with one limb at a time, whereas bilateral training refers to exercises in which both limbs act simultaneously to produce force [7, 9]. The outcomes examined in this review are closely related to key determinants of athletic performance. Strength and muscle hypertrophy provide the neuromuscular and morphological basis for force production, whereas sprint, jump, and change-of-direction performance reflect acceleration, explosive power, and rapid movement reorientation, respectively. These abilities are frequently required in many competitive sports, particularly during actions such as sprinting, jumping, cutting, landing, kicking, and sport-specific transitions [10–14]. Therefore, clarifying whether unilateral or bilateral training produces different adaptations in these outcome domains has practical relevance for training prescription.

Traditionally, bilateral exercises, such as squats, bilateral knee extension, and double-leg drop jumps, have been widely used as primary training methods to improve athletic ability, given their established role in enhancing strength and power, as well as their potential relevance to balance-related performance [1, 15–18]. In contrast, the corresponding unilateral exercises have more often been regarded as supplementary strategies, commonly used to reduce limb asymmetries, support rehabilitation, or complement other training modalities [7, 19, 20]. However, many sport-specific actions involve unilateral support, braking, or propulsion rather than purely bilateral simultaneous force production [10–14]. From the perspective of training specificity and transfer to sport-specific tasks [21], unilateral training may therefore elicit adaptations that are relevant to these movement demands, although whether these adaptations translate into superior performance outcomes remains unclear [1, 22]. Accordingly, the potential value of unilateral training for athletic performance enhancement has received increasing attention, prompting a growing number of comparative studies. As this line of research has progressed, some scholars have proposed that unilateral training may induce distinct adaptations under certain conditions [23, 24]. One commonly discussed explanation is the bilateral deficit, whereby the summed output produced by each limb separately may exceed that produced when both limbs act simultaneously [6, 25]. However, this mechanism is more likely to explain strength-related adaptations, particularly unilateral force production, rather than all performance outcomes. On this basis, some studies have suggested that unilateral training may be more effective than bilateral training for selected performance outcomes, although this finding has not been consistently observed across all outcomes.

Nevertheless, the differences between unilateral and bilateral training are not limited to movement pattern alone [26], but also extend to biomechanical characteristics [20], postural stability [27], the force–velocity relationship [28], psychological state [29], and lumbar loading [20]. For example, unilateral exercises may place greater demands on postural control and joint stabilization, whereas bilateral exercises may permit higher absolute loads and produce different force–velocity characteristics. Therefore, the adaptive mechanisms and transfer effects of these two training modes may not be the same. Based on the currently available evidence, there is still no consensus regarding which performance outcomes are more favorably improved by unilateral training or bilateral training [30–35]. For coaches and practitioners, the choice between these two modes therefore remains controversial.

Some studies have combined multiple performance-related outcomes into a single composite outcome and reported no significant differences between unilateral training and bilateral training [8, 36]. As the number of studies has increased, some researchers have begun to distinguish specific outcome domains and to examine the differential effects of unilateral training and bilateral training on strength, sprint performance, jump performance, change-of-direction performance, and muscle hypertrophy. However, the findings remain inconsistent [9, 17]. For example, regarding strength development, the meta-analysis by Zhang et al. [17] indicated that unilateral training was more advantageous for improving unilateral maximal strength, whereas bilateral training was more beneficial for improving bilateral maximal strength. By contrast, the meta-analysis by Liao et al. [9] found that bilateral training was superior to unilateral training for improving bilateral maximal strength, whereas unilateral training did not demonstrate a significant advantage for improving unilateral strength.

Previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses have mainly relied on conventional two-level meta-analytic approaches [9, 17, 37]. These methods typically assume independence among effect sizes and may therefore be insufficient to appropriately account for the hierarchical structure and dependency created by multiple effect sizes derived from the same study. Compared with previous reviews, the present study adds value by incorporating more recent comparative evidence, examining a broader range of performance-related outcomes, and applying a three-level meta-analytic model to better handle the dependency among multiple effect sizes within studies. Against this background, the present study aimed to compare the effects of unilateral training and bilateral training on multiple outcomes, including muscle hypertrophy, strength, sprint performance, jump performance, and change-of-direction performance. To integrate multiple effect sizes from individual studies more accurately, a three-level meta-analytic model was applied to synthesize the available evidence. This approach was intended to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of the differential effects of unilateral training and bilateral training across outcome domains and to offer more targeted evidence for training-mode selection in practice.

Methods

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement [38]. The complete PRISMA 2020 checklist is provided in Supplemental Material Table S1. In addition, this review was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under registration number CRD420261293507.

Information sources

A systematic search was conducted in PubMed, Web of Science Core Collection, Cochrane Library, Embase, and EBSCO. Only studies with accessible full texts were considered eligible. No a priori restrictions were imposed on publication date, sample characteristics, or language. In addition, three supplementary systematic search approaches were used through citation tracking and reference screening: (a) screening the reference lists of all included studies; (b) identifying subsequent studies that cited the included studies; and (c) expanding the search using the “similar articles/find similar” function available in PubMed and Embase. The search was performed on January 25, 2026.

Search strategy

The search strategy was developed based on previous systematic reviews addressing related topics. In addition, PROSPERO and the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews were searched to determine whether any systematic review protocols relevant to the present study had already been published. The detailed search strategies for the remaining databases are presented in Supplemental Material Tables S2–S6.

Study selection

All records identified through the database search were independently managed by one reviewer (XYX) using EndNote X9 (Clarivate Analytics, Philadelphia, PA, USA) for automatic duplicate removal, followed by manual verification. After deduplication, two independent reviewers (QLL and YQG) screened the titles and abstracts according to the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Any disagreement between the two reviewers was first resolved through discussion. If consensus could not be reached, a third independent reviewer (JX) was consulted to make the final decision.

Full-text articles that passed the initial screening were then independently assessed by the same two reviewers (QLL and YQG) to determine final eligibility. Disagreements arising during full-text screening were first discussed between the two reviewers and, if necessary, resolved by consultation with the third reviewer (JX). In addition, potentially eligible studies that were not captured in the initial database search were further identified by screening the reference lists of previous systematic reviews on related topics and through manual searching.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion and exclusion criteria were established a priori according to the PICOS framework, including Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcomes, and Study design.

The definition of the target population was based on the approach used by McKay et al. [39]. Human participants were eligible for inclusion regardless of sex, age, or training status. Studies involving animals or participants with chronic diseases were excluded. For the intervention, only studies that directly compared unilateral training with bilateral training within the same trial were included. Unilateral training was defined as exercises performed primarily in a unilateral manner, whereas bilateral training was defined as exercises performed simultaneously with both limbs [9]. The intervention duration had to be at least 4 weeks. Based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria used in previous studies [8, 9, 17, 36, 40], eligible exercise interventions included resistance training, plyometric training, and complex training. In the present review, complex training was defined as a training method that combines high-load, low-velocity resistance exercises with low-load, high-velocity or plyometric exercises within the same training session, with load or movement velocity varied between sets and/or exercises [41]. For the comparator, only studies that directly compared unilateral training and bilateral training within the same experiment were considered eligible. For outcomes, studies were required to report at least one measure of athletic performance or muscle hypertrophy. Eligible outcomes were as follows: (a) muscle hypertrophy, including changes in muscle thickness, cross-sectional area, muscle volume, or muscle mass; (b) strength, restricted to maximal dynamic strength (e.g., 1RM) and isokinetic strength, assessed using either unilateral or bilateral tests; (c) sprint performance, defined as short-distance sprint time over 5–40 m (s); (d) jump performance, including vertical jump, horizontal jump, and drop jump performance, expressed as jump height or distance and assessed using either unilateral or bilateral tests; and (e) change-of-direction performance, defined as the time required to complete change-of-direction tests (s), assessed using either unilateral or bilateral tests. In terms of study design, only original randomized controlled trials with a between-group comparative design were included. PICOS-based inclusion criteria

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (a) acute studies; (b) dissertations, conference abstracts, books, and case reports; (c) studies for which the full text could not be obtained; (d) studies from which valid outcome data could not be extracted and for which attempts to contact the authors were unsuccessful; (e) duplicate publications; and (f) studies involving special populations, such as patients undergoing rehabilitation or individuals with acute injuries.

Data extraction

Data extraction was conducted by two reviewers (XYX and QZL) using a predesigned and finalized custom Excel-based data extraction form (Microsoft Excel, Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA). The two reviewers independently extracted the following information: first author and year of publication, study characteristics, participant demographic characteristics, training protocols, and outcome measures. When key outcome data were incompletely reported or when results were presented only in graphical form, the original authors were contacted to obtain the required information. If the necessary numerical data could not be obtained from the authors and were available only in figures, the relevant data were extracted using WebPlotDigitizer 4.1 [42]. If the data required for a specific outcome remained unavailable after these procedures, the study was not included in the quantitative synthesis for that outcome. For each outcome in each study group, the pre-intervention and post-intervention means, standard deviations (SD), and sample sizes were extracted.

Risk of bias and methodological quality assessment

The risk of bias of the included studies was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool (RoB 2) [43]. RoB 2 evaluates bias across five domains: bias arising from the randomization process, bias due to deviations from intended interventions, bias due to missing outcome data, bias in outcome measurement, and bias in the selection of the reported result. Any disagreements were first resolved through discussion between the two reviewers (PFW and BTW). If consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer (JX) was consulted for arbitration.

In addition, the methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the Physiotherapy Evidence Database (PEDro) scale [44]. PEDro scores range from 0 to 10, with scores of ≥ 6 indicating high quality, 4–5 indicating moderate quality, and ≤ 3 indicating low quality.

Meta-analysis and heterogeneity

All analyses were conducted in R (V.4.2.0; R Core Team, Vienna, Austria) [55] using the meta and metafor packages [55]. When individual studies reported nested or multiple effect sizes (e.g., for jumping and sprint performance), these effect sizes were often statistically dependent. Including all effect sizes simultaneously in a conventional meta-analysis may violate the assumption of independence [56], whereas selecting only one effect size per study may be overly conservative and may fail to fully capture the true intervention effect [57]. To address this issue, a three-level meta-analysis was performed according to the approach proposed by Assink and Wibbelink [58]. Specifically, three-level random-effects models were fitted using rma.mv in the metafor package with the structure random = ~ 1 | studyID/effectsizeID. Separate models were fitted for each predefined outcome domain to account for dependent effect sizes within studies. This method decomposes the total variance into sampling variance (Level 1), within-study variance (Level 2), and between-study variance (Level 3), thereby accounting for the dependency and hierarchical structure of effect sizes [59]. By retaining multiple effect sizes from the same study, three-level meta-analysis can improve statistical power and provide a more accurate estimate of the overall effect [58]. In the three-level models, parameters were estimated using restricted maximum likelihood (REML). Tests of individual coefficients and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were based on the t distribution [60]. In addition, prediction intervals (PIs) were calculated based on the t distribution. Unlike 95% CIs, PIs reflect the range within which the true effect of a future study is likely to fall after accounting for between-study heterogeneity, and therefore provide important complementary information in random-effects models [61, 62]. Although several indices are available to assess heterogeneity (e.g., Cochrane’s Q, I², and τ²), I² remains one of the most commonly used and widely reported measures in the methodological literature [63]. Accordingly, the present study primarily reports I² values for Level 2 and Level 3.

Subgroup analysis and meta-regression

To explore potential sources of between-study heterogeneity and identify possible moderators, subgroup analyses and meta-regression analyses were performed to examine the moderating effects of categorical and continuous variables, respectively [64]. Methodological guidance generally recommends that at least 10 studies should be available for each meta-regression analysis, whereas subgroup analyses should ideally include at least five studies per subgroup [65, 66].

The variables included in the subgroup analyses were: (a) sex; (b) training status; (c) training modality; (d) outcome type; (e) intervention duration; and (f) weekly training frequency. Based on prior training background, participants were categorized as untrained individuals, trained individuals, or athletes. Following previous research, outcome type was first classified directly as bilateral or unilateral outcomes [7]. On this basis, and with reference to previous classifications of performance outcomes, some unilateral test outcomes were further categorized according to side-specific measures, including the right side, left side, dominant side, and weaker side, particularly for unilateral jumping and change-of-direction outcomes [23, 30, 32, 67].

Univariable meta-regression analyses were conducted using restricted maximum likelihood (REML), which is considered a robust estimation method [68]. The predictors entered the analyses included participant characteristics (baseline age and baseline body mass), intervention-related variables (weekly training frequency and intervention duration), and publication year. All meta-regression models were fitted using the metafor package.

Publication bias and sensitivity analyses

Publication bias was assessed using contour-enhanced funnel plots [69] and Egger’s regression test [70, 71]. In Egger’s test, a p-value > 0.05 was considered to indicate no significant evidence of publication bias. If the funnel plot showed asymmetry and Egger’s test yielded a p-value < 0.05, the trim-and-fill method was further applied to evaluate the potential impact of publication bias on the pooled results. Funnel plots and Egger’s regression test were used to assess the symmetry of the overall effect-size distribution from both visual and quantitative perspectives, thereby evaluating the potential risk of publication bias among the included studies.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted at three levels. First, sensitivity analyses were performed using different approaches for handling standard deviations. The primary analysis calculated the standard deviation of change scores (SDchange) based on a pre-post correlation coefficient of r = 0.5. Sensitivity analyses then recalculated SDchange using r = 0.3 and r = 0.7, and additionally compared these results with those obtained using the baseline standard deviation approach, to examine the robustness of the findings. Second, a leave-one-out analysis was conducted. Because some studies contributed multiple effect sizes, each effect size was sequentially removed as the unit of analysis to evaluate the influence of individual effect sizes on the overall pooled estimate. Third, potential outliers were identified for each outcome using three-level meta-analysis, and their influence on the pooled effect size was examined in the primary model. Specifically, in the three-level meta-analysis, Hat values, Cook’s distances [72], and studentized residuals [73] were jointly used to diagnose leverage points, outliers, and influential cases at both the within-study level (Level 2) and the between-study level (Level 3). After excluding these potential outliers, the three-level meta-analysis was repeated to assess the stability of the model results.

Certainty of evidence

The certainty of evidence was assessed using the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation) approach when interpreting the findings. The assessment considered risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias, and the certainty of evidence was rated as high (further research is very unlikely to change confidence in the estimate of effect), moderate (further research is likely to have an important impact on confidence in the estimate of effect and may change the estimate), low (further research is very likely to have an important impact on confidence in the estimate of effect and is likely to change the estimate), or very low (any estimate of effect is very uncertain) [74]. The GRADE assessment was conducted independently by two reviewers (XPL and ZXH), and any disagreements were resolved by a third reviewer (JX).

Results

Search results

A total of 3200 records were initially identified, including 3196 from the major databases and 4 from other sources. After screening, 24 studies met eligibility criteria and were included in the systematic review, and in the meta-analysis (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of the included studies

All 24 included studies were randomized controlled trials, comprising a total of 588 participants. Of these, 315 were male and 207 were female, while sex was not reported for the remaining 66 participants. Sample sizes ranged from 7 to 33 participants per group. The reported participant ages ranged from 9.9 to 55.8 years. Most studies included athletic populations, with 17 studies involving athletes, whereas 3 studies included trained participants and 4 studies included untrained participants. Among the included studies, 8 used resistance training, 8 used plyometric training, and 8 used complex training. Detailed characteristics of the participants and intervention protocols are provided in Supplemental Material Table S7.

Meta-analysis results

The comparative effects of unilateral training and bilateral training across all outcomes are presented in Fig. 2. For strength, jump performance, and muscle hypertrophy, positive effect sizes favor unilateral training; for sprint and change-of-direction performance, which were measured in seconds, negative effect sizes favor unilateral training. Compared with bilateral training, unilateral training showed a significant advantage for unilateral strength (k = 22, g = 0.42, 95% CI: 0.23 to 0.60, p < 0.001; GRADE: moderate). In contrast, for bilateral strength, bilateral training showed a small but significant advantage over unilateral training (k = 16, g = -0.28, 95% CI: -0.54 to -0.02, p = 0.034; GRADE: low), indicating that the effect favored bilateral training. For muscle hypertrophy, no significant difference was observed between the two training modalities (k = 11, g = 0.01, 95% CI: -0.25 to 0.26, p = 0.957; GRADE: low).

With respect to jump performance, unilateral training demonstrated a significant advantage over bilateral training for unilateral jump performance (k = 32, g = 0.35, 95% CI: 0.14 to 0.56, p = 0.002; GRADE: moderate). However, no significant difference was found between unilateral training and bilateral training for bilateral jump performance (k = 30, g = -0.08, 95% CI: -0.27 to 0.11, p = 0.389; GRADE: low).

For sprint and change-of-direction performance, unilateral training did not differ significantly from bilateral training in sprint performance (k = 25, g = -0.01, 95% CI: -0.18 to 0.16, p = 0.887; GRADE: low). For change-of-direction performance, unilateral training showed a trend toward improvement; however, the difference did not reach statistical significance (k = 25, g = -0.35, 95% CI: -0.73 to 0.01, p = 0.058; GRADE: very low).

Regarding heterogeneity, the three-level variance decomposition showed that Level 2 heterogeneity was negligible for most outcomes, with only unilateral jump performance showing a small within-study component (I² = 2.66%). This indicates that multiple effect sizes extracted from the same study generally contributed little to the overall heterogeneity. In contrast, Level 3 heterogeneity was more evident for bilateral strength (I² = 10.30%), bilateral jump performance (I² = 20.09%), unilateral jump performance (I² = 23.59%), and especially change-of-direction performance (I² = 60.58%). These findings suggest that heterogeneity was mainly attributable to between-study differences rather than within-study dependence. For unilateral strength, muscle hypertrophy, and sprint performance, both Level 2 and Level 3 heterogeneity were negligible or very low, indicating limited variability across the included effects. The pooled forest plots for all outcomes are presented in Supplemental Material Figures S1–S7.

Moderator analyses

Subgroup analyses

To explore potential sources of heterogeneity, subgroup analyses were performed for outcomes showing at least some degree of heterogeneity (I² > 20%). These analyses primarily examined whether participant characteristics, training protocols, and study design factors influenced the effects of unilateral training versus bilateral training on unilateral jump performance, bilateral jump performance, and change-of-direction performance. Across all three outcomes, no statistically significant between-subgroup differences were observed for any potential moderator (all tests for subgroup differences, p > 0.05), indicating that there is currently no evidence that sex, training type, training status, intervention duration, training frequency, or test type significantly moderated the relative effects of unilateral training and bilateral training. Although statistically significant effects were observed within some individual subgroups, the absence of significant between-subgroup differences suggests that these findings should be interpreted only as possible directional trends rather than evidence of true moderator effects.

For unilateral jump performance, no significant between-subgroup differences were detected. However, statistically significant pooled effects were observed within several individual subgroups. For example, unilateral training significantly improved unilateral jump performance in male participants (g = 0.38), and a similar result was observed in athletes (g = 0.34). With respect to training type, plyometric training (g = 0.23), strength training (g = 0.23), and complex training (g = 0.61) all showed effects favoring unilateral training, with the largest effect size observed for complex training. Regarding intervention duration, studies with training periods of ≤ 8 weeks showed a significant effect (g = 0.39), whereas studies lasting > 8 weeks did not reach statistical significance. In terms of training frequency, significant effects were found in both the < 3 sessions/week subgroup (g = 0.29) and the ≥ 3 sessions/week subgroup (g = 0.69). For test type, unilateral training showed a significant advantage in unilateral jump tests (g = 0.30), with relatively large effect sizes also observed for the dominant side (g = 0.80) and non-dominant side (g = 0.77). Overall, although several within-subgroup estimates were statistically significant, no significant moderator was identified. Therefore, these subgroup results should be regarded as exploratory and interpreted cautiously (Fig. 3).

For bilateral jump performance, no significant between-subgroup differences were observed, although some descriptive trends were evident within individual subgroups. In the sex-based analysis, a significant effect was found only in male participants (g = -0.24). No statistically significant results were observed in the remaining subgroups defined by training type, training status, intervention duration, or training frequency. Details are provided in the Supplemental Material (Table S8).

For change-of-direction performance, no significant between-subgroup differences were observed, but significant effects were identified within some subgroups. In terms of training type, a statistically significant pooled effect was observed only in the complex training subgroup (g = -0.68). Regarding training frequency, a statistically significant pooled effect was observed in studies with < 3 sessions/week (g = -0.47), whereas no statistically significant effect was observed in studies with ≥ 3 sessions/week. For test type, statistically significant pooled effects were observed in right-sided (g = -0.66) and left-sided (g = -0.83) change-of-direction tests, whereas unilateral tests, dominant-side tests, and non-dominant-side tests did not reach statistical significance. Details are presented in the Supplemental Material (Table S9). Overall, no significant moderators were identified for COD performance. Although some within-subgroup pooled effects reached statistical significance, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and should not be considered evidence that specific training conditions or testing contexts modified the relative effects of unilateral and bilateral training.

Meta-regression analyses

Meta-regression analyses did not identify any significant moderators. For bilateral jump performance, unilateral jump performance, and change-of-direction performance, age, body mass, training duration, training frequency, and publication year were not significantly associated with effect size (all p > 0.05). In addition, none of the regression coefficients for these covariates reached statistical significance, and all 95% confidence intervals crossed zero (see Supplemental Material Tables S10–S12). These findings suggest that, within the range of studies included in the present analysis, age, body mass, training duration, training frequency, and publication year did not significantly influence the relative effects of unilateral training versus bilateral training on these three outcomes.

Risk of bias and methodological quality

The results of the risk-of-bias assessment for the included studies are presented in Fig. 4 and Supplementary Figure S8. According to the RoB 2 assessment, with the exception of Fisher et al. [75], most studies were rated as having some concerns in the domain of the randomization process, indicating that information regarding randomization methods and allocation concealment was generally insufficiently reported. Some studies were also judged as having some concerns in the domains of deviations from intended interventions and missing outcome data, whereas most studies were rated as low risk in the domain of measurement of the outcome. In addition, except for Duan et al. and Deng et al. [76, 77], most studies were rated as having some concerns in the domain of selection of the reported result. Overall, most of studies were judged as having an overall risk of bias of some concerns.

Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots in combination with Egger’s test (Fig. 5). The results showed that only COD performance demonstrated evidence suggestive of possible publication bias (p = 0.001), whereas no significant evidence of publication bias was observed for the remaining outcomes. A subsequent trim-and-fill analysis for COD performance imputed four effect sizes on the left side of the funnel plot, and the pooled effect changed from non-significant to significant (Figure S9; g = -0.53, 95% CI: -0.76 to -0.30), suggesting the presence of funnel plot asymmetry. Given that the corrected pooled effect became larger in magnitude, this asymmetry may not be attributable solely to conventional publication bias, potentially reflecting also small-study effects or an uneven distribution of studies. Therefore, this outcome should still be interpreted with caution. The mean PEDro score across all included studies was 5.17, indicating that the overall methodological quality of the included studies was moderate (Supplemental Material Table S13).

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analysis using different SD handling methods

Sensitivity analyses were conducted by comparing the SDchange used in the primary analysis (r = 0.5) with SDchange values calculated under different assumed correlation coefficients (r = 0.3 and r = 0.7), as well as with results standardized using the pooled SD of baseline scores. Overall, the direction of the effect sizes and the statistical conclusions were generally consistent across different assumptions. Specifically, unilateral strength and unilateral jump remained statistically significant under different correlation assumptions and when the pooled SD of baseline scores was used. In contrast, muscle hypertrophy, bilateral jump, sprint performance, and COD performance remained non-significant across these alternative SD handling methods. By comparison, bilateral strength was relatively more sensitive to the method used to handle SD. Under the r = 0.7 assumption and when using the pooled SD of baseline scores, the results remained significant (p = 0.016 and p = 0.038, respectively), whereas significance was lost only under the r = 0.3 assumption (p = 0.058). Overall, different SD handling methods did not alter the main conclusions, although they had a slight influence on the statistical significance of the bilateral strength outcome (see Supplemental Material Table S14).

Leave-one-out analysis

Additionally, leave-one-out sensitivity analyses were performed for all primary pooled effects. These analyses were not conducted by removing one study at a time; rather, they were conducted at the level of individual comparison effect sizes, with one effect size omitted in each iteration. The results showed that, for most outcomes, the pooled effect sizes and their statistical significance remained stable after the removal of any single comparison effect size. For unilateral strength, muscle hypertrophy, bilateral jump, unilateral jump, and sprint performance, statistical significance did not change after omitting any individual comparison effect size. However, for some outcomes, several comparison effect sizes still had a noticeable influence on the overall results. In the analysis of bilateral strength, the statistical significance changed from significant to non-significant after the removal of two specific comparison effect sizes. In the analysis of change-of-direction performance, the leave-one-out results indicated that removing any one of four specific comparison effect sizes changed the pooled result from non-significant to significant, suggesting that this outcome was relatively sensitive to individual effect sizes. Detailed results are provided in Supplemental Material Figures S10–S16.

Outlier analysis

Potential outliers were identified based on Cook’s distance and studentized residuals. The distribution of outliers differed across outcomes. No outliers were detected for bilateral strength, unilateral strength, muscle hypertrophy, or bilateral jump. One outlier was identified for unilateral jump and one for sprint performance, whereas two outliers were identified for change-of-direction performance.

For unilateral jump, after removal of one outlier (g = 0.83), the pooled effect size was 0.31 (95% CI: 0.07 to 0.55, p = 0.012), and the statistical significance remained unchanged. For sprint performance, after removal of one outlier (g = -0.69), the pooled effect size was 0.04 (95% CI: -0.13 to 0.21, p = 0.634), and the statistical significance likewise remained unchanged. For change-of-direction performance, after removal of two outliers (g = -1.84 and g = -4.02), the pooled effect size was − 0.28 (95% CI: -0.46 to -0.10, p = 0.003). Although the absolute magnitude of the effect size decreased, the confidence interval became markedly narrower, and the result changed from non-significant to significant, indicating that this outcome was relatively sensitive to outliers. Detailed results are presented in Supplemental Material Table S15.

Overall, most outcomes showed acceptable robustness. However, bilateral strength appeared to be relatively sensitive to different SD handling methods and to certain individual comparison effect sizes, whereas COD performance was mainly sensitive to individual effect sizes and outliers. Therefore, both outcomes should be interpreted with caution.

Discussion

Main findings

The primary aim of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to compare the relative effects of unilateral training and bilateral training on athletic performance and muscle hypertrophy. The findings can be summarized as follows. First, unilateral training showed a significant advantage for improving unilateral strength (p < 0.001), whereas bilateral training demonstrated a small but significant advantage for improving bilateral strength (p = 0.034). No significant difference was observed between the two training modes in muscle hypertrophy (p = 0.957). Second, for jumping performance, unilateral training significantly improved unilateral jump performance (p = 0.002), whereas no significant difference was found between unilateral training and bilateral training in bilateral jump performance (p = 0.389). Third, no significant difference was observed between unilateral training and bilateral training for sprint performance (p = 0.887). Finally, for COD performance, unilateral training showed a favorable trend, although this did not reach statistical significance in the main analysis (p = 0.058). After outlier handling, the effect became significant and the confidence interval narrowed, suggesting that this outcome was sensitive to individual studies. At the same time, Egger’s test indicated the possible presence of publication bias for COD; therefore, this finding should be interpreted cautiously.

Strength adaptations

The present findings support a task-specific pattern in strength adaptations. Specifically, unilateral training showed a significant advantage over bilateral training for improving unilateral strength (g = 0.42), whereas bilateral training showed a small advantage over unilateral training for improving bilateral strength (g = 0.28). With respect to unilateral strength, most previous studies have reached relatively consistent conclusions [7, 17, 40]. Liao et al. [9] also reported results favoring unilateral training, although the difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.07). For bilateral strength, the findings of Kassiano et al. [7] and Liao et al. [9] were consistent with the present results. By contrast, Zhang et al. [17] and Wu et al. [40] did not find that bilateral training was more effective than unilateral training for improving bilateral strength. These discrepancies may be related to differences in eligibility criteria, participant characteristics, and statistical models. Wu et al. [40] included only athletes, whereas some of the studies included by Zhang et al. [17] had not undergone rigorous peer review, which may partly explain why their findings were not fully consistent with ours. Building on previous reviews, the present study included a more comprehensive body of evidence and applied a three-level meta-analytic model to account for the dependence among multiple effect sizes within the same study. This approach was therefore more appropriate for the current data structure.

Overall, these findings suggest that neither unilateral training nor bilateral training is inherently superior for strength development. Rather, their relative effectiveness appears to depend largely on the degree of correspondence between the training task and the testing task. This interpretation is consistent with the principle of specificity and the concept of transfer of training [1, 78, 79]. When training and testing tasks share similarities in support pattern, force-production mode, joint coordination, and neural control demands, training-induced adaptations are more likely to transfer to performance in the corresponding test [1, 78]. Accordingly, unilateral training may be more effective for unilateral strength outcomes, whereas bilateral training may be more effective for bilateral strength outcomes [80].

Several mechanisms may partly explain this task-specific pattern, including bilateral deficit [6, 18], differences in muscle recruitment and stabilization demands [81, 82], cross-education [83, 84], and previous exposure to bilateral training stimuli [26, 40, 85]. In addition, previous evidence has shown that unilateral and bilateral strength training generally produce similar effects on muscle mass [86], limb circumference [87] and cross-sectional area [7, 88]. Therefore, the strength differences observed in the present study may be more closely related to neuromuscular adaptations than to differences in muscle hypertrophy. However, these mechanisms should be regarded as supportive explanations rather than definitive evidence, because the present meta-analysis was designed to compare training effects rather than directly test neuromuscular mechanisms.

Muscle hypertrophy

With regard to muscle hypertrophy, no significant difference was observed between unilateral training and bilateral training in the present study, which is consistent with the findings reported by Kassiano et al. [7]. From a mechanistic perspective, hypertrophy adaptations are primarily driven by training-related variables such as mechanical tension, training volume, proximity to failure, and progressive overload, rather than being determined solely by whether an exercise is performed unilaterally or bilaterally [89, 90]. When these key variables are reasonably matched between training conditions, both unilateral training and bilateral training appear capable of providing sufficient anabolic stimuli to induce comparable hypertrophic adaptations [90]. In addition, many of the included studies adopted volume-matched designs, which may partly explain the similar hypertrophic outcomes observed between the two training modalities [3].

Some studies have suggested that bilateral exercises may be more favorable for hypertrophy due to their ability to handle greater absolute loads or elicit higher acute neuromuscular activation. However, such assumptions have not been consistently supported by evidence from long-term adaptations [91]. Moreover, current evidence does not uniformly support the notion that higher external loads necessarily result in greater hypertrophy [92, 93], and the assumption that greater electromyographic activity directly predicts superior hypertrophic responses has also been challenged [92, 94]. Therefore, although bilateral training may allow for higher absolute loading and more stable force production, these advantages do not necessarily translate into greater hypertrophic outcomes. Conversely, while unilateral training may involve lower absolute loads, its characteristics—such as localized muscular demand, increased motor control requirements, and unilateral loading patterns—may still provide sufficient stimulus for hypertrophy. Bilateral training, on the other hand, may achieve similar adaptations through higher loading capacity and more stable mechanical tension [91]. Although the underlying pathways may differ, current evidence suggests that these two training modes do not exhibit clear divergence in long-term hypertrophic adaptations.

From a practical perspective, when the primary goal is muscle hypertrophy, appropriate manipulation of key training variables—including volume, intensity distribution, proximity to failure, range of motion, and progressive overload—may be more important than simply distinguishing between unilateral training and bilateral training [90]. In other words, both unilateral training and bilateral training can be considered effective strategies within hypertrophy-oriented programs, and the choice between them should be guided by overall program design, task demands, and individual characteristics.

Jump performance

Regarding jump performance, the present study found that, compared with bilateral training, unilateral training conferred a significant advantage for unilateral jump performance (g = 0.35), whereas no significant difference was observed between unilateral training and bilateral training for bilateral jump performance (g = -0.08). These findings are generally consistent with previous studies. Prior evidence has shown that, relative to bilateral training, unilateral training is more effective for improving unilateral jump performance [9, 17, 37, 40], whereas no significant difference is usually observed between unilateral training and bilateral training for bilateral jump performance [9, 17, 37, 40]. This pattern suggests that training-induced adaptations are more likely to be expressed in testing tasks that match the training movement in structure, force-production pattern, and stability demands [6, 19, 95].

The advantage of unilateral training for unilateral jump performance may be mainly related to the matching of task demands. Unilateral jumping requires greater trunk stability, coordinated control of the hip, knee, and ankle, and single-limb force production, all of which are closely aligned with the characteristics of unilateral training [6, 19, 95]. Bogdanis et al. [34] also reported that unilateral jump training was more effective than bilateral jump training in improving unilateral rate of force development. In addition, the smaller base of support in unilateral tasks may increase the involvement of synergist and stabilizing muscles, thereby improving single-limb stability and force transmission during take-off and landing [96, 97]. Some electromyographic studies have also shown that unilateral jumping may elicit greater activation of the vastus intermedius, gastrocnemius, and vastus medialis([98–100]. although this activation advantage may be task-specific, as higher quadriceps activation has been observed during bilateral knee extension in other studies [101].

From a mechanical perspective, unilateral jumping requires one limb to propel a greater relative load without load sharing by the contralateral limb, usually accompanied by a longer take-off time and greater impulse. These characteristics may provide a stronger stimulus to the ankle, knee, and hip extensors [102]. Participants’ previous training background may also influence these findings. Because individuals may be more frequently exposed to bilateral training stimuli in routine practice, there may be relatively limited room for further adaptation in bilateral jump tasks([103]. By contrast, unilateral training is less commonly used in practice, making performance gains easier to detect in unilateral jump tasks. Bishop et al. [104] likewise showed that an 8-week combined bilateral and unilateral strength-training program significantly improved unilateral jump height, whereas improvements in bilateral jump performance were not significant.

By contrast, although the overall effect size for bilateral jump performance was directionally slightly in favor of bilateral training, the magnitude was trivial and did not reach statistical significance. This suggests that both training modes may improve bilateral jump performance, but the advantage of bilateral training is not robust. Bilateral training is more closely aligned with bilateral jumping in movement structure and synchronous force production, and may therefore provide a certain degree of specificity. However, contact times in bilateral countermovement jumps are generally shorter than those in unilateral countermovement jumps (approximately 178–190 ms vs. >250 ms) [28], indicating that bilateral jumping places greater emphasis on rapid synchronous force production within a shorter time window. In contrast, adaptations derived from unilateral training may not fully transfer to the brief, synchronous force output required in bilateral jump tasks [28].

The absence of a significant between-group difference in bilateral jump performance may also be related to the multidimensional nature of this test. Bilateral jumping is influenced not only by bilateral coordination, but also by several shared determinants, including maximal force-producing capacity of the extensor muscles, tendon stiffness, and the ability to utilize the stretch-shortening cycle [105]. Because both unilateral training and bilateral training may improve these common capacities, between-group differences may have been diluted. In addition, bilateral deficit and changes in interlimb asymmetry may also influence how training adaptations are expressed across different jump tasks [9, 106]. Overall, the present findings suggest that the advantage of unilateral training is mainly evident in unilateral jump tasks, whereas both training modes may produce similar effects on bilateral jump performance.

Sprint and change-of-direction performance

Regarding sprint performance, the present study found no significant difference between unilateral training and bilateral training. This finding is consistent with most previous studies, which also reported no significant difference between unilateral training and bilateral training in improving sprint performance [9, 40]. However, a small number of studies have suggested that unilateral training may be superior to bilateral training for enhancing sprint performance [17, 37]. However, a small number of studies have suggested that unilateral training may be superior to bilateral training for enhancing sprint performance [17, 37] also incorporated grey literature such as theses and dissertations. Therefore, rather than indicating a stable advantage of one training mode over the other, the current evidence may suggest that both training modes can improve sprint performance through different pathways and ultimately produce comparable effects [17, 33, 107, 108].

Regarding sprint performance, the present study found no significant difference between unilateral training and bilateral training. This finding is consistent with most previous studies, which also reported no significant difference between unilateral training and bilateral training in improving sprint performance [38, 42].

Although sprinting involves clear unilateral support during movement execution, the present findings do not support the simple assumption that unilateral training is inherently more transferable to sprint performance [8]. Sprint performance is a multifactorial ability that involves repeated unilateral support, rapid gait transitions, and inter-limb coordination, and is also influenced by horizontal force-production capacity, step frequency, ground contact time, sprint-specific technique, neuromuscular coordination, and testing methods [8, 109, 110]. Therefore, similarity in movement appearance or support pattern does not necessarily translate into greater training transfer. Based on the current evidence, improvements in sprint performance are more likely to depend on the correspondence between the training stimulus and the key demands of the sprint task, rather than solely on whether the training mode is unilateral or bilateral.

Regarding COD performance, unilateral training showed a favorable trend compared with bilateral training (g = -0.35), although the pooled effect did not reach statistical significance in the main analysis (p = 0.058). This finding is generally consistent with several previous studies, which also reported non-significant between-group differences while showing results that tended to favor unilateral training and approached statistical significance [9, 17, 40]. In contrast, Zhang et al. [37] reported a statistically significant advantage of unilateral training for this outcome. This discrepancy may be partly attributable to differences in eligibility criteria, as their review included only studies using plyometric training. Taken together, these findings may indicate that unilateral training has a potential, but as yet inconclusive, advantage for improving COD performance.

Because COD is a complex motor ability jointly determined by deceleration capacity, re-acceleration capacity, lower-limb strength and reactive strength, movement strategy, and task characteristics [11, 14, 111, 112], the present findings should be interpreted with caution. On the one hand, substantial heterogeneity was observed in the COD outcome, and sensitivity analysis indicated that individual studies may have exerted a considerable influence on the pooled estimate. On the other hand, publication-bias analysis suggested a potential risk of publication bias for COD, and the statistical result changed from non-significant to significant after trim-and-fill analysis. Therefore, although the direction of the pooled effect favored unilateral training, the current evidence does not support a definitive conclusion that unilateral training is superior to bilateral training for improving COD performance.

The lack of robustness in the COD findings may be related to substantial heterogeneity in measurement methods and intervention designs within this field [112]. Previous studies have shown that COD tests differ considerably in task characteristics, including change-of-direction angle, sprint distance, and whether the movement is pre-planned, all of which may lead to different test outcomes [112, 113]. Therefore, different COD tests may reflect different performance components, which may be an important reason for the substantial heterogeneity observed in the present study and for the relatively strong influence of individual studies on the pooled effect.

From the perspective of task specificity, the favorable trend for unilateral training in COD performance may be reasonable. COD actions typically involve rapid deceleration, unilateral braking, and re-acceleration phases, all of which depend largely on unilateral force production and stability control [14]. Compared with bilateral training, unilateral training more closely reflects the movement characteristics of COD tasks, including single-leg support, unilateral braking, and unilateral re-acceleration. In addition, COD performance is closely related to eccentric braking capacity and its rapid transition into the re-acceleration phase, and previous studies have shown that eccentric strength is moderately to very strongly correlated with COD performance and that eccentric overload training can improve COD performance [114–116]. Therefore, unilateral training may provide a certain degree of task-specific stimulus in some COD tasks, which may explain the favorable direction of effect. However, these explanations should be regarded as supportive mechanisms rather than definitive evidence.

Moderating analysis

For unilateral jump performance, bilateral jump performance, and change-of-direction performance, subgroup analyses and meta-regression analyses were further conducted to explore potential moderators. However, the results showed that sex, training type, training status, intervention duration, training frequency, age, and body mass did not significantly moderate the effect differences between unilateral training and bilateral training.

Nevertheless, the subgroup analyses still revealed several trends worthy of attention. Specifically, in subgroups with intervention durations of less than 8 weeks, the point estimates for some outcomes were numerically in the direction of greater improvement after unilateral training. However, these findings did not constitute evidence of a significant moderating effect. To some extent, this suggests that the relative advantages of unilateral training may be more likely to emerge during shorter intervention periods. One possible explanation is that participants are typically more frequently exposed to bilateral training in their previous training routines; therefore, unilateral training may provide a training stimulus that differs from their habitual loading patterns, thereby eliciting more pronounced early-stage adaptations. Additionally, no clear differences in training effects were observed between the dominant and non-dominant limbs or between the left and right sides. This may be partly attributable to the limited number of studies available within these subgroups.

These findings should, however, be interpreted with caution. First, meta-regression generally requires adequate statistical power, whereas the number of studies included in several subgroups was relatively small, which may have reduced the ability to detect true moderating effects. Second, substantial between-study differences remained in training program design, participant characteristics, and testing methods, and these uncontrolled factors may also have contributed to the observed heterogeneity. Finally, intervention duration itself may be intertwined with other factors, such as training content, load prescription, and participants’ training background; therefore, no clear causal inference can be drawn at present. More well-designed studies with sufficient sample sizes are needed to further clarify the specific roles of different moderators in adaptations to unilateral training and bilateral training.

Practical implications

From a practical perspective, most of the significant findings in the present study were only of small to moderate magnitude. Both unilateral training and bilateral training can improve athletic performance, but their relative advantages appear to be consistent with the principle of movement specificity. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as modest relative advantages rather than large or decisive differences between unilateral and bilateral training. Nevertheless, small effects may still be practically meaningful in athletic settings, particularly when they are aligned with the target movement pattern, accumulated across a training cycle, or relevant to performance contexts where marginal gains are important.

More specifically, when the primary training goal is to improve unilateral strength or unilateral jump performance, unilateral training may be more advantageous. For change-of-direction performance, the current evidence does not support a clear advantage of either training mode, although unilateral training may still be considered when the target sport involves frequent unilateral braking, support, or re-acceleration demands. In contrast, when the goal is to enhance bilateral strength or to perform tasks requiring a high degree of bilateral coordination and permitting greater external loading, bilateral training may be more appropriate. Therefore, when designing training programs, coaches should not simply seek to determine whether one training mode is universally superior to the other, but should select training methods based on the characteristics of the target movement, athlete-specific features, sport-specific demands, and the nature of the performance test.

Additionally, the appropriate combination of unilateral and bilateral training may provide athletes with a broader range of training stimuli. Therefore, in practical settings, coaches may consider incorporating both training modes according to the target movement, athlete characteristics, and sport-specific demands [117].

Limitations

Some potential limitations should be acknowledged. First, substantial variation existed across the included studies in terms of training type, including resistance training, plyometric training, and complex training, as well as intervention duration, training frequency, and participants’ training background. These differences may have increased between-study heterogeneity and affected the consistency of result interpretation. Second, the number of studies included for some outcomes was relatively limited, and the representativeness of the included samples was also constrained, particularly by the underrepresentation of female participants, which may have reduced the stability of the effect estimates and restricted the certainty and generalizability of the related conclusions.

Additionally, many studies did not clearly report information on dominant versus non-dominant limbs, nor did they specify whether unilateral outcomes were derived from the left side, the right side, the average of both sides, or the dominant side. This limits further interpretation of limb asymmetry characteristics and the direction of training adaptation transfer. Finally, the risk-of-bias assessment indicated that most studies were judged as raising “some concerns,” suggesting that the methodological quality of the current evidence still has room for improvement. Therefore, current findings of this study should be interpreted cautiously in light of these limitations, and more rigorously designed, well-reported, high-quality randomized controlled trials are still needed for further verification.

Future research directions

Future research comparing unilateral training and bilateral training should place greater emphasis on the alignment between outcome measures and task specificity. For sports characterized by predominantly unilateral movement patterns, relying solely on bilateral performance measures may not adequately capture training adaptations. Accordingly, both unilateral and bilateral performance outcomes should be included to provide a more comprehensive and ecologically valid assessment of training effects. In addition, studies should clearly report which limb is assessed in unilateral tests. If combined values (e.g., sum or average of both limbs) are used, this should be explicitly described in the methods section.

Furthermore, existing evidence is largely derived from chronic interventions, whereas the acute responses to unilateral training and bilateral training—and their underlying mechanisms—remain insufficiently explored. Future investigations should integrate measures such as fatigue characteristics, biomechanical parameters, and muscle activation patterns to better elucidate the mechanisms driving potential differences in adaptations between training modalities.

Whether unilateral training is more effective in improving performance of the weaker limb remains unclear due to limited evidence. Future research should further examine asymmetrical adaptations between dominant and non-dominant limbs, as well as the potential role of unilateral training in reducing inter-limb asymmetry.

Conclusions

Training adaptations exhibit task-specific differences across certain performance outcomes. Specifically, unilateral training demonstrated advantages in unilateral strength and unilateral jump performance, whereas bilateral training showed a relative advantage in bilateral strength. In contrast, no significant differences were observed between unilateral training and bilateral training in bilateral jump performance, sprint performance, change-of-direction performance, or muscle hypertrophy. Therefore, both unilateral training and bilateral training may be considered effective training strategies, and their application should be guided by the specific demands of the target sport and movement task.
