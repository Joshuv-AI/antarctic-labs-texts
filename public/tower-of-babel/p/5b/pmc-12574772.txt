Indian nurses’ attitudes toward patients with substance use problems: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Anjali Rathee, Priyanshi Dixit, Rinkal Jangid, Tejshwi Dabi, Suman Sharma, Devendra Singh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Substance abuse is a significant public health concern in India, often leading to social stigma, poor support for treatment, difficulties in interpersonal relationships, challenges in securing employment, and strained family dynamics.Aim:To investigate the factors influencing nurses’ attitudes toward patients with substance use disorders.Materials and Methods:This cross-sectional quantitative study recruited 272 participants through a total enumeration sampling technique, including registered nurses working in a Tertiary Hospital, who met the inclusion criteria. Data were collected using the Brief Substance Abuse Attitude Survey (BSAAS), and appropriate statistical tests were applied.Results:The study reported a mean BSAAS score of 77.15 ± 17.07, indicating predominantly positive attitudes. A statistically significant negative correlation was found between age and attitude scores (r = –0.05, P ≤ 0.05), suggesting that older nurses had slightly less positive attitudes. Similarly, greater work experience (r = –0.05, P ≤ 0.05) was associated with lower attitude scores. In-service and continuing education showed a weak positive correlation (r = 0.02, P ≤ 0.05), indicating minimal impact.Conclusion:Indian nurses generally exhibited a moderately positive attitude toward patients with substance use disorders. Regular sensitization through targeted training programs is recommended to enhance nurses’ attitudes.

Substance use is a major health and social problem in today’s world. The addiction that can result from the use of any psychoactive substance (or polysubstance) does not make a distinction between race and age, ethnicity and social class, or educational level and occupation. The potential side effects of substances such as amphetamines and cocaine on users involved in accidents and trauma can significantly increase the risk of mortality and comorbidity.[1] The relationship between substance use and physical injury has been well established[2] with studies to suggest that up to 50% of hospital admissions may be directly associated with substance use-related problems.[3] Therefore, the likelihood that clinical nurses in all specialities have opportunities to take care of patients whose admission was caused by substance use problems is enormous. However, they often fail to identify patients with these problems or address the issues through assessment and intervention. Such failure can be attributed, in part, to nurses’ negative attitudes toward clients with substance use problems and to their inadequate knowledge and skills in appropriate substance use screening and treatment.[4567]

Nurses and other clinical professionals often hold negative attitudes toward individuals with substance use problems, and this may significantly influence the quality of care delivered to these clients.[8] Gerace[9] pointed out that some nurses hold counterproductive ideologies regarding these patients, such as possessing moralistic and pessimistic attitudes or holding stereotypical views that may impede early identification, treatment, and referral. Moralistic attitudes may include punitive notions about substance use problems in general, with particular beliefs that addiction is a matter of weak will, and pessimistic attitudes with the idea that little or nothing can be done about addiction.[10] If nurses believe that some health problems occur as the result of patients’ immoral and/or criminal behaviors, rather than as significant health issues, the nursing care they provide may be influenced by these attitudes[11] and favor punishment and interdiction rather than the provision of comprehensive prevention and treatment services to these patients.[12]

As front-liners of primary health care, nurses need to be aware of their own beliefs and ideas, as well as those of their peers, about the many aspects of substance abuse, so that they can provide care that is unbiased and supportive. The alarming prevalence of substance use in primary care settings dictates the need for examination of the attitudes of the health care providers who are responsible for identifying the problem. Several studies have indicated that these attitudes can be significantly improved through educational interventions.[131415] Additionally, there is evidence that incorporating substance use content into the nursing curriculum may increase students’ clinical competence in dealing with patients who have a substance use problem. As the nurse assumes a vital role in primary care, having the knowledge and skill to identify clients with a substance use problem is imperative to intervene appropriately and prevent further complications from developing. The literature on understanding nurses’ attitudes toward clients with substance use problems in India is limited. Therefore, the purpose of this study is to examine factors influencing nurses’ attitudes toward clients with substance use problems.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

A cross-sectional quantitative study was conducted utilizing a total enumeration technique to explore the factors influencing nurses’ attitudes toward patients with substance use disorders. Google forms were circulated among 310 registered nurses who were working in different tertiary hospitals and were meeting designated inclusion criteria and were willing to participate in the study.

Among 310, a total of 272 registered nurses filled google forms. The response rate was 87.74%. The study sample comprised 272 registered nurses. Participants were required to have a minimum of 3 months of experience in their current unit, possess proficiency in the English language, and provide informed consent to participate. Before enrolment, the study’s objectives and purpose were explained to the participants, and confidentiality and anonymity were strictly maintained. Ethical approval for the study was granted by the Institutional Ethics Committee (Ref. No. AIIMSA00805/05.03.2024).

Tools for data collection

Data were collected using self-structured and standardized questionnaires. A data sheet was developed to collect information on sociodemographic variables. Nurses’ attitudes toward substance abuse were assessed using the Brief Substance Abuse Attitude Survey (BSAAS), a 25-item instrument with established psychometric properties. Each item is rated on a five-point Likert scale, ranging from strongly agree to strongly disagree. The raw scores are initially calculated and then converted using a standardized table provided by the questionnaire developers. A higher score indicates a more positive attitude. The BSAAS evaluates four key factors: stereotype, moralism, treatment optimism, and treatment intervention, with subscale reliability coefficients ranging from 0.63 to 0.77.

Statistical analysis

The current study conducted data analysis and interpretation to reduce, organize, and give meaning to the data. Statistical analyses were carried out using SPSS software (version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, N.Y., USA). Both descriptive and inferential statistics were employed to analyze the data from the current study. Descriptive statistics, including percentages, means, and standard deviations, were calculated. Independent t-tests and ANOVA were conducted to examine differences in mean attitude scores. Pearson’s correlation was applied to evaluate the relationships between dependent variables. Multivariate linear regression analyses were performed to identify predictors of nurses’ attitudes toward clients with substance use problems. All statistical tests were two-tailed, with statistical significance set at P ≤ 0.05.

Factors associated with nurses’ attitudes

Table 2 outlines the differences in attitude scores based on demographic and professional factors. The results of correlational statistics [Table 3] showed a small but statistically significant negative correlation between age and attitude scores (r = –0.05, P ≤ 0.05), indicating that older nurses may have slightly less positive attitudes. Similar to age, more significant work experience (r = –0.05, P ≤ 0.05) was associated with slightly lower attitude scores. Total hours of in-service education and continuing education showed a weak positive correlation (r = 0.02, P ≤ 0.05), suggesting that more education hours may slightly improve attitudes, but the effect is minimal. Additionally, the results of hierarchical regression analysis [Table 4] revealed that age had a small but significant positive effect on attitudes (B = 0.16, P = 0.049). The work unit variable demonstrated a significant negative association (B = –0.40, P = 0.033), indicating that nurses in nonpsychiatric settings held less positive attitudes compared to those in psychiatric units. Education level was positively associated with attitude scores (B = 6.61, P = 0.018), suggesting that higher educational attainment correlates with more favorable views toward patients with substance use problems. Although nurses with prior experience caring for substance use clients tended to have more positive attitudes, this relationship was not statistically significant (P = 0.143).

DISCUSSION

The present study highlights the attitudes of health nurses in tertiary centers, showing that they generally hold moderately positive attitudes toward patients with substance use disorders.

Findings from this study revealed that health nurses working in tertiary centers had a moderately positive attitude toward patients with substance use problems. Talking about sociodemographic variables such as age, educational levels, current work unit, taking care of substance abuse clients, total working experiences, and in-service education were determinants affecting attitude toward substance abuse clients. Consistent with the present findings, the study[616] done among undergraduate students’ perception regarding substance users reported positive views, and participants rejected the stereotypes of unpleasant feelings about working or caring with these patients. Congruent findings were also observed in a study;[17] they said that professional working in the psychiatric department feels that drug and alcohol dependence are treatable illnesses and they work with nondiscriminatory attitudes.

Apart from the positive attitude reported in the present study, the authors[18192021] reported a negative attitude was reported among health care providers. These variations may be due to the administered tool and different study settings compared to the present study. Among health care providers, nurses had a more negative attitude (64%) than psychologists (54%), whereas the study concluded that the attitude toward patients with substance abuse disorder is influenced by the time spent with the client, so nurses are more directly involved in the care of these patients compared to other health care providers.[22] That is why nurses are more vulnerable to violence. This vulnerability may act as a barrier to treatment.[1923] and may contribute to the more negative attitude of nurses. The study reported that half of the respondents aged less than 40 years had a positive attitude toward substance use. The present study also found that participants’ age acts as an influencing factor for attitude. Substance abuse is more socially acceptable among the younger generations, as reported by a study.[24] This may explain the attitude of the younger health workers in the current study.

In the present study, the mean score of the participants who were working in psychiatric departments was a more positive attitude compared to nonpsychiatric nurses; a congruent result was also observed in the studies,[20212225] in which it has been concluded that those who had worked in the psychiatry ward for over 5 years had a positive attitude compared to 37.5% among those who had worked for less than 5 years. This reporting may be because of more exposure to patients with substance use problems, which makes nurses less stigmatized and leads to a more positive attitude. A study[26] done among the general population identified sociodemographic factors such as gender, age, education, religious beliefs, and history of addiction treatment as influencing attitudes toward addicted individuals; congruent results were also reported concerning variables like age and education only. The present study talks about the sociodemographic variables such as age, educational levels, current work unit, taking care of substance abuse clients, total working experiences, and in-service education, which were determinants affecting attitude toward substance abuse clients, whereas inconsistent results were also reported by the study[27] that sociodemographic variables such as gender, age, years of experience, and history of addiction did not affect the attitude score. Inconsistent with present findings, studies in the literature also suggested that age[27] and gender[27] do not affect attitudes both in society and among healthcare professionals, similar to the results of the studies.[26]

The present study indicates that participants with a bachelor’s degree had a higher attitude score as compared to diploma nurses, a consistent result found in the study[27] that noted that individuals with higher levels of education demonstrated elevated ASAS scores. This may be understood by higher education, making a person think more broadly and leading to a positive attitude. In the present study, the mean score of the participants who were working in psychiatric departments was a more positive attitude compared to nonpsychiatric nurses; a noncongruent result was revealed in the study[1927] and concluded no significant disparities in scale scores between those who had prior experience working with individuals with addiction and those who had not.

Interestingly, in the study mentioned, there were no significant differences in scale scores between those who had prior experience working with an addict and those who had not. This inconsistency could be because of more exposure to patients with substance use problems, which makes nurses less stigmatized and leads to a more positive attitude in the present study. In the study, 18.8% of nurses stated they would not socialize with addicted individuals, while 12.6% expressed willingness to improve communication and offer support for their treatment. Early detection and referral to treatment for a nurse suspected of addiction are critical for fostering self-confidence, overcoming denial, and encouraging initiation and continuation of treatment.[2829] However, such a finding was not reported by the present study. A study[30] was conducted among nurses for findings and attitudes toward perinatal substance abuse. This study reported a mean of 33.81, indicating more negative results and factors such as substance abuse information and family history of substance abuse had a positive effect among nurses toward substance abuse. Consistent results were also reported by the present findings in which participants who were taking in-service education, training, or a session regarding substance abuse led to a higher mean of attitude score as compared to those who did not. However, the results were inconclusive with a P value (P > 0.05). Nurses who received training in drug and alcohol were less likely to hold negative attitudes toward drug and alcohol problems,[31] The present study also reported a consistent correlation.

Limitations

Future research could enhance the generalizability of the findings by including a larger sample size and participants from diverse geographical regions and cultures to assess nurses’ perspectives. Future studies should explore broader demographic samples and employ longitudinal designs to better assess changes in attitudes over time and their impact on patient care. The use of a self-report questionnaire may result in respondent bias. Other important limitations were the single-centric study and selection bias as participants who belonged to psychiatric departments or took part in substance use were fewer. Other healthcare providers, such as doctors, psychologists, and social workers, were not included, so comprehensive findings could not be obtained. Due to the cross-sectional nature of the study, causality could not be determined. Therefore, caution is recommended when interpreting the study results. Educational interventions could be implemented to improve mental health nurses’ attitudes toward this, and this education should cater to the specific nursing group who did not work in the psychiatric department.

CONCLUSION

Indian nurses generally hold moderately positive attitudes toward patients with substance use disorders, particularly those working in psychiatric units and with prior experience in caring for such clients. However, variations in attitudes across educational levels, work settings, and exposure to substance use cases highlight the need for targeted interventions. To improve attitudes and care quality, it is essential to implement comprehensive strategies including skill-building workshops, continuous in-service education, and specialized training programs tailored for nurses, particularly those in nonpsychiatric settings.
