Thermal conditions modulate urban forest therapy outcomes: a meta-analytic review
Authors: Doyun Song, Sieon Kim, Minseo Park, Choyun Kim, Chorong Song, Geonwoo Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNature-based interventions (NBIs) such as forest therapy improve stress-related outcomes; however, their efficacy may depend on thermal conditions. This systematic review and meta-analysis investigated the moderating role of thermal conditions on the psycho-physiological outcomes of forest-based interventions. Following PRISMA guidelines (PROSPERO: CRD42022353168), studies of general populations exposed to forests under varying Discomfort Index (DI) conditions were synthesized. Outcomes included heart rate (HR) and Profile of Mood States (POMS; tension, fatigue, confusion, and vigor). A random-effects meta-analysis with subgroup tests by DI category was performed; risk of bias was assessed with ROB 2/ROBINS-I, and certainty of evidence with GRADE. From 3,384 records, 9 studies (30 cases) were included. Under thermally neutral conditions, forest exposure reduced HR (SMD − 1.19, 95% CI − 1.72 to − 0.66) and improved POMS tension (− 1.36, − 2.08 to − 0.64), fatigue (− 0.90, − 1.24 to − 0.57), confusion (− 1.10, − 1.61 to − 0.60), while increasing vigor (+ 1.23, 0.37 to 2.09). In cold and warm-hot conditions, effects were inconsistent. Evidence certainty was moderate for psychological outcomes and very low for physiological outcomes. These results suggest that psycho-physiological benefits of forest exposure appear contingent on thermal conditions. Because the included studies are geographically concentrated and between-study heterogeneity is substantial, the pooled estimates should be interpreted with caution. Prioritizing thermally neutral DI conditions (16–24) may enhance the effectiveness of NBIs, and future trials using standardized thermal indices in more diverse populations are warranted.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-24331-x.Keywords: Forest therapy, Thermal environment, Thermal conditions, Psychological restoration, Discomfort index, Nature-based interventionsSubject terms: Health care, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

In modern urban societies, individuals spend most of their daily lives in built environments, often saturated with psychological and physiological stressors1. These artificial environmental conditions can deplete human psycho-physiological resources, manifesting as cognitive fatigue and emotional distress2,3. Sustained exposure to such environments is associated with an increased risk of stress-related disorders and decreased well-being4,5.

In response, nature-based interventions are non-pharmacological approaches to mitigate urban living’s adverse effects. Growing interdisciplinary evidence indicates that exposure to natural environments, particularly forest settings, helps stress recovery6, mood stabilization7, attentional restoration8, and autonomic regulation9,10. Two prevailing theories provide a theoretical basis for examining these effects. The attention restoration theory explains how nature facilitates effortless attention recovery11,12. The stress reduction theory posits that natural environments elicit calming responses rooted in evolutionary adaptation13–15. Several countries institutionalized forest-based interventions into public health programs16,17. The Shinrin-yoku in Japan18, national forest healing system in South Korea19 and Klimatherapie in Germany20 have integrated forest environments into preventive care, reflecting growing recognition of forests as therapeutic infrastructures within national healthcare systems.

The COVID-19 pandemic highlighted accessible green spaces’ importance21–23. Prolonged lockdowns, reduced mobility, and increased psychological distress led to renewed interest in natural environments as buffers against urban stress24,25. However, recent research suggests that forest-based interventions may not have uniform benefits. Instead, they depend on the natural settings’ specific environmental features, including microclimatic factors such as temperature and humidity26–28. Moreover, recent reviews and syntheses consistently indicate that climate factors29, macro- and micro-climates30, vegetation-indicating parameters31, and urban geometric parameters32 jointly determine outdoor thermal comfort.

Outdoor thermal perceptions and neutral temperatures vary with macro-/micro-climate and context. Consequently, thermal comfort can moderate restorative responses to green exposure. It may influence psychological well-being, stress response, and autonomic function33,34. Recent reviews show climate factors systematically shift neutral temperatures and perceived comfort29 and that water surfaces35 and urban geometry32 significantly alter outdoor thermal environments. Thermally uncomfortable environments may interfere with beneficial recovery effects36. Prior studies have suggested that thermally uncomfortable conditions hinder psychological recovery and physiological relaxation, reducing nature-based interventions’ overall efficacy37,38. However, the influence of thermal comfort on psycho-physiological outcomes remains unclear due to limited synthesis.

This study addressed this gap by systematically reviewing and quantitatively synthesizing available evidence on how thermal comfort moderates forest-based interventions’ impacts on psycho-physiological health outcomes. This review followed the population, intervention, comparator, outcome, and study design (PICOS) framework39, focusing on studies involving general populations exposed to forest environments under different thermal conditions, compared to urban or non-forest settings. The outcomes examined include physiological indicators such as heart rate (HR) and psychological indicators measured using the Profile of Mood States (POMS). This study aimed to offer evidence-based insights to inform the design of more effective, contextually adapted forest therapy programs. These insights have implications for public health policy, urban planning, and preventive healthcare delivery.

Methods

This study followed guidelines established in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA), shown in Supplemental Tables 1, 202040 and Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions41. Moreover, this systematic review and meta-analysis were prospectively registered on PROSPERO (registration number CRD42022353168).

Search strategy and study selection

To identify relevant studies, we used a comprehensive search strategy across PubMed, EBSCO, Scopus, and Web of Science databases, which cover diverse biomedical, psychological, and environmental science literature. We combined search terms related to forest therapy, thermal comfort, and physiological and psychological indicators. We conducted a structured and systematic search using terms related to environmental settings (50), interventions (47), thermal environment (42), and health outcomes (44). The search focused on English-language articles published during 2010-2023 for which full-text versions were accessible. To ensure comprehensive coverage, we also examined reference lists of relevant systematic reviews and studies. Supplemental Table 2 presents details on the search terms.

Following the initial search, the identified records were curated using the Rayyan online application42, reference management software, to ensure data integrity and facilitate screening. Titles and abstracts were screened to identify studies meeting the inclusion criteria. After removing duplicates, four independent investigators (DS, SK, MP, and GK) independently screened titles and abstracts to determine eligibility based on the inclusion criteria.

The first author (DS) used a standardized form to extract the following information from each included study: author’s name, publication year, study location, study design, participants, type of environment under investigation and control, procedural and timing details, thermal environmental variables, outcomes, and outcome measures. Data were extracted into a coding frame using Microsoft Excel, synthesized, and tabulated. For studies presenting data only in figures, data were extracted using WebPlotDigitizer software43. A second researcher (GK) checked all data extraction forms, and the researchers resolved any disagreements through discussion until reaching consensus.

This meta-analysis selected three categories of indicators to evaluate outdoor thermal environments’ effects. First, the thermal environment was operationalized using the Discomfort Index (DI). Second, physiological responses included HR. Third, psychological states were measured using the POMS, comprising a set of scales on negative (e.g., tension, fatigue, and confusion) and positive emotions (e.g., vigor).

In our dataset, participant-reported thermal sensation or comfort votes (e.g., the ASHRAE 7-point scale) were not available; thus, comfort was inferred from exposure-based categories (DI) rather than measured directly as subjective comfort. DI, a widely used composite indicator, combines air temperature and relative humidity to quantify thermal stress on the human body. DI approximates heat stress but is not a comprehensive comfort metric; unlike PMV/PPD (ISO 7730) or outdoor indices such as UTCI and PET, DI does not incorporate wind, radiation, clothing, or metabolic rate. It was selected for large-scale assessments of outdoor thermal discomfort because of its conceptual simplicity, ease of calculation, and broad applicability44, as well as its theoretical relevance and practical utility. Because many included studies did not provide detailed microclimatic data, such as solar radiation, wind speed, or mean radiant temperature, applying more comprehensive indices was impracticable. Air temperature and relative humidity were consistently reported, enabling DI estimation. Due to missing other microclimate variables, more complex thermal environment indices could not be used. The DI categories were defined as follows: DI < 10 = extreme discomfort; 10–15 = moderate discomfort; 15–18 = relatively comfortable; 18–21 = no discomfort; 21–24 = < 50% of the population feels discomfort; 25–27 = > 50% feels discomfort; 28–29 = most of the population feels discomfort; 30–32 = very strong and dangerous discomfort; >32 = medical emergency46,47. For subgroup analyses, thermal conditions were defined using the DI as follows: cold (DI ≤ 15), thermally neutral (16–24), and warm-hot (DI ≥ 25).

Physiological and psychological indicators were chosen based on their relevance and availability across the selected studies. HR is a limited but practical marker of acute autonomic arousal48; decreases generally accompany parasympathetic activation and relaxation. HR is also the most consistently reported physiological variable in the forest-exposure literature, enabling synthesis across heterogeneous designs. POMS is a validated instrument for transient affect with established reliability49 and construct validity and frequent use in nature-based interventions (NBIs) such as forest therapy50; importantly, tension, fatigue, confusion, and vigor subscales were the most commonly reported, facilitating meta-analysis.

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across four electronic databases: PubMed (n = 297), EBSCO (n = 1,666), Web of Science (n = 273), and Scopus (n = 1,415). After removing 231 duplicates, 3,384 records were screened at the title and abstract levels and excluded 3,319 studies because of a lack of relevance. Fifty-nine full texts were reviewed and excluded 44 because of irrelevant interventions (n = 14), lack of quantitative thermal comfort data (n = 24), or inappropriate outcomes (n = 6). An additional four studies were identified through manual screening of references in systematic reviews and meta-analyses. In total, 19 studies met the eligibility criteria, and 30 cases extracted from nine studies were selected for quantitative synthesis. Several studies appear to meet the inclusion criteria; however, microclimate indices could not be extracted for each experimental day51. Supplemental Fig. 1 summarizes the methodological workflow, and Supplemental Fig. 2 is a schematic representation of the literature selection and review protocol.

Study characteristics

For quantitative synthesis, investigators DS, SK, MP, CK, and GK reviewed several study-level variables (e.g., participant characteristics; geographical coordinates; climate classification; season; microclimate information of forest settings; and interventions’ duration, frequency, and activity intensity) to rule out confounding factors. The meta-analysis included 30 cases from nine studies, comprising RCTs, crossover RCTs, and NRCTs, conducted during 2017-2023 in Japan, China, and Poland, which involved healthy adults, university students, and hypertensive patients. Sample sizes ranged from 12 to 120. Participants were exposed to forest environments with diverse microclimatic conditions, including air temperatures of 6–31 °C and relative humidity of 35.5–92.4%. The target sites were classified as urban forests, comprising coniferous forests, deciduous forests, and camphor woodlands. Study settings included both urban forests and urban environments, providing a comprehensive comparative context.

Intervention activities were categorized by exercise intensity into very low (≤ 1.0 MET), such as viewing and sitting, and moderate (≥ 2.0 MET), such as walking and cycling. No cases of intense exercise were observed. Sessions lasted from 11 min to three hours and occurred during different seasons. The comparators included urban environments or baseline conditions without forest exposure. Primary outcomes measured included physiological indicators (HR) and psychological responses (POMS). For studies involving hypertensive participants, we intentionally excluded physiological outcomes and focused on psychological measures. Quality assessment and evidence certainty

To evaluate the included studies’ methodological quality, tool Risk of Bias 2 was used for parallel-group and crossover randomized controlled trials (RCTs)52. Non-randomized controlled trials (NRCTs) were assessed using Risk of Bias in Non-randomized Studies - Interventions45. The Risk of Bias 2 tool evaluated potential biases in the randomization process, trial design, management of dropouts and missing data, and outcome measurement. The ROBINS-I tool assessed biases caused by confounding factors, participant selection, intervention classification, dropout and missing data, outcome measurement, and reporting. Three independent investigators (DS, CS, and GK) assessed the risk bias based on responses to signaling questions for RCTs and NRCTs, respectively.

Evidence quality was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) methodology53,54. We used GRADEPro GDT (https://gradepro.org) to evaluate evidence certainty and generate Supplemental Table 355. According to the GRADE framework, evidence quality was evaluated across five dimensions: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. For each outcome, evidence quality was rated on a scale from high to very low, based on a comprehensive assessment of confidence in effect estimates56. Independent researchers performed all assessments and resolved disagreements through discussion until reaching consensus.

Statistical analysis

The systematic review and meta-analysis followed the PRISMA guidelines40. Statistical analysis was performed using RevMman 7.2.057, R 4.4.0, and RStudio with the “meta,” “metafor,” and other R packages58,59. RevMan was used for forest plot and subgroup analyses to confirm effect differences according to DI-defined thermal conditions. The R program was used for sensitivity and publication bias analyses.

Standardized mean difference (SMD) was used to synthesize outcomes measured with different scales across studies. A random-effects model accounted for between-study heterogeneity because of the hypothesized influence of thermal comfort and related physical factors on the effects of forest exposure. Given the limited number of included studies, this study is a small-scale meta-analysis. The Hartung-Knapp-Sidik-Jonkman method followed the Cochrane Handbook41.

SMD, calculated using a random-effects model, measures efficacy through the mean, standard deviation, and sample size of interventional and control groups. A zero SMD value indicates no difference in effect between the intervention and control conditions. When improvement corresponds to higher (lower) scores on outcome measures, an SMD greater (less) than zero shows the extent to which the intervention exceeds (is less effective than) the control. According to Cohen’s guidelines60, SMD values of 0.20-0.49, 0.50-0.79, and ≥ 0.80 are classified as “small,” “medium,” and “large” effect sizes, respectively.

Heterogeneity and sensitivity analyses

Heterogeneity of effect sizes among the selected studies was assessed using Cochran’s Q test and Higgins’ I2 statistic. A p-value of < 0.10 for the Q test indicates significant heterogeneity. The I2 statistic quantifies the percentage of total variation across studies attributable to heterogeneity rather than chance. I2 values of 25%, 50%, and 75% represent low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively61.

Finally, the “leave-one-out” method was employed to conduct a sensitivity analysis41,62. Publication bias was evaluated through visual analysis using funnel plots and quantitative assessment using Egger’s regression test63,64. When publication bias was detected, trim-and-fill analysis was conducted to estimate and correct the number of missing studies65.

Results

Risk-of-bias results

The included studies’ methodological quality was assessed using validated tools specific to each study design. For RCTs, the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 tool was applied52. This tool evaluates the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of outcomes, and the reported result selection. Both RCTs66,67 had low risk in randomization and intervention but showed concerns in outcome measurement and reporting bias, resulting in an overall rating of “some concerns.”

A risk-of-bias assessment was conducted for crossover RCTs according to Chap. 23 of the Cochrane Handbook68,69. Reference70 was rated as having “some concerns,” because of the consideration of period and carryover effects. In contrast71, was rated as “low risk” across most domains, with adequate consideration of period and carryover effects.

For NRCTs, we used the ROBINS-I tool, which evaluates seven domains: confounding, selection bias, classification of interventions, deviations from intended interventions, missing data, outcome measurement, and selection of the reported result45. All five NRCTs70,72–75 had “moderate” overall risk of bias, primarily because of residual confounding and potential bias in the selection and reporting of outcomes, despite low risk in other domains.

Four reviewers (DS, SK, CK, and GK) conducted assessments independently. They resolved disagreements through discussion or consultation with another reviewer (CS). Table 3 summarizes methodological quality assessments.

Meta-analysis

Thermal environment evaluation

This meta-analysis examined how ambient thermal conditions influenced the effectiveness of forest-based interventions. It used thermal environment categories defined by DI, which incorporates ambient temperature and relative humidity. These categories stratified interventions into warm-hot, neutral, and cold conditions. Statistical analyses evaluated differences in physiological and psychological outcomes across these conditions.

Physiological outcomes: HR

This meta-analysis assessed the effects on HR of thermal environments classified as warm-hot, neutral, and cold, based on DI (Fig. 1). In the neutral subgroup (n = 14), forest exposure significantly reduced HR compared to control conditions (SMD = -1.19; 95% CI = -1.72 to − 0.66; p = 0.0003), indicating a large effect size according to Cohen’s criteria. However, substantial heterogeneity was present (I2 = 89%, p < 0.00001), suggesting considerable variation among studies. Potential sources include differences in participant demographics, forest types, seasonality, and intervention duration.

Meta-analysis results of heart rate according to thermal environment.

In the warm-hot subgroup (n = 2), significant HR reduction was observed (SMD = -2.14; 95% CI = -3.70 to − 0.58; p = 0.04), with no observed heterogeneity (I2 = 0%). However, the small number of studies limits robustness and generalizability. The cold subgroup (n = 2) did not yield a significant effect (SMD = -1.49; 95% CI = -9.18 to 6.20; p = 0.25), whereas heterogeneity was substantial (I2 = 68%, p = 0.08).

The overall pooled estimate across all subgroups indicated significant HR reduction (SMD = -1.32; 95% CI = -1.76 to − 0.88; p < 0.00001) but high heterogeneity (I2 = 87%). A test for subgroup differences yielded a significant result (p = 0.002), suggesting that thermal conditions moderate forest therapy’s physiological impact.

Psychological outcomes: tension

Meta-analysis of 20 comparisons across nine studies evaluated how forest exposure affected the tension psychological indicator from the POMS scale (Fig. 2). In the neutral subgroup (n = 12), forest exposure significantly reduced tension (SMD = -1.36; 95% CI = -2.08 to − 0.64; p = 0.0001), with a large effect size. However, heterogeneity was high (I2 = 82%, p < 0.00001), indicating variability in effect magnitude between studies, likely because of variations in measurement timing, population health status, or intervention type (e.g., walking vs. viewing).

Meta-analysis results of POMS (tension) according to thermal environment.

The warm-hot subgroup (n = 1) yielded a nonsignificant effect (SMD = 0.08; 95% CI = -0.91 to 1.08; p = 0.83), and no heterogeneity could be assessed due to the single comparison. The cold subgroup (n = 7) yielded no significant tension reduction (SMD = 0.23; 95% CI = -1.02 to 1.48; p = 0.52) and high heterogeneity (I2 = 80%). The wide confidence interval and nonsignificant effect raise concerns regarding statistical power and sampling error.

The overall pooled analysis showed a significant overall reduction in tension (SMD = -0.74; 95% CI = -1.46 to − 0.03; p = 0.008) but with extremely high heterogeneity (I2 = 90%). The subgroup comparison showed significant differences (p < 0.0001), indicating that thermally neutral modifies psychological outcomes.

Psychological outcomes: fatigue

Meta-analysis of 20 comparisons across nine studies indicated forest exposure’s impact on the fatigue psychological indicator from the POMS scale (Fig. 3). In the neutral subgroup (n = 12), it significantly reduced fatigue (SMD = -0.90; 95% CI = -1.24 to − 0.57; p < 0.0001), exhibiting a medium-to-large effect size. Heterogeneity was moderate (I2 = 59%, p = 0.006), indicating inter-study variation, possibly due to seasonal effects or variation in intervention duration.

Meta-analysis results of POMS (fatigue) according to thermal environment.

The cold subgroup (n = 7) showed no significant effect (SMD = 0.33; 95% CI = -1.03 to 1.37; p = 0.47), with high heterogeneity (I2 = 83%, p = 0.0001), indicating imprecision and low statistical power. The warm-hot subgroup (n = 1) also yielded a non-significant result (SMD = -0.32; 95% CI = -1.08 to 0.45; p = 0.42). Heterogeneity was not assessed due to the single data point.

The overall pooled analysis showed a significant reduction in fatigue (SMD = -0.48; 95% CI = -0.91 to − 0.05; p = 0.03) with high heterogeneity (I2 = 83%). Subgroup analysis revealed significant differences (χ2 = 6.43, df = 2, p = 0.01), suggesting that thermally neutral moderates the effectiveness of forest therapy in reducing fatigue.

Psychological outcomes: confusion

The meta-analysis of 20 comparisons across nine studies evaluated the effect of forest exposure on confusion, a psychological indicator from the POMS scale (Fig. 4). In the neutral subgroup (n = 12), forest therapy significantly reduced confusion (SMD = -1.10; 95% CI = -1.61 to − 0.60; p = 0.0006), corresponding to a large effect size. Substantial heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 83%, p < 0.00001), possibly due to variation in participant characteristics or exposure duration.

Meta-analysis results of POMS (confusion) according to thermal environment.

In contrast, the cold subgroup (n = 7) showed no significant change (SMD = -0.33; 95% CI = -1.11 to 0.45; p = 0.38), with high heterogeneity (I2 = 77%, p = 0.001), potentially reflecting measurement variability or contextual differences. The warm-hot subgroup included only one comparison (SMD = 0.21; 95% CI = -0.55 to 0.97; p = 0.58), yielding a non-significant result with no heterogeneity assessment.

The overall pooled estimate showed a significant reduction in confusion (SMD = -0.55; 95% CI = -1.06 to − 0.05; p = 0.03) with substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 89%). Subgroup differences were significant (χ2 = 16.03, df = 2, p = 0.0003), suggesting that thermal conditions may influence forest exposure’s psychological restoration effect on confusion.

Psychological outcomes: Vigor

Meta-analysis of 20 comparisons across nine studies evaluated how forest exposure affected vigor, a psychological indicator from the POMS scale (Fig. 5). In the neutral subgroup (n = 12), forest exposure significantly increased vigor (SMD = 1.23; 95% CI: 0.37 to 2.09; p = 0.009), with a large effect size. However, heterogeneity was extremely high (I2 = 94%, p < 0.00001), indicating wide variability in outcomes likely arising from differing intervention intensities, session durations, or sample characteristics.

Meta-analysis results of POMS (vigor) according to thermal environment.

The cold subgroup (n = 7) also showed statistically significant improvement (SMD = 0.72; 95% CI: 0.02 to 1.41; p = 0.04), although with a smaller effect size. Heterogeneity was moderate (I2 = 54%, p = 0.04). Only one study was available in the warm-hot subgroup (SMD = -0.28; 95% CI = -1.05 to 0.48; p = 0.47), showing a non-significant result.

The overall effect size was statistically significant (SMD = 1.01; 95% CI: 0.45 to 1.57; p = 0.001) with high heterogeneity (I2 = 90%). Subgroup differences were significant (χ2 = 7.95, df = 2, p = 0.02), indicating that thermal context influences positive affective outcomes in forest-based interventions.

Exercise intensity in a thermally neutral environment

DI used has limitations in reflecting individuals’ perception of thermal comfort, which varies according to physical exertion intensity76–78. Forest activities generally require considerable exercise. However, such activity can be challenging for older adults and people with limited mobility. If forest activities’ positive effects differ depending on exercise intensity, or if there is no difference, this becomes an important criterion for forest exposure for these groups. Therefore, we classified studies by exercise intensity and examined changes in psychological outcomes within thermally neutral environments.

The analysis focused on thermally neutral conditions to (1) include sufficient studies and (2) ensure consistent baseline thermal conditions, reducing potential bias in interpreting exercise intensity effects. This meta-analysis examined only psychological indicators because physical activity influences physiological indicators. This enabled the assessment of exercise intensity’s psychological effects while minimizing confounding effects related to thermal variability. Supplemental Figs. S3–S6 show psychological changes according to exercise intensity in thermally neutral environments.

For tension, both very low and moderate intensity exercises were associated with significant reductions, with SMDs of − 1.19 (95% CI = -1.89 to − 0.49, p = 0.005) and − 1.70 (95% CI = -2.82 to − 0.59, p = 0.02), respectively. The overall pooled effect was large (SMD = -1.36, 95% CI = -1.87 to − 0.85, p = 0.001). However, the analysis did not show a significant difference in subgroup outcomes (χ2 = 1.26, p = 0.26), suggesting that the observed variations in effect size among intensity levels are not statistically significant.

For fatigue, both subgroups showed significant reductions. The SMD was − 0.78 (95% CI = -1.26 to − 0.29, p = 0.007) for very low intensity and − 1.19 (95% CI = -1.66 to − 0.72, p = 0.004) for moderate intensity. The overall pooled effect was also significant (SMD = -0.90, 95% CI = -1.24 to − 0.57, p < 0.0001). Although the subgroup difference was not statistically significant (χ2 = 2.63, p = 0.11), a trend toward a larger effect at higher intensity was observed.

For confusion, we observed significant reductions at both intensity levels. Very low intensity produced an SMD of − 0.95 (95% CI = -1.70 to − 0.21, p = 0.02), and moderate intensity produced an SMD of − 1.44 (95% CI = -2.33 to − 0.55, p = 0.01). The overall effect was significant (SMD = -1.10, 95% CI = -1.61 to − 0.60, p = 0.0006), whereas subgroup differences were not (χ2 = 1.35, p = 0.25).

For vigor, the overall pooled effect was significant (SMD = 1.23, 95% CI = 0.37 to 2.09, p = 0.009). However, neither the very low (SMD = 0.92, 95% CI = -0.08 to 1.92) nor the moderate intensity subgroup (SMD = 1.87, 95% CI = -0.70 to 4.44) showed individually significant effects. Heterogeneity was extremely high (I2 = 94%), and the subgroup difference was not significant (χ2 = 1.09, p = 0.30), indicating inconsistent results across intensity levels.

This meta-analysis suggests that physical activity, regardless of intensity, may provide psychological benefits. Moderate-intensity exercise showed larger effect sizes overall. For all other POMS domains, a lack of significant between-group differences indicates that exercise’s psychological benefits may not depend linearly on intensity.

Sensitivity analysis

To assess the meta-analytic findings’ robustness, leave-one-out sensitivity analyses were conducted for both arm-level and contrast-level data across all nine outcomes, including physiological (HR) and psychological (tension, fatigue, confusion, and vigor). Supplemental Figs. S7, S8, S9, S10 and S11 present results of these analyses.

Across all arm-level analyses, the pooled effect sizes showed directional stability and statistical significance, even under leave-one-out conditions, substantiating the findings’ robustness. The HR physiological outcome (SMD = -1.56; I2 = 82.7%) showed robust effects, with minimal changes observed when individual studies were excluded. Among psychological outcomes, confusion, depression, and tension displayed stable but more heterogeneous effects (I2>75%). Vigor (SMD = 1.10; I2 = 89.6%) showed a consistent positive outcome, substantiating the stability of effect estimates regardless of study exclusion. However, contrast-level analyses showed lower consistency and did not reach statistical significance across all outcomes. The effect directions were often reversed or reduced, and heterogeneity was generally high (I2>80%), undermining these estimates’ reliability, which may be attributable to the limited number of contrast-level studies.

Overall, the sensitivity analyses confirmed the arm-level findings’ robustness as excluding individual trials did not alter effects’ direction, significance, or pooled estimates. This supports the observed effects’ internal validity, particularly HR, tension, fatigue, confusion, and vigor.

Publication bias and evidence certainty

To assess potential publication bias and small-study effects, we conducted Egger’s regression test and applied the trim-and-fill method for each outcome. Supplemental Tables 4 and 5 and Supplemental Figs. S12 and S13 present the analysis results. For fatigue and confusion, Egger’s test indicated significant funnel plot asymmetry (p < 0.05), suggesting small-study effects or selective reporting bias. In contrast, no evidence of asymmetry was observed for tension, vigor, and HR (p > 0.10), indicating a lower likelihood of publication bias. To further quantify the potential impact of publication bias, we applied the trim-and-fill method. Notably, no additional studies were imputed in any outcome domain. This suggests that although Egger’s test flagged possible asymmetry in some outcomes, the effect on the overall pooled estimates was likely minimal.

Evidence certainty for each outcome was assessed using the GRADE approach, which evaluates evidence quality based on study limitations, consistency of effect, imprecision, indirectness, and publication bias40. Supplemental Table 3 presents the results. For the HR physiological indicator, overall certainty was rated as very low because of concerns about methodological limitations and substantial heterogeneity among studies, with I2 values exceeding 70%. Most included trials were NRCTs and implemented minimal blinding or allocation concealment, leading to an initial low rating and further downgrading.

In contrast, the POMS scale, originally selected due to its inclusion in RCTs and crossover RCTs, demonstrated high-level certainty in tension, fatigue, confusion, and vigor. However, these outcomes were ultimately rated as having moderate certainty.

Overall, psychological indicators showed moderate certainty, whereas physiological outcomes were supported by weaker evidence. Although the effects’ direction was consistent, the small sample sizes and methodological limitations reduced the overall confidence.

Discussion

Numerous studies have demonstrated the beneficial effects of forest environments on human well-being79,80. The next challenge is to identify which specific structural and environmental forest features most effectively enhance these benefits. We examined various contributing factors81–83 including temperature84, relative humidity, altitude85,86, stand density87,88, and canopy structure86. However, various structural variables’ health-related effects are not fully understood.

This systematic review and meta-analysis examined how thermally neutral affected psycho-physiological outcomes in urban forest therapy, integrating data from 30 cases across nine studies. Results showed consistent and significant improvements in the physiological (HR) and psychological (POMS) outcomes, particularly under thermally neutral conditions. High heterogeneity (I2>80%) indicates potential variability in populations, climates, or forest types. Subgroup analyses by region or intervention type may further clarify these sources.

Specifically, HR showed a large and statistically significant reduction under thermally neutral environments, with an SMD of − 1.19 (95% CI = -1.72 to − 0.66) based on 14 studies. The physiological results of the studies all targeted the immediate aftermath of the intervention, making it challenging to confirm the long-term stabilizing effect. Nonetheless, physiological indicators show a consistent decrease in HR among participants exposed to a thermally neutral forest environment, suggesting enhanced parasympathetic nervous activity or relaxation responses. These findings align with existing research indicating the stress-reducing effects of forest environments89,90. This effect may indicate potential cardiovascular benefits associated with forest exposure under thermally neutral conditions.

In thermally neutral conditions, we consistently observed improvements across all four subscales of the POMS. However, these results must be interpreted with caution because of moderate-to-high heterogeneity. Negative mood states, including tension (SMD = -1.36; 95% CI = -2.08 to − 0.64), fatigue (SMD = -0.90; 95% CI = -1.24 to − 0.57), and confusion (SMD = -1.10; 95% CI = -1.61 to − 0.60), showed significant decreases, whereas we found a substantial increase in vigor (SMD = 1.23; 95% CI = 0.37 to 2.09), indicating a more positive emotional state. In contrast, under cold or warm-hot conditions, psychological benefits were inconsistent or statistically nonsignificant. In cold conditions, tension (SMD = 0.23; 95% CI = -1.02 to 1.48) did not show clear improvements. In warm-hot conditions, interpretation remains limited because of the small number of studies. This suggests that thermal comfort may promote more positive emotional outcomes during forest therapy and reduce thermally uncomfortable environments’ adverse effects. Thermal discomfort may act as an environmental stressor that disrupts psychological restoration processes, consistent with research showing that physical discomfort, including thermal strain, can diminish natural environments’ positive effects on mental health38,91.

Meta-analytic findings show that in thermally neutral environments, differences in psychological outcomes according to exercise intensity are not consistent. Both very low (≤ 1.0 MET) and moderate (≥ 2.0 MET) intensity activities produced significant psychological benefits, although these effects’ magnitude did not differ significantly. Tension decreased similarly in both intensity groups (very low: SMD = -1.19; moderate: SMD = -1.70; p = 0.26). These findings indicate that psychological benefits may appear even at low-level exercise intensity, given a thermally neutral environment. The findings highlight the potential moderating role of thermal comfort in psychological responses to exercise and suggest that environmental context may play an important role, alongside exercise parameters. Therefore, forest-based interventions not requiring vigorous physical exertion may still be effective in improving emotional well-being.

Most studies were conducted in East Asia. Thermal comfort perceptions vary across climates, even at the same temperature92–94, which may explain differences in analysis results95. Reanalyzing the data with only East Asian (excluding European studies67 reduced overall heterogeneity without altering research direction (Supplementary Figs. S14–S17). All studies regarding physiological factors were conducted in East Asia.

Despite the meta-analytical findings, methodological limitations warrant cautious interpretation of the results. The overall evidence certainty for physiological outcomes was rated as very low, primarily because of non-randomized designs, limited blinding procedures, and considerable between-study heterogeneity (I2>70%). Such heterogeneity hinders direct comparisons across studies and limits the interpretability of pooled estimates. Several subgroup analyses, particularly those stratified by warm-hot and cold conditions, relied on a limited number of studies or even single-study estimates. Small sample sizes reduced statistical power and results’ generalizability. DI, a pragmatic metric for quantifying thermal exposure, was used in numerous studies because of its consistent reporting. However, DI has certain limitations. Specifically, it does not consider critical factors such as wind speed, solar radiation, and individual metabolic rate, potentially oversimplifying the thermal load experienced during interventions. Future research should consider more comprehensive thermal indices, such as the Physiological Equivalent Temperature or Universal Thermal Climate Index, to improve ecological validity. While Egger’s test suggested the potential for asymmetry in selected psychological outcomes, the trim-and-fill method did not incorporate imputed studies. Nonetheless, the possibility of small-study effects cannot be excluded, which could potentially affect the synthesized evidence.

From this perspective, the results should be interpreted with caution. Measurement variability, individual thermal sensitivity, and design heterogeneity may influence observed effects. Standardized protocols and controlled thermal conditions are needed to clarify mechanisms by which thermal comfort contributes to psychological efficacy in forest-based interventions. Even with these caveats, the evidence indicates that thermal conditions provided by forest microclimates may act as a potential moderator of therapeutic effects, supporting the selection of suitable natural environments for health promotion. These recommendations are subject to the limitations of the evidence base. DI indexes exposure, and thermal comfort is subjective. Aligning session timing and microsite selection with thermally comfortable conditions may improve program efficacy. For DI ≥ 24 °C, programs may consider shorter or lower-intensity sessions, increasing shade and airflow (e.g., denser canopy, wind corridors), and scheduling sessions earlier or later in the day. For DI < 21 °C, programs may prioritize wind shelter and layered clothing, and include brief warming phases.

Taken together, urban forest therapy appears to foster physiological and psychological well-being, particularly under thermally neutral conditions, whereas benefits diminish under thermal discomfort. Despite moderate-to-high heterogeneity, the consistent direction of effects supports a plausible link between thermal comfort and health outcomes. However, many studies were excluded due to missing environmental data, such as site location, dominant tree species, forest density, and microclimatic conditions96. To accurately evaluate therapeutic effects, future studies should consistently report these variables and examine how forest structure, vegetation type, and site-level microclimate interact to shape health outcomes. A clearer understanding of these relationships could improve predictive models and inform more effective, evidence-based forest-therapy interventions97,98.
