Symptom clusters and sentinel symptoms during post-transplant recovery in recipients of allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Ping Xu, Shuyi Ding, Li Zheng, Yin Cheng, Yishan Ye, Lin Li, Zhiru Li, Yuexian Tao, Huafen Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study explored symptom clusters and sentinel symptoms among recipients of allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT) during the recovery period, with the aim of identifying clustering patterns and establishing a clinical reference framework for early detection and management.MethodsA convenience sample of 209 hematologic oncology recipients who had completed allo-HSCT and were in the recovery phase, attending regular outpatient follow-up in China between December 2024 and March 2025, was recruited. Data were collected using a general information questionnaire and the Chinese version of the Memorial Symptom Assessment Scale. Symptom clusters were identified through exploratory factor analysis, and sentinel symptoms were determined via association analysis using the Apriori algorithm.ResultsA total of 209 allo-HSCT recipients were included, with a median age of 39.6 years (interquartile range [IQR] 33.7–48.2) and a median time from diagnosis to transplantation of 6.0 months (IQR 4.8–9.8). During recovery, recipients reported a median of 5 symptoms (IQR 3–9), with a maximum of 18 concurrent symptoms. The most prevalent symptoms were lack of energy (56.5%), worrying (43.5%), and difficulty sleeping (42.1%), with median severity scores of 3.0 (IQR 1.0–4.0), 3.0 (IQR 1.0–4.0), and 2.0 (IQR 1.0–3.0), respectively. Exploratory factor analysis (Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin [KMO] ​= ​0.737; Bartlett's χ2 ​= ​1269.134, P ​< ​0.001) identified four symptom clusters—psychological, fatigue, digestive, and neurological—explaining 68.4% of the total variance. Association rule analysis using the Apriori algorithm revealed anxiety as the sentinel symptom of the psychological cluster, difficulty sleeping as the sentinel symptom of the fatigue cluster and itching as the sentinel symptom of the neurological cluster; no sentinel symptom was identified for the digestive cluster.ConclusionsRecipients of allo-HSCT continue to experience diverse symptoms during recovery, which aggregate into distinct clusters. Importantly, three sentinel symptoms act as early indicators that markedly impair quality of life. Nurses should prioritize these sentinel symptoms as key intervention targets to guide precision nursing strategies, mitigate symptom progression, and improve clinical outcomes.

Introduction

Hematologic malignancies (HM), characterized by high aggressiveness, rapid progression, and significant treatment challenges, now rank as the fourth most prevalent type of cancer worldwide.1 Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) remains the primary therapeutic approach for HM.2 Allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT), compared to autologous transplantation, elicits a stronger graft-versus-tumor effect, which promotes eradication of residual malignant cells and lowers relapse rates, thereby solidifying its status as the preferred therapeutic approach in clinical practice.3 With advancements in transplantation protocols, the population of long-term HSCT recipients continues to expand.4 As a result, as survival outcomes improve, awareness of the post-treatment challenges faced by this growing population has become central to its care.5 However, post-transplant recipients frequently endure multifaceted physical, emotional, and cognitive sequelae. These include fatigue, appetite disturbances, anxiety, and related comorbidities, which often co-occur and persist for months to years, exerting prolonged detrimental effects on quality of life.6 The significant challenges, needs, and growth that allo-HSCT survivors experienced during survivorship highlight the urgent requirement for targeted survivor-centered care; however, the symptom clusters to these experiences remain inadequately explored, thus necessitating focused research to inform effective interventions.7

Symptom clusters are defined as two or more clinically interrelated symptoms that co-occur temporally while maintaining distinct phenomenological characteristics.8 Allo-HSCT recovery is a complex and lengthy process, and long-term survivors frequently report enduring clusters of co-occurring symptoms.9 While most recipients achieve effective control of graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) by one year post-transplantation,10 expert consensus indicates that substantial functional recovery is generally achieved by two years post-transplantation.11 Most HSCT recipients experience several physical, emotional, and cognitive symptoms.12,13 Current research on HSCT-associated symptom clusters primarily examines pre-transplant preparation, acute post-transplant phases (< 1 year), and long-term follow-up (> 2 years).14, 15, 16 However, there is a notable lack of studies specifically investigating symptom clusters in allo-HSCT recipients during the 1–2-year recovery period. This transitional phase—pivotal for connecting acute recovery to long-term survival—involves critical shifts in symptom cluster correlations.17 The existing knowledge gap severely limits clinicians' ability to provide targeted interventions during recovery monitoring.18,19 Addressing this gap through systematic investigation of symptom clusters in this period is essential for optimizing symptom management strategies and improving clinical monitoring efficacy.

To identify actionable targets for symptom cluster interventions, Brown et al.20 introduced “sentinel symptoms.” These specific symptoms appear early within a cluster, serving as indicators that predict or trigger the onset or worsening of other related symptoms.21 In clinical practice, sentinel symptoms are valuable assessment tools. Identifying them may help uncover the underlying mechanisms of symptom cluster formation, streamlining the assessment and management of complex symptom groups. As early warning signs, they are critical intervention points because they can predict and influence symptom progression.22 Clinically, identifying sentinel symptoms may elucidate the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms driving symptom clustering, thereby streamlining the assessment and management protocols.23 These sentinel symptoms have now been preliminarily applied in cancer patient populations, with their clinical utility further validated.24 Therefore, research on sentinel symptoms holds critical significance for enabling targeted interventions for symptom clusters.25 This study aims to systematically investigate symptom profiles, identify characteristic symptom clusters, and explore potential sentinel symptoms in allo-HSCT recipients during the 1- to 2-year recovery phase. The findings are expected to inform evidence-based symptom management frameworks tailored to this vulnerable population.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study aimed to investigate the symptom clusters and sentinel symptoms in recipients during the recovery phase following allo-HSCT, with the dual objectives of characterizing symptom clustering patterns and establishing clinical references for early identification and management. The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) Statement26 was used as a guideline in this study (Supplementary File 1). This study employed a convenience sampling method to recruit recipients with hematologic malignancies who had undergone allo-HSCT 1–2 years prior, were in the recovery phase, and attended regular outpatient follow-ups at a tertiary grade-A hospital between December 2024 and March 2025. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (a) Histopathologically or cytologically confirmed diagnosis of hematologic malignancy; (b) Completed allo-HSCT with confirmed clinical engraftment; (c) Regular outpatient follow-up and a post-transplantation duration of 12–24 months; (d) Ability to comprehend questionnaire content and communicate verbally; (e) Recipients with full awareness of their diagnosis, who provided informed consent and voluntarily participated in the study. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (a) End-stage disease; (b) History of severe psychiatric disorders or concurrent malignancies; (c) Current participation in other clinical trials that may confound study outcomes; (d) Post-transplantation relapse or secondary HSCT.

Sample size

The sample size was determined based on requirements for exploratory factor analysis,27 which stipulates a minimum sample size of 5 times the number of scale items. The symptom assessment scale used in this study contains 32 items. Multiplying this by 5 yielded an initial sample size of 160. To account for potential data collection inefficiencies common in telephone surveys, such as refusal to participate, non-contact, and incomplete responses, an expected non-response/incompletion rate of 10% was used:Finalsamplesize=Initialsamplesize1−expectednon−response/incompletionrate=1601−0.10=1600.9≈178

The final required sample size was calculated to be at least 178 participants.

Data collection

After screening allo-HSCT recipients through the hospital's electronic medical record system to confirm their eligibility based on inclusion/exclusion criteria, researchers trained in unison contacted recipients by phone. The research team consisted of nursing graduate students, HSCT nurses, and experts in the transplant field. Interviews were conducted in a dedicated, quiet room within the department, equipped with a designated follow-up telephone. Although participants attended outpatient follow-ups, we prioritized telephone interviews to avoid prolonging clinic visits, accommodate unpredictable recovery schedules of allo-HSCT recipients, minimize infection risks for immunocompromised individuals, and ensure standardized symptom assessment across diverse locations, with quality assurance provided by trained nursing postgraduates. After screening allo-HSCT recipients through the hospital's electronic medical record system to confirm eligibility based on inclusion/exclusion criteria, members of the research team contacted eligible recipients by phone. The research team comprised three graduate nursing researchers (two master's candidates and one PhD candidate), four senior HSCT clinical nurses holding advanced professional titles (Chief Nurse/Associate Chief Nurse/Supervisor Nurse), and two HSCT physicians (MD/PhD), thus combining research execution capabilities with specialized HSCT clinical expertise. The telephone survey interviews were primarily conducted by two of the graduate student researchers, while the senior clinical nurses and physicians provided crucial support through participant screening, oversight and guidance, and subsequent in-depth analyses. To ensure effectiveness and accuracy, recipients were contacted in advance. Researchers politely identified themselves, stated the purpose of the call, inquired about the recipient's availability, and provided an estimated duration for the interview to allow for scheduling. Before commencing the interview, researchers confirmed the participants' environment was suitable and free from excessive distractions to safeguard data quality and reliability. At the start of the call, researchers ensured participants fully understood the study purpose, procedures, and data confidentiality measures. The procedure involved reiterating the research purposes using plain-language explanations, verifying participant comprehension through structured feedback (e.g., “Could you summarize your expected involvement?”), and documenting verbal authorization via dual investigator signatures on consent forms. Additional clarifications were provided as necessary, with strict adherence to voluntary participation principles and privacy protection protocols throughout. All participants received explicit assurances that their data would remain confidential and be used strictly for academic purposes, emphasizing that there were no right or wrong responses and underscoring the imperative of capturing authentic experiences in their own words. Researchers ensured participants understood each item, clarifying any questions promptly to guarantee data authenticity and completeness. The questionnaire comprised two sections: recipient self-reports (demographic characteristics and the Chinese version of the Memorial Symptom Assessment Scale) and physician-reported medical data extracted from the EMR system. To ensure response accuracy, a bidirectional “question-confirmation” communication model was employed. Researchers inquired about items interactively, providing real-time clarification when necessary. The average interview duration was 15 minutes. During questioning, researchers recorded in real-time the frequency, severity, and distress of symptoms reported by the recipient. For data quality assurance, researchers implemented centralized daily verification of all collected questionnaires. A dual verification protocol for all questionnaires post-collection, including reconciling discrepancies through follow-up communications, cross-validating responses with medical records, and verifying or supplementing incomplete/contradictory data through direct participant confirmation. Data deemed invalid due to apparent carelessness during the interview or significant inconsistencies with electronic medical record records were excluded. All datasets were anonymized according to de-identification standards prior to database entry, with strict adherence to privacy protection protocols throughout the process. From 212 attempted telephone interviews, 209 completed surveys met quality criteria (3 excluded due to non-response to core items stemming from physiological barriers like oral mucositis/pain and psychological mechanisms including distrust and symptom concealment), yielding a 98.58% valid response rate. This rigorous methodology ensured reliable capture of both subjective recipient's experiences and objective clinical parameters while maintaining ethical research standards.

Measures

Demographic information questionnaire

Designed by the research team, the form covers demographic data (age, gender, registration, marital status, education, number of children, insurance type) and disease-related information (hematologic malignancy type, interval from diagnosis to transplant, pre-transplant relapses, chemotherapy cycles, pre-transplant condition, donor relationship, ABO compatibility).

Memorial Symptom Assessment Scale-Chinese version (MSAS-Ch)

Developed by the Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center and adapted into Chinese by scholars like Cheng,28 this scale is widely used in recipients with hematological malignancies and includes a wide range of symptoms, with good reliability and validity. It includes 32 physical and psychological symptoms. For the first part of 24 symptoms, recipients mark " ​× ​" if a symptom is absent. If present, they rate its frequency, severity, and distress. Frequency and severity are assessed on a four-point Likert scale (1 ​= ​“seldom” to 4 ​= ​“continuous”), and distress on a five-point scale (0 ​= ​“none” to 4 ​= ​“much”). The second part of 8 symptoms lacks the frequency dimension but uses the same scoring for other dimensions. MSAS comprised three subscales, namely the physical symptom subscale, the psychological symptom subscale, and the global distress index. The Cronbach's α values for these subscales ranged from 0.782 to 0. 87429, indicating good reliability.

Validity and reliability

This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. In accordance with research ethical guidelines, all study participants provided informed consent. ensuring adherence to appropriate standards for human research. Methods and procedures were conducted in compliance with relevant regulations and guidelines stated in the declaration. The scales used in the study had good reliability and validity. Strict quality control was performed during data collection, including investigator training, standardization of assessment tools and guidelines and screening of participants according to inclusion and exclusion criteria. The investigators ensured completeness, accuracy and authenticity of the data, promptly addressing any missing data by communicating with the recipients.

Data analysis

We established a database using EpiData 3.1 and conducted statistical analyses with SPSS Statistics 26.0 and SPSS Modeler 18.0. Quantitative data following a normal distribution were presented as mean ​± ​standard deviation (Mean ​± ​SD), while data that did not follow a normal distribution were presented as median (interquartile range) [M (P25, P75)]. Qualitative data were described using counts and percentages (%). Exploratory factor analysis was performed using principal component analysis with varimax rotation, extracting factors with eigenvalues ≥ 1 and factor loadings ≥ 0.4. The Apriori algorithm in SPSS Modeler 18.0 was used to identify sentinel symptoms within symptom clusters, with minimum support > 40% and confidence > 60%.

The cohort was aged 16–59 years with a median age of 39.60 [33.65, 48.15] years. Time from diagnosis to transplantation ranged from 1.9 to 201.2 months (median 6.00 [4.80, 9.75] months). Recovery phase symptoms and symptom clusters after allo-HSCT

The number of symptoms in the recovery period of allo-HSCT recipients was 5.00 (3.00, 9.00), with a maximum of 18 symptoms simultaneously. The incidence of each symptom ranges from 0.96% to 56.46%. Lack of energy is the most common symptom (56.46%), followed by Worrying (43.54%) and Difficulty sleeping (42.11%), with severity scores of 3.00 (1.00, 4.00), 3.00 (1.00, 4.00), and 2.00 (1.00, 3.00), respectively. Symptoms with a severity score ≥ 1 were considered to have occurred (incidence ​= ​number of recipients with the symptom / total sample size ​× ​100%). Referring to previous studies, to eliminate interfering factors and ensure clinical significance, 15 symptoms with an incidence ≥ 20% were analyzed. Principal component analysis and varimax rotation were performed based on symptom severity scores. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure yielded a value of 0.737, and Bartlett's sphericity test result of χ2 ​= ​1269.134 (P ​< ​0.001), indicating suitability for exploratory factor analysis. With a factor loading threshold of ≥ 0.4, four factors were extracted, explaining 19.30%, 18.53%, 15.60%, and 14.95% of the variance, respectively, with a cumulative contribution rate of 68.39%. Discussion

The 1- to 2-year period following allo-HSCT is pivotal to recipient outcomes. As evidenced by Kliman D's study,18 recipients surviving ≥ 2 years post-transplant achieve survival rates approaching those of the general population, underscoring the critical prognostic value of this phase for long-term survival. Concurrently, Lin C's research30 demonstrates that while allo-HSCT recipients typically experience quality-of-life declines in the first year, gradual recovery and sustained improvement emerge during the subsequent 1- to 2-year window. This aligns with our study's primary objective—to extract symptom clusters via exploratory factor analysis and identify sentinel symptoms using the Apriori algorithm. Our findings reveal that although recipients continue to experience symptom burdens during this period, both the incidence and severity of these symptoms were lower than those observed at 1-year post-transplantation.14 This amelioration likely reflects the subsidence of acute treatment reactions, immune system reconstruction, and restoration of physiological/psychological functions. Therefore, the 1- to 2-year phase represents a critical transition where focused symptom cluster management, with sentinel symptoms as intervention targets, leverages natural recovery trajectories to optimize long-term survival and quality of life.

Current status of symptoms and symptom clusters during the recovery period in allo-HSCT recipients

The findings of this study revealed four symptom clusters during the recovery period after allo-HSCT: psychological symptom cluster (feeling nervous, feeling irritable, feeling sad, and worrying), fatigue symptom cluster (pain, difficulty sleeping, feeling drowsy, lack of energy, and difficulty concentrating), gastrointestinal symptom cluster (lack of appetite, dry mouth, and diarrhea), and neurological symptom cluster (itching, changes in skin, and numbness/tingling in hands/feet). These results indicate that although various symptoms may emerge during follow-up care for allo-HSCT recipients in the recovery phase, symptoms tend to occur in clustered patterns. Health care professionals should pay particular attention to these symptom clusters and implement preventive measures. Furthermore, developing targeted interventions addressing these symptom clusters may prove crucial for improving the overall symptom experience in this recipient's population during this recovery phase.

Among the 30 symptoms assessed, lack of energy was the most common and severe symptom. The fatigue symptom cluster was notably prevalent during the recovery phase in this population, consistent with findings from Eriksson et al.,31 which emphasized that fatigue during recovery significantly impacts recipients' quality of life. Given its severity and detrimental effects on quality of life, fatigue should be prioritized as a key target for clinical intervention. Previous studies have identified fatigue as a core symptom in cancer recipients, with the fatigue symptom cluster persisting as the most central and enduring cluster across all disease stages post-transplantation.32 This persistence may be attributed to incomplete recovery of hematopoietic function after stem cell transplantation, leading to insufficient erythropoiesis. Reduced hemoglobin levels in red blood cells impair oxygen delivery to tissues and organs, contributing to fatigue,33 while gut microbiota dysbiosis may also play a role.34 Research has identified fatigue symptom clusters not only in HSCT recipients, but also across various cancers such as breast cancer and colorectal cancer.35 This highlights their persistence in HSCT recipients and prevalence in different cancer types. Studies indicate that fatigue peaks around day 100 post-transplant,33 Gielissen et al.'s36 investigation revealed that 41% of participants suffered from profound exhaustion 1–5 years following HSCT, with 32% continuing to experience persistent fatigue 5–10 years post-transplant.it might gradually decreases, but does not return to normal levels seen in the general population. In contrast, for other cancer patients, fatigue tends to be an acute effect of the cancer or its treatment and subsides for most individuals as they resume normal life after treatment ends.37 Despite being a common post-transplant symptom that severely compromises quality of life, fatigue remains poorly understood and inadequately addressed in clinical practice, often being underestimated or undertreated.38 These findings highlight the critical need for fatigue-specific interventions in clinical care to improve physical symptom management and treatment outcomes. Clinicians should routinely assess fatigue severity, address underlying etiologies, and recommend tailored strategies such as sleep/rest optimization and exercise regimens. Personalized approaches should be considered, particularly given evidence that younger allo-HSCT recipients may derive greater benefit from mindfulness-based fatigue interventions.39 Continuous collaboration with recipients remains essential to understand their experiences and refine effective strategies through ongoing evaluation.40

The second symptom cluster identified in this study is the psychological symptom cluster, which aligns with findings from a 5-year longitudinal study by Esser et al.9 Psychological symptoms persist as a stable cluster in allo-HSCT recipients over time. However, previous research has predominantly focused on short-term symptom clusters in the early post-transplant phase, suggesting that anxiety and depression in allo-HSCT recipients predominantly occur during hospitalization.41 Janicsak et al.42 documented high incidence rates of anxiety and depression in allo-HSCT recipients. Notably, our findings extend existing evidence by revealing that mental health challenges continue to affect recipients during the recovery phase. This persistence may stem from chronic complications such as chronic graft-versus-host disease (cGVHD), prolonged treatment burdens causing physical discomfort and dysfunction, insufficient family and social support, and challenges in social function recovery.43 This study also observed differences in symptom composition between the psychological symptom cluster during the recovery phase versus the acute post-transplant phase. Acute-phase psychological distress typically manifests as anxiety and tension,16 whereas worrying emerges as the predominant psychological symptom during recovery, with a prevalence of 43.54% in this investigation. Untreated psychological distress may persist, potentially diminishing quality of life, compromising treatment adherence, and hindering rehabilitation.44 Furthermore, psychological issues could weaken immune function, increase complication risks, delay physical recovery, and ultimately impede social reintegration. Health care providers should prioritize psychological symptom management, as timely interventions are critical for improving prognosis and quality of life. Addressing post-transplant psychological challenges requires comprehensive strategies integrating disease management, psychosocial factors, and individual characteristics,45 Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) can be adapted to target recovery-phase concerns like chronic worrying. ACT help recipients develop psychological flexibility, thereby reducing avoidance behaviors and enhancing engagement in rehabilitation activities. Through multidimensional, ACT-informed approaches, health care teams can alleviate psychological distress more effectively and promote holistic recovery in allo-HSCT recipients.46

This study also identified gastrointestinal and neurological symptom clusters in the recipients. The results of this study demonstrate consistency with the gastrointestinal symptom cluster composition identified by Frödin et al.,47 further validating the stability of gastrointestinal symptom clusters. Compared to the nausea and vomiting occurring within the first postoperative month,48 the incidence and severity of gastrointestinal symptoms during the recovery phase of allo-HSCT recipients had significantly decreased from the acute phase (< 1 month).49 However, persistent symptoms including anorexia, mild diarrhea, and taste alterations were still reported, primarily attributed to chronic GVHD, immunosuppressant side effects (e.g., tacrolimus), gut microbiota dysbiosis, and prolonged metabolic abnormalities. These findings emphasize the clinical importance of monitoring this symptom cluster, as effective interventions could substantially improve recipients' long-term quality of life.50 The results suggest that health care providers should maintain vigilance regarding gastrointestinal manifestations in HSCT recipients with hematologic neoplasms. To prevent symptom exacerbation, a range of multidisciplinary interventions should be implemented. Specifically, dietary modifications and personalized nutritional support are crucial. For instance, in recipients experiencing gastrointestinal symptoms post-transplant, it is recommended to adopt a diet low in fat and fiber, as these components may delay gastric emptying and exacerbate symptoms such as early satiety and postprandial fullness.51 In addition to dietary modifications, personalized nutritional support is essential. This can involve tailored nutritional plans based on the individual recipient's needs and symptoms, ensuring they receive adequate nutrition while minimizing gastrointestinal distress.52 During extended recovery periods, chronic cGVHD frequently involves cutaneous and neurological systems, manifesting as sensory abnormalities and dermatological symptoms. Concurrently, pharmacological agents (e.g., targeted therapies, immunosuppressants) and viral infections (e.g., cytomegalovirus) may contribute to neurological complications.53 This study identified a neurological symptom cluster with internal composition differing from that reported by Bergkvist et al.,54 whose investigation of recipients at a median of 5 years post-transplantation classified xerostomia as part of the neurological symptom cluster. These discrepancies may be attributed to variations in the time points of data collection, analytical methodologies, and evolving treatment approaches and medication regimens across different study periods. The conclusions, derived from symptom clusters and compositions observed during the recovery phase of allo-HSCT recipients, deepen the understanding of the long-term recovery trajectory in this population. These findings provide a scientific foundation for developing precise symptom identification and management strategies, nursing care planning, and targeted intervention development.

Analysis of Sentinel Symptoms in Symptom Clusters During the Recovery Period of allo-HSCT Recipients.

Worrying is the sentinel symptom of the psychological symptom cluster

The findings of this study reveal that worrying serves as a sentinel symptom within the psychological symptom cluster among allo-HSCT recipients during their 1- to 2-year recovery period, distinct from the sentinel symptoms identified in psychological clusters of other cancer survivors.55 This discrepancy in results may be attributed to differences in measurement tools and the unique characteristics of hematologic malignancies. During the 1–2-year period following allo-HSCT, recipients may experience distinctive physiological and psychological changes. This critical recovery phase occurs after the high-risk period for acute complications but still presents risks of chronic complications,43 which not only impact physical health but also contribute to psychological stress and emotional distress.56 Post-transplant recipients face dual pressures of immune system reconstruction and cumulative physical symptoms. The prolonged recovery period itself constitutes a persistent psychological stressor, compounded by long-term medication use and ongoing medical follow-up requirements, collectively exacerbating psychological burdens.57 Through algorithmic analysis, this study identified worrying as the sentinel symptom in psychological symptom clusters among HSCT recipients during recovery. This phenomenon may be associated with multiple triggers including uncertainty regarding disease prognosis, physical functional changes, and family financial pressures, which collectively precipitate excessive anxiety about future outcomes. Further analysis demonstrates that emotional states do not exist in isolation but rather activate a cascade of secondary psychological symptoms such as tension, irritability, and sadness through psychological linkage mechanisms, ultimately forming symptom clusters. Based on these findings, health care providers should enhance communication with recipients to analyze specific causes of worrying, implement positive psychological interventions (PPIs)58 to strengthen positive mental states, and target worrying as an intervention focus for psychological symptom clusters. As the sentinel symptom, worrying enables early detection of potential psychological issues by clinicians. During follow-up visits, recipients should receive psychological education and health guidance to enhance self-awareness and coping capacities, thereby improving psychological symptom clusters.

Difficulty sleeping is the sentinel symptom of the fatigue symptom cluster

Among allo-HSCT recipients during the 1- to 2-year recovery period, this study demonstrates that difficulty sleeping serves as a sentinel symptom within the fatigue symptom cluster, aligning with findings reported by Noyan et al..15 Although sleep disturbances are not prioritized in most quality-of-life assessments for this recipients population, mounting evidence indicates severe sleep problems among allo-HSCT recipients' post-transplantation,59 which corroborates our findings. Nelson et al.60 revealed significant sleep disturbances in allo-HSCT recipients within 12 months post-transplant, with 69% reporting poor sleep quality during early recovery and 48% continuing to experience moderate-to-severe disturbances at 12 months. Compared to our observation of 42.11% allo-HSCT recipients having trouble falling asleep, these data further emphasize the critical need to address this symptom. Concurrently, as fatigue symptom clusters persist as long-term challenges for post-transplant survivors, our identification of sleep initiation difficulty as the sentinel symptom suggests that targeted sleep interventions could systematically improve fatigue-related symptom clusters. As a sentinel symptom, difficulty sleeping may lead to diminished physical functioning and heightened neurological sensitivity, subsequently reducing pain tolerance and amplifying pain perception.61 Previous experiments have found that sleep deprivation elevates adenosine levels, which inhibit acetylcholine release, impairing attention and cognitive performance.62 Notably, sleep quality not only profoundly impacts quality of life but also serves as a critical physiological foundation for maintaining hematopoietic homeostasis and immune defense mechanisms.63 Our findings propose that difficulty sleeping should be incorporated into routine follow-up assessments for this recipient's population as a key indicator for evaluating fatigue-related symptom clusters. Clinicians should prioritize addressing sleep onset insomnia (SOI) by implementing structured stimulus control protocols (e.g., leaving bed after 20 minutes of unsuccessful sleep initiation).64 Concurrently, prescribe individualized physical activity regimens—incorporating moderate-intensity exercise ≥ 4 hours before bedtime paired with morning daylight exposure—to augment physical resilience and strengthen circadian entrainment.65 Crucially, instruct recipients in establishing precision sleep hygiene protocols focused on modifiable high-impact factors, including caffeine restriction, light-management, and noise/temperature control.66 Beyond these foundational behavioral strategies targeting SOI, physical resilience, and sleep hygiene, evidence supports the efficacy of complementary psychosocial and traditional interventions. Existing studies confirm that psychosocial interventions—such as music therapy and cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia67—can reduce cortisol levels, alleviate stress responses, and improve sleep quality. Additionally, traditional Chinese medicine approaches including acupressure and acupuncture for managing sleep initiation difficulties have been validated by multiple studies.68,69 These comprehensive interventions not only address sleep disturbances but also mitigate fatigue symptom clusters, ultimately enhancing recipients' quality of life.

Itching is the sentinel symptom of the neurological symptom cluster

This study demonstrates that itching serves as a sentinel symptom within the neurological symptom cluster among allo-HSCT recipients in their 1- to 2-year recovery phase. While previous research has addressed post-transplant neurological symptom clusters, none have focused on identifying early warning signals within these clusters. Our survey further revealed a high incidence of itching among recipients during the recovery phase, consistent with prior studies.70 The underlying mechanisms may involve alterations in neurotransmitters, abnormal neuronal activity, and neuroinflammatory processes. In cGVHD following allo-HSCT, cutaneous fibrotic lesions release pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and IL-31. These cytokines activate TRPV1/TRPA1 channels in epidermal C-fiber nerve endings, which not only induce itching71 but may also cause conduction dysfunction in sensory nerve fibers, leading to peripheral numbness or paresthesia.72 Babakoohi et al.73 identified itching as a common post-transplant complication and emphasized the critical role of dermatological management in improving recipients' quality of life. These findings highlight the necessity for health care providers to closely monitor cutaneous and neurological symptom changes during recovery, promptly identify potential manifestations of cGVHD, and integrate neurological self-monitoring education into follow-up protocols to enhance recipients' ability to recognize sensory abnormalities. By improving the timeliness and accuracy of nursing assessments, early detection of neurological complications can facilitate timely diagnosis and intervention, ultimately preventing symptom cluster progression. As sentinel symptoms represent indicators of symptom cluster development, rigorous monitoring of itching-initiated neurological manifestations during recovery and immediate intervention are crucial for allo-HSCT recipients. Clinicians should systematically evaluate itching intensity, duration, and associated symptoms during follow-up.74

This research not only provides novel insights into the pathogenesis of post-transplant neurological symptom clusters but also establishes quantifiable monitoring parameters and intervention targets for clinical practice. Future multicenter randomized controlled trials (RCTs) should validate the health-economic value of sentinel symptom-based interventions in improving quality of life and reducing health care utilization, ultimately advancing patient-centered precision medicine models.

Implications for nursing practice and research

The study identified four distinct symptom clusters experienced by allo-HSCT recipients during their 1- to 2-year recovery. Crucially, three sentinel symptoms were identified as predictive markers for specific clusters: Worrying predicts the psychological cluster, Difficulty sleeping foreshadows the fatigue-related cluster, and Itching signals the neurological cluster (often linked to neuroinflammation). These sentinel symptoms act as practical biomarkers, giving health care providers clear starting points during follow-up visits. To establish a foundation for precision nursing interventions, clinical staff should prioritize monitoring and addressing these sentinel symptoms in symptom cluster management. These findings open new precision pathways for clinical practice and academic research. During clinical follow-up and outpatient visits for recipients recovering from allo-HSCT, sentinel symptoms serve as early warning signs for specific symptom clusters. This enables a shift from “universal screening” to “sentinel-directed stratified management” in follow-up care. Rapid responses by multidisciplinary teams significantly improve the efficiency of early symptom intervention. Targeted early interventions based on these markers may mitigate the progression of symptom clusters. Furthermore, the customized development of recipient education and self-management tools empowers recipients and their families to participate in symptom monitoring, creating a “prevention-identification-intervention” closed loop. Academically, this research advances symptom cluster analysis from “phenomenological description” towards “mechanism exploration.” By integrating previous literature with the current analytical results, it preliminarily explores the biological basis of sentinel symptoms and fosters interdisciplinary collaboration. This accelerates the development of sentinel symptom-directed intervention strategies, ultimately establishing a “recipient-centered” precision rehabilitation system. Future research should focus on creating and testing tailored strategies – such as specialized itch treatments, mindfulness programs for worrying, or sleep-focused therapies – aimed directly at modulating these sentinel symptoms to improve allo-HSCT survivors' quality of life. This significantly improves the long-term quality of life and prognosis for post-transplant recipients.

Limitations

The current research has certain limitations. Firstly, our utilization of the generic MSAS-Ch may not comprehensively capture the full spectrum of symptoms experienced by allo-HSCT recipients during the recovery phase. Future research should develop and validate symptom-specific assessment instruments tailored to this unique population. Secondly, the relatively small sample size limits generalizability. Future studies should prioritize expanding the cohort. Thirdly, detailed clinical data (e.g., medication regimens, GVHD activity, infections) were not captured, precluding analysis of their influence on symptom clusters. Additionally, while our focused investigation on the 1–2-year post-transplant period addresses a critical clinical gap in allo-HSCT care, this design inherently precludes analysis of acute-phase (< 1 year) or long-term (> 2 years) symptom clusters and prevents tracking of sentinel symptom trajectories across the entire recovery spectrum. We therefore recommend longitudinal studies to map symptom evolution, specifically identifying sentinel symptoms from immediate post-transplant through long-term survivorship.

Conclusions

In summary, our study revealed the existence of multiple symptom clusters with identifiable sentinel symptoms during the recovery phase of allo-HSCT. We recommend that clinical nursing practitioners use these sentinel symptoms as early warning indicators to help analyze the underlying causes of individual symptoms. This approach facilitates the implementation of precision-oriented symptom cluster management strategies, which may prevent or mitigate the progression of symptom clusters and ultimately enhance the long-term quality of life for this recipient's population. Furthermore, by leveraging sentinel symptoms as intervention starting points, health care teams should conduct prospective monitoring and systematic evaluations to accurately predict potential manifestations of additional symptoms. Early intervention strategies based on such predictions could effectively alleviate symptom exacerbation and reduce the emergence of new symptoms, thereby significantly improving clinical outcomes for allo-HSCT recipients. This paradigm shift from “reactive management” to “proactive prevention and control” holds significant potential for advancing transplantation nursing practices.
