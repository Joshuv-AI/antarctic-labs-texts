Prolonged KRAS-MAPK Inhibition Induces Interferon Signaling That Promotes Cell State Transition and Confers Therapeutic Vulnerabilities
Authors: Ashenafi Bulle, Yali Chen, Huaping Li, Timothy Hung-Po Chen, Iftikhar Ali Khawar, Lin Li, Yu Wang, Peng Liu, Vikas Kumar Somani, Richard Kurupi, Yutong Geng, Ofejiro Blessing Pereye, Sapana Bansod, Son B. Le, Marianna B. Ruzinova, David D. Tran, Kian-Huat Lim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
TRIM22 drives interferon-induced EMT and NF-κB–dependent TROP2 upregulation to drive resistance to KRAS-MAPK inhibition in pancreatic cancer, which can be overcome by combining sacituzumab govitecan with KRAS-MAPK inhibitors.

Introduction

Recent breakthroughs in the development of KRAS inhibitors, including KRASG12C inhibitors and emerging KRAS(ON) inhibitors, are revolutionizing the treatment of patients with pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC). Despite this progress, KRASG12C inhibitors have shown limited and transient responses that last ∼4 to 5 months. Analyses of PDAC tumors from patients and preclinical mouse models reveal that tumor cells can acquire secondary mutations that reactivate KRAS-MAPK signaling or transition to an alternative transcriptional state that is inherently refractory to KRAS pathway inhibition (1, 2). High-resolution single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) showed that PDAC tumors consist of transcriptionally distinct cell states, including the classic (more epithelial), basal (more mesenchymal), and an intermediate coexpressing (IC), or “mixed” state defined by a partial epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (pEMT) program (3). Importantly, transition between these states is at least partially reversible and driven by cues in the tumor microenvironment (TME). Specifically, strongly basal subtype is associated with worse survival likely due to resistance to chemotherapy (4), whereas the classic subpopulation is more resistant to KRAS-MAPK inhibition (5). Although initial KRAS inhibition seemed to preferentially suppress the more basal subtype and enrich the classic subtype, prolonged KRAS inhibition results in emergence of resistant clones that display predominantly pEMT, followed by the mesenchymal states (1). These studies collectively establish cell state transition as a therapeutic challenge to KRAS-MAPK inhibitors and underscore the need to identify the molecular mechanisms that drive acquisition of the pEMT and basal states.

In this study, we analyzed transcriptomic data from biopsied PDAC tumors obtained from patients who received prolonged (2 weeks) treatment with the ERK inhibitor ulixertinib in a phase 1 clinical trial, as well as early-passage patient-derived PDAC cell lines (PDCL) subjected to treatment with ulixertinib or KRAS inhibitors (MRTX1133 or AMG-510) with the same duration. Our analyses identified the interferon (IFN) response as a key driver cell state transition, mediated by upregulation of the E3 ubiquitin ligase, tripartite motif containing 22 (TRIM22). We demonstrate that TRIM22 promotes proteasomal degradation of IκBα, resulting in NF-κB pathway activation and progression to a more basal state with unique therapeutic vulnerabilities.

Materials and Methods

Study approval and rigors

All animal procedures were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments and were approved by Washington University in St. Louis Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC; protocol #22-0101). Animal experiments and analyses were conducted in a blinded manner by independent laboratory members to ensure rigor. In vitro experiments were replicated at least three times and with different cell lines.

Clinical data

In this single-institution, open-label, phase 1b clinical trial (IRB# 201601098) conducted at Washington University in St. Louis, we evaluated the safety, tolerability, and preliminary efficacy of ulixertinib in combination with gemcitabine and nab-paclitaxel (GnP) in patients with untreated metastatic PDAC. The study consisted of a dose de-escalation phase followed by an expansion cohort at the recommended phase II dose (RP2D). Eligible patients were ≥18 years of age with histologically confirmed metastatic PDAC, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status <1, and adequate organ and bone marrow function. Key exclusion criteria included central nervous system metastases, significant ascites, gastrointestinal disorders affecting drug absorption, or human immunodeficiency virus infection. Patients received a 2-week lead-in of ulixertinib monotherapy at 600, 450, or 300 mg twice a day (fasting) using a modified Fibonacci design, followed by combination treatment with GnP (gemcitabine 1,000 mg/m2 and nab-paclitaxel 125 mg/m2 administered on days 1, 8, and 15 of a 28-day cycle). Core needle tumor biopsies were collected before and 2 weeks after ulixertinib treatment (6). The current study evaluated ulixertinib treatment-induced molecular changes and mechanisms of therapeutic resistance using bulk RNA-seq and histologic staining.

Cell lines

All cell lines were purchased from the ATCC and authenticated on their own cell lines. Early-passaged Pa01C, Pa02C, Pa03C, and Pa14C were kind gifts from Dr. Channing Der at UNC-CH and were used for <6 months after receipt or resuscitation from cryopreservation. All cell lines were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Mycoplasma testing was performed semiannually using MycoSEQ Detection Kit (Applied Biosystems). ERKi-R and KRASi-R cells were developed by culturing naïve cells in the indicated drug concentrations over a 2-week period to mimic patient treatment conditions. Culture media and drugs were refreshed twice weekly.

Mouse models and experiments

No sex-based analyses were performed. In xenograft models, 2 to 5 million PDAC cells or 5 × 5 × 5 mm cryopreserved PDX tumor fragments were mixed 1:1 (v/v) with Matrigel and implanted subcutaneously into both flanks of 8- to 12-week-old female NOD-SCIDγ mice (JAX #005557), at least 5 mice/group, via needle injection or small incision. Treatments were initiated when tumors reached ∼100–150 mm3. Mice were excluded from analysis if they met humane endpoints, including tumor ulceration and >20% body weight loss within 1 week of treatment start. Mice were euthanized when tumors reached ∼2,000 mm3 or at humane endpoints specified in the IACUC. Drug dosing: ulixertinib 100 mg/kg, oral 5 days/week; sacituzumab govitecan (SG) 25 mg/kg, tail vein injection weekly; ruxolitinib 100 mg/kg, MRTX1133/AMG-510 10 mg/kg, intraperitoneal daily; RMC-6236 25 mg/kg, oral daily.

Drugs

Drugs were obtained from the following: gemcitabine and SG-hziy (Washington University Siteman Cancer Center Pharmacy), 5-FU (Sigma #F6627), oxaliplatin (Selleckchem #S1224), 5-FU, SN38 (Selleckchem #S4908), hydroxychloroquine (Sigma #H0915), selumetinib (AZD6244, Selleckchem#S1008), trametinib (Selleckchem #S2673), bortezomib (Selleckchem #S1013), ruxolitinib (Selleckchem#s1378), dabrafenib (Selleckchem, cat#S2807), GDC-0994 (Selleckchem, cat. #S7554), GDC-0941 (Selleckchem, cat. #S1065), vemuraferanib (Selleckchem, cat#S1267), sotorasib (AMG510, MCE # HY-114277), MRTX1133 (MCE #HY-134813), daraxonrasib (RMC-6236, MCE # HY-148439), and IMD-0354 (MCE # HY-10172). Ulixertinib was provided by BioMed Valley under MTA, and copanlisib and afatinib were provided by the NCI Cancer Therapy Evaluation Program under MTA. For all drug treatments, an applicable concentration of 0 (zero) was treated with vehicle.

In vitro cell viability assay

Cells (5,000–10,000 per well) were seeded in triplicate in 96-well plates and allowed to adhere overnight. The following day, cells were treated with inhibitors at the indicated concentrations. After four or 5 days of treatment, cell viability was assessed using a resazurin (Alamar Blue) colorimetric assay, as previously described (7). All experiments were performed independently three times in triplicate, and representative data from one experiment are shown.

Reporter assays

Cells stably or transiently expressing Firefly–Renilla or NanoGlo luciferase reporters under the control of serum response element, interferon (IFN)-stimulated response element (ISRE), or NF-κB promoters were seeded at equal densities per well in 96-well plates. Cells were transfected in triplicate with 0.5 µg of the appropriate plasmid DNA per well. Twenty-four hours after transfection, cells were treated with the indicated drugs and incubated for an additional 24 hours. Reporter activity was then assessed 48 hours after transfection using either the Dual-Glo Luciferase Assay System or the NanoGlo Luciferase Assay System. Luminescence was measured using a SpectraMax i3 microplate reader. Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity, and data were further normalized to the control condition. The results were analyzed and graphed using GraphPad Prism (v10).

Lentiviral and retroviral production and transduction

Lentiviral particles were generated by cotransfecting 293T cells with short hairpin RNA (shRNA) or single-guide RNA (sgRNA) plasmids (Supplementary Table S1) and packaging plasmids psPAX2 and pMD2.G at a ratio of 6 µg:3 µg:1.5 µg, respectively, in serum-free DMEM. Polyethyleneimine (PEI; 1 mg/mL) was added at 4 µL per 1 µg of total DNA. For retroviral production, 293T cells were transfected with 4 µg each of the retroviral vectors encoding the gene of interest (Supplementary Table S1) and the envelope plasmid PCL10A1 using the same PEI protocol. After a 15-minute incubation at room temperature, transfection mixtures were added dropwise to 293T cells in 10-cm dishes. The medium was replaced with fresh DMEM containing 10% FBS 14 to 16 hours after transfection. Viral supernatants were collected at 48 and 72 hours, filtered through a 0.45-µm filter, and used to transduce target cells in the presence of 10 µg/mL polybrene (Sigma). After 24 hours, the medium was replaced, and cells were allowed to recover for 2 days before selection with puromycin (2 µg/mL), hygromycin (200 µg/mL), or neomycin (500 µg/mL) depending on the antibiotic selection gene for 7 to 10 days, until 100% confluency. Cells were then split for cryopreservation and expression analysis before in vitro experiments.

Western blot

Western blotting was performed according to standard procedures. After appropriate treatment, the medium was removed, and cells were washed twice with ice-cold 1X PBS and lysed with ice-cold 1% Triton-X lysis buffer (25 mmol/L Tris, pH 7.4, 150 mmol/L NaCl, 5 mmol/L EDTA, and 1% Triton-X) containing 1X protease (10 μg mL−1 leupeptin, 700 ng mL−1 pepstatin, 170 ng mL−1 aprotinin, and 1 mmol/L PMSF) and phosphatase (10 mmol/L NaF, 1 mmol/L Na3VO4, 1 mmol/L Na4P2O7, and 5 mmol/L Na β-glycerophosphate) inhibitors. Lysate was transferred to a 1.5-mL microcentrifuge tube on ice and centrifuged at 13,500 rpm, 4°C, for 10 minutes. The pellet was discarded, and the supernatant was isolated, quantified by Bradford assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific), equalized for protein concentration, denatured, reduced by 6× SDS sample buffer, and boiled for 5 minutes. Proteins (30 to 40 μg) were resolved by SDS-PAGE and transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Membranes were probed overnight at 4°C with appropriate primary antibodies diluted in 5% bovine serum albumin, followed by appropriate horseradish peroxidase–conjugated secondary antibodies (anti-mouse or anti-rabbit, The Jackson Laboratory; at 1:5,000 dilutions) and imaging using a chemiluminescent substrate (Pico or Femto, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Densitometry was performed using Image Lab software. All blots are representative of three independent experiments. Details on antibodies are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Coimmunoprecipitation and immunoblot analysis

293T stable cell lines expressing either Myc-TRIM22 or empty vector were plated in 10-cm dishes and transiently transfected with an HA-IκBα expression plasmid (pCDNA3.1). To facilitate detection of polyubiquitination, bortezomib was added for 6 hours prior to lysis. Cells were washed twice with cold PBS and lysed in Triton-X lysis buffer (25 mmol/L Tris pH 7.4, 150 mmol/L NaCl, 5 mmol/L EDTA, and 1% Triton-X) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Lysates were incubated overnight at 4°C with magnetic beads conjugated to either anti-HA (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. #88837) or anti-Myc (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. #SAE0201) antibodies. Beads were washed with TBS-T or TBS, and bound proteins were eluted in 2× SDS sample buffer and boiled for 10 minutes at 95°C. Samples were separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred to nitrocellulose membranes, and analyzed by immunoblotting using the indicated primary antibodies (Supplementary Table S1).

Protein degradation assay

293T cells stably expressing myc-TRIM22 were treated with cycloheximide (CHX; 10 µg/mL) or bortezomib (0.1 µmol/L), whereas TRIM22 knockout Pa01C cells were treated with CHX (10 µg/mL) for the indicated time points. Cells were harvested and subjected to immunoblotting to assess endogenous IκBα protein levels. Band intensities were quantified using densitometry (Image Lab, Bio-Rad), and IκBα half-lives were calculated by fitting the data to a one-phase exponential decay model using GraphPad Prism v10.

Cytoplasmic and nuclear protein extraction

Cytoplasmic and nuclear proteins were extracted and enriched from mammalian cells using the ProteoExtract Subcellular Proteome Extraction Kit (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. #539790) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. This method enables sequential isolation of proteins based on subcellular localization, allowing efficient separation of cytoplasmic and nuclear fractions for downstream analyses.

Wound healing assay

Ulixertinib-resistant Capan-1 and Pa01C cells were seeded into six-well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well and cultured for 24 hours to form a confluent monolayer. A linear scratch (“wound”) was made across each well using a sterile 200 μL pipette tip, and detached cells were removed by washing with PBS. The cells were then incubated in DMEM containing reduced FBS under standard conditions (37°C, 5% CO2) for 48 hours. Wound closure was monitored and imaged using a phase-contrast microscope. Images were captured from three independent biological replicates.

Invasion assay

Cell invasion was assessed using BioCoat Matrigel Invasion Chambers (Corning). Ulixertinib-resistant Pa01C and Capan-1 cells (7.5 × 104) were seeded into the upper chambers in serum-free medium. The lower chambers were filled with medium containing 10% FBS to serve as a chemoattractant. After 48 hours of incubation, noninvading cells on the upper surface were removed with a cotton swab. Inserts were then fixed and stained using DiffQuick (Medion Diagnostics AG). Invaded cells on the lower surface of the membrane were counted in five random fields per insert under a microscope at 40× magnification.

Flow cytometry

For surface and total TROP2 expression analysis, cells were stained with Alexa Fluor 647 anti-human TROP2 (Cell signaling Technology). The results were analyzed and quantified using FlowJo software (BD bioscience) using gating strategy as shown in Supplementary Fig. S8E.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation assay

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays were performed using the SimpleChIP Plus Sonication Chromatin IP Kit (Cell Signaling Technology, cat. #56383) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Chromatin was immunoprecipitated using antibodies against IRF1, IRF9, and NF-κB p65, with Trimethyl-Histone H3 (Lys4) serving as a positive control and normal rabbit IgG as a negative control (Supplementary Table S1). The immunoprecipitated DNA was then analyzed by PCR using primers specific to the IRF1, IRF9, and TROP2 promoter regions (primer sequences listed in Supplementary Table S1).

CUT&RUN-seq assay

CUT&RUN assays were performed to profile chromatin-bound transcription factors in Pa01C pancreatic cancer cells using the CUT&RUN Assay Kit (Cell Signaling Technology, cat. #86652S) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 104 cells/cm2 in complete DMEM media and treated with ERK and KRAS inhibitors for 2 weeks, with media refreshed every 48 hours. At the end of treatment, 500,000 cells per sample were harvested, washed with Wash Buffer, and immobilized on activated concanavalin A–coated magnetic beads. Cells were permeabilized using a Digitonin-containing buffer and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies targeting H3K4me3 (positive control), rabbit IgG (negative control), IRF1, IRF9, or NF-κB p65 (antibodies listed in STAR Methods). After washing off unbound antibodies, samples were incubated with Protein A-MNase for 1 hour at 4°C, followed by activation of MNase with calcium on ice to cleave DNA at antibody-bound regions. The reaction was stopped with Stop Buffer to release cleaved DNA fragments, which were then purified using the DNA Spin Column Kit (Cell Signaling Technology, cat. #14209). Sequencing libraries were prepared using the DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina Systems (Cell Signaling Technology, cat. #56795S), including end-repair, adapter ligation, PCR amplification with Multiplex Oligos (cat. #47538S), and size selection with AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, cat. #A63881). DNA concentration and quality were assessed using the Qubit dsDNA High Sensitivity Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, REF# Q32854) and Qubit Assay Tubes (REF# Q32856). Final libraries were pooled and sequenced using paired-end 75 bp reads on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform.

For sequence analysis, briefly, paired-end FASTQ files were processed using the BBTools suite (v38.42). Duplicate reads were removed using clumpify with the parameters –dedupe –subs = 2, followed by adapter and quality trimming using bbduk (ktrim = r, k = 21, mink = 11, hdist = 1, tpe, and tbo). Trimmed reads were aligned to the human reference genome (hg19) using Bowtie2. SAM files were converted to sorted BAM files using SAMtools. Aligned reads were converted to BED format after removing reads mapping to chrM, chrUn, or chromosomes labeled as “random.” Regions overlapping the ENCODE hg19 blacklist (v2) were excluded. Transcription factor binding peaks were identified using MACS2 (v2.1.1.20160309) with the parameters callpeak -g hs -f BAMPE -q 0.05 -B. Peak visualization and inspection were performed using the Integrative Genomics Viewer, comparing signal enrichment between input controls and treatment conditions at loci of interest.

Quantitative (real-time) PCR

Total RNA was isolated using PureLinkTM RNA Mini Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific cat. #12183025), cDNA was generated using High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 4368814), and quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed using SYBR Green reagent (Applied Biosystems, cat. #4309155). Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 1. All experiments were performed with three independent experiments, each performed with biological and technical triplicates.

Bulk RNA-seq

Total RNA was isolated from tumor tissues using RNAzol RT (Sigma, cat. #R4533-200ML) and from PDAC cells using the PureLink RNA Mini Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. #12183025), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Samples for bulk RNA-seq were prepared as per the manufacturer’s protocol, indexed, pooled, and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq. Illumina’s bcl2fastq software was used for base calls and demultiplexing. A Python demultiplexing program, with a maximum of one mismatch in the indexing read. RNA-seq reads were aligned to the Ensembl release 76 top-level assembly using STAR version 2.0.4b1. Gene counts were derived from the number of uniquely aligned unambiguous reads using featureCount, version 1.4.52. The isoform expression of the known Ensembl transcripts was estimated using Sailfish version 0.6.133. Sequencing performance was assessed for the total number of aligned reads, total number of uniquely aligned reads, and detected features. Ribosomal fraction and read distribution over known gene models were performed using RSeQC version 2.34. All gene counts were then imported into the R/Bioconductor package EdgeR5, and TMM normalization size factors were calculated to adjust for sample differences in the library size. Ribosomal genes and genes not expressed in the smallest group size minus one sample greater than one count-per-million were excluded from further analysis. The TMM size factors and matrix of counts were then imported into R/Bioconductor package Limma6. Weighted likelihoods based on the observed mean–variance relationship of every gene and sample were then calculated for all samples using voomWithQualityWeights7. The performance of all genes was assessed using plots of the residual standard deviation of every gene to their average log-count, with a robustly fitted trend line of the residuals. Differential expression analysis was then performed to analyze the differences between conditions, and the results were filtered for only those genes with Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) adjusted P-values less than or equal to 0.05. For each contrast extracted with Limma, global perturbations in known Gene Ontology (GO) terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways were detected using the R/Bioconductor package GAGE8 to test for changes in expression of the reported log2 fold changes (FC) reported by Limma in each term versus the background log2 FCs of all genes found outside the respective term. The R/Bioconductor packages heatmap39 and Pathview10 were used to display heatmaps or annotated KEGG graphs across groups of samples for each GO term or KEGG pathway (respectively) with a Benjamini–Hochberg FDR adjusted P value of less than or equal to 0.05.

scRNA-seq data analysis

For drug treatment, we used 6-week-old KPPC mice treated with vehicle or ulixertinib or RMC-6236 for 14 days. Chromium Single-Cell 5′v2 ReagentKit (10× Genomics) was used for encapsulated samples into droplets, and libraries were prepared. These libraries were sequenced using NovaSeq 6000 sequencing system (Illumina) with average target of 50,000 mean reads per cell. The CellRanger mkfastq pipeline (10× Genomics) was used to convert Illumina base call files into FASTQ files. Each sample was demultiplexed with >95% valid barcodes and >95% q30 reads. Filtered feature matrices files were used for Seurat v5 as individual objects. For quality control (QC), seurat objects were filtered for genes expressed in less than three cells, and cells that expressed less than 1,000 or more than 6,000 genes were excluded from the analysis. Cells with more than 10% mitochondrial RNA content were excluded. Cell-cycle phase for each cell were calculated by “CellCycleScoring” and assigned based on the expression signatures for S and G2/M genes. SCTransform normalization with default parameter was used to normalize and scale the expression matrix with regression of percentages of mitochondrial genes. The difference between S phase and G2–M cell-cycle score was regressed using SCTransform.

Sample objects representing individual data sets were merged into a single seurat object. Variable features for each sample independently calculated by “SelectIntegrationFeatures” and ranked them based on the number of samples they were independently identified. Principal component analysis (PCA) reduction was performed by “RunPCA” with the top 3,000 variable features was used to identify projection vectors that defined shared biological states among samples and maximized overall correlations across datasets. Different datasets were integrated using R Harmony integration “RunHarmony” with the SCT assay and calculated PCA on 3,000 previously calculated genes using “RunPCA” function and grouped together as per the treatments. A Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) dimensional reduction was performed on merged objects using “RunUMAP” function. “FindNeighbors” were used to refine the edge weights between two cells based on Jaccard similarity and clusters cells together by “FindClusters,” which used shared nearest neighbor modularity optimization (“algorithm = 3”). Next, objects were further filtered based on marker genes, nCount_RNA, and nFeature_RNA to generate seurat objects with identifiable populations of interest. Doublet cells were removed from all objects using R DoubletFinder with standard parameters run on each sample. All samples were rerun through SCTransform until the seurat object consisted of distinct cell clusters of interest. The annotation and identification of cluster and cells marker gene were calculated by “FindAllMarkers” function with MAST test, logfold threshold = 0.25, and minimum 0.25-fold difference in percentage expression. For each cluster-based differential gene set expression analysis (GSEA), “FindMarkers” was used with MAST test with parameters min.pct of 0.1 and logfc.threshold of -Inf. To further identify common clusters across data sets, “AddModuleScore” was used with the top 50 to 100 marker genes of the target subset. GSEA was performed on the “RNA” assay after log normalization and scaling of data. For GSEA, differentially expressed gene (DEG) lists from each dataset were ranked based on gene expression (log2 FC) and processed with R clusterProfiler to test for Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) gene sets. These gene sets were run into GSEA to test GO, KEGG pathway, Wiki pathway, Reactome, and Hallmark database gene set with FDR < 0.05 using R clusterProfiler. Software used for scRNA-seq plot were generated using R v4.3 (https://www.r-project.org), R Seurat v5 (https://github.com/satijalab/seurat), R sctransform (https://github.com/satijalab/sctransform), R DoubletFinder (https://github.com/chris-mcginnis-ucsf/DoubletFinder), R clusterProfiler (https://github.com/YuLab-SMU/clusterProfiler), R CellChat (https://github.com/sqjin/CellChat), and R SingleR (https://github.com/dviraran/SingleR).

GSEA

Genes in the RNA-seq differential expression data were ranked by log2 FC, and preranked GSEA was performed using ranked lists. The ranking metric was set to “difference-of-classes” because the expression data were in log2 units. Otherwise, GSEAv.4 was used for analysis in the default format. The data generated were exported and graphed using the GraphPad Prism v8 software. RNA-seq differential expression data of patients with PDAC treated with ulixertinib for 2 weeks, PDAC pancreatic cancer cell lines treated with ulixertinib for 2 weeks, and PDCLs treated with MRTX1133 for 2 weeks were deposited in the NCBI’s Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), accession number (GSE213797, GSE302981, GSE303051, GSE303323, GSE303104, and GSE303105).

Identification of master regulators (GeneRep-nSCORE)

RNA-seq gene expression profiles of pancreatic cancer cell lines treated with ulixertinib for 2 weeks was used to generate a pancreatic adenocarcinoma gene interaction network. The GeneRep–nSCORE algorithm, a network-based machine learning approach for identifying master regulators of network perturbations, was used to uncover regulators of resistance (8). A pancreatic adenocarcinoma gene network was constructed using The Cancer Genome Atlas data (https://www.cancer.gov/ccg/research/genome-sequencing/tcga) with the GeneRep algorithm. NSCORE was then applied to raw RNA-seq count data, obtained as described in the Methods section. The code for the GeneRep–nSCORE pipeline is available on GitHub: https://github.com/TranLabUSC/NETZEN-classic.

Calculation of Bliss scores for synergism/additive effects

Additive or synergistic effects of drug combinations were evaluated using the Bliss independence method. This approach compares the observed therapeutic effect of a drug combination with the expected additive effect calculated from the corresponding single-agent treatments. Bliss scores were computed using R software. Tumor growth curves were generated to visualize treatment effects over time and identify time points at which combination treatments outperformed individual drugs, with comparisons made relative to vehicle-treated controls. Time points lacking corresponding vehicle control measurements were excluded from the analysis.

Statistical analyses

All results, where applicable, are expressed as the mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses were performed using Prism 10 and R software. Unpaired or paired Student t tests were used to compare two groups, as appropriate. For scRNA-seq analyses, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to compare two groups. Comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey post hoc test. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Results

Prolonged ERK inhibition induces inflammatory and EMT responses

From our recent phase 1 clinical trial (NCT02608229) combining ulixertinib with gemcitabine and nab-paclitaxel for patients with metastatic PDAC (6), we obtained and performed bulk RNA-seq on paired tumor biopsies taken before and after 2 weeks of ulixertinib (600 mg twice a day) monotherapy. Post-treatment samples revealed downregulated KRAS and MAPK signatures, demonstrating on-target effect, and upregulated IFN, NF-ĸB, and EMT-related signatures (Fig. 1A; Supplementary Fig. S1A). To model these changes, we treated early-passaged patient-derived PDCLs with ulixertinib for 2 weeks at 2 µmol/L, which maximally suppressed phosphorylation of RSK (9), and performed RNA-seq. Mirroring patient samples, PDCLs that survived ulixertinib treatment (termed ERKi-resistant, or ERKi-R) displayed upregulated IFN, NF-ĸB, and EMT signatures and downregulated KRAS/MAPK cascades (Fig. 1B; Supplementary Fig. S1B). As confirmation, whereas ERKi-R cells displayed paradoxically increased phospho-ERK1/2, which is unique to the mechanism of action of ulixertinib (10), phospho-RSK stayed suppressed; we also observed decreased junctional/total E-cadherin and ZO-1 and increased vimentin and N-cadherin expression (Fig. 1C and D). ERKi-R cells were morphologically spindled, contrary to the cobblestone morphology of naïve cells, and had increased migratory and invasive capacity (Fig. 1E–G). Next, ERKi-R cells were washed and maintained in culture either with (ERKi-R) or without (ERKi-R-off) ulixertinib for another 2 weeks. Whereas ERKi-R cells exhibited sustained resistance to ulixertinib as well as to a different ERK inhibitor, GDC-0994 (Fig. 1H; Supplementary Fig. S1C), ERKi-R-off cells also maintained resistance to ulixertinib rechallenge, indicating irreversibility of the phenotype. These characteristics of ERKi-R cells collectively fulfill the criteria for in vitro EMT (11) and implicate irreversible transition to a basal-like state as a resistance mechanism to prolonged ERK inhibition.

To determine whether this EMT shift occurs in vivo, we treated autochthonous PDAC mice (p48-Cre; Tp53flox/flox; KRasG12D, or KPPC) with ulixertinib for 2 weeks and performed scRNA-seq on tumor cells using differential expression genesets (DEG) defined by Dilly and colleagues (Supplementary Fig. S1D; ref. 1). Compared with control tumors, ulixertinib-treated tumors had lower and higher proportions of the classic and basal subpopulations, respectively (Fig. 1I and J). Ulixertinib-treated tumor cells collectively expressed less epithelial marker Krt19 and more mesenchymal marker Sparc, whereas other canonical markers, including Cdh1, Cdh2, and Snai, were not significantly different, perhaps due to their low baseline expression in this model (Supplementary Fig. S1E, S1F).

Prolonged MAPK inhibition upregulates TRIM22 expression

To identify signaling hubs driving transcriptomic reprogramming in ERKi-R PDCLs, we applied the Gene network Reconstruction pipeline (GeneRep; ref. 8), a mutual information–based network inference algorithm adapted from ARACNe [Algorithm for the Reconstruction of Accurate Cellular Networks (12)], to reconstruct regulatory interactions among genes that were enriched by at least twofold in ERKi-R cells. Genes within these networks were prioritized using nSCORE (network Systems Calculation of Optimal Ranking Engine), a flexible, automated node-importance scoring framework capable of incorporating diverse scoring schemes with predefined parameters (8). This GeneRep-nSCORE platform nominated TRIM22, CREB3L1, MMP14, TRIM29 (upregulated), and HIST1H3B (downregulated), based on their network centrality and potential to coordinate ERKi-induced adaptive responses as candidate determinants that reprogram PDAC cells following prolonged ERK inhibition (Fig. 2A; Supplementary Fig. S2A). As validation, we independently performed qRT-PCR on naïve and ERKi-R cells and found TRIM22 to be the only gene that was markedly upregulated (Fig. 2B). TRIM22 was upregulated as early as 1 day after ulixertinib treatment, as also illustrated by Klomp and colleagues (13), and more robustly after 2 weeks (Supplementary Fig. S2B). Pa01C cells treated with ulixertinib or GDC-0994 for 2 weeks upregulated TRIM22, which was sustained 2 weeks after drug removal (Fig. 2C), indicating irreversibility.

From bulk RNA-seq of PDAC samples, TRIM22 showed a trend of being upregulated (P = 0.13), whereas HIST1H3B was significantly downregulated (P = 0.0009) in ulixertinib-treated cohorts (Supplementary Fig. S2C). However, by immunohistochemistry (IHC) and H-scoring, TRIM22 expression was markedly upregulated almost exclusively in neoplastic cells in ulixertinib-treated patient samples, as well as in Pa02C and Pa14C tumors grown in nude mice (Fig. 2D; Supplementary Fig. S2D and E), potentially explaining the statistically insignificant upregulation of TRIM22 by bulk RNA-seq of patient samples, which include stromal cells. TRIM22 protein was likewise upregulated in ERKi-R PDAC cells by Western blot (Fig. 2E). Because ulixertinib and GDC-094 inhibit both ERK1 and ERK2, we knocked down or knocked out ERK1 or ERK2 by shRNA, or sgRNA using CRISPR/Cas9 and found that suppression of either ERK kinase increased TRIM22 expression (Fig. 2F; Supplementary Fig. S2F).

To determine whether TRIM22 upregulation is specific to MAPK pathway inhibition, we treated Pa01C cells with ERKi (ulixertinib and GDC-0994), MEKi (trametinib and selumetinib), BRAFi (vemurafenib and dabrafenib), AKTi (GDC-0941), pan-HERi (afatinib), and PI3Ki (copanlisib) for 2 weeks. Interestingly, only ERKi and MEKi, and to a lesser extent afatinib, which also suppresses MAPK pathway, induced TRIM22 expression (Fig. 2G). BRAFi, which paradoxically activates the MAPK cascade in RAS-mutant cells (14), did not increase TRIM22 expression. In contrast, chemotherapeutic agents such as oxaliplatin, SN38, and 5-FU had little or no effect on TRIM22 levels, and they each activated ERK1/2 to varying extents (Fig. 2H). Like human PDAC cells, cultured murine KPPC cells treated with ulixertinib and trametinib for 2 weeks demonstrated increased expression of Trim30a and Trim30d, the murine orthologs of human TRIM22 (Fig. 2I). By immunofluorescence (IF), PDAC from autochthonous KPPC mice treated with ulixertinib for 2 weeks showed elevated TRIM30 expression, predominantly in CK19+ tumor cells (Fig. 2J).

TRIM22 drives EMT and resistance to MAPK inhibitors

Next, we investigated the role of TRIM22 in the transcriptional reprograming of PDAC cells. We stably overexpress TRIM22 in PDAC cells, which took around 2 weeks, and found that TRIM22 expression was sufficient to upregulate EMT, MAPK, inflammatory including NF-κB, and IL6 signatures (Fig. 3A and B), increased CDH2 and SNAI1 expression (Fig. 3C), reduced expression of ZO-1 and E-cadherin, and increased vimentin levels in naïve PDAC cells (Fig. 3D and E). Phenotypically, TRIM22-overexpressing PDAC cells had a more spindled morphology (Supplementary Fig. S3A), as well as enhanced organoid formation and migratory capability (Fig. 3F and G). Conversely, knockdown or knockout of TRIM22 in ERKi-R PDAC cells led to increased expression of ZO-1 and E-cadherin and decreased vimentin levels but did not significantly affect N-cadherin (Fig. 3H). TRIM22-overexpressing PDAC cells were more resistant to ulixertinib and trametinib (Fig. 3I). Notably, knockdown or knockout of TRIM22 had no impact on naïve cells but rendered ERKi-R PDAC cells more sensitive to ulixertinib (Fig. 3J), indicating a context-specific role of TRIM22 in therapeutic resistance. Analysis of human tumors from the GEPIA2 (Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis-2) database (15) demonstrated a moderately strong positive correlation between EMT signatures and TRIM22 expression (Supplementary Fig. S3B) and associated high TRIM22 expression with inferior overall survival (OS) among patients with PDAC (Supplementary Fig. S3C). Interestingly, overexpression of TRIM22 also upregulated MAPK signature (Fig. 3A), and increased phosho-ERK1/2 in Capan-1, 293T, and Pa01C cells (Supplementary Fig. S3D). In TRIM22-overexpressing Pa01C cells, increased phopho-ERK1/2 could be suppressed by both KRASG12D and RAS(ON) inhibitors (Supplementary Fig. S3E), suggesting that TRIM22 may utilize both wild-type (WT) and mutant RAS to activate the MAPK cascade, which warrants further investigation in the future. Together, these data demonstrate that PDAC cells acquire an EMT phenotype and therapeutic resistance via upregulation of TRIM22.

IFN signaling drives TRIM22 expression

TRIM22 is an IFN-stimulated E3 ligase that is strongly induced in virus-infected cells, where it promotes proteasomal degradation of viral proteins and restricts viral replication (16). Interestingly, we also observed significant upregulation of IFNα (type 1) and IFNγ (type 2) signatures in ulixertinib-treated human PDAC samples and PDCLs (Fig. 4A), as well as IFNA1, IFNB, and IFNG ligands and genes that encode different IFN receptors (Fig. 4B). Ulixertinib-treated autochthonous KPPC tumors also showed increased expression of Trim30a and Trim30d, as well as enrichment of type 1/2 IFN signatures exclusively in the pEMT and basal subpopulations (Supplementary Fig. S4A; Fig. 4C). To confirm these findings, we created Capan-1 and Pa01C cells stably expressing luciferase driven by ISRE, a specific DNA sequence element that binds to the conserved DNA-binding domain of multiple IFN regulatory factors (IRF; ref. 17). In both ISRE reporter cell lines, ulixertinib treatment increased ISRE activity after at least 7 days of treatment (Fig. 4D), suggesting increased expression or activation of IRFs. In Capan-1 cells, ulixertinib-induced ISRE activity was lower than that induced by 1,000 U/mL of recombinant IFN-β, an in vitro concentration known to kill PDAC cells with prolonged culture (Supplementary Fig. S4B; ref. 18). In mammals, nine IRF genes (IRF1-9) have been identified, each of which is activated by distinct upstream IFN signaling pathways and transactivates different genes in a context-dependent manner (19). Bulk RNA-seq from ERKi-R PDCLs showed IRF1, IRF5, and IRF9 to be significantly upregulated (Supplementary Fig. S4C). We similarly cultured KRASG12D-mutant PDAC cells (Pa03C, Pa14C, and HPAC) in MRTX1133 0.5 µmol/L for 2 weeks to create KRASi-R cells, which by RNA-seq also upregulated IRF1 and IRF9 expression (Supplementary Fig. S4D). As confirmation, ERKi-R Capan-1 and Pa01C cells expressed higher mRNA and protein levels of IRF1 and IRF9 (Fig. 4E and F), along with increased phospho-JAK1 and -STAT1 (Supplementary Fig. S4E), indicating IFN pathway activation. Analysis of the GEPIA2 database revealed higher IRF1 and IRF9 expression in PDAC compared with normal pancreatic tissue (Supplementary Fig. S4F), with IRF1 showing a trend toward and IRF9 being significantly associated with poorer OS (Supplementary Fig. S4G).

As an IFN-response gene, we hypothesized that TRIM22 expression is driven by IRF1 and/or IRF9. In support, GEPIA3 analysis revealed a moderately strong positive correlation between IRF1 or IRF9 and TRIM22 expression in pooled noncancerous, peritumoral and PDAC samples, with higher expression of all three genes in tumor samples (Fig. 4G). Indeed, knockdown of either IRF1 or IRF9 in ERKi-R cells reduced TRIM22 mRNA (Supplementary Fig. S4H) and protein levels (Fig. 4H). Conversely, stable overexpression of either IRF in naïve PDAC cells not only increased TRIM22 and vimentin expression but also decreased E-cadherin (Fig. 4I; Supplementary Fig. S4I). IRF9-overexpressing cells also displayed a more spindle-like morphology (Supplementary Fig. 4J). To determine whether IRF1 or IRF9 directly regulate TRIM22 transcription, we performed CUT&RUN (Cleavage Under Targets & Release Using Nuclease) assays using antibodies against endogenous IRF1 or IRF9 and observed higher occupancy of both IRF1 and IRF9 on the promoter region of TRIM22 in ERKi-R Pa01C cells (Fig. 4J and K).

The mechanistic link between ERK inhibition and IRF upregulation remains incompletely understood. Interestingly, combined bioinformatic and microarray analyses, followed by ChIP-qPCR, demonstrated that unphosphorylated ERK2 can bind to the IRF9 promoter and that this occupancy is reduced following IFN treatment, suggesting that ERK2 transcriptionally represses IRF9 (20). We therefore hypothesized that ERK inhibition might relieve this repression to permit IRF9 expression. Supporting this notion, ChIP-qPCR demonstrated that ERK2, and to a lesser extent ERK1, binds to the promoters of IRF1 and IRF9, and this occupancy was reduced in ERKi-R cells (Fig. 4L and M). By cell fractionation, ulixertinib treatment decreased total nuclear ERK2 and increased cytoplasmic and nuclear phospho-ERK2 (Supplementary Fig. S4K). We posit that as phospho-ERK2 is conformationally more compact than the more open, unphosphorylated ERK2 (21), it may not occupy IRF1/9 promoters as readily as unphosphorylated ERK2.

Besides increased expression, IRF1 and IRF9 are also phosphorylated by the upstream JAK-STAT cascade to promote expression of IFN-stimulated genes, including the IRFs themselves (22). In support, combined treatment with ulixertinib and the JAK1/2 inhibitor ruxolitinib blocked phospho-STAT1 and the expression of TRIM22 and IRF9 (Supplementary Fig. S4L) and more potently suppressed tumor growth in mice, exhibiting synergy in Capan-1 and additive/synergistic effects in Pa01C models (Fig. 4N; Supplementary Fig. S4M). Our findings suggest that sustained ERK inhibition drives IFN signaling and TRIM22 expression to promote EMT and therapeutic resistance.

TRIM22 degrades IκBα to promote NF-κB signaling

Next, we sought to delineate the mechanism by which TRIM22 promotes EMT. To complement the mRNA-based GeneRep data (Fig. 2A), we constructed a TRIM22-centered protein interactome using the BioGRID database (23), an open-access resource containing manually curated protein–protein interactions (24). This analysis identified NFKBIA (IκBα), IKBKG (IKKγ), and CHUK, central components of the canonical NF-κB pathway, as top interacting partners of TRIM22 (Fig. 5A). Pathway enrichment analysis of this interactome further nominated TRIM22 as a positive regulator of the NF-κB cascade (Fig. 5B). In support, NF-κB transcriptomic signatures and pathways genes, including RELA, NFKB1, IKBKG, and CHUK, were upregulated in ulixertinib-treated patient PDAC samples and PDCLs, as well as when TRIM22 was overexpressed (Figs. 1A, B, and 3A; Supplementary Fig. S5A). Interestingly, Dilly and colleagues (1) also reported that PDAC cells resistant to KRAS inhibitors exhibit elevated EMT signatures and increased phosphorylation of RELA (NF-κBp65). Analysis of PDAC data from GEPIA2 revealed strong positive correlation between TRIM22 and Biocarta NF-κB signature (R = 0.78; Supplementary Fig. S5B).

We showed that ERKi-R cells exhibit decreased IκBα, along with increased TRIM22 and phospho-RELA levels (Fig. 5C; Supplementary Fig. S5C). ERKi-R and KRASi-R Pa01C cells also had higher nuclear RELA levels (Fig. 5D). In autochthonous KPPC tumors, NF-κB signature was enriched within the pEMT and basal-like PDAC subpopulations (Fig. 5E), which also displayed upregulated IFN and EMT signatures. TRIM22 contains an N-terminal RING finger with E3 ligase activity, a B-box domain that facilitates protein–protein interactions, a coiled-coil (CC) region involved in homodimerization, and a C-terminal PRY/SPRY domain implicated in substrate recognition (25). We found that overexpression of WT but not the RING-truncated or catalytically inactive C15/18A TRIM22 (26) was sufficient to activate NF-κB–driven luciferase activity in 293T cells (Fig. 5F). Furthermore, TRIM22 reduced IκBα and increased phospho-RELA levels (Fig. 5G), leading us to hypothesize that TRIM22 degrades IκBα to enable RELA phosphorylation and nuclear translocation (27). Indeed, overexpression of TRIM22 accelerated attrition of endogenous IκBα in CHX time-chase assays (Fig. 5H), and this effect was blocked by the proteasome inhibitor bortezomib (Fig. 5I). TRIM22 binds and promotes K48-polyubiquitination of IκBα (Fig. 5J). In ERKi-R Pa01C cells, TRIM22 knockdown led to elevated total IκBα, reduced phospho-RELA levels (Fig. 5K), and delayed IκBα degradation (Fig. 5L). Supporting NF-κB activation as the driver of EMT, overexpression of RELA in naïve PDAC cells decreased E-cadherin, increased vimentin expression, and drove acquisition of spindled morphology (Fig. 5M; Supplementary Fig. S5D). In KPPC cells, Trim30a or Trim30d knockdown also resulted in increased IκBα and E-cadherin and decreased Snail1 expression (Supplementary Fig. S5E). Therefore, TRIM22 drives EMT at least in part by driving NF-κB.

Prolonged KRAS inhibition activates IFN response and promotes EMT

Next, we sought to determine whether our findings with ulixertinib extended to KRAS inhibitors. To this end, we treated KRASG12D- or KRASG12C-mutant PDAC cells with MRTX1133 or the KRASG12C inhibitor AMG-510, respectively, for 2 weeks to create KRASi-R cells. We observed strikingly similar transcriptomic changes between KRASi-R and ERKi-R cells, including upregulated EMT, IFN, and NF-κB, and the expected downregulated KRAS and MAPK signatures (Fig. 6A and B; Supplementary Fig. S6A–S6C), corroborating a recent study demonstrating a high degree of overlap between the transcriptomic signatures of ERK and KRAS pathways (13).

Next, we applied the GeneRep-nSCORE algorithm on the transcriptomic data on vehicle and KRASi-R PDAC cells. As with the ulixertinib data, TRIM22 was nominated as the highest-ranked master regulator orchestrating the transcriptomic changes in KRASi-R cells (Fig. 6C), whereas TRIM29 and IRF1 were also found to be relevant. KRASi-R cells had a more spindled morphology (Fig. 6D) and expressed less ZO-1, E-cadherin, and IκBα and more vimentin, TRIM22, IRF1, and IRF9 (Fig. 6E–G). CUT&RUN assays demonstrated enhanced occupancy of IRF1 and IRF9 at the promoter regions of TRIM22 in KRASi-R cells (Fig. 6H and I). ChIP-qPCR showed decreased binding of ERK2 both IRF1 and IRF9 promoter regions (Fig. 6J and K), recapitulating findings with ERKi-R cells.

Recently, the multiselective RAS(ON) inhibitor RMC-6236 has shown very promising efficacy in an early-phase clinical trial for chemorefractory patients with PDAC. We treated autochthonous KPPC mice with RMC-6236 (25 mg/kg) for 2 weeks and performed scRNA-seq on the resulting PDAC tumors. Similar to ulixertinib, RMC-6236 treatment induced a shift in PDAC cell subpopulations from a classic subtype toward a more basal-like state (Fig. 6L), characterized by upregulated type I/II IFN, NF-κB, and EMT gene signatures (Fig. 6M, N, and O; Supplementary Fig. S6D). Consistently, cultured KPPC cells treated with RMC-6236, MRTX1133, or ulixertinib for 2 weeks expressed less E-cadherin and more N-cadherin and TRIM30 (Supplementary Fig. S6E). Neither ulixertinib nor RMC-6236 induced TRIM22 expression in 293T cells after 2 weeks of treatment (Supplementary Fig. S6F), suggesting that this response is specific to RAS-mutant cancers. These findings demonstrate shared transcriptional reprogramming induced by prolonged KRAS and ERK inhibition in PDAC cells.

TROP2 is upregulated following EMT and represents a therapeutic vulnerability

By analyzing genes differentially expressed between naïve, ERKi-R, and KRASi-R PDCLs, we identified tumor-Associated Calcium Signal Transducer 2 (TACSTD2), which encodes TROP2, as one of the significantly upregulated genes, along with EMT-, IFN-, and NF-κB–related genes (Fig. 7A and B). As we did not detect increased occupancy of the TACSTD2 promoter regions by IRF1 or IRF9 in ERKi-R cells, we performed CUT&RUN using anti-RELA antibody and detected increased binding and reads of TACSTD2 promoter in ERKi-R PDAC cells (Fig. 7C), implicating RELA as a transcription factor that drives TACSTD2 expression. In support, GEPIA3 database revealed strong positive correlations between TACSTD2 and TRIM22 or RELA across noncancerous pancreatic tissues and PDAC (Fig. 7D). Additionally, TACSTD2 expression was elevated in PDAC compared with normal pancreatic tissue (Supplementary Fig. S7A) and was higher in advanced-stage (III–IV) tumors (Supplementary Fig. S7B). High TACSTD2 levels were also associated with significantly shorter disease-free survival and showed a trend toward reduced OS (Supplementary Fig. S7C and D).

By independent qRT-PCR, both ERKi-R and KRASi-R PDAC cells express significantly higher TACSTD2 (Fig. 7E and F), which was supported by IF and flow cytometry showing higher total and surface TROP2 protein levels (Fig. 7G; Supplementary Fig. S7E). Pa01C cells treated with ulixertinib, MRTX1133, or RMC-6236 for 2 weeks also exhibited elevated TRIM22 and TROP2 levels (Supplementary Fig. S7F). Notably, although prolonged treatment with ulixertinib and MRTX1133 resulted in increased phosphorylation of IKKα/β, IκBα, and RELA, decreased total IκBα, as well as increased TROP2 expression, these changes were blocked by cotreatment with the IKKβ inhibitor IMD-0354 (Supplementary Fig. S7G). Overexpression of RELA was sufficient to drive TROP2 expression in naïve PDAC cells (Fig. 5M). Ulixertinib-treated patient PDAC tumors and xenograft tumors treated with MRTX1133, selumetinib, trametinib, or ulixertinib displayed increased TROP2 expression by IHC by H-score (Fig. 7H; Supplementary Fig. S7H). Next, we pooled and reanalyzed scRNA-seq datasets from 40 primary PDAC samples published in three independent studies (28–30) using DEGs defined by Dilly and colleagues (1) and found that TACSTD2 expression was enriched within the pEMT and basal subpopulations (Fig. 7I and J). Supporting our data, the basal subpopulation displayed stronger type I/II IFN and NF-κB signatures (Fig. 7K; Supplementary Fig. S7I).

TROP2-targeted antibody–drug conjugates (ADC) including SG and datopotamab deruxtecan, are now FDA-approved for patients with breast and lung cancers (31), but their therapeutic potential in PDAC remains unexplored. To this end, cotreatment with ulixertinib or KRAS inhibitors and SG more potently suppressed PDAC xenograft growth in mice and more synergy was observed with KRASi (Fig. 7L; Supplementary Fig. S7J). Notably, combo-treated mice maintained stable body weight throughout the course of treatment (Supplementary Fig. S8A). When euthanized at humane endpoints, we did not observe histologic abnormalities in the major organs, including the lungs, liver, kidneys, and intestines, of combo-treated mice (Supplementary Fig. S8B–S8D). However, it should be noted that SG does not recognize murine TROP2, and thus the systemic toxicities from TROP2-ADC cannot be assessed. Despite this limitation, our data provide sound preclinical rationale for advancing KRAS or MAPK inhibitors in combination with TROP2-ADCs for patients with PDAC, with close monitoring for toxicities.

Discussion

Our study provides a molecular roadmap linking prolonged KRAS/MAPK inhibitor–induced IFN signaling to TRIM22 expression and NF-κB activation, culminating in EMT, a cell state associated with therapeutic resistance. This process is triggered by relieving ERK-mediated suppression of IRF1/9, which drives TRIM22 expression. Coincidentally, Klomp and colleagues (13) showed TRIM22 as one of the upregulated genes following 1 day of ERK inhibition, along with enrichment of IFN and EMT signatures following prolonged (8–14 days) of ERKi or KRASi treatment. Genomic analyses of tumors that become resistant to KRASG12C inhibitors revealed secondary KRAS mutations and/or activation of receptor tyrosine kinase pathways. To become resistant, tumor cell population as a whole must survive the initial insult of KRAS inhibition by signal rewiring, adaptive transcriptional reprogramming, and/or selection of subclones with reduced KRAS dependency. In this study, we also showed that IFN, NF-κB, and EMT signatures are enriched predominantly within the basal populations and that prolonged ERK/KRAS blockade further exacerbates these programs in these two subpopulations, corroborating findings by others that prolonged KRAS inhibition leads to enrichment of the basal state subpopulations that exhibit EMT, IFN, and TNF-NF-κB signatures (1, 3).

Based on and corroborating a previous report by Hu and colleagues (20) showing ERK2 as a transcriptional repressor for IRFs, we show that ulixertinib treatment blocked the occupancy of ERK2 on the promoters of IRF1/9. Interestingly, Hu and colleagues showed that kinase-dead (K54R) ERK2 could block IRF9 transcription to the same extent as WT ERK2. Although the K54R mutation in the N-lobe ATP-binding pocket impairs ERK2’s kinase activity, it does not alter ERK2’s conformation or affect protein–protein interaction (32). On the contrary, phosphorylation at T183/Y185 triggers extensive global and local conformational changes that transform ERK1/2 from an inactive, open state to a highly active, compact form (21). We posit that spuriously hyperphosphorylated ERK1/2 induced by ulixertinib is defective in recognizing or interacting with other corepressors to occupy IRF1/9 promoters, as we observed decreased ERK2 occupancy on IRF1/9 promoters in ERKi-R cells. In KRASi-R cells, we also observed decreased occupancy of ERK2 on IRF1/9 promoters. This can be explained by the well-established knowledge that phosphorylation of cytoplasmic ERK1/2 at T183/Y185 is required for nuclear translocation (33). Once in the nucleus, phospho-ERK1/2 is dephosphorylated by the DUSP phosphatases, thereby allowing the phosphorylated ERK to repress IRF1/9.

We identified TRIM22 as an IFN-response gene that orchestrates the reprogramed transcriptomic network of ERKi-R or KRASi-R PDAC cells and further found that combined ulixertinib and ruxolitinib treatment intercepted TRIM22 expression and more potently suppressed PDAC tumor growth. As ulixertinib causes significant toxicities, including rash, fatigue, diarrhea, and myalgias (6), despite suppressing KRAS signatures, it is tempting to hypothesize that these symptoms are IFN-driven. In future clinical trials, combining KRAS/MEK and JAK inhibitors may achieve the dual goals of better treatment efficacy and mitigation of side effects, if shown to be IFN-related. Beyond the JAK pathway, we identified TROP2 as an additional, actionable vulnerability that emerges following ERKi-induced cell state transition. TROP2, initially characterized as a tumor-associated calcium signal transducer, activates the PI3K/AKT, Wnt/β-catenin, MAPK, and JAK/STAT mainly via calcium signaling (34). Therefore, under sustained KRAS inhibition, TROP2 may help cells circumvent RAS-driven MAPK signaling through utilizing these alternate pathways for survival. Accordingly, combining TROP2-ADC with KRAS inhibitors represents a rational strategy to suppress the emergence of resistant clones more effectively.

Our study has the following limitations. First, although we conducted mechanistic experiments largely in PDCLs, where extended culture can promote EMT, we minimized this confounder by using early-passaged PDCLs and validated our findings using state-of-the-art KPPC mice and matched human PDAC samples. Second, whereas we focused mechanistically on TRIM22 as a driver of NF-κB and EMT, other candidate regulators, including CREB3L1, TRIM29, and MMP14, were not examined in depth. Third, our ability to assess systemic toxicity was limited by the use of murine models, as SG does not recognize mouse TROP2.

In summary, our study uncovers a mechanistic link between prolonged KRAS–MAPK inhibition and IFN-driven NF-κB activation orchestrated by TRIM22, culminating in a basal-like, TROP2-high cell state. These findings nominate the IFN pathway and TROP2 as actionable therapeutic vulnerabilities that can be targeted to improve and prolong the efficacy of KRAS inhibitors.
