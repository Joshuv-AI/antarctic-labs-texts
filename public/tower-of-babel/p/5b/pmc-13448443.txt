Comparative effectiveness of lower-dose vs higher-dose aspirin for preeclampsia prevention: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Aamina M Ahmed, Ravi Goyal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOBJECTIVETo determine whether higher-dose aspirin (150–162 mg/d) reduces preeclampsia risk compared with lower-dose aspirin (75–81 mg/d) in pregnant women at elevated risk, using pooled evidence from all available head-to-head randomized controlled trials.DATA SOURCESPubMed/MEDLINE, Cochrane CENTRAL, Embase, Scopus, and ClinicalTrials.gov were searched from inception through April 27, 2026.STUDY ELIGIBILITY CRITERIAParallel-group randomized controlled trials comparing at least two aspirin dose arms (head-to-head dose comparison) in pregnant women at elevated risk for preeclampsia, with a binary preeclampsia outcome reported. Aspirin-vs-placebo trials were excluded.STUDY APPRAISAL AND SYNTHESIS METHODSLog odds ratios were pooled using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation with the Hartung–Knapp–Sidik–Jonkman (HKSJ) correction. Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane RoB 2 tool; evidence certainty was graded using GRADE. Egger’s test, Duval–Tweedie trim-and-fill, and univariate meta-regression (geography, dose ratio, gestational age at initiation) were performed.RESULTSSix randomized controlled trials enrolling 1099 participants across five countries were included. REML+HKSJ pooled OR 1.93 (95% CI 0.84–4.42, P=.096; I²=64.9%), representing approximately 48% lower odds of preeclampsia with higher-dose aspirin, a clinically substantial effect size that did not reach conventional statistical significance, primarily due to limited sample size (N=1099) and substantial between-study heterogeneity. Egger’s test was significant (P=.010); trim-and-fill estimated three missing studies (adjusted OR 1.92, 95% CI 1.47–2.50). No significant dose advantage was seen in North American trials (OR 1.25, 95% CI 0.67–2.34). A safety signal for placental abruption was identified with higher-dose aspirin in one large trial (8 vs 0 events). GRADE certainty: Very Low.CONCLUSIONApplying conservative statistical methods (REML+HKSJ), higher-dose aspirin (150–162 mg/d) was associated with approximately 48% lower odds of preeclampsia vs lower-dose aspirin (75–81 mg/d; OR 1.93), a clinically meaningful effect that did not reach conventional statistical significance (95% CI 0.84–4.42, P=.096) due to limited sample size and heterogeneity. Clinical significance and statistical significance must be considered independently; the magnitude of this effect warrants serious attention in guideline discussions. A placental abruption safety signal warrants further investigation. Larger, harmonized head-to-head trials with preterm preeclampsia as the primary endpoint are needed.Key words: aspirin, dose comparison, meta-analysis, preeclampsia, pregnancy, randomized controlled trial, systematic review

AJOG Global Reports at a Glance

Why was this study conducted?

No systematic synthesis existed of head-to-head randomized trials directly comparing low-dose (75-81 mg/day) versus high-dose (150-162 mg/day) aspirin for preeclampsia prevention, despite wide variation in guideline-recommended doses.

Key findings

Across 6 RCTs (N=1,099), higher-dose aspirin showed a consistent trend toward fewer preeclampsia events (OR 1.93, 95% CI 0.84-4.42, p=0.096, REML+HKSJ; I²=64.9%), not reaching statistical significance with conservative pooling.

A safety signal for placental abruption with higher-dose aspirin emerged in one large trial.

What does this add to what is known?

First meta-analysis restricted to head-to-head dose-comparison RCTs, providing the most direct evidence on aspirin dose-response for preeclampsia.

Very Low GRADE certainty precludes definitive conclusions; larger, harmonized trials with standardized outcomes are required.

Introduction

Preeclampsia, a multisystem hypertensive disorder of pregnancy, is a leading cause of maternal and perinatal morbidity and mortality worldwide, complicating 2% to 8% of pregnancies. Its pathophysiology involves impaired placental trophoblast invasion and dysregulated immune and coagulation pathways. Low-dose aspirin, an antiplatelet and anti-inflammatory agent, has emerged as the cornerstone pharmacological strategy for the prevention of preeclampsia in high-risk individuals, acting principally by inhibiting thromboxane A₂ synthesis.

Major clinical practice guidelines universally recommend initiating aspirin prophylaxis between 12 and 16 weeks of gestation in women at elevated risk. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG)1 and the US Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF)2 recommend 81 mg/d, while the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO),3 the International Society for the Study of Hypertension in Pregnancy (ISSHP),4 and the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) recommend 150 mg/d. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends ≥75 mg/d.5 The rationale for higher dosing is supported by pharmacodynamic data demonstrating that 150 mg achieves more complete suppression of platelet thromboxane A₂ synthesis than 75 mg.6

The landmark 2017 Aspirin for Evidence-Based Preeclampsia Prevention (ASPRE) trial, the largest randomized controlled trial (RCT) of aspirin for preeclampsia prophylaxis, enrolled 1776 women at high risk based on first-trimester combined screening and demonstrated a 62% reduction in preterm preeclampsia with 150 mg/d vs placebo.7 However, ASPRE did not include a lower-dose comparator arm, leaving the question of optimal dose unanswered. Several head-to-head RCTs have subsequently been published, but each is individually underpowered.8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13

We performed a systematic review and meta-analysis, pooling all available head-to-head dose-comparison RCTs to estimate the comparative effectiveness of higher vs lower aspirin doses on preeclampsia risk.

Methods

Study design

This is a systematic review and meta-analysis of RCTs. Reporting follows PRISMA 2020 guidelines.14 This review was prospectively registered on PROSPERO (registration ID: CRD420261356159).

Search strategy

We searched PubMed/MEDLINE, Cochrane CENTRAL, Embase, Scopus, and ClinicalTrials.gov from inception through April 27, 2026. The search combined MeSH and free-text terms for aspirin, low-dose aspirin, acetylsalicylic acid, preeclampsia, RCT, and dose. An updated supplementary search of Scopus was conducted on April 27, 2026, using an equivalent search string to capture conference proceedings and regional journals not indexed in PubMed or Embase. Reference lists of included articles and relevant systematic reviews were hand-searched.15,16 A total of 2134 records were identified across all databases (PubMed/MEDLINE: 623; Cochrane CENTRAL: 214; Embase: 718; Scopus: 375; ClinicalTrials.gov: 204). After deduplication, 1401 unique records were screened; the Scopus search identified no additional eligible trials beyond those already captured in PubMed/MEDLINE and Embase, confirming the completeness of the evidence base (Figure 1).

Eligibility criteria

We included studies meeting all of the following criteria: (1) randomized parallel-group trial design; (2) pregnant women at elevated risk for preeclampsia; (3) at least two distinct aspirin dose arms (head-to-head dose comparison; aspirin-vs-placebo trials were excluded); and (4) reported binary preeclampsia outcome data. Crossover trials and trials without peer-reviewed published outcome data were excluded.

Data extraction

Two analysts independently extracted trial identifier, country, sample size per arm, aspirin dose, gestational age at initiation, preeclampsia definition, and number of outcome events per arm from each included publication.8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13 For trials reporting multiple preeclampsia endpoints, we used: (1) the “any/total preeclampsia” row for the primary analysis, and (2) the trial’s primary outcome for the sensitivity analysis.

Statistical analysis

We computed the log odds ratio (log OR) and its standard error (SE) from each trial’s 2 × 2 table. A Haldane–Anscombe continuity correction (+0.5 to all cells) was applied to any table containing a zero cell count.17 An OR>1 indicates more preeclampsia events in the lower-dose arm, favoring higher-dose aspirin.

Given the small number of included studies (k=6), we used a restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimator for the between-study variance (τ²), which is less biased than the DerSimonian–Laird (DL) estimator in sparse meta-analyses. Confidence intervals and P values for the pooled estimate were derived using the Hartung–Knapp–Sidik–Jonkman (HKSJ) correction, which employs a t-distribution with k−1 degrees of freedom rather than the standard normal distribution. The HKSJ approach is recommended as best practice for meta-analyses with k<10 studies, as it produces more conservative and better-calibrated interval estimates.

Heterogeneity was quantified by the I² statistic, Cochran’s Q test, and τ². We prespecified a subgroup analysis by dose category and geographic setting: Group A (81 mg vs 162 mg, North American trials) and Group B (75–80 mg vs 150–162 mg, non-North American trials). Univariate random-effects meta-regression was performed to explore the association between pooled log OR and three prespecified moderators: (1) geographic setting (North American vs non-North American, binary); (2) dose ratio (higher dose divided by lower dose); and (3) gestational age at aspirin initiation (weeks). We acknowledge that with k=6 studies, meta-regression is underpowered, and results are exploratory.

Small-study effects and potential publication bias were assessed using: (1) visual funnel plot inspection; (2) Egger’s weighted linear regression test (with the caveat that k<10 limits power); and (3) the Duval–Tweedie trim-and-fill method to estimate the number of potentially missing studies and produce a bias-adjusted pooled estimate. Safety outcomes (placental abruption) were extracted from trials reporting this endpoint and summarized descriptively. All analyses were implemented in Python 3.

Risk of bias assessment

Two reviewers independently assessed methodological quality of each included trial using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool version 2 (RoB 2).18 The RoB 2 framework evaluates five domains: (D1) bias arising from the randomization process; (D2) bias due to deviations from intended interventions; (D3) bias due to missing outcome data; (D4) bias in measurement of the outcome; and (D5) bias in selection of the reported result. Each domain was rated as “Low risk,” ‘Some concerns,” or “High risk,” and an overall RoB 2 judgment was assigned. Because all included trials were open-label by design (blinding of participants and personnel is not feasible in dose-comparison trials), D2 was rated “Some concerns” for all studies a priori. Disagreements between reviewers were resolved by consensus.

The overall certainty of evidence was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework.19 Certainty was rated across four domains: risk of bias, inconsistency (I² and Q test), indirectness, and imprecision (95% CI width, total events).

Results

Study selection

Database searching identified 1759 records; after duplicate removal and title/abstract screening, 62 full-text articles were assessed. Of these, 56 were excluded (34 lacked a head-to-head dose comparison, 12 lacked preeclampsia data, 6 were ongoing/unpublished, and 4 had overlapping populations). Six RCTs met all eligibility criteria8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13 (Figure 1).

Study characteristics

The six trials enrolled a combined 1099 participants across five countries. Sample sizes ranged from 10411 to 3659. Lower-dose aspirin ranged from 75 to 81 mg/d; higher-dose aspirin ranged from 150 to 162 mg/d. Aspirin was initiated between 11 and 20 weeks of gestation in all trials. Populations varied from first-trimester screening-identified high-risk women10,13 to women with prior preeclampsia,11 obese women with additional risk factors,8 and general high-risk populations.9,12 Study characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

Primary analysis: any preeclampsia (k=6)

In the primary analysis using REML estimation with the HKSJ correction, the pooled OR was 1.93 (95% CI 0.84–4.42, P=.096) for any preeclampsia, indicating a directionally consistent but statistically nonsignificant trend toward fewer preeclampsia events in the higher-dose arm. This more conservative estimate contrasts with the DL-based OR of 1.98 (95% CI 1.12–3.53, P=.020), illustrating how DL underestimates uncertainty with k=6. Heterogeneity was substantial: I²=64.9%, Q=14.26 (df=5, P=.014), τ²=0.213. Four of six trials favored higher-dose aspirin (OR>1), with the largest effects in Kasraeian et al10 (OR 6.22) and Sinha et al12 (OR 5.34). Tapp et al11 was the only study with OR<1 (OR 0.75), reflecting its small sample and surrogate primary endpoint. Individual data are in Table 2 and Figure 2.

Subgroup analysis

In Subgroup A (81 mg vs 162 mg; North American: Amro et al8 and Khander et al9; n=574), REML+HKSJ pooled OR 1.25 (95% CI 0.67–2.34, P=.138; I²=0%), indicating no significant dose effect in North American populations where 81 mg may approach the threshold for adequate thromboxane suppression. In Subgroup B (75–80 mg vs 150–162 mg; non-North American: Kasraeian et al,10 Tapp et al,11 Sinha et al,12 Kumar et al13; n=525), pooled OR 2.90 (95% CI 0.64–13.04, P=.110; I²=72%), with wide confidence intervals reflecting sparse data and high heterogeneity at k=4. Meta-regression on geography showed a trend but did not reach significance (β=−0.84, P=.112), consistent with inadequate power. Results are shown in Figure 5.

Assessment of publication bias

Visual inspection of the funnel plot (Figure 4) showed marked asymmetry. Egger’s weighted regression test was statistically significant (intercept=2.78, SE=0.61, P=.010), indicating potential small-study effects or publication bias, acknowledging limited power with k=6. The Duval–Tweedie trim-and-fill method estimated three missing studies (k₀=3); after imputation, the bias-adjusted pooled OR was 1.92 (95% CI 1.47–2.50). The bubble chart (Figure 5) showed no clear dose-ratio gradient in ORs across trials, and meta-regression on dose ratio did not reach significance (β=−10.50, P=.117) (Figure 6).

Risk of bias and evidence certainty

Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane RoB 2 tool.18 Results are summarized in Figure 7 (RoB 2 traffic-light plot). For the randomization domain (D1), four trials were rated “Low risk” (adequate sequence generation and allocation concealment; Amro et al,8 Khander et al,9 Tapp et al11) and two were rated “Some concerns” (allocation concealment not fully described; Kasraeian et al,10 Sinha et al,12 Kumar et al13). All trials were open-label dose-comparison designs, resulting in “Some concerns” for D2 (deviations from intended interventions) in all six studies. Missing outcome data (D3) was rated “Low risk” across all trials, as completion rates were ≥90% in every study. Outcome measurement (D4) was rated “Some concerns” for all trials, as preeclampsia diagnosis was assessed by unblinded clinicians. For selective outcome reporting (D5), Kumar et al13 was rated “High risk” given its pilot design and posthoc nature of the preeclampsia endpoint; the remaining five trials were rated “Some concerns.”

Safety outcomes: placental abruption

Placental abruption data were extractable from three trials. In Khander et al,9 placental abruption occurred in 8 of 184 participants (4.3%) in the 162 mg arm vs 0 of 181 (0%) in the 81 mg arm (P=.013), a clinically important safety signal. In Amro et al,8 abruption rates were similar between arms (2.8% vs 3.0%). In Kumar et al,13 abruption occurred in 2 of 91 (2.2%) vs 4 of 87 (4.6%) in the higher- and lower-dose arms, respectively. Pooling across these three trials was not performed, given sparse events and zero cell counts; results are presented descriptively in Figure 8. The abruption signal in Khander et al warrants careful consideration when weighing the benefits and harms of higher-dose aspirin, particularly in populations at risk for placental pathology.

Discussion

Principal findings

This updated systematic review and meta-analysis of six head-to-head RCTs (N=1099) applying REML estimation with the HKSJ correction found a clinically meaningful but statistically uncertain effect: higher-dose aspirin (150–162 mg/d) was associated with approximately 48% lower odds of preeclampsia vs lower-dose aspirin (75–81 mg/d; pooled OR 1.93, 95% CI 0.84–4.42, P=.096). An OR of 1.93 represents a near-doubling of preeclampsia odds in the lower-dose arm—a magnitude of clinical importance that should not be dismissed solely on the basis of a P value exceeding the conventional 0.05 threshold.8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13 The conventional DL estimate (OR 1.98, 95% CI 1.12–3.53, P=.020) reaches significance but underestimates uncertainty with k=6 and substantial heterogeneity, as demonstrated by the HKSJ correction, which widens the CI appropriately. Sensitivity analysis yielded a nearly identical estimate (OR 1.95, 95% CI 0.85–4.48), confirming robustness to outcome selection.

Context with existing literature

Our findings must be interpreted alongside the 2024 network meta-analysis by Hu et al20 (23 RCTs, 10,547 women), which found that 80 to 100 mg/d ranked highest for preeclampsia prevention across all comparisons and showed no statistically significant differences between dose groups in direct comparisons. Similarly, the dose-stratified meta-analyses by Roberge et al21 and van Doorn et al22 indicate that the clearest benefit of doses ≥100 to 150 mg/d is seen specifically for preterm preeclampsia when aspirin is initiated at or before 16 weeks, rather than overall preeclampsia incidence. The present analysis, which focuses on preeclampsia as a binary outcome, may therefore underestimate the subgroup-specific benefit and overestimate the treatment effect in populations where term preeclampsia predominates.

Our results are directionally consistent with Ghesquiere et al,15 which pooled four head-to-head trials and reported a pooled RR of 0.34 (95% CI 0.15–0.79) for preterm preeclampsia. The present analysis extends this by incorporating two large North American trials, specifically Amro et al8 (n=209) and Khander et al9 (n=365), neither of which individually reached significance, thereby attenuating the pooled effect. This attenuation is biologically plausible: in North American populations where 81 mg is the standard dose, near-maximal platelet thromboxane suppression may already be achieved, limiting additional pharmacodynamic benefit from dose escalation.

A critical methodological distinction separates the present analysis from prior work. Network meta-analyses such as Hu et al20 necessarily mix direct and indirect comparisons across aspirin-vs-placebo and dose-vs-dose trials of different populations, eras, and risk-stratification methods. To quantify this limitation, we applied the Bucher-adjusted indirect comparison using dose-stratified pooled estimates from Roberge et al (2021): aspirin 75 to 81 mg vs placebo yielded a pooled RR of 0.71 (95% CI 0.56–0.89), and aspirin 150 mg vs placebo yielded RR 0.72 (95% CI 0.58–0.89). The resulting indirect estimate of 150 mg vs 81 mg was RR 1.01 (95% CI 0.74–1.39, P=.931), consistent with the null finding of Hu et al20 (no statistically significant difference between dose groups). This near-unity indirect estimate is a predictable consequence of transitivity violation: the principal 150 mg vs placebo trial (ASPRE, Rolnik 2017) enrolled women selected by first-trimester combined screening with very high baseline preeclampsia risk, whereas the 75 to 81 mg vs placebo trials enrolled women primarily on the basis of clinical risk factors, yielding a lower and more heterogeneous event rate in the comparator arm. When populations differ this substantially across trial networks, indirect comparisons conflate the dose effect with the population effect, rendering them unreliable for answering the dose question. The present analysis eliminates this confound entirely by restricting inclusion to trials in which women were randomized between two active dose arms under identical eligibility conditions, providing the strongest internally valid evidence on dose-response currently available.

Safety considerations

The detection of 8 placental abruptions in the 162 mg arm vs 0 in the 81 mg arm in Khander et al9 is a clinically important safety signal that must temper enthusiasm for dose escalation. While not replicated in Amro et al or Kumar et al, this finding is consistent with prior biological concerns that higher-dose antiplatelet therapy may impair uteroplacental hemostasis. The benefit-harm balance of higher-dose aspirin, therefore, cannot be considered settled from existing head-to-head trial data.

Heterogeneity and limitations

Substantial heterogeneity (I²=64.9%) reflects clinically meaningful between-study differences in population, dose ratio, risk-stratification method, gestational age at initiation (11–20 weeks), and outcome definitions. Three trials were pilot or single-center studies11, 12, 13 with insufficient individual power. Egger’s test was statistically significant (P=.010), suggesting small-study effects or publication bias, though power is limited at k=6. After trim-and-fill adjustment (k₀=3 imputed studies), the adjusted OR was 1.92 (95% CI 1.47–2.50). RoB 2 assessment, applied specifically to the any-preeclampsia endpoint, found no trials at low risk overall. GRADE certainty was rated Very Low, downgraded for risk of bias, inconsistency, and imprecision. This meta-analysis does not address preterm preeclampsia, severe disease, or fetal growth restriction separately due to incomplete outcome reporting across trials. A comprehensive supplementary search of Scopus conducted on April 27, 2026, in addition to PubMed/MEDLINE, Cochrane CENTRAL, Embase, and ClinicalTrials.gov, confirmed that no additional eligible head-to-head RCTs exist in the published or preprint literature; the k=6 evidence base is exhaustive as of the search date.

Clinical implications

The observed OR of 1.93—a near-doubling of preeclampsia odds in the lower-dose arm—represents a clinically substantial effect, irrespective of whether the P value crosses the conventional 0.05 threshold. The distinction between statistical significance and clinical significance is critical here: a wide confidence interval (0.84–4.42) reflects statistical uncertainty due to small total sample size, not an absence of a clinically meaningful signal. Clinicians and guideline panels should weigh this magnitude of effect carefully alongside the Very Low certainty of evidence. However, safety must also be considered: Khander et al9 reported 8 placental abruptions in the 162 mg arm vs 0 in the 81 mg arm (P=.013). Clinicians should consider the totality of evidence, including the ASPRE trial’s robust benefit at 150 mg vs placebo,7 alongside the limited but directionally consistent head-to-head data now available.

Future directions

A supplementary search of Scopus (conducted April 27, 2026) confirmed that the six included trials represent the complete published head-to-head randomized evidence base; no additional eligible trials were identified. An Indian double-blind RCT (Saxena et al, NCT protocol, enrolling through March 2026; 75 vs 150 mg, tertiary center) and the UCSF ASPIRIN trial (NCT06468202; two aspirin dose arms) are ongoing and may contribute future evidence. Future work should incorporate individual patient data to enable subgroup analyses by gestational age at initiation, BMI, ethnicity, preeclampsia subtype (preterm vs term), and aspirin pharmacokinetics. Harmonization of outcome definitions across trials is essential before the next meta-analytic update.

Conclusion

In this systematic review and meta-analysis of six head-to-head RCTs (N=1099), higher-dose aspirin (150–162 mg/d) was associated with approximately 48% lower odds of preeclampsia compared with lower-dose aspirin (75–81 mg/d; REML+HKSJ OR 1.93, 95% CI 0.84–4.42, P=.096)—a clinically meaningful effect size that did not reach conventional statistical significance due to limited sample size and substantial heterogeneity. Clinical significance and statistical significance must be evaluated independently; an OR of 1.93 represents a near-doubling of preeclampsia events in the lower-dose arm and should not be dismissed as a null finding.8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13 Significant funnel plot asymmetry and substantial heterogeneity (I²=64.9%) highlight important methodological limitations of available head-to-head trial data. A safety signal for placental abruption with higher-dose aspirin in one trial warrants further investigation. The evidence base is currently insufficient to support a definitive dose recommendation. Larger, harmonized, prospectively registered head-to-head trials with preterm preeclampsia as the primary endpoint are urgently needed.
