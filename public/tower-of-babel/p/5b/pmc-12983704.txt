Exploration of Ascending-Descending-Exiting-Entering in Yi Xue Zhong Zhong Can Xi Lu: from classical philosophical theory to a novel diagnostic framework
Authors: Gedi Zhang, Hongning Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract“Medical Records of Integrating Traditional Chinese and Western Medicine: Yi Xue Zhong Zhong Can Xi Lu” (abbreviated as YXZZCXL), authored by the influential physician Xichun Zhang, a pioneering authority on the integration of Chinese and Western medicine, enriched the path of integrated medicine. The formulas documented in this work are renowned as “formulas of proven efficacy” with excellent clinical efficacy, playing a significant role in the development of modern Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM). This raises a key question: what is the most effective way to study and understand YXZZCXL? Drawing on their learning journey, clinical practice, and prior research, we propose that commencing with the Qi philosophy concept of “Ascending-Descending-Exiting-Entering” (ADEE) provides a straightforward yet effective approach to comprehending the prescription composition within YXZZCXL and the underlying TCM treatment theories it encompasses. Crucially, this approach serves to synthesize Zhang’s scattered clinical insights into a coherent ADEE framework. The framework helps fill systematic gaps in the structure of his academic thought on formula design and treatment principles. Building upon this foundational ADEE framework, we may achieve a leap from ADEE theory towards the development of a novel Qi-movement-centered ADEE syndrome differentiation method. This method serves as a clinical diagnostic and therapeutic tool, effectively linking TCM’s ADEE theory to formulas exerting corresponding regulatory effects, and is particularly indicated for disorders characterized by abnormal Qi movement. It must be emphasized that utilizing ADEE for formula categorization in this study is not a mechanical exercise; rather, it offers four fundamental conceptual perspectives through which disease mechanisms and treatments can be understood. Notably, due to the lack of systematic elaboration on the specific concept of “Exiting-Entering” in classical Chinese medical texts, this study proposes a preliminary interpretation grounded in TCM Qi theory and invites scholarly discourse to refine its theoretical connotation. This three-stage research pathway—synthesizing classical theory, constructing a theoretical framework, and developing a novel differentiation method—is proposed as a potential model for studying other TCM classics. Overall, the most significant academic contribution of this study lies in the systematic proposal and construction of a novel theoretical framework for treatment based on syndrome differentiation, which emerges centuries after the last major advancement in TCM diagnostic approaches.Keywords: Qi movement; Ascending-Descending-Exiting-Entering (ADEE); Medical Records of Integrating Traditional Chinese and Western Medicine, Yi Xue Zhong Zhong Can Xi Lu (YXZZCXL); Xichun Zhang; Fang-zheng (Formula-pattern)

Introduction

“Medical Records of Integrating Traditional Chinese and Western Medicine: Yi Xue Zhong Zhong Can Xi Lu”, abbreviated as YXZZCXL, is a seminal masterpiece written by Xichun Zhang [1]. Spanning an impressive thirty volumes, this comprehensive work is a testament to the systematic knowledge and extensive clinical expertise accumulated by Zhang and his disciples over the years. Every prescription in the book is the result of their hands-on clinical practice, embodying the integration of theory and real-world application. So, A key feature of the book is its systematic integration of principles, methods, formulas, and herbs into a coherent framework. Moreover, the explanations of medical and pharmacological theories are presented with such clarity and precision that even the most complex concepts are easily understandable. No wonder YXZZCXL is widely regarded as “the most exemplary book in modern times” [2]. Due to its relatively recent origin, the book is notably accessible to contemporary learners, written in a style that artfully blends classical and vernacular Chinese. The linguistic and integrative approach serves as a bridge, facilitating a deeper comprehension of the text.

Zhang’s work offers a systematic exposition of the concept of Qi movement known as Ascending-Descending-Exiting-Entering (ADEE). Qi, a core conceptual category within traditional Chinese philosophical thought, constitutes a fundamental principle assimilated by TCM into its unique theoretical framework. This concept posits Qi as embodying dual attributes of substance and function: constituting the essential material foundation of the human body while simultaneously governing the functional operations of life activities, with these aspects being inseparably intertwined. Crucially, the dynamics of Qi are governed by the fundamental patterns of ascending, descending, exiting, and entering. For Zhang, this theoretical framework was not merely abstract; it formed the cornerstone of his therapeutic methodology. He employed the regulation of Qi movement via the ADEE framework as a primary therapeutic strategy. Accordingly, to address diverse pathological deviations in Qi movement, Zhang meticulously crafted a plethora of efficacious formulas. For instance, the Shengxian Decoction was formulated to invigorate and elevate the Pectoral Qi [3]. The Zhengan Xifeng Decoction [4] and Jianling Decoction [5] were meticulously designed to subdue and pacify the rebellious Liver Qi, and the Zhenshe Decoction [6] was ingeniously created to suppress the unruly, rebellious Stomach Qi. These formulas have found widespread and successful application in clinical settings. When the prescribed formulas align precisely with the patient’s syndrome, they often yield astonishing results, thereby garnering widespread acclaim and respect within the medical community.

Zhang’s exploration of the ADEE of Qi has significantly enriched the theoretical framework of Qi transformation in TCM. It has not only provided crucial theoretical guidance but also invaluable practical experience for clinical treatment. His insights offer a systematic and illuminating perspective on the intricate workings of the human body’s physiology and pathology, as well as on the art and science of formulating prescriptions and utilizing herbs [7]. During the process of studying and applying YXZZCXL, the authors have discovered that using the ADEE of Qi as an overarching framework is an extremely effective way to distill and summarize the book’s rich and diverse content. The approach not only highlights the timeless relevance of Zhang’s contributions but also underscores their potential to revolutionize our understanding and treatment of health and disease. Furthermore, to facilitate understanding for readers with insufficient familiarity with TCM and its theoretical underpinnings, while improving the text’s accessibility, this study will perform a comparative analysis of the distinct ontologies and epistemologies underpinning TCM and modern science, and examine the pathways for their integration. Detailed elaborations on this aspect are provided in Sect. “The ontological and epistemological foundations of the ADEE theory and its comparison with modern science”.

A concise synopsis of the accounts on ADEE in the classic works of TCM across different dynasties

A brief account of the records about the theory of ADEE in the classic works of TCM

In the Pre-Qin era, the Huangdi Neijing (Yellow Emperor’s Inner Canon) already contained records of the theory of ADEE in the realms of physiology and pathology [8]. Specifically, in Chap. 68, titled “On the pioneering Treatise of the Six Microcosmic Principles: Liuweizhi Dalun Pian” within Suwen, it is inscribed: “If the functions of Entering and Exiting cease, the spiritual mechanism shall be extinguished; if the movements of Ascending and Descending come to a standstill, the Vital Qi will be left in isolation and peril. “Moreover, in the chapter of “On the Great Treatise on Yin-Yang and Response Images: Yingyang Yingxiang Dalun” of Suwen, it is stated that “When the turbid Qi lodges in the upper part of the body, abdominal distension ensues.” These passages systematically articulate the fundamental role of ADEE in human physiology and pathology, stating that its disruption leads to disease [9]. They clearly highlight that any deviation from the appropriate state of the Qi mechanism is likely to give rise to various ailments.

The Treatise on Febrile and Miscellaneous Diseases: Shanghan Zabing Lun, authored by Zhongjing Zhang during the Han Dynasty, demonstrates an exceptional ability to correspond formulas with specific syndromes [10]. However, it places less emphasis on theoretical exploration, resulting in the absence of a direct exposition of the ADEE theory within its text. Nevertheless, this concept is vividly embodied throughout the formulas detailed in the treatise. For instance, Gegen Decoction is renowned for its efficacy in dispelling cold and alleviating muscular exterior symptoms, thereby facilitating the upward dispersion of Yang Qi along the Taiyang Meridian. Xuanfu Daizhe Decoction excels at tonifying deficiency and suppressing adverse Qi, effectively reducing rebellious Stomach Qi. Wuling Powder is adept at relieving exterior syndromes and promoting Qi transformation, aiding in diuresis and the elimination of dampness, thus Exiting the syndrome of water retention in the bladder. Sini Decoction, on the other hand, can restore Yang and rescue the body from collapse, primarily targeting the Yang of the Heart and Kidney.

During the Song and Jin dynasties [11], Gao Li stated in On the Spleen and Stomach: “When food enters the Stomach, the refined essence is first transported to the Spleen and then to the Lungs, Ascending in accordance with the patterns of spring and summer. After Ascending, it is then transported downward to the bladder to transform and excrete waste. and is finally discharged after digestion.” This vividly describes the ADEE functions of the Spleen and Stomach. In the Yuan Dynasty [12], Danxi Zhu recorded in The Heart Methods of Danxi: Dan Xi Xin Fa: “When that which should ascend fails to do so, that which should descend cannot descend, and that which is supposed to transform fails to transform, this indicates an abnormality in the process of transformation and transportation.” This shows the important role of the Ascending and Descending of Qi movement in the body.

During the Ming Dynasty [13], Jingyue Zhang expounded in Volume of Jingyue Quanshu: “The disharmony of visceral qi presents diversely in aspects of deficiency, excess, cold, and Heat. The stagnation within the meridians varies depending on the states of qi, blood, phlegm, and dampness. Nevertheless, when delving into the core essence, it all boils down to the ADEE of Qi and Blood.” This illuminates the fact that anomalies in the ADEE mechanism can give rise to a multitude of diseases, thereby uncovering the fundamental pathogenesis of these ailments.

During the Qing Dynasty, the theory of ADEE underwent comprehensive development and extensive dissemination. a number of classic medical texts documented this theory. For instance, medical works such as Case Records as Guidance for Clinical Practice: Linzheng Zhinan Yian by Tianshi Ye [14], Corrections of Medical Works: Yilin Gaicuo by Qingren Wang [15], Systematic Identification of Warm Diseases: Wenbing Tiaobian by Jutong Wu [16], Random Notes on Reading Medical Classics: Duyi Suibi by Xuehai Zhou [17], and Medical Reflections: Yuyi Cao by Jiayan Yu [18] delved deep into the crucial role of the ADEE theory in both physiological and pathological aspects. Each of these works approached the topic from distinct perspectives and entry points. Simultaneously, they expounded upon the systematic significance of this theory in the realm of treatment methodologies.

In the preceding text, we have presented a succinct overview of the historical records pertaining to the theory of ADEE as found in traditional Chinese medical classics across various epochs. It is clear that this theory has been a fundamental component of TCM since its earliest days, constituting one of its core principles. Moreover, this theory is deeply embedded in every aspect of the theoretical framework of TCM. Its impact is extensive, influencing the understanding of physiology and pathology within the domain of TCM. Additionally, it plays a important role in guiding clinical treatment strategies. Consequently, further comprehensive research on this theory is warranted. Zhang inherited and further developed the theory of ADEE. The theory is evident throughout his work, YXZZCXL, with specific expositions provided below. It was precisely this widespread application within the text that initially inspired us to propose using ADEE as the overarching framework for understanding and organizing the content of YXZZCXL.

The core content of the theory about ADEE

Following our overview of the historical evolution of ADEE, this section focuses on elucidating its fundamental principles. The theory of ADEE represents a distinct and exclusive theoretical perception within TCM. It encapsulates the systematic theoretical characteristics and unique treatment features inherent in this ancient medical system. Originating from Huangdi Neijing, the concept of ADEE holds systematic significance. As stated in the classic, “Should the processes of Entering and Exiting come to a halt, the spiritual mechanism will be extinguished; if the movements of Ascending and Descending cease, the Vital Qi will be left in a state of isolation and jeopardy. Thus, without the processes of Entering and Exiting, the cycle of growth, development, maturity, aging, and death cannot occur; without the movements of Ascending and Descending, the natural rhythm of growth, development, transformation, harvesting, and storage will be disrupted.”

Ascending denotes an upward tendency, signifying the upward movement of essential substances within the body, such as Qi, Blood, and Body Fluids. While descending signifies the downward movement and flow of these substances, the organism simultaneously promotes the downward movement of substances. These complementary actions engage in repeated cycles, ultimately establishing a persistent dynamic motion. Exiting implies an outward and dispersive tendency, indicating that substances inside the body are discharged either to the body surface or into the external environment, often accompanied by the expulsion of metabolic waste. Entering represents an inward and receptive tendency, suggesting that refined substances from the external environment enter the body, playing a crucial role in nourishing and supporting the body, thereby maintaining its normal physiological functions.

Under normal physiological conditions, the body’s ADEE mechanism serves as the bedrock for sustaining human life activities. By harmoniously coordinating the functions of the internal organs, it ensures the seamless and orderly operation of all parts of the body, keeping the body in a state of optimal health. However, in a pathological state, any disruption to the movement of ADEE can lead to disease. For example, when the Spleen Qi fails to ascend, symptoms like diarrhea and rectal prolapse may manifest. If the Stomach Qi fails to descend, one may experience symptoms such as nausea, vomiting, and abdominal distension. In cases where the Lung Qi is not properly diffused, cough and asthma may ensue. And when the Liver Qi rebels upward, symptoms like headache, dizziness, and even vomiting of blood may present themselves. Therefore, the true value of ADEE, both physiologically and pathologically, lies in its provision of a dual perspective that simultaneously delineates internal movement mechanisms (Ascending and Descending) and boundary exchange functions (Exiting and Entering), which represents a precise embodiment of TCM’s holistic and dynamic worldview within the realm of diagnostics.

The ontological and epistemological foundations of the ADEE theory and its comparison with modern science

Qi and ADEE: a process-relational ontology [19]

The theory of ADEE is deeply rooted in the concept of “Qi” in TCM. To avoid confusion with Western scientific categories, it must be clarified that Qi is not a discrete material substance nor a measurable form of energy. Rather, it embodies the fundamental dynamic process of life—a continuous state of becoming expressed through specific functional patterns. In this sense, TCM’s ontology aligns more closely with process philosophy [20, 21] than with classical Western substance metaphysics. From this perspective, the human body is not a static anatomical structure but a dynamic network of interrelated functional processes. Health is thus defined as the harmonious and directionally defined flow of Qi—namely, its proper ADEE.

Consequently, the spatial and temporal attributes within the ADEE framework are qualitative and relational rather than metric. “Ascending” and “descending” do not refer to movement along axes within a neutral, container-like geometric space. Instead, they describe dynamic functional polarities within the living organism—for example, the upward movement of “clear Yang” to the sensory orifices to enable cognition, and the downward movement of “turbid Yin” for excretion via urination and defecation. Similarly, “Exiting” and “Entering” refer to functional exchanges across the boundary between the living system and its environment, such as the intake of nutrients and clear air (Entering) and the discharge of metabolic waste and pathogens (Exiting).

Crucially, the internal-external boundary presumed here is not a fixed geometric division but a dynamic functional interface through which the organism continuously exchanges matter, energy, and information with its environment. In TCM, the internal is not an arbitrarily delineated region within an absolute space but the functional integrative center through which the organism sustains its vital activities; the external is the source and field of exchange and feedback. This distinction between internal and external is functional and relational, dynamically changing according to physiological or pathological states. For example, when pathogenic factors invade from the exterior to the interior, the treatment principle is oriented toward “Exiting.”

This framework emphasizes relationships and directional changes over fixed material properties, thereby shifting the diagnostic focus from structural lesions to patterns of dysfunction within dynamic relations. This process-relational ontology constitutes a fundamental philosophical distinction between TCM and anatomy-based Western medicine, providing the necessary theoretical context for the ADEE diagnostic framework.

The qualitative dimension of time: sequence and direction in ADEE

In contrast to the Western scientific abstraction of time as a uniform, measurable, event-independent container or pipeline, the TCM conception of time is deeply embedded in the rhythms and directions of life processes. The temporality implied by the ADEE theory is qualitative, immanent, and directionally defined.

Firstly, ADEE itself constitutes a cyclical temporal structure. “Ascending” and “Exiting” are often associated with generative, outward-reaching, dispersive tendencies, corresponding to spring, summer, and the morning/noon of a day; “Descending” and “Entering” are linked to consolidating, inward-holding, settling tendencies, corresponding to autumn, winter, and the evening/night of a day. This is not the uniform passage of clock time but the rhythmic unfolding of life functions.

Secondly, time in pathological processes carries directional qualitative significance. The progression of pathogenic factors from the exterior to the interior, from superficial to deep layers (abnormal Entering), or the upward rebellion or downward collapse of pathological Qi (abnormal Ascending/Descending) are not merely changes in spatial location but represent different qualitative stages in the temporal evolution of disease. Therapeutic strategies such as following the trend to guide the flow are formulated precisely based on this directional temporal-pathological trajectory, rather than simply opposing a static lesion.

This view of time rejects the notion of time as a neutral dimension independent of life. On the contrary, time is the very form in which life processes unfold. As stated in the Huangdi Neijing: “If Exiting and Entering cease, the divine mechanism dissolves; if Ascending and Descending stop, the vital foundation is isolated and endangered.” The cessation of ADEE means the end of life’s time. Therefore, identifying ADEE patterns in diagnosis is simultaneously an assessment of the rhythm, sequence, and direction of the patient’s lived time.

Contrasting epistemologies: pattern recognition versus statistical generalization and their potential integration

The diagnostic approach derived from ADEE theory, like all TCM syndrome differentiation, operates on an epistemology of holistic pattern recognition. It aims to identify the unique qualitative configuration—the pattern or syndrome—of functional imbalance in an individual at a specific point in time. The ADEE framework provides a high-level taxonomy for such dynamic patterns (e.g., insufficient ascending, rebellious descending). The validity of this approach is established pragmatically through repeated clinical observation of treatment efficacy when the formula correctly matches the pattern. Such an epistemology aligns with Rein Raud, who againsts ontological essentialism, argues that “it is empirically justified to consider the workings of reality as a process to be primary, and its constituents, available as they are to us in a context-free form only as products of our mind, to be secondary” [22].

Unlike biomedical research that relies on statistical generalization, TCM’s pattern recognition focuses on individual functional configurations—a distinction that highlights complementary rather than contradictory approaches. Biomedical research relies on statistical generalization from controlled group studies to establish average causal effects. Biomedicine excels at identifying universal mechanisms and standardized treatment protocols, while TCM’s pattern-based reasoning is optimized for personalized, context-sensitive care that addresses the whole person in their unique presentation. These are not contradictory but complementary cognitive modes, each suited to different problem domains—the former to generalizable pathophysiology, the latter to individualized clinical encounters.

So, TCM’s pattern-based reasoning (e.g., ADEE) is not a precursor to biomedical science but a distinct knowledge system, cognitively adequate for individualized, context-rich healthcare. Recognizing this allows for a more nuanced integration of medical paradigms, moving beyond hierarchical judgments of “scientific” versus “unscientific” toward a complementary model in which statistical generalization and pattern-based reasoning jointly enrich our understanding of health and healing.

Toward an integrative epistemology: functional boundaries and symbiotic relationships

The ADEE framework thus embodies a qualitative, relational understanding of space and time that fundamentally differs from the metric, container-like spacetime of classical physics. In TCM, “Ascending” and “Descending” is not motion along an axis but a directional tendency within a living matrix of functions. Likewise, “Exiting” and “Entering” do not presuppose a static inside-outside boundary but describe the dynamic, permeable interface through which the organism interacts with its environment. This reflects a worldview in which spatial and temporal terms are inseparable from the organism’s functional state—a context-dependent temporality and a functional topology.

Ultimately, the theory of ADEE invites us to think of the body and the world in another way: the body is not an object trapped within a spacetime container but a dynamic relational field that generates itself in time and unfolds in space through the continuous processes of ADEE. Health is the directional and rhythmic harmony of these processes; disease is their directional disorder and rhythmic disharmony. This ontological and epistemological stance forms the genuine philosophical foundation for understanding ADEE as a diagnostic framework and opens a conceptual space for fruitful dialogue between Chinese and Western medicine.

The compatibility between the Fang-zheng and the basic theory of TCM

Zhang held the theories of Huangdi Neijing in systematic reverence. Without a doubt, Huangdi Neijing serves as the primary wellspring of the theories expounded in YXZZCXL, with the theory of ADEE being an integral part of this theoretical lineage [23]. Objectively speaking, within the pages of YXZZCXL, Zhang did not explicitly coin or present the term ADEE as a distinct theory. Nevertheless, whether in his interpretations of physiological processes, analyses of disease mechanisms, or descriptions of treatment approaches, the systematic theoretical implications of ADEE are evident throughout. Regarding the ADEE of the Qi mechanism, YXZZCXL expounds on the interrelationships among the pathogeneses of patients by means of the relationships between Zang-Fu organs, among zang organs, and between Zang-Fu organs and meridians. After obtaining the corresponding results of syndrome differentiation, formulas are formulated for treatment.

From a physiological perspective, taking the relationship between the Heart and the Kidney as an example, Zhang believed that the Heart stores the Monarch Fire, and the Kidney also stores the Ministerial Fire. These two organs support each other vertically, with the elements of Water and Fire in a harmonious and balanced state, thus ensuring the continuation of life. The Yin Essence of the Kidney ascends to nourish the Heart, and the Yang Qi of the Heart descends to interact with the Kidney. When the Heart and the Kidney communicate with each other and Yin and Yang are in a harmonious and balanced state, the body is in a healthy condition. This understanding of the physiological relationship vividly demonstrates the mutual coordination and support between the Heart and the Kidney in terms of physiological functions. The downward movement of Heart Fire can prevent Kidney Water from being too cold, and the upward movement of Kidney Water can restrain Heart Fire from being overly exuberant. The two work in concert to maintain the normal physiological functions of the Heart and the Kidney.

YXZZCXL also contains numerous records of the coordinated Ascending and Descending interactions among many other organs. For example, the Liver is located on the left side and is responsible for Ascending, generating and dispersing, while the Lung is located on the right side and is in charge of Descending and purifying. The Liver and the Lung interact with each other to jointly maintain the Ascending and Descending of the Qi mechanism. The Spleen and the Stomach are located in the Middle Jiao and play a crucial role. The Spleen is responsible for Ascending the Clear Qi, and the Stomach is responsible for Descending the Turbid Qi, regulating the Qi mechanism. On one hand, they ensure the intake, digestion, transformation of food and the excretion of metabolic waste; on the other hand, they assist in the ADEE of the Qi mechanism, thereby maintaining the normal physiological functions of the body. Similarly, there is also such an Ascending and Descending relationship between the Liver and the Kidney. The Kidney stores Essence, and the Liver governs the free flow of Qi. They coordinate with each other in the storage and release of Essence and Qi, enabling the proper regulation of the storage and release of Essence, and jointly play a role in realizing functions such as reproduction.

However, once this intricate physiological relationship within the body is disrupted, corresponding diseases will ensue. For example, when there is a deficiency of both Yin and Blood in the Liver and the Kidney, the Liver Yang cannot be adequately nourished. This leads to the internal movement of Liver Yang, disturbing the clear orifices. At the same time, the Liver Qi rushes upward, obstructing the descent of Lung Qi, thus triggering symptoms such as coughing and wheezing. Shenzhe Zhenqi Decoction is one of the formulas used to treat such related ailments.

In the seminal work, YXZZCXL, a plethora of analogous formulas are meticulously crafted adhering to this lineage of thought. This comprehensive work integrates “theory, principle, formula, and medicinal,” delving into the indications for these formulas with clarity and depth, unearthing the foundational tenets of TCM, and ultimately elucidating their application through clinical case studies. This method of diagnosis, treatment, and documentation vividly embodies the systematic concept of “Fang-zheng” thinking. The core of “Fang-zheng” lies in the distillation of effective medical practice experiences. A “syndrome” acts as a disease model, upon which the corresponding “prescription” is employed for treatment. A complete Fang-zheng correspondence should comprise a relatively fixed and intrinsically correlated set of symptoms, physical signs, tongue manifestations, pulse presentations, and information on urine and stool. In clinical practice, as long as a patient exhibits all these characteristics, the corresponding prescription can be applied. All the formulas within YXZZCXL were meticulously formulated by Zhang during his extensive clinical practice, guided by the fundamental theories of TCM. These formulas are not only “reliable and verifiable” but are also celebrated as “formulas with consistent efficacy.” As long as the prescription precisely matches the syndrome, remarkable clinical efficacy can be achieved. The developmental approach of TCM, which refines formula-pattern relationships under the guidance of fundamental TCM theories, substantiates that there is no inherent conflict between formula-pattern theory and the holistic theoretical system of TCM [24–25].

The theories of TCM stem from practical applications and embody a subjective interpretation of these practices. Ancient practitioners integrated classical philosophical concepts such as Essence Qi, Yin-Yang, and the intangible with their understanding of natural and societal laws. Through clinical observation, treatment outcomes, and rational knowledge formation, they interpreted the effective practice results of TCM, thus establishing its foundational theories. However, due to ancient scientific and technological limitations, a comprehensive revelation of TCM theories has not been fully realized. Consequently, TCM relies on accumulated past effective experiences (formula-pattern correspondence) and foundational theoretical guidance for diagnosis, treatment, and development. Over time, unique theories, including the concept of ADEE, have been progressively developed and applied to guide clinical practice, encapsulating the essence of diagnostic and therapeutic processes in TCM.

Categorization of formulas in YXZZCXL utilizing the principles of ADEE

The definition of the concepts of “Exiting and Entering” in TCM theory

Within the theoretical framework of TCM, the principles of “Ascending and Descending” have undergone extensive scholarly examination and validation, leaving little room for controversy. In stark contrast, records pertaining to “Exiting and Entering” remain comparatively scarce, and their theoretical implications lack the clarity and definition afforded to “Ascending and Descending.” Consequently, clarifying the theoretical boundaries of “Exiting and Entering” assumes heightened urgency, and we attempt to address this gap.

Based on clinical practice and literature review, we have established a preliminary definition of “Exiting”. Physiologically, “Exiting” manifests as the body’s natural elimination of metabolic waste through processes such as sweating, urination, and defecation. In pathological contexts, however, “Exiting” evolves into a therapeutic mechanism for expelling pathogenic factor or the excessive loss of the body’s refined substances, such as Qi, Blood, Body Fluids, and others. This encompasses the directed movement of pathogens from deep to superficial body layers and from internal organs toward the exterior—fundamentally creating an exit pathway for pathogenic influences. Notably, even when techniques like “clearing the bowels and suppressing turbidity” employ downward-draining herbs to expel pathogens via defecation, the intervention remains intrinsically classified under “Exiting”. Conversely, “Entering” physiologically denotes the absorption of beneficial substances, such as the reception and digestion of nutrients. Pathologically, it focuses on repairing deficiencies and nourishing debilitated constitutions. Specifically, all therapeutic strategies that tonify Qi and Blood, nourish Body Fluids, or harmonize Yin-Yang fall within the scope of “Entering.” This approach emphasizes fortifying the fundamental substances (e.g., Qi-Blood-Yin-Yang) and assimilating nourishment, primarily targeting patterns of Healthy Qi deficiency and functional decline. When clinically indicated, it may be synergistically combined with other therapeutic modalities. By establishing a preliminary definition of the connotation of “Exiting-Entering”, the definition bridges the critical gap in TCM theory, where the concept had previously been emphasized for its significance yet lacked substantive elaboration.

Although classical TCM texts provide few explicit definitions for the concept of “Exiting-Entering”, this study systematically elucidates its specific connotations within the theoretical framework of TCM. However, these interpretations may inevitably provoke academic debate. Here, we briefly trace its theoretical origins. Our research centers on the functional properties of Qi: TCM posits that Qi exhibits propelling, consolidating, defensive, and mediating effects, serving as an indispensable subtle substance crucial to sustaining life activities. Accordingly, under physiological conditions, functions like sweat secretion, urine production, bowel movement transmission, and pulmonary respiration (processes moving from inside outward) are categorized as “Exiting”; whereas processes such as receiving and digesting food/water, and inhaling natural Qi (movements occurring from outside inward) are regarded as “Entering.” Pathological diagnosis and treatment also adhere to this cognitive model for pattern differentiation and formula selection. It should be stressed that the definition of “Exiting-Entering” remains grounded in classical TCM theory, for instance, the Huangdi Neijing puts forward three principles for the treatment of edema, namely “opening the ghost gate, purifying the clean residence, and removing stagnated and obsolete accumulations”. Specifically, the “ghost gate” refers to sweat pores; the “clean residence” refers to the bladder; and “removing stagnated and obsolete accumulations” means shoveling away stagnated and obsolete accumulations. This vividly illustrates the “Exiting” principle in TCM for treating diseases, rather than a product of subjective conjecture. More significantly, the classification system effectively encompasses numerous TCM formulas, aiding practitioners in grasping their core principles and facilitating their clinical application.

The records of formulas exhibiting the function of “Ascending” in YXZZCXL

The concept of “Sunken Pectoral Qi” represents one of Zhang’s notable contributions to TCM [26]. He conducted extensive research on the theory of Pectoral Qi as presented in Huangdi Neijing. His groundbreaking assertion that Pectoral Qi is identical to Pectoral Qi has effectively addressed the challenging issue of defining the core nature of Pectoral Qi, thereby providing a robust theoretical foundation for treating diseases associated with abnormal Pectoral Qi. Drawing from his own medical practice and guided by the fundamental theories of TCM, Zhang identified the disease pattern known as “the syndrome of Sunken Pectoral Qi” and formulated Shengxian Decoction as its treatment. Additionally, he developed tailored treatments for patients presenting with concurrent syndromes: Huiyang Shengxian Decoction is used for those with concomitant Yang deficiency of the Heart and Lungs; Liyu Shengxian Decoction is prescribed for cases with concurrent qi stagnation and blood stasis; and Xingpi Shengxian Decoction is administered for extreme Spleen Qi deficiency accompanied by difficult urination. These new formula-pattern correspondence models were developed based on Zhang’s clinical experience.

Importantly, according to the disease model of the syndrome of “Sunken Pectoral Qi,” Shengxian Decoction can be applied to the patients who meet the criteria of this Fang-zheng. When concurrent syndromes are present, modifications can be made according to TCM theories to form new Fang-zheng models. The effectiveness of these Fang-zheng models further validates the underlying theory. Moreover, these new models can be reinterpreted from a theoretical perspective, contributing to the development of TCM theories and achieving mutual progress in both TCM theory and practice.

Additionally, YXZZCXL established astragalus membranaceus as the primary herb for tonifying Pectoral Qi [27]. Astragalus membranaceus has the effect of Ascending Yang and lifting the sunken. Depending on clinical needs, other herbs such as bupleurum chinense (to ascend Liver Qi) [28], platycodon grandiflorum (to ascend Lung Qi) [29], and cimicifuga foetida (to ascend the Qi of the Spleen and Stomach) [30] can be selected and combined to enhance the “Ascending” effect. The meaning of “Entering” is to make up for the deficiency of Qi, Blood, Yin, Yang, and Body Fluids in the body. Guided by this concept, we analyzed and organized the relevant formulas in YXZZCXL. From this, we can discover another significant contribution of Zhang to TCM: he formulated numerous formulas based on the principle of nourishing Yin, thus enriching and developing the theory of nourishing Yin treatment [35]. Among these formulas, the commonly used Yin-nourishing herbs by Zhang include Chinese yam, rehmannia root, scrophularia root, anemarrhena rhizome, white peony root, ophiopogon root, asparagus root, wolfberry fruit, etc. These seemingly ordinary herbs, under the ingenious application of Zhang, can exert infinite effects through flexible addition and subtraction of ingredients. Take the combination of astragalus membranaceus, anemarrhena rhizome, and rehmannia root as an example. Anemarrhena rhizome can nourish Yin and clear Heat. When paired with astragalus membranaceus, it can obtain a rising force. At the same time, rehmannia root, which is excellent for tonifying genuine Yin, is added as an adjunct. As is said in the book, “When Yang ascends and Yin responds, there will be a wonderful harmony like clouds moving and rain falling.” This shows that Zhang had an extremely accurate understanding of herbs, and his compatibility was truly exquisite, thus effectively nourishing the body’s Yin fluids.

In the compatibility of Zhang’s Yin-nourishing formulas, there is an important characteristic: he often added herbs to regulate the Spleen and Stomach. In this way, while the whole prescription exerts its effect of nourishing Yin, it will not interfere with the normal functions of the Spleen and Stomach. Take Zisheng Decoction as an example. In this prescription, Chinese yam and scrophularia root can nourish the Yin of the Stomach and Kidney, supplementing both the acquired and congenital aspects. Atractylodes macrocephala and chicken’s gizzard-membrane can strengthen the Spleen and improve digestion. Pioneering burdock achene has the effect of benefiting the Lungs and lubricating the intestines, and can promote the downward movement of Qi in the bowels. The whole prescription is exquisitely formulated. As a result, it is capable of increasing appetite and thus making the whole body strong and vigorous. Therefore, one is unlikely to suffer from consumption. Precisely for this reason, Zisheng Decoction can treat the disease of Yin deficiency and consumption.

Regarding “Yang”, Zhang believed that “human body Heat” mainly consists of two types: “Ruler Fire” and “ministerial fire”. The “Ruler Fire” is located in the Heart, and the “ministerial fire” is in the Kidney. The “Ruler Fire” is the fire within Yang. Its Heat descends to warm the Spleen and Stomach. If the Ruler Fire is weak, the digestive ability of the Spleen and Stomach will immediately decline. The “ministerial fire” in the Kidney is responsible for promoting strength and reproduction. If the “ministerial fire” is weak, the Kidney will be deficient and unable to perform strong functions [36, 37]. In terms of treatment, there are relatively few records in YXZZCXL. Among the formulas for treating Yang deficiency, only Dunfu Decoction, a prescription for treating the decline of ministerial fire, is recorded. For the treatment of the deficiency of Ruler Fire, YXZZCXL does not give a clear prescription. By analyzing Liyin Decoction and Huiyang Shengxian Decoction, which are used to treat the accumulation of cold fluid in the upper jiao and the sinking of Pectoral Qi caused by the Yang deficiency of the Heart and Lungs, both these formulas contain cassia twig and dried ginger. Thus, it can be seen that cassia twig and dried ginger are important herbs for warming and tonifying the Heart Yang.

In terms of tonifying Qi and Blood, Zhang’s method is different from the conventional ones. His commonly used prescription is Danggui Buxue Decoction. Zhang believed that Danggui Buxue Decoction can not only tonify Qi to generate blood but also promote the circulation of Qi and Blood. It is truly an excellent prescription for tonifying Qi and Blood. According to our initial definition of the meaning of “Exiting,” we have listed numerous corresponding formulas in YXZZCXL. The significance of “Exiting” lies in Exiting pathogenic factors from the body. Building on classic theories, Zhang combined his own clinical practice to expand traditional classic formulas. He modified or formulated numerous formulas with excellent clinical efficacy for Exiting pathogenic factors. For example, when using Mahuang Decoction, Anemarrhena asphodeloides can be added. On the one hand, it can clear Heat and promote the production of Body Fluids, facilitating sweating to expel pathogenic factors. On the other hand, it can prevent Mahuang Decoction from being too pungent and warm to damage Body Fluids. This is an inheritance and development of classic formulas [38].

Regarding the content of “Exiting,” in our opinion, what best reflects Zhang’s academic thoughts is his elaboration on the treatment of warm diseases, especially in the application of Gypsum Fibrosum (Raw). Zhang believed that gypsum is slightly cold rather than extremely cold, and its texture is heavy while its Qi is clear. The heavy texture can drive Heat downward, and the clear Qi can drive Heat upward, leaving no place for the externally contracted Heat pathogen to hide, thus enabling a rapid reduction of fever. In clinical practice, it is often combined with herbs that expel pathogenic factors, such as Forsythia suspensa, Cicada slough, and Mentha haplocalyx, and the dosage of the drugs is adjusted according to the needs of the condition. The curative effect is “even better than the best medicine.” To a certain extent, this clarifies the indications of gypsum and provides an important theoretical basis for its clinical application [39]. Additionally, Zhang advocated that gypsum must be used raw and not calcined. Because after being calcined, the properties of raw gypsum will change from purging and clearing to astringing. If patients with excess Heat use it, the pathogenic Heat will be blocked and unable to be expelled, thus aggravating the condition. At this time, gypsum changes from “the best medicine” to “a poison” [40]. Therefore, this requirement must be clearly understood in clinical practice.

In light of the foregoing comprehensive discussion, we have delineated the fundamental aspects of ADEE, encompassing its latent theore tical foundations within YXZZCXL and the corpus of relevant formulas. This limitation significantly impedes the translation of the theoretical construct into clinical practice, thereby highlighting an imperative need to establish a complementary methodology for “Qi movement syndrome differentiation.” Based on the theoretical framework of TCM’s ADEE and our novel definition of “Exiting-Entering,” we have established the ADEE syndrome differentiation method. Syndromes under the “Ascending” category of the ADEE syndrome differentiation

Definition

Ascension Pattern Syndrome refers to a pathological state characterized by insufficient Ascending movement or excessive Descending movement of Qi, leading to failure of Clear Yang to ascend and sinking of essential nutrients. Its core manifestations include fatigue, visceral ptosis, and chronic diarrhea. The essential pathology lies in the ​inability of the lifting force to sustain normal upward movement of Qi.

Clinical manifestations

Sallow complexion, anorexia with fatigue, dizziness and blurred vision; or exertional dyspnea aggravated by activity, hypophonia, spontaneous diaphoresis; or chronic diarrhea with rectal prolapse, uterine/gastric ptosis, dragging hypogastric pain; pale tongue; faint pulse at the cunposition (distal segment of radial artery).

Descending pattern syndrome denotes a pathological state characterized by dysfunctional Qi Descending movement or Ascending counterflow, resulting in failure of Turbid Yin to descend and pathogenic Qi surging upward. Core manifestations include vomiting, cough with dyspnea, headache, and vertigo. The essential pathology lies in the failure of visceral purification-descent mechanisms to sustain normal downward Qi movement.

Clinical manifestations

Headache with vertigo, facial flushing/conjunctival injection, irritability; or nausea/vomiting, belching/hiccups, epigastric distension with tenderness; or paroxysmal cough with wheezing, gurgling sputum, substernal oppression; wiry and forceful pulse at cunposition (radial styloid process level).

Vomiting orcough with dyspnea orheadache with distending pain, combined with wiry pulse at cunposition, constitute the cardinal diagnostic features (Table 6).

Definition

Efflux Pattern Syndrome refers to a pathological state characterized by dysfunctional outward Qi transformation, leading to either pathogen blockade with impaired discharge or unconsolidated leakage of essential substances. Its essence lies in the failure of the opening-discharging mechanism to maintain physiological balance between pathogen elimination and essence retention.​​.

Clinical manifestations

Type A: Pathogen Blockage: fever with anhidrosis, edema with oliguria, constipation with abdominal pain; or non-ruptured suppurations, dyspnea with copious sputum due to phlegm obstruction; forceful pulse. In summary, we have delineated the specific content of the ADEE syndrome differentiation method and have preliminarily developed a novel diagnostic tool for clinical practice in TCM. This marks a novel attempt to reconstruct a new method of syndrome differentiation centuries-long after the establishment of the Wei Qi Ying Xue and San Jiao differentiation systems. While this tool differs from conventional TCM syndrome differentiation methods, it remains closely interrelated with them. A detailed discussion will be presented in the following section.

Distinctions and interrelationships between ADEE syndrome differentiation and traditional TCM syndrome differentiation methods

Distinctions and interrelationships between ADEE syndrome differentiation and Eight-Principle syndrome differentiation

The ADEE syndrome differentiation method differs significantly from the Eight-Principle syndrome differentiation in the following three key aspects: First, there is a notable divergence in diagnostic focus. The ADEE method emphasizes the dynamic state of Qi movement, specifically addressing imbalances in its ascent and descent or obstacles in its Exiting and Entering. Its theoretical foundation and clinical application are closely centered on dynamic Qi transformation. In contrast, the Eight-Principle approach is grounded in the overall attributes of disease, encompassing dimensions such as yin and yang, exterior and interior, cold and Heat, and deficiency and excess. This method tends to provide a generalized, static characterization of pathological conditions. Second, the two methods differ in theirpoints of entry into pathological mechanisms. The ADEE approach highlights imbalances in Qi dynamics, positing abnormal Qi movement as a critical factor in the onset and progression of disease. Conversely, the Eight-Principle method focuses more on categorizing the nature of pathology, summarizing the essence of disease through the framework of its eight principles. Lastly, they offer distinct guidance for treatment. The ADEE method corresponds directly with Qi-regulating therapies, which aim to correct abnormal ADEE of Qi to restore physiological balance. On the other hand, the Eight-Principle method is more complex in its therapeutic application; it often must be integrated with other diagnostic systems, such as Zang-Fu organ differentiation, to formulate specific and comprehensive treatment strategies.

In this article, we conducted an in-depth exploration of the differences between the ADEE syndrome differentiation and the Eight-Principle syndrome differentiation. However, this does not imply that the two approaches are mutually exclusive or irreconcilable. On the contrary, they can be organically integrated and function synergistically in clinical practice. From a certain academic perspective, ADEE can be regarded as a refinement and important supplement to the Eight-Principle framework. Specifically, the concepts of “deficiency” and “excess” within the Eight Principles correspond closely to the states of Qi movement described by ADEE. For instance, deficiency patterns often involve inadequate Ascending or Entering of Qi, such as in sinking of Pectoral Qi, which generally falls under the category of Qi deficiency. Conversely, excess patterns frequently arise from impaired Descending or Exiting of Qi, as seen in Lung Qi obstruction, reflecting a pathological state of disrupted Qi dynamics. In terms of diagnosis, ADEE effectively fills a “gap” in the Eight-Principle framework regarding dynamic description. While the Eight Principles focus primarily on characterizing the static “outcomes” of disease, ADEE elucidates the dynamic processes of Qi movement, thereby complementing the relative lack of dynamic analysis in the Eight Principles. By combining the two, a more comprehensive and holistic understanding of disease mechanisms can be achieved, providing more precise theoretical guidance for clinical diagnosis and treatment. In clinical application, the ADEE method can be used in conjunction with the Eight-Principle differentiation. For example, the “Descending syndrome” must be further differentiated in terms of cold, Heat, deficiency, and excess. Based on the specific pattern identified, different “Descending” formulas are selected, forming a diagnostic chain of “disease nature-Qi movement-treatment principle.” This demonstrates how the two approaches can be effectively integrated in practice.

Distinctions and interrelationships between ADEE syndrome differentiation and Zang-Fu organ differentiation

Similar to Eight-Principles syndrome differentiation, ADEE syndrome differentiation demonstrates both distinct differences and essential interconnections with Zang-Fu organ differentiation, enabling synergistic application in clinical practice. The core distinctions manifest in three dimensions: Firstly, ADEE centers on directional Qi movement dynamics to address functional imbalances, whereas Zang-Fu organ differentiation focuses on static organ pathologies defined by structural-functional attributes, this dynamic-versus-static duality provides complementary diagnostic perspectives. Secondly, in pathomechanism interpretation, ADEE reveals dysfunctional Qi transformation processes, while Zang-Fu organ differentiation identifies organ-specific dysfunctions with precise lesion localization; consequently, ADEE requires integration with Zang-Fumethodology for accurate pathological site identification. Thirdly, regarding therapeutic targeting, ADEE directly guides Qi-modulating interventions (e.g., prescribing Descending formulas like Zhengan Xifeng Decoctionfor Rebellious Qi), whereas Zang-Fu organ differentiation necessitates organ-function-based strategies aligned with intrinsic physiological properties (e.g., selecting Liver-soothing herbs for constrained Liver Qi per its ‘governing free flow’ characteristic).

Conversely, beyond these distinctions, ADEE syndrome differentiation and Zang-Fu organ differentiation operate synergistically. Physiologically, Qi movement dynamics require zang-fuorgans as their functional substrates, exemplified by Zhang’s axiom in YXZZCXL: “Liver Ascension-Lung Descending constitutes the cornerstone of Qi equilibrium.” Pathologically, organ dysfunctions manifest through ADEE disturbances—conditions like ‘rebellious Liver Qi ascent’ or ‘Spleen Qi sinking’ represent quintessential expressions of visceral functional disorders mediated by impaired Qi movement. In clinical practice, these frameworks integrate to establish a comprehensive TCM diagnostic system: ADEE identifies specific Qi movement disorder patterns (e.g., ascension deficiency or Descending failure), while Zang-Fu organ differentiation precisely localizes pathological sites (e.g., Liver or Spleen involvement). This dual approach enhances diagnostic accuracy and therapeutic precision, ultimately optimizing clinical outcomes.

In summary, this study systematically elucidates the theoretical and clinical distinctions and interconnections between the newly constructed ADEE syndrome differentiation system and traditional diagnostic frameworks—Eight-Principles and Zang-Fu organ differentiation. Our analysis demonstrates that ADEE methodology does not contradict conventional approaches but achieves organic integration through dynamic Qi-modulation mechanisms, enabling synergistic clinical application. Notably, due to space constraints, this work has not explored cross-validation with other classical systems (e.g., Six-Meridian, Wei Qi Ying Xue, or San Jiao Syndrome Differentiation). Preliminary investigations, however, indicate comparable compatibility between ADEE and these methods in clinical practice. Such multidimensional integration not only expands the applicability boundaries of traditional diagnostic paradigms but also provides theoretical foundations for constructing a novel TCM diagnostic and therapeutic model.

Specific application case of the ADEE syndrome differentiation

To demonstrate the clinical application of the ADEE pattern differentiation methodology, we present a representative case study of a 32-year-old female with a five-year history of abdominal distension exacerbated over the preceding three months, accompanied by nausea, vomiting, and globus pharyngeus. The abdominal symptoms initially manifested following significant weight reduction (70 kg to 50 kg over four months) commencing six months post-cesarean deLivery. Despite intermittent traditional Chinese medicine interventions during this period, she experienced recurrent symptom exacerbations causing substantial quality-of-life impairment. During the recent exacerbation phase, she developed nausea with emesis of non-bilious, non-hematemetic gastric contents and persistent globus sensation refractory to swallowing or expectoration. Clinical findings at presentation included clear consciousness with intact cognition, pharyngeal paresthesia, xerostomia with polydipsia, epigastric distension with nausea, constipation (defecation frequency: 1/2–3 days), erythematous tongue with sparse coating and papillary hypertrophy, and pulse characteristics demonstrating wiry-tense quality at the left cun position with faint quality at the left chi position.

We now analyze the patient’s condition using the ADEE syndrome differentiation framework. The nausea and vomiting indicate rebellious Stomach Qi, necessitating treatment with descending methods, corresponding to the Descending pattern within the ADEE system. Constipation and impaired bowel movement require medicinal intervention to facilitate excretion, aligning with the Exiting pattern. The manifestations of a red tongue with scant coating, dry tongue surface, dry mouth, and constipation collectively signify Yin fluid deficiency, demanding Yin-nourishing and fluid-increasing therapies, which classify as an Entering pattern. The pulse characteristics—a wiry, hard Cun pulse and weak Chi pulse—combined with rebellious Stomach Qi conform to the pathogenesis of Yin fluid deficiency in the Lower Jiao failing to restrain Yang, resulting in upward rebellion of Yang Qi. Synthesizing these findings, the patient’s primary pattern presentation is Descending pattern, complicated by concomitant Exiting and Entering patterns. Consequently, the treatment strategy prioritizes the descending method, supplemented by exiting and entering methods.

Guided by the established TCM diagnosis and therapeutic principles, we formulated the herbal prescription as follows: The treatment principle prioritizes descending, thus Haematitum (Daizheshi)—the foremost medicinal for Descending rebellion—was selected as the monarch agent. It was assisted by Codonopsis Radix (Dangshen) to augment Spleen-Stomach function and generate fluids while mitigating the potential gastrointestinal irritation from mineral-based Haematitum. Arctii Fructus (Niubangzi) was employed to descend Qi of the large intestine, Pinelliae Rhizoma Praeparatum (Fabanxia) to descend Stomach Qi, and Perillae Fructus (Zisuzi) to descend Lung Qi. Trichosanthis Semen (Gualouren) moistened the intestines and promoted defecation (Exiting effect), synergizing with Haematitum to enhance descending rebellion. Raw Dioscoreae Rhizoma (Shengshanyao), Scrophulariae Radix (Xuanshen), and Raw Rehmanniae Radix (Shengdihuang) were incorporated to nourish Yin and generate fluids (Entering effect), addressing the weak Chi pulse and supplementing Lower Jiao deficiency. Notably, Raw Rehmanniae Radix and Scrophulariae Radix constitute a key combination derived from Zengye Decoction, further assisting bowel movement. This therapeutic approach exemplifies the clinical integration of ADEE theory with pharmacologically targeted interventions. By applying ADEE syndrome differentiation to connect theoretical frameworks with directional herbal actions, we achieved resolution of the patient’s condition.

This case exemplifies the “N-of-1” methodology [41] intrinsic to TCM practice and the proposed ADEE differentiation. The therapy was tailored not to a disease label but to the specific ADEE pattern configuration unique to this patient at that time. While such individualized, dynamic assessment differs from the population-based evaluation standards dominant in global clinical trials, it aligns with a growing interest in personalized medicine and complex systems science. The ADEE framework offers a sophisticated, theory-grounded language for describing and intervening in the individual’s functional state, representing a valuable complement to approaches focused solely on standardized pathologies.

Overall, the case vividly illustrates that the core of the ADEE syndrome differentiation method lies in providing four fundamental perspectives for understanding diseases, which together form an organic and dynamic unity, rather than serving as a means for the mechanical and static fragmentation of connections among disease mechanisms. In the context of this case, if analyzed solely based on the Eight-Principle syndrome differentiation, it may be differentiated as an “interior syndrome, heat syndrome, and deficiency-excess complex.” If assessed exclusively according to Zang-Fu organ differentiation, the disease location involves the “Stomach, intestines, and Lungs.” However, neither of these two syndrome differentiation methods elucidates the rationale for selecting Haematitum as the sovereign medicinal or the necessity of combining it with medicinals that nourish Yin and promote bowel movement. From the perspective of coordinating the three dynamic tendencies of “Descending, Exiting, and Entering” within the ADEE theory can the prescription essence and therapeutic mechanism of this formula be thoroughly interpreted. Similarly, applying the ADEE framework to classify formulas in YXZZCXL is not merely a categorical exercise, but aims to offer conceptual guidance for their clinical application. Admittedly, there are numerous other formulas within TCM that regulate ADEE movements. This study, however, focuses specifically on YXZZCXL as an entry point and does not extend to other classical texts—a key priority for future research.

Conclusion

This study preliminarily establishes the ADEE framework as a coherent and effective lens for systematically analyzing the clinical reasoning and formula architecture in YXZZCXL. By adopting the framework, we have organized the formulas from the text into a structured taxonomy based on their primary directional action on Qi movement. So, the approach elucidates Zhang Xichun’s therapeutic principles and addresses a gap in the systematic presentation of his academic thought. The main theoretical contribution of this work potentially lies in the preliminary construction of a novel syndrome differentiation method centered on Qi movement. We have preliminarily defined diagnostic criteria for the four ADEE patterns, thereby transforming this classical theory from a philosophical concept into a practical clinical tool. Grounded in a process-relational view of the body, this method complements existing diagnostic systems by focusing on the dynamic directionality of functional disorders, as illustrated in the representative case. In summary, this study aims to advance a model for engaging with TCM classics through theoretical synthesis, framework construction, and clinical tool development. The proposed ADEE diagnostic system offers a new research pathway for interpreting classical texts and evolving TCM diagnostics. However, its broader clinical validation and application to other classics require further verification.
