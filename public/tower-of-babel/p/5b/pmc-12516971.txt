OCT and OCTA quantitative metrics for polypoidal choroidal vasculopathy
Authors: Yiyun Zeng, Bingjie Qiu, Xiaosi Chen, Xinyuan Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPolypoidal choroidal vasculopathy (PCV) is characterised by distinctive polypoidal lesions, recurrent serosanguineous retinal pigment detachments and branched neovascular networks. The incidence of PCV is notably higher among Asian populations, leading to inferior clinical outcomes in the absence of intervention. The relationship between PCV and neovascular age-related macular degeneration remains a controversial. The advent of optical coherence tomography (OCT) and OCT angiography (OCTA) technologies has revolutionised our approach to understanding PCV pathogenesis. Innovative OCT and OCTA biomarkers, including double layer sign, the sign of polyps, vessel density, the diameter of choroidal vessels, choroidal vascularity index, and choroidal vortex have emerged as critical imaging parameters for elucidating the pathological intricacies of PCV. Quantitative analysis of the choroid has become an essential adjunct for investigating the mechanisms underlying PCV. This comprehensive review encapsulates the array of quantitative OCT and OCTA biomarkers vital for the diagnosis, prognosis, and clinical management of this complex ocular condition.

Introduction

Polypoidal choroidal vasculopathy (PCV) is distinguished by polypoidal lesions, multiple recurrent serosanguineous retinal pigment detachments and branching neovascular networks. Yannuzzi et al first documented PCV at the American Academy of Ophthalmology’s annual meeting in 1982.1 Despite over 30 years of multidisciplinary investigation encompassing experimental, clinical, anatomic and genetic studies, the pathogenesis of PCV remains elusive.

The evolution of optical coherence tomography (OCT) and OCT angiography (OCTA), particularly through the introduction of advanced quantitative analysis tools, has significantly enriched our comprehension of retinal and choroidal vascular diseases’ pathogenesis, diagnosis and prognosis. In 2022, the PCV Working Group of the Asia-Pacific Ocular Imaging Society (APOIS) introduced diagnostic criteria for PCV using OCT and colour fundus photography.2 In this review, we have compiled and focused on a range of quantitative OCT and OCTA metrics essential for diagnosis, treatment and prognosis.

Literature search

In this review, we conducted a systematic search across multiple databases, including PubMed, SpringerLink, Google Scholar, the Cochrane Library and the EMbase Medline database, covering the period from 1990 to 14 August 2025. English articles were retrieved using key terms such as ‘polypoidal choroidal vasculopathy’, ‘OCT’, ‘OCTA’, ‘quantitative’, ‘quantify’, ‘metric’, ‘biomarker’, ‘imaging’, ‘artificial intelligence’, ‘comorbidity’. A total of 68 studies were reviewed, comprising 1 randomised controlled trial, 10 case–control studies, 38 cross-sectional studies and 19 cohort studies, for inclusion in the analysis (figure 1).

Overview of PCV

In recent years, pachychoroidal spectrum disorders (PCD) have emerged as a novel entity, with PCV standing out as a significant phenotype of PCD. PCV exhibits pathological characteristic choroidal vasculature features, such as choroidal layer thickening, abnormal vasculature dilation in the Haller’s layer, compression of the Sattler’s and choroidal capillary (CC) layers, and increased choroidal permeability. Typical polyp-like lesions observed in indocyanine green angiography (ICGA) are the gold standard for diagnosing PCV,3 combined with other non-invasive multimodal imaging techniques, PCV lesions can be better visualised and analysed than ever (figure 2A,B).

PCV is categorised into quiescent, active forms (exudative, haemorrhagic and mixed). The diagnosis of PCV adheres to the 2020 non-ICGA diagnostic consensus established by the APOIS,4 EVEREST Study II5 and Modified EVEREST Criteria.6 Various systemic risk factors are associated with PCV, including plasma viscosity, thrombocytopenia, hypertension, etc. Anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) therapy, photodynamic therapy (PDT), thermal therapy and combination therapy have been PCVs standard therapeutic strategies.

While PCV is presently categorised as a subtype of neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD), significant distinctions between PCV and nAMD have been identified. Epidemiological investigations have highlighted the higher prevalence of PCV in Asian populations compared with nAMD, in Caucasian populations, 8%–13% of cases initially diagnosed as nAMD are in fact PCV.7 Unlike nAMD, the clinical presentation of PCV lacks soft or hard drusen and subretinal drusenoid deposits, prompting the characterisation of PCV as a primary ‘pachychoroid’ driving disease.8 Furthermore, genetic analyses have unveiled variations in the pathogenic genes associated with PCV compared with nAMD, suggesting that the distinct genetic profiles of PCV are underlying different pathogenesis.

Advantages of OCT and OCTA for retina and choroid evaluation

OCT offers both cross-sectional scan (B-scan) and a frontal scan (C-scan, En face OCT) for different observational perspectives. Since its inception in ophthalmology in 1990, OCT technology has advanced from the time domain-OCT, spectral domain (SD)-OCT, enhanced depth imaging (EDI)-OCT, to swept-source (SS)-OCT, continuously enhancing resolution, scanning speed and fundus imaging capabilities. Although ICGA remains the gold standard, there is a growing preference for non-invasive diagnostic method for PCV. One study reported that the SD-OCT features mentioned above demonstrated a sensitivity of 94.6% and a specificity of 92.9% in identifying PCV lesions.9

The introduction of EDI-OCT technology by Spaide in 2008 facilitated improved delineation of choroidal-scleral boundaries. SS-OCT represents a new OCT technology, featuring a lengthier scanning wavelength (nm), faster scanning speed (400 000 A-scans per second) and enhanced resolution (axial pixels 6–8 µm) that enables clear visualisation of choroidal vascular structures.10 11 En-face SS-OCT observes choroidal vessels in Sattler’s and Haller’s layers and SS-OCTA excels in artefact management and effectively visualises CC blood flow precisely and clearly.

OCT B-scan quantitative metrics and PCV

Choroidal thickness

The choroid comprises blood vessels and a matrix consisting of Bruch’s membrane (BrM), CC layer, Sattler’s and Haller’s layers and suprachoroidal cavity. Choroid thickness (ChT) is measured as the vertical distance from the outer retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) or BrM to the sclerochoroidal interface.12 The assessment of the ChT is typically conducted by EDI-OCT and SS-OCT imaging techniques. Additionally, the quantitative analysis can be performed using the ETDRS Automatic Segmentation Method (figure 2E). Measurement varies if there is a pigment epithelium detachment (PED), as shown in figure 2F. The choroid is crucial for maintaining its normal metabolism and influencing the progression of various retinal and choroidal diseases due to structural changes and functional alterations.

Previous studies have shown that eyes with a ChT ≥300 µm are 5.6 times more likely to develop PCV.13 Additionally, PCV patients with a thinner baseline ChT tend to show a better response to anti-VEGF therapy.14 A reduction in ChT following intravitreal anti-VEGF injections can serve as an indicator for evaluating treatment efficacy.15

However, ChT varies with several physiological factors such as age, sex, refractive error, axial length and vascular permeability. Furthermore, it remains uncertain whether its thickening is due to an increase in matrix volume from vascular hyperosmolality or dilation of choroidal vessels near the lesions. The mechanism underlying choroidal vasodilation and associated pathological changes remains poorly understood. Consequently, ChT provides limited insights into specific choroidal alteration.16

Subfoveal choroidal thickness

Subfoveal choroidal thickness (SFCT), defined as the distance from BrM to the sclerochoroidal interface at fovea,17 is crucial in studying retinal and choroidal pathophysiology disorders.

PCV manifests in two forms: pachychoroid PCV (pachy-PCV) and non-pachychoroid PCV (non-pachy-PCV).18 Criteria such as mean SFCT±SD and age-adjusted SFCT ≥5 percentiles as indicative of pachychoroid are utilised to define pachy-choroid and non-pachy-choroid types.1921 Recently, our team established a cut-off value for the pachychoroid by analysing the likelihood ratio, adjusted for age, sex and diopter.22 We further suggested that the two PCV subtypes, classified based on SFCT, may have distinct aetiological differences.23 The pachychoroid PCV tends to exhibit better BCVA and a younger age profile18 with choroidal hyperpermeability, a larger maximum vascular diameter ratio and an increased choroidal vascularity index (CVI).24 Increased SFCT concomitant with larger total lesion area and the polypoid lesion area, indicating that choroidal thickness may be associated with the phenotype or progression pattern of PCV.25 Several studies have utilised SFCT and nasal peripheral choroidal thickness to evaluate the efficacy of anti-VEGF therapy, with higher ratios correlating with an increased risk of adverse reactions.2628

We previously found that SFCT is thinner in nAMD than in PCV with pachychoroid, whereas it is thicker in nAMD than in PCV with non-pachychoroid, suggesting a different pathophysiological process between the two phenotypes.29 Non-pachy-PCV and nAMD share similarities in vascular density and thick vascular patterns of the large choroidal vascular layer.30

The diameter of large choroidal vessels

Choroidal vessel diameter (CVD), defined as the largest diameter of the choroidal hyperreflective lumen, serves as a promising imaging biomarker for detecting and understanding the pathogenesis of vascular retinal and choroidal disorders (figure 2F).31

Dilation of large choroid vessels, rather than stromal expansion, is a pathological feature of PCV.32 PCD eyes with pathological choroidal large vessel dilation had a poor response to VEGF therapy using the CVD ≥180 µm and SFCT ≥220 µm as pachy-choroidal disorders.33 Additionally, SFCT in PCV eyes with higher CVD significantly reduced after combined with PDT compared with anti-VEGF therapy alone.34 This large vessel dilation is a risk factor for the recurrence of exudative lesions in PCV, suggesting that CVD is a useful biomarker for exudative changes, independent of pachychoroid presence.33

Moreover, Vortex vein congestion, associated with CVD dilation, is considered a principal mechanism in pachychoroid disorders, including PCV. The congestion leads to impaired venous outflow and choroidal blood flow stasis, promoting vascular remodelling and polypoidal lesion formation. Over 90% of eyes with PCD exhibit vortex vein anastomosis at both the superior and inferior retina.35 Type I vortex veins, characterised by high resistance, are more commonly observed in PCV and contribute to choroidal circulatory disturbance and venous hypertension.36 Quantitative imaging parameters, such as SFCT and CVD, can be utilised to assess disease progression and activity in PCV.35

Compared with nAMD, PCV is characterised by a larger choroidal lumen diameter and increased choroidal thickness. PCV exhibits vascular dilation, vascular calibre changes are more common in nAMD, suggesting that choroidal vascular remodelling in nAMD may primarily be driven by intravascular wall lesions, while in PCV, it may be predominantly influenced by extravascular venous outflow disorders.37 In PCV patients, visual acuity and the frequency of intravitreal injections over a 1-year follow-up period are associated with the CVD of Haller’s layer. In contrast, for nAMD patients, these outcomes are more closely related to vessel branch length and the lacunarity of the branching neovascular network (BNN).38

Choroidal vascularity index

CVI is defined as the ratio of the choroidal vascular lumen area (LA) to the total choroidal area (TCA), as measured by OCT B-scan (figure 2G).39 CVI provides an accurate representation of the choroidal vascular distribution and is not influenced by the absolute size of the choroidal vessel or matrix.40 Unlike SFCT, macular CVI remains stable and is not affected by age, visual axis, blood pressure or intraocular pressure, making it a more reliable metric for assessing choroidal disease.

CVI is more sensitive than SFCT and serves as a crucial indicator for evaluating early choroidal vascular perfusion injuries. There was no significant difference in mean SFCT between PCV-affected eyes and normal controls, yet CVI was significantly lower in PCV eyes.41 After controlling for age, axial length and gender, although SFCT did not vary significantly between the two groups, CVI was significantly lower in PCV eyes.42 The findings suggest that LA was greater in PCV than in nAMD eyes, indicating potential pathogenic disparities.16

Furthermore, CVI is effective than SFCT for subclassifying PCV. It is a superior marker for assessing changes in choroidal vascular and matrix composition.43 PCV eyes with a high CVI exhibited a higher prevalence of choroidal vascular hyperpermeability, increased choroidal thickness and reduced CC vascular density. Baseline elevated CVI is the sole factor associated with poor response after 3 months of anti-VEGF injection.37 41 Higher CVI values have been observed in PCV eyes with pachychoroid compared with those with non-pachychoroid.24 Furthermore, typical PCV displayed higher CVI compared with polypoidal choroidal neovascularisation, demonstrating that CVI can differentiate between PCV subtypes through dilated anastomotic vessels.44 These findings underscore the potential of CVI for detailed pathological analysis in various subtypes of PCV.

PCV has a comparable CVI with nAMD. However, the reduction of CVI indicates the potential progression of the lesion, which is related to the growth and recurrence of BNN in PCV, but not related to the growth of BNN in nAMD.45

Quantitative metrics of PED

PED is defined as the separation of the RPE from the inner collagenous layer of the BrM. Long-standing PED is linked with the development of secondary BNN and is a predictor of the risk for severe visual loss. PED can also be categorised into drusenoid serous, fibrovascular or mixed PED based on its pathological changes. It can also be classified by its morphological features on OCT B-scan, which include sharply peaked PED, sub-RPE ring-like lesion within the PED and complex or multilobular PED.46

Peaked PED appears as a thumb-like protrusion with a sharp vertical slope on OCT, which is one of the specific manifestations of PCV and indicates the activity of PCV.47 When combined with the sub-RPE ring-like lesion within the PED, the diagnostic sensitivity of PCV reaches 85%.47 Complex or multilobular PED includes notched, haemorrhagic and multilobular PED. Notched PED represents that the polypoid lesions are located at the edge of the PED.48 Haemorrhagic and exudative PEDs are commonly associated with PCV and tend to be larger than those observed in nAMD,49 result from a dysfunction of the RPE-Bruch membrane complex, and occur more frequently in RPE tear.50 51 Moreover, studies have reported that PED with notched or multilobulated PED detected by OCT has a sensitive predictability of up to 0.90.52 Other PEDs, like small dome-shaped PEDs exhibit heterogeneous internal reflectivity and double-layer signs, may indicate the presence of PCV polypoid lesions (figure 2F).39

Quantitative analysis of PED can aid in predicting the prognosis of PCV and inform treatment strategies.53 The parameters of PED include volume, height, diameter and maximum linear diameter. Volume is specifically measured within a 5 mm diameter circle centred on the fovea as visualised on OCT. PED-related volumetric parameters offer additional predictive value beyond traditional biomarkers of disease activity and can serve as important indicators for assessing the efficacy of anti-VEGF monotherapy in PCV patients.54

Fibrovascular PED exceeding 1000 µm is a critical risk factor for poor visual outcomes.55 The dimensions of PED—height, area and volume—are closely associated with changes in visual acuity. Furthermore, RPE tearing in PCV, often linked to large PED (basal diameter or height >400 µm), a small ratio of CNV size to PED size or high RPE hydrostatic pressure due to haemorrhage, necessitates interventional,51 however, this morphological parameter metrics at baseline are not correlated with vision outcomes over 1 year.56 57

Unlike PCV, the volume of the PED in a typical nAMD patient tends to be smaller.56 58 Nevertheless, the PED morphological factors in both PCV and nAMD do not significantly affect the visual acuity outcomes at 12 months.15

OCTA/en-face OCT quantitative metrics of PCV

Choroidal vessel density

CC vessel density (CCVD) is quantified as the percentage area occupied by blood vessels, the blood vessels are defined as pixels with decorrelation values above the threshold level (figure 2J). CVD measurement is crucial for understanding the choroidal vasculature pathophysiology.16 42

CC layer, located between the RPE/Bruch membrane and Sattler’s layer, is essential for supplying nutrients and maintaining the primary metabolism of the RPE and outer retina.59 In pathological conditions, impaired vasculature in the CC layer can lead to RPE dysfunctions and contribute to CC layer atrophy, a process involved in the pathogenesis of AMD and PCV.60 61 CC atrophy occurred in the early stage of PCV, showing as the attenuation of CVD.62 Moreover, CVD is a predictor of visual acuity following anti-VEGF treatment.63

However, challenges such as artefact interference, PCV lesion interference and limited resolution of images impact CVD measurement acuity. However, SS-OCTA enhances image quality by improving the signal-to-noise ratio through multiple scans, facilitating more reliable quantitative analysis. Other quantitative indicators for CC assessment include vessel length density and vessel diameter index (VDI), which calculate the total length of vessels per pixel area and the average vessel diameter, respectively.

In comparative studies, CVD in the contralateral eye in nAMD was significantly lower than in contralateral PCV and normal eyes, indicating different roles of CC injury in the early pathogenesis of these conditions.64 Differences in vessel density between PCV and nAMD could be attributed to the age-related establishment of collateral circulation.

Quantitative metrics of the BNN

In 2020, APOIS renamed ‘branching vascular network (BVN)’, an important pathological feature of PCV, to “BNN”. Quantitative analysis of BNN is critical for predicting disease activity and guiding treatment (figure 2H,I). OCTA offers superior image resolution, sensitivity and specificity for BNN recognition and assessing blood flow change in BNN compared with ICGA.65 Therefore, OCTA has become a routine diagnostic and follow-up tool in clinical practice.

BNN in PCV patients is classified into three subtypes based on OCTA: trunk, glomeruli and stick.66 Each of which correlates with distinct clinical features and prognosis. The trunk pattern is associated with the worst baseline BCVA, the most polypoidal lesions, frequent BrM rupture and the thinnest SFCT.67 The glomeruli pattern exhibited larger PED and subretinal fluid (SRF) volumes, while the stick pattern, despite having the best baseline BCVA, shows the smallest BVN area and thickest SFCT,68 69 but a higher rate of SRF recurrence. Treatment responses also varied: the trunk pattern had a lower SRF recurrence after treatment, whereas the glomeruli and stick patterns exhibited higher recurrence.66 68 However, anti-VEGF therapy did not lead to statistically significant BCVA differences across subtypes.69 Key parameters used to evaluate BNN include lesion area, flow density and flow area of the BNN. The delineation of neovascular tissue boundaries is achieved through a combination of automated segmentation algorithms (eg, Orthoplane Image Viewer) and manual adjustments. The BNN area is defined as the space within the outermost boundary of manually detected and delineated pathological neovascular tissue. BNN flow density is calculated as the percentage of vascular blood flow signal within this area; and BNN blood flow area is determined by multiplying BNN blood flow density by the lesion area. Additional quantitative BNN parameters, such as vessel diameter, vessel length, branching vessel length, fractal dimension, vascular perfusion density, greatest linear dimensions and lacunarity,3 are useful for evaluating treatment response or distinguishing PCV from other diseases.70 71

ICGA studies have differentiated polypoid and idiopathic PCV. Polypoid PCV, sharing a similar genotype with AMD, typically presents with thinner choroidal thickness and responds well to anti-VEGF therapy.38 72 Conversely, idiopathic PCV is characterised by thicker choroidal thickness and shows a better response to PDT, reflecting the traits of PCD.

Blood flow cavity area in the CC layer

In 2013, Spaide et al introduced the concept of flow voids (FVs),73 which is identified in OCTA presents as dark particles within the CC layer, signifying areas of reduced or absent blood flow (figure 2J). Recently, some researchers have redefined FVs as flow deficits (FDs) to highlight that not only blockages but also slow or impaired blood flow can cause low OCTA signals. This redefinition encompasses a decrease in the number of CC vessels per unit area, a reduction in their calibre or complete capillary occlusions. It was also noted that smaller FVs (under 24 um in diameter) might represent speckle noise from SS-OCT rather than conveying a physiologically relevant signal.74 Quantitative metrics for FVs include the area covered by FVs, the percentage of area affected (FV%), complexity and aspect ratio, etc.

FVs or FDs reflect the extend of CC ischaemia and may serve as a potential indicator of systemic microvasculature health. In healthy individuals, the largest FV typically occurs in the fovea and its prevalence increases with age.75 In the fovea region, where RPE cells are densely packed and the metabolic byproduct density from RPE and photoreceptors is higher, the CC is more susceptible to metabolic stress. This stress increasingly impairs subfoveal blood flow as individuals age, marking a critical juncture that precipitates progressive pathological changes in the RPE.

Under pathological conditions, particularly in PCV, the FV area enlarges significantly, and CC perfusion initially increases as choroidal vessels dilate.75 76 However, further dilation can raise hydrostatic pressure, subsequently reducing CC perfusion. In addition, in the contralateral eyes of PCV, the number of small FVs (with an area of 400–750 µm²) is significantly less than that in the eyes of the normal control group.77 The quantitative changes in the area and number of FVs can be used as evaluation indicators after anti-VEGF treatment.

Studies have also linked FVs in the CC layer with cardiovascular disease and hypertension, they are considered independent risk factors for intermediate AMD.73 78 The CC flow density (CFD) in the contralateral eyes of nAMD is markedly lower than that in PCV and healthy control eyes, which indicates that CC loss has distinct roles in the early pathogenesis of nAMD and PCV.64

Quantitative metrics of polypoid lesions

The anatomical details of the polypoid complex are crucial for understanding the pathogenesis of PCV. Chi et al utilised three-dimensional technique to measure the average depth of the choroidal stalk and CVNs as well as the distance between the choroidal stalks and CVNs, CVNs and polypoidal structures, confirmed that PCV may involve feeding vessels originating from the choroid.79 However, due to the relatively slow blood flow within the polyps, SS-OCTA often fails to detect polyps throughout the entire PCV lesion. The Variable Interscan Time Analysis (VISTA) algorithm, a novel enhancement for OCTA, improves the detection of relative blood flow velocities in the retina and choroidal vasculature, enabling the visualisation of blood flow velocities in fundus lesions such as PCV polyps and microaneurysms. Researchers have observed that the blood flow velocities in PCV lesions (polypoid lesions and BNN) differ from those in normal vessels, with flow velocity being relatively faster at the periphery of large polyps compared with their internal vessels.80 Vista OCTA not only compares various images and consecutive scans but also calculates blood speeds from time-series OCTA data, enhancing its ability to capture slower flow velocities. By providing insights into blood flow dynamics, VISTA-OCTA could play a significant role in evaluating the activity of lesions. In the future, quantifying these velocities will likely deepen our understanding of the pathological changes associated with PCV.

Other quantitative metrics for PCV

Blood vessel tortuosity (BVT) is a quantitative metric to measure the degree of blood vessel distortion, relatively smooth and less curved blood vessels can effectively transport blood; vessel perimeter index (VPI) has defined the ratio of the total contour length of the vascular boundary to the entire vascular area, which is an indicator of vascular signal loss or early ischaemia; fovea avascular zone contour irregularity can be used to evaluate irregularity of the foveal structure, which correlates with the ischaemia status of the retina; fractal dimension and LAC are used to quantify vessel complexity index to assess the activity of neovascularisation. Decreased blood transport efficiency affects the bifurcation of the vasculature, and the geometry can reflect changes such as the vascular branching and sub-branching angle quantification. Combined with the diameter of blood vessels, the structural changes of blood vessels due to bifurcation, including vessel branching coefficient and child width ratios, can be quantitatively evaluated; these geometric changes correlate with vascular remodelling. Furthermore, reduction of polypoidal complex area correlates with decreased macular subretinal and intraretinal fluid following anti-VEGF therapy.81 Choroidal atrophy, a consequence of prolonged disease, has significant clinical implications. Indicators of PCV in association with other retinal diseases

PCV has been reported in association with various retinal and choroidal disorders, including high myopia, choroidal nevus and retinitis pigmentosa (RP) among others. Importantly, imaging indicators may be influenced by these coexisting conditions. For instance, PCV patients with high myopia often show a thinner choroid with a thickened choroidal phenotype, though the size and number of BVN remain unaffected.67 In cases where PCV coexists with staphyloma, polypoidal lesions are often located at the junction between the staphyloma and adjacent normal chorioretinal tissue, typically presenting as serous PED.82 PCV lesions have also been observed near or overlying choroidal nevi, possibly due to localised alterations in choroidal haemodynamics.83 A few case reports describe the coexistence of PCV with RP, a condition characterised by reduced choroidal blood flow and thickness, however, ChT appears relatively preserved in PCV patients with RP.84 Although PCV can occasionally mimic intraocular tumours clinically,85 documented cases of PCV coexisting with true intraocular tumours remain rare.

Application of AI in assessing quantitative metrics from OCT and OCTA in PCV

Artificial intelligence (AI) is an emerging field that simulates human-like intelligent activities through computer systems. Deep learning (DL)-based Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) show great promise for medical tasks, particularly in recognition, diagnosing and predicting. However, the application of DL in PCV remains limited.

SS-OCT effectively quantifies retinal and choroidal lesions in PCV, which is typically characterised by pachychoroid and dilated choroidal vessels. Combining ChT and sublayer measurements improves AI accuracy in distinguishing normal pachychoroid from PCV.86 87 However, ChT provides limited information and cannot differentiate between stromal and luminal vascular components. Parameters such as LA, TCA and CVI are used to distinguish the vascular structures of PCV from other retinal diseases and are commonly incorporated into AI models for disease prediction and progression monitoring.88 89 Deep learning models automate the identification and quantification of PCV features, such as sharp PED peaks, BVNs and polypoidal lesions, achieving high sensitivity (89%–95%) in PCV screening.9 90 91

Deep learning-based automated segmentation of the FAZ-A assists in disease diagnosis and treatment. Additionally, CCVD and intrapolyp flow signals can evaluate the efficacy of anti-VEGF drug treatment in PCV. Parameters like BVT, vessel dispersion and vessel rarefaction are positively correlated with the morphology of MNV and the staging and severity of AMD, and these metrics are crucial in predicting disease progression.92 93 Furthermore, AI systems can automatically extract quantitative OCTA features, such as blood vessel calibre, VPI, VDI and blood vessel density. However, these metrics are primarily used for screening diabetic retinopathy (DR) populations, and their application in other retinal diseases requires further investigation.94

Integrating quantitative biomarkers from OCT and OCTA into learning models has significantly enhanced the accuracy of AI recognition and expanded its application scope. However, challenges such as image artefacts and resolution limitations persist. Currently, the establishment of a public database for PCV imaging remains incomplete, and the availability of large-scale image resources for machine learning is limited. Further efforts are needed to improve algorithm efficiency and maximise the use of imaging resources. Moreover, these quantitative metrics still require standardisation, and the creation of an international consensus for the effective analysis of these parameters is essential.

Conclusions

Advancements in multimodal imaging techniques have enhanced our comprehension of the structural characteristics of choroidal vessels in PCV. However, several areas warrant further investigation, including defining and quantifying pathological choroidal large vessel dilation and polypoid lesions, understanding the causal relationship between choroidal large vessel dilation and inner choroidal ischaemia thinning, establishing PCV classification criteria and exploring its relationship with neovascular AMD.

Recent enhancements in OCT and OCTA algorithms, such as SMART Track eye tracking, artefact removal, expanded scanning ranges, vascular density quantification and reduced postprocessing time, have widened the scope of OCT technology to capture a broader range of images.95 This progress facilitates the analysis of choroidal vascular and blood flow alterations, fostering a deeper understanding of the imaging features in PCV.

The amalgamation of AI and multimodal imaging quantitative research, along with big data analysis, holds promise in further elucidating the pathophysiological processes underlying PCV and identifying improved imaging quantitative indicators for its diagnosis and treatment.
