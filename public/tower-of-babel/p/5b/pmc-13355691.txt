Accuracy and Reliability of Magnetic Resonance Imaging Measurement of Medial Femoral Condyle for Femoral Component Sizing in Oxford Unicompartmental Knee Arthroplasty
Authors: Arthit Gromprasit, Witoon Thremthakanpon, Kittitad Siripocaratana, Wiboon Wanitcharoenporn, Surasak Jitprapaikulsarn, Burin Sutthapakti, Artit Laoruengthana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccurate femoral component sizing is critical for Oxford unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (UKA) because inappropriate sizing can lead to early implant failure. Therefore, we developed a magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) measurement of medial femoral condyle (MRMFC) technique and compare its accuracy to existing methods.MethodsThis study included 54 Oxford UKAs. Five sizing methods were assessed: radiographic templating, intraoperative sizing spoon, anthropometric estimation (based on patient height and gender), Yang et al.’s MRI method, and the MRMFC. The MRMFC determined 3 reference points of medial femoral condyle on the sagittal plane of MRI. The primary outcome was an overhang or underhang of femoral component over posterior femoral condyle as determined on postoperative lateral radiographs which ±2 mm are considered as ideal size.ResultsThe MRMFC method yielded the highest accuracy (50/54; 92.6%; 95% CI: 82.1–97.9), followed by the intraoperative sizing spoon (38/54; 70.4%, 56.4–82.0), Yang et al. MRI method (32/54; 59.3%, 45.0–72.4), anthropometric estimation (30/54; 55.6%, 41.4–69.1), and radiographic templating (25/54; 46.3%, 32.6–60.4). Pairwise testing demonstrated that MRMFC was better than the sizing spoon (P = .008), radiographic templating (P < .001), Yang et al. MRI method (P < .001), and anthropometric estimation (P < .001). The sizing spoon was superior to radiographic templating (P = .029). No significant differences were observed among radiographic templating, Yang et al. MRI method, and anthropometric estimation.ConclusionsThe MRMFC demonstrated the highest accuracy and reproducibility in preoperative planning for Oxford UKA. Intraoperative spoon sizing, however, remains a simple and practical method and performed moderately well with significantly better than radiographic templating.Keywords: Unicompartmental knee arthroplasty, Oxford, Femoral component, MRI

Introduction

Unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (UKA) has become an interesting option for anteromedial osteoarthritis, offering faster recovery, lower morbidity, and more natural knee biomechanics compared with total knee arthroplasty (TKA) [1]. Oxford UKA is an established implant choice, demonstrating high success rates and long-term durability [[2], [3], [4], [5]]. However, suboptimal femoral component sizing remains a frequent concern, as inappropriate sizing can lead to early implant failure, abnormal kinematics, and reduced survivorship [[6], [7], [8]]. An oversized femoral component may accelerate wear of the patellar cartilage and result in poorer knee function [9]. An undersized component may inadequately cover the femoral condyle, accelerate wear of the posterior femoral condyle in deep flexion, and ultimately reduce implant longevity [10].

Several methods for femoral component sizing have been proposed, including preoperative radiographic templating [11,12], intraoperative sizing spoons, anthropometric estimation based on patient height and gender [[13], [14], [15]], and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)–based measurements [16]. The reported accuracy and reproducibility of these methods vary across studies, and no consensus standard has been established. The Oxford operation manual currently recommends preoperative radiographic templating combined with intraoperative spoon sizing, but these techniques frequently demonstrate limited accuracy [11,12,14,15]. Anthropometric estimation may improve prediction in certain cohorts but lacks generalizability across populations [14].

Although the role of MRI in UKA remains controversial, some studies suggest that MRI may help evaluate the appropriateness of UKA [[17], [18], [19], [20]]. Yang et al. [16] reported that MRI measurement could improve the accuracy of femoral component sizing. Nevertheless, this approach remains complex and has rarely been validated for interobserver and intraobserver reliability. To address these gaps, we developed MRI measurement of medial femoral condyle (MRMFC) technique which determined 3 reference points of medial femoral condyle on the selected sagittal plane of MRI image. This circle-based approach is conceptually aligned with the design of the femoral component of Oxford UKA. We hypothesized that the MRMFC may provide superior accuracy for sizing of Oxford femoral component compared with 4 established methods: (1) preoperative radiographic templating, (2) intraoperative sizing spoon, (3) anthropometric estimation, and (4) another MRI measurement method [16]. Therefore, the purpose of the present study is to (1) evaluate the accuracy and reliability of the MRMFC for templating Oxford femoral component to achieve the most appropriate size, and (2) compare its accuracy to other techniques mentioned above.

Materials and methods

This diagnostic accuracy study was conducted between January 2022 and March 2024 after approval by the institutional review board. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Forty-seven patients (56 knees) undergoing Oxford Phase III UKA according to Goodfellow’s criteria were included. Exclusion criteria were inability to complete the MRI protocol or refusal to participate.

Patient demographics (height and gender) were recorded. Preoperative knee radiographs consisted of (1) standard weight-bearing anteroposterior view and (2) lateral view 20 degrees of flexion. Preoperative MRI was performed with a 1.5 T scanner, with patients in the supine position and knees fully extended. MRI sequences included proton density–weighted fast relaxation fast spin echo with fat suppression (axial, coronal, and sagittal), T1-weighted fast spin echo without fat suppression (coronal), and a 3-dimensional T2-weighted sequence (sagittal).

The MRMFC technique was developed from the principle that the femoral component in UKA is designed to follow a circular contour of the femoral condyle. To reflect the true prosthetic size, the conventional MRI plane—which may not correspond to the orientation of the implant (Fig. 1A)—needs to be adjusted to a plane perpendicular to the posterior condylar axis (Fig. 1B). With this adjustment, the circle-based measurement becomes anatomically and conceptually consistent with the geometry of the Oxford UKA femoral component.

(A) Conventional protocol, the sagittal images are obtained parallel to the lateral femoral condyle on the axial image (white line), which does not capture the true size of the medial femoral condyle. (B) The protocol of the present study, the sagittal images (“2” red line) are obtained perpendicular to the posterior condylar axis (“1” red line), which reflects the true size of the medial femoral condyle. ∗ Lateral femoral condyle.

After the MRI has been set in the proper plane, the measurement is performed in a standard sequence. The axial image at the deepest part of the intercondylar notch is first identified on the coronal MRI (Fig. 2A). From this axial image, the sagittal slice that passes through the center of the medial femoral condyle is selected (Fig. 2B). On this sagittal view, a circle is drawn using 3 points: the lowest point of the femoral condyle, the posterior end of the condylar cartilage, and a third point created by dropping a perpendicular from the midpoint of the line connecting the first 2 points (Fig. 3). The diameter of this circle is then measured, and the femoral component size is selected according to the closest matching diameter.

The red line represents the axis of the image cut, the yellow line is posterior condylar axis (A) Step 1 selected the axial plane image at the deepest intercondylar notch in coronal plane, (B) Step 2 selected the component sizing sagittal plane image at the center of medial femoral condyle in the previously selected axial image.

(A) 3 points were selected for component sizing. (B) Circle with these points on the circumference. (C) Diameter of the circle represents the actual size of femoral component UKA implant.

Femoral component size was predicted by using 5 methods. First, radiographic templating was performed on standardized anteroposterior and lateral radiographs using manufacturer-provided templates. Second, intraoperative sizing was recorded from the sizing spoon according to the surgical manual. Third, anthropometric estimation was calculated from patient height and sex following the method described by Tu et al. [13]. Fourth, Yang et al.’s MRI method [16] was applied, in which the anteroposterior diameter of the medial femoral condyle is measured on sagittal MRI slices. Finally, the MRMFC technique was used, which determines the component size from a circle-based measurement aligned with the posterior condylar axis.

One fellowship-trained arthroplasty surgeon and 1 orthopaedic resident independently performed all measurements. Interobserver reliability was evaluated by comparing the measurements between the 2 observers. For intraobserver reliability, each observer repeated the full set of measurements after a 4-week interval while blinded to their previous results. Postoperative lateral radiographs obtained within 6 weeks after surgery were used as the reference standard. Femoral component position was classified as underhang, ideal, or overhang component relative to the posterior femoral condyle as determined on postoperative lateral radiographs which ±2 mm is considered as ideal size (Fig. 4). Accuracy was defined as the proportion of cases in which the predicted femoral component size matched the implanted size that achieved an ideal fit.

(A) Underhang component, smaller than posterior condyle (correct size = one size larger). (B) Ideal component, within 2 mm of posterior condylar edge (correct size = used size). (C) Overhang component, >2 mm beyond posterior condyle (correct size = one size smaller).

Statistical analysis

Sample size was calculated using the formula for 2 independent proportions. From our pilot study, the MRMFC technique demonstrated an accuracy of 92%, compared with 69% using the Yang et al.’s MRI method. P1 (MRI circle measurement) = 0.92, P2 (Yang et al. method) = 0.69, R ratio = 1, α = 0.05, β = 0.20. The required sample size was calculated to be 43 patients.

The accuracy of each method was calculated as the proportion of correct predictions. Differences in accuracy among methods were analyzed with McNemar's test. A P value <.05 was considered statistically significant. Interobserver and intraobserver reliability were assessed for all preoperative methods using Cohen’s weighted kappa (κ); the intraoperative spoon was excluded because it was measured once during surgery. Interobserver and intraobserver reliability was assessed using Cohen’s weighted kappa (κ) 0–0.20 slight agreement, 0.21–0.40 fair agreement, 0.41–0.60 moderate agreement, 0.61–0.80 substantial agreement, and 0.81-1.00 almost perfect agreement.

Results

Patient demographics

A total of 47 patients (56 knees) were evaluated for inclusion. Two patients (2 knees) were excluded due to intraoperative findings not suitable for UKA. The final cohort included 45 patients (54 knees): 10 men (10 knees) and 35 women (44 knees). The overall mean height was 158.89 cm; the standard deviation (SD) was 9.01 and range 147–177 cm. The mean height of female patients was 154.94 cm (SD: 5.37; range: 147–170 cm), while the mean height of male patients was 172.70 cm (SD: 3.89; range: 165–177 cm).

Accuracy

MRI method (κ = 0.286 [0.171–0.342], P = .003; and κ = 0.714 [0.523–0.806], P < .001; agreement 74.1% and 89.8%) (Table 3).

One complication was observed; a 52-year-old woman developed a periprosthetic joint infection 1.2 years after left UKA following an episode of foot cellulitis. She was treated with irrigation and debridement with bearing exchange.

Discussion

The number of surgeries for treating knee osteoarthritis has steadily increased in recent years. Alongside the rise in TKA, UKA has also become increasingly popular. However, the failure or reoperation rate of UKA is higher than TKA. The surgical technique and implant position are crucial for patient satisfaction and UKA survivorship [21,22]. The appropriate size of prosthesis, particularly the femoral component, also impacts on the outcomes of UKA. For Oxford UKA, the femoral component is designed to mimic the circumference of medial femoral condyle which allows optimal gliding of the polyethylene [23]. Therefore, our proposed MRMFC technique could achieve 92.6% accuracy, with all errors being within one size. The interobserver and intraobserver reliability were substantial to almost perfect, confirming its reproducibility even across different levels of surgical experience. Across methods, MRMFC provided the highest and most consistent accuracy, the sizing spoon technique performed moderately well (better than radiographic templating but comparable to Yang et al. MRI method and Tu et al. anthropometric estimation). While other methods were largely comparable to each other and were inferior to the MRMFC.

The intraoperative sizing spoon is the recommended method in the Oxford surgical manual and is the most commonly used technique. Reported accuracy ranges from 67.3% to 74.6% [14,16]. The measurement errors likely arise from multiple factors such as cartilage wear, posterior condylar osteophytes, and intraoperative variability. In our study, the intraoperative sizing spoon had 70.4% accuracy which is lower than the accuracy of the MRMFC method, with statistical significance. However, post hoc testing showed that the spoon was significantly more accurate than radiographic templating, and its accuracy is comparable to Yang et al. MRI method and anthropometric estimation. Although we could not evaluate reliability of the spoon technique, the UKAs in the present study were independently performed by 3 surgeons. Hence, the intraoperative sizing spoon remains a practical and reproducible method with the proximity to the performance of the MRMFC method.

Among height- and gender-based methods, 3 approaches have been published [[13], [14], [15]]. Fawzy et al. [15] reported 75% accuracy in a Caucasian population, Malhotra et al. [14] reported 74% in an Indian population, and Tu et al. [13] reported 88% in a Chinese cohort. However, Yang et al. [16] found poor accuracy rates of 42.3%, 25%, and 23% when applying these criteria to a Chinese population. In the present study, we selected the method by Tu et al. [13] because it was studied in a Chinese population, which is ethnically closest to our patient population. Our findings, however, showed Tu et al’s criteria yielded only 55.6% accuracy which was markedly lower accuracy compared to the original report. This emphasizes the inconsistency and limited generalizability of such anthropometric methods across different populations.

Radiographic templating has been widely used, but its accuracy and reliability are inconsistent [12,13,16]. Kasis et al. [12] reported high intraobserver but low interobserver reliability, likely due to the lack of reliable anatomical landmarks on plain radiographs or inadequate instruction on proper template use. Fawzy et al. [15] reported a 67% accuracy rate, with deviations of no more than one size, whereas Yang et al. [16] found accuracy as low as 42.3%, with errors exceeding 2 sizes. We also found the radiographic templating achieved only 46.3% accuracy, and these variations highlight the limitations of radiographic templating, particularly regarding image magnification and landmark identification. Although some improvement may be possible with clearer definitions of bony landmarks, advanced techniques such as computed tomography scans or MRI may be required to enhance precision.

MRI-based sizing methods have been investigated as potential alternatives. Yang et al. [16] reported 90.3% accuracy using their MRI-based method, with all errors within one size. However, in our study, accuracy was only 59.3%, though misclassifications remained within a single size deviation. On the other hand, our proposed MRMFC technique achieved the highest accuracy (92.6%), and all errors were within one size. Conceptually, the MRMFC technique aligns with the circular geometry of the Oxford femoral component, which likely explains its superior performance. Moreover, the MRMFC offers a good interobserver and intraobserver reliability. These findings suggest that MRMFC may serve as a good preoperative templating, particularly when the precise femoral component is required. Nonetheless, differences in imaging protocols, interpretation, and population characteristics may confound the accuracy observed in our cohort. Additionally, preoperative MRI is usually not a part of routine workflow, and thus further study may be warranted to determine its cost-effectiveness.

This study has some limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small, with a predominance of female patients from single institution. Consequently, extra-small and small femoral component sizes were commonly used, whereas large and extra-large sizes were absent. This may affect the external validity of our findings. Second, the MRMFC requires specific MRI set-up protocol, and thus standardizing MRI protocol across centers is still a challenge. These reasons may limit generalizability of the MRMFC method, and thus further research in larger, more diverse populations, with inclusion of all component sizes, may be required to validate our results.

Conclusions

The MRI MRMFC for femoral component sizing in Oxford UKA demonstrated high accuracy and reliability and may be a good preoperative templating. Intraoperative spoon sizing, however, remains a simple and practical method and performed moderately well with significantly better than radiographic templating.
