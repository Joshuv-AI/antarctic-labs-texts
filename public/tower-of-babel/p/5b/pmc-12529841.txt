Perceptions, attitudes and behaviors regarding professionalism and their relationships reported by newly enrolled Chinese residents
Authors: Xinzhi Song, Chunyu Xin, Honghe Li, Ning Ding, Yan Wang, Charice Augustine, Deliang Wen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe professionalism perceptions of newly enrolled residents determine how to implement targeted professionalism training during residency, but few studies have paid attention to this point. Moreover, it is unclear whether residents’ perceptions reflect their attitudes and behaviors regarding professionalism when examined quantitatively. This study aimed to investigate residents’ professionalism perceptions (their understanding of professionalism and approach to teaching professionalism), and to examine the relationships between perceptions, attitudes, and behaviors regarding professionalism.MethodsUsing cluster sampling, we conducted a cross-sectional survey of newly enrolled residents from 23 training bases of China Medical University in Liaoning Province, China. A self-designed qualitative questionnaire was used to understand the residents’ professionalism perceptions, and the revised Penn State College of Medicine Professionalism Questionnaire and the China Medical Professionalism Inventory were used to assess their professionalism attitudes and behaviors respectively. Directed and conventional content analysis was used to analyze qualitative data. Descriptive statistics, Spearman rank correlation, and univariate/multivariable linear regressions were used to analyze the quantitative data.ResultsA total of 838 (84.3%) residents effectively completed the survey. Through analysis of residents’ professionalism perceptions, we identified 23 professionalism attributes across five domains (Compassion, respect, communication, and collaboration; Integrity and duty; Pursuit of excellence; Equity in health care, humanitarianism, adherence to ethical guidelines; and Other), 25 undergraduate courses involving professionalism in four disciplines, and 12 approaches to improving professionalism at five levels. We also found positive associations between professionalism perceptions and attitudes (β = 0.54, SE = 0.26), and between attitudes and behaviors (β = 1.18, SE = 0.04), but no association between perceptions and behaviors.ConclusionsResidents collectively demonstrated a comprehensive understanding of professionalism, but there are inadequacies at the individual level, suggesting the need to strengthen the professionalism education during residency. No association was found between perceptions and behaviors, indicating the gap between them, and efforts are still needed to realize the translation from knowing into doing.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12910-025-01310-5.Keywords: Professionalism, Perception, Attitude, Behavior, Resident, Chinese

Background

Professionalism, as a core competency of physicians, signifies a set of values, behaviors, and relationships that underpin the trust of the public [1], and thus becomes the basis of medicine’s contract with society [2]. Until now, there is still a lack of uniformity in the definition and understanding of professionalism in the medical community [3]. This lack of consensus is due to the complex, multidimensional, and integrated construct of professionalism that varies across time, region and culture [4]. In fact, professional societies and medical institutions across the globe have defined their own professionalism attributes, and the commitment to professionalism they expect practitioners to demonstrate, undoubtedly inclusive of both congruency and variance. For example, the American Board of Internal Medicine (ABIM), European Federation of Internal Medicine (EFIM), Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME), Royal College of Physicians and Surgeons of Canada (RCPSC), UK’s General Medical Council (GMC), and the Chinese Medical Doctor Association (CMDA) have all successively put forth requirements for professionalism, each including multidimensional and culturally adapted professionalism attributes [2, 5–8].

Although professional societies describe their professionalism attributes and set goals for teaching professionalism among medical learners (medical students, clinical clerks, and residents), it remains unclear whether there is a disparity in the understanding of professionalism between professional societies and medical learners. Generally, medical learners’ perceptions regarding professionalism rely on explicit formal curriculum, as well as implicit informal curriculum and hidden curriculum (e.g., role modeling) [9], which was traditionally heavily depended upon in clinical practice and remains essential. However, it is now recognized that solely relying on the hidden curriculum is no longer sufficient [3, 10]. In response to this, educators have reached a general agreement that professionalism must be taught explicitly at the undergraduate and graduate levels to ensure that medical learners understand, demonstrate a commitment to, and internalize the values of the profession [3, 11]. Accreditation bodies have also required this, and medical schools or training bases have independently explored and implemented teaching practices that adapt to their institutions, as is the case in China. However, despite these efforts, limited research has been conducted to explore the understanding of professionalism and the approach to teaching professionalism from the perspective of medical learners. To a certain extent, such studies may help to examine the effectiveness of professionalism teaching practices implemented by institutions.

Previous studies on professionalism perceptions of medical learners mainly focused on two aspects: (1) subjective definitions and perspectives of professionalism, along with factors which define or affect professionalism [12–15]; (2) perspectives on the professionalism curriculum, the approach to teaching professionalism and suggestions for improvement [13, 14]. Other aspects include the challenges or impediments in developing professionalism [13], and the contributions of each professionalism attribute to the concept of professionalism [16]. These studies can be categorized as qualitative [13, 17], quantitative [16, 18] and mixed method [12, 14, 15]. Among them, quantitative studies mostly use standardized measuring tools. Despite involving large samples, they may limit the expression of participants’ autonomous perspectives on professionalism and may confound professionalism perceptions and attitudes [19, 20]. Qualitative studies, although valuable in providing in-depth insights into participants’ understanding of professionalism, often involve a limited number of participants. In contrast, utilizing open-ended questionnaire allows for a compromised approach. This approach not only involves a larger number of participants, but also explores the participants’ autonomous understanding of professionalism [14, 21], and will be used for this study.

The relationships between perceptions, attitudes, and behaviors have always been a central focus in behavioral change theories. One such theory, the theory of reasoned action states that behavioral intention is the primary predictor of behavior, highlighting attitudes and social norms as two factors that impact behavioral intention [22]. Professionalism, as the behaviors expected by society, should also be able to be explained using this theory. Professionalism perceptions (understanding of professionalism) can be understood as the social norms perceived by individuals. Most current studies on the relationships between professionalism perceptions, attitudes and behaviors are viewpoint-based [23] or qualitative [24, 25] (e.g., explaining remediation of unprofessional behavior [3]), but few studies have examined this from a quantitative perspective.

Residents play a pivotal and multifaceted role in the healthcare system, not only as trainees, but also as practitioners and educators, their behaviors can have significant influence on both patients and medical students [26]. However, newly enrolled residents entering a new clinical setting often experience uncertainty and ambiguity surrounding their roles and duties [27], as well as negative role models, which may negatively impact their perceptions, attitudes, and behaviors regarding professionalism. To resist this, it has become a consensus to strengthen professionalism education during residency to support their identity formation [3, 28]. In light of this, it is necessary to understand the perceptions, attitudes and behaviors of newly enrolled residents regarding professionalism. Such insights are crucial when determining how to implement targeted professionalism training in residency, which encompasses an array of possible professionalism training programs and modules that aim to close specific perception/skill gaps (targeted) for residents in training. Additionally, assessing the residents’ existing perceptions and attitudes toward professionalism is essential to successfully designing instructional plans that have the highest likelihood of improving professional behavior [29].

Therefore, our research focuses on newly enrolled residents and aims to achieve two objectives: (1) to investigate their professionalism perceptions, including the understanding of professionalism and the approach to teaching professionalism; and (2) to examine the relationships between perceptions, attitudes, and behaviors regarding professionalism.

Methods

Context

Residency is a critical period in the development of residents’ professional identity, with the goal of preparing them to become qualified physicians who are eligible to practice independently [3]. A system for national standardized residency training in China was established in 2014. Residency programs were set at three years in duration regardless of specialty, and all medical graduates looking for work in a clinical capacity must first complete standardized residency training [30]. Despite having similar models of medical training to many other countries (e.g., Japan, Brazil, Netherlands, UK) [31], the resident training system in China involves three types of residents [8]: (1) residents who are currently completing a postgraduate degree alongside standardized residency training, (2) residents who are affiliated with an existing healthcare employer and need to fulfill standardized residency training, and (3) residents who are neither currently completing a postgraduate degree nor affiliated with an employer but are completing standardized residency training as a prerequisite for future employment. Of these three types of residents, postgraduate residents predominate, accounting for more than 60% according to a previous study [32]. As China’s standardized training system is fully implemented and continuously standardized, the proportion of this type of resident is expected to gradually increase.

Study design and participants

In July 2021, we conducted a cross-sectional study on perceptions, attitudes, and behaviors regarding professionalism among all 994 newly enrolled postgraduate residents from 23 training bases of China Medical University in Liaoning Province using a cluster sampling method. The survey on perceptions should be given priority over the survey on attitudes and behaviors; otherwise, it may interfere with participants’ understanding of professionalism, thereby exaggerating the quantity and frequency of professionalism attributes mentioned by participants. To avoid this potential bias, we first distributed questionnaires on perception, collected them after all participants had completed their responses, and then distributed questionnaires on attitude and behavior. We obtained authorization from the related graduate administration departments and organized trained investigators to distribute the paper questionnaire at every survey site. Investigators were able to answer any questions raised by participants on the content of the survey. All residents voluntarily participated in the survey and signed written informed consent forms. Participants were able to withdraw from the survey at any time. All questionnaire information was used only for the purpose of this study and was kept confidential.

Main measures

We collected demographic and working characteristics of the residents. Based on the purpose of this study, a self-designed qualitative questionnaire consisting of open-ended questions was used to understand the residents’ perceptions regarding professionalism, and two standardized instruments were used to assess their professionalism attitudes and behaviors respectively (see Appendix A). Detailed descriptions of each instrument are as follows:

Professionalism perceptions

The residents’ perceptions regarding professionalism were assessed using a self-designed qualitative questionnaire consisting of four open-ended questions based on a literature review of previous perceptions of professionalism [12–15]. Two questions were used to assess residents’ understanding of the professionalism concept, including their definitions of professionalism and the aspects in which they think professionalism encompasses (e.g., excellent character, relationships with patients, peers, society, and related enterprises). The other two questions were used to explore the approach to teaching professionalism from the resident’s perspective, including undergraduate courses involving professionalism and the approach to improving professionalism. There were no restrictions imposed on the word count for residents’ responses.

Professionalism attitudes

The residents’ attitudes toward professionalism were assessed using the revised Penn State College of Medicine (PSCOM) Professionalism Questionnaire [33]. The PSCOM Professionalism Questionnaire [29] was introduced into the Chinese context by Song et al. [33], underwent the processes of translation, back translation and expert review, and was validated by a large-scale survey among over 4000 Chinese physicians with good reliability and sufficient validity evidence. The revised PSCOM Professionalism Questionnaire included six domains: accountability, altruism, duty, enrichment, integrity, and respect, and comprised 27 items. Respondents were asked to rate the extent to which each statement reflected their definition of professionalism on a five-point Likert scale (1 = never, 2 = little, 3 = some, 4 = much, and 5 = great deal), with total scores ranging from 27 to 135.

Professionalism behaviors

The residents’ self-reported professionalism behaviors were assessed using the China Medical Professionalism Inventory (CMPI) developed by Li et al. [34], which showed good reliability and sufficient validity evidence in multiple independent samples of over 5,000 Chinese physicians. The CMPI included four domains: respect, compassion, and communication; integrity; excellence; and responsibility, and comprised 40 items. Respondents were asked to rate the extent to which they fulfilled each statement of professionalism behavior in their clinical practice on a five-point Likert scale ranged from 1: ‘not at all’ to 5: ‘very much’, with total scores ranging from 40 to 200.

Covariates

A consensus has been reached that professionalism varies with time, region, and culture [4]. Even institutions in the same region may differ in their professionalism culture, resulting in differences in individual perception and expression regarding professionalism. Based on this, the participant’s geographical region of the undergraduate alma mater was collected as covariates, allowing for a more precise examination of the relationships between the variables of interest. In addition, previous studies suggested that professionalism (perception, attitude, or behavior) was affected by individual demographics (e.g., age, sex) and working characteristics (e.g., specialty, working hours, amount of overtime) [35, 36]. Therefore, these demographics and working characteristics, including age, sex, parental occupation, specialization, and internship duration, were also included as covariates in this study.

Data analysis

Data analysis includes both qualitative and quantitative analysis.

The qualitative data included four open-ended questions on participants’ perceptions regarding professionalism (two of these questions focused on their understanding of the professionalism concept, and the other two questions on the approach to teaching professionalism). Directed content analysis [37] was used to analyze participants’ understandings of professionalism concept. This research method provides a predefined template, allowing researchers to make adjustments during interpretation of data, including adding, modifying, or deleting codes. This provided some flexibility while also supporting and extending existing theory or framework. Two open-ended questions on understanding of professionalism concept were combined to analyze professionalism attributes. The behavior-based professionalism framework by Li et al. was used as a predefined template, which included four domains and 12 attributes [34]. Two researchers (XZS and CYX) independently coded all texts, mapped codes with the same or similar meaning (words, phrases, or sentences) to the predefined framework, and screened out disputed codes (codes with uncertain classification or inconsistent classification) and thus the initial codebook was generated. The research group (HHL, ND, YW, XZS, and CYX) reviewed the initial codebook, discussed all the disputed codes, and used negotiated consensual validation [38] to decide whether these codes can be understood as professionalism attributes. If so, the codes were mapped to the corresponding attributes of the predefined framework, or summarized as new attributes to be added to the framework, and thus the final codebook was formed.

To quantitatively describe the professionalism attribute, researchers (XZS and CYX) counted the quantity/frequency of professionalism attributes mentioned by each participant (quantity/frequency-per-respondent) according to the codebook. When counting the quantity-per-respondent of attributes, each attribute was first regarded as a binary variable, with 1 indicating that the attribute was mentioned and 0 indicating that it was not; then, the number of all attributes mentioned was added together to show the total number of different attributes each respondent mentioned. When counting the frequency-per-respondent of attributes, each attribute was first regarded as a continuous variable, with 0 to N representing the number of times a single attribute was mentioned by a participant; then, the frequencies of all mentions of the attribute were added together. After that, the quantity and frequency mentioned of each professionalism attribute by all participants were counted and interpreted as the quantity/frequency-per-attribute. The conventional content analysis was used to analyze the other two open-ended questions on the approach to teaching professionalism. This analysis process also involved both independent coding and group discussion.

The quantitative data included the professionalism attitudes, professionalism behaviors, covariates, and quantified professionalism attributes (quantity/frequency-per-respondent). To ensure accuracy, data was double-entered into EpiData version 3.1 and then exported for a column-by-column review, at which point outliers were detected and corrected. Based on a standard approach to listwise deletion [39], we excluded questionnaires with 20% or more missing data from subsequent analysis. If any portion of the questionnaire was partially (< 20%) incomplete, each unanswered question was imputed by the mean for continuous variables or 0 for categorical variables.

Before data analysis, the normal distribution of variables was examined by P-P diagram analysis and the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Based on whether the data obeys or approximates normal distribution, we performed a descriptive analysis, with values for categorical variables reported as frequencies and percentages, and values for continuous variables reported as mean (SD) or median (interquartile range). Then, we examined the professionalism attributes, attitudes, and behaviors in the different categorical covariates by using Wilcoxon rank sum test/Kruskal-Wallis H test or t-test/one-way ANOVA. To examine the relationships between professionalism attributes, attitudes, and behaviors, we used Spearman rank correlation to analyze the correlation between these variables, assessed linearity by visually inspecting scatter plots and multicollinearity by examining the variance inflation factor (VIF), and then used univariate/multivariable linear regression models to estimate the associations between these variables (only covariates with statistically significant differences in univariate analysis were included in multivariable linear regression models). Model fit was judged by adjusted R-squared and root mean squared error (RMSE). Regression coefficients (β) and standard errors (SE) were estimated for these associations. A two-tailed p value < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant. All analyses were carried out using SPSS version 27.

Results

Of the 995 postgraduate residents invited to participate in this survey, 838 (84.3%) effectively completed open-ended questionnaires on professionalism perceptions, and 777 (78.2%) effectively completed standardized questionnaires on professionalism attitudes and behaviors. After data matching, it was found that 723 (72.7%) residents effectively completed the previously mentioned questionnaires. The mean age of the participants was 23.49 ± 1.24 years, mean internship duration was 10.89 ± 2.67 months, median (interquartile range) [minimum-maximum range] quantity/frequency-per-respondent of professionalism attributes were 5.0 (2.0) [1.0–10.0] and 7.0 (4.0) [1.0–19.0], mean scores of professionalism attitudes and behaviors were 122.86 ± 11.78 and 176.14 ± 17.90.

Professionalism perceptions

Professionalism attributes

Residents mentioned a total of 23 professionalism attributes, the corresponding codebook was shown in Table 2 (see Appendix C for the bilingual version in Chinese and English). In addition to the four domains and 12 attributes of the predefined framework, one domain (Other) and 11 attributes have been added in this study. These newly added attributes were annotated in the footnotes in Table 2. As shown in Table 3, four attributes (duty and accountability, collaboration, compassion and benevolence, dedication) were mentioned by more than 50% of participants, while 12 attributes were mentioned by less than 10%. Besides collaboration and compassion and benevolence, humanitarianism and communication, the ranking of the frequency-per-attribute was consistent with the ranking of the quantity-per-attribute.

Approach to teaching professionalism

As shown in Table 4, the undergraduate courses involving professionalism mentioned by residents included 25 courses in four disciplines (11 in humanities and social sciences, nine in clinical medicine, three in public health and preventive medicine, and two in biology and basic medicine). The most frequently mentioned courses were Medical ethics (386, 46.1%), Introduction to clinical medicine (134, 16.0%), and Physician-patient communication (90, 10.7%). The other courses are mentioned less than 10%. As shown in Table 5, the approaches to improving professionalism mentioned by residents included 12 approaches at five levels (five in individual, two in relational, three in institutional, one in societal and one in national level). The most frequently mentioned approaches were lectures, seminars, or training session (331, 39.5%), self-directed learning in clinical practice (315, 37.6%), courses (266, 31.7%), teaching by example/positive role modeling (175, 20.9%) and media publicity (172, 20.5%). The other approaches were all mentioned less than 20%.

Discussion

Through the analysis of residents’ professionalism perceptions, we identified 23 professionalism attributes across five domains, 25 undergraduate courses involving professionalism categorized into four disciplines, and 12 approaches to improving professionalism categorized into five levels. Furthermore, we found a positive association between residents’ professionalism perceptions (measured by the quantity-per-respondent of professionalism attribute mentions) and attitudes, and a positive association between attitudes and behaviors, but no association between perceptions and behaviors.

It is widely recognized that medical learners’ understanding of professionalism depends on context. In our study, the professionalism attributes mentioned by residents covered all the requirements of the CMDA regarding professionalism but were different from some of the professionalism attributes emphasized in the Western framework (e.g., cultural humility) [5]. As reported in previous studies, “one size does not fit all”, the understanding of professionalism among medical learners is not universal [40]. This may be attributed to the difference in cultural contexts between Chinese and Western regions, specifically, the influence of collectivism and individualism [41].

China’s collectivist society views individuals as interconnected and embedded in interdependent social relationships, emphasizing “we” consciousness, collective identity, group solidarity, emotional dependence, group decision, as well as duties and obligations [42]. This seems to explain why duty and accountability, collaboration, and altruism and dedication were all mentioned by over 50% of residents. In addition, the historical influence of Confucianism has deep roots in China [43] and has established “Ren a.i.” (benevolence) as a core value. This principle emphasizes the importance of treating everyone with tolerance and kindness. This cultural emphasis likely contributed to the high frequency of compassion and benevolence mentioned by medical learners also exceeding the 50% threshold. It is also important to consider the impact of the social environment on the expression of residents’ professionalism. Our survey was conducted during the period of the COVID-19 epidemic, a time when physicians bravely fought against the epidemic and saved patients from danger. This display of professional behaviors fully demonstrated the dedication of the Chinese nation [44] and has influenced the perception of residents, leading to the classification of dedication as a professionalism attribute.

In contrast, individualist society in Western countries views individuals as separate and autonomous entities, emphasizing “I” consciousness, valuing autonomy, emotional independence, individual initiative, and the right to privacy [42]. This seems to explain why respect for patient autonomy, patient confidentiality, and informed consent are less frequently mentioned by residents in this study, with the proportion being below 20%. Respect for patient autonomy is listed as one of the three fundamental professionalism principles in the “Physician Charter” and strongly advocated by Western individualistic societies [2]. However, in China, due to the strong impact of collectivism, Confucianism, and family unit on society and culture, patients’ individual autonomy, privacy, and informed consent are not realized to the greatest extent. Within this cultural milieu, group autonomy and shared decision-making are often emphasized over patients’ individual rights [45]; the family’s role is clearly acknowledged within the physician-patient relationship, and families may determine the extent of the patient’s involvement in discussions regarding their medical condition. This is seen as a manifestation of fulfilling their familial obligations within the family unit and protecting the patient from potential suffering. Additionally, physicians acting in the best interest of patients may choose to involve family consultation within the decision-making process, including disclosing information to family members without the patient’s consent [28].

Some specific expressions of residents’ professionalism are relevant with traditional Chinese ethics, which is deeply influenced by Confucianism. The viewpoints of Confucianism on benevolence and material interests, on good and evil, on kindheartedness, and on character cultivation are all inherited by generations of medical workers and have thus became prominent in Chinese traditional medical ethics [46]. For example, humanitarian ethical thinking as embodied in the theory of Confucianism exerted a direct effect on the concept of the value of human life and human dignity. For this reason, physicians of various dynasties throughout Chinese history have all stressed that they should appreciate the value of life, rescue the dying, and heal the wounded by all means available to them. This could explain many of the codes mentioned in the Humanitarianism attribute, especially “The entrustment of life is heavier than Mount Tai” (Mount Tai, a very famous mountain in China, is often used to symbolize greatness or importance). As another example, in ancient China, medicine was called “the craft of benevolence” or “the craft of saints”, and physicians were regarded as having the highest and noblest professional morality. This represented the hope that physicians would act by the Confucian concept of goodness and rites, and practice medicine by the teachings of Confucian saints [46]. These explain why residents mentioned many related codes in the Generality attribute, such as “(Xuan Hu/Jing Shu/Le Dao/Ming Ren/Fu Shang) Benefiting mankind”, “Medicine is the product of benevolence”, “Cultivation of moral character and self-improvement”, and “To practice medicine, one must possess moral integrity”. It is also a phenomenon to which we should pay attention when we encounter residents with cultural roots in Confucius-influenced societies, such as those of Korea, Japan, and Singapore.

Although residents mentioned 23 professionalism attributes, 12 attributes out of the total were mentioned less than 10%. With the exception of duty and accountability, all other attributes were mentioned less than once on average. This finding suggests that professionalism as a concept, may be perceived as more abstract and not easily understood and actionable compared to other competencies such as medical knowledge and clinical skills [47]. Furthermore, another contributing factor may be the inadequacy of explicit professionalism training received by residents during their undergraduate period. While residents reported that professionalism was incorporated into various courses, these courses failed to introduce the concepts and attributes of professionalism in a manner that would allow residents to completely integrate professionalism into their own knowledge system. Given that residency training takes place mostly at the workplace, both formal and informal teaching on professionalism would be necessary, which serves not only to strengthen their understanding of the professionalism concept but also to provide them with the necessary skills to effectively translate that understanding into tangible and professional actions [48]. In conclusion, it is crucial for medical educators to prioritise the development of a deep understanding of professionalism attributes among residents by integrating professionalism into graduate curricula. Additionally, targeted intervention programs should be designed based on approaches to teaching professionalism from the residents’ perspectives. To achieve this objective, faculty development is essential to ensure that clinician teachers possess a shared cognitive foundation of professionalism with their residents. This will enhance their skills to teach and assess professionalism, provide support for identity formation, and cultivate proficiency in explicit role modeling and promoting reflection [3]. These comprehensive approaches will ensure that residents have a deep understanding of the professionalism attributes and are better able to consistently act professionally in their clinical practice.

Not surprisingly, we found that residents with different personal characteristics (sex, specialization, and geographical region) reported different professionalism perceptions (quantity/frequency-per-respondent). One possible reason is that residents come from medical schools of different geographical regions and may have been deeply influenced by the atmosphere of professionalism training in undergraduate medical education in that region. This is reflected in the fact that many of their descriptions of the professionalism attributes are based on their understanding of the mottos of their respective medical schools. However, we did not find differences in professionalism attitudes or behaviors among these personal characteristics. Although they experienced about 10 months of clinical clerkship during their undergraduate years, residents in this study had mostly focused on clinical observation and lacked direct interaction with patients and healthcare professionals in their undergraduate medical training. As differences have been reported in previous studies [32, 36], we predict that these differences may become apparent for some time following their entry into residency. Evidence in this study was found to prove a weak association between professionalism perceptions and attitudes, and a strong association between attitudes and behaviors, but no association between perceptions and behaviors, indicating that further efforts are needed to translate the residents’ professionalism perceptions into self-reported or tangible workplace behaviors. Previous studies have revealed why and how disconnect arise between knowing and doing in health professions education and practice, and proposed that the path to translating knowledge into action is to set an intention to act, and overcome overcoming cognitive, affective, and social barriers to action [49]. This includes engaging in cognitive and affective self-regulatory processes and navigating social and cultural norms. Although residents’ self-reported professionalism behaviors may not necessarily reflect their actual behaviors in the workplace [50], findings based on residents’ self-perceptions are still important. Self-assessment plays an important role in assessing professionalism and professional identity formation because the presentation of self is so central to one’s identity [3] and self-assessments can shed light and provide motivation for stimulating change and improvements in professional behavior [51]. It is important to acknowledge the subjectivity of self-reported behaviors, which may be affected by personal willingness, such as the desire to conceal certain behaviors. Behaviors in the workplace exist objectively, but are often difficult to directly observe and measure, and may be masked or disguised. Therefore, it is necessary to comprehensively consider self-reported and workplace behaviors in order to more accurately understand an individual’s performance during research, investigation and evaluation.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. Firstly, the low quantity of professionalism attributes mentioned by residents may be due to a lack of incentive and motivation. Although they participated voluntarily and were given sufficient time to fill in the questionnaire, their initiative and engagement could not be guaranteed. This may be an inherent limitation of open-question surveys. Secondly, this study relied on self-reported professionalism attitudes and behaviors, which are susceptible to reporting bias. Residents may overestimate their level of professionalism, resulting in a ceiling effect in the data. Similar trends have been observed in previous studies [52]. This requires medical educators to reflect on the reliability of self-reported professionalism and to consider future studies to further examine the consistency between self-reported and workplace-based assessments. Thirdly, this study found a weak association between professionalism perceptions and attitudes, and a lack of significant relationship with behaviors, possibly due to the inconsistency of survey tools, but it is still a useful exploration. Given the ceiling effect of self-reported standard questionnaires, future research may use open-ended questions to re-examine the relationships between perceptions, attitudes, and behaviors.

Conclusion

As indicated by the quantity and frequency of professionalism attributes mentioned, residents collectively demonstrated a comprehensive grasp of professionalism, but there are deficiencies in the understanding of professionalism among residents at the individual level. This highlights the need to strengthen the explicit professionalism education during residency. Positive associations were found between professionalism perceptions and attitudes, and between attitudes and behaviors, but no association was found between perceptions and behaviors, indicating that there is a gap between the two variables, and efforts are still required to bridge the gap and transform knowledge from knowing into practical action.
