Understanding influenza-like illness dynamics in Jiangsu, China: impacts of COVID-19 control measures and meteorological factors on the population health during 2018–2023
Authors: Yi Yin, Xin Jiang, Shuhan Tang, Qigang Dai, Yuhang Ma, Jianli Hu, Changjun Bao, Yanping Zhang, Zhihang Peng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSince the emergence of the COVID-19 pandemic, how meteorological factors and COVID-19 control measures in China impact the transmission dynamics of influenza-like illness (ILI) across age groups remains unclear.ObjectiveThis study aims to explore the changes in the seasonal ILI epidemics and the effects of meteorological factors across age groups in Jiangsu, China, before the COVID-19 pandemic and after the relaxation of COVID-19 control measures.MethodsThe time-varying reproduction number (Rt) and doubling time of ILI were deployed to describe the trend and iteration time of the ILI epidemic, and the effect of Chinese government response to COVID-19 on the ILI epidemic, respectively. Considering the stage factors, the role of various meteorological factors in the incidence rate of ILI across age groups from 2018 to 2023 was explored.ResultsThe Rt value decreased sharply at the beginning of the COVID-19 pandemic, but dramatically increased at the end of 2022 with the adjusted policy response to COVID-19 in China. The maximum doubling time in Stage II (COVID-19 pandemic period from 23 December 2019 to 26 December 2022) was 57.66 (95% CI: 55.16 to 60.39), while the shortest doubling time of 13.94 (95% CI: 12.91 to 15.13) was observed in Stage III (the period of implementing COVID-19 ‘opening up’ policies from 26 December 2022 to 25 December 2023). By controlling the stage factors, temperature change contributed most to the incidence of ILI in the young group. The number of sunshine hours had a small impact on the incidence of ILI among all age groups.ConclusionsThe relaxation of the COVID-19 prevention and control measures triggers increases in the population mobility, population contact rates and epidemic transmission. Meteorological factors pose various impacts on ILI dynamics. We recommended designing seasonal health interventions and vaccination plans against ILI based on meteorological changes.

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Existing literature has confirmed the relationship between meteorological factors and influenza incidence rate, as well as the impact of COVID-19 pandemic on the influenza dynamics. However, the potential impacts of meteorological factors and age on influenza dynamics during the period from 2020 to 2023, where Chinese government response to COVID-19 is constantly adjusted, remains unclear. Our results specifically described the influenza rate influenced by above conditions.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

This study primarily contributes to enhancing the public understanding and control of ILI epidemics through the following points: (1) age and meteorological factors pose various impacts on ILI dynamics; (2) preventive measures and vaccination against ILI can be dynamically adjusted based on seasonal changes; (3) effective strategies for preventing and controlling ILI epidemics by public health departments and healthcare institutions should be formulated by fully considering the outbreak patterns and transmission characteristics across age groups; (4) monitoring and control measures against ILI epidemics should be timely adjusted in response to public health emergencies like COVID-19. Additionally, monitoring and early warning systems for meteorological factors are required for adjusting ILI control strategies accordingly.

Introduction

Since the emergence of the COVID-19 pandemic in December 2019, the global dynamics of influenza has accordingly changed. Feng et al1 found that the influenza activity significantly decreased in northern and southern China and the USA during the influenza season from 2019 to 2020. Tran et al2 reported that the influenza season during 2019–2020 was shortened in most of the surveyed regions of four continents, accompanied by a significantly decreased incidence of influenza. Both the influenza virus and severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (the causative virus of COVID-19) are RNA viruses sharing similar transmission methods and pathogenesis.3 Therefore, the COVID-19 pandemic and corresponding intervention policies are bound to influence influenza dynamics. In China, a dynamic zero-COVID-19 policy has been adopted to curb its spread, including mandating the maintenance of social distance and restricting personal activities (eg, closing public entertainment places, closing schools, restricting domestic and international travel and issuing stay-at-home orders),4 which effectively controls the early spread of the COVID-19 pandemic.5

Zhang et al6 evaluated the effectiveness of non-pharmacological interventions (NPIs) during the COVID-19 pandemic in Beijing and Hong Kong on the intensity of influenza outbreaks. Putting a strict halt on unnecessary public activities and domestic travel greatly decreases the case number of influenza-like illness (ILI) in Beijing. A restriction of overseas travel is closely related to a decreased number of ILI cases in the Hong Kong Special Administrative Region. Tan et al7 analysed influenza monitoring data from 43 sentinel hospitals in Guangdong Province between September 2017 and August 2021. They unmasked that the number of ILIs during the outbreak of COVID-19 from March 2020 to March 2021 was almost zero, and the epidemic strain changed from influenza A virus (IAV) to influenza B virus (IBV). An influenza survey in Chongqing, southwestern China, found that the NPIs during the COVID-19 pandemic from 2020 to 2022 cause a significantly lower influenza activity than the previous three influenza seasons.8 A comparative study revealed a significantly lower influenza intensity after the COVID-19 outbreaks in Wuhan and Hangzhou, China, than that before the COVID-19 pandemic, possibly due to the implementation of the dynamic zero COVID-19 policy.9

Meteorological factors, such as the temperature, humidity and precipitation, greatly contribute to seasonal patterns of influenza dynamics.10 Estimates from the influenza sentinel monitoring network in China highlight the role of absolute humidity in driving seasonal influenza, resulting in a U-shaped curve of influenza transmission.11 Specific meteorological factors in a region greatly influence influenza dynamics. Zhang et al12 found that the precipitation, relative humidity and temperature are the three leading meteorological factors influencing the transmissibility of the influenza virus in the subtropical regions of Southern China, and higher temperature and heavier rainfall indicate an increased transmission. Bai et al13 found that lower temperature and lower relative humidity in some northern temperate regions of northeast China correlate with a higher incidence of influenza.

So far, the potential influence of meteorological factors on influenza dynamics across age groups, particularly in the COVID-19 era, remains largely unclear. In children aged 0–17, Chen et al14 found that low temperature and low relative humidity favour the control of influenza in the 0–3 age group, because extreme weather conditions limit the social range and the time spent on social activities among preschool children. On the contrary, such a climatic condition significantly increases the risk of influenza in children aged 7–12. Guo et al15 found that meteorological factors, such as the average temperature, relative humidity and atmospheric pressure, all impact the prevalence of influenza among children. Li et al16 found that young children (0–4 years old) and older adults (≥65 years) suffer from an increased rate of hospitalisation for influenza due to meteorological factors. Thus, age plays a decisive role in influencing the association between meteorological factors and influenza dynamics.

Immediately responding to the COVID-19 pandemic in late 2022, the zero-COVID-19 policy was adopted and praised for the effective control and officially ended in January 2023. With the dynamic adjustments for the response to COVID-19 in China, there lacks sufficient data on unveiling the association between meteorological factors and ILI dynamics across age groups. The present study explored the incidence of ILI in different age groups influenced by meteorological factors during the three stages of COVID-19 response, aiming to provide a reference for the future surveillance of influenza epidemics.

Materials and methods

Study design

Influenza surveillance data, meteorological data and relevant policies during the COVID-19 pandemic were analysed. With the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic as the starting point, we categorised the study period into three stages based on the changes in the key policies in China to combat the pandemic. The effect of the COVID-19 pandemic on the incidence of influenza, as well as the relationship between meteorological factors and influenza across age groups, was investigated. The time-varying reproduction number (Rt) was calculated to describe the dynamic changes in the ILI epidemic. The doubling time in the three stages was calculated by age groups to assess the epidemic spread and the influence of interventions against COVID-19 on ILI dynamics. Finally, the relationship between meteorological factors and the incidence of ILI in young, middle-aged and older adults in the three stages was identified.

Influenza-like illness data and the division of three stages

ILI is defined as a fever (temperature ≥38°C) accompanied by a cough or sore throat symptoms. According to the National Influenza Monitoring Programme requirements, such data are monitored by medical staff at sentinel hospitals (in internal medicine outpatient departments, internal medicine emergency departments, fever clinics, paediatric outpatient departments and paediatric emergency departments). The number of ILI cases and the total number of outpatient visits by age group are registered daily and the data are entered into the China Influenza Monitoring Information System at specified times. The ILI percentage is defined as the ratio of ILI cases reported weekly to the total number of outpatient visits for that week.

With support from the Major National Science and Technology Projects for the Prevention and Control of Infectious Diseases, China has established a national influenza monitoring network. Currently, the network comprises 411 laboratories and 556 sentinel hospitals, which together form one of the world’s largest influenza monitoring systems. The network provides a solid data foundation for the assessment and analysis of ILI epidemics. In the present study, ILI data reported by sentinel hospitals in Jiangsu Province from the 1st week of 2018 to the 52nd week of 2023, including the case number of ILI classified by city codes and institution codes and the total number of outpatient visits per week across age groups, were recorded.

Based on the COVID-19 timeline and Chinese government response, the study period was classified into three stages: Stage I, before the emergence of the COVID-19 pandemic from 8 January 2018 to 23 December 2019; Stage II, implementation of NPIs (eg, home isolation, city lockdowns and mask-wearing policies) from 23 December 2019 to 26 December 2022; Stage III, lockdown measures eased with the ‘opening up’ policies from 26 December 2022 to 25 December 2023.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research

Meteorological data

Provincial-level meteorological data were obtained through the establishment, data collection, transmission, processing, analysis and publication processes of the meteorological monitoring stations. Equipped with advanced meteorological measurement systems, local meteorological data, such as temperature, humidity, wind speed and precipitation, were recorded in the meteorological monitoring stations and transmitted via radio, satellite communication or the Internet. Following quality control and processing, meteorological data were then published on public platforms. We collected the mean temperature (℃), relative humidity (%), precipitation (mm), number of sunshine hours (h) and wind speed data (m/s) from the high-resolution (0.25°×0.25°) data sets (http://data.cma.cn/).

Statistical analysis

The Rt for ILIs transmission was estimated using the time-dependent method based on a Bayesian statistical framework via averaging over the entire transmission network and matching with weekly reported ILI cases. The generation interval (GI) was the time between the onset of symptoms in infected individuals and that in their infectors. A continuous GI following a gamma distribution was assumed, with a mean value of 2.6 and a SD of 1.5.17 After smoothing over a week, Rt values from the 2nd week of 2018 to the 51st week of 2023 were computed.

As different age groups may have different ILI infection rates, the population was divided into three age groups: 0–25 years (0, 25), 25–60 years (25, 60) and 60+ (60, +) years and above.18 19 This age group division allowed the analysis and comparison of the effect of age-related factors on the occurrence and transmission of ILIs. We calculated the doubling time to assess the speed and scale of disease transmission during the study period.

Using a generalised linear model (GLM), the relationship between the cumulative incidence of ILI and the number of days since disease onset was identified, followed by the estimations of corresponding growth rate and doubling time. The GLM was fitted using a quasi-Poisson distribution with a logarithmic link function for prediction. Coefficients were extracted from the fitted GLM model results, representing estimates of the growth rate and its 95% CI. Finally, the doubling time and its 95% CI for the three stages across age groups were calculated.

Generalised additive quasi-Poisson regression models combined with the distribution lag non-linear model (DLNM) were adopted to explore the relationship between the meteorological factors and the incidence of ILI among the three age groups. A ‘cross-basis’ matrix of each meteorological factor was built to capture the non-linear and lag effects through natural spline functions with a lag period of 4 and 5 df. Results

Statistics reported by various monitoring stations showed 78 680 967 outpatient and emergency visits from 1 January 2018 to 31 December 2023, in which ILIs (case number of 4 925 297) accounted for 6.26% of all visits. Since 2020, the proportion of ILI cases has decreased to approximately 5%. At the beginning of 2023, China officially ended its zero-COVID-19 policy. The ‘opening up’ policy in response to COVID-19 has greatly increased the proportion of ILIs to 8.14%. Meteorological factors remained stable during the 6 years of the study period (appendix, online supplemental table S5). Online supplemental figure S1 depicts the ILI cases in the five age groups during the three stages. The 0–5 years age group contained the greatest number of ILI cases. We calculated the weekly Rt values from 2018 to 2023, showing a median value of 1.0161 (95% CI: 1.0068 to 1.0255) in stage I, 1.0108 (95% CI: 0.9972 to 1.0245) in stage II and 1.0924 (95% CI: 1.0824 to 1.1024) in stage III (figure 1 and table 1). Similar to the changing trend of ILIs, the Rt values possessed multiple peaks but became relatively stable from 2018 to 2019. However, the Rt values of ILIs significantly decreased with the emergence of the COVID-19 pandemic and rapid implementation of NPIs at the end of December 2019, indicating the role of lockdowns and other restrictive measures in reducing the transmission of other respiratory diseases. From 2020 to the end of 2022, the Rt values remained stable in a cyclical pattern. At the beginning of 2023, the number of ILIs surged with the ‘opening up’ policy against COVID-19 in China. To counter the post-reopening COVID-19 wave, home isolation significantly decreased the contact rate, as did the case number of ILI. However, the relaxation of control measures in the post-COVID-19 era accompanied a new sharp increase in the ILI epidemic.

By quantifying the RR values for the 1st and 99th percentiles of each meteorological factor, meteorological factors posed various influences on the ILI dynamics across age groups (table 3). The change in temperature was most closely related to the ILI epidemic in the young population; that is, the number of ILI cases in the young population was susceptible to significant changes in temperature. The change in relative humidity had a significant impact on all three age groups, but the exposure–response relationship curve showed that higher or lower humidity was not conducive to the spread of ILI and had a greater effect on the older adult and middle-aged populations than the young population. The relationships between the amount of precipitation, the number of hours of sunshine and the number of ILI cases were similar among the three age groups. In comparison to other meteorological factors, the number of sunshine hours, however, presented a weaker association with the case number of ILI. Changes in the wind speed were closely related to the number of ILIs in the older adult and middle-aged groups, but barely influenced that in the young group.

Discussion

In this study, we collected the weekly ILI data for Jiangsu Province during 2018–2023. A total of 4 925 297 ILI cases were included and analysed based on three stages to explore the relationship between meteorological conditions and ILI incidence rates in different age groups in recent years. The Rt value and doubling time were calculated to reflect the changing trend of the ILI epidemic and explored the changes in the occurrence of ILIs in China before and after the policy adjustments. When calculating the doubling time, we divided the entire population into three age groups: 0–25 years, 25–60 years and over 60 years to compare the differences in the doubling time of ILI cases among different age groups at different stages. To explore the role of meteorological factors in the spread of ILIs in the different age groups in recent years, we used a generalised additive Poisson regression model combined with a DLNM to explore the relationship between ILI prevalence and meteorological factors in the different age groups.

Based on the analysis of the number of confirmed ILI cases, the doubling time was longest in Stage II, at 57.66 (95% CI: 55.16 to 60.39), and shortest in Stage III, at 13.94 (95% CI: 12.91 to 15.13). Research has shown that the first wave of an epidemic has a medium level of intensity, the second wave has the lowest intensity and the third wave has the highest intensity. The model fitting results and numerical calculation results were consistent with the actual situation, and also with research results from other regions.20 According to the results of this study, after the relaxation of some of the COVID-19 containment policies, the intensity of the ILI outbreaks significantly increased in Jiangsu Province, which is consistent with previous reports.21 A study of the impact of NPIs on ILI trends in China showed that NPIs partially alleviated seasonal ILI and potential ILI pandemics, but after the relaxation of certain NPIs, ILI activity significantly increased.21 The results of this study show a similar pattern. The reason for the rebound of ILIs after the COVID-19 pandemic is not yet clear, but it may be related to the general decline in immunity to ILIs of the population during the pandemic.

We analysed the exposure–response relationships for the three age groups among the average temperature, relative humidity, number of hours of sunshine, amount of precipitation and wind speed and the incidence of ILI for each age group. The results demonstrated varying degrees of relationship between the meteorological factors and ILI incidence among the different age groups. The differences in the relationship between meteorological factors and influenza incidence across age groups may be related to differences in immune systems, behavioural patterns, health status and the adaptability of the influenza virus to different age groups.22 In terms of the average temperature, the young group was significantly different from the other two groups, as the risk of ILI occurrence was significantly and positively correlated with temperature in that group. The young group’s significantly higher risk of influenza occurrence in relation to temperature may be due to factors such as a weaker immune response, increased exposure to viruses through social interactions and a higher likelihood of engaging in behaviours that promote viral transmission, all of which make them more susceptible to temperature fluctuations.23 Yin et al24 consistently found that young people are more vulnerable to influenza with the changing temperature (RR=4.59), probably due to the immature immune system and the susceptibility to environmental factors.25 Morphologically in a flat inverted ‘U’ shape, both high and low temperatures were associated with an increased risk of ILI in the middle-aged and older adult groups (online supplemental figure S5). The risk of ILIs in the young group increased first and then decreased with the increasing precipitation (online supplemental figure S4). In rainy days, people are more engaged in indoor activities that favour the transmission of ILIs, while a heavier rainfall greatly hinders indoor gatherings and limits the contact.26 China has a vast territory with a wide range of climates. Lau et al found a positive correlation between the precipitation and influenza risk in Zhejiang,27 while Zhang et al reported a negative correlation in Guangzhou.12 In our study, the precipitation was more likely to affect the risk of ILIs in young people from Jiangsu who generally spent more time on outdoor activities (RR=1.4).

The relative humidity and number of sunshine hours similarly influenced the ILIs epidemic among the three age groups. Neither an increase nor a decrease in humidity was conducive to the spread of ILI, and there was a U-shaped correlation between humidity and the transmissibility of the virus (online supplemental figure S6), which is consistent with the research results of Ali et al.11 Changes in humidity had a significant effect on the risk of ILI in middle-aged and older people but not the young; this may be due to the older age group’s potentially weaker immune systems, greater vulnerability to environmental changes and pre-existing health conditions that make them more susceptible to the effects of humidity.28 The correlation between the number of hours of sunshine and ILI prevalence was relatively weak, but for middle-aged and older adult groups, an increase in the number of hours of sunshine reduced the occurrence of ILI to some extent, which may be related to the increased absorption of vitamin D from sunlight and the resulting enhancement of immunity.29 The change in wind speed had a significant effect on the risk of ILIs in older people only (RR=1.36), wind-induced changes in temperature and air circulation may affect the immune response in older adults, making them more susceptible to respiratory infections.30 The response curves for wind speed and ILIs exposure in the three age groups indicated that a high wind speed reduced the spread of ILI, as such conditions dilute the concentration of the virus and reduce the risk of individual exposure.

This study holds several strengths. First, there is a lack of sufficient data to support supporting the impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic and Chinese government response to COVID-19 on the ILI dynamics, and the role of meteorological factors in influencing the spread of ILI before and after the COVID-19 pandemic across age groups. This study fills these research gaps. The impact of meteorological factors on the incidence rate of ILIs was assessed in different age groups, and the findings provide new ideas for exploring the relationships among climatic factor changes and ILI epidemics. Second, we quantified ILIs transmissibility influenced by meteorological factors and modification of policies against COVID-19 in China among age groups, thus minimising potential biases. Finally, data used in this study were from a high-resolution, extensive data set from Jiangsu Province, in which only individuals who strictly meet the clinical standards are classified as ILI cases. These data are verified and screened by dedicated personnel before being reported uniformly, resulting in high data quality.

Several limitations of this study should be noted. First, parallel studies conducted in other regions outside of Jiangsu are scant. In the future, more cross-dimensional and multiregional studies are required to consolidate our findings. Second, we did not specifically determine the subtypes of influenza due to the lack of laboratory testing data; thus, it is not possible to determine the epidemic risk of different subtypes of influenza. This constraint limits the application value of the results. Finally, the ILI case data and meteorological monitoring data used in this study were collected, summarised and reported by the monitoring stations, which have limited human and material resources. The coverage of the stations is also limited, and thus, the data may not reflect the actual weather situation, which affects the accuracy of the results.
