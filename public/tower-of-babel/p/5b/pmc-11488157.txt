Areal specializations in the morpho-electric and transcriptomic properties of primate layer 5 extratelencephalic projection neurons

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYLarge-scale analysis of single-cell gene expression has revealed transcriptomically defined cell subclasses present throughout the primate neocortex with gene expression profiles that differ depending upon neocortical region. Here, we test whether the interareal differences in gene expression translate to regional specializations in the physiology and morphology of infragranular glutamatergic neurons by performing Patch-seq experiments in brain slices from the temporal cortex (TCx) and motor cortex (MCx) of the macaque. We confirm that transcriptomically defined extratelencephalically projecting neurons of layer 5 (L5 ET neurons) include retrogradely labeled corticospinal neurons in the MCx and find multiple physiological properties and ion channel genes that distinguish L5 ET from non-ET neurons in both areas. Additionally, while infragranular ET and non-ET neurons retain distinct neuronal properties across multiple regions, there are regional morpho-electric and gene expression specializations in the L5 ET subclass, providing mechanistic insights into the specialized functional architecture of the primate neocortex.

INTRODUCTION

The neocortex is organized into specialized areas, defined by differences in cytoarchitecture and intrinsic/extrinsic connectivity.1,2 Regional architectonic differences are enhanced in primates and are often assumed to be causally related to differences in function.3-5 Less is known regarding the composition of neocortical circuits at the resolution of neuron types and how they vary across regions. It remains unclear whether functionally related properties, like intrinsic physiology and dendritic morphology, vary between areas for the same cell types. Striking cross-areal differences exist in the morpho-electric properties between supragranular pyramidal neurons in the primate brain,6-8 but it is unclear whether these differences reflect regional specializations within a single neuronal subclass or different neuronal subclasses. Part of the challenge in this regard has been to comprehensively identify cell types for each region to enable rigorous comparisons across regions.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) or single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) techniques have been crucial in bounding the problem of cellular diversity in the mammalian brain through the construction of transcriptomically defined cell type taxonomies in which related cell types group into subclasses that are conserved between human, non-human primate species, and mouse.9,10 These subclasses are shared across neocortical areas, albeit at different relative abundances,11-13 and exhibit substantial regional variation in gene expression within a single subclass.12 Whether any one subclass exhibits phenotypic variation between areas remains largely untested in primates. Such data may provide critical mechanistic insights into the cell-type-specific and regional susceptibility to neurodegenerative disorders. Using the Patch-seq technique,14-16 which combines scRNA-seq with patch-clamp recordings in living brain slices, phenotypic distinctions between transcriptomically defined cell types/subclasses within a neocortical area have been identified.17-22 However, with few exceptions in the mouse neocortex,23 whether such distinctions are conserved across neocortical areas remains largely untested.

To gain insight into cross-areal variation in transcriptomic cell type cellular properties, we focused on the extratelencephalically projecting layer 5 (L5 ET) subclass. In rodent species, L5 ET neurons are distinct from nearby non-L5 ET neuron subclasses in their long-range projections, dendritic morphology, and intrinsic physiology.24 Unlike neighboring neurons, L5 ET neurons send projections outside of the telencephalon to subcerebral regions like the medulla, spinal cord, and pons. L5 ET neurons have a thick tufted apical dendrite that extends to the pial surface, whereas other infragranular excitatory cell types are often tuftless or thinly tufted. Compared with non-ET neurons, L5 ET neurons have unique physiological properties, especially those associated with hyperpolarization-activated cyclic nucleotide-gate (HCN) channel expression, including voltage sag and subthreshold membrane resonance. Furthermore, L5 ET neurons in the rodent also exhibit subtle cross-areal differences in cellular physiology.25,26

In primates, putative L5 ET neurons have been identified using transcriptomic cell type taxonomies based on gene expression similarity to anatomically identified L5 ET neurons in the mouse, but with substantial cross-areal variation in gene expression and abundance.9,10,12 Testing for cross-areal variation in the morpho-electric properties of L5 ET neurons in the human neocortex is impeded by difficulty in accumulating sufficient data in any two neocortical areas. Additionally, accessing human primary motor areas is infeasible. Transcriptomically defined L5 ET neurons in the human middle temporal gyrus (MTG), the most prevalent site of ex vivo physiology studies, exhibit morpho-electric similarities to and differences from their mouse counterparts.21 Sparse sampling in motor areas demonstrates that primate transcriptomic ET neurons exhibit different unique specializations compared with mouse neurons.10 Furthermore, whether these neurons project to subcerebral regions has only been inferred from indirect measures. To address these gaps, we performed Patch-seq recordings from glutamatergic infragranular neurons from the motor cortex (MCx) and temporal cortex (TCx) of the macaque to test the following: (1) whether transcriptomically defined L5 ET neurons project to sub-cerebral regions, (2) whether the physiological properties that distinguish L5 ET neurons from non-ET neurons are shared across regions, and (3) whether there are areal specializations in L5 ET subclass properties.

RESULTS

Differences in ion channel- and neurofilament-related gene expression between infragranular neuron subclasses and areas

We first asked whether there are expression differences between subclasses and areas in gene families related to morphology/electrophysiology, such as those encoding ion channels and neurofilaments. To this end, we curated a subset of previously published snRNA-seq (Cv3 10×) datasets from TCx27 (MTG) and MCx10 (primary motor cortex [M1]) to include ion channel, neurofilament, and axonal targeting genes from infragranular glutamatergic subclasses (STAR Methods; Figure 1A; Table S1). Because these datasets come from two different macaque species, we also analyzed human snRNA-seq datasets (Cv3 10×) from MCx (M1)10 and TCx (MTG),27 focusing on subclass and areal gene expression patterns conserved in macaque and human. We used these datasets to ask whether ion channel/neurofilament gene expression can distinguish (1) L5 ET neurons from non-L5 ET neurons regardless of area and (2) L5 ET neurons between the TCx and MCx.

To visualize gene expression variation between neuronal subclasses and neocortical areas, we constructed uniform manifold approximation projection (UMAP28) views of expression of genes related to morphology and electrophysiology. Infragranular glutamatergic subclasses formed separate clusters in both human and macaque datasets (Figure 1B) and maintained clear separation when the gene list was restricted to ion channels (Figure S1A). Color coding the same UMAPs by neocortical area revealed additional, smaller variation in gene expression within each subclass (Figure S1B). Thus, while all L5 ET nuclei were distinct from neighboring subclasses, L5 ET nuclei from TCx versus MCx were also qualitatively nonoverlapping (Figure 1C), even when restricting the gene list to ion channels (Figure S1C). Joint projections revealed substantial overlap in L5 ET gene expression between human and macaque (Figure S1D). Together, these projections provide a qualitative view of gene expression variation across species, areas, and subclasses.

To explicitly test for differences in the expression of genes related to morphology and electrophysiology between subclasses, we identified 29 genes with ≥0.5 log2-fold difference between L5 ET and non-ET neuronal subclasses (Figure S1E) and for which the transcriptomic subclass effect size (partial η2) was ≥0.05 from a 3-way analysis of variance (ANOVA testing species, subclass, area; false discovery rate (FDR)-corrected p < 0.05; Figure S1F; Table S2). Several of these genes encode K+ channel proteins (Figures 1D and S1F), including KCa2.1 (KCNN1) and KCa2.2 (KCNN2), which each contribute to small-conductance calcium-activated K+ channels (SK channels).29 Genes encoding voltage-gated sodium channel subunits were also represented, including SCN4B, which encodes a β subunit implicated in resurgent sodium current and fast, repetitive action potentials.30,31 HCN1, which encodes a pore-forming subunit of HCN channels,32 was enriched in L5 ET neurons, suggesting that L5 ET and non-ET neurons can be differentiated by subthreshold filtering properties in both the MCx and TCx.24 Finally, several neurofilament genes were differentially expressed, including NEFH, which encodes the heavy-chain neurofilament protein that is associated with large, long-range-projecting neurons with intense reactivity to the SMI-32 antibody in primates.33

We applied the same approach to test for cross-areal differences in gene expression within the L5 ET subclass (Figure S1E). We identified 30 genes with significantly different expression and with η2 ≥ 0.05 between L5 ET neurons in M1 versus MTG datasets (Figure S1G; Table S2; two-way ANOVA, FDR-corrected p < 0.05). Again, genes encoding K+ channel subunits, including those associated with SK channels, were different between areas (Figures 1E and S1G). Additionally, several genes encoding pore-forming subunits of voltage-gated K+ channels (KCNC2; Kv3.2) or regulatory proteins (KCNIP1) were also different between areas. Thus, several ion channel and neurofilament genes may contribute to morphological and electrophysiological differences between L5 ET neurons and other subclasses regardless of neocortical region as well as differences between L5 ET neurons in the MCx vs. TCx.

Patch-seq reveals subclass and areal distinctions in infragranular neuron physiology

Differences in ion channel gene expression suggested that infragranular neurons were likely to exhibit physiological differences between subclasses in both cortical areas. To test this, we performed Patch-seq experiments from infragranular neurons in acute and cultured brain slices prepared from MCx and TCx of M. nemestrina and M. mulatta (Figure 2A). In a subset of experiments (n = 3 animals), we performed Patch-seq experiments on corticospinal (CS) neurons labeled by an injection of adeno-associated virus (AAV)-retro-CAG-tdTomato in the spinal cord (Figures 2A-2C). This provided us with the ability to test the ground truth that neurons with extratelencephalic projections (e.g., CS neurons) indeed map to the transcriptomically defined L5 ET subclass.

Using this approach, we collected 177 high-quality Patch-seq samples (Figure S2A) that mapped to various transcriptomically defined infragranular cell types in the reference taxonomy (Figure 2D). Consistent with our targeting of L5 glutamatergic neurons for recording, these samples covered the major L5 and some L6 subclasses (Figure S2D). We visualized the gene expression landscape of the snRNA-seq reference and Patch-seq datasets in a common UMAP (Figure 2E). Patch-seq samples obtained from different brain areas and preparations overlapped in UMAP visualizations with their matching subclasses in the reference taxonomy (Figure S2C). Retrogradely labeled CS neurons mapped to the L5 ET subclass in both the MTG (15 of 16 samples) and M1 (16 of 16 samples) reference taxonomy (Figures 2F and S2E). Together, these data provide direct evidence that transcriptomically defined L5 ET neurons have axons that project outside of the telencephalon.

To probe the physiological properties of infragranular neurons in the TCx and MCx, we performed a battery of current injection protocols (STAR Methods) from the high quality Patch-seq samples described above as well as neurons that yielded high-quality electrophysiology data but failed transcriptomic quality control metrics (n = 333 recordings). From these protocols, we extracted 40 sub- and suprathreshold intrinsic properties that provided a multi-dimensional “fingerprint” of each neuron that can be visualized in UMAPs (Figure 3; Table S3). This allowed us to explore how physiological properties vary across transcriptomic subclass, including retrograde labeled CS neurons (Figure 3A), preparation type (Figure 3B), and cortical region (Figure 3C). Several qualitative features emerged from the physiology UMAP. CS neurons (triangles) exclusively distributed with the transcriptomically defined L5 ET neurons (Figure 3A) and had little overlap with non-L5 ET neurons. This separation did not depend upon slice preparation type (Figure 3B), cortical region (Figure 3C), or macaque species (Figure S3A).

Next, we leveraged the high-quality Patch-seq samples and retrogradely labeled neurons to build a classifier that could be used to predict to which transcriptomic subclass a neuron belonged, based on physiology alone. Because we rarely sampled the distinct L6b, L6 intratelencephalic (IT) Car3, and L6 corticothalamic (CT) subclasses, we trained the classifier using only L5 ET, L5 IT, L5/6 near projecting (NP), and L6 IT subclasses. Classifiers trained and tested on 100% of the dataset had an average accuracy of 92.8% weighted across subclasses (Figure 3E) and maintained close to that level of performance when holding out 50% (87.9%; Figure 3F) or 75% (82.2%; Figure 3G) of the dataset for cross-validation. Classification was especially robust for the L5 ET subclass, where accuracy was 98.6% when trained on 100% of the dataset and dropped to 93.8% when holding out 75% of the dataset for cross-validation. Similar results were obtained by building classifiers using sparse principal components (sPC) of physiology features (Figure S3B), suggesting that the ability to discriminate subclasses based on physiology was not due to overfitting a few highly correlated features. These results demonstrate that neurons lacking transcriptome information from Patch-seq or projection target information from retrograde labeling can be confidently assigned as L5 ET or non-ET based on physiology (Figure 3A, gray circles). Consequently, we used this classifier to predict subclass identity for Patch-seq samples that failed transcriptomic quality control. Predicted cell subclass identities distributed as expected on the UMAP—those classified as L5 ET neurons colocalized with transcriptomically defined L5 ET neurons but not non-ETs (Figure 3D).

The classifier indicated that infragranular subclasses have distinguishable physiological properties. To test which factors contributed most to variation in physiology, we performed a 3-way ANOVA on each physiological property with projection type, preparation, and cortical area as factors. While preparation type and cortical area contributed to some variance, effect sizes, on average, were largest for neuronal projection type (Figure 3H; Table S4). Combined, these data suggest that physiological properties can be used to distinguish L5 ET versus non-ET neurons and, with some confidence, can distinguish infragranular neuron subclasses regardless of preparation type and cortical region.

L5 ET and non-ET neurons are differentiated by several physiological features in both the MCx and TCx

To gain more insight into which features distinguish L5 ET from non-ET neurons regardless of brain region and preparation, we ranked the projection type effect size (L5 ET vs. non-ET) for each significantly different physiology feature (Figure 4A; Table S4). The features with the largest effect size were those related to subthreshold membrane properties and HCN conductance (resonant frequency, input resistance, and 3dB cutoff). Consistent with previous reports examining a single neocortical area from rodents34 and human and/or non-human primates,10,21 L5 ET neurons had a higher resonant frequency (Figures 4B, 4C, and S3C) and a lower input resistance than non-L5 ET neurons regardless of other factors (Figures 4D, 4E, and S3C).

L5 ET neurons also had suprathreshold properties distinct from non-ET neurons, exhibiting faster action potential kinetics (maximum [max] dVdt, minimum [min] dVdt, and shorter action potential [AP] half-width). Action potential repolarization was faster in L5 ET neurons compared with non-ET neurons in both neocortical areas and preparations (Figures 4F, 4G, and S3C). Notably, projection type-by-area interactions were small, suggesting that no features were strongly different between L5 ET and non-ET neurons in one area and not the other (Figure S3D). Resonant frequency, input resistance, and measurements of action potential kinetics were also significant contributors to the logistic regression classifier, based on raw features as well as sPCs (Figures S3E and S3F). Together, our data highlight several subthreshold and suprathreshold intrinsic membrane properties that differentiate L5 ET and non-ET neurons in both the MCx and TCx and suggest that physiological differences between subclasses are well conserved between areas.

We also tested for differences in the physiological properties of cells mapping to different L5 ET subtypes. For this analysis, we restricted the dataset to the TCx, where the sample size was largest. Although we did not detect statistically significant differences between L5 ET types (FDR-corrected p > 0.066), effect sizes for some features were large (Figures S3G and S3H), suggesting that subtle physiological differences among L5 ET subtypes may be revealed with additional sampling.

Finally, we used Patch-seq samples to corroborate our findings that ion channel/neurofilament gene expression varies between infragranular subclasses (Figure 1). Using the same analysis approach we performed on the snRNA-seq datasets, we identified 47 ion channel, neurofilament, and axonal targeting genes from our Patch-seq data with significantly different expression between L5 ET and non-ET neurons with η2 ≥ 0.05 (three-way ANOVA, FDR-corrected p < 0.05; Figures 4H and S4A-S4C). Only a subset (21) of these genes overlapped with those identified from the snRNA-seq datasets, perhaps reflecting differences in transcriptomic assays.35 Overlapping genes included those encoding several pore-forming ion channel and auxiliary subunits (K+ channels: KCa2.2/KCNN2, KNa1.2/KCNT2, Kv3.2/KCNC2, DPP6/DPP6, Kv4-interacting protein/KCNIP4, and Kv10.2/KCNH5; Na+ channels: Nav1.1/SCN1A and Nav4beta/SCN4B; non-selective cation channels: nocta-min-4/ANO4, HCN/HCN1, TRPC4/TRPC4, and TRIP8B/PEX5L; and intracellular store-related channel: ryanodine receptor-3/RYR3). Among these ion channel genes, some exhibited expression patterns consistent with physiological differences. Higher expression of HCN and TRIP8B, which modulates the surface expression of HCN channels,36 in L5 ET neurons compared to non-ET neurons were consistent with lower input resistance and higher resonant frequency. Additionally, some genes encoding cytoskeletal proteins (medium and heavy chains/NEFM and NEFH), ionotropic receptors (GluA3/GRIA3 and GluK2/GRIK2), and synaptic release (synaptotagmin-2/SYN2) and axon guidance proteins (semaphorins 3a and 6a/SEMA3A and SEMA6A) also exhibited differential gene expression in both datasets, consistent with potential cell-type-specific differences in morphology, synaptic connections, and transmission.

Regional morpho-electric specializations in the L5 ET subclass

We next examined gene expression variation depending upon cortical area within the L5 ET subclass using our Patch-seq data. We identified 43 genes with significantly different expression and with η2 ≥ 0.05 between L5 ET neurons from the MCx versus TCx (Figures S4E and S4F; two-way ANOVA, FDR-corrected p < 0.05). Several of these genes encode cytoskeletal proteins (light and medium chains/NEFL, NEFM, alpha-internexin/INA, and vimentin/VIM) suggesting that there may be morphological differences between areas.

To explore morphological differences in finer detail for the L5 ET subclass, we asked whether there are regional specializations in the dendritic morphology of L5 ET neurons in the MCx vs. TCx. We generated dendritic reconstructions of L5 ET neurons (Figures 5A and S5A) sampled with Patch-seq in the MCx (n = 12) and TCx (n = 11) and calculated morphological features. Although neurons in both areas possessed hallmark L5 ET apical tufts that extended to the pial surface,21,24,37 we noted several areal differences in the fine structure of the basal and apical dendrites. Several L5 ET neurons in the MCx, including identified CS neurons, possessed prominent “tap root” basal dendrites that, in the most extreme cases, extended ~1.4 mm from the soma. In rare cases, we obtained reconstructions of two neighboring MCx neurons in which the basal dendrites of one neuron extended laterally toward the neighboring soma and dendrites of the other CS neuron (Figure S5B). Retrogradely labeled CS neurons from a 1 × 1 × 1.5-cm tissue block that was cleared, expanded, and imaged using selective plane illumination imaging (Exa-SPIM)38 offered a fully intact view of the apical and basal dendrites of MCx L5 ET neurons (Figures S5C and S5D). The morphologies from Patch-seq and ExA-SPIM (n = 2) were qualitatively consistent, although the latter showed neurons with four radially arrayed “tap root” dendrites, whereas only a few Patch-seq samples had multiple tap root dendrites. The Patch-seq based reconstructions of L5 ET neurons in the TCx lacked tap root dendrites altogether but had more complex apical tuft dendrites in the superficial layers. The total length of dendrites in each layer differed between neocortical areas, with L5 ET neurons in the TCx having more dendrites in L1 and L5 ET neurons in the MCx having more dendrites in deeper layers (Figure 5B). Basal dendrites were longer (FDR-corrected p = 0.0008, two-sided t test, d = 2.22) and extended farther in the horizontal direction (FDR-corrected p = 0.0006, two-sided t test, d = 2.65) in the MCx compared to the TCx, and L5 ET neurons in the TCx had both more apical dendrite branches (FDR-corrected p = 0.009, two-sided t test, d = 1.56) and higher-order apical dendrite branches (i.e., greater maximum number of branchpoints; Figures 5C and 5D; FDR-corrected p = 0.009, two-sided t test, d = 1.55). As a complementary approach to test for regional morphological differences, we tested whether a classifier could predict neocortical area based on the dendritic morphological features alone. Despite the relatively small dataset, the classifier obtained an accuracy of 100% when trained and tested on the whole dataset and maintained better-than-chance levels of performance when holding out 50% (average weighted f1 score = 91.9%) or 75% (average weighted f1 score = 76.1%) of the dataset for cross-validation (Figure 5E). These data demonstrate that there are areal specializations in the dendritic morphology of L5 ET neurons in the MCx versus the TCx.

We next investigated whether morphological differences were accompanied by differences in physiology, as suggested by the cross-areal differences in ion channel gene expression. We fit each physiology feature with a two-way ANOVA, with preparation and area as the factors, and calculated the effect sizes for features that differed significantly between neocortical areas. This analysis revealed substantial cross-areal variation in subthreshold and suprathreshold properties (Figure 6A; Table S5). Resonant frequency and the 3dB cutoff in response to the chirp stimulus were higher in L5 ET neurons in the MCx compared to the TCx (Figure 6B). Thus, the subthreshold filtering properties not only differentiate L5 ET neurons from non-ET neurons (Figure 4) but also distinguish L5 ET neurons between the MCx and TCx. Additionally, L5 ET neurons in the MCx had a more pronounced fast afterhyperpolarization (fAHP) and exhibited faster, more symmetrical (ratio of the maximum dVdt/minimum dVdt) action potentials compared to those in the TCx (Figures 6C-6E).

L5 ET neurons in the MCx and TCx also exhibited distinct firing patterns in response to current steps of increasing current amplitude. We constructed frequency-current (FI) curves for both the maximum instantaneous firing rate (1/interspike interval [ISI]min; Figures 6F and 6G) and the average firing rate (1/ISIavg; Figures S6A and S6C). L5 ET neurons in the MCx required more current than their counterparts in the TCx to drive similar average firing rates, likely reflecting differences in input resistance across areas, but the slopes of the average-rate FI curves were not significantly different (Figures 6A, S6A, and S6C). However, the patterns of action potentials of the L5 ET neurons from each region were distinct. In the TCx, L5 ET neurons exhibited doublets or high-frequency bursts of action potentials at the onset of the current that were rarely observed elsewhere in the sweep or in MCx L5 ET neurons (Figures 6F and 6G). Onset bursts/doublets in L5 ET neurons drove two key properties that distinguished them from MCx L5 ET neurons; both the initial and maximum slope of the maximum instantaneous FI curve were higher in L5 ET neurons in the TCx (Figures 6A and 6H). The instantaneous FI curve in many TCx L5 ET neurons was highly non-linear (arrows in Figure 6G), a feature that was rarely observed in MCx L5 ET neurons. We also identified 43 ion channel genes in the Patch-seq dataset with significantly different expression between L5 ET neurons of MCx versus TCx that may contribute to these cross-areal physiological differences (Figures S4 and S6E). Among these, 10 genes were differentially expressed in L5 ET neurons from MCx versus TCx L5 ET neurons for both the snRNAseq and Patch-seq datasets. These included K+ channel-related genes (KCa2.2/KCNN2, KCa2.3/KCNN3, Kv3.2/KCNC2, and DPP6/DPP6) and a voltage-gated Ca2+ channel gene (Cav2.1/CACNA1A). Additionally, an axon guidance gene (semaphorin 6a/SEMA6A) and several ionotropic receptor genes were differentially expressed (GluK1-3/GRIK1-3 and glycine receptor alpha-3/GLRA3), suggesting that L5 ET neurons may be regionally specialized in their synaptic connections as well.

Functional specializations of MCx L5 ET neurons support sustained activity and low beta-band oscillations

Primate CS neurons can sustain high firing rates—upward of 100 Hz for several seconds—during the preparation and execution of movements.39-41 Moreover, CS neuron activity is coherent with prominent beta-band oscillations (14–30 Hz) in local field potentials and muscle electromyography.42,43 We tested the hypothesis that L5 ET neurons in the MCx exhibit areal specializations in their intrinsic membrane properties that could contribute to these features.

Kv3 channels are implicated in facilitating sustained high rates of firing and fast spikes with deep fAHPs.44,45 Considering higher gene expression of KCNC1 (Kv3.1) and KCNC2 (Kv3.2) in MCx L5 ET neurons compared to the TCx, we predicted that MCx L5 ET neurons might surpass the TCx L5 ET neurons’ firing rate with stronger depolarizing current injections. We thus tested injection amplitudes up to 4 nA (Figures 6I-6K, S6C, and S6D) in a subset of L5 ET neurons in both areas. L5 ET neurons in the MCx sustained higher average spike counts compared to L5 ET neurons in the TCx at maximal current injections (Figure S6C; maximal average firing rate, p = 0.0002, two-sided t test, d = 1.0). Although depolarizing current injections less than 1 nA drove L5 ET neurons from the TCx to higher instantaneous rates (Figures S6D), MCx neurons were capable of firing equivalently at higher current injection amplitudes (4 nA; Figure S6D; maximal instantaneous firing rate, p = 0.99, two-sided t test, d = 0.003). Furthermore, L5 ET neurons in the TCx were more likely to undergo depolarization-induced block of action potential generation in response to these higher current injection amplitudes (MCx = 1 of 42 neurons; TCx = 14 of 26 neurons). These differences were likely related to areal specializations in the L5 ET neurons’ capability to maintain spike kinetics over the depolarizing step.46 We quantified these changes by calculating the ratio of the last-to-first spike for each step over a range of average firing rates. At the maximal average firing rates, the spike width in TCx L5 ET neurons more than doubled, whereas MCx L5 ET neuron spike widths only increased 1.5 times (Figure 6J; p = 1.7e−09, two-sided t test, d = 2.02). Similarly, the maximum dVdt decreased to ~20% of the first spike value in TCx neurons versus only ~50% in the MCx (Figure 6K; p = 3.2e−10, two-sided t test, d = 1.69). These data suggest that MCx neurons possess ionic conductances that enable repetitive, sustained action potential generation.

Next, we considered whether L5 ET neurons in the MCx have intrinsic membrane properties that might contribute to their spiking coherence with beta frequency bands observed in local field potentials in vivo.42,43 Fast, symmetrical spikes and prominent fAHPs like those we observed in L5 ET neurons of the MCx are often accompanied by subthreshold oscillations and intermittent bouts of irregular or stuttering firing in other neuron populations.44 In such cases, the firing rate during spiking bouts is similar to the prominent subthreshold oscillatory frequency.47,48 We asked whether L5 ET neurons in the MCx similarly displayed prominent subthreshold oscillations and irregular firing patterns during 10-s-long current injections just above rheobase.

L5 ET neurons in both areas displayed irregular firing patterns (e.g., Figures 7A, 7B, S7A, and S7B). To quantify spiking regularity, we calculated the coefficient of variation of ISIs (ISI SD/ISI mean), which revealed more variable ISIs in MCx L5 ET neurons across a wide range of average firing rates during the 10-s current step (Figures S7F and S7G; p < 0.001, area effect, two-way ANOVA). Oscillations during the intervals between spiking epochs in L5 ET neurons from both regions (Figures 7A-7H) increased in power modestly as a function of membrane potential (fast Fourier transform [FFT] strength; Figure 7K; MCx: r2 = 0.08, p < 0.001, n = 91 observations from 59 neurons; TCx: r2 = 0.04, p = 0.078, n = 82 observations from 53 neurons). However, the peak frequency of these oscillations increased with membrane potential only in the MCx, whether measured from autocorrelation (Figure 7I; MCx: r2 = 0.31, p < 0.001, n = 79 observations from 52 neurons; TCx: r2 = 0.002, p = 0.75, n = 66 observations from 49 neurons) or Fourier analysis (Figure 7J; MCx: r2 = 0.32, p < 0.001, n = 91 observations from 59 neurons; TCx: r2 < 0.001, p = 0.146, n = 82 observations from 53 neurons). Oscillations occurred in the presence of blockers of fast synaptic transmission, albeit at a slightly reduced frequency (Figures S7D and S7E; two-way ANOVA, p = 0.0496) and, thus, can be attributed to mechanisms intrinsic to the neuron.

To analyze how subthreshold oscillations related to the firing rate during spiking epochs, we calculated the modal instantaneous firing rate (the most common value of instantaneous firing rate) during the 10-s sweep current step.48 In the MCx, L5 ET modal firing rates were as high as ~30 Hz, accompanied with ISIs longer than 650 ms (the minimum window for subthreshold oscillation analysis; STAR Methods), reflecting irregular ISIs in these neurons even at relatively high average firing rates (Figure S7G). In contrast, no TCx neurons had modal firing rates greater than ~8 Hz with ISIs longer than 650 ms, reflecting the tendency of this neuronal population to fire regularly at higher average firing rates (Figure S7G). Consequently, the instantaneous firing rate during spiking epochs was well correlated with the prominent subthreshold oscillatory frequency in L5 ET neurons in the MCx (Figure 7L; r2 = 0.63, p < 0.001), but not in the TCx (r2 < 0.001, p = 0.683). Together, these observations suggest that subthreshold oscillations in the alpha/beta band promote firing rates in the same frequency band in MCx L5 ET neurons and suggest that the intrinsic properties of these neurons may help to contribute to their coherence with beta-band oscillations observed in vivo.

DISCUSSION

Despite evidence of enhanced functional and architectonic specializations across neocortical areas in primates, direct comparisons of the morpho-electric properties of defined transcriptomic cell types across neocortical regions is less studied and challenging for human tissue, where primary areas are not accessible. In this study, we found that distinctions in the intrinsic physiology of transcriptomically defined infragranular glutamatergic neuron subclasses are conserved across two different neocortical areas of the macaque. By retrogradely labeling CS neurons in the MCx of the macaque, we provided direct evidence that neurons within the transcriptomically defined L5 ET neuron subclass project subcerebrally in the primate—a property of these neurons that has been inferred. Additionally, within the L5 ET subclass, we identified several regional specializations in gene expression, morphology, and physiology that are likely related to the function of these output neurons in the MCx versus the TCx.

Functional implications of subclass and regional differences in physiology

Although morpho-electric differences are not always readily predictable from transcriptomic analyses,19,49 we found that infragranular subclasses were distinguishable based on physiology alone, especially for the L5 ET subclass. Among the physiological measurements separating L5 ET from non-ET neurons, those related to HCN channels were strongest, consistent with previous findings in rodents and humans.21,34,50 HCN channels contribute to L5 ET neuron function as coincidence detectors of inputs across cortical layers and, in turn, gate neocortical output to subcortical structures.51,52 As targets of neuromodulation, neurodegenerative, and neurological disorders,34,50,53-55 HCN channels may render L5 ET neurons particularly sensitive to these processes in the primate brain.

In vivo, the firing patterns of L5 ET neurons differ from adjacent intratelencephalically projecting neurons in baseline firing rates, firing regularity, and bursting behavior.41,56-59 We observed differences in the spiking properties of infragranular neurons in our recordings from quiescent slices, suggesting that the intrinsic properties of these neuron classes shape their activity in vivo. L5 ET neurons, especially those in the MCx, exhibited faster action potential kinetics than non-ET neurons. This observation is consistent with in vivo physiological measures of “thin” spikes in a subset of infragranular pyramidal neurons,60-62 which fire at high rates for sustained periods compared to other neuron populations.39-41

The spike properties and firing patterns of L5 ET neurons in the MCx versus TCx we observed ex vivo could be related to the specialized functions to which they contribute in their respective areas. In the MCx, L5 ET neurons fired robustly for prolonged periods at high firing rates with minimal change to individual spike waveforms. We propose that these properties contribute to their role in the maintenance of motor output during sustained behaviors in vivo.39,63,64 Similarly, alpha/beta-frequency signals we observe in CS neurons may help to entrain their spiking to network-level beta-band oscillations in the MCx during motor planning and execution.43 Conversely, L5 ET neurons in the TCx displayed onset burst firing, reminiscent of human TCx neurons, which display strong dendritic electrogenesis.21 The functional consequences of onset bursts remains unclear, although it is notable that they enhance transmission to downstream post-synaptic targets.65,66

Interareal specializations in the dendritic morphology of primate L5 ET neurons

One particularly striking areal specialization was the dendritic morphology of L5 ET neurons. In the TCx, L5 ET dendrites spread laterally only in superficial layers, a pattern reminiscent of L5 ET dendrites in the human MTG21,67 and L5b neurons in the somatosensory cortex of rodents,68,69 which can generate long-lasting dendritic plateau potentials in response to coincident inputs across the dendritic arbor.70-72 Whether the onset burst firing of TCx L5 ETs that we observed also involves dendritic electrogenesis will require direct dendrite recordings, as it is likely to be shaped by both the dendrites’ length73 and species-specific differences in dendritic excitability of these neurons.21,37,67 In contrast, L5 ET neurons in the MCx had dendrites that spread laterally in all layers and basal dendrites that could extend more than a millimeter. CS and other L5 ET neurons in the macaque MCx may thus be impacted strongly by inputs impinging onto the deeper layers in contrast to more superficial ones, while the reverse would be true of TCx L5 ET neurons. It remains to be tested whether the long “tap root”74 basal dendrites in macaque L5 ET neurons exhibit non-linear boosting dominated by N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptors and/or other voltage-gated ion channels in a manner similar to thin-caliber rodent basal dendrites.75

Linking gene expression differences to morpho-electric differences

Strong links between gene expression and physiological properties have been observed across rodent auditory brain stem neuron subclasses.45 Likewise, some of the differences in ion channel gene expression we observed qualitatively matched physiological differences. However, many physiological differences could not be directly linked to ion channel gene expression. Stronger HCN channel-related properties in MCx L5 ET neurons compared to TCx L5 ET neurons were not accompanied by detectable differences in HCN1 or PEX5L gene expression, and there was higher KCNC1-3 (Kv3) expression in non-ET neuron subclasses compared to ET neurons despite non-ET neurons exhibiting relatively broad spikes and modest firing rates. This may not be surprising, as RNA expression does not capture protein expression at the plasma membrane, post-translational modifications, or subcellular localization, which each can profoundly shape a neuron’s intrinsic properties. Furthermore, ion channels with overlapping complementary and opposing actions on neuronal physiology allow different combinations of ion channel genes to produce similar physiological properties,76 and thus it may not be expected that individual ion channel gene expression patterns provide simple direct explanations for physiology. Our data underscore the necessity of directly measuring physiological properties of neuron subclasses over inferring them from transcriptomic data alone.

Limitations of the study

Our sampling of L6 was limited, most notably in the MCx, where intense myelination in deeper layers makes targeted neuronal recordings difficult. Targeting L6 neurons more efficiently will likely require fluorescent labeling, either from retrograde labeling or cell-type-specific enhancers. In addition to differences in neurofilament and ion channel gene expression, we also identified differences in gene expression of synaptic receptors, release machinery, and some axon guidance molecules. How these relate to the neuronal function remains untested, although cell-type-specific differences in synaptic strength and short-term dynamics in ET versus non-ET neurons have been demonstrated in rodents.77,78 Semaphorin gene expression patterns suggest that the local axonal projection patterns may differ between neuronal subclasses and neocortical areas, but we did not consistently capture enough of the local axon to explicitly test this hypothesis. The axonal guidance genes that guide ET versus non-ET long-range projections during development79 were likely missed with our analysis because we studied differences in adulthood. Finally, we did not probe for differences in gene expression that could provide mechanistic explanations for the areal and cell-type-specific pathophysiology observed in neurodegenerative disorders, such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, Parkinson’s disease, and Alzheimer’s disease. While not directly addressed here, our multimodal datasets nonetheless provide a resource for future studies.

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

METHOD DETAILS

Spinal injection procedure and motor cortex collection

All animal procedures have been approved by the University of Washington IACUC committee and conformed to the NIH’s Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Retrograde labeling of corticospinal neurons was performed in 2 female (11 yr, 4 mo; 10.55 kg and 9 yr; 5.89 g) and 2 male (2 yr 5 mo.; 3.97 kg and 8 yr, 11.11 kg) Macaca nemestrina designated for the Tissue Distribution Program. Patch-seq corticospinal data included in this study was collected from three of these animals (excluding the 9 yr old female). Animals were anesthetized with isoflurane after an initial sedation with ketamine and monitored extensively by a trained surgical technician for pulse oximetry, body temperature, ECG, blood pressure, capnography, and inspired oxygen. External thermal support was provided for the duration of the surgery, and an intravenous line for intravenous drug and isotonic fluid administration, a urethral catheter was inserted to maintain fluid volume and physiological homeostasis. A paralytic was administered intravenously to prevent movement during the procedure and the animal was artificially ventilated. Under sterile conditions, a partial laminectomy of the C5-C7 vertebrae was performed to expose the unilateral dorsal surface of the cervical enlargement and the dura mater was opened. Using a stereotaxic manipulator (Kopf Instruments, Tujunga, CA) on a custom frame, targeted microinjections were performed with a Nanoject II (Drummond Scientific, Broomall, PA). A glass pipette with a broken tip (barrel of tip = 50 μm), filled with AAV-CAG-tdTomato (plasmid from Addgene (#59462), packaged in-house with serotype AAV9-retro, titer of 1.88 10^13 viral genomes/mL), was inserted into the spinal cord. Using the dorsal root entry points as a guide, multiple injection tracks were used to target ventrolateral part of the gray matter in the C6/C7 spinal segments. Each track had five injections (138 nL of virus at 23 nL/s) positioned 100 μm apart −4.1 to −3.7 mm from the surface of the cord. Track locations spanned 2.2 mm anterior-posterior and were in two rows (1 mm apart), evenly spread with slight adjustments to avoid hitting the vasculature. A 1-min wait period was used prior to each first injection within the track, a 2-min wait period after each injection before moving the pipette and a 5-min wait period before removing the electrode after the final injection for each track. The injector’s efficacy at ejecting virus was confirmed between each injection track. After the injections were complete, artificial dura was placed over the durotomy, the musculature and skin were sutured, and an anti-paralytic agent (atropine) was delivered. Post-operative monitoring and care were performed to minimize pain and distress. Thirty days after the original injections, the animal was anesthetized as described above, and then euthanized using a lethal dose of pentobarbital solution.

Brain slice preparation

Macaques were anesthetized with isoflurane gas and were perfused through the heart with carbogenated (95% O2/5% CO2) artificial cerebrospinal fluid (aCSF) consisting of (in mM): 92 N-methyl-D-glucamine (NMDG), 2.5 KCl, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 30 NaHCO3, 20 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid (HEPES), 25 glucose, 2 thiourea, 5 Na-ascorbate, 3 Na-pyruvate, 0.5 CaCl2•4H2O and 10 MgSO4•7H2O. After exsanguination, the cerebrum was extracted and the neocortical area of interest was dissected and placed in chilled, continuously carbogenated NMDG aCSF for transport back to the Allen Institute or in sucrose aCSF (in mM: 2.5 KCl, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 25 NaHCO3, 0.5 CaCl2, 7 MgCl2, 7 dextrose, 205 sucrose, 1.3 ascorbic acid, and 3 sodium pyruvate, bubbled with 95% O2/5% CO2 to maintain a pH of 7.4) for processing at the University of Washington.

Brain specimens were sectioned in NMDG aCSF or sucrose aCSF on a vibrating blade microtome (VT1200S, Leica Biosystems; Compresstome VF-300, Precisionary Instruments; Vibratome 3000) at 300–350 μm perpendicular to the pial surface. Slices made in NMDG aCSF were then transferred to a carbogenated and warmed (34°C) chamber containing NMDG aCSF for 10 min and then transferred to a room temperature (~22°C) aCSF consisting of (in mM): 92 NaCl, 2.5 KCl, 1.2 NaH2PO4, 30 NaHCO3, 20 HEPES, 25 D-glucose, 2 thiourea, 5 sodium-L-ascorbate, 3 sodium pyruvate, 2 CaCl2.4H2O and 2 MgSO4.7H2O. Slices made in sucrose aCSF were transferred to a carbogenated warmed (34°C) holding aCSF (in mM: 125 NaCl, 2.5 KCl, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 25 NaHCO3, 2 CaCl2, 2 MgCl2, 10 dextrose, 1.3 ascorbic acid, and 3 sodium pyruvate). Afterward, slices destined for acute brain slice recordings were maintained in their respective holding aCSF at room temperature until time of recording) until the time of Patch-seq experiments.

Slices destined for interface culture were placed onto membrane inserts (Millipore) in 6 well plates containing 1mL per well of culture media consisting of: 8.4 g/L MEM Eagle medium, 20% heat-inactivated horse serum, 30 mM HEPES, 13 mM D-glucose, 15 mM NaHCO3, 1mM ascorbic acid,2mMMgSO4·7H2O, 1 mM CaCl2.4H2O, 0.5 mM GlutaMAX-I and 1 mg/L insulin. The slice culture medium was carefully adjusted to pH 7.2 to 7.3 and osmolality to 300–310 mOsmoles/kg by addition of pure H2O, sterile-filtered and stored at 4°C for up to 2 weeks. Culture plates were placed in a humified, 35°C, 5% CO2 incubator. Culture medium was replaced every 2–3 days until the slices were used in experimentation. The use of cultured brain slices afforded two advantages. First, it enabled us to utilize adeno associated viral (AAV) genetic tools to label deep layer neurons with a fluorophore. A subset of cultures was infected by direct application of concentrated AAV viral particles (see key resources table for virus constructs) on the slice surface 1–12 h after plating. This was especially crucial for successfully targeting deep layer neurons in MCx, where heavy myelination impedes the visualization of neurons.10 Second, the use of cultures extended the life of tissue specimens for several days beyond the viability of acute brain slices thereby maximizing tissue use.

Patch-seq recordings

Prior to data collection, all surfaces and equipment were cleaned with RNAse Zap (Sigma-Aldrich) and nuclease-free water. Brain slices were placed in a submerged, heated (32°C–34°C) chamber that was continuously perfused with fresh, carbogenated aCSF consisting of (in mM): 119 NaCl, 2.5 KCl, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 24 NaHCO3, 12.5 glucose, 2 CaCl2·4H2O and 2 MgSO4·7H2O (pH 7.3–7.4). In a subset of experiments we included either 25 μM d-APV, 20 μM DNQX and 5 μM GABAzine or 20 μM DNQX, 25 μM d-APV and 100 μM picrotoxin. Neurons were visualized with upright microscopes (Olympus BX51WI/Scientifica/Zeiss) equipped with infrared differential interference contrast (IR-DIC) optics and either a 40x or 60× water immersion objective.

Glass patch clamp pipettes were pulled to an open tip resistance of 2–6 MΩ when filled with the internal recording solution consisting of (in mM): 110.0 K-gluconate, 10.0 HEPES, 0.2 EGTA, 4 KCl, 0.3 Na2-GTP, 10 phosphocreatine disodium salt hydrate, 1 Mg-ATP, 20 mg/mL glycogen, 0.5U/mL RNase inhibitor (Takara, 2313A), 0.5% biocytin and 0.02 Alexa 594 or 488 – pH adjusted to 7.3 with KOH. Whole cell somatic recordings were acquired using either a Axoclamp 2B or a Multiclamp 700B amplifier and custom acquisition software written in Igor Pro (MIES or using custom protocols written by Rick Gray and modified by N. Dembrow). Electrical signals were digitized at 20 or 50 kHz by an ITC-18 (HEKA) and were filtered at 10 kHz. The pipette capacitance was compensated, and the bridge was balanced (5–30 MΩ) during the entirety of the current clamp recording.

AAV vector cloning and viral packaging

The CN1633 AAV vector (rAAV-hsA2-eHGT_140h-minRho-SYFP2-WPRE3-BGHpA)80 and the full plasmid sequence and map are available from Addgene (plasmid ID 163503). This vector labels Pvalb neurons in the mouse and macaque neocortex when administered in vivo but exhibits loss of specificity in macaque cortical slice culture, preferentially labeling many large pyramidal neurons. This pattern of viral reporter labeling was deemed useful for targeting L5 pyramidal neurons based on soma shape and size. The CN2787 enhancer AAV vector contains an optimized 3XCore concatemer of enhancer eHGT_140h and imparts faster onset of expression and higher level of reporter expression with identical pattern of labeling, which is useful for macaque brain slice culture experiments where targeted recording in the early days in culture is desired. The CN2787 enhancer AAV vector full plasmid sequence and map are available on Addgene (plasmid ID 191726).

The pAAV2/9-retro packaging plasmid was created by gene synthesis according to the previously published capsid sequence81 by inserting the 10-mer peptide sequence LADQDYTKTA from AAV2-retro82 in between the Q588 and A589 residues of the AAV9 capsid using the pAAV2/9 plasmid backbone (Cell Biolabs). Small-scale crude AAV preps were generated by triple transfecting 15 μg ITR plasmid, 15 μg AAV9-retro capsid plasmid (pAAV2/9-retro), and 30 μg pHelper (Cell Biolabs) into one 15-cm plate of confluent HEK-293T cells using PEI Max (Polysciences Inc., catalog # 24765-1). At one day post-transfection we changed the medium to low serum (1% FBS), and after 3 days the cells and supernatant were collected, freeze-thawed 3x to release AAV particles, treated with benzonase nuclease (MilliporeSigma catalog #E8263-25KU) for 1 h to degrade free DNA, then clarified (3000 g × 10 min) and concentrated to approximately 150 μL by using an Amicon Ultra-15 centrifugal filter unit (NMWL 100 kDa, Sigma #Z740210-24EA) at 5000g for 30–60 min, yielding a titer of approximately 3–5.0E+13 vg/mL. For large-scale gradient preps for intraparenchymal injection into NHP, we transfected 10 x 15-cm plates of cells and purified by iodixanol gradient centrifugation. We titered both crude and gradient AAV preps by digital droplet PCR on a BioRad QX200 system.

Processing of patch-seq samples

Patch-seq samples were processed by previously established standardized procedures.14 We used the SMART-Seq v4 Ultra Low Input RNA Kit for Sequencing (Takara, 634894) to reverse transcribe for 20 PCR cycles for cDNA amplification. Samples proceeded through library construction using Nextera XT DNA Library Preparation Kit (Illumina FC-131-1096) according to the manufacturer’s instructions except at 0.2x reaction size. Samples were sequenced to 500k paired-end 50b reads/sample. Paired-end reads were aligned to the Mmul_10 rhesus macaque reference genome (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/datasets/genome/GCF_003339765.1/). Only uniquely aligned reads were used for gene quantification. Exonic and intronic reads were summed for total gene counts.

Biocytin histology

Following patch-clamp/Patch-seq experiments, slices were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 1–2 days and then kept in PBS at 4°C until staining. A horseradish peroxidase enzyme reaction using diaminobenzidine (DAB) as the chromogen was used to visualize biocytin-filled neurons.83 Slices were stained with DAPI and then incubated in 1% hydrogen peroxide for 30 min. Following permeabilization with 2% Triton X- in PBS, slices were incubated in ABC with 0.1% Triton at 4°C overnight and up to 2 days. Slices were then washed in PBS three times.

After DAB processing slices were mounted and imaged using an upright AxioImager Z2 microscope (Zeiss, Germany). First, two-dimensional tiled overview images of the entire slice were obtained in both the brightfield and DAPI fluorescence channels using a 20× objective. High resolution image stacks of individual neurons were acquired at higher resolution using a 63x lens at an interval of 0.44 μm in the z axis.

Morphological reconstructions

For a subset of neurons that mapped confidently to the L5 ET subclass and/or were identified CS neurons based on retrograde labeling we obtained reconstructions of the dendrites and axon. Reconstructions were generated by using the 63x image stack obtained following DAB processing.83 All reconstructions were newly generated for the current study except for one which was previously published.84

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

Neurophysiology features

For the current study, we presented data from 300 new patch-clamp/Patch-seq experiments, 168 of which were included in transcriptomic analyses. We also included data from 20 cells from a previously published study on M110 and 13 cells from a Patch-seq methods manuscript14 (in the original study no physiology was analyzed and Patch-seq samples were mapped to a human reference taxonomy). For all electrophysiology experiments we used four basic current stimuli to probe the intrinsic membrane properties of most neurons in our dataset. Means and standard errors for all 40 extracted features can be found in Table S3. The first stimulus was a chirp stimulus that increased in frequency logarithmically from 0.2 to 40 Hz in 20s. The amplitude of the chirp was adjusted for each neuron to produce a peak-to-peak deflection of ~10 mV. Impedance amplitude (ZAP) and phase profiles (ZPP) were calculated as the ratio of the Fourier transform of the voltage response to the Fourier transform of the chirp.21 The frequency at which the maximum impedance occurred was defined as the resonant frequency (fR). Resonance strength was defined as the ratio of the maximum impedance to the impedance at .5 Hz. The 3 dB cutoff was defined as the frequency at which the ZAP attenuated to (√.5Zmax). Total inductive phase was defined as the area under the inductive portion of the ZPP. Synchrony phase was defined the frequency at which the ZPP was 0.

The second stimulus set was a series of 1 s square wave steps that typically varied from −200pA to 50 pA in +50 pA steps. For neurons with a high or low input resistance this protocol was scaled down or up, respectively to produce similar voltage deflections across neurons. Maximum and steady-state input resistance were calculated from the linear portion of the current voltage relationship in response to this protocol. Voltage sag was defined as the ratio of maximum to steady state input resistance. Rebound slope was defined as the slope of the rebound amplitude as a function of steady-state membrane potential.

The third stimulus set consisted of a series of 1 s square wave depolarizing current injections that increased in amplitude by +50 pA/step to a maximum of 1 nA or until depolarization induced spike failure occurred. We measured the number of spikes and the maximum instantaneous firing rate in response to each current injection. Maximum instantaneous firing rate was defined as 1/interspike interval(min). Gain was defined as the linear slope of these frequency/current plots. Maximum gain measurements corresponded to the maximum instantaneous slope and initial gain corresponded to the instantaneous slope where each neuron began firing. Single action potential properties were measured from the voltage response to the lowest current injection amplitude that produced a spike. Action potential threshold was defined as the voltage where the first derivative of the voltage response exceeded 20 V/s. Action potential width was measured at half the amplitude between this threshold and the peak voltage. The fast AHP was defined as the minimum voltage following a spike relative to threshold. Spike frequency accommodation (SFA), medium AHP and coefficient of variation were measured from the first spike train that produced at least 10 spikes. SFA was defined as the ratio of the second ISI to last ISI and the mAHP was defined as the minimum voltage after the spike train relative to resting potential.

The fourth stimulus was a series of 3 ms square wave current steps that increased in amplitude by 100 +pA from an initial value of +500 pA until spike generation. From this protocol, we measured the same single spike properties as described for the 1 s current step protocol.

For neurons presented in Figures 6I-6K and 7, we applied two additional protocols. First, to measure subthreshold oscillations and associated firing patterns we applied a series of 10s square wave current steps. The amplitude of this current step was manually adjusted to probe near rheobase responses up until each neuron fired approximately 5 spikes within the first second. Fast Fourier transform (FFT) and autocorrelogram analysis of oscillations relative to membrane voltage was performed on sweeps that contained at least one spike, and analysis was restricted to sweeps with no more than 200 spikes across the 10 s.

FFT and autocorrelogram analysis of interspike intervals was performed using the R programming language for statistical computing. Briefly, action potentials were identified and the interspike intervals were isolated. Segments were further isolated from the AHP by fitting a curve to identify the return to baseline, and the 50 ms preceding the subsequent AP was dropped to avoid including features of the ensuing AP runup. Prior to FFT analysis, segments were filtered at a lower bound of 3 Hz and an upper bound of 50 Hz. Segments of a duration greater than 650ms were then individually analyzed using fast-Fourier transforms (FFT) to determine signal strength in the frequency domain, and the peak frequency and strength of each individual segment. A smooth spline curve was fitted to determine the average peak frequency and signal strength of the sweep. Autocorrelogram analysis was also performed on each segment. For segments of duration greater than 1 s, autocorrelation analysis was restricted to the last 1 s of the segment. Evaluation of the peaks and troughs in the corresponding autocorrelogram was used to reveal frequency features within each segment. Voltage segments used to assess FFT frequency and strength, and autocorrelogram frequency, were aggregated into 5 mV bins for individual cells. For sweeps with at least 10 spikes we calculated the coefficient of variation of inter-spike intervals. We excluded spikes that occurred in the first 100 ms of each sweep to minimize the effect of onset bursts on the coefficient of variation. To calculate the modal firing rate, first the reciprocal of every interspike interval (ISI) was calculated, resulting the instantaneous firing rate. The modal firing rate was the most common values of the instantaneous firing rate over the entire 10 s current injection.

To measure changes in action potential kinetics during spike trains we probed each neurons’ response to a series of 1s square wave current injections that increased in amplitude by 200 pA up to a maximum value of 4 nA (Figures 6I-6K), which corresponded to the maximum current injection permitted by the headstage feedback resistor used in our patch clamp setup. We measured the properties of each spike in response to this stimulus set. For each step amplitude we calculated the ratio of the last to first spike property. Depolarization induced block of spike generation was identified as a negative slope in the average rate FI curves.

Initial analyses involving a 4-way ANOVA with species, preparation, area, and subclass as the factors revealed no significant effects of species on any of the 40 physiological features extracted for analysis. As such, to simplify analysis, we grouped macaque species together for subsequent analyses.

Physiology UMAP and classification

The generation of physiology UMAPs and logistic regression classifiers required preprocessing to accommodate missing values and the influence of acute versus culture preparation. We excluded neurons with more than 75% of physiology features missing. For the remaining cells we imputed missing feature values as the mean of the two nearest neighbors (KNNImputer). Features were then transformed to z-scores (StandardScaler). Z score transformations were performed separately for neurons recorded in acute versus culture.

Following this preprocessing, UMAP visualizations were generated by first performing principal component analysis on the feature z-scores. PCs that explained at least 1% of the variance were then used to generate UMAPs. To predict transcriptomic subclass based on physiology, we used a multiclass logistic regression classifier using feature z-scores as the training data. To assess within dataset performance, we randomly sampled 25% or 50% of the dataset for training and using the held-out data for cross validation. This process was repeated 100 times for each percentage of held out data, with classifier predictions on test data aggregated to calculate confusion matrix performance. As an additional validation step, we repeated this procedure on sparse PCs of features rather than z-scores of individual features. Scores are presented as average f1 scores. We trained the whole Patch-seq/retrograde labeling dataset and used this classifier to predict subclass membership of samples without transcriptomic/retrograde labeling.

Which physiological features the classifier (Figure 3) utilized to distinguish L5 ET and non-ET neurons in MCx and TCx were identified by further analysis of the regression coefficients. These coefficients correspond to a change in the log-odds that a recorded neuron belongs to a given subclass given a one unit change in a particular physiology feature. The features with the largest coefficients for predicting L5 ET versus non-ET subclasses matched features with the largest effect size in the ANOVA (resonance frequency, input resistance, action potential max dV/dt and min dV/dt).

Morphology feature analysis

Prior to morphological feature analysis, reconstructed neuronal morphologies were expanded in the dimension perpendicular to the cut surface to correct for shrinkage85,86 after tissue processing. The amount of shrinkage was calculated by comparing the distance of the soma to the cut surface during recording and after fixation and reconstruction.

Features were calculated using the skeleton keys (https://github.com/AllenInstitute/skeleton_keys) and neuron morphology (https://github.com/AllenInstitute/neuron_morphology) Python packages83,87. Features were extracted from neurons aligned in the direction perpendicular to pia and white matter. Although local axon was reconstructed it was not included in analysis due to inconsistent sampling.

Patch-seq sample mapping

To determine the corresponding transcriptomic cell type of each Patch-seq sample, we used a correlation-based mapping strategy. For each neuron, we computed its correlation in gene expression with a set of marker genes that define each branch of the reference taxonomy. We mapped Patch-seq samples to both a macaque MTG reference taxonomy and an M1 reference taxonomy. In general, mapping calls at the subclass level were consistent between reference taxonomies. Only 21 samples mapped to different subclasses between taxonomies and for all but one of these samples, the mismatch was between L6 IT and L5 IT subclasses. The M1 reference was generated from an L5 dissection and consisted of only 15.8k nuclei compared to 89.1k nuclei from all layers in the MTG reference. Because it is a more complete reference taxonomy, which likely improves the annotation of marker genes that are used for Patch-seq mapping, and because it yielded higher correlations (Figure S2), we reported subclass annotations of Patch-seq samples mapped to the MTG reference taxonomy unless otherwise specified. We focused on subclass annotations because of evidence that subclasses are conserved across areas, but finer cell type distinctions may not be.12,88,89 Patch-seq mapping was performed using the R based scratch-mapping package (https://github.com/AllenInstitute/scrattch.mapping). Patch-seq samples were included in the dataset if they: 1) had a normalized sum “on” type marker-gene expression (NMS90) greater than 0.7 and 2) had a correlation score of at least 0.525.

Gene expression analysis/visualization

We utilized published snRNA-sequencing datasets derived from the human and macaque MTG and M19,10,27 to identify L5 ET enriched ion channel, neurofilament and axon-guidance genes (Table S1). All reference datasets were acquired using Cv3 10x sequencing. Gene sets were curated from HUGO gene nomenclature using gene group hierarchy maps for ion channels and intermediate filaments. For ion channel genes we included all genes listed under the Ion channel gene group (330 genes total; https://www.genenames.org/data/genegroup/#!/group/177). For neurofilament genes we included genes listed under the Intermediate filament group (73 total; https://www.genenames.org/data/genegroup/#!/group/607). We also handpicked a small subset of genes known to regulate ion channel function or expression (19 total; e.g., PEX5L, DPP6, DPP10, KCNIP1-436,91,92). We also included semaphores (20 total; https://www.genenames.org/data/genegroup/#!/group/736) because of their association with axon targeting,93 which might provide some additional insight into functional differences. This axon targeting gene list is not exhaustive, which we deemed appropriate given our focus on morpho-electric properties. The RNA-seq datasets were filtered to include samples from transcriptomic subclasses enriched in the infragranular layers. For most analyses, log2(counts per million (CPM) + 1) values were used). UMAP visualizations were generated by first performing principal component analysis on z-scores of the summed exonic and intronic reads. PCs that explained at least 1% of the variance were then used to generate UMAPs. We also generated UMAPs based all 442 genes in our curated list as well as for only the 330 ion channel genes to determine how gene selection affects UMAP.

Joint visualization of Patch-seq and macaque snRNA-seq datasets was performed using the Seurat pipeline.94 First, we excluded genes most highly expressed in nonneuronal cells in the 10x reference and gene enriched in each platform (Patch-seq or 10x). The Seurat data integration pipeline (FindIntegrationAnchors and IntegrateData) was used to scale both datasets and to perform PCA on the resultant integrated space. UMAP coordinates were calculated based on the top 30 principal components using the RunUMAP function. Metadata (preparation, subclass, etc.) were then color coded on these UMAP coordinates.

For differential gene analysis in Figure 1, we down sampled the reference. 10x taxonomies such that each combination of factors (e.g., L5 ET M1 macaque, L5 ET M1 human, L5 ET MTG macaque, L5 ET MTG human, etc) had an n = 50. Then, we defined differentially expressed genes as those with at least 0.5 log2-fold difference between L5 ET and non-ET neuronal subclasses or L5 ET neurons in TCx versus MCx. We chose a threshold of 0.5 log2-fold in accordance with previous publications,10,21 which permits for the detection of small, but consistent differences in gene expression between groups. We then fit expression data for each of these differentially expressed genes with a 3-way ANOVA (for ET versus non-ET analysis; species, area and subclass as factors) or a two-way ANOVA (for cross-areal L5 ET analysis; species and area as factors). We computed the effect size (partial eta squared; η2) of each factor and combination of interacting terms. The number of Patch-seq samples for each cell type in violin plots showing expression differences of specific genes (Figures 4H and S6C) are listed in Table S2.

Statistical comparisons

Comparisons between groups are presented as whisker boxplots or violin plots. Statistical significance was assessed using ANOVAs, t-tests, Mann-Whitney U tests or Pearson’s correlation. p values were FDR adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini/Hochberg method. Effect sizes are reported as partial eta-squared or Cohen’s d values. Details of statistical comparisons can be found in the text, figure legends and supplemental tables. N represents animals or cells as can be found in figure legends or statistical tables.
