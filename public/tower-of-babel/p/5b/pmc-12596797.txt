Protocol-based postoperative brain scan after drainage of chronic subdural hematoma in a low-resource setting (ProtoScan study)
Authors: Uchenna Ajoku, Linda Iroegbu-Emeruem
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background: Routine postoperative computed tomography (CT) scan in chronic subdural hematoma (cSDH) management is not supported by literature. Yet, many institutions perform routine postoperative scans for several reasons. The dangers are potential for abuse, healthcare cost, and unnecessary exposure to ionizing radiation.Methods: Data were collected prospectively on patients who had surgery for cSDH between January 2021 and December 2023. Number of postoperative CT, complication rate, average length of hospital stay, recurrence rate, and functional outcome were analyzed.Results: One hundred and six patients had surgery and 6 months follow-up record after surgery. There were 89 males (84%) and 17 females (16%). Of the 11 (10.38%) patients who had postoperative brain CT, three had postoperative scan due to new neurological deficits, four did not improve 48 h after surgery, one had cortical injury, and three had initial improvement but then deteriorated within 1 week. Our data showed recurrence in 7 (6.6%) patients, deep vein thrombosis 2 (1.9%), and wound infection and subdural empyema 1 (0.9%). Duration of hospital admission significantly affected complication; 5.9% in patients who stayed <7 days on admission against 18.4% in those who stayed more than 7 days (P = 0.05). Outcome at 6 months revealed that 100 (94.3%) patients had good outcome while 6 (5.7%) had bad outcome.Conclusion: Routine brain CT scan after uncomplicated drainage of cSDH in patients without new neurological symptoms adds no value. A rational clinical approach based on neurologic evaluation should guide the decision on a postoperative scan.

INTRODUCTION

Chronic subdural hematoma (cSDH) is one of the commonest clinical conditions in neurosurgery.[29,34,37] The incidence is highest in the elderly population in most series. However, recent studies suggest that a significant number of middle-aged patients are presenting with this pathology especially in developing countries.[36] The higher incidence in younger patients in developing country is likely due to a lower life expectancy and more traumatic etiology. CSDH could result from head trauma, although only 50% of the patients have a history of trauma.[36] Predisposing factors for cSDH include cerebral atrophy, use of anticoagulants, and chronic alcohol use.[4,28,33]

The diagnosis is most often based on a brain computed tomography (CT) scan or magnetic resonance imaging. The acceptable treatment for cSDH is surgical drainage in majority of the cases, although some researchers have described non-surgical treatment in special circumstances.[11,12,15,32] Several surgical techniques have been described, each with their merits and demerits. There is no agreement regarding the best surgical technique when evacuating cSDH. The goal of surgery is decompression of the brain and minimizing the risk of recurrence. Issues around the use of drains, number of burr-holes, etc., have formed the basis for most research endeavors in recent years.[5-7,30]

Another subject of controversy is the value of routine postoperative CT scan in minimizing surgical complications and prediction of recurrence. There are wide geographic and institutional variations in the use of postoperative CT scan as a surveillance tool in the management of cSDH.[21,26,27] Although some authors have recommended routine postoperative CT scan as a tool to detect complications and predict recurrence, existing literature has not shown that routine postoperative CT led to better clinical outcomes.[14,16,31]

In our institution, we do not routinely perform a postoperative CT following the drainage of cSDH. The reason is due to additional cost to the patient, and sometimes unavailability of CT scan. However, in certain circumstances, we order a CT scan after surgery when we assess the risk of complications or recurrence to be high. We have since developed a protocol for brain imaging after surgery for cSDH. Anecdotally, we have observed that our results were comparable with what is reported in the literature.

In this study, we present our 3-year result on outcome of cSDH using a locally developed postoperative CT scan protocol with the aim of comparing our results with available literature.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This is a review of prospectively collected data on patients who had surgery for cSDH between January 2019 and December 2023 at the University of Port Harcourt Teaching Hospital in Port Harcourt, Nigeria. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics and Research Committee of the University of Port Harcourt Teaching Hospital (UPTH/ADM/90/S.11/VOL.XI/1714).

Patients

Between January 2019 and December 2023, 120 patients were referred to our institution for surgical treatment of cSDH. Surgery was performed in all cases. For this review, 14 cases for which we could not get complete clinical information and/or up to 6 months follow-up were excluded.

Surgical management

We had two broad categories of patients; those who presented with impaired consciousness and had urgent surgery; and those who were fully conscious with/without neurologic deficit who had planned/elective surgery. For the emergency group, surgery was, if possible, done on the day of admission.

Hematological parameters were corrected and antiplatelets stopped at least 7 days before surgery for patients in the elective group. For this group, local infiltration with xylocaine/bupivacaine as well as analgesics was used as the anesthesia. In patients with Glasgow coma scale (GCS) 9–14, general anesthesia was employed while coagulation profile was corrected where feasible.

The standard surgical procedure involved the use of a frontal and/or parietal burr hole.

Patients’ CT imaging in the axial, coronal, and sagittal planes were used to plan surgical incision sites. The patients were positioned supine, with head slightly elevated on a cotton head ring, and turned to the contralateral side for a unilateral lesion. Skin incision was made at the thickest layer of the hematoma and carried down to the periosteum. A 1.5–2 cm burr hole was made and dura coagulated using bipolar diathermy and then opened in a cruciate fashion. The hematoma cavity was continuously irrigated with warm antibiotic saline until the effluent became clear. When deemed necessary by the surgeon, a subdural or subgaleal drainage tube was inserted and brought out through a separate stab incision. Surgical wound was closed with nonabsorbable suture and the drain connected to a reservoir.

Our patients were nursed in supine position for 48 h until the drain was removed, usually when the output was <30 mL in 24 h. After removal of the drain, the patients were encouraged to get out of bed and physiotherapy was commenced. Most patients were discharged home within 1 week of surgery if there were no complications. They were followed up through the surgical outpatient clinic. The routine follow-up included a clinical visit at 1 week after surgery, then 4 weeks, 3 months, and 6 months, unless a complication arose before these time periods. Patients’ regular medications like anticoagulant/antiplatelets were restarted in consultation with their primary care physician.

Pro forma and variables

We developed a pro forma for prospectively collecting data of all patients undergoing surgery for cSDH. Data set points for each patient included patient sex, age, medical co-morbidity, known alcohol use, use of anticoagulant and/or antiplatelet drugs, and history of head trauma leading up to the diagnosis. The following examination findings were captured; neurologic status at presentation (using the GCS and the Markwalder scales), brain imaging finding, laterality of hematoma, surgery and surgical procedure including complications, use of drains, length of hospital stay, outcomes at 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months and recurrence (defined as reappearance of symptoms leading to another surgery). A modified Rankin score (mRS) was calculated for every patient at the time of initial review, discharge from the hospital and at 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months post-surgery.

Outcome variables

We analyzed the complication rate in our study population, the average length of hospital stay, recurrence rate, outcome at the various time points, and the CT scan rate.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM Statistics is a statistical software (SPSS) statistical package (Version 20.0, SPSS Inc., Chicago IL, USA). Categorical variables were described using counts and percentages, while continuous variables were expressed using mean, median, range, and interquartile range. Only outcomes of cases for whom follow-up data were available were included. In the baseline and outcomes analyses, categorical variables were compared using chi-square while continuous variables were analyzed with Fisher’s exact tests. The Mann–Whitney U-test was used to explore the difference between the cohorts who had CT and those who didn’t. Any value of P below 0.05 was considered significant.

DISCUSSION

The overarching principle in the management of cSDH is premised on relieving patients of their symptoms, minimizing complications, and prevention of recurrence.[17] Neurologic examination and neuroimaging are often used to achieve these objectives.[10,35] However, with the widespread availability of brain CT and the ease of image acquisition, the potential for overuse is a real concern in most healthcare system.

Our hypothesis was that postoperative CT informed by either a surgical event or neurological finding were most likely to yield clinically useful information. By following this rational approach, unnecessary CT scan is avoided without added risks to patients. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first attempt at developing a practical stepwise protocol to determine the need or otherwise of postoperative CT scan after drainage of cSDH in a low-resource setting.

Our study showed that, of the 106 patients that had successful burr hole drainage of cSDH, 95 (89.63%) did not have any postoperative CT at the time of last follow-up. Eleven (10.38%) patients had postoperative CT scan based an apriori criteria developed for the purpose of identifying patients who truly needed it. None of the patient who did not have postoperative scan developed any complications or poor outcome at the last follow-up at 6 months.

In most neurosurgical literature, the outcome after evacuation of cSDH is considered favorable. In a randomized controlled trial, Santarius and Hutchinson reported a good 6-month functional outcome (mRS score <4) in 84% of patients in the treatment arm.[29] Our patient population who had a 6-month good functional outcome was 94.3% (mRS of <4). We suspect that our superior outcome may be related to the younger median age in our study population.

The recurrence rate from our study cohort was 6.6%. Recurrence rate may be as high as 70% over time, but most estimated reoperation rates range between 10 and 20%[9,13,37]

Studies from Africa by Mersha, et al. reported a recurrence rate of 6.6% while Aseged and Leake reported a recurrence rate of1.6%, respectively.[3,20] Our mortality rate was 2.8%, compared to 4.2% by Aseged and Leake[3] in Ethiopia, 2% by Abdullah et al. in Ghana,[1] 2.8% by Amirjamshidi et al. in Iran,[2] and 7% by Dowlati et al. from the USA.[8] While Posti et al. reported a case – fatality rate of 9–10% from the Finnish Registry.[24] Rashid et al.[25] reported 20% mortality in their study in Tanzania.

Of the 11 patients who had postoperative CT scan during their management, seven had recurrence, with five of them being over 75 years old. In this category of patients, we noted severe brain atrophy, and failure of the brain to re-expand’ at surgery. This lack of brain “re-expansion” creates a dead space around the brain. Such spaces increase movement of the brain in the cranial cavity and predisposes such patients to frequent small vessel trauma.[19] Unfortunately, our study population was not large enough for subgroup analysis on the influence of age on hematoma recurrence.

Four of the patients had postoperative CT scan but no recurrence at the time of the last follow-up. One was a 66-year-old man with bilateral cSDH who came in unconscious. He had an emergency burr hole drainage and had marked clinical improvement 24 h after surgery. However, his clinical condition worsened over the next 48 h which prompted a postoperative scan based on our protocol. The brain CT done revealed parenchymal injury from the subdural drain as well as tension pneumocephalus [Figure 4]. He had an emergency repeat surgery and subsequently made good neurological recovery and was discharged home. At the time of his 6-month follow-up, he had returned to his pre-morbid state and was back to work.

Two patients died from DVT/pulmonary complications while on admission. The other patient died at home under uncertain circumstances. Proponents of routine postoperative brain scan argue that routine postoperative imaging would help the early identification of potentially life-threatening complications such as rebleeding or pneumocephalus before they cause harm to the patients. We believe, as we have shown in this series that meticulous neurological evaluation would help identify patients at risk of surgical complications without compromising patients’ overall safety.

Most institutions still carry out routine brain CT after drainage of cSDH and several studies have questioned its necessity. A retrospective analysis of a series of 202 Danish cSDH patients performed by Pedersen et al.[23] showed no benefit of routine CT scan 6 weeks after drainage of cSDH. They demonstrated in their study cohort that recurrence of neurological symptoms always occurred before the scheduled 4–6 weeks postoperative scan.[23]

In a study to evaluate the usefulness of routine postoperative brain CT in various brain pathologies by Garrett et al.,[14] it was found that only six of 126 head CT scans showed clinically significant finding. Of these, only 1 (0.8% of the overall group) resulted in re-intervention. Their study evaluated whether the result of all cranial CT ordered in the neurosurgical unit led to change in the patients’ management. Only CT scans studies obtained after changes in patients’ neurological status revealed a new radiographic finding. Scans obtained as a routine (for surveillance) without a neurological change did not detect an abnormality and when they occasionally did, patient did not require further intervention.[14]

Khaldi et al. found that none (0%) of 251 patients returned to the operating room due to findings of routine CT scans obtained within 24 h after cranial surgery without new neurological findings, compared to 30% who had CT findings after neurological symptoms had appeared.[18] This finding seems to suggest that CT scan was more clinically relevant when there were concordant neurologic findings.

One reason some surgeons perform that routine postoperative scan is to evaluate residual hematoma volume after surgical drainage. Such residual hematoma is thought to influence brain expansion and predict recurrence.[35] A study by Ng et al., on predictive value of routine early postoperative CT on residual hematoma volume reported that the routine postoperative CT was not a predictive factor for brain re-expansion after cSDH surgery; therefore, routine postoperative CT scan was not deemed necessary.[22]

Hulsbergen et al.[16] showed that a US academic hospital had a 5-fold higher postoperative CT rate than a university medical center in the Netherlands. At the US hospital, multiple CT scans were routinely obtained postoperatively to monitor patient’s recovery. In their report, they stated that there was no formal policy recommending the number and timing of the scans. The scans were obtained both immediately after surgery and during outpatient follow-up. Conversely at the Dutch hospital, patients were only monitored clinically, and a CT scan was ordered if neurological symptoms appeared. They showed no significant difference in the re-intervention rates and clinical outcomes between patients that had routine postoperative CT and those that did not between the two hospitals. Nevertheless, the economic implication was about $817,000 savings by the Dutch hospital, with no disadvantages in terms of patient outcomes.

While we are not averse to brain imaging when indicated, the purpose of this work is to highlight that a protocol-based approach in the postoperative CT scanning of patients with cSDH minimizes cost and reduces exposure to unnecessary radiation and wastage in the healthcare system without compromising patients’ safety.

Study limitation

Our study has several limitations. The first is that it is not a randomized study and so may not rank high in the hierarchy of clinical evidence. Our aim however was not to prove the superiority of our protocol-based approach over the routine postoperative scan but to review our protocol-based approach of obtaining a postoperative brain scan after draining a cSDH and to compare our results with what is reported in literature.

Our limited follow-up period of 6 months is probably short since there is a possibility that some patients experienced recurrence or other complications after this period. However, we think that it is unlikely that any complications occurring 6 months surgery would be directly related to the surgical procedure.

The sample size of 106 patients may be small given that cSDH is a common pathological condition and our study population may have oversimplified the true picture of the disease condition. To validate our finding, a multi-center prospective randomized trial with larger sample size is needed.

CONCLUSION

Our results show that routine postoperative scan after uncomplicated drainage of cSDH in patients without new neurological symptoms/signs add no clinical value. A rational clinical approach based on neurologic evaluation should guide the decision of a postoperative scan without compromising patients’ outcome.
