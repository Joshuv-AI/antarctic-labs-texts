A theoretical framework suggests that the early phase of psychosis represents a critical window where interventions might have important long-term implications.1 This framework has driven substantial efforts toward developing specialized early intervention services. These services focusing on the early stage of illness lead to better outcomes compared to treatment as usual, though evidence suggests these benefits are primarily observed during the typical window of treatment of 2-5 years.2 Antipsychotic drugs, the cornerstone of treatment in psychosis, target psychotic symptoms. However, some individuals experience persistent psychotic symptoms despite such interventions. Understanding the factors contributing to persistent psychosis during the early illness phase and the outcomes associated with persistent psychosis may provide valuable insights into illness trajectories and treatment targets aiming at improving outcomes.

Some studies have focused on the prevalence of persistent psychosis, its predictors, and outcomes. Using data from 246 individuals participating in the NIHR Genetics and Psychosis (GAP) study conducted in South London from 2005 to 2010, Lally et al. found that among individuals followed for 5 years after their initial treatment, 23% of the cohort presented with residual psychotic symptoms based on medical record data.3 In this study, younger age at baseline, male sex, and individuals who self-identified as Black race were associated with greater risk of persistent psychosis, although the long-term consequences were not examined. In another sample of 169 patients from the PAFIP cohort in Spain, participants were followed every several months after their first treatment for psychosis for up to 10 years. In this study, individuals with persistent psychosis were not analyzed separately, but it was found that the extent of persistent psychosis for each given individual was associated with worse social functioning (but not functional recovery) at year 10 after treatment onset.4 Meta-analytically, primary treatment resistance, defined as persistent psychotic symptoms after a first diagnosis of schizophrenia, has been estimated to occur in 22.0% of patients diagnosed with schizophrenia.5 While the prevalence of primary treatment resistance provides important context, persistent psychosis may reflect treatment resistance, lack of adequate treatment, or lack of adherence to adequate treatment. Treatment resistance is therefore a lower bound on persistent psychosis. Persistent psychosis is agnostic to treatment history and may therefore be a more tractable marker for clinical use.

The present study aims to characterize the prevalence of persistent psychosis in a well-defined epidemiological sample and to understand its predictors and long-term implications. A sizeable proportion of individuals during the early phase of a psychotic disorder will present with persistent psychotic symptoms. However, it is not clear whether there are variables prior to or at illness onset that could be intervened upon to reduce the risk of persistent psychosis. Furthermore, the consequences of persistent psychosis during the early course of illness are not well understood. Addressing these questions is necessary to identify whether specific interventions may be necessary for this subgroup of individuals, as well as to develop those treatment and preventive interventions.

To address this gap in the literature, we analyzed data from 526 individuals who participated in the Suffolk County Mental Health Project, which involved follow-up assessments for 25 years after first inpatient treatment onset for a psychotic episode. Specifically, the primary objective of this study was to determine the prevalence of persistent psychosis during the 4 years following initial treatment for psychosis, identify the clinical and sociodemographic factors that preceded persistence of psychosis, and assess long-term outcomes associated with persistent psychosis in the early phase of treatment.

Methods

Sample

For this analysis, we used data from the Suffolk County Mental Health Project, an epidemiological cohort of patients with first-admission psychosis.6 During the enrollment period (1989-1995), individuals in their first admission for psychosis were recruited from all 12 inpatient facilities in Suffolk County, New York. Inclusion criteria were residence in Suffolk County, age 15-60, ability to speak English, no intellectual disability, first admission within the past 6 months, current psychosis, no apparent medical etiology for psychotic symptoms, and capacity to provide informed consent. Seventy-two percent of eligible individuals enrolled in the study. The Stony Brook University Committee on Research Involving Human Subjects and the hospital institutional review boards approved the protocol annually. Written consent was obtained from all study participants, or from their parents for those who were minors.

Measures

Persistent Psychosis

Participants were reassessed in face-to-face interviews 6 months, 2 years, and 4 years after first admission. In addition, telephone interviews were performed every 3 months until the 2-year follow-up, and every 6 months until the 4-year follow-up. These frequent contacts allowed raters to create a timeline of symptoms with day-level resolution for each participant over the 4 years following first admission.7 Persistent psychosis was defined as meeting criterion A of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual (DSM)-IV criteria for schizophrenia, minus duration requirements, for ≥90% of the observed days within the 4 years following first admission. This threshold was chosen to identify individuals with nearly continuous psychotic symptoms despite treatment, representing a clinically significant subgroup with limited symptom-free periods. To validate this approach, we conducted sensitivity analyses using alternative thresholds (≥75% and ≥50% of days with psychotic symptoms) to examine if our findings were robust across different definitions. Individuals were included in the analysis if they had sufficient data to establish the presence or absence of psychotic symptoms for at least 80% of days within the 4-year follow-up period.

Predictors

Sociodemographic variables included self-identified racial and ethnic categories, occupational prestige8 and years of education9; clinical variables include age of psychosis onset, duration of untreated psychosis (DUP) extracted from the Structured Clinical Interview for the DSM (SCID),10 diagnoses as described in previous publications,11 family history of psychiatric illness, premorbid function,12,13 global function measured by the Global Assessment of Function (GAF), cognitive performance,14 social and work function from the Quality of Life Scale (QLS),15 drug use,16 body mass index (BMI), symptom severity at study entry by the Schedule for the Assessment of Positive Symptoms and Schedule for the Assessment of Negative Symptoms,17,18 with ratings scored for Avolition-Apathy, Inexpressivity, Reality Distortion, and Disorganization.19 More details on the predictors are included in the Supplemental Data.

25-Year Outcomes

We used as outcomes at 25 years symptom severity, GAF, BMI, and QLS’ social and role function. Remission and Recovery. Symptom remission was operationalized according to Andreasen’s definition,20 using a score of no more than mildly severe on selected items of the Scale for the Assessment of Positive Symptoms and the Scale for the Assessment of Negative Symptoms in the past month.17,18 Recovery was defined according to Liberman’s criteria21 as explained in detail elsewhere.22 Participants were rated as having experienced homelessness if they considered themselves to have been homeless and if they had, for at least 1 night, slept in a shelter, park, or abandoned building, in the street, or in a bus or train station because they had nowhere else to stay.23 Residential independence was defined as living in their own household vs. living with relatives, supported housing, or an institution. Similarly, economic independence was defined as the participant and/or their spouse fully supporting their family vs relying on relatives or financial assistance for financial support. Frailty was assessed using the Short Physical Performance Battery.24 The UCLA Performance-Based Skills Assessment (UPSA) was used as a measure of skills in community living.25

Statistical Methods

Individuals with persistent psychosis were compared to those without persistent psychosis in all predictor and outcome variables. Independent sample t-test were used for continuous variables, with differences expressed in Cohen’s d. For skewed variables such as the DUP, we used the non-parametric test of the median. For binary variables, we used Wald tests, with effect sizes expressed in difference in proportions. We used the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure26 to correct for multiple comparisons.

Predictors that survived the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure were carried forward into a multivariable logistic regression model with forward-entry of predictors. In these models, persistent psychosis was the dependent variable, predicted by sociodemographic and baseline clinical variables. Variance accounted for by the resulting predictive model was expressed in terms of Nagelkerke’s R-squared, as well as the area under the curve (AUC) of the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) (ie, predicted vs observed caseness).

Outcomes that survived the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure were confirmed in linear regression models for continuous outcomes, and logistic regression models for binary outcomes. Outcomes were regressed on persistent psychosis as well as the baseline or 6-month value of the outcome, in order to evaluate the effect of persistent psychosis on changes in the outcome variables, rather than mean differences. Variance in outcomes accounted for by persistent psychosis was expressed in terms of change in Nagelkerke’s R-squared when persistent psychosis was added to the baseline model.

Stepwise regression models were used to compare the variance in outcomes accounted for by persistent psychosis and diagnosis. Diagnosis was dichotomized into schizophrenia vs other psychotic disorders. In 1 set of models, persistent psychosis and baseline or 6-month values of the outcome were added to the model in step 1. Diagnosis was added in step 2. In a second set of models, diagnosis and baseline or 6-month values of the outcome were added to the model in step 1, and persistent psychosis was added in step 2. The incremental variance explained by the step 2 variable was expressed in terms of change in Nagelkerke’s R-squared.

To test the robustness of our findings, we conducted sensitivity analyses using alternative definitions of persistent psychosis (symptoms present for ≥75% or ≥50% of observed days). We also performed subgroup analyses examining persistent psychosis separately for individuals with schizophrenia/schizoaffective disorder vs other psychotic disorders, to determine whether predictors and outcomes differed by diagnostic category.

Finally, a tetrachoric correlation test was used to evaluate the association between persistent psychosis and diagnostic shifts.

Sociodemographic and Clinical Characteristics of the Cohort at 25 Years

During the 25-year follow-up interval, 89 participants died, 130 were lost to follow-up for reasons other than death, and 307 were re-assessed. The right half of Supplementary Table S3 reports results of a comparison of the outcome subsample and the 130 individuals who were lost to follow-up. Those who were lost were 1.9 years older and were less likely to be White (66.9% vs 79.8%).

Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of those with persistent psychosis:

Out of the 526 individuals with available data (mean age = 27.4 ± 9.4 years, males = 56.8%, schizophrenia/schizoaffective disorder diagnosis at baseline = 30.0%), 101 (19.2%) exhibited persistent psychosis during first 4 years after treatment onset as previously defined (Table 1). Sensitivity analyses using alternative definitions of persistent psychosis yielded similar results. Our sensitivity analyses revealed that the distribution of psychosis burden in the sample was bimodal, with a concentration of individuals at the extremes—either very high or very low proportions of days with psychosis—and fewer individuals in intermediate ranges. This distributional pattern explains the relatively modest incremental samples captured by lowering the threshold from 90% to 75% (additional 6.7%, 136 individuals (25.9%) were classified as having persistent psychosis) and from 75% to 50% (additional 7.4%, 175 individuals (33.3%) met criteria) (Supplementary Tables S1 and S2).

In univariate analyses of baseline variables, persistent psychosis was significantly more likely given longer DUP, more severe negative symptoms, lower family socioeconomic status, lower education level, lower cognitive performance, worse premorbid and baseline global functioning, role function, and social functioning, and greater BMI. Individuals with persistent psychosis were more likely to have been on public assistance before their first admission. Also, individuals who presented with persistent psychosis were more likely to have a baseline diagnosis of schizophrenia or schizoaffective disorder, and less likely to have a baseline diagnosis of bipolar disorder (Table 1). Additionally, individuals with a family history of an affective disorder were less likely to experience persistent psychosis.

Notably, the category of persistent psychosis was not redundant with diagnosis. Only 1 in 3 individuals with a diagnosis of schizophrenia or schizoaffective disorder experienced persistent psychosis (n = 52, 33.1% of those with a diagnosis of schizophrenia or schizoaffective disorder). Approximately half of those individuals in the persistent psychosis group had other diagnoses (n = 49, 48.5% of those with persistent psychosis). Furthermore, persistent psychosis was not confounded by antipsychotic treatment. Notably, the occurrence of persistent psychosis differed by diagnostic category. Among individuals with a baseline diagnosis of schizophrenia or schizoaffective disorder (n = 158), 33.1% (n = 52) experienced persistent psychosis, compared to 13.3% (n = 49) of those with other psychotic disorders (n = 368). However, it’s important to note that persistent psychosis was not limited to schizophrenia spectrum disorders, as approximately half (48.5%) of those with persistent psychosis had diagnoses other than schizophrenia or schizoaffective disorder at baseline. Individuals in the persistent psychosis group were prescribed antipsychotics for 55% of days observed in the 4 years following first admission, whereas those without persistent psychosis were prescribed antipsychotics for only 40% of days (P < .01).

In our longitudinal analyses, 307 participants (76% of survivors) had data 25 years after the initial treatment onset. Persistent psychosis was not associated with either death (P = .83) or attrition for other reasons (P = .62). We did not observe any statistically significant difference between individuals who were followed up through the 25-year endpoint and those who dropped out, except for age and race, with older individuals of self-reported races other than White being more likely to drop out (Table 2) (Supplementary Table S1).

At endpoint, 25 years after the initial treatment onset, in univariable analyses, individuals who had had persistent psychosis during the initial 4 years after treatment onset were significantly less likely to be in remission or recovery, were less likely to be economically and residentially independent, and had worse global, role, and social functioning, and social skills. Additionally, individuals with persistent psychosis had more severe symptoms at 25-year follow-up in all domains (Supplementary Table S2).

Baseline Predictors of Persistent Psychosis

In the multivariable model, low premorbid cognitive performance (odds ratio [OR] = 2.08, 95% CI, 1.05-4.12), lower GAF (OR = 1.59, 95% CI, 1.16-2.13) at first admission, poorer role function (OR = 1.49, 95% CI, 1.03-2.16)) and worse social function (OR = 1.52, 95% CI, 1.03-2.22) 6 months after initial hospitalization independently predicted persistent psychosis. These predictors collectively accounted for 22% of the variance in persistent psychosis status. The model had an AUC of 0.78.

Next, we studied these associations separately for individuals with a baseline diagnosis of schizophrenia vs those with other psychotic disorders. The association between low baseline cognitive performance and persistent psychosis was more pronounced in individuals with schizophrenia (n = 207, 32.9% with persistent psychosis; OR = 4.22, 95% CI, 1.54-11.54), but not significant in those with other psychotic disorders (n = 368, 13.3% with persistent psychosis). Low social functioning was also a stronger predictor of persistent psychosis in schizophrenia (OR = 0.45, 95% CI, 0.27-0.76) and not significant among those with other psychotic disorders. Alternatively, in individuals who had psychotic disorders other than schizophrenia, lower role function in the QLS was significantly associated with persistent psychosis status OR = 0.49, 95% CI, 0.32-0.76), as were lower scores in the GAF at study entry (OR = 0.60, 95% CI, 0.40-0.90) (Table 3).

We then examined predictors of persistent psychosis separately for individuals with baseline schizophrenia/schizoaffective disorder vs those with other psychotic disorders. Among those with schizophrenia/schizoaffective disorder (n = 158), the strongest predictors were low premorbid cognitive performance (OR = 4.22, 95% CI, 1.54-11.54) and worse social functioning (OR = 0.45, 95% CI, 0.27-0.76). For individuals with other psychotic disorders (n = 368), significant predictors included lower Global Assessment of Functioning (OR = 0.60, 95% CI, 0.40-0.90) and poorer role functioning (OR = 0.49, 95% CI, 0.32-0.76) (Table 3). This suggests that while the predictive model has good accuracy across diagnostic categories (AUC = 0.78), the specific factors that best predict persistent psychosis may vary somewhat by diagnosis.

Sensitivity analyses, in which persistent psychosis was defined as 75% and 50% of days in a psychotic episode, are reported in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2. Predictors of persistent psychosis remained largely the same and explained a similar proportion of variance in persistent psychosis.

Importantly, persistent psychosis remained a significant predictor of poor outcomes even after controlling for baseline diagnosis, suggesting that the presence of persistent symptoms during the early illness phase has prognostic value beyond diagnostic category.

Association of Persistent Psychosis Status vs Primary Psychotic Disorder Diagnosis with Clinical Outcomes at 25 Years

Aside from public assistance and residential independence, persistent psychosis explained more variance than primary diagnosis in all 25-year outcomes examined. When both persistent psychosis and diagnosis were included in prediction models, persistent psychosis explained significant additional variance beyond diagnosis for all outcomes except public assistance. In contrast, diagnosis only explained additional variance beyond persistent psychosis for avolition, GAF, residential independence, remission, and recovery (Table 4). Persistent psychosis explained more than twice the variance in disorganization symptoms at 25 years compared to diagnosis. These findings suggest that early persistent psychosis may be a more powerful predictor of long-term outcome than diagnostic category for several important clinical domains. Of the 368 individuals with diagnoses of bipolar disorder, major depressive disorder, substance-induced psychosis, and other psychosis, 117 shifted to a diagnosis of schizophrenia or schizoaffective disorder by the 25-year follow-up. Persistent psychosis was significantly associated with such shifts (rtet = 0.51, P < .01). Trajectories of GAF by persistent psychosis groups show significant differences emerging up to 5 years prior to the first treatment, with those who went on to develop persistent psychosis to show persistently poorer function over their premorbid phase and course of illness (Figure 1).

Discussion

Approximately 1 in 5 individuals in this county-wide epidemiologic sample experienced persistent psychosis over 4 years following first hospitalization. Using measures of cognitive performance and 3 measures of function within the initial 6 months of treatment, this model was able to predict with 78% accuracy the individuals who went on to develop persistent psychosis. Furthermore, function was clearly deteriorated during the premorbid phase in those who went on to develop persistent psychosis. Altogether, these data challenge the simple notion of a “critical window” where short-term interventions alone can alter long-term trajectories. Instead, our findings suggest that some individuals demonstrate markers of a more severe illness course from the earliest stages, which may require sustained interventions. The predictors of persistent psychosis are not easily modifiable through clinical intervention at the point of first treatment contact. However, early identification of individuals at risk for persistent psychosis remains clinically valuable, as it can guide the deployment of sustained and intensive interventions aimed at mitigating the long-term impact of the illness, including comprehensive psychosocial approaches and optimized pharmacological treatment. Persistent psychosis early in the course of illness was associated with various adverse outcomes more than 2 decades later, including limited economic and residential independence, impaired psychosocial function, more severe symptoms, and diminished prospects of remission and recovery. Persistent psychosis was a more potent predictor of these outcomes than a diagnosis of schizophrenia or schizoaffective disorder. Furthermore, persistent psychosis was associated with diagnostic shifts from other psychotic disorders to schizophrenia or schizoaffective disorder over the course of the illness.

The incidence of persistent psychosis in 19.2% if individuals in the early phase of psychotic illness found in this study is similar to other reports in the literature.5,27 In a first episode study using medical record data, the incidence of treatment resistance from illness onset was 23%,3 whereas only a relative minority of individuals who went up to develop treatment resistant schizophrenia had shown response to antipsychotic treatment before developing treatment resistance.3,28 In our work, rather than treatment resistance, the phenomenon of interest was persistent psychosis, which could reflect a lack of adequate treatment, non-adherence to treatment, or treatment resistance. Future studies that include detailed information on medication adherence or that prospectively assess response to assured treatment delivery (such as with long-acting injectable antipsychotics) could help differentiate persistent psychosis due to provision of adequate treatment, treatment non-adherence, and treatment resistance. This distinction is particularly relevant given the absence of clearly modifiable risk factors identified in our study.29 The finding that global functioning, low cognitive performance, and lower levels of social and role functioning at baseline predicted persistent psychosis points to a pattern of early functional impairment in this group. While premorbid social adjustment alone was not independently predictive in our multivariate model, the trajectory analysis of GAF scores clearly shows that individuals who developed persistent psychosis had poorer functioning well before their first treatment. This suggests that functional deficits emerge gradually during development rather than representing a sudden decline, specifically at illness onset.30

Our sensitivity analyses using alternative thresholds (75% and 50%) yielded similar predictor sets and comparable proportions of variance explained, suggesting that the persistence of psychosis operates along a severity dimension rather than demarcating a discrete subgroup. We retain the term “persistent psychosis” for clinical utility—to identify individuals at the severe end of this dimension who may benefit from intensified interventions—while acknowledging that the underlying structure is likely dimensional. From a clinical standpoint, individuals meeting the 75% and 50% thresholds also experienced substantial psychosis burden and may similarly warrant intensified intervention.

There is no question that persistent psychosis should be addressed to improve quality of life and mitigate associated medical and safety risks. It is less clear; however, whether the adverse long-term outcomes observed in individuals with persistent psychosis could be modified through specific treatment approaches. While evidence supports the effectiveness of comprehensive treatment strategies, including appropriate use of clozapine for treatment-resistant psychosis, our observational data cannot directly address whether such interventions would have altered the trajectories we observed. The presence of poorer functioning even before treatment initiation raises the possibility that both persistent psychosis and worse long-term outcomes may partly reflect underlying illness severity rather than having a simple causal relationship. Despite the clear benefit of early intervention programs for psychosis—which emphasize the treatment of residual symptoms and a focus on functionality—in the period surrounding illness onset,2 these gains are mostly lost after the specialized treatment is discontinued.31 Similarly, several factors already present at treatment onset, including worse levels of functioning and cognitive performance, suggest that individuals with persistent psychosis may start with greater deficits, which only persist over the long-term. We cannot rule out the possibility that both the persistence of psychosis and worse long-term outcomes may be expressions of the underlying illness and do not directly influence each other. Notably, our results are consistent with findings from an umbrella review of candidate predictors of key outcomes within and across mental disorders, where lack of remission in people with schizophrenia was significantly predicted by lower educational level, worse functioning, and more severe illness-related symptoms.32 Regardless, addressing persistent psychosis should be a treatment priority, not only in the context of early intervention services but also continuously over the course of illness. Furthermore, effective psychosocial interventions, such as social skills training, cognitive remediation, and vocational rehabilitation, can be deployed to directly target some of the poor outcomes associated with persistent psychosis.33-35

The ability to identify individuals at high risk for persistent psychosis from their initial clinical presentation has important implications for service delivery. Our findings suggest that a combination of baseline measures, including cognitive function, global functioning, and social/role functioning, can identify this high-risk group with reasonable accuracy. This early identification could guide clinical decision-making regarding treatment intensity and duration, potentially enabling more personalized approaches to care. For example, individuals predicted to be at high risk for persistent psychosis might benefit from more intensive and sustained multidisciplinary interventions from the earliest stages, rather than transitioning to standard care after a brief period of specialized early intervention. Such an approach would align with the growing recognition that schizophrenia and related disorders comprise heterogeneous subgroups requiring different treatment strategies and intensity of services.

These results should be interpreted in the context of several limitations. First, in order to have outcomes at 25 years after initial treatment onset, recruitment for this study had to start several decades ago, and the treatment of early-phase psychosis has evolved since then. With the advent of early intervention services as the new standard of care in psychosis36 the outcomes 25 years hence may be different. Second, this dataset does not contain granular information about treatment adherence, not allowing to discriminate between persistent psychosis as a result of non-adherence and treatment resistance. Third, given the longitudinal design, there was attrition in our sample; however, the drop-out rate was smaller than in comparable cohorts,37 and we did not observe any major differences between dropouts and completers in baseline characteristics. Fourth, racial categories in clinical research frequently serve as proxies for structural disadvantage rather than inherent biological differences. Our findings should be interpreted with this context in mind. Notably, race was not retained as a significant predictor in our multivariable model. Fifth, our final predictive model retained only 4 clinically interpretable variables. While we acknowledge that modest correlations among predictors may partly reflect measurement error rather than true construct independence, the parsimonious nature of the model and the consistency of predictors across alternative threshold definitions provide reassurance regarding stability. External replication in independent samples remains an important next step.

In conclusion, this study highlights that a sizeable proportion of individuals with psychosis will have persistent symptoms during the early phase of illness. This subgroup of individuals appears to have greater impairment in function prior to treatment onset, which persists throughout their illness course. The ability to identify these individuals early using baseline clinical measures could facilitate more targeted and sustained interventions. Despite lack of clearly modifiable factors that could reduce the incidence of persistent psychosis, various treatment approaches may help mitigate its impact, including appropriate consideration of clozapine for treatment resistance and comprehensive psychosocial interventions to address functional impairments. Taken together, these data emphasize the importance of both early identification of those at risk for persistent psychosis and the need for sustained, intensive interventions throughout the course of illness for this subgroup.
