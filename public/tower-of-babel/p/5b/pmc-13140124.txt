Understanding the Phenotypic Heterogeneity Within the Sporadic Creutzfeldt–Jakob Disease MV1 Subtype
Authors: Satish K Nemani, Leonardo M Cortez, Jennifer Myskiw, Ignazio Cali, Stephanie A Booth, Gerard H Jansen, Valerie L Sim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe MV1 subtype of sporadic Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease (sCJD) is one of the least studied. Cases are defined by the presence of the methionine/valine (MV) polymorphism at codon 129 of the PRNP gene and a type 1 immunoblot pattern of pathological prion protein (PrPD), where the unglycosylated PrPD fragment migrates at ~21 kDa (T21). Because the originally described MV1 cases had T21 plus brain pathology indistinguishable from MM1 cases (small vacuoles), the MV1 subtype has historically been grouped with MM1. However, the recent identification of MV1 cases with immunoblot and pathological features similar to the VV1 subtype (T21–20 doublet, larger vacuoles, ballooned neurons) has raised the possibility that the MV1 subtype is more heterogeneous than originally proposed. Here, we report three MV1 cases that further reveal the heterogeneity of this subtype. All cases were atypical in that they had persistent T20 fragments, in isolation or combination with T21 or T19, when typed at pH 6.9, a finding that would have been missed if typing were undertaken at pH 8.0 only. Fragment proportions differed in different brain regions, and further examination found that the cases had mixed MM1/VV1 features, predominant VV1‐like characteristics or features of the MV2C subtype. Two cases had small to intermediate‐sized vacuoles and synaptic PrP staining. The third case had intermediate vacuolation, weak synaptic staining and coarse PrP deposits. We also describe, for the first time, the application of asymmetric‐flow field‐flow fractionation as a novel tool to discriminate sCJD subtypes based on size distributions of protease‐resistant and protease‐sensitive PrPD. Overall, we propose that MV1 is a more heterogeneous subtype than previously appreciated, potentially existing along a spectrum from MM1‐like to VV1‐like phenotypes. Importantly, this biochemical heterogeneity may be underrepresented when subtyping is done at pH 8.0, so we recommend CJD subtyping be performed at pH 6.9 to avoid missing atypical or mixed cases.Keywords: Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease (CJD), neuropathology, phenotype, prion disease, protein conformation, strain typing

Introduction

Sporadic Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease (sCJD) is a rapidly progressive fatal neurodegenerative disease associated with the accumulation of disease‐associated misfolded prion protein (PrPD). Different conformations of PrPD can occur, resulting in a wide spectrum of clinical and histopathological variants [1]. Attempts to correlate the structural differences in PrPD with the clinical and pathological profiles have traditionally relied on the following two PrPD determinants: (1) methionine (M) or valine (V) polymorphism at codon 129 and (2) electrophoretic profile after proteolytic cleavage by proteinase K (PK), which yields either a type 1 (T1) or type 2 (T2) pattern. While the combination of these two determinants can theoretically generate six possible sCJD subtypes (MM1, MV1, VV1, VV2, MM2 and MV2), this is an imperfect classification system, as MM1 and MV1 have been proposed to be the same subtype, MM2 can exist with large cortical vacuoles (MM2C) or prominent thalamic atrophy (MM2T), and MV2 has been further expanded to include cases with Kuru plaques in the cerebellum (MV2K) or coarse aggregates in the cortex (MV2C) [2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7]. In addition, this subtyping approach may lump together heterogeneous cases, and subsequent studies have found cases that contain mixed types 1 and 2 and even novel PrPD characteristics in cases that share the same polymorphism and electrophoretic mobility [8, 9, 10].

The electrophoretic profile seen after PK digestion is defined based on the SDS‐PAGE mobility of the unglycosylated PK‐digested (resPrPD) bands, with T1 migrating at 21 kDa (T21) and T2 at 19 kDa (T19) at a buffer pH of 6.9 [11]. While the size of the fragment is thought to relate to the initial PrPD structure, there are reports of cases producing differently sized fragments under different pH conditions during PK digestion. For example, most MM1, MV1 and VV1 cases generate a 20 kDa (T20) fragment if digestion is performed at pH 8.0 [11, 12]. Conversely, others have reported rare VV1 and MM1 cases where the 21 kDa fragment is present at pH 8.0 [9, 10, 13]. Thus, not only the fragment size but also its dependence on buffer pH should be taken into account when using this method of subtyping.

Further complicating the use of fragment size as a typing strategy is the recent identification of T20 fragments in atypical MV1 cases [10, 14]. In the current classifications, MM1 and MV1 are considered the same subtype, MM (MV)1, based on fragment size, clinical presentation and pathology, although it is important to recognise that only eight MV1 cases were included with 203 MM1 cases to create this original description [3]. In 2022, Gelpi et al. identified six MV1 cases with atypical clinical and neuropathological features that more closely resembled VV1 cases, leading the authors to propose that these cases were a new sCJD subtype, which they called VM1. Another case mimicking this VM1 subtype has since been reported by Cracco et al. [10]. Given the rarity of MV1 cases (in a large study of 497 clinically confirmed sCJD cases, only 8% were MV1 [15]), there may be even more heterogeneity within the MV1 group that has yet to be described.

These recently named VM1 cases have clinical, histopathological and PrPD characteristics distinct from classic MV1 cases [10, 14]. Classic MV1 cases are more often associated with periodic sharp wave complexes (PSWC) on electroencephalogram and have a disease duration ranging from 4.5 to 9 months [3, 10]. In contrast, PSWC have not been observed in the VM1 cases, and the disease duration is longer, from 10 to 24 months. Histopathologically, classic MV1 is characterised by small vacuoles and prominent synaptic staining, whereas VM1 displays slightly larger vacuoles (‘medium‐size’), weaker or more delicate synaptic staining and the presence of cortical balloon‐shaped neurons. Regarding PrPD fragments, MV1 predominantly contains T21 after PK digestion at pH 6.9, whereas VM1 includes both T21 and T20 or T20 only [14]. Additionally, while both subtypes exhibit 12 kDa unglycosylated C‐terminal fragments (U‐CTF), these fragments are present in significantly higher proportions in VM1 [14].

While it is not clear what drives the phenotypic differences between MV1 and VM1 cases, one possibility is an effect of allele proportion. We have previously observed this type of allele effect in MV2 cases, where higher levels of the M allele were associated with the MV2C phenotype, which is indistinguishable from MM2, and higher levels of the V allele were linked to the MV2K phenotype, which resembles VV2 [6]. However, in MV1 cases that present as MM1, levels of each allele are equally represented, suggesting that any M allele effect is dominant over any V allele effect in these type 1 cases [6]. For the recently described VM1 cases, it has been proposed that high levels of the V allele overcome the M allele effect and drive the overall PrPD conformation and phenotype towards VV1 [14]. This raises the question of whether more MV1 subtypes may exist along a spectrum of varying allele proportions.

Here, we present a detailed clinical, histopathological and biochemical characterisation of three MV1 cases obtained from the Prion Diseases Section of the National Microbiology Laboratory (NML), Public Health Agency of Canada (PHAC). We initially identified these cases as being atypical based on the presence of T20 fragments on immunoblot. We went on to characterise the clinical, pathological and PrPD profile features of each case and found that our MV cases had features of both MM1 and VV1 subtypes, suggesting that MV1 cases are indeed part of a heterogeneous spectrum. In addition, one case had clinical, pathological and PrPD profile features suggesting a T2 pattern, indicating that T2 structures, even if co‐existing with T1 structures, may affect both clinical and pathological features of the disease [8, 9]. In addition to standard analytical techniques, we used asymmetric‐flow field‐flow fractionation (AF4) to separate PK‐sensitive and PK‐resistant PrPD oligomers of distinct sizes and correlate with subtype. This is the first time this technique has been applied to human prion disease.

Materials and Methods

Brain Tissues

Frozen and fixed brain tissue from the three atypical MV cases were obtained from the Prion Diseases Section of the National Microbiology Laboratory (NML), Public Health Agency of Canada (PHAC) in Winnipeg, Manitoba. Additionally, 10 pure sCJD control cases (3 MM1, 1 MV1, 3 MM2 and 3 VV1 cases) defined according to common sCJD subtype classification were obtained from NML/PHAC and the National Prion Disease Pathology Surveillance Centre, Cleveland, Ohio.

Clinical Case Descriptions

Clinical histories were obtained by nurses at the CJD Surveillance System of Canada, both from interviews with the patients' family, attending physicians and from medical chart review, according to a standardised questionnaire. This questionnaire included the date of symptom onset, duration of symptoms and date of death. In addition, the presence of MRI DWI hyperintensity (in cortex, basal ganglia or both) and EEG periodic sharp wave complexes or triphasic complexes were captured. The date of onset of each clinical symptom was recorded. Clinical symptoms included rapidly progressive dementia, memory problems, anxiety/depression, personality changes, agitation/irritability, paranoia, other psychiatric symptoms, visual hallucinations, double vision, blurry vision, bradykinesia, tremor, rigidity, other extra‐pyramidal/parkinsonism signs, pyramidal signs, spasticity, myoclonus, gait ataxia, slurred speech/dysarthria/mutism, incoordination/loss of balance, insomnia/parasomnia/REM sleep disorder, dysphasia/dyslexia, language comprehension, paraesthesia/numbness, allodynia, hypersalivation, pseudobulbar affect, orthostatic dizziness, posturing and corticobasal syndrome. Exposure risk was captured by obtaining a history of surgical procedures, blood or tissue transplant, receipt of growth hormone from a human source and cervid consumption.

Reagents and Antibodies

Tris‐buffered saline (TBS), 15% Criterion Tris–HCl polyacrylamide gels, tris/glycine buffer, Tween 20, β–mercaptoethanol, dithiothreitol (DTT), Laemmli sample buffer and nonfat dry milk were procured from Bio‐Rad Laboratories (Hercules, California, USA). Benzonase and polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) (Immobilion‐P and Immobilon‐FL) were purchased from Millipore Sigma (Burlington, Massachusetts, USA). 1 M Tris–HCl pH 8.0, N‐Lauroylsarcosine sodium salt solution, 8 M Guanidine‐hydrochloride (GdnHCl), phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) and Proteinase K (PK) were obtained from Sigma‐Aldrich. NuPAGE 4%–12% Bis‐Tris gradient gels, Nu PAGE 12% Bis‐Tris gels, NuPAGE MES SDS running buffer (20×), silver staining kit, SuperSignal West Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate, ethanol and methanol were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc. (Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). Attophos substrate was procured from Promega (Madison, Wisconsin, USA). The following antibodies were used: 3F4 (recognises human PrP residues 106–110) [16]; 12F10 (recognises PrP residues 142–160, Cayman chemicals, Ann Arbor, Michigan, USA); Tohoku‐2 polyclonal antibody (recognises PrP residues 97–103) [17]; SAF70 (recognises PrP residues 156–162, Cayman chemicals, Ann Arbor, Michigan, USA), secondary antibodies: horseradish peroxidase‐conjugated goat anti‐mouse IgG and goat anti‐rabbit IgG horseradish peroxidase (Bio‐Rad Laboratories [Hercules, California, USA]), alkaline phosphatase (AP)‐goat anti‐mouse IgG and goat anti‐rabbit IgG (Promega [Madison, Wisconsin, USA]).

Molecular Genetics

DNA was isolated from frozen brain tissues, and genotyping of the PRNP coding region was performed as previously described [2]. In brief, PCR was performed on genomic DNA in duplicate with primers specific to the PRNP gene. The resulting amplicons were sequenced with multiple primers to ensure overlapping coverage. The sequencing data were aligned and compared to reference sequences to determine codon 129, as well as identify any mutations or insertions/deletions. This approach is sufficient to exclude any rare PRNP mutations with confidence. Consent for genetic testing only covered testing for the PRNP gene, so other genetic causes of dementia were not tested; however, a detailed neuropathological analysis was performed to determine co‐pathologies.

Histopathology

Histopathology and immunohistochemistry were performed essentially as described previously [8, 12], except that 12F10 was used for immunostaining in this study.

PK‐Resistant PrPD (resPrPD) Analysis

Brain homogenates (BHs) (10% w/v) were prepared as previously described [11]. Briefly, grey matter from the frontal cortex and thalamus was homogenised by resuspending in LB100 (100 mM Tris–HCL, 100 mM NaCl, 10 mM EDTA, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.5% NP‐40) prepared at two different pH levels, 6.9 and 8.0. BHs from both pH levels were centrifuged at 1000 × g for 10 min, and supernatant (S1) was collected. S1 was treated with 27 U/mL (when using LB100, pH 6.9) and 10 U/mL (LB100, pH 8.0) proteinase K (PK), respectively, and incubated at 37°C for 1 h. Protease digestion was stopped by adding 4 mM PMSF. Samples were mixed with equal volumes of 2× Laemmli buffer, boiled for 10 min and incubated with 8‐fold prechilled methanol overnight at −20°C. The following day, the samples were centrifuged at 18,200 × g for 30 min, pellets were resuspended in 1× sample buffer by sonication and subjected to immunoblotting. Protein samples (brain tissue equivalent 0.1–3 mg of wet tissue) were separated in 4%–12% or 12% NuPAGE Bis‐Tris gradient gels and blotted into Immobilon‐FL membranes for 1 h at 100 V, blocked with 5% nonfat dry milk in TBS‐T (1 × TBS with 0.1% tween 20) and probed with the indicated antibodies. Immunoblots were visualised by ImageQuant LAS 4000 as described by the manufacturer.

Conformational Stability Immunoassay

Conformational stability immunoassay (CSI) was performed according to previous protocols [12, 18, 19] with slight modifications. Aliquots of BH (10% w/v) prepared in LB100 (pH 8.0) were centrifuged at 1000 × g for 10 min at 4°C, and the S1 fractions were collected. A 50 μL aliquot of S1 was diluted with an equal volume of GdnHCl to obtain final GdnHCl concentrations ranging from 0 to 3.5 M, and samples were incubated for 1.5 h at 22°C. GdnHCl was subsequently removed by incubating each sample with an excess of 8‐fold prechilled methanol overnight at −20°C, followed by centrifugation at 18,200 × g for 30 min. Pellets were resuspended in 50 μL LB100 (pH 8.0) by sonication. Each aliquot was digested with 10 U/mL PK for 1 h at 37°C, and the reaction was terminated by the addition of 4 mM PMSF. Samples were denatured and loaded onto 15% precast Tris–HCl gels or 12% NuPAGE Bis‐Tris gels. PrP amounts at different GdnHCl concentrations were measured by ImageQuant TL software. The conformational stability curves (i.e., fraction of remaining PrPD as a function of GdnHCl concentration) for unglycosylated PrPD were fitted to a sigmoidal equation using GraphPad Prism. The mean [GdnHCl]½ values ± standard error of the mean (SEM) were calculated and compared between histotype case groups.

Proteinase K Titration Assay

The Proteinase K (PK) titration assay was performed as described previously with slight modification [9]. 30 μL of S1 from BHs prepared at pH 6.9 were treated with different concentrations of PK (0.625, 1.25, 2.5, 5, 10, 20, 40, 80, 160 U/mL) for 1 h at 37°C. Additionally, S1 was incubated with 160 U/mL of PK for 12 h at 37°C. The PK digestion was terminated with 4 mM PMSF; equal aliquots of 2X sample buffer were added, and samples were boiled for 10 min, then subjected to immunoblotting with 3F4 and Tohoku‐2. Densitometry values for the unglycosylated resPrPD fragments at each PK concentration were best fitted using a one‐phase decay model, and PK½ index values were determined and compared across the three cases and brain regions.

Asymmetric‐Flow Field‐Flow Fractionation

Asymmetric‐flow field‐flow fractionation (AF4) was carried out as previously described with slight modifications [20]. 1.5 mL S1 was treated with 75 U/mL benzonase for 1 h at 4°C and centrifuged at 150,000 × g for 1 h. S2 was discarded, and P2 was solubilised in 150 μL of solubilisation buffer (50 mM Tris, pH 7.4; 150 mM NaCl; 10 mM EDTA; 2% Sarkosyl [Sigma]) and incubated for 1 h at 4°C. Solubilised P2 was then centrifuged at 5000 × g for 10 min at 4°C, supernatant was collected, and 350 μg total protein was passed through AF4 on a Wyatt Eclipse system using 50 mM HEPES pH 7.4 (containing 50 mM sodium chloride and 0.05% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) to minimise interaction of proteins with the channel membrane) as the running buffer. The channel was 26.5 cm long and 400 μm high, constructed with a trapezoidal spacer of maximal width 21 mm at the inlet, and lined with a 10 kDa cutoff polyethersulfone membrane at the accumulation wall. Samples were focused for 4 min and then eluted at a channel flow of 0.5 mL/min with constant cross‐flow for the first 10 min, decreasing from 1.5 to 0.35 mL/min in the following 15 min, from 0.35 to 0 mL/min in the next 30 min, and run with no cross‐flow for the last 10 min. Fractions of 0.2 mL were collected.

Hydrodynamic Radius Measurements From In‐Line Dynamic Light Scattering

Multiangle light scattering (MALS) and dynamic light scattering (DLS) values were collected simultaneously at a wavelength of 662 nm using a DAWN HELEOS II detector (Wyatt Technology), placed in‐line with the AF4. DLS data were collected every 2 s at a 140.1° scattering angle for the determination of the hydrodynamic radius (Rh) of the AF4 eluting particles. Fitting the data to an autocorrelation function was performed using the cumulants model. Data analysis was performed with ASTRA 8.1.2 software (Wyatt Technology). The injection of 350 μg of protein in each AF4 run was confirmed by monitoring UV absorbance in real time with an in‐line Shimadzu SPD‐20A Prominence UV/VIS Detector. The collected fractions were then immunoblotted for PrP, and elution profiles were generated by plotting PrP intensity from each fraction against the average R h of the particles present in that fraction, with the area under the curve from the entire run normalised to 1 to facilitate profile comparisons among subtypes.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical significance was assessed with the Student's t‐test or ANOVA, where indicated, supported by GraphPad Prism 8.0.

Results

Genetic Analysis

Genetic analysis was performed by the Prion Diseases Section of NML/PHAC with patient/family consent obtained through the CJD Surveillance System. Methionine (M)/valine (V) heterozygosity at codon 129 (129MV) of the prion protein (PrP) gene was observed in all the three cases. No other variants, pathogenic or otherwise, were found in the open reading frame of the PrP gene.

Clinical History

Case 1

A male in his late 60s presented with memory problems that rapidly progressed 3 months into the course and became associated with anxiety, personality changes, agitation, visual hallucinations and slow, repetitive speech. Two months later, he developed tremors, incoordination and impaired comprehension. He underwent extensive investigations, which included a head CT and workup for infectious, inflammatory and other causes, with inconclusive results. Multifocal cortical restricted diffusion was present on MRI, and no periodic sharp wave complexes were seen on EEG. CSF 14‐3‐3 and total Tau were positive. EP‐QuIC on CSF was not available pre‐mortem, but was positive on brain material post‐mortem. His total disease duration was 10 months.

The potential for iatrogenic causes was deemed to be negligible; there was no history of any surgical procedures, no history of blood receipt or organ tissue transplants, no receipt of growth/FSH hormone therapy from a human source, and no cervid exposures (no hunting, no occupational cervid exposures and no game consumption).

Case 2

A 50‐year‐old male presented with anxiety, depression, personality changes, paranoia and tactile hallucinations. Five months later, he developed rapidly progressive dementia, followed by gait ataxia, visual hallucinations, mumbling speech and then insomnia. Hypersalivation was reported near the end. An MRI of the brain showed widespread cortical restricted diffusion. An EEG showed 7 triphasic complexes but without periodicity. CSF 14‐3‐3 and total Tau were positive. EP‐QuIC on CSF was positive. There was a family history of Alzheimer's disease. His total disease duration was 10 months.

The potential for iatrogenic causes was deemed to be negligible; there was no history of any surgical procedures, no history of blood receipt, no history of organ or tissue transplant, no receipt of growth/FSH hormone therapy from a human source and no hunting or occupational cervid exposures. Deer meat was occasionally consumed in the past (less than once per year), with origin outside of CWD‐endemic areas.

Case 3

A female in her 60s presented with the following symptoms at onset: bilateral foot paraesthesia, gait ataxia, parkinsonism, incoordination and myoclonus. Memory problems and tremors were noted 2 months later. Bradykinesia was specifically documented in her chart 7 months after onset. An MRI brain showed restricted diffusion in a cortical ribboning pattern. EP‐QuIC on CSF was positive. 14‐3‐3 and total Tau were negative. She opted for medical assistance in dying (MAID) 15 months after onset.

The potential for iatrogenic causes was deemed to be negligible; there was no history of blood receipt/donation or organ tissue transplants, no receipt of growth/FSH hormone therapy from a human source, no cervid exposures (no hunting, no occupational cervid exposures and no game consumption). There was a history of retinal detachment surgery 23 years before symptom onset. There were no other surgical procedures involving high‐infectivity tissues.

Histopathological Evaluation

Case 1

Haematoxylin‐Eosin Staining

Spongiosis was mostly microvacuolar, and in most affected layers, larger vacuoles were present, with rare coalescent vacuoles. In the most affected regions, moderate neuronal loss and gliosis were found. In the striatum, thalamus and molecular layer of the cerebellum, SD was mild (Figures 1C,E and 2 and Table 1). The hippocampus had some neuronal loss, senile plaques, neurons with neurofibrillary degeneration and occasional granulovacuolar degeneration. There was no neuronal loss, florid or multicentric plaques in the cerebellum. SD was characterised by variably sized vacuoles (Figure 1A).

PrP staining revealed a fine punctate to focal synaptic pattern throughout the cerebral cortex, basal ganglia and cerebellum (Figure 1B,D,F).

Intermediate Alzheimer neuropathological changes were present (NIA‐AA: A2B2C2). There were no other tauopathies, no Lewy body pathology and no TDP43 pathology.

Case 2

Haematoxylin‐Eosin

Severe SD was pronounced in the frontal convexity and visual cortex (Figures 1G and 2 and Table 1). In severely affected regions and moderately affected regions, there was moderate to extensive neuronal loss and gliosis. Moderate SD was detected in the temporal cortex, entorhinal cortex, striatum, thalamus and cerebellum (Figures 1I,K and 2 and Table 1). There were no senile, florid or multicentric plaques. SD was characterised by variably sized vacuoles (Figure 1G).

PrP Immunochemistry

Synaptic staining was seen in the cerebral cortex, basal ganglia and cerebellum (Figure 1H,J,L).

Co‐Pathologies

There were no Alzheimer type neuropathological changes (NIA‐AA: A0B0C0) and no Lewy body pathology, no tauopathy and no TDP43 pathology.

Case 3

Haematoxylin‐Eosin

Moderate SD was seen in the visual, entorhinal and focally in the parietal cortices, with mild SD in the frontal, temporal cortices, basal ganglia and cerebellum (Figures 1M,O,Q and 2 and Table 1). The striatum was relatively spared. Senile and diffuse Alzheimer type plaques were detected. Additionally, a few neurofibrillary tangles were detected in the parahippocampal cortex. The SD was characterised by variably sized vacuoles and occasional clustered/coalescent vacuoles.

PrP staining was very faint and diffuse in the frontal and parietal cortices and cerebellum (Figure 1N,R). More focal coarse perivacuolar staining was seen in a few areas in the basal temporal cortex (Figure S1). The basal ganglia were relatively spared (Figure 1P). The cerebellum showed focal granular deposits (Figure 1R).

Co‐Pathologies

Alzheimer neuropathological changes ‘intermediate’ (NIA‐AA: A3B2C1), no other tauopathy, no Lewy body pathology and no TDP43 pathology.

Detection and Typing of resPrPD

It is well established that unglycosylated resPrPD in MM1 and classical MV1 migrates as T21 when PK digestion is performed at pH 6.9 and as T20 when PK digestion is performed at pH 8.0 [11]. Surprisingly, the unglycosylated resPrPD from the frontal cortex of our three MV cases migrated as T20 at both pH 6.9 and 8.0 (Figure 3A). This was also true for the thalamic sample from Case 3. Unglycosylated resPrPD from the thalamus of Cases 1 and 2 migrated as T21–20 (at pH 6.9) and underwent a shift from T21 to T20 at buffer pH 8.0, as seen in classical MV1 cases [10].

To further subtype the resPrPD, a panel of antibodies was used after PK digestion at pH 6.9 (Figure 3B). As seen previously at this pH with 3F4, the unglycosylated resPrPD from frontal cortex from Cases 1 and 2 had only T20, while the thalamus had predominantly T21 with traces of T20. Case 3 frontal cortex and thalamus had mostly T20 with trace amounts of what appeared to be T19. To confirm whether T19 from our cases was comparable to that seen in classical T2 of MM2, we used Tohoku‐2 antibody, which preferentially binds to T2 resPrPD. While trace amounts of T2 were seen in Cases 1 and 2, significantly higher levels were found in Case 3 and in our MM2 control.

To expand the analysis further, we probed with the C‐terminal antibody SAF70, which detects a smaller 12 kDa unglycosylated C‐terminal fragment (U‐CTF) of resPrPD that can be variably present with different subtypes [21]. This U‐CTF was present in all atypical MV1 and controls, but the intensity of the band varied. The percentage U‐CTF signal relative to unglycosylated PrPD has been used to discriminate CJD subtypes, with higher percentages of ~72% for VV1 and lower percentages of 22% and 2.6% for MM (MV)1 and MM2, respectively [21]. We therefore compared the percentages of U‐CTF from our control MM1, MM2 and VV1 cases with those from our three MV1 cases (Figure 3C,D). The percentages from our controls were comparable to those previously reported (VV1: 71% ± 7.1%, MM1: 19% ± 2.2%, MM2: 4.7% ± 1.1%), but those for our MV1 cases were 62%, 47% and 75%, respectively, with Cases 1 and 3 bearing closest resemblance to VV1 and Case 2 appearing intermediate between VV1 and MM1.

PrPD Conformational Assays

CSIs and PK titration assays were performed in all three cases to assess the structural stability of resPrPD and further discriminate sCJD subtypes. Pure MM1 and MM2 subtypes that are known to have distinct conformations were used as controls. Of note, these experiments were performed at pH 8, so the unglycosylated resPrPD from MM1 runs as T20. For frontal cortex resPrPD samples, Cases 1 and 2 had similar conformational stabilities, with [GdnHCl]½ of 1.25 and 1.4 M, respectively, whereas for Case 3, the [GdnHCl]½ was higher at 2.1 M. Thalamic resPrPD stabilities were similar across Cases 1 and 3 (1.67, 1.75 and 1.9 M, respectively), and closely resembled that of MM1 (Figure 4A, Figure S2A and Table 1).

We then compared the PK resistance of PrPD from each case using a PK titration assay, where we measured the [PK]½ index (concentration of PK required to hydrolyse 50% PrPD) (Figure 4B, Figure S2B and Table 1). For frontal cortex resPrPD samples, Cases 1 and 2 PK resistance curves were comparable to each other, with [PK]½ index values of 7.7 and 11 U/mL, respectively, whereas that from Case 3 was much higher, with a [PK]½ index of 102 U/mL. Interestingly, the pattern was different for PrPD from the thalamus, with Case 3 having the lowest [PK]½ index of 24 U/mL, followed by Cases 1 and 2 at 47 and 81 U/mL, respectively.

Total PrPD and resPrPD Size Distribution

Asymmetric flow field‐flow fractionation (AF4) is a fluid‐based separation technique that has been used to determine the size distributions of PrPD in different types of prion disease [20, 22]. We have previously used it to discriminate between the otherwise indistinguishable strains, Hyper and 263 K, two hamster‐adapted strains [20]. We therefore applied this method to human CJD samples to further discriminate subtype differences. Of note, PrPC is also fractionated by this method, but elutes almost completely in the initial fractions, as demonstrated by fractionations and immunoblots of normal BH (Figure S3).

The size distributions of total PrPD from MM1, MM2, VV1 and the three MV1 cases were evaluated. For MM1, MM2 and VV1 subtypes, distinct distributions of total PrPD were observed (Figure 5A,D). MM1 had the lowest concentration of smaller particles (5–15 nm Rh) and the highest concentration of large particles (44–100 nm Rh). MM2 and VV1 both had predominantly smaller particles, but while MM2 also had fractions containing larger aggregates, VV1 contained fewer aggregates larger than 30 nm Rh. The size distribution profiles of total PrPD for the three MV1 cases displayed some variability (Figure 5B,C,E,F), but Cases 1 and 2 frontal cortex and thalamus had profiles similar to that of MM2. The profile from Case 3 was closer to MM1, with more PrP signal in the larger aggregate distribution.

We previously reported that PrPD becomes partially resistant to PK at an R h of 15 nm and proposed that the ratio of PK‐sensitive: PK‐resistant species determines the phenotype of hamster‐adapted prion strains [20]. To investigate whether a similar phenomenon occurs in human CJD, all fractions were subjected to PK digestion, and the size distribution of resPrPD was analysed by immunoblotting with 3F4. For all samples, including controls, resPrPD signals emerged after 15 nm R h (Figure 6). Comparing size distributions of the control samples revealed similar profiles of resPrPD for MM1 and MM2 (Figure 6A,D), where a predominance of larger aggregates > 44 nm R h was seen, although MM2 contained more large aggregates than MM1. In contrast, the majority of resPrPD particles from VV1 were in the 19‐ to 35‐nm range with a few larger aggregates (Figure 6A,D). Interestingly, the distributions of resPrPD for the three cases were most similar to that of VV1 (Figure 6B–D,F).

Size fractionation allows the separation of nonpathological PrPC particles (< 6 nm Rh) from the slightly larger but PK‐sensitive PrPD oligomers (Figure S3). By comparing levels of resPrPD with total PrPD levels, we can estimate the relative amount of PK‐sensitive PrPD particles present, that is, pathological PrPD other than PrPC that is removed by PK digestion. The PK‐sensitive PrPD particle distribution varied by strain: for MM2 and VV1, there were more PK‐sensitive PrPD particles, whereas the majority of PrPD in MM1 contained larger PK‐resistant particles. For all three MV1 cases, the distributions of PK‐sensitive PrPD particles were again most similar to VV1, with the majority of aggregates in the 19‐ to 35‐nm R h range.

Discussion

In mammalian prion diseases, specific pathologies are induced by infection with distinct PrPD conformations, supporting the structural basis of prion strains [5, 23]. However, in spontaneous prion disease, as seen in the majority of human cases, multiple PrPD assemblies may co‐exist, complicating efforts to link specific structural variants with particular phenotypic presentations. In addition to the structural heterogeneity of PrPᴰ assemblies, host genetic factors, such as heterozygosity at codon 129, and the type of PrPD can further complicate the picture. From our detailed analysis of three MV1 cases, we found that clinical presentation alone was not sufficient to identify atypical cases. Instead, we believe that detailed subtyping by immunoblot is required.

There is growing evidence that multiple prion conformations can coexist within the same individual, and their interactions may influence disease dynamics. For example, in mixed cases, prion subtypes may compete, propagate independently or modulate disease kinetics, potentially accelerating or delaying disease onset compared to single‐strain conditions [8, 24]. Unfortunately, when subtype analysis relies on fragment profiling at pH 8.0, which is a common practice in surveillance centres, it is possible to miss atypical or mixed subtypes (Table 2). For example, classical cases of MM1 or MV1 have PrPD fragments of 20 kDa when run at pH 8 and 21 kDa when run at pH 6.9. These are the ‘true’ type 1 cases. However, VV1 and VM1 cases also have PrPD fragments of 20 kDa when run at pH 8 but a mixture of 20 kDa and 21 kDa when run at pH 6.9, implying a different starting PrPD conformation or a mixture of conformations, as compared to ‘true’ type 1 cases. For our cases, we found examples of 20 kDa PrPD fragments at pH 8 that remained as 20 kDa when run at pH 6.9, a novel finding that implies the existence of a different initial PrPD conformation or combination of conformations than seen in MM1, MV1, VV1 or VM1 cases. Clinical features such as disease duration and symptom presentation would not have predicted this.

In animal models of prion disease, duration of disease or ‘incubation period’ is a defining feature of a prion strain; in humans, duration can be influenced by a variety of comorbid factors and caregiving choices. For our cases, duration was not key in identifying an atypical subtype. Cases 1 and 2 had durations of 10 months, and Case 3 was 15 months (but died by MAID, so we cannot know how long the disease may have progressed without this intervention). For reference, the average duration for MM (MV)1 is 4 months [25], for VV1 15 months, and for VM1 20.5 months [14]. In addition, all cases were screened for co‐existent pathologies that might influence presentation, and only Cases 1 and 3 had intermediate levels of Alzheimer neuropathological change, with no Lewy body, other tauopathy or TDP43 pathology seen in any of the cases.

With respect to clinical symptoms, each case was different. Case one began with memory loss, followed by personality change and hallucinations, with ataxia late in the disease. Case two began with personality changes and tactile hallucinations, and only later developed dementia, ataxia and visual hallucinations. Case 3 began with symptoms of parkinsonism and ataxia, with memory loss only developing later. These presentations themselves would not have flagged these cases as atypical, although it is interesting to note that some T2 (T19) subtypes of CJD have predominant ataxia with minimal or late dementia (the Brownell–Oppenheimer clinical variant, often VV2) or parkinsonism (often associated with MV2K). It is tempting to speculate that, given the fact that Case 3 had brain pathology with T19 (MV2C) features, the clinical presentation and pathological appearance of Case 3 were predominantly influenced by T19 PrPD, even though the case contained more T20 PrPD by immunoblot.

From a neuropathological perspective, all three of our cases had vacuoles that were larger than seen in classical MV1 but comparable to those observed in the six VM1 cases reported by Gelpi et al. [14]. Our cases differed in other respects, though. Five of the six reported VM1 cases had ballooned neurons, which are often seen in VV1 subtypes, but these were not seen in our cases. Coarse, patch‐like deposits were reported in the cerebellar molecular layer of the VM1 cases, but only Case 3 had coarse granular deposits in the cerebellum, in addition to a few coarse vacuolar deposits seen in the frontal cortex and many more coarse deposits in the temporal cortex, which are more typical of MM2 or MV2C. Looking at the lesion profiles, the reported VM1 cases had an intermediate severity of spongiform change between MM (MV)1 and VV1. Our cases did not fit this pattern and were more varied, with the greatest differences occurring in frontal and temporal cortices and striatum. The significance of this finding is unclear, as variability is not uncommon in sCJD. Case 3 had the lowest scores in the frontal and temporal cortex and no spongiform change in the striatum, but this may relate to the patient undergoing MAID and so not reaching the biological end‐stage seen in the other two cases.

Turning to the structural characteristics of PrPD, we found that all three atypical MV1 cases shared some similarities with VV1 and the previously reported VM1 cases. A unique feature of VV1 and VM1 resPrPD is its generation of T20 fragments with co‐existing T21 after PK digestion at pH 6.9 [11, 14]. This has not been reported previously for MM1 or classical MV1 subtypes, where the resPrPD typically generates T21 fragments and indistinguishable pathological profiles [9] (Table 2). It is tempting to speculate that the T20 fragment may result from a specific conformation formed by PrPD containing V129, given that T20 has been identified in other cases with V alleles, specifically MV2K [5, 26], VM1 [14] and VV1 [9, 27]. If so, its presence may correlate with levels of V129 PrPD in the brain. In our samples, PK digestion of Cases 1 and 2 produced predominantly T20 from the frontal cortex and T21 plus traces of T20 from the thalamus, whereas Case 3 only generated T20 in both the frontal cortex and thalamus. This could indicate that more V129 PrPD was present in the frontal cortex of Cases 1 and 2, and in both the frontal cortex and thalamus in Case 3.

Interestingly, in the regions where T20 was generated, but not in regions with T21 predominance, we noted there were comparably higher proportions of 12 kDa U‐CTF relative to levels of unglycosylated resPrPD. This high ratio is a typical characteristic of VV1 [21], again suggesting that regions producing T20 contained PrPD structures resembling those seen in VV1. It may not be a pure V allele effect, though, as the 12 kDa U‐CTF ratio seen for VV2 cases is similar to MM1 cases (~20% compared with ~70% for VV1 [21]), suggesting that the combination of V allele and T1 conformation, and/or another undefined factor, contributes to the high 12 kDa U‐CTF ratios.

Further PrPᴰ differences were noted when we studied conformational stability and PK resistance. PrPD from the frontal cortex of Case 3 had increased conformational stability and markedly increased proteinase resistance when compared with Cases 1 and 2. In addition, the [PK]½ values differed between the frontal cortex and the thalamus in all cases, with Cases 1 and 2 having lower values in the frontal cortex and Case 3 having lower values in the thalamus. This is an important observation because it reflects the variety of PrPD conformations that may co‐exist in the brains of patients and highlights the need to look at multiple brain regions in order to truly classify a prion subtype or strain.

In addition to testing different brain regions, it is also important to note that no one biochemical assay can discriminate all subtypes or strains [9, 18, 19, 28, 29]. Using asymmetric flow‐field flow fractionation (AF4), our group has previously discriminated the hamster prion strain Hyper from 263 K, two strains that cannot be discriminated by other biochemical methods [20]. Here we report, for the first time, the application of AF4 to discriminate human CJD subtypes based on their PrPD size distribution profiles. We observed that classical MM1, MM2 and VV1 cases had distinct PrPD profiles: MM1 contained predominantly PK‐resistant large particles, VV1 predominantly PK‐sensitive small particles, and MM2 many PK‐sensitive small particles but also some large PK‐resistant particles. The three MV1 cases reported here showed more variability in their profiles, but in all of them, the size distributions of resPrPᴰ and PK‐sensitive PrPᴰ were most comparable to those of VV1.

There are several limitations to our study, namely the small sample size and lack of direct functional correlation between prion protein conformations and clinical symptoms. We also note that Case 3 underwent medical assistance in dying, potentially limiting the observation of the full disease course.

In conclusion, our analysis of three MV1 cases with atypical T20 fragments has revealed a greater spectrum of phenotype for this subtype than previously reported. We found that clinical presentation and duration did not predict the presence of atypical or mixed PrPD fragments and that this may be due to a dominant effect of particular PrPD conformers; Cases 1 and 2 had both T20 and T21 fragments, implying coexisting conformers, but the histopathological phenotype matched that seen in cases with only T21 fragments, consistent with a dominant T21 effect. The possibility of a V allele effect was also observed in all cases, with the generation of T20 fragments, high U‐CTF ratios and PrPD aggregate size distributions comparable with VV1. For Case 3, it is possible that the presence of type 2 (T19) PrPD conformations exerted a dominant effect on both pathology (MV2C‐like) and phenotype (early ataxia and parkinsonism as seen in VV2 and MV2K, respectively). These findings emphasise the importance of careful subtyping of different brain regions using defined buffer conditions and screening for low levels of alternate conformations. The rates of mixed cases may be higher than previously thought. Beyond simply the identification of novel subtypes, which is relevant in an era of potentially zoonotic prion diseases such as chronic wasting disease, the fact that many research approaches to prion therapies rely on targeting specific PrP conformations means that detailed biochemical characterisation of PrPD may become critical for successful treatment development and application.
