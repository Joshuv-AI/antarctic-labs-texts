Prediction of Heart Failure With Reduced Ejection Fraction With Artificial Intelligence Electrocardiography in Patients With Atrial Fibrillation
Authors: Stacie J Shropshire, Sheila M Manemann, Ruoxiang Jiang, Lisa E Vaughan, Peter A Noseworthy, Konstantinos C Siontis, Zachi I Attia, Bernard J Gersh, Alanna M Chamberlain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo determine whether a low ejection fraction artificial intelligence electrocardiogram (AI-ECG) algorithm predicts incident heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) in patients with atrial fibrillation (AF) independently of known HF risk factors.Patients and MethodsA validated AI-ECG algorithm for low ejection fraction (EF) detection was applied to a cohort of patients aged 18 years or older from Olmsted County, Minnesota, with incident AF between January 1, 2000, and December 31, 2014. Risk of HFrEF by tertiles of AI-ECG score were calculated, and model performance was assessed using C-statistic estimates derived from Cox proportional hazard regression models.ResultsAmong 2569 patients with AF, 248 (9.7%) developed incident HFrEF (EF<50%) over an average follow-up of 7.0 years. Patients in the highest risk AI-ECG tertile had an increased risk of developing HFrEF in comparison with those in the lowest risk tertile after adjusting for common HF risk factors (hazard ratio [HR], 3.50; 95% CI, 2.51-4.87). The C-statistic was significantly higher in a model combining the AI-ECG + HF risk factors (0.76; 95% CI, 0.73-0.80) than a model with HF risk factors only (0.67; 95% CI, 0.63-0.71; P<.0001) and a model with the AI-ECG only (0.73; 95% CI, 0.69-0.76; P=.019). Similar results were observed for HF with EF of 35% or less (highest vs lowest risk AI-ECG tertile: HR, 5.20; 95% CI, 3.03-8.91).ConclusionIncorporation of the AI-ECG algorithm into routine clinical care may provide enhanced ability to identify patients with AF at risk of developing HFrEF with predictive performance that is superior to current clinical models.

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common cardiac arrhythmia,1 with an estimated 33.5 million people affected globally,2 a lifetime risk of approximately 16%,3 and increasing incidence occurring in recent decades.4, 5, 6 The prevalence of AF has exponentially grown and surpassed previous estimations, with the current prevalence of diagnosed AF within the United States recently estimated to be 10.55 million.7 Moreover, AF has also been shown to be associated with increased morbidity and mortality, including heart failure (HF).1,8 Patients with AF experience approximately a 3-fold increased risk of developing HF,9, 10, 11, 12, 13 and those who develop HF are at a substantially higher risk of hospitalization and death in comparison with patients with AF but without HF.14 Heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) has been found to heighten overall long-term risk of all-cause mortality rates in patients with AF by 17% to 40%,2 and treatment options for patients with AF-HFrEF include similar principles to treating AF such as anticoagulation, rate control, rhythm control, and risk factor management,2 in addition to guideline-directed medical therapy for HF. Rhythm control strategies have been emphasized with class IIb recommendations for AF catheter ablation in patients with both AF and HFrEF.15 Recent guidelines from the 2020 European Society of Cardiology/European Association of Cardio-Thoracic Surgery indicate a class I recommendation for catheter ablation for patients with AF and subsequent HFrEF, where arrhythmia-induced cardiomyopathy is highly probable regardless of symptoms.16 These guidelines are based on evidence from randomized controlled trials that have shown AF catheter ablation to reduce mortality, reduce hospitalizations for worsening HF, improve ejection fraction (EF), maintain sinus rhythm, and improve overall quality of life.15

An artificial intelligence (AI) algorithm using a convolutional neural network has been developed and validated to predict low EF by utilizing routinely collected 12-lead electrocardiograms (ECGs).17,18 Use of the AI-ECG algorithm as a screening tool has been found to assist in early identification of patients with asymptomatic left ventricular systolic dysfunction (LVSD), with the network model finding an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.93 for low EF (EF≤35%), a sensitivity of 86.3%, specificity 85.7%, and accuracy of 85.7%.17 Multiple studies have since assessed the existing developed and validated AI-ECG algorithm, including a cluster-randomized clinical trial of 22,641 patients who had no previous diagnosis of HF.19 The trial found that utilization of the AI-ECG tool, compared with usual care, increased the odds of a low EF diagnosis within 90 days of the ECG by 30% (odds ratio, 1.32; 95% CI, 1.01-1.61) in the overall cohort and by 43% among the participants identified as having a high likelihood of low EF (odds ratio, 1.43; 95% CI, 1.08-1.91). Recent retrospective cohort studies have also examined the capabilities of the AI-ECG algorithm in detecting LVSD among specific patient groups including patients with immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy,20 Graves disease,21 and emergency department patients presenting with dyspnea.22 Results from these studies have shown the AI-ECG algorithm to be reliable in predicting asymptomatic LVSD in the respective cohorts examined; however, it is unknown whether the existing AI-ECG algorithm can identify patients at risk of HFrEF subsequent to AF. Therefore, the current study has 2 specific aims: (1) to determine whether a previously developed and validated low EF AI-ECG algorithm can be used to predict HFrEF after incident AF and (2) to evaluate whether the low EF AI-ECG algorithm is an independent predictor of HFrEF after adjusting for other known HF risk factors.

Patients and Methods

Study Population

The results reported in this article use data from the Rochester Epidemiology Project, which includes identifiable patient information (protected health information) derived from electronic health records (EHRs). As such, the full data sets cannot be shared freely. However, limited or deidentified data sets may be shared in accordance with Mayo Clinic, Olmsted Medical Center, and Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act policies, which require institutional review board approval and enactment of data use agreements for defined studies. Please direct queries regarding data sharing to the corresponding author of the manuscript. Dr Chamberlain had full access to the data in the study and takes responsibility for its integrity and the data analysis.

This study was conducted in Olmsted County, MN, using the resources of the Rochester Epidemiology Project (REP). The REP is a records-linkage system of local providers, mainly Mayo Clinic, Olmsted Medical Center, and their respective affiliated hospitals that deliver most health care to county residents, thus enabling virtually complete capture of health outcomes in Olmsted County, MN.23,24 Generalizability of REP data has been previously described, with demographic and ethnic characteristics of Olmsted County found to be representative of the state of Minnesota and the United States Midwest region and age- and sex-specific mortality rates being similar to national mortality rates.24 This study was approved by the Mayo Clinic and Olmsted Medical Center institutional review boards. The study was considered minimal risk by both institutional review boards; therefore, the requirement for informed consent was waived. However, records of any patient who had not provided authorization for their medical records to be used for research, as per Minnesota statute 144.335, were not included.

Ascertainment of AF

Adult (≥18 years) Olmsted County residents with an incident AF diagnosis between January 1, 2000, and December 31, 2014, were identified in the REP using International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision, Clinical Modification codes 427.31 and 427.32 and Mayo Clinic ECGs. Manual review of medical records was performed among possible incident cases to validate AF, as previously described.25

Artificial Intelligence Algorithm for Low EF

The closest ECG available on the same day or within 90 days after a patient’s AF diagnosis was retained for analysis and used to derive the AI-ECG score to predict the probability of reduced EF.17 The ECG date was used as the index date for the analysis. Patients without at least 1 standard, digitally stored, 10-second, 12-lead ECG on the same day or within 90 days of the AF diagnosis date were excluded from analysis. The AI-ECG algorithm was developed using a convolutional neural network, as previously described,17 and has been prospectively validated showing high accuracy.18 The output of the AI-ECG model is a continuous variable between 0 and 1, which represents the probability of low EF. The probability of low EF was categorized into tertiles (T1, <0.0265; T2, 0.0265-0.09253; and T3, ≥0.09254) for analysis. In sensitivity analyses, the probability of low EF was separately modeled as quintiles, as well as using a binary cutoff of an AI-ECG score of 0.5 or more.

Ascertainment of HFrEF

Incident HF during follow-up was ascertained via International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision, Clinical Modification code 428 and International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification code I50 assigned during any type of encounter (inpatient or outpatient) and collected up to December 31, 2019. Medical records were manually reviewed to validate HF events using the Framingham Criteria.26 Mean EF, determined by an echocardiogram within 90 days of the HF diagnosis, was obtained to characterize the type of HF. The outcome of interest was HFrEF, defined as HF with EF of less than 50%. Patients with EF of 50% or more (HF with preserved EF [HFpEF]) or unknown type of HF were retained and included as censoring events in the statistical analysis. Because the AI-ECG algorithm for low EF was created to detect EF of 35% or less, we performed additional analyses defining our outcome as HF with EF of 35% or less. Patients with prevalent HF (HFrEF, HFpEF, or unknown type of HF) occurring before or on the date of their ECG (index date) were excluded from the cohort for analysis.

Clinical Data Collection

Traditional risk factors for HF including age, sex, race, body mass index, systolic blood pressure (BP), total cholesterol, smoking status, antihypertensive medications, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), along with histories of comorbidities, were all collected at the time of the patient’s AF diagnosis. The variables age, sex, race, systolic BP, total cholesterol, antihypertensive medications, eGFR, and comorbidities were ascertained from the EHR using the REP. To maximize available data, systolic BP values within 1 year and cholesterol and eGFR values within 5 years before the patient’s AF date were retained for analysis. Antihypertensive medication was defined as patients who were prescribed this medication at the time of their AF diagnosis and included the following drug classes (US Department of Veterans Affairs drug class): β blockers (CV100), α blockers (CV150), calcium channel blockers (CV200), antihypertensive combinations (CV400), other antihypertensives (CV490), peripheral vasodilators (CV500), thiazides/related diuretics (CV701), loop diuretics (CV702), carbonic anhydrase inhibitor diuretics (CV703), potassium sparing/combinations diuretics (CV704), angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (CV800), angiotensin II inhibitors (CV805), and direct renin inhibitors (CV806). Comorbid conditions from the US Department of Health and Human Services list of chronic conditions were defined as 2 codes separated by at least 30 days and within 5 years before AF.27,28 The CHA2DS2-VASc (congestive HF, hypertension, age 75 years or older, diabetes, prior stroke or transient ischemic attack–vascular disease, age 65-74 years, sex category) score was calculated using comorbidities present at the time of AF or within the 5 years before AF diagnosis. Smoking status at the time of AF diagnosis was abstracted from the medical record by trained nurse abstractors.

Statistical Analyses

Baseline patient characteristics were reported as median (interquartile range [IQR]) for continuous variables and as frequencies (%) for categorical variables. AI-ECG scores were categorized into tertiles for the primary analysis and considered as quintiles and a binary variable using a cutoff of AI-ECG score of 0.5 or more in sensitivity analyses. Differences in characteristics between AI-ECG score tertiles were compared using the Jonkheere–Terpstra test or Cochran–Armitage test, depending on the variable type.

For time-to-event analysis, patients were followed up from the time of their index ECG date until their HFrEF event and censored on their last follow-up date, end of study (December 31, 2019), or other type of HF event (HFpEF or HF with unknown EF), whichever occurred first. All analyses were conducted in duplicate using 2 thresholds of EF for the HFrEF event: HF with EF of less than 50% and HF with EF of 35% or less. A landmark analysis starting follow-up at 30 days after index date was also included as a sensitivity analysis. The Aalen–Johansen estimator was used to derive the cumulative incidence curves and estimates at 1- and 5-year follow-up. Cox proportional hazards regression was used to determine associations between AI-ECG score groups and traditional risk factors with HF with EF of less than 50% and HF with EF of 35% or less. The risk factor model included known HF risk factors from 2 published HF risk prediction models.29,30 As opposed to assigning regression coefficients based on the published risk model equations, we constructed a traditional Cox proportional hazards regression model including each of the individual variables included in either of the previously published risk models. This approach was chosen to avoid excluding a large number of individuals with values outside of the range used to develop the risk score. For example, the PREVENT-HF risk score was developed in persons aged 30-79 years, whereas our cohort comprised 32% patients with AF diagnosed at ages of 80 years and older. Furthermore, we included only the base variables from the PREVENT-HF model owing to a large amount of missing data for the novel predictors. Hazard ratios (HRs) and corresponding 95% CIs were reported. In addition, C-statistic estimates and 95% CIs were derived from the Cox proportional hazards regression models, and differences in estimates across models were compared using Uno's method.31,32 Finally, C-statistics for the models were estimated in subgroups by age (<75 and ≥75 years), sex (male and female), and ECG rhythm (AF and sinus/other rhythm).

All analyses were conducted using SAS software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute) and R software, version 4.0.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). P values were considered to be statistically significant at the α level of .05.

Results

Sample Size

There was a total of 5062 adult Olmsted County residents aged 18 years or older diagnosed with incident AF between January 1, 2000, and December 31, 2014. Patients were excluded if they refused research authorization (n=134; 2.6%), did not have an available ECG within 90 days after their AF diagnosis (n=470; 9.3%), had a pre-existing HF (HFrEF, HFpEF, or unknown type of HF) diagnosis before or on the date of their index ECG (n=1103; 21.8%), or were missing covariate observations (n=786; 15.5%). This yielded a final sample of 2569 patients in the analysis cohort (Supplemental Figure, available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/).

Baseline Patient Characteristics

The sample (n=2569) was composed primarily of male (n=1361; 53.0%) patients in their mid-70s (median, 74 years; IQR, 64-82 years), with a majority having a previous diagnosis of hypertension (n=1862; 72.5%) and being in AF at the time of ECG recording (n=2120; 82.5%). Baseline patient characteristics are reported both overall and by AI-ECG algorithm tertile in Table 1. Compared with the other tertiles, patients in tertile 3 (the highest risk tertile) tended to be older, were more likely to be males, had lower cholesterol (both total and high-density lipoprotein), and had higher rates of coronary artery disease, chronic kidney disease, and diabetes (P<.05 for all). Patients in the highest risk tertile (tertile 3) also more frequently had elevated CHA2DS2-VASC scores (≥2 in men or ≥3 in women).

Incident HFrEF During Follow-up

During an average (SE) follow-up of 7.0 (0.10) years, a total of 805 (31.3%) patients developed incident HF; 248 developed HF with EF of less than 50% (9.7%), 399 developed HFpEF (15.5%), and 158 developed unknown HF (6.2%). The risk of HF with EF of less than 50% and HF with EF 35% or less were found to significantly differ by tertiles of AI-ECG score (log-rank P<.001) (Figure 1A and B). The event rates increased with increasing risk tertile of AI-ECG score (Table 2). For HF with EF of less than 50%, the rates were 8.72 (95% CI, 6.59-11.54) per 1000 person-years for the lowest risk tertile (tertile 1) compared with 35.63 (95% CI, 30.24-41.97) for the highest risk tertile (tertile 3). For HF with EF of 35% or less, the rates were 3.03 (95% CI, 1.88-4.87) per 1000 person-years for the lowest risk tertile compared with 17.69 (95% CI, 14.02-22.32) for the highest risk tertile. The highest risk AI-ECG tertile also had the highest cumulative incidence of HF with EF of less than 50% and HF with EF 35% or less at 1 year and 5 years.

In the AI-ECG algorithm only model, patients in the highest risk tertile had a significantly increased risk of developing HFrEF in comparison with those in the lowest risk tertile (HR, 3.83; 95% CI, 2.76-5.29, for HF with EF <50%; 5.39; 95% CI, 3.17-9.15, for HF with EF ≤35%; P<.001 for both) (Table 3). Patients in the middle tertile had a modest but nonsignificant increased risk of incident HF with EF of less than 50%, but had a nearly 2-fold increased risk of HF with EF of 35% or less (HR, 1.90; 95% CI, 1.05-3.43; P=.03). Results were similar after fitting the combined model of AI-ECG algorithm + risk factors. For HF with EF of less than 50%, age, a history of diabetes, eGFR, and smoking status were also found to be significant risk factors in both the risk factors only model and in the combined model of AI-ECG algorithm + risk factors. However, age was the only significant risk factor for HF with EF of 35% or less. Results were consistent in sensitivity analyses, with increasing risk of HFrEF with increasing quintile of AI-ECG score, and a more than 4-fold increased risk of HF with EF of less than 50% and HF with EF of 35% or less for patients who had an AI-ECG score of 0.5 or more compared with scores of less than 0.5 (Supplemental Table 1, available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/). Similar pattern of associations were also observed in landmark analyses starting follow-up at 30 days after index, however, HRs comparing the highest with lowest risk tertiles were attenuated.

The C-statistic for predicting incident HF with EF of less than 50% in the AI-ECG + risk factors combined model was 0.76 (95% CI, 0.73-0.80), which was significantly higher than the C-statistic derived from the risk factors only model (0.67; 95% CI, 0.63-0.71), indicating prediction is significantly improved by the addition of AI-ECG (estimate for δ, 0.091; 95% CI, 0.056-0.127); P<.0001) (Supplemental Table 2, available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/). When comparing the AI-ECG only model (C-statistic, 0.73; 95% CI, 0.69-0.76) with the AI-ECG + risk factors combined model, the C-statistic was also found to be significantly improved (estimate for δ, 0.040; 95% CI, 0.007-0.072; P=.019) (Figure 2A). The C-statistics were similar for the models predicting HF with EF of 35% or less.

Subgroup Analysis

When stratified by age group (≥75 vs <75 years), C-statistics were found to consistently perform better in the younger ages than those in the older age group (Figure 2B), with the highest C-statistic achieved in the combined AI-ECG + risk factors model for ages younger than 75 years (C-statistic, 0.81 for HF with EF<50% and 0.82 for HF with EF≤35%). Across sexes, the models tended to perform better in men than those in women, with the exception of the risk factors only models, which performed better in females (Figure 2C). Patients with sinus or other rhythm at the time of ECG recording had slightly higher C-statistics estimates than patients in AF across all models (combined AI-ECG + risk factors model: 0.81 in sinus/other vs 0.76 in AF for HF with EF<50%; 0.89 in sinus/other vs 0.76 in AF for HF with EF≤35%) (Figure 2D).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the use of an AI-ECG algorithm in predicting the development of HFrEF in patients with AF. Our results suggest that AI-ECG has the potential for real-world application for patients diagnosed with AF in routine clinical practice to identify those at risk of future HFrEF over and above existing comorbid risk factors for HFrEF.

It is well known that AF and HFrEF can be interconnected with shared risk factors, with bidirectional causality between the 2 conditions and through adverse remodeling.16,33 The presence of both conditions also greatly increases the risk for additional adverse outcomes including stroke and all-cause mortality.16 Early identification of patients with AF and low EF and a high risk of developing HFrEF is important in determining appropriate therapies and avoiding delays to care. For patients with AF-HFrEF, rhythm management is a priority, and catheter ablation has been shown to improve cardiac outcomes in this patient population.34 Although arrhythmia recurrence is common after ablation, early intervention with more advanced ablation techniques35,36 may reduce AF recurrence and improve patient outcomes. Using AI tools, such as AI-ECG algorithms, can improve risk stratification and substantially impact precision medicine by enabling personalized risk evaluations that may perform better than traditional risk scores. In this study, the risk factors found to be associated with subsequent HF with EF of less than 50% included older age, current or former smoking status, lower eGFR, and a history of diabetes, which aligns with previous literature.33,37 When analyzing the AI-ECG score by tertiles, our results found that patients with the highest probability of low EF (tertile 3) had almost a 4-fold increased risk of developing HF with EF of less than 50% and more than a 5-fold increased risk of developing HF with EF of 35% or less compared with those with the lowest probability of low EF (tertile 1), even after adjustment for traditional HF risk factors. However, additional research is warranted to determine whether the use of the AI-ECG model to guide treatment decisions, such as identifying patients who may benefit from ablation, results in improved clinical outcomes in these patients.

Our results are similar to studies that have previously examined this AI-ECG algorithm in different populations.20, 21, 22 In one example, a retrospective cohort of 1606 emergency room patients presenting with dyspnea found that the AI-ECG algorithm was a strong predictor of LVSD, with an AUC of 0.89, and patients who were positively identified by the algorithm also had a significantly higher risk of being hospitalized with HF.22 Another retrospective cohort study found the AI-ECG algorithm had acceptable discriminatory ability of predicting left ventricular dysfunction (AUC, 0.74; 95% CI, 0.60-0.87) among 89 idiopathic immune-mediated myopathy patients.20 For patients with Graves disease, the AI-ECG score by itself was also found to have excellent discrimination in predicting HFrEF, with a C-statistic of 0.85 (95% CI, 0.84-0.94), and when the AI-ECG algorithm was added to the clinical model, the C-statistic improved from 0.66 to 0.89.21

Further evidence indicates that AI-ECG models initially developed for detection of conditions at the time of the ECG may also be valuable for assessing risk of future cardiovascular events such as HF. For example, an AI-ECG model developed to identify hypertension was found to predict incidence of HF with similar discrimination as the Pooled Cohort Equations to Prevent Heart Failure (PCP-HF) risk score (C-statistics of 0.804 and 0.806, respectively).38 Furthermore, an AI-ECG model for LVSD using a single-lead ECG was found to predict incidence of HF (C-statistics ranging between 0.723 and 0.828 across 3 cohorts) and improved prediction of HF when added to HF risk models, including the PCP-HF and Predicting Risk of Cardiovascular Disease Events (PREVENT).39 In the current study, we found further evidence to support the addition of the AI-ECG algorithm to clinical risk factor models for incident HFrEF to improve predictive accuracy among special populations, specifically those with AF. Compared with using traditional clinical risk factors alone, the addition of the AI-ECG algorithm to the model significantly improved the ability to predict HF with EF of less than 50% (C-statistics, 0.67 vs 0.76, respectively).

It should be noted that previous studies have shown the reliability of the AI-ECG algorithm in predicting low EF (EF≤35%), regarding sex, age, and body mass index among general populations.40 However, for the current study, slight variances of AI-ECG algorithm performance occurred when examining subgroups stratified by age, sex, and rhythm, with discrimination tending to be better for males, those younger than 75 years of age, and those in sinus or other rhythm at the time of ECG recording. Although being younger has previously been seen to slightly improve the performance of the AI-ECG algorithm in predicting LVSD among emergency patients presenting with dyspnea, research has identified a slight increased predictive ability of the AI-ECG algorithm in females rather than that in males, which conflicts with the results of this study.22 Although these differences appear modest and suggest that use of the AI-ECG algorithm should not be limited to only patients with AF of a specific sex, age, or rhythm at time of ECG, future prospective studies are warranted to validate the AI-ECG algorithm’s predictive ability of HFrEF among patients with AF based on patient subgroups.

Limitations and Strengths

This study is best understood when considering its limitations. First, it is possible that patients with asymptomatic AF are underrepresented in the current sample. Such patients may be at increased risk of AF-related complications, including HFrEF, owing to delayed AF diagnosis. Second, HFrEF events may have also been potentially missed if the diagnosis occurred outside Olmsted County. However, a notable strength of our study is the manual review of medical records to validate both AF and HF diagnoses and inclusion of events occurring both in inpatient and outpatient settings. Third, the study was retrospective in nature, using EHR data as part of routine clinical care, and thus, some patients were excluded who did not have available ECG data. Fourth, the performance of the AI-ECG model differed by ECG rhythm, with lower discrimination for ECGs in AF; thus, AI-ECG models specifically developed for patients with AF may need to be developed or recalibrated using ECGs in AF. Fifth, although we reported good discrimination of our model for predicting incident HFrEF in patients with AF, the AI-ECG model was not initially developed for this purpose. As such, training a new AI-ECG model for this specific purpose may perform better and be an important area for further research. Finally, the results of this study may not be generalizable to the United States as whole because the sample population was limited to Olmsted County, MN; however, the population of the county has been shown to be representative of Minnesota and of the broader US Midwest region.24 Nevertheless, additional studies across diverse populations may be warranted to replicate our findings.

Conclusion

Among patients diagnosed with AF, an AI-ECG algorithm previously developed and validated to predict low EF was found to predict subsequent HFrEF, with incremental predictive performance when added to a clinical model of common risk factors for HF. Incorporating the AI-ECG algorithm into routine clinical care may provide enhanced ability to identify the early development of HFrEF in adult patients with AF. Utilization of the AI-ECG algorithm in addition to common risk factors can inform decisions for intensified surveillance for incident HFrEF and potentially earlier utilization of rhythm control and other preventive strategies for HFrEF.
