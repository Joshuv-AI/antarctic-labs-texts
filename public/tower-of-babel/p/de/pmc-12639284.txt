Not all Parkinsons patients with thoracolumbar spinal fusion are created equal: Highlighting the difference between long and short fusions
Authors: Matthew Lindsey, Hannah Levy, Tyler Allen, Andrew Pumford, Abdelrahman Hamouda, Brian Kelley, Brian Goh, Brett Freedman, Arjun Sebastian, Ahmad Nassr
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundParkinsons disease (PD) is a devastating neuromuscular disease that has several distinct spinal syndromes in addition to common degenerative spinal disorders. Multiple studies have concluded that patients with Parkinson's disease are at increased risk for perioperative medical complications and poor surgical outcomes such as failure of hardware and reoperation. PD patients exist in a spectrum of spinal disease with presentations, indications and surgical paradigms varying widely, yet the literature tends to treat them as a homogenous group. A more nuanced investigation and comparison of PD patients undergoing TL fusion will yield important differences in presentation, indication and outcomes. There is scant evidence on the most appropriate fusion paradigm for treating Parkinson's patients. We set out to compare patients who had short or long (>3 levels) thoracolumbar fusions to contrast the rate of surgical failure, and complications between each surgical approach and identify any patient, surgical, or disease risk factors for poor outcomes.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study of a single center experience with spinal fusion patients with concomitant Parkinson's disease at the time of spinal surgery. We included adult patients who underwent thoracolumbar spinal fusion and had a concomitant diagnosis of PD at an academic center between 2017 and 2022. Revision, oncologic and infection cases were excluded. Primary outcome measures were radiographic (stenosis, listhesis, hardware, fracture) evidence of failure, and morbidity. Secondarily, a multivariate regression was performed to identify patient risk factors for failure. We extracted and analyzed demographic information, Parkinson's specific metrics, in and out of hospital complications, surgical data, and radiologic outcomes and compared patients who underwent either short or long fusion constructs.Results92 patients were identified after inclusion and exclusion. 63 (68%) underwent short fusion and 29 (32%) had long fusion constructs. Short fusion constructs were more strongly indicated for radiculopathy 51% vs. 21% p=.01) while extended fusions had a higher indication of spinal deformity (41%vs. 10% p=.001) or fracture (34%vs. 8% p=.004) and were more likely to have decreased mobility prior to surgery (79.7%vs. 53.9%; P<.001). Short constructs had a higher rate of listhesis (20%vs. 0% p=.01) or stenosis (34.5%vs. 4% p=.008) at the level above the upper instrumented level, proximal screw loosening (13%vs. 0% p=.009), proximal junctional complications (53%vs. 12% p=.001) and proximal junctional failures (37%vs. 8% p=.02). In hospital complications, 90 day and 1 year mortality, reoperation rate, infection rate, progressive neurologic decline, and fusion rates were statistically similar between groups.ConclusionsHow to approach surgical treatment of PD patients remains a clinical conundrum. This study compared PD patients who had short and long constructs and showed statistical equipoise in mortality, complications and re-operations between the 2 cohorts. While differing in presentation, preoperative debility and indications, both short and long fusions in PD patients have elevated but acceptable complication rates. When long fusion is properly indicated, it can be performed without major increases in complications. Background

Parkinsons disease (PD) is a progressive neuromuscular disease characterized by generalized slowing of movements (bradykinesia) and at least 1 other symptom of resting tremor or rigidity. It affects approximately 1% of the population over the age of 60 with increasing incidence and prevalence with age [1]. With an increasing understanding of pathophysiology and increasing treatment options, patients with Parkinson's disease are mitigating the effects of the disease into later age [1]. Nonetheless, Parkinsons patients suffer from falls, gait difficulties, and dementia in the later stages of the disease. The quality of life for most patients with PD is poor [2]. Furthermore, PD can develop postural changes, the archetype of which is stooped posture, dropped head, and a flexed trunk, hips, and knees. These changes can evolve into disabling spinal deformities, such as scoliosis, antecollis, camptocormia, and Pisa syndrome in addition to concomitant age-related degenerative spinal disorders [3]. A higher rate of osteoporosis and osteopenia within the PD population significantly complicates any surgical consideration of these extreme degenerative or postural crises [4].

Multiple studies have cataloged the increased rate of medical, radiological, and mechanical complications and ultimately higher revision rates following thoracolumbar fusion (TL) in PD patients [5,[7], [8],11,[13], [14], [15], [16]]. Specifically, A recent meta-analysis concluded that PD patients are more likely to suffer from SSI and mechanical complications than non-PD patients undergoing spinal fusion [13]. Due to the scarcity of evidence in these patients, many studies consider all PD patients with TL fusion as a homogenous group. However, comparing all PD patients undergoing TL fusion belies the complexity of decision making when approaching disparate indications, presentations and disease progression.

Many PD patients are well indicated for long segment fusion. Additionally, some literature argues for extended instrumentation such as (1) concomitant fusion with ALL decompressions, (2) extension of fusion to the pelvis, and (3) extension beyond the thoracolumbar junction [6,7]. This is in support of the idea that PD requires additional biomechanical support through longer fusion surgeries to avoid failure.

Other PD patients might only require limited fusion and could even incur additional complications with additional levels of surgical burden. Supportive evidence argues that PD patients are at higher risk of medical complications from surgery, and surgical insult should be minimized [8,15].

Understanding that not all PD patients are created equally and that conservative and extensive paradigms exist within the TL fusion of Parkinson's, we set out to compare patients who had short (1-3 level) or long (>3 levels) thoracolumbar fusions to contrast the outcomes between each surgical approach. The primary outcome of this study was radiographic outcomes at 1 year (evaluation of alignment, stenosis, hardware and fusion). The secondary outcomes included clinical morbidity, mortality and complications.

Methods

The study design of this investigation is a retrospective cohort study of a tertiary academic single center experience with spinal fusion patients with concomitant Parkinson's disease at the time of spinal surgery. All adult PD patients who underwent posterior TL fusion for deformity, degenerative, and traumatic indications at an academic center between 2017 and 2022 were retrospectively identified. Patients were identified using combination of Slicer Dicer and SONIX search functions within the electronic medical record by ICD 10 code for Parkinson’s Disease, and cross referenced with CPT code for thoracic or lumbar spinal fusion. This database was comprised of multiple spine surgeons from both Orthopedic and Neurosurgery backgrounds within a single tertiary center and its affiliated hospitals. To be included, the patients also needed preoperative imaging, and greater than 1 year of follow up as well as post operative imaging greater than 1 year (CT, X-ray, or MRI). We excluded infection and oncologic cases. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by institutional/regional/national ethics/committee/ethics board of The Mayo Clinic Office For Human Research Protection (FWA00005001) and individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived.

Data extraction

Preoperative medical comorbidities, functional status, Parkinson’s specific medical treatments, bone and muscle quality, operative indication, and construct related factors were extracted. Patient demographic and medical data including age, race, sex, smoking status (never/former/current), body mass index (BMI), presence of DBS before surgery, use of gait aid, chronic falls, use of nonsinemet medications, dementia status, and length of Parkinson's diagnosis were recorded via chart review. Preoperative bone quality was assessed Hounsfield units (HU) at L3 and L5, iliopsoas (IP) area, and IP HU at the level of the L3-4 interspace. Surgical factors including operative indication (Deformity, Instability, radiculopathy), interbody use, length of construct (>3 defined as long construct), use of BMP. Postoperative clinical complications included superficial or deep infections, delayed healing or wound dehiscence, postoperative hematoma, reoperation, respiratory or cardiac event, DVT/PE within 90 days as well as 90-day or 1 year mortality.

Radiographic analysis

Preoperative radiologic information was scrutinized for radiographic presence of spondylosis. Spondylosis was defined as: the presence of listhesis in any direction >4mm or >2 cardinal signs of arthritis on x-ray; all of the x-ray criteria or Facet grade >1 or the presence of a vacuum disc or greater than or equal to moderate stenosis on CT scan; or all x-ray and CT criteria or Modic >2 or Disc >3 on MRI. Preoperative instability was defined as listhesis within the levels of the construct, excluding a fractured level.

Spondylosis and fusion radiographic measurements were performed by single orthopedic reviewers, with standardized teaching of 10 sample patients by a senior surgeon to enforce standardization. Furthermore, a senior surgeon performed randomized compliance checks, with remediation and re-accomplishment if measurements did not fall within the established literature margins of error. In a similar manner, radiographic complications and alignment measures were performed by multiple reviewers, with standardized teaching examples, and quality assurance and remediation to enforce standardization within the established literature margins of error.

Postoperative clinical and radiographic complications by x-ray, CT scan, and MRI were analyzed. Post operative radiographic outcomes that were extracted were UIV-UIV-1 listhesis (>4mm) and stenosis, LIV-LIV+1 listhesis (>4mm) and stenosis, presence or absence of fusion defined as continuous osseous bridging on 1-year postoperative CT scan in either the gutter or disc space. Hardware complications were defined as screw loosening (haloing or back out on 1 year x-ray or CT scan), or hardware fracture. Proximal junctional complications included UIV-UIV-1 stenosis, fracture, spondylolisthesis, or UIV screw loosening. Distal junctional complications included LIV-LIV+1 stenosis, fracture, spondylolisthesis, or LIV screw loosening. Proximal junctional failure was defined as kyphosis beginning at the UIV junction that was not present prior to surgery, and distal junctional failure was defined similarly at the LIV junction.

Statistical analysis

Univariate analysis compared clinical and radiologic outcomes in patients who underwent either short or long fusion constructs. Variables were chosen based on significant prior literature, clinical intuition, and statistical criteria. Descriptive statistics were used to compare patient demographics in terms of mean and standard deviation or number of occurrences and percent of total. Continuous and categorical variables were analyzed with t-tests and chi-square tests or the corresponding nonparametric tests, respectively.

Missing data was excluded from individual calculations so as not to tarnish percentages in each specific statistic. When less than 10% is missing, it is unlikely to have an effect on the results according to missing data assumptions in a normal population. All statistical analysis was performed with R Studio Version 4.0.2 (Boston, MA). A p-value <.05 was statistically significant.

Clinical and radiographic outcomes

The overall mortality rate was 2.2% at 90 days and 6.5% at 1 year. The rate of fusion among all cases was 72.9% at 1 year. Major complications occurred in 20.6% of all cases. Fusion was more likely to occur in the gutter in both cohorts. There was no statistical difference in complications between the groups. Furthermore, the reoperation rate was statistically similar between the groups. There was a higher percentage of patients with new falls and progressive neurologic deficits within the short fusion group, but this did not rise to the level of statistical significance. There was a higher 1-year mortality within the long construct group, however this did not rise to statistical significance. In hospital complications, 90 day and 1 year mortality, reoperation rate, infection rate, progressive neurologic decline, and fusion rates were statistically similar between groups (Table 3).

Discussion

This retrospective review of patients with Parkinsons disease who underwent thoracolumbar instrumented arthrodesis represents the largest published single center experience with this patient demographic to date. We sought to answer the question of whether PD TL fusion outcomes differed between patients who had long or short fusion construct by comparing postoperative radiographic outcomes and secondarily clinical outcomes.

An interesting finding of this study is that longer constructs resulted in a statistically significant lower occurrence of proximal level failures as defined by radiographic evidence of UIV/UIV −1 stenosis, listhesis, and screw loosening. The proximal junction of a construct sees a significant amount of stress due to its harsh transition from a fixed to a mobile segment. This stress requires robust support to maintain, and it is known that PD results in a weakened posterior tension band and paraspinal muscles that are diminutive in bulk and changed in character compared to control patients [9,6]. PD patients may not have the compensatory muscle power to sustain this robust force and may require additional fixation to compensate for unfavorable biomechanical balance. The failure of biomechanically insufficient support is strengthened by recent cohort investigation demonstrating that PD fusions fail for mechanical reasons at a higher rate than non-PD patients [14]. Biomechanically meager could result in persistent malignment and increased risk for failure and reoperation thus more robust and extensive fusion is necessary. This finding concurs with other published literature by Koller, and Babat. Koller followed 23 patients and found a high rate of failure without appropriate attention to lumbopelvic parameters, suggesting a low threshold for extension of fusion for success [6]. Babat reviewed 14 patients with a mean of 66 months follow up. They noted that all patients without instrumentation failed at the index level and that relentless kyphosis at the proximal junction was the mode of failure for all other patients followed by instability [7]. Their findings, together with this study contribute to an argument for robust fixation, together with a profound respect for biomechanical stability, to avoid unnecessary complications and increase the long-term durability of the construct.

Certainly, no unnecessary fusion should be advocated, however short fusions with concomitant pathology should be cautiously and carefully considered and closely followed for rapid progression of adjacent complications.

In this study the reoperation rate did not differ. Whether these changes did not rise to the level of clinical debility or whether the operating surgeon opted against reoperation for another reason is not clear. Overall reoperations occurred in 15 of the 92 patients (16.3%). If excluding infection and hematoma, 11 of the 92 (12.0%) patients had a reoperation due to clinical deterioration or radiographic findings. This reoperation percentage is decreased compared to existing retrospective analysis of PD patients who cite a rate of 36-86% [5,7,10,11]. It is unclear why some of the patients who had radiographic failure did not go onto reoperation, however it does not represent all patients who had radiographic failure. Perhaps the radiographic abnormality does not arise to clinical significance, while another theory is that at the subsequent evaluation, the patient may no longer be a candidate for surgical treatment or elect to forego treatment.

Not to be overlooked is the incidence of radiographic complications among all patients. Failure fusion occurred in 27.1% of surgeries. Hardware failure or loosening occurred in a large portion of cases (19%). Radiographic complications (40% proximal, 35% distal) and failures at both the proximal and distal junctions (27% of all cases) were similarly elevated compared to acceptable elective rates. In an elective setting, and under optimal circumstances, and in a non-Parkinson’s population, this would represent an extreme risk of failure. These incidences of significant perioperative radiographic complications, while less than some, fall within literature data of 11-77% [5,7,10,[11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16]].

An interesting secondary finding is that the rates of major medical complications and mortality were not increased with increased levels of fusion. While this affirms that long segment fusion can be performed safely, it does disagree with some other published data. Prior literature argued PD patients undergoing surgical fusion are at an increased risk for complications and a larger surgical physiologic burden could expose already at-risk patients to unacceptable surgical morbidity. Supportive evidence argues that PD patients are at higher risk of medical complications from surgery, and surgical insult should be minimized [15]. McClelland et al. [8] performed a registry investigation of 4301 patients with PD who had TL instrumented fusion. Their findings suggested that increasing the level of fusion beyond 2 levels increased the risk of mortality by 240% and several measures of perioperative morbidity ranging from 100% to more than 600%. As a counterpoint, Shah et al. found that there was not an overall risk of increased mortality or major medical complications comparing PD patients to a matched cohort [16]. Our findings contribute to the argument that PD patients are not at higher risk of short-term morbidity or mortality. This study was performed at a quaternary care center in an environment of high resource availability may not be generalizable to all situations.

With the finding of post operative complications at the upper and lower ends of the constructs, it is fair to ask whether this represents a worsening of pre-existing spondylosis or if this is a rapid development of de novo disease. A Post Hoc evaluation of the presence of preoperative spondylosis reveals that there was a high rate of pre-existing spondylosis at both the UIV/UIV-1 (52.3%) and LIV/LIV+1 (70.4%) levels. This high rate is consistent with the baseline rate of spondylosis in the age group of the patient population and infers pre-existing degenerative changes to the spine in these patients. The rate of preop listhesis was 3/88 (3.4%) at the UIV/UIV-1 and 1/44 (2.3%) at the LIV/LIV+1 levels while the rate of preop moderate or greater stenosis was 21/88 (23.9%) and 15/44 (34.1%) at the UIV/UIV-1 and LIV/LIV+1 respectively. The overall rates of listhesis increased considerably (UIV: 3.4% to 12.5%; LIV: 2.3% to 11.4%), while those of stenosis did not see the same magnitude of change from preoperative (UIV: 23.0% to 23.9%; LIV: 34.1% to 12.5%). Suffice it to say, many of the patients had signs of degeneration and stenosis prior to surgery at the levels that experienced failure, but it is important to acknowledge that listhesis may have occurred rapidly de-novo. This should be interpreted with caution as it is a post hoc evaluation, and not the primary aim of the study. Further studies could be helpful.

This study is not without important limitations which should be considered strongly. Firstly, although this is a comparatively large study in a specific population, it still represents a small number of patients overall. Thus, a potential Type II error exists by selection bias in the few patients. To reduce type II risk data integrity was enforced, and all eligible patients were included. This study, although low in numbers, represents the largest single center study on this rare population. Secondly, this is a retrospective study and has not been proven as a prospective treatment algorithm. The reader should take caution to know that the findings suggest associations and not necessarily causations. Another important limitation is that of surgical indications. Those patients with extended constructs were more likely to be primarily indicated for deformity or fracture, while those with shorter constructs had a higher rate of radicular symptomatology. This leads to a potential source of selection bias in that the patient populations differed in their rationale for surgery. Additionally, trauma patients often have comorbid injuries and conditions that cannot be optimized and thus may represent a heterogeneous patient population. Furthermore, the patients also differed in their level of debility before surgery. Those who required more assistance with ambulation before surgery, were more commonly found in the long fusion cohort. This may have affected the surgical decision-making calculus, however, the longer construct patients encountered less proximal failures. This latter fact strengthens the argument for appropriately indicated long construct fusion. Conversely, it may be that the longer construct patients demanded less of their long constructs. Providers would do well to consider the patients' demands while deciding on surgical treatments. Lastly, the patient population was more predominantly white (97.8%) than the national average (86.6%). PD is more common in Whites than Blacks, Hispanics, and Asians, and is nonrandomly distributed in the Midwest and Northeastern US [12]. Thus, these differences limit the generalizability of this study.

Conclusion

In what appears to be the largest cohort study to date evaluating outcomes following thoracolumbar spinal fusion in PD patients we investigated the radiographic and clinical outcomes in a single center retrospective review. We identified that patients who had short vs. long constructs showed statistical equipoise in mortality, complications and re-operations. While the indications for surgery differed statistically, the radiographic and junctional outcomes differed between these cohorts. Furthermore, the number of complications, radiographic changes, and hardware in this investigation continues to prompt appropriate trepidation prior to surgical intervention on patients with PD. Both short and long fusions in PD patients have relatively high, but acceptable complication rates, and when long fusion is appropriately indicated it can be performed without major increases in complications. Special caution should be taken in the case of short fusions due to the high rates of UIV/UIV-1 complications and rapid progression of DiNovo listhesis and stenosis. In the context of limited literature, this study sheds light on the expected outcomes and prognosis of differing types of PD patients that may require TL fusion.
