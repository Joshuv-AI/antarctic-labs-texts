Oxygen tension alters cartilage redox balance in response to traumatic impact
Authors: Jingyi Wang, Greta E Scheidt, Corinne R Henak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsRedox balance plays a key role in cellular homeostasis. Disruption of redox balance contributes to initiation and progression of multiple diseases including osteoarthritis, a common joint disease that is characterized by progressive cartilage damage. Therefore, monitoring cartilage redox balance can be used to evaluate cartilage health and disease status. Previous studies have investigated the effect of oxygen tension and traumatic impact on cartilage redox balance separately, but their interplay is not well known. The aim of this study is to quantify time- and position-dependent peracute responses of cartilage redox balance to impact injury at controlled oxygen tension.MethodsOptical redox imaging (ORI) was used to evaluate redox balance in porcine cartilage explants. ORI measures the autofluorescence from cofactors in cellular redox reactions to provide a real-time evaluation of redox balance. Porcine cartilage explants underwent traumatic impact at normal (room) or low oxygen tension, with imaging before and after loading. Samples were snap frozen shortly after imaging for gene expression analysis. Achieved loading parameters were calculated based on high-speed camera images.ResultsAt normal oxygen tension, fluorescent intensity increased after impact loading in both channel 1 (corresponding to reduced nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH) and its phosphate (NADPH)) and channel 2 (corresponding to flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD)), but this effect was not observed at low oxygen tension. In addition, a significant temporal effect was only observed for optical redox ratio (reflecting the relative accumulation of the cofactors), which was elevated after 28 minutes post-impact. Despite the measured changes in redox, gene expression did not change significantly.ConclusionLow oxygen tension was protective against impact injury-induced autofluorescence changes. Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a prevalent joint disease characterized by cartilage degeneration, subchondral bone thickening, inflammatory responses, and pain. During disease progression, cellular redox balance shifts due to excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production. ROS are a class of highly reactive electron acceptors that are produced by cells. Common ROS include hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), superoxide anion (O2−), and nitric oxide (NO).1 ROS can regulate the activity of enzymes and transcription factors as intracellular messengers in a physiological environment, but are also involved in the production of cytotoxic molecules and cause DNA damage in pathological conditions.2 The imbalance between ROS production and cellular intrinsic antioxidant capability causes oxidative stress, which promotes the development of many pathologies across tissues, including cancer3 and various chronic inflammatory and degenerative diseases.4,5 In cartilage, redox imbalance can cause genomic instability, promote chondrocyte apoptosis, and negatively affect the formation of the extracellular matrix, all of which contribute to OA initiation and progression.6,7

Since cartilage redox imbalance is a key feature of OA, monitoring cartilage redox balance may be beneficial for evaluating cartilage health and screening therapeutics. Prior studies have used cell-permeant fluorescent dye to quantify intracellular ROS. These studies have shown that ROS increase in cartilage treated with a pro-inﬂammatory mediator,8,9 and that ROS levels are higher in chondrocytes from aged animals compared to those from young animals.10 Given that elevated ROS is closely related to mitochondrial dysfunction, measuring mitochondrial membrane potential – an indicator of mitochondrial activity – is also informative. Using a polarity-sensitive fluorescence probe, a 2018 study found that injurious impact induced mitochondria depolarization in cartilage.11 However, the application of fluorescence dye in clinics is limited by the usage of exogenous dye, motivating the imaging of autofluorescent molecules for future translation.

Optical redox imaging (ORI) is a label-free, real-time technique to quantify redox balance in living tissue.12 ORI captures the autofluorescence of oxidized flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD), reduced nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH), and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH). NADH and NADPH share the same fluorescence spectrum and are labeled NAD(P)H together. FAD and NAD(P)H are vital cofactors in cellular redox reactions, and therefore can be used as natural biomarkers of redox balance in cells.13 Fluorescent intensity correlates with accumulation of the corresponding compounds, and optical redox ratio, calculated using FAD and NAD(P)H intensity, is an indicator of redox balance. A previous study from our laboratory showed that ORI metrics can predict mitochondrial membrane potential measured by fluorescent dye in cartilage explants,14 demonstrating the utility of ORI in comparison to established measures of mitochondrial function. Although measurement of autofluorescent signals in cartilage explants is challenging due to other autofluorescent molecules, the bandpass filters used in this study have little overlap with collagen spectra (Supplementary Figure a), therefore they are able to reliably capture intracellular autofluorescence from the targeted cofactors.

Cellular redox balance is responsive to various environmental stimuli, such as oxygen tension. Multiple prior studies showed that culturing chondrocytes at 18% to 21% oxygen (room air) impairs cellular redox balance by increasing ROS production and thereby causing more oxidative damage compared to lower, more physiological, oxygen tension.15-17 Moreover, a hypoxic environment (1% to 2% oxygen) is beneficial for cartilage. Disruptor of telomeric silencing 1-like (DOT1L), which aids cartilage health, was increased under hypoxia (1% oxygen) versus room oxygen (21% oxygen) in human chondrocytes.18 Hypoxia also has a positive effect on chondrocyte matrix synthesis. Bovine chondrocytes cultured at 2% oxygen produced more type II collagen and proteoglycan compared to their counterpart at 21% oxygen.19 In both healthy and OA chondrocytes, expression of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) decreased when culturing at 2% oxygen, indicating the suppression of catabolic activity under hypoxia.20 Thus, the effect of oxygen concentration as an isolated stimulus on cartilage is well established.

Due partly to the limited capacity of cartilage to regenerate, mechanical injury is a well-recognized risk factor for OA and joint dysfunction, and impact injury is a well-established ex vivo mechanical injury model.21 Previous studies have shown that mechanical overloading can cause mitochondrial dysfunction and therefore impair cartilage redox balance. After a single impact injury, basal oxygen consumption rate, maximum oxygen consumption rate, and relative mitochondrial membrane potential decreased, revealing that decreased respiration function and impaired integrity of the mitochondrial inner membrane were acute responses of the mitochondria to trauma.11 Mechanical overloading induced by surgery disrupted cellular redox balance by upregulating mitochondrial superoxide production and downregulating superoxide dismutase 2, which could balance out excessively produced ROS.22

Despite extensive in vitro studies that evaluate the effects of oxygen availability or mechanical loading on cartilage separately, their interaction is largely unknown. A previous study investigated the effect of oxygen tension on NO production induced by intermittent compression,23 but to our knowledge, no study has measured cartilage response to traumatic impact in altered oxygen levels. There is still a lack of understanding about the response of cartilage to mechanical injury at differential oxygen tension. Therefore, the objective of this study was to quantify time- and position-dependent peracute responses of cartilage redox balance to impact injury at controlled oxygen tension using ORI. The findings could enhance understanding of the initiation and progression of mechanical injury-associated OA through the lens of redox balance.

Methods

Sample preparation

A total of 40 cartilage explants were collected from nine conventional pigs acquired from a local abattoir (age 4 to 5 months, sex unknown and assumed random). The right patella from each pig was harvested within two hours of euthanasia and wrapped with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS)-soaked gauze for transportation from the on-campus abattoir to the dissection space. The patellae were unwrapped for dissection and kept hydrated during dissection using PBS. Two to three 6-mm diameter cylindrical cartilage samples were collected from each patella using a biopsy punch and scalpel. Cylinders were then bisected into hemicylinders. All hemicylindrical samples were stored in 12-well plates containing chemically defined chondrocyte medium (1× Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) with high glucose, 50 μg/ml L-Ascorbic acid 2-phosphate salt, 50 μg/ml L-proline, 1% insulin-transferrin-selenium (ITS), 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 100 nM dexamethasone, and 1% penicillin-streptomycin)24,25 and incubated at 5% CO2 and 5% O2. Chemically defined medium was selected rather than serum-supplemented medium to avoid the potential metabolic effects of unknown factors in serum. Samples were incubated for a minimum of 14 hours prior to testing, in order to reach a consistent baseline. Samples were incubated for up to 72 hours, based on preliminary tests showing that cell viability and ORI metrics were constant during the timeframe.

Impact loading

Half of the samples (n = 20) underwent impact loading in a custom-built microscope-top device (Figures 1a and 1b). The device enables measurement of real-time changes of cartilage redox balance in response to mechanical stimuli at controlled oxygen levels. The device consisted of an aluminium base, backplate, clamp, 3D-printed oxygen chamber, and impact rod connected to an actuator (Aerotech ACT165D; Aerotech, USA). Each hemicylindrical sample was attached to the backplate using a small amount of cyanoacrylate with the cut, flat surface facing down. The backplate was clamped onto the base using two screws. The position of the impact rod was adjusted until the impact tip aligned with the centre of the sample and lightly touched the articular surface. During impact, the impact rod was actuated to a consistent target strain (-100%) at a consistent target deformation rate (500 mm/s). Deformation of the sample and the backplate were captured by a high-speed camera (Phantom V1211 monochrome; Vision Research, USA) at 7,000 frames per second under brightfield imaging at a magnification of 4×.

Oxygen controlled impact-loading device. a) Prospective view, showing the 3D-printed component with nitrogen inlet, the leaf-spring like backplate, and the impact tip. b) Inverted view as seen by the microscope. c) Sample view showing imaging locations.

Optical redox imaging

Images were acquired at a magnification of 20× using an inverted epifluorescent microscope (Olympus IX-71). Overall, 14 regions of interest (ROIs) were selected along the articular surface of the sample (Figure 1c). Two sets of redox images were taken at the centre of the sample (ROI 1 and 2, Figure 1c) before impact, serving as baseline. The remaining 12 ROIs were divided into three distance bins according to their distance to the centre of the sample, where impact occurred (short: 0.3 to 0.7 mm, medium: 1.3 to 1.7 mm, and far: 2.3 to 2.7 mm). After impact, redox images were taken in three time bins (0 to 5 mins, 15 to 18 mins, and 28 to 31 mins post impact). In each time bin, one ROI from each distance bin was randomly selected and imaged in a random order. Randomization was conducted using custom Matlab (R2021b) code (Matlab; MathWorks, USA).

At each ROI, redox images were acquired as previously described.26 Briefly, two filter cubes were integrated into the microscope. A DAPI filter cube (excitation 361 to 389 nm, emission 435 to 485 nm) was applied to measure the autofluorescence signal from NAD(P)H in channel 1, and a green filter cube (excitation 470 to 490 nm, emission 500 to 550 nm) was applied to capture signal from FAD in channel 2. The exposure time for the two channels were fixed at 25.64 ms and 666.70 ms, respectively, for all samples. The average intensity in each image was measured using ImageJ (ImageJ 1.53 k; National Institutes of Health, USA) and normalized to the average of two baseline intensities.

For samples in the no impact group, redox images were taken in the three time bins starting at about eight minutes after baseline ORI, to match the time for setting up and conducting mechanical loading in the impact group. ROI selection followed the previously described rules.

Oxygen control

In both impact and no impact groups, half of the samples were tested and imaged under normal oxygen tension (ranging from 4.0 to 6.2 mg/l throughout the test, with a mean value of 4.74 mg/l (SD 0.49)) and the other half were under controlled oxygen tension (ranging from 0.6 to 3.9 mg/l throughout the test, with a mean value of 1.87 mg/l (SD 0.68)). Oxygen tension was adjusted using the 3D-printed oxygen chamber with a nitrogen inlet (Figure 1a).27 The inlet connected to a nitrogen tank, which was turned on to create a low oxygen condition. The chamber was covered by parafilm to reduce exchange with ambient air. Throughout each test, the dissolved oxygen level in the solution was measured using an oxygen probe (MW600 dissolved oxygen metre; Milwaukee Instruments, USA).

Cell viability and crack imaging

Following post-impact ORI, cartilage samples were removed from the backplate and put into a well containing 2 ml PBS, 1 µl calcein AM, and 4 µl of EthD-III. Samples were incubated for 15 minutes at room temperature, then were rinsed using PBS before imaging. Images of the flat surface were acquired in green (excitation 470 to 490 nm, emission 500 to 550 nm) and red fluorescent (excitation 550 to 570 nm, emission 600 to 640 nm) channels at a magnification of 4× and 10×. After viability imaging, samples were wiped with India ink to visualize cracks and brightfield images of the articular surface were acquired at 4×.

Gene expression analysis

A second set of samples (an additional 24 samples from an additional 3 pigs, n = 6 for each group) were used for reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR). Shortly after post-loading ORI was done, samples were snap frozen using liquid nitrogen and stored at -80°C until further processing. Total RNA extraction, complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis, and RT-qPCR was performed as previously described.28 Briefly, samples were manually crushed and homogenized in TRIzol (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) with a handheld tissue homogenizer (Omni International, USA), and homogenized samples were mixed with chloroform (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After centrifuging, supernatant was transferred to E.Z.N.A. Tissue RNA Kit column system (Omega Bio-tek, Inc., USA) and total RNA was extracted following the manufacturer’s instructions. Template RNA was then used for cDNA synthesis using qScript cDNA SuperMix (Quantabio, USA), and gene expression was quantified by RT-qPCR with PerfeCTa SYBR Green Fastmix (Quantabio).

Genes that were measured include collagen type II (Col2), aggrecan (ACAN), matrix metalloproteinase-3 (MMP3), and hypoxia-inducible factor 1 (HIF1α). Peptidylprolyl isomerase A (PPIA) was used as the housekeeping gene. Succinate dehydrogenase complex flavoprotein subunit A (SDHA) was originally selected as housekeeping gene, but was not used because it failed to amplify in some of the samples. A disintegrin and metalloproteinase with thrombospondin motifs 5 (ADAMTS5) was measured but omitted from analysis for the same reason. Primer sequences were obtained from other studies using the same species (Supplementary Table i).28-30 Gene expression was normalized to that of the housekeeping gene and then calculated as fold-change relative to the average level of the ‘no impact’ group in low oxygen.

Post-processing

A custom Matlab (R2021b) code was used to track the deformation of the sample and backplate during impact. The aluminium backplate was highly reflective, and therefore had the highest intensity in the images. The cartilage explant had lower intensity, but was brighter than the impact tip. By finding the position where the intensity changed, the Matlab code automatically marked the boundaries of the sample. Engineering strain directly under the impact tip (ϵ), in each frame was calculated by dividing the thickness (t) of the sample in that frame (distance between the impact tip and the backplate platen) by the reference thickness before impact. Force (F) was calculated based on the leaf spring-like behaviour of backplate using F = k * d, where d is the backplate displacement and k is the spring constant previously determined by fitting an experimental force-displacement curve. After applying a second-order Savitzky-Golay filter, peak strain and peak force were determined by the max function in Matlab. Strain at the time of peak force was also obtained.

Statistical analysis

For ORI results, all 40 samples were included in statistical analysis. The samples were divided into four experimental groups: impact at normal oxygen tension, impact at low oxygen tension, unloaded control at normal oxygen tension, and unloaded control at low oxygen tension (n = 10 in each group). For samples in the unloaded at low oxygen tension group, one set of measurements (channel 1 intensity, channel 2 intensity, and ORR, all normalized to the baseline value of each sample) made in the far distance bin in the second time slot (15 to 18 mins) was excluded because of low image quality. For samples of ‘impact at low oxygen tension’ group, one set of measurements made in the far distance bin in the first time slot (0 to 5 mins) was excluded for the same reason.

Statistical analysis was performed using Matlab (R2021b) and SPSS (v.28.0.2; IBM, USA). The normality of channel 2 intensity and ORR were confirmed by a Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Channel 1 intensity underwent a Yeo-Johnson transformation and the normality of transformed data was confirmed by a Kolmogorov-Smirnov test (Supplementary Table ii). An N-Way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied to examine the effects of impact, oxygen level, time slot, distance bin, and their interactions on the ORI metrics. Significance level was set as 0.05. For significant main effects, post-hoc Tukey-Kramer comparisons were used to investigate the pairwise significance.

For gene expression results, a one-way ANOVA was applied to detect difference of fold-change level among the four experimental groups (n = 6 in each group). Analysis was also performed in Matlab (R2021b) with a significance level of 0.05.

Results

Interaction effects between impact, oxygen, and time

The interaction between impact and oxygen level significantly affected all ORI outcomes (Figure 2). Impact in the normal oxygen group had significantly higher channel 1 and channel 2 intensities compared to other groups (impact in low oxygen, no impact in normal oxygen, and no impact in low oxygen).

Interaction effects of impact and oxygen tension on a) Channel 1, nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NAD(P)H); b) Channel 2, flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD); and c) ORR. Lines and analysis of variance p-values indicate significance by post-hoc analysis.

There was no difference in intensity in either channel among all time slots for both impact and no impact groups (Figure 3). Within 28 to 31 minutes after impact, ORR was significantly higher than all the other groups. Full statistical results are available in Supplementary Tables ii to vi.

Interaction effects of impact and post-impact time on a) Channel 1, nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NAD(P)H); b) Channel 2, flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD); c) ORR. Lines and p-values indicate significance by post-hoc analysis.

Achieved loading parameters

Because of the deformation of the backplate, the achieved maximum strain was lower in magnitude than the set target (-100%). The maximum strain was -37.50% ± 10.32%. The maximum force was -62.23 ± 12.00 N, while the engineering strain corresponding to the maximum force time was -31.73% ± 7.38%. Peak contact pressure was estimated as -9.69 ± 1.77 MPa.

Visual evaluation of crack patterns, cell viability, and gene expression

After impact, all loaded samples had a single crack at the centre (Figure 4, Supplementary Figure b). On the focal plane, there were visibly fewer cells stained green (alive) in impacted samples compared to unimpacted ones, especially around the crack (Figure 5, Supplementary Figures c and d).

There was no significant difference found between any of the groups for any of the four genes that were measured (Supplementary Figure e). Specifically, neither loading nor oxygen tension affected the gene expression in these samples.

Discussion

This study applied ORI to measure the temporal changes of cartilage redox balance in response to impact injury at distinct oxygen tension, and found that oxygen tension altered cartilage post-impact responses. At normal oxygen tension (4.74 ± 0.49 mg/l), the intensity of both FAD and NAD(P)H increased after impact compared to non-impact control. At lower oxygen tension (1.87 ± 0.68 mg/l), however, there was no difference between impact and non-impact groups in terms of FAD and NAD(P)H intensity. Impact at normal oxygen tension had significantly higher relative intensity compared to all the other interactions, indicating that any individual effect of impact and oxygen on fluorescent intensity is dominated by the interplay of impact and normal oxygen level. Within 28 to 31 minutes after impact, ORR significantly increased, indicating that increased FAD might be a progressive response to injury.

The increased NAD(P)H observed in this study may reflect the accumulation of NADH due to post-impact mitochondrial dysfunction, as seen in previous studies.11,32,33 Because NADH is oxidized to its non-fluorescent form at mitochondria complex I,34 mitochondrial dysfunction can result in accumulated NADH. The increased FAD intensity might indicate decreased consumption of FAD. At mitochondrial complex II, succinate is oxidized to fumarate and FAD is reduced to FADH2,35 which is then oxidized back, supplying electrons in subsequent redox reactions and producing ROS.36,37 Mitochondrial dysfunction can impair oxidation of succinate and therefore prevent conversion of FAD.

In this study, we found that injurious impact-induced cartilage autofluorescence changes observed at normal oxygen tension were absent at lower oxygen tension. Previous research showed that oxygen tension alters cartilage responses to mechanical loading. Specifically, intermittent compression loading on full-thickness cartilage increased production of NO at 20% oxygen, but not at 1% or 5% oxygen.23 Further, hypoxia (2% O2) and anoxia (< 1% O2) reduced ROS level in human OA chondrocytes.38 Culturing cartilage at 2% O2 can decrease MMP expression20 and increase type II collagen and proteoglycan production.19 Together, these studies suggest that low oxygen tension could be protective against cartilage damage, which may explain why changes in fluorescent intensity were not seen at low oxygen in our study. It is worth noting that the absence of redox changes to traumatic injury at low oxygen tension does not imply that redox is irrelevant to in vivo trauma; this study only focused on peracute response of redox to trauma instead of the long-term progression. In addition, in vivo oxygen tension might change after cartilage damage,39,40 which would affect the long-term consequences of impact injury.

Responses of cartilage to impact loading vary across studies due to multiple sources of variability. For example, half of bovine cartilage explants had visible damage after an averaged peak stress of 24 MPa,41 while no rabbit cartilage explants had visible damage after peak stress of 30 MPa.42 High-rate loading at the strain rate of 80%/sec with peak stresses of 1 to 8 MPa significantly reduced cell viability in loaded bovine cartilage compared to the unloaded control,43 while another group concluded that cell death initiated at 10 MPa (strain rate not reported).44 In our current study, peak contact pressures of approximately 10 MPa did not induce widespread cell death; however, slight increases in cell death around cracks and peak stresses in the same range as previous studies indicate that our impact loading is comparable to published results. In our previous study, we demonstrated that both NAD(P)H and FAD intensity decreased after impact loading compared to baseline,45 which appears contradictory to our current results. The present study included a non-impact group rather than comparing to baseline, and found elevated fluorescent intensity in both channels compared to the control group. Given that samples were hemicylinders, the centre part, where baseline images were taken, was the thickest, which may have led to variation in intensities. In the unloaded control group, the mean ratio of channel 1 intensity of images taken in the short, medium, and far distance bin to baseline value was 0.92 (SD 0.08), 0.96 (SD 0.10), and 0.94 (SD 0.10), respectively, while that of channel 2 was 0.78 (SD 0.11), 0.72 (SD 0.10), and 0.61 (SD 0.11). Therefore, the differences in conclusions of these two studies may be partially due to the spatial variation in intensity. Moreover, the achieved mean contact pressure in the previous study was -1.37 MPa (SD 1.01), lower than that in the current study (-9.62 MPa (SD 1.73)). All samples had visible cracks after loading in the current study, while only 13 out of 17 samples had cracks in the previous study, so it is also possible that the loading in this study exceeded a certain threshold and triggered different downstream responses. Taken together, impact loading does not induce binary responses in cartilage. Instead, variations in factors including species and loading regime (including but not limited to peak stress and strain rate) alter its effect.

Although different from our expectations, the lack of significance in gene expression (negative results) remains important. This lack of response to mechanical loading may be due to the timeframe at which we analyzed gene expression being insufficient for responses, indicating that redox balance dynamics are faster than gene expression changes for the selected genes. Previous studies have often cultured tissue to allow time between impact and gene expression,46,47 however this would not accurately reflect the peracute measures that were the focus of this study. Alternatively, other studies indicate that the genes cJUN and cFOS are more quickly responsive to mechanical loading, as well as having an important relationship to mitochondrial dysfunction in cartilage.48,49 Given the limited RNA yield from each explant, we were unable to analyze these genes in this study. The lack of effect of oxygen tension on gene expression likely stems from the relatively short time over which samples were under varied oxygen tension. Again, however, the effect of oxygen tension was a significant factor in ORI metrics, emphasizing that redox balance dynamics are faster than more traditional measures such as gene expression.

The current study has some limitations: the spatial variation in fluorescent intensity observed in non-impact samples might have been caused by the hemicylinder shape, making direct comparison within the same sample difficult. Additionally, the nitrogen tank was manually turned on and off, introducing variation in oxygen tension across tests. This study measured dissolved oxygen concentration in the buffer solution, which is not directly comparable to gas percentage, a metric commonly used in other studies culturing cartilage explants or chondrocytes under different oxygen tension. The epifluorescent microscope used in this study only allowed imaging close (within 50 µm) to the cut surface, where the process of bisecting the cartilage could affect cellular metabolic status; however, samples were cultured between bisecting and imaging to allow the response to settle. Lastly, the cartilage was harvested from skeletally immature pigs, which is expected to have greater cell density and more robust cellular responses to stimuli than cartilage from mature animals or from adult humans.

In summary, this study showed that cartilage NAD(P)H and FAD autofluorescence intensities increased after traumatic impact at room oxygen tension, but this effect was not observed at lower oxygen tension. More studies are needed to further understand the underlying mechanisms of altered autofluorescence intensity, and build a detailed correlation between ORI readout and other redox balance indicators like ROS production and gene expression.
