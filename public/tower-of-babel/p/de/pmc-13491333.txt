Transcatheter Aortic Valve Migration and Embolization During Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement: A Contemporary Management Review With a Practical Bailout Algorithm
Authors: Mohammad As Sayaideh, Eric Broadfoot, Taylor Goulding-Avedisian, Nagaraj Sanchitha Honganur, Mohammed Elzeneini, Bassam Omar, Nasser Lakkis, Kevin DeAndrade
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Transcatheter aortic valve replacement has become an established therapy for severe aortic stenosis across the surgical-risk spectrum. Although procedural safety has improved, transcatheter valve embolization or migration remains an uncommon but high-impact complication that may require immediate rescue under conditions of severe acute aortic regurgitation, hemodynamic instability, coronary compromise, or branch-vessel risk. Existing evidence is derived primarily from registries, observational cohorts, technical reviews, systematic reviews, and case-based reports rather than prospective comparative trials. Building on prior descriptions of device-embolization mechanisms and location-based management, this review synthesizes contemporary evidence into an operator-oriented phenotype-based framework. We classify transcatheter valve embolization or migration into left ventricular/left ventricular outflow tract migration, annular malposition with instability, ascending aortic embolization, distal aortic embolization, and delayed migration, while emphasizing that these phenotypes may overlap or evolve during the procedure. For each phenotype, we summarize reported mechanisms, platform-specific considerations, hemodynamic hazards, and commonly described bailout strategies. Because comparative data are lacking, the proposed framework should be interpreted as an evidence-informed practical decision aid rather than recommendation-level guidance. Standardized reporting and contemporary multicenter data are needed to refine risk prediction, define procedural success, and validate phenotype-specific rescue pathways.

Introduction

Transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) has transformed the treatment of severe aortic stenosis and is now routinely performed across the surgical-risk spectrum. As procedural volume and anatomic complexity have increased, operators are increasingly required to manage uncommon but high-consequence complications, including those occurring in bicuspid anatomy, valve-in-valve (ViV) procedures, and off-label treatment of pure native aortic regurgitation (AR).1,2 Among these complications, transcatheter valve embolization or migration (TVEM) remains one of the most dramatic intraprocedural failures.3, 4, 5

Although modern valve platforms, refined computed tomography (CT) planning, and increased operator experience have reduced its frequency, TVEM continues to occur in approximately 0.5% to 1% of contemporary TAVR procedures in major data sets, with reported incidence varying according to device generation, anatomy, indication, center experience, and definitions used.4, 5, 6, 7 The risk is not uniform across clinical settings. In particular, pure native AR presents a distinct anchoring challenge because the annulus and leaflets are often large, noncalcified, and dynamically unstable; contemporary analyses suggest that migration and reintervention risks differ materially between off-label conventional platforms and dedicated AR devices.1,2,8,9

Prior studies have described the incidence, mechanisms, outcomes, and technical management of TAVR valve embolization and migration, including registry analyses, device-embolization reviews, case-based rescue strategies, CT follow-up studies, and location-based classifications.3, 4, 5, 6, 7,10,11 The incremental purpose of this review is not to replace these prior frameworks or propose a new end point definition. Rather, this review consolidates available evidence into a practical, operator-oriented algorithm that links the observed displacement phenotype with hemodynamic hazard, device platform, wire control, coronary and branch-vessel risk, and feasibility of catheter-based rescue.

When TVEM occurs in the catheterization laboratory, the key question is procedural: what should the operator do next? The answer depends on the direction of displacement, the degree of hemodynamic compromise, the valve platform and generation, whether wire position and frame control are preserved, and whether critical structures such as the coronary ostia, mitral apparatus, ascending aorta, or arch vessels are threatened. A summary of an operator-facing approach to transcatheter valve embolization or migration during TAVR. Management begins with identification of the migration phenotype and the dominant hemodynamic or anatomic hazard, followed by assessment of whether percutaneous rescue remains feasible. Percutaneous rescue is most favorable when wire access is preserved, the valve frame can be stabilized, and coronary, branch-vessel, mitral, and aortic wall safety can be maintained. Phenotype-specific pathways include selected repositioning for shallow stable left ventricle (LV)/left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) migration, second-valve implantation for annular malposition with severe residual AR, snare stabilization with controlled aortic parking for ascending aortic embolization, selected endovascular fixation or surgery for distal aortic embolization, and heart team reassessment with redo TAVR or surgery for delayed migration. Phenotype-based bailout algorithm for transcatheter valve migration and embolization during transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR). A summary of an operator-facing approach to transcatheter valve embolization or migration during TAVR. Management begins with identification of the migration phenotype and the dominant hemodynamic or anatomic hazard, followed by assessment of whether percutaneous rescue remains feasible. Percutaneous rescue is most favorable when wire access is preserved, the valve frame can be stabilized, and coronary, branch-vessel, mitral, and aortic wall safety can be maintained. If feasibility is lost because of wire loss, frame instability or deformation, refractory shock, severe aortic regurgitation (AR) with instability, coronary or branch-vessel compromise, aortic wall injury, deep left ventricular position, or mitral apparatus interaction, early surgical or endovascular escalation should be considered. Phenotype-specific pathways include selected repositioning for shallow stable left ventricle (LV)/left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) migration, second-valve implantation for annular malposition with severe residual AR, snare stabilization with controlled aortic parking for ascending aortic embolization, selected endovascular fixation or surgery for distal aortic embolization, and heart team reassessment with redo TAVR or surgery for delayed migration. Evidence acquisition and synthesis

This manuscript is a narrative management review rather than a formal systematic review or meta-analysis. It was designed to synthesize contemporary evidence on TVEM during TAVR with emphasis on mechanisms, device-platform considerations, anatomic phenotype, prevention, and intraprocedural bailout strategy.

PubMed/MEDLINE and Google Scholar were searched from database inception through April 2026 for English-language registries, multicenter cohorts, single-center cohorts, technical reviews, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, editorials, case series, and case reports addressing TAVR valve embolization, valve migration, malposition, urgent supplementary valve implantation, ViV bailout, snaring, retrieval, valve parking, endovascular fixation, pure native AR, and dedicated AR devices. Search terms included combinations of “TAVR,” “TAVI,” “valve embolization,” “valve migration,” “malposition,” “supplementary valve,” “two-valve TAVR,” “valve-in-valve rescue,” “snare,” “retrieval,” “aortic parking,” “left ventricular migration,” “native aortic regurgitation,” and “dedicated aortic regurgitation device.” Sources were selected for inclusion when they provided information on incidence, mechanisms, phenotype classification, procedural rescue, prevention, follow-up, or terminology relevant to TVEM management.

Because prospective comparative trials are lacking, the evidence was interpreted according to study design, sample size, era, device generation, relevance to contemporary practice, and direct applicability to procedural rescue. Registry and multicenter cohort data were prioritized for incidence, temporal trends, and outcomes. Technical reviews and case reports were used primarily to illustrate rare phenotypes, procedural cues, and rescue techniques that cannot be adequately studied in a randomized fashion. Table 1 was, therefore, reorganized as a practical evidence map with interpretive weight, rather than a simple chronological bibliography. Similarly, Table 3 separates commonly reported pathways from case-based or anatomy-dependent techniques so that feasibility is not overinterpreted as comparative efficacy.

The evidence base has important limitations. Registry and administrative data sets may misclassify or merge malposition, migration, embolization, urgent supplementary valve implantation, and multiple-valve implantation. Case reports and technical series are particularly vulnerable to publication bias because successful or novel rescue maneuvers are more likely to be reported than unsuccessful attempts or cases proceeding directly to surgery. Selection bias is also substantial because operators may attempt snaring, repositioning, ViV implantation, or endovascular fixation only in anatomies that appear technically favorable, whereas unstable or unfavorable cases may proceed directly to surgery or hemodynamic rescue without detailed publication. Reported strategies are also heterogeneous across valve platforms, recapturable vs nonrecapturable systems, annular and root anatomy, indication, operator experience, surgical backup, and era of device technology. As a result, published procedural success rates are not consistently extractable or directly comparable, and the feasibility of a technique should not be interpreted as proof of generalizable safety, durability, or superiority over alternative bailout approaches.

Definitions and nomenclature

The terminology surrounding failed valve positioning has historically been inconsistent.3,28 Prior TAVR literature has variably used malposition, migration, embolization, ectopic deployment, and urgent second-valve implantation to describe overlapping clinical scenarios. The Valve Academic Research Consortium (VARC)-3 provides standardized end point definitions for aortic valve clinical research and remains the preferred framework for clinical-trial comparability; however, bedside bailout decisions often require more granular procedural descriptors than end point terminology alone can provide.12 The definitions used here are intended to support intraprocedural communication and do not propose a new formal end point.

In this review, the terms malposition, migration, and embolization are used as related points along a procedural continuum rather than mutually exclusive events. Ectopic deployment refers broadly to release outside the intended annular landing zone. Malposition refers to deployment that is too high or too low while the valve remains at least partly engaged with the annulus. Migration refers to subsequent displacement after partial or complete release, while some interaction with the annular complex may persist. Embolization refers to loss of functional annular position with displacement into the left ventricle (LV)/left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT), ascending aorta, arch, or descending thoracic aorta.

This distinction is pragmatic. A case may begin as annular malposition with severe residual AR, progress to instability, and ultimately require urgent implantation of a second valve or management as frank embolization. Similarly, shallow LVOT migration may evolve into deep ventricular embolization, frame inversion, or interaction with the mitral apparatus. The proposed phenotype framework is, therefore, intended to help operators recognize the dominant hazard at a given moment while acknowledging that the phenotype may change dynamically during the procedure.

Delayed migration refers to displacement recognized after the index procedure, ranging from hours to years later. Although uncommon, delayed migration is clinically important because it may present outside the controlled procedural environment, with heart failure, prosthetic dysfunction, or incidental imaging findings, and may require redo TAVR or surgery.24, 25, 26

Stabilization, as used in this review, refers to securing the embolized valve in a fixed, non-obstructive position—most commonly through snare-assisted repositioning or endovascular fixation—rather than retrieval, thereby allowing the safe implantation of a second valve and subsequent observation.5,20 This approach is distinct from conservative observation, as it involves active procedural measures to prevent further migration or vascular compromise.

Epidemiology and clinical impact

Early pooled reports based predominantly on case reports and small series suggested an incidence approaching 1%.4 More reliable estimates subsequently emerged from registry and cohort-level data. In the international TRAVEL registry, TVEM occurred in 273 of 29,636 TAVR procedures (0.92%), with ascending aortic embolization representing the most common phenotype and ventricular migration occurring less frequently but generally carrying a greater procedural hazard.5

The clinical importance of TVEM far exceeds its incidence. In TRAVEL, TVEM was associated with higher mortality, more frequent multiple-valve implantation, and occasional conversion to surgery compared with uncomplicated TAVR.5 These consequences are biologically plausible because an unstable valve may produce severe acute AR, abrupt loss of forward flow, coronary obstruction, branch-vessel compromise, prolonged catheter manipulation, embolized-frame complications, or need for urgent second-valve implantation.3,5,14

It is important to distinguish 3 related but nonidentical entities: frank valve migration or embolization, annular malposition requiring urgent supplementary valve implantation, and multiple-valve implantation scenarios more broadly. The Landes et al6 multicenter cohort of urgent supplementary valve implantation provides important insight into 2-valve TAVR practice, but it should not be interpreted as synonymous with all TVEM events because many cases are driven by malposition with severe residual regurgitation rather than complete valve embolization. Likewise, early PARTNER multiple-valve analyses remain historically important but reflect older-generation devices and procedural practice.10

Temporal trends should also be considered. Contemporary device iterations, recapturable systems, improved CT planning, and greater operator experience have likely reduced preventable malposition and embolization in routine calcific aortic stenosis. However, TVEM remains relevant as TAVR expands into anatomically complex and lower-anchoring substrates, particularly pure native AR, very large annuli, horizontal aortas, bicuspid valves, ViV anatomies, and cases with challenging coaxiality.1,2,7, 8, 9

Mechanisms and predisposing factors

Transcatheter valve embolization or migration is usually multifactorial and reflects the interaction of patient anatomy, prosthesis characteristics, and procedural technique. In calcific aortic stenosis, leaflet and annular calcium contribute to anchoring. In contrast, pure native AR is a classic migration-prone substrate because the annulus may be large, elliptical, and relatively noncalcified, depriving the prosthesis of reliable fixation points.1,2 Other anatomic risk features include large annular dimensions, annular eccentricity, bicuspid morphology, root or sinotubular junction dilation, large sinus dimensions, horizontal aorta, poor coaxial alignment, and potentially hyperdynamic ventricular or outflow conditions that may destabilize a marginally anchored prosthesis.2,7,29,30

Procedural contributors include high or low initial positioning, undersizing, loss of rapid pacing capture during balloon-expandable deployment, inadequate coaxiality, incomplete assessment of annular or LVOT dimensions, postdilation-related displacement, and catheter, balloon, or wire interaction after release.3, 4, 5,14 Some associations are well supported at the substrate level, such as pure native AR, low calcification, poor anchoring, and migration risk. More specific phenotype-platform associations are drawn from smaller observational data sets and technical reports and should, therefore, be interpreted as tendencies rather than deterministic rules.

Device generation also matters. Older platforms were less forgiving and less recapturable. Newer-generation systems may reduce some deployment errors but introduce platform-specific considerations related to frame length, radial force, recapturability, sealing skirts, commissural alignment, coronary access after rescue, and feasibility of ViV implantation. Device platform–specific considerations

The mechanism, direction, and salvageability of TVEM are partly influenced by the valve platform, but platform associations should be framed as tendencies rather than fixed rules. Balloon-expandable valves may embolize cephalad during deployment if rapid pacing is inadequate, positioning is high, balloon inflation occurs before stable annular engagement, or the valve is ejected by ventricular pressure.3,5 When these events are immediately recognized, and wire position is preserved, snare stabilization, controlled aortic parking, and prompt implantation of a second valve may be feasible.5,14

Self-expanding valves may migrate ventricularly or demonstrate delayed instability when anchoring is inadequate, especially in large annuli, low-calcification anatomy, pure native AR, or suboptimal coaxial alignment.3,7,15 Recapturable systems can provide an opportunity for correction during partial deployment, but after full release, management depends on frame position, depth, interaction with the mitral apparatus, coronary risk, and ability to maintain wire control. Shallow LVOT migration may be amenable to selective repositioning in rare stable cases, whereas deep ventricular migration, frame inversion, or mitral apparatus interaction should prompt early consideration of ViV rescue or surgery rather than prolonged manipulation.15, 16, 17,27

Valve generation and design features affect bailout selection. Operators should consider radial force, frame height, commissural or coronary access implications, recapture capability, sealing skirt position, the ability to safely snare without frame deformation, and whether a second valve can be implanted without compromising the coronaries, mitral apparatus, or aortic wall. Because pure native AR is a poor-anchoring substrate, dedicated devices with anchoring mechanisms may reduce device failure, migration, residual regurgitation, or reintervention compared with off-label conventional devices in selected patients; however, availability, anatomy, local expertise, and long-term durability remain relevant considerations.8,9

Phenotype-based classification

For practical management, TVEM can be organized into 5 clinically relevant phenotypes: retrograde migration into the LV or LVOT, annular malposition with instability, ascending aortic embolization, distal aortic embolization into the arch or descending thoracic aorta, and delayed migration after the index procedure.3, 4, 5,24,25 Prior literature has already distinguished aortic from ventricular events, acute from delayed migration, and location-based rescue strategies. The incremental value of the present classification is the integration of displacement phenotype with hemodynamic hazard, platform-specific constraints, wire and frame control, and a practical bailout pathway.

Retrograde ventricular migration is generally among the most dangerous phenotypes because even limited downward movement can precipitate severe acute AR, loss of forward flow, interaction with the mitral apparatus, or rapid hemodynamic deterioration. Annular malposition may precede complete embolization and should be considered unstable when associated with severe residual regurgitation, frame rocking, progressive movement, or inability to achieve a durable seal. Ascending aortic embolization is the most common direction of movement in published series and is often the most amenable to catheter-based stabilization when coronary flow is preserved, and the displaced valve can be controlled. Distal embolization raises additional concerns regarding arch-vessel obstruction, aortic wall trauma, and stability of the parked frame. Delayed migration typically presents with prosthetic dysfunction, heart failure, or imaging findings and requires individualized heart team evaluation.4,5,7,24,25

Hybrid and edge-case phenotypes should be anticipated. Annular malposition with severe AR can rapidly evolve into frank embolization. An inverted or severely deformed frame may not fit neatly into shallow-versus-deep migration categories. Delayed migration in ViV anatomy may differ mechanistically from native-valve TAVR migration. Therefore, the proposed classification should be used as a real-time organizing framework rather than a rigid taxonomy.

Bailout strategies by location

Management of TVEM is best interpreted through the lens of phenotype rather than a single pooled rescue metric. Because comparative prospective data do not exist, the strategies summarized below and in Table 3 represent commonly reported rescue patterns and expert synthesis, not guideline-level recommendations. Table 3 also identifies evidence-level cautions and escalation triggers to reduce the risk of overinterpreting case-based strategies as established standards.

For shallow LVOT migration, controlled balloon- or snare-assisted repositioning has been described when the valve remains close to the annulus, wire control is preserved, and the patient is sufficiently stable for deliberate manipulation.15, 16, 17 However, this evidence consists primarily of case reports and technical experience; immediate technical success should not be generalized across anatomies or assumed to imply durable safety. If severe AR, progressive instability, frame deformation, or hemodynamic collapse develops, prolonged repositioning attempts should be abandoned in favor of definitive ViV rescue or surgery.

Deep ventricular migration carries a narrower safety margin. The dominant hazards are severe regurgitation, loss of forward flow, LV cavity instability, frame inversion, and interaction with the mitral apparatus. Published experience is limited and often cautionary.4,16,17 In reported practice, early definitive rescue is often favored over extended catheter manipulation, particularly when the valve is deep, inverted, entangled, or cannot be stabilized.

Annular malposition with severe residual regurgitation is most commonly managed with urgent implantation of a second valve when the initial valve remains in or near the annular complex but fails to achieve a stable seal.5,6 This scenario overlaps with, but is not identical to, frank embolization. The goal is rapid restoration of competence and forward flow before malposition progresses to complete migration or hemodynamic collapse.

Ascending aortic embolization is often salvageable percutaneously when the valve can be stabilized, coronary flow is preserved, and there is no aortic injury. Commonly reported strategies include snare-assisted stabilization, intentional parking of the displaced valve in a non-obstructive ascending aortic position, and implantation of a second valve at the annulus.3,5,7,14 Coronary obstruction, inability to engage or protect the coronaries, progressive cephalad movement, or inability to stabilize the frame should prompt early escalation.

Distal arch or descending aortic embolization is more heterogeneous. Some cases can be managed with distal parking if the valve is stable and branch vessels remain uncompromised. Selected reports describe endovascular fixation, scaffold-type techniques, or branch-preserving stent strategies when the embolized frame threatens branch vessels or remains unstable.20, 21, 22, 23 These techniques remain supported primarily by case reports and small technical series and should be described as anatomy-dependent rescue options rather than established pathways.

Delayed migration requires individualized heart team management guided by echocardiography, CT, symptom status, prosthesis function, coronary and branch-vessel relationships, and feasibility of repeat anchoring. Redo TAVR may be feasible in selected anatomies, whereas surgery may be preferable when repeat transcatheter fixation is unlikely to be durable or when adjacent structures are threatened.24, 25, 26

Interpreting bailout success

A key limitation of the TVEM literature is that comparative prospective data do not exist, and reported success is inconsistently defined. Success may refer to immediate hemodynamic rescue, final valve competence, avoidance of surgery, technical stabilization of an embolized frame, 30-day survival, stroke avoidance, preserved coronary or branch-vessel flow, or absence of late aortic wall injury. These outcomes are related but not interchangeable.4, 5, 6, 7

For this reason, bailout outcomes should be interpreted across 3 domains. Procedural success refers to immediate stabilization, restoration of forward flow, elimination of severe AR, and preservation of coronary and branch-vessel perfusion. Clinical success refers to survival without disabling stroke, emergent surgery, recurrent collapse, or major vascular complication. Anatomic durability refers to stable valve position, absence of delayed embolized-frame complications, preserved aortic and branch-vessel integrity, and no late aortic wall injury on surveillance imaging when indicated.

These domains may diverge. A valve that is successfully snared and parked during the index procedure may still warrant follow-up imaging depending on final location, frame orientation, aortic wall contact, and branch-vessel proximity. Frumkin et al’s7 CT follow-up experience is particularly relevant because it emphasizes that apparently successful acute management does not eliminate the need to consider late anatomic consequences. Accordingly, terms such as “most reliable,” “dominant,” or “successful” should be tied to the specific outcome being described and should not be interpreted as comparative efficacy.

Several additional limitations should temper the interpretation of the proposed framework. The pathway is not a validated management algorithm and should not be interpreted as recommendation-level guidance. Rather, it is an expert-informed synthesis of rescue approaches described in observational series, registry reports, technical reviews, and case-based literature. Several techniques discussed, including snaring, valve parking, complex retrieval, fixation, and repositioning strategies, derive largely from anecdotal experience and highly specialized structural heart programs; they may not be broadly generalizable or reproducible across all TAVR programs and may require substantial operator experience, institutional expertise, surgical or endovascular backup, and individualized heart team judgment. Procedural decisions are, therefore, best tailored to migration phenotype, hemodynamic stability, anatomy, available equipment, operator experience, and local institutional resources.

Prevention

Because bailout evidence is limited and rescue may be time-critical, prevention remains central to TVEM management. CT planning is foundational for assessing annular area and perimeter, annular eccentricity, leaflet and LVOT calcification, LVOT dimensions, coronary height, sinus of Valsalva dimensions, sinotubular junction size, aortic root geometry, and deployment coaxiality. In poor-anchoring anatomies, particularly pure native AR, the heart team should explicitly determine whether secure fixation is realistically achievable with the planned prosthesis or whether a dedicated AR device or surgical approach is more appropriate.1,2,8,9

Indication-specific prevention is important. In pure native AR, a lack of leaflet and annular calcification, annular dilation, root dilation, and large sinus or sinotubular junction dimensions may reduce anchoring and increase the risk of migration. In bicuspid valves, asymmetric calcium and elliptical annular geometry may create eccentric deployment forces. In ViV procedures, prior frame geometry, true internal diameter, coronary height, and anchoring within the surgical or transcatheter scaffold should be considered.

Platform-specific preparation should be explicit before valve deployment. In balloon-expandable valve cases, rapid pacing capture, wire position, balloon alignment, and coaxial deployment should be confirmed before inflation. In self-expanding valve cases, operators should use the recapturable phase to correct malposition, avoid accepting unstable low or high deployment, and maintain awareness of frame interaction with the LVOT, aortic root, and coronaries. Across platforms, obvious instability should prompt early corrective action before partial malposition progresses to complete embolization.

High-risk cases should be prepared as potential bailout procedures before the first valve is deployed. Practical steps include ensuring the availability of snares, appropriately sized balloons, a second valve, large-bore access management tools, coronary protection equipment when indicated, hemodynamic support planning in selected unstable patients, and rapid access to surgical or endovascular backup. Prevention, therefore, includes both avoiding migration and ensuring that rescue can be executed quickly if migration occurs.

Recurrent migration should prompt reassessment beyond simple valve sizing because prosthesis instability may reflect dynamic ventricular or outflow conditions, unfavorable root geometry, inadequate anchoring substrate, or interaction between the initial prosthesis and the second valve.

Proposed bailout algorithm

The proposed bailout framework can be summarized in 4 steps. First, identify the dominant phenotype: LV/LVOT migration, unstable annular malposition, ascending aortic embolization, distal embolization, or delayed migration. Second, define the immediate hemodynamic hazard: severe acute regurgitation, loss of forward flow, coronary obstruction, branch-vessel compromise, mitral apparatus interaction, or aortic wall injury. Third, determine whether percutaneous rescue is feasible using practical cues such as wire control, frame stability, patient stability, coronary and branch-vessel safety, and absence of prohibitive frame deformation or mitral interaction. Fourth, select an individualized definitive pathway: selective repositioning in rare shallow stable ventricular cases, urgent second-valve implantation for unstable annular position or severe residual regurgitation, snare stabilization and aortic parking for selected ascending aortic embolization, anatomy-specific endovascular rescue for selected distal embolization, or redo TAVR versus surgery for delayed migration.

Percutaneous rescue is most feasible when the wire position is preserved, the frame can be stabilized without worsening embolization, the patient remains sufficiently stable for controlled manipulation, coronary and branch-vessel flow are preserved or protectable, and the valve is not inverted, severely deformed, entangled with the mitral apparatus, or associated with aortic injury. In contrast, catheter-based strategies should be abandoned or rapidly escalated when there is loss of secure wire access, inability to stabilize the frame with snare or balloon support, progressive frame instability, severe residual AR with rapid hemodynamic deterioration, unresolvable coronary compromise, branch-vessel obstruction, aortic wall injury, deep ventricular position, mitral interaction, or recurrent migration despite attempted anchoring.

Coronary risk should be assessed systematically during rescue. Operators should evaluate whether the embolized or second valve may obstruct the coronary ostia, whether coronary engagement remains possible after valve parking or ViV implantation, whether coronary protection is needed before definitive valve deployment, and whether the displaced frame compromises sinus flow or catheter access. This is especially important in ascending aortic embolization, ViV rescue, low coronary heights, small sinuses, bulky leaflet calcification, and anatomies in which a parked frame may interfere with future coronary access.

The Central Illustration should, therefore, be interpreted as a structured decision aid rather than a rigid protocol. The evidence gradient varies by phenotype: urgent second-valve implantation for malposition with severe residual regurgitation is supported by larger observational cohorts; snare stabilization and aortic parking for ascending embolization are supported by registry, cohort, and technical experience; distal endovascular fixation and selective LVOT repositioning remain lower-level, anatomy-dependent strategies supported mainly by case reports and technical series. Conclusion

Transcatheter valve embolization or migration is uncommon in contemporary TAVR practice but remains one of the most consequential intraprocedural complications. Available evidence from registries, observational cohorts, technical reviews, systematic reviews, and case reports suggests that displacement direction is a dominant organizing principle for management, but not the only determinant. Device platform, valve generation, anatomy, implant depth, wire control, coronary and branch-vessel interaction, hemodynamic status, and local surgical or endovascular backup also shape the optimal response.4, 5, 6, 7

Ascending aortic embolization is the most frequently reported phenotype and is often amenable to catheter-based stabilization and second-valve implantation when coronary flow is preserved, and the displaced frame can be controlled. Retrograde migration into the LV or LVOT has a narrower safety margin and should prompt early definitive rescue when severe regurgitation, frame instability, deep ventricular position, or mitral interaction is present. Annular malposition with severe residual regurgitation overlaps with TVEM and is commonly treated with urgent supplementary valve implantation, but it should be distinguished from frank embolization when interpreting outcomes.

By framing TVEM within a phenotype-based and platform-aware decision pathway, operators can approach this rare complication with a more structured procedural strategy. However, the proposed framework has not been prospectively validated and should be viewed as an evidence-informed practical aid rather than guideline-level direction. Future multicenter registries using standardized VARC–aligned terminology and consistent reporting of procedural success, clinical outcomes, anatomic durability, and follow-up imaging are needed to refine risk prediction and validate bailout pathways.
