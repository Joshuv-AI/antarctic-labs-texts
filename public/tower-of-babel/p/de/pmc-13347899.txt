Hidden cost of care-burden and quality of life among family caregivers of chronically ill older adults in Abha, Saudi Arabia – a cross-sectional study
Authors: Asalh Mohammed Alqahtani, Manal Mohammed Hawash, Maha Ali, Mervat Mostafa Arrab, Ali Mohieldin, Khursheed Muzammil, Wagida Wafik, Mohammad Suhail Khan, Adam Dawria
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo assess the perceived burden and quality of life (QoL) of family caregivers (FCs) of chronically ill older adults in Abha, Saudi Arabia; explore the relationship between burden and QoL; and identify key sociodemographic, clinical and caregiving-related predictors of both outcomes.DesignA cross-sectional study.SettingPrimary healthcare centres in Abha city, the capital of the Asir region in southwestern Saudi Arabia.ParticipantsA total of 400 FCs of community-dwelling older adults with chronic diseases, selected using a multistage probability sampling technique. Data were collected using validated Arabic versions of the Zarit Burden Interview-22 and the Adult Carer Quality of Life questionnaire. Descriptive statistics, correlation analysis and multivariate regression models were used to identify factors influencing caregiver burden and QoL.ResultsMost caregivers were young, female, unemployed and cared for dependent older adults. More than half (59.5%) reported mild burden, 22.8% moderate and 1% severe. Regarding QoL, 47.8% had low and 52.2% moderate levels. Caregiver burden was significantly associated with poorer QoL (χ², p<0.05). Predictors of higher burden included caregiver illness, co-residency, longer caregiving duration, higher recipient dependency and being a first-degree relative. Sufficient income, being single, shorter caregiving duration and access to home care services predicted better QoL. Multivariate analysis indicated that access to home healthcare services reduced burden and improved QoL.ConclusionsCaring for chronically ill older adults in Abha imposes a considerable psychosocial and physical burden on FCs, adversely affecting their QoL. The findings underscore the urgent need for integrated caregiver support programmes, including home healthcare services, financial and educational assistance and mental health resources, to promote caregiver well-being and sustain informal elder care within Saudi Arabia’s ageing society.Keywords: Aged, Quality of Life, Caregivers, Caregiver Burden

Introduction

The increased longevity and healthcare technological advancements have resulted in more older adults living with chronic illnesses, placing high demands on healthcare systems and family caregivers (FCs) worldwide. Chronic diseases are demanding and require long-term and complex care, particularly among the ageing population.1 As per the National Council on Ageing, nearly 95% of older adults worldwide live with at least one chronic illness, while almost 80% have comorbidities concurrently.2 This increase in chronic diseases impacts not just the health of older adults but also profoundly affects the caregivers who encounter numerous challenges in delivering continuous informal care, particularly in the Arab regions, such as the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA), which is witnessing a rise in healthcare requirements owing to the ageing population and lifestyle changes, underscoring increased chronic disease prevalence exacerbated by limited health resources, ultimately intensifying the caregiving burdens.3

Caregiving is intrinsically complex and demands a multidimensional approach to meet older adults’ evolving and diverse needs. Caregiving is also deeply rooted in Saudi society, where cultural and social expectations place a significant burden on FCs to provide fundamental and primary care for older relatives.4 FCs must be able to perform various caregiving tasks daily, including medical management, medication management, mobility assistance and emotional support. This also heightens the demand for support systems that address caregivers’ training and learning needs. Therefore, with the Saudi healthcare system and services being limited, caregiving for older adults is both physically and emotionally exhausting. Caregivers may also face even greater demand as they lack sufficient psychosocial support, increasing the burden on FC members.5

FCs often experience significant physical, psychosocial and financial burdens. Due to the chronic nature of caregiving activities, chronically ill older adult caregivers are highly exposed to burnout and burden, which may manifest in frustration, fatigue, worry and emotional distancing, undermining their mental health and their capacity to provide adequate and effective care. FCs can also reduce their working hours or leave their jobs to fulfil caregiving duties, resulting in income loss, financial instability and increased medical costs. Hence, economic challenges are highly prominent, increasing the burden on FCs.6 7 Over time, FCs also may unintentionally neglect their health and well-being, which can lower their overall quality of life (QoL).8

QoL is essential in every aspect of healthcare, particularly for FCs who support older patients with chronic diseases over an extended period.9 The WHO defines QoL as ‘an individual’s perception of their position within the context of cultural and value systems and in relation to their goals, expectations, standards, and concerns’.10 Maintaining the QoL for FCs is crucial to ensuring effective caregiving and protecting the health of both FCs and the older adults they care for. However, caregiving requires considerable physical, emotional and social burdens with negative impacts on caregivers’ mental health, social relationships and overall satisfaction and QoL.11 12 Research indicated that female FCs encounter more significant stress, burden and worse QoL than their male counterparts due to their traditional caregiving roles.13 Long-term caregiving and social support deficiency can worsen FCs’ psychological distress and a variety of physical health issues, including fatigue, sleep disturbances and depression. Financial hardship can reduce caregivers’ work productivity and impose economic burdens, while social isolation restricts their participation in social and leisure activities.11 Several studies have also indicated that other correlated variables, such as the caregiver’s age, education, social status, socio-cultural background, living arrangement, caregiving duration and the recipient’s dependency and health condition, also shape and impact the caregiver’s burden and QoL. Thus, comprehensive interventions addressing all these inter-related factors, emotional, economic and professional support, and well-tailored educational programmes, are crucial to alleviating caregivers’ burden, improving their QoL and enhancing the quality and outcome of care.14,16

The findings are intended to inform policymakers and healthcare professionals in the development of targeted, supportive and educational interventions aimed at reducing caregiver burden (CB), enhancing FCs’ QoL and ultimately improving care for older adults. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to focus on FCs of community-dwelling older adults with chronic diseases in Abha. Accordingly, the study aimed to assess levels of CB and QoL, examine their inter-relationship, and identify factors and predictors associated with both outcomes in this setting.

Despite the growing global reliance on family caregiving in an ageing population with chronic illness, evidence from Middle Eastern settings remains limited. This research represents one of the limited but growing efforts in the Middle East to examine the perceived burden and QoL among FCs caring for chronically ill older adults. Specifically, the study contributes to the global literature by providing culturally contextualised evidence from Saudi Arabia, a country experiencing a rapid demographic transition toward population ageing, where cultural norms strongly emphasise family responsibility and formal long-term care systems are still developing. These contextual features offer an important setting for examining caregiving dynamics that may differ from those reported in Western and other regional contexts. The insights derived from this research aim to guide policymakers and healthcare professionals in the development of targeted, supportive and educational interventions aimed at reducing CB, enhancing FCs’ QoL and ultimately improving care for older adults. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study focusing on FCs of community-dwelling older adults with chronic diseases in Abha. Accordingly, the study aimed to assess levels of CB and QoL, examine their inter-relationship and identify factors and predictors associated with both constructs among FCs in this setting.

Methods

Study Design

This study used a cross-sectional design to examine the association between perceived CB and its impact on the QoL of FCs of older adults with chronic illnesses. The research was conducted from December 2024 to March 2025 and is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.17

Study setting

The research was conducted in primary healthcare centres (PHCCs) in the Abha district of the Asir region in Saudi Arabia. These PHCCs operate under the auspices of the Ministry of Health and serve as the primary point of contact for most individuals seeking preventive, acute and chronic disease care. PHCCs constitute the backbone of outpatient healthcare delivery in KSA and play a central role in the routine follow-up and long-term management of chronic conditions, which makes them a suitable setting for recruiting FCs of community-dwelling older adults. The study was conducted in Abha, the capital of the Asir region in southwestern Saudi Arabia, a geographically distinctive area characterised by mountainous terrain, a moderate climate and a mixed urban–rural population.

Study target population

The target population for this study consisted of informal FCs providing care to chronically ill older adults aged 60 years and above, who were attending PHCCs in Abha City, Saudi Arabia. To ensure the quality and relevance of the sample, specific inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied. Eligible participants were required to be 18 years or older, have been providing care to an older adult with a chronic illness for a minimum duration of 6 months, and possess the ability and willingness to communicate effectively during the data collection process. In contrast, individuals were excluded if they were formal caregivers working in professional home care settings, caring for older adults without chronic diseases or had been providing care for less than 6 months.

Sample size calculation

According to the General Directorate of Health Affairs, Aseer Region and the Population Health Management Department, approximately 20 000 older adults with chronic conditions reside in Abha City, KSA.18 The sample size was calculated using Cochran’s formula, with a 95% CI, an estimated proportion of 50% and a margin of error of 5%. An additional 10% was added to the sample size to account for non-respondents and attrition bias. A total of 427 FCs were approached to participate in the study to achieve the required sample size.19

Sampling technique

A probability multistage sampling technique was employed to recruit participants for this study. First, a list of the 10 PHCCs in Abha City was obtained from the Ministry of Health and used as the initial sampling frame. Formal approval was secured from the Health Sector Administration, which subsequently authorised data collection at selected sites. To ensure unbiased selection, five PHCCs were randomly chosen using Python’s random sampling function without replacement. The selected centres were Al-Mansak, Al-Mwzafeen, Al-Manhel, Central Abha and Al-Azizya. Afterward, permission was obtained from each PHCC principal to facilitate recruitment. Within each selected PHCC, a list of attending FCs of chronically ill older adults was obtained and served as a secondary sampling frame. Participants were then randomly selected using the Research Randomizer V.0.4 tool. A trained researcher collected data through structured, face-to-face interviews. To ensure proportional representation, the sample was allocated according to the weekly patient visit frequency rate at each PHCC, which served as a proxy indicator for caregiver distribution. The five centres reported a combined weekly visit volume of 16 812, resulting in the following sample allocation: Al-Mansak (33.7%, 135 participants), Al-Mwzafeen (29.8%, 119 participants), Al-Manhel (26.6%, 107 participants), Central Abha (5.4%, 22 participants) and Al-Azizya (4.3%, 17 participants). This stratified sampling approach enhanced the representativeness and generalisability of the study findings across the FC population in Abha City.

Recruitment process

Initially, 427 FCs were approached to participate in the study in order to achieve the calculated sample size. Following eligibility screening, 12 FCs who had been providing care for less than 6 months and those caring for older adults without chronic illnesses were excluded. Additionally, five FCs declined to participate and three withdrew from the study. Consequently, the final sample consisted of 400 FCs

Data collection instruments

Demographic and clinical data sheet for FCs and older adults

This sheet includes questions about the caregivers’ age, gender, education, marital status, employment status and monthly income. The sheet also examines the caregiver’s relationship with chronically ill older adults, such as whether they live with the care recipient, the number of hours spent providing care per day and the duration of caregiving. Additionally, it assesses whether home care services or other family members provide caregiving support and includes questions regarding chronic diseases affecting caregivers. For older adults, five questions were included to collect information on their age, educational attainment, marital status, medical diagnoses and level of dependency.

Zarit Burden Interview Arabic version

The Zarit Burden Interview (ZBI) is one of the most widely validated and used instruments for assessing CB in both community and clinical settings. Originally developed as a 29-item scale by Zarit et al,20 it was refined into a more psychometrically robust 22-item version in 1985.21 This shorter version has since been validated across various populations and languages, including Arabic, as demonstrated by Hébert et al.22 The Arabic version of the ZBI-22 has shown strong reliability, with reported Cronbach’s alpha values ranging from 0.85 to 0.92, indicating good internal consistency. In the present study, the reliability of the Arabic ZBI-22 was confirmed, yielding a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.89, which denotes excellent internal consistency. The ZBI-22 assesses the physical, emotional and financial burden experienced by caregivers using a 5-point Likert scale, with item responses ranging from 0 (never) to 4 (almost always). Total scores range from 0 to 88, where higher scores reflect greater perceived burden. According to Schreiner et al,23 the standard interpretive cut-off points are as follows: 0–20 indicates little or no burden, 21–40 reflects mild to moderate burden, 41–60 denotes moderate to severe burden and 61–88 represents severe burden.

Adult Carer Quality of Life questionnaire

The Adult Carer Quality of Life (AC-QoL) questionnaire is a self-report instrument developed by Elwick et al in 2010 to assess the overall QoL of adult caregivers.24 The tool consists of 40 items grouped into eight key domains: caring support, choice of caring, stress of caring, money matters, personal growth, sense of value, ability to care and caregiver satisfaction. Each item is rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (Never) to 3 (Always). Items 6–16, 19, 37 and 38 are negatively worded and are reverse-scored to maintain consistency in interpretation. Higher total scores indicate better QoL, with possible scores ranging from 0 to 120. Commonly used cut-off points classify QoL levels as follows: 0–40 indicating low QoL, 41–80 moderate QoL and scores above 81 representing high QoL. Originally developed in English, the AC-QoL was professionally translated into Arabic and then back-translated to ensure linguistic and conceptual accuracy. A panel of five experts evaluated the Arabic version for relevance, clarity and appropriateness via academic consultation. The panel confirmed that the instrument was clear and suitable for the target population. Internal consistency of the Arabic version was strong, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.88, indicating high reliability. Construct validity was examined using exploratory factor analysis. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) Measure of Sampling Adequacy yielded a value of 0.877, which is considered meritorious and confirms the adequacy of the sample for factor analysis. Furthermore, Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity was statistically significant (χ²=390.04, df=28, p<0.001), verifying that the correlation matrix was suitable for factor analysis.25 26

Procedure

Preliminary test

After obtaining official approval from the relevant authorities, the questionnaire underwent pilot testing involving 40 participants from the target population within the same study setting. This pilot study aimed to assess the instrument’s feasibility and clarity. The findings indicated that the scales were reliable and effectively measured the intended constructs. Consequently, no modifications were necessary, and the data collected during the pilot phase were excluded from the primary analysis to maintain the integrity of the study’s outcomes.

Data collection

The purpose and objectives of the study were clearly explained to the participants before the interviews, and informed consent was obtained to ensure privacy and confidentiality. Every interview was about 20–25 min long and focused heavily on the participants’ privacy. FCs were informed that participation was voluntary and that they could withdraw without repercussions. The researcher meticulously reviewed the responses to the data collection instruments. No incentives were offered for participation, and all reactions remained strictly confidential. Several measures and techniques were taken to avoid potential biases in this study. First, the recruitment process was designed to be appealing to a diverse range of FCs. Survey questions were translated carefully to eliminate leading or suggestive language that might influence respondents’ answers. Furthermore, the survey was anonymous, allowing respondents to provide their comments freely. Since the surveys were handled separately by the researcher, it is essential to emphasise the requirement for neutrality and avoid any unintentional coercion of the respondents. Additional analyses were conducted to adjust for potential confounding variables that may influence the correlation between family CB and their QoL. Statistical adjustments for other relevant variables were made, so this result is robust and reliable.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences Statistics for Windows, V.27 (IBM, Armonk, New York, USA). The collected data were structured, coded and tabulated using descriptive statistics. Categorical variables were summarised using frequencies and percentages. Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were calculated to assess the internal consistency of the ZBI-22 and the AC-QoL questionnaire. Sampling adequacy and suitability for factor analysis were evaluated using the KMO measure and Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity, followed by exploratory factor analysis to assess construct validity. The KMO value exceeded the recommended threshold (0.87), and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was statistically significant (p<0.001), confirming good factorability and construct validity of the scales. Inferential analyses were conducted to examine associations between CB, QoL and sociodemographic, clinical and caregiving-related characteristics. CB and QoL were categorised based on established cut-off scores; associations were assessed using cross-tabulation and the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Variables that demonstrated statistically significant associations in bivariate analyses (p≤0.05) were simultaneously entered into multivariable logistic regression models to identify independent predictors of high CB and low QoL. Multivariate logistic regression results were reported as ORs with 95% CIs. A two-sided p value of ≤0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, recruitment, conduct or analysis of this study. The research questions and outcome measures were selected by the investigators using previously validated tools to address identified gaps in caregiver research. The findings will be disseminated through this peer-reviewed publication and are intended to inform healthcare professionals and policymakers, with the aim of indirectly improving support services and care for FCs and the older adults they care for.

Ethics and human subjects’ Issues

The researcher conducted data collection once adequate permission was obtained. All subjects were sufficiently informed about the study’s goals and requirements, and informed consent was obtained. Moreover, participants were informed that participation was voluntary, that no information would be shared with any party other than the researcher, that all data would be kept confidential and that all participants would be treated with anonymity. Participants were informed that they were free to withdraw from the study at any time without needing to justify their decision. Lastly, participants were asked to complete the questionnaires accurately and truthfully.

Results

Out of 427 participants approached, 400 were included in the final analysis, resulting in a response rate of 94% and a dropout rate of 6%.

Table 1 presents that most of the FCs were between 31 and 40 years old (44.0%), followed by those under 30 years (40.7%), 41–50 years (12.3%) and above 50 years (3.0%). Most caregivers were female (69.3%) and held a university degree (69.5%), with smaller proportions having completed secondary (19.5%) or primary/intermediate education (11.0%). Regarding marital status, 63.5% were single, while 36.5% were married. In terms of employment, 60.7% were unemployed and 39.3% were employed. A majority reported having insufficient income (61.0%), and 17.3% reported having a chronic illness themselves. For the older adults receiving care, most were aged between 70 and 80 years (45.5%), followed by those under 70 years (33.5%) and over 80 years (21.0%). Females comprised 55.0% of care recipients. Educationally, a large portion were illiterate (59.8%), while others had primary/intermediate (20.3%), secondary (13.0%) or university education (7.0%). Most were married (82.2%), while 17.8% were single. Regarding chronic health conditions, the most prevalent illnesses included diabetes mellitus (52.3%) and hypertension (41.8%), followed by osteoarthritis/osteoporosis (25.5%), coronary artery disease (24.5%), neurological disorders (21.3%), Alzheimer’s disease (AD) (18.0%) and malignancies (17.8%). In terms of functional status, 57.5% of older adults were partially dependent, 30.5% were totally dependent and only 12.0% were independent in their daily activities.

Figure 1 presents the burden levels individuals perceived, indicating that they reported experiencing some level of burden. Respondents reported experiencing mild burden (59.5%) most frequently, followed by moderate burden (22.8%). Only 1.0% experienced a severe burden, and 16.8% reported no burden. The results of this study suggest that caregivers experienced significant levels of stress and difficulties while providing care, with stressors and burden levels negatively impacting their well-being.

The participant selection process followed a multi-stage probability sampling approach. Of the 427 initially approached family caregivers of older adults, 400 met the eligibility criteria and agreed to participate. Participants were excluded due to refusal to participate (n=5), caregiving duration of less than 6 months (n=12), caring for older adults without chronic illness (n=7), or withdrawal from the study (n=3).

Figure 2 shows that participants reported low and moderate QoL for caregivers (AC-QoL), respectively, at 47.8% and 52.2%, with 0.0% reporting high AC-QoL. This finding is significant because it suggests that caregiving is often associated with a low QoL. Overall, this suggests that most caregivers struggle to maintain a reasonable QoL, likely due to the physical, emotional and financial burdens associated with caregiving.

Table 4 evaluates how caregivers’ QoL relates to their sociodemographic factors. Significant associations were observed with gender (p=0.034), marital status (p<0.001), education (p<0.001), employment (p=0.051), income (p<0.001) and relationship to the care recipient (p<0.001). Additionally, hours and duration of care, and availability of home care services, were significantly associated with QoL (p<0.001). Female caregivers, those with higher education, sufficient income and access to home care services, were more likely to report moderate QoL. In contrast, more caregiving hours and years, and being closely related (eg, spouse), were linked to lower QoL.

Discussion

This study aimed to determine the multifaceted aspects of family caregiving, focusing on FCs’ burdens and QoL and their associations with various sociodemographic and clinical characteristics. The findings highlight FCs’ significant challenges and underscore the need for targeted interventions to support their well-being.

A primary finding noted in this study was that FCs reported varying levels of burden, with more than half experiencing a mild burden, over one-fifth facing a moderate burden and a minority facing a severe burden. On the other hand, fewer than one-fifth reported no burden, indicating the widespread issue of under-reporting of FCs’ stress due to cultural expectations. Relatively low severe burden rates could also mean that caregivers have not yet reached a critical threshold of exhaustion or that most manage their stress before experiencing severe burden. The predominance of mild to moderate burden among FCs may reflect an early stage of caregiver strain or the presence of individual resilience or coping strategies that temporarily mitigate stress even amidst significant challenges. However, the presence of any burden—even in its mildest form—should not be underestimated, as it still reflects emotional exhaustion, disrupted routines and limited personal time, especially in contexts lacking formal support systems. This was consistent with Zhylkybekova et al, who found that informal caregivers frequently report high emotional and logistical demands that persist across all levels of burden severity, regardless of whether they label their burden as ‘severe’.27 Research also similarly documented mild-to-moderate caregiving burdens of stress depending on the intensity of care required and the caregiver’s coping mechanisms.28 A recent study by Kim and Park found that factors such as optimism, resilience, social support and problem-solving skills were associated with lower burden levels.29 Furthermore, research added and documented those cultural differences in how CB is perceived and managed, with some cultures placing a higher value on filial piety and viewing caregiving as a natural part of family life, potentially buffering the negative effects of burden.30 On the other hand, Liu et al highlighted a higher proportion of caregivers experiencing high levels of burden due to factors such as severity of dementia, duration of caregiving and lack of social support.31 Bongelli et al also reported a higher incidence of severe burden among caregivers, particularly those supporting care recipients with mental health issues or advanced dementia.12 A study by Shubair SA in Saudi Arabia revealed that caregivers were more likely to experience a higher burden with older male care recipients with severe AD.32 This contrast in the degree of caregiving burdens may stem from differences in the health status of care recipients, the availability of services or caregiver education levels.

In this study on Adult Caregiver Quality of Life (AC-QoL), a notable percentage of the caregivers reported low and moderate QoL, and none reported high QoL. This could suggest that some caregivers may have protective factors or coping strategies to maintain a reasonable level of well-being despite the challenges they face. The absence of high QoL scores highlights the pervasive and profound impact of caregiving responsibilities on the lives and well-being of FCs. These are consistent with similar research results that revealed the detrimental effects of long-term caregiving on various domains of QoL.33 34 This contrasts with a study by Schulz and Sherwood, who found that some caregivers report positive aspects of caregiving, such as increased feelings of purpose and connection, highlighting the complex dual nature of the caregiving experience.35 Conversely, Chica-Pérez et al found that caregivers reported a higher QoL after incorporating health programmes, such as regular check-ins and long-term engagement, which showed more significant improvements in QoL.36

Significant associations were observed between QoL and several variables. Female caregivers reported lower QoL compared with males (p=0.034). Unmarried caregivers demonstrated lower QoL than married caregivers (p<0.001). Higher QoL was observed among caregivers with higher educational levels (p<0.001) and those with sufficient income (p<0.001). Caregivers who were employed tended to report a lower QoL compared with unemployed caregivers (p=0.051). Additionally, caregivers who were closely related to the care recipient (eg, spouses or children) generally experienced lower QoL compared with more distant relatives (p<0.001).

The key findings of this study revealed the presence of a statistically significant inverse relationship between the perceived burden of FCs and their QoL for chronically ill older adults within the local Saudi context. It was also identified as one of the strongest predictors of low QoL, where caregiving is often performed without formal training or structured support. Caregivers who reported higher levels of burden consistently experienced lower levels of AC-QoL. In contrast, those with moderate to mild burden tended to report better, though still compromised, QoL. This significant association highlighted the interconnected nature of emotional strain and well-being in the context of long-term caregiving. This is consistent with prior evidence-based studies that have documented the negative, profound effects of caregiving demands on FCs’ physical and mental health, highlighting the role of chronic stress and emotional and financial burdens in diminishing caregivers’ QoL across several domains, including emotional stability, physical health and social functioning.12 31 35 37 Contrary to these findings, Bastawrous argued that while CB and QoL are typically inversely related, the relationship is not entirely linear.38 Despite significant agreement concerning the negative effect of caregiving, extensive research has also emphasised the positive rewards and experiences associated with caregiving, such as satisfaction, fulfilment or personal growth from their roles, which may preserve certain aspects of QoL even under a moderate burden.39,41 However, these positive aspects often do not offset caregiving fatigue, especially when care responsibilities are extended over time.

This study examined sociodemographic and clinical characteristics in relation to FCs’ burden and AC-QoL, shedding light on the multifaceted impacts of caregiving and highlighting several key factors that influence informal CB or stress and QoL. The analysis confirmed that FCs’ burden and AC-QoL are multifactorial outcomes influenced by both caregiver and care-recipient variables, even after adjusting for potential confounders. Gender was found to be a significant variable associated with caregiving experience outcomes, with females reporting lower AC-QoL levels compared with males. These findings could be related to the notion that female caregivers often assume more caregiving tasks and experience higher emotional and physical strain. This supports previous findings by Abdelhalim et al, who emphasised the disproportionate responsibilities shouldered by women in caregiving roles, often at the expense of their health and autonomy.42 This result also aligns with Zwar et al, who noted that female caregivers often experience greater emotional and physical burdens, which can adversely affect their QoL.43 Contrary to these findings, Siregar et al reported that male caregivers reported poorer QoL compared with female caregivers, contradicting broader trends.44

Social status likewise positively influences AC-QoL, with married individuals reporting better outcomes than their single counterparts. The regression analysis of this study also confirmed that marital status emerged as a strong predictor for higher AC-QoL, with an OR of 2.0571, p=0.017. This is likely due to the increased emotional and practical support that married individuals may receive from their spouses. Shared caregiving responsibilities and the encouragement available from a partner can contribute to higher resilience and coping capacity, thereby reducing the burden on caregivers. Research supported these conclusions and indicated the positive impact of social support on caregiver well-being.45,48 Schulz and Sherwood also supported this notion, noting that spousal support often acts as a buffer against caregiver burnout and enhances the sense of purpose.35 However, Kin et al contradicted these results and revealed that the spousal support role predicts worse QoL outcomes independent of caregiving hours or dementia severity.49

Interestingly, employment status emerged as a significant factor, with employed caregivers experiencing a greater burden and lower QoL. Additionally, there was a negative correlation between employment status and both AC-QoL and CB. This implies that unemployed caregivers may face additional stressors, including financial insecurity, which adversely affects their QoL. Recent studies are in the same line with these findings, which discussed how employment provides not only economic benefits and a sense of normalcy but also social interactions that can buffer against the stresses of caregiving and increase caregivers’ sense of identity and self-efficacy, which are important for maintaining QoL.50 51 On the contrary, these outcomes are inconsistent with studies that found that employment can serve as a protective factor against CB and enhance QoL.35 52

One of the most prominent findings was that monthly income emerged as the strongest predictor of improved AC-QoL, with an OR of 5.2571, p<0.0001. The study also found a strong, significant negative correlation between insufficient income and higher levels of burden. A higher income was also significantly associated with better AC-QoL and lower CB. This suggests that financial stability can alleviate some of the stresses associated with caregiving and enhance the caregiver’s QoL. These results underscore that financial security plays a crucial role in mitigating caregiving-related stress and improving overall QoL. Financial stability alleviates concerns related to the costs associated with caregiving by enabling access to supportive services, healthcare services, medical expenses and the potential need for paid assistance. These outcomes echo findings from Bastawrous that financial strain significantly contributes to caregiver stress, emphasising the need for economic support for caregivers.38 These findings are consistent with Schulz and Sherwood, who indicated that economic security plays a crucial role in reducing stress and improving the overall well-being of caregivers.35 Similarly, Pinquart and Sörensen highlighted the impact of socio-economic status on caregiver stress and well-being.52 This corroborates the findings by Mittelman et al, who note that financial strain is a significant predictor of decreased caregiver well-being.48 Another study in Saudi Arabia by Aljuaid et al found similar conclusions that highlighted that financial hardship was a leading cause of psychological and emotional strain among caregivers in Saudi Arabia and lower QoL.53

The health conditions of caregivers were significantly correlated with increased burden. This study found a significant correlation between health conditions and CBs, but it did not show a substantial correlation with AC-QoL. The regression analysis of this study also confirmed that FCs with chronic illness emerged as a strong predictor for higher family CBs. Informal caregivers who have chronic diseases experience a considerably increased burden, with an OR of 1.6689, p=0.0267. This finding underscores the compounded challenges faced by caregivers who are not only responsible for the care of others but are also managing their own health issues. Similarly, Schulz and Sherwood35 and Choi et al54 concluded that FCs with chronic illnesses or comorbidities were positively associated with increased CB. On the contrary, Alshammari et al5 confirmed that while many FCs in Saudi Arabia experience a significant burden, the extent varies based on individual circumstances, such as the availability of support systems and the caregiver’s health status. This variability implies that not all caregivers with chronic illnesses or financial constraints experience a heightened burden to the same degree.5

Another significant predictor verified in this study was the caregiver’s relationship with the older adult concerning both the FCs burden and the AC-QoL. This may be due to the emotional complexities and expectations involved in caring for a loved one, where personal attachment can intensify the sense of responsibility and emotional strain. The current study also documented that FCs’ relationships with care recipients influence their burden levels, with adult children experiencing the greatest strain and burden levels. However, they reported higher QoL compared with other relatives. This may be due to a greater sense of responsibility or emotional reward derived from fulfilling cultural and familial duties, reflecting the closer bonds or a sense of filial responsibility that can enhance personal satisfaction and perceived QoL. This also aligns with the research by Fenton et al55, which highlighted the emotional complexities and responsibilities inherent in caring for a parent. Despite this, the caregiving role leads to an increased burden, and it was found that it was associated with a higher QoL, lower levels of depressive symptoms and better self-rated health among adult-child caregivers.55 Similarly, Choi et al reported that emotional rewards come from caring for a loved one, despite the challenges involved, suggesting a complex dynamic where emotional closeness can buffer some of the negative impacts of caregiving.54 In the same vein, Kin et al highlighted that while close relationships may increase emotional strain, they also enhance the sense of meaning and purpose that supports overall life satisfaction.49 In contrast, da Silva Falcão and Paulson56 showed that adult–child caregivers face role-reversal complexities that may strain traditional parent–child bonds. While caregiving can deepen intergenerational understanding, the inherent power shift may limit emotional reciprocity, exacerbating feelings of isolation despite physical proximity.56

This study additionally examined the nature and extent of caregiving roles among FCs who support chronically ill older adults. The primary finding revealed that most caregivers lived with the care recipients and were predominantly adult children, followed by grandchildren, while spouses and siblings had more limited involvement. The regression analysis of this study also validated that co-residency with older adults emerged as a strong predictor for higher family CBs and AC-QoL. This may relate to co-residency with older adults, possibly leading to increased day-to-day stress and reduced personal space and freedom. Living with older adults and the constant nature of caregiving also contribute to the intensive, ongoing care responsibilities that heighten caregiver stress and cause emotional fatigue, consequently reducing AC-QoL. This highlights the need for a comprehensive caregiver support environment and programmes, including social, financial and professional support, to enhance the QoL of FCs. These results were consistent with findings by Almehmadi and Alrashed, who reported that caregivers living with their care recipients often experienced higher psychological strain due to a lack of boundaries between caregiving and personal time.57 These findings are in the same line with Martsolf et al,58 Mittelman et al,48 Conde-Sala et al59 and Kalandari et al60 who underscored that cohabitation could blur boundaries between caregiving and personal life, leading to higher stress levels and less personal time, which in turn increases the burden.

The current study further showed that the length of time spent giving care positively correlated with burden, indicating that long-term caregiving is associated with increased stress. Conversely, it was significantly negatively correlated with AC-QoL, highlighting the toll that extensive caregiving hours can take on QoL. These could be related to caregivers dedicating long hours to caregiving, resulting in reduced well-being because of sleep disturbances, social withdrawal and reduced personal freedom. These conclusions are supported by Schulz and Sherwood,35 Alrashed61 and Aloudiny et al62 and Cheng et al63 who concluded that time-intensive caregiving demands accumulate over time and can lead to emotional, physical and social burnout and compromised life satisfaction, impacting the caregiver’s QoL.

Interestingly, caregiving was often long-term and sustained without formal support in this study, reflecting a predominantly informal caregiving system deeply rooted in cultural and familial expectations. The study’s regression analysis also confirmed that longer durations of caregiving were a strong predictor for increased FCs’ burden, with an OR of 1.8317, p=0.009. These findings indicate the cumulative stress and potential burnout that can develop over extended periods of caregiving, as caregivers may face ongoing demands without sufficient respite. The analysis of this study also proved that caregiving duration showed a significant inverse association with ACQoL. Furthermore, a longer caregiving duration was an important predictor for a lower AC-QoL, with an OR of 2.1251, p=0.0001. This may indicate that extended caregiving can result in burnout and stress accumulation, adversely impacting QoL. Numerous studies supported these findings and reported that extended caregiving responsibilities without sufficient respite or support can lead to caregiver burnout and significantly lower physical and mental well-being and QoL.35 48 61 63 64

Another significant finding of this study was that older adults’ dependency levels strongly predicted FCs’ burden. The regression analysis proved that caring for highly dependent older adults significantly increases the caregiver’s burden, with an OR of 2.1734, p=0.001. This study’s regression analysis also demonstrated that the AC-QoL caring for highly dependent older adults is negatively impacted, with an OR of 0.5437, p=0.018. Those caring for highly dependent older adults face more complex physical care demands, decision-making responsibilities and emotional pressure. This result might be due to the intensive care needs and constant vigilance required for those with substantial dependencies. Several studies are in harmony with these findings and noted that the caregiving burden escalates in direct proportion to the functional limitations of older adults.65,67

Importantly, the use of formal home care services was a strong predictor of a reduction in FCs’ burden. The regression investigation concluded that CB is significantly decreased by receiving home care services. The use of home care services also significantly impacted and enhanced AC-QoL. This suggests that external support from healthcare professionals can alleviate physical workload, provide psychological relief and reduce the time-intensive nature of caregiving, thus improving the caregiver’s QoL. This is possibly related to the decrease in direct caregiving duties and the provision of professional care that these services offer. Numerous studies found similar findings and concluded that formal home healthcare services, such as home nurses or trained caregivers, offer essential respite and promote caregiver sustainability over time, which can alleviate FC duties and enhance their QoL.62 68 On the contrary, A study conducted in Japan by Oh et al revealed that formal support, excluding family physicians, did not significantly alleviate the burden, indicating variability in the effectiveness of home services.69 Conversely, Huo and Kim noted that barriers to accessing professional care services often lead families to rely predominantly on informal care networks, which could lead to FCs’ burnout.39

Conclusion

This study provides valuable insight into the dynamics of caregiving in ageing and chronic illness contexts in Abha, Saudi Arabia, particularly focusing on CB and its impact on QoL. Findings revealed that caregiving is mainly performed by younger, educated women facing financial challenges and unemployment, highlighting the associated economic strain. Older adults receiving care exhibited high dependency levels and a significant prevalence of chronic illnesses, compounding caregivers’ responsibilities. Most caregivers were sons and daughters living with the older adults, delivering long-term support that often exceeds 3 years and 4 hours daily without external help, reflecting filial responsibility in Saudi culture, which results in higher burdens and decreased QoL. The study showed that CB is typically mild to moderate but significantly impacts and predicts a lower QoL, as none reported high QoL levels. A significant negative correlation exists between perceived burden and QoL, suggesting that increased caregiving stress reduces QoL. Key predictors of higher CB included poor FC health due to chronic illnesses, longer caregiving duration, primary caregiver status (especially among sons and daughters), high dependency levels and limited access to home care services. Factors contributing to low QoL were unmarried status, insufficient income, extended caregiving duration, primary caregiver status and limited home care access. The study emphasises how socio-economic disadvantages, lack of support and high care demands strain FCs in Abha, Saudi Arabia. These findings underscore the urgent public health concern regarding informal caregiving, which could have long-term consequences for both caregivers’ and care recipients’ well-being.

Clinical implications

The findings of this study have several important implications for healthcare practice, education and policy. First, nurses and healthcare professionals play a critical role in identifying and assessing CB early through routine screening in primary healthcare settings. By incorporating tools such as ZBI-22 and the AC-QoL questionnaire, they can systematically evaluate caregiver needs and intervene promptly. Second, educational programmes can empower FCs by providing them with skills in patient care, stress management and resource navigation, thereby reducing their burden and enhancing their confidence and competence. Additionally, nurses and other healthcare professionals can advocate for the integration of home care services and psychosocial support programmes, which have been shown to significantly improve caregiver outcomes. Finally, involve policy development to ensure that caregiver support systems are recognised as a public health priority, especially in regions with ageing populations and limited formal eldercare infrastructure.
