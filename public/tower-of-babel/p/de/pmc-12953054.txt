Altered locus coeruleus links to atrophy and hypometabolism in individuals with high Alzheimer's disease biomarkers
Authors: Emilie Foyard, Tao Blanchard, Brigitte Landeau, Léa Chauveau, Mikaël Naveau, Géraldine Poisnel, Eric Bui, Gaël Chetelat, Robin de Flores, for the Medit‐Ageing Research Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONThe locus coeruleus (LC), the first region to contain tau pathology in Alzheimer's disease (AD), releases norepinephrine, which has a neuroprotective role. We examined whether LC integrity relates to whole‐brain integrity and how AD biomarkers may moderate this relationship.METHODSLC intensity and volume were estimated using automatic segmentation on neuromelanin‐sensitive magnetic resonance imaging acquired in n = 71 cognitively unimpaired elderly from the Age‐Well randomized controlled trial. We investigated the associations between LC integrity and whole‐brain gray matter volume (GMV) and glucose metabolism, as well as the interactions with amyloid positron emission tomography and plasma phosphorylated tau231, neurofilament light chain and glial fibrillary acidic protein.RESULTSWhile only LC intensity was directly associated with GMV, both LC measures (intensity and volume) interacted positively with AD biomarkers to predict GMV and glucose metabolism.DISCUSSIONOur results support concomitant LC and gray matter alterations in the context of aging and elevated AD biomarker levels.CLINICAL TRIAL REGISTRATIONAge‐Well randomized clinical trial of the Medit‐Ageing European Project. Trial registration number: EudraCT:2016–002,441–36; IDRCB:2016‐A01767–44; ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT02977819.Keywords: aging, Alzheimer's disease, amyloid positron emission tomography, astrocytes, axonal integrity, glucose metabolism, locus coeruleus, magnetic resonance imaging, neuromelanin, plasma biomarkers, tau

BACKGROUND

The locus coeruleus (LC) is a small nucleus located in the supero‐posterior pons. 1 It is the main source of cortical norepinephrine (NE), which protects neurons from inflammation‐related damage. 2 NE also reduces spontaneous firing, 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 favors neurovascular coupling, 7 and increases astrocyte production of lactate, a neuronal energy source. 8 In a context of reduced NE supply, neurons may thus lack energy substrates, which can cause excitotoxicity. 9 , 10

Importantly, the LC is the first site to contain tau pre‐tangles in primary age‐related tauopathy and Alzheimer's disease (AD). 11 Higher Braak stages are accompanied by an increasing density of subsequent tau neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs) in the LC, associated with a lower neuromelanin content, suggesting neuronal death. 12 The LC's pivotal role in neuroprotection and its vulnerability to early AD pathology highlight the importance of assessing its integrity in vivo in humans.

In recent years, dedicated magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) sequences on which the LC appears hyperintense were developed, and this hyperintensity co‐localizes with neuromelanin‐containing neurons. 13 , 14 , 15 Using this approach, several studies showed a decrease of LC intensity during late life, with one study showing that this decrease was limited to individuals with high amyloid or tau pathology. 12 , 16 , 17 , 18 Another study found no association between LC volume and age. 16 Conversely, in the cohort studied here, age was not associated with LC intensity (maybe due to a limited variability in age, as participants were 69 to 84 years old), but was negatively associated with LC volume. 19 Furthermore, LC intensity was consistently shown to be higher in cognitively unimpaired (CU) participants than in AD patients. 12 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 A lower LC volume in AD patients compared to CU participants was also reported, although differences with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) participants were inconsistent. 23 , 24 This further supports a gradual alteration of LC integrity throughout the course of AD.

The LC has widespread projections, 25 and given that: (1) phosphorylated tau (p‐tau) spreads through neuronal pathways, 26 and (2) the LC plays a neuroprotective role, it is likely that LC loss of integrity impacts whole‐brain integrity. Indeed, positive associations between LC intensity and cortical thickness were found in the literature, but inconsistently, 27 , 28 , 29 and several studies reported non‐significant associations between LC intensity and gray matter volume (GMV). 21 , 22 , 30 , 31 Furthermore, one unique study found a positive association between LC volume and GMV, involving the temporal lobe and the insula. 32 Notably, Engels‐Domínguez et al. 29 showed a significant positive interaction between LC intensity and both amyloid and tau positron emission tomography (PET) predicting cortical thickness in a group of older adults which included 90% CU. The interaction with amyloid PET did not persist in an analysis including only CU participants. Overall, the methods used to measure LC integrity in these studies were heterogeneous, each with specific advantages and limitations in terms of spatial coverage and rater consistency. Therefore, replications of these results are needed. Interaction analyses between LC integrity and AD biomarkers should also be extended to markers of neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration.

Although fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) PET has been described as more sensitive than MRI to detect neurodegeneration 33 and can reflect metabolic astrocyte changes, 34 , 35 , 36 only one study investigated the associations between LC integrity and FDG PET, in a population mixing CU, MCI, and AD patients, showing that FDG PET was positively associated with LC intensity in the frontal and left temporal lobes, and with LC volume in the left amygdala, parahippocampus, and orbitofrontal cortex, as well as around the left central sulcus. 23 Whether similar associations exist within a population of CU older adults remains to be confirmed.

In the present study, we tested for the association between LC integrity measures (intensity and volume), estimated using a tailored automatic segmentation algorithm, and both gray matter signal from T1‐weighted MRI and FDG PET in a population of CU older adults. We also evaluated the modulating effects of amyloid PET as well as plasma p‐tau231, neurofilament light chain (NfL; measuring axonal degeneration), and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP; measuring astrogliosis) levels on these associations.

METHODS

Participants

One hundred four participants from the Age‐Well randomized clinical trial of the Medit‐Ageing European Project were initially included in the present study. 37 Participants were recruited from the general population from November 2016 until March 2018, were older than 65 years, native French speakers, retired for at least 1 year, educated for at least 7 years, and performed within the normal range on standardized cognitive tests. They did not show evidence of a major neurological or psychiatric disorder (including alcohol or drug abuse), history of cerebrovascular disease, presence of a chronic disease or acute unstable illness, and current or recent medication usage that could interfere with cognitive functioning. Participants were randomized into three groups. One group received an 18‐month meditation training, another group received an equivalent English training, and the third group received no intervention. Data used in this study were acquired during the second post‐intervention visit, which took place from October 2020 until November 2022 (between 2.5 and 3 years after the end of the intervention), except for florbetapir PET, which was acquired at the end of the intervention. Three participants were not included in the analyses due to poor‐quality scans or discomfort during the MRI session (no images were acquired), yielding an analyzable sample of 101 participants. Among them, two participants refused FDG PET acquisition, and plasma p‐tau231 level was not available for four additional participants. Demographics of the cohort are reported in Table 1. The Age‐Well randomized clinical trial was approved by the local ethics committee (Comité de Protection des Personnes Nord‐Ouest III, Caen, France; trial registration number: EudraCT:2016–002,441–36; IDRCB:2016‐A01767–44; ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT02977819), and was carried out in accordance with Good Clinic Practice guidelines. All participants gave written informed consent prior to the examinations.

MRI data acquisition

Each subject underwent an MR scan at the Cyceron center (Caen, France) using a 3T General Electric Healthcare SIGNA Premier scanner. First, T1‐weighted 3D BRAVO sagittal structural images (referred to as T1‐weighted MRI in the rest of the article) were acquired (repetition time [TR] = 7.2 ms; echo time [TE] = 2.9 ms; flip angle = 6°; 180 slices; slice thickness = 1 mm; field of view [FoV] = 256 × 256 mm2; matrix = 256 × 256; in‐plane resolution = 1 × 1 mm2; acquisition time = 5 minutes 28 seconds). In addition, three ultra‐high resolution T1‐weighted turbo spin echo images with additional magnetization transfer contrast (referred to as LC MRI in the rest of the article) were acquired aligned with the anatomic axis of each participant's brainstem (TR = 660 ms; TE = 12 ms; MT pulse duration = 16 ms; magnetization transfer [MT] offset = 1200 Hz; slice thickness = 3 mm; FoV = 216 × 216 mm2; matrix = 512 × 512; in‐plane resolution = 0.42 × 0.42 mm2; number of signal averages [NSA]: 2; acquisition time = 4 minutes 17 seconds). The three scans were then coregistered and averaged using SPM12 as well as resampled to a resolution of 0.3 × 0.3 × 0.75 mm3 with a Sinc interpolation kernel using c3d (https://github.com/pyushkevich/c3d).

T1‐weighted MR images pre‐processing

T1‐weighted MRI were iteratively segmented and spatially normalized to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) template. The segmentations were realized with multiple channels, more precisely, fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) images (3D‐CUBE T2 FLAIR sagittal; TR/TE/inversion time = 7000/120/1943 ms; flip angle = 90°; 180 slices with no gap; slice thickness = 1 mm; FoV = 256 × 256 mm2; in‐plane resolution = 1 × 1 ×1 mm2) were used in addition to T1‐weighted MRI to improve accuracy. Then, these images were modulated to correct for normalization‐induced volume changes and smoothed with a 8 mm full width at half‐maximum (FWHM) Gaussian filter. Finally, images were masked to exclude non‐gray matter voxels from the analyses. All these steps were performed using SPM12.

PET data acquisition

All participants were scanned on the same PET camera (Discovery RX VCT 64 PET‐CT scanner; General Electric Healthcare) at the Cyceron center (Caen, France). Florbetapir PET and FDG PET scans were acquired in two separate sessions, with a resolution of 3.76 × 3.76 × 4.9 mm3 (FoV 157 mm). Forty‐seven planes with a voxel size of 1.95 × 1.95 × 3.27 mm3 were obtained. Before the PET acquisition, a transmission scan was performed for attenuation correction. For florbetapir PET, each participant (n = 101) underwent a 10‐minute PET scan beginning 50 minutes after the intravenous injection of ≈ 4 MBq/kg of florbetapir. For the FDG PET scan, participants (n = 99) fasted for at least 6 hours before scanning. After a 30‐minute resting period in a quiet and dark environment, ≈ 180 MBq of FDG was injected intravenously as a bolus and a 10‐minute PET acquisition scan was acquired 50 minutes after injection.

PET images pre‐processing

PET images were first aligned with T1‐weighted images in native subjects’ space. Then, they were normalized to MNI space using the same transformation as for normalization of T1‐weighted images. Finally, images were scaled by dividing each voxel value by the mean cerebellum value, and FDG PET images were smoothed with a 10 mm FWHM Gaussian filter to allow for voxelwise analyses. Florbetapir PET standardized uptake value ratios (SUVR; referred to as amyloid PET throughout the article) were extracted from an AD signature mask. 38 This mask was derived from regions reported in the literature to show high amyloid PET retention in AD and normal aging 39 , 40 and consists of Automated Anatomical Labeling atlas regions, namely the superior, middle, and inferior frontal gyri; rolandic opercula; olfactory cortex; superior medial frontal gyri; medial orbitofrontal cortex; rectus gyri; insula; cingulate cortex; parietal lobules; supramarginal and angular gyri; precunei; caudate nuclei; putamen; pallida; Heschl's gyri; superior temporal gyri; temporal poles; and the middle and inferior temporal gyri.

LC integrity measures

The LC of 30 randomly chosen subjects was manually segmented in LC MRI native space. These subjects are referred to as the atlas set in the rest of the article. Manual segmentations were used for training “Automatic Segmentation of Hippocampal Subfields” (ASHS), an automatic segmentation algorithm initially developed for hippocampal subfields segmentation, 41 which was subsequently used for automatic segmentation of the LC of all subjects (n = 101; see Supplementary Methods in supporting information for more details regarding the segmentation procedure). Mean intensity in the segmented bilateral LC region of interest (ROI) was extracted from LC MRI images. Mean intensity from a reference region, defined as a cube of 729 mm3 in the rostral pontomesencephalic area in MNI space and converted to the native space of each subject, was also extracted. LC intensity was computed as the ratio between intensity extracted from LC ROI and intensity extracted from the reference region, as classically done in the literature. 42 , 43 Volume was calculated from the total number of voxels of the segmented LC ROI, and corrected for total intracranial volume with the residuals method. The validation procedure and metrics of the automatic segmentation are described in the supporting information (Supplementary Methods and Figure S1). For more details, see de Flores et al. 19

Plasma biomarkers

Fasting blood samples were collected in the morning, between 60 days before and 46 days after LC MRI. Plasma p‐tau231, NfL, and GFAP were measured using SIMOA technology with commercial kits from Quanterix (p‐Tau231 Advantage #102,292, Neurology 2‐plexB #103,520) on an HD‐X analyzer (Quanterix; PMID: 20,495,550). Randomized samples for N2PB and for p‐tau231 were measured in duplicate over two analytical runs for each marker. All NP2B samples were detected and 100% displayed values above the lower limit of quantification reported for the assay (SIMOA NfL, LLOQ = 0.800 pg/mL; SIMOA GFAP, LLOQ = 16.6 pg/mL). All p‐tau231 samples were detected and 95.5% (381/399) displayed values above the LLOQ reported for the assay (SIMOA p‐tau231 = 0.617 pg/mL). Two control samples were run at the beginning and at the end of the plate to assess variability. Intra‐ and inter‐assays coefficients of variation (CV) were, respectively, 8.0% and 5.7% for plasma NfL; 7.4% and 9.3% for plasma GFAP; 9.59.% and 6.27% for plasma p‐tau231. One subject with elevated NfL level (> 3 standard deviations [SDs] above the mean) underwent a cerebellar cyst surgery the year preceding the measure. As this intervention may have increased NfL level, this subject was excluded from all analysis involving this biomarker (unless explicitly stated otherwise). More details can be found in the supporting information.

Statistical analyses

Main analyses were performed using data from the subjects not included in the atlas set. The relationships between LC integrity measures (both intensity and volume) and whole brain images (both T1‐weighted MRI and FDG PET) were evaluated in four separate models using voxelwise partial correlations in SPM12. Age, sex, level of education, and intervention group were included as covariates. We made the hypotheses of stronger positive relationships between LC integrity and brain integrity in the presence of more AD pathology. Accordingly, positive interactions with amyloid PET SUVR, plasma p‐tau231, GFAP, and NfL were evaluated: the main effect of the considered biomarker was added as a covariate in each model detailed above, and the independent variable was defined as the product of, respectively, LC intensity and LC volume with this biomarker. Note that analyses involving amyloid PET additionally included delay between amyloid PET acquisition and LC MRI acquisition as a covariate. Significance was defined as a P value < 0.001 and a cluster size ≥ 50 voxels. In addition, cluster‐level family‐wise error (FWE) correction for multiple comparisons, based on random field theory, was applied with a minimal P value of 0.05 and a cluster size ≥ 50 voxels. Clusters surviving FWE correction were identified from the SPM results table.

RESULTS

Descriptive characteristics of the sample

Participants’ mean age was 73.71 (SD: 3.71) in the analyses set, 59.15% were female, 28.17% were APOE ε4 carriers, and 26.76% were amyloid positive (above a Centiloid threshold of 12). Mean education level was 13.52 years (SD: 3.03). The atlas set did not differ significantly from the main analyses set in any of these characteristics, nor on amyloid PET SUVR, plasma NfL, or plasma GFAP level. The atlas set showed marginally lower p‐tau231 values (P = 0.05). For more details, see Table 1.

Associations between LC integrity and T1‐weighted MRI or FDG PET

Voxelwise multiple regression showed a positive association between LC intensity and T1‐weighted MRI in the frontal lobes (right orbital area, pars triangularis and middle frontal area, left supplementary motor area [SMA] and bilateral prefrontal cortex [PFC] and premotor cortex), in the right superior parietal lobule, postcentral gyrus and supramarginal gyrus, bilateral ventral anterior cingulate, most of the right lateral temporal lobe, left temporopolar area, bilateral hippocampi, accumbens nuclei, and the right perirhinal cortex. This association survived FWE correction at the cluster level in the right superior temporal gyrus and the anterior and dorsolateral PFC. Conversely, there was no association between LC volume and T1‐weighted MRI (Figure 1). There was no significant association of LC intensity and LC volume with FDG PET (Figure 2).

Interaction effects between AD biomarkers and LC intensity on T1‐weighted MRI

Interaction effects between AD biomarkers and LC intensity on T1‐weighted MRI are illustrated in Figure 3. LC intensity interacted with amyloid PET to predict T1‐weighted MRI signal, such that a higher amyloid PET uptake was associated with a higher slope of the association between LC intensity and T1‐weighted MRI in the right anterior PFC and the left dorsal anterior cingulate. There was no interaction between LC intensity and p‐tau231 on T1‐weighted MRI signal. However, there was an interaction between LC intensity and plasma NfL on T1‐weighted MRI such that the slope of the association between LC intensity and T1‐weighted MRI signal in the left superior parietal lobule was higher for higher NfL levels. Similarly, plasma GFAP modulated the association between LC intensity and T1‐weighted MRI signal in the left superior parietal lobule, angular area, subgenual cingulate area, and orbitofrontal cortex, and in the right peristriate area, such that it was more positive for higher GFAP levels. None of these interactions survived FWE correction at the cluster level.

Additional sensitivity analyses

To assess the robustness of our results, we performed additional analyses: with no covariates, including data from the atlas set, removing outliers on amyloid PET SUVR, plasma p‐tau and GFAP levels, adding the outlier on plasma NfL level (one outlier was identified per biomarker, each corresponding to a different participant), adding APOE ε4 status as an additional covariate, as well as testing for negative associations and interactions. These analyses showed that our results were independent of the covariates used in the model and confirmed when the sample size was increased by including data from the atlas set. Outliers influenced some interactions, in a way coherent with their unique profile (e.g., AD diagnosed after the end of the study or widespread atrophy suggestive of Lewy body disease). In particular, adding the outlier on NfL level resulted in a strong positive interaction between LC intensity and NfL level on FDG PET in the right frontal lobe; the right posterior superior temporal gyrus and middle temporal area; in the left PFC, anterior caudate nucleus, ventral entorhinal cortex, and anterior hippocampus; and in the bilateral supramarginal gyri. Significant negative associations and interactions were scarcely found in small areas.

Most significant associations of AD biomarkers with T1‐weighted MRI and FDG PET signals were negative and located in the frontal lobe.

More details can be found in the supplementary text and Figures S4–18 in supporting information.

DISCUSSION

The present study aimed at evaluating the associations between LC integrity and both gray matter structure and metabolism, and their modulations by AD biomarkers, in CU older adults. LC intensity was positively associated with frontal and temporal GMV, partially surviving FWE correction. No additional significant associations were found with GMV or FDG PET. Furthermore, we found larger interactions between LC integrity measures and AD biomarkers on FDG PET than on GMV.

A positive correlation between LC intensity and GMV or cortical thickness was inconsistently reported in the literature, likely due to small samples and heterogeneous LC measurement methods. 21 , 22 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 Two of three studies found associations with cortical thickness of the fusiform, precentral, lingual and superior temporal gyri, while associations in the anterior PFC, orbital area, pars triangularis, temporal poles, or the inferior and middle temporal gyrus were only reported once. Tang et al. 32 found an association between LC volume and GMV, notably in the fusiform and supramarginal gyri, orbital area, and most of the medial and lateral temporal lobes. These regions overlap with those in which we found a GMV and LC intensity association. However, we found no association with LC volume. This discrepancy could reflect methodological differences, as the authors measured LC volume on standard T1‐weighted MRI, as opposed to our dedicated LC MRI, and used a published mask in MNI space. 44 Overall, these studies and ours highlight a positive association between LC's and structurally connected brain regions’ integrity. 25 This could reflect concomitant neurodegeneration, or a co‐developmental link. In the first case, in our CU population, we would expect stronger associations between LC integrity and FDG PET, considered an earlier neurodegeneration biomarker than atrophy measured on T1‐weighted MRI. 33 , 45 , 46 , 47 , 48 However, although a previous study showed a positive association between LC integrity measures and FDG PET in a mixed AD/MCI/CU population, 23 we found no such effect in CU participants, suggesting that hypometabolism linked to LC damage may be undetectable in this population. However, it could emerge in participants bearing heavier AD pathology.

Indeed, we found positive interactions between LC integrity and AD biomarkers on GMV and FDG PET. Significant interactions on GMV were limited and heterogeneous, but unthresholded maps were similar across AD biomarkers. However, LC intensity interacted with plasma p‐tau231 and GFAP, and LC volume with plasma GFAP, to predict FDG PET in the right anterior PFC; with strikingly similar unthresholded maps. Interestingly, the interaction of p‐tau231 with LC intensity in the right frontal lobe was more positive after excluding a subject presenting a high p‐tau231 value and frontal atrophy, reducing FDG PET signal despite high metabolism. In contrast, excluding a subject with a high GFAP value (later diagnosed with AD), reduced the positive interaction between GFAP and LC intensity on frontal FDG PET; and when a subject with high NfL values possibly linked to white matter damage or to a cerebellar cyst surgery was included, a positive interaction between LC intensity and plasma NfL on frontal FDG PET was found. Interestingly, most AD biomarkers were associated with frontal atrophy and hypometabolism. The anterior PFC, commonly hypometabolic in aging, 49 , 50 is among the regions most strongly connected to the LC, as assessed with tractography, and (inconsistently) functional connectivity. 25 , 51 Two studies described aging‐related LC connectivity changes in opposite directions. 51 , 52 Our findings may therefore reflect LC axonal degeneration, increasing plasma NfL and altering LC connections. This seems to impact neuronal metabolism partially through astrocytes (even though plasma GFAP level may be influenced by other factors 53 ), particularly in pre‐clinical AD patients. NE stimulates through β receptors astrocytic lactate release, a neuronal energy source. 7 , 54 , 55 , 56 β2 receptors are densely expressed in the frontal and temporal cortex, 57 aligning with our unthresholded interaction maps.

The present study also shows an interaction between LC volume and amyloid PET predicting FDG PET in the medial and lateral temporal lobes (cluster‐level FWE corrected), left anterior PFC, and the SMA. Interestingly, Engels‐Domínguez et al. 29 found an interaction of LC intensity with entorhinal tau PET and global amyloid PET, predicting cortical thickness of the medial and lateral temporal lobes, and the insula, respectively, in an older CU population. Including cognitively impaired participants extended these results to all cerebral lobes. Interactions observed on FDG PET in the present study may thus reflect earlier changes than those uncovered by these authors on standard T1. Interestingly, most areas in which these authors and we found interactions between LC integrity and amyloid PET were reported as increasing their connectivity with the LC in aging. 51 , 52 This suggests that amyloid pathology exacerbates age‐related LC hyperconnectivity, maybe through LC hyperactivity. Indeed, one study with convergent mice and AD patients’ data showed that amyloid beta oligomers increase spontaneous LC firing. 58

Overall, the interactions between LC integrity and AD biomarkers on FDG PET could reflect two seemingly opposite phenomena: first, when AD pathology is present, higher LC intensity, reflecting greater LC integrity, could predict increased glucose metabolism, reflecting reduced neurodegeneration. Conversely, LC hyperactivity might drive cortical hyperactivity and/or inflammation, thereby elevating glucose metabolism. 59 , 60 These mechanisms may coexist.

LC hyperactivity would increase NE release. Despite NE's neuroprotective role, 61 , 62 an excess can exacerbate tau pathology and induce behavioral deficits in ADLP‐tau mice. 63 LC hyperactivity may also directly favor tau propagation from LC neurons. 64 Moreover, increased NE production may increase LC neuromelanin accumulation, 65 , 66 occurring during normal aging. 67 , 68 , 69 It clutters the cytoplasm and disrupts vesicular trafficking and protein degradation, leading to neuronal death, 65 , 70 which may decrease NE release. However, compensatory axonal sprouting and NE recapture decrease occur. 71 NE signaling changes in AD remains controversial, as human studies focused on indirect measures, with conflicting results. 71 One hypothesis is an increased tonic LC activity, reducing phasic responses. 72 , 73

In this study, we used a LC MRI sequence sensitive to MT effects. These arise from selective magnetization of macromolecule‐bound protons, transferred to free‐water protons. Regrettably, their exact biological substrate remains unclear. The large LC neurons’ size increases cytosolic volume and free‐water‐bound protons, enhancing MT effects. 74 , 75 However, other brain regions with similar macromolecule‐to‐water proton ratios are not hyperintense. 76 The high LC's copper content might contribute, yet regional copper concentrations show no correlation with MT effects. 75 Neuromelanin is another likely contributor: LC intensity follows an inverse U‐shaped age variation, 16 , 17 , 18 paralleling neuromelanin content. 67 However, it is important to note that although the mouse LC lacks neuromelanin, MT effects are still present. 75 Conversely, inducing neuromelanin synthesis in rats’ substantia nigra generates MT contrast, suggesting that neuromelanin is one, although not the sole, MT source. 70 While neuromelanin phantoms show no MT effect, they shorten T1, 74 which may increase MT under low macromolecule‐to‐free water proton ratios. 76 , 77 Lipids, which accumulate within neuromelanin granules, could further contribute to MT effects. 74

Interestingly, before cell death, p‐tau induces neuritic degeneration, 78 decreasing cytoplasm volume, which may reduce MT effects. It may thus decrease both LC intensity and volume.

To our knowledge, no study directly investigated how LC activity affects MRI‐based LC measures. LC intensity tends to positively correlate with pupillary response to reinforcement learning losses, reflecting LC phasic activity. 79 Additionally, increased NE production may increase neuromelanin accumulation. Chronic LC hyperactivity might therefore increase intensity. However, neurons bearing more neuromelanin are more susceptible to p‐tau–related toxicity, 65 , 80 mitigating this effect.

LC volume and intensity may therefore both decline with LC neurodegeneration, and intensity might also reflect functional changes. This could explain interaction differences. However, studies combining neuropathology with MRI measurements are needed to test this hypothesis.

Overall, our results support a higher association between LC integrity and brain integrity in the context of aging and AD pathology, consistent with LC's roles in tau propagation and neuroprotection.

Our study has several strengths. First, the high‐resolution MRI sequence used allowed a sensitive LC detection. 81 Then, robust and individualized automatic LC segmentation yielded two complementary LC integrity measures: intensity and volume. These measures are rarely compared, which is important to understand how each is affected by different LC alterations. We leveraged multi‐modal imaging to compare associations with GMV and glucose metabolism. We reported separately statistically robust (FWE corrected), and more exploratory results. Finally, to our knowledge, this is the first study testing interactions of LC integrity measures with astrogliosis and neurodegeneration biomarkers on GMV and FDG PET.

However, limitations should be acknowledged. The cross‐sectional design precludes causal inferences. The small LC size makes volume measures sensitive to segmentation errors and partial volume effects linked to LC MRI sequence anisotropy. These measures are likely underestimated as they are inferior to the estimate that can be inferred from a previous post mortem study. 1 More precisely, our LC segmentations seem to be similar to a previously published template‐based method, 82 but to be less extensive rostrally than a formerly published meta‐mask. 83 The automatic segmentation method used was intended for the present population and was not tested for generalization on broader populations. In addition, the highly educated CU sample was relatively small, reducing statistical power. Yet, significant findings in this homogeneous group suggest stronger effects in broader populations, which warrants confirmation in further studies. The consistent spatial patterns across biomarkers and their overlap with regions known to be affected by aging and AD further support biological validity. Finally, plasma p‐tau reflects both tau and amyloid pathology. 84 Here, its interactions patterns differed from those of amyloid PET, but more studies are needed to assess whether they truly reflect interactions with tau pathology.
