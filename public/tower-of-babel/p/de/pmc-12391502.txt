Biomechanical analysis reveals hormonal crosstalk in inflorescence bending of cut stock flower
Authors: Sepideh Famil Irani, Mostafa Arab, Kourosh Vahdati, Mahmoud Lotfi, Mahmoud Reza Roozban
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn cut stock (Matthiola incana L.), inflorescence bending is primarily caused by the bending moment generated by the weight of the floral structure. This phenomenon results in drooping of inflorescence onto neighboring plants, diminishing ornamental quality and increased space requirements among plants. Reduction of the bending moment increases the inflorescence’s curvature radius, limits apical deviation and enables more compact plant spacing. This study investigated the individual and interactive effects of three auxins (IAA, NAA and IBA) and three cytokinins (KIN, BA and ZT), each applied three times beginning at the 10-leaf stage and repeated at 7-day intervals at the concentrations of 0, 10 and 100 ppm. Treatments were evaluated through a series of nine factorial experiments. Of the 81 auxin–cytokinin combinations tested, 15 treatments completely prevented inflorescence bending by producing upright stems. The effective treatments significantly reduced the length-to-diameter ratio (LDR) (p < 0.05), which was strongly correlated with both apical deviation (δ) and bending stress. LDR had a greater influence on δ than the stem’s Young’s modulus (E), suggesting that hormonal crosstalk primarily affected stem geometry rather than material stiffness. Notably, combinations of IBA with either KIN or ZT improved biomechanical stability by lowering the center of gravity, and shortening the torque arm, thereby reducing bending stress below critical levels. Treatments containing BA were associated with higher E and mechanical resistance index (MRI), indicating enhanced stem stiffness. Among the most effective combinations, IAA 10 ppm + BA 10 ppm yielded the most visually desirable inflorescences—long, straight stems with ideal floret arrangement.Keywords: Auxin–cytokinin interaction, Inflorescence biomechanics, Stem curvature, Lodging resistance, Mechanical properties, Racemose inflorescenceSubject terms: Auxin, Cytokinin, Biological physics

Introduction

Matthiola incana L., commonly known as double-flowered stock, is a highly valued cut flower worldwide due to its vibrant color palette and pleasant fragrance1. The species’ aesthetically appealing inflorescence structure contributes significantly to its commercial value. However, the substantial weight of the dense flower clusters often induces curvature in the stem, typically just below the inflorescence. This bending causes the flower head to lean onto nearby plants, and as the inflorescences separate, some florets may lose at the connection points. Controlling stem curvature is, therefore, crucial for producing high-quality floral stems2.

Stem bending is a common pre- or post-harvest issue in several cut flower species, including roses, Gerbera, Tulips, Gladiolus and Snapdragons3–8. In stock, curvature typically manifests after the full development of the inflorescence and is more prevalent in pigmented cultivars. Genetic background significantly affects stem mechanical strength; for instance, highly double white cultivars often possess stronger stems compared to the more phenotypically diverse, colored ‘selectable’ types9. Due to their broad color range, selectable cultivars are frequently targeted in research focused on improving stem rigidity. Past studies have evaluated the roles of superphosphate, micronutrients (e.g., zinc), biostimulants and biofertilizers10,11 on stem strength, while commercial cultivation often relies on physical supports such as nylon nets to prevent bending. Despite the economic and horticultural significance of M. incana, the physiological and biomechanical mechanisms underlying stem curvature in this species remain poorly understood. Understanding these mechanisms is particularly important because M. incana serves as both a commercially important cut flower and a representative model for studying stem stability in dense-inflorescence ornamental crops.

Auxins (AUX), including the naturally occurring indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) and the synthetic analogs, indole-3-butyric acid (IBA) and 1-naphthaleneacetic acid (NAA), are key regulators of cell elongation and division, particularly during stem bending. AUX promotes cell wall loosening, facilitating elongation, while inflorescence stems indole-3-butyric acid (IBA) shows limited mobility and indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) is transported basipetally. This uneven distribution leads to differential cell expansion and, consequently, curvature12–14. When stem cells lack sufficient structural integrity to counter gravitational and mechanical stress, this hormonal asymmetry exacerbates deviation from vertical orientation15. Curvature is often attributed to inadequate energy for cell wall stiffening and turgor maintenance in the bending zone16. Although NAA functions similarly to natural auxins in plant bioassays, its role in stem biomechanics is less understood17.

Kinetin (KIN) and 6-benzylaminopurine (BA) are common synthetic cytokinins (CKs), while Zeatin (ZT) is the predominant natural form, enriched at the inflorescence base; CKs in active growth regions such as inflorescence stems promote xylem development, fiber differentiation, secondary cell wall formation, and regulate inflorescence arrest and stem length18–20.

Auxins and cytokinins rarely act in isolation; rather, their synergistic interactions orchestrate complex developmental processes, including stem bending, with AUX capable of modulating CK biosynthesis locally and often suppressing endogenous CK levels21–23. Exogenous AUX may inhibit the function of endogenous hormones by altering hormonal balance and metabolism, and in some cases, such as IAA, can even downregulate its own biosynthesis24–26. Hormonal signaling is further complicated by feedback mechanisms, whereby exogenous application of one hormone triggers compensatory responses from endogenous pathways. Manipulating AUX transport and perception can influence phototropic and gravitropic responses, thereby altering stem orientation and curvature27. While AUX application affects the regulatory pathways involved in stem curvature, CKs enhance tissue resistance to bending torque by promoting cell wall thickening and lignification19.

Despite extensive studies on hormone interactions in other ornamental species—such as African marigold, Lilium longiflorum, Chrysanthemum morifolium and Gladiolus28–31 —the combined application of AUX and CK in stock flower remains underexplored, with no adverse effects reported in related species.

This study employs a novel integrative approach, combining mathematical modeling with plant biomechanics, to evaluate whether exogenous hormones can enhance resistance to mechanical forces causing inflorescence bending. Through geometric analysis and physical simulation, we quantitatively assess how AUX–CK combinations influence stem architecture, mechanical properties, and overall structural stability. Building upon the existing literature, our work differs from previous reports in four key aspects: (1) it evaluates the biomechanical stability of M. incana inflorescences under hormonal treatments using a comprehensive set of mechanical parameters, including bending moment, bending stress, radius of curvature, Young’s modulus, and stem slenderness ratio; (2) it introduces novel mechanical indices to assess floral bending resistance; (3) it applies center of gravity and torque arm analysis to explain the relationship between morphological traits and inflorescence stability; and (4) it integrates biomechanical and aesthetic assessments—an approach not previously reported. These innovations offer a new perspective for improving ornamental plant quality and shelf life.

Materials and methods

Experimental design and procedure

Seeds of Matthiola incana L. cv. Pacific Crimson were procured from PanAmerican Seed Company®. To ensure the exclusive selection of double-flowered phenotypes, seedlings with pale green cotyledons were identified and separated from those with dark green cotyledons at the four-leaf stage, following the method described by32. After 20 days, the seedlings were transplanted into 20 cm diameter pots filled with a growing medium comprising 50% peat moss, 35% perlite, and 15% coarse sand, pre-mixed with soil.

Hormone stock solutions were prepared by accurately weighing the powdered AUX and CKs (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). Auxins were dissolved in 0.1 M sodium hydroxide (NaOH), and CKs were dissolved in 0.1 M potassium hydroxide (KOH) to prepare concentrated stock solutions at 10 mg/mL (10,000 ppm). Working solutions at final concentrations of 10 ppm and 100 ppm were freshly prepared by diluting the stock solutions with distilled water at ratios of 1:1000 and 1:100, respectively. To reduce surface tension and ensure uniform foliar application, 0.01% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was added to all hormone solutions. All solutions were prepared approximately one hour prior to use to maintain freshness and ensure experimental consistency.

Due to the absence of a standardized concentration model for mitigating stem bending via hormone treatment, three AUX (IAA, NAA and IBA) and three CK (BA, KIN and ZT) were tested at concentrations of 0, 10 and 100 ppm. The 0-ppm concentration served as a control, while 10 and 100 ppm were chosen to evaluate putative effective treatment levels. A total of nine completely randomized factorial experiments were conducted under controlled greenhouse conditions at 20/16 °C (day/night). In each experiment, two hormones (one AUX and one CK) were tested at three concentrations, with three replicates per treatment, resulting in 27 pots per experiment. The experiments included: IAA-BA (Exp. 1); IAA-KIN (Exp. 2); IAA-ZT (Exp. 3); NAA-BA (Exp. 4); NAA-KIN (Exp. 5); NAA-ZT (Exp. 6); IBA-BA (Exp. 7); IBA-KIN (Exp. 8); IBA-ZT (Exp. 9). Exp. 9: IBA–ZT.

Hormones were applied as foliar sprays under dark conditions using light-insulated containers at a temperature of 16–18 °C. Applications were carried out at three critical developmental stages, each spaced seven days apart: (1) The 10-leaf stage (prior to inflorescence emergence); (2) Early inflorescence emergence; and (3) One week after inflorescence emergence.

This interval was selected to allow adequate hormone absorption and physiological response while minimizing the risk of treatment overlap or phytotoxic effects. The application strategy was designed to modulate endogenous hormonal pathways that govern stem elongation and curvature, thereby enhancing structural integrity prior to full inflorescence development.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (v.26, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), chosen for its robust statistical capabilities, user-friendly interface, and its wide acceptance in plant science research. Data were analyzed using factorial ANOVA. When the omnibus F-test was significant, treatment means were compared using Duncan’s Multiple Range Test at the corresponding significance level (p < 0.05 or p < 0.01). All reported values represent the mean of three biological replicates (n = 3). Pearson correlation analysis was employed to evaluate relationships between curvature rate and hormone-induced traits. Graphs and visualizations were generated using MATLAB R2018a.

Results

Hormonal regulation of inflorescence curvature

Independent effects of auxin and cytokinin

Both auxins (AUX) and cytokinins (CK) significantly reduced stem curvature; however, none of the treatments completely corrected the deviation. Among AUX, NAA at 100 ppm was the most effective (P < 0.01), reducing curvature to 0.17 radians cm-1 (approximately 10°) by increasing stem diameter and reducing inflorescence length. In contrast, IAA significantly promoted stem elongation, biomass accumulation, and floret spacing (P < 0.01), which indirectly contributed to a reduction in curvature. IBA exhibited minimal effects and only at higher concentrations, with no observable impact on stem diameter (Fig. 2a–c).

Independent effects of auxin and cytokinin treatments on inflorescence curvature, stem length, and stem diameter in Matthiola incana L. cv. Pacific Crimson.

Cytokinins, including BA, KIN and ZT, also significantly reduced curvature (P < 0.01), primarily by increasing stem thickness and reducing internode length. BA induced a compact growth form with shorter inflorescences, while ZT produced the thickest stems. However, plants treated with ZT maintained relatively high deviation angles (18°–25°), indicating that increased stem diameter alone is insufficient to mitigate curvature in the absence of reduced floral load (Fig. 2d–f).

Structural enhancements for curvature mitigation

The combined application of auxins and cytokinins yielded superior results compared to individual treatments. The NAA10 + BA10 ppm combination was the most effective, resulting in 44.4% of treated plants producing fully straight inflorescences (Fig. 3g). This treatment significantly increased stem diameter (12.51 mm) and promoted elongation of both the stem and the inflorescence.

Interactive effects of auxin and cytokinin combinations on inflorescence curvature in Matthiola incana L. cv. Pacific Crimson. Treatments resulting in fully straight inflorescences are indicated by a curvature (K) of 0. The red line demarcates the concentration threshold beyond which adverse effects were observed.

The IAA10 + BA10 ppm treatment resulted in 33.3% straight inflorescences, with significant enhancements in stem diameter, length, and fresh weight (Fig. 3a). The highest stem diameter (> 8 mm) was recorded under the IAA100 + BA100 ppm treatment.

Treatments combining IBA10 + KIN (10, 100 ppm) produced straight inflorescences in 22.2% of plants (Fig. 3e), with stem diameters exceeding 8 mm and inflorescence lengths exceeding 40 cm. While IAA100 + ZT10 ppm extended inflorescence length to 45 cm (Curvature = 0, Fig. 3c), its visual appeal was diminished due to a reduced floret density (66%). Conversely, the IBA10 + ZT10 ppm treatment resulted in straight inflorescences (11.1%) with 43 cm inflorescence length and higher floret density.

The NAA10 + ZT10 ppm treatment produced the longest stems (52 cm) and the highest fresh weight (120.12 g), with all treated plants exhibiting straight inflorescences (Fig. 3i). However, combinations of IAA + KIN did not yield any straight inflorescences (Fig. 3b).

The IBA + BA combination reduced stem deviation at higher BA concentrations, though it did not result in any completely straight stems (Fig. 3d). In contrast, the IBA10 + ZT10 ppm and NAA100 + KIN10 ppm combinations achieved straight inflorescences in 11.1% of treated plants (Fig. 3f, h). Across all nine experiments, auxin alone failed to mitigate inflorescence bending, whereas cytokinin co-application effectively restored uprightness (Fig. 4).

Stabilization of inflorescence architecture

In this naturally single-stemmed species, the combination of KIN 10 + NAA 100 ppm induced the formation of multiple stems. Additionally, BA 100 ppm combined with IBA, NAA, or IAA (100 ppm) transformed the native raceme into compound umbels, cymes, or panicle-like structures, demonstrating considerable developmental plasticity (Fig. 5).

While these architectural modifications effectively reduced stem bending, they compromised ornamental value due to significant deviations from the typical floral form. In contrast, treatments such as IBA 10 + KIN (10 or 100) selectively enlarged basal florets -from 3.0 to 5.0–5.5 cm—resulting in a frustum-like inflorescence. This morphology consistently emerged in the IBA—KIN, IBA—ZT, and IAA—ZT experiments, effectively lowering the center of mass (<), as supported by the geometric model (Fig. 6). The downward shift in the center of mass reduced the effective moment arm, thereby lowering bending stress and enhancing stem stability, even in plants with increased height and biomass.

Biomechanical analysis

Significant variation in the mechanical properties of inflorescence stems was observed across the nine hormonal treatments (P < 0.001). Effective treatments demonstrated substantially higher moments of inertia (0.03–0.07 cm4) compared to the control (~ 0.01 cm4), indicating increased resistance to gravitational bending. ANOVA confirmed the significance of these differences (P < 0.05).

All successful treatments significantly reduced the LDR index (range: 12.0–44.4), which correlated with enhanced mechanical stability in accordance with beam theory (Eq. 7). Young’s modulus values ranged from 0.1 to 0.4 GPa. The highest stiffness (0.4 GPa, curvature = 0.0039 rad cm⁻1) was observed in the IAA 100 + KIN 100 ppm treatment, while the lowest (0.1 GPa, curvature = 0.022 rad cm⁻1) was recorded under NAA 100 + ZT 100 ppm. Control plants exhibited intermediate values (E = 0.128 GPa; curvature = 0.02 rad cm⁻1). Notably, 15 treatments resulted in completely straight stems, reflecting infinite stiffness and no observable tip displacement.

The Mechanical Resistance Index (MRI) was used as an integrated measure of stem performance, ranging from 0.03 to 0.05, with control plants registering at 0.02. The highest MRI values (0.05) were found in treatments such as NAA 10 + KIN 10 ppm and IAA 100 + BA 10 ppm. However, complete curvature elimination often required higher hormone concentrations (e.g., NAA 100 + KIN 10 ppm). In contrast, lower MRI values in treatments like IBA 100 + ZT 10 ppm indicated limited or even negative effects on structural integrity.

Treatments involving NAA–BA and IAA–BA combinations yielded optimal reinforcement, with elevated bending moments (1.2–1.5 N cm) and moderate stress levels (1.3–2.7 MPa). Conversely, IAA–KIN and IBA–BA treatments exhibited mechanical sensitivity, as evidenced by higher stresses (4.5–6.8 MPa) despite lower bending moments. These findings underscore the importance of hormonal combinations in modulating stem biomechanics and improving structural resilience.

Selection of effective treatments

To identify the most effective treatments for maintaining inflorescence aesthetics, the high-concentration treatments (100 ppm), despite their structural effectiveness, were excluded due to adverse effects on floral appearance. Instead, low-concentration treatments were prioritized for their balanced performance. These included: IBA 10 + KIN 10, NAA 10 + ZT 10, IAA 10 + BA 10, IBA 10 + ZT 10, and NAA 10 + BA 10 ppm. Treatments were evaluated based on key visual and structural traits using normalized mean scores (Fig. 11). An overall performance index was calculated by averaging the normalized scores across all parameters. For the trait pedicel distance, normalization was inverted so that lower values received higher scores.

Among the tested combinations, IAA 10 + BA 10 ppm achieved the highest overall performance, demonstrating superior preservation of both structural integrity and inflorescence aesthetics. While IBA 10 + ZT 10 ppm performed well in traits related to elongation, its effectiveness across other parameters was inconsistent. Notably, even the top-performing treatments still scored lower than the control in floral aesthetic traits, highlighting a trade-off between mechanical reinforcement and ornamental quality.

Discussion

Hormonal control of inflorescence curvature

Stem curvature in Matthiola incana L. is primarily driven by hormonal interactions, particularly the asymmetric distribution of auxins (AUX), such as indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) and indole-3-butyric acid (IBA), which induce differential cell elongation43,44. In contrast, CK, such as zeatin, enhance mechanical strength by promoting secondary cell wall formation45. AUX suppresses CK biosynthesis, underscoring a key hormonal antagonism that regulates structural stability46. Plants can redirect AUX to specific tissues to correct growth imbalances that might otherwise result in curvature or lodging. Disruptions in this hormonal balance—whether intrinsic or through external application—can alter stem orientation and bending resistance47.

Application of AUX alone was insufficient to prevent stem curvature in M. incana L. cv. Pacific Crimson, indicating that redistribution of growth regulators alone does not adequately counterbalance bending stress.

Hormonal modulation of stem rigidity and geometry

Statistically significant differences (p < 0.01) were observed among treatments in both biomechanical and morphological traits. Fifteen treatments effectively increased stem stiffness and reduced the length-to-diameter ratio (LDR), thereby enhancing the stem’s resistance to bending induced by floral mass. According to the bending equation ( M/I = σ/y = E/R48, stem curvature is influenced by both tissue stiffness (E) and structural geometry (I, R). Hormonal treatments primarily improved stem rigidity by altering geometric characteristics—specifically, reducing LDR—which increased resistance to bending moments and minimized tip deflection. These outcomes align with established biomechanical models49 and confirm the structural basis for stem erectness. A strong positive correlation between LDR and both curvature and maximum bending stress (Fig. 10a) supports the use of LDR as a reliable mechanical indicator for dynamic stem stability37.

Architecture and mechanical stability

Approximately 33% of the successful treatments reduced inflorescence bending in Matthiola incana L., suggesting that geometric alterations of the inflorescence significantly contributed to enhanced mechanical stability. This finding is consistent with previous research demonstrating that plant architecture influences performance under gravitational loading50,51. Treatments involving IBA in combination with KIN or ZT promoted the development of larger basal florets, resulting in a frustum-like inflorescence architecture. Prior studies have reported that IAA, BA, and KIN increase floret diameter in lilium and orchid52,53. In this study, the combination of KIN and ZT significantly increased floret size, with 54% of large-flowered stocks exhibiting no bending, compared to only 30% among typical plants.

In M. incana, frustum-shaped inflorescences displayed reduced curvature due to the lower positioning of heavier florets, which shortened their moment arms and reduced the overall bending moment (;34. According to beam theory (δ ∝ ML2/EI), this reduction in bending moment led to improved structural stability, reinforcing the functional role of architectural modifications in mechanical reinforcement. Treatments that reduced or eliminated curvature effectively stabilized inflorescences under load. These findings highlight the value of simple morphological and biomechanical metrics as diagnostic tools for evaluating the anti-lodging effects of hormone treatments54.

Mechanical predictors of stem stability

CK is known to delay flowering and promote inflorescence elongation by maintaining meristematic activity and delaying floral transition20. Although inflorescence length increased and blooming was delayed, stem curvature did not rise correspondingly—likely due to concurrent increases in stem diameter, which enhanced mechanical stability. Similar responses have been observed following AUX and CK application in other ornamental species, including carnation, tulip, zinnia, pothos and gladiolus55–59.

The model δ ∝ LDR4/E—where δ denotes tip deviation—suggests that inflorescence displacement is highly sensitive to increases in LDR and only modestly affected by changes in tissue stiffness (E). The moderate positive correlation between δ and LDR (R2 = 0.24) supports this model, while the weak correlation between δ and E (R2 = 0.03) suggests a relatively minor role for intrinsic material properties. These findings indicate that hormonal treatments primarily influenced stem geometry, rather than its mechanical material properties49, providing a functional explanation for enhanced structural performance through reduced slenderness60.

A comparative analysis of the Mechanical Resistance Index (MRI) across hormone treatments revealed significant differences in stem strength (p < 0.05). Treatments yielding the highest MRI values (> 0.05) also exhibited the lowest stem curvature (< 0.01 radians/cm), suggesting a strong link between structural rigidity and mechanical resistance. MRI, defined as the ratio of bending moment to stress (MRI = M/σ), simplifies to I/r in circular stems, where I = πr4/4. This formulation reveals MRI to be proportional to r3, emphasizing the role of geometry in mechanical efficiency. Accordingly, MRI serves as a concise and practical index for evaluating stem resistance under hormonal influence38,61. BA-treated plants consistently showed higher MRI values, indicating superior mechanical resilience.

Hormones and inflorescence quality

Notably, 50% of the successful treatments involved combinations of BA with AUX, underscoring BA’s central role in enhancing structural integrity. These findings align with prior studies demonstrating BA’s benefits for flower quality and development in species such as orchid and Laelia anceps62,63. Successful treatments also reduced floret density (< 1 floret/cm) and increased pedicel distance (> 1 cm), thereby lowering the bending moment along the inflorescence axis. This adjustment likely enhanced mechanical stability by distributing floral loads more evenly and reducing leverage forces at the distal end64.

Auxin also regulates pedicel and internode spacing, as demonstrated in Arabidopsis65, where a negative correlation between internode length and stem diameter (p < 0.01) suggests that thicker, more stable stems are associated with shorter internodes (< 0.8 cm). The influence of exogenous AUX and CK on internode length has been documented in other ornamentals, including African marigold66 and tulip56.

Limitations and future directions

This research successfully combines hormonal treatments with biomechanical measurements specific to clustered inflorescences, providing novel insights into factors controlling inflorescence bending. While our study offers novel insights through the combined hormonal and biomechanical approach, it is limited by the absence of molecular and cellular-level analyses as well as some advanced biomechanical tests. These limitations reflect the scope and resources of the current work and highlight important avenues for future research to build on and expand our findings.

Conclusions

This study shows that hormonal treatments effectively eliminate inflorescence curvature by enhancing flexural rigidity (E × I ≥ M). Key mechanisms include geometric optimization via reduced stem slenderness (LDR), which minimizes tip displacement (δ) and mechanical stress without changing tissue stiffness (E). Hormones also shift the floral mass closer to the stem base, reducing torque and increasing stability. Notably, geometry (LDR) has a stronger impact on stem posture than material stiffness (E), highlighting the importance of structural traits. Practically, IAA and BA at 10 ppm improve inflorescence quality and mechanical integrity without phytotoxicity. Future research should focus on elucidating the anatomical and molecular mechanisms underlying these hormonal effects, and on testing their potential application in improving the architectural stability of other ornamental species.
