The utility of [18F]Fluciclovine PET/CT in Evaluating Nonmetastatic Castrate-Resistant Prostate Cancer Patients (nmCRPCp): diagnostic performance and impact on management
Authors: Moataz Soliman, Kareem Elfatairy, Reham Ellessy, Yury S Velichko, Ryan Avery, Linda C kelahan, Ashley E Ross, Hatice Savas
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective:To evaluate the role of [18F]Fluciclovine PET/CT scan in restaging nmCRPCp and its impact on management.Methods and materials:This retrospective study included all patients with nonmetastatic castrate-resistant prostate cancer, who underwent [18F]Fluciclovine PET/CT scans for restaging who had concern for disease progression. Two radiologists independently reviewed the PET/CT studies, assigned an overall impression, and reported the site and number of radiotracer activities in consensus and impact on management was recorded. Available tissue diagnosis and/or six-month clinical and imaging follow-up were used as reference standards.Results:Thirty-five patients were included in this study. At least one lesion was detected in 73% (26/35) of the scans. Management changed in 71% (25/35) of patients, (22 positives and three negative scans). 26.9% (7/26) of patients were found to have an oligometastatic disease. Based on the reference standards, the diagnostic performance of [18F]Fluciclovine PET/CT in detecting recurrence in nmCRCP has 86%, sensitivity, 83% specificity, 96.1% PPV, and 55.5% NPV. There was no relationship between the Gleason score and a positive PET/CT scan in our patient population.Conclusion:Detecting the source of recurrence is challenging in nmCRCP patients when conventional imaging fails. Given the high PPV, sensitivity, and specificity, [18F]Fluciclovine PET/CT can be used instead of conventional imaging as a first-line choice due to its superiority over bone scan and added value of detecting soft tissue metastasis regardless of the initial Gleason score.Advances in knowledge:The study highlights the added value of [18F]Fluciclovine PET/CT in detecting soft tissue metastasis regardless of the initial Gleason score, which is not possible with conventional imaging such as bone scans.The study highlights the potential role of [18F]Fluciclovine PET/CT guiding management change for nonmetastatic castrate-resistant prostate cancer patients, particularly those with oligometastatic disease.

Introduction

Prostate cancer is one of the most common cancers among males in the United States. One out of nine males will be affected by prostate cancer. It is the second leading cause of cancer-related death in males in the United States. 1 Despite the decline in prostate cancer-related deaths by 50% between 1993 and 2013 due to early detection and advanced treatment, the American cancer society estimates 288,300 new cases in 2023 with about 34,700 prostate cancer-related death. 2

Castrate-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC) is defined as disease progression (e.g., rising serum prostate-specific antigen [PSA], new metastasis, or progression of existing metastasis) while having castrate levels of serum testosterone (<50 ng dl−1). 3 The term CRPC does not imply that those patients will not respond to further androgen blockage, i.e., second-generation androgen blockers. It is estimated that 80–90% of patients will eventually develop hormone resistance. 4

CRPC can be divided into metastatic and nonmetastatic CRPC according to conventional imaging, bone scan, and PET/CT studies. 5 Patients with mCRPC have poorer prognoses with overall survival of less than two years. Detection of metastasis may prompt a change in management. Therefore, early detection is important to guide treatment and improve outcomes (6 month disease-free survival). 6 Additionally, the site and the number of metastasis have implications for prognosis and management. Bone and lymph nodes are amongst the most common locations for metastasis that tend to respond to locoregional radiation therapy. On the other hand, visceral organ metastasis, although less common, are more aggressive and resistant to treatment (i.e., liver and lung metastatic disease). 7,8 Several novel therapies have been developed in recent years for mCRPC, such as next-generation hormonal agents, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and PARP inhibitors which tend to improve survival and patient outcomes. 8 However, knowledge of the diagnostic performance of second-generation imaging modalities and head-to-head comparison between whole body MRI, [68Ga]Ga-PSMA-11, and [18F]Fluciclovine PET/CT in restaging CRPCp is lacking. The classification of mCRPC and nmCRPC relies heavily on imaging techniques such as conventional imaging and bone scans. However, the sensitivity of conventional imaging is limited at low PSA levels, which presents an opportunity for the use of second-generation molecular imaging modalities such as [18F]Fluciclovine PET/CT and [68Ga]Ga-PSMA-11 PET/CT. While these modalities offer higher sensitivity, there is still no agreement on the implications of detecting distant disease through second-generation imaging, which may have been missed on conventional imaging. 9 The emergence of those highly sensitive imaging modalities allows the detection of oligometastatic disease (<3 metastatic lesions). 10 Those cases can be managed by aiming for a complete cure. 11

There are limited studies in the medical literature utilizing second-generation imaging modalities including [18F]Fluciclovine PET/CT in restaging nmCRPC patients or describing patient characteristics of this cohort. 12 To our knowledge, there is no clinical data available that investigates the additional benefits of using [18F]Fluciclovine PET/CT for the evaluation of nmCRPC and its impact on clinical management.

The purpose of this study is to evaluate the diagnostic performance of [18F]Fluciclovine PET/CT in restaging patients with nmCRPC on initial conventional imaging and its impact on management and patient outcomes.

Methods and materials

Patient population

In this study, we retrospectively reviewed the medical records of 824 patients who had a [18F]Fluciclovine PET/CT scan at our system in a large tertiary center between March 2017 and March 2021 after our institutional review board approval. We included patients with non-metastatic CRPC on conventional imaging who met the following inclusion criteria: 1) underwent curative therapy (surgery, radiation therapy), 2) started to receive androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) due to a rise in their prostate-specific antigen (PSA) levels (first-generation ADT, second-generation ADT, LHRH agonist, and GnRH agonist), 3) had a rising PSA level despite castrate testosterone levels (<50 ng dl−1), 4) had available PSA levels within two weeks of the [18F]Fluciclovine PET/CT scan. Patients with a known concurrent other malignancy or lack of adequate clinical follow-up were not included in the study.

Clinical data and chart review

We collected clinical information from patients’ charts, including basic demographics, primary pathology results, primary definitive treatment (such as surgery or radiation therapy), the type of androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) given prior to the [18F]Fluciclovine PET/CT scan (anti-androgen, CYP17 inhibitor, LHRH agonist, LHRH antagonist, or second-generation ADT), PSA levels, PSA kinetics within 2 weeks prior to the [18F]Fluciclovine PET/CT study. Any change in the management plan was recorded. We defined change of management as if the patient received specific therapeutic intervention based on the results of the [18F]Fluciclovine PET/CT scan, such as chemotherapy, loco-regional radiotherapy, change in ADT therapy, or the introduction of second-generation radiotherapy. The change of management was defined as effective based on the following criteria: PSA response with a decline of more than 50% from the pre-scan baseline, stable PSA with less than 10% variability on at least two occasions over a minimum of three months following initiation of a new therapy, or adequate imaging-based progression-free survival on either follow-up [18F]Fluciclovine PET/CT or conventional scans such as MRI and CT within a six-month interval of management change.

[18F]Fluciclovine PET/CT imaging protocol

The patients were given a standard dose of 10 mCi (+20%) [18F]Fluciclovine through an intravenous injection while lying down on the scanner. The image acquisition started after 3–5 min of injection, and the arms of the patients were positioned overhead. The scanning began at the upper thigh and continued toward the base of the skull, taking approximately 20–30 min, using Biograph mCT or Biograph Vision Digital PET/CT scanners (Siemens Healthineers, Knoxville, TN, United States). Low-dose CT images were obtained over the same anatomical range with 3-mm slice reconstruction.

[18F]Fluciclovine PET/CT studies analysis

In this study, two radiologists, with 3 and 6 years of [18F]Fluciclovine PET/CT interpretation experience, independently examined the imaging studies. They determined whether each scan showed metastatic disease (positive) or not (negative) and recorded the location and number of any disease activities. The radiologists based their interpretation on visually identifying abnormal radiotracer activity. A lesion was considered positive if it was larger than 1 cm and showed intense uptake greater than the background bone marrow activity (specifically, the L3 vertebral body), or if it was smaller than 1 cm but had activity near or equal to the bone marrow and was significantly higher than the blood pool, on qualitative radiological evaluation. The sites examined included the prostatic bed, pelvic lymph nodes, abdominal lymph nodes, extra-abdominal lymph nodes (including the hilar, mediastinal, and supraclavicular lymph nodes), bones, and visceral organ involvement, such as lungs and liver.

The standard reference of truth

To establish a reference standard for the accuracy of the scans, we collected and analyzed data from six months after the scans were performed. These data included other imaging tests (such as X-ray, CT, or MRI) that could confirm or rule out the presence of metastasis, as well as clinical and laboratory examinations (PSA trends), and tissue samples obtained within three months of either scan (available for 9 patients).

Statistical analysis

In this study, to compare between continuous variables (e.g., age or PSA levels), we conducted the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U-test and report the median and interquartile range [IQR]. We utilized the Pearson correlation coefficient to explore the correlation between categorical variables and continuous variables. The detection rate was determined by calculating the percentage of scans that detected at least one positive activity site. Meanwhile, the site-specific positivity rates were reported as the proportion of all positive lesions. We assessed the diagnostic performance of the scans by measuring sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV). To determine predictors for treatment change, we applied binary logistic regression analysis. The data analysis was done using (IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 27.0, 2020, Armonk, NY.)

Basic population demographics

PSA kinetics

The median PSA before scan was 3.4 ng ml−1 (IQR 0.67–9.02 ng ml−1).31.4% (11/35) of patients had PSA levels less than 1 ng ml−1, 8.6% (3/35) of patients had PSA between 1 and 2 ng ml−1, and 60% (21/35) of patients had PSA >2 ng ml−1. PSA velocity and doubling time were available for 94.2%. (33/35) of patients with the median PSA doubling time being 3.1 months (IQR 1.7–10.35 months). There were significantly greater detected lesions (90 lesions) in patients with PSA >2 ng dl−1 compared to those with PSA <2 ng dl−1 (16 lesions) (p-value = 0.006). However, there was no statistical significant difference in positivity rate (positive scan) between the three PSA groups (p-value = 0.679).

Detection rates and sites of recurrence per PSA and primary Gleason scores

At least one lesion was detected in 74.3% (26/35) of the scans regardless of PSA level. A total of 106 lesions were detected in 35 cases. The detection rate was 42.9% (15/35) in the prostate/bed, 22.9% (8/35) in pelvic bones, 34.3% (12/35) in pelvic lymph nodes, 20% (7/35) in abdominal lymph nodes, 14.3 (5/35) in the spine, 14.3% (5/35) in the ribs, 8.6% (3/35) in the sternum, 17.1% (6/35) in the extremities including the clavicle and the scapula, and 2.9% (1/35) in extra-abdominal lymph nodes. In patients with PSA doubling time below 12 months, the detection rate was found to be significantly higher compared to those with a doubling time of 12 months or more. Specifically, 84% (21 out of 25) of patients with PSA doubling time below 12 months had positive scans, while only 50% (4 out of 8) of those with a doubling time of 12 months or more had positive scans (p value < 0.05).

The detection rates for lesions with primary grade groups of 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 were 66.7% (2/3), 50% (4/8), 90% (9/10), 100% (4/4), and 77% (7/9), respectively (p value < 0.05).

Five patients had a PSA of less than 2 ng ml−1 and more than 12 months of doubling time. One out of five patients (20%) was diagnosed with oligometastatic disease to abdominal lymph nodes and received locoregional radiation therapy with adequate treatment response. The remaining four patients had suspected local disease recurrence and had no change in their management (three false positives and one true positive).

Diagnostic performance

The overall [18F]Fluciclovine PET/CT scan sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV were 86% (95% CI: 73.6–89.7), 83% (95% CI:53.5–103), 96.1% (95% CI:88.7-NA), and 55.5% (95% CI: 23–88), respectively.

Impact of [18F]Fluciclovine PET/CT on management and effectiveness of management change

In the changed management group, 88% (22/25) of the management change was based on positive [18F]Fluciclovine PET/CT. Management changes were as follows: Locoregional radiation therapy was recommended in seven patients (four with adequate response). Introduction of a second-generation ADT agent was recommended in 17 patients (12 with adequate response). Two patients received chemotherapy with an adequate response.

In the unchanged management group, 60% (6/10) of patients had negative [18F]Fluciclovine PET/CT scans. The other four positive scans were maintained on the initial ADT therapy. One patient declined the recommended management change.

The assessment of treatment adequacy was performed on 25 patients using the criteria mentioned earlier. The management change was deemed satisfactory in 76% (19/25) of the cases. Out of this group, 89.4% (17/19) of the patients had their management changed after having a positive 18F-Fluciclovine PET/CT scan. On the contrary, 40% (4/10) of the patients had a positive scan but no change in management. A positive scan and PSA doubling time of more than 12 months were independent predictors of change in management in CRPC patients undergoing restaging from the binary logistic regression analysis.

Discussion

The aim of this retrospective study was to determine whether [18F]Fluciclovine PET/CT restaging can detect early disease recurrence and guide treatment decisions in non-metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (nmCRPC) patients with concern for disease progression and negative conventional imaging. We also evaluated the outcomes of any changes in management based on the PET/CT results. Our results showed that [18F]Fluciclovine PET/CT had a high sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) of 86%, 83%, 96.1%, and 55.5%, respectively. Moreover, 88% of the patients with a positive [18F]Fluciclovine PET/CT finding had their management plan altered, and 89.4% of them responded well to treatment (including four patients who achieved complete remission). We did not find a statistically significant correlation between a positive [18F]Fluciclovine PET/CT scan (finding metastatic disease) and a lower PSA doubling time, contrary to previous reports, which showed a link between metastatic disease and lower PSA doubling time. 13

Our study included patients with PSA lower than 2 ng dl−1 which does not conform to the definition of CRPC that implies rising PSA above two despite ADT and castrate levels of testosterone. There are no definitive criteria to identify the emergence of castrate resistance in clinical practice. The European Association of Urology identifies progression following definitive treatment as two consecutive rises > 0.2 ng ml−1. 3 On the other hand, The NCCN (National comprehensive cancer network) recognizes hormonal resistance as clinical, radiographical, or biochemical progression while serum testosterone is less than 50 ng dl−1. No specific PSA rise was identified as progression. 14 We aimed to determine whether [18F]Fluciclovine PET/CT can detect early disease recurrence and guide curative therapy in patients with negative conventional imaging. In our study, regardless of the PSA level, the overall detection rate was 74.3% which is less than the reported positivity rates of PSMA-11 PET/CT, 90% in a similar cohort. 12 On the other hand, [18F]Fluciclovine PET/CT positivity guided beneficial management change in 70% of cases with PSA lower than 2 ng ml−1, similar to the findings reported by Aveline et.al., who reported similar rates observed using [68Ga]Ga-PSMA-11 PET/CT, suggesting a comparable clinical utility. A positive [18F]Fluciclovine PET/CT result successfully guided targeted curative therapy in four patients.

Our study demonstrates a correlation between PSA levels and the number of detected lesions. We observed a significant difference in lesion detection between patients with PSA levels above and below 2 ng dl−1, suggesting that higher PSA levels may indicate a more significant disease burden or more extensive tumor spread. However, we did not find a significant difference in the overall positivity rate among the three PSA subgroups. These findings suggest that the presence of metastatic disease cannot be solely determined by PSA levels. Instead, a comprehensive clinical approach is necessary to guide treatment decisions, including selecting appropriate imaging techniques and needing further investigations.

We acknowledge several limitations in our study. The retrospective nature of this study, along with thesmall sample size. However, this study is the largest available study evaluating the impact of [18F]Fluciclovine PET/CT on nmCRPC patient management. It was not feasible to acquire biopsies in negative [18F]Fluciclovine PET/CT scans. Thus, the diagnostic performance analysis was performed on available tissue diagnosis and 6-month follow-up with conventional imaging. The histological diagnosis was only feasible in 31.4% (11/35). Additionally, the impact on management may be affected by possible selection bias due to the characteristics of the patient population included in this study.

Conclusion

[18F]Fluciclovine PET/CT has added value in restaging patients with nmCRPC with suspicion of disease progression and negative conventional imaging and may play a vital role in guiding management change, even at low PSA levels. Further evaluation with prospective studies and a larger patient population is warranted.
