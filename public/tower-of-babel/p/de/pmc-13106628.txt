Abrasion-resistant wearable skins based on bilayered solid/liquid stretchable conductors
Authors: Zejun Wang, Puyuan Shi, Yixuan Li, Ting Liu, Boxiang Zhu, Jiayi Liu, Zejun Deng, Yan Wang, Yunmeng Zhao, Zhu Xiao, Shu Gong, Wenlong Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Soft bioelectronic skins represent next-generation wearable technologies for continuous and unobtrusive biomonitoring, enabled by diverse active materials and topological designs. Despite encouraging progress, most wearable skins reported to date remain unable to match the abrasion-resistant sensing functions of human skin. Here, we present abrasion-resistant wearable skins based on a bilayer stretchable conductor architecture. The top layer, composed of silver particle (AgPs)–impregnated styrene-ethylene–butylene–styrene (SEBS), provides superior abrasion resistance, while the underlying layer of liquid metal particle (LMPs)–impregnated SEBS ensures high conductivity under large strains (ε > 900%). While being ultrathin (13.3 µm), the bilayer wearable skins could deliver exceptional durability under extreme mechanical and chemical demands, maintaining electromechanical stability during repeated abrasion, large deformations, and exposure to strong acidic/alkaline environments. They reliably capture high-fidelity mechanical and electrophysiological signals in abrasion-intensive scenarios, such as skin–cloth friction and facial rubbing. Finally, we demonstrate a soft, multimodal system for pressure and biopotential monitoring, enabling applications in braille recognition and facial expression detection with 98.75% prediction accuracy.

Introduction

Wearable and highly stretchable electronic skins (E-skins)1–5 constitute fundamental components for emerging soft electronic technologies owing to their ability to provide a comfortable, second-skin-like experience for continuous health monitoring6,7, perceptive robotics8, and seamless human–machine interfaces9. These systems are typically constructed with solid or liquid active materials using either extrinsically stretchable designs, such as wrinkle10, kirigami11, serpentine patterns12,13 and wavy architectures14, or intrinsically stretchable designs including ionic liquids15, liquid metals16, metallic nanowires17,18, metallic nanoparticles19,20, carbon-based nanomaterials21–23, conductive polymers24, and nanocomposites25,26.

Systems with solid functional materials could match the sensing functions of human skin to perceive mechanical pressures27, strain28,29, temperatures24, chemicals17 and so on. Despite these appealing functionalities reported to date, it is rare to see that they can match human skin’s durability, such as the ability to maintain sensing performance under abrasive yet highly stretchable conditions due to materials cracking and/or delamination. While solid/solid bilayer designs can improve mechanoelectrical performance and mitigate some drawbacks of single-material systems, their stretchability and long-term durability remain fundamentally constrained by the inherent tendency of solid conductors to crack and/or delaminate30. As for liquid functional materials, ionic liquids suffered from low electrical conductivity due to high viscosity and low mobility compared to electronic conductors31. Alternatively, liquid metal (LM) has been substantially explored in recent years32–34 for the design of wearable skins as they could circumvent cracks and delamination while maintaining high electrical conductivity. However, the inherent fluidity of LMs renders them susceptible to leakage and mechanical abrasion. Although direct elastomer encapsulation has been attempted to shield LM circuits from friction and leakage35, this approach inherently obstructs direct skin/conductive media contact required for high-fidelity electrophysiological signal transduction. Liquid metals encapsulated by solid shells have also been integrated with semiconductive particle leading to enhanced photoactivity36. In this context, it is crucial to develop a tattoo-like, ultra-stretchable, and abrasion-resistant LM-based electrophysiological and electromechanical sensing system37–39 for the long-term signals acquisition from the brain, muscles, and the nervous system.

Here, we propose a bilayered solid/liquid hybrid architecture to establish an abrasion-resistant yet highly stretchable wearable skin for reliable biomonitoring. This bilayer architecture is realized with strongly bonded interface in Styrene-Ethylene-Butadiene-Styrene (SEBS) matrix in an ultrathin form factor, offering extreme stretchability and superior abrasion resistance. In this architecture, a silver particle (AgPs)-impregnated SEBS served as an abrasion-resistant layer, while a liquid metal particle (LMPs)-impregnated SEBS served as the electrically conductive layer during large deformation. SEBS was employed to bridge the AgPs and LMPs layers with strong adhesion through thermal annealing, achieving an ultrathin (13.3 µm), dermally compatible interface. The AgPs/LMPs bilayer maintains structural integrity and negligible electrical conductivity changes throughout 1500 s standard Radio Corporation of America (RCA) abrasion testing. We found the architecture demonstrated exceptional conductivity even with 700% tensile strain due to electron bridging of LMPs that reconnected isolated AgPs islands through vertical electrical conductivity. More importantly, abrasion-induced particle fusion between AgPs and LMPs was observed after the RCA test, indicating that a conductive pathway could be well maintained after combined tensile strain and abrasion. The bilayer structure exhibits no LM leakage and strain sensitivity degradation under large tensile strain (600%) or strong acid/alkali conditions (2 M HCl/NaOH solution). We further proved that the bilayer architecture could be used as abrasion-resistant epidermal interconnects, electrophysiological sensors, and mechanical sensors. As a proof-of-concept, we developed an AI-powered multimodal and anti-friction sensing platform (Fig. 1a) that can interpret braille and facial expression during daily friction settings. Results

Fabrication and characterization of abrasion-resistant AgPs/LMPs bilayer conductors

We start with LMPs pastes fabricated by mixing EGaIn with SEBS dissolved in terpineol. A three-roll mill (TRM) with a 1 μm interspacing was used to reduce the particle size of LMPs. LMPs pastes showed a typical ellipsoid shape with size distribution in the range of 1–10 μm (Supplementary Fig. 1). AgPs pastes were fabricated using a similar process with the same mass ratio. The AgPs/LMPs bilayer thin film was produced through the spin-coating of AgPs and LMPs pastes onto a silicon wafer successively. Thermal annealing is carried out in between the coating steps to thoroughly eliminate the residual solvents, forming a uniform polymer matrix with a clear boundary between Ag and LM layers (Fig. 1b). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Fig. 1b insert) and energy dispersive spectrometer (EDS, Fig. 1c–f) results exhibited an evenly distributed C element and distinct boundary between Ag and Ga/In elements throughout cross-section of the bilayer structure with a typical thickness of ~13.3 µm, indicating both AgPs and LMPs were well connected by SEBS. The homogeneous matrix structure contributes strong adhesion at the AgPs/LMPs interface without showing delamination even with the Scotch tape peeling test (Supplementary Movie 1). In contrast, AgPs/SEBS pastes could not form a strongly bonded interface on LMPs/Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) surface, evidenced by a similar tape test, due to the heterogeneous matrix structure between SEBS and PDMS layer (Supplementary Movie 2). The ultrathin bilayer enables an intimate conformal attachment on human fingertips by replicating the fingerprint patterns (Fig. 1g, h).

The LMPs/SEBS composite showed poor abrasion resistance, leaving LMP residues on Scotch tape after peeling (Supplementary Fig. 2a, b). In contrast, the AgPs/LMPs bilayer retained an intact surface and residue-free tape, confirming effective encapsulation of LMPs by the AgPs/SEBS layer (Supplementary Fig. 2c, d). Quantitative testing with a standardized RCA abrasion tester (175 g load) showed the bilayer withstood >1500 s of abrasion with electrical resistance variation below 10 Ω cm⁻¹ (Fig. 1i, j). In control experiments, LMPs/SEBS and AgPs/SEBS monolayer with similar thickness (Supplementary Fig. 3) lost their conductivity permanently after 32 s and 403 s of abrasion, respectively (Fig. 1j, red and orange lines). The AgPs/SEBS composite played a major role in resisting abrasion for the first 800 s, where the reduction of AgPs/SEBS thickness did not affect the effective conductive pathway of the LMPs layer (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b). After that, AgPs and LMPs start to fuse due to friction-induced vertical pressure and strong AgPs/LMPs interface in SEBS homogenous matrix (Supplementary Fig. 4c), leading to a gradual increase of resistance from 800 to 1500 s. The AgPs/LMPs fused layer finally broke as a result of continuous abrasion for >1500 s, indicating permanent cutting down of conductive pathways (Supplementary Fig. 4d), which, to the best of our knowledge, corresponds to the highest abrasion resistance for conductive and intrinsically stretchable bioelectrodes reported in literatures40,41. A similar RCA abrasion test was performed on LMPs/SEBS samples with sputter-coated Ag, which showed permanent electrical conductivity failure after 30 s of abrasion (Supplementary Fig. 5), indicating the indispensable role of AgPs/SEBS layer to resist abrasion. Furthermore, a bilayer structure of AgPs/PDMS with LMPs/SEBS was subjected to RCA testing to evaluate the role of the interfacial bond. This weakly bonded bilayer lost conductivity after only 330 s of RCA testing, underscoring the indispensable role of robust interfacial adhesion in our system (Supplementary Fig. 6).

Surprisingly, our designed electrodes showed uncompromised conductivity and abrasion resistance even with large tensile strains of >600%. Figure 2a shows the variation curve of the electrical resistance changes of the bilayer electrode in its pristine state, under 0–600% stretching state and subjected to RCA abrasion testing under 600% stretching. The electrode was kept in its pristine state from 0 to 300 s, subjected to a progressive tensile strain of 0–600% from 300 to 600 s, and then subjected to RCA abrasion from 600 to 900 s. A real-time resistance monitoring indicates a gradual shift of 1 Ω cm−1 as a result of 0–600% strain, attributed to electron bridging of LMPs underneath AgPs that reconnected isolated AgPs islands through vertical electrical conductivity (Fig. 2a, insert). On the contrary, the AgPs/SEBS monolayer lost its conductivity after 100% of tensile strain without electron bridging of LMPs (Supplementary Fig. 7). We also found abrasion-induced particle fusion between AgPs and LMPs after the RCA test (Fig. 2a, insert), indicating that an abrasion-resistant conductive pathway could be well maintained after large mechanical stretching. The AgPs/LMPs interface was examined via cross-sectional SEM when subjected to 600% strain without observed delamination. This result further confirms the robustness of the interfacial bonding (Supplementary Fig. 8). SEM images of the bilayer structure before and after tensile strain and abrasion (Fig. 2b–d and Supplementary Fig. 9) further supported our electron bridging and particle fusion argument. In addition, the AgPs/LMPs bilayer showed no LM leakage after releasing from 600% uniaxial strain (Supplementary Fig. 11a–c). In comparison, large LMPs extruding were observed from LMPs/SEBS and AgPs+LMPs mixed-layer composites (Supplementary Fig. 11d–i). This may be due to the compact and finely distributed Ag microparticles in the range of 0.8–1 µm, which do not form large cavities or defects due to the mismatch between material interfaces caused by stretching (Supplementary Fig. 12a). We also used larger microparticles with a typical size of 4–6 µm adopting a similar recipe, collapsed with catastrophic cracks occurring at only 40 % of strain (Supplementary Fig. 12b). The X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy and X-ray diffraction spectrum further proved that no peaks corresponding to Gallium (Ga) or Indium (In) were observed on the surface of the AgPs/LMPs bilayer film in both its pristine state and after releasing from 600% strain (Supplementary Figs. 13 and 14). The corresponding binding energy of the Ag0 peak shifted 0.4 eV, which is possibly due to the strain-induced deformation of the silver particles and the surrounding polymer matrix, likely affecting the interface between the Ag particles and the polymer, the extent of particle exposure, and the particle aggregation. The binding energy of 284.8 eV is attributed to the carbon skeleton of the SEBS, while the oxygen peak observed at 532 eV in the SEBS spectrum likely originates from ambient atmospheric contamination42.

Moreover, we tested the electromechanical properties of AgPs/LMPs bilayers after being treated with strong acid and base solutions. The variation in its resistance remains within 10% during 300% cyclic strain and shows remarkable consistency across diverse environments, including air, water, 2 M HCl, and 2 M NaOH (Fig. 2e). On the other hand, surface-exposed LMPs/SEBS monolayers are vulnerable under strong acid/alkaline conditions (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 15). This occurs because the Ga₂O₃ shells on surface-exposed LMPs were dissolved in strong acid and base solutions, which induces particle re-aggregation and subsequent electromechanical alteration43. In contrast, AgPs/SEBS encapsulation effectively prevents direct contact between the liquid metal and corrosive media (Supplementary Fig. 16). Similar leakage-free and corrosion-resistant properties were also observed in LMP/SEBS structures using pure SEBS encapsulation (Supplementary Figs. 17 and 18). However, this design showed compromised abrasion resistance (Supplementary Fig. 19), which is likely because the addition of AgPs enhances the overall rigidity of the surface layer. More importantly, our double-conductive-layer design provides dual-sided conductivity, thereby enabling seamless integration at the skin-device interface. The excellent abrasion resistance of the AgPs/LMPs bilayer electrode can also be reflected in its washing resistance. After 6 h of standard laundering cycles, the resistance drift of the AgPs/LMPs bilayer electrode is less than 20%, while the conductivity of the LMPs/SEBS monolayer electrode decreases by 234 times (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Fig. 20). Surface silver coating also contributed to excellent antibacterial performance for on-skin application, where a typical 2 mm inhibition zone was observed after incubating Escherichia coli with the bilayer electrodes for 16 h (Supplementary Fig. 21a), outperforming non-inhibitory LMPs/SEBS monolayer and AgPs+LMPs mixed layer (Supplementary Fig. 21b, c). The AgPs/LMPs bilayer demonstrates excellent biocompatibility, as confirmed by cytotoxicity and skin irritation testing. Cytotoxicity assessment via a 24-h co-culture with human immortalized keratinocytes in artificial sweat showed high cell viability (>95%) with no statistical difference from the blank control (Supplementary Fig. 22). Similarly, a 24-h skin patch test revealed no adverse dermal reactions upon bilayer removal (Supplementary Fig. 23).

Abrasion-resistant wearable interconnects

The stretchable AgPs/LMPs bilayer conductors could serve as abrasion-resistant interconnects. To demonstrate this, we could light up Light-Emitting Diodes (LEDs) without luminance degradation after both hands rubbing and abrasion wear test (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Fig. 24a and Supplementary Movie 3). Both morphological and electromechanical analyzes confirmed that the AgPs/LMPs bilayer retains its structural integrity (Supplementary Fig. 24b–e) and maintains stable strain-sensing performance (Fig. 3b) even after a 500-cycle abrasion test. Our strategy could be extended to rough three-dimensional surfaces. By adopting a screen-printing method, the AgPs/LMPs interconnect was successfully printed on textiles, withstanding continuous cloth abrasion of >10,000 cycles at 2 N loading and RCA paper abrasion of >600 s before losing its electrical conductivity (Supplementary Fig. 26). A Central South University logo could be easily patterned with our bilayer interconnects on SEBS substrates with excellent weldability, exhibiting a reliable connection to light-up LED circuits under deformation (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Movie 4). The thickness uniformity of films fabricated by screen-printing and spin-coating was characterized using a 3D microscope with depth-of-field imaging. The screen-printed film exhibited a mean thickness of 7.2 ± 0.57 μm, whereas the spin-coated bilayer measured 10.92 ± 0.7 μm (Supplementary Fig. 27). Despite this difference, both methods yielded comparable average sheet resistances of approximately 0.11 Ω sq⁻¹ (Supplementary Fig. 27), indicating similar overall electrical conductivity.

Abrasion-resistant wearable electrophysiological sensors

For reliable on-body physiological monitoring, soft electrodes require strong adhesion and skin compatibility. To achieve this, we incorporated 2.5 wt.% acrylic ester into the LMPs paste, introducing hydroxyl groups in the LMPs/SEBS layer that form hydrogen bonds with amino moieties on the skin (Fig. 4a, left). The oxide layer of LMPs and AgPs also form hydrogen bonds with hydroxyls (Fig. 4a, right). Consequently, the LMPs/SEBS side of the bilayer adheres firmly to the skin via interactions between the acrylic ester and epidermal keratin44. The skin-adhesive bioelectrodes achieve ultra-strong interfacial adhesion of 130 N m−1, which is twice as high as that without acrylic ester addition (Fig. 4b). The AgPs/LMPs bilayer film retained over 60% of its initial adhesion after repeated wear (Supplementary Fig. 28a) and over 74% after repeated washing (Supplementary Fig. 28b). To prevent skin damage during removal, 75% alcohol pads were employed for gentle electrode peeling. Owing to the high electrical conductivity and strong adhesion, our bioelectrodes achieve an ultralow electrode-skin impedance of 105–1416 Ω across a frequency range of 10–106 Hz. This value is more than an order of magnitude lower than that of commercial Ag/AgCl electrodes and LMPs/SEBS monolayers (Fig. 4c). The electrode/skin contact impedance remained in a low range even after subjecting to 2000 abrasion cycles (carnation circles, Fig. 4c). The greatly enhanced performance can be attributed to its exceptional abrasion-resistance, conductivity-unsacrificed stretchability, and intimate skin-electrode contact (Supplementary Table 1).

We further evaluated the capability of the AgPs/LMPs stretchable conductors as wearable electrophysiological sensors that could function under severe abrasion conditions. Two pairs of devices were conformally adhered to the subject’s chest and forearm for electrocardiography (ECG) and electromyography (EMG) monitoring (Fig. 4d). 500 cycles of wear abrasion test were introduced to quantitatively evaluate sensors’ performances mimicking abrasion-intensive daily use scenarios (Fig. 4e, f). A Bluetooth-enabled portable wireless module was enabled for real-time ECG and EMG signals acquisition. Our AgPs/LMPs ECG bioelectrodes exhibited minimal signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) variation (20.49 ± 1.03 dB vs. 18.75 ± 0.32 dB post-abrasion). This contrasts sharply with the significant SNR degradation observed in AgPs/SEBS and LMPs/SEBS bioelectrodes (Fig. 4g, h). Compared to traditional gel electrodes, our tattoo-like adhesive bioelectrodes achieve superior ECG monitoring performance. They capture characteristic waveforms with a higher SNR (Supplementary Fig. 29) and exhibit reduced susceptibility to motion-induced artifacts (Supplementary Fig. 30). This performance enables stable, low-impedance ECG recording over seven consecutive days (Supplementary Figs. 31 and 32). To demonstrate the sustained performance of our ECG bioelectrodes, Supplementary Fig. 33 showed a 12-h ECG monitoring result during routine activities, including desk work, sleep, jogging and experimental tasks with activity-dependent heart rate quantification using an RF-ECG2 system.

Similarly, our bilayer electrodes could collect high-quality EMG signals with negligible SNR variation (33.39 ± 1.36 dB vs. 27.86 ± 0.9 dB) before and after 500 cycles of mechanical abrasion in response to a typical fist-clenching maneuver, outperforming AgPs/SEBS and LMPs/SEBS monolayer structures (Fig. 4i, j). Our bioelectrodes also showed lower signal interference compared to commercial gel electrodes under scenarios including vibrational noise, jogging, and water exposure (Supplementary Figs. 34–37), demonstrating exceptional adaptability for daily activity tracking. The metallic bioelectrodes utilize efficient electron transport mechanisms for biosignal acquisition, which enables sub-millisecond response times to high-frequency muscle activation (Supplementary Fig. 38), eliminating the stability concerns associated with dehydration in conventional gel-based electrodes (Supplementary Fig. 39).

Abrasion-resistant wearable sensing systems

We further demonstrated a wearable sensing platform in braille interpretation for visual impairment assistance and facial expression decoding in autism spectrum therapy under abrasive conditions. We developed a custom abrasion-resistant multimodal sensing system capable of conformal integration on skin surfaces for the applications mentioned above. As illustrated in Fig. 5a, the braille interpretation system architecture consists of a U-shaped strain sensor (I) attached to the index fingertip, combined with two forearm EMG electrodes (S1), forming an integrated tactile/neuromuscular signal sensing system. A 3D-printed braille dotted plate was mounted on a linear stage moving at constant velocity to generate characteristic pulse signals via fingertip-dot sliding. The stroke distance and number of cycles were controlled by linear stage, while the pressure between finger and braille dotted plate was loaded by our UTM loading stage. The abrasion between the strain sensor and the braille dots induced localized strain, resulting in reversible resistance changes even after more than 500 repeated tests due to our anti-abrasion bilayer design (Fig. 5b, c). We further validated the system’s performance using a comprehensive braille array plate. Facial expression analysis was conducted using a three-channel EMG array (S1-S3) targeting the Levator labii superioris and Corrugator supercilii (CS) muscles, respectively (Fig. 5e). Recordings of 100 cycles of prototypical expressions (smile, laugh, surprise, and anger) produce highly reproducible signal patterns (Fig. 5f). More importantly, the abrasion-resistant and adhesive bioelectrodes enabled a robust skin-sensor interface that could withstand friction-intensive activities such as facial cleansing without causing signal degradation (Fig. 5g, h). We used a custom signal processing and Convolutional Neural Network framework17 to classify four facial expressions with our tri-EMG sensing system. Fivefold cross-validation was used to estimate its performance (Supplementary Fig. 40). The training and testing samples were randomly selected on the basis of an 8:2 ratio from the baseline dataset (Supplementary Fig. 41). The confusion matrix and accuracy variation during training achieve an accuracy of 98.75% for the four expressions (Fig. 5i and Supplementary Fig. 42). Notably, this high accuracy depended on simultaneous multi-EMG monitoring to capture coordinated muscle movements. In comparison, single-sensor EMG evaluations using a similar model show markedly lower classification accuracies, as illustrated by confusion matrices (Supplementary Fig. 43). To demonstrate the capability of the electrodes in more realistic abrasion-intensive use cases, we developed a washable ECG T-shirt. It features two circular, abrasion-resistant, and leakage-free AgPs/LMPs/AgPs ECG electrodes throughout the textile (Fig. 5j, left). The dual-sided conductive design facilitates seamless integration across the device, textile, and skin interfaces (Fig. 5j, right). The AgPs/SEBS encapsulation applied to both sides of the textile also ensured excellent ECG monitoring through daily wear and after repeated laundry cycles (Fig. 5k, l).

Discussion

Skin-like stretchable biosensors have long struggled with structural failure in abrasion-intensive environments, limiting their long-term usability in real-world. Here, we introduce an ultrathin bilayer architecture that combines exceptional stretchability with abrasion tolerance through interfacial engineering. The abrasion-resistant AgPs/SEBS top layer provides stable electron transport under extreme mechanical environments, while the adhesive LMPs/SEBS underlayer ensures low interfacial impedance (527 Ω at 1000 Hz) under extreme deformation, enabling high-fidelity electrophysiological recording. Importantly, thermal annealing-promoted, SEBS-mediated the AgPs/LMPs interface withstands >1500 s of RCA abrasion testing and >600% strain without LM seepage, even in corrosive chemical environments. This bilayer design addresses the persistent challenge of interfacial failure in soft electronics and demonstrates strong potential for robust, long-term wearable biomonitoring rivalling human skin.

Methods

Materials

Spherical Ag powder with different sizes was purchased from Hunan GNGR Silver Based Material Technology Co., Ltd. Styrene-Ethylene-Butadiene-Styrene (SEBS, H1221) was obtained from Asahi Kasei Corporation. Terpineol (purity: 99.9%) was purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. EGaIn (Gallium Indium eutectic, 99.99%), and acrylate were purchased from Sigma Aldrich.

Paste preparation

30% wt. SEBS granules were dispersed in 70% wt. terpineol under continuous mechanical agitation at 80 °C in the oil bath for 2 h, yielding a transparent, particle-free solution. LM microparticles (LMPs) paste preparation: 70 wt.% EGaIn was homogenized with 30 wt.% SEBS/terpineol solution using a TRM. Sequential gap settings of 80, 40, 20, 10, and 5 μm were applied during TRM processing to mitigate particle Agglomeration and to ensure paste uniformity. Ag microparticles (AgPs) paste preparation: AgPs paste was fabricated following the same mass fraction ratios and fabrication processes as LMPs paste. 2.5 wt.% extra acrylic ester was added to the LMPs paste to fabricate adhesive electrophysiology electrodes, enhancing interfacial adhesion between LMPs/SEBS layer and skin interface.

AgPs/LMPs bilayer electrode fabrication

Ultrathin AgPs/LMPs bilayer film electrode was fabricated through a spin-coating strategy composed of a silver and liquid metal paste layer. In detail, 2 mL of AgPs paste was dispensed onto a 4-inch silicon wafer mounted on a spin coater vacuum chuck. A three-stage spin-coating protocol (2000, 5000, 7000 rpm; 90 s per stage) was used to ensure thickness uniformity, followed by 5 min of thermal curing at 120 °C. The LMPs layer was deposited using identical spin-coating parameters before thermal annealing at 150 °C for 20 min to remove residual solvents, yielding an ultrathin AgPs/LMPs bilayer film with a typical thickness of approximately 6 µm. AgPs/LMPs bilayer film with a thickness ranging from 6 to 30 µm could be achieved by adjusting the final spin-coating speed to 7000–2000 rpm. An AgPs/LMPs bilayer textile electrode was fabricated through screen printing. A scraper blade was used to make uniform paste coatings. The first layer uses LMPs/SEBS paste, and then spreads the AgPs/SEBS layer after 5 min of thermal curing at 120 °C. The preforms were then heat annealed at 150 °C for 20 min as mentioned above.

Customized AgPs/LMPs bilayer circuitry fabrication

Customized patterns were achieved through a screen-printing method. The pattern was first designed using Auto CAD, followed by a mask manufacturing process from JingHong Screen-printing equipment Co., Ltd, China. The patterns were then printed through a manual screen-printing machine (received from DELO Technology development Co. Ltd, China) with LM paste, followed by 5 min of thermal curing at 150 °C. After that, a second AgPs layer was printed with AgPs paste, followed by 20 min of thermal curing at 150 °C.

LMPs/SEBS, AgPs/SEBS, and AgPs+LMPs mixed films fabrication

The LMPs/SEBS film was fabricated following the same process as the AgPs and LMPs layers in AgPs/LMPs bilayer electrode fabrication. 2 mL of LM paste was dispensed onto a 4-inch silicon wafer mounted on a spin coater vacuum chuck. To keep the thickness of the samples consistent, one more LM layer was deposited after the first LM layer had dried. The film was then baked at 150 °C for 20 min. The AgPs/SEBS film was fabricated by the identical process with the LMPs/SEBS film, although using AgPs instead of LMPs. The AgPs+LMPs mixed electrode fabrication procedure was the same as the LMPs/SEBS film, but an AgPs+LMPs mixed paste rather than the LM paste was used. The Ag layer on LMPs/SEBS was sputtered by a high vacuum Magnetron sputtering system (GVC-2000TD, Beijing Gewei Instrument Co., Ltd, China).
