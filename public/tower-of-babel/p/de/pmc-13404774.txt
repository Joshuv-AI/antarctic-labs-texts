Getting the Wheels in Motion to Deal With Unmet Psychological Need: A Reply to “The Pulmonary Hypertension Global Patient Survey: Physical and Psychosocial Impacts on Health‐Related Quality of Life”
Authors: Gregg H Rawlings, Iain Armstrong, Andrew R Thompson, David G Kiely
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

The authors of “The Pulmonary Hypertension Global Patient Survey: Physical and Psychosocial Impacts on Health‐Related Quality of Life,” along with the participants who bravely shared their experiences, are to be commended for this important contribution to the field. In their paper, the data from 3329 adults with pulmonary hypertension (PH) across 88 countries were analyzed. Patients were asked a range of questions covering the impact of PH on daily functioning, emotional and physical wellbeing, treatment and side effects, employment, birth planning, and patient‐reported outcome measures [1]. The findings support the growing evidence base documenting the psychological and emotional impact of the condition [2, 3, 4]. The authors conclude that psychological support is “highly requested but frequently unmet.” The question we ask in this letter is: why is it unmet?

This letter is not intended to undervalue the efforts of PH healthcare professionals who strive to deliver holistic care and support patients' emotional needs. However, the recurring high rates of psychological distress in this clinical group, alongside reported barriers to accessing support, as reported in the Global Patient Survey and elsewhere [5, 6, 7], cannot be overlooked.

Greater efforts are needed to better understand the underlying causes and related to this, to also develop effective psychological interventions. To achieve this, we want to propose using well‐established frameworks to inform the development, evaluation, and implementation of interventions aimed at changing behavior. These approaches have been used to improve health outcomes in other long‐term health conditions but so far, we are not aware of their systematic application within PH. As an illustrative example, the Behaviour Change Wheel (BCW) is one of the most cited frameworks of intervention development and comprises three interconnected layers [8]. Here, we apply this framework to the context of PH (see Figure 1).

Applying the Behaviour Change Wheel to address the unmet need of psychosocial support for patients with pulmonary hypertension (PH).

The inner layer, also known as the COM‐B model, seeks to understand the determinants of behavior by examining how it may be influenced by factors related to Capability, Opportunity, and Motivation. This provides a comprehensive approach to identifying targets for intervention in the subsequent layers. For example, do healthcare providers feel confident asking patients with PH about their mental health or responding to emotional distress, do patients feel able to share their psychological difficulties with providers, do current service pathways provide an opportunity to explore mental health difficulties or is there a perception that psychological wellbeing is not a core component of PH care. Overall, there is a significant lack of evidence exploring staff and patients about their perspectives of mental health support in PH. These gaps need addressing. Importantly, such work will help us to understand why patients in the Global Patient Survey reported they often perceive “a lack of empathy” when discussing the emotional impact of PH with their PH specialist.

The middle layer of the BCW is informed by issues identified at the COM‐B level helping to identify appropriate “intervention functions.” In many cases, multiple intervention functions may be required. For example, if there is a lack of awareness of how PH impacts mental health, strategies to improve education and knowledge amongst providers and patients may be required. If staff feel insufficiently equipped to recognize or respond to psychological difficulties, training may be indicated such as familiarization with screening tools for psychological distress [9]. If there is a risk that physical health outcomes dominate clinical interactions, environmental restructuring may be helpful through prompts such as adding relevant questions to clinical interview scripts and using posters and patient information leaflets encouraging discussion of mental health. This may be particularly helpful in services where psychological support is typically viewed as a short‐term approach or when patients are in crisis or receiving end of life care. Indeed, a greater emphasis and integration of mental health support throughout the patient's care can help avoid the situation where psychological interventions are “too little or too late” [10]. If clinicians are struggling to explore the mental health of their patients as consultation time is limited, consideration may be given to adapting service delivery to enable sufficient time for psychological discussions or to redistribute tasks across the care pathway. The growing integration of accessible healthcare tools into services could mean that patient data are captured outside of appointments and reviewed during consultations [11].

The outer layer of the BCW considers the system‐level changes required to support the real‐world implementation of interventions. For example, treatment guidelines on PH could expand on current recommendations of “psychosocial support” for patients with PH [12] informed by a stronger evidence base on interventions that are effective, acceptable, and safe for this population to guide care plans. Strategies may be necessary to influence service provision; for example, incorporating information related to mental health support within PH clinical audits could help standardize care delivery. Limited availability of specialist psychological services is likely to be one of the most common barriers to access. While not specific to mental health, patients and providers have already highlighted the need to strengthen links between community services and specialist PH centers to improve access to care [5]. Building on this, greater collaboration with nonspecialist psychological services could support workforce upskilling, while dedicated funding would enable the development and integration of interventions within existing care pathways. Closer integration with PH organizations may further enhance implementation, collectively supporting intervention functions and addressing key COM‐B components.

To conclude, while research documenting the psychosocial impact of PH is essential, if we are to achieve the goal highlighted by patients in the Global Survey of “psychological care as a mandatory part of PH treatment” a greater emphasis is needed on also identifying effective ways to address these challenges and better support this patient group. Also demonstrated by the Global Survey, the foundation of any intervention proposed should be grounded in collaboration with patients, carers, and providers to ensure a comprehensive understanding of the issues and make sure interventions developed are acceptable, meaningful, and relevant.

We know that mental health difficulties are common in PH regardless of patient's age, gender, ethnicity, marriage or employment status, culture, or severity and duration of PH.

Continuing to overlook and failing to adequately support patients' emotional wellbeing in routine PH care could constitute a form of epistemic injustice. We hope we have outlined here a systematic, evidence‐based approach to help meet the current unmet need.
