Effects of concurrent training on body composition, metabolic, and inflammatory in postmenopausal women: a meta-analysis
Authors: Chenghao Liu, Zhenyu Zhang, Yilin Zhang, Hao Kong, Enran Zhao, Yun Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo evaluate the effects of concurrent training (CT) on body composition, metabolic health, and inflammatory outcomes in postmenopausal middle-aged and older women, and to examine whether training experience, exercise order, and training dose moderate these effects.MethodsThis review followed PRISMA 2020. Web of Science, PubMed, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library were searched for randomized controlled and randomized crossover trials last searched on July 1, 2025. Effect sizes (Hedges’ g) were synthesized using multilevel mixed-effects models (REML) with robust variance estimation to account for within-study dependence. Subgroup analyses and meta-regression explored potential moderators, and certainty of evidence was assessed using GRADE.ResultsTwenty-three studies (n = 1001) involving naturally postmenopausal women were included. CT significantly improved body composition (ES = − 0.11, 95% CI [− 0.20, − 0.02], P = 0.02) and metabolic health (ES = − 0.22, 95% CI [− 0.40, − 0.04], P = 0.02), with moderate-certainty evidence for both outcomes. The pooled effect on inflammatory markers was not statistically significant (P = 0.39; low-certainty evidence). No significant moderators were identified, and meta-regression showed no significant dose–response relationship for training volume or intervention duration. Subgroup analyses suggested reductions in body fat percentage and increases in lean mass, alongside small-to-moderate improvements in blood pressure, blood glucose, triglycerides, and maximal oxygen uptake. Biomarker-specific analyses suggested changes in IL-10, IL-6, and TNF-α (P < 0.05), but these findings were based on limited data and were low certainty. Exploratory within-group analyses indicated significant effects in sedentary participants and in studies prescribing resistance training before endurance training or three sessions per week.ConclusionCT may be associated with small improvements in body composition and modest improvements in metabolic health in postmenopausal women. Given the small pooled effect sizes and the lack of MCID-based interpretation, the clinical relevance of these changes remains uncertain. Because moderator analyses were non-significant, subgroup patterns should be considered hypothesis-generating. Larger, adequately powered trials with standardized reporting are needed to clarify clinically meaningful effects and potential effect modifiers.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-026-04359-5.Keywords: Concurrent training, Postmenopausal women, Body composition, Metabolic health, Inflammation, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Menopause is defined as the absence of menstruation for at least 12 consecutive months [1]. This stage is characterized by declines in ovarian hormones, including estrogen and progesterone [1, 2], and is frequently accompanied by increased visceral adiposity [3–5], metabolic syndrome [6–9], cardiovascular disease [10, 11], and elevated inflammatory risk [12, 13]. Notably, adipose tissue—the body’s primary energy-storage organ—regulates metabolic health through the uptake of glucose and fatty acids and the secretion of diverse bioactive factors (e.g., adipokines) [14]. However, excess adiposity can promote the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines from adipose tissue, thereby triggering cytokine-mediated inflammatory responses [15] Therefore, maintaining optimal body composition, metabolic health, and inflammatory balance is critical for postmenopausal and middle-aged women to prevent metabolic syndrome, cardiovascular disease, and other non-communicable diseases.

Concurrent training was first introduced by Hickson in 1980 to examine whether combining endurance and resistance exercise confers performance advantages over a single-modality approach [16]. Over the subsequent decades, researchers recognized that manipulating session timing, intensity, and sequence can substantially attenuate the interference effect and markedly improve cardiorespiratory fitness and muscular strength in the general population [17]. These insights laid the theoretical and practical foundations for moving strength–endurance training from competitive sport into public health practice. It was not until 2000 that Maiorana and colleagues systematically investigated the potential effects of CT on cardiovascular health [18]. As evidence expanded from sport to clinical and public health domains, CT has increasingly been recognized as an effective exercise prescription to enhance cardiorespiratory endurance and muscular strength and to modulate metabolic and inflammatory profiles. In 2020, the World Health Organization (WHO) recommended multicomponent programs for older adults—including balance, resistance, and endurance training—to reduce cardiovascular risk and prevent chronic disease [19]. However, the cardiometabolic effects of concurrent training remain uncertain. For instance, Al-Mhanna et al. [20] reported that CT significantly reduced body fat percentage in individuals with type 2 diabetes and overweight, but did not significantly change body weight, waist-to-hip ratio, fat mass, or lean mass. At the same time, other meta-analyses comparing exercise modalities in adults with overweight/obesity have suggested that CT may elicit more favorable improvements than continuous endurance training, interval training, resistance training, or combined/mixed exercise approaches [21, 22]. Conversely, some reviews have not observed meaningful cardiometabolic benefits of CT. A recent meta-analysis found no significant effect of CT on resting heart rate in overweight/obese populations and Valenti et al. similarly reported no significant improvement in HbA1c following combined aerobic and resistance training in postmenopausal women with type 2 diabetes [23, 24]. Collectively, these discrepancies may be explained by differences in baseline physical activity and cardiometabolic risk, as well as variation in intervention characteristics such as total training dose, session frequency, intervention duration, and exercise intensity.

Women undergoing menopause differ from the general aging population in several distinct physiological and metabolic aspects. First, in terms of body composition, evidence indicates that fat mass increases with age in middle-aged women, with postmenopausal women exhibiting a significantly greater increase than those in premenopausal stages [25]. Although similar patterns are observed in other older adults, the association between age and adiposity is not statistically significant. Second, in terms of metabolic health, the marked decline in estrogen levels during menopause contributes to an increased risk of chronic diseases [26]. In addition, prior evidence suggests that, compared with other older populations, postmenopausal women may exhibit a relatively unfavorable inflammatory profile, highlighting inflammation as an important domain of concern in this group [27, 28]. These observations underscore the need to consider postmenopausal women as a distinct population for targeted prevention and intervention strategies.

In recent years, although the number of studies investigating the effects of concurrent training on body composition, metabolic health, and inflammation in postmenopausal women has increased, existing evidence demonstrates substantial heterogeneity in study designs and inconsistent findings across trials. First, with respect to prior experience level, Friedenreich et al. [29] reported in a one-year randomized controlled trial (RCT) of healthy postmenopausal women that reductions in C-reactive protein (CRP) were more pronounced among those with greater experience level. Furthermore, Markov et al. [30] highlighted the significance of prior experience level in their meta-analysis, although limited sample sizes prevented subgroup analyses based on this factor; nonetheless, they emphasized that experience level remains a key determinant influencing study outcomes.

Moreover, intervention duration, weekly frequency, and total training volume vary considerably across studies, and consensus has yet to be reached regarding the optimal training dosage for postmenopausal women. In light of these gaps, the present study aims to systematically evaluate the effects of CT on body composition, metabolic health, and inflammatory responses in healthy postmenopausal women. Furthermore, it compares the effects of CT across populations with varying experience level, exercise order, and training volume, and employs dose–response analyses to identify the optimal ranges of training load and duration, thereby providing evidence-based guidance for exercise prescription in postmenopausal women.

Methods

The systematic review followed the PRISMA 2020 guidelines (Supplementary Material). The protocol was retrospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251155853) after completion of the initial analysis while the updated search and all subsequent re-analyses were conducted after registration in accordance with the registered protocol.

Search strategy

Prior to the formal literature search, one researcher (C.H.L.) conducted a preliminary screening of the databases. Subsequently, two independent reviewers (Z.Y.Z. and Y.L.Z.) performed a comprehensive search on July 1, 2025, across four major databases: Web of Science, PubMed, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library. The search strategies integrated Medical Subject Headings (MeSH terms) with free-text keywords tailored to each database, and the detailed search combinations are presented in the Supplementary Materials.

Research screening and inclusion criteria

One researcher (C.H.L.) conducted the initial literature identification and screening. Two independent reviewers (Z.Y.Z. and Y.L.Z.) subsequently screened the full-text articles according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion, and a third reviewer (C.H.L.) made the final decision when consensus could not be reached. All screening procedures were conducted using Zotero reference management software. In this review, concurrent training was defined as a structured exercise programme in which both resistance/strength and aerobic/endurance components were performed within the same training session (i.e., within-session integration). Eligible CT interventions were required to explicitly describe the inclusion of both components during each session (or clearly state a repeated within-session format across the intervention). Studies that delivered resistance and aerobic training on separate training days without a within-session combination were not considered CT for the primary synthesis. Building on this operational definition, the overall inclusion criteria were developed using the PICOS framework (P: Participants; I: Interventions; C: Comparators; O: Outcomes; S: Study design), and the detailed criteria are presented in Table 1.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Risk of bias assessment

The Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 tool (ROB 2.0) [31] was employed to assess the methodological quality and potential risk of bias of all included studies. The ROB 2.0 tool comprises five domains: (1) the randomization process, (2) deviations from intended interventions, (3) missing outcome data, (4) measurement of outcomes, and (5) selection of the reported results. Each domain was rated as “Yes,” “Probably yes,” “No,” “Probably no,” or “Unclear.” In accordance with the Cochrane Handbook [32], the overall risk of bias was categorized as “low risk,” “some concerns,” or “high risk.” The criteria for overall judgment were as follows: studies were classified as having a high risk of bias if any domain was rated “high risk”; as having some concerns if at least one domain was rated “some concerns” but none “high risk”; and as having a low risk of bias if all domains were rated “low risk” [32].

Data extraction

Data were extracted for key study characteristics, including participants’ age, experience level, CT intervention order, duration (weeks), weekly frequency, total session duration, and pre- and post-intervention outcomes for both the CT and control (CON) groups. Participants’ age was analyzed using the mean values reported in the original publications. When only an age range (e.g., 18–35 years) was reported, the midpoint value was adopted as the representative age. If age data were separately reported for CT and CON groups, the overall mean age was computed by summing the group means and dividing by the number of groups. Experience Level was included as a moderator a priori because it is a plausible effect modifier of exercise-induced adaptations and a potential source of between-study heterogeneity [33, 34]. Experience Level was categorized into six levels (0–5) according to the criteria proposed by McKay et al. [35]: sedentary, recreationally active, developing or trained, highly trained or national-level, elite or international-level, and world-class athletes. Participants were classified as sedentary if they engaged in < 150 min/week of moderate-intensity activity or < 75 min/week of vigorous-intensity activity. Training sequence was pre-specified as an a priori data item and extracted before analysis; it was coded based on the descriptions in the original trials as either resistance training followed by aerobic training within the same session (RT→AT) or aerobic training followed by resistance training (AT→RT). In addition, intervention duration (weeks), weekly training frequency (sessions/week), and session duration (min/session) were extracted from the intervention protocols. Training volume was defined as the product of intervention duration and weekly training frequency (i.e., total number of sessions), and total training duration was defined as the product of intervention duration, weekly training frequency, and session duration (i.e., total minutes), as previously described [36]. For studies with incomplete or missing data, corresponding authors were contacted via email to request the full datasets. For studies that reported outcome data only in graphical form, numerical values were extracted using WebPlotDigitizer (version 2024; Ankit Rohatgi, © 2010–2024).

Statistical analysis

Data synthesis and effect size estimation

In this study, all extracted data were based on pre- and post-intervention measurements. Therefore, before conducting the meta-analysis, change scores were computed for each group, and between-group differences in change magnitude were subsequently analyzed. Change scores and effect sizes (ES) were calculated using the following equations:

Mpre and Mpost denote the mean exercise performance values before and after the intervention for the CT and CON groups, respectively; n1 and n2 indicate the sample sizes for the CT and CON groups; SDpooled refers to the pooled standard deviation. The correlation coefficient (r) between pre- and post-intervention measurements was set to 0.6. Effect sizes were estimated using Hedges’ g to adjust for small-sample bias. Because included trials reported conceptually related outcomes using different indicators within each domain, we pre-specified outcome domains and pooled all eligible endpoints within each domain using standardized mean differences (Hedges’ g). This composite pooling was used to maximize study inclusion and avoid discarding trials that assessed the same construct with different measures. We acknowledge that averaging heterogeneous endpoints may attenuate domain-level pooled estimates; therefore, we additionally report outcome-specific analyses to aid interpretation. According to Cohen’s benchmarks, effect sizes were interpreted as trivial (< 0.2), small (0.2–0.5), moderate (0.5–0.8), or large (> 0.8). Between-study heterogeneity was assessed using the I², τ², and Q statistics. Values of I² at 25%, 50%, and 75% represented low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively, and statistical significance for the Q test was defined as p < 0.1. These indices reflect both the relative and absolute magnitude of residual heterogeneity, representing the proportion of variance unexplained by the model. Additionally, 95% prediction intervals (PI) were computed to estimate the potential dispersion of true effect sizes expected in future comparable studies.

Multilevel meta-analysis

Given that many included trials reported multiple outcomes and therefore contributed more than one effect size, we used a multilevel mixed-effects meta-analytic framework. This approach avoids treating within-study dependent effect sizes as independent observations, which could otherwise underestimate standard errors. Effect sizes were modelled using a nested random-intercept structure, with effect sizes clustered within studies. This specification captures variability both between and within studies, partitions heterogeneity into between-study and within-study variance components, and was estimated using restricted maximum likelihood (REML); inference for fixed effects was based on the t distribution (two-sided tests; α = 0.05; 95% CI) [37].

To account for within-study dependence and to improve the robustness of statistical inference, we applied cluster-robust variance estimation (RVE) with small-sample adjustments. This approach yields more reliable standard errors, confidence intervals, and significance tests. Because the true within-study correlation structure is typically unknown, we assumed a within-study correlation of 0.6 in the primary analysis. Sensitivity analyses were conducted using alternative correlations of 0.4 and 0.8. The pooled estimates and inferential conclusions were materially consistent across these specifications, supporting robustness to the assumed dependence structure [38].

To enhance transparency in model selection, we compared the fit of the multilevel model with that of a conventional two-level random-effects model. The final specification was chosen based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). Effect sizes were weighted by the inverse of their sampling variances to reflect estimation precision. All analyses were performed in R (version 4.3.0; R Core Team, Vienna, Austria). Multilevel models were fitted using the metafor package, and RVE with small-sample adjustments was implemented using the clubSandwich package [39, 40].

Subgroup and meta-regression analyses

Subgroup analyses were performed according to categorical moderators—such as CT order, participants’ experience level, weekly training frequency, and outcome measures—to examine their moderating effects on CT outcomes. In the categorical subgroup analysis, we classified the groups based on the frequency levels reported in the primary trials; three sessions per week was the most prevalent training prescription, which therefore yielded the most stable estimates for this subgroup, whereas higher-frequency categories were sparsely represented due to limited data. Meta-regression analyses were further conducted to explore the optimal implementation period of CT. All models were compared under linear and multiple nonlinear meta-regression frameworks, including simple linear and cubic polynomial models. To address model convergence issues, the optim algorithm was applied with control parameters specified as control = list(optimizer = “optim”). All meta-regression models were implemented using the metafor package in R, and visualization of model outputs was conducted via the ggplot2 package [41].

Publication bias and sensitivity analyses

Publication bias was evaluated through visual inspection of funnel plots in conjunction with Egger’s regression test, where P > 0.05 was interpreted as the absence of publication bias [42]. Leverage values (hat values) and Cook’s distances were computed to identify leverage points, outliers, and influential observations across all meta-analytic models. Data points were classified as outliers when both leverage values and Cook’s distances exceeded three times the mean and the absolute studentized residual exceeded 3. After excluding identified outliers, analyses were re-estimated. If the results remained stable, the original model was retained; otherwise, the model excluding outliers was adopted [42].

Assessment of evidence certainty

The certainty of the body of evidence was evaluated following the GRADE Handbook, with evidence quality classified into four levels—high, moderate, low, and very low—using the GRADEpro GDT software [43]. The assessment domains encompassed risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias [43]. Initially, all evidence was assumed to be of high certainty and subsequently downgraded based on the following criteria: (1) Risk of bias—downgraded by one level for general bias and by two levels for serious bias; (2) Inconsistency—downgraded by one level if substantial heterogeneity existed without plausible explanation; (3) Indirectness—downgraded by one level if the evidence did not fully adhere to the PICOS criteria; (4) Imprecision—downgraded by one level when sample size was insufficient or confidence intervals were excessively wide; (5) Publication bias—downgraded by one level when evident. Evidence certainty could be upgraded when: (1) a large magnitude of effect was observed; (2) a clear dose–response gradient was evident; or (3) residual confounding or bias was more likely to attenuate than exaggerate the observed association [44].

Results

Search results

A total of 1,499 records were initially identified through database searches. After duplicate removal using Zotero reference management software, 865 unique records remained for screening. Subsequently, titles and abstracts were screened according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria (Table 1), resulting in 18 studies being retained for full-text assessment. In addition, five eligible studies were identified through manual searches on supplementary platforms, including Google Scholar and Baidu Scholar. Ultimately, a total of 23 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final meta-analysis (Fig. 1).

Certainty assessment

Main effects

Based on model fit indices, a three-level meta-analytic model was selected to analyze outcomes related to body composition, metabolic health, and inflammation. After testing models with different assumed correlation coefficients (r) and excluding outliers, consistent results were observed for metabolic and inflammatory outcomes, while the result for body composition changed from non-significant to significant following outlier removal. Concurrent training significantly improved body composition (ES = -0.11, 95% CI [-0.20, -0.02], P = 0.01, I²₂ = 59.13%, I²₃ = 0%) and metabolic health (ES = -0.22, 95% CI [-0.40, -0.04], P = 0.01, I²₂ = 64.8%, I²₃ = 0%). Egger’s regression test initially revealed significant asymmetry; however, after the exclusion of outliers, the significance disappeared (P > 0.05), indicating no publication bias. However, CT did not result in a significant improvement in inflammatory markers (ES = -0.27, 95% CI [-0.95, 0.41], P = 0.39). Detailed forest plot results are shown in (Fig. 2).

Subgroup analyses

Outcome measures

Subgroup analyses indicated that outcome type was the principal moderating factor influencing the overall intervention effects (P < 0.0001). Concurrent training significantly improved body composition (P = 0.01) and metabolic health (P = 0.01) among postmenopausal women. Specifically, regarding body composition, CT significantly reduced body fat percentage (ES = -0.39, P = 0.0002) and increased lean body mass (ES = 0.89, P < 0.0001). For metabolic outcomes, CT led to significant improvements in diastolic blood pressure (DBP) (ES = -0.47, P = 0.0048), fasting glucose (ES = -0.37, P = 0.02), systolic blood pressure (SBP) (ES = -0.39, P = 0.02), triglycerides (ES = -0.49, P = 0.0025), and maximal oxygen uptake (VO₂max) (ES = 0.90, P = 0.0002). Although the overall effect of CT on inflammatory responses was not statistically significant (P > 0.05), analysis of individual biomarkers revealed that CT significantly increased IL-10 (ES = 1.04, P = 0.0029) while decreasing IL-6 (ES = -0.80, P = 0.02) and TNF-α (ES = -0.67, P = 0.02). Detailed results are presented in Fig. 3.

Experience level

Subgroup analyses by participants’ prior experience level indicated that it did not significantly moderate the effects of concurrent training on body composition outcomes. Within-group analysis further showed that sedentary participants exhibited a small but significant improvement in body composition (ES = -0.11, P = 0.04). Regarding metabolic outcomes, sedentary participants demonstrated a small yet statistically significant improvement (ES = -0.26, P = 0.01). For inflammatory outcomes, neither sedentary individuals nor those with previous experience level showed significant improvements (P > 0.05, Fig. 4).

Effects of different experience levels on CT

Sequence

Variations in training order did not significantly affect the overall intervention effects of concurrent training (P > 0.05). In terms of body composition, performing resistance training prior to aerobic exercise yielded a marginally greater improvement (ES = -0.09, P = 0.03), whereas no significant improvement was observed in protocols that prioritized endurance training. A similar pattern was observed for metabolic outcomes, with the resistance-before-aerobic sequence yielding a small yet statistically significant improvement (ES = -0.28, P = 0.01). In contrast, subgroup analyses for inflammatory outcomes revealed no significant differences between the two training sequences(P > 0.05, Fig. 5).

Effects of different sequence on CT

Frequency

A training frequency of three sessions per week significantly improved body composition in postmenopausal women (ES = -0.18, P = 0.0044). No significant effects were observed when the training frequency was greater than three sessions per week (P = 0.56) or fewer than three sessions per week (P = 0.57). Similarly, subgroup analysis of metabolic outcomes yielded consistent results, showing that training three times per week resulted in a small yet statistically significant improvement in metabolic health (ES = -0.27, P = 0.02, Fig. 6). To further explore whether outcomes changed systematically with increasing training frequency, we additionally performed a simple linear regression analysis treating weekly training frequency as a continuous variable. This analysis did not show a statistically significant linear association between training frequency and effect size. The regression results and the corresponding frequency–effect plots are presented in the Supplementary Materials.

Meta-regression analysis

Based on the comparison of model fit indices, the linear meta-regression model demonstrated the best overall fit to the data. No significant dose–response relationshipwas identified across the included studies. Linear regression results indicated that session duration, intervention length (weeks), total training volume, training frequency, and cumulative training time were not significant predictors of improvements in body composition, metabolic health, or inflammatory outcomes in postmenopausal women (Fig. 7). Similarly, participant age did not significantly predict the magnitude of concurrent training effects.

Discussion

This study systematically evaluated the effects of concurrent training on body composition, metabolic health, and inflammatory responses in postmenopausal women, and further explored the moderating roles of training experience, training order, and training frequency. The findings indicated that CT produced a small effect on body composition, a modest improvement in metabolic health, and no significant overall change in inflammatory outcomes. Regarding body composition, CT yielded a small reduction in body fat percentage and moderate-to-large improvements in fat-free mass and lean body mass, whereas no significant changes were observed in other body composition parameters. In terms of metabolic outcomes, CT significantly reduced blood pressure and fasting glucose with small effect sizes, produced a moderate reduction in triglyceride levels, and elicited a large improvement in maximal oxygen uptake (VO₂max). Although CT did not significantly alter inflammatory markers at the overall level, subgroup analyses revealed significant modulation of IL-10, IL-6, and TNF-α, each demonstrating moderate-to-large effect sizes.

Neither moderator analyses nor meta-regression identified significant moderators. Although statistically significant intervention effects were observed within subgroups of sedentary individuals, those employing a resistance-aerobic training order, or those prescribing three sessions per week, these findings should be considered exploratory. As such, they represent preliminary evidence and should not be directly extrapolated to exercise prescription without further support from higher-level evidence.

Body composition

The present study demonstrated that, compared with the control group, concurrent training significantly improved body composition in postmenopausal women, particularly by reducing body fat percentage and increasing lean body mass. This finding aligns with the meta-analysis by Khalafi et al. [45], which reported that different exercise modalities exert beneficial effects on indicators such as lean body mass in postmenopausal women. Although no significant improvements were observed in BMI or body weight, this could be due to small sample sizes, relatively short intervention durations, or insufficient training intensities in the included trials. For example, outcomes such as waist-to-hip ratio were derived from single studies, rendering the results less robust and generalizable. Furthermore, the lack of standardized exercise intensity across included studies may have limited the interpretability and comparability of findings. Notably, Fogelholm et al. [46] suggested that achieving clinically meaningful weight loss requires at least 60 min of moderate-to-vigorous intensity exercise daily, whereas low-intensity exercise alone exerts minimal effects on body weight. In this context, BMI and body weight, as global measures of overall body mass, may be relatively insensitive to variations in training modality, duration, or intensity, and thus fail to capture exercise-induced internal adaptations such as fat loss or gains in lean mass. Consistent with this, Willis et al. [46] noted that BMI and body mass are external indicators of overall weight that may not capture internal adaptations such as fat loss or gains in lean mass. Therefore, even when substantial reductions in body fat occur, BMI and total body weight may not show statistically significant changes.

Postmenopausal women commonly exhibit decreased lean mass accompanied by increases in visceral and total fat, which collectively heighten the risk of functional decline and all-cause mortality [47]. In this context, concurrent training has been proposed as an effective intervention for reducing body fat percentage, potentially through decreasing adipose tissue and increasing lean body mass [48]. Previous studies have demonstrated that repeated endurance training markedly increases mitochondrial density. As a key regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis, AMPK activation through prolonged endurance exercise upregulates PGC-1α expression, thereby promoting mitochondrial biogenesis and enhancing lipid oxidation capacity [49]. Over time, this adaptive process substantially reduces adipose accumulation. In contrast, resistance training primarily activates the IGF-1 signaling cascade, which enhances muscle protein synthesis and promotes skeletal muscle hypertrophy, leading to increased lean body mass and a reduction in body fat percentage [50]. Collectively, CT exerts a synergistic effect through two complementary mechanisms: the endurance-related AMPK/PGC-1α pathway promotes mitochondrial biogenesis and lipid oxidation, whereas the resistance-related IGF-1 pathway facilitates protein synthesis and skeletal muscle hypertrophy. By integrating these two pathways, concurrent training not only optimizes fat distribution and overall body composition in postmenopausal women but may also reduce cardiometabolic and all-cause mortality risks. Consequently, concurrent training represents a promising non-pharmacological intervention with long-term health benefits, underscoring its potential for widespread application in this population.

Metabolic

Evidence from other special populations further supports the broader cardiometabolic benefits of structured exercise interventions. For example, meta-analytic evidence in individuals with diabetes indicates that high-intensity interval training can significantly improve multiple metabolic health biomarkers and cardiovascular risk indices [51, 52]. In addition, other syntheses suggest that resistance training and aerobic exercise improve body composition and metabolic profiles in adults with overweight or obesity [53, 54]. Collectively, these findings provide relevant context for interpreting the present results and are broadly consistent with our conclusion that concurrent training is associated with improved metabolic health in postmenopausal women. With respect to cardiometabolic parameters, CT significantly enhanced maximal oxygen uptake (P = 0.0002), reduced systolic blood pressure (P = 0.0002), and lowered diastolic blood pressure (P = 0.02) in postmenopausal women. These results align with findings from Khalafi and Loaiza-Betancur et al. [55, 56], who similarly reported that various exercise modalities improved VO₂max and blood pressure in postmenopausal women, with CT producing the greatest overall benefit. Similarly, Xi et al. [57] found that CT led to reductions of 0.81 mmHg in systolic and 0.62 mmHg in diastolic blood pressure among postmenopausal women. At the mechanistic level, Previous research has demonstrated that exercise-induced increases in VO₂max are primarily attributed to expanded red blood cell volume and enhanced cardiac output [58]. CT enhances left ventricular end-diastolic volume, resulting in increased stroke volume and improved cardiac contractile function, which together support higher VO₂max levels [59]. The observed reduction in blood pressure is primarily attributed to exercise-induced improvements in endothelial function, which decrease peripheral vascular resistance. This mechanism is further supported by Silva et al.’s meta-analysis, which demonstrated that exercise significantly improves flow-mediated dilation (FMD) and attenuates atherosclerotic development, thereby contributing to reductions in blood pressure [60].

Regarding lipid and glycemic profiles, concurrent training significantly improved fasting glucose (P = 0.02) and triglyceride levels (P = 0.0025) in postmenopausal women. These findings contrast with those reported by Bueno-Notivol et al. [61], whose meta-analysis concluded that exercise interventions—regardless of duration—did not significantly alter IGF-1, glucose, or triglyceride concentrations. The inconsistency may largely stem from the limited number of included studies—only seven trials—raising concerns about the robustness and reliability of their pooled estimates. Previous evidence has indicated that exercise-induced improvements in glucose uptake are primarily mediated by enhanced insulin sensitivity [62]. In the present meta-analysis, however, no significant change in HOMA-IR (P > 0.05) was detected, likely due to the inclusion of only two studies, rendering this finding less robust. Nevertheless, previous randomized controlled trials and meta-analytic evidence have consistently demonstrated that CT significantly enhances insulin sensitivity [23, 63]. Future research should include larger sample sizes and longer intervention durations to further validate the beneficial effects of CT on insulin sensitivity. The observed reduction in triglyceride concentrations may be attributed to increased affinity of VLDL particles for LPL-mediated hydrolysis, leading to enhanced LPL activity, accelerated VLDL-TG catabolism, and consequently lower plasma triglyceride levels [64]. Similarly, Wang et al. [65] also reported that exercise augments lipoprotein lipase activity, thereby facilitating triglyceride hydrolysis and clearance.

Although concurrent training appears to confer Moderate improvements in metabolic outcomes in postmenopausal women, its clinical relevance should be interpreted cautiously. Specifically, the present meta-analysis primarily synthesized results using standardized effect sizes and did not evaluate minimal clinically important differences (MCIDs), which limits inference regarding whether the observed changes reach clinically meaningful improvement at the individual level. While statistically significant improvements were observed in maximal oxygen uptake, blood pressure, fasting glucose, and triglycerides, it remains uncertain whether these changes cross established risk thresholds or translate into sustained long-term cardiovascular benefit. Such translation is likely contingent on the magnitude of change, baseline risk profiles, and variability in intervention protocols. Accordingly, CT may have potential clinical relevance for improving cardiorespiratory fitness; however, confirmation requires further trials that report absolute changes and incorporate MCID-based and responder analyses.

Inflammatory

In this meta-analysis, concurrent training did not produce a significant overall effect on inflammatory markers in postmenopausal women, and the wide confidence intervals indicate substantial imprecision. Biomarker-specific analyses (e.g., IL-6, IL-10, and TNF-α) suggested possible trends; however, because these findings were based on only a small number of studies per biomarker, they should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. In addition, between-trial variation in training modality, intensity, and recovery intervals may have contributed to inconsistent inflammatory responses. Overall, the current evidence remains insufficient to draw definitive conclusions regarding CT-induced inflammatory modulation, and larger, well-controlled randomized trials with standardized biomarker assessment are warranted.

Moderator analysis

Experience level

The present meta-analysis revealed that experience level did not significantly moderate the effects of concurrent training on body composition, metabolic health, or inflammatory responses among postmenopausal women. However, significant within-group differences were identified between participants with different baseline activity levels. Specifically, sedentary individuals demonstrated significant improvements in both body composition and metabolic outcomes, whereas no significant effects were detected in participants with previous exercise experience. This pattern may primarily reflect baseline differences in metabolic and physiological profiles across groups. Previous studies have consistently shown that sedentary individuals tend to have higher adiposity and fasting glucose levels compared with regularly active individuals [66]. Consequently, under comparable exercise intensities, sedentary participants tend to achieve larger relative improvements than their regularly active counterparts. This interpretation is supported by prior evidence indicating that regular physical activity attenuates insulin resistance and metabolic syndrome risk, while sedentary individuals derive proportionally greater benefits from structured exercise interventions [67]. Moreover, sedentary individuals may exhibit greater adaptive capacity in response to exercise interventions. Kullmann et al. [68] further demonstrated that short-term exercise interventions enhanced both central and peripheral insulin sensitivity in sedentary and metabolically impaired individuals, whereas these effects were attenuated among participants with previous training experience. In contrast to body composition and metabolic findings, the present analysis did not detect comparable or statistically significant effects on inflammatory biomarkers, likely due to the limited number of available studies. Consequently, the current evidence base remains insufficient to draw definitive conclusions regarding these effects. Future research should incorporate larger samples and explore the moderating effects of experience level on CT-induced inflammatory adaptations in postmenopausal women.

Sequence

Subgroup analyses indicated that training order did not significantly moderate the overall effects of concurrent training. However, within-group comparisons revealed that performing resistance training before aerobic exercise led to significant improvements in body composition and metabolic health among postmenopausal women. Consistent with these findings, Gao et al. [69] reported that performing resistance training prior to aerobic exercise resulted in greater gains in lower-limb muscle mass. Previous research has demonstrated that resistance training markedly increases circulating catecholamines, free fatty acids, and glycerol levels, thereby enhancing fat oxidation efficiency during subsequent aerobic activity [70]. This mechanism likely confers a metabolic advantage for reducing body fat percentage in postmenopausal women. Furthermore, because postmenopausal women are at higher risk of sarcopenia and glucose–lipid metabolic dysfunction, performing resistance exercise first may deplete muscle glycogen, thereby enhancing endurance-related signaling pathways and improving lipid metabolism and insulin sensitivity [71]. Initiating sessions with resistance training also facilitates neuromuscular adaptations—such as enhanced motor unit recruitment, optimized firing frequency, and increases in muscle cross-sectional area and strength—thereby promoting lean mass accretion. Skeletal muscle serves as the primary peripheral site for insulin-stimulated glucose uptake; therefore, increases in fat-free mass (FFM) are likely to enhance whole-body insulin sensitivity [72, 73]. This adaptive process holds potential clinical relevance for mitigating sarcopenia and metabolic disturbances prevalent among postmenopausal women. Conversely, initiating exercise sessions with endurance training may attenuate neural activation during subsequent resistance exercise. Cadore et al. [74] corroborated this finding, reporting that performing resistance exercise prior to endurance training significantly enhanced relative knee extensor strength. In contrast, when endurance training was performed before resistance exercise, no significant changes in muscle thickness were observed [75]. In summary, although training order did not emerge as a significant moderator of CT efficacy, the observed within-group differences underscore the need for further investigation into how training sequence influences long-term health outcomes among postmenopausal women.

Training volume

Training frequency did not emerge as a significant moderator of concurrent training effects. However, between-group analyses indicated that performing CT three times per week produced significant improvements in body composition and metabolic outcomes among postmenopausal women. Model fit comparisons suggested that the linear regression model yielded the best overall fit to the data. Meta-regression analyses did not demonstrate a statistically significant dose–response relationship for training volume or total intervention duration. Although a weak, non-significant trend in the expected direction was observed, it should be interpreted cautiously given limited statistical power and residual heterogeneity. The current analysis did not detect a statistically significant dose–response relationship, which may be attributed to limited sample size and methodological heterogeneity across included trials. Prior research has demonstrated that small sample sizes decrease the likelihood of detecting true effects and increase sampling variability in effect size estimates, thereby reducing the statistical power to identify dose–response relationships [76]. Moreover, variations in training protocols and exercise modalities may further influence observed outcomes. For instance, Jeon et al. [77] found that a 12-week CT intervention incorporating dance and elastic-band exercises significantly improved insulin sensitivity in older adults. In contrast, van Gemert et al. [78] implemented a year-long CT program combining running and functional resistance training, yet observed no significant changes in insulin sensitivity among postmenopausal women. These findings suggest that training design—including modality, sequencing, and duration—may critically determine the effectiveness of CT interventions. In summary, the findings of this meta-analysis should be interpreted with caution. Future randomized controlled trials with larger sample sizes and rigorously standardized exercise prescriptions—ensuring consistent intensity, modality, training sequence, and rest intervals—are warranted to determine the dose–threshold effects of CT on metabolic outcomes in postmenopausal women.

Methodological factors

A growing body of evidence suggests that integrating aerobic and resistance training within the same program may not always produce optimal physiological adaptations, primarily due to several methodological factors. Specifically, such discrepancies are largely attributable to variations in training design and analytical approaches. When evaluating the effects of concurrent training, it is essential to account for factors such as participants’ experience level, exercise volume, intensity, modality, frequency, sequencing, and inter-session recovery intervals—all of which may substantially affect observed outcomes. For instance, Huiberts et al. [33] demonstrated that experience level moderates the interference effect in CT—untrained individuals are more susceptible to small-to-moderate decrements in strength and power compared with those with prior experience level. Similarly, a recent meta-analysis found that greater training volume significantly improved body fat percentage and lipid profiles but followed a threshold effect, underscoring the need to avoid excessive training loads—particularly among older adults and postmenopausal women [79]. Moreover, the time interval between resistance and aerobic sessions represents another critical methodological variable. Prior research has indicated that performing aerobic exercise within six hours after resistance training significantly reduces running economy, suggesting that insufficient recovery intervals may attenuate performance and adaptive responses [80]. From a statistical perspective, the limited inclusion of female participants restricts the extrapolation of CT findings derived from male populations to women. Several studies have documented sex-specific hormonal adaptations following CT interventions [81, 82]. Additional evidence suggests that men and women also exhibit distinct recovery kinetics and temporal patterns of physiological restoration [83]. Therefore, future research and practical applications should rigorously report and standardize key variables—including training intensity, volume, modality, sequence, and inter-session intervals—to enhance methodological transparency and reproducibility.

In addition, future trials should incorporate pre-registered statistical analysis plans, ensure adequate sample sizes, and conduct stratified analyses by sex and experience level to better elucidate the true effects, dose–response dynamics, and population-specific applicability of CT.

Limitations

Although the present meta-analysis demonstrated that concurrent training had beneficial effects on body composition and metabolic health in postmenopausal women, several limitations must be acknowledged, as they are essential for appropriate interpretation of the findings. First, substantial heterogeneity was evident across the included studies in key prescription parameters, including exercise intensity, aerobic and resistance modalities, session duration, and recovery intervals. Pooling such diverse interventions under the umbrella of concurrent training may obscure modality- or intensity-specific effects. Accordingly, the pooled estimate should be interpreted as an average effect under a broadly defined CT framework rather than the effect of any single optimal CT protocol. Furthermore, the overall risk-of-bias assessment revealed some concerns, and several outcome domains exhibited substantial heterogeneity, potentially reducing the robustness and generalizability of the conclusions.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis of 23 randomized crossover and randomized controlled trials indicates that concurrent training is associated with small improvements in body composition (ES = − 0.11) and modest improvements in metabolic health (ES = − 0.22) in postmenopausal women, with moderate certainty of evidence. However, the pooled effects are small in magnitude, and the clinical relevance of these average changes remains uncertain, particularly because clinically meaningful thresholds (e.g., MCIDs) and absolute changes were not synthesized. Moderator analyses showed that training experience, exercise order, and training frequency did not significantly influence CT effects; therefore, subgroup patterns (e.g., sedentary participants, resistance-before-aerobic sequencing, and three sessions per week) should be regarded as exploratory. Meta-regression did not identify a significant dose–response relationship, and any observed trends warrant cautious interpretation. Future adequately powered studies with larger samples and standardized reporting of key training variables are needed to clarify dose–response relationships and inform CT prescription, including the potential role of recovery interval duration.

Future research directions

Future studies should aim to identify the optimal intervention dose of concurrent training for enhancing body composition, metabolic health, and inflammatory outcomes in postmenopausal women. In the present meta-analysis, the limited number of included studies and the high heterogeneity observed across certain outcomes prevented the detection of a significant dose–response relationship. Accordingly, future research with larger sample sizes is warranted to clarify the optimal dosing parameters of CT in this population. Moreover, this meta-analysis did not explicitly examine the effects of inter-session recovery intervals, primarily due to insufficient reporting in the included trials. Nevertheless, the potential influence of interval duration on CT outcomes should not be overlooked. Previous research has demonstrated that endurance exercise of varying intensities and durations can transiently impair subsequent resistance exercise performance, with such effects typically dissipating within 4–8 h following endurance activity [84]. Future studies should systematically investigate the impact of varying aerobic–resistance interval durations on CT efficacy and establish the optimal recovery interval that maximizes training-induced adaptations.
