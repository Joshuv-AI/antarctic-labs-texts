Long-term follow-up results of anti-BCMA CAR-T cell therapy combined with autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplantation in relapsed/refractory multiple myeloma with extramedullary disease
Authors: Xin Li, Can Liu, Siyan Niu, Ru Li, Shuquan Gao, Rui Cui, Jia Wang, Yao Qi, Jingyi Li, Qi Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRelapsed/refractory multiple myeloma (R/R MM) with extramedullary disease (EMD) has a poor prognosis. Responses to current therapies, including autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (auto-HSCT) and chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy, remain unsatisfactory or show frequent early progression despite an initial response. In this study, we aimed to investigate the efficacy of a therapeutic approach combining CAR T-therapy with auto-HSCT.MethodsEighteen patients with R/R MM with EMD were enrolled in clinical trials evaluating anti-BCMA CAR-T therapy. Of these, eight patients were treated in combination with auto-ASCT in combination (T-C group), while the remaining ten underwent CAR T-cell therapy alone (C group). We retrospectively compared the clinical responses, CAR T-cell expansion kinetics, T-cell subset profiles, serum interleukin-6 (IL-6) levels, treatment-related toxicities, and long-term outcomes between the two cohorts.ResultsIn the T-C group, all 8 patients achieved an overall response (ORR) (ORR) based on the combined haematologic and imaging assessments of EMD. In contrast, among the 10 patients in the C group, 8 met the haematologic criteria for ORR, but only 6 demonstrated a radiographic response in EMD lesions. Progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) were markedly improved in the T-C group. This cohort also exhibited higher peak levels of both CAR- T cells and IL-6. On day 28 post-infusion, the proportion of CD3⁺CD4⁺ T cells was significantly higher in the T-C group. While cytokine release syndrome (CRS) tended to be more severe in this group, the incidence and severity of the immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) was comparable between the two groups. Haematologic recovery was delayed in the T-C group, and three of the eight patients developed poor graft function.ConclusionWe retrospectively summarised the results of a 3-year follow-up and found that the combination of anti-BCMA CAR-T cell therapy and auto-HSCT was associated with improved PFS and OS in R/R MM patients with EMD. Moreover, CD4⁺ T-cell dynamics may be associated with the durable clinical response observed in the T-C group. Although the T-C group experienced higher-grade CRS and prolonged haematological toxicity, no treatment-related deaths due to treatment-related toxicities were observed. Introduction

Multiple myeloma (MM), a malignant neoplasm of the plasma cells, accounts for approximately 10% of all haematological malignancies and has an annual incidence of 6.6 cases per 100,000 individuals [1]. In recent years, the introduction of novel therapeutic agents including proteasome inhibitors (PIs), immunomodulatory drugs (IMiDs), monoclonal antibodies, autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (auto-HSCT), and chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapy has substantially improved the survival outcomes of patients with MM [2–4]. Despite these advances, MM remains incurable, with recurrent relapses representing a major therapeutic hurdle [5]. Extramedullary disease (EMD) is a frequent complication of relapsed/refractory (R/R) MM and is consistently associated with poor survival outcomes [6]. At the initial diagnosis, EMD is present in approximately 2–5% of patients with MM and in 20–40% of patients with R/R MM [7–9]. MM patients with EMD have a significantly poorer prognosis compared to those without EMD, with shorter median progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) [10, 11]. In particular, for EMD unrelated to bones, the prognosis is worse [12]. MM patients with EMD respond poorly to conventional therapies, including PIs, chemotherapy, and autologous stem cell transplantationASCT, resulting in poor PFS and OS [13]. In CAR- T cell therapy for R/R MM, EMD is also an adverse factor affecting the prognosis, especially in MM patients with non-bone-related EMDs, who have the poorest prognosis [14–16]. Effective strategies to improve prognosis and prolong survival in MM patients with EMD remain an unmet clinical need. To address this challenge, we attempted to combine therapeutic approach combining CAR-T therapy with auto-HSCT. So far, there have been no reports on the long-term outcomes of this combined therapy in MM patients with EMD. Our study represents a retrospectively observation with 3-year follow-up, which is helpful in exploring the sustained efficacy and safety of this combined therapy. The preliminary observation results indicate that this strategy enhances both PFS and OS in R/R MM patients with EMD.

Methods

This three-year cohort retrospective study aimed to explore the effectiveness of a therapeutic approach combining CAR T-therapy with auto-HSCT in MM patients with EMD.

Patients enrolled in the study

Between June 2020 and December 2022, 18 patients with R/R MM and at least one EMD site of EMD were enrolled in a clinical trials of anti-BCMA CAR-T therapy (ChiCTR2000033925). Eight patients received anti-BCMA CAR- T therapy in combination with auto-HSCT and constituted the T-C group. A concurrent control group (C group) included ten R/R MM patients with EMD who received anti-BCMA CAR T therapy alone; all were unable or did not receive sufficient stem cells for auto-HSCT. The enrolment period for this study concluded in December 2022, and the cutoff date for analysis was December 2025.

Anti-BCMA CAR T-cell therapy

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells were collected from patients with R/R MM in both the T-C and C groups and used to manufacture anti-BCMA CAR T-cells. The humanised anti-BCMA CAR construct, designated as lenti-BCMA-2rd-CAR, was provided by Shanghai Genbase Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Transduction efficiency was assessed using flow cytometry (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) on the day of harvest following ex vivo expansion.

All patients in the C group received lymphodepleting chemotherapy with fludarabine (30 mg/m2) and cyclophosphamide (400 mg/m2) from day − 4 to day − 2. Autologous humanised anti-BCMA CAR T-cells (2 × 106 cells/kg) were infused on day 0 in the C group or 2–4 days after stem cell infusion in the T-C group (Fig. 1).

Auto-HSCT

Before stem cell mobilisation, all R/R MM patients with EMD in the T-C group achieved only minimal response (MR) or stable disease (SD) based on the haematologic criteria. For mobilisation, either cyclophosphamide (CTX, 3–5 g/m²) followed by granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF, 300 µg/day, initiated 5–7 days after CTX and continued until stem cell collection) or G-CSF alone (300 µg/day on days 1–5) was administered. Peripheral blood stem cells were then collected via leukapheresis. In the T-C group, the conditioning regimen consisted of high-dose melphalan (200 mg/m²) [17]. The required dose of CD34+ stem cells infused in the T-C group was > 2.0 × 106/kg.

Maintenance treatment after anti-BCMA CAR T-cell therapy

All patients obtained objective response rate (ORR) initiated lenalidomide or pomalidomide maintenance therapy 2–3 months following CAR-T cell infusion once hematologic toxicity had resolved. For patients who do not respond to CAR-T cell therapy, salvage therapy was immediately initiated (1–2 months following CAR-T cell infusion). One patient in the C group subsequently underwent allogeneic haematopoietic stem cell transplantation three months after CAR T-cell therapy.

Criteria for diagnosis and evaluation criteria for therapeutic efficacy

The diagnoses of R/R MM with EMD and the clinical response to humanised anti-BCMA CAR T-cell therapy were assessed according to the International Myeloma Working Group Guidelines uniform response criteria for MM [18]. The proportion of MM cells was determined by bone marrow (BM) morphology and FCM. BCMA expression in MM cells before and 28 days after CAR-T cell therapy were measured by FCM. M proteins were detected by immunofixation electrophoresis. Assessable EMD was detected using CT, PET/CT or MRI. Therapeutic efficacy was assessed monthly during the first three months from the infusion day of CAR-T cell in all patients, including those in both the T-C and C groups. Thereafter, evaluations were conducted every two to three months. We chose the efficacy evaluation results three months after CAR T-cell infusion as the time point for comparing the two groups. Clinical responses included stringent complete response (sCR), complete response (CR), very good partial response (VGPR), partial response (PR), minimal response(MR), stable disease༈SD༉, and progressive disease (PD). We assessed the ORR (including sCR, CR, VGPR, and PR), overall survival (OS), and progression-free survival (PFS).

Adverse events in the combination therapy

The proportion of anti-BCMA CAR T-cells in the peripheral blood was assessed by flow cytometry on days 0, 4, 7, 14, and 28 following CAR T-cell infusion. Serum interleukin-6 (IL-6) concentrations were measured at the same time points (days 0, 7, 14, and 28) using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). The adverse events associated with humanised anti-BCMA CAR T-cell therapy were systematically monitored. Cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and immune-effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) were graded according to the consensus criteria established by the American Society for Transplantation and Cellular Therapy (ASTCT) [19], while hematologic toxicities were classified using the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), version 5.0 [20].

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as the mean ± SE. The probabilities of PFS and OS were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared with the log-rank test. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 7 and SPSS 17.0. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

R/R MM patient characteristics

The baseline characteristics of the 18 patients with R/R MM enrolled in this clinical trial are summarised in Table 1. All patients with R/R MM underwent at least one bone-unrelated EMD before enrolment in the clinical trial. No significant differences were observed between the T-C and C groups with respect to age, sex, high-risk cytogenetics, International Staging System (ISS) stage III, or median number of prior lines of therapy. All 18 patients were followed up for more than three years.

Transduction and amplification efficiency, infusion dose of the humanised anti-BCMA CAR T-cells

The mean anti-BCMA CAR transduction efficiency in the final products of T-C group and C group was 40.12 ± 6.92% and 37.41 ± 7.11%. On days 2–4 after stem cell infusion a dose of 2.09 ± 0.66 × 106 cells/kg anti-BCMA CAR T-cell was infused in the T-C group, and on day 0 a dose of 2.12 ± 0.49 × 106 cells/kg anti-BCMA CAR T-cell was infused in the C group.

Stem cell infusion in the T-C group

A total of 2.46 ± 0.72 × 106 CD34+ cells/kg were infused in the T-C group on day 0 during their auto-HSCT process.

BCMA expression in MM cells before and after anti-BCMA CAR-T cell therapy

Before CAR-T cell therapy, the median expression of BCMA in MM cells was 79.2 (IQR 8.8–92.8) % in T-C group, while it was 69.8 (IQR 13.1-78.78) % in C group. There was no difference of the expression between the two groups (P = 0.7739) before CAR-T cell therapy. At 28 days after CAR-T cell infusion, the median expression of BCMA in MM cells was 0.025 (IQR 0-0.09) % in T-C group, it was lower than that of in C group (0.225 (IQR 0–4.0) %) (P = 0.2318). Furthermore, the expression of BCMA in MM cells was not correlated with the efficacy of anti-BCMA CAR-T cell therapy in T-C group (R2 = 0.0221, P = 0.7256) or in C group (R2 = 0.5940, P = 0.0834) (Supplementary Figure).

Clinical response to therapy

After the infusion of anti-BCMA CAR T-cells, we evaluated the therapeutic effect based on the proportion of myeloma cells, M protein levels, and imaging examination of EMD. Regarding the best haematological responses, all eight patients (8/8, 100.0%) in the T-C group achieved ORR 3 months after CAR T-cell infusion. Moreover, the EMD disappeared in all eight patients in the T-C group at this time. Therefore, regardless of whether it was combined with imaging examinations (best imaging EMD response), the ORR in the T-C group was 100.0%. In the C group, eight patients (8/10, 80.0%) achieved an ORR 3 months after CAR T-cell infusion. However, only six patients (6/10, 60.0%) in the C group reached the best imaging EMD response at this time (Fig. 2A-B).

We retrospectively compared the efficacy of the two groups at 2 and 3 months after CAR T-cell infusion. In the T-C group, the best haematological responses were evaluated two and three months after CAR T-cell infusion; seven patients (7/8, 87.5%) achieved an sCR/CR, and one patient (1/8, 12.5%) achieved a VGPR. The best imaging EMD response was the same as the haematological responses at two and three months after CAR T-cell infusion in the T-C group. In the C group, the best haematological responses were observed three months after CAR T-cell infusion: three patients (3/10, 30.0%) achieved an sCR/CR, two patients (2/10, 20.0%) achieved a VGPR, three patients (3/10, 30.0%) achieved PR, and two patients (2/10, 20.0%) achieved SD only. However, the best imaging EMD response in this group (the EMD of six patients in the C group) disappeared three months after CAR T-cell therapy (Fig. 2C-D).

Re-progression of the disease and survival time

With a median follow-up of 37.5 months in T-C group (estimated by the reverse Kaplan-Meier method), two patients (Pt 1 and Pt 8 in T-C group) who obtained ORR experienced BM relapse without EMD at 7 months and 19 months post CAR-T cell infusion. One of these two patients died of progressive MM, and the other died of infection. The remaining six patients (6/8, 75.0%) remained alive with sCR or CR at the cutoff. With a median follow-up of 10.0 months in C group (estimated by the reverse Kaplan-Meier method), four patients (Pt 2, Pt 4, Pt 5, Pt 6 in C group) who obtained ORR had disease progression again shortly after anti-BCMA CAR-T cell therapy. All these four patients had a BM relapse, and at the same time, they had primary or new EMD. Only four patients (4/10, 40.0%) survived in the CR/VGPR/PR state until the cutoff date in the C group (including one patient who underwent allogeneic haematopoietic stem cell transplantation) (Fig. 3A).

The PFS and OS were higher in the T-C group than in the C group (PPFS=0.0015 and POS=0.0015). There were no differences in PFS or OS between the T-C and C groups at 6 months after therapy (PPFS 6 m=0.0500 and POS 6 m=0.1939). Expression of anti-BCMA CAR T cells, T cells and IL-6 in peripheral blood

In both groups, the proportion of anti-BCMA CAR T cells was detected at 0, 7, 14, and 28 days after CAR T-cell infusion. The peak of anti-BCMA CAR T cells in CD3+ T cells in peripheral blood was 45.24 (interquartile range [IQR] 31.9, 63.4%) in the T-C group, and 23.66% (IQR 1.6–46.7%) in the C group. The peak number of CAR T cells was higher in the T-C group than in the C group (P = 0.0040) (Fig. 4A). The time to reach the peak number of CAR T cells was 15.25 (IQR 10, 21) days in the T-C group, and 9.25 (IQR 4, 14) days in the C group. The time to reach the peak number of CAR T cells was longer in the T-C group than in the C group (P = 0.0052) (Fig. 4B). In both groups, the level of IL-6 was detected at 0, 7, 14, and 28 days after CAR T-cell infusion. The median peak of IL-6 in peripheral blood was 434.58 (IQR 86.3, 998.6) pg/mL in the T-C group, and 104.44 (IQR 19.2, 506.7) pg/mL in the C group. The median peak IL-6 level was higher in the T-C group than in the C group (P = 0.0035) (Fig. 4C).

Safety and adverse effects

In both groups, the patients developed fever with or without chills, fatigue, headache, nausea, tachycardia, oedema, and other symptoms 2–8 days after the CAR T-cell infusion. The onset time of symptoms in the C group was 2.68 ± 0.95 days, which was earlier than in T-C group (4.85 ± 1.53 days; P = 0.0225). These symptoms lasted for 7–22 d and then subsided.

The CRS grade was higher in the T-C group than in the C group (P = 0.0405); however, there was no difference in the ICANS grade between the two groups (P = 0.5596) (Fig. 6A).The ≥ grade 3 of CRS was found in three patients (3/8, 37.5%) in the T-C group and in only one patient (1/10, 10.0%) in the C group. The ≥ grade 1 of ICANS was noted in two patients (2/8, 20.0%) in the T-C group, and in 1 patient (1/10, 10.0%) in the C group. The correlation between the CRS grade and ICANS grade was not statistically significant in either group (P = 0.0784 and 0.0517, respectively) (Fig. 6B).

In our study, there was no statistically significant correlation between CRS grade and the peak number of CAR T-cells in either group (P = 0.1876 and 0.1855, for the T-C and C groups, respectively) or between the CRS grade and IL-6 peak in the T-C group (P = 0.3891). However, the CRS grade correlated with the peak IL-6 level in the C group (P = 0.0491) (Fig. 6CD). Further, the ICANS grade correlated with the peak of CAR T cells in the T-C group (P = 0.0097) and with the peak of IL-6 in the C group (P < 0.0000). However, there was no statistically significant correlation between the ICANS grade and the peak of CAR T cells in the C group (P = 0.0539) or between the ICANS grade and the peak of IL-6 in the T-C group (P = 0.2683) (Fig. 6EF).

None of the patients in either group died of bacterial infections or invasive fungal diseases in the course of the combination therapy or CAR T-cell therapy.

Discussion

Although MM remains an incurable malignancy, auto-HSCT is still recommended as the standard therapy for eligible patients according to international guidelines. Compared to treatment regimens based on proteasome inhibitors(PIs) and immunomodulatory drugs༈IMiDs༉, auto-HSCT has been shown to significantly improve both PFS and OS [21]. While a subset of studies has shown that auto-HSCT might cure a small number of patients, ranging from 6.3% to 31.3% [22], the survival benefits of auto-HSCT are limited [23].

EMD is recognised as a high-risk feature of MM. Although auto-HSCT may confer a survival benefit in MM patients with EMD, the response rates to auto-HSCT in this subgroup remain suboptimal [12]. Moreover, among MM patients receiving auto-HSCT, the PFS and OS are significantly shorter in patients with EMD than in those without EMD [24, 25]. When malignant plasma cells acquire the ability to proliferate independently of the BM microenvironment and cause EMD, they often exhibit enhanced invasiveness and resistance to therapies. These distinct biological features of EMD likely contribute to the significant clinical challenges associated with its treatment. The advent of novel therapeutic agents has led to substantial improvements in the treatment of MM. A study investigating the use of cyclophosphamide, etoposide, and dexamethasone as salvage therapies in patients with R/R MM (including patients with EMD) reported an ORR of 52%. Notably, 23% of these patients subsequently underwent auto-HSCT or CAR T-cell therapy, underscoring the potential role of this chemotherapy regimen as a bridge to auto-HSCT or CAR T-cell therapy for R/R MM and EMD [26]. For transplant-eligible patients with newly diagnosed MM (NDMM), auto-HSCT remains the standard of care, as endorsed by international guidelines including those from the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) and the European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) [21]. Although auto-HSCT remains a cornerstone of therapy for eligible patients with MM, current evidence regarding its ability to mitigate the adverse prognostic impact of EMD remains conflicting [27, 28]. In R/R MM patients with EMD, the role of double auto-HSCT in achieving deeper remission remains controversial. Current evidence suggests that double auto-HSCT offers only marginal improvements in PFS and OS compared to single auto-HSCT [29]. Furthermore, no survival benefit has been observed with double auto-HSCT in patients staged as ISS III or with renal impairment and should therefore be chosen with caution [30]. Collectively, these findings suggest that auto-HSCT, whether single or double, may not reliably improve the outcomes in MM patients with EMD.

BCMA CAR T-cell therapy has shown excellent and durable clinical efficacy in patients with R/R MM [31–33]. However, owing to the distinct biological features and adverse prognoses associated with EMD, the efficacy and optimal development strategies of CAR T-cell therapy in R/R MM patients with EMD have become a focus of attention [34]. In long-term follow-up studies, including our own previous results, the median PFS and median OS of R/R MM patients with EMD after anti-BCMA CAR T-cell therapy were significantly shorter than those without EMD [16, 35–38]. In our retrospective study, the optimal efficacy evaluation for patients in C group might at 2 or 3 months after the CAR-T cell infusion. However, patients in C group might experience rapid disease progression again after achieved ORR in CAR-T cell therapy. Therefore, based on the literature reports and our retrospective results, we suggest that R/R MM patients with EMD who received CAR-T therapy should evaluate the efficacy every month to better determine the initiation time of maintenance treatment or salvage treatment. The efficacy and durability of CAR T-cell therapy in R/R MM patients with EMD remain limited and pose significant clinical challenges. The invasiveness and drug resistance of EMD are attributed to its distinctive tumour microenvironment and complex molecular and genetic alterations; however, the precise mechanisms underlying its evasion of immune surveillance and resistance to targeted therapies remain incompletely understood and warrant further investigation [39]. Optimising combination therapeutic strategies for R/R MM patients with EMD is an urgent clinical priority for enhancing treatment efficacy and prolonging remission duration. In a clinical trial of BCMA/G protein-coupled receptor class C group 5 member D (GPRC5D) dual-target CAR T-cell therapy in R/R MM patients with EMD, the ORR of nine patients reached 100%, among whom 44.4% achieved CR, and the 1-year OS rate was 60% [40]. However, its long-term efficacy remains unclear.

Although the ORR and CR were satisfactory, more than half of the patients who received anti-BCMA CAR T-cell therapy relapsed within one year, especially R/R MM patients with EMD, whose prognosis was particularly poor [41]. Whether auto-HSCT and CAR-T cell therapy combine the advantages of both and is it feasible in terms of mechanism? When there was no difference in the baseline characteristics between the two groups, the combination of CAR-T cell therapy and auto-HSCT was associated with improved PFS and OS in R/R MM patient with EMD. Of course, the number of patients included in our study was very small, and the results were merely preliminary observations. The expansion of CD8+T cells has been proven to be associated with a favourable prognosis for anti-BCMA CAR T-cell therapeutics [42, 43]. Following auto-HSCT, the CD4⁺/CD8⁺ T cell ratio remains inverted over an extended period, accompanied by the rapid proliferation of CD8⁺ T cells [44]. In this retrospective study, we did not observe a sustained increase in the proportion of CD8⁺ T-cells in the T-C group. Unexpectedly, however, the proportion of CD4⁺ T cells in the T-C group was significantly elevated 28 days after CAR T-cell infusion. Whether this shift in CD4⁺ T cell dynamics is associated with the durable clinical response observed in the T-C group warrants further investigation in prospective, randomized and multicenter trial. In a study on CAR T-cell therapy for aggressive B-cell lymphoma, low CD4⁺ T cell counts one month after infusion predicted worse 12-month PFS [45], This report might support our results about the CD4⁺ T cell dynamics is associated with the durable clinical response.

Previous studies have suggested that auto-HSCT enhances the antitumour efficacy, expansion, and persistence of CAR T-cells [46, 47]. Strategies to enhance the efficacy of CAR T-cell therapy include overcoming resistance mechanisms such as antigen escape and the immunosuppressive tumour microenvironment [48]. Moreover, the conditioning regimen associated with auto-HSCT may favourably modulate the tumour microenvironment, thereby creating a more conducive setting for subsequent immunotherapy and promoting CAR T-cell expansion, particularly in patients with EMD [47]. In our retrospective study, the peaks of CAR T-cells and IL-6 in the T-C group were higher. T-cell dysfunction is commonly observed following auto-HSCT. Administering CAR T-cell therapy after auto-HSCT may facilitate the restoration of T lymphocyte function, thereby enhancing the intensity and durability of the antitumour response [49]. Therefore, the combination of auto-HSCT and CAR T-cell therapy has demonstrated promising clinical efficacy and represents a feasible therapeutic approach for R/R MM patients with EMD, as supported by our retrospective observational results. After a retrospective 3-year follow-up, the T-C group demonstrated superior ORR, PFS, and OS than the C group. In theory, auto-HSCT prior to CAR T-cell infusion may reduce the tumour burden, thereby mitigating the severity of CRS and ICANS. However, similar results were not observed in the present study. Notably, the higher incidence of high-grade CRS in the T-C group may be attributed to the higher level of CAR T-cell expansion in this group.

To date, data on the combination of auto-HSCT and CAR T-cell therapy in patients with R/R MM remain limited, with most published studies having small sample sizes. Although patients in the T-C group experienced higher rates of AEs in our retrospective observation, a 3-year follow-up revealed that anti-BCMA CAR T-cell therapy combined with auto-HSCT was associated with improved PFS and OS in R/R MM patients with EMD. Moreover, CD4⁺ T-cell dynamics may be associated with the durable clinical response observed in the T-C group. The adverse events observed in the T-C group may be attributable, at least in part, to auto-HSCT. However, it is difficult to determine whether these adverse effects are caused by auto-HSCT or CAR T-cell therapy. Prospective randomized multi-centre clinical trials should be conducted in large scale to clarify the aetiology of these adverse effects.

Conclusions

Our findings suggest that the combination of anti-BCMA CAR T-cell therapy and auto-HSCT may be a feasible and effective combination treatment in MM patients with EMD. The results are encouraging suggesting that it may benefit this challenging group of patients. Further studies should validate these findings.
