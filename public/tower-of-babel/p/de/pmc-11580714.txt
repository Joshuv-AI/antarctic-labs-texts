Cryo‐EM reveals transition states of the Acinetobacter baumannii F1 ‐ATPase rotary subunits γ and ε, unveiling novel compound targets
Authors: Khoa Cong Minh Le, Chui Fann Wong, Volker Müller, Gerhard Grüber
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPriority 1: critical WHO pathogen Acinetobacter baumannii depends on ATP synthesis and ATP:ADP homeostasis and its bifunctional F1FO‐ATP synthase. While synthesizing ATP, it regulates ATP cleavage by its inhibitory ε subunit to prevent wasteful ATP consumption. We determined cryo‐electron microscopy structures of the ATPase active A. baumannii F1‐αßγεΔ134–139 mutant in four distinct conformational states, revealing four transition states and structural transformation of the ε's C‐terminal domain, forming the switch of an ATP hydrolysis off‐ and an ATP synthesis on‐state based. These alterations go in concert with altered motions and interactions in the catalytic‐ and rotary subunits of this engine. These A. baumannii interacting sites provide novel pathogen‐specific targets for inhibitors, with the aim of ATP depletion and/or ATP synthesis and growth inhibition. Furthermore, the presented diversity to other bacterial F‐ATP synthases extends the view of structural elements regulating such a catalyst.

INTRODUCTION

Antibiotic resistance is an urgent global threat to food industry and public health. The rise of antibiotic‐resistant bacteria like Acinetobacter baumannii, which is regarded as one of the most antibiotic‐resistant bacteria, represents a silent pandemic. 1 A. baumannii causes infections in the hospital and community, with pneumonia being mostly observed. The number of pan‐drug‐resistant A. baumannii strains arises with a concomitant increase in mortality rate. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 The bacterium has developed several defense mechanisms by modifying their specific targets, expressing numerous enzymes that neutralize drugs, altering the permeability of the cell wall, or upregulation of drug efflux pumps, which are ATP‐ or proton‐motive force (pmf) driven 4 and representing a main causative factor for A. baumannii's drug resistance.

As a strictly aerobic organism, 1 A. baumannii is dependent on pmf‐driven oxidative phosphorylation and thereby the F1FO‐ATP synthase catalyzing the formation of ADP and inorganic phosphate (Pi) to ATP. The atomic structures of the AbF1FO‐ATP synthase (α3:β3:γ:ε:δ:a:b 2 :c 10) 6 and AbF1‐ATPase (α3:β3:γ:ε) 7 revealed the alternating subunit αβ‐pairs, including the catalytic and nucleotide‐binding sites at their interfaces and the central stalk subunit pair γ‐ε (Figure 1A). The latter connects proton translocation via the FO‐domain subunits a‐c 10 and ATP synthesis in the α3:β3‐sector. The interfaces of the three nucleotide‐binding subunit pairs α and β form the catalytic centers. 6 Subunits b 2 :δ build the peripheral stalk and smoothen transmission of power between the rotary γ:ε pair and the c 10‐ring. 6

Like in the most studied Escherichia coli (E. coli, Ec) 8 and Geobacillus stearothermophilus (G. stearothermophilus, Gs) 9 F1FO‐ATP synthase/F1‐ATPase, the enzyme catalyzes also the cleavage of ATP to ADP + Pi, where ATP binding to an open and nucleotide‐empty αβ‐pair (αβ)E induces an anti‐clockwise rotation of subunit γ, followed by ATP hydrolysis in the (αβ)TP pair and ADP—and inorganic phosphate (Pi) release from the (αβ)DP pair, which triggers a continuous γ rotation. 8 ATP hydrolysis drives proton transport through the FO‐domain and contributes to the pmf, which is essential for the regulation of oxidative phosphorylation and the function of in‐ and efflux pumps. Like the F1FO‐ATP synthase/F1‐ATPase of the pathogenic Mycobacterium tuberculosis and Mycobacterium abscessus, 10 , 11 , 12 the A. baumannii enzyme reveals latent ATPase activity to prevent the bacterium from the unnecessary consumption of ATP, to control the pmf and thereby the efficacy of pumps involved in drug in‐ and out‐take. The A. baumannii subunit ε (Abε) is the major ATP hydrolysis inhibitory element. 6 , 7 It consists of an N‐terminal β‐barrel domain (ε1–85), connected by a linker (ε86–90) to the C‐terminal domain (CTD, ε91–139). 6 , 7 Two structural snapshots have been made of the Abε, 6 , 7 where the CTD is shown in an extended conformation (Abεe) 6 , 7 and a compact form (Abεc). 7 In Abεe the CTD consists of a long α1‐helix (ε91–125), a short α2‐helix (ε129–134), 6 , 7 and the two unstructured segments ε126–128 and ε135–139 (Figure 1A). 6 , 7 α2‐helix and ε135–139 form several interaction with subunits α, β, and γ. Mutational studies have demonstrated that ε134–139 causes the inhibition of ATP hydrolysis and is essential for ATP synthesis and cell growth. 7 In contrast to Abεe, the CTD of Abεc is compost of two long α‐helices (α1 (ε91–136) and α2 (ε111–136)), connected by a short loop (Figure 1B). 7 Helix α2 interaction with the N‐terminal domain (NTD) keeps the subunit in the compact formation and brings the enzyme into the catalytically active state.

The pressing questions are, (i) what the transition positions are and (ii) the structural transformation of Abε's CTD on its journey from an inhibitory Abεe state to an Abεc or vice versa. To address these, we present here four conformational states (C1–C4) of the ATPase active AbF1‐αßγεΔ134–139 mutant, which shine light into the various positions, structural transformations, and interactions of the Abε CTD on its journey from an Abεe− to an Abεc conformation. Such drastic alterations go along with large movements of Abγ's N‐ and C‐terminal helices and twisting of γ's foot domain and the εNTD. The concerted changes in γ's N‐ and C‐termini, εCTD as well as the drastic movements of γ's foot‐domain and the εNTD, provide insights into the coupling processes of catalytic events in the α3:β3‐hexamer and proton‐transport via subunits a–b and vice versa. These features have the potential to be used in the design of new targets and compounds against the pathogen.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Generation, purification, and characterization of recombinant AbF1 ‐αßγεΔ 134–139 mutant

The mutant AbF1‐αßγεΔ134–139 was generated, produced, and purified as described by Saw et al. 7 ATP hydrolysis was investigated using a continuous ATP hydrolysis assay. 10

Cryo‐EM data acquisition

The mutant AbF1‐αßγεΔ134–139 was purified and concentrated to 1.5 mg/mL, from which 3.5 μL was applied to a holey carbon grid (Quantifoil® 300 mesh R1.2/1.3 holey carbon grid; Quantifoil Micro Tools GmbH, Germany) that was previously glow‐discharged in air for 1 min. Grids were blotted twice with FEI Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific (RRID:SCR_008452); Waltham, MA, USA) for 2.5 s at 4°C and 100% humidity before plunge‐freezing in liquid ethane.

Cryo‐EM data of the mutant AbF1‐εΔ134–139 were acquired with a FEI Titan 300 kV TEM electron microscope (ThermoFisher Scientific; Waltham, MA, USA) equipped with a Gatan K3 direct electron detector (Gatan; Pleasanton, CA, USA). Data collection was performed automatically with SerialEM 4.0, 13 in which 8635 movies were recorded in super‐resolution mode with 40 exposure frames per movie. Each movie was recorded at 105 000× magnification, yielding a pixel size of 0.858 Å/pixel. The total exposure for each movie was calibrated to be 64.8 e − per Å2.

Cryo‐EM data analysis and conformers separation

All movies were imported into cryoSPARC (RRID:SCR_016501) V4.2.1 14 and preprocessed with the typical pipeline. Local beam‐induced motion was corrected with Patch Motion Correction to correct each particle motion. Defocus and astigmatism were estimated with Patch CTF. 15 Initial template picking was performed with 2000 micrographs utilizing the Blob Picker function. A total of 3 738 901 particles were picked from all micrographs, which were subsequently filtered through multiple rounds of 2D classifications and subsequently further sorted with ab‐initio reconstruction to remove incomplete αβγε‐complexes. One final model, with 556 440 particles, was then utilized for downstream 3D refinements and 3D classifications. Through iterative 3D classifications, four particle groups representing conformational state 1 through 4 were identified. The information on each class is listed in Tables S1–S3.

Model refinement and PDB deposition

Different models were utilized to fit into the final map of each conformational state. For C1, the wild type (WT) AbF1‐ATPase model (PDB 7YRY 7 ) was used to fit into the map, with the last five residues of εCTD being manually removed in Coot (RRID:SCR_014222). 16 In the case of the conformational states C2 and C3, the α3:β3:γ complex was fitted with the AbF1‐ATPase model (PDB 7YRY 7 ), while the Abε was taken from the crystallographic Escherichia coli γ‐ε stalk structure (PDB 1FS0 17 ). Non homologous amino acids of the E. coli subunit ε were manually replaced with the residues of the AbεΔ134–139. Although a “half‐up” conformation of subunit ε was described in a recent cryo‐EM structure of the E. coli F‐ATP synthase, 18 this structure lacks the C‐terminal ε residues 104–138. Therefore, the E. coli γ‐ε crystal structure (PDB 1FS0 17 ) was used as the preferred model. The model of C1 was used to analyze the map of C4, with the εCTD residues 84–139 being removed, resulting in the structure of the globular domain (residues 2–83).

Regardless of the conformation states, the initial models were first rigidly fitted into their respective EM maps in ChimeraX. 19 The model was manually adjusted and refined in Coot, 16 and further real‐space refinements were performed in Phenix. 20 This process was iterated multiple times to optimize the final model. The quality of the final models was then evaluated with MolProbity (RRID:SCR_014226). 21

Visualizations, generation of figures, and/or movies were performed with PyMOL (RRID:SCR_000305), 22 UCSF ChimeraX, 19 and UCSF Chimera. 23 The structural data of the cryo‐EM study of mutant AbF1‐αßγεΔ134–139 are available in wwPDB under PDB ID 8ZI0, 8ZI1, 8ZI2, 8ZI3, and EM Data Bank at https://www.ebi.ac.uk/emdb/ under EMDB ID EMD‐60117, EMD‐60118, EMD 60119, and EMD‐60120 for C1, C2, C3, and C4, respectively.

RESULTS

The mutant enzyme AbF1 ‐αßγεΔ 134–139 and its extended ε conformation

The variant enzyme AbF1‐αβγεΔ134–139 was produced and purified as described recently 7 (Figure S1A). The recombinant variant shows an ATP hydrolysis activity of 5.3 ± 0.03 μmol·min−1 (mg of protein)−1, which is approximately 26 times higher than the latent WT AbF1‐ATPase (0.2 ± 0.001 μmol·min−1 [mg of protein]−1) and similar to the ε‐free AbF1‐αβγ complex (4.6 ± 0.02 μmol·min−1 [mg of protein]−1) (Figure S1B). These data underscore the recently described importance of Abε, its C‐terminal domain (CTD) and in particular its C‐terminal residues 134IKNRAQ139 in ATP hydrolysis autoinhibition 7 and that the enzymatically active AbF1‐αβγεΔ134–139 is a suitable model to study structural intermediates between Abε's extended (εe) and complex (εc) form during enzymatic turnover.

Therefore, cryo‐EM data of the freshly purified AbF1‐αβγεΔ134–139 were collected as described under Materials and Methods, resulting in 8635 movies. Like in the recently resolved AbF‐ATP synthase 6 and the WT AbF1‐ATPase, 6 no exogenous nucleotides were added to the AbF1‐αβγεΔ134–139 sample, to generate similar nucleotide conditions. After initial processing, 556 440 particles were finalized for 3D reconstruction. By employing several rounds of 3D classification 15 (Figure S1C), numerous states of the active enzyme were separated from each other, resulting in four distinct conformational states associated with the coupling of the subunit ε and its interactions with the αβγ subunits (Figures 2 and S1C).

The first conformational state (C1) of the AbF1‐αβγεΔ134–139 structure at an overall resolution of 3.18 Å (Figure S2A–D) reveals the εCTD in an extended conformation (Figure 2A,B). The AbF1‐αßγεΔ134–139 structure contains the following amino acids (aa): αE, αTP, and αDP 26 to 514; βE, βTP, and βDP 2 to 464; γ 2 to 289; ε 2 to 133. All three α subunits were occupied with MgATP (Figure S3A). The catalytic βE subunit was empty, while βDP and βTP were occupied by inorganic phosphate (Pi) and MgADP, respectively (Figure S3A), which is identical to the published WT AbF1‐ATPase (PDB ID 7YRY 7 ) and the AbF1FO‐ATP synthase (PDB ID 7P2Y 6 ). When aligned to the WT AbF1‐ATPase structure, the root mean square deviation (RMSD) between the two structures is 0.679 Å. Figure S4 confirms the similar rotational states of the AbF1‐αβγεΔ134–139 C1‐structure and the WT enzyme, with the catalytic heads and central stalk subunit γ (RMSD of 1.058 Å) of both structures aligning well to each other. Similarly, the N‐terminal domain (NTD) residues εΔ134–139 can be nicely fitted into the density map (Figure S2C) and superimposed on the WT subunit ε (RMSD of 1.7 Å, Figure 3) and the long helix 1 (ε91–125) of both structures run in parallel. The two helical turns formed by residues E129–I134 are unstructured in εΔ134–139, which may be due to the truncation of the terminal residues I134–Q139 that form close interactions with γ's C‐terminal helix. 6 , 7 Overall, the AbF1‐αβγεΔ134–139 in C1, showing a similar rotation state, nucleotide occupation in the catalytic sites, and aligning well in regard to ε's NTD and the orientation of the ε's CTD, can be considered as a representative state of the εe conformation in the WT enzyme.

Conformational changes in ε and γ during transition from C1 to C2 and C3

In comparison to the AbF1‐αβγεΔ134–139 C1, the AbF1‐αβγεΔ134–139 structures of the conformational states C2 and C3, determined at an overall resolution of 2.92 and 2.99 Å (Figures S1C, S5–S7, and 2C,D), respectively, differ significantly to the C1 in regard to the central stalk subunits ε and γ. Aligning the εNTDs of the three structures highlights the structural alteration of the extended helix 1 (ε91–125) in the εe form of the C1 conformation, which breaks into three smaller helices of the C2 and C3 conformers, made up of amino acids ε91‐98, ε102–108, and ε120–131 (Figure 4). Helices ε91–98 and ε102–108 wrap around γ's Rossman‐fold domain, thus, being in proximity to residue(s) γ155 and γV123, V133, A136 (C2; Figure S8A), respectively. Helix ε120‐131 turns by 98° (C2) or 75° (C3) relative to the helices ε102‐108 and comes in neighborhood of amino acids γI93, K97, V100, K104, Q107 (C2; Figures S8A and 4). This turn may be enabled by the flexible stretch of residues ε109–119, providing the structural traits for the ε transition states between an εe and εc. Most of the γ residues being in proximity to the three C‐terminal helices of ε are A. baumannii specific and not present in the human subunit counterpart (Figure S8B).

The comparison of the εCTD in the εC2 and εC3 structures underscores that helix ε91‐98 of εC3 extends higher toward the catalytic head than that of εC2 (Figure S9). Meanwhile, the εC3 helix ε102‐108 alters its axis by 22° relative to that of εC2. Both differences allow helix ε120‐131 of εC3 to extend further to the left of the subunit γ Rossman‐fold as compared to εC2.

Movements of the rotary subunit γ could be observed when comparing the AbF1‐αβγεΔ134–139 structures of conformers C1–C3 (Figure 5). When aligned to the C‐terminal helix of γ within the C1 (γC1), the N‐terminal helix of γC2 (C2 structure), and γC3 (C3 structure) move by 8° and 10° relative to the one of γC1 (Figure 5A). In addition, the C‐terminal helix of γC2 and γC3 move by 8° and 18° relative to γC1, respectively (Figure 5A). In concert with this, the γ‐foot domain, consisting of the loop residues γL188–E213, twists in γC2 (12°) and γC3 (25°) (Figure 5BI and Movie 2) relative to one in the γC1 conformation. Interestingly, the amino acids γN201–E213 of this loop form the major interactions with the c‐ring loop residues c40‐43 in the AbF‐ATP synthase, 6 indicating that the 12° and 25° twists of the γL188–E213‐loop in γC2‐ and γC3 would transduce a stepwise movement in the c‐ring turbine during catalysis (Figure 5CI). Importantly, the large twisting observed in the foot domain of γC2‐ and γC3 also affects the placement of the N‐terminal β‐barrel domain of εC2 and εC3 within the enzyme (Figure 5BIII,CIII and Movie 2), highlighting a concerted action of the two coupling subunits γ and ε.

Changes in the catalytic sites of states C1, C2, and C3

From an ATP hydrolysis perspective of the AbF1‐αßγεΔ134–139, the observed movements and secondary structural changes in the stalk subunits should be accompanied by changes in nucleotide occupation and changes in the catalytic sites of subunit β. As shown in Figure S6B,C, the catalytic βE and βDP subunits were empty and βTP has MgADP bound in the C2 and C3 conformers, when compared to the empty βE‐ (empty) and βDP‐ (Pi) and βTP (MgADP) site in C1 with subunit ε being extended (Figure S3A). The βE‐site in C2 (C2‐βE) can be assigned by its βE‐catch loop (residues βD307‐D310) electrostatic interactions with the γ‐tether residues γN268, γN272 (Figure S10A), which is an important feature in the rotary motor. 8 The C‐terminal 377DIIAILGMDE386‐loop (homolog to the E. coli DELSEED motif) in C2‐βE, which contributes to the angular velocity of rotary subunit γ, 8 , 9 moves by 8° and 36° in C2‐βDP and C2‐βTP, respectively (Figures 6 and S10A,B). Similarly, a movement of the phosphate‐binding loop (P‐loop; residues 147–154) in C2‐βE relative to C2‐βDP (6.2 Å) and C2‐βTP (9.9 Å) can be observed (Figure S10B). Importantly, helix βT182–S195 in βE, which is close to the P‐loop, becomes unstructured between residues βT182–D187 in βDP and βTP in C2 and C3 (Figure S11B,C). In comparison, helix βT182‐S195 is structured in all three β subunits of C1 (Figure S11A). This difference may cause a movement of the βE180‐R181 loop in C2 and C3, in which βE180 orients away from the catalytic site and βR181 moves toward the left of the P‐loop, thereby “pushing” a hydrolyzed product out of the catalytic site or “pulling” a new ATP in for hydrolysis.

Another striking difference in C1‐βDP compared to its counterpart in C2‐βDP and C3‐βDP is the downward movement of the β‐catch loop (Figure 7). This disrupts the connection between amino acid βD307 with γK5 and consequently the interaction with the rotary γ subunit (Figure 7). Furthermore, the C‐terminal 377DIIAILGMDE386‐loop in C2‐βDP moves by 9° in comparison to C1‐βDP (Figure 7A). The loop movement in C3‐βDP is even larger (13°) when compared to C1‐βDP (Figure 7B). Specific features of state C4

The cryo‐EM structure of state C4 was resolved at an overall resolution of 2.89 Å (Figures S1, S12, and S13) and contains the amino acids: αE, αTP, and αDP 26 to 514; βE, βTP, and βDP 2 to 464; γ 2 to 289; ε 2 to 89. Here, βE (C4‐βE), βDP (C4‐βDP), and βTP (C4‐βTP) were empty, occupied with MgADP and MgADP·Pi, respectively (Figure S3D). The different nucleotide occupancy in the three sites is reflected by large movements of the P‐ and the 377DIIAILGMDE386‐loop of C4‐βDP and C4‐ßTP relative to C4‐βE (Figure 6A). The comparison of the three βE, βDP, and βTP sites within one state as well as all the four states (C1–C4) in Figure 6A underscores the different movements of these mechanistically important loops and how nucleotide‐ and Pi‐binding alters the position of these loop elements to transfer the information to the rotary and coupling subunits γ and ε. Finally, the wide variation in subunit β conformations and nucleotide occupancy proves the activity of the enzyme and the different conformational states trapped in these four structures. When the C1‐βDP and C4‐βDP states are aligned at the globular domain (Figure 6B), the β‐catch loop of both states is similar in shape and position with deviation at the P‐loop and an 18° upwards movement of the 377DIIAILGMDE386‐loop in C4. At C1‐βDP an “opening” in the P‐loop conformation could observed, to accommodate for its interaction with a possibly “leaving” Pi, while the P‐loop of C4‐βDP takes a more regular “loose” conformation to interact with its MgADP (Figure 6B). Interestingly, βT182–S195 in C4‐βTP, C4‐βDP, and C4‐βE form an entire α‐helix (Figure S11D) comparable to the counterparts in C1 and different to the states of C2 and C3 (Figure S11B,C). Accompanying this difference are the changes in the βE180‐R181‐loop and the hinge region of C4 (βG148–V178), in which both features of βDP closely resemble βTP instead of taking an intermediate form like in C1.

The structural alterations of the catalytic β subunits of C4 are connected to movements in the rotary γC4 compared to γC1 where the N‐ and C‐terminal helix of γC4 move by 4° relative to the ones in γC1 (Figure S14A). These movements go along with a 10° rotation of the γC4 foot domain (loop γL188–E213) compared to the respective domain in γC1 (Figure S14B).

Subunit ε in C4 (εC4) was resolved at around 5 Å. The ε structure of AbF1‐ATPase WT (PDB 7YRY 7 ) was used for rigid‐body fitting into the map, enabling the resolution of the NTD, while the CTD could not be resolved, probably due to high flexibility of this domain (Figures S12 and S13). Superimposition of the NTD of εC1 and εC4 shows a 4° movement of the latter, in line with the movements seen for the γC4 foot domain (Figure S14B).

DISCUSSION

As a nonfermenting and strictly aerobic pathogen, A. baumannii relies on the oxidative phosphorylation pathway, including the electron transport chain (ETC) and the energy converter F‐ATP synthase to generate the main currency of life, ATP. A prerequisite for ATP synthesis is (i) an ATP/ADP homeostasis since both nucleotides are regulators in glycolysis and the citric acids cycle providing the electrons for the ETC, as well as (ii) the pmf. The latter is in part regulated by ATP‐hydrolysis driven proton‐pumping of the F‐ATP synthase, which goes in line with a controlled use of ATP cleavage, to prevent waste of ATP within the pathogen. Abεe with its C‐terminal residues 134–139 has been visualized to move into an ɑTP:βTP:γ interface 6 , 7 and shown to form the essential segment to control ATP hydrolysis inhibition. 7 Removal of these amino acids in the AbF1‐αßγεΔ134–139 variant triggers the enzyme into an ATPase active machine, where the β‐catch‐, P‐loop, and lever domain (377DIIAILGMDE386‐loop) of state C1 move when compared to the transition states C2 and C3, disrupting the connection with the rotary γ subunit (Figure 7A,B). Similarly, the close proximities of Abε amino acids pair εK99–γS145 (4.4 Å), εE102–γA143 (4.5 Å), εD113–γR245 (3.5 Å), εE123–γS13 (4.2 Å), and εE123–γT17 (4.1 Å), respectively, which kept the long α1‐helix (ε91–125) in the extended position (Figure S15 and Movie 1) are lost. Like elastic tent poles, we propose that the extended Abε α1‐helix (ε91–125) becomes split into smaller entities to leave the ɑ:β:γ interface and to transform into states C2 and C3 with the three smaller α‐helices ε91–98, ε102–108, and ε120–131 (Figures 4 and 8, Movie 1). These three helices come in proximity to subunit γ's globular domain with differences in position and orientation between states C2 and C3, respectively (Figure S9). The flexible stretch (ε109–119) may provide the necessary flexibility to allow movement(s) of helix ε120–131 along subunit γ between the transition states C2–C3. The interaction of the three Abε helices may not only allow a “climbing” downward of the CTD from an Abεe to an Abεc but also provide the force for the structural transformation from a long α1‐helix (ε91–125) into three smaller ones. Secondary structural transformation by interaction of the rotary γ subunit has been described for the randomly structured C‐terminus of the mycobacterial α subunit, forming a β‐sheet after binding to the globular γ‐domain and preventing the engine from rotation and ATP hydrolysis. 11 , 24 To reach the final compact form, the small helices have to alter into the two larger helices α1 (εE91–N107) and α2 (εD111–N136; Figure 8A and Movie 1). 7 These new structural features are stabilized by the interactions of helix α2 with the NTD residues εP47, εG48, εP49, εI61, εV62, εY63, εA86, and εL89. 7 In particular, residues εT130 and εY63 have been demonstrated to play a critical role in forming and stabilizing the structural elements of Abεc 7 and underscores the necessity of ε's CTD interactions at the α:β:γ interface, γ's globular domain, and the NTD‐CTD interface to allow structural transformations, which are prerequisites for the transition from an Abεe to an Abεc.

Abε's movements and structural alterations form the C1‐ to the C2‐C3 transition states, go in concert with distinct movements of γ's N‐ and C‐terminal helices and a twisting of its foot domain relative to the c‐ring, enabling coupling of alterations in the α3:β3 catalytic head due to ATP cleavage to the bottom of the central stalk, being the coupler to the ATP‐driven proton‐translocating c‐ring (Figure 5). This large γ‐foot twisting is combined with ε's NTD movement within the enzyme, enabling an orchestral structural transformation of ATP‐driven hydrolysis to proton‐translocating via the c‐ring (Figure 5C) and finally bringing ε's helix α2 close to the NTD to stabilize their interaction and conformation.

In the canon of epitope–epitope interactions for secondary structural alterations and stabilization of ε's CTD is the resolved state C4, where the εCTD is unresolved (Figure S13). The lack of εCTD density could come from the high structural and dynamic flexibility of transitions between different conformational states, where no epitope–epitope interactions occur, and secondary structural alterations are just in transition.

The trapped conformational states (C1–C4) described above also shine light on the proposed transition states of the regulatory ATP hydrolysis subunit ε in the Escherichia coli F1‐ATPase/F‐ATP synthase 18 , 25 , 26 and the G. stearothermophilus F1‐ATPase. 26 While the overall structural elements of the εc form of the AbF1‐ATPase, EcF1‐ATPase, 27 , 28 and GsF1‐ATPase 26 , 29 are similar as shown in Figure 8B, the CTD of εe differs significantly among the three enzymes with different helical content, length, and positioning within the ɑ:β:γ‐complex (Figure 8B). In particular, the Abε C‐terminal residues 134–139 which are critical for latent ATP hydrolysis of the pathogenic enzyme (0.2 ± 0.01 μmol min−1[mg of protein]−1) 7 move further up into the αTP:βTP:γ interface when compared to the Ecε/Gsε C‐terminal element of the non‐latent EcF1‐ATPase (10 ± 0.5 units/mg [μmol of ATP hydrolyzed/min/mg of enzyme] 30 ) or GsF1‐ATPase (10 ± 0.5 μmol min−1[mg of protein]−1units/mg [μmol of ATP hydrolyzed/min/mg of enzyme] 31 ). So far, no intermediate positions between the εc 26 and ‐εe state 27 within the GsF1‐ATPase have been described. In comparison, an Ecε “half‐up position” of the helix 1 (aa ε86–103) has been resolved by the EcF‐ATP synthase cryo‐EM structure in the presence of 10 mM MgATP, with the main C‐terminal residues ε104–138 lacking. 18 This Ecε helix 1 extends at a 60° angle from the plane of the membrane and binds to γ's globular domain (Figure 8C). This position has been proposed to stabilize the central stalk and increase the stiffness of the rotary element with the potential to impede enzyme turnover. 18 In addition, an intermediate εCTD conformation has been described by the crystallographic structure of the E. coli γ‐ε complex only and lacking the N‐ and C‐terminal helices of γ (residues 11–258 17 ). In this structure the two helices Ecα1 (ε86–103) and ‐α2 (ε113–133) have been determined, wrapping around γ and packed end‐to‐end at a right angle (Figure 8C). Ecα1 of the γ′‐ε complex superimposes well with the one of the “half‐up position” EcF‐ATP synthase‐ as well as with helix α1 of εe in the crystallographic EcF1‐ATPase structure (Figure 8C), indicating the importance of Ecα1 in maintaining the structure of the half‐up and extended states. To reach the final εe conformation, Ecα2 in the half‐up position has to transform into a longer loop (ε104–111), helices α2 (ε112–125), and α3 (ε129–134), and to reorient to allow helix α2 to interact with subunits γ and β, and helix α3 with the α:β:γ interface (Figure 8C). Looking into the direction of the compact Ecε, helices α1 (ε86–103) and ‐α2 (ε113–133) of the half‐up position have to reorient close to the NTD, to alter into longer helices (ε90–108 and ε111–136), to facilitate interactions with the NTD (Figure 8C).

The comparison of the half‐up state of Ecε (Figure 8C) with the C2 and C2 states of Abε (Figures 8A and S9) highlights not only the drastic differences of helical formations, numbers and orientations, it also underscores the need for different transitions and transformations of their CTD because of the drastic difference in helical formations, numbers and orientation of their extended ε's, which interact with different epitopes of the rotary subunit γ and α:β:γ interfaces (Figure 8A–C), which in case of the AbF1‐ATPase results in an ATPase latent enzyme. 7

Importantly, truncation studies of Abε 7 and Ecε 32 strongly suggest that ε's very C‐terminus is also essential for ATP formation, with a direct impact on cellular growth. A high pmf is proposed to trigger subunit ε into the compact conformation and increase ATP formation, while under unfavorable growth conditions, the CTD alters into the extended state, to reduce ATPase activity and maintain a moderate level of net ATP formation. 33 While Abε has no ATP‐binding site, 7 the ATP:ADP ratio under unfavorable and favorable physiological conditions in the cytoplasm, in which the F1‐domain is located, may contribute together with the pmf in regulating ε's up and down movements. The CTD transition state(s) of the Abε presented and the Ecε described, 17 , 18 have been studied in hydrolysis directions. Although the new transition states presented demonstrate the drastic difference in structures, orientations, and interactions between both species, they indicate that similar transition states may occur also in the synthesis direction, which follows the paradigm of secondary structural transformation and contact loss for the next transition, where interaction with the new epitope partner triggers a stable structural feature.

These novel transition states add not only to the intermediate positions and structural alterations of Abε and movements of ‐γ, but also provide insights into novel inhibitor/stimulator targets. As demonstrated for the Mycobacterium tuberculosis ε (Mtbε) and Mycobacterium abscessus ε (Mabε), understanding their atomic insights in particular to their shorter C‐terminal helices of the compact Mtbε and Mabε compared to their bacterial and human counterparts as well as their NTD‐CTD interfaces have paved the way for novel anti‐tuberculosis and anti‐nontuberculosis targets and compounds. 34 , 35 The interfaces of α‐helices ε91–98, ε102–108, and ε120–131 of the C2 and C3 transition states and the γ epitopes within the globular domain (Figure S8) have the potential to be used as a new compound target and the design of new hit molecules, which like “molecular glues” 36 could stabilize the structural features, locking this confirmation and directing the enzyme in an ATP hydrolysis wasting mode or in ATP synthesis direction to prevent ATP formation.

Taken together, we presented for the first time a detailed picture of the transition states and structures of the regulatory εCTD of the A. baumannii F1‐ATPase, which enable the transformation of an Abεe conformation and latent ATP hydrolysis mode, into an Abεc, to trigger the enzyme into an active form. Such translocation and structural transformation go in concert with interactions of Abε's CTD with subunit γ, movements of γ's N‐ and C‐terminal helices as well as a major twisting of γ′ foot‐ and ε's N‐terminal domain. Such synergistic movements and structural alterations connect ATP cleavage events in the α3:β3‐headpiece and proton transport in the FO‐domain. As discussed, such events may also couple proton pumping and ATP formation in the reverse synthesis direction. Because of the fundamental role in ATPase activity and ATP synthesis of the rotary γ‐ε pair, the resolved C2 and C3 states of the A. baumannii engine and the interacting sites of the three ε helices with γ provide a novel target for compounds, whose binding may disturb these interactions and stimulate ATPase activity, with the aim of ATP depletion and/or ATP synthesis inhibition to prevent bacterial growth. The high specificity of these epitopes to the class of A. baumannii would avoid obvious toxicity problems in the patient.
