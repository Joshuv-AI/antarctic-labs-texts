Antimicrobial resistance in the Americas: a tale of multiple realities
Authors: The Lancet Regional Health – Americas
Published: 2023

Over a century has passed since the first antibiotic, Salvarsan, was discovered in 1910, a few years before penicillin was introduced and revolutionised the field of medicine and health, increasing life expectancy by several decades. Subsequently, the 1940s–1960s, known as the golden age of antibiotics, saw a rapid expansion in the antimicrobial drug arsenal—many of which are still in use today. Frequently referred to as “the foundation of modern medicine”, the effectiveness of antimicrobials has been threatened by the emergence of multi-resistant microorganisms. Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) emerges naturally in microorganisms and confers reduced or loss of susceptibility to a given antimicrobial; however, increasing overuse and misuse of antibiotics are driving an accelerated growth in AMR and threatening the lives of millions. Global estimates indicate 1.27 million deaths were directly attributable to AMR in 2019, with 11.1% of these deaths occurring in the Region of the Americas, despite this region contributing to only 13% of the global population.

In this issue, the first regional profile of the AMR burden in the Americas, covering 35 countries in the region, reveals that, as of 2019, more than two in every five deaths that involved an infection in the Americas were associated with AMR. Infections represented the largest share of deaths in Haiti, where the highest age-standardised mortality rate associated with AMR (>90 per 100 000 person-years) was also observed, followed by Bolivia, Guatemala, Guyana, and Honduras. By contrast, in Canada, Colombia, Cuba, Jamaica, and the USA, less than 50 deaths per 100 000 person-years were reported. Heterogeneity throughout the Americas is reflected in infection burden, AMR-attributable deaths, and antibiotic resistance patterns as a direct consequence of differences in access to antibiotics and basic health-care services. AMR is not only responsible for invasive, hospital-acquired infections in at-risk populations (ie, severely ill, very young, and very old patients), but it can also threaten every-day procedures such as caesarean sections and urgent appendectomies, by exposing patients to a higher risk of resistant infections, especially in less resourceful settings.

The picture painted in this regional profile is one of contrasting realities and can be an important tool to guide the development of much needed National Action Plans (NAPs) against AMR. While reducing overall infections should be a priority for Haiti, in Canada—where infections represent a very small fraction of deaths—resistance surveillance should be prioritised, with a greater focus on rational antimicrobial use and control of resistance across sectors. Alarmingly, none of the ten countries with the highest age-standardised mortality rates associated with AMR had published an AMR NAP, while the four countries with the lowest AMR mortality rates—Chile, Colombia, Costa Rica, and the USA—had published and financed an AMR NAP on or before 2019.

Investing in the fight against AMR through a national tailored plan is a cost-effective approach as AMR threats not only humans but the entire interconnected triad of humans, animals, and the environment. The AMR crisis increases the cost of health care, with lengthier hospital stays and more intensive care required, impacts worker's productivity, and threats global trade and food security. If nothing changes, WHO projects AMR could have a global cost of US$100 trillion by 2050. In that direction, WHO published the first global research agenda last June, directed at scientists and outlining “the most urgent human health priorities to combat antimicrobial resistance”. Among the 40 outlined research topics, a clear One Health approach is suggested, and understanding the role of community antimicrobial use and waste management is an important goal. A pioneering study in Brazil, part of the CAMO-Net consortium of research, will investigate the impact of integrated community interventions in primary health care in improving antimicrobial use, community carriage, and wastewater contamination of AMR as drivers of resistance. This will be a major contribution to understanding the patterns of antimicrobial consumption in the country and could serve as a pilot to be replicated in other settings.

Antibiotic overuse and misuse are key elements that must be carefully addressed to control AMR spread. Sales of unprescribed antibiotics are still a major issue in many Latin American countries, despite stricter regulations prohibiting over-the-counter sales and retention of prescriptions in some instances and places. This practice harms not only the patient who does not receive ideal care but also contributes to AMR development and compromises the ability to monitor antibiotic consumption. Training of both health-care workers and community workers on the detrimental implications of overprescriptions and the value of educating the lay population on the importance of adherence to and completeness of treatment can be powerful approaches to reduce community spread of AMR and ensure surveillance continuity.

The rise in AMR is not a new threat. Almost 10 years ago WHO launched a collaborative effort, the Global Antimicrobial Resistance and Use Surveillance System (GLASS), with a set of recommended routines to identify, control, and report antimicrobial resistance and consumption (AMC). Although considered a great advance, as of 2023 only seven countries from the Americas contribute data to the GLASS AMR dashboard, and only Canada, Colombia, and Peru contribute to AMC data. Tracking the use of antimicrobials with locally functional antimicrobial stewardship programmes that monitor resistance across sectors is key to keep AMR under control; however, for most countries in this region, this is still a distant reality. The growing movement calling for stronger commitments from individual countries to contribute to this fight is auspicious but will require multisectoral compromises. As a scientific journal, we will continue to contribute to this cause by publishing the best available evidence on regional progress and supporting local scientific communities to push forward the agenda to combat AMR.
