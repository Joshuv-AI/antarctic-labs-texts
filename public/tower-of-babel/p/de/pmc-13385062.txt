Dyslipidemia-Induced Mitochondrial Dysfunctions in the Brains Does Not Reach Pathological Levels in the ApoE-Knockout Mice
Authors: Chun-Hsien Hsu, Pei-Wen Chu, Yi-Ying Chen, Min-Hao Ho, En-Chih Liao, Shau-Kwaun Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough dyslipidemia and lipid accumulation are established risk factors for numerous neurological diseases, including stroke and neurodegenerative disorders, whether dyslipidemia directly causes neuronal death or acts as a secondary factor remains debatable. To answer this question, ApoE-knockout is a more suitable model than ApoE4 mutants to study dyslipidemia because the E4 allele manifests an isoform-specific structural conformation that produces allele-specific effects. In this study, we examined neurological phenotype and mitochondrial and metabolic alterations in ApoE-knockout mice, which exhibited elevated serum cholesterol and triglyceride levels from an early age. These mutant mice exhibited mild cognitive phenotypes, suggesting that the functions of the cerebral cortex were affected by lipid dysregulation. Decreased electron transport chain complex IV activity indicated compromised mitochondrial function in 1-year-old mutant mice. Increased oxidative stress in cortical tissues, and downregulated expression of the key antioxidative genes indicated increased oxidative stress and mitochondrial damage in the mutant mice. Decreased mitochondrial mass was also observed, possibly due to the increase of mitophagy. However, no extensive cell death or significant reduction in cortical neuronal count was detected although the neurites degenerated in 1-year-old mutant mice. Upregulation of the Pgc1a gene, a master regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis, suggested the presence of protective mechanisms in the brain. Collectively, these findings, together with the phenotypes developed in Ldlr−/− mutant mice, suggest that hyperlipidemia alone may be insufficient to induce significant neurodegeneration. There should be additional factors that play a crucial role in the pathogenesis of these diseases.Keywords: Dyslipidemia, Neurodegeneration, ApoE, Mitochondria

Introduction

Lipids typically comprise 50% to 60% of the brain’s dry weight and are essential for neurophysiology, structural integrity, energy metabolism, and signaling. They are also essential for forming myelin sheaths, insulating neurons for rapid nerve impulse conduction, and facilitating synaptic plasticity [1–3]. Impaired lipid metabolism in the brain induces lipid accumulation and lipotoxic metabolite production, which may trigger central nervous system diseases and injuries [4, 5]. Clinical studies have identified dyslipidemia as a major risk factor for several neurological diseases, including ischemic stroke, vascular dementia, and Alzheimer’s disease, and potentially exacerbating demyelinating conditions, such as multiple sclerosis [6–8]. Dyslipidemia may lead to neurodegeneration through various mechanisms, such as chronic neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, impaired blood–brain barrier integrity, and the accumulation of toxic protein aggregates, such as β-amyloid and α-synuclein [9, 10].

However, there are some contradictory evidences against the notion that dyslipidemia causes neurodegeneration and neuronal death. High cholesterol levels in middle-aged adults have been linked to a higher dementia risk, but some studies have observed no such association in older adults or even a protective effect [11, 12]. Although elevated low-density lipoprotein cholesterol is a known risk factor for stroke and related brain injury, very low levels of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (< 25–70 mg/dL) or total cholesterol (< 160 mg/dL) have been associated with potential neurologic toxicity and a higher risk of hemorrhagic stroke or dementia [13]. Statins are widely used to treat dyslipidemia, but evidence is conflicting regarding whether these cholesterol-lowering drugs increase the risk of cognitive decline (neurotoxic) or protect against it (neuroprotective), with inconsistent findings reported by studies on dementia, Alzheimer’s disease, and Parkinson’s disease [14, 15]. Another notable inconsistency in the literature pertains to the association between genetic predisposition to dyslipidemia and dementia risk. Some studies have observed no increase in the risk of dementia in individuals with familial hypercholesterolemia [7, 16].

Findings from rodent models also remain inconclusive. The low-density lipoprotein receptor–knockout (Ldlr–/–) mouse model exhibits various neurological phenotypes primarily driven by chronic hypercholesterolemia, systemic inflammation, and impaired cholesterol transport in the brain [17, 18]. Ldlr–/– mice manifest substantial cognitive and behavioral impairments, including age-related deterioration of spatial and working memory, due to synaptic loss and reduced neurogenesis in the brain. Despite apoptotic pathway upregulation, no structural alterations or shrinkage resulting from massive neuronal death has been observed in this mutant mouse model [19]. In summary, Ldlr–/– mice exhibit certain neurological phenotypes but do not closely recapitulate the features of the most prevalent neurodegenerative diseases.

ApoE encodes an apolipoprotein, a class of proteins that bind and transport lipids in the circulation and are crucial for maintaining optimal lipid metabolism [20, 21]. The ApoE protein is predominantly expressed in the liver, which plays a pivotal role in cholesterol and lipid homeostasis; in the brain, ApoE gene is primarily expressed in astrocytes [22]. Under stress or pathological conditions, however, neuronal ApoE expression is markedly increased. This neuron-derived ApoE is widely regarded as a component of a survival mechanism that repairs damaged neurons, remodels axons, and modulates neuronal metabolism [23]. ApoE is polymorphic, exhibiting three major alleles: E2, E3, and E4 [24]. ApoE4 is the strongest genetic risk factor for sporadic, late-onset Alzheimer’s disease and is associated with neurodegeneration, neuronal damage, and tau phosphorylation [25]. ApoE4 targeted-replacement or humanized ApoE4 mouse models typically manifest neurological, behavioral, and molecular phenotypes that recapitulate the early stages of Alzheimer’s-like pathology, often without substantial plaque or tangle formation. A hallmark of the ApoE4 mouse phenotype is reduced neuronal complexity, characterized by fewer, shorter, and less branched dendrites. This includes a major reduction in dendritic spine density in the hippocampus (specifically the dentate gyrus and CA1 regions) and the entorhinal cortex [26–28]. Some of these phenotypes are believed to arise from an allele-specific conformation of ApoE4: a unique arginine at position 112 (cysteine in the other two isoforms) shifts the hinge region of the protein, enabling the N-terminal domain (residues 1–167) to directly interact with the C-terminal domain (residues 206–299) [29]. This conformation plays a crucial role in the phenotype of ApoE mutant mice [30]. Therefore, the phenotype exhibited by these transgenic mice may not accurately reflect that of individuals with dyslipidemia. Conversely, ApoE deficiency disrupts lipid homeostasis, resulting in elevated cholesterol and triglyceride levels in plasma from early adulthood. Mice homozygous for ApoE loss-of-function alleles develop atherosclerosis and have been used as a model of cardiovascular disease due to lipid dysregulation [31]. This mutant mouse model is therefore better suited for our research purposes, and is employed in this research to study the effects of dyslipidemia on brain functions.

Materials and Methods

Animal Study

ApoE-knockout mice (ApoE knockout: RMRC13302, C57BL/6-Apoeem1Narl/Narl (https://www.ncb.niar.org.tw/RMRC/webc/html/data/show.aspx?ix=1&page=1&strain=apoE)) and C57BL/6 controls (C57BL/6 control: RMRC11005, C57BL/6JNarl (https://www.ncb.niar.org.tw/p3_animal2_detail.asp?cid=1&nid=3&ppage=0&type=1) were obtained from the National Center for Biomodels, National Institutes of Applied Research, Taiwan. These mice were maintained at 23 ± 2 °C on a 12-h light–dark cycle, with a standard diet and water provided ad libitum. After the behavioral test, the mice were killed, and their brain tissue was collected for the evaluation of gene expression profiles and enzyme activity.

For blood collection, the mice were humanely restrained to expose the cheek, and a blood collection needle was inserted vertically at the intersection of the outer canthus of the eye and the corner of the mouth. Blood samples were subjected to centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 10 min, and the clear, pale-yellow serum layer was collected for subsequent analysis. Serum total cholesterol (T-CHO) and triglyceride (TG) levels were determined using a Fuji DRI-CHEM 4000 dry chemistry analyzer.

Behavioral Test

To conduct an open-field test, mice were transported to the designated testing area in their standard home cages and acclimated for ≥ 2 h. The open-field arena (a 60 cm x 60 cm x 60 cm box) was meticulously sanitized with 75% ethyl alcohol and allowed to dry thoroughly between tests. To assess habituation to a novel environment, each mouse was placed in the corner of the arena and allowed to explore for 10 min. The total distance traveled in the entire field was recorded using a SMART Video Tracking System and software (Panlab, Barcelona, Spain).

A Y-maze test was conducted using a black opaque plastic Y-shaped apparatus with three identical arms (35 cm long, 5 cm wide, and 15 cm high walls) positioned at 120° angles; each mouse was placed at the center of the Y-maze facing a random corner (between arms) and allowed to explore freely for 8 min to assess spontaneous alterations, with arm entries recorded to evaluate spatial working memory.

Ethical Considerations

All animal experiments were conducted in strict accordance with the regulations of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines. Every effort was made to minimize both animal suffering and the number of mice used.

Immunohistochemistry

Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and perfused with normal saline followed by a fixative solution (4% paraformaldehyde in 1× phosphate-buffered saline [PBS]). Brains were postfixed by immersion in the same fixative solution and then transferred to 30% sucrose for cryoprotection. The processed brains were then embedded in 2% gelatin and sectioned at 20 μm. These sections were washed three times with 1% PBS for 5 min each, soaked in 0.2% Triton-X for 1 h to permeabilize the cell membranes, and washed again three times for 5 min each. Primary and secondary antibodies were diluted in CytoQ Immuno Diluant & Block (Innovex Biosciences). The sections were incubated with primary antibodies (mouse anti-NeuN, 1:1000, MAB 377; Sigma-Aldrich/Merck & rabbit anti-MAP2, 1:400, A22205, ABclonal) for 12–16 h and then with a secondary antibody (goat anti-mouse IgG, Alexa Fluor Plus 488, 1:500; Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 2 h. Finally, the sections were mounted with VECTASHIELD Mounting Medium with DAPI (Vector).

β-Secretase Activity Assay

β-Secretase activity in murine hippocampal samples was determined using a β-Secretase Activity Kit (KA0900; Abnova, Taiwan). This assay is based on the cleavage of a secretase-specific peptide conjugated to the fluorescent reporter molecules EDANS and DABCYL (nonfluorescent), which releases a fluorescent signal. Briefly, hippocampal tissue was homogenized in an ice-cold extraction buffer and incubated on ice for 10 min. After centrifugation, the resultant mixture was thoroughly combined with the substrate and incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 1 h. Finally, the fluorescent signal was detected with a microplate reader at an excitation/emission wavelength of 335/495 nm, and the degree of secretase enzymatic activity was found to be proportional to signal intensity.

In Vitro Detection of Reactive Oxygen and Nitrogen Species

Tissue reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reactive nitrogen species (RNS) levels were measured using an OxiSelect In Vitro ROS/RNS Assay Kit (green fluorescence; Cell Biolabs, cat. STA-347). Dissected brain tissues were homogenized, cleared of debris by centrifugation at 5000 ×g for 5 min, and brought to 50 mg/mL with PBS. Subsequently, a 50-µL aliquot of the tissue sample was combined with 50 µL of the provided catalyst to accelerate the oxidation reaction. After 5 min of incubation at room temperature, 100 µL of DCFHDiOxyQ probe solution was added to the mixture to measure the total free radical population, particularly because the DCFH probe reacts with the free radical molecules of both ROS and RNS. The samples were then incubated at the ambient temperature for 30 min and read on a fluorescence plate reader at an excitation/emission wavelength of 480/530 nm. The standard curve of H2O2 was used to quantify free radical content in cell lysates.

Mitochondrial DNA to Nuclear DNA Ratio

Mitochondrial and nuclear DNA was extracted from the cerebral cortical tissue of the experimental mice. The collected tissues were homogenized, and lysis buffer (Tris 10 mM, NaCl 100 mM, EDTA 10 mM, and SDS 0.5%) and proteinase K (0.2 mg/mL; Thermo Fisher Scientific) were added at 55 °C for 3 h, followed by RNase A (100 µg/mL; Thermo Fisher Scientific) treatment at 37 °C for 30 min. Total DNA was precipitated by the combination of ammonium acetate and isopropanol and centrifuged at 15,000 ×g for 10 min at 4 °C. The pellets were then thoroughly washed with 70% ethanol dissolved in Tris-EDTA buffer. Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) was quantified using real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) based on the fluorescent dye SYBR Green, performed on an ABI 7300 thermal cycler (Applied Biosystems, USA). Alterations in the relative quantity of mtDNA in relation to nuclear DNA (2 N) were expressed as a ratio between the number of copies of the mitochondrial ND1 gene and that of the 16 S rRNA gene of nuclear DNA. The primer sequences are listed in the following section. Data were analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCT method [32].

Results

ApoE-Knockout Mutant Mice Exhibited Defective Cholesterol Homeostasis

The metabolic phenotypes of the mice were confirmed at various stages of life. As with other apolipoprotein loss-of-function mutant mice, ApoE-knockout mice exhibited early-onset hyperlipidemia and hypercholesterolemia from 12 weeks of age (Fig. 1a). Similar to the metabolic phenotype of Ldlr−/− mice, the serum triglyceride and cholesterol levels remained constant throughout the experimental period (Fig. 1b). This early-onset metabolic defect makes the ApoE-knockout mouse model suitable for examining the effects of hypercholesterolemia and hyperlipidemia at different stages.

Blood samples were collected through facial vein bleeding. Serum was separated using centrifugation, and total cholesterol and triglyceride levels were measured using a Fuji DRI-CHEM 4000 dry chemistry analyzer. Data are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) for n = 6 mice per group in ApoE-knockout and WT control groups. ***p ≤ 0.001 and ****p ≤ 0.0001 versus control

Cognitive Defects were Observed in ApoE-Knockout Mutant Mice

To determine the physiological impact of disrupted lipid homeostasis on brain functions, open-field and Y-maze tests were used to evaluate locomotor activity and working memory/cognitive functions, respectively, in ApoE-knockout mice. The cohorts comprised adult mice (aged 3–4 months) and aged mice (aged 12–14 months). In the open-field test, mutant mice exhibited no alterations in motor ability or locomotor activity at an early age (Fig. 2a), although at the time of testing they had already exhibited the metabolic phenotype. The motor ability and locomotor activity of the aged mice, irrespective of their genotype, exhibited a decline. However, the disruption in lipid metabolism had no substantial impact on these parameters. By contrast, the aged mutant mice exhibited a reduced alternative triplicate in the Y-maze, suggesting that prolonged hyperlipidemia led to impaired working memory and cognitive defects (Fig. 2b).

Cognitive defects were developed in ApoE-knockout mice. Behavioral characterization of younger and aged ApoE-knockout mice. Analysis of performance in the open-field test (a), assessed using total distance traveled as an indicator of motor ability and general locomotor activity, revealed no significant differences between younger mice (3–4 months of age). Similarly, cognitive and exploratory behavior in the Y-maze test (b), quantified as the percentage of spontaneous alteration triplets, remained comparable between groups at this younger age. However, in aged mice (12–14 months of age), those with ApoE knockout exhibited a significant decrease in the percentage of Y-maze triplets compared, indicating impaired spatial working memory. Data are expressed as mean ± SEM (n = 8). Statistical significance was determined using Student’s t test. *p ≤ 0.05 versus control

Altered Lipid Metabolism Did Not Affect Secretase Activity

One of the most prominent phenotypes of ApoE4 transgenic mutant mice involves the rapid development of amyloid plaques, which is a hallmark of Alzheimer’s disease. These mutant mice exhibit altered brain metabolism, which results in the accumulation of aggregated proteins, including the formation of β-amyloid. ApoE4 mice generally exhibit increased amyloidogenic processes as a result of elevated β-secretase (BACE1) and δ-secretase activity [33]. We therefore examined β-secretase activity in the cerebral cortex. Figure 3 demonstrates that the cortical β-secretase activity of the mutant mice was comparable to that of the control mice at different stages. The results also indicated differences between the ApoE4 mutant and ApoE-knockout mice. Amyloid plaque formation was not manifested by ApoE gene loss of function or by long-term dyslipidemia. Additionally, the cognitive defects observed in these metabolic disorder models were not associated with the dysregulation of secretase activity or the formation of Aβ.

Assays were conducted using a commercially available fluorometric assay kit in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions, and the resulting fluorescent signals were normalized to the total protein content of each sample. No significant differences in enzyme activity were observed in either the 6–8-month-old group or the 12–14-month-old group. Data are expressed as mean ± SEM (n = 4–7). No Major Neuronal Death was Detected in Aged Knockout Mouse Brains

We examined the potential consequences of prolonged exposure to elevated levels of cholesterol or triglycerides on cerebral health. At the tissue level, histochemical analysis revealed no detectable differences in brain size or gross anatomical organization between genotypes. Given the observed impact of mutations on the working memory of the mutant mice, we examined the cortical neuronal counts by calculating the number of NeuN-positive cells. Cortical neuronal count was similar in the mutant and control groups (Fig. 4a and b). The neurites were also labeled with MAP2 antibody. The intensity of MAP2 labeling was significantly decreased in ApoE-knockout mice (Fig. 4c and d), indicating that the neurites degenerated under dyslipidemia conditions. Furthermore, the expression levels of proapoptotic genes Bax and Bak were not increased, suggesting the absence of neuronal death (Fig. 4e and f). Together, these findings indicate that prolonged hypercholesterolemia and hyperlipidemia do not result in major cell death in the brain. Together with the results of the secretase analysis (Fig. 3), these findings suggest that the Alzheimer’s-like phenotype of ApoE4 mutant mice is likely allele-specific and is not attributable to ApoE loss of function.

Electron Transport Chain Activity was Decreased in Knockout Mouse Brains

Although no major cell death was observed, as evidenced by the absence of substantial increases in ApoE knockout, further analysis was conducted to assess other potential alterations in cellular metabolism. Cholesterol has been reported to integrate into cell membranes and alter membrane fluidity. This process influences biochemical reactions occurring on plasma membranes and in organelles, including mitochondria. Elevated free cholesterol levels influence the properties of mitochondria in cultured cells [34, 35]. To monitor mitochondrial function, we measured the activity of the electron transport chain, which is located on the inner mitochondrial membrane and is responsible for oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and ATP production. We observed that the activity of the electron transport chain complex IV was reduced by 15% in the mutant mouse brains (Fig. 5), suggesting that cholesterol also integrates into the mitochondrial membrane and influences its functions.

Activity was determined spectrophotometrically and normalized to total mitochondrial protein content to control for variations in mitochondrial mass. Data are presented as mean ± SEM (n = 6). *p ≤ 0.05 versus control. Statistical significance was determined using Student’s t test

The Functions of Antioxidant Enzymes were Impaired in Aged Knockout Mouse Brains

Elevated cholesterol levels can impede mitochondrial functionality, and prolonged exposure to conditions characterized by high cholesterol levels may result in mitochondrial dysfunction. Conversely, oxidative stress and mitochondrial damage have been identified as pivotal factors in the development of neurodegenerative disorders. In the present study, we evaluated oxidative stress and antioxidant enzyme function in brain tissues. ROS/RNS levels in mouse cerebral cortical tissues were determined using a specific fluorogenic probe, namely 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein (DCF) [36]. Cerebral cortex tissues were isolated and homogenized, followed by the addition of a DCF probe. The DCF readout of the samples was used as an indicator of the total reactive species levels (ROS/NOS) present in the tissues. We observed that the DCF values were increased in the aged mutant mice’s cortex, indicating elevated oxidative stress (Fig. 6a). This oxidative stress may be attributable to either increased reactive species generation or compromised mitochondrial antioxidant capacity. Given that OXPHOS, a major source of oxidative stress, exhibited a decline in the mutant mice, we hypothesized that the mitochondrial antioxidant enzymes were downregulated. Analysis of the expression levels of antioxidants in the mitochondria revealed that all the enzymes tested, including SOD1, GPx4, and catalase, were transcriptionally downregulated (Fig. 6b and d). Taken together, these findings indicate that hypercholesterolemia can impede mitochondrial antioxidant systems, thereby contributing to oxidative stress.

Mitochondrial Biogenesis was Altered in Knockout Mice

Our results indicated the presence of oxidative stress in the cerebral cortex of the mutant mice. We therefore examined whether the mitochondrial mass altered by calculating the ratio of mtDNA to nuclear DNA through quantitative PCR. We observed a 20% reduction in mitochondrial mass in the mutant mice’s cerebral cortex (Fig. 7a). Next, we examined how mitochondrial biogenesis changes in response to these alterations. The upregulation of Pgc1a and Nrf2 genes, both of which are crucial transcription factors for mitochondrial biogenesis (Fig. 7b and c) was observed, indicating enhanced mitochondrial biogenesis. We also noted that the expression levels of other fission and fusion genes, Drp1/Fis1 for fission and Mfn1/Opa1 for fusion, exhibited no substantial differences between the mutant and control groups (Fig. 7d and g), showing that the equilibrium between mitochondrial fission and fusion remained unaltered under hyperlipidemic conditions.

Discussions

Since dyslipidemia is a risk factor for degenerative diseases, several neurological phenotypes have been examined in rodent models with defective lipid metabolism. In ApoE4 transgenic mice, lipid metabolism phenotypes develop primarily because of the widespread dysregulation of lipid transport and homeostasis, resulting in the accumulation of lipids, specifically triglycerides and cholesterol, in glial cells, particularly in the brain [37]. In contrast to other major alleles, such as E2 and E3, the unique arginine residue of the E4 allele produces a new domain interaction property [29]. This leads to Aβ aggregation and an extraordinary neurological phenotype that is not solely attributable to dyslipidemia. This hypothesis is substantiated by the phenotype observed in ApoE-knockout mutant mice, which exhibit dyslipidemia from early adulthood. Notably, ApoE-knockout mice exhibit substantially different neurological phenotypes to those of ApoE4 mice. The most prominent differences are altered secretase activity and increased protein aggregation, as well as enhanced cell death, which can only be detected in ApoE4 mutant mice. In addition, the cognitive and behavioral defects that develop during the early stages of the disease are more severe in ApoE4 mutant mice than in ApoE-knockout mice.

Certain neurological phenotypes have also been characterized in Ldlr–/– mice, a mouse model of familial dyslipidemia. These mutant mice exhibit defective spatial learning and memory, impaired neurogenesis, increased neuroinflammation, and enhanced susceptibility to stroke-induced brain injury. Similar to the phenotypes observed in ApoE-knockout mutant mice, no protein aggregation or major cell death was observed in the other two mouse models [18, 19]. However, brain mitochondrial function has not been evaluated in Ldlr–/– mice. Comparison of the phenotypes observed in all three mutant mouse models with dyslipidemia suggested that dyslipidemia induces metabolic defects and mitochondrial dysfunction in the brain, which in turn trigger neuroinflammation, synaptic loss, and cognitive defects. However, these effects did not attain pathological levels, possibly because of innate neuroprotective mechanisms.

In this study, we observed that the activity of the electron transport chain complex IV was decreased in the homogenized cortical tissues of the mutant mice, suggesting that this electron transport chain complex was impaired. A few possibilities can explain this phenomenon. Mitochondrial membrane damage seems not to be the primary cause since intrinsic apoptotic pathways (Bax/Bak) were not upregulated in ApoE knockouts. Another possibility is membrane property alterations attributable to the accumulation of cholesterol and fatty acids, causing changes in electron transport chain activity. Because of their physical properties, alterations in plasma lipid species may directly influence cellular characteristics and functions. Cholesterol can be integrated into the membrane and alter membrane fluidity. Preliminary studies have demonstrated that the accumulation of cholesterol in cultured macrophages can modulate electron transport and ATP production in the mitochondrial inner membrane [35]. This phenomenon may be attributable to alterations in membrane fluidity, which can affect the process of OXPHOS. The blood–brain barrier plays a unique role in regulating the selective permeability of lipids in the brain, a function that distinguishes it from other tissues. Cholesterol and fatty acid transport is facilitated by specific transporters, and apolipoprotein intake is crucial for their absorption [38]. A previous study demonstrated that the lipid content of ApoE-knockout mutant mice differs from that of wild-type (WT) mice [39]. However, the causes underlying OXPHOS impairment in mutant mice remain to be elucidated.

Excessive lipid accumulation has been reported to increase oxidative stress and promote alterations in mitophagy [40, 41]. Oxidative stress elevation is believed to be the primary mechanism through which mitochondrial dysfunction leads to neurodegenerative disorders. Excessive lipid accumulation can contribute to oxidative stress through several potential mechanisms, including mitochondrial overload, lipid peroxidation, and antioxidant depletion. As previously indicated, the brain is a highly specialized organ that contains various types of lipids. Some of these components, including unsaturated fatty acids and cholesterol, are prone to oxidation under conditions of aerobic and physiological activity [42, 43]. Lipid peroxidation and its associated form of cell death, ferroptosis, may play a key role in the etiology of neurodegenerative disorders. An alternative hypothesis is that this phenomenon is attributable to a reduction in the activity of mitochondrial antioxidant enzymes. In the present study, we observed an increase in the levels of ROS/RNS, as well as a decrease in the expression of antioxidant genes, including SOD1, catalase, and GPx4, in the cerebral cortex of ApoE-knockout mice. We also noted that the expression of Nrf2, which encodes a transcription factor responsible for activating the expression of protective enzymes, was upregulated [44]. Whether dysregulated lipid metabolism disrupts the activity of other transcription factors, such as NF-κB, and inhibits the transcription of these specific genes requires further investigation. Furthermore, our findings revealed a reduction in mitochondrial mass in the mutant mice’s brains, suggesting that lipid accumulation upregulates autophagy or mitophagy in ApoE-knockout mice.

In summary, excessive lipid accumulation results in impaired OXPHOS, increased oxidative stress, and decreased mitochondrial mass, possibly through increased mitophagy in ApoE-knockout mice. In ApoE4 mutant mice, mitochondrial phenotypes include increased fusion, disrupted mitophagy, and calcium overload [45–47]. Enhancement of mitochondrial fusion and suppression of fission, along with the inhibition of mitophagy, all of which can only be detected in ApoE4 mutant mice, lead to the elevation of oxidative stress. As mitochondrial alterations induced by elevated oxidative stress are key factors involved in synaptic degeneration and cell death in neurodegeneration [48, 49], these phenomena may be the reason underlying the severe neurological phenotypes observed in ApoE4 mice.

Notably, we observed that the expression of key genes of mitochondrial biogenesis, including Pgc1a and Nrf2, was upregulated. This finding suggests that mitochondrial biogenesis is upregulated and that the machinery responsible for maintaining normal mitochondrial dynamics functions properly in ApoE-knockout mice. Moreover, we noted that mitochondrial alterations did not induce severe mitochondrial damage, indicating that the expression of BAX/BAK was not upregulated. These phenomena suggested that the mitochondrial alterations observed in these mutant mice did not reach pathological levels.

Although the ApoE-knockout mutant mice exhibited a typical hyperlipidemia phenotype, this phenotype may not be entirely analogous to the clinical features of patients with neurodegenerative disorders. In addition to hyperlipidemia, we observed that the lipid transport process was disrupted in these mutant mice. However, the extent to which lipid content and composition, which are theoretically crucial variables that determine the cellular properties of neurons and glia, are altered during ApoE ablation remains to be elucidated. Furthermore, blood–brain barrier defects have been previously reported in ApoE-knockout mice [42]. At the cellular level, lipid transportation across the plasma membrane substantially influences the properties of organelles and the dynamics of biochemical reactions. Further clinical research is required to determine whether lipid transport is also disrupted in patients. In the present study, mitochondrial assays were conducted using a bulk-tissue design. Whether the mitochondrial alterations observed in this study are common to most cell types or are specific to certain cell types remains to be elucidated in future studies.
