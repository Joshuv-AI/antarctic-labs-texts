Prevalence and determinants of work-related stress among nurses in Ethiopia: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Mulualem Gete Feleke, Tadele Lankrew Ayalew, Kidist Ashager, Hailu Asmare Beyene, Moges Wubneh Abate, Nigusie Selomon Tibebu
Published: 2025

Background

Work-related stress is a dynamic interaction between the job environment and the individual, resulting in psychological and physiological changes that impair normal functioning and job performance [1, 2]. It can significantly impact both physical and mental health, ultimately reducing job productivity [3, 4]. Occupational stress is now recognized not only as a personal health concern but also as a major public health and economic [5, 6]. In modern occupational settings, work-related stress has become a widespread and costly issue. According to the American Institute of Stress, approximately 80% of workplace injuries are related to stress, and nearly 40% of work-related financial losses can be attributed to stress-induced outcomes [5].

Among healthcare professionals, nurses are particularly vulnerable to high levels of job-related stress due to the nature of their responsibilities, emotional demands, and high patient loads [7, 8]. Evidence from global studies has consistently reported that work-related stress is highly prevalent among nurses [9–11] with prevalence of ranges from 30% to 70% [6, 10, 12]. In Ethiopia, prevalence rates has ranged from 33.3% to 66.2% [13, 14].

The impact of occupational stress on nurses extends beyond individual health concerns. It has been linked to a range of adverse outcomes, including anxiety, depression, burnout, sleep disturbances, reduced job performance, increased absenteeism, high staff turnover, and low job satisfaction [15–18]. Furthermore, numerous studies have identified several factors associated with increased levels of work-related stress among nurses. These include excessive workload, work shift, work setting (e.g., emergency or intensive care units), years of experience, gender, workplace conflict, marital status, educational level, low job satisfaction, poor working environment, and lack of recognition or incentives [14, 19–28].

Although several primary studies have been conducted in Ethiopia to examine the prevalence and associated factors of work-related stress among nurses [13, 14, 19–25, 29, 30], the overall national prevalence and its key determinants remain insufficiently synthesized. A previous systematic review conducted in Ethiopia focused primarily on the pooled prevalence of job stress among nurses but did not explore predictors in depth [31]. Since then, additional studies have been published, and a comprehensive synthesis that includes both updated prevalence and contributing factors is warranted.

Therefore, the aim of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to estimate the pooled national prevalence of work-related stress among nurses in Ethiopia and to identify the major associated factors contributing to this burden.

Methods and materials

Study design and reporting guidelines

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines [32]. The methodology follows the PRISMA-recommended order, detailing eligibility criteria, information sources, search strategy, study selection, data collection, outcome measurement, risk of bias assessment, and data synthesis.

Exclusion criteria

Qualitative studies, editorials, commentaries, letters, reviews, studies without full-text access or insufficient outcome data, and studies including other healthcare professionals without reporting separate nurse outcomes were excluded.

Information sources and search strategy

To identify all relevant studies on the prevalence and associated factors of work-related stress among nurses in Ethiopia, a comprehensive search was conducted in PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, Science Direct, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar (for gray literature). Searches limited to English-language documents published up to April 30, 2025. Boolean operators (AND/OR) were used to combine keywords related to job stress, associated factors, nurses, and Ethiopia. Filters and limits applied to each database are detailed in Appendix 2, ensuring reproducibility.

Study selection

All retrieved records were imported into EndNote reference manager to remove duplicates. The screening process was conducted manually in three stages: title screening, abstract screening, and full-text review. Two reviewers (MG and MW) independently screened all records at each stage. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion, and if consensus could not be reached, a third and fourth reviewer were consulted. This manual, stepwise approach ensured rigorous and unbiased selection of eligible studies.

Data extraction

Two reviewers (MG and SN) independently extracted data using a standardized Excel spreadsheet, which served as the final data record. Extracted variables included author, year of publication, study setting, study design, sample size, response rate, measurement tools, prevalence of work-related stress, and associated factors. Discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion and consensus. The finalized spreadsheet was then exported to STATA version 14 for statistical analysis.

Outcome measurement

The primary outcome of this review was the prevalence of work-related stress among nurses in Ethiopia. Work-related stress was defined in the included studies as the psychological and physiological response to job demands exceeding an individual’s capacity to cope. The assessment tools reported by the included studies included the Expanded Nursing Stress Scale (ENSS), Workplace Stress Scale (WSS), and from the previous literature to measure occupational stress among nurses. The prevalence was calculated by dividing the number of nurses experiencing stress by the total number of nurses in the study, multiplied by 100.

The secondary outcome was the identification of factors associated with work-related stress among nurses. These factors included occupational and personal characteristics. Data on associated factors were extracted from 2 × 2 tables to calculate odds ratios (ORs).

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for Analytical Cross-Sectional Studies [33]. This tool evaluates key aspects of study design and reporting across nine domains, including the clarity of inclusion criteria, measurement of exposure and outcome variables, identification and management of confounding factors, and appropriateness of statistical analysis.

Two reviewers (MG and KA) independently assessed each included study using the JBI checklist. Each item on the checklist was rated as “Yes,” “No,” “Unclear,” or “Not applicable.” Studies that met at least 70% of the criteria (i.e., 6 or more “Yes” responses out of 9) were considered to be of acceptable methodological quality and were included in the meta-analysis. Any disagreements between reviewers were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

The extracted data were entered into Microsoft Excel and exported to STATA version 14 for statistical analysis. The pooled prevalence of work-related stress among nurses in Ethiopia was estimated using a random-effects meta-analysis model, which accounts for variability between studies. Heterogeneity among studies was assessed using the Cochran’s Q test and quantified by the I² statistic. An I² value of > 50% was considered indicative of substantial heterogeneity.

To identify factors associated with work-related stress, odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were extracted or calculated from individual studies. For factors reported in at least two studies, pooled odds ratios were calculated using the random-effects model.

Subgroup analyses were conducted based on region, sample size, publication year, and stress measurement tools to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. In addition, sensitivity analysis was performed to assess the influence of individual studies on the overall pooled estimates. Publication bias was assessed using funnel plot symmetry and Egger’s regression test, with a p-value of < 0.05 indicating potential publication bias.

Results

Search results and study selection

A comprehensive search of electronic databases identified a total of 586 records. After removing 108 duplicate records, 478 records remained. These were screened by title and abstract, resulting in the exclusion of 434 records due to irrelevance to the topic or failure to meet inclusion criteria.

he full texts of 44 reports were retrieved for further assessment, but 5 could not be obtained. Of the 39 full-text articles reviewed, 26 were excluded for the following reasons: outcome of interest not reported (n = 12), study population not limited to nurses (n = 8), and conducted in a different setting (n = 6). As a result, 13 studies comprising a total of 3,702 participants were included in the final meta-analysis. The detailed process of study selection is illustrated in Fig. 1(PRISMA 2020 Flow Diagram).

Characteristics of included studies

This review applied no restrictions on publication year; however, the 13 studies included were conducted across different regions of Ethiopia between 2014 and 2024, involving a total of 3,702 nurses. All studies employed cross-sectional designs and utilized structured questionnaires to measure work-related stress. The prevalence of work-related stress reported in these studies ranged from 29.3% [29] to 66.2% [14]. Most of the included studies used the Expanded Nursing Stress Scale (ENSS) for measurement. Quality assessment using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale adapted for cross-sectional studies yielded scores between 6 and 9, indicating moderate to high methodological quality (Table 1).

Prevalence of work related stress among nurses in Ethiopia

In this systematic review and meta-analysis the estimated pooled prevalence of work related stress among nurses in Ethiopia was 47.84% (95% CI: 46.28–49.41) with p-value = 0.000 (Fig. 2).

Sub-group analysis

Due to significant different heterogeneity among the publications included in this study, a region-based sub-group analysis was performed to investigate the likely cause of heterogeneity among the studies. The sub-group analysis shows the highest prevalence was observed in the Hareri region with a pooled prevalence of 66.2% (95% CI: 61.37–71.05) and the lowest was observed in Amhara 42.92% (95%CI: 40.04-45. 80) as shown in Fig. 3.

Factors associated with work related stress among nurse in Ethiopia

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, several factors were significantly associated with work-related stress among nurses in Ethiopia. Nurses working in shifts were more than twice as likely to experience stress compared to those with regular schedules (AOR = 2.68; 95% CI: 2.02–3.55). Those assigned to emergency units had nearly six times higher odds of experiencing stress than nurses in other departments (AOR = 5.95; 95% CI: 3.69–9.57). Nurses with less than five years of work experience were also significantly more likely to report stress compared to their more experienced colleagues (AOR = 4.41; 95% CI: 1.42–13.7). Having children doubled the likelihood of experiencing work-related stress (AOR = 2.13; 95% CI: 1.36–3.33). Furthermore, nurses working in intensive care units (ICUs) faced the highest risk, with over six times greater odds of stress compared to those in other units (AOR = 6.33; 95% CI: 3.30–12.12).

These findings indicate that both personal factors, such as parenting responsibilities and limited work experience, and occupational factors, including shift work and assignment to high-pressure units like ICU and emergency, play substantial roles in contributing to work-related stress among nurses (Fig. 4).

Assessment of publication bias

To assess the presence of publication bias in the included studies on the prevalence of work-related stress among nurses in Ethiopia, both visual inspection and statistical tests were utilized. The funnel plot demonstrated a symmetrical distribution of effect sizes, suggesting the absence of publication bias. This was further supported by Egger’s regression test, which yielded a non-significant result (p = 0.493), indicating no statistically significant evidence of publication bias (Fig. 5).

egger (A) and funnel plot (B) test to asses’ publication bias for work related stress among nurses in Ethiopia, 2025

Sensitivity analysis

To evaluate the influence of individual studies on the overall meta-analysis, a sensitivity analysis was performed using a random-effects model. The results demonstrated that omitting each study one at a time did not significantly alter the pooled prevalence estimate. This indicates that no single study had a disproportionate impact on the overall findings. The closeness of each study’s individual estimate to the overall combined estimate suggests the stability and reliability of the meta-analysis results (Fig. 6).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis revealed that the pooled prevalence of work-related stress among nurses in Ethiopia is 47.84% (95% CI: 46.28–49.41). This finding suggests that nearly one in every two nurses experiences significant levels of occupational stress, underscoring a major public health concern within the Ethiopian healthcare system. The result is consistent with the findings of several primary studies conducted in different regions of Ethiopia [20, 23, 25, 31], supporting the notion that work-related stress is a widespread issue among the country’s nursing workforce. Notably, the included studies showed considerable regional variation in stress prevalence, ranging from a low of 29.3% in the Amhara Region [29] to a high of 66.2% in Harari Region [14]. These discrepancies could be attributed to regional differences in healthcare infrastructure, hospital management practices, workload intensity, support systems, and awareness or stigma surrounding mental health and stress reporting [14, 25].

When compared internationally, the pooled prevalence in this review is higher than the rate reported 45.5% in Saudi Arabia [28], 34.9% in Isfahan, Iran [36]. However, it is lower than studies done in Delhi, India: 87.4% [37] and Iran, where a systematic review reported 69% [10], with individual studies reaching up to 78.4% [38]. These international differences may be attributed to variations in stress measurement tools, thresholds for classification, workload intensity, nurse-patient ratios, institutional support structures, and socio-cultural attitudes toward stress and mental health [29, 39].

The subgroup analysis showed significant regional differences in work-related stress among nurses in Ethiopia, with the highest prevalence in Harari (66.2%) and the lowest in Amhara (42.92%). These variations likely stem from differences in healthcare resources, staffing, patient load, management practices, and cultural attitudes toward stress [40, 41].

This systematic review and meta-analysis identified several key factors that significantly associated with work-related stress among nurses in Ethiopia, including shift work, working in high-acuity environments (ICUs and emergency departments), having children, and less than five years of work experience.

Shift work was consistently associated with higher levels of stress. Nurses engaged in rotating or night shifts were more likely to experience circadian rhythm disruption, poor sleep quality, and reduced opportunities for rest and family engagement. In the Ethiopian context, these challenges are often exacerbated by staffing shortages and heavy workloads, limiting recovery time between shifts as supported by multiple studies [14, 24, 25, 28, 30].

Working in high-acuity environments such as intensive care units [14, 23] and emergency departments [24, 26, 30] were also linked to increased stress. These environments require rapid decision-making and expose nurses to frequent critical incidents, patient deterioration, and death. In low-resource settings like Ethiopia, the stress burden is intensified by inadequate medical supplies, limited human resources, and high patient-to-nurse ratios [42].

Parental responsibilities emerged as another key factor. Nurses with children reported significantly higher levels of stress, likely due to the dual burden of demanding professional duties and childcare responsibilities. The absence of supportive workplace policies, such as flexible hours or on-site childcare, may further heighten this challenge [14, 25, 42].

Furthermore, nursing experience played a critical role. Those with fewer than five years of work experience were more vulnerable to stress, potentially due to limited clinical exposure, underdeveloped coping skills, and reduced confidence in managing complex patient care scenarios. The lack of structured mentorship and support systems in many Ethiopian healthcare institutions may further contribute to early-career stress [20, 23, 42].

Limitations of the study

This systematic review and meta-analysis has several important limitations that should be considered. First, all included studies used cross-sectional designs, which limit the ability to draw causal inferences between the identified factors and work-related stress. Second, there was considerable variability in the measurement tools, including the Expanded Nursing Stress Scale (ENSS), Workplace Stress Scale (WSS), and self-structured questionnaires. The use of different instruments may have introduced subjectivity, reduced comparability across studies, and contributed to heterogeneity in the pooled estimates. Third, most studies relied on self-reported data, which are susceptible to social desirability and recall biases, potentially compromising the accuracy of reported stress levels.

Fourth, although studies were conducted across multiple Ethiopian regions, some areas were underrepresented or not represented at all, limiting the generalizability of the findings to the national context. Fifth, only studies published in English were included, potentially excluding relevant research in other languages or in grey literature, and the absence of longitudinal studies restricts understanding of how work-related stress develops over time and its long-term impact on nurses. Finally, this review did not include studies evaluating interventions for stress reduction, leaving a gap in evidence regarding effective strategies to mitigate stress among nurses in Ethiopia.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis highlight that nearly half of nurses in Ethiopia experience significant work-related stress. Major factors contributing to this stress include rotating shifts, placement in demanding units such as emergency and intensive care, limited work experience, and responsibilities related to parenting. These findings underscore the combined impact of professional pressures and personal obligations on nurse well-being and healthcare delivery.

Recommendations

To address these challenges, healthcare managers and policymakers should improve shift scheduling, ensure adequate staffing in critical care areas, provide accessible mental health support, and implement mentorship programs for early-career nurses. Family-friendly policies, including flexible hours and childcare support, are recommended to help nurses balance work and personal life. National workforce strategies should integrate stress reduction measures, promote supportive work environments, and encourage employee recognition. Future research should prioritize longitudinal and interventional studies, with mixed-methods approaches providing deeper insights into factors influencing nurse stress.
