Macro-level facilitators and impediments to fertility transition in Ethiopia: implications for reorienting family planning towards sustainable development
Authors: Tesfay Brhane Gebremariam, Mitike Molla, Wubegzier Mekonnen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFertility transition is essential for health and development, yet Ethiopia continues to face high fertility and rapid population growth. Due to limited evidence on macro-level derivers of fertility, this study assesses Ethiopia’s readiness and implementation capacity for fertility transition, alongside structural and programmatic barriers.MethodA convergent parallel mixed-methods design was used, combining 14 key informant interviews with reviews of national policies and demographic data. Informants were purposively selected from Federal, Regional States, and Non-governmental institutions. Thematic and content analyses followed a predefined framework covering political commitment, legal and policy integration, institutional coordination, and implementation outcomes. NVivo 14 was used for coding, while policy texts and official speeches were systematically mapped and triangulated with fertility trends from national surveys and census data.ResultOver the past three decades, Ethiopia has recognized the burden of high fertility, endorsed global agendas, prepared policies, and has achieved substantial expansion of services. Consequently, the CPR rose from less than 5% in 1990 to 42% in 2019, and the TFR dropped from 6.4 in 1990 to 4.1 in 2019. However, the transition is dominated by periodic declines without substantial changes in completed cohort fertility, preference for low fertility, and limiting behaviors, which are rooted in limited domestic political advocacy, weak multisectoral coordination, and blurring of the fertility, population, and development agenda from the family planning services. Slow development and economic transformation, pronatalist norms, and political instability can hamper further fertility decline.ConclusionEthiopia’s period fertility decline has been derived by supply and service expansions rather than a fundamental shift in preference for low fertility and the power to do so. Accelerating the transition and prevention of stalling requires reorienting the right-based family planning into informed, voluntary, and autonomous demand for low fertility, which integrates reproductive rights with development goals, through family planning-population-development nexus.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12978-026-02307-y.

Introduction

Fertility transition is defined as the sustained decline in fertility from its high to a low level, finally reaching at or below replacement level [1]. Period changes due to tempo effects (postponement) that can result in recuperation or stalling are initial symptoms of the transition but do not prove quantum fertility decline, which is central driver of the demographic transition and a key precondition for the opening the demographic dividend [1, 2]. In 2024, the global fertility rate was 2.2 births per woman, down from around 3.3 in 1990. However, more than one in ten countries, predominantly in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), continues to experience fertility rates of 4.0 or higher. The sluggish economic development, large family norms, slow adaptation of contraceptive methods, and limited commitment and capacity to implement policies leads to slow or stalling fertility transition in SSA [2–10].

Fertility transition plays a multifactorial role in shaping population health and is foundation for the achievement of most of the Sustainable Development Goals (SDG) [11, 12]. First, it helps reduce high-risk fertility behaviours, thereby contributing to improved health outcomes. Second, it lessens the childbearing burden on women, transforming their role from primarily reproductive to economically productive. It also reshapes the demographic age structure, creating a favourable environment for a demographic dividend and sustained economic growth. Reducing dependency and increasing per capita income enables greater investments in education and healthcare, thereby helping to break the cycle of poverty. Furthermore, with proactive measures to avoid below-replacement fertility, fertility transition can support a stable population and promote environmental-friendly and sustainable development [2, 13, 14]. The united nation also recognize the importance of slower population growth for achieving sustainable development in low-income, high-fertility societies [15, 16].

Being multifactorial, fertility transition can generally follow two pathways: a neutral decline resulting from socioeconomic development and structural changes, or a proactive decline driven by deliberate policy interventions. The former, which is observed in developed countries, is explained by the retrospective classical-economic and demand [17, 18] theories. The later, shaped by the neo-Malthusian view, is based on state-led proactive fertility reduction measures [19], and led coercive population control measures [20]. In response to the expansion of such coercive measures, the Cairo International Conference on Population and Development (ICPD-1994) introduced the rights-based framework that shifted the focus of family planning service (FPS) from macro-level, demographic targets to individual reproductive autonomy [21].

Single and traditional fertility transition theories are insufficient to explain the fertility dynamics of pro-natal societies in low-income countries [22], such as Ethiopia, where fertility is determined by an interaction of individual, social, economic, political conditions and the global practices. To guide this analysis, we propose a new conceptual framework rooted in classical demographic transition theory and the ICPD Programme of Action. The framework links the structural, economic, and demographic contexts with political commitment, which in turn drives policy, legal, and institutional readiness to influence fertility behaviours and rights (Fig. 1). This approach provides a structured lens to understand how macro-level governance can shape individual reproductive choices and enable a sustainable fertility transition.

Ethiopia, with nearly 130 million people, is among the top five countries projected to add 100 million to the global population by 2050 [23]. Despite improvements in child survival and life expectancy due to reproductive health interventions, high birth rates persist, keeping the total fertility rate at 3.9 in 2024 and is projected to remain above replacement level until 2075 [1]. Although several studies have examined individual-level determinants of fertility [24–26], macro-level evidences remain scarce. Reports by the Ethiopian Academy of science [27], and other authors [28, 29] assessed the implementation of the Ethiopian population policy, but these reports examine broader policy objectives without in-depth analysis of the fertility agenda. Taking the 1993 population policy and the 1994 ICPD as key milestones, this study explores Ethiopia’s political commitment, policy and institutional readiness, and implementation capacity for fertility transition. Specifically, the paper reviews the political commitment, policy preparedness and integration, institutional arrangement, coordination and partnership towards implementations of policies and programs for fertility decline, with particular focus to the Ethiopian FPS.

Method

Study design and setting

This study employed a convergent parallel mixed methods design within a pragmatic research paradigm, combining key informant interviews (KIIs) with document reviews. The study was conducted in Ethiopia, the second most populous country in Africa, which is experiencing rapid population growth [23]. With a 5.4% annual urbanization rate, only 21% of the population is urban. Ethiopia’s economy is rooted in agriculture and has faced periods of fluctuations, inequalities, and struggle with deep poverty and limited human development [30]. Political transitions have introduced more liberal economic policies but have also been marked by instability and conflict, including a destructive war that caused mass displacement, infrastructure damage, and a shift in government priorities [31]. Ethnic federalism in Ethiopia, compounded with population-based grant allocation to regional states, has led to increased ethnic polarization, competition over resources, and regional disparity in the implementation of policies and programs [32]. Ethiopian culture, shaped by strong religious traditions, patriarchal norms, and ethnic diversity, favors early marriage and a preference for large families [33, 34].

Data source and data collection procedures

This study is based on primary data from KIIs and secondary data from document review. A total of 14 key-informants (Table 1) purposively selected from the Federal Government, the Somalia Region (Jigjiga), the Southern Ethiopia Region (Arba Minch), and Nongovernmental organizations (NGOs). Arbaminch (Southern region) is selected to include perspectives of Christian dominant communities while Jigjiga provides insights from pastoralist and Muslim-majority settings.

Population or family planning (FP) experts working in the governmental and non-governmental organizations were interviewed using a semi-structured tool focusing on political commitment, policy and institutional readiness, and implementation capacity (supplementary file-1). Interviews were conducted by the principal investigator and two trained qualitative researchers. All interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed into English, and stored securely.

Secondary sources related to population, fertility, or FP includes 1) Speeches of Ethiopia’s higher officials, 2) National legal frameworks, policies, strategies, plans, and guidelines, and 3) national census and survey reports. Relevant documents were retrieved from electronic platforms1, organizational websites, and direct contact with sector offices. Documents were screened and selected using a non-systematic, purposeful approach based on their content related to goals, objectives, strategies, and indicators related to fertility, population, and FP.

Data analysis

A mixed-methods approach was used, combining thematic analysis of KIIs, content analysis of policy documents and speeches, and descriptive trend analysis of census and survey data. Each source was analyzed independently and then synthesized to identify convergences, contradictions, and contextual patterns. Interview transcripts were coded in NVivo 14 using a predefined codebook, supplemented with emerging codes. Following the conceptual framework (Fig. 1), codes were organized under four themes: (1) Political recognition and commitment to fertility transition; (2) Policy and legal framework development and integration; (3) Institutional arrangement, coordination, and partnerships; and (4) Implementation and outcomes. Ministerial speeches were chronologically mapped and analyzed for shifts in discourse and are triangulated with Theme 1. The policy statements (goals, objectives, strategies, or targets) from the national policy, plans, guidelines, and legal frameworks were extracted using a structured template and discussed under theme 2 The strategies of key partner organizations were also assessed and used to describe the third theme. Results

Political commitment

Government political commitment was measured by the recognition of the burden of the high fertility rate and the commitments towards reducing the fertility rate. Federal and regional experts recognize the rapid population growth specially the youth bulge as a serious socioeconomic burden particularly due to pressure on social services, employment leading to food security, migration, social unrest, and conflict. They also noted that uncontrolled high fertility hampers national development, perpetuates intergenerational poverty, and strains service delivery. A regional expert stated that, ‘The birth rate is increasing and not balanced with the country’s economy. We are facing many problems in expanding schools and health facilities; parents cannot educate and feed their children properly. People are displaced because of a lack of space and get quarreled over political tension. There’s also deforestation and farming on the mountains. So, the policy must be revised and implemented as in China. Otherwise, it would be difficult to continue as a country.’ (Male, government population expert).

Experts outside the population office, including political leaders, however, were not aware of the role of fertility decline in changing the age structure. Rather, they perceive it as a measure to control population growth rate (PGR) and to reduce population size. A male regional population expert stated that, ‘higher officials in our region do not even know how the population is growing and how it will impact the economy’.

When asked about their opinion on the government’s level of political commitment, some praised efforts to revise the national population policy and support FPS, while others noted the declining visibility of the population agenda by describing it as almost neglected at present. An expert stated that, ‘Population is not a government priority; it has other pressing issue that commands its attention. Ten years ago, you could see contraceptive promotion on TV and radio. Now I do not see any activity; I do not even hear news about the population.’ (Male, government population planning expert).

The politicization of population size by some regional states, viewing it as a means to achieve better political wins and economic resources, signifies poor political commitment. A demographer reflects this by considering population growth as an asset rather than a threat. ‘I do not think the government considers high fertility as a challenge. It is also highly politicized; an increase in population in each region is viewed as a positive thing. When you go to certain regions and talk about fertility regulation, you will be in danger.’ (Female, population research advisor, NGO).

Inline to the advisor’s view, regional expert also noted that political commitment to fertility reduction in their region has moved from relatively stronger institutional engagement to lower prioritization.“The government commitment and ownership towards fertility reduction is not similar with other activities. Once the donor’s budget stops, every activity related to the population policy was stopped. . . The government also doesn’t allocate budget. The structure and other systems were changed over time… established councils does not continue, concerned bodies up to federal level has not working actively.” (Male regional government population expert). Most speeches related to population or FP from Ethiopian leaders was delivered at high-level global or regional events, with limited domestic advocacy (Table 3). For instance, the Prime Minister’s 1994 speech at the ICPD highlighted the need for population control to alleviate poverty. Similarly, a 2006 address by the Ministry of Finance recognized high population growth as a threat to development. A paper by the Prime Ministers of Ethiopia and Rwanda urged more funding for FP due to its multi-dimensional benefits [35]. The Ethiopian Ministry of Health has framed FP within a broader context of sexual and reproductive health. The Minister of Planning and Development’s 2024 statement emphasized integrating population into the development agenda to maximize the demographic dividend. Despite these commitments, some leaders emphasize pro-natalist views. For instance, the late Prime Minister’s remark that “a child is born not only with a stomach but also with working hands and legs” undermines the economic rationale of fertility decline. There are also current higher-official speeches that advocate opportunities rather than challenges of high population growth (Table 3, #10).

Preparation and integration of policies and legal frameworks

Ethiopia has developed extensive policy documents, and legal frameworks, focused on fertility reduction (Table 4). The Constitution, legal codes, and proclamations addressed fertility issues in terms of the right to use FP, access safe abortion services, and prohibit early marriage. Article 35 (9) of the 1995 Ethiopian Constitution stated that ‘to prevent harm arising from pregnancy and childbirth and to safeguard their health, women have the right of access to FP education, information and capacity’. Ethiopia’s first national population policy (NPP), ratified in 1993, was a major milestone indicator for Ethiopia’s recognition of the need for harmonizing population growth with economic growth and aimed to lower the TFR from 7.7 in 1990 to 4.0 by 2015 [36]. Other development policies and strategies also incorporated fertility and population-related indicators as central objectives or as crosscutting themes.

The most recent 10-year perspective plan set targets to reduce the PGR and dependency ratio by 2030. The health sector transformation plan aimed to reduce the TFR to three by 2025. The National Employment Policy and Strategies and the 2019 Ethiopia Climate Resilient Green Economy Plan recognized the need for balancing a PGR with economic growth. National food security and nutrition programs consider FP as a nutrition-sensitive intervention. However, not all government sectors have set specific fertility targets. The social sector, including education, children, youth, women, and social affairs, promoted FP as a cross-cutting strategy without defining specific fertility goals. The second phase of the Growth and Transformation Plan and the Health Sector Transformation Plan also omitted fertility related indicators. The sustainable development and poverty reduction program, the rural development policy and strategy, and the pastoralist policy do not integrate the population, fertility, or FP agenda at all.

Institutional arrangement, coordination, and partnership

Institutional arrangement and coordination

Currently, the Population Affairs Directorate, under the Ministry of Planning and Development, manages national population issues, including the preparation of policy documents and population events. However, it operates with limited resources. The Ministry of Health (MoH) and Regional Health Bureaus provide FP services across the healthcare system. Although the FP program is central to the NPP, the MoH does not collaborate closely with the population directorate. FP guidelines overlook the directorate as a stakeholder, instead naming other ministers like the Ministry of Education and the Ministry of Women and Youth as key stakeholders. Even though the national population plan identified numerous coordinating bodies, including the Council of Ministers and the National Population Council, key informants indicated that there is no established coordination structure or platform, resulting in haphazard integration of fertility or population issues into sector plans and fragmented implementation and monitoring. A population expert said: Following the preparation of the policy, various ministerial organizations received their assigned responsibilities. However, we lacked a structure to monitor their activities. Thus, lack of coordination is the primary reason for ineffective policy execution.’ (Male, government population expert).

Key informants cited inconsistent reasons for the lack of coordination: some attribute it to the government’s diminishing concern for population growth, while others see a strategic shift from demography to FP. Participants’ negative attitudes towards advocating birth limitation and the focus on FP over demographic indicators in national policy documents support the latter reason (strategic shift).

Global and local partnerships

Ethiopia has many partners promoting FPS (Table 5). For instance, donors funded 69% of FP costs in 2016 [37]. The United Kingdom allocated 90 million pounds to maintain and expand FPS coverage from 2017/18 to 2020/21 [38]. The Ministry of Health collaborated with organizations like Marie Stopes International, Family Guidance Association, and DKT Ethiopia for social franchised FPS. FPS was also set as a prerequisite for collaboration among public-private health facilities [39]. Professional associations such as the Ethiopian Public Health Association and the Ethiopian Midwifery Association have also notable engagement. Except for few partners such as UNFPA, who also work with the population directorate, most partners works with the MoH, focusing on the FPS. Their preference for the FP program reflects the political priority of maternal and child health. An expert stated that: ‘As of my observation in the last 15–20 years, the population policy gives more emphasis to health-related issues rather than population growth, and our support is under the maternal health package. The mother has the right to give birth to what she wants, but the service must be available to her.’ (Female, SRH advisor, NGO).

Implementation and achievements

Information, education, communication and behavioral change communication

Ethiopia’s first National Population Strategy for Advocacy, Information, Education, and Behavioral Change Communication (IEC/BCC) was prepared in 1997 and revised in 2023 [40]. The MoH also developed FP communication guidelines and an FP message guide. In collaboration with the population media center, the national theme “Small, Happy, and Prosperous Family” has been widely broadcasted through the government Media [41]. While this campaign broadly targeted reduced morbidity, mortality, and fertility, the central theme in the Somalia region emphasized birth spacing. The Whole Society Strategy [42] and the A360 Smart Start program [43] are also remarkable SRH-related IEC/BCC campaigns. Following these efforts, contraceptive knowledge increased from 63% in 1990 to 95% in 2019. The total demand for FP among married women increased from 45% in 2000 to 58% in 2016. However, the demand for limiting births remains stable, and relatively low at 23% in 2016 [44].

Improvements in health services and women’s empowerment

Following extensive investments in universal access to healthcare and education, tremendous improvements in life expectancy, child survival, and women’s empowerment have been achieved over the last three decades [45]. For example, the percentage of women who completed secondary or higher education increased from 4.6% in 1990 to 20% in 2019; the infant mortality rate declined from 110 in 1990 to 35 in 2024. These developments are recognized as essential grounds for fertility decline. A key-informant stated: ‘. . If a mother is busy with education or work, she won’t have time to bear many children. She will also be powerful enough to convince her husband. Fortunately, I am educated and have an income. Therefore, my husband cannot dictate my life, and if he tries, I will challenge him. So, we are working to create such empowered women’ (Female, government FP expert).

Contraceptive supply and service delivery

In partnership with civil society organizations and the private sector [39, 46, 47], essential contraceptive commodities and services have been universally available across the country. For instance, in 2015, 96% of primary healthcare facilities offered at least three types of contraceptive methods, while 96% of the secondary and tertiary facilities provided at least five types of methods [48]. To mitigate some declines in funding since 2014, the government committed to increasing its domestic funding for FP commodities by 1% annually, aiming to raise secured funding from 48% to 62% by 2030 [49]. Key informants also appreciate the government’s tax-free supply and provision of cost-exempted contraceptives across all public facilities. ‘The government procures contraceptives and provides them free of charge. We invest 2 billion birr annually in contraceptives; this is a large investment. Contraceptives are not taxed, which also indicates the extra commitment of the government.’ (Female, government FP expert).

These initiatives have resulted in significant advancements in contraceptive use and a decrease in fertility rates. Between 1990 and 2019 (Table 6), the contraceptive prevalence rate (CPR) increased from below 5% to 42%. Almost all users take modern methods, women who take traditional method ranges from 1.9% in 1990 to 0.6% in 2016, and then to 1% in 2019. Unmet need for modern method increases from less than 1% in 1990 to 36% in the 2000s and 22% in 2019. While the TFR declined from 6.4 to 4.1, the mean completed cohort fertility (number children born to women aged 45–49) remained stable (6.9 in 1990 and 6.7 in 2019). The PGR decreased from 3.2 in 1990 to 2.6 in 2024, and the child dependency ratio dropped from 109 to 67 in the same period. However, the trends in the average complete cohort fertility, average household size, and mean age at first marriage and childbirth remain stable.

Alongside these developments, Ethiopia’s FPS also faced multiple implementation gaps. First, wide disparities, high unmet need, underuse of long-acting contraceptives, and low contraceptive demand for limiting were observed implementation gaps. The regional discrepancies in CPR and TFR were high and consistent throughout time and led to political contentions. An expert highlighted, ‘the community believes that some groups use FP while others do not, and this is assumed to be deliberate, to reduce the population size of certain regions. The mob killing of a researcher, suspecting that ‘he is making children infertile!’ in one region was linked with the claim that its population is declining faster than other regions’. (Male, government FP expert)

Despite the anti-natal view of the NPP, the FPS is implemented under the rationale of maternal and child health, where birth spacing is considered the central advocacy theme. The main objective of the 2018 national FPS guidelines, for example, is stated as “to support reduction of preventable maternal and child death as well as solve infertility problems through provision of standardized FPS at all levels”. Consistent with this, key informants also reported that, ‘Fertility reduction is not our agenda. We would rather focus on and promote to consider spacing among children so that the mother and child get healthier. We also advise families to have children they can feed, teach, and raise properly, but the Ministry does not teach families to have 2 or 3 children; it has no right to do so.’ (Female, Government FP expert).

Population experts also show strong resistance for advocating birth limitation, arguing that promoting fertility targets would lead to coercive actions, violate rights, and provoke community resistance,. A senior population expert’s express,‘We can’t talk about limiting fertility to some level like what other countries did. Even the current international regulations didn’t allow you to talk about it. Whether you believe it or not, the government has no interest in advocating a specific fertility target because it can be seen as imposing. The current situation is difficult and there is also poor commitment among regions. We just want to say having fewer children will benefit your economy and wellness’.

The avoidance of birth limitation agenda from the FPS or lack thereof in any other sector is reflected in women’s fertility behaviours. From 2000 to 2016, demand for limiting only rose slightly (18% to 23.7%), the ideal number of children remained around five, nearly half of women with five children still wanted more, and the completed cohort fertility remained high (6.7 in 2019). Discussions

Drawing on expert interviews and document reviews, and guided by an integrated analytical framework, this study offers a comprehensive assessment of Ethiopia’s political commitment, institutional arrangements, and implementation landscape for fertility transition. The findings contribute to the existing literature by identifying the macro-level impediments and provide contextual recommendations for accelerated fertility transition in Ethiopia.

Our review found that Ethiopian leaders have raised the population issue in many international and regional forums. Official statements have evolved from addressing the burden of population pressure in the 1990s into advocacy of the FPS since 2012. Ethiopia has also developed national policies and strategies that promote the FPS, endorsed global frameworks such as the Cairo Plan of Action, and collaborate with local and global patterns. Other studies also report presence of favorable policy environment for FPS in Ethiopia [50–52]. Contrary to earlier critiques that claimed population issues were not integrated into sectoral plans [27], our review reveals mainstreaming of population, fertility, and FP targets into various sectoral strategies supported by integration tools and guidelines [53–56]. Ethiopia has also been expanding health services, IEC/BCC campaigns [41, 42], and women’s empowerment initiatives [57], which are vital for fertility reduction. Ethiopia’s FPS was known for its high-impact initiatives, such as community-based services through the health extension program [58, 59], over-the-counter distribution of contraceptive pills [60], tax and cost-exempted contraceptive supplies and services, and integration with maternal and child health services. Following these interventions, the CPR rose from less than 5% in 1990 to 36% in 2024, and the TFR dropped from 6.4 in 1990 to 4.0 in 2024 [61]. The success of Ethiopia’s FPS is confirmed by many other studies [62, 63].

Despite the above developments, there is persistently high completed cohort fertility and desire for more children, high prevalence of risky fertility behaviors, and low uptake of long-acting contraceptives methods. These stagnations occurred in a situation where there is universal access to free modern contraceptives [48] with nearly-home FPS services [64], implying that the supply-dominant paradigm, repeatedly framed as ensuring access to universal SRH is inadequate to diffuse the desire for lower fertility. The FPS is also constrained by poor service quality, high discontinuation rate, persistent unmet need, and high disparity [63, 65–68], leading to persistent disparity of the fertility rate across residential, educational, geographic, and culturally different populations [69]. These FPS related gaps implies that Ethiopia’s’ fertility transition relies on access than building social preferences and agency for the transition.

These gaps are rooted on the inadequate socioeconomic and institutional transformations and weak commitments towards development oriented fertility transition. The document review and key-informant opinions concurrently revealed lack of high-level domestic political advocacy for fertility transition. While Ethiopian leaders have consistently expressed FP and population issues in global forums, these commitments have not been demonstrated in internal political or public discourse. So far, there is no recorded public advocacy of the targets outlined in the NPP or the developmental benefits of lower fertility or slower population growth. This may indicates a focus on diplomatic signaling and engagement than authentic domestic political ownership. The fact that the NPP, which sets its fertility targets up to 2015, has not been revised over the last three decades also indicate none-progressive government commitment.

Lack of coordination and alignment is another critical gap. As reflected in previous national and regional assessments too [27, 28, 70, 71], there is poor institutional arrangement, non-functional integration, and lack of multisectoral coordination. Fertility transition requires harmonized and coordinated efforts for delaying age at birth (which is multisectoral in its nature), spacing, and limiting births, implementation has been fragmented, monitoring is weak, and progress toward fertility reduction has been uneven across regions. Such lack of alignment and coordination also led to the fragmentation of the fertility transition-development agenda, leaving it siloed under the health-sector lead FPS.

Ethiopia’s FPS also has major advocacy and service delivery limitations. The FP messaging and interventions merely focus on birth spacing for maternal and child health, ignoring the socioeconomic benefits of birth limitation. Experts also have a strong negative attitude towards setting and promotion advocacy of the national fertility targets, which was written in the policy documents. Previous study also indicates Ethiopian decision-makers have a lower interest in the economic, demographic, and environmental rationale of FP [72]. While this lack of public advocacy on birth limitation aligns with FPS norms, it can make Ethiopian women to adopt contraceptive use for spacing but not for limiting [33]. The avoidance of targeting and advocacy for birth limitation is likely to originate from the ICPD’s repositioning of population policies from ‘government population planning’ into ‘individual FP’, and from development oriented into right-based advocacy [73, 74]. Evidences show that SSA countries were discouraged from proactive advocacy of fertility and demographic targets, and camouflaged their FPS under birth spacing. Studies also found limited fertility-impact of such spacing [3–7, 75–83] and overall effect of FPS in the region [5, 84].

Beyond the above institutional and programmatic issues, broader structural and contextual factors can also impede the fertility transition. First, there are persistent pro-natalist cultural and religious norms [33, 65, 85–88], predominantly among the less developed and pastoralist regions. Regions such as Somali and Afar exhibit sustain high fertility and low uptake of FP driven by religion, pastoralist livelihoods, and lagged socioeconomic development [89, 90]. These inequities aggravated by the federal state structure and population-based budget allocations to regions can intensify the political sensitivities and mistrust toward FP, which has undermined the coherence of national efforts. Second, with almost 80% rural population, huge unemployment, and low productivity, the economy remains heavily reliant on agriculture and shows sluggish growth and structural transformations [91]. The country is still among the poorest countries in the world, where nearly 70% of the population lives below the national poverty line, 10.2 million are severely food insecure, and 39% of under-five children are chronically malnourished in 2025 [92]. These conditions maintain the socioeconomic utility of children, limiting responsiveness towards contraceptive availability and utilization efforts. Third, political instability, conflict, and war can influence fertility by altering values to marriage and childbirth, diminishing educational and employment opportunities and disrupting SRH/FP service. It also diverts government and partner focus and funding from SRH/FP towards security [93]. Fourth, the shifting in global priorities towards preventing ultra-low fertility [94], and pandemic and conflict agendas are resulting in FP funding gaps [95, 96]. These contextual, structural, and implementation gaps can delay or stall Ethiopia’s fertility transition. The massive reproductive age population and improvements in reproductive efficiency are likely to increase the number of live births and PGR, creating an ever-growing population, with a high dependency ratio. Unless curbed by rapid socioeconomic growth supported with rapid fertility decline, these conditions can lead to demographic strain rather than a demographic dividend, putting the society under chronic poverty and insecurity [97].

Policy implication

While fertility decline can follow development, high fertility in underdeveloped countries can also constrain development, requiring proactive measures to reap socioeconomic benefits of low fertility. Current evidences and strategic documents [2, 11, 98–101] recommend slowing the PGR in developing countries through meeting the unmet need and emphasizing the health perspectives of FPS. These global norms overlooked demand creation for low fertility and the demo-economic benefits of FPS. Ethiopia, a pre-dividend, low-income country, with pronatalist culture, low demand for FPS, and fragmented institutional arrangement for demographic issues, therefore, requires integration of the right, health, and demographic rationalization of FPS for sustainable development.

Based on UNFPA’s holistic framework for human right based FP [102] and a guidance notes for SSA [103], we propose a “family planning-population-development nexus (FPDN)”, a framework that frames FP into its health, social, and demographic impacts, thereby triggering the demographic transition and dividend required for sustainable development. The framework builds up on three mutually reinforcing pillars. 1) Enacting and reinforcing laws to safeguard reproductive rights and autonomy; 2) creation of demand, autonomy, and agency for low risk fertility behaviors, including voluntary birth limitation; and 3) Ensuring universal/equitable access to high-quality SRH services. The first pillar of this FPDN advocates individuals’ SRH rights, including the right to autonomous, informed reproductive choices, get services to meet choices, and be protected from coercion. Adhering to the right-based principles of FPS [102, 104], leaders and service providers should advocate for couples’ irrevocable right to prefer, decide, and meet their fertility needs. Unlike the existing FPS which deals only about spacing and unmet need, the second pillar of framework underscores diffusion of demand for birth limitation, which requires autonomy, agency, and access to high-quality long-acting contraceptives. This paper is trying to bridge reproductive autonomy with demo-economic goals of FPS by simply informing that people will get health, social, and economic benefit if they adopt the delaying, spacing, and limiting behaviors. While this breaks the silence about socioeconomic benefits of birth limitation and FPS, it also calls for advocacy and protection of SRH rights. The micro and macro-level benefits of voluntary birth limitation can be promoted; asserting that smaller, well-planned families enable greater investment in health, nutrition, education, empower women for productive employment, and reduce household level dependency ratio, and then improve per-capita income and saving [105]. Advocating such benefit of right-based FP without prescribing or persuading for some fertility targets will facilitate diffusion of conscious preference for low fertility and contraception, which are vital for sustainable demographic transitions and development [15, 98, 100, 106–110]. Many Asian countries have achieved rapid fertility decline through implementation of such state-led, development oriented voluntary FPS [13, 111–116].

This research uses multiple data sources with triangulated interpretations and provides valid evidence on the trends and drivers of the fertility transition in Ethiopia. This study also has limitations: first, the document review is limited to national-level policy documents, lacking regional materials. While this sample provides a saturated data on the main research questions, opinions from some key international organizations, high-level political leaders, and regional officers are missed due to non-responsiveness, insecurity and other reasons.

Conclusion

Ethiopia has made commendable strides in expanding reproductive health services and integrating population concerns into national development frameworks; however, there is persistently high completed cohort fertility, desire for more children, and pronatalist norms which are rooted in slow development, long-term poverty, and labor-based agrarian or pastoralist economic structures. Overall, Ethiopia’s fertility transition is characterized by ‘supply-demand imbalance’ where contraceptive availability expanded faster than the preference and agency for birth limitation. Policy and program implementations have been also constrained with weak domestic political advocacy, fragmented institutional arrangement, and lack of coordination, leaving the fertility and population agenda under the health rationalized FPS. These and other local and global factors can impede the countries progress towards demographic dividend and sustainable development.

While reaping demographic dividend and attaining sustainable development requires socioeconomic transformation, human capital accumulation, and women empowerment, fertility transition serves as a precursor that opens the window for the dividend. In order to accelerate the transition that can open the demographic dividend and prevent fertility stalling, Ethiopia should position its fertility agenda into family planning-population-development nexus, one that harmonizes reproductive autonomy with long-term sustainable development goals. This framework reorients the FPS packages (ICC/BCC and contraceptive counseling) from the narrow spacing perspective into the full components of fertility transition (delaying, spacing, and limiting births), that can advance peoples’ demand, autonomy, and agency towards fertility transition. Advocacy for such fertility transition should also be integrated with renewed focus on ensuring SRH rights, optimizing access to high-quality, equitable, and resilient FPS, with greater investment in long-acting contraceptive methods. To this end, there is a need for political signaling and domestic advocacy revise the population policy based on this holistic framework of reproductive right, universal access to services, demographic dividend, sustainable development, and demographic resilience.
