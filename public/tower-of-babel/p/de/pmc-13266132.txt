Bundibugyo Ebola in the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Uganda: A PHEIC in a countermeasure gap
Authors: Mohamed Abdirahim Omar, Ahmed Abdinasir Abdule
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

The World Health Organization (WHO) designation of the Bundibugyo virus disease outbreak in the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) and Uganda as a Public Health Emergency of International Concern (PHEIC) marks a critical inflection point for regional and global health security. On May 16, 2026, the WHO determined that Ebola disease caused by Bundibugyo virus in the DRC and Uganda met PHEIC criteria under the International Health Regulations. As of May 15, 2026, WHO reported 246 suspected cases and 80 deaths in affected health zones in Ituri Province, DRC, with laboratory-confirmed imported cases reported in Uganda [1]. Both affected countries are Partner States of the East African Community (EAC), a bloc comprising Burundi, DRC, Kenya, Rwanda, Somalia, South Sudan, Uganda, and Tanzania [2]. Therefore, the outbreak should be viewed as a national emergency in both countries and a direct test of EAC health-security coordination.

The current outbreak highlights a dangerous species-specific gap in filovirus preparedness. Over the past decade, major advances have strengthened the response to Ebola disease caused by Orthoebolavirus zairense, including vaccination and monoclonal antibody therapies. However, Bundibugyo virus remains neglected. Identified during the 2007 Uganda outbreak, Bundibugyo ebolavirus is a distinct species with substantial pathogenic potential [3]. Yet, nearly two decades later, WHO reports no licensed vaccines or therapeutics for Bundibugyo virus disease [1]. This creates a countermeasure gap: the response must rely primarily on early detection, isolation, supportive care, infection prevention, safe burial, contact tracing, and community engagement.

The epidemiological context has increased concern. WHO notes a critical detection delay between symptom onset and laboratory confirmation, health worker deaths, and weak contact follow-up in a setting of insecurity and mobility [1]. These are warning signs for health care-associated amplification and community transmission. Ituri Province is a commercial hub bordering Uganda and South Sudan, where undetected cases may cross borders before surveillance responds. National response plans are insufficient.

The EAC should urgently operationalise a regional Bundibugyo virus preparedness and response mechanism. This includes real-time cross-border alerts, harmonized case definitions, joint contact-tracing protocols, rapid specimen referral, shared laboratory capacity, and coordinated risk communication across border routes. Border closures should be avoided; WHO advises against trade restrictions as they push movement into informal crossings and undermine surveillance [1]. A more effective approach is risk-based exit screening, traveller information, rapid referral of symptomatic individuals, and cross-border contact notification.

Health care preparedness must be a regional priority. Facilities in at-risk EAC border districts should immediately strengthen triage for febrile illness, isolation pathways, personal protective equipment, occupational exposure protocols, and infection prevention supervision. Early health worker deaths in the current outbreak are a sentinel signal that facilities can accelerate transmission if infection prevention measures are not reinforced. Community engagement is essential in this regard. Local leaders, religious authorities, transport workers, miners, women's groups, and community health workers should be integrated into surveillance, contact tracing, safe burials, and risk communication.

The outbreak also demands a renewed research and development agenda. The successful rVSV-ZEBOV vaccine trial in Guinea demonstrated that rapid vaccine deployment can transform Ebola outbreak control when effective countermeasures are available [4]. The PALM trial similarly reshaped the treatment for Zaire Ebola virus disease by identifying superior monoclonal antibody therapies [5]. However, these achievements do not automatically translate to Bundibugyo virus disease. Preparedness for one ebolavirus species is not equivalent to preparedness for all species. Candidate Bundibugyo-specific vaccines, pan-ebolavirus vaccines, broad-spectrum therapeutics, and improved diagnostics should be prioritised through ethical, pre-approved, and adaptive trial platforms that can be activated during outbreaks.

In conclusion, the 2026 Bundibugyo virus disease PHEIC in DRC and Uganda is a warning that global Ebola preparedness remains uneven and species-specific. The absence of licenced Bundibugyo-specific vaccines and therapeutics places extraordinary importance on early detection, supportive care, infection prevention, contact tracing, community trust, and regional coordination. Because the outbreak involves two EAC partner states and threatens highly mobile border regions, the EAC must act collectively and immediately. Closing the Bundibugyo countermeasure gap is not only a scientific priority but also an urgent requirement for regional health security.
