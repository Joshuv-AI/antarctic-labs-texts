The neglected second wound: a qualitative study of patients’ experiences of lower limb vascular harvesting for coronary artery bypass grafting
Authors: Si-Yu Liu, Fei Li, Yu-Hong Chen, Shan-Shan Liu, Xiao-Min Zhang, Guo-Zhen Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to understand the experiences and feelings of patients undergoing lower limb vein harvesting for coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG).DesignQualitative, phenomenological research methodology with purposive sampling. Semistructured interview data were analysed using Colaizzi’s seven-step method with the NVivo 12 Plus software.SettingA single-centre study in a Grade A hospital in Nanjing, China.ParticipantsA total of 23 patients who underwent CABG with saphenous vein harvesting between October 2024 and March 2025.ResultsThe participants’ experiences were summarised into four themes: preoperative cognitive differences influencing decision-making, postoperative physiological changes triggering psychological distress, diverse coping strategies and support needs, and reconstruction of body image and identity.ConclusionMedical staff should pay attention to the feelings of patients undergoing CABG with vein harvesting from the lower limbs. By actively implementing shared decision-making, promoting high-quality nursing services, improving multidimensional support systems, and enhancing patients’ psychological and social adaptability, healthcare professionals can improve patients’ perioperative experiences and promote their comprehensive recovery.

Introduction

According to the 2021 Global Burden of Disease study, 31.87 million cases of ischaemic heart disease occurred worldwide in 2021, and coronary artery disease remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality globally.1 2 Coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), a critical surgical procedure for myocardial revascularisation, has favourable long-term outcomes. Globally, more than 20 million CABG procedures have been performed since the technique was introduced in the 1960s.3 4 CABG requires the use of autologous arteries or veins as grafts, with the great saphenous vein being the most commonly employed bridge vessel for non-left anterior descending coronary regions, in 80%–90% of patients.5 Post-grafting local inflammatory responses and impaired venous return in the lower limbs can lead to various complications, including cyanosis, swelling, numbness, pain and limited mobility. Studies reported incidence rates for lower limb oedema of approximately 41%, numbness or paraesthesia of up to 61% and incision infection of 1.4%–8.2%.69 These complications delay ambulation, prolong hospital stays, increase medical costs and may necessitate surgical intervention in severe cases.10 Although lower limb complications impair patient recovery, the clinical focus predominantly centres on cardiac outcomes, sternal wound healing, graft patency and cardiac rehabilitation, whereas the discomfort associated with lower limb grafting is often regarded as an anticipated clinical cost rather than receiving adequate attention.1114 The existing literature reports the incidence and influencing factors of lower limb complications, but these studies primarily concentrate on physiological parameters and clinical outcomes, lacking a systematic exploration of how these symptoms are experienced by patients and contribute to their psychological and physical burdens. These strains may compromise the patient’s rehabilitation experience, thereby affecting their overall satisfaction with treatment and adherence to rehabilitation protocols.

The biopsychosocial model emphasises15 that disease impacts biological, psychological and social functional domains and that postoperative care must focus on the patient’s overall well-being. Among these factors, patient experience serves as the core pathway to the psychosocial dimension, whereas symptom burden is the key mechanism linking biological symptoms to psychosocial functional outcomes. Current qualitative studies on patients with CABG primarily concentrate on preoperative psychological states, postoperative cardiac rehabilitation, caregiver experiences and barriers to returning to work.1621 These studies provide valuable insights into the overall psychosocial journey of patients with CABG but fail to centre their research on the lower limb as the vascular graft site. The existing literature has not systematically examined patients’ preoperative perceptions of lower limb grafts, their actual experiences with postoperative lower limb symptoms, coping strategies for limb discomfort, or care expectations during rehabilitation. Thus, the present study employed the biopsychosocial model as its theoretical framework and a phenomenological approach to thoroughly analyse the genuine experiences of patients with CABG regarding their lower limb grafts, thereby providing a basis for improving postoperative lower limb management and enhancing overall nursing quality.

Methods

Research paradigms and study design

The interpretivist paradigm22 posits that objective reality does not exist independently but is progressively constructed through individuals’ subjective perception and meaning-making. Employing a descriptive phenomenological approach for this study, the participating researchers have actively suspended their theoretical assumptions and preconceptions, adopting an open-minded attitude to understand the subjective experiences of patients with CABG regarding their graft limb. This study was reported in accordance with the SRQR (Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research) checklist.23

Researcher background and reflection

The Department of Cardiothoracic Vascular Surgery at Nanjing First Hospital operates three cardiac surgery wards and performs approximately 900 CABG procedures annually. All members of the research team were registered nurses with more than 10 years of clinical experience in cardiovascular nursing. The primary interviewer (Liu), a cardiac surgery nurse at the centre, was not assigned to the same ward as the participants and did not directly participate in their treatment or care. This arrangement facilitated trust building with participants but may have introduced social desirability bias, as participants might conceal their dissatisfaction with the nursing team out of gratitude or respect. To mitigate these risks, the interviewer maintained reflective journals throughout data collection and analysis. Additionally, the team held regular discussions, and a second coder independently reviewed the analysis. The investigators informed the participants that the interviews were conducted solely for research purposes, all responses were anonymised, participation did not affect their medical care, and participants could withdraw at any time.

Study participants and sampling strategy

The research team employed purposive sampling supplemented by maximum variation sampling strategies to ensure diversity of perspectives, including gender, age, educational level, presence of comorbidity, vascular access method, and type of health insurance. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Patients underwent CABG with great saphenous vein grafting. (2) Participants had normal auditory and verbal comprehension abilities and were proficient in Mandarin. (3) Patients provided informed consent to document voluntary participation. Exclusion criteria were: (1) Presence of other severe organic diseases or malignancies. (2) Prior history of lower limb conditions, such as varicose veins or joint replacement. From October 2024 to March 2025, the research team screened eligible patients in the cardiac surgery ward of the research centre. One day before surgery, patients were approached in person and informed about the study objectives, methodology, voluntary participation principles and confidentiality measures. Eligible patients were preliminarily screened 3–5 days after surgery. Family members were present during the informed consent discussion, but the final decisions were made by the patients themselves, who personally signed a written informed consent form. Interviews were conducted 1 day before discharge. Of 26 patients who met the inclusion criteria, three declined participation due to a lack of interest or unwillingness to participate in the audio recording. Thus, 23 patients participated in the study. Their baseline characteristics are detailed in table 1.

Data collection

For this study, semistructured, in-depth interviews were conducted. Based on the research objectives, existing literature and consultations with clinical experts, a preliminary interview outline was developed and tested in two patients. The complete interview topic guide in both Mandarin Chinese and English is provided in online supplemental file 1. The final interview outline consisted of the following questions:

All interviews were conducted in a department meeting room in a quiet and comfortable environment, lasting 30–60 min, with full audio recording. The results were anonymised using reference numbers (P1–P23) to protect the privacy of the participants. Interview data were strictly confidential and intended solely for research purposes. During the interviews, the researchers maintained an open and neutral stance, employing follow-up questions (eg, “Could you elaborate on the specific discomfort sensations in your leg?”) to gather more detailed information. The sample size was determined based on the principle of information saturation.24 By the 18th interview, additional information had significantly diminished; by the 21st interview, core themes had stabilised; and by the 23rd interview, no new meaningful units or thematic categories had emerged, confirming information saturation. A total of 23 participants were ultimately included, with their experiences spanning the entire trajectory from preoperative cognition and postoperative acute-phase symptoms to rehabilitation adaptation, ensuring the phenomenological completeness of the research findings.

Data analysis

Within 24 hours of the interview, the researchers transcribed the audio recordings into text and encoded the transcripts using NVivo 12Plus software, employing the Colaizzi seven-step analysis method, as detailed in table 2. For publication purposes, the transcripts were translated into English by the first author, who is bilingual in Mandarin and English. Back-translation was performed by a second bilingual researcher to ensure accuracy. The participant quotations presented in this paper were translated from Mandarin into English.

Reliability strategy

The interviews were independently coded by two researchers. Discrepancies were resolved through group discussions with reflective suspensions maintained throughout the process to set aside preconceptions and conduct analyses with an open and neutral attitude. The results were verified by the participants, and all data were fully preserved to ensure the traceability of the research process.

Results

In total, four core themes and 16 subthemes were identified in this study.

Preoperative cognitive differences influence decision-making

Defensive avoidance

Some respondents adopted defensive avoidance strategies because of anxiety and proactively delegated the decision-making authority to family members. P3: “The doctor said a blood vessel needs to be removed from my leg, but how could I possibly understand that? I had my son handle the discussions and signing”. P7: “I was afraid; I didn’t try to understand anything—I just followed my daughter’s advice”. These statements indicate that the patients were not incapable of comprehending medical information but rather chose to avoid it actively, shifting the decision-making responsibility to trusted family members to alleviate their psychological burden.

Selective attention

Some respondents exhibited attention bias, meaning that individuals prioritised allocating attention resources to life-threatening risks when facing survival threats, while actively ignoring non-life-threatening information.25 P1 stated, “I didn’t consider my leg issue before the surgery; as long as the heart surgery was successful, whether I had a leg or not didn’t matter”. P13 similarly remarked, “At that time, I didn’t think about my leg at all”. This reflects that in the pre-CABG context, cardiac issues were perceived by patients as ‘life-and-death matters’, whereas vascular harvesting from the lower limb was regarded as a ‘secondary concern’. This uneven allocation of attentional resources led patients to psychologically filter out information about their lower limbs even when such information was available.

Uncertainty and hesitation

Respondents exhibited hesitation in weighing the two approaches for CABG vascular harvesting from the lower limb (traditional open surgery vs minimally invasive endoscopic surgery), each with its own advantages and disadvantages requiring intraoperative decision-making. P4 stated, “The minimally invasive approach results in smaller incisions, but it’s not reimbursable; if unsuccessful, we’d have to switch back to open surgery—wouldn’t that mean enduring the same risks twice? It took extensive family discussion before we made up our minds”. P9 sought advice from multiple healthcare providers due to concerns about complications: “I heard this surgery requires full leg dissection—it sounds terrifying. I visited three hospitals before finalising my choice”. These hesitations reflect the inherent conflict between the patients’ concerns about lower limb risks and their desire for cardiac benefits.

Composed and informed understanding

After gaining knowledge about the surgical techniques and sites for vascular harvesting, some respondents demonstrated calmness and composure, and they actively participated in decision-making. P2: “I saw on the corridor information board what vascular harvesting entails. The physician explained that due to my diabetes, minimally invasive vascular harvesting was recommended, which I found acceptable. I participated fully in both the discussion and signature process”. P16: “There’s no need to worry; my wife is a nurse who explained the procedure to me”. With a clear understanding of the vascular harvesting procedure, the patients’ anxiety substantially diminished, and they became more willing to engage in decision-making processes.

Postoperative physiological changes trigger psychological distress

Early fear of movement caused by limb pain

In the early postoperative period, the respondents exhibited fear of movement due to limb pain. P5 described, “It’s manageable if the leg remains still, but sudden positional changes are unbearable… Even slight stretching or pulling causes intense pain”. P11 reported that nocturnal pain disrupted sleep: “My leg jitters and hurts during sleep… The slightest movement wakes me up”. P13 noted, “The moment I stand up from bed, the pain is unbearable; I have to mentally prepare myself before getting out of bed”. These accounts reveal a direct correlation between pain and movement; patients avoid active lower limb movement due to fear of triggering pain, thereby hindering early ambulation.

Changes in body integrity and concerns about wound healing

After removing the elastic bandage from the lower limb, the condition of the leg wound and skin had a visual impact on patients. P2: “There are wounds at three locations—from the thigh to the calf; the entire leg is injured”. P8: “When changing my pants, I saw my leg—oh, it was unbearable”. Postoperative exudate from lower limb incisions raised concerns regarding wound healing. P2: “The wound below keeps oozing fluid; with my elevated blood sugar, I’m worried about infection”. The interviewees’ genuine experiences indicated that the visual appearance of the wound and the associated exudate not only caused physical discomfort but also triggered concerns and anxiety about compromised body integrity and poor wound healing.

Helplessness and frustration due to impaired lower limb function

The respondents felt helpless due to swelling and weakness in their lower limbs. P3: “This leg has lost all strength; I need assistance to walk”. P6 described, “For the first two days, the leg was extremely painful; after standing up, it could not bear weight and became weak”. The decline in function severely undermined the patients’ self-efficacy, making daily activities, such as standing and walking, difficult and leading to a sense of frustration about ‘losing control over their bodies’.

Discrepancies between expectations and reality, leading to anger

Respondents experienced emotional distress when their actual postoperative lower limb function differed from their preoperative expectations. P11 stated, “I didn’t expect the incision to be so long; my entire leg was injured”. P13 reported, “There was a large red, painful area on my leg; I applied topical medication for several days. I originally came for cardiac consultation but ended up seeing a leg specialist”. P14 added, “When I woke up, I felt something was wrong with my leg—I knew it was over”. This finding illustrates counterfactual thinking; when actual outcomes fall short of expectations, patients develop regret and may attribute it to insufficient information.26 The discrepancy between expectations and reality stemmed from preoperative information limited to ‘wounds’ without disclosure of potential functional impairments or long-term discomfort, causing patients a postoperative feeling of ‘deception’ and, consequently, anger and disappointment.

Diverse response strategies and support needs

Self-tolerance

To avoid worrying about their families or disturbing medical staff, the respondents chose to endure the symptoms of lower limb discomfort alone. P4: “I have a large bruise on my leg; I didn’t ask the doctor, and the doctor was quite busy”. P7: “I can tolerate this; I don’t want my daughter to worry”. P9 tolerated the discomfort due to gender roles: “I’m a man; this pain isn’t significant—I’ll just endure it”. P17: “My leg feels sore and weak; I see everyone experiencing the same”. These behaviours reflect the patients’ mindset of conserving medical resources, misconceptions about pain and family responsibility.

Going with the flow

Recognising that lower limb discomfort is an inevitable process, some respondents faced it with a calm mindset. P2 stated, “I do not feel like it is a big deal. It will just heal slowly. Once the wound heals, I should not feel anything”. P5 demonstrated a sense of ‘cost awareness’: “Recovery can only proceed slowly now; there’s nothing anyone can do, and we can’t use someone else’s blood vessels”. This approach of ‘going with the flow’ represents both a reluctant compromise and a psychological adaptation resulting from patients rationalising lower limb injuries as a ‘life-saving cost’.

Proactive rehabilitation

Some respondents actively sought rehabilitation knowledge and employed self-adjustment to alleviate their lower limb discomfort. P12 reported, “Every morning after getting up, I move my legs slightly in bed, performing leg elevation and flexion exercises before walking; this significantly improves my condition”. P15 improved symptoms by acquiring nursing knowledge: “Initially, my legs were swollen and numb. After consulting the nurse, I learned that the surgery had impaired blood circulation in my legs, so I consistently performed ankle pump exercises daily”. This experience demonstrates that patients who receive targeted preoperative guidance or possess higher health literacy exhibit more proactive health management behaviours.

Demand for professional information guidance

In this study, most respondents indicated that healthcare providers paid insufficient attention to the affected limb, and the patients expressed a strong desire for professional guidance. P2 complained, “No one has explained the potential long-term effects of this leg injury; very little educational information was provided”. P4 noted, “There is extensive bruising on the leg, yet neither the doctor nor the nurse addressed it”. P10 stated, “I don’t understand either; I hope they could explain the details more thoroughly”. P3 expressed confusion about the underlying mechanism of the symptoms: “The leg swelled severely initially, and I didn’t understand why it needed to be bandaged”. These experiences demonstrate that patients not only need to understand the manifestations of postoperative lower limb discomfort but also expect to learn about the causes of the symptoms and appropriate management strategies.

Expectation for continuous support and care

Some respondents expressed anxiety due to the lack of medical guidance on returning home. P6: “I will not go home after discharge; I will stay at a small hospital in our hometown. I still cannot walk properly with my leg”. P20: “There are still a few stitches on my leg that are pulling very tightly. Can I be discharged like this?” Owing to insufficient rehabilitation knowledge, some respondents feared that they might perform their lower limb rehabilitation exercises incorrectly, leading to accidents or injuries, and thus requested home-based rehabilitation guidance. P4: “My home is on the third floor; can I climb stairs normally after returning home? When can I start squatting?” P23: “The doctor recommended rehabilitation exercises—how should I perform them at home? How can I protect the wound on my leg from reopening?” Other respondents highlighted the need for family and social support due to factors such as prolonged postoperative recovery, limited mobility and financial constraints. P5: “Minimally invasive vascular surgery results in smaller wounds, but these costs are not reimbursable”. P10: “My home is right next to the hospital, making it very convenient to visit”. P18: “My husband doesn’t believe I’ll recover in three months; he thinks I’m lying to him, so I’m staying with my daughter first”.

Body image and identity reconstruction

Body image and social avoidance

With the restoration of bodily awareness and resolution of the core surgical issue, respondents began to focus on the ‘lost veins’. P10: “I asked the doctor whether the removed vein could regrow. The doctor said no, adding that it wouldn’t affect future life, but the leg would definitely be different”. P12: “It was necessary to save my life, but I’ll definitely have impaired limb coordination afterward”. P13: “I’m now an incomplete person”. Some respondents experienced shame due to exposed scars: P20: “Such a large scar looks so ugly; I definitely can’t wear a skirt anymore”. This experience demonstrates that the sense of bodily incompleteness and shame stemming from lower limb trauma and scar appearance leads to social avoidance—patients’ reluctance to be scrutinised by others.

Changes in social roles

Some respondents reported that impaired lower limb function restricted their occupational capabilities and ability to fulfil family responsibilities. P1: “I used to work on construction sites, always climbing those scaffolds; I don’t know if I can do that anymore”. P20: “I can’t even stand to cook now; my son has to work while taking care of me—it’s really tough!” Limitations in physical function prevent patients from fulfilling their original family and social roles, leading to a sense of identity discrepancy.

Psychological adjustment and acceptance

As their physical function gradually recovered, the respondents began to re-examine the meaning of life and their self-worth. P9 viewed pain as a life-saving price: “This blood vessel was removed to save my life; any hardship endured was worthwhile!” P19 transformed forced rest into an opportunity for self-care: “I’ve been traveling extensively for business all these years; now I can finally rest without exhaustion and with peace of mind”. P2 reinterpreted the scar as a positive symbol: “This leg is my hero—I must thank it properly; this scar is my medal, constantly reminding me to live well”. This psychological shift reflects the patients’ internal adjustment and proactive reconstruction.

Discussion

This study identified four core themes experienced by patients with CABG during vascular harvesting from the lower limb: preoperative cognitive differences, psychological impacts arising from postoperative physiological changes, diverse coping strategies and support expectations, ultimately culminating in the reconstruction of body image and social identity. This experiential journey exemplifies interplay within the biopsychosocial model.

Implications of preoperative cognitive differences for shared decision-making (SDM)

In this study, some respondents opted for close relatives to exercise full authority over medical decision-making. This choice was influenced by factors such as the traditional medical authority model, disparities in patient health literacy and insufficient shared decision-making (SDM) training. The adoption rate of SDM in China’s medical settings remains relatively low.2729 Moreover, within family-centric cultures, relatives often shield patients from negative information, further prompting patients to voluntarily transfer decision-making power to their families.30 31 The present study also found that some respondents prioritised cardiac issues above all else and considered lower limbs as non-fatal sampling sites, leading to their marginalisation in perception—even when patients possess the necessary understanding, they may actively disregard ‘secondary’ information due to psychological stress. When postoperative lower limb experiences diverge from expectations, significant psychological dissonance arises, undermining rehabilitation motivation and treatment compliance. Previous research indicated that implementing SDM enhances patient self-efficacy and exercise adherence.32 However, existing qualitative SDM studies predominantly focus on factors, such as patients’ knowledge gaps, cultural habits or lack of standardised protocols,3335 with limited discussion of ‘attention resource allocation’. Therefore, when applying SDM, healthcare providers must not only deliver concise, unbiased information but also actively guide patients to shift their focus from a ‘single cardiac focus’ to ‘holistic systemic recovery’. For example, using anatomical diagrams to illustrate lower limb vascular pathways and potential postoperative discomfort (eg, swelling or numbness affecting mobility) helps patients comprehend the procedure and develop a comprehensive bodily perspective. From ‘passive endurance’ to ‘active prevention’: reflections on nursing humanities

In this study, the patients experienced postoperative physiological changes, including limb pain, swelling, wound exudation and diminished lower limb function. These experiences showed considerable discrepancies compared with the preoperative expectations. Many patients remarked, “I didn’t expect the leg pain to be so severe”, whereas healthcare providers often responded with “It’s nothing”. This sense of neglect aligns with the findings of Zhang et al,36 highlighting a notable gap between the care perceived by patients and the care reported by nurses who may feel that the patient concerns have been addressed, whereas patients experience a lack of attention and understanding. Additionally, some respondents reported a sense of deprivation, believing that the removal of seemingly less important blood vessels adversely affected postoperative mobility and undermined their confidence in physical activity. Notably, some patients transitioned from passively enduring discomfort to actively participating in rehabilitation by independently researching or consulting healthcare providers. This underscores a critical gap in current nursing practices. The management of lower limb discomfort should shift from ‘passive response’ to ‘active prevention and control’. Care is at the core of nursing; we recommend that healthcare providers not only deliver professional technical interventions to address patients’ physiological needs but also attentively recognise and respond to their psychological needs, practising humanistic care to enhance patients’ confidence and improve their experience of lower limb rehabilitation.37 38

Lack of continuous support: establishment of a multidimensional support system

This study found that impaired lower limb function not only affects patients’ daily activities but also compromises their ability to fulfil family and occupational roles. Some patients experience guilt due to concerns about becoming a burden on their families and the desire for continuous professional guidance. Current continuous care primarily focuses on overall cardiac rehabilitation,39 40 whereas the present study highlights that limitations specific to lower limb function represent a prominent concern for patients. Questions such as “when can I climb stairs normally”, “can I squat” and “how to prevent wound dehiscence” are frequently overlooked in routine education. Multidisciplinary team-assisted continuous care improves patients’ quality of life and disease-related outcomes.41 It is recommended to establish a tripartite collaborative continuous care model involving hospitals, communities and families using the Internet to provide home-based rehabilitation guidance. Additionally, family members should be encouraged to participate in rehabilitation skills training to maintain patients’ self-worth and a sense of family belonging. In this study, patients exhibited both gratitude and guilt toward their families, desiring independence, yet were compelled to rely on them while also seeking emotional support and understanding from their partners. A prior study confirmed that timely counselling, emotional support and professional guidance are effective measures for alleviating patient anxiety.42 During rehabilitation, patients and their families should jointly receive education on lower limb-related knowledge and rehabilitation skill training to enhance their family caregiving capabilities. They should collaboratively develop lower limb functional exercise plans, assist in recording symptom self-assessment forms (eg, pain, swelling and infection) or use smart wristbands for exercise reminders.43 Progressive rehabilitation incentives should be implemented to strengthen patients’ sense of control and improve their adherence to rehabilitation.

Body image and identity reconstruction: from the ‘damaged self’ to ‘post-traumatic growth’

This study revealed that a minority of patients demonstrated post-traumatic psychological resilience by viewing scars as medals, thereby reassigning value to their traumatic experiences, which is consistent with findings in Zhang et al44 regarding the life experiences of amputees. When patients experience physical trauma with value and meaning, their psychological adaptation levels improve considerably. Psychosocial adaptation refers to the comprehensive adjustment of an individual’s emotions, cognition and habits in coping with illness, playing a crucial role in both physical/mental health and social functioning.45 46 While previous studies predominantly focused on negative experiences, such as scar-related shame, inferiority, social avoidance and anxiety,47 48 the present study identified the dual significance of lower limb scars in patients undergoing CABG—they serve both as ‘neglected injuries’ and ‘the price of survival’. The shift from acceptance to pride observed in patients correlates with psychological mechanisms related to post-traumatic resilience, with positive self-cognitive reconstruction playing a pivotal role in postoperative mental health. Therefore, nursing staff should address scar management not merely through cosmetic concerns but also by actively facilitating post-traumatic growth. Post-traumatic growth49 denotes positive psychological changes experienced after enduring highly challenging life circumstances, manifesting in five domains: appreciation for life, interpersonal relationships, new possibilities, personal strength and mental transformation. Nurses can assist patients in reconstructing their cognition by viewing scars as indicators of successful surgery, inviting peers who have achieved growth to share their journeys and employing targeted questioning techniques (eg, “Some patients describe their leg as a hero and their scar as a medal—do you share this sentiment?”). This establishes a positive psychological adaptation pathway encompassing cognitive reappraisal, psychological acceptance and post-traumatic growth, ultimately facilitating comprehensive physical, mental and social recovery for patients and enabling their smooth reintegration into family and society.

Strengths and limitations

This study employed a descriptive phenomenological approach focusing on lower limb vascular grafting during CABG, a recovery dimension that has previously received limited attention, and delved into patients’ first-hand, subjective experiences. However, as a single-centre qualitative study, the data were derived solely from patients’ perspectives and did not incorporate views from family members or healthcare providers, potentially limiting the richness and generalisability of the findings. Because the interviewers were cardiac surgery nurses from the same hospital, the participants may have concealed their dissatisfaction with the nursing team out of gratitude or respect, potentially introducing a social desirability bias. Furthermore, the study excluded patients who developed severe lower limb infections postoperatively, resulting in the absence of data for this critical subgroup. Future research should conduct multicentre studies that integrate diverse perspectives from patients, families, and healthcare providers and specialised phenomenological investigations targeting patients with severe lower limb infections to further enrich and deepen our understanding of this recovery issue.

Conclusions

The actual experiences of patients after CABG surgery regarding lower limb vascular recovery are complex and diverse, resulting from the interplay of psychological, physiological and social factors, and cannot be simplified to merely ‘mild postoperative discomfort’ as often described in medical records. Effective CABG rehabilitation requires comprehensive cardiac and limb care, emphasising the success of coronary revascularisation and actively addressing the physiological changes, psychological impact and social role restructuring associated with lower limb vascular graft placement. To this end, healthcare providers should actively implement SDM to help patients establish realistic expectations, establish multidisciplinary teams for the early prevention and management of lower limb complications, and fully leverage social resources to enhance patients’ psychosocial adaptability and achieve synergistic rehabilitation of both the heart and lower limbs.
