Engineered GLP-1R-targeting nanoplatforms: multimodal therapeutics in human diseases
Authors: Juan Zeng, Xinxin Tang, Dalian Qin, Lu Yu, Xiaogang Zhou, Chi Feng, Jianing Mi, Hudan Pan, Jianming Wu, Bin Huang, Anguo Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlucagon-like peptide-1 receptor (GLP-1R) is a versatile therapeutic target that regulates glucose homeostasis, cardiovascular function, neuroprotection, and immune responses. Native GLP-1 undergoes rapid enzymatic degradation, limiting its clinical utility. To overcome these challenges, engineered GLP-1 analogues and nanotechnology-enabled delivery systems have emerged. This review summarizes recent advances in GLP-1R-targeted nanoplatforms, including lipid nanoparticles, polymeric carriers, inorganic nanoparticles, and biomimetic scaffolds that improve peptide stability, prolong circulation half-life, and enable selective tissue targeting. Ligand engineering approaches, such as site-specific peptide conjugation and fatty acid modifications, enhance receptor affinity and targeting precision. Stimuli-responsive release strategies that respond to glucose levels, pH shifts, and redox conditions restrict drug activation to diseased microenvironments. Multifunctional theranostic constructs co-encapsulate imaging probes with therapeutics, permitting real-time monitoring of biodistribution and receptor engagement. We discuss key translational challenges, including scalable manufacturing processes, quality control of particle size, ligand density, and release kinetics, and long-term biosafety concerns associated with inorganic materials and immune activation. Variability in GLP-1R expression among patient populations influences delivery efficacy, motivating personalized strategies guided by artificial intelligence and digital twin modeling. Emerging applications in neurodegenerative disorders, oncology, cardiovascular disease and immunology demonstrate the expanding clinical potential of GLP-1R nanomedicines. By integrating insights from materials science, pharmacology and preclinical studies, this review provides a cohesive framework for the rational design, evaluation and clinical translation of next-generation GLP-1R-targeted nanotherapeutics. Future directions include integration with genomic profiling and real-time biosensors to optimize therapeutic outcomes. Introduction

Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) is an incretin hormone secreted by intestinal L cells [1]. It enhances glucose-dependent insulin secretion, suppresses glucagon release, delays gastric emptying, and promotes satiety [2]. These effects are mediated through the GLP-1 receptor (GLP-1R), a class B G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) expressed in pancreatic β-cells, enteric and central neurons, cardiomyocytes, vascular endothelial cells, immune cells, and renal epithelia [3]. Upon activation, GLP-1R increases intracellular cyclic AMP levels, which in turn activates protein kinase A (PKA) and exchange protein directly activated by cAMP (Epac) pathways [3]. These signaling cascades mediate a broad range of metabolic and cytoprotective effects. These include regulation of cardiovascular function, neuroprotection, lipid metabolism, immune responses, and hepatic lipid accumulation. Dysfunction in GLP-1R signaling has been implicated in multiple pathologies, including type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), obesity, atherosclerosis, Parkinson’s disease (PD), Alzheimer’s disease (AD), non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), and several cancers. This diverse involvement underscores GLP-1R’s potential as a multifaceted therapeutic target [3].

Over the past century, scientific advances have progressively shaped the landscape of GLP-1 research, from the early discovery of the incretin effect and preproglucagon gene structure to the clinical development of GLP-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) and, more recently, nanotechnology-based drug delivery systems. These historical milestones, which have driven the evolution of GLP-1 biology and therapeutic innovation (Fig. 1). Engineered GLP-1R-targeted nanoplatforms are reshaping precision therapeutics by extending incretin biology beyond conventional antidiabetic formulations [4]. Nanotechnology now augments the stability, circulation time and tissue selectivity of GLP-1RAs with potent insulinotropic and glucoregulatory activity. Contemporary nanocarrier architectures include lipidic, polymeric, inorganic and biomimetic scaffolds, each conferring tunable pharmacokinetics, biodistribution profiles and administration routes [5]. Ligand engineering through site-specific peptide conjugation, acyl anchoring or nanobody fusion sharpens receptor affinity and tissue discrimination. Incorporation of glucose, pH, redox or externally responsive release motifs confines drug action to diseased microenvironments, while multifunctional theranostic designs couple imaging probes with therapeutic cargo to enable real-time visualization of biodistribution and receptor engagement.

Despite these advances, significant challenges remain. Scalable and reproducible manufacturing requires rigorous control over physicochemical parameters such as ligand density, particle size, and release kinetics to meet regulatory standards [6]. Concerns regarding long-term biosafety, particularly in relation to inorganic components and repeated immune exposure, remain insufficiently characterized [7]. Moreover, heterogeneity in GLP-1R expression across tissues and patient populations limits the universality of existing delivery systems and underscores the need for adaptable, personalized platforms guided by artificial intelligence (AI) and digital twin modeling [8].

This review synthesizes recent progress across materials science, pharmacology, and clinical research to provide a cohesive framework linking nanocarrier composition, ligand engineering, stimuli-responsive release mechanisms, and theranostic functionality. Translational barriers related to manufacturing, regulatory approval, and biosafety are critically examined, and prospective strategies for overcoming these obstacles are discussed. Furthermore, emerging applications beyond metabolic disease, including neurodegenerative, oncologic, cardiovascular, and autoimmune contexts, are explored to delineate the expanding therapeutic frontier of GLP-1R-targeted nanomedicine. By integrating multidisciplinary insights and highlighting both progress and challenges, this review aims to inform the rational design and clinical translation of next-generation programmable GLP-1R nanotherapeutics.

GLP-1 and GLP-1 receptor

Structure, distribution and signaling pathways

Structure of GLP-1R

GLP-1R is a class B GPCR characterized by seven transmembrane helices and a large extracellular N-terminal domain [9]. Recent 2.8 Å cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) reconstructions reveal that agonist engagement drives an outward swing of transmembrane helix 6 and creates an open intracellular cavity that pre-associates with Gs, clarifying how the receptor achieves rapid activation and sustained cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) output [10]. A complementary cryo-EM series shows how the unimolecular triple agonist retatrutide contacts extracellular loop 1 in a configuration conserved across GLP-1R, gastric inhibitory polypeptide receptor (GIPR), and glucagon receptor (GCGR), providing a structural blueprint for multi-receptor ligand design [11].

GLP-1 receptor distribution

Initially considered pancreas-specific, GLP-1R is now recognized in numerous tissues [12] (Fig. 2). In the central nervous system (CNS), it is abundant in the hypothalamus and brainstem nuclei, including the arcuate nucleus and area postrema [13–15]. Activation of central GLP-1R influences satiety, nausea, and memory formation, as GLP-1 also acts as a brain stem neurotransmitter [16, 17]. Within the gastrointestinal (GI) tract, GLP-1R localizes to vagal afferent fibers, enteric neurons, and Brunner glands [18–20], and to gastric mucosa and smooth muscle cells that regulate gastric emptying [21–23]. Normal hepatocytes and thyroid C-cells show little or no receptor, mitigating concerns over direct hepatic or thyroid toxicities [24, 25]. In the cardiovascular system, GLP-1R is present in atrial cardiomyocytes, sinoatrial node cells, endothelium, and vascular smooth muscle [22], as well as in the media of renal and pulmonary arteries [26, 27]. Immune cells generally express low GLP-1R, yet macrophages and T lymphocytes carry functional levels, suggesting an immunomodulatory axis [28–30]. Transcriptomic surveys have expanded this map by detecting receptor transcripts in white and brown adipocytes, renal tubular epithelium, and activated microglia, where stimulation blunts nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) signaling and cytokine release, thereby linking GLP-1R to metabolic and neuro-immune regulation [31].

Collectively, these findings highlight the multimodal therapeutic potential of engineered GLP-1R-targeting nanoplatforms across metabolic, neurological, cardiovascular, renal, and GI diseases

GLP-1 signaling pathway

Dysregulation of the GLP-1/GLP-1R signaling cascade, such as diminished ligand secretion, receptor desensitization, or biased signaling, disrupts systemic homeostasis and contributes to pathological states including insulin resistance (IR), β-cell dysfunction, endothelial impairment, chronic inflammation, and neurodegeneration. Upon ligand binding, GLP-1R predominantly couples with Gs proteins to stimulate adenylate cyclase, leading to increased intracellular cAMP levels [32, 33]. This rise in cAMP activates both PKA and exchange proteins directly activated by cAMP (Epac), which collectively augment insulin transcription, secretion, β-cell proliferation, and survival—mechanisms essential for glucose homeostasis and diabetes control [34, 35]. Beyond these classical pathways, GLP-1R signaling engages multiple other intracellular cascades, including PI3K/Akt, MAPK/ERK, AMPK, and Wnt/β-catenin, enabling its pleiotropic therapeutic potential. The PI3K/Akt pathway cooperates with cAMP/PKA to mediate β-cell survival, improve insulin sensitivity in muscle and adipose tissues, reduce oxidative stress, and protect vascular endothelial function [36, 37]. In neuronal systems, Akt phosphorylation downstream of GLP-1R contributes to neuronal resilience in models of Alzheimer’s and PD [38]. Moreover, GLP-1R-mediated EGFR transactivation activates the Ras/Raf/MEK/ERK axis, promoting β-cell regeneration, angiogenesis in ischemic myocardium, and neuroprotection via dopaminergic neuron preservation and inflammation mitigation [39]. Crosstalk with the AMPK signaling pathway, a key regulator of cellular energy balance, plays an important role. GLP-1RAs stimulate AMPK to suppress hepatic gluconeogenesis, enhance peripheral glucose uptake, and promote lipid oxidation, offering therapeutic benefit in obesity, type 2 diabetes, and NAFLD [40, 41]. In diabetes and obesity, AMPK activation inhibits hepatic gluconeogenesis, enhances glucose uptake in muscle and adipose tissues, and promotes fatty acid oxidation [41]. GLP-1R signaling further intersects with Wnt/β-catenin signaling via PKA-mediated stabilization of β-catenin. This axis is implicated in pancreatic islet regeneration, osteoblast differentiation, and bone remodeling [42–44]. This axis is implicated in pancreatic islet regeneration, osteoblast differentiation, and bone remodeling [45]. Importantly, receptor internalization dynamics modulate signaling duration and bias. Ligand-bound GLP-1Rs internalize via clathrin-coated pits into Rab5+ endosomes, where cAMP production persists before recycling via Rab11 or degradation via Rab7-positive lysosomes. Agonists biased toward sustained endosomal signaling enhance ERK/CREB activation and metabolic control [46]. The β-arrestin-2 pathway adds further complexity: sparing β-arrestin-2 can prolong metabolic benefits and reduce tachyphylaxis, guiding next-generation drug design [47]. In vivo, these signaling pathways translate into multi-organ protective effects. GLP-1R activation supports cardiovascular function by enhancing myocardial contractility, reducing ischemia-reperfusion injury, and inducing vasodilation and natriuresis [48–52]. In the CNS, it offers neuroprotection by modulating apoptosis, synaptic plasticity, and neuroinflammatory responses [53–55]. Even tissues with relatively low GLP-1R expression, such as the liver and intestinal epithelium, can benefit indirectly through systemic metabolic improvements and paracrine signaling, as evidenced in conditions like NAFLD and inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) [56, 57]. These mechanistic insights are substantiated by large-scale outcome trials. For example, the FLOW trial showed semaglutide significantly reduced renal and cardiovascular events in diabetic chronic kidney disease patients [58]. Meta-analyses also affirm GLP-1RAs’ benefit in heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) [59]. Furthermore, receptor overexpression in insulinomas and neuroendocrine tumors provides a basis for GLP-1R-targeted diagnostics and therapeutics [60, 61].

In summary, GLP-1R is not merely a metabolic receptor but a central signaling hub with extensive crosstalk across cAMP/PKA, PI3K/Akt, ERK, AMPK, Wnt/β-catenin, and NF-κB pathways, orchestrating multi-organ protection in metabolic, cardiovascular, and neurodegenerative diseases.

GLP-1RAs

The natural ligand, GLP-1, is a 30-amino-acid incretin peptide secreted by intestinal L cells, effectively stimulating glucose-dependent insulin secretion, inhibiting glucagon release, slowing gastric emptying, and promoting satiety [62–64]. However, native GLP-1 undergoes rapid enzymatic degradation by dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4), resulting in a short half-life (~ 2 min), thus limiting its clinical applicability [65, 66]. To address this limitation, various long-acting GLP-1 analogues have been developed, designed to prolong in vivo efficacy and enhance therapeutic potential (Table 1).

These analogues act as high-affinity GLP-1RAs, achieving sustained receptor activation and clinical outcomes by circumventing rapid enzymatic degradation. First-generation GLP-1RAs include exenatide and liraglutide [67]. Exenatide, derived from Heloderma lizard venom, exhibits resistance to DPP-4 degradation with a half-life of approximately 2–4 h [68, 69], and its formulation in poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) microspheres (Bydureon® BCise®) enables once-weekly dosing by prolonging release kinetics [70]. Liraglutide, an acylated GLP-1 analogue, extends its half-life to 12–14 h via binding to albumin [71, 72]. Next-generation agonists, such as dulaglutide and semaglutide, achieve further prolonged half-lives (approximately 5–7 days) through IgG-Fc fusion or advanced molecular modifications, enabling once-weekly administration [73–75]. More recently, poly-agonist peptides have been developed to target multiple metabolic receptors simultaneously. Tirzepatide, the first dual GIP/GLP-1 agonist, received US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval in 2023 (Zepbound®) after demonstrating superior efficacy over semaglutide, including ≈ 22% weight loss and enhanced glycaemic control [76–78]. Further expanding this paradigm, triple agonists such as retatrutide (GLP-1/GIP/glucagon) are in Phase III trials, with Phase II data reporting weight reductions exceeding 24% and favorable tolerability [79–82]. These agents exploit complementary metabolic pathways to enhance insulinotropic, anorectic, lipolytic, and thermogenic effects, thereby addressing obesity-related comorbidities more comprehensively than mono-agonists. Parallel to advances in peptide design, structure-guided screening of the GLP-1R has led to the emergence of non-peptidic, orally available GLP-1RAs [83, 84]. Orforglipron, a once-daily small molecule, met key efficacy endpoints in the Phase III ACHIEVE-1 trial [85, 86]. Danuglipron, developed by Pfizer, showed 8–13% placebo-adjusted weight loss in Phase IIb studies, though high discontinuation rates highlight ongoing challenges with GI tolerability and formulation [87, 88]. If safety and tolerability can be optimized, oral small molecules hold the promise of eliminating cold-chain logistics and injection-related barriers, thereby expanding treatment accessibility globally [89, 90].

Across all generations of GLP-1RAs, GI adverse events, such as nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea, remain the primary tolerability concern and are typically manageable with gradual dose escalation [91, 92]. Post-marketing data have also noted rare risks such as gallbladder disease, acute kidney injury in volume-depleted patients, and potential worsening of diabetic retinopathy during rapid glycaemic control [93]. Ongoing long-term outcome studies continue to refine the safety profile and inform clinical risk stratification.

The diversified GLP-1RAs landscape, which includes acylated peptides, Fc-fusions, depot microspheres, poly-agonist macromolecules, and orally active small molecules, offers versatile opportunities for nanotechnology integration. Peptidic and antibody-based agents contain accessible lysine residues that allow for site-specific conjugation or surface adsorption onto lipid- and polymer-based NPs [94, 95]. This facilitates co-delivery with complementary therapeutics, such as amylin analogues or sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors, as well as with diagnostic agents [96, 97]. Small-molecule drugs can be incorporated into stimuli-responsive matrices or micelles to control release profiles while maintaining oral bioavailability [98]. In addition, the multivalency of dual- and triple-agonists supports the development of nanocarriers with enhanced avidity, promoting receptor clustering and downstream signaling without requiring increased systemic exposure [99]. These advances have expanded the role of GLP-1RAs from short-acting injectables to multifunctional oral formulations, creating new opportunities for targeted nanoplatforms in both metabolic and non-metabolic diseases [100, 101].

Engineered GLP-1R-Targeted nanoplatforms

Engineered GLP-1R-targeted nanoplatforms comprise modular nanosystems designed for precise receptor targeting, stimulus-responsive drug release, and multimodal therapeutic functions. The rational development of these platforms begins with selecting suitable core nanomaterials, such as lipid-based vesicles, polymeric matrices, inorganic NPs, or biomimetic scaffolds. Each material type offers specific advantages regarding biocompatibility, drug-loading capacity, degradation profiles, and routes of administration. Liposomes and lipid NPs, for example, exhibit high drug encapsulation efficiency and closely mimic biological membranes. In contrast, polymeric NPs provide adjustable drug-release kinetics and structural stability. Inorganic NPs enhance imaging capabilities and physical responsiveness; however, they may raise concerns related to long-term tissue retention or potential toxicity. Biomimetic systems, meanwhile, excel in immune evasion and tissue-specific delivery. Preparation methods, including thin-film hydration, emulsion solvent evaporation, nanoprecipitation, and self-assembly, critically influence the particle size, morphology, and surface ligand distribution. Further surface functionalization with GLP-1 analogues, nanobodies, or fatty acid chains involves strategies such as covalent conjugation, hydrophobic anchoring, or ligand insertion. These approaches ensure robust GLP-1R affinity and significantly impact pharmacokinetics, receptor internalization, and endosomal escape. Additionally, by integrating material-specific properties with stimuli-responsive release mechanisms triggered by conditions like pH changes, glucose levels, or redox reactions, GLP-1R-targeted nanoplatforms become programmable delivery vehicles (Table 2). Consequently, these systems effectively overcome biological barriers, minimize off-target effects, and enhance therapeutic outcomes across diverse diseases.

Nanoparticle carrier systems

Lipid-based nanoparticles

The design of nanoplatforms targeting GLP-1R involves considerations such as nanocarrier types, surface functionalization with targeting ligands, stimulus-responsive release mechanisms, and multifunctional integration. The most common approach is based on lipid NPs (liposomes and lipid nanostructures) [110]. Liposomes are spherical vesicles containing one or more phospholipid bilayers [111]. Prepared through thin-film hydration or reverse-phase evaporation techniques, they form spherical structures with biocompatibility that can carry hydrophilic drugs in their aqueous core or lipophilic drugs in their membrane layer [111]. Their advantages include high biocompatibility, degradability, and ease of surface modification [112]. For instance, polyethylene glycolated (PEGylated) liposomes encapsulating exenatide can significantly prolong the drug’s half-life and target pancreatic β-cells [113]. However, liposomes exhibit poor long-term storage stability, requiring optimization through lyophilization technology [112]. Lipid NPs (such as solid lipid NPs, SLNs), prepared via microemulsification, offer higher drug loading capacity and cell membrane fusion capability, making them suitable for messenger RNA (mRNA) or hydrophobic drug co-delivery [114]. Nevertheless, some lipid components may trigger mild inflammatory responses, necessitating further formulation optimization. Lonizable lipid NPs (LNPs) dominate current GLP-1R-targeted formulation research because their head group pKa can be matched to endosomal pH, driving a “cone-shape flip” that destabilizes the vesicle membrane and improves cytosolic release [115]. A pancreas-tropic series of ionizable LNPs based on DLin-MC3-DMA analogues selectively delivered luciferase messenger ribonucleic acid (mRNA) to β-cells in vivo [116]. At an equivalent dose, protein expression in islets exceeded that in the liver or spleen by more than 10-fold, indicating efficient endosomal escape and receptor-mediated uptake [117]. Applying the same ionizable scaffold to semaglutide preserved peptide integrity and normalized fasting glucose and homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) in Western-diet mice within 48 h, whereas 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DSPC)-based liposomes did not reach significance [118]. To reduce injection burden, an oral nanomicellar formulation (~ 125 nm) built from medium-chain phospholipids and enteric surfactant increased liraglutide bioavailability 4.6-fold versus free drug (5.1% vs. 1.1%) and maintained glycaemic control for 24 h after a single gavage in rats [119]. For imaging, superparamagnetic iron oxide NPs (SPIONs) decorated with exendin-4 were encapsulated in phospholipid bilayers, forming magnetoliposomes with a T2 relativity of 220 mM−1 s−1 that permit β-cell magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) without additional contrast agents [120]. Such data illustrate how lipid chemistry now spans systemic, oral and image-guided GLP-1RA delivery [102].

Polymeric nanoparticles, hydrogels and microneedles

Polymer NPs are typically composed of biodegradable materials such as PLGA, polylactic acid (PLA), polycaprolactone, and natural polymers like chitosan and gelatin [121]. PLGA NPs are commonly fabricated through emulsion–solvent evaporation, with drug release kinetics modulated by adjusting the lactic-to-glycolic acid ratio [122]. Hyaluronic acid (HA), a biocompatible and biodegradable poly-anionic biopolymer, is a key component of the extracellular matrix and plays essential roles in hydration, tissue repair, and structural organization. Its chemical modifiability and compatibility with biological systems make it widely employed in drug and gene delivery [123]. In this study, PLGA was used to encapsulate liraglutide, and HA coating was applied to form the LIRA@PLGA/HA sustained-release system. Local application of this formulation effectively reduced necrotic apoptosis in DP models [124]. Polymeric NPs can be engineered as nanospheres or nanocapsules to encapsulate GLP-1 analogs, protecting them from enzymatic degradation. An important advantage is the tunable release profile, which can be optimized by altering polymer composition or molecular weight. For instance, PLGA microspheres containing GLP-1 analogs can sustain drug release over days to weeks [125]. Chitosan NPs, prepared via ion cross-linking, exhibit strong mucoadhesive properties that enhance intestinal absorption of orally administered GLP-1 analogs [126]. Silk fibroin (SF), a natural protein-based polymer, offers a unique combination of biocompatibility, mechanical strength, and functional versatility, making it suitable for loading various therapeutic agents [127]. NP incorporation into SF matrices can be achieved via direct blending, in situ synthesis, or feeding through silkworms [128]. An injectable hydrogel system, Diabogel, has been developed using alginate and 3-aminophenylboronic acid to deliver modified GLP-1, islet-derived extracellular vesicles, and telmisartan. In vitro studies confirmed its compatibility, reduced oxidative stress, enhanced insulin production, and increased glucose uptake [129]. In high-fat diet (HFD) and STZ-induced diabetic mice, Diabogel effectively reduced blood glucose, preserved body weight, improved insulin expression, and restored islet function by modulating macrophage phenotype and NF-κB signaling [129]. A separate strategy involves co-packaging β-cells with GLP-1-loaded NPs within alginate hydrogels, which significantly improved cellular metabolism and resulted in an 8-fold increase in insulin secretion [130]. Despite their promise, the hydrophobic nature of certain carriers may lead to premature drug release, which can be mitigated by PEG modification or hydrophilic coatings [131]. Chitosan-based NPs enhance mucosal adhesion and are particularly effective for oral GLP-1 delivery [132]. These systems typically maintain stability, show low immunogenicity, and are efficiently taken up by cells when sized within 100–200 nm. Supercritical fluid extraction of emulsions (SFEE) has enabled the production of exenatide-loaded PLGA microspheres with narrow size distributions and prolonged release over 30 days, maintaining neutral internal pH to prevent peptide degradation [133]. Oral formulations utilizing zein, Eudragit, and chitosan NPs have achieved high drug loading and protection in simulated GI conditions, with relative bioavailability of 14–18% in beagle dogs and pharmacokinetic performance comparable to daily injections [134]. Natural polysaccharides and proteins such as sodium alginate and albumin also serve as favorable carriers due to their low immunogenicity and strong biocompatibility [135]. Sodium alginate forms gel microspheres via calcium ion cross-linking, making it well-suited for oral formulations. Albumin NPs, prepared without harsh solvents, improve stability of hydrophobic drugs [136]. PEG-based hydrogels with borate end-groups demonstrate pH- and glucose-responsive behavior. These materials, formed by cross-linking PEG copolymers with glucose, enable sustained drug release [137]. In diabetic rats, a single injection of semaglutide-loaded hydrogel maintained therapeutic levels for 42 days, and pharmacokinetic modeling suggests quarterly dosing may be feasible in humans [138, 139]. Minimally invasive systems include microneedle patches incorporating glucose-responsive phenylboronic acid along with exendin-4 and glucose oxidase (GOx). In diabetic swine, these patches enhanced peptide flux under hyperglycemic conditions and maintained normoglycemia for six hours without causing hypoglycemia [140]. Together, these advancements in nanocarrier design demonstrate the versatility and clinical potential of polymer- and protein-based delivery systems for GLP-1RAs. By enabling sustained release, targeted delivery, and improved bioavailability, these platforms offer promising strategies for enhancing therapeutic outcomes in metabolic diseases. Continued refinement of material properties and formulation techniques will be critical for translating these technologies into effective, patient-friendly treatments.

Inorganic and hybrid nanoparticles

Inorganic nanomaterials such as iron oxide, gold, silica, and quantum dots exhibit unique properties including magnetic responsiveness and easy surface functionalization [141, 142]. Magnetic iron oxide NPs (Fe3O4 NPs) have been extensively studied due to their distinctive physical characteristics. These magnetic Fe3O4 NPs are synthesized through co-precipitation and can be targeted to the pancreas under external magnetic field guidance, enabling simultaneous MRI imaging and drug delivery [143]. SPIONs functionalized with GLP-1 analogues furnish a highly specific magnetic resonance (MR) handle for pancreatic β-cell imaging [144]. exendin-4-conjugated PEG-coated SPIONs (core ≈ 10 nm) demonstrated selective uptake in GLP-1R-positive INS-1 cells. Labeling 2,000 islets produced a readily detectable hypointense focus on 3 T MRI, whereas non-targeted SPIONs generated no contrast [120]. Subsequent research employing manganese-doped “magnetism engineered” iron oxides (MnMEIO-Ex4) enabled prolonged tracking of transplanted islets for up to 17 weeks without impairing insulin secretion, highlighting the long-term biocompatibility of this imaging probe [145]. In parallel, a multifunctional nanoreactor (GOx@HCuS@HA) was developed based on Gox-loaded hollow mesoporous copper sulfide nanoparticles (NPs). By integrating divinyltriamine-conjugated chlorin e6 and PEGylated hyaluronic acid, the system exhibits enhanced targeting and cellular uptake. Upon activation within the acidic tumor microenvironment, the nanoreactor induces substantial tumor cell destruction while maintaining a favorable safety profile, demonstrating strong therapeutic potential in oncological applications [146]. However, prolonged retention of inorganic materials may lead to toxicity, necessitating size regulation (< 5 nm) or biodegradable coatings to enhance biocompatibility. The difficulty in degrading solid inorganic cores could limit drug delivery unless the particles are excreted from the body or reduced to sufficiently small sizes (< 5 nm) for renal clearance. Long-term tissue retention of conventional SPIONs raises regulatory concerns, prompting the development of renal-clearable ultra-small SPIONs. Zwitterion-coated 3 nm cores (ZES-SPIONs) preserved high T1 relativity (r1 ≈ 5 mM−1 s−1) yet underwent > 80% urinary clearance within 24 h of i.v. administration in mice, essentially eliminating residual iron by one week [147]. Combining such ultrasmall cores with β-cell-specific peptides represents a logical advancement toward transient, on-demand GLP-1R imaging probes. Gold NPs (AuNPs) were prepared by the Turkkevich method, and the surface could be coupled with GLP-1 peptide through thiol bond, which had both delivery and optical imaging functions [148]. Plasmonic gold nanostructures further introduce therapeutic optionality. Approximately 15 nm albumin-binding AuNPs coated with exendin-4 exhibited a plasma half-life of 43.6 h and sustained hypoglycaemic control for three days in high-fat-diet mice [149]. Independently, AuNPs achieved robust photothermal heating (> 10 °C rise under 808 nm, 1 W cm−2, 5 min) while maintaining low cytotoxicity, illustrating how a biopolymer cloak can couple photothermal actuation to a peptide drug surface [150]. Translating this paradigm to GLP-1R ligands could enable spatiotemporally gated peptide release or tumor ablation in GLP-1R-rich neuroendocrine cancers. Organic-inorganic hybrids aim to blend the magnetic sensitivity of iron oxides with the pharmacokinetic stealth of lipids. Hybrid systems (such as liposome-coated iron oxide NPs or PLGA-gold nano hybrid particles) combine the advantages of different materials to achieve multi-functional integration (targeting + imaging + controlled release). Lipid-wrapped SPIONs, prepared by solvent evaporation re-hydration with DOTAP/DSPE-PEG, display reduced protein corona formation and prolonged blood half-life, while the outer lipid leaflet offers a convenient anchor for peptide ligands [151]. Early prototypes that embed semaglutide or other incretins in such lipid shells are showing concurrent β-cell-targeted contrast and receptor-mediated glucose lowering in diabetic mice, underscoring the therapeutic synergy achievable when magnetic cores are cloaked in a biologically familiar membrane milieu [152]. Taken together, iron oxide, gold, and hybrid nano-architectures offer complementary strengths, combining high-resolution MR tracking, photothermal actuation, and pharmacokinetic tuning. These capabilities are now being leveraged to extend GLP-1R-targeted nanomedicines beyond metabolic disorders toward imaging-guided, multimodal interventions.

Emerging vectors

Despite significant advances, conventional NPs continue to face key limitations, including rapid clearance from the bloodstream, immune recognition, and poor accumulation at target sites [153]. Biologically derived nanocarriers such as virus-like particles (VLPs) offer a promising alternative. Composed of non-genomic protein shells, VLPs retain efficient cellular entry mechanisms while demonstrating a favorable safety profile [154]. They have been successfully employed in human papillomavirus vaccines and evaluated in candidates for COVID-19 immunization. Another emerging platform involves DNA origami nanostructures, which enable the programmable self-assembly of DNA into defined architectures for precise drug delivery and molecular sensing [155]. More recently, approaches such as exosome encapsulation and cell membrane camouflage have further enhanced the targeting accuracy and immune evasion capacity of nanocarriers, particularly in tumor-targeted applications [156, 157]. Among these, cell membrane-coated nanoparticles (CMCNPs) have gained attention as a versatile biomimetic strategy. By cloaking synthetic cores with membranes derived from various cell types, such as cancer cells, stem cells, platelets, or immune cells, CMCNPs inherit functional membrane proteins that improve circulation time, reduce immunogenicity, and enhance tissue-specific targeting [158].

A novel approach utilizing polyelectrolyte and polymeric nanocarriers to simulate viral structures has been developed for efficient oral delivery of GLP-1 analogs [159]. Given the short half-life of GLP-1, researchers have explored adeno-associated virus (AAV)-mediated delivery of GLP-1 fusion proteins to prolong its therapeutic duration. Additionally, adenoviral vectors (Ad.GLP-1R) have been employed to transfect mouse bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BM-MSCs), enabling high expression of GLP-1R. This system facilitates investigation of exendin-4 effects on cellular proliferation [160]. Platelet membranes exhibit intrinsic targeting ability and high precision in drug delivery, making them well-suited for constructing nanoparticle-based carriers in antithrombotic applications. In one study, a novel platform was developed by coating bovine serum albumin (BSA)–liraglutide nanoparticles (BSA@LIR) with platelet membrane fragments (PMF), forming BSA@LIR-PMF. This system enabled targeted delivery and effectively mitigated atherosclerosis in diabetic mouse models [161]. In another approach, fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21) was encapsulated in charge-neutral NPs for targeted delivery to ischemia–reperfusion (I/R) sites. The formulation preferentially accumulated in I/R lesions, bypassing hepatic metabolism, and enhanced anti-inflammatory and anti-apoptotic responses while improving cardiac function [162].The development of intelligent nanocarriers has garnered significant attention. Bionic nanocarriers, which utilize red blood cell membranes or cancer cell membranes to encapsulate synthetic NPs, not only enhance biocompatibility but also enable specific targeting and immune evasion [163, 164]. Self-propelled nanomotors, driven by chemical reactions (like H2O2 catalytic decomposition) or external fields (light/magnetic), can actively penetrate biological barriers [165–168]. This capability demonstrates unique advantages in drug delivery systems, particularly in gastric administration. Self-assembling β-sheet peptide nanofibers displaying GLP-1 epitopes form injectable hydrogels (storage modulus ≈ 1.2 kPa) capable of co-encapsulating islets [169]. A recent study reported the development of hypoxia-activated nanoclay (HOISNDs) constructed via the self-assembly of SPIONs with engineered organic polymer ligands. These nanostructures exhibit strong oxygen-generating capacity and dual-mode imaging functionality. Notably, HOISNDs demonstrate high stability and serve as effective agents for MR and fluorescence imaging–guided photodynamic therapy (PDT). By increasing local oxygen availability and enhancing reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, they alleviate tumor hypoxia and improve therapeutic efficacy. Exploiting the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect and the Fenton reaction, HOISNDs convert intratumoral hydrogen peroxide into oxygen, further boosting treatment outcomes. In vivo studies have confirmed the platform’s potential for integrated diagnostic and therapeutic use, supporting its application in dual-mode imaging-guided cancer therapy [166]. In streptozotocin (STZ)-induced diabetic mice, renal capsule transplantation of these hydrogels doubled the normoglycaemic period and increased CD31⁺ vascular density by 2.3-fold compared to Matrigel [170]. Exosomes are endogenous nanoscale vesicles capable of transporting proteins, lipids, nucleic acids, and therapeutic agents [171]. As natural carriers, they offer several advantages for targeted drug delivery, including resistance to phagocytosis by macrophages and monocytes, high biocompatibility, low immunogenicity, minimal antigenicity, and reduced systemic toxicity. Exosomes can traverse biological barriers such as the blood–brain barrier without the need for chemical modification, preserving the functional integrity of their cargo. Surface engineering further enhances their targeting specificity. Owing to these properties, exosome-based delivery systems enable precise therapeutic modulation with reduced adverse effects, offering significant promise in disease treatment [172, 173]. Milk-derived small extracellular vesicles (sEVs) are emerging for oral incretin delivery. Recent work demonstrated that sEVs loaded with semaglutide or tirzepatide crossed the gut barrier intact and produced significant 4-hour post-prandial glucose reductions in mice without inducing detectable anti-drug antibodies, underscoring their innate immune stealth [174]. Milk-derived exosomes (mExos), known for their capacity to traverse epithelial barriers, have emerged as promising carriers for oral delivery of protein and peptide therapeutics [175]. To enhance their delivery efficiency, a hybrid vesicle system (mExos@DSPE-Hyd-PMPC) was developed by integrating functionalized liposomes with mExos. This formulation, loaded with somatilide (SET), features adaptive surface properties designed to overcome the limitations of native mExos and improve oral peptide transport [175]. In a related approach, a nanoparticle system modified with 25-hydroxycholesterol (25HC NPs) was engineered to mimic the endogenous “top-down” absorption route of cholesterol. Using liraglutide (Lira) as a model GLP-1 receptor agonist, these NPs effectively alleviated diabetic symptoms in db/db mice [176]. Lipoprotein-based NPs have also shown potential for targeted drug delivery via ligand–receptor interactions [177]. Looking ahead, lipoprotein-mimicking systems may offer a platform for delivering GLP-1RAs to the liver, with potential applications in treating non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and hepatocellular carcinoma, as well as enabling tumor-targeted imaging. Generation 4 (G4) polyamidoamine (PAMAM) dendrimers multivalently decorated with GLP-1 mimetics exhibit a 15-fold increase in receptor avidity compared to monomeric peptides and can co-deliver a C-X-C motif chemokine receptor 4 (CXCR4) antagonist [178]. In a PD model, this combination reduced microglial activation by 70%, although cardiotoxicity and scalability remain ongoing concerns [179]. Finally, layered double hydroxide (LDH) nanosheets (Mg: Al = 2) intercalate GLP-1 between hydroxide layers. Upon acid-triggered lattice dissolution, the peptide is released while transiently buffering the intravesicular pH [180]. This mechanism protected 78% of the peptide from proteolysis in vitro and reduced HOMA-IR by 42% in insulin-resistant mice following twice-weekly dosing [181]. Collectively, lipidic, polymeric, inorganic, and biomimetic systems now provide a modular toolkit for GLP-1 analogue delivery. These platforms can shield peptides from enzymatic degradation, tailor pharmacokinetics from hours to months, enable MRI or photothermal readouts, and support painless or oral administration, thereby laying the groundwork for multimodal GLP-1R-targeted nanotherapies. To provide a comprehensive overview of the diverse nanocarrier systems discussed in this section, a comparative analysis of their key properties for targeted GLP-1R delivery is presented. In the future, nanocarrier technology will evolve in three key directions: multimodal integration (combining drug delivery with imaging capabilities), AI-assisted design (optimizing carrier structures and release kinetics), and green synthesis processes (reducing toxic solvent usage). These innovations will significantly advance precision medicine and personalized treatment, playing a pivotal role in combating cancer, neurodegenerative diseases, and infectious diseases.

Ligand engineering and surface functionalization

Target selectivity in GLP-1R-targeted nanoplatforms is achieved primarily by engineering the presentation of the peptide agonist or alternative affinity ligands on the carrier surface. Early approaches employed random amine coupling of full-length exendin-4 using 4-Maleimidobutyric acid N-hydroxysuccinimide ester (GMBS) to tether the peptide to Cy5.5-labelled iron oxide cores, forming the MN-Ex5/10-Cy5.5 probe. This construct preserved r₂ relaxivity and induced a 40% T₂ signal reduction in isolated islets, confirming that chemical conjugation can maintain both imaging functionality and receptor-binding fidelity [144, 182]. A dual-catalytic oxide nanosponge (DON) has been developed to integrate remotely activated catalysis with immunotherapy. Following intravenous administration, DON selectively accumulates in tumor tissues, where cerium oxide-coated iron oxide nanocubes mediate sustained ROS generation and glutathione depletion, enabling effective chemodynamic therapy (CDT). Upon exposure to high-frequency magnetic fields, the catalytic activity is further enhanced, accelerating hydrogen peroxide decomposition and promoting continuous redox cycling of iron and cerium [183]. In a related study, iron oxide NPs coated with M1 macrophage membranes (IO@MM) were engineered as an immunotherapeutic strategy for lung-metastatic colorectal cancer. These NPs exhibit tumor-penetrating capacity and induce the polarization of naïve D0 macrophages toward the anti-tumor M1 phenotype, thereby amplifying immune-mediated tumor suppression [184]. Subsequent studies introduced site-selective conjugation strategies to reduce product heterogeneity. Three-carbon disulfide re-bridging chemistry, combined with structure-guided PEGylation originally developed for fibroblast growth factor 2, has now been applied to GLP-1 scaffolds. This approach yields single-site conjugates with defined orientation, improved pharmacokinetics, and significantly reduced aggregation [185, 186]. Parallel to covalent methods, acylated agonists such as liraglutide and semaglutide take advantage of their intrinsic C16 and C18 fatty acid chains to insert non-covalently into lipid bilayers. Systematic surveys of lipophilic anchors demonstrate that both chain length and branching influence insertion depth and residence time [134, 187]. Post-insertion of liraglutide into hydrogenated soybean phospholipids (HSPC) and cholesterol liposomes co-encapsulating metformin has been shown to improve glycaemic control in diabetic rodents. Comparable benefits appear with zein/eudragit/chitosan oral NPs, which achieve 14–18% relative bioavailability in dogs [134, 188].

PEGylation and fatty acid acylation represent two primary strategies for prolonging the half-life and enhancing the pharmacological performance of GLP-1-based drugs, each with distinct mechanisms and biological implications. Site-specific PEGylation involves covalent attachment of polyethylene glycol (PEG) chains to predefined residues on the peptide backbone, forming a hydrophilic steric shield that reduces renal clearance, masks immunogenic epitopes, and protects against enzymatic degradation [189]. This approach has been shown to extend the half-life of exenatide from approximately 2 h to over 24 h [190]. Furthermore, site-specific conjugation ensures that the receptor-binding domain remains accessible, thereby preserving bioactivity while minimizing off-target immunogenic responses. In contrast, fatty acid acylation enhances binding affinity to plasma albumin by incorporating lipophilic chains (commonly C16 or C18) into the peptide structure [191]. This albumin-binding confers a depot effect, allowing for sustained drug release and extended systemic exposure. As exemplified by liraglutide and semaglutide, this strategy enables once-daily and once-weekly dosing regimens, respectively [192]. While fatty acylation preserves receptor interaction and facilitates tissue penetration, its pharmacokinetics may be influenced by endogenous albumin levels, and long-term use could potentially trigger immune responses due to the lipid moieties. Comparatively, PEGylation offers superior control over immunogenicity and is more amenable to integration into multifunctional nanocarriers for targeted or stimuli-responsive delivery. However, repeated exposure may elicit anti-PEG antibodies in some individuals. Fatty acid acylation, while simpler in design and manufacturing, provides efficient delivery via endogenous albumin pathways and maintains strong receptor engagement. To harness the complementary advantages of both strategies, emerging formulations increasingly employ hybrid designs, such as loading PEGylated nanocarriers into fatty acid–modified GLP-1 analogs, combining prolonged circulation with active targeting. In summary, PEGylation excels in immunological stealth and modular assembly, while fatty acid acylation favors simplified sustained delivery. Their rational combination represents a promising direction in engineering next-generation GLP-1R-targeted therapeutics.

When targeting and pharmacological activity must be functionally decoupled, truncated peptide motifs or heterologous binding domains are employed. A long-acting GLP-1/GLP-1R nanobody fusion protein prolonged fasting glucose normalization to 96 h with a single low i.v. dose, illustrating how bivalent scaffolds can unite high-affinity with extended systemic exposure [193]. Modular coronas further improve tissue selectivity. Intestinal Fc receptor-targeted polymeric micelles increased the oral exposure of semaglutide 6-fold in type 2 diabetic mice while preserving its glucose-lowering efficacy [194]. In a separate study, liver-homing peptides discovered by cell surface proteomics boosted hepatic nanoparticle deposition by 2.5-fold compared with untargeted controls, indicating potential for hepatocyte-focused gene editing or metabolic modulation [195]. In order to improve the oral availability of preparations, pH modification is a good method in practical application [196]. More elaborate dual-ligand constructs conceal a protease-resistant GLP-1 analogue beneath a plaque-activated shell to reprogram vascular smooth muscle cells in atherosclerosis. Simultaneously, these constructs provide MRI visibility through a quenched fluorophore that is activated upon matrix metalloproteinase (MMP)-mediated cleavage [197]. A self-powered, charge-conversion nanoplatform (Sp-NPs) was constructed using oxygen-triggered self-assembly of the PIMA-mPEG-API-EDA-TCPP polymer ligand coordinated with Fe³⁺ ions. This design improves tumor targeting and structural stability, enables pH-responsive disassembly within the tumor microenvironment, and reduces photosensitizer quenching. Upon disassembly, the system releases Fe ions that catalyze ROS generation and facilitate oxygen release, thereby addressing tumor hypoxia and enhancing the efficacy of PDT [198, 199]. In a separate strategy, glucose-responsive polymeric NPs encapsulating a GLP-1 analogue were engineered to display stem cell adhesion peptides on their surface. This configuration improved β-cell engraftment and promoted glucose homeostasis in autoimmune diabetes models, demonstrating potential for enhancing regenerative therapies in metabolic disorders [200]. Taken together, covalent conjugation, lipophilic insertion, and modular surface engineering all reflect a common design principle in nanoparticle functionalization. The active epitope should project outward from the nanoparticle corona, conjugation chemistry must minimize heterogeneity, and auxiliary ligands may be introduced to enhance tissue-specific tropism when required. When these guidelines are met, GLP-1R nanomedicines achieve precise homing, stimulus-controlled release, integrated imaging, and consistent pharmacological efficacy within a single construct.

Stimuli-responsive release strategies

Smart GLP-1 nanocarriers increasingly rely on bioresponsive linkers that stay inert during circulation yet disassemble when they sense a disease-associated cue. In hyperglycaemia, GOx co-encapsulated with a GLP-1 analogue oxidizes excess glucose to gluconic acid, lowering the local pH and triggering dissolution of acid-labile mineral or polymer matrices. This mechanism underpins both phenylboronic acid hydrogels, which swell by breaking covalent glucose-boronate bonds, and a dual-mineralized microneedle patch that restored normoglycaemia for 24 h in type 2 diabetic mice without affecting euglycaemic animals [140, 201, 202]. Copper sulfide (CuS) nanosheets coated with membrane-degradable polymers have been developed to enhance deep brain tumor penetration by modulating intercellular interactions. These nanosheets enable near-infrared II (NIR-II) photothermal therapy and promote dendritic cell recruitment, facilitating self-amplifying immunotherapeutic responses [203]. A similar glucose-responsive mechanism has been applied to nanoscale “Trojan horse” particles encapsulating a liraglutide analogue within a boronate-crosslinked shell. Under hyperglycemic conditions, cleavage of boronate bonds accelerates peptide release. In the context of stem cell co-therapy, this system helps maintain graft viability by reducing postprandial glucose surges [200]. Additionally, a wireless, mitochondrial-targeted nanoantenna (WINA) has been designed for membrane disruption and mitochondrial depolarization, in conjunction with dendritic cell-mediated immunotherapy. By enhancing convective transport, WINA enables targeted delivery to deep-seated tumors and mitochondria through cationic TPP interactions with polyglutamine, thereby amplifying therapeutic precision and cellular disruption [167].

Complementary internal triggers exploit pathological microenvironments rather than systemic metabolites. Acid-sensitive polyester or chitosan coatings dissolve in the gastric environment and then resolidify at intestinal pH, which permits oral delivery of GLP-1 analogues. After endocytosis, intracellular glutathione cleaves disulfide bonds in the carrier matrix and releases the payload, a design principle detailed in recent reviews of stimuli-responsive polymers [204, 205]. External fields offer an additional level of control. Magnetic NPs can generate heat or oscillate under an alternating magnetic field to disrupt lipid bilayers, while thermoresponsive polymers liquefy above 40 °C, enabling brief, localized warming to trigger on-demand agonist release. These strategies are discussed in broader frameworks of chemical and physical stimulus-responsive systems [202, 204, 205]. A biodegradable hollow mesoporous MnO₂-based nanoplatform (HAMnO₂) has been developed for tumor microenvironment-responsive therapy. This system integrates artemisinin (ART) and a surface-conjugated chlorosulfinyl e6 photosensitizer to enable dual-mode imaging-guided treatment, combining photodynamic and chemodynamic effects. Under acidic and glutathione-rich conditions, the nanoplatform disassembles, releasing oxygen, manganese ions, and ART to alleviate tumor hypoxia. The released manganese ions facilitate MRI, while the liberated ART and enhanced Fenton-like reaction generate abundant ROS, resulting in synergistic tumor cell killing [206]. Together, these glucose, pH, enzyme, redox, and field-triggered mechanisms promise “logic-gated” GLP-1 delivery that synchronizes drug release with metabolic need or lesion biology, maximizing efficacy while limiting off-target exposure.

Therapeutic applications of GLP-1R-targeting nanoplatforms in various human diseases

GLP-1R-targeting nanoplatforms have emerged as a versatile and powerful strategy to enhance drug delivery and therapeutic outcomes across a wide range of human diseases. Initially focused on metabolic disorders, these nanotechnologies improve bioavailability, overcome physiological barriers, and enable precise tissue targeting, thus broadening their clinical applications. Advances in GLP-1R-targeted nanomedicines now extend to neurological, cardiovascular, oncological, and autoimmune diseases, addressing complex pathophysiological mechanisms with multifunctional therapeutic approaches (Table 3). Metabolic diseases

Type 2 diabetes mellitus

GLP-1RAs are well-established treatments for T2DM, enhancing insulin secretion and suppressing glucagon release in a glucose-dependent manner, thereby improving glycaemic control [108, 214]. Nanoplatforms aim to overcome limitations such as frequent injections by enabling oral administration of GLP-1 analogues. Nanoformulations, including polymeric and LNPs, can protect peptides from GI degradation and enhance transmucosal absorption [4]. For example, chitosan or lipid-coated NPs encapsulating exenatide demonstrated improved bioavailability and glucose-lowering effects in animal models compared to unprotected peptides [211, 212, 215]. A recent self-nanoemulsifying drug delivery system (SNEDDS) raised the oral bioavailability of exenatide 7-fold in rats and produced post-prandial glycaemic reductions comparable to subcutaneous dosing [168]. Transportertargeted carriers that display vitamin B12 or folate on the droplet surface further exploit intrinsic factor or folate-mediated uptake to boost intestinal transcytosis [216].

Another innovative approach involves injectable depots, such as hydrogels developed at Stanford University, which incorporate GLP-1 analogues for sustained release over approximately 4 months following a single injection. This strategy reduces the dosing frequency from daily to three times per year and has the potential to significantly enhance patient compliance [138]. For instance, PLGA microspheres encapsulating exenatide provide sustained one-week release, underpinning the once-weekly Exenatide formulation [125, 217]. Blending the microspheres with magnesium hydroxide creates buffered diffusion channels and extends exenatide release to four weeks in non-human primates [218]. Recently, a novel self-assembling peptide nanofiber hydrogel (SupraGel + GLP-1-FFG) has been designed, with its synthesis procedure and application in a mouse islet transplantation model schematically represented [170] (Fig. 3A). A cartoon schematic illustrating the formation of supramolecular nanofiber depots from GLP-1RAs is also presented [209] (Fig. 3B). Furthermore, an in vivo evaluation of a PA-GLP1/dPA hydrogel formulation in a type 2 diabetic rat model demonstrates similar therapeutic effects to daily semaglutide injections by reducing blood glucose levels and mitigating weight gain [209] (Fig. 3C). Building on previous advances, further efforts have focused on optimizing drug delivery and improving therapeutic outcomes through innovative nanoparticle strategies. One such approach involves NPs engineered to target the neonatal FcRn, which markedly enhanced the efficacy of semaglutide in a T2DM mouse model. These NPs not only improved glycemic control and stimulated insulin secretion, but also extended the intestinal residence time of the drug. This prolonged exposure contributed to enhanced insulin production and the restoration of pancreatic β-cell function [194] (Fig. 4).

Additionally, NPs have been explored for pancreatic β-cell protection or regeneration. Polyethylenimine (PEI) NPs delivering GLP-1 genes have improved β-cell function and endogenous insulin secretion, and combined nanoparticle systems delivering insulin and GLP-1 agonist genes exhibited synergistic improvements in glycaemic control [219, 220]. A follow-up study using chitosan/GLP-1 plasmid nanocomplexes produced sustained endogenous GLP-1 expression, enhanced insulin secretion, and significantly lowered fasting glucose for more than two weeks after a single intravenous dose in db/db mice [213]. Thus, GLP-1R-targeted nanotherapies for T2DM are primarily designed to enhance therapeutic convenience through oral or infrequent dosing and to improve efficacy through targeted pancreatic delivery, with the potential to substantially improve clinical outcomes.

Diabetic nephropathy

Diabetic nephropathy (DN) is a major complication of diabetes, resulting from long-term hyperglycemia leading to renal microvascular damage, eventually progressing to kidney failure and proteinuria [221–224]. GLP-1RAs have garnered significant attention for their protective effects. Studies have shown that they can regulate renal hemodynamics, inhibit oxidative stress and inflammation, and improve mitochondrial function, thereby delaying the progression of DN [225–227].

To optimize GLP-1RAs therapy for DN, scientists are exploring nanotechnology-based delivery strategies to enhance drug targeting and bioavailability. Current research explores a range of nanotechnologies with distinct therapeutic functions. Polyethylene glycol-modified nanoparticles (PEG NPs) are investigated to improve drug delivery, while zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs) aim to protect renal function and reduce inflammation. ROS-responsive nanocapsules are being developed to optimize metabolic regulation, and kidney targeting peptide-modified NPs are designed to enhance renal tubular uptake [228–230]. Recent studies indicate that ZnO NPs significantly improve renal function and reduce proteinuria in rats with type 1 diabetes induced by STZ. This improvement is achieved by activating the Nrf2/HO-1 antioxidant pathway, inhibiting the NF-κB-mediated inflammatory response, and blocking the TGF-β/Smad3 fibrotic pathway as well as the Ripk1/Ripk3/Mlkl necroptosis pathway [231]. Furthermore, the GCGR may play a role in renal energy metabolism, and GLP-1R/GCGR dual agonists such as 1907B have shown superior efficacy to GLP-1R single agonists in murine DN models, more effectively reducing proteinuria and improving renal function [232]. 1907B has been demonstrated to effectively reduce glomerular mesangial expansion and tubulointerstitial fibrosis in db/db and unilateral ureteral obstruction (UUO) models. Future prospects include exploring the formulation of 1907B in lipid NPs to enhance renal deposition, and further designing complementary ROS-responsive nanocapsules to synchronize agonist release with hyperglycemia-induced oxidative bursts, aiming for more precise renal protection [197]. In the future, the strategic encapsulation of GLP-1R or GLP-1R/GCGR dual agonists within advanced nanocarriers holds the potential to significantly enhance the precision and effectiveness of DN therapy.

Obesity

Obesity remains a major global health challenge, closely associated with metabolic disorders and increased cardiovascular risk. GLP-1RAs have emerged as effective pharmacological agents for weight management by modulating central appetite regulation and energy homeostasis. High-dose GLP-1RAs significantly reduce body weight by suppressing appetite and caloric intake [104, 233]. Agents such as liraglutide (3.0 mg daily) and once-weekly semaglutide have been approved for obesity treatment, achieving weight reductions of approximately 5–15% in many patients Highdose GLP-1RAs significantly reduce body weight by suppressing appetite and calorie intake [234]. Nanoplatforms contribute to obesity therapy by enhancing drug delivery, extending pharmacokinetic half-life, and maintaining continuous GLP-1R stimulation, which is essential for sustained weight loss [235]. In addition, NPs facilitate combination therapies that simultaneously target multiple appetite-regulating pathways. Co-delivery strategies incorporating GLP-1RAs with other anorectic agents, including Gcgr small interfering RNA (siRNA), peptide YY, cannabinoid receptor antagonists, or amylin agonists, have demonstrated additive effects on weight reduction [220, 236, 237].

Targeting NPs to central appetite control centers may significantly enhance anti-obesity efficacy through localized delivery and synergistic action. GLP-1RAs, such as liraglutide, have been shown to activate brown adipose tissue (BAT) via CNS pathways, promoting thermogenesis and increasing energy expenditure independently of food intake [238–240]. An oral liraglutide formulation was developed by integrating GLP-1RAs with bile acid derivatives through electrostatic interactions and nanocapsule assembly, in combination with the nonionic surfactant n-dodecyl-β-D-maltoside (DDM). This formulation significantly enhanced drug bioavailability via bile acid transporter-mediated uptake. In long-term treatment, it improved glycaemic control, reduced IR, and favorably modulated lipid metabolism in patients with T2DM, highlighting a promising strategy for oral GLP-1RA delivery [119] (Fig. 5). An injectable supramolecular nanofiber hydrogel (PA-GLP-1) has demonstrated sustained GLP-1 release for over 40 days and achieved blood glucose reduction and weight control outcomes comparable to daily semaglutide in a rat model of T2DM induced by STZ and nicotinamide (NA) [209]. Chronic activation of GLP-1R further upregulates fibronectin type III domain-containing 5 (FNDC5)/irisin and uncoupling protein 1 (UCP1), supporting the rationale for BAT-targeted polymer-lipid hybrid NPs co-loaded with β3-adrenergic agonists to amplify thermogenic energy expenditure [241]. Clinically, the FDA approval of semaglutide (Wegovy®) marks a significant advance in obesity therapy, demonstrating not only substantial weight reduction but also reduced cardiovascular risk in high-risk populations [242]. Sustained-release platforms such as PA-GLP-1 represent a promising direction for long-acting obesity and metabolic disease management [209].

In conclusion, these advances in GLP-1RA-based nanoplatforms underscore the transformative potential of integrating nanotechnology with metabolic therapeutics for more effective obesity management. Ongoing development of targeted, sustained-release systems and multimodal combination strategies holds promise for improving long-term clinical outcomes and expanding access to individualized treatment.

Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease and nonalcoholic steatohepatitis

NAFLD, particularly its inflammatory subtype NASH, is closely associated with obesity and T2DM [243]. GLP-1RAs have shown beneficial effects on hepatic steatosis and inflammation [244, 245]. Clinical trials, including the LEAN trial, demonstrated significant histological remission of NASH following liraglutide treatment [246]. These effects are mediated through weight loss, enhanced insulin sensitivity, and direct hepatic actions such as activation of adenosine AMPK, which contributes to reduced hepatic lipid accumulation [247–249].

Nanoplatforms offer a promising strategy to enhance hepatic delivery of GLP-1 analogues [250]. For example, galactosylated NPs that target hepatocytes via the asialoglycoprotein receptor have demonstrated increased hepatic accumulation of GLP-1 analogues [251, 252]. One study using galactose-decorated PLGA NPs reported significantly enhanced hepatocyte uptake of encapsulated peptides, and suggested that adapting this system for semaglutide could further improve intrahepatic exposure and anti-steatotic efficacy [253]. A NASH mouse study confirmed that using vitamin A-modified liposomes to target hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) and deliver SREBP2-siRNA or anti-miR-33a can reduce cholesterol accumulation in activated HSCs, thereby significantly improving liver fibrosis. Based on this, in the future, encapsulating GLP-1 analogs within vitamin A-modified liposomes, while simultaneously targeting HSCs and leveraging the anti-inflammatory and metabolic regulatory effects of GLP-1 analogs, is expected to synergistically reduce HSC activation and improve the hepatic metabolic environment, thus offering a more promising innovative therapy for NASH and related liver fibrosis [254, 255]. The same platform has been shown to reverse stellate cell lipid loading and collagen (Col) deposition, supporting its potential for co-encapsulation of GLP-1 analogues in combined metabolic and anti-fibrotic therapy [256]. Given the frequent co-existence of NASH and T2DM, combination nanotherapies co-delivering GLP-1RAs with agents such as farnesoid X receptor (FXR) agonists or GS-834,356 offer a multi-pronged strategy targeting both metabolic and fibrotic pathways [243, 257, 258]. The Phase II WAYFIND trial reported that semaglutide combined with cilofexor, with or without firsocostat, achieved histological improvement rates exceeding 80% in patients with compensated NASH cirrhosis [259]. Preclinical studies further support the efficacy of such multidrug nanotherapeutics in reducing hepatic fat content and fibrosis [260–262]. Moreover, ultrasmall superparamagnetic iron oxide (USPIO) NPs have emerged as diagnostic tools for non-invasive molecular MRI-based evaluation of liver fibrosis [263, 264]. Ultra-small manganese-doped iron oxide NPs (UMIOs), with their low r2/r1 ratio and hepatocyte-targeted uptake capacity, enable highly sensitive T1-weighted liver MRI and are cleared via dual hepatobiliary and renal pathways, demonstrating good biocompatibility [265]. In the future, leveraging the coordination between the metal sites on the surface of UMIOs and catechol, Semaglutide can be co-loaded onto their surface to construct MRI-visible anti-fibrotic nanomedicines, thereby achieving “single-injection, synchronous imaging-therapy” for precise liver management. This enables “single-injection, synchronous imaging-therapy” for precise liver management. More broadly, the strategic integration of GLP-1R-targeting agents like semaglutide into such USPIO-based nanoplatforms—potentially co-loaded with synergistic agents such as antioxidants like vitamin E and engineered for precise release control—represents a significant leap towards advancing personalized and comprehensive treatment strategies for NASH.

In summary, engineered GLP-1R-targeting nanoplatforms represent a transformative approach in the management of metabolic liver disease, enabling targeted delivery, enhanced pharmacokinetics, and multifunctional therapeutic capabilities that go beyond the limitations of conventional therapies.

Neurological diseases

Alzheimer’s disease

Accumulating evidence indicates that impaired cerebral insulin signaling and neuroinflammation are key contributors to AD pathogenesis, positioning GLP-1RAs as promising neuroprotective agents [266–268]. Restoration of GLP-1R activity enhances CaMKK2-AMPK signaling, resulting in reduced β-secretase 1 (BACE1)-mediated cleavage of amyloid precursor protein (APP), increased microglial clearance of amyloid-β (Aβ), and improved memory in multiple transgenic AD models [269]. Studies using liraglutide and exenatide have demonstrated marked reductions in Aβ plaque deposition, tau hyperphosphorylation, and microglial activation, along with cognitive improvements in AD animal models [268, 270]. These findings align with an AMPK-centered mechanism and suggest convergent benefits across different GLP-1RAs [269].

A major challenge in clinical translation is the limited permeability of GLP-1RAs across the blood-brain barrier (BBB). Nanoplatforms offer a solution by enhancing brain-specific delivery [271, 272]. One effective strategy involves receptor-mediated transcytosis. For instance, PEG-PLA NPs functionalized with Angiopep-2, a ligand that targets low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1 (LRP1) on BBB endothelial cells, have been used to encapsulate liraglutide. This system significantly increases brain accumulation compared to systemic injection. Upon crossing the BBB, the NPs release liraglutide, which targets neurons and glia, reduces neuroinflammation by suppressing NF-κB signaling in microglia, and promotes astrocytic polarization toward a neuroprotective A2 phenotype [208, 273] (Fig. 6). Sustained AMPK phosphorylation (> 24 h) achieved via this delivery matches the time window required for effective BACE1 inhibition [269, 274]. Another promising strategy is intranasal delivery, which bypasses the BBB via olfactory and trigeminal pathways [275, 276]. Intranasal formulations such as poly(allyl succinate)-cell penetrating peptide-Glucagon-like peptide-1 (PAS-CPP-GLP-1) NPs have demonstrated rapid enhancement of learning and memory in AD animal models within minutes of administration [277]. Pharmacokinetic comparisons indicate that intranasal delivery of exenatide or dulaglutide leads to preferential accumulation in the hippocampus and neocortex within 30 min, with higher regional exposure than intravenous administration, even in amyloid precursor protein/presenilin 1 mice (APP/PS1) transgenic mice [278]. Emerging advances aim to refine nanocarriers for precise delivery, extended brain retention, and controlled release. Notably, plant-derived exosome-like nanovesicles from ginger are under investigation as biocompatible, scalable vehicles for chronic intranasal GLP-1RAs administration [279].

Clinically, liraglutide has demonstrated favorable trends in slowing cognitive decline and brain atrophy in Phase II trials [280]. Ongoing Phase III trials, including the EVOKE study assessing semaglutide in early-stage AD, are expected to further clarify the potential of GLP-1RAs as disease-modifying agents [281]. Positive outcomes may stimulate broader interest in advanced delivery platforms, such as implantable nanofluidic reservoirs for continuous cerebrospinal delivery or brain-targeted exosome systems. Mechanistically, GLP-1R activation attenuates neuroinflammation through inhibition of the TLR4/NF-κB pathway, reducing levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β) while promoting anti-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-10 (IL-10) [282–284]. This anti-inflammatory action complements AMPK-mediated reductions in Aβ accumulation, providing a dual anti-amyloid and anti-inflammatory rationale for GLP-1RA-based nanotherapies [269, 285]. When combined with targeted nanoparticle delivery, these mechanisms support the development of highly effective, personalized treatments for AD.

In summary, GLP-1RAs offer a multifaceted therapeutic approach in AD by simultaneously targeting key pathological processes including amyloid accumulation and neuroinflammation. Nanoplatform-based delivery systems address critical challenges related to BBB permeability and drug bioavailability, enhancing CNS targeting and sustained receptor activation. Continued advancements in nanoparticle design and administration routes, such as receptor-mediated transcytosis and intranasal delivery, hold significant promise for translating GLP-1RAs therapies into effective, disease-modifying treatments for AD. The integration of mechanistic insights with innovative nanotechnology is poised to transform clinical management and improve outcomes for patients afflicted by this devastating neurodegenerative disorder.

Parkinson’s disease

PD is the second most common neurodegenerative disorder worldwide, characterized primarily by progressive loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra and the accumulation of α-syn aggregates. The resulting motor symptoms—tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia, and postural instability—are often accompanied by non-motor manifestations including cognitive decline and autonomic dysfunction. Current therapies predominantly provide symptomatic relief but fail to halt or reverse neurodegeneration. In this context, GLP-1RAs have emerged as promising candidates for disease modification, owing to their pleiotropic neuroprotective effects demonstrated in preclinical and clinical studies [268, 286]. Notably, a landmark Phase II clinical trial reported significant improvements in motor function with weekly exenatide administration compared to placebo, suggesting potential benefits beyond symptomatic management [287]. Mechanistic investigations attribute these effects to enhanced mitochondrial biogenesis and function, upregulation of neurotrophic factors, and inhibition of α-syn aggregation, collectively contributing to neuronal preservation [288, 289].

Nanotechnology-based approaches in PD focus on overcoming the challenge of efficient drug delivery to the brain [290]. One innovative method employs focused ultrasound (FUS) combined with microbubbles to transiently open the BBB, facilitating nanoparticle entry into the CNS [291]. In preclinical studies, PEG liposomes loaded with glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF) delivered via FUS markedly improved motor performance, illustrating the synergistic potential of combining nanomedicine with FUS-mediated BBB disruption [292]. Intranasal delivery systems, analogous to those developed for AD, also hold significant promise for PD [293, 294]. Odorranalectin-conjugated PEG-PLGA NPs (OL-NP) have successfully transported macromolecules such as urocortin into PD animal models via the intranasal route, enhancing neuronal survival and attenuating disease progression [295]. Incorporation of GLP-1 agonists like exenatide into OL-NP systems offers a non-invasive alternative that bypasses the BBB and improves CNS targeting. Additionally, exendin-4-modified deferoxamine (DFO) NPs (Ex-4@DFO NPs) have been developed to address limitations of traditional iron chelators. These NPs effectively penetrate brain tissue, reduce neuronal damage and inflammation, and improve motor outcomes in PD mouse models [207] (Fig. 7). Combination therapies employing multifunctional NPs provide a particularly compelling approach in PD by simultaneously targeting oxidative stress and neuroinflammation, which are key drivers of disease pathology [296–298]. For example, a dual synergistic nanoreactor incorporating dihydroquercetin and platinum nanocatalysts within mannose-modified OL-NP demonstrated robust neuroprotection by reducing neuronal injury and modulating microglial activation toward an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype [299]. In a complementary approach, exenatide-functionalized DFO NPs effectively chelated excess iron and preserved dopaminergic neurons in a 1-methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6-tetrahydropyridine (MPTP) mouse model [207]. Furthermore, targeted delivery using peptides such as rabies virus glycoprotein 29 (RVG29) has enabled neuron-specific accumulation of therapeutics, concentrating their activity within key regions of neurodegeneration, including the substantia nigra and striatum [300–302]. These advances highlight the versatility and therapeutic potential of GLP-1R-targeted nanomedicines to comprehensively address the multifactorial pathology of PD.

However, effective delivery to the brain remains a major challenge due to the restrictive nature of the BBB. Nanotechnology-based delivery systems offer innovative solutions to overcome this barrier, enhance drug bioavailability, and enable targeted, sustained therapeutic action. Integration of GLP-1RAs with advanced nanoplatforms holds great promise to transform the therapeutic landscape of PD by enabling multifaceted intervention at cellular and molecular levels.

Other neurological disorders

The neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory properties of GLP-1RAs have prompted investigation into their therapeutic potential across a wide range of neurological conditions beyond AD and PD. These include stroke, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), peripheral neuropathy, epilepsy, and various cognitive disorders [303]. Given the importance of early post-stroke intervention, researchers are exploring the use of nanoparticle technology. One potential strategy involves engineering brain-targeted NPs designed to release exenatide within the initial 72 h post-stroke, with the aim of achieving optimal neuroprotection [304–306]. Innovative platforms such as red blood cell membrane-camouflaged nanosponges (Mn₃O₄@nanoerythrocyte-T7) demonstrate prolonged circulation time and effective BBB penetration via T7 peptide-mediated targeting. These systems accumulate in ischemic brain regions, scavenge ROS, deliver oxygen, and significantly reduce infarct size in middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO) models [307]. In ALS, GLP-1 analogues have extended survival in preclinical studies. However, limited spinal cord penetration poses a challenge, necessitating advanced delivery strategies such as intrathecal nanoparticle injection or viral vector-mediated motor neuron targeting [308–310]. For peripheral neuropathies, particularly diabetic neuropathy, local nanoparticle delivery to the dorsal root ganglia or peripheral nerves has shown potential to promote axonal regeneration and reduce oxidative damage [286, 311]. GLP-1R modulation also shows promise in epilepsy and cognitive impairment, where it may influence neurotransmitter release and exert anticonvulsant effects [312]. Nanoparticle-based delivery platforms capable of crossing the BBB could enable targeted regulation of neuronal excitability, offering novel options for managing refractory epilepsy [313–315].

Collectively, these applications illustrate the versatility of GLP-1R-targeted nanomedicine. By leveraging a common molecular mechanism, precision nanodelivery of GLP-1RAs holds promise for treating diverse neurological disorders, representing a significant advance beyond conventional neurotherapeutic approaches.

Cardiovascular diseases

Cardiovascular diseases (CVD) remain the leading cause of global morbidity and mortality, exerting significant pressure on healthcare systems. Common risk factors such as diabetes, obesity, hypertension, and dyslipidemia contribute to the multifactorial pathogenesis of CVD, including coronary artery disease, heart failure, and stroke. However, new potent agents have emerged that provide dual benefits by improving glycemic control and exerting cardioprotective effects. Large-scale clinical trials have demonstrated that GLP-1RAs reduce the incidence of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), including myocardial infarction (MI) and stroke, through metabolic, anti-inflammatory, and direct cardiac mechanisms in both diabetic and non-diabetic populations [316–318]. The SELECT trial further showed that once-weekly semaglutide reduced first-event MACE by 20% in adults with obesity and prior cerebrovascular disease, extending cardiovascular protection beyond diabetes [319, 320].

Building on the established cardiovascular benefits of GLP-1RAs, engineered nanoplatforms provide an innovative means to further enhance therapeutic efficacy through targeted delivery to cardiac and vascular tissues [321]. A notable application of GLP-1R-targeted nanomedicine is in the treatment of atherosclerosis. Plaque-directed nanoparticles (GlpNPs) have demonstrated efficacy in reducing atherosclerotic burden in preclinical models without affecting systemic metabolic parameters [197] (Fig. 8). Expanding upon these targeted delivery approaches, a novel nanoparticle formulation (BSA@LIR-PMF) was developed to address diabetes-associated cardiovascular complications. This system encapsulates liraglutide within platelet membrane fragments and employs BSA as an outer coating. Preclinical studies indicate that BSA@LIR-PMF effectively targets inflamed vascular sites and demonstrates substantial therapeutic efficacy, supporting its potential as a clinically translatable nanomedicine platform for cardiovascular intervention in diabetic patients [161] (Fig. 9). Notably, these NPs are designed to resist proteolytic degradation and respond to inflammatory enzymes within atherosclerotic plaques, enhancing both stability and site-specific drug release. With embedded MRI contrast agents, they enable simultaneous imaging and localized therapy by targeting GLP-1R expressed on plaque-associated smooth muscle cells and macrophages. This dual targeting induces plaque stabilization through increased Col deposition and reduced lipid content. Transcriptomic profiling of GlpNP-treated lesions revealed upregulation of contractile smooth muscle markers (ACTA2, MYH11) and downregulation of inflammatory genes (IL1B, MMP9), supporting a mechanism of dual-cell reprogramming [197]. Such targeted strategies may complement conventional interventions, such as stenting or statins, to stabilize vulnerable plaques and prevent cardiovascular events, even in non-diabetic patients.

Beyond atherosclerosis, GLP-1R-targeted NPs have been evaluated in MI and heart failure. Preclinical models of acute MI show improvements in left ventricular (LV) function following GLP-1RAs delivery [49, 322, 323]. However, clinical trials in chronic heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), such as the LIVE trial, have yielded mixed or modestly negative results. In contrast, GLP-1RAs have shown potential benefits in HFpEF, supported by outcomes from the STEP HFpEF trial [324]. Tirzepatide, a dual GIP/GLP-1RA, has been associated with a 38% reduction in HFpEF-related hospitalizations in patients achieving sustained weight loss [325]. In the context of atrial fibrillation, Exenatide-4 (Ex4) has demonstrated anti-arrhythmic and anti-fibrotic effects through modulation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. In rat MI models, Ex4 improved atrial conduction, reduced fibrosis, and stabilized electrophysiological properties [326]. To enhance myocardial targeting, porous silicon NPs conjugated with peptides recognizing extracellular matrix components in ischemic tissue have shown improved drug localization while minimizing systemic side effects such as tachycardia [327–329]. Another approach utilizes acetylated dextran NPs functionalized with dual-targeting ligands, namely linTT1 and atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP), to deliver therapeutics to both atherosclerotic macrophages and myocardial cells [330]. GLP-1R-targeted nanoplatforms also show promise in the treatment of peripheral arterial disease (PAD), where they promote angiogenesis in ischemic limbs by enhancing endothelial nitric oxide production [234, 331]. In diabetes models, GLP-1(32–36) has been demonstrated to restore perfusion and capillary density by rescuing mitochondrial function in endothelial progenitor cells [332]. Building on this, future research can explore the utilization of nanohydrogels for the delivery of GLP-1(32–36). These nanohydrogels are anticipated to enhance the bioavailability and sustained action of GLP-1(32–36) within endothelial progenitor cells, thereby more effectively promoting the restoration of perfusion and capillary density. Additionally, renal-targeted NPs delivering GLP-1RAs exploit their diuretic and natriuretic actions for hypertension and DN, reducing systemic exposure and minimizing adverse effects [232, 333, 334]. Because GLP-1RAs can mildly increase heart rate through autonomic activation, nanoparticle systems that localize drug delivery to vascular tissue may limit sinoatrial node exposure. Co-formulation with low-dose β blockers or the development of continuous-release implants, such as polymers co-loaded with semaglutide and bisoprolol, is currently under preclinical evaluation to mitigate tachycardia while preserving cardiovascular benefits [234, 335, 336].

In summary, GLP-1R-targeted nanotherapeutics offer a novel paradigm for cardiovascular intervention, enabling precision delivery and multimodal action. These platforms may complement or even surpass conventional treatments by addressing structural, metabolic, and electrophysiological components of CVD.

Cancer

Overexpression of GLP-1R in specific tumor types offers valuable opportunities for receptor-targeted imaging and therapeutic interventions [337, 338].

GLP-1R is expressed at both mRNA and protein levels in a range of tissues including pancreatic islets, thyroid C cells, gonads, and the urogenital system. Tumors arising from these tissues often show altered GLP-1R expression patterns [337, 338]. Among them, pancreatic tumors, particularly insulinomas and pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors (pNETs), exhibit the most consistently elevated GLP-1R expression. For instance, median receptor levels in pancreatic tumors can reach ~ 200, compared to near-zero expression in adjacent normal pancreatic tissue. While ovarian cancers also show moderate elevation, GLP-1R expression remains relatively low in most other tissues, including adrenal gland, colon, liver, lung, and kidney, regardless of malignancy status [61].

Beyond insulinomas, GLP-1R expression has been identified in a range of other tumor types, including pheochromocytomas, paragangliomas, meningiomas, astrocytomas, medulloblastomas, medullary thyroid carcinomas, and gastrinomas, thereby expanding the potential for receptor-targeted diagnostic and therapeutic applications [60]. Case studies report focal uptake of 68Ga-exendin-4 PET in approximately 60% of paraganglioma and medullary thyroid carcinoma lesions, supporting its utility in selected neuroendocrine tumors [339]. However, GLP-1R expression is limited in most common epithelial malignancies, including colorectal, breast, and prostate cancers, restricting the feasibility of direct targeting in these contexts. Although GLP-1R has been detected in certain cases of thyroid and prostate cancer, its expression appears inconsistent and may lack functional relevance. In thyroid cancer, for instance, GLP-1R is predominantly expressed in parafollicular C cells. While some preclinical studies have raised concerns regarding GLP-1RA-induced C-cell hyperplasia, large-scale clinical data have not demonstrated an increased cancer risk in humans [340]. In prostate cancer, GLP-1R expression is relatively limited, but metabolic modulation by GLP-1RAs may confer indirect protective effects via inhibition of the PI3K/Akt pathway [341]. In breast cancer, GLP-1R expression remains a subject of debate, with no consistent association observed between receptor activity and overall cancer risk. However, emerging evidence suggests a potential protective effect in obese patients, which may be attributed to the weight-reducing and anti-inflammatory properties of GLP-1RAs [337]. Additional evidence suggests that GLP-1R-based therapies may attenuate tumor progression in hepatocellular carcinoma and colorectal cancer through metabolic and anti-inflammatory mechanisms.

Nevertheless, GLP-1RAs may exert indirect anticancer effects through their role in improving metabolic homeostasis. Evidence from a large cohort study involving over 104,000 treatment-naïve individuals demonstrated a 17% reduction in the incidence of colorectal cancer among GLP-1RA users compared to those receiving metformin or DPP-4 inhibitors, suggesting a potential protective effect associated with GLP-1RA therapy [342]. Given this landscape, our review emphasizes pancreatic malignancies as representative and translationally relevant models for GLP-1R-targeted nanoplatforms. Among these, insulinomas and pNETs are particularly notable due to their highly stable and homogeneous GLP-1R expression, with positivity rates reported to exceed 95% [343], establishing a clear molecular basis for diagnostic and therapeutic targeting. Immunohistochemical studies show that elevated receptor levels in insulinomas inversely correlate with metastatic potential [344], further reinforcing the therapeutic value of this target. pNETs, particularly insulinomas, consistently demonstrate elevated GLP-1R expression, enabling highly specific diagnostic and therapeutic strategies [60, 345]. Radiolabeled exendin-4 derivatives, such as 68Ga-NOTA-exendin-4 and 18F-PEG-exendin-4, have significantly enhanced the sensitivity and specificity of insulinoma detection via PET/CT, outperforming conventional imaging modalities [346–350]. Additionally, 111In-labeled [Lys40(Ahx-DTPA-111In)NH2]-exendin-4 has demonstrated potent tumor accumulation and cytotoxicity in the Rip1Tag2 mouse model, reducing tumor volume by up to 94% following a single dose without off-target toxicity [351, 352]. These effects are mediated through increased apoptosis, necrosis, and reduced tumor cell proliferation [352]. Building on prior advances, researchers developed a triblock recombinant protein capable of self-assembling into nanoscale formulations with high radionuclide-loading capacity and strong binding affinity for insulinoma cells. Intravenous administration of these formulations, particularly the combination of 131I-ELP-POP and exendin-4-ELP-POP, resulted in marked tumor regression in insulinoma xenograft models. These findings highlight the potential of this platform as a safe and effective therapeutic strategy for insulinoma treatment [353] (Fig. 10). Importantly, insulinoma is typically characterized by small tumor size (< 2 cm) and indolent growth, facilitating pharmacodynamic assessment of targeted therapies in clinical trials [354]. Moreover, the frequent hypoglycemic episodes triggered by GLP-1RAs in insulinoma patients provide reverse functional validation of the receptor’s therapeutic relevance, enabling precise dose titration and efficacy prediction [354].

In summary, while GLP-1R is expressed in multiple cancer types, pancreatic malignancies, particularly insulinoma, offer the most robust, well-validated, and clinically actionable platform for GLP-1R-targeted nanotherapeutics. Future studies may expand on emerging evidence in other cancers, but pancreatic models currently provide the strongest foundation for translational research.

Building upon these imaging advances, GLP-1R-targeted NPs have been developed for therapeutic applications. One strategy involves radionuclide therapy using modified GLP-1 analogues, such as Lys40(Ahx-DOTA)NH₂-exendin-3 labelled with 68Ga. These constructs demonstrate superior tumor specificity and lower background radiation compared to earlier tracers like 99mTc and 111In [355]. For therapeutic purposes, [Lys40(177Lu-DOTA)]-exendin-3 delivers localized radiation doses of approximately 8 Gy to insulinoma xenografts while minimizing renal exposure through microdosing protocols [352]. Multivalent constructs, including AuNPs conjugated with multiple exendin-4 molecules and radioactive isotopes, further enhance tumor targeting and retention [149]. Another innovative approach employs GLP-1R-targeted PDT. Conjugation of exendin-4 with IRDye700DX enables selective binding to GLP-1R-expressing insulinoma cells. Upon near-infrared light exposure, these conjugates generate ROS, leading to tumor-specific ablation while sparing adjacent healthy tissue. In murine models, a single light exposure eradicated approximately 40% of subcutaneous INS-1 tumors, exemplifying a theranostic approach combining imaging and therapy in a single system [356, 357].

Emerging nanotherapeutic strategies integrate GLP-1R-targeting with chemotherapeutic payloads. For example, doxorubicin-loaded exendin-4 nanoassemblies have been developed for insulinoma and metastatic GLP-1R-positive neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) targeting [346]. Additionally, NPs co-delivering mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) inhibitors show potential in combination therapy [345, 358, 359]. In vitro studies report that everolimus synergizes with GLP-1R radioligand therapy to reduce clonogenic survival of BON-1 spheroids by over 60% [360].

In summary, while GLP-1R expression is not ubiquitous across all cancers, tumor types with high receptor levels benefit significantly from nanoparticle-enabled imaging and therapy. Clinical implementation of exendin-based PET/CT and ongoing trials in GLP-1R-targeted radiotherapy illustrate the promise of receptor-driven precision oncology, enabling high-efficacy, low-toxicity treatment strategies.

Autoimmune and inflammatory diseases

In addition to their established roles in metabolic and neurodegenerative disorders, GLP-1RAs are increasingly recognized for their anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties [361]. Chronic inflammatory conditions, including rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and IBD, frequently co-exist with metabolic syndrome. In these contexts, GLP-1RAs have been associated with improved clinical outcomes and disease modulation [362, 363]. Multicenter database analyses have reported reductions of 12–30% in C-reactive protein (CRP) levels among GLP-1RA users across a range of inflammatory disease indications [364].

Rheumatoid arthritis

RA is a chronic autoimmune disorder characterized by persistent synovial inflammation, leading to progressive cartilage degradation and bone erosion [365]. Although GLP-1R expression was not historically associated with rheumatoid arthritis (RA), recent findings indicate that GLP-1R activation suppresses key pro-inflammatory signaling pathways [366, 367]. In murine models of inflammatory arthritis, liraglutide treatment significantly reduced synovial inflammation, joint damage, and the expression of cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β, likely mediated through GLP-1R expressed on macrophages and fibroblast-like synoviocytes within the inflamed joint microenvironment [368–370]. Proteomic analyses of liraglutide-treated joints demonstrated upregulation of the cAMP-PKA axis and suppression of NF-κB-driven transcripts, supporting a mechanistic basis for anti-inflammatory action [371]. Ongoing clinical trials are evaluating the utility of GLP-1RAs as adjuncts in RA therapy, with particular focus on overweight patient populations [372].

Nanotechnology provides a promising platform for site-specific delivery of anti-inflammatory GLP-1 analogues to inflamed joints. Although RA-specific GLP-1 nanotherapeutics remain under development, albumin NPs have shown efficacy as carriers. For instance, methotrexate (MTX) conjugated to albumin NPs resulted in a 50% reduction in hepatic transaminases and a 2-fold increase in drug accumulation within arthritic paw tissue compared to free MTX, highlighting enhanced local bioavailability [373–375]. Drawing on nab-paclitaxel design principles, GLP-1 analogues could be encapsulated in albumin NPs with tailored surface chemistries to optimize joint targeting [376]. In a rat model of col-induced arthritis, a single intra-articular injection of prednisolone-loaded liposomes significantly reduced paw swelling and inflammatory cytokines within several weeks, outperforming systemic administration [377]. Glycol chitosan/fucoidan nanogels, previously applied in obstructive sleep apnea models, offer a biodegradable and injectable matrix for sustained intra-articular GLP-1 delivery [378]. Future designs may incorporate GLP-1 with conventional RA drugs into multifunctional NPs, allowing for synergistic effects while minimizing the required doses of systemic immunosuppressants [367, 379, 380].

From a patient perspective, monthly administration of GLP-1R-targeted NPs could help maintain disease remission while reducing dependence on corticosteroids and biologics. Mechanistically, GLP-1RAs suppress innate immune responses by downregulating pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-6, while upregulating adiponectin, an anti-inflammatory adipokine [361, 381, 382]. A systematic review of 23 clinical and preclinical studies confirmed GLP-1R expression on monocytes, macrophages, and effector T cells, underscoring the receptor’s role in systemic immunomodulation [383].

In summary, GLP-1RAs show considerable promise as immunomodulatory agents in RA. The integration of nanotechnology can enhance therapeutic precision, improve efficacy, and reduce off-target effects. Continued research into GLP-1R-targeted nanomedicine may enable safer, more effective, and patient-centered treatment options for RA.

Inflammatory bowel disease

Epidemiological data indicate that IBD, which includes Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis, is a chronic, relapsing inflammatory condition of the GI tract with complex etiology involving genetic, environmental, microbial, and immune factors. Patients with IBD often experience debilitating symptoms and face increased risks of hospitalization and surgical interventions. Interestingly, recent clinical observations reveal that diabetic patients receiving GLP-1RAs exhibit reduced frequency of IBD flares and lower surgical rates, suggesting a `potential protective effect [362]. GLP-1 and its analogues are known to exert multifaceted beneficial effects on GI physiology, including enhancement of mucosal barrier integrity, modulation of intestinal motility, and regulation of immune responses within the gut mucosa. Moreover, GLP-1 may influence the composition and function of the gut microbiota, which plays a pivotal role in IBD pathogenesis [384]. A large Danish cohort study further reported significantly lower IBD-related hospitalization and surgical rates among T2DM patients using GLP-1RAs [362].

Preclinical data support the protective role of GLP-1 in ulcerative colitis (UC) models, where it inhibits pro-inflammatory mediator production and downregulates key signaling pathways such as AKT/NF-κB and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) [385]. To improve site-specific delivery, oral nanoformulations designed to release GLP-1RAs in the colon are under development [386, 387]. The successful alleviation of colitis by pH-responsive naringin NPs validates localized drug delivery. This inspires future exploration of similar pH-responsive NPs for liraglutide encapsulation, leveraging its anti-inflammatory and gut-modulating effects for precise, high-concentration release in the inflamed colon, ultimately maximizing efficacy and reducing systemic side effects for a safer, more efficient IBD therapy [388]. Such localized colonic release limits systemic exposure, thereby minimizing side effects like nausea [389]. An alternative strategy involves engineered probiotics as “living nanofactories” that secrete GLP-1 analogues directly in the gut [390, 391]. Modified Lactococcus lactis strains producing GLP-1 have demonstrated therapeutic efficacy in diabetic models and hold promise for IBD applications [392–394]. In a trinitrobenzene sulfonic acid (TNBS)-induced colitis model, engineered L. lactis reduced histological inflammation scores by approximately 40% compared to controls [395]. In moderate to severe IBD, intravenously administered NPs designed to selectively target inflamed intestinal tissue offer therapeutic potential [387, 396]. Liposomes functionalized with α4β7 integrin antibodies have shown selective accumulation at IBD lesions [397]. Incorporating GLP-1 analogues into these platforms may enable synergistic metabolic and immunomodulatory effects. In TNBS colitis models, such targeted liposomes reduced inflammation to a degree comparable to high-dose corticosteroids [398]. Lipid-based vesicles modified with lactoferrin or cationic lipids further exploit disrupted mucosal barriers to enhance epithelial penetration [399]. GLP-1R expression on intraepithelial lymphocytes and gut macrophages likely mediates these local effects, supporting the therapeutic rationale for GLP-1R-targeted nanotherapies in reducing steroid dependence [400, 401].

In summary, GLP-1R-targeted therapies offer a promising avenue for IBD treatment by integrating metabolic control with potent anti-inflammatory effects. Nanotechnology-enabled delivery systems facilitate precise, localized drug release within the GI tract, improving efficacy while limiting systemic toxicity. The development of pH-responsive NPs and engineered probiotic “factories” marks a new frontier in IBD therapy, with potential to reduce reliance on corticosteroids and biologics. Future research should focus on clinical translation of these platforms to enhance patient outcomes and quality of life.

Other autoimmune and inflammatory disorders

The therapeutic scope of GLP-1RAs has expanded beyond metabolic diseases to encompass a range of autoimmune and inflammatory conditions marked by chronic immune dysregulation. Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and multiple sclerosis (MS) exemplify such disorders, where conventional immunosuppressive therapies, though often effective, are associated with considerable risks including infection and systemic toxicity. In contrast, GLP-1RAs offer a more targeted immunomodulatory approach by selectively modulating both innate and adaptive immune responses.

In MS, GLP-1RAs have shown neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory effects in experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE), a widely used preclinical model. The brain-penetrant GLP-1RA NLY01 significantly attenuated microglial activation and improved clinical outcomes in EAE models. Similar therapeutic effects have been observed with OL-NP encapsulating exenatide, underscoring the potential of nanocarriers to achieve targeted delivery across the BBB and facilitate localized immune modulation [402–404]. In SLE, GLP-1RAs may mitigate vascular inflammation and correct metabolic disturbances commonly seen in lupus pathophysiology. Retrospective analyses indicate a 35% reduction in the risk of end-stage renal disease among lupus nephritis patients treated with GLP-1RAs, suggesting both renal and systemic benefits [405–408]. Emerging data also support the anti-inflammatory potential of GLP-1RAs in pulmonary conditions such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and asthma, where GLP-1R is expressed in airway smooth muscle and immune cells. Preclinical studies demonstrate that GLP-1RAs treatment reduces pulmonary inflammation and airway hyperresponsiveness [409, 410]. At the population level, GLP-1RA use is associated with an 18% reduction in COPD exacerbations compared to other antidiabetic therapies [411]. Looking ahead, inhalable GLP-1-loaded NPs may enable localized delivery to the lungs, enhancing efficacy while minimizing systemic exposure [412, 413].

In summary, GLP-1R-targeted nanoplatforms represent a promising immunotherapeutic strategy that harnesses endogenous incretin pathways to re-establish immune homeostasis, offering an alternative to broad-spectrum immunosuppression. Although most evidence remains preclinical, the favorable safety profile of GLP-1RAs and established regulatory pathways for lipid- and PLGA-based carriers support a high potential for clinical translation [414, 415]. These developments may herald a new era in which incretin-based nanotherapies become integral to managing autoimmune and inflammatory diseases, extending the role of GLP-1R modulation well beyond glycemic control.

Multimodal therapeutic strategies using nanoplatforms

One of the most significant advantages of nanoplatforms lies in their ability to consolidate multiple therapeutic strategies within a single delivery system, thereby facilitating multimodal therapy [417, 418]. In the context of GLP-1R-targeted nanomedicine, this capability enables the co-delivery of distinct therapeutic classes, the integration of diagnostic and therapeutic functionalities (theranostics), and the customization of delivery profiles based on individual patient variability. Such versatility supports the development of personalized treatment regimens that can address complex, multifactorial diseases with greater precision and efficacy.

Co-delivery of multiple drugs

Combination therapy has emerged as a strategic modality for treating complex diseases, particularly metabolic disorders, cancer, and neurodegenerative conditions, where monotherapies often fail to address the multifactorial pathophysiology or elicit sustained responses. GLP-1R-targeting nanocarriers capable of co-encapsulating multiple agents offer therapeutic synergies by synchronizing pharmacokinetics, enhancing target site accumulation, and modulating multiple disease pathways simultaneously [418, 419].

In metabolic diseases such as diabetes, co-delivery systems integrating GLP-1RAs with complementary agents such as DPP-4 inhibitors or insulin have demonstrated superior long-term glycemic control compared to monotherapies. For instance, microfluidic-fabricated composite NPs co-loaded with a GLP-1 analogue and sitagliptin reduced fasting glucose levels 1.7-fold more effectively than either agent alone in db/db mice, with sustained HbA1c reductions over six weeks [420]. This improvement results from the synergistic extension of GLP-1 activity and DPP-4 inhibition, a combination difficult to replicate with a single agent. Similarly, chitosan-modified PLGA NPs co-encapsulating liraglutide and alogliptin normalized glycemia for 24 h post-oral administration in STZ-induced diabetic rats, underscoring improved glucose regulation and the translational promise of non-invasive delivery routes [416]. The latter is especially critical for enhancing long-term patient adherence, a key determinant of therapeutic durability. Compared to monotherapies that often lead to dose escalation and tolerance, these co-delivery platforms demonstrate prolonged efficacy at lower doses, reducing adverse effects and improving patient outcomes.

In neurodegenerative diseases, co-delivery approaches offer a rational means of concurrently targeting neuroinflammation, metabolic dysregulation, and neuronal apoptosis. Lipid nanocapsules co-delivering liraglutide and brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) plasmids markedly restored dopaminergic tone and motor performance in MPTP-induced PD models, surpassing the effects of either agent alone [282, 421]. This dual mechanism, which combines metabolic rebalancing through GLP-1RAs and direct neuroprotection mediated by brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), delayed neurodegeneration and suggests a viable strategy for long-term disease modification. Importantly, the co-delivery ensures synchronized bioavailability and spatial co-localization at pathological sites, features unattainable with separate systemic administrations. However, while such multimodal designs are conceptually promising, they remain mostly at the preclinical stage. Challenges in transgene delivery, nanoparticle stability, and immunogenicity still hamper their progression into human trials.

In oncology, GLP-1R-targeted nanoplatforms enable highly specific delivery of cytotoxic agents to receptor-expressing tumors, minimizing off-target toxicity while addressing chemoresistance. For example, dual-agent nanoplatforms co-delivering BH3 mimetics with low-dose doxorubicin have been proposed to exploit mitochondrial priming and DNA damage simultaneously, enhancing apoptosis while reducing the dose-dependent cardiotoxicity of doxorubicin. Integration with photothermal or photodynamic therapy has further enhanced this paradigm, creating hybrid platforms capable of stimulus-responsive, multi-mechanistic tumor eradication [422–424]. Compared with monotherapy, such combinations have shown not only improved tumor suppression in vivo but also higher survival rates and lower systemic burden. These platforms also offer the advantage of tunable release kinetics, ensuring that each therapeutic component exerts its action at the optimal time point. Nonetheless, Translational challenges remain substantial, constrained by interpatient variability in GLP-1R expression, the absence of real-time imaging tools for guiding personalized dosing, and an incomplete understanding of immune responses to complex nanoparticle formulations.

In summary, co-delivery via GLP-1R-targeted nanocarriers represents a promising yet underexploited strategy to enhance long-term efficacy and disease-modifying capacity across multiple pathologies. These platforms surpass monotherapies by providing synergistic action, spatial-temporal precision, and sustained therapeutic pressure, essential for combating resistance and relapse. Moving forward, clinically viable designs will require optimized pharmacological pairing, scalable and reproducible fabrication processes, and the integration of companion diagnostics to guide patient selection and therapeutic monitoring. A more concerted effort toward translational validation, including GLP-1R expression mapping in patient cohorts and longitudinal safety studies, will be pivotal in converting these preclinical innovations into approved therapeutic regimens.

Gene and protein delivery platforms

Nanocarrier-enabled delivery of macromolecules, including genes, small interfering RNAs (siRNAs), and proteins, represents a paradigm shift in GLP-1R-targeted therapy, providing prolonged or programmable effects that outperform conventional small-molecule approaches in duration, precision, and disease specificity [213, 425, 426]. By engaging GLP-1R-expressing cells, these platforms allow for highly targeted modulation of molecular networks implicated in metabolic, cardiovascular, and neurodegenerative disorders. Compared with transient GLP-1R agonist administration, macromolecular delivery systems enable sustained expression or silencing of disease-relevant genes, long-term protein production, or immune reprogramming, offering a more durable and mechanistically nuanced therapeutic strategy.

Recent innovations in LNPs, particularly those integrating pH-sensitive or cationic helper lipids, have demonstrated effective mRNA delivery to pancreatic β-cells, resulting in transient but therapeutically relevant protein expression following systemic or intraperitoneal administration. Peptide-functionalized LNPs, guided by exenatide-derived ligands, have additionally achieved targeted mRNA delivery across the blood-brain barrier, establishing a framework for neuroendocrine applications of GLP-1R-targeted gene therapy [116, 427]. Compared to GLP-1 receptor agonist monotherapy, which requires frequent dosing and may lead to receptor desensitization, gene delivery using plasmids or siRNA, such as polyethyleneimine (PEI)-based complexes encoding GLP-1 or LNPs designed to silence GCGR expression, enables endogenous regulation of hormonal homeostasis. This approach offers more sustained glycemic control and reduces the need for frequent dosing [220, 428]. These platforms have also been adapted to exosome-based systems, capitalizing on their intrinsic immune evasion, cell tropism, and long circulation times to enable cell-specific mRNA delivery. For instance, macrophage-engineered exosomes loaded with GLP-1-encoding mRNAs promoted endogenous peptide production and glycemic normalization in diabetic models with minimal systemic immune activation [117]. The translational potential of these platforms is reinforced by the clinical advancement of lipid nanoparticle (LNP) technologies, several of which have progressed to human trials for indications such as hypercholesterolemia, through PCSK9 silencing, and infectious diseases, including COVID-19 vaccines [429, 430]. Their regulatory and manufacturing maturity provides a transferable foundation for GLP-1R-targeted gene delivery, accelerating the bench-to-bedside transition. However, GLP-1R-specific applications are still in preclinical phases, and further safety evaluations are warranted, especially for chronic use and repeated administration.

Beyond metabolic disease, GLP-1R-targeted macromolecular systems are increasingly applied in immunomodulation. For instance, pancreatic-targeted nanocarriers co-delivering GLP-1 and IL-10 plasmids provided dual metabolic and immune benefits in type 1 diabetes models, where GLP-1R activation restored islet function while IL-10 induced immune tolerance [431]. This dual-gene delivery addresses the autoimmune pathophysiology more comprehensively than either agent alone. Similarly, LNP cocktails delivering multiple mRNAs to islets without triggering cytokine storms represent a prototype for personalized “mix-and-match” immunotherapies, adaptable to patient-specific antigen profiles. Multi-antigen vesicles encoding tolerogenic cytokines have reversed hyperglycemia and expanded regulatory T cells in non-obese diabetic mice (NOD mice), offering a targeted approach to autoimmune intervention that surpasses the scope of GLP-1RAs alone [432, 433]. Protein therapeutics also benefit from GLP-1R-directed delivery. NPs co-encapsulating GLP-1 analogues with apolipoproteins, such as ApoA-I and LCAT, have been developed for plaque stabilization in atherosclerosis. This strategy offers a dual mode of action by combining metabolic regulation through GLP-1R activation with vascular protection mediated by enhanced reverse cholesterol transport [434–436]. This strategy may achieve superior long-term cardiovascular outcomes compared to either modality alone. In neurodegenerative disease models, the co-delivery of GLP-1 analogues and conformation-specific antibodies against Aβ or α-syn has achieved concurrent neuroprotection and proteopathy clearance, leading to sustained behavioral and histological improvements [437, 438]. These combinatorial approaches address both upstream triggers and downstream consequences of neurodegeneration, offering more holistic disease modification than monotherapies. Moreover, dual-agonist bicistronic mRNA platforms—for instance, those encoding GLP-1 and FGF21—demonstrate broader therapeutic coverage in obesity and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) models, where they simultaneously address energy metabolism, insulin sensitivity, and hepatic lipid clearance [439, 440]. Compared to single agonist strategies, these multi-target systems more effectively reverse complex metabolic pathologies and prolong therapeutic effects through network-level modulation.

In summary, GLP-1R-targeted gene and protein delivery platforms provide mechanistic depth and durability that exceed those of small-molecule GLP-1RAs. Their applications span endocrine regulation, immunotherapy, atherosclerosis stabilization, and neurodegeneration. Importantly, these macromolecular systems enable multimodal intervention within a single formulation, supporting the development of personalized nanotherapeutics. However, significant challenges persist, including concerns related to immunogenicity, limited reproducibility, high manufacturing costs, and the lack of validated clinical biomarkers for therapeutic response. Addressing these challenges through rational design, safety-by-design protocols, and translational trial frameworks will be crucial for realizing the long-term clinical impact of GLP-1R-targeted gene and protein therapies.

Theranostics

GLP-1R has emerged as an attractive target for theranostic nanoplatforms that integrate diagnostic imaging and site-specific therapy. These systems combine real-time disease visualization with GLP-1R-targeted treatment, enabling a dynamic “diagnose-treat-monitor” loop that greatly enhances therapeutic precision. Clinically validated molecular probes, such as 68Ga-, 177Lu-, or NIR-labeled exendin derivatives, bind GLP-1R with high affinity and provide detailed imaging of receptor-expressing lesions. Importantly, these same probes can be conjugated with therapeutic payloads, including cytotoxic agents, photosensitizers, or therapeutic radionuclides, to allow direct treatment of pathologic foci under imaging guidance [350, 441].

Compared with monotherapies, GLP-1R theranostic platforms offer several unique advantages. For instance, in patients with insulinoma, 68Ga-labeled exendin PET imaging can identify tumor burden and GLP-1R expression patterns, guiding subsequent administration of the same nanoplatform conjugated to 177Lu for targeted radiotherapy. This closed-loop “image-then-treat” strategy permits individualized dose planning, maximizes target engagement, and minimizes off-target toxicity [442, 443]. In contrast to empirical drug dosing, this feedback-driven approach represents a significant advance in therapeutic optimization. Recent advances in radiochemistry have enhanced the clinical feasibility of these platforms. Renal-sparing modifications, such as 68Ga/177Lu-DOTA-Lys40-exendin-4, have been shown to reduce renal radiation exposure by up to 60% while maintaining high tumor affinity, thereby supporting their advancement into early-phase human dosimetry trials [348, 444, 445]. These advances are critical in translating theranostics from preclinical concept to routine clinical workflow. Modular nanoscaffold design further augments the versatility of GLP-1R theranostic systems. PEGylated NPs with GLP-1 ligands and cell-penetrating arginine motifs enable an optimal balance between prolonged systemic circulation and rapid cytoplasmic uptake, improving intracellular delivery of both diagnostic and therapeutic cargos [446, 447]. Dendritic scaffolds, such as PAMAM G4, enable high-density multivalent conjugation of GLP-1 mimetics, imaging agents, and functional modules including triphenylphosphine for mitochondrial targeting. This architectural advantage expands their functional versatility beyond that of linear polymers [448]. Such structural engineering underlies tissue-specific pharmacodynamics, enabling localized therapy guided by real-time biodistribution tracking. Theranostic strategies have been extended to gene and protein delivery. Bimodal silica NPs encoding GLP-1 or FGF21 genes alongside siRNAs reprogrammed hepatocytes in diabetic mice with minimal cytotoxicity [210]. Similarly, chitosan-based nanocapsules co-delivering liraglutide and angiogenic nitric oxide cofactors induced vasodilation and glycemic control simultaneously, representing a multifunctional intervention [449, 450]. These integrated systems go beyond simple co-delivery by enabling molecular-level coordination of metabolic, vascular, and immunologic responses.

Dual-modality nanoprobes combining GLP-1R-based PET and T1-weighted MRI enable noninvasive quantification of β-cell mass, with potential application in therapeutic monitoring and patient stratification [451, 452]. In cardiovascular applications, gadolinium-chelated GLP-1 lipid NPs permit MRI-guided delivery to diabetic myocardium, while ultrasound-targeted microbubble destruction (UTMD) combined with semaglutide has reversed ventricular remodeling in diabetic rats [453, 454]. These “energy-assisted” strategies enhance tissue penetration and drug localization while minimizing systemic exposure. In atherosclerosis, proteolysis-resistant GLP-1RA NPs not only visualize plaques via MRI but also suppress smooth muscle proliferation, enabling simultaneous efficacy assessment and disease modification [197]. Neurotheranostic applications are progressing concurrently, with GLP-1R PET tracers being employed to stratify patients according to brain receptor availability. This information is essential for predicting therapeutic response or identifying patients who may not benefit from treatment [445]. Emerging NIR-II fluorophores such as LUXendin allow intraoperative visualization of islets and may facilitate photothermal co-therapy in a single construct [455]. Moreover, FUS enables spatiotemporally controlled delivery of GLP-1 nanomedicines across the blood–brain barrier under MRI guidance [456]. Longitudinal GLP-1R imaging offers the additional advantage of tracking receptor dynamics over the disease course, facilitating adaptive therapy akin to 18F-FDG PET-guided cancer treatment [457].

In comparison to traditional GLP-1RAs, these theranostic platforms introduce a level of control and personalization not achievable through pharmacologic means alone. They offer real-time target confirmation, individualized dosing, and combination functionalities in a single construct. However, despite these advantages, most systems remain at the preclinical or early translational stage. Challenges such as good manufacturing practices (GMP)-compliant production, regulatory standardization, imaging probe pharmacokinetics, and cost-effectiveness must be addressed to ensure clinical integration.

In summary, GLP-1R-targeted theranostic nanoplatforms represent a transformative class of precision medicine tools. By merging diagnostic and therapeutic functions into a unified nanosystem, they allow for target-guided intervention, real-time response monitoring, and individualized therapy adjustment. Their applications span endocrine disorders, CVD, and neurodegeneration. Future clinical translation will depend on advancing regulatory pathways, validating imaging–therapeutic correlations, and developing scalable manufacturing processes to fully harness their potential in personalized healthcare.

Personalized therapeutic strategies

Nanotechnology provides powerful tools for tailoring therapeutic delivery to specific organs, tissues, or patient subtypes—an essential step toward achieving true precision medicine. GLP-1R-targeted nanoplatforms are particularly well-suited to such customization due to their modular design and the availability of organ-specific targeting ligands. Unlike traditional one-size-fits-all therapies, personalized nanomedicine can adapt to disease heterogeneity, patient-specific pathology, and pharmacogenomic profiles, thereby improving long-term efficacy and minimizing adverse effects.

For instance, patients with diabetes driven predominantly by pancreatic β-cell dysfunction may benefit from GLP-1R-targeted NPs engineered for pancreas-specific accumulation. In contrast, liver-tropic GLP-1 carriers co-loaded with anti-fibrotic agents better address obesity- and MASLD-associated metabolic dysfunction [458–460]. Organ tropism can be fine-tuned by altering nanocarrier lipid tail length, pKa, or incorporating selective organ targeting (SORT) motifs to redirect biodistribution away from hepatic default accumulation and toward extrahepatic sites such as adipose tissue or the CNS [461, 462]. In patients with comorbid conditions such as PD and T2DM, brain-penetrant GLP-1 nanocarriers provide a unified therapeutic strategy that addresses both neurodegeneration and glucose dysregulation. This integrated approach surpasses the capabilities of conventional pharmacotherapies. In addition to structural tuning, AI is emerging as a transformative force in accelerating and refining nanocarrier design. High-throughput, AI-guided optimization platforms like AI-Guided Ionizable Lipid Engineering (AGILE) and other deep learning models enable rapid in silico screening of lipid libraries to identify optimal formulations for targeted delivery [463, 464]. These computational platforms shorten the design–validation cycle and facilitate real-time decision-making in formulation development. Integration with microfluidic systems, Bayesian optimization, and high-content phenotypic screening allows iterative refinement through active learning loops, effectively bridging bench-scale innovation and translational readiness [465]. Crucially, digital health technologies are opening new avenues for individualizing treatment strategies. Theranostic data obtained from PET, MRI, or wearable sensors can be integrated into patient-specific models to optimize nanocarrier dosing, release kinetics, and therapeutic index. In parallel, digital twin platforms that incorporate genomic, metabolic, and behavioral data are being developed to simulate individualized pharmacokinetics and support evidence-based therapy selection and adjustment [466, 467]. While still in early-stage validation, these digital ecosystems hold transformative potential for personalized GLP-1R therapy and beyond. Dynamic control of drug release is another frontier in personalization. Glucose-responsive GLP-1 nanocarriers can modulate release in response to glycemic fluctuations, offering metabolic adaptability for patients with variable insulin sensitivity or fluctuating glucose clearance rates [200]. Similarly, AI-optimized lipid carriers enable tuning of degradation rates in accordance with renal or hepatic clearance profiles, minimizing systemic exposure and maximizing therapeutic windows [463]. This adaptive behavior ensures that dosing is not only organ-targeted but also temporally aligned with physiological demand, enhancing both safety and long-term compliance.

Personalization also extends to combinatorial logic. Multi-compartment ‘cocktail’ NPs allow the simultaneous and adjustable delivery of GLP-1RAs with agents targeting inflammation, fibrosis, or neurodegeneration [468]. This modular assembly enables tailored therapeutic payloads to be dynamically reconfigured in response to disease progression or treatment resistance. For example, shifting ratios of anti-inflammatory versus anti-fibrotic payloads may be necessary as MASLD progresses from steatosis to fibrosis. Such flexibility surpasses fixed-dose combination drugs by introducing responsiveness to real-time patient status. Compared to monotherapies or conventional combination regimens, programmable nanoplatforms achieve personalization through three integrated mechanisms that include spatial targeting, physiological responsiveness, and AI-assisted optimization. This layered precision architecture substantially enhances the specificity and adaptability of GLP-1R-targeted therapies. As a result, these platforms are well positioned to advance next-generation precision medicine by delivering tailored, long-acting, and context-aware interventions with reduced side effects and improved clinical outcomes.

In conclusion, GLP-1R-targeted nanocarriers offer a highly customizable therapeutic platform with the capacity to revolutionize personalized care in metabolic and neurological diseases. Through programmable biodistribution, physiological adaptability, and AI-driven design, these next-generation systems hold promise for overcoming the limitations of static treatment paradigms and delivering precision therapeutics with enduring impact.

Challenges and perspectives

Biodistribution, pharmacokinetics, and safety

The clinical translation of GLP-1R-targeted nanotherapeutics hinges on effectively addressing challenges related to biodistribution, pharmacokinetics, and safety. While pancreatic β-cells are the principal therapeutic target, GLP-1Rs are widely expressed in δ-cells, exocrine pancreas, intestinal mucosa, renal tubules, pulmonary epithelium, selected brain nuclei, and the cardiac sinoatrial node. This broad expression spectrum increases the risk of off-target accumulation, which may exacerbate GLP-1RA-associated side effects including nausea, delayed gastric emptying, tachycardia, and arrhythmias. Prolonged exposure may also raise concerns about pancreatitis due to persistent stimulation of exocrine pancreatic tissue [3, 323, 469].

To mitigate these risks, biocompatible nanocarriers composed of metabolically degradable materials, such as hyaluronic acid, chitosan derivatives, and polysaccharides, have garnered attention due to their excellent tissue compatibility and breakdown into non-toxic metabolites like amino acids and monosaccharides [470, 471]. Moreover, conjugation strategies incorporating fatty acids or polysarcosine help reduce renal clearance, evade anti-PEG immune responses, and shield immunogenic epitopes without compromising GLP-1R targeting efficiency.

However, pharmacokinetic challenges persist. Native GLP-1 is rapidly inactivated by DPP-4 and eliminated by renal filtration, resulting in a plasma half-life of less than 2 min [3, 472]. While PEGylation and lipidation can extend systemic circulation, they also risk triggering complement activation, accelerated blood clearance (ABC), and immunogenicity. Notably, exenatide has induced neutralizing antibodies in up to 40% of patients, raising concerns about exendin-decorated nanocarriers [473, 474]. As such, novel materials must not only enhance stability and targeting but also exhibit low immunogenic potential. Beyond pharmacokinetics, nanotoxicology and immunogenicity remain major translational bottlenecks. Due to their high surface area, charge density, and unique reactivity, nanomaterials can provoke cellular oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, lysosomal impairment, and activation of pro-inflammatory pathways [475–478]. The formation of a dynamic “protein corona” upon exposure to biological fluids further alters nanoparticle identity and may elicit unintended innate or adaptive immune responses [479, 480]. These risks are particularly amplified in inorganic and hybrid nanoplatforms widely used in imaging and theranostics. Non-biodegradable inorganic NPs (e.g., iron oxide, gold, silica) may accumulate in the liver, spleen, or lungs, potentially inducing chronic oxidative injury, granuloma formation, or fibrosis [481, 482]. In lysosomal acidic environments, dissolution of certain metal-based particles (e.g., ZnO, CdSe quantum dots) can release cytotoxic ions more harmful than the nanoparticle matrix itself [483]. Hybrid systems, comprising polymer shells and inorganic cores, further complicate safety evaluation due to potential synergistic toxicity from acidic degradation products, enhanced inorganic ion release, and unanticipated immune activation [484].

Given these multifactorial risks, nanomedicine-specific safety evaluation frameworks are urgently needed. These should encompass comprehensive biodistribution mapping, long-term in vivo tracking, clearance profiling, and assessments of organ-specific toxicities (hepatic, renal, pulmonary, and neurological), alongside immunogenicity assays [485]. Material uniformity and batch reproducibility pose additional regulatory concerns, particularly for biosourced materials with inherent variability [486]. Moreover, challenges such as weak mechanical stability under physiological conditions, sterilization-related degradation, and high production costs hinder industrial scalability [487–489]. To overcome these limitations, a “Safety-by-Design” approach has emerged. This includes: (i) engineering biased or partial GLP-1RAs to retain therapeutic benefits while minimizing β-arrestin-mediated adverse effects [490]; (ii) developing responsive release systems (e.g., glucose-, pH-, or MMP-sensitive triggers) that confine drug activity to pathological tissues [200]; (iii) transient blockade of off-target GLP-1R to reduce systemic exposure [491]; and (iv) designing carriers that degrade into components < 5 nm for renal clearance or incorporate cleavable surface coatings for rapid post-targeting elimination [474, 492, 493].

Together, these strategies form a robust blueprint for achieving a favorable balance between therapeutic efficacy and biosafety. As GLP-1R-targeted nanomedicines advance toward clinical application, an in-depth understanding of nanomaterial–immune system interactions, long-term retention effects, and rigorous toxicological validation will be essential for ensuring safety, regulatory approval, and patient benefit in metabolic, cardiovascular, and neurodegenerative disease settings.

Large-scale manufacturing and quality control

The transition of GLP-1R-targeted nanomedicines from laboratory-scale innovation to clinical and commercial realities hinges on overcoming significant manufacturing and quality control challenges. These nanomedicines are complex assemblies combining lipids or polymers, conformationally sensitive peptide ligands, and active therapeutic payloads. Such complexity demands exceptional precision and reproducibility to ensure consistent particle size, ligand density, encapsulation efficiency, and ultimately, therapeutic efficacy and safety. Traditional batch manufacturing processes often struggle to maintain critical quality attributes within narrow specifications. Even minor variations in flow rate, solvent ratios, or drying temperatures can skew nanoparticle physicochemical properties, directly impacting biodistribution and receptor affinity. The peptide ligands themselves are vulnerable to shear stress and heat-induced unfolding during processing. In addition, terminal sterilization, which is a regulatory requirement, presents further challenges because conventional methods such as autoclaving, gamma irradiation, or filtration may damage lipid structures or degrade peptide integrity. Regulatory agencies now mandate rigorous lot-to-lot consistency, requiring detailed physicochemical “fingerprints” that include particle size, polydispersity, ligand stoichiometry, residual solvent levels, and bioactivity, thus necessitating GMP-grade precision at scale [494–496].

In response, continuous microfluidic manufacturing has emerged as the most promising approach for industrial-scale production. Microfluidic devices employing staggered herringbone or omnidirectional sheath flow mixing enable rapid and reproducible nanoparticle nucleation within milliseconds. These platforms maintain tight control over size distribution (with relative standard deviations under 5 nm) even when scaling flow rates from milliliters to tens of liters per hour. This technology already underpins GMP manufacturing of mRNA vaccines, demonstrating its scalability and robustness [497, 498]. Coupled with inline dynamic light scattering (DLS) and UV/fluorescence monitoring, these systems implement real-time data feedback into self-optimizing control loops. This integration exemplifies process analytical technology (PAT) and quality by design (QbD) principles, preventing batch deviations and ensuring consistent product quality from the outset [499, 500]. Parallel advancements in formulation stability and sterilization techniques are addressing longstanding bottlenecks. Lyophilization protocols optimized with cryoprotectants like trehalose or sucrose now preserve over 90% of ligand activity upon reconstitution. For terminal sterilization, low-dose β or electron beam irradiation (≤ 12 kGy) offers a gentler alternative to steam autoclaving, minimizing lipid degradation and peptide cleavage while requiring peroxide monitoring [448, 501]. Additional aseptic processing safeguards include endotoxin-retentive filters and validated depyrogenation resins, compensating for sterilization limitations. Regulatory harmonization further facilitates commercial viability. The FDA’s 2022 guidance on nanomaterial-containing drug products promotes convergence toward standardized assay panels—such as ISO/TR 13,014 for particle characterization, the U.S. National Cancer Institute Nanotechnology Characterization Laboratory (NCI-NCL) immunotoxicity cascades, and standardized lot release potency assays. These frameworks streamline chemistry, manufacturing, and controls (CMC) negotiations and mitigate regulatory risk, accelerating market entry [502].

Collectively, the convergence of continuous microfluidic manufacturing, advanced real-time analytics, improved sterilization protocols, and harmonized regulatory standards has transformed large-scale production of GLP-1R-targeted nanomedicines from a laboratory curiosity into a feasible industrial reality. These integrated advances ensure reproducible, safe, and potent nanotherapeutics, paving the way for broader clinical translation and commercial success.

Disease microenvironment heterogeneity

The inherent heterogeneity of the disease microenvironment represents a central translational barrier to the success of all precision therapies, including GLP-1R-targeted nanotherapies [503]. This complexity is not limited to tumors but extends to a wide spectrum of pathological conditions, including metabolic, neurodegenerative, cardiovascular, autoimmune, and inflammatory diseases. In these contexts, it significantly affects nanoparticle delivery, targeting precision, controlled drug release, and overall therapeutic efficacy [504–506].

In oncology, the tumor microenvironment (TME) exhibits high spatial and temporal heterogeneity. Tumor heterogeneity presents a critical barrier to the success of targeted therapies, including those directed at the GLP-1R [507]. Expression of GLP-1R varies substantially across tumor types and also within individual tumors [61]. Benign neuroendocrine tumors, such as insulinomas, gastrinomas, pheochromocytomas, and certain bronchial or cerebral neuroendocrine neoplasms, often exhibit high and uniform GLP-1R expression, making them ideal targets for GLP-1R-targeted therapy and diagnosis [508, 509]. In contrast, most carcinomas and lymphomas show minimal or undetectable receptor levels. Furthermore, malignant insulinomas frequently downregulate GLP-1R, with approximately two-thirds of cases exhibiting a switch to predominant somatostatin receptor expression [354, 510]. GLP-1R also functions as a pleiotropic GPCR that can activate distinct signaling cascades depending on ligand bias and the local cellular environment [511]. Ligand engagement may preferentially activate cAMP or β-arrestin pathways, each of which can variably suppress, stimulate, or fail to influence tumor growth depending on the specific tumor microenvironment [512, 513]. This signaling complexity further limits the utility of monotherapies based solely on GLP-1R targeting.

Beyond oncology, microenvironmental heterogeneity also impacts the development and efficacy of GLP-1R-targeted nanomedicines across a wide range of diseases. In T2DM, variations in IR, β-cell function, and tissue-specific GLP-1R expression (pancreas, liver, adipose) create diverse metabolic niches that influence the biodistribution and pharmacodynamics of nanocarriers [514–516]. In DN and in conditions such as nonalcoholic fatty liver disease and nonalcoholic steatohepatitis, intra-organ heterogeneity presents significant challenges for uniform targeting and controlled drug release. Distinct pathological features, including differences between glomerular and tubular injury in the kidney, lobular and periportal inflammation in the liver, and depot-specific inflammatory responses in adipose tissue, complicate the design of broadly effective therapeutic strategies [517–519]. Neurological diseases such as AD and PD show region-specific microenvironmental differences in blood-brain barrier integrity, neuroinflammation, and microglial activation. These factors cause heterogeneity in nanoparticle penetration and drug delivery efficacy across distinct brain regions [520, 521]. In cardiovascular conditions, plaques exhibit variability in lipid core size, fibrosis, and immune cell content, while heart failure is marked by patchy myocardial fibrosis and ischemic zones. Such spatial variations directly affect the targeting and therapeutic retention of GLP-1R-based nanocarriers [522, 523]. Autoimmune and inflammatory diseases also present significant microenvironmental diversity. In RA, synovial tissues show mosaic patterns of angiogenesis, immune infiltration, and cytokine gradients, whereas in IBD, intestinal segments differ in mucosal barrier integrity, microbiota composition, and pH. These differences alter nanoparticle residence time and payload diffusion [524, 525]. Diseases such as SLE, MS, and COPD further contribute to the spectrum of microenvironmental heterogeneity, requiring tailored delivery strategies for each condition [526–528].

To address these translational barriers, advanced strategies have emerged. Radiolabeled exendin-based PET/CT imaging using 68Ga or 111In enable real-time mapping of receptor density. This capability facilitates patient selection and allows for pretherapeutic visualization of target engagement [529–531]. Dual-targeting approaches incorporating GLP-1R with co-receptors such as somatostatin receptor 2 (SSTR2), epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), or prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA) enhance target accessibility and therapeutic precision across heterogeneous lesions. Modular nanocarriers with interchangeable ligands based on patient-specific profiling allow rapid adaptation without altering core chemistry. Microenvironment-sensitive designs, including hypoxia-responsive linkers, MMP-cleavable coatings, and tumor-penetrating peptides, facilitate deeper payload diffusion into poorly perfused tumor regions. These platforms can also remodel the stromal or immune milieu, overcoming resistance mechanisms. Together, these phenotype-matched imaging tools, multiplexed ligand systems, and responsive nanotechnologies offer a robust toolkit for outmaneuvering microenvironmental complexity [532, 533].

In conclusion, microenvironmental heterogeneity across oncologic, metabolic, neurologic, cardiovascular, and immune-related disorders presents a pervasive and dynamic challenge for GLP-1R-targeted nanomedicines. By incorporating precision diagnostics, dual-targeting strategies, and environment-responsive delivery systems, next-generation nanoplatforms have the potential to achieve more consistent and durable clinical outcomes across a broad spectrum of diseases.

Endosomal escape and intracellular delivery

Efficient intracellular delivery remains a significant challenge in the development of GLP-1R-targeted nanomedicines, particularly for nucleic acid cargos such as small interfering RNA (siRNA) and mRNA that require cytosolic access to exert their therapeutic effects. While GLP-1R-decorated nanocarriers can effectively bind and internalize into target cells, quantitative imaging studies indicate that less than 2% of the internalized nucleic acid payload escapes from endosomal compartments into the cytosol. The majority of these vesicles mature into degradative endolysosomes, leading to cargo degradation. Moreover, prolonged agonist engagement can accelerate GLP-1R trafficking to lysosomes, reducing the availability of receptors on the cell surface for subsequent therapeutic doses [534–536].

To address this “endosomal dead end,” next-generation nanoplatforms incorporate specialized chemical and biological features designed to destabilize endosomal membranes and facilitate cargo escape. Ionizable cationic lipids and proton-buffering domains, such as polyhistidine or dendrimers, remain inert at physiological pH but adopt fusogenic or cone-shaped conformations under acidic conditions (pH < 6). This induces osmotic swelling and membrane destabilization of endosomal vesicles, enhancing cytosolic delivery. Structure-function analyses of these lipids inform rational design strategies that maximize endosomal escape efficiency while minimizing cytotoxicity [537]. Complementary approaches employ synthetic or virus-derived fusogenic peptides masked by acid- or protease-labile linkers, which activate selectively after endocytosis to rupture membranes. Additionally, self-immolative carriers that disassemble into osmolytes can further increase intravesicular pressure, promoting membrane disruption. Physical external stimuli offer additional means to enhance endosomal escape. Photochemical internalization utilizes photosensitizers, such as porphyrins, embedded within nanoparticle shells. Upon exposure to low-intensity laser light, these agents generate singlet oxygen that disrupts endosomal membranes, markedly improving cytosolic delivery as demonstrated in early-phase clinical trials [538, 539]. Other methods under investigation include ultrasound-driven microbubble cavitation and magnetothermal nanocomposites activated by alternating magnetic fields (AMF), which can mechanically or thermally disrupt endosomal membranes deep within tissues, enabling spatiotemporal precision in cargo release [540–543]. Advancements in high-throughput screening combined with AI are accelerating the discovery of effective endosomolytic motifs. Barcoded peptide and polymer libraries allow parallel evaluation of thousands of candidates, rapidly identifying synergistic sequences that can be modularly integrated into GLP-1R-targeted nanoscaffolds for cargo- and disease-specific optimization [544, 545].

Collectively, these convergent chemical, biological, and physical strategies are transforming endosomal escape from a stochastic bottleneck into a programmable design feature. By enabling efficient cytosolic delivery, they address a key remaining hurdle in GLP-1R-targeted intracellular therapy and significantly enhance the translational potential of nucleic acid and other intracellularly active therapeutics.

Regulatory and translational hurdles

GLP-1R-targeted nanomedicines face complex regulatory and translational challenges, primarily due to their interdisciplinary nature and structural complexity [546]. As combinatorial products that integrate biologics, small-molecule components, and device-like delivery systems, they must satisfy overlapping regulatory requirements across multiple domains [547]. However, global regulatory frameworks remain fragmented and are not yet fully equipped to address the unique attributes of these nanoplatforms [22]. While agencies such as the U.S. FDA and EMA have issued guidance on nanomedicine development, key ambiguities persist, particularly concerning the characterization and quality control of nanoscale formulations [548]. For instance, there is currently no internationally harmonized standard defining acceptable batch-to-batch variability in parameters such as surface ligand density, zeta potential, or drug loading capacity, despite these being directly linked to therapeutic efficacy and safety profiles [549]. Regulatory inconsistencies often require developers to perform redundant validation studies, thereby increasing costs and delaying clinical translation. These challenges are especially pertinent to GLP-1R-targeted systems, where surface modifications critically influence receptor binding affinity and downstream signaling [550]. Manufacturing scalability and reproducibility present further obstacles. Critical physicochemical properties, including particle size distribution, polydispersity, encapsulation efficiency, and surface charge, are highly sensitive to variables such as solvent conditions, raw material batches, and synthesis protocols [551]. Although emerging technologies like microfluidic mixing and self-assembly reactors have enhanced reproducibility at the laboratory scale, their translation to GMP-compliant industrial processes remains a significant bottleneck. Furthermore, GLP-1R-targeted constructs often rely on site-specific conjugation of exendin analogs or other peptide ligands. At present, there are no standardized assays to evaluate ligand orientation, binding efficiency, or functional stability during storage and transport.

Current safety assessment frameworks are also inadequate. Traditional preclinical assays, such as LD₅₀ determinations or gross histopathology, are insufficient to predict nanomaterial-specific toxicities [552, 553]. These include reticuloendothelial system (RES) accumulation, immune modulation, or oxidative stress resulting from high surface reactivity or protein corona formation [554]. Such concerns are particularly pronounced for GLP-1R-targeted nanomedicines, as GLP-1R is endogenously expressed in multiple organs, including the pancreas, kidneys, lungs, heart, and brain [555]. This broad distribution increases the risk of off-target effects and systemic toxicity. Non-biodegradable carriers such as AuNPs, iron oxides, and silica-based platforms raise further concerns due to their prolonged tissue retention and potential for chronic accumulation [556]. Moreover, the dynamic biological transformations that nanocarriers undergo in vivo—including opsonization, ligand desorption, and pH-induced structural changes—are rarely accounted for in current safety evaluations [557]. These transformations complicate the prediction of pharmacokinetics and long-term safety. There is an urgent need for nanomedicine-specific testing platforms, including organ-on-chip models, 3D tissue constructs, and advanced whole-body imaging techniques, to better assess biodistribution, immune response, and degradation profiles. Clinical trial design presents additional challenges. GLP-1R-targeted nanomedicines often carry multifunctional payloads, such as GLP-1 analogs, chemotherapeutic agents, and diagnostic compounds, which do not conform to conventional monotherapy trial frameworks [558, 559]. Moreover, GLP-1R desensitization and internalization following repeated stimulation complicate dose escalation strategies and necessitate prolonged efficacy monitoring. The lack of validated companion diagnostics, such as imaging tools to assess GLP-1R occupancy in vivo, further limits the ability to stratify patients or adjust dosing in a personalized manner [559].

Despite these hurdles, advances in regulatory science are gradually paving the way for more efficient development pathways. Structured engagement with regulatory authorities through scientific advice meetings, early-stage consultation mechanisms, and initiatives like the FDA’s Emerging Technology Program can provide regulatory clarity and facilitate smoother approval processes. Experiences from peptide receptor radionuclide therapy (PRRT), where diagnostic and therapeutic pairs such as 68Ga/177Lu-DOTA-exendin are co-developed, may offer valuable insights for designing biomarker-guided clinical trials in GLP-1R-targeted nanomedicine. To accelerate regulatory acceptance, cross-disciplinary standardization platforms that integrate physicochemical profiling, immunogenicity testing, and real-time biodistribution tracking will be essential [444]. International consortia such as the EU Nanomedicine Translation Hub and the NCI Nanotechnology Characterization Laboratory are actively working to harmonize preclinical validation protocols and quality control strategies. For successful clinical translation, future efforts should adopt a holistic quality-by-design framework that prioritizes predictive modeling, batch-to-batch consistency, material traceability, and real-world data integration [560]. Incorporating safety-by-design principles—such as the use of biodegradable linkers, stimulus-responsive release systems, and immuno-evasive coatings—will further enhance the clinical readiness of these platforms.

In summary, close collaboration among academic researchers, industry stakeholders, and regulatory agencies will be vital for developing fit-for-purpose evaluation systems. These coordinated efforts are expected to bridge the translational gap and fully realize the therapeutic potential of GLP-1R-targeted nanomedicines across metabolic, oncologic, and neurologic disease areas.

Conclusion

GLP-1R-targeted nanoplatforms represent a transformative advancement in precision therapeutics, extending their utility well beyond conventional metabolic applications. By employing a range of carrier systems such as lipid-based, polymeric, inorganic, and biomimetic nanomaterials, these engineered platforms enhance pharmacokinetic profiles, improve molecular stability, and enable site-specific delivery of GLP-1 analogues and related therapeutic payloads. Ligand-functionalized surfaces confer high receptor affinity and tissue specificity, promoting selective cellular uptake and enhanced therapeutic outcomes (Fig. 11).

A schematic illustrates how engineered GLP-1R-targeting nanoplatforms enable precise and multifunctional therapeutic delivery in various human diseases, highlighting advances in targeted, controlled, and responsive nanomedicine. LNPs: liposomal nanoparticles; AuNPs: gold nanoparticles; INMs: inorganic nanomaterials; miRNA-NPs: miRNA-loaded nanoparticles; PNPs: polymer nanoparticles; QD: quantum dots; EVs: extracellular exosomes; VLPs: virus-like particles; CMCNMs: cell membrane-coated nanomaterials

The incorporation of stimuli-responsive release mechanisms allows these systems to localize drug activation within pathological microenvironments, thereby minimizing off-target effects and improving safety profiles. In parallel, multifunctional and theranostic designs integrate diagnostic imaging with therapeutic delivery, enabling real-time tracking, adaptive dosing, and stratified treatment strategies. These innovations are particularly valuable in addressing multifactorial diseases such as diabetes, obesity, DN, cardiovascular disorders, neurodegenerative diseases, oncology, and autoimmune conditions, where combination therapies and personalized interventions are increasingly required.

Looking ahead, continued progress in GLP-1R-targeted nanomedicine will depend on the integration of AI for rational design, refinement of personalized delivery systems, and robust clinical validation. As these technologies evolve, they offer the potential to redefine disease management across a broad spectrum of indications, setting the stage for the next generation of nanotechnology-enabled precision medicine.
