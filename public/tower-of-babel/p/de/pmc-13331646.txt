Better 10‐Year Cerebrovascular Outcome After Transplant Than on Standard‐Care in Sickle Cell Anemia: DREPAGREFFE Trial
Authors: Francoise Bernaudin, Suzanne Verlhac, Elisabeth Ducros‐Miralles, Cécile Arnaud, Isabelle Genty, Marie Petras, Catherine Paillard, Gonzalo De Luna, Benoit Meunier, Isabelle Thuret, Annie Kamdem, Corinne Pondarré, Giovanna Cannas, Christelle Chantalat‐Auger, Nadia Firah, Sophie Pertuisel, Laure Joseph, Florence Missud, Mariane de Montalembert, Corinne Guitton, Claire Pluchart, Valentine Brousse, Monique Elmaleh, Vincent Gajdos, Emmanuella Leveillé, Ekaterina Belozertseva, Mabel Gaba, Catherine Poirot, Virginie Barraud‐Lange, Philippe Chadebech, France Pirenne, Myriam Bernaudin, Bénédicte Neven, Pierre Frange, Marie Angoso, Karima Yakouben, Bénédicte Bruno, Nathalie Dhédin, Jean‐Hugues Dalle, Regis Peffault Delatour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTManagement of cerebral vasculopathy in sickle cell anemia (SCA) includes standard‐care, that is, chronic transfusion (CT) or hydroxyurea, and hematopoietic cell transplantation (HCT). DREPAGREFFE‐1 (December 2010/June 2013), a French multicenter trial, was the first prospective trial comparing standard‐care to match sibling donor (MSD)‐HCT in 67 (35F/32M) SCA children (5–15 year) on CT for abnormal time‐averaged mean maximum velocities (TAMMV ≥ 200 cm/s). Seven had a stroke history. We reported that MSD‐HCT reduced the highest TAMMVs at 1‐ and 3‐year (p < 0.001) and improved quality of life (QoL) for physical and school functioning. In stroke‐free patients, the 3‐year stenosis score was lower (p = 0.010). Nevertheless, no significant difference was observed for silent cerebral infarcts (SCI) and cognitive performance. This prompted us to initiate DREPAGREFFE‐2 to reevaluate the outcomes at 10 years (September 2022/August 2024) with the same 67 SCA children. No death or stroke occurred in either arm. No rejection or chronic‐GvHD arose in the MSD‐HCT group (n = 32). In the standard‐care group (n = 35), 16 were on hydroxyurea, and 16 on CT at Year 10, and 3 received haploidentical‐HCT. After MSD‐HCT, the QoL was better, even for social functioning, and the number of hospitalizations, hospitalized days (p < 0.001), and crises (p = 0.001) was lower than on standard‐care. In stroke‐free patients, stenosis (p = 0.027) and SCI scores (p = 0.041) decreased significantly more after MSD‐HCT than on standard‐care; working memory (p = 0.016) and processing speed (p = 0.011) improved significantly after MSD‐HCT, but worsened on standard‐care. These effects were not previously detected with shorter follow‐up. This supports earlier consideration of HCT for SCA children with MSD to preserve neurologic function and QoL for a more productive future.

Introduction

In children with sickle cell anemia (SCA), abnormally high flow velocity in the cerebral arteries is associated with ischemic stroke risk [1, 2, 3], stenoses [4, 5], and silent cerebral infarcts (SCI) [4, 6]. Strokes are devastating complications, responsible for motor and neurocognitive sequelae [7], whereas SCI are associated with cognitive deficiency [8] and risk of overt stroke [9].

In patients with time‐averaged mean of maximum velocities (TAMMV) ≥ 200 cm/s, identified at risk for stroke by transcranial Doppler (TCD) ultrasonography [1, 2, 3, 4], the STOP‐1 trial demonstrated the efficiency of chronic transfusion (CT), reducing by 90% the risk of first stroke [3]. However, the STOP‐2 study showed that stopping transfusions in patients with normalized velocities and no stenosis resulted in a high risk of recurrence of abnormal‐TCD velocities and stroke occurrence, suggesting that life‐long CT might be needed for primary stroke prevention [10]. Presence of alloimmunization, iron overload, and high cumulative incidence of abnormal‐TCD velocities (30%) in a newborn cohort [11] led to considering other preventive approaches, such as switching to hydroxyurea [12, 13] or allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation (allo‐HCT) [14]. Allo‐HCT with a matched‐sibling donor (MSD‐HCT) offers a 95% chance of cure in children with SCA and is associated with lower cerebral velocities [14], prompting us to initiate this first controlled prospective trial (DREPAGREFFE) comparing MSD‐HCT to standard‐care (CT/hydroxyurea).

The results of DREPAGREFFE‐1 after 1 and 3 years were reported [15, 16], demonstrating that the highest TAMMVs at Years 1 and 3 were significantly lower (p < 0.001), the stenosis score at Year 3 in stroke‐free patients was decreased (p = 0.010), and the quality of life (QoL) was better for physical and school functioning in MSD‐HCT compared to standard‐care. Nevertheless, no significant difference in SCI and cognitive performance was observed, except for a significantly higher progression of the processing speed in the transplantation group from Years 1 to 3 than in the standard‐care group, suggesting that significant differences between both groups might be observed with a longer follow‐up [15].

Thus, we initiated the DREPAGREFFE‐2 trial, reevaluating all patients enrolled in the previous trial after 10 years to test whether differences in cognitive performance and SCI had changed significantly. This was the first long‐term comparison between standard‐care and MSD‐HCT in 67 patients with SCA, which showed significant benefits of MSD‐HCT, including in cognitive function, supporting recommending early myeloablative HCT in children with symptomatic SCA and an MSD.

Patients and Methods

Study Design

The DREPAGREFFE‐1 trial (NCT01340404; www.clinicaltrials.gov), approved by an Institutional Review Board affiliated with the French Ministry of Health, was a controlled, open‐label interventional study defined by the random availability of an MSD, conducted at nine sites in France. The rationale and detailed protocol were published [15, 17].

Familial HLA‐typing was performed after consent and patients were assigned to the transplantation group if an MSD was available and to the standard‐care group (CT for at least 1 year, followed by a switch to hydroxyurea if normalized TAMMV [< 170 cm/s] and no stenosis) if no MSD was available. Moreover, CTs resumed in the event of recurrence of abnormal velocities (TAMMV ≥ 200 cm/s) or the onset or recurrence of stenosis as previously reported in the Créteil cohort [18], and this protocol was adhered to during the 10‐year follow‐up.

The DREPAGREFFE‐2 trial (NCT05053932), approved by an Institutional Review Board affiliated with the French Ministry of Health, is a non‐interventional study consisting of the reevaluation at 10 years of all patients enrolled in DREPAGREFFE‐1 using retrospective and prospective data recording. The only change in standard‐care during the follow‐up period occurred in 2013 when haploidentical transplantation became available in France for patients who had experienced severe crises or worsening of cerebral vasculopathy despite standard‐care and had no HLA‐compatible donor.

Drepagreffe‐2 Study Population

Between September 2022 and August 2024, all 67 patients enrolled in DREPAGREFFE‐1 were contacted, and clinical evaluation, routine biological checkup, intracranial and cervical color Doppler ultrasound [19], cerebral magnetic resonance imaging/angiography (MRI/MRA/neck‐MRA [19]) were performed and neurological evaluations occurred within 6 months of enrollment. Non‐opposition consent was required for cognitive testing (WISC‐V and WAIS‐IV), biological research, and QoL (PedsQL) assessment. Events and treatment changes between Years 3 and 10 were routinely recorded in our database.

Outcomes

Primary Outcome

The primary outcome was the evolution of cerebral vasculopathy at Year 10, assessed by the number of additional patients having developed SCI, and results of the stenosis and SCI score, QoL, and cognitive performance in both arms compared to enrollment among stroke‐free patients.

Secondary Outcomes

Events occurring between enrollment and Year 10, that is, annual rate of hospitalizations, days of hospitalizations and crises.

Outcome Measures

The highest TAMMV in a cerebral artery was recorded. MRI/MRA/neck‐MRA imaging, performed on a 1.5 or 3 T machine with FLAIR, T1, T2 diffusion weighted sequences and circle of Willis 3D‐time of flight angiography, was reviewed by 2 experts blinded to the treatment group. The MRA stenosis scores, ranging from 0 (best outcome) to 32 (worst outcome), were defined as the weighted sums over the 8 assessed cerebral arteries, as 0 if no stenosis, 1 if mild stenosis (25%–49%), 2 if moderate stenosis (50%–74%), 3 if severe stenosis (75%–99%), and 4 if occlusion. SCI (ischemic lesion without a history of cerebrovascular events) was defined as a lesion of at least 3 mm in maximal diameter in one view seen on two planes on FLAIR imaging [20]. The MRI‐scores in the stroke‐free patients were obtained by adding up the sizes (mm) of each white matter ischemic lesion from the left and right sides, ranging from 0 (best outcome) to 85 (worst outcome). These scores were defined by the trial committee of neuroradiologists.

QoL assessment was collected using the French version of the Pediatric and Adult Quality of Life Inventory Generic Core Scale (0 = worst outcome, 100 = best outcome) via self‐report for adolescents and adults [21].

Full Scale Intelligence Quotient was measured by the Wechsler Intelligence Scale (WISC‐V) for adolescents until age 16 and Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale (WAIS‐IV) for patients older than 16 (40 = worst outcome, 160 = best outcome).

Statistical Analysis

Participant characteristics at Year 10 were summarized using percentages, mean (standard deviation, SD) or median quartiles (Q1, Q3). Ninety‐five percent confidence intervals (95% CI) around point estimates were computed. Exact Fisher tests were used to compare proportions and Mann–Whitney tests to compare continuous distributions between MSD‐HCT and standard‐care groups. Changes between enrollment and Year 10 were calculated in each patient and compared between both groups. All statistical tests were two‐sided, with p‐values of 0.05 or less denoting statistical significance. Statistical analyses were performed with the SPSS‐version‐24 and MedCalc (Belgium) software packages.

Results

Family Structure

In France, all patients with SCA have the same health coverage and receive 100% government‐funded care (including treatments, hospitalizations, transplant care, ovarian cryopreservation, and posttransplant follow‐up, etc.). Thus, the socioeconomic status does not affect the patient's chances of receiving a transplant and long‐term follow‐up. The only factor affecting the likelihood of finding a suitable donor is the number of non‐SCA siblings. In the DREPAGREFFE trial, the parents were predominantly first‐generation African immigrants (45.8% from West Africa, 39.0% from Central Africa, or 15.3% from the West Indies). The median (range) number of children in the 67 families was 3 (2–9) and 47.8% of the 67 patients had a donor. This percentage did not differ significantly from the expected 39.8%, as outlined in the online supplementary material of the 2019 issue of JAMA [15].

Clinical Outcome

In the MSD‐HCT group, the mean age (SD) at enrollment was 6.7 years (2.7) and 6.9 (2.7) at transplant and 8.3 years (3.3) at enrollment in the standard‐care group. At Year 10, the mean age (SD) was 18.0 years (2.8) in the MSD‐HCT group and 19.2 years (3.7) in the standard‐care group. No death, no new, or recurrent strokes occurred during 10‐year follow‐up for all 67 patients (32 boys, 35 girls).

In the MSD‐HCT group (n = 32), the follow‐up was stopped in 2 patients at 7.0 and 9.7 years posttransplant due to family return to Africa and UK. At the last visit, they had no complication, no chronic‐GvHD, and total donor chimerism. At Year 10, the 30 other patients had no chronic‐GvHD and the median (range) donor chimerism was 92.5 (19%–100%). Two patients had donor chimerism < 50% (19%, 37%) with persistent hemolytic anemia (10.2 and 9.8 g hemoglobin/dL; 412 and 208 reticulocytes × 109/L).

These two patients had no stenosis at the time of enrollment and did not develop any stenosis during the 10‐year follow‐up period. They had SCI at the time of enrollment, but despite the low donor chimerism, their SCI score improved between enrollment and the end of the 10‐year follow‐up, for example, −1.10 in the patient with 19% donor chimerism and −24.4 in the patient with 37% donor chimerism. No disease‐modifying therapy was initiated after transplantation. The patient with 19% donor cells is doing very well and has not experienced any crisis. The patient with 37% chimerism developed a COVID‐19 infection 9.1 years after the transplant, which was followed by autoimmune hepatitis 4 months later, requiring tapering of the steroids and azathioprine doses. Currently, 13 years after transplant, the autoimmune hepatitis is in remission, but she is still on azathioprine to avoid recurrence.

In the standard‐care group (n = 35), all patients were alive at Year 10. Between Years 1 and 10, 23/35 patients with normalized TAMMV (< 170 cm/s) and no stenosis were switched to hydroxyurea after a median (range) of 2.1 years (1.1–4.9) since enrollment on CT. Three received a successful haploidentical HCT for worsening of cerebral vasculopathy in two and persistent high rate of crises in one, but 2/3 developed chronic‐GvHD (mild and severe). At Year 10, after MSD‐HCT, weight (p = 0.036) and BMI (p = 0.032) were higher; hemoglobin and HbA% were higher, while leukocytes, neutrophils, reticulocytes, total bilirubin, LDH, and ferritin were lower (p < 0.001 for all) than on standard‐care.

In the MSD‐HCT group (20F, 12M), ovarian cryopreservation was performed before conditioning in 19/20 girls at a median age (range) of 6.4 years (3.9–14.0), with one parental refusal in a girl with severe sequelae following a stroke. All were prepubertal. After MSD‐HCT, 19/20 required hormone replacement therapy to reach puberty despite a spontaneous onset of puberty observed in 9 and only one of 20 developed complete spontaneous puberty. At Year 10 post‐HCT, only 3/20 had spontaneous normal hormone levels and regular menstruation. No ovarian reimplantation has yet been performed. No pregnancies occurred in the MSD‐HCT and standard‐care groups during the 10‐year follow‐up. At the 10‐year mark, the women mean age (SD) was 18.4 years (3.2) in the MSD‐HCT group and 18.6 years (3.4) in the standard‐care group. All boys were prepubertal at the time of HCT, and testicular cryopreservation was performed in 12/12 at a median age (range) of 6.8 years (4.2–11.1). During the 10‐year follow‐up, all developed normal spontaneous puberty. Hormonal data at Year 10 show that FSH and LH levels were significantly higher and estradiol and AMH levels lower in women after MSD‐HCT than on standard‐care, while there was no difference in men for FSH, LH, and testosterone between groups.

Hospitalizations During the 10‐Year Follow‐Up

During the 10‐year follow‐up, after exclusion of the initial hospitalization in the transplantation unit, the median (Q1, Q3) of the annual rates of hospitalizations, days of hospitalizations, and crises (Figure 1B) were strongly lower after MSD‐HCT than on standard‐care (p < 0.001 for all).

Cerebral Vasculopathy Outcome in Stroke‐Free Patients

Considering that stroke history is known to be a major factor modifying the cerebral vasculopathy outcome, only the data from 60 stroke‐free patients at enrollment (MSD‐HCT n = 28; standard‐care n = 32) were analyzed. At Year 10, velocities and MRI/MRA were available in 26/28 MSD‐HCT patients and in 31/32 stroke‐free patients in the standard‐care group.

Velocities

At Year 10, the highest TAMMV was significantly lower on average after MSD‐HCT (90.4 cm/s) than on standard‐care (119.3 cm/s; difference, −28.9 cm/s [95% CI, −14.4 to −43.5], p < 0.001), but TAMMV normalized (< 170 cm/s) in all patients, except for one on standard‐care who still had TAMMV = 216 cm/s in the left eICA despite CT.

Stenoses

In the MSD‐HCT group, stenosis was present at enrollment in 14/28 (10 intracranial stenosis, 4 isolated eICA stenosis). During the 10‐year follow‐up, no new patient developed stenosis, and no worsening of stenosis was observed. Scores were reduced in 13/14 patients and remained stable in only one patient with mild stenosis (25%).

In the standard‐care group, all patients with stenosis at enrollment (14/31) (9 intracranial stenosis, 5 isolated eICA stenosis) were maintained on CT until stenosis disappearance. During the 10‐year follow‐up, new stenosis developed in four patients despite CT (n = 1) or after a switch to hydroxyurea (n = 3). These three patients were placed again on CT. One of them received a haplo‐HCT 6.7‐years post‐enrollment, resulting in a reduction of the stenosis score from 2 to 0 post‐haplo‐HCT. Moreover, while a reduction of the stenosis scores was observed in 11/14, worsening occurred in 3/14 despite CT. One of them received a haplo‐HCT 7.2‐years post‐enrollment, with ensuing reduction of stenosis score from 3 to 1.

SCI on MRI

At enrollment (T0), SCI were more frequent in the MSD‐HCT group (12/28 = 43%) than in the standard‐care group (5/31 = 16%; p = 0.042). However, during the 10‐year follow‐up, no additional patients developed SCI after MSD‐HCT, while SCI appeared in 5 additional patients on standard‐care (p = 0.041) (Figure 2D), with 4 on CT and in one on hydroxyurea. SCI were no longer visible or < 3 mm in 5 patients after MSD‐HCT and in 3 patients on standard‐care at Year 10. SCI scores decreased significantly more after MSD‐HCT, with an average SCI score change between enrollment and Year 10 of −2.05 versus 0.51 on standard‐care (difference −2.56 [95% CI: −5.00 to −0.11]; p = 0.041) (Figure 2C).

Quality of Life

At Year 10, QoL was available in 56/60 stroke‐free patients (25 MSD‐HCT and 31 standard‐care) (Table 4). Physical and social functioning were higher after MSD‐HCT compared to standard‐care, with significant differences being on average 5.8 (95% CI: −1.5 to 13.2; p = 0.023) and 4.5 (95% CI: −1.3 to 10.2; p = 0.024), respectively. More specifically, patients after MSD‐HCT had less difficulties engaging in sport (p = 0.012), had less pain (p = 0.006), feeling less angry (p = 0.035), were able to do more activities (p = 0.014), being less forgetful (p = 0.032), and missed less school or work because they were not feeling well (p = 0.033) than those on standard‐care.

Discussion

The DREPAGREFFE‐2 trial aimed to analyze at Year 10 the outcomes of the 67 patients initially enrolled in DREPAGREFFE‐1, comparing for the first time MSD‐HCT (n = 32) to standard‐care (CT/hydroxyurea) (n = 35) in SCA patients at risk of cerebral vasculopathy. This decade of follow‐up demonstrates significant differences between the two groups as MSD‐HCT effectively prevents SCI and improves working memory and processing speed, while worsening was observed on standard‐care. No rejection or chronic‐GvHD was observed after MSD‐HCT, performed before age 15 after myeloablative conditioning. Furthermore, no new strokes, deaths, or malignancy occurred after MSD‐HCT or on standard‐care.

Determining the risk–benefit ratio between MSD‐HCT and other treatments is critical, considering that life‐long transfusions expose patients to a high risk of alloimmunization and iron overload, require good venous access, and affect daily life. Furthermore, long‐term treatment with hydroxyurea has faced scrutiny because of the infertility risk in men and the issues of compliance, while MSD‐HCT may result in early transplant‐related mortality (TRM), GvHD, and infertility risk. Nevertheless, since the first MSD‐HCT reported by Belgian [22], French [14, 23], and US [24, 25] teams, significant progress has been made [26]. In the French series, using the same myeloablative conditioning regimen as in DREPAGREFFE, cumulative chronic‐GvHD incidence at 5 years in patients transplanted before age 15 was 5.4% (95% CI: 1.8%–9.0%), but later resolved, and event‐free survival at 5 years post‐MSD‐HCT reached 97.8% (95% CI: 95.6%–100%) in patients transplanted since year 2000 [27], similar to the survival at age 18 of 97.8% (95% CI: 95.2%–100%) in the Créteil newborn SCA cohort (2007–2014) [28].

The present trial demonstrates that MSD‐HCT patients experienced significantly fewer hospitalizations and crises than those on CT or hydroxyurea, resulting in improved QoL, and most likely, in a drastic reduction in costs [29, 30]. Cerebral velocities were significantly more reduced after MSD‐HCT than on standard‐care [15]. The TWiTCH trial [13] showed that hydroxyurea was no less effective than CT in maintaining cerebral velocities in children with a history of abnormal velocities and recommended hydroxyurea after at least 12 months on CT; however, we observed recurrence of abnormal velocities requiring reinitiation of CT [18] and the present trial suggests that HCT should be recommended following CT in children with a history of abnormal velocities if an MSD is available. Furthermore, MSD‐HCT in stroke‐free children provides continued and better outcomes for stenosis at Year 10 than CT. No new patients developed stenosis, and no worsening of stenosis was observed after MSD‐HCT, while on standard‐care, stenosis worsened in 3/14 and appeared in 4 additional patients. This favorable impact of MSD‐HCT on stenosis outcome is particularly important as there is a significant association between stenosis and SCI/stroke risks [5, 6, 31, 32, 33]. This underlines that only HCT allows to safely stop CT in patients with stenosis. In addition, we demonstrate for the first time that MSD‐HCT treatment provides significantly more effective prevention of SCI than standard‐care over the 10‐year follow‐up. No new patients developed SCI in the transplanted group, while 5 new patients did in the standard‐care group. A greater reduction in SCI scores was observed in the MSD‐HCT group than in the standard‐care group, a new finding compared to Year 3 [15]. Among stroke‐free patients, no difference in cognitive performance was observed based on the presence or absence of SCI. This finding is consistent with a previous French multicenter prospective study showing that, among patients with SCI, only those with severe anemia exhibited cognitive deficits [34], and with recent publications comparing patients with and without SCI to healthy siblings, showing no significant impact of SCI on cognitive performance [35, 36, 37]. Importantly, during the 10‐year follow‐up, there was a significant improvement in working memory and speed processing after MSD‐HCT, which worsened on standard‐care, a statistically significant difference between the groups. We previously showed that even SCA patients without stroke or SCI had lower cognitive performance than their non‐SCA siblings and the most significantly impaired index was the processing speed [34], while the major risk factor for cognitive deficiency was the presence of severe anemia [34]. Several studies have used MRI to evaluate cognitive performance pre‐ and post‐HCT [38, 39, 40] from unrelated donor [38], MSD [39, 40], or haploidentical donor [40], reporting stable MRI and stable or improved cognitive performance at Year 2 after HCT, but the number of patients was small and there was no control group. Another trial testing pre‐ and post‐HCT [41] in 47 SCA adults transplanted from MSD using the NIH non‐myeloablative conditioning and 22 non‐SCA siblings showed that patients had significantly slower processing speed than their siblings prior to HCT, and that a significant improvement was observed 12 months after HCT in patients but also in siblings, demonstrating the positive impact of new testing during follow‐up and raising questions about conclusions in other trials without a control group. It is important to note that in this trial, testing was performed only in patients at enrollment, 1‐, 3‐, and 10‐year follow‐up in both groups, thereby ruling out any bias in the results.

Assessment of QoL is important to understand the disease burden experienced by patients, and to measure the effectiveness of treatment interventions [42]. Improvement of QoL by CT compared to observation was previously demonstrated in the randomized SIT trial [43]. The impact of hydroxyurea was more difficult to demonstrate because of the strong correlation with adherence to treatment [44, 45]. Several publications have reported QoL improvement (physical and social abilities) after HCT [46, 47, 48, 49] but only one trial compared HCT to standard‐care, showing at Year 2 significantly decreased fatigue and increased ability to participate in social activities in the HCT group. Unfortunately, only 10 patients completed QoL assessment after HCT, preventing definitive conclusions [50]. Here, we show better QoL after MSD‐HCT than on standard‐care at Year 10, with significant differences for physical and social functioning. Patients in the MSD‐HCT group had significantly less difficulties engaging in sport, less pain, feeling less anger, experiencing greater ability to do more activities, being less forgetful, and missing less school or work because not feeling well.

Given that MSD‐HCT is superior to standard‐care in reducing crises, preventing SCI, reducing stenosis and SCI scores, as well as improving QoL and cognitive performances, the primary concern about myeloablative HCT remains the premature ovarian insufficiency (POI) and risk of infertility associated with busulfan‐based conditioning, known to be highly toxic to the ovaries and leading to diminished ovarian reserve and POI [51, 52, 53, 54, 55]. In DREPAGREFFE‐2, only 1/20 girls (5%), prepubertal at the time of transplantation, developed spontaneous puberty after transplant. Ten years after MSD‐HCT, among women, whose mean age (SD) was 18.4 years (3.2) in the MSD‐HCT group and 18.6 years (3.4) in the standard‐care group, no pregnancies occurred, and no reimplantation of ovarian fragments was done. Furthermore, 3/20 (15%) had normal hormone levels with regular spontaneous menstruation, while 17/20 (85%) still had abnormal hormone levels, suggesting poor compliance with hormone replacement therapy after puberty. Thus, reimplantation of ovarian fragments, when in sufficient number, should be considered for fertility, but also for hormonal balance. In contrast, all boys developed normal spontaneous puberty post‐HCT, as previously reported [14, 27], although semen analysis was not assessed. To preserve fertility, gonadal tissue cryopreservation, which is free of charge for SCA children in France, and available for ovarian [14, 27, 56] and testis [27, 57] since 1998 and 2010, respectively, is strongly recommended. Successful puberty induction, pregnancies and births following ovarian tissue transplantation (OTT) have been recently reported [58, 59, 60, 61]. In prepubescent males whose testicular tissues were cryopreserved prior to HCT, autologous testicular fragment transplantation has not yet been performed [57], although promising results have been reported in rhesus macaques [62]. Nevertheless, spontaneous pregnancies and births were also observed in a small proportion of ex‐SCA women transplanted before age 10, while several ex‐SCA men spontaneously fathered 10–15 years after MSD‐HCT [27]. Providing this information to patients and parents is essential to facilitate shared and informed decision‐making. Another means to reduce infertility risks and prevent chronic‐GvHD is to use the non‐myeloablative conditioning successfully developed for adult patients at the National Institutes of Health (NIH). This conditioning, inducing mixed chimerism, allowed safe transplantation for adults, without TRM or GvHD, resulting in 87% SCA free survival, but 13% rejection rate and autologous reconstitution [47, 63, 64, 65]. However, several hematological malignancies were reported in SCA adults transplanted with non‐myeloablative conditioning after rejection of their graft [66, 67] or gene therapy [68]. In contrast, no hematologic malignancy post‐HCT was observed in this study and in the long‐term French series using myeloablative conditioning, despite a high rate of mixed chimerism and several rejections [27], suggesting that it is critical to use myeloablative conditioning and perform HCT at a younger age to avoid hematological malignancies post‐HCT. It is also important to keep in mind that SCA itself [69, 70, 71] and hydroxyurea may also be associated with a higher risk of hematological malignancy [71, 72, 73, 74, 75] and some gonadal dysfunction [76, 77, 78, 79, 80, 81, 82] and that patients remain homozygous carriers of the S‐gene after HCT.

Another important issue to address here is the indication for MSD‐HCT in 2026. In 2010, when DREPAGREFFE‐1 was launched, hydroxyurea was only prescribed in symptomatic children, and the number of candidates for HCT was high as the cumulative incidence of abnormal velocities reached 30% by age 8 [11]. Starting hydroxyurea treatment earlier reduced velocities and the risk of abnormal intracranial velocities [28, 83, 84, 85, 86]. However, we recently showed in a prospective newborn cohort that SCI incidence was significantly reduced by detection and management of extracranial arteriopathy, and not by earlier hydroxyurea initiation [28]. While early hydroxyurea initiation is still recommended to prevent cerebral vasculopathy, our experience indicates that systematic detection of abnormal velocities in intracranial and extracranial internal carotid arteries from the 2nd year of life, and detection of stenosis and SCI by MRI/MRA/neck‐MRA from the age of 5–6 years are both necessary to manage stenosis and SCI by CT followed by HCT if an MSD is available.

This study has several limitations. Treatment allocation was based on genetic availability of an MSD and not on true randomization. We acknowledge that the initial imbalance in SCI, arising by chance, complicates the interpretation of the 10‐year absolute results. However, the SCI baseline prevalence was higher in the MSD‐HCT group and, more importantly, the main conclusion of the study was based on the longitudinal trend, demonstrating the absence of new patients with SCI in the MSD‐HCT group compared with 5 new patients in the standard‐care group, as well as a greater reduction in the SCI scores in the MSD‐HCT group than in the standard‐care group. Moreover, patient transfers from the standard‐care group to the haplo‐HCT group confounded the interpretation of the absolute 10‐year results, but the results are presented separately for the enrollment groups (MSD‐SCT vs. standard‐care) and the 10‐year follow‐up (allo‐HCT including MSD‐HCT and haplo‐HCT vs. standard‐care). In addition, we recognize that our results may not translate to HCT‐indications other than cerebral vasculopathy. Transplantation may not currently be possible in regions of the world without access to comprehensive medical care, such as in sub‐Saharan Africa where most children with SCA are born, although the chance of finding an MSD is greater because of large families. However, the strength of this prospective comparative trial between transplantation and standard‐care lies in the defined inclusion criteria, the homogeneity of donor type, conditioning, and GvHD prophylaxis for HCT, as well as in its long‐term follow‐up and excellent compliance with follow‐up examinations.

In conclusion, this first 10‐year prospective trial in SCA‐children (5–15 years) on CT for abnormal cerebral velocities, comparing myeloablative MSD‐HCT to standard‐care, demonstrates that MSD‐HCT is safe in the short‐ and long‐term and is associated with a lower hospitalization rate, no crises, better QoL, higher reduction of stenosis and SCI scores, prevention of new stenosis and SCI, and better working memory and processing speed than standard‐care. These findings support recommending myeloablative HCT before the age of 15 in children with symptomatic SCA and MSD to preserve cognitive functions, enabling them to realize their social and professional dreams.
