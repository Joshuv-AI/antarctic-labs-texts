Characteristics of Patients Diagnosed With Cancer After Emergency Medical Center Transport in Japan
Authors: Yoshitaka Ooya, Mayumi Ishida, Nozomu Uchida, Izumi Sato, Akira Yoshioka, Hideki Onishi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAlthough early cancer detection relies on routine primary care and screening, some patients first receive a cancer diagnosis after emergency transport. We described patients diagnosed with (or strongly suspected of having) cancer after emergency transport to a tertiary Emergency and Acute Medicine Center in Japan, focusing on access to primary care and family support.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cross‐sectional study of patients transported between April 2007 and December 2023 who were diagnosed with or strongly suspected of cancer at the initial visit. We extracted age, sex, cancer site, presenting symptoms, presence of an established primary care medical institution, and cohabiting family members.ResultsAmong 76,989 patients who visited the Center during the study period, 202 (0.6%) were diagnosed with (or strongly suspected of having) cancer. Mean age was 73.1 years (range 22–101), and 141 (69.8%) were male. The most common cancer sites were colorectal/rectal (n = 49), gastric (n = 46), lung (n = 29), and liver (n = 21). Presentations commonly involved bleeding, pain, dyspnea, or general deterioration. Overall, 142 patients (70.3%) had an established primary care medical institution, and 152 (75.2%) lived with family members.ConclusionsMany patients with emergency‐based cancer diagnoses had documented access to primary care and cohabiting family members. These descriptive findings underscore the need for future comparative studies to clarify diagnostic pathways and factors associated with emergency‐based cancer diagnosis.

Introduction

Even today, with advances in modern medicine, cancer remains the leading cause of death among Japanese people. According to the National Cancer Registry in Japan, colorectal, lung, and gastric cancers are among the most common cancer types in Japan [1]. Early‐stage cancer often presents few subjective symptoms, and regular cancer screenings or examinations by primary care physicians are recognized as important for its early detection [2]. However, reports from overseas have noted that many patients receive their first cancer diagnosis when visiting an emergency department [3, 4]. It has been reported that patients diagnosed with cancer in the emergency department are more likely to present with advanced disease (Stage III or IV) compared with those diagnosed by other methods and consequently have lower survival rates [2, 5]. Population‐based data also suggest that presenting symptoms are related to stage at diagnosis [6]. For example, in Taiwan, despite the promotion of free cancer screenings, about 76.4% of cancer patients diagnosed in the emergency department are found to have advanced disease (Stage III or IV), and their risk of death has been shown to be 1.46 times higher compared with patients diagnosed through other pathways [5]. In particular, elderly patients, those with lower education or income levels, and those with multiple comorbidities tend to have a higher risk of being diagnosed with cancer in the emergency department [5]. In symptomatic cancer, longer time to diagnosis and treatment has been examined in relation to outcomes, and patients' perspectives highlight the burden of the pre‐diagnosis interval [7, 8].

Moreover, receiving a cancer diagnosis in addition to the sudden worsening of symptoms requiring emergency transport not only places significant psychological stress on patients [9] but may also create challenges for the healthcare system, including the lack of clear clinical guidelines, difficulties in patient follow‐up after diagnosis, and limited support for families [3]. In addition, there are concerns about interruptions in the continuity of patient care [4].

Even in Japan, where access to medical care is relatively easy compared with other countries, there are patients who are diagnosed with cancer in the emergency department. Understanding pathways leading to emergency‐based cancer diagnosis may help inform early detection efforts and care coordination. Japan's healthcare system, which is characterized by universal health coverage, a large number of medical institutions, and widespread availability of advanced imaging equipment, allows patients to access medical care at an early stage. Nevertheless, the presence of emergency‐based cancer diagnoses suggests that further work is needed to describe and understand diagnostic pathways within the Japanese context.

Although emergency transports leading directly to an initial cancer diagnosis are uncommon, a certain number of patients are transported with conditions such as gastrointestinal perforation or bleeding, abdominal pain, or respiratory distress and are subsequently diagnosed with cancer.

Therefore, this study aimed to describe patients who were transported to our Emergency and Acute Medicine Center and were diagnosed with or strongly suspected of having cancer at the initial visit, including their demographic and clinical characteristics, documented access to an established primary care medical institution, and cohabiting family members.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This study is a retrospective cross‐sectional observational study conducted at the Emergency and Acute Medicine Center of Saitama Medical University International Medical Center, a tertiary emergency facility. This study is reported in accordance with the STROBE statement.

Data Extraction

We extracted case details for patients who were transported to the outpatient department of our hospital's Emergency and Acute Medicine Center, were initially evaluated by emergency physicians, and received a new diagnosis of cancer or were strongly suspected of having cancer at the initial visit between April 2007 and December 2023. We aimed to capture newly diagnosed cancers; patients with a documented history of malignancy prior to the index visit were excluded. Cases were classified as pathologically confirmed malignancies or strongly suspected cancers. Strongly suspected cancer was defined as a case with imaging findings highly suggestive of malignancy (e.g., mass lesion with metastatic features) for which pathological examination and/or other tests required for definitive diagnosis could not be performed because consent could not be obtained and/or invasive diagnostic procedures (e.g., biopsy) were not pursued due to advanced age or poor general condition. We abstracted age, sex, cancer site, presenting symptoms, presence of an established primary care medical institution, and cohabiting family members from the electronic medical record (Figure 1). An established primary care medical institution was defined as documentation in the medical record that the patient had a regular outpatient clinic or hospital providing ongoing care before the index visit (e.g., listed in past history, referral documentation, or medication reconciliation). Cohabiting family members were defined as documentation that the patient lived with at least one family member at the time of the index visit (e.g., nursing intake or social work notes). Because cancer screening history was unavailable for many patients, it was not included in the analysis.

Statistical Analysis

Given the descriptive aim of this study, we summarized variables using descriptive statistics only (means with standard deviations or medians with ranges, and counts with percentages) without hypothesis testing. To assess robustness to inclusion of strongly suspected cases without pathological confirmation, we conducted a sensitivity analysis restricted to pathologically confirmed cases and summarized key proportions.

Ethical Considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Saitama Medical University International Medical Center (Approval number: KOKU 2024–053). The Ethics Committee waived the requirement for individual informed consent because of the retrospective design; study information was disclosed on the hospital website and other platforms, and patients were afforded the opportunity to decline participation through an opt‐out process.

Results

During the study period, 76,989 patients visited the Emergency and Acute Medicine Center. Of these, 36,211 patients were managed by emergency physicians as they had no established department for follow‐up at our hospital at the time of their initial visit. Among these patients, 202 (0.6%) were diagnosed with or strongly suspected of having cancer at the Center (Figure 1). Of these 202 cases, n = 155 were pathologically confirmed, and n = 47 were classified as strongly suspected based on imaging findings without pathological confirmation. The mean age of patients was 73.1 years (range, 22–101 years), with a majority being male (141 male, 61 female). The most common cancer type was colorectal cancer, including the rectum, with 49 cases, followed by gastric cancer (N = 46), lung cancer (N = 29), and liver cancer (N = 21), with one case each of spinal, small intestine, prostate, ovarian cancers, and one of unknown origin (Table 1).

The reasons for emergency transport in the most common cases of colon cancer, stomach cancer, and lung cancer were as follows. For those with colon cancer, the most common reason was abdominal pain (21 cases, 42.9%), followed by bloody stools (11 cases, 22.4%) and general deterioration, including altered consciousness or difficulty moving (10 cases, 20.4%). For gastric cancer, the most common presenting symptom was hematemesis (28 cases, 60.9%), followed by general deterioration (7 cases, 15.2%) and abdominal pain (5 cases, 10.9%). For lung cancer, the most common symptom was dyspnea (10 cases, 34.5%), followed by general deterioration (6 cases, 20.7%), and hemoptysis (3 cases, 10.3%).

At the time of transport, 142 patients (70.3%) had an established primary care medical institution (Table 1). An analysis of the presence of primary care physicians among the most common cancer types (colorectal, gastric, and lung cancer) showed that 36 of 49 colorectal cancer patients (73.5%), 34 of 46 gastric cancer patients (73.9%), and 22 of 29 lung cancer patients (75.9%) had a primary care physician (Table 2).

Discussion

This study descriptively characterized patients who received a first cancer diagnosis or were strongly suspected of having cancer at their initial presentation after emergency transport to a tertiary Emergency and Acute Medicine Center. Although such cases represented a small proportion of emergency‐physician–managed visits, a measurable number of patients were identified through this pathway.

Colorectal, gastric, lung, and liver cancers were the most frequently observed sites. Common reasons for emergency transport included bleeding, pain, dyspnea, and general deterioration (e.g., altered consciousness or difficulty moving). Symptom patterns were consistent with acute presentations that may accompany malignancy (e.g., abdominal pain or hematochezia in colorectal cancer, hematemesis in gastric cancer, and dyspnea in lung cancer). These findings underscore the practical need in emergency care to manage acute symptoms while also considering underlying malignancy when clinically appropriate, and to ensure timely linkage to diagnostic evaluation, specialty referral, and follow‐up planning.

A notable descriptive finding was that many patients had documented access to a primary care medical institution and cohabited with family members. This indicates that emergency‐based cancer diagnosis is not restricted to patients lacking any access to healthcare or social support. However, because this was a single‐center descriptive study without a comparison group, we cannot infer whether access to primary care or cohabitation with family members increases or decreases the likelihood of an emergency‐based cancer diagnosis. Future studies incorporating an appropriate comparator (e.g., patients diagnosed through non‐emergency pathways during the same period) are needed to examine factors associated with emergency‐based diagnosis.

Prior primary‐care research, including qualitative studies, has proposed potential contributors to delayed cancer diagnosis, such as the nature of primary care, responses to initial symptoms, the diagnostic testing process, and consultation/referral pathways [10], as well as non‐specific symptoms, competing diagnoses, system constraints, communication challenges, and patient hesitancy [11]. Prior work has also reported that endoscopic evaluation for rectal bleeding is not consistently performed in primary care, even when guideline criteria are met [12]. However, because the present study is descriptive and based on a limited set of variables abstracted from medical records, we could not assess whether these factors were present or contributed to emergency‐based diagnosis at the individual level. Future studies incorporating non‐emergency comparator groups and pathway‐level information (e.g., follow‐up processes and diagnostic work‐up completion) are needed to evaluate these hypotheses.

Cancer screening is a key strategy for early detection, and physician recommendation has been associated with improved screening uptake [13, 14]. Because screening history was unavailable for many patients in our dataset, we could not evaluate the relationship between screening participation and emergency‐based cancer diagnosis. Future research, including screening history and follow‐up data, may clarify whether interventions to support screening participation and completion of recommended diagnostic work‐up can reduce the proportion of cancers first identified through emergency presentation. Family context may also influence health behaviors, including screening participation [15], and family‐inclusive approaches warrant further study.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it was a retrospective, single‐center descriptive study without a comparison group (e.g., cancers diagnosed through non‐emergency pathways). Therefore, we cannot infer associations between primary care access or family cohabitation and the likelihood of emergency‐based cancer diagnosis, nor can we make causal interpretations.

Second, the cohort included both pathologically confirmed malignancies (n = 155) and cases classified as “strongly suspected cancer” (n = 47). Lack of pathological confirmation was more common for tumor types that typically require biopsy for diagnosis (e.g., liver, pancreatic, biliary tract, and brain tumors), particularly when invasive testing was not pursued because of older age or poor general condition. In contrast, pathological specimens were more often obtained in cases that underwent endoscopy or surgery for gastrointestinal bleeding or perforation. Thus, confirmation status may be influenced by tumor site and clinical context.

Third, key clinical outcomes such as stage at diagnosis, time to treatment initiation, and survival were not available for robust analysis; consequently, the relationship between emergency‐based diagnosis and advanced disease or diagnostic delay cannot be established from this dataset.

Fourth, key variables (presence of a primary care medical institution and living arrangement) were determined from medical record documentation and may not capture the intensity or quality of care, the degree of family support, or other contextual factors. Finally, although prior qualitative work identifies potential contributors to delayed diagnosis—such as non‐specific symptoms, competing diagnoses, system constraints, and communication challenges—our data do not permit evaluation of these factors [10, 11]. Screening history was unavailable for many patients and was not included in the analysis.

Conclusions

Emergency‐based cancer diagnosis occurred in a subset of patients evaluated at our Emergency and Acute Medicine Center, and many had documentation of an established primary care medical institution and cohabiting family members. These descriptive findings underscore the importance of considering malignancy in patients presenting with symptoms such as bleeding, pain, dyspnea, or general deterioration, and highlight the need for future comparative studies to clarify diagnostic pathways and opportunities to strengthen follow‐up and continuity of care.
