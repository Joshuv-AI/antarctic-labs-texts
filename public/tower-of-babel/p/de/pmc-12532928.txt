The impact of Pecha Kucha on enhancing basic life support training: randomized controlled trial
Authors: Maide Yeşilyurt
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBasic Life Support (BLS) is essential for saving lives, yet traditional teaching methods may not ensure lasting learning. Pecha Kucha, a dynamic and visual presentation style, has been suggested to boost engagement and retention. This study aimed to determine the effect of Basic Life Support (BLS) training given with the Pecha Kucha (PK) method on the level of learning and practicing Basic Life Support among students studying health services.MethodsA randomized controlled study was conducted according to the CONSORT guideline. This randomized controlled pretest-posttest study included 120 students receiving first-aid courses. Data were collected using a descriptive characteristics form, which was created by the researchers in line with the literature, and the “BLS Skills Questionnaire”. The forms were applied before, shortly after, and 4 weeks after practicing PK.ResultsIt was determined that the level of knowledge and practice in the PK group significantly increased shortly after the training and one month after the training compared to the pre-training period. When the levels of knowledge and practice scores before, after, and one month after the training were compared within the group, it was found that there was a significant change in the mean scores of the PK group.ConclusionsPecha Kucha significantly improved BLS knowledge and skills, with effects lasting up to one month. This method offers a powerful and lasting impact on learning—making it a valuable tool for BLS training.Trial registrationThis study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (trial registration number: NCT06213961; registered on March 27, 2024).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08064-z.Keywords: Basic life support, Pecha Kucha, Student

Introduction

Given the persistently low survival rates from cardiac arrest worldwide, effective training methods for Basic Life Support (BLS) are urgently needed to improve outcomes among both healthcare providers and lay responders [1]. Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) survival rates have remained relatively stagnant over the past three decades, typically ranging between 8% and 10%, despite notable advancements in emergency medical care [2]. Despite the need for BLS competency, significant knowledge and skill gaps are observed among health sciences students, and their performance often fails to meet international guideline standards [3–5]. Low-quality BLS and CPR practices can lead to preventable adverse outcomes, emphasizing the need for innovative educational strategies to strengthen the competencies of future clinical practitioners [1, 2, 4].

Previous studies have demonstrated that BLS training significantly improves students’ theoretical knowledge and practical skills. For instance, research conducted in Turkey highlighted notable gains in knowledge and skills among health sciences students following structured BLS training [5]. A multi-country study involving 1012 medical students across 14 countries similarly emphasized the need to enhance cardiac arrest and CPR knowledge among medical students in Europe [5]. In the literature, various educational methods are used to improve students’ knowledge and skills in BLS and CPR. In particular, simulation-based BLS training has been reported to significantly increase nursing students’ knowledge and psychomotor skills [1, 2]. Furthermore, it has been noted that nursing students who receive BLS training in nursing practice laboratories also experience a significant increase in their knowledge and practical skills [6].

Pecha Kucha (PK), derived from the Japanese term meaning “chit-chat,” is an internationally recognized presentation format designed to facilitate creative and visually focused communication [7, 8]. The format consists of 20 slides, each displayed for 20 s, totaling 6 min and 40 s. Its structured and time-constrained nature enables presenters to deliver essential information clearly and effectively without deviation from the main topic [9–11]. Literature suggests that PK presentations enhance learning outcomes and promote long-term retention of information more effectively than traditional formats [11, 12]. Furthermore, in health education, PK-based trainings on various topics have been shown to significantly improve students’ knowledge of pressure injuries [13] and stoma care skills [14]. Additionally, PK enhances students’ presentation skills and actively engages them in the learning process [15, 16]. Given the time-sensitive and skill-intensive nature of BLS, the concise and structured PK format is particularly suitable for delivering both essential knowledge and practical training efficiently.

Currently, no study has investigated the effectiveness of PK-based BLS education, highlighting a significant research gap. This study aims to examine the impact of BLS training delivered via the PK method on students’ theoretical knowledge and practical skills. The research questions guiding this study are as follows:

Does BLS training using the PK method improve students’ theoretical knowledge?

Does BLS training using the PK method improve students’ theoretical knowledge?

Does BLS training using the PK method improve students’ practical skills?

Does BLS training using the PK method improve students’ practical skills?

This study aims to contribute initial empirical evidence on the potential effectiveness of the PK method in BLS education.

Methods

Study objective and type

This randomized controlled trial was planned to determine the effect of the training given to students studying health services using the Pecha Kucha method on the levels of BLS knowledge and CPR practice of the students. The Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) checklist was used for reporting the study (Fig. 1) [17]. This study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov with the trial registration number NCT06213961 on March 27, 2024.

CONSORT flow diagram for this trial

Study setting and sample

The research population consisted of 126 students taking First Aid courses at a public university in Türkiye during the 2023–2024 academic year. Since the aim was to reach the entire population, no sampling method was preferred. The inclusion criteria for the study were as follows: Students who volunteered to participate in the study, were 18 years of age or older, and were taking First Aid courses at a public university Vocational School of Health Services were included in the study. Ethic statement

The study was conducted following the principles of the Helsinki Declaration [18]. Ethical committee approval for this study was obtained from the Aksaray University Human Research Ethics Committee (Approval No: 2023/07–17). In addition, institutional permission was granted for conducting the research in the relevant setting (Permission No: E-23897576-604).

Human ethics and consent to participate

Prior to participation, all students included in the research sample were informed about the purpose of the study, and their participation was entirely voluntary. They were included in the study after providing their informed consent. The participants included in the study sample answered the questions after reading and after giving written approving the “Informed Voluntary Consent Form” about the purpose of the study and that participation in the study was voluntary.

Data collection tools

The data were collected using a descriptive characteristics form which was developed by the researcher in line with the literature [19–21] and the “Basic Life Support Skills Questionnaire”. The data collection tool Basic Life Support Skills Questionnaire used in this study was a questionnaire developed by the author based on the relevant literature. To assess the reliability of the instrument, Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was calculated. The questionnaire demonstrated good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.857, indicating that the items reliably measured the intended constructs.

Content validity

The content validity of the “Basic Life Support Application Checklist” and the “Basic Life Support Knowledge Questionnaire” used in the study was evaluated by five academics specializing in nursing. Items in both scales were scored using a 4-point Likert scale; I-CVI values ranged from 0.80 to 1.00. These values indicate that the scales have a high level of content validity. Differences in opinion among the experts were discussed and resolved, minimizing the risk of bias.

Descriptive characteristics form

The Descriptive Characteristics Form consisted of items including age, gender, high school graduation status, academic grade point average, previous BLS training experience, and whether the participant had previously witnessed BLS.

Basic life support skills questionnaire

The form consists of 10 multiple-choice questions prepared according to the guidelines of the European Resuscitation Council [19] and the American Heart Association [20] and 15 items Basic Life Support Implementation Checklist for the assessment of application skills. Each correct answer/application of the student in the form questioning the levels of knowledge and practice about basic life support application is scored as 1 point and the mean score is calculated. In order to ensure the content validity of the developed questionnaire, expert opinions were obtained from academicians specialized in the field, and the content validity was established based on their evaluations.

Data collection

Data were collected between December 1, 2023, and January 30, 2024. In this study, models with feedback on lighting were used to evaluate students’ BLS application performance. The “Basic Life Support Skills Questionnaire” was used to assess students’ knowledge status in the pre-test and post-test phases of the study, while the Basic Life Support Implementation Checklist was used to determine their application status. The relevant forms are provided as Supplementary File 1. During the pre-test/post-test phases, two independent observers, other than the researchers, assisted in completing the checklist. The data collection tool used in this study was a questionnaire developed by the author based on the relevant literature (see Supplementary File 1 for the English version) (Fig. 2).

Research Implementation Process

Interventions applied to the experimental group

Students were informed about the study before being accepted into the training class, and those who met the volunteer and inclusion criteria were accepted into the class. Before providing students with basic life support training, a descriptive characteristics form, a basic life support skills questionnaire, and a checklist were administered (pre-test). The checklist was collected by an independent observer and consisted of items assessing how the student performed CPR steps. Shortly after the students were given a PK presentation on basic life support skills (Post-test 1), the skills questionnaire and checklist were administered again 4 weeks later (Post-test 2). The BLS and CPR training, consisting of 20 slides summarizing the step-by-step application procedures, was delivered using the Pecha Kucha method, which lasted a total of 8 min. Unlike traditional presentations, the PK method used a format that emphasized concise and visually focused information within a time constraint and required students to actively pay attention throughout the session.

Interventions applied to the control group

First, students who volunteered to participate in the study and met the inclusion criteria were identified. Before providing BLS and CPR training to the students, a descriptive characteristics form, a basic life support skills questionnaire, and a checklist were administered (pre-test). The checklist was collected by an independent observer and consisted of items assessing how the student performed the CPR steps. Shortly after the traditional educational presentation on basic life support skills was delivered to the students (Post-test 1), the skills questionnaire and checklist were administered again 4 weeks later (Post-test 2). The traditional presentation of basic life support training given to the students lasted approximately 30 min and was delivered in a classroom setting using a standard PowerPoint presentation. Unlike the PK approach, the traditional presentation relied on a longer, lecture-style format.

Randomization and blinding

The study was planned as a single-blind randomized controlled trial. The randomization of participants was ensured using Research Randomizer (https://www.randomizer.org/). A statistician, who was not involved in the study, randomly generated a list of participants before the onset of the study. The total number of participants was recorded in the program and the experimental and control groups were created automatically. The experimental group received PK training and the control group received traditional presentation training.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics are presented as numbers, percentages, means, and standard deviations. The normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, the normal distribution curve, and tests for skewness and kurtosis. Parametric tests were applied to data showing a normal distribution: The Independent Samples t-test was used to compare the means of two independent groups; One-way ANOVA and Repeated Measures ANOVA were used to compare the means of more than one group or the measurements of the same group at different time points; The Pearson Chi-square test was used to evaluate the relationship between categorical variables. The statistical significance level was set at p < 0.05.

Results

The students’ descriptive characteristics are presented in Table 1. The students in the traditional training and Pecha Kucha training groups were similar in terms of all descriptive characteristics such as age, sex, high school of graduation, academic achievement score, the status of previously receiving BLS training and witnessing BLS (p > 0.05) (Table 1). The average age of students participating in the study was determined to be 20.45 ± 1.34 in the traditional presentation group and 20.08 ± 1.12 in the PK group. In the traditional presentation group, 75% of the students were female and 25% were male; in the PK group, 70% were female and 30% were male. The majority of participants were high school graduates, with 65% in the traditional group and 61.7% in the PK group. Academic grade point averages were similar in both groups. The percentage of students who had previously received BLS training was 35% in the traditional group and 26.7% in the PK group, while the percentage who had witnessed BLS was 10% and 20%, respectively. In group comparisons, students who had previously received BLS training had significantly higher pre-test scores in both the PK group (t = 5.148, p < 0.001) and the traditional group (t = 2.555, p = 0.013). Since this situation was similar in both groups, no significant difference was observed in between-group comparisons.

There was a statistically significant difference between the groups in terms of knowledge and practice scores before and after the training (Table 2) (p < 0.001). There was a significant increase in the levels of knowledge and practice in the PK group shortly after the training and one month after the training compared to the pre-training period (p < 0.001).

Discussion

In this study, which was carried out to determine the effect of BLS training given to students studying health services with PK method on the levels of knowledge and practice of students, it was determined that there was a significant increase in the levels of knowledge and practice in the PK group shortly after the training and one month after the training compared to the pre-training period. These results directly address our research question by showing that PK training significantly improves both knowledge and practical skills in BLS compared to the pre-training levels and the traditional method. Specifically, students in the experimental group quickly and effectively absorbed information through 20 time-limited, visually focused slides during the PK session. This structure supported the development of their practical skills by maintaining their high level of attention. In contrast, the traditional method used in the control group took the form of a 30-minute in-class PowerPoint presentation, and interaction remained limited.

In intragroup comparisons, the levels of knowledge and practice of the PK group were found to be significantly higher (Tables 2 and 3). In the literature, there is no study in which BLS training has been given with the PK method. However, there are studies in which students studying in the department of health services have received BLS training with different methods for clinicians. Similar to the study result, BLS training given in a simulation environment has been found to increase the knowledge and skills of nursing students [1, 2, 21–24]. Training provided to students in the field of health services and health professionals online [25, 26] or face-to-face [6, 27–29] has also been shown to be effective in increasing BLS knowledge and skills. These literature findings support the research findings.

In the study, it was found that the students’ performance of BLS application steps increased with PK and traditional training (Table 4). In some studies supporting the findings of the study, nursing students’ ability to control the safety of the environment [22], control consciousness and respiration [22, 25, 26] take the appropriate position for BLS and determine the appropriate pressure site [23], maintain the hands in the appropriate position and apply adequate pressure [22, 25–27, 30], apply pressure without bending the elbows [22, 24], perform 30 pressures and give 2 breaths [19, 20], and control pulse and respiration after 5 cycles [22, 27] increased.

In studies conducted with university students [30], it was recommended that trainings be repeated theoretically and practically at certain intervals. The studies indicate that there is a need for change in BLS training [4]. The Pecka Kucha method also enhances learning by providing effective time management with visual images. In studies conducted using the PK method in medical education, students have reported that they were satisfied with the learning experience in training given with the PK presentation method [9, 10]. In the study, the increase in the levels of knowledge and practice of the students in the PK group compared to the students who received education with the traditional method showed that the students benefited from the PK method. In both groups of students, those who had previously received BLS training scored significantly higher on the pre-test; however, this did not result in a significant difference between the groups. Therefore, the effectiveness of the PK method can be reliably evaluated. The traditional method consisted of a 30-minute classroom PowerPoint presentation with minimal interaction, lacking the concise visual format and active engagement required in the PK sessions, which likely contributed to the differences in learning outcomes.

Repeated measurements in both groups revealed a significant increase in students’ BLS knowledge and application levels compared to pre-training (p < 0.001). Although an increase in knowledge and application levels was also recorded in the traditional group, this increase was more limited and lower compared to the PK group; the PK method provided a more pronounced improvement in students’ learning outcomes. In a study in which traditional presentation and PK presentation were compared in nursing education, it was emphasized that the satisfaction of the students in the PK group was higher and that the PK technique should be used in the subject that educators want to draw attention to [31]. It has been stated that the PK method is an innovative way of presentation for educators and students in nursing education [7]. Students studying health services were given training on the prevention of pressure injuries with the PK presentation method and it was determined that the level of knowledge of the students increased with the PK method [13]. Studies in the literature in which the PK presentation technique has been used in student education in the fields of medicine, nursing, and health services have indicated that this method is effective, efficient, and increases student satisfaction [9, 10, 13, 31].

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, the research was conducted with a relatively small sample size from a single institution, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to broader populations. Second, the follow-up assessment was limited to one month after the training, restricting the ability to evaluate long-term knowledge retention and skill sustainability. Additionally, the study relied on self-reported performance and structured observation during skills assessment, which may be influenced by participant bias or observer subjectivity. Lastly, while expert opinion was used to establish the content validity of the measurement tool, further psychometric testing could strengthen its reliability and validity.

Conclusion

The research findings revealed that BLS training given to students studying in the field of health services through the PK method was more effective compared to the traditional presentation. In intergroup and intragroup comparisons, it was determined that the levels of BLS knowledge and practice of the PK group were significantly higher. It is recommended that the PK method, which is an up-to-date and new method in Basic Life Support training, be used and more comparative studies be conducted.
