Development and Validation of an Artificial Intelligence-Driven Model for Accurate Classification of Erythrodermic Psoriasis Severity: Erythrodermic Psoriasis Integrated Classification System (EPICS)
Authors: Yuyan Yang, Chao Wu, Xinyuan Zhang, Chenyang Yu, Hanlin Zhang, Hongzhong Jin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundErythrodermic psoriasis is a rare subtype of psoriasis with widespread skin lesions, with some patients experiencing severe systemic symptoms.ObjectiveWe aimed to develop and validate an artificial intelligence-driven model for accurate classification of erythrodermic psoriasis severity by integrating clinical and laboratory indicators.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted at Peking Union Medical College Hospital (2005–22). Patients were divided into mild and moderate-to-severe groups using k-means clustering. After imputing missing values, we trained seven candidate algorithms—K-Nearest Neighbors, Artificial Neural Network, Random Forest, Extreme Gradient Boosting, Support Vector Machine, Bayesian classifier, and logistic regression—using repeated, stratified ten-fold cross-validation with three repeats (10 × 3 CV); performance was summarized by the mean area under the receiver operating characteristic curve across folds. Feature importance was assessed using SHAP (Shapley Additive exPlanations), a game-theoretic approach that quantifies each features contribution to individual model predictions, ten indicators were incorporated into a diagnostic scoring system. The optimal cut-off for mild/moderate-to-severe cases classification was selected with the Youden index on the cross-validated receiver operating characteristic curve.ResultsOf 260 screened records, 242 erythrodermic patients met the study criteria. Histology confirmed psoriasis in 108 cases, while the remaining patients were diagnosed based on clinical presentation and medical history. K-means clustering assigned 94 patients to the moderate-to-severe group and 148 to the mild group. Moderate-to-severe erythrodermic psoriasis was characterized by a higher inflammatory burden (median neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio 4.11 vs 2.70, p < 0.001), more frequent fever (88% vs 41%, p < 0.001), greater edema severity (16% vs 1.4%, p < 0.001), lower albumin and higher calcium levels (both p < 0.001), and longer hospitalization (median 26 vs 20 days, p = 0.005). After adjustment for age and sex, moderate-to-severe cases required systemic therapy roughly twice as often as mild cases (odds ratio 2.21, p < 0.05). Of seven machine-learning algorithms, the Artificial Neural Network yielded the highest mean validation area under the curve. The SHAP analysis highlighted the ten most influential predictors adopted from the Artificial Neural Network—edema, edematous erythema (defined as the combination of both redness and swelling of the skin), fever, albumin, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, serum calcium, white blood cell count, acute-phase reactants (C-reactive protein or erythrocyte sedimentation rate), pruritus, and superficial lymphadenopathy—and these were converted to integer points to form the bedside score. The receiver operating characteristic analysis identified 33.5 points as the optimal threshold for distinguishing between mild and moderate-to-severe cases. The model, named ‘EPICS’ (Erythrodermic Psoriasis Integrated Classification System), effectively stratified patients, as evidenced by internal validation. This model is currently available online (https://pumch-dermatology.shinyapps.io/classification/).ConclusionsThe EPICS model is a robust tool for assessing erythrodermic psoriasis severity, offering precise classification based on easily accessible clinical and laboratory indicators. However, its effectiveness in clinical practice requires further validation through additional research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40257-025-00980-6.

Key Points

As a rare variant of psoriasis characterized by severe cutaneous manifestations and diverse systemic symptoms, severity classification of erythrodermic psoriasis remains unclear.The Erythrodermic Psoriasis Integrated Classification System (EPICS) is a novel scoring tool applicable in the outpatient department, which can assess erythrodermic psoriasis severity and help in therapeutic guidance.

Introduction

Erythrodermic psoriasis (EP) is a rare yet potentially life-threatening variant of psoriasis, accounting for approximately 0.7–2.25% of all psoriasis cases among patients with psoriasis vulgaris [1, 2]. It is characterized by generalized confluent erythematous plaques covering over 90% of the body surface area (BSA), often accompanied by severe systemic inflammatory responses and homeostatic disturbances [3]. Patients with EP frequently present with complications such as fever, lymphadenopathy, hypoalbuminemia, electrolyte imbalances, and a high risk of infection, which can lead to significant morbidity and mortality if not promptly and effectively managed.

Assessing disease severity in EP remains a significant clinical challenge, as accurate stratification is critical for selecting the appropriate management approach. Traditional psoriasis metrics, including Psoriasis Area and Severity Index, BSA, and Physician’s Global Assessment, consistently reach ceiling values in patients with EP and thus fail to effectively distinguish mild from severe presentations [4, 5]. Historically, isolated retrospective studies have correlated factors such as hypoalbuminemia, leukocytosis, and elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) or erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) with poor clinical outcomes. Despite this, a universally accepted composite severity scale has yet to be established. In response, our team introduced the 2018-Erythrodermic Psoriasis Severity Assessment (2018-EPSA), a scoring system encompassing three clinical indicators: fever (body temperature > 37.3 °C), significant swelling and exudation affecting more than 50% of skin lesions or lower extremity edema, and superficial lymphadenopathy [6]. While useful in differentiating moderate-to-severe cases from mild presentations, the 2018-EPSA primarily addresses systemic symptoms and lacks comprehensive consideration of cutaneous manifestations and laboratory indicators of systemic imbalance. Furthermore, it inadequately captures the complexity of interactions among multiple clinical factors that may influence disease progression.

Traditional statistical models, such as logistic and Cox regression, assume linearity, additive effects, and minimal multicollinearity—conditions seldom met in EP, where laboratory parameters including albumin, serum calcium, body mass index, CRP/ESR, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and white blood cell counts exhibit significant inter-correlations and non-linear relationships with disease severity. Emerging research in dermatology has demonstrated that advanced machine learning and artificial intelligence methodologies, including tree-based algorithms and deep-learning models, can outperform conventional statistical approaches in predicting psoriasis severity and treatment responses. However, existing artificial intelligence studies have primarily focused on plaque psoriasis and image-based assessments, rather than EP-specific clinical and laboratory parameters [7–9].

In this study, we set out to develop and validate an artificial intelligence-driven model, named ‘EPICS’ (Erythrodermic Psoriasis Integrated Classification System), that can reliably flag moderate-to-severe EP at the point of care, translate its predictions into transparent risk scores, and integrate both clinical manifestations and routine laboratory data. By doing so, the model is intended to guide earlier escalation to systemic or biologic therapy, support personalized treatment pathways, and help clinicians allocate limited inpatient resources, such as monitored beds or intensive nursing, to the patients who will benefit most.

Methods

Study Population

This retrospective, observational, comparative cohort study was conducted at Peking Union Medical College Hospital between 2005 and 2022. Patients were eligible if they met all of the following inclusion criteria: (i) age ≥ 12 years; (ii) had a definite diagnosis of psoriasis, established either by clinical presentation or histological confirmation during the index admission; and (iii) presented with generalized confluent erythematous plaques covering at least 90% of the BSA. Patients were excluded if they met any of the following exclusion criteria: (i) discharge against medical advice; (ii) incomplete clinical data because of an inter-departmental transfer; and (iii) inability to definitively rule out other causes of erythroderma (e.g., drug-induced eruption, eczema, atopic dermatitis, eosinophilic dermatosis) according to clinical manifestations and pathological examinations. Clinical data were extracted from the patients’ medical records.

Data Collection

To develop this classification system, we considered the various acute cutaneous and systemic symptoms that patients with EP exhibit. Patients in the acute phase are prone to internal homeostasis disruption. Pruritus is the most common subjective symptom in both acute and chronic cases, often leading to insomnia and decreased quality of life [10]. Certain parameters such as desquamation were excluded because of measurement difficulties in patients with chronic disease using topical treatments, and nail or hair changes were omitted because their association with disease severity remains controversial [11]. Fever is a common systemic abnormality associated with thermoregulatory disorders caused by systemic inflammation, scale accumulation, and secondary skin infection [12]. Superficial lymphadenopathy indicates excessive lymphocyte proliferation induced by aseptic inflammatory responses or acute immunological disorders [5]. Hypoalbuminemia can monitor the patient’s negative nitrogen balance due to fever, excessive desquamation, or low protein intake [13, 14]. Both ESR and CRP are sensitive inflammatory indicators [15]. Edematous erythema is defined as the combination of redness and swelling of the skin, a typical manifestation in EP, while exudation reflects severe skin barrier dysfunction [2, 16]. To clarify the subjective measures, edema and edematous erythema were evaluated by two trained clinicians during patient consultations, following standardized scoring methods. Edema severity was graded as follows: (1) Grade 1: a shallow indentation of approximately 2 mm that recovers within 10 s after pressure release; (2) Grade 2: an indentation of approximately 4 mm that takes 10–30 s to recover; (3) Grade 3: an indentation of 6 mm with recovery time exceeding 30 s, often accompanied by tight and shiny skin; and (4) Grade 4: an indentation greater than 8 mm with very slow recovery (minutes), possibly accompanied by skin exudation or ulceration [17]. These measurements were made in real time during the consultation and were not based on a retrospective image review. To ensure consistency and accuracy, all clinical findings were cross-checked with patient medical records.

Based on these considerations, clinical and laboratory indicators related to the severity of EP were collected for each patient. These indicators included sex, smoking history, presence of edema, erythema, skin itchiness, lymph node involvement, exudation presence, fever, albumin levels, calcium levels, acute-phase reactants (ESR and CRP) protein levels, and Charlson Comorbidity Index, which are known to influence disease severity. In addition to these severity-related indicators, hospitalization duration and the use of systemic therapy (particularly corticosteroids, which were previously administered because of the limited available treatments) were also collected as prognostic indicators, helping to assess the disease’s progression and the patients’ prognosis.

Data Preprocessing

We utilized the mice package in R for imputing missing data, specifying classification mean matching (pmm) for numeric variables and logistic regression (logreg) for binary variables. For unordered categorical variables with more than two levels, polynomial regression (polyreg) was applied. This comprehensive approach allowed us to address missing values effectively across different types of data within our study.

Binary Severity Classification (Mild vs Moderate to Severe)

To categorize the severity of EP among the patients, K-means clustering was employed. This unsupervised machine learning algorithm grouped the patients into distinct clusters representing varying degrees of disease severity, effectively classifying them into mild and moderate-to-severe categories.

Addressing Multicollinearity

Before applying machine learning models, we addressed multicollinearity among predictors to enhance the robustness of our analysis. We started by constructing an initial linear model using the generalized linear model function with a binomial family in R. We then calculated the variance inflation factor for each predictor to identify and quantify multicollinearity, ensuring the variables used in our models were independent.

Classification Modeling Construction and Evaluation

We developed several machine learning models to predict the severity of EP, each chosen for their specific strengths in handling different aspects of the dataset. K-Nearest Neighbors was utilized for its simplicity and effectiveness in classification tasks based on feature similarity. Artificial Neural Network (ANN) was implemented to capture complex non-linear relationships within the data. Random Forest was employed for its robust capability to handle high-dimensional data and model interactions between variables. Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) was used for its efficiency and high performance with structured data. Additionally, Bayesian methods were included to provide probabilistic insights, while logistic regression was applied to model the odds of clinical outcomes.

To enhance the reliability and stability of our predictions, we generated five independent 80/20 random splits of the full cohort. For each split, we trained our candidate algorithms on 80% of the patients, using repeated ten-fold cross-validation within that training subset to tune hyperparameters and guard against overfitting. We then evaluated each tuned model on the held-out 20% validation set. Finally, we aggregated performance metrics (area under the curve) across the five validation folds to report both mean accuracy and variability, ensuring a more dependable estimate of each model’s true generalization. In addition, we summarized each model’s validation, set area under the curve-receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve across the five splits by calculating the mean, standard deviation, and minimum and maximum values, in order to capture both central tendency and variability in performance.

Feature Importance and Diagnostic Standard Construction

To interpret the contribution of individual features to the predictions across our models, we employed the SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) method. This approach allowed us to calculate the impact of each feature on the model output, providing a measure of feature importance termed as normalized weight. After computing the SHAP values for each feature, we identified and selected the top ten indicators based on their normalized weight as the key factors for classifying the severity of EP. These indicators were selected because they demonstrated the strongest influence on the classification models’ outcomes, thus were believed to be the most clinically relevant in terms of assessing disease severity. Then, the SHAP values for each indicator were normalized to ensure their cumulative weight summed to 100%. To establish an optimal cutoff score for distinguishing between mild and moderate-to-severe EP, we conducted a ROC curve analysis on the total scores derived from our classification model. The ROC curve plots the true-positive rate (sensitivity) against the false-positive rate (1 − specificity) at various threshold settings. We employed the Youden index method to determine the optimal threshold.

Benchmark Analysis

A benchmark analysis was performed on the full cohort of 242 patients, comparing the newly developed EPICS with the previously developed 2018-EPSA score. The goal was to evaluate the discriminatory ability of both models in terms of their association with the need for systemic therapy and hospital length of stay (LOS). For the systemic therapy need comparison, age- and sex-adjusted ROC curves were generated to assess the performance of each model. For LOS, a t-test was used to compare the hospitalization duration between the moderate-to-severe and mild groups defined by EPICS and 2018-EPSA.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline variables were summarized overall and by classification group. Continuous data are reported as medians with inter-quartile ranges and were compared between groups with the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical data are expressed as counts and percentages and were compared with Pearson’s χ2 test; Fisher’s exact test was applied when expected cell counts were < 5. Two-sided p-values below 0.05 were interpreted as statistically significant.

Results

Patient Clustering and Baseline Characteristics

The flow chart of this research is illustrated in Fig. 1. Of 260 eligible patients identified between January 2015 and December 2022, 18 were excluded because of excessive data missing, leaving 242 patients for analysis. In our study, 108 patients had a histologically confirmed diagnosis of psoriasis through biopsy. For the remaining patients, the diagnosis was based on clinical presentation and medical history of psoriasis, with histological confirmation not required as it was considered an exclusionary diagnosis for other conditions. The final cohort consisted of 242 patients (178 men [73.5%] and 64 women [26.4%]), with a median age of 48.05 years (range: 13–82 years). Baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1. Body mass index data were missing in 31 patients (12.8%) and imputed using multiple imputations. After imputation, patients were grouped using k-means clustering into two distinct severity categories: 94 in the moderate-to-severe group (example case shown in Fig. 2a, b) and 148 in the mild group (example cases shown in Fig. 2c, d). Statistical analysis revealed a significant difference in hospital stay durations between the two clusters, with the moderate-to-severe group having a notably longer hospital stay (26 days vs 20 days, p = 0.005), suggesting that the clustering method effectively captured clinical distinctions in treatment burden and disease progression. The baseline characteristics of patients with mild (n = 148) and moderate-to-severe (n = 94) EP were compared, revealing significant differences. Patients with moderate-to-severe EP were older, with a median age of 52 years compared with 48 years in the mild EP group (p = 0.015). Fever was more prevalent in moderate-to-severe cases (88%) than in mild cases (41%, p < 0.001). Edema severity also differed significantly, with severe edema observed in 16% of patients with moderate-to-severe EP compared with only 1.4% in the mild group (p < 0.001). The NLR, a marker of systemic inflammation, was higher in the moderate-to-severe group (median 4.11) compared with the mild group (median 2.70, p < 0.001). Calcium levels were elevated in 83% of moderate-to-severe cases compared with 66% in the mild group (p < 0.001). Additionally, patients with moderate-to-severe EP had significantly lower albumin levels, with more pronounced hypoalbuminemia observed in this group (p < 0.001). Because the use of systemic corticosteroid therapy could be confounded by age and sex, a multivariable logistic-regression model was fitted to estimate the age- and sex-adjusted association between classification category and corticosteroid use. After matching, patients with moderate-to-severe EP required systemic therapies, which historically included corticosteroid treatment because of limited available options, approximately twice as often as mild cases (odds ratio = 2.21; 95% confidence interval 1.11–4.46; p < 0.05), after controlling for age and sex. Model Performance Summary

In our study, the classification models demonstrated varying levels of accuracy, as quantified by the AUC metrics. For the training set, we evaluated the performance of seven machine learning models using five independent 80/20 random splits of the dataset. Across the training sets, Random Forest and XGBoost models achieved near-perfect AUC scores, both reaching average training AUCs around 1, demonstrating excellent fitting capability (Fig. S1a, b of the Electronic Supplementary Material [ESM]). The Neural Network model also showed strong training performance (Fig. S1c of the ESM). On the validation sets, Neural Network emerged as the best-performing model with an average AUC of 0.995, indicating superior generalization ability (Fig. S2 of the ESMc). Random Forest and logistic regression followed closely with average validation AUCs of 0.980 and 0.972, respectively (Fig. S2b, f of the ESM). The XGBoost maintained strong validation performance as well, with an average AUC of 0.995 on training but a slightly lower yet still robust validation AUC of 0.986 (Fig. S2a of the ESM). Support Vector Machine also demonstrated consistent results with an average validation AUC of 0.99 (Fig. S2g of the ESM). Naive Bayes and K-Nearest Neighbors models showed relatively weaker validation performance, with AUCs around 0.97 reflecting more limited adaptability to unseen data (Fig. 3c, Fig. S2d, e of the ESM). Summary statistics, including mean, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum AUC values across the five splits, were used to evaluate model performance and assess volatility (Fig. 3a, Table 1 of the ESM). The XGBoost model demonstrated the highest stability, combining a high average AUC with the smallest variability, indicating minimal performance fluctuation. Although XGBoost exhibited the least volatility, the Neural Network model showed a similar strong performance, with only a minimal difference in variability. Despite XGBoost’s highest average AUC on the validation sets, the Neural Network achieved the best overall performance across both training and testing datasets, demonstrating excellent accuracy and stability. The final EPICS model incorporates a Neural Network approach, which has demonstrated consistent results and a minimal performance gap making it the most reliable model choice.

SHAP Value Analysis and Diagnostic Standard Construction

Based on SHAP value analysis, we developed a points-based scoring system for EP by quantifying the impact of various clinical and laboratory indicators on model predictions (Table 2). Edema was graded into four levels with scores of 0, 9, 17, and 26 points corresponding to none, < 25%, 25–75%, and > 75% BSA involvement, respectively. Edematous erythema was similarly scored as 0, 5, 11, and 16 points for the same respective levels of skin involvement. Fever was assigned 9 points if present and 0 if absent. Albumin levels reflecting nutritional status received scores of 0, 2, 5, and 7 points for > 35 g/L, 30–35 g/L, 25–30 g/L, and < 25 g/L, respectively. The NLR was categorized into two groups with 0 points for ≤ 4 and 8 points for > 4, indicating increased systemic inflammation in the latter. Serum calcium levels were scored as 0 points for normal levels (≤ 2.20 mmol/L) and 4 points for hypercalcemia (> 2.20 mmol/L). Exudation was scored 0 points for < 10 × 10⁹/L and higher points (2, 5, or 7) for more severe levels reflecting acute inflammation. Pruritus was assigned 0, 1, 3, and 4 points for absent, mild, moderate, and severe itching, respectively. Superficial lymphadenopathy was scored 0, 4, 7, and 11 points based on severity. The Charlson Comorbidity Index was grouped as 0, 3, 5, and 8 points for no, mild, moderate, and severe comorbidities, respectively. The total score was calculated by summing all indicator scores, with a maximum of approximately 100 points, reflecting the relative importance of each feature determined by SHAP values and validated through a ROC curve analysis. The ROC analysis identified a score of 33.5 as the optimal cut-off, maximizing the Youden index and providing the best balance between sensitivity and specificity for distinguishing between mild and moderate-to-severe EP (Fig. 4c). This threshold was adopted as the cut-off for defining disease severity, with higher scores indicating more severe disease. We have named this classification standard as the Erythrodermic Psoriasis Integrated Classification System (EPICS), and it is currently available as a web-based system (https://pumch-dermatology.shinyapps.io/classification/). The resulting EPICS provides a comprehensive, easy-to-use, and reproducible tool for clinicians, integrating clinical and laboratory parameters into a single score that enhances severity classification and supports timely therapeutic decision making. Benchmark Analysis

The benchmark analysis showed that EPICS outperformed 2018-EPSA in predicting the need for systemic therapy. The age- and sex-adjusted ROC curve for EPICS resulted in an AUC of 0.635, while 2018-EPSA had an AUC of 0.581, indicating that EPICS is more effective in discriminating the need for systemic therapy (Fig. 4a). Regarding hospital LOS, EPICS was significantly associated with longer hospitalization (median 26 days for moderate-to-severe cases vs 20 days for mild cases, p = 0.009), while 2018-EPSA did not show a significant association with LOS (p = 0.11) [Fig. 4b]. These findings suggest that EPICS offers more clinically relevant information, particularly for guiding treatment decisions and hospital resource management.

Discussion

This study presents the development and validation of the EPICS, an artificial intelligence-driven model designed to classify the severity of EP using a combination of clinical and laboratory indicators. By leveraging machine learning techniques on a substantial dataset of 242 patients collected over 17 years at Peking Union Medical College Hospital, we identified key predictors of disease severity and constructed a diagnostic standard that demonstrates high classification accuracy and clinical relevance.

Our findings underscore the efficacy of the ANN model, which we have named EPICS, in predicting EP severity, with excellent performance as demonstrated by its high AUC scores in both training (AUC = 1) and testing sets (AUC = 0.998). The ANN model outperformed other machine learning models, including Random Forest, XGBoost, logistic regression, Bayesian methods, and K-Nearest Neighbors, particularly in terms of generalizability and stability across datasets. This superior performance emphasizes the ANN’s ability to capture complex non-linear relationships inherent in clinical data, making it a valuable tool for disease severity classification.

The use of SHAP values enhanced the interpretability of our model by quantifying the contribution of each predictor to the severity classification of EP. We identified ten key clinical and laboratory indicators as the most influential factors determining EP severity: edema, edematous erythema, fever, albumin levels, NLR, serum calcium levels, exudation, pruritus severity, superficial lymphadenopathy, and the Charlson Comorbidity Index. These indicators collectively capture the complex systemic inflammation, skin involvement, nutritional status, symptom burden, and overall comorbidity influencing disease severity.

From a clinical perspective, these predictors are highly relevant to the pathophysiology and clinical presentation of EP. Edema and edematous erythema are direct manifestations of severe inflammation and vascular permeability in patients with EP, indicating extensive skin involvement and systemic inflammatory response [18, 19]. Fever reflects systemic involvement and is often associated with severe cases, signifying a heightened inflammatory state [10]. Hypoalbuminemia is a common finding in severe dermatological conditions like EP due to protein loss through the damaged skin barrier and increased metabolic demands, reflecting both malnutrition and systemic inflammation [20–22]. The NLR is a readily available marker of systemic inflammation and immune dysfunction; elevated NLR has been associated with increased disease severity and poor prognosis in psoriasis [23, 24]. Altered calcium levels may result from extensive skin turnover and impaired metabolism in EP, affecting keratinocyte proliferation and differentiation [25]. An elevated white blood cell count indicates an active inflammatory process and potential infection risk, correlating with disease severity [26].

Our study’s results are in line with previous research that has identified similar predictors of EP severity. For instance, Deng et al. emphasized the significance of inflammatory markers and immune response in assessing psoriasis severity, which aligns with our identification of markers like the NLR and white blood cell count as key predictors [27]. Tanojo et al. highlighted the utility of NLR in predicting the severity of erythema-related conditions, supporting our findings on its classification value in EP severity [28]. Similarly, Zhou et al. explored the association between nutritional markers, such as albumin levels, and psoriasis severity, reinforcing the relevance of albumin in our model [20]. Moreover, Wang et al. discussed the impact of calcium levels on immune response and disease severity in dermatological conditions, which corroborates our finding of calcium levels being an influential predictor in EP severity [24]. The consistency of our key predictors with existing literature validates our approach and supports the applicability of our findings in broader clinical settings.

Comparatively, previous studies on EP severity have been limited by smaller sample sizes and less comprehensive analytical approaches. Traditional assessment methods, such as the Psoriasis Area and Severity Index and BSA, often rely on subjective clinical evaluations, can be time consuming, and exhibit considerable inter-observer variability [29]. While the Psoriasis Area and Severity Index combines lesion severity and affected area into a single score, and BSA quantifies the extent of skin involvement, both methods may not fully capture the systemic manifestations of EP, a severe and generalized form of psoriasis [30, 31]. Moreover, prior research often lacked advanced interpretability methods like SHAP values. Our study advances the field by employing the EPICS model, a large-scale data-driven tool that not only predicts disease severity with high accuracy but also offers insights into the underlying factors contributing to severity. Despite EP being a rare subtype of psoriasis, assembling a comparatively large cohort is a significant achievement, allowing us to provide robust predictions and a deeper understanding of EP. The benchmark analysis revealed that EPICS outperforms the 2018-EPSA model in predicting both the need for systemic therapy and the length of hospital stay. Specifically, EPICS achieved a significantly higher AUC in predicting systemic therapy requirements (0.635 for EPICS vs 0.581 for 2018-EPSA) and showed stronger capability in identifying prolonged hospitalizations (p = 0.005 for EPICS vs p = 0.11 for 2018-EPSA). These findings underscore EPICS’s superior predictive performance and its clinical relevance in guiding resource allocation and treatment planning. Additionally, the use of SHAP values for model interpretability, as introduced by Lundberg, enables a transparent understanding of each predictor’s contribution, aligning with the growing emphasis on personalized medicine [32]. This approach facilitates the tailoring of treatment strategies based on individual patient profiles, enhancing clinical decision making and addressing the limitations of previous methods.

Clinically, the EPICS model has significant implications. By providing a quantifiable and objective method for assessing disease severity, it enables clinicians to make more informed decisions regarding treatment strategies. Patients classified as moderate to severe (cumulative score >33.5) may benefit from more aggressive interventions and closer monitoring, potentially improving outcomes and reducing hospital stays. The web-based availability of the model enhances its accessibility and ease of integration into clinical workflows, facilitating its adoption in various healthcare settings.

The strengths of this study include its substantial sample size, the extensive period over which data were collected, and the rigorous analytical methods employed. Given the rarity of EP, with an estimated prevalence of less than 2% among all psoriasis cases, the sample size is commendable. The use of advanced machine learning techniques and the application of SHAP values for model interpretation represent methodological advancements in the study of dermatological diseases.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. The retrospective and single-center nature of the study may limit the generalizability of the findings. The patient population, drawn from a tertiary hospital, may not reflect the broader population with EP, particularly in different geographic regions or healthcare settings. Additionally, while the model demonstrated high accuracy, external validation in diverse cohorts is necessary to confirm its applicability. Furthermore, although the EPICS score is linked to disease severity, which is known to influence clinical outcomes such as hospitalization duration, we were unable to capture mortality data or predict long-term clinical outcomes because of the retrospective design and lack of a systematic post-discharge follow-up. This limits our ability to assess the model’s potential for predicting outcomes such as mortality or the duration of hospital stay beyond the index admission. Future studies incorporating post-discharge follow-up and mortality data would be valuable for enhancing the clinical utility of the EPICS model.

Future research should focus on prospective studies to validate the EPICS model in different populations and clinical environments. Investigating the integration of additional variables, such as genetic markers or detailed immunological profiles, could further enhance the model’s classification capabilities. Moreover, exploring the model’s utility in guiding treatment decisions and its impact on patient outcomes would provide valuable insights into its clinical effectiveness.

Conclusions

The EPICS model converts ten routine admission variables into an auto-generated risk score that flags more severe EP and supports prompt systemic or biologic therapy. A single calculation at admission standardizes severity assessment and guides initial management, potentially shortening hospital stays. Embedding EPICS in the electronic record thus offers a practical decision aid for this rare high-risk subtype.
