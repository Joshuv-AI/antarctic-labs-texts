Toward Explainable Carcinogenicity Prediction: An Integrated Cheminformatics Approach and Consensus Framework for Possibly Carcinogenic Chemicals
Authors: Huynh Anh Duy, Tarapong Srisongkram
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
A carcinogenicity assessment of possibly carcinogenic chemicals (International Agency for Research on Cancer: IARC class 2B) was conducted using a consensus framework constructed from three complementary machine learning models: BiLSTM with MACCS fingerprints, LightGBM with RDKit descriptors, and Random Forest (RF) with E-state features. These models were developed and rigorously evaluated on benchmark carcinogenicity data sets, with LightGBM emerging as the top performer (accuracy = 0.800, MCC = 0.615, AUROC = 0.882, sensitivity = 0.739, specificity = 0.857). Consistent and competitive performance was also observed for RF and BiLSTM, affirming the reliability of individual predictions. Notably, LightGBM maintained strong generalization ability on independent human carcinogen test sets from IARC and IRIS (accuracy = 0.753, MCC = 0.535, AUROC = 0.842). For the ISSCAN internal test set, the top three models achieved MCC values ranging from 0.564 to 0.615, with AUROC scores between 0.858 and 0.882. For the human carcinogen test set, the top three models attained MCC values from 0.335 to 0.535 and AUROC scores ranging from 0.785 to 0.842. The consensus model was subsequently applied to 47 within-domain compounds from the 2B category, classifying them into 16 potential carcinogens, 8 presumed noncarcinogens, and 23 cases with inconclusive results. To uncover structural correlates, a SHAP-based interpretation of the BiLSTM model was performed, revealing discriminative molecular features including MACCS fingerprint keys and core Bemis–Murcko scaffolds associated with predicted carcinogenicity. To support practical applications, a freely accessible web server for carcinogenicity assessment has been developed and is available at https://carcinogenicity-predictor.streamlit.app.

Introduction

Cancer has escalated into a significant public health challenge, currently recognized as the second major cause of death and disability worldwide. In 2024, there were 20 million new cases and more than 9.7 million deaths due to cancer, with forecasts suggesting an ongoing rise in cases worldwide. The increase in cancer incidence and mortality reflects a variety of factors linked to socioeconomic conditions, lifestyle habits, hygiene practices, and exposure to both occupational settings and everyday life. Carcinogens are one of the factors that induce tumors by indirectly interacting with DNA or disrupting cellular metabolic activities. In the pharmaceutical industry, many synthetic chemicals are manufactured to meet demand. During this process, the chemical properties of the molecules can be altered, leading to the formation of carcinogens. As a result, carcinogenicity assessment of these residues or novel compounds is critical. Regulatory agencies such as the International Council for Harmonisation of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceuticals for Human Use (ICH), Organisation for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD), and European Chemicals Agency (ECHA) also mandate the assessment of mutagenicity and carcinogenicity for manufactured products or byproducts. However, traditional carcinogenicity assessments require long-term animal testing, typically spanning at least two years, which is both time-consuming and costly. Consequently, there is a need for alternative methods that are faster, more cost-effective, and involve fewer animals in carcinogenicity assessment protocols.

Quantitative structure–activity/toxicity relationship (QSAR/QSTR) is a gold standard new alternative method (NAM) used to evaluate data gaps in the chemical risk assessment. , This method relies on a statistically validated relationship between chemical structure attributes and their activities. The chemical attributes can be varied from a chemical representation, such as a Simplified Molecular-Input Line Entry System (SMILES), to molecular descriptors or molecular fingerprints. The mathematical algorithm of the QSAR method can also be varied from a simple multiple linear regression to sophisticated machine learning (ML) , and deep learning (DL) techniques. , Previous studies have constructed deep learning models (i.e., capsule networks and attention mechanisms) for carcinogenic assessments, which have received excellent performance on external validation sets. − This prompted us to further experiment with the potential of other ML and DL models to improve the carcinogenicity assessments.

Additionally, the “possibly carcinogenic to humans” substances classified by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) under Group 2B are particularly concerning. These substances are not conclusively proven as carcinogens in humans due to limited evidence from human studies and insufficient evidence in animal experiments. , The carcinogenicity assessment focusing on IARC class 2B is critical as it represents a gap area where the potential carcinogens may exist and are not yet fully understood. Examples of IARC class 2B substances are aspartame, griseofulvin, or digoxin, which are being used in daily life. Therefore, assessing their safety potential can reduce risks associated with exposure to these agents in both medical and industrial environments.

Predictive computational models for chemical toxicity are increasingly recognized as essential for regulatory decision-making and reducing animal testing. Their adoption, however, requires transparent, comprehensive, and methodologically rigorous documentation to ensure both scientific credibility and practical applicability. To this end, the OECD has established five fundamental principles that predictive models must meet to achieve scientific validity and regulatory acceptance. Specifically, (i) the model should have a clearly defined end point directly relevant to human health or environmental safety; (ii) there must be a scientifically sound rationale underlying the relationship between chemical structure and the predicted activity; (iii) the applicability domain should be explicitly characterized, thereby delineating the chemical space within which reliable predictions can be made; (iv) predictive performance must be demonstrated through appropriate statistical evaluations; and (v) the model should be transparent and interpretable, ensuring that predictions can be critically assessed.

In this study, a bidirectional long short-term memory (BiLSTM), a light gradient-boosting machine (LightGBM), and a Random Forest (RF) were experimented with the carcinogenicity data for constructing effective QSAR models. These three models were selected based on the distinct learning mechanisms, which effectively capture structure–toxicity relationships, as demonstrated in our previous studies. − The carcinogenic features tested include molecular fingerprints, physicochemical properties, and SMILES notation. Only the molecular fingerprints and physicochemical properties are human-crafted features, whereas SMILES notation is a chemical notation language, an end-to-end feature that requires no human intervention for feature selection and is suitable for both individual, mixtures, and salt forms of chemicals. Following the development of multiple QSAR models, the most robust performers were integrated into a novel consensus framework designed to reliably identify carcinogenic compounds. This framework was subsequently applied to external validation data, with a particular focus on assessing the carcinogenic potential of compounds classified as possibly carcinogenic to humans (IARC class 2B). To gain structural correlates into the molecular basis of these predictions, an in-depth cheminformatics analysis was performed, focusing on the identification of key structural scaffolds and substructural fingerprints that may drive carcinogenic potential. Experimental Methods

Carcinogenic Data Preparation

The curated carcinogenic data set was primarily acquired from the previous study, before data preprocessing. The detailed descriptions are as follows:1.The Carcinogenic Potency Database (CPDB) is a standardized resource that contains the results of chronic, long-term animal cancer tests conducted since the 1950s. These compounds are primarily based on experiments in mice, rats, and hamsters. 2.The Chemical Carcinogenesis Research Information System (CCRIS) database is a chemical record with carcinogenicity, mutagenicity, tumor promotion, and tumor inhibition test results (https://datadiscovery.nlm.nih.gov/Chemicals-and-Drugs/Chemical-Carcinogenesis-Research-Information-Syste/uvv3-fqwr/about_data).3.The Istituto Superiore di Sanità Carcinogenicity and Mutation (ISSCAN) database is a long-term carcinogenicity bioassay results on rodents (rat and mouse) database provided by the Istituto Superiore di Sanità Institute. After retrieving the curated combination version of these three databases from the previous study, the missing data of canonical SMILES and toxicity end points were then checked. The canonical SMILES were checked using the RDKit Python package (https://rdkit.org/). The duplicate entries were screened. After that, the redundant molecules compared to the external test sets described in Section were removed from the training data, as they were used for external validation. The final data was 1599 compounds (see Supporting Information), comprising 872 noncarcinogens and 727 carcinogens, and used as a training data set.

This study consists of two external test sets, which were used to assess the model’s generalization and translation of the prediction to identify human carcinogens. The two external test sets were described as follows:1.ISSCAN: Forty molecules from the ISSCAN database were selected as the primary test set to evaluate the model’s performance. This test set was obtained from the previous studies , and was curated by checking the inorganics, mixtures, and duplicate molecules. The redundant molecules between this data set and the training set were removed from the training set. The IRIS data set was critically evaluated and reviewed by the United States Environmental Protection Agency (EPA). Possibly Carcinogenic Chemicals

The possibly carcinogenic chemicals were obtained from group 2B (possibly carcinogenic as they are insufficient evidence of human carcinogenicity) of the IARC database. The canonical SMILES were retrieved from the PubChem database. The IARC class 2B set was further preprocessed by removing missing data and duplicating entries. The redundant molecules of this carcinogenic assessment set compared to the training set were removed as they have their animal testing results from the training set.

We provide a detailed clarification regarding the entry “Kava extract”. In the original data set, this entry was annotated as “Kava extract” but was accompanied by complete SMILES information. Further verification using the PubChem database confirmed that this entry corresponds to a well-defined chemical entity, annotated either as “Kava” or by its systematic name, 2-[(E)-2-cyclohexa-1,5-dien-1-ylethenyl]-4-methoxy-2,3-dihydropyran-6-one. This compound belongs to the kavalactone family, a group of phytochemicals found in the Kava plant (Piper methysticum). Importantly, this record does not represent an undefined extract or crude mixture; rather, the apparent ambiguity arises solely from the data set annotation. Therefore, we retained this record for subsequent analyses.

Furthermore, the record “Carbon black”, represented by the SMILES notation “[C]”, constitutes an ambiguous and structurally undefined input. Owing to the lack of meaningful molecular descriptors, it was excluded from the data set to ensure the reliability of ML-based predictions. As a results, only 104 candidate compounds remain to assess their carcinogenic assessment.

Molecular Representations

In this study, we utilized six molecular representations, including four chemical fingerprints (ECFP, MACCS, RDKit, E-state), SMILES tokenization (abbreviated as Tokens) and physicochemical descriptors (abbreviated as Phychem). These features were used as inputs for predictive modeling.

ECFP, also known as circular fingerprints, were calculated using the Morgan algorithm with a radius of 10 and a bit vector length of 4096. MACCS fingerprints, consisting of 167 predefined structural keys, were generated to represent the presence or absence of specific substructures within molecules. RDKit fingerprints were computed as 2048-bit path-based binary vectors that encode linear molecular fragments and capture overall topological connectivity. E-state fingerprints, computed using RDKit package, encode atom-level electronic and topological features into a 79-dimensional continuous vector that reflects molecular reactivity and local atomic environments.

For the SMILES tokenization, a unique vocabulary is contrasted from all characters present in the SMILES strings. Each character is assigned a unique integer index, with a special index reserved for padding purposes. After that, each SMILES string is converted into a sequence of integers based on the constructed vocabulary. Sequences are either truncated or padded to a fixed 100 maximum length characters to ensure uniformity across inputs.

A set of 12 physicochemical descriptors was computed using RDKit package to capture fundamental molecular properties. These included molecular weight (MolWt), Log P, number of hydrogen bond donors (NumHDonors), number of hydrogen bond acceptors (NumHAcceptors), topological polar surface area (TPSA), number of rotatable bonds (NumRotatableBonds), number of aromatic rings (NumAromaticRings), number of saturated rings (NumSaturatedRings), number of hetero atoms (NumHeteroatoms), number of rings for a molecule (RingCount), number of heavy atom (HeavyAtomCount), and number of aliphatic rings (NumAliphaticRings).

BiLSTM Classification of Carcinogenic Potential

A bidirectional long short-term memory (BiLSTM) neural network was implemented in Python using Keras 3.4.1, and Tensorflow 2.17.0 as a backend. The model architecture consisted of two stacked bidirectional LSTM layers, with 64 and 32 hidden units, respectively. To mitigate overfitting, dropout layers with a rate of 0.3 were applied after each BiLSTM block. The BiLSTM outputs were followed by a dense layer of 100 neurons with rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation, and a final output layer with a single neuron using the sigmoid activation function for binary classification.

The input features were reshaped into a three-dimensional format suitable for recurrent layers, with each compound represented as a one-step time series of molecular descriptors. The model was compiled with a binary cross-entropy loss function. A method for stochastic optimization (ADAM) was used as an optimizer, with a fixed learning rate of 0.001. The training data were further split into training and validation sets in an 80:20 ratio. Model training was performed for 20 epochs with a batch size of 32. To ensure reproducibility, all experiments were conducted using a fixed random seed (42) across NumPy, Python, and TensorFlow environments. After training, the model was evaluated on the external test set.

RF Classification of Carcinogenic Potential

A RF classifier was employed to predict the carcinogenic potential of chemical compounds based on molecular descriptors. The model was implemented using the RandomForestClassifier from the scikit-learn library, with 100 decision trees (n_estimators = 100) and default hyperparameters unless otherwise specified. The data set was randomly partitioned into training and validation subsets using an 80:20 split. A fixed random seed (random_state = 42) was applied to ensure reproducibility. Model performance was evaluated on the external test set.

LightGBM Classification of Carcinogenic Potential

In parallel, a gradient boosting classifier based on the LightGBM framework was used to assess the carcinogenic potential of compounds. The LGBMClassifier from the lightgbm library was initialized with 100 estimators and default hyperparameters. Input features were preprocessed as NumPy arrays to ensure compatibility with the model. The same 80:20 training–validation split and random seed (random_state = 42) were applied for consistency. Performance evaluation was conducted on the external test set following the same protocol as for the RF classifier.

Performance Evaluation Metrics

In this study, we employed a suite of complementary performance metrics that capture diverse aspects of classification behavior. Accuracy (eq ) was used as a baseline metric, representing the overall proportion of correctly classified instances across both classes. We also included the Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) (eq ), which provides a balanced assessment by incorporating all four components of the confusion matrix: true positives (TP), true negatives (TN), false positives (FP), and false negatives (FN).

To further dissect model behavior, we reported both sensitivity (eq ), which reflects the true positive rate, and specificity (eq ), which indicates the true negative rate. These metrics offer insights into the model’s ability to correctly identify active and inactive compounds, respectively.

Finally, we included the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) as a threshold-independent metric. Rather than measuring classification accuracy at a fixed cutoff, AUROC reflects the model’s ability to rank positive instances above negative ones across all possible decision thresholds, effectively summarizing the trade-off between sensitivity and 1-specificity. 1 accuracy=TP+TNTP+TN+FP+FN 2 MCC=(TP×TN)−(FP×FN)(TP+FP)(TP+FN)(TN+FP)(TN+FN) 3 sensitivity=TPTP+FN 4 specificity=TNTN+FP

Model Evaluation Via Train, Test Split and Cross-Validation

Model validation was performed on the full original training data set using both a fixed training/test split and stratified 5-fold cross-validation (5-fold CV), focusing on the three top-performing models identified in our study: LightGBM_RDKit, RF_E-state, and BiLSTM_MACCS.

First, a single stratified hold-out split was applied to the full original training data set, with 80% used for training and 20% for test. Stratification ensured that the class distribution was preserved across both partitions. This split was kept fixed for all experiments to guarantee fair comparison among models. Each model was trained with the same set of hyperparameters as in prior experiments to ensure consistency. To account for stochasticity in training, each model was trained independently three times with different random seeds. The seeds affected model initialization and batch shuffling, but the data split remained unchanged. Reported metrics of accuracy, MCC, AUROC, sensitivity, and specificity were recorded as the mean ± SD for the training and test partitions, while prediction probabilities (y predicted) were averaged over three runs and retained to construct the corresponding confusion matrices for each partition.

In addition, a 5-fold CV was conducted on the full original training data set. Stratification again preserved class ratios within each fold. We employed StratifiedKFold (n_splits = 5, shuffle = True) to ensure reproducibility. In each round, a model was trained on four folds (training portion) and evaluated on the remaining fold (test portion). To enhance model diversity, a distinct random seed was assigned to each fold. This ensured variability in model initialization and optimization across folds while preserving identical data partitions. Each sample served as the test instance exactly once. Performance metrics were recorded as the mean ± SD across all five test portions. For reporting prediction probabilities (y predicted) in cross-validation, we highlight the best-performing fold, selected based on the highest MCC, with AUROC and accuracy serving as secondary criteria in the event of a tie. The corresponding y predicted outputs were subsequently used to construct the associated confusion matrices.

Applicability Domain Analysis

To ensure the reliability of model predictions and to identify regions of trustworthy extrapolation, we employed an Applicability Domain (AD) analysis based on a k-nearest neighbors (k-NN) distance-based approach in the chemical descriptor space. , The Euclidean distance between each test sample and the training data was computed using molecular fingerprints, followed by classification of test samples into within-domain and out-of-domain categories. The AD of the model can be expressed using the following mathematical conditions (eqs and ).

For compounds considered within-domain: 5 Di≤Dk+Z·σ

For compounds judged as out-of-domain: 6 Di>Dk+Z·σ

Here, D i denotes the Euclidean distance between the candidate compound and its nearest k training samples. The term D k is the mean of such distances computed across the training set, while σ is their standard deviation. The parameter Z is an empirically selected scaling factor to control the sensitivity of the AD boundary; in this study, Z was fixed at 0.5.

To rigorously assess domain applicability, we independently conducted AD analysis for each model using distinct molecular descriptor sets, specially RDKit for LightGBM, E-state for RF, and MACCS keys for BiLSTM. The evaluation was performed across a range of k-values from 2 to 10 to define the optimal neighborhood size in the k-nearest neighbors (k-NN) space. For each model, only the test compounds classified as with-in domain were retained for further analysis. The optimal k-value was selected based on a dual criterion: maximizing performance improvement across key metrics (accuracy, MCC, AUROC, sensitivity, and specificity) while simultaneously minimizing the proportion of out-of-domain samples, thereby balancing predictive robustness with domain coverage.

Activity Cliff Analysis

Activity cliff networks for the ISSCAN data set and the Human carcinogen data set were constructed using three molecular fingerprint representations: RDKit, E-state, and MACCS. The pairwise structural similarity between molecules was quantified using the Tanimoto coefficient, defined as T(A,B)=|A∩B||A∪B| where |A∩B| represents the number of common features (bits) present in both fingerprints A and B, and |A∪B| represents the total number of features present in either A or B. Molecular pairs with Tanimoto similarity greater than or equal to a threshold (τ = 0.85) were considered high-similarity pairs. A pair was classified as an activity cliff if the two molecules had different activity labels (carcinogen vs noncarcinogen).

Deployment of Carcinogenicity Predictor Based on Web Server Tool

To enable real-time and user-friendly access to our carcinogenicity prediction models, we developed an interactive web platform based on the Streamlit framework (v1.46.0) in Python 3.10. The application supports both single-molecule and batch-mode predictions, accepting input as either individual SMILES strings or CSV files. All molecular structures were first standardized via canonicalization. Subsequently, a comprehensive set of molecular descriptors was computed using the RDKit cheminformatics toolkit. Predictions were obtained through a consensus-based approach that aggregated the outputs of three top-performing models. These model pipelines were serialized in .keras or .pkl formats.

The web server was hosted on Streamlit Cloud and synchronized with a public GitHub repository for transparent and reproducible deployment. Prediction results included both binary classifications (Possible carcinogen/Noncarcinogen) and corresponding confidence probabilities. All computations were executed within a containerized environment, ensuring session-specific processing without external data transmission or storage. This architecture ensured secure handling of user-submitted chemical data while promoting broad accessibility and scientific reproducibility.

Bemis–Murcko Scaffold Decomposition

To systematically characterize the core structural frameworks of the compounds, Bemis–Murcko scaffolds were extracted from their SMILES representations using the RDKit cheminformatics toolkit (version 2023.9.6). For each molecule, the scaffold was generated via the GetScaffoldForMol function from the MurckoScaffold module, which retains all ring systems and the linkers connecting them, while removing side chains and terminal substituents. , These resulting scaffolds reflect the central molecular architectures commonly associated with biological activity. Scaffold extraction enabled consistent comparisons across structurally diverse compounds and facilitated the identification of recurring cores potentially linked to carcinogenicity. The chemical structures and corresponding names of the extracted scaffolds were retrieved from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/).

Cheminformatics Profiling Employing BiLSTM-Based SHAP Analysis

To elucidate the influence of specific molecular features on the BiLSTM model’s carcinogenicity predictions, we employed SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis, a model-agnostic, game-theoretic approach that delivers consistent and locally faithful estimates of feature importance. In this study, SHAP values were derived using the KernelExplainer on the training data, with MACCS structural keys and Bemis–Murcko scaffolds serving as input descriptors to capture both substructural elements and core molecular frameworks. This integrative strategy enabled a detailed interpretation of the chemical patterns most strongly driving the model’s classification decisions between carcinogenic and noncarcinogenic compounds. Results

Performance of Predictive Models on the ISSCAN External Test Set

In this study, six chemical representations (ECFP, E-state, MACCS, Phychem, RDKit, and Tokens) and three ML architectures (BiLSTM, LightGBM, and RF) were employed to construct a total of 18 predictive models. Their performance was evaluated using five metrics, as illustrated in Figure . Activity Cliff Network Analysis across Fingerprint Types

We constructed activity cliff networks for the ISSCAN and human carcinogen data sets using RDKit, E-state, and MACCS fingerprints at a Tanimoto similarity threshold of τ = 0.85. In the ISSCAN data set, none of the three fingerprints, RDKit, E-state, or MACCS, identified any activity cliffs. This absence suggests a well-behaved and consistent activity landscape, in which structurally similar compounds tend to exhibit similar biological responses. Such a profile is highly favorable for machine learning, as it supports the core assumption underlying most predictive models in cheminformatics.

In contrast, the human carcinogen data set revealed a more complex activity landscape. Among seven highly similar molecular pairs, three were identified as activity cliffs–all exclusively detected by the E-state fingerprint. Neither RDKit nor MACCS detected any cliffs under the same threshold, suggesting a more stable structure–activity relationship. While E-state may capture subtle structural variations that lead to divergent activities, this sensitivity also exposes regions of the chemical space where predictive modeling is likely to struggle. In comparison, the consistency observed with RDKit and MACCS is likely to support more reliable model performance.

Web-Server Tool for Carcinogen Predictor Based on a Consensus Framework

Subsequently, we employed this consensus framework, combining the three best-performing models (i.e., LightGBM_RDKit, BiLSTM_MACCS, and RF_E-state), to deploy a web-based tool for carcinogenicity prediction. A publicly accessible web interface was developed to allow real-time prediction of carcinogenic potential directly from SMILES input. The platform is available at https://carcinogenicity-predictor.streamlit.app/ and supports both single-compound and batch-mode analysis with an emphasis on usability and data confidentiality. Each SMILES string, after preprocessing, was transformed into three distinct molecular fingerprint representations: MACCS keys, RDKit fingerprints, and E-state descriptors. These representations served as input features for three independently trained models: a BiLSTM neural network (MACCS), a LightGBM model (RDKit), and a RF classifier (E-state). For each compound, the final carcinogenicity prediction was derived by averaging the output probabilities of the three models. A compound was classified as a possible carcinogen if the mean probability exceeded 0.5; otherwise, it was categorized as noncarcinogenic.

In single-input mode, users can submit an individual SMILES string to obtain a binary classification alongside a confidence probability score. In batch-processing mode, a comma separate value (CSV) file containing multiple SMILES entries can be uploaded to perform high-throughput screening. The prediction results are rendered dynamically within the interface and are also available for download in a structured tabular format for further analysis. Lasiocarpine (No. 1 in Table ) emerged as the top-ranked compound in our consensus prediction, exhibiting both the highest mean predicted probability and unanimous agreement across all individual models in its classification as a carcinogen. This naturally occurring pyrrolizidine alkaloid (PA) is well-documented for its potent hepatotoxicity. In previous research, lasiocarpine has been reported to induce liver and skin tumors in rat models. Recent toxicological data indicate that, among structurally related PAs, lasiocarpine exhibits the highest level of toxicity, as evidenced by its lowest benchmark dose lower confidence limit for a 10% response (BMDL10) value of 0.07 mg/kg bw/day. Noted that the BMDL10 is a toxicology and risk assessment statistical value representing the lower 95% confidence limit of a benchmark dose (BMD) associated with a 10% increase in a specific adverse effect. This finding highlights lasiocarpine as the most hazardous PA among those with available tumorigenicity data. In general, pyrrolizidine alkaloids are well recognized for their toxic potential and their capacity to pose significant risks to both human and animal health, particularly when consumed chronically at high levels. Notably, human health concerns associated with dietary exposure to PAs, especially through contaminated honey, tea, herbal infusions, and food supplements, have been increasingly documented and emphasized in recent risk assessments.

Subsequently, 2-Amino-3-methyl-9H-pyrido­[2,3-b]­indole (No. 3 in Table ), a representative heterocyclic aromatic amine (HAA), was identified as one of the carcinogenic compounds predicted by our model. HAAs are a class of chemical compounds formed during the cooking of meat at high temperatures, such as grilling, broiling, pan-frying, or barbecuing. Several epidemiological studies have reported a positive association between the consumption of well-done or high-temperature cooked meat and the risk of cancers, particularly colorectal cancer. − However, conflicting findings have also been observed, with some studies reporting no significant association. A major limitation contributing to this inconsistency is the difficulty in accurately assessing dietary HAA exposure. Due to the role of metabolic activation in HAA induced carcinogenesis, recent studies have examined genetically susceptible individuals, particularly those with high activity CYP1A2 and/or N-acetyltransferase 2 (NAT2) phenotypes. While no interaction between red meat intake and NAT2 genotype was found in Whites, a significant gene diet interaction was observed in Japanese and African Americans, populations with higher colorectal cancer rates and a greater prevalence of the rapid NAT2 phenotype. In these groups, red meat consumption was most strongly linked to cancer risk among rapid acetylators, less so in intermediates, and not significant in slow acetylators. In light of emerging evidence on the carcinogenic potential of HAAs in certain population groups combined with the warnings of HAA-related compounds by our model, these findings may help raise awareness of HAAs as possible dietary risk factors. These findings may serve as a helpful reference for public health efforts, supporting early recognition of potential dietary risks associated with HAAs.

Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) represent a class of organic compounds characterized by the presence of two or more fused aromatic rings, typically originating from benzene structures. Recognized among the earliest identified environmental carcinogens, many PAHs exhibit strong carcinogenic properties, either directly or following metabolic activation. Due to their widespread occurrence and persistence, PAHs pose significant public health concerns, having been associated with a range of adverse effects, including carcinogenesis as well as cardiovascular, pulmonary, and reproductive toxicity. , Notably, several PAHs function as procarcinogens, requiring enzymatic biotransformation within the body to yield active carcinogenic metabolites. A recent meta-analysis evaluating the carcinogenic effects of PAHs reported a 10.75% increased overall cancer risk and an 8.40% higher incidence of breast cancer in individuals exposed to PAHs compared to nonexposed groups. These findings further emphasize the importance of raising awareness about the potential health risks associated with PAH exposure. Specifically, benz­[j]­aceanthrylene (No. 4 in Table ) and benzo­[c]­phenanthrene (No. 12 in Table ), two representative PAHs, were identified by our framework as carcinogenic compounds.

PAHs impact various biological systems, from the respiratory to the endocrine, via multiple pathways. These include binding to intracellular targets, altering gene transcription, and interfering with hormonal signaling cascades. Due to their lipophilic nature, PAHs readily permeate cell membranes through passive diffusion, particularly following inhalation. While the parent PAH molecules are not directly genotoxic, they act as procarcinogens; their metabolic derivatives are responsible for initiating carcinogenesis. In hepatic metabolism, PAHs are primarily processed through three enzymatic routes: (1) the cytochrome P450 1A1/1B1 with epoxide hydrolase pathway (CYP/EH), (2) the peroxidase-mediated CYP pathway, and (3) the aldo-keto reductase (AKR) pathway. , These pathways successively convert PAHs into reactive intermediates. Among these, the CYP/EH pathway is the most prominent, in which PAHs undergo oxidation to form PAH oxides that are subsequently converted into dihydrodiols. These intermediates are further enzymatically activated to generate diol epoxides. Both oxides and diol epoxides are capable of forming covalent adducts with DNA, thereby exerting carcinogenic effects. If this DNA damage is not efficiently repaired, it may initiate mutagenic processes that contribute to tumorigenesis. The second metabolic route involves cytochrome P450 enzymes with peroxidase-like activity, which oxidize PAHs to form highly reactive, short-lived radical cations. These electrophilic intermediates readily interact with DNA, thereby inducing mutagenic lesions. In the third pathway, dihydrodiol dehydrogenase (a member of the aldo-keto reductase superfamily) catalyzes the conversion of PAH dihydrodiols to o-quinones. Subsequent redox cycling of these quinones generates reactive oxygen species (ROS), which exacerbate oxidative stress and promote carcinogenesis through oxidative DNA damage. The multifaceted mechanisms through which PAHs exert their carcinogenic effects are summarized in Figure , using benz­[j]­aceanthrylene as an example.

Additionally, our predictive framework identified eight of the 16 compounds with carcinogenic potential as halogenated hydrocarbons, a chemical class historically linked to elevated cancer risks and adverse health effects upon chronic exposure. The aliphatic or olefinic halogenated hydrocarbons identified in this study are characterized by the presence of chlorine and bromine substituents. Within this group, 1,2-dichloroethane (1,2-DCE) (No. 6 in Table ) emerged as the compound with the highest predicted carcinogenic risk. 1,2-DCE is a high-volume industrial solvent primarily used in vinyl chloride production and is frequently detected in both air and water environments. Extensive research has demonstrated its genotoxicity across diverse test systems and exposure routes, with effects mediated through metabolic activation by key enzymes, particularly P450 2E1 (CYP2E1) and glutathione S-transferases (GSTs), leading to DNA adduct formation, gene mutations, and chromosomal aberrations. Furthermore, another industrial solvent, chloroform (No. 15 in Table ), was also identified within the predicted subset of carcinogens. Chloroform is widely used in organic extraction and purification processes and has long been under regulatory scrutiny due to its adverse health effects. The accurate identification of such well-established hazardous compounds underscores both the robustness and the practical relevance of our model, highlighting the continued need for vigilance regarding carcinogenic risks posed by commonly used industrial solvents.

Mounting evidence highlights the profound and long-lasting health consequences of early life exposure to carcinogenic substances, which substantially elevates cancer risk in later stages of life. During childhood and adolescence, the heightened cellular proliferation and ongoing organogenesis render individuals particularly susceptible to the genotoxic effects of carcinogens. Among these, PAHs are a ubiquitous class of well-characterized carcinogens to which young populations are routinely exposed through contaminated food, drinking water, ambient air, and various environmental media. In our model, benz­[j]­aceanthrylene and benzo­[c]­phenanthrene, both members of the PAH family, were confidently identified as carcinogenic compounds with high predictive scores. Furthermore, cumulative risk assessment conducted in 2018 for the occurrence of contaminants in United States drinking water (2010–2017) estimated that over 100,000 lifetime cancer cases could be attributable to chronic exposure to carcinogenic chemicals in tap water. Notably, chloroform, a disinfection byproduct, was identified as one of the primary contributors. Based on risk thresholds defined by the California Office of Environmental Health Hazard Assessment, a chloroform concentration of 0.4 μg/L in drinking water corresponds to a 10–6 lifetime cancer risk. With an estimated exposed population of 203 million individuals, this translates to approximately 8,687 lifetime cancer cases. As corroborated in our model, chloroform was classified as a high-confidence carcinogenic compound, reinforcing its public health relevance. These findings underscore the urgent need to identify and characterize potential carcinogens with high correctness. Scientifically robust and practically applicable predictive tools are essential to support regulatory decision-making aimed at alerting vulnerable populations to carcinogens.

The present study systematically addresses the OECD principles in the context of carcinogenicity prediction. In line with OECD Principle 1, carcinogenic potential is defined as the end point of interest, reflecting the urgent need to address ongoing cancer risks and to provide complementary evidence for compounds that remain controversial within the IARC Group 2B classification. Consistent with OECD Principle 2, the framework leverages ML and DL approaches, which have demonstrated strong capacity in toxicity modeling and in capturing QSAR relationships, thereby providing a scientifically sound rationale for linking chemical structure to predicted toxicity. To satisfy OECD Principle 3, the AD is explicitly delineated using Euclidean distance–based k-nearest neighbor (k-NN) methods, enabling robust confidence assessment of predictions. In accordance with OECD Principle 4, model performance is rigorously evaluated through a comprehensive suite of statistical metrics, including accuracy, MCC, AUROC, sensitivity, and specificity. Finally, addressing OECD Principle 5, cheminformatics profiling is incorporated to probe structural associations between carcinogenicity and chemical features, with particular emphasis on substructures and scaffolds, thereby enhancing transparency and interpretability. As a whole, this alignment with OECD principles underscores the scientific robustness of our predictive framework and highlights its potential regulatory relevance.

The models developed in this study were primarily designed to predict and classify carcinogenicity using molecular descriptors and structural features, showing strong promise as practical tools for compound screening and prioritization. As such, they can help identify and compare chemical compounds, enabling the early elimination of candidates with high carcinogenic potential and indirectly guiding the design of safer analogs. In addition, the cheminformatics analyses presented here provide detailed insights into substructures and scaffolds that distinguish carcinogens from noncarcinogens, offering valuable direction for optimizing molecular design toward safer compounds. Nonetheless, the de novo design of low-toxicity compounds remains a complex challenge that requires multiobjective optimization and experimental validation. As such, our model can be served as a supportive tool within the broader context of drug discovery and safety evaluation. Overall, these findings underscore the potential of integrating predictive modeling with cheminformatics to accelerate the development of safer compounds and improve early stage safety assessment in drug discovery.

Advantanges and Challenges

In this study, a consensus-based framework was constructed by integrating three independently trained models: LightGBM utilizing RDKit molecular fingerprints, RF employing E-state descriptors, and a BiLSTM network operating on MACCS keys. To improve the reliability of carcinogenicity predictions, a probabilistic voting strategy was employed, in which a compound was classified as carcinogenic only when all three models yielded predicted probabilities greater than 0.5. This stringent decision criterion was designed to enhance confidence in the outcomes by requiring agreement across models that capture complementary aspects of molecular structure, including topological connectivity, physicochemical properties, and substructural sequence patterns. By enforcing agreement among all predictive models, the framework effectively reduces the risk of false-positive classifications that may arise from biases or overfitting within any single model. This conservative strategy enhances the overall robustness and interpretability of the predictions, particularly when applied to structurally heterogeneous compounds such as those in the IARC class 2B data set. As a result, the proposed framework serves as a reliable decision-support tool for identifying and prioritizing potential carcinogens with increased predictive certainty.

A limitation of this study arises from the overlap between certain chemicals in the IARC class 2B and 3 classifications and those included in the CPDB training data set. To ensure the integrity of model evaluation and prevent bias due to prior exposure during training, these overlapping compounds were excluded from the IARC-based test set during data preprocessing. As a result, although not present in the final evaluation phase, these chemicals did contribute to model learning through their inclusion in the training set based on animal carcinogenicity data. For example, aspartame was used as a training compound labeled as noncarcinogenic in animals, despite ongoing scientific debate and insufficient evidence regarding its carcinogenicity in humans. The exclusion of such compounds, while necessary for methodological rigor, reduced the number of chemicals available for IARC-based assessment.

Future Perspectives

Although the proposed consensus framework demonstrated promising performance in carcinogenicity prediction, future research could further enhance its predictive capability and generalizability by incorporating additional molecular representations, such as graph-based neural networks or quantum chemical descriptors, given their growing potential to capture intricate structural and electronic features relevant to chemical toxicity. − Furthermore, expanding the training set with well-curated human carcinogenicity data, when available, would also improve relevance to real-world risk assessment. Finally, the application of this approach to broader chemical spaces, such as food additives, environmental contaminants, and pharmaceuticals, could facilitate high-throughput screening and prioritization in regulatory toxicology.

Conclusions

In this study, we developed a robust consensus framework for the prediction of chemical carcinogenicity, with a particular emphasis on compounds classified as possibly carcinogenic to humans (IARC class 2B). Three complementary ML and DL models, including BiLSTM, LightGBM, and RF, were integrated following their training on benchmark data sets. The application of this framework to the IARC 2B compounds enabled meaningful stratification into potential carcinogens, presumed noncarcinogens, and inconclusive cases, offering a data-driven basis for prioritizing further toxicological investigation. Furthermore, SHAP-based interpretability analysis revealed chemically intuitive and mechanistically relevant features, including MACCS substructures and Bemis–Murcko scaffolds, that substantially contributed to model predictions. To facilitate practical adoption and future research, a publicly accessible web server was deployed, providing an interactive platform for carcinogenicity prediction. In summary, these findings underscore the potential of interpretable ML to support early hazard identification and inform regulatory decision-making in chemical safety assessment.
