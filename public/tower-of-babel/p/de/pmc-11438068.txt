The experience of chronic protracted mandibular dislocation treatment: manual vs. surgical reduction
Authors: Jiaojiao Zou, Lin Wang, Kiran Acharya, Xiaoru Hou, Lifeng Li, Yanqiu Li, Xiaoyi Hu, Xiaotao Xing
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Temporomandibular joint dislocation is a common challenge in dental care, but it can be promptly addressed through manual realignment, making it standard procedure in the realm of oral health. Nonetheless, effectively preventing and treating chronic protracted mandibular dislocation (CPMD), characterized by prolonged dislocation, remains a significant challenge. Hence, a retrospective analysis was conducted on the clinical data of 10 patients diagnosed with chronic protracted mandibular dislocation (CPMD), encompassing diagnosis, treatment, and prognosis details. CPMD tends to be more prevalent among the elderly population, with an average age of 67.2±11.9 years and a male-to-female ratio of 1:9. All diagnosed patients presented with bilateral anterior dislocation, each requiring diverse pre-treatment approaches. The most significant risk factor contributing to CPMD was unconsciousness resulting from nervous system injury, which delayed the perception of symptoms associated with temporomandibular joint dislocation. 90% of CPMD patients experienced successful treatment through manual reduction, while a refractory CPMD cohort, resistant to manual reduction, necessitated surgical intervention for resolution. All patients underwent treatment involving elastic intermaxillary traction, which served as the primary means of achieving reduction. Subsequent analysis of CT data revealed that condylar displacement beyond the zygomatic arch served as an indication for surgical reduction. Consequently, manual reduction under general anesthesia combined with elastic intermaxillary traction proved effective for managing CPMD cases. However, cases displaying excessive vertical displacement beyond the zygomatic arch should be considered for surgical intervention based on CT findings.

Introduction

Chronic persistent/protracted/prolonged mandibular dislocation (CPMD) represents a distinctive form of mandibular dislocation characterized by prolonged absence of the condylar head within the glenoid fossa [10]. Among temporomandibular joint (TMJ) dislocations, bilateral anterior dislocation is the most prevalent [9]. This abnormal mandibular positioning manifests through symptoms such as an anterior tragus void, challenges in mouth closure, elongation of facial features, speech impediments, and eating difficulties [9, 10]. These symptoms significantly impact both the appearance and functionality of affected individuals. While fresh dislocations are typically manageable through manual reduction [6], muscle spasms and tissue hyperplasia around the joint, resulting from prolonged duration, complicate the repositioning of the mandible to its normal alignment [2]. Consequently, timely and accurate diagnosis, coupled with an efficient treatment regimen for CPMD, holds immense importance in restoring TMJ function and facilitating the return to a normal life for patients.

The prolonged duration sets CPMD apart from other types of dislocations like acute and chronic recurrent cases, making it challenging due to tissue deterioration and the stubborn nature of the condition [18]. Physicians have diligently sought reasons behind the prolonged persistence of dislocation without effective treatment. The factors contributing to this dilemma include delays caused by concurrent illnesses, instances of missed or incorrect diagnoses, and neglect of the condition [16, 19]. Unfortunately, medical staff often lack a comprehensive list of related risk factors for CPMD, hindering their ability to preempt or reduce its occurrence.

Reports on standardized treatment guidelines for CPMD are notably lacking, resulting in varying therapy methods across different medical institutions. While conventional manual reduction, commonly employed for fresh mandibular dislocations [6], is frequently utilized for CPMD, its efficacy often relies on supplementary measures such as local or general anesthesia, muscle relaxation, and pain management [18]. Additionally, conservative treatments such as intermaxillary traction and occlusal splints are commonly employed to restore TMJ alignment [4, 6, 13]. In extreme cases where these methods fail, surgical reduction becomes necessary as a last-resort traumatic therapy [1, 8, 12]. Arthroscopy may also be utilized to maximize CPMD reduction and restore TMJ function [3]. Despite these options, there remains a need for further exploration to optimize treatment regimens for CPMD. Therefore, establishing a methodological approach, ranging from conventional to surgical interventions based on clinical data, is imperative.

Hence, this study undertook a comprehensive review of clinical data from 10 patients, aiming to summarize and analyze both risk factors and the manual reduction experience concerning CPMD. Notably, a refractory case was detailed to offer a comprehensive understanding of CPMD treatment processes. The overarching goal of this study is to furnish valuable insights and reference points for the prevention and treatment of CPMD within clinical practice.

Materials and methods

The collection of patients’ basic information

This study was conducted under the relevant guidelines and regulations approved by the Clinical Research Center of Shaanxi Province for Dental and Maxillofacial Diseases, College of Stomatology, Xi’an Jiaotong University. Ethical approval for the research was obtained from the ethical committee of the Stomatology Hospital of Xian Jiaotong University. It was confirmed that written and informed consent was obtained from all the subjects and/or their legal guardians before the start of the study.

The study involved a retrospective review and analysis of data from 10 patients diagnosed with CPMD and admitted to the Stomatology Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University between December 2019 and May 2023. Basic patient information, including age, gender, CPMD type, duration of dislocation, concurrent diseases, consciousness status, immediate/delayed symptom perception, prior treatment history, and history of TMJ dislocation, was collected and summarized for analysis.

The regimen and effect of CPMD

After hospitalization, manual reduction without anesthesia was attempted. If successful, intermaxillary elastic traction was applied, and patients were discharged. In cases of failed reduction, routine preoperative preparations were initiated for anesthesia-assisted treatment. 9 patients underwent manual reduction under general anesthesia, while 1 patient, due to contraindications to general anesthesia, received local anesthesia and sedation. For the surgical reduction case, bilateral anterio-auricular crutch incisions were made to expose the dislocated condyle [12] (Fig. 2c, d). Peri-auricular adhesive tissue was then released, and the condyle was maneuvered from the arch to return it to the fossa. In manual reduction procedures (Fig. 1a-e), intraoral and extra-oral methods were utilized to mobilize the dislocated condyle, facilitating its passage over the articular tubercle and posterior return to the glenoid fossa [6] (Fig. 2a, b). The follow-up duration regarding treatment

Following treatment completion, patients undergoing manual reduction were discharged the following day, while those undergoing open reduction were discharged in the absence of any abnormalities. All patients received instructions to maintain intermaxillary traction for three weeks. Subsequently, the intermaxillary traction device was removed, and the TMJ function was restored through opening and closing exercises. During this period, the recommendation was applied to patients to practice small mouth openings to prompt TMJ function recovery, while restricting largemouth openings to prevent recurrence, and to be cautious of avoiding extensive mouth opening in daily life, particularly after the release of MMF. Telephone and outpatient follow-up appointments were scheduled one, three, or six months after discharge.

CT/CBCT analysis of the position of the condyle

From the pool of 10 patients, those with available preoperative CT data were chosen for analysis, resulting in 6 cases: 5 treated with manual reduction and 1 with surgical reduction. Measurements were taken in the coronal plane to determine the vertical distance between the outer pole of the condyle and the upper border plane of the ipsilateral zygomatic arch. Additionally, in the sagittal plane, the horizontal distance between the midpoint of the articular fossa and the midpoint of the ipsilateral condylar head was measured.

Statistical description

Descriptive statistical analysis was employed to analyze the data. Measurement data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (x±s), while composition ratios for each group were calculated using counting data.

Results

Analysis of basic characteristics of CPMD patients

To comprehend the fundamental characteristics of CPMD, data from 10 patients were collected and analyzed. Table 1 presents key information including age, gender, dislocation onset, duration, and prior treatments before hospitalization. The average age of these patients was 67.2±11.9 years, with the oldest being 88 years and the youngest 51 years. The male-to-female ratio was 1:9, indicating a higher prevalence among elderly individuals and a predominance of females. All patients were diagnosed with bilateral anterior dislocation, with varying durations. The average dislocation duration was 2.1±1.5 months, with most patients experiencing dislocation for 1-2 months, and the longest duration being 6 months. Regarding pre-hospitalization treatments, 60% of patients underwent manual reduction followed by anti-inflammatory, intermaxillary traction, and analgesic therapy, suggesting the persistent challenge in effectively managing CPMD. Analysis of the risk factors during the onset of CPMD patients

To identify the risk factors associated with CPMD, a retrospective analysis of concomitant diseases, consciousness status, symptom perception during disease onset, and dislocation history was conducted (see Table 2). The accurate diagnosis of CPMD was confirmed through CT/CBCT examinations. Nervous system injuries, including cerebral infarction, cerebral hemorrhage, and Cranio-cerebral injuries, accounted for approximately 70% of cases, representing the most closely associated factor. The remaining 30% of cases were attributed to denture restoration, Alzheimer’s disease, and painless gastroparesis. Further analysis revealed that patients with these concomitant diseases often exhibited poor consciousness during CPMD onset. The ratio of unconsciousness reached 80%, resulting in only 20% of patients being able to immediately perceive CPMD symptoms. The associated factors of CPMD

Upon further analysis of the dentition conditions, it was found that only 30% of the patients had a complete dentition, while the remaining 70% were partially edentulous, with missing molars in either the maxillary or mandibular regions. Data on temporomandibular joint dislocation history indicated that only 20% of patients had a prior history of dislocation, suggesting no direct relationship between dislocation history and CPDM occurrence. In conclusion, these findings suggest that the primary risk factor for CPMD is unconsciousness, leading to delayed perception of symptoms due to abnormal nervous system function.

Analysis of the regimen and therapeutic effects of CPMD

Patients who failed conventional manual reduction underwent alternative reduction regimens under appropriate anesthesia in the hospital. Data indicated that 90% of patients underwent reduction with general anesthesia, while 1 patient received local anesthesia due to contraindications for general anesthesia (Table 3). Manual reduction was successful in 90% of patients, with an average duration of 23.3±11.9 minutes (Fig. 2a, b). Surgical reduction was required for one patient after failed manual attempts, which took approximately 360 minutes (Fig. 2c, d). Intermaxillary traction was employed to consolidate the effect and reduce the risk of re-dislocation. 80% of patients received elastic traction with traction screws and rubber bands, while two patients utilized elastic bandages due to osteoporosis. Patients who underwent manual reduction reported no discomfort or complications, except for the surgical case experiencing pain in operative areas and facial nerve injury for a period. No patients experienced a recurrence of dislocation during the 6-month follow-up period. In conclusion, these findings suggest that manual reduction under general anesthesia combined with elastic intermaxillary traction using traction screws could effectively treat CPMD, significantly reducing the need for surgical interventions and minimizing postoperative discomfort and complications. Analysis of the position of dislocated condylar

The previous data indicated that only 10% of patients required surgical reduction, and no significant correlations were found in age, dislocation type, or dislocation time. Therefore, CT data were utilized to further analyze whether the location of the dislocated condyle was associated with the reduction regimen (Table 4). Six patients, including five treated with manual reduction and one with surgical reduction, were selected for analysis based on available pretreatment CT data. The analysis revealed that in all 5 patients treated with manual reduction, the bilateral condyles remained below the zygomatic arch (Fig. 3a, b), while in the case requiring surgical reduction, the condyle was located beyond the arch (Fig. 3c, d). Horizontal shift distances of the condyle relative to the mandibular fossa in manual treatment ranged from 2.38 to 2.82 in the right lateral position and from 2.21 to 2.76 in the left, whereas in the surgical case, the distances were 2.3 and 2.49 in the right and left, respectively. Vertical shift distances between the outer pole of the condyle and the superior margin of the zygomatic arch in manual treatment ranged from 2.95 to 8.38 in the right and from 2.47 to 7.98 in the left, whereas in the surgical reduction case, the distances were -7.85 and -2.81 in the right and left, respectively. These findings from the CT data indicate that when the dislocated condyle is located beyond the zygomatic arch, the manual reduction becomes difficult, and the vertical shift distance rather than the horizontal shift distance becomes the key determinant for the CPMD regimen. Discussion

Temporomandibular joint (TMJ) dislocation is a prevalent condition in oral health, typically managed through manual reduction in fresh cases [6, 10]. However, treating chronic protracted mandibular dislocation (CPMD) remains challenging [2]. The prolonged absence of the mandibular condyle in its correct position can significantly impact eating, speech, and overall mental and physiological well-being [2]. Despite its significance, there is currently no standardized treatment protocol for CPMD. In our retrospective study, we made several key findings that could enhance the diagnosis and treatment of CPMD. Firstly, we identified Cranio-cerebral injury resulting in unconsciousness as the most closely associated risk factor for CPMD onset, providing valuable insights for preventive measures. Then, we found that manual reduction under anesthesia, coupled with elastic traction, effectively restored condyle position, and facilitated TMJ function recovery. However, patients with refractory TMJ dislocation may still require surgical intervention to achieve optimal outcomes. By shedding light on these findings, our study aims to contribute to the development of more effective strategies for the prevention and treatment of CPMD, ultimately improving patient outcomes and quality of life.

The analysis of basic information regarding CPMD patients revealed several intriguing observations. Firstly, the age distribution aligns with previous studies, showing a higher incidence rate among the elderly compared to younger individuals [19]. However, the significantly skewed female-to-male ratio of 9:1 diverges from previous data indicating a near 1:1 ratio [19]. This discrepancy suggests that our gender data might be attributed to chance, rather than reflecting a genuine gender-related trend in CPMD occurrence. Secondly, it’s noteworthy that all patients suffered from bilateral anterior dislocation, with no cases of unilateral dislocation observed. This phenomenon could be attributed to the clinical manifestations of unilateral dislocation, which typically exhibit more noticeable facial asymmetry and jaw movement abnormalities, making them easier for patients to recognize [6]. Additionally, unilateral dislocation may be more challenging to maintain over a prolonged period due to anatomical considerations [7]. Finally, the observation that all patients had endured dislocated condyles for at least 1 month indicates that previous treatments prior to our intervention had failed, likely due to unclear diagnoses or incorrect treatment protocols [7, 15]. In conclusion, these findings underscore the importance of early diagnosis and proper treatment regimens for CPMD to effectively restore TMJ structure and function.

To prevent the occurrence of CPMD or alleviate the difficulty of its reduction, early diagnosis, and effective treatments are crucial. Firstly, we conducted a study to identify and analyze the risk factors associated with CPMD, with severe Cranio-cerebral injury emerging as the most significant factor. Such injuries can lead to the loss of normal consciousness, hindering the timely perception of joint dislocation and feedback from the patient. Further investigation revealed that patients with severe Cranio-cerebral injuries often require intensive care involving anesthesia intubation or sedation, during which relaxation of oromaxillary muscles and wide mouth opening procedures can exacerbate the occurrence of CPMD [16]. In addition to Cranio-cerebral injury, other factors such as dentition defects, masticatory muscle wasting, and drug use were also identified as risk factors for CPMD occurrence [5, 14]. Therefore, we conclude that special caution should be exercised, particularly in the elderly population accompanied by unconsciousness, during procedures requiring wide mouth opening under the aforementioned circumstances to prevent the occurrence of CPMD.

The treatment of CPMD presents both simplicity and difficulty. The simplicity lies in adhering to the treatment principle of starting with conservative measures, progressing to minimally invasive interventions, and resorting to surgical procedures only as a last resort. However, the difficulty arises from the challenges encountered at each stage of treatment [6, 10]. Numerous studies have highlighted that compared to fresh TMJ dislocations,CPMD poses greater challenges for manual reduction due to tissue proliferation and muscle spasm. In our study, all patients who underwent manual reduction received it under facial muscle relaxation or sedation, either with local or general anesthesia. Therefore, the failure of manual reduction in CPMD cases without anesthesia may be attributed to muscle spasms induced by patient pain and tension during the reduction process. Bilateral mandibular nerve blocks were administered to alleviate pain during manual reduction in partial patients; however, they were ineffective in this study. We believe that the patient’s anxiety also played a significant role, which could not be alleviated by local nerve blocks alone. Minimally invasive techniques, such as traction and endoscopy, have been explored to address CPMD by pushing hyperplasia or facilitating condylar reduction [17]. However, in cases where the condyle is beyond the zygomatic arch, as observed in our surgical reduction patients, traction and endoscopy are not feasible, necessitating surgical exposure of the condyle to facilitate restoration. While other surgical methods, such as amputation and trimming, are available, they should be employed judiciously based on individual circumstances, with preference given to minimally invasive and low-trauma approaches [13]. Overall, treatment should prioritize maximizing efficacy and minimizing complications.

Furthermore, traction techniques have been employed to aid in joint structure and function recovery [17]. In our case, traction screws and rubber bands were utilized to limit jaw movement, prevent recurrence, and encourage condylar movement to prevent adhesion rigidity. Data from patient follow-up periods demonstrated that manual reduction under anesthesia combined with elastic traction was effective in treating CPMD.

Throughout the treatment process, most patients were successfully managed with manual reduction, while a subset required surgical intervention. Factors influencing the choice of reduction method were subsequently examined. Data analysis revealed that variables such as age, sex, dislocation type, and dislocation duration did not differ significantly between patients treated with manual reduction and those requiring surgery. However, upon analyzing the position of the condyle relative to the zygomatic arch using CT scans [11], it became evident that this factor determined the appropriate reduction method. Horizontal condylar migration did not significantly differ between the two reduction methods. Instead, vertical displacement emerged as the critical factor in determining whether the condyle became lodged within the zygomatic arch. Therefore, if CT scans indicate that the dislocated condyle extends beyond the zygomatic arch with excessive vertical displacement, surgical reduction should be considered as the preferred treatment plan. However, given that we have only one patient who underwent surgical treatment, the data is insufficient to perform statistical analysis. We plan to continue collecting data on surgically treated patients in future clinical work to provide more robust evidence.

Conclusion

In summary, this study retrospectively analyzed the clinical and imaging data of CPMD patients to identify risk factors such as cranial injury, unconsciousness, and medical procedures involving wide mouth opening. It was concluded that manual reduction under general anesthesia, combined with elastic intermaxillary traction using screw and rubber, proved to be an effective treatment for CPMD. Additionally, excessive vertical displacement of the dislocated condyle, leading to impaction within the zygomatic arch, emerged as a potential indicator for surgical intervention. These findings hold significant clinical relevance for the diagnosis and treatment of CPMD. However, it’s important to acknowledge the limitations of the analysis data, which are currently constrained in scope. Further studies with expanded patient datasets will be necessary to validate the relationship between condyle position and zygomatic arch impaction, particularly in the context of surgical reduction.
