Urethral leiomyoma mimicking urethral diverticulum: a rare case report and brief literature review
Authors: Tae Woong Han, Ki Hong Kim, Ji Hye Lee, Si Hyun Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUrethral leiomyoma is an uncommon benign smooth-muscle tumor that typically affects women of reproductive age and presents with lower urinary tract symptoms or a periurethral mass. Preoperative misclassification as urethral diverticulum or Skene’s gland disease is frequent because clinical and imaging features overlap. Although magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can suggest a solid periurethral mass and delineate its relation to the urethral lumen and sphincter, definitive diagnosis requires surgical excision with histopathologic confirmation. Introduction

Urethral leiomyoma is an uncommon benign smooth-muscle tumor that usually arises in women of reproductive age and presents with lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) or a periurethral mass. Because the presenting symptoms, such as the classic “3Ds” (dysuria, dyspareunia, and dribbling), overlap significantly with those of other periurethral lesions, urethral leiomyoma is frequently misclassified as urethral diverticulum during the initial evaluation (1-4). Accurate preoperative characterization matters: it avoids a diverticulum-centered diagnostic pathway, informs the surgical approach, and frames counseling on expected recovery. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) helps by demonstrating a solid lesion, the absence or presence of luminal communication, and spatial relations to the urethral sphincter complex; however, the diagnostic standard remains histopathologic confirmation following excision (5-7). We report a mid-urethral leiomyoma initially suspected to be a diverticulum and detail the imaging discriminants, operative strategy, and pathologic profile that supported an uneventful course with symptom resolution. We present this article in accordance with the CARE reporting checklist (available at https://tau.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tau-2026-1-0013/rc).

Case presentation

Patient information and presentation

A 44-year-old woman presented on 18 April 2025 with urethral pain and dysuria. She denied fever, gross hematuria, or urinary incontinence. On physical examination, a firm and tender mass was palpated on the anterior vaginal wall. There was focal periurethral tenderness with no visible discharge or bleeding from the urethral meatus. Based on the specific anatomical location and the presence of voiding symptoms, a urethral diverticulum was initially considered.

Imaging findings

Pelvic MRI (6 May 2025) employed axial/sagittal/coronal T2-weighted imaging with oblique T2WI, axial T1-weighted imaging, fat-suppressed T1-weighted imaging, diffusion-weighted imaging with apparent diffusion coefficient mapping, and pre-/post-contrast axial T1-weighted imaging. A sharply marginated, well-circumscribed solid mass measuring approximately 3 cm was centered at the mid urethra. No definable neck or communication with the urethral lumen was identified. The urethral lumen remained preserved and the sphincter complex was not distorted. The constellation of a solid periurethral mass, absent luminal communication, and preserved urethral contour favored a neoplasm rather than a diverticulum (5-7) (Figure 1).

Operative management

After counseling, the patient underwent excision under general anesthesia in the lithotomy position via a vaginal approach with an inverted U-shaped incision. Intraoperatively, a firm, encapsulated mass of approximately 3 cm was palpated adjacent to the urethra. The lesion was dissected off the vaginal wall and urethra and removed en bloc. The urethra was intact without iatrogenic injury. Estimated blood loss was minimal; operative time was approximately 1 hour. Layer-by-layer closure of the vaginal wall was performed.

Pathology

Grossly, the specimen measured 3.8 cm × 3.0 cm, tan-white, and well-circumscribed. Microscopy showed intersecting bundles of bland spindle cells without significant atypia or mitoses, consistent with leiomyoma. Immunohistochemistry demonstrated smooth muscle actin (SMA) and desmin positivity with S-100 and CD34 negativity; the Ki-67 index was approximately 1%. This immunoprofile supports smooth-muscle origin and helps exclude neural (e.g., schwannoma) and vascular neoplasms (4,6,8,9) (Figure 2).

Histopathology and immunohistochemistry of the excised leiomyoma. (A,B) Hematoxylin and eosin staining, magnification: ×12.5 (A); ×400 (B). (C) Smooth muscle actin: diffuse positivity (magnification: ×400). (D) Desmin: diffuse positivity (magnification: ×400).

Postoperative course

Pain control with acetaminophen alone was sufficient. A Foley catheter was maintained for 1 day. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 1. At 3-month follow-up, there were no complications and dysuria had resolved.

Discussion

Clinical context and spectrum

Although rare, urethral or paraurethral leiomyoma should be considered in reproductive-aged women who present with LUTS, dyspareunia, or a palpable bulge in the periurethral region (1-4,10). Published case series and reviews describe a tendency for distal-to-mid urethral location and generally benign behavior (2,3). Under-recognition at the initial visit is common because symptoms overlap with those of urethral diverticulum and Skene’s gland disorders. A careful, structured approach improves classification and reduces unnecessary delays in definitive treatment (11).

Diagnostic pitfalls and the role of MRI

The chief pitfall is a diverticulum-first assumption in the face of non-specific LUTS. MRI can effectively shift the pretest probability by identifying three practical discriminants: (I) solid tissue characteristics rather than cystic content; (II) the absence of a definable luminal neck; and (III) a stable relationship to the sphincter complex without evidence of a sac extending from the urethral lumen (5-7). When these criteria are present, a smooth-muscle tumor becomes the more likely diagnosis allowing operative planning to prioritize organ-sparing excision over interventions designed for a diverticulum. Furthermore, MRI is essential for distinguishing benign leiomyoma from leiomyosarcoma, which are rare but highly aggressive malignancies. Unlike the well-circumscribed and relatively homogeneous appearance of leiomyoma, leiomyosarcoma typically presents as a large, heterogeneous mass frequently containing areas of internal necrosis or degeneration. Malignant lesions often exhibit infiltrative borders with invasion into surrounding structures and show intense, heterogeneous enhancement on post-contrast images (12,13). In the present case, the MRI demonstrated a solid periurethral mass without luminal communication and with a preserved urethral contour. The sharply marginated borders and lack of necrotic features strongly supported a benign smooth-muscle tumor, thereby justifying the decision for an organ-sparing en bloc excision via a transvaginal approach rather than more radical surgery.

Operative principles and approach selection

Complete local excision is the mainstay of treatment and is typically curative (2,3,8,9,14). The approach depends on site and size. For distal and mid-urethral lesions, transvaginal or transmeatal excision usually provides adequate exposure, while large lesions with posterior extension may require abdominal or combined approaches (2,7,14,15). Recent reports have also described successful transvaginal management of more complex urethral leiomyomas, supporting individualized dissection with preservation of urethral integrity (16). Technical priorities include: (I) exposure that protects the sphincter complex; (II) dissection along the tumor capsule; (III) avoidance of urethrotomy whenever feasible; (IV) layered reconstruction of the vaginal wall to preserve support; and (V) selective use of catheterization based on intraoperative findings. When these steps are followed, continence and urethral support are commonly preserved, and hospitalization is brief. Our patient underwent en bloc vaginal excision through an inverted U incision with minimal blood loss and an uncomplicated recovery, findings that mirror the experience in prior reports (8,9,14).

Pathology and immunophenotype-based differential diagnosis

Leiomyoma typically exhibits interlacing bundles of bland spindle cells with low mitotic activity. The immunoprofile (positive for SMA and desmin; negative for S-100 and CD34; low Ki-67 proliferation index) is characteristic and helps to exclude schwannoma (commonly S-100 positive) and vascular tumors (often CD34 positive) (4,6,8,9). Awareness of these distinctions is practical because periurethral lesions may mimic a caruncle or occur within a diverticulum, which could alter both the incision strategy and the threshold for urethrotomy (5,17). In the present case, the immunohistochemical pattern and low Ki-67 supported a benign smooth-muscle tumor, consistent with the imaging-guided clinical impression.

Outcomes, recurrence, and follow-up

Available series and case reports suggest favorable outcomes with low recurrence after complete excision and maintenance of continence when urethral support and mucosa are protected (2,3,8,9,14). Catheter duration and time to discharge vary by extent of dissection but are typically short in uncomplicated cases, reflecting limited tissue trauma and the organ-sparing nature of the procedure (8,9,14). For surveillance, a clinical-first strategy is reasonable—periodic symptom review and focused pelvic examination—with imaging reserved for recurrent symptoms, equivocal findings, or suspicion of residual disease (2,3). Because the literature remains limited in size and follow-up duration, consistent case reporting using standardized elements (symptom set, MRI discriminants, operative steps, immunohistochemistry, and follow-up interval) will improve comparability across studies and allow more confident counseling. Practical implications for periurethral mass work-up

The case reinforces three practical points for daily practice. First, in women with a periurethral mass and non-specific LUTS, MRI should be assessed specifically for solid tissue and absence of a neck; if both are present, diverticulum becomes less likely and a smooth-muscle tumor should be kept on the differential (5-7). Second, for mid-urethral lesions without mucosal involvement, vaginal en bloc excision is an efficient method that permits negative margins and protects continence when layered closure is performed (2,3,8,9,14). Third, a concise panel of immunostains (SMA, desmin, S-100, CD34, ± h-caldesmon) confirms lineage and excludes mimics, streamlining postoperative counseling and the choice of follow-up (4,6,8,9). In short, combining targeted MRI review, organ-sparing surgery, and confirmatory immunohistochemistry yields a predictable and favorable pathway for patients with periurethral leiomyoma.

Strengths and limitations of the present report

Strengths include complete documentation of imaging sequences used for periurethral evaluation, a clear description of the operative pathway with an en bloc strategy that preserved urethral integrity, and histopathologic confirmation with a standard immunopanel. A primary limitation of this case report is the relatively short follow-up duration of 3 months. While the patient experienced complete resolution of dysuria without early complications, long-term surveillance is necessary to definitively rule out delayed recurrence, given the rarity of this tumor in the mid-urethra.

Nonetheless, the trajectory—early catheter removal, same-day to next-day discharge, symptom resolution—matches prior reports of benign postoperative courses for completely excised leiomyomas (2,3,8,9,14). Future pooled analyses would benefit from uniform reporting of MRI signs (presence/absence of a neck, signal characteristics, enhancement), operative planes, and standardized continence outcomes to refine guidance.

Conclusions

Urethral leiomyoma is an infrequent benign tumor that can mimic urethral diverticulum in women with non-specific LUTS. In this case, MRI showed a solid periurethral mass without luminal communication, prompting a transvaginal organ-sparing excision. Histology and immunohistochemistry confirmed leiomyoma. Recovery was uncomplicated with resolution of dysuria. The diagnostic and operative principles summarized here: focused MRI review, local excision with preservation of urethral integrity, and confirmatory immunostaining are broadly applicable to periurethral smooth-muscle tumors encountered in routine practice.
