Deviceization of high-performance and flexible Ag2Se films for electronic skin and servo rotation angle control
Authors: Yue-Xing Chen, Xiao-Lei Shi, Jun-Ze Zhang, Mohammad Nisar, Zhong-Zhao Zha, Zi-Nan Zhong, Fu Li, Guang-Xing Liang, Jing-Ting Luo, Meng Li, Tianyi Cao, Wei-Di Liu, Dong-Yan Xu, Zhuang-Hao Zheng, Zhi-Gang Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAg2Se shows significant potential for near-room-temperature thermoelectric applications, but its performance and device design are still evolving. In this work, we design a novel flexible Ag2Se thin-film-based thermoelectric device with optimized electrode materials and structure, achieving a high output power density of over 65 W m−2 and a normalized power density up to 3.68 μW cm−2 K−2 at a temperature difference of 42 K. By fine-tuning vapor selenization time, we strengthen the (013) orientation and carrier mobility of Ag2Se films, reducing excessive Ag interstitials and achieving a power factor of over 29 μW cm−1 K−2 at 393 K. A protective layer boosts flexibility of the thin film, retaining 90% performance after 1000 bends at 60°. Coupled with p-type Sb2Te3 thin films and rational simulations, the device shows rapid human motion response and precise servo motor control, highlighting the potential of high-performance Ag2Se thin films in advanced applications.Subject terms: Thermoelectric devices and materials, Thermoelectrics

Introduction

Based on the Seebeck effect, flexible thermoelectric materials and their devices can directly convert heat from irregular-shaped heat sources into electricity, greatly expanding their application compared to rigid thermoelectrics1. Examples include wearable thermoelectric electronics (converting body heat into sustainable electricity) and recycling other irregular-shaped heat sources such as wastewater and exhaust pipes2. To enhance the efficiency of converting heat energy into electricity and ensure that the p-type and n-type thermoelectric materials in the device have high thermoelectric performance near room temperature and certain flexibility, it is also necessary to optimize the structure of flexible thermoelectric devices3. Regarding the former, in recent years, researchers have spared no effort in developing new materials with high thermoelectric potential near room temperature and certain plasticities, such as silver and silver-copper chalcogenides (Ag2Q and AgCuQ (Q = S, Se, Te))4,5 or finding ways to improve the performance and flexibility of existing thermoelectric materials near room temperature (such as n-type Bi2Te3−xSex and p-type BixSb2−xTe3)6. Generally, forming thermoelectric materials into thin films can effectively enhance their flexibility, although sometimes this may slightly decrease thermoelectric performance7. Simultaneously, the use of suitable flexible substrates, such as polyimide (PI)8, nylon9, etc., can effectively protect the thermoelectric thin films from damage during bending, maintaining their flexibility6. For the thermoelectric performance of these flexible thin films, considering that their thermal conductivity (κ) values are difficult to accurately measure, the power factor S2σ is commonly used to evaluate their thermoelectric potential, where σ is the electrical conductivity and S is the Seebeck coefficient10. However, σ and S are usually difficult to simultaneously improve due to their strong coupling with the carrier concentration n11. Therefore, rational compositional and micro/nanostructural designs are needed to further improve their thermoelectric performance2. As for the structural optimization of flexible thermoelectric devices, since the focus of researchers is currently on the design of flexible thermoelectric materials and the optimization of their performance6, this aspect is less explored and has become one of the research gaps and challenges, leading to a stagnation in research and industrialization in this field.

Compared to traditional thermoelectric materials based on Bi2Te3 and Sb2Te310,12,13, ductile Ag2Q (Q = S, Se, Te)4,14, especially Ag2Se15, have demonstrated n-type thermoelectric performance near room temperature that is comparable to Bi2Te3. For thin films9,15–58, through reasonable composition and structural optimizations, such as adjusting the ratio of Ag and Se (e.g., Ag1.8Se27 and Ag2.06Se34), doping (e.g., Te-doping54, Ga-doping29, and S-doping36,46), texturing (e.g., (00l)-orientated texturing16,38,47,54,55 and (013)-orientated texturing39), introducing secondary phases (e.g., Ag/Ag2Se33,37,53, Ag2Se/CuAgSe59, and Ag2Se/Ag/CuAgSe23), and designing inorganic/organic hybrids (e.g., polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP)/Ag2Se composite films25,30,56, Ag2Se/terpineol composite films58, AgxSe/polypyrrole (PPy) composite films57, poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene) (PEDOT)/Ag2Se/CuAgSe ternary composite films22, PEDOT/Ag2Se/Ag composite films31, Ag2Se/Se/PPy composite films9, and single-walled carbon nanotubes (SWCNT)/Ag2Se composite films28,42), the S2σ of Ag2Se-based thin films can exceed 20 μW cm−1 K−2 at near-room temperatures20,23,32,41,54, while different flexible substrate such as PI15,27,32,38,60, poly(vinylidene fluoride) (PVDF)26,61,62, polyethylene terephthalate (PET)46, flexible glass18,26,39,63, and Nylon9,19,21–23,25,28,29,31,33,36,40,42,48,52 have been used to support them with high flexibility. Additionally, compared to traditional Bi2Te3 and Sb2Te3 thin films, its higher plasticity (stemming from its unique crystal structure15) and lower cost (compared to the more expensive Bi and Te (Supplementary Table S1) and poor elements abundance (Supplementary Table S2) make it better for practical application with greater commercial potential15. The flexible thermoelectric device can be employed in various scenarios, such as low-cost heat recovery and wearable devices, where high thermoelectric performance and high output power density (ω) are essential3,6. However, as mentioned earlier, its current insufficient thermoelectric performance and immature device design limit its further application6, which motivates us to further explore its potential.

Results and discussion

To achieve these objectives, we first focused on further improving the thermoelectric performance of Ag2Se thin films and boosting their flexibilities, then optimized the flexible device design based on these high-performance and flexible thin films, and finally attempted to expand their application scenarios to demonstrate their application potential. Based on the diffusion reaction between Se vapor and Ag precursor film in the solid-gas phase, we fabricated Ag2Se thin films on flexible PI substrates by selenizing Ag precursor films pre-deposited by magnetron sputtering (Supplementary Fig. 1). By optimizing the selenization time, we achieved a highly (013)-oriented Ag2Se thin film, which exhibited high carrier mobility (μ) and in turn σ. Ag films can display a (013) preferential orientation at higher deposition energies. By adjusting the selenization time, the tube furnace applies additional heat to the precursor Ag films, which promotes the Ag2Se films to exhibit a (013) preferential orientation. The purpose of optimizing the selenization time also included reducing the content of Ag interstitial atoms (Agi) to control the overall n of the film, optimizing the S, and ultimately achieving a S2σ of close to 30 μW cm−1 K−2 at 363 K (23 μW cm−1 K−2 at room temperature), both of which are highly comparable to previously published works (Fig. 1a)19,25,27,32–34,38. Based on this, we designed a highly flexible organic composite protective layer composed of polyvinyl laurate, which significantly improved its flexibility without reducing the thermoelectric performance of the thin film. Even after bending 1000 times at a bending degree of 60°, its performance could still be maintained at about 90% (Fig. 1b). Furthermore, we rationally designed a seven-leg flexible device with a ω of up to 65 W m−2 at a temperature difference (ΔT) of 42 K, which far exceeds similar types of flexible thermoelectric devices (Fig. 1c) including both Ag2Se-based19–23,25,32,33 and other flexible inorganic-based devices64–71.

Characterizations of thin films

The high thermoelectric performance is mainly derived from the optimization of the preparation process of Ag2Se thin films. In this work, to ensure the high-quality crystallinity of the Ag2Se film, a solid-gas phase diffusion reaction between Se vapor and the Ag precursor film was carried out at a certain reaction temperature (254 °C). However, it is important to note the presence of reverse reactions. Excess Se powder was pre-set in the selenization tube to form saturated Se vapor, facilitating the forward selenization chemical reaction and yielding high-quality Ag2Se thin films. Additionally, due to the high selenization temperature and the inability to effectively fix the flexible PI substrate, we used specialized aluminum metal fixtures (Supplementary Fig. 2) to maintain the flatness of the flexible substrate during the reaction, which improved the adhesion of the Ag2Se thin film to the substrate. In the experiment, we first obtained the Ag precursor film by magnetron sputtering. Subsequently, using the fixtures to hold the Ag precursor film in place, excess Se powders were placed in two ceramic boats, and then the precursor film was placed between the two boats and introduced into the selenization tube. Since the tube furnace needs to be heated and operates at high temperatures, the Ag elements are prone to oxidation to a certain extent. Therefore, before starting the experiment, the selenization tube was evacuated and filled with high-purity nitrogen gas to ensure that the oxygen content in the selenization device was insufficient to cause oxidation of the experimental materials. After the purging process, a certain degree of negative pressure (10−4 Pa) was maintained, and the ends of the selenization tube were sealed before controlling the selenization temperature and time. The maximum temperature of the selenization tube we used can reach 1200 °C. To avoid overly intense reactions during the experiment, leading to uncontrolled factors such as thermal expansion and contraction, phase changes, and reverse reactions affecting the film surface, the heating rate was set to 10 K min−1, and no carrier gas was introduced during the reaction. During the reaction process, sufficient selenium vapor was introduced to the precursor Ag film to accelerate the diffusion reaction. The experiment was conducted with precise control, and the fixture used allowed for adjustment of the diffusion direction from top to bottom, leading to the production of higher-quality Ag2Se films. Additionally, after the reaction was completed, the device and experimental materials were cooled naturally to prevent excessive undercooling, which can lead to the generation of many defects and dislocations inside the crystal, adversely affecting phonon and carrier transport, ultimately reducing the thermoelectric performance.

During the thin-film fabrication process, optimizing the selenization time is crucial for adjusting the micro/nanostructures and actual compositions of the thin films to optimize their thermoelectric performance. We prepared thin-film samples with selenization times of 45, 50, 55, and 60 min, resulting in actual compositions of Ag2.22Se, Ag2.21Se, Ag2.20Se, and Ag2.19Se respectively (based on energy dispersive spectrometry (EDS) composition analysis, Supplementary Figs. 3–6). While the elemental ratios obtained from EDS may have some inaccuracies, they facilitate sample identification and understanding. It can be observed that as the selenization time increases, the Ag content gradually decreases, approaching the theoretical ratio of 2:1. This results in a larger average grain size, indicating higher crystallization quality (see Supplementary Figs. 7 and 8). Additionally, the thickness of the samples can be adjusted synchronously. The thickness plays a crucial role in the characterization of electrical performance for thin films, and the samples can be precisely regulated to approximately 500 µm (Supplementary Table 3). However, the exact form of Ag presence remains unknown, necessitating further characterization to verify its form. To achieve this goal, Fig. 2a shows the X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of these films. All peaks of the films can be indexed to the standard peaks of Ag2Se without significant impurity peaks, indicating that these films possess relatively pure phases and high crystallinity. Additionally, all films exhibit strong (013) orientations, which are beneficial for achieving high μ14,19. As the selenization time increases, the continuous occurrence of the forward reaction leads to a stronger (013) preferred orientation in the thin films, and the Ag2.19Se sample (60 minutes selenization) no longer exhibits (002) diffraction peaks. Furthermore, by magnifying the (013) diffraction peak at 36.9°, we found no significant peak shifts (Supplementary Fig. 9a). However, by comparing the higher-angle (014) peaks of all films with their corresponding standard peaks, we observed that all films exhibit a rightward shift (Supplementary Fig. 9b). As the selenization time is adjusted, the added Ag undergoes a transition from cluster doping to interstitial doping. Ag atoms in the interstitial sites cause lattice expansion, which results in a leftward shift of the XRD diffraction peak position. This phenomenon is supported by XPS characterization results (Fig. 2d). Considering that the films are single-phase, this phenomenon may be attributed to the presence of Agi. Additionally, detailed data on the material crystal structure can be obtained through refined XRD characterization, such as for Ag2.19Se (Supplementary Fig. 10). By comparing with standard crystal structures, we obtained the lattice constants a, b, c, and the unit cell volume V (Supplementary Fig. S11). It can be observed that with increasing selenization time, both lattice constants and unit cell volume gradually decrease, indicating a decrease in Agi content corresponding to the decrease in Ag content. Moreover, we calculated the orientation factors (F) of different samples in the (002) and (013) directions (Supplementary Fig. 12), and found that as the selenization reaction temperature increases, the F(013) gradually increases, demonstrating that the prepared Ag2Se thin film materials exhibit a strong preferred orientation in the (013) direction. Figure 2b, c displays XRD pole figures of the Ag2.19Se sample in various directions, confirming (013) as the preferred orientation. Additionally, XRD refinement indexed by the standard crystal structure further supports the presence of this preferred orientation.

Besides XRD analysis, we further characterized the elemental valence states within the thin-film materials using X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), including Ag 3d (Fig. 2d) and Se 3d (Supplementary Fig. 13). Due to insufficient selenization time for the Ag2.22Se thin film, resulting in poor film continuity, XPS characterization could not be accurately performed. Therefore, we only compared the XPS results of the Ag2.21Se, Ag2.20Se, and Ag2.19Se thin-film samples. The absence of characteristic peaks for oxygen in the full spectrum meets our experimental requirements (Supplementary Fig. 14). Moreover, the Ag 3d electron orbitals split into 3d3/2 and 3d5/2, and Gaussian peak fitting of the XPS spectra revealed the presence of elemental Ag peaks (Ag0). The binding energy difference between Ag0 and Ag+ is approximately 0.5 eV, confirming the presence of Ag in the material in the form of either metallic Ag as a secondary phase or Agi. As XRD results demonstrated the absence of metallic Ag, the presence of the Ag0 peak suggests the more likely existence of Agi. Furthermore, with prolonged selenization time, the gradual decrease in Ag content corresponds to a reduction in the Ag0 peak, further corroborating the decrease in Agi content, thus mutually validating the XRD results.

We further characterized the thin-film samples using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) to analyze differences in their surface morphologies. Figure 2e displays the top-view SEM image of the Ag2.19Se thin film, while top-view SEM images of the other thin-film samples at the same magnifications are provided in Supplementary Fig. 7 for comparison. We observed a significant increase in grain size with prolonged selenization time, indicating further enhancement of crystallinity. This enhancement reduces grain boundary intensity, minimizing carrier scattering and thereby aiding in the improvement of μ and σ. In addition to SEM, we conducted further characterizations of the micro/nanostructures using transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Figure 2f presents the morphology of the cross-section of the Ag2.19Se thin film sample, prepared by a focused ion beam (FIB) technique, revealing a uniform internal structure, demonstrating that the diffusion reaction method does not result in incomplete selenization. The inset shows the EDS mapping images of elemental distributions on the cross-section, confirming the uniform distribution of elements. Subsequently, we performed high-resolution atomic-level morphology characterization of the central region of the thin film. Figure 2g shows a spherical aberration-corrected scanning TEM (Cs-STEM) high-angle annular dark-field (HAADF) image of the Ag2.19Se thin film (60 minutes selenization). The insets display the corresponding indexed fast Fourier transform (FFT) pattern (top-right) and a magnified Cs-STEM HAADF image with overlapped atomic arrangement (bottom-right). Supplementary Fig. 15 also exhibits a corresponding higher-resolution atomic arrangement. The FFT of the atomic arrangement reveals a lattice predominantly oriented along the [200] direction in reciprocal space, exhibiting a dominant orientation along the (001). Supplementary Fig. 16 illustrates the simulated lattice arrangement of Ag and Se atoms in the (001) direction, which correlates well with the actual lattice arrangement (Supplementary Fig. 17). Figure 2h depicts another high-resolution image of the thin film in a region closer to the PI substrate and corresponding strain maps. The high-resolution image is also available in Supplementary Fig. 18. A notable difference in brightness is observed between the left and right sides of the image, likely attributed to differences in lattice orientation or internal atomic arrangement. However, corresponding FFT results reveal consistent crystal orientations on both sides, with both exhibiting a [200] crystal band axis. Subsequent geometric phase analysis (GPA) of this region revealed microscopic strains in different directions across the boundary line. GPA analysis relies on the occurrence of microscopic strains within the material, indicative of potential differences or distortions in lattice constants, allowing for qualitative or quantitative analysis in high-resolution TEM images. Such microscopic strain phenomena may arise from excess Ag (Agi) entering the lattice during material preparation, causing lattice distortion or deformation. Finally, the characterization of line scan intensities (Fig. 2i) revealed regular Ag and Se elemental intensities, with certain points (circled) exhibiting higher-than-normal Ag intensity, possibly indicating the presence of Agi in the lattice. The Agi ensures high σ, and with continued adjustment of the selenization time, the proportion of Agi in the lattice gradually decreases. This microstructure ensures high n while minimizing scattering effects and gradually enhancing μ.

Thermoelectric performance evaluation of thin films

We carefully measured the temperature-dependent thermoelectric properties in the in-plane direction of AgxSe-based thin films (x = 2.22, 2.21, 2.20, and 2.19), including σ, absolute Seebeck coefficient |S|, and determined S2σ, as shown in Fig. 3a–c. The temperature range for all samples tested was 303-393 K, as higher temperatures would induce phase transitions leading to a significant decrease in thermoelectric performance (Supplementary Fig. 19). It can be observed that with prolonged selenization time, from Ag2.22Se to Ag2.21Se (45 and 50 min selenization), the σ initially decreases due to the reduction in Agi content, leading to a decrease in n (Fig. 3d), but the grain size remains relatively unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 7), resulting in reduced σ. The presence of original Agi is due to insufficient selenization of the precursor Ag film. As selenization time increases, the Agi content decreases, leading to a continuous reduction in carrier concentration (Fig. 3d). Despite this, there is a notable increase in average grain size and a significant reduction in grain boundary intensity, which decreases carrier scattering. This results in a substantial improvement in and, consequently, a significant increase in σ (Fig. 3d). Due to the relationship with n, σ exhibits a certain inverse coupling with the |S|. Ultimately, the Ag2.19Se thin film sample achieved an ultra-high S2σ of 23.3 μW cm−1 K−2 at room temperature and 29.1 μW cm−1 K−2 at 393 K, which is highly competitive compared with reported results (Supplementary Table 4)9,18–20,23,25,32,38,40–43,46,47,60,63,72–76. It is worth noting that by further prolonging the selenization time, the film will be damaged, displaying a semi-transparent ted (Supplementary Fig. 20). Hence thermoelectric performance cannot be measured. Meanwhile, the excellent electrical properties also impact electronic thermal conductivity. The determined κ and figure-of-merit ZT values exhibit consistent fluctuations with σ, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 21. It is important to note that due to the PI substrate of the film and the technical challenges in measuring flexible films, the obtained κ is not highly accurate. Therefore, the ZT values provided are for reference only.

To further understand the as-measured thermoelectric performance, Fig. 3e depicts the relationship between the |S| and n at room temperature, obtained from the single parabolic band (SPB) model, as well as the effective mass m* of all thin-film samples. With the extension of selenization time, n decreases but m* increases slightly, demonstrating that higher-energy carriers are preserved after weakening carrier scattering. Additionally, the calculated deformation potential Edef values of the thin-film samples reveal that when the grain size of the film is larger, Edef is relatively lower (Fig. 3f). Since the Edef represents the ability of the material’s overall lattice deformation ability, lower values indicate easier deformation of the material, implying higher potential plasticity, which contributes to maintaining structural stability and enhancing flexibility during bending. Figure 3g illustrates the comparison between experimentally obtained and calculated n and S2σ values at room temperature, indicating that the n of the thin-film materials we prepared has been optimized to a level close to achieving peak S2σ. Further reduction in n could potentially lead to a peak room-temperature S2σ approaching 30 μW cm−1 K−2. To demonstrate the thermal stability and thermoelectric performance stability of the thin-film material, we also prepared a new Ag2.19Se thin film and conducted consecutive temperature-dependent thermoelectric performance tests for five cycles (Supplementary Fig. 22), confirming the high thermal cycling stability and repeatability of the high thermoelectric performance of the thin films we prepared. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations based on first principles revealed that the presence of Agi can shift the Fermi level of Ag2Se into the conduction band (Fig. 3h, i), which contributes to the enhancement of n and σ, consistent with our experimental results. Considering Agi may be located at different positions in the crystal structure of Ag2Se, more cases for the calculated band structures and their corresponding density of states (DOS) are provided in Supplementary Fig. 23, representing the same results.

Flexibility and deviceization of thin films

Although we have achieved ultra-high thermoelectric performance in Ag2.19Se thin films, the flexibility of these films still needs further improvement to adapt to flexible application environments. We cut the films into strips (30 cm long) using a novel fixture to secure the larger substrate and then mounted them on a bending test platform. The films were subjected to repeated bending tests at curvatures of 200, 400, 600, 800, and 1000 cycles. The bending curvature is defined as the ratio of the height d of the film lifted after bending to the distance D between the two ends of the film at that time. Through testing, it was observed that the flexibility of the films was poor (Supplementary Fig. 24). This is because the surface of the film is exposed to air, and during the bending process, the film is prone to cracking along the grain boundaries8, resulting in increased resistance (R). Based on this, we designed a protective layer based on a highly flexible organic composite. We first applied polyvinyl laurate material and then used ultraviolet (UV) to form a protective layer on the surface of the film to prevent cracking after bending. In the experiment, we tested coatings with thicknesses of 100 nm and 150 nm. Through testing, it was found that the flexibility was greatly improved when the surface of the film was coated with a polyvinyl laurate organic layer. Under the same 20% bending curvature and 200 bending tests, the resistance change rate ΔR/R0 of the 100 nm coated layer decreased to 10%, while the ΔR/R0 of the 150 nm coated layer was only 1.8%. For the highest 59% bending curvature, after 1000 bending tests, the 150 nm coated layer also exhibited only a 12.7% ΔR/R0 (Fig. 1b); this excellent flexibility is highly competitive compared to reported values for Ag2Se-based films (Supplementary Table 5). Additionally, we achieved a normalized power density (ωn) of up to 3.68 μW cm−2 K−2 with a ΔT of 42 K. These mechanical performance improvements and sufficient ω meet the requirements for repeated applications of materials and related wearable devices.

Based on this, we began designing high-performance flexible thermoelectric devices with reasonable structures and components. We first focused on selecting electrode materials for the Ag2.19Se thin film to further reduce the internal resistance of the device. We selected seven different commonly used metal electrode materials: Ti, Mo, In, Mg, Au, Al, and Cu, in the hope of forming ohmic contact with the Ag2.19Se material. We first used ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) to determine the surface Fermi level of the materials. By characterizing the surface Fermi level of the cleaned materials using UPS (Fig. 4a), the portion near the highest occupied state is called the secondary electron cutoff, and the electron cutoff is the Fermi edge. By measuring the position of the secondary electron cutoff and the known energy of the light source, the position of the Fermi level relative to the vacuum level can be determined. The position of the Fermi level relative to the vacuum level can be expressed by the formula: EF = hν − Esec − ϕ77, where hν is the energy of the incident photons, and in this test, a helium lamp (21.22 eV) was used as the characterization source. Esec is the energy difference between the secondary electron cutoff edge and the Fermi edge calculated in the test, and ϕ is the work function. By comparing the Fermi level of the surface electronic band structure with the work function of the selected metal electrode materials, it was found that the work functions of the Au and Cu electrode materials are relatively close to the Fermi level. This results in a smaller interface barrier, which is more conducive to current transmission. However, due to the higher cost of Au electrodes, they were not selected for this work.

Although we initially conducted a simple selection through UPS, practical validation of electrode material compatibility still requires experimental results. We investigated the contact resistance between different metal electrode materials and Ag2.19Se material, mainly using the transmission line model (TLM)78. The Ti, Mo, In, Mg, Al, and Cu electrodes were all prepared using magnetron sputtering, while the Au electrode was prepared using thermal evaporation, all with a thickness of 100 nm. The TLM method is a technique used to measure the contact resistance and intra-device resistance of semiconductor materials and devices. This method is particularly suitable for evaluating the properties of metal-semiconductor contacts, such as ohmic contacts or Schottky contacts, as well as the resistance characteristics of thin films. The test involves first preparing a series of parallel metal electrodes with different spacings (L = 0.1 mm, 0.2 mm, 0.3 mm, 0.4 mm, 0.5 mm), and using probes to measure the resistance between adjacent metal electrodes (Fig. 4b). The resistance values at different transmission distances are linearly regressed, and the slope of the obtained straight line represents the contact resistance of the secondary metal electrode. Through TLM testing, it was found that the Cu electrode was more consistent with our initial experimental expectations. Additionally, the Cu electrode is easy to prepare and cost-effective, making it an excellent electrode material. Subsequently, we also tested the contact resistance of Cu electrodes with thicknesses of 100 nm, 300 nm, and 460 nm (inset in Fig. 4b). Eventually, we found that the 300 nm Cu electrode could form an ohmic contact with the Ag2.19Se thin film and had a smaller contact resistance.

Based on this, we constructed a π-type thermoelectric device using seven Ag2.19Se thermoelectric thin films as legs connected by 300 nm Cu electrodes. The legs were sized at 20 × 2.5 mm2, and were pasted onto the Cu electrodes prepared using a mask template to form an in-plane thermoelectric device. Figure 4c, d illustrates the structure and components of the seven-leg device, along with a photo of the device before bending. Figure 4e displays the measured open-circuit voltages (V) and output powers (P) of the device as a function of loading current (Iload) at different ΔTs. Through output power testing, the as-fabricated device exhibited outstanding P exceeding 500 nW under a ΔT of 42 K. Figure 4f shows the ΔT-dependent ω of the device compared with reported thin-film-based devices (including inorganic/organic hybrid thin-film-based devices)20–23,25,27,33,41,69,79. The device demonstrated higher ω at various ΔTs. The maximum of ω (ωmax = 65 W m−2 at a ΔT of 42 K) is highly competitive compared with reported results (Supplementary Table 6)9,19,21,25,27,28,31–33,36,42. The excellent thermoelectric performance of the device stems from the high near-room-temperature thermoelectric performance of the Ag2.19Se thin films and the rational selection of electrode materials and size design to significantly reduce internal resistance. Figure 4g displays the flexibility data of the device, with the inset showing the optical image of the bending test. It can be observed that the two-dimensional (2D) flexible device can maintain over 80% of its output performance after 1000 bends at a bending angle of 60 degrees along the out-of-plane direction, demonstrating its good flexibility.

Applications

In addition to the seven-leg device consisting solely of n-type Ag2.19Se thin films, we also attempted to design a flexible full device containing both n-type Ag2.19Se thin films and p-type Sb2Te3 thin films to expand its application scope. The selected Sb2Te3 flexible thin film is a high-performance p-type film designed by us, and its specific thermoelectric performance can be referenced in Supplementary Table 7. Due to the addition of p-type legs, the structure of the device needs to be redesigned and optimized. Through finite element simulations, we first investigated the influence of the filling ratio of thermoelectric materials on the output performance of the device under the same substrate area. To ensure the calculations are accurate and supportive of device integration, key parameters for the finite element simulations were based on actual values, including S, σ, κ, density, and heat capacity. The proportion of thermoelectric legs per unit area is positively correlated with the output power of the entire device. However, when the thermoelectric leg spacing is close, lateral heat transfer greatly affects the temperature gradient established at the ends of the material, which reduces the overall output performance of the device. We established four different material filling ratios: 30%, 50%, 70%, and 90%, and explored the most suitable filling ratio through the same loading temperature. We found that a filling ratio of 70% was optimal. At this filling ratio, there is no significant lateral heat conduction between the thermoelectric legs, allowing for an effective temperature gradient to be established. Based on this, we further studied the effect of different numbers of thermoelectric leg pairs (1–5) per unit area under the same filling ratio (70%) on the output performance, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 25. Theoretical analysis reveals that as the number of thermoelectric leg pairs increases, the P shows a positive correlation. However, at the same time, the internal resistance of the device also increases exponentially, including the contact resistance between multiple pairs of thermoelectric legs and the electrodes (Supplementary Fig. 26). Ultimately, finite element calculations indicate that a 70% filling ratio with three pairs of thermoelectric legs is optimal (Supplementary Figs. 27 and 28).

Figure 5a illustrates the structure and components of the three-leg flexible thermoelectric device composed of Ag2.19Se thin films as n-type legs and Sb2Te3 thin films as p-type legs (top-right), along with corresponding optical images of the device in its normal state (top-left) and bent states from both top view (bottom-left) and side view (bottom-right). This three-leg device also exhibits good flexibility, maintaining around 75% of its performance even after undergoing 1000 bends at a 60° bending angle (Supplementary Fig. 29). To verify the potential of this device as a component of electronic skin (acting as a thermoelectric sensor) for detecting human motion signals, Fig. 5b shows the signal sensitivity of the thermoelectric sensor at three different bending degrees (Stages 1–3), demonstrating extremely high sensitivity. The performance of these sensors was evaluated using a high sampling rate and high-precision source meter. Each data point, representing a dataset, fluctuated around the mean value, and statistical plotting methods were employed to demonstrate signal stability (Supplementary Fig. 30). The performance of K and L thermocouples is also included in Supplementary Fig. 30 for comparison. This high sensitivity is attributed to the low internal resistance of the device and the ultra-high σ of the thin film materials. This is also one of the reasons why Ag2.19Se thin-film materials are more suitable as core components of flexible thermoelectric electronic devices. Figure 5c–e displays the voltage signals generated by a ΔT of 10 K at different bending degrees (ratios of d/D). The difference in voltage signals can serve as feedback for the body movement status. To verify this, based on the device design, we attached the sensors to the inside of the finger joints and elbow joints, generating signals through bending movements of the finger and arm. The ends of the sensors mainly provide body temperature as the high-temperature side, and the raised middle part contacts the body at a certain frequency to generate signals (Supplementary Figs. 31). Figure 5f, g illustrates the devices attached to the elbow and finger, generating corresponding signals through bending movements. Here BPM is abbreviated from beats per minute. The elbow joint performs bending exercises at a frequency of 10 s per cycle, and it can be observed that the thermoelectric sensor can generate signals of over 1.5 mV when exposed to the temperature difference between the body and the environment, which is sufficient for signal detection in wearable devices. The finger joint bends according to different rhythms, including 120 BPM, as well as tests at 30 BPM, 36 BPM, and 90 BPM (Supplementary Fig. 32). Even with the short contact time of the 120 BPM test, the voltage signal averages over 0.8 mV, indicating the enormous potential of our device as a component of electronic skin for detecting human motion signals.

In addition to serving as a key component of electronic skin, we also attempted to leverage the high signal responsiveness of the three-leg device to achieve more complex functionalities. For example, we utilized the characteristics of flexible thermoelectric devices as signal sources and designed servo motors to respond to changes in the signal source through microcontrollers and other control systems, thereby adapting to various applications and accomplishing a variety of complex tasks (Supplementary Fig. 33). Figure 5h shows the schematic diagram of the test circuit principle for using the device to control servo rotation angles. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 34a, the test device is divided into three temperature zones. To simulate real-world scenarios, the two temperature zones in red are loaded with the same heat source, and the ΔT is controlled through the middle-temperature zone. Figure 5i displays optical images representing the angles of the servo controlled by the device at different ΔTs. The output electrical signal of the device varies with the magnitude of the ΔT, and larger ΔT leads to larger output voltage. Therefore, by evaluating the different signal magnitudes generated by different ΔTs and applying threshold judgment, stable and reliable commands can be sent to the servo motor to control its rotation angle. Additionally, a simple application scenario demonstrating the control of servo motor angle using human body temperature can be referenced in Supplementary Movie 1. Furthermore, the conversion of signals using human body temperature can also be displayed in real time on a computer (Supplementary Movie 2). Moreover, to achieve higher response speed and precision, we further optimized the circuit system design, as shown in the photos in Supplementary Fig. 34. The new system can rapidly control the servo motor rotation angle under different ΔTs with more precision. For instance, when the heat sources at both sides of the device are set to 40, 45, and 50 °C respectively, the servo motor accurately rotates to 45°, 90°, and 135° (Supplementary Movies 3–5). Figure 5j displays the stability of signal acquisition after optimization. The signals fluctuate within different color bands, with narrower bands indicating more stable signals and higher response speed and precision. Compared to the result before system optimization (Supplementary Fig. 35), the signals are more stable with higher response precision. Statistical data are provided in Supplementary Table 8 for reference. We also attempted multi-stage responses under the same ΔT (Supplementary Movie 6), where the heat sources at both sides were set to 45 °C, the servo motor started at the maximum angle, and then the heat source in the middle zone was loaded to reduce the ΔT and decrease the servo motor angle. Finally, removing the heat source resulted in an increase in ΔT and an increase in the servo motor angle. Since there were no heat dissipation measures, the signal responded faster to the decrease in ΔT than to its increase. These results confirm the rapid response characteristics of the flexible device we designed, which holds significant potential for various applications in signal detection and control fields.

In conclusion, we rationally designed a flexible thermoelectric device based on high-performance Ag2.19Se thin films and explored their applications. The optimization of the selenization time results in a high (013) orientation in Ag2.19Se thin films, leading to high μ and σ. Such an optimization simultaneously suppresses excessive Agi in the microstructures, reducing the excessive n and improving S, ultimately achieving a high maximum S2σ of up to 29 μW cm−1 K−2. Further design of a protective layer significantly enhanced its flexibility without compromising its thermoelectric performance, maintaining performance at over 90% even after bending 1000 times at a 60° bending angle. Based on this, we design a seven-leg flexible device with a rational selection of electrode materials and structural design, resulting in an ω of up to 65 W m−2 at a ΔT of 42 K. Additionally, by matching with p-type Sb2Te3 thin films and based on finite element simulations, we further design a three-leg p-n flexible device with an optimized 70% filling ratio, capable of various applications including rapid response to human motion signals as electronic skin, and precise control of servo motor angles. This work demonstrates the unprecedented application potential of high-performance Ag2Se thin films combined with rational device design.

Methods

Synthesis

The Ag precursor films involved in this study were prepared using magnetron sputtering technology, with a base vacuum of 5 × 10−4 Pa, working pressure of 0.5 Pa, and sputtering power of 20 W DC sputtering. The substrate used was a PI film with dimensions of 2 × 2 cm2. Subsequently, the Ag precursor films were placed in a selenium atmosphere reaction furnace. The selenium atmosphere reaction furnace eliminates the influence of oxygen and moisture in the furnace by injecting inert gas (such as nitrogen) and precisely controlling the reaction temperature and time to adjust the concentration of selenium vapor and create the desired atmosphere. The selenium material (Se powder) is typically placed in quartz boats or carriers inside the furnace tube. These quartz boats are evenly distributed inside the furnace tube to ensure uniform heating and reaction of the material. A certain degree of negative pressure (1 × 104 Pa) is maintained inside the tube. In the experiment, the atmosphere was provided by placing two quartz boats at both ends of the Ag precursor film. The preparation method involves the preparation of substrates and other equipment, mainly including (1) cleaning of polyimide substrates. Flexible PI substrates are first cut into 2 × 2 cm2 pieces, then soaked in pure water, alcohol, and acetone for 20 min under ultrasound, and finally dried; (2) selenium boat treatment. During the diffusion-reaction process in the tube furnace, selenium powder is mainly placed in ceramic boats, which are then placed in the tube furnace together with the Ag precursor film for diffusion-reaction; and (3) mold clamping. Temperature variations during the diffusion reaction of flexible PI substrates in the tube furnace can lead to deformation and bending, which negatively impacts the quality of flexible thermoelectric thin films. To address this issue, we employed a custom-made aluminum clamp to maintain the flatness of the flexible substrate. The selenization temperature is 254 °C, and the selenization times are 45, 50, 55, and 60 min, respectively.

Characterization

The SEM equipment model is Supre55 Sapphire, provided by Zeiss. The EDS equipment model is EDS Quantax, manufactured by Bruker in Germany. The XRD instrument model is Ultima IV, provided by Rigaku in Japan. The XPS characterization equipment model is Escalab 250Xi, manufactured by Thermo Fisher Scientific. The TEM experimental equipment model is the FEI Titan Cubed Themis G2 300, manufactured by FEI Company in the Netherlands.

Thermoelectric performance evaluation

The S and σ measurements were conducted in a helium gas protection environment with a flow rate of 50 sccm. Using the four-probe method, the S and σ of the materials were measured at different temperatures. The temperature range used was from room temperature to 800 °C, with an error of ≤3% for the S and ≤7% for the σ. The heating rate during the heating process was set to 5 K per second. The experiments were performed using a Nezsch SBA458 manufactured by NETZSCH. Hall experiments utilized the HL5500PC equipment manufactured by Lakeshore.

Flexibility evaluation

The Keithley 2400 source meter and vacuum bending test platform were primarily used to test the device output performance and mechanical properties. The vacuum bending platform was manufactured by Shenyang Scientific Instrument Center Co., Ltd. The bending test platform measures the resistance R of the sample in the bent state and the resistance R0 of the unbent sample. By calculating the change in resistance ΔR before and after bending, the electrical performance loss of the flexible material under multiple repeated tests can be determined. The films were cut into strips measuring 30 cm in length and then fixed onto the vacuum bending test platform. The films were subjected to repeated tests at different bending curvatures: 200, 400, 600, 800, and 1000 times. The bending curvature was defined as the ratio of the height d lifted by the film after bending to the distance D between the ends of the film at that time. The mechanical properties of the film surface were greatly improved by coating it with a layer of polyvinyl alcohol stearate. The experiment also involved testing with 200, 400, 600, 800, and 1000 repetitions.

Device

We investigated the contact resistance between various metal electrode materials and Ag2.19Se using the TLM method. This technique measures both contact resistance and intra-device resistance in semiconductor materials and devices. In our study, a custom-made hard mask was used for electrode deposition, resulting in the successful fabrication of a promising Cu electrode. Then a π-type thermoelectric device was constructed using seven Ag2.19Se-based thermoelectric legs and 300 nm thick Cu electrodes. The thermoelectric legs were all sized at 20×2.5 mm2. Subsequently, the thermoelectric legs were pasted onto the Cu electrodes prepared through a mask to form an in-plane thermoelectric device. For the thermoelectric sensor, in-plane conduction devices were constructed using p-type Sb2Te3 and n-type Ag2.19Se thin films. Devices with 1–5 pairs of thermoelectric legs were prepared. The output performance characterization tests of different in-plane conduction devices were conducted under ΔTs of 30, 40, and 50 K. The materials were prepared on a 40 × 50 mm2 substrate, and the dimensions of each thermoelectric leg were 20 × 2 mm2. The sensitivity and responsivity of the device are achieved using high sampling rates and high-precision source meters on a custom-built platform. During data collection and testing, the measured values fluctuate around the average, so statistical methods are employed to illustrate signal stability. Each data point represents a measurement. Additionally, responsivity, a key parameter in evaluating thermocouple performance, refers to the time required for a thermal trigger to be detected.
