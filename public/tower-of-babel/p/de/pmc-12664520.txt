Utility of substance-free alternatives in treating and preventing harmful substance use: A narrative review of the translational research spectrum
Authors: Justin T Van Heukelom, Lesleigh A Stinson, Jalie A Tucker
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractInterventions primarily focused on constraining access to substances have largely failed to reduce substance use and associated harms. Alternative approaches informed by behavioral theories of choice focus on reducing harmful substance use by increasing access to and engagement in substance-free rewards that can compete with substance use. This narrative review of the translational research spectrum, organized by research phases in the Food and Drug Administration’s Drug Approval Process, summarizes wide-ranging research on associations between substance-free alternatives (palatable stimuli, money, social interaction, physical activity) and substance use and related harms. Studies reviewed span laboratory experiments with nonhuman animals to human experiments and treatment and prevention research. With limited exceptions, research across the translational spectrum supported the role of substance-free alternative rewards in reducing harmful substance use. Effective substitutes for substance use under specific conditions included palatable stimuli in animal studies and monetary incentives and social interaction opportunities with non-substance using peers in human studies. Evidence for physical activity was mixed, with stronger support found in animal than human research. Emerging research further suggests that increasing access to substance-free activities in broader socioecological contexts surrounding harmful use (e.g., communities, drug-related policy) also are needed. Increasing access to substance-free rewards through individual- and community-level interventions is a promising alternative to conventional approaches that primarily focus on reducing substance use and related negative consequences by constraining access to substances. Community-level and public health policy efforts guided by behavioral theories of choice merit further development and evaluation.Keywords: Substance use disorder, Behavioral theories of choice, Behavioral economics, Substance-free reinforcement, Socioecological determinants of health, Substance-related harms

Introduction

Numerous perspectives have been developed to address substance use that contributes to individual and community harms in functional, social, health, legal, and economic domains. Many researchers have sought to explain harmful substance use by focusing on individual-level factors associated with use (e.g., neurological events, psychological states). Prominently, Leshner (1997) and others (e.g., Volkow et al., 2016, Volkow et al., 2019) have argued that drug addiction is a brain disease resulting from drug-induced neurological changes. Although such approaches can be useful, they remain limited in scope because they underemphasize the environmental contexts in which substance use occurs (Vuchinich et al., 2023, Witkiewitz and Tucker, 2024).

The present review is guided by behavioral theories of choice that view harmful substance use as a temporally extended pattern of behavior that involves choosing to engage in substance use over substance-free rewards benefitting life-health functioning (e.g., Herrnstein and Prelec, 1992; Rachlin, 1997). Here, the term “choice” is distinct from its colloquial definition (Acuff et al., 2024) and refers instead to behavioral allocation among alternatives that vary in constraints on their access (e.g., price, delay to receipt, effort to obtain). Behavioral theories of choice, informed by the pioneering work of experimental behavior analysts (e.g., Baum and Rachlin, 1969; Herrnstein, 1961), prominently include behavioral economics, which merges operant choice findings with demand theory in microeconomics (Hursh, 2014, Hursh et al., 2013). Behavioral choice theories have been widely applied to understand how preference or demand for commodities (e.g., substance use) vary over time as a function of changing constraints on access to other commodities and activities in the choice context. A shared implication of this work is that harmful substance use can be reduced by increasing constraints on access to substances and by decreasing constraints on access to alternative substance-free rewards (Vuchinich and Tucker, 1988). Some interventions that focus primarily on constraining substance use are effective when constraints are present (e.g., Chaloupka et al., 2012; Wagenaar et al., 2009), but this drug supply reduction approach is generally unsuccessful as a dominant strategy for reducing harmful substance use (Ennett et al., 1994, MacCoun and Reuter, 2001, Kleinman and Morris, 2021). Though relatively neglected until recently, increasing access to substance-free rewards offers an alternative approach to reducing harmful substance use. This is the focus of the present review.

Several other reviews addressed the utility of behavioral choice theories in shifting behavioral allocation away from substance-related activities, and focused on preclinical research (Kearns, 2025, Marcus and Banks, 2025), clinical interventions (Fazzino et al., 2019, Murphy et al., 2022), specific substance-free alternatives (Strickland and Acuff, 2023), or measurement of substance-free rewards (Acuff et al., 2019; Johansen et al., 2024; Weinsztok et al., 2023a). A more theoretically oriented review by Acuff et al. (2023) called for increased focus on the choice environment when devising treatment and public policy to reduce harmful substance use. Other theoretically oriented reviews guided by the socioecological model of health (Institute of Medicine, 2003) also extended consideration of influences on individual behavior to the wider community and policy environment, thereby broadening the conceptualization and measurement of substance-free rewards in natural environments (Rachlin et al., 2018, Tucker et al., 2023, Vuchinich et al., 2023, Witkiewitz and Tucker, 2024).

The present review covers research across the translational spectrum relevant to assessing the utility of substance-free alternatives in treating and preventing harmful substance use. Its overarching aim is to guide development of coordinated intervention efforts that target individual and community systems for change, and the review differs from others in several respects. First, we summarize research organized around several distinct types of alternative rewards (i.e., palatable stimuli, money, social interaction, physical activity), which provides important information regarding how useful different alternatives are for intervention development and application. Second, our review covers behavioral research across the translational research spectrum, based on the four-phase Food and Drug Administration (FDA) Drug Approval Process. As Fig. 1 shows, FDA phases range from preclinical basic science, intervention development and establishment of safety, evaluation of intervention efficacy and effectiveness with larger-sized samples, and intervention dissemination and implementation. Developed to guide pharmaceutical drug-testing (Williams, 2016), the FDA translational process has been extended to social, behavioral, and clinical research (e.g., Fernandez et al., 2019; Onken et al., 2014; Powell et al., 2021). Third, the current review includes a section (5) titled Beyond Phase 4, which covers naturalistic and intervention research that informs how community-level environments and engagement in substance-free activities can prevent or reduce harmful substance use.

Translational research processes. The Food and Drug Administration (FDA) Drug Approval Process (top) applied to the translational research spectrum concerning substance-free alternatives to harmful substance use (bottom).

This is necessarily a narrative review because the diverse methods utilized practically limit synthesis of the body of work in a quantitative (e.g., meta-analytic) or systematic review framework. The review focuses on representative individual studies across the translational spectrum that examined effects of substance-free alternatives on substance-related outcomes. Given the scope of the research reviewed, details of individual studies (e.g., design, population, sample size), as well as concise study summaries, are provided in the tables accompanying each text section. Each research phase is described as it is introduced.

Palatable alternatives to substance use in nonhumans: preclinical research

Preclinical research includes studies in which basic properties relevant to intervention were examined in nonhumans and primarily focused on drug self-administration during steady states (Sidman, 1960). Substance-free alternative reinforcers evaluated included food (e.g., Aigner and Balster, 1978) and sucrose or saccharin (e.g., Lester et al., 1952). Subjects typically were presented with two levers in discrete-choice trials, and each lever was associated with either a drug reinforcer (i.e., self-administration) or a non-drug palatable reinforcer.

As Table 1 summarizes, environmental manipulations involving reinforcement magnitude, reinforcement delay, and response requirements were found to reduce drug self-administration. Specifically, self-administration often decreased when alternative palatable reinforcers were larger in magnitude (e.g., Carroll, 1985; Huskinson et al., 2016; Nader and Woolverton, 1991), delivered more immediately (e.g., Huskinson et al., 2016; Maguire et al., 2013), and required less effort to obtain (e.g., Nader and Woolverton, 1992; Petry and Heyman, 1995). These effects were observed for a variety of substances, including alcohol (Petry and Heyman, 1995), cocaine (Huskinson et al., 2016, Nader and Woolverton, 1991, Nader and Woolverton, 1992), remifentanil (Maguire et al., 2013), and phencyclidine (Carroll, 1985). Although higher drug doses can diminish the efficacy of alternative reinforcement (e.g., Maguire et al., 2013; Nader and Woolverton, 1991, 1992), palatable substance-free reinforcers were chosen over substances in nonhumans under certain conditions.

Monetary alternatives to substance use in humans: phase 1

Phase 1 human research includes experimental laboratory studies with elements resembling those from the preclinical phase but also includes observational studies. Palatable stimuli are rarely arranged as substance-free alternatives in Phase 1 studies with humans (Jones and Comer, 2013), although a few studies found that monetary rewards suppressed choice of alcohol (Zacny et al., 1992) and cocaine (Stoops et al., 2010) more effectively than food.

Phase 1 experiments demonstrated that alternative monetary rewards reduced substance use, including when monetary rewards were larger (Bisaga et al., 2007, Higgins et al., 1994, Vuchinich and Tucker, 1983, Vuchinich et al., 1987), delay to their receipt was shorter (Roll et al., 2000, Vuchinich and Tucker, 1983, Vuchinich et al., 1987), and they were less effortful to obtain (Bennett et al., 2013, Stoops et al., 2012; but also see DeGrandpre et al., 1994). These effects were found with alcohol (Vuchinich and Tucker, 1983, Vuchinich et al., 1987), cigarettes (Bisaga et al., 2007, Roll et al., 2000), cocaine (Higgins et al., 1994, Stoops et al., 2012), and methamphetamine (Bennett et al., 2013). Like preclinical findings with alternative palatable reinforcers, monetary rewards tended to be associated with reduced substance use in Phase 1 studies.

Small-scale contingency management evaluations: phase 2

Phase 2 comprises small-scale evaluations of interventions informed by the previous phases but does not include randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Some Contingency Management (CM) intervention studies align with Phase 1 research and have shown that contingent monetary rewards reduced choice of substances. In CM, clients typically are provided with vouchers exchangeable for substance-free goods and services, usually contingent upon biological verification of abstinence from substance use (Higgins et al., 2013). Such rewards are assumed to function as positive reinforcers, though other mechanisms also may be involved (e.g., social rewards; Davidson et al., 2025). Phase 2 evaluations of CM demonstrated that contingent cash (Stitzer and Bigelow, 1982, Stitzer et al., 1980) and vouchers (Higgins et al., 1991, Silverman et al., 1996) promoted abstinence from cocaine (Higgins et al., 1991), opioids (Silverman et al., 1996, Stitzer et al., 1980), and cigarettes (Stitzer and Bigelow, 1982). Recent technological advances have allowed the quantity, rather than the mere presence, of substance use to be continuously monitored in CM (e.g., transdermal alcohol monitoring). Some Phase 2 studies using continuous monitoring suggested that contingent cash can reduce heavy drinking (Dougherty et al., 2014, Dougherty et al., 2015).

Larger-scale contingency management evaluations: phase 3

Phase 3 involves RCT and longitudinal intervention evaluations that are generally larger in scale than Phase 2 studies. In Phase 3, CM’s effects on abstinence were compared to control conditions, including noncontingent incentives or conventional counseling. Voucher-based CM increased abstinence from cannabis (Budney et al., 2000) and cocaine (Higgins et al., 1993, Higgins et al., 1995, Higgins et al., 2000), both during and following treatment. Further, results from an RCT of the Therapeutic Workplace, a variant of CM that involves delivering contingent wage supplements (Silverman et al., 2019), indicated that abstinence, employment, and living outside of poverty were promoted (Holtyn et al., 2020); however, these effects were not maintained after treatment (Novak et al., 2022). Moreover, recent meta-analyses of RCTs (Bolívar et al., 2021, Secades-Villa et al., 2020) reported that CM was effective in promoting abstinence from various substances, although benefits did not persist at follow-ups (but see Ginley et al., 2021). In summary, Phase 2 and 3 studies demonstrated that CM promoted abstinence from and reductions in substance use, at least when contingencies were in effect, and suggested that CM can benefit other areas of life functioning.

Contingency management studies in usual care settings: phase 4

Phase 4 involves intervention evaluations in usual-care settings. RCTs of CM’s effectiveness in usual care settings have been evaluated with prize-based CM, which utilizes contingent but probabilistic incentives that vary in magnitude (Petry and Alessi, 2008). A few RCTs conducted in community-based clinics (Olmstead and Petry, 2009, Petry et al., 2005, Petry et al., 2007) suggested that prize-based CM is less costly to implement than voucher-based CM, while being similarly effective in promoting abstinence from cocaine and opioids. Given that incentive costs are a common barrier to implementing CM in community-based clinics (Petry, 2010), these trials represent important dissemination efforts.

Taken together, contingent substance-free alternatives such as palatable stimuli (preclinical research) and monetary incentives (Phases 1–4) have been found to reduce substance use. Well-controlled preclinical and Phase 1 research helped pave the way for the development of CM, an evidence-based intervention that remains to be implemented at scale. Because treatment improvements are not always maintained when CM is discontinued (e.g., Novak et al., 2022), long-term access to contingent substance-free rewards may be necessary for some people in recovery. Despite its detractors (e.g., Gagnon et al., 2021), barriers to dissemination should continue to be investigated given CM’s effectiveness.

Utility of social interaction as an alternative to substance use

The value of social support and access to substance-free social interaction has long been considered protective against harmful substance use and important for recovery (Fisher, 1996, Rachlin, 2000). Like palatable and monetary alternatives, social support has been provided contingent on substance-free behavior, and the overall effects of enriching social contexts on substance use also have been examined. Social interaction and substance use in nonhumans: preclinical research

Preclinical studies demonstrated that contingent access to a social peer can reduce drug self-administration in rats. Subjects typically were placed in two-lever operant chambers, wherein pressing one lever produced either a substance reinforcer (self-administration) or a social reinforcer involving limited access to a peer rat (Venniro and Shaham, 2020). Self-administration of alcohol (Augier et al., 2023, Marchant et al., 2023), opioids (St. Onge et al., 2022, Venniro et al., 2018), and stimulants (Venniro et al., 2018, Venniro et al., 2021) were reduced by various parametric adjustments, such as decreasing delay to peer access (Venniro et al., 2018, Venniro et al., 2021). However, increasing the duration of peer access did not reduce drug self-administration (Augier et al., 2023, Marcus et al., 2022), and self-administration sometimes was lower in females than males (Augier et al., 2023, St. Onge et al., 2022).

Other methodologies suggest that social variables can affect substance use. Peitz et al. (2013) and Smith (2012) found that cocaine self-administration was lower when rats were with peers who did not have the opportunity to self-administer cocaine compared to when they were with peers who could. In another approach, distal access to enriching environments, including group housing with peers, protected against self-administration of amphetamine (Bardo et al., 2001) and opioids (Hofford et al., 2017). Preclinical studies thus suggest that self-administration can be reduced by exposing subjects to non-using peers and to socially enriching environments, which are implicated in subsequent phases.

Social interaction and substance use in humans: phase 1

Early Phase 1 studies by Griffiths et al., 1974, Griffiths et al., 1977 demonstrated that drinking was reduced in people with alcohol use disorder (AUD) when social interaction was restricted contingent upon drinking, suggesting that social interaction functions as an alternative reinforcer. Observational research indicated that substance-free peer interaction was negatively correlated with alcohol-related harms (Murphy et al., 2005) and that some social activities may substitute for and reduce drinking (e.g., “having a conversation”) whereas others are complementary and associated with increased drinking (e.g., “going to a party”) (Weinsztok et al., 2023b).

Other observational studies demonstrated that, like the preclinical studies by Peitz et al. (2013) and Smith (2012), having peers who engage in little to no substance use is associated with reductions in harmful use (Acuff et al., 2020, Tucker et al., 2015). These findings are reminiscent of early human laboratory studies which showed that one’s drinking rate can be influenced by a peer’s drinking rate (Caudill and Marlatt, 1975; Garlington and Dericco, 1977) and is elevated in social, relative to isolated, contexts (Doty and de Wit, 1995, Lindman, 1982). These Phase 1 findings extended the generality of findings from preclinical research and informed the interventions described next.

Evaluations of interventions involving social interaction: phases 2 and 3

Several intervention evaluations have found that access to social interaction can reduce harmful substance use. The Community Reinforcement Approach (CRA) involves designing a substance-free social environment by providing substance-free activities in which clients may engage and by training family members and friends to reinforce abstinence and punish harmful substance use (Hunt and Azrin, 1973). CRA reduced harmful use of alcohol (Hunt and Azrin, 1973, Smith et al., 1998), opioids (Abbott et al., 1998, Schottenfeld et al., 2000), and cocaine (Schottenfeld et al., 2000, Secades-Villa et al., 2011). Systematic reviews further suggest that a derivative of CRA can promote treatment entry and treatment engagement (Archer et al., 2020, Roozen et al., 2010).

Mutual help groups provide social support for recovery, the most prominent of which is Alcoholics Anonymous (AA; Kaskutas et al., 2002; Tonigan, 2022). Though less amenable to RCT investigation because of anonymous participation, a Cochrane review suggested that limited RCTs of AA and 12-Step Facilitation treatments supported drinking-related improvements (Kelly et al., 2020). For example, a few RCTs of Network Support, a 12-Step Facilitation treatment that encourages individuals to build a support system of non-drinking peers, has been found to promote abstinence and increase AA attendance (Litt et al., 2007, Litt et al., 2016).

More limited research on other mutual help groups has yielded mixed findings. Most notably, Self-Management and Recovery Training, or SMART, is a mutual help group that, unlike AA, is secular and does not view addiction as a disease (Horvath and Yeterian, 2014). While one longitudinal study found that SMART was less effective than a 12-Step Facilitation treatment in reducing alcohol use (Brooks and Penn, 2003), another suggested no differences when accounting for baseline treatment goals (Zemore et al., 2018). The effectiveness of both AA and SMART is improved by having a close friend or sponsor (Hester et al., 2013, Pagano et al., 2013, Zemore et al., 2018). Taken together, Phase 2 and 3 research demonstrated that social support from peers, family members, and fellow mutual help group members is associated with reductions in harmful substance use.

Interventions involving social interaction in usual care settings: phase 4

Investigation of treatments involving social interaction is sparse in usual care settings and RCTs are lacking. Nonetheless, the dissemination potential of some treatments has been investigated. For example, Adolescent-CRA, a variant of CRA in which caregivers design substance-free social environments for their adolescent children (Godley et al., 2001), has been successfully disseminated to criminal justice-involved youth (Henderson et al., 2016) and across racial groups (Godley et al., 2011). While the dearth of Phase 4 studies highlights the need for more dissemination research, findings from previous phases demonstrate that substance-free social interaction can be an effective alternative to harmful substance use and that disruption of social interactions that promote substance use can reduce harmful use.

Utility of physical activity as an alternative to substance use

Physical activity is often considered beneficial in reducing harmful substance use and promoting recovery (e.g., Marlatt and Gordon, 1985) and may function as a viable substance-free alternative (Weinsztok et al., 2024). Relevant mechanisms implicated in physical activity, such as mood improvement (Mahindru et al., 2023) and sleep improvement (Dolezal et al., 2017), also likely contribute to its overall health-promotive utility in changing harmful substance use. However, unlike monetary and social rewards, physical activity is rarely provided contingent on behavior; rather, effects of physical activity on subsequent substance use are typically assessed. Physical activity and substance use: preclinical and phase 1 research

Preclinical research found that, when given free access to physical activity (i.e., a running wheel) over extended periods of time, rodents generally self-administered less alcohol (McMillan et al., 1995), amphetamine (Kanarek et al., 1995), cocaine (female rats in Cosgrove et al., 2002), and methamphetamine (Miller et al., 2012); however, reductions in substance use were not always observed (male rats in Cosgrove et al., 2002; Ozburn et al., 2008). Nevertheless, other experiments showed that distal access to wheel-running promoted subsequent reductions in self-administration of cocaine (Smith et al., 2008, Smith et al., 2016) and other psychoactive drugs (Aarde et al., 2015).

By comparison, human Phase 1 results were decidedly mixed. Phase 1 experiments demonstrated that brief bouts of physical activity subsequently decreased cigarette cravings and increased delays to smoking (Faulkner et al., 2010, Kurti and Dallery, 2014, Taylor and Katomeri, 2007). But other research indicated that hypothetical choices between exercise and alcohol consumption were independent of one other (Weinsztok et al., 2024). In human observational studies, physical activity was sometimes associated with reductions in substance use (e.g., Meshesha et al., 2018) but was also associated with increased use among people who exercised to offset alcoholic beverage calorie consumption (Abrantes et al., 2017b) or exercised with a substance-using social group (e.g., athletic teams; Terry-McElrath et al., 2011). Thus, whereas preclinical studies showed that substance use can often be reduced by access to physical activity, findings from Phase 1 studies were mixed. This contrasts with the consistent supportive evidence from Phase 1 studies of monetary and social rewards. However, as noted, differences in how physical activity and other rewards were provided may contribute to variations in findings.

Physical activity intervention evaluations: phases 2 and 3

Phase 2 studies showed that, to varying extents, physical activity interventions promoted reductions in harmful use of alcohol (Abrantes et al., 2017a), cannabis (Buchowski et al., 2011), and cigarettes (Ussher et al., 2008). However, Phase 3 RCT evaluations yielded mixed evidence. Some physical activity interventions reduced cigarette smoking (Horn et al., 2011, Oncken et al., 2020) and alcohol consumption (Abrantes et al., 2024a, Brown et al., 2014), whereas others did not affect smoking (Abrantes et al., 2024b, Ussher et al., 2015) or alcohol use (Roessler et al., 2017); further, physical activity promoted reductions in depressive symptoms in one RCT (Abrantes et al., 2024b) but not others (Abrantes et al., 2024a, Brown et al., 2014, Ussher et al., 2015). Discrepant findings could be related to differences in study populations (e.g., pregnant women in Ussher et al., 2015; postmenopausal women in Oncken et al., 2020) and variability in intervention components (e.g., strength training was only included in Oncken et al., 2020). Systematic reviews of RCTs indicated that findings should be considered inconclusive due to low certainty of evidence (Ussher et al., 2019; Thompson et al., 2020).

Overall, evidence appears mixed concerning whether physical activity is associated with reduced substance use. Given that the strongest support was found in preclinical and Phase 1 experimental studies, further research in controlled settings may offer suggestions for physical activity interventions. Phase 4 studies on the effectiveness of physical activity interventions in usual care settings remain to be conducted. Pending further research, physical activity may best be considered as a potentially beneficial ancillary component of other established substance use disorder (SUD) treatments (Murphy et al., 2022).

Naturalistic studies of substance-related behavior change

Naturalistic studies of contexts surrounding reductions in harmful substance use laid an empirical foundation for individual and community-based interventions. For example, a series of longitudinal studies of natural recovery attempts among untreated persons with AUD (Tucker et al., 2020b, Tucker et al., 2021) revealed personal economic contexts that were favorable to recovery, particularly stable moderation drinking, a preferred recovery goal relative to abstinence among many persons with alcohol use disorder (Tucker et al., 2020a). Compared to persons who abstained or resumed heavy drinking during the year after stopping heavy drinking, those who maintained stable moderation (i.e., low-risk drinking practices without associated harms or heavy drinking episodes; National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism, 2025) had unique monetary allocation patterns to different commodities: Although all outcome groups spent relatively more on alcohol than saving for the future prior to quitting heavy drinking, the moderation outcome group had more balanced pre-resolution discretionary spending on alcohol and saving compared to the abstinent and heavy drinking outcome groups, who had similar spending patterns heavily favoring alcohol expenditures. Then, after stopping heavy drinking, the moderation outcome group increased their expenditures in multiple commodity classes, resulting in the collective receipt of more goods and services than the other outcome groups, which presumably reinforced their recovery and included limited drinking without associated harms. Similarly, another study found that diminished access to alternative rewards among adolescents of lower socioeconomic status was associated with greater substance use compared to adolescents of higher socioeconomic status (Leventhal et al., 2015).

Other naturalistic research investigated whether community characteristics were associated with individual-level harmful substance use. Wiley et al. (2022) found that greater access to residential green space was associated with lower rates of binge drinking and cigarette smoking (though greater likelihood of cannabis use). Likewise, Martin et al. (2020) reported that persons living in greener neighborhoods were less likely to smoke cigarettes and, among those who had ever smoked, were more likely to have stopped. In another study that assessed multiple environmental characteristics (Tucker et al., 2025), people with lower-risk AUD profiles resided in community ZIP code areas with lower poverty and greater access to substance-free rewards, including educational services, religious organizations, fresh foods, and sport, recreation, and fitness facilities. However, evidence is mixed regarding associations between alcohol outlet density and individual-level drinking outcomes (Gmel et al., 2016). Overall, these studies using non-clinical samples suggest that reduced substance-related risk is associated with having substance-free rewards in one’s natural environment.

Environmental contexts and substance use change processes and outcomes in treatment samples

Recent studies have examined associations between engagement in substance-free activities and treatment outcomes using clinical samples. In a secondary analysis of data from Project MATCH (Mattson et al., 1993), a large multi-site RCT of AUD treatments, Kuhlemeier et al. (2024) found that alcohol-free reward engagement during the early post-treatment interval predicted subsequent reductions in drinking and improved life functioning a year after treatment ended. Relatedly, Murphy et al., 2012, Murphy et al., 2019 developed and evaluated the Substance-Free Activity Session (SFAS) aimed at reducing substance use by increasing engagement in substance-free activities. Guided by behavioral theories of choice and combined with a brief motivational intervention (e.g., Borsari and Carey, 2000; Murphy et al., 2001), the SFAS involves individuals stating their personal goals, assessing how their current substance use may interfere with goal attainment, and identifying substance-free alternatives in their natural environments. RCTs by Murphy et al., 2012, Murphy et al., 2019 found that heavy-drinking college students who received the SFAS reported reductions in harmful alcohol use in follow-ups lasting up to 16 months. Moreover, changes in drinking were mediated by engagement in substance-free rewards and use of protective behavioral strategies.

Like the SFAS, Life Enhancement Treatment for Substance Use (LETS ACT) encourages engaging in substance-free activities and identifying life values. In contrast to the SFAS, LETS ACT is typically conducted over multiple sessions in a group setting, targets individuals with both SUD and depressive symptoms, and utilizes behavioral contracting and self-monitoring (Daughters et al., 2016). Relative to control conditions, LETS ACT has been shown to increase environmental reward and reduce depressive symptoms (Daughters et al., 2008), substance use, and negative consequences (Daughters et al., 2018) among individuals receiving residential treatment.

Collectively, these studies suggest that behavioral allocation to rewarding substance-free activities can promote positive changes in harmful substance use. Further, recent treatment innovations (e.g., SFAS) offer interventions to increase engagement in substance-free alternatives that are lower threshold, lower cost, and therefore, in principle, easier to disseminate to persons in need, although dissemination research is lacking. Community-based intervention programs focused on prevention are discussed next.

Community-based interventions

The Icelandic Prevention Model (IPM) targeting adolescent substance use is conceptually similar to interventions guided by behavioral theories of choice but is unique in its scale of application. The IPM involves working with community stakeholders to develop and implement community-wide preventive interventions, including encouraging adolescents’ participation in substance-free activities (e.g., sports; Kristjansson et al., 2020a, Kristjansson et al., 2020b; Sigfusdottir et al., 2009). The IPM was implemented throughout Iceland over a 12-year period (Kristjansson et al., 2010) and in Tarragona, Spain over a 5-year period (Meyers et al., 2023). Evaluations showed that the program delayed onset and levels of adolescent substance use, and increased engagement in substance-free activities. While the availability of alternative activities likely plays a large part in the effectiveness of the IPM, it includes other features that may constrain access to substances (e.g., increased supervision, parental monitoring, restrictions on access to substances).

Housing First is another community-based program that provides housing to unhoused people regardless of whether they use substances (Kerman et al., 2021, Kertesz et al., 2009). While Housing First could in theory reduce barriers to obtaining healthy substance-free alternatives, supportive evidence is mixed. Other programs that provide housing contingent upon abstinence and SUD treatment enrollment are associated with similar (Padgett et al., 2006, Somers et al., 2015, Tsemberis et al., 2004) or lower (Padgett et al., 2011, Tsemberis et al., 2012) rates of substance use and treatment enrollment compared to Housing First. This suggests that contingent housing may, in some cases, be unnecessarily restrictive and that noncontingent housing may provide stability.

Finally, the Healing of the Canoe is a community-based program that teaches Native American adolescents about their tribal community history, provides information about community resources (e.g., tribal Elders), and includes substance use prevention education. Donovan et al. (2015) found that the program promoted lower substance use and higher participation in cultural practices, suggesting that cultural practices offered rewarding substance-free alternatives.

Taken together, these community-based programs that include increasing access to substance-free rewards show promise in reducing or preventing harmful substance use. However, further research with proper comparison groups is needed because causal mechanisms were difficult to identify in these multi-component programs.

Summary, limitations, and future directions

As this review documents, decades of behavioral research, ranging from preclinical experiments to community-based intervention studies, has repeatedly shown that, with some qualifications, harmful substance use can be reduced by providing access to healthy substance-free alternative rewards. Although the lack of control conditions or experimental manipulation in some studies limit causal inferences, the convergence of findings with controlled experimental research is striking. Thus, as a generalized treatment and prevention strategy across a wide range of settings, levels, and populations, substance-free alternative activities and commodities can be arranged to decrease human substance use and associated harms.

In the context of substance use, the findings provide robust support for behavioral theories of choice and modern behavioral economic extensions that focus on resource allocation patterns among available activities and commodities available under different constraints (Hursh, 2014, Hursh et al., 2013, Rachlin, 1997, Vuchinich and Tucker, 1988). Of central importance are findings across multiple research phases indicating that changing constraints on access to alternative substance-free rewards affects substance-related behaviors—specifically, increasing access to rewards often reduces harmful substance use. These cross-commodity relations deserve greater investigation and emphasis in treatment and preventive interventions for harmful substance use. Traditionally, the focus of interventions (ranging from treatment to drug control policy) has been on reducing access to substances themselves without much consideration of the broader environment surrounding substance availability, including the extent of access to substance-free rewards. This narrow focus on choice of a single commodity class and manipulations aimed at reducing preference for drugs has well-documented limitations as a drug control strategy (Ennett et al., 1994, Kleinman and Morris, 2021, Wildeman and Wang, 2017). Conceivably, increasing access to substance-free rewards while also providing some nonpunitive constraints on drug access may prove optimal for select populations and settings, but this depends on a more complete understanding of the dynamic role of the broader environment in initiating, maintaining, and reducing harmful substance use (Witkiewitz and Tucker, 2024). Relevant research is in its infancy.

This review identified several activities and commodities that effectively functioned as substitutes for substance use under specific conditions. Research on Contingency Management, for example, supported the utility of providing contingent monetary incentives to promote abstinence from substance use, both during and after treatment (Higgins et al., 2013, Silverman et al., 2019, Petry and Alessi, 2008). Evidence for beneficial effects of physical activity was mixed in human research, although it functioned effectively as a substitute for substance use in preclinical animal studies. Extensive evidence supports the key role of access to social interaction in substance use patterns, but its relations with substance use are complex and deserve more research attention. Under some circumstances, access to non-substance-using peers and social networks (e.g., mutual help groups) functioned as substitutes and were associated with reduced use (Hester et al., 2013, Pagano et al., 2013, Zemore et al., 2018). However, observational research suggested that the presence of substance-using social peers predicted substance use (Acuff et al., 2020, Tucker et al., 2015), including when one is also engaging in physical activity with peers (Terry-McElrath et al., 2011).

With these exceptions, the research reviewed across the translational research continuum on the whole supported the benefits of substance-free alternative rewards to reduce harmful substance use. The body of work highlights the need to expand conceptualizations of variables influencing substance-related behaviors beyond individual-level variables to include the broader environment surrounding substance use. The review thus reaches a similar conclusion to other recent reviews that used different review methodologies and had somewhat different content foci (e.g., Acuff et al., 2019, Acuff et al., 2023; Fazzino et al., 2019; Strickland and Acuff, 2023).

Limitations

Due to significant heterogeneity across topic areas, this was not a quantitative review and analyses of effect sizes could not be conducted. Although beyond the scope of the present review, discussion of the range of methodologies used in the reviewed studies (e.g., research design, measurement) is available elsewhere (e.g., Carroll, 1995; Critchfield et al., 1998; Kazdin, 2023; Sidman, 1960), and the tables accompanying each section provide methodological details that allow for inspection of study quality. Potential mechanisms responsible for reductions in substance use and associated measures were not always clear. This issue deserves greater investigation (Abrantes and Blevins, 2019, Witkiewitz et al., 2022).

Future directions: prevention of harmful substance use

Programs focused on prevention of harmful substance use have been slower to develop than treatment interventions (Biglan and Van Ryzin, 2019, Stockings et al., 2016), and many individual-level interventions are not equipped to address the socioecological contexts in which substance-related harms occur (Holland, 1978, Witkiewitz and Tucker, 2024). As a general approach to the prevention of unhealthy behavior, Chater and Loewenstein (2023) argued for greater recognition of systemic (or s-frame) approaches, in contrast to individualistic (or i-frame) approaches that are more limited in focus. Behavioral researchers and policymakers have historically emphasized i-frame interventions aimed at individual substance use in natural environments that typically are substance-rich. While i-frame interventionists recognize that behavior occurs within a broader environment external to the individual (Hallsworth, 2023), the levels at which behavior change is targeted within these interventions are limited in time, place, and scope.

As recently discussed (Acuff et al., 2023, Rachlin et al., 2018, Vuchinich et al., 2023, Witkiewitz and Tucker, 2024), behavioral theories that address the broader context of substance use can be extended from individual-focused interventions to interventions that address meso- and macro-level variables in the community and beyond. There are several likely reasons why systemic prevention of harmful substance use has not been prioritized in the U.S. First, American cultural ideology emphasizes “personal responsibility” (Skinner, 1971). Efforts to publicly fund prevention are at odds with this ideology because people are deemed responsible for their drug use, which likely results in stigmatization of persons with SUD by laypeople and health professionals alike (McGinty and Barry, 2020, van Boekel et al., 2013). But the research reviewed here makes clear that harmful substance use is a function of environmental contingencies, indicating that the causal notion of personal responsibility is misplaced.

Second, declining trust in the U.S. federal government may also contribute to the lower value placed on publicly funded prevention efforts. This was made clear during the COVID-19 pandemic (e.g., Thompson et al., 2021) when trust in public health officials and programs declined. Moreover, some previous government-funded drug prevention programs aligned with the ideology of personal responsibility were found to be ineffective despite widespread implementation (e.g., Drug Abuse Resistance Education; Ennett et al., 1994). While much of the community-based intervention research described in 5 was federally funded (e.g., Housing First was funded by the US Department of Housing and Urban Development in Tsemberis et al., 2012), these s-frame approaches have not been widely disseminated, adopted as best practices, or implemented at scale. Researchers, practitioners, and policymakers thus face several barriers to evidence-based management of substance-related harms that utilize s-frame interventions and the tools of public health. However, likely due to the wide-reaching nature of the opioid epidemic, the public now favors access to SUD services (Sparks et al., 2023) and uses less stigmatizing language regarding SUD (Conway et al., 2022, McLaren et al., 2023).

Further research is needed on the potential of substance-free rewards as a matter of drug-related policy. Though unrelated to substance use, behavioral economic research conducted by Hursh et al. (2020) and Strickland et al., 2022a, Strickland et al., 2022b provided excellent examples of novel ways to investigate public health issues related to COVID-19 (e.g., refusing to mask) using simulated discounting and purchase tasks (for a review, see Reed et al., 2022). This work is important because it offers a relatively quick way to investigate public health challenges and harms that require rapid solutions and cannot be investigated using a more conventional and typically slower translational research process. Relatedly, substance-free alternatives in the form of hypothetical next-day activity scenarios (e.g., going to the gym; going to work; having an exam) have been shown to reduce demand for alcohol (Skidmore and Murphy, 2011, Miller et al., 2023) and cannabis (Ferguson et al., 2021, Miller et al., 2025). Thus, simulation studies can quickly provide important information about the types of substance-free rewards that may reduce and prevent harmful substance use. These research methodologies offer the possibility of interventions guided by behavioral choice theories to have broader impact on public health.

Conclusion

In conclusion, a solid empirical foundation spanning the translational research continuum supports interventions guided by behavioral theories of choice that focus on increasing access to alternative substance-free rewards. Reducing harmful substance use requires not only intervening when harmful use arises but also requires preventative measures that include providing community access to substance-free alternatives and making use of the tools of public health (e.g., Chater and Loewenstein, 2023; Rachlin et al., 2018; Tucker et al., 2023; Tucker and Grimley, 2011; Witkiewitz and Tucker, 2024). These views echo a statement made decades ago by the behavior analyst Holland (1978):

If the very theory on which behavior therapy is based is correct, then the solution to a behavioral problem cannot rest in the specially arranged contingencies in the special environment in the clinic. The contingencies of the natural environment must be modified if the problem is to be corrected (p. 166).

Thus, a wholistic approach to effectively managing harmful substance use requires developing and sustaining environments that are rich with alternative sources of substance-free rewards.
