Exploring the electrocatalytic potential of CdTiO3 for sustainable overall water splitting
Authors: Eshagh Irandoost, Neda Sadat Barekati, Hossein Farsi, Shokufeh Moghiminia, Ahmad Hajizade
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The development of efficient, cost–effective, and stable bifunctional electrocatalysts for overall water splitting is of paramount importance in alkaline mediums. In this study, we report the synthesis of the CdTiO3 nanoparticles using the simple low–temperature sol–gel approach. CdTiO3 as an electrodic material exhibits enhanced catalytic performance, advantageous reaction kinetics, and remarkable longevity for both the oxygen evolution reaction (OER) and hydrogen evolution reaction (HER). The nanostructured CdTiO3 demonstrates the overpotentials of 270 and 320 mV to attain a current density of 10 mA cm−2 for OER and HER in 1.0 M KOH electrolyte, respectively. It reveals the low Tafel slope values of 63 and 79 mV dec−1 for OER and HER, respectively. This work proposes a sustainable strategy for developing enhanced bifunctional electrocatalysts for hydrogen fuel production via overall water splitting, which has the potential to replace noble metals in a wide range of renewable energy systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-11358-3.

Introduction

The growing energy demands of modern society and the pressing need to reduce greenhouse gas emissions have driven global research efforts toward clean and renewable energy technologies1. Among various clean energy carriers, hydrogen stands out due to its high energy density and zero–emission potential when used in fuel cells2,3. A promising method of producing hydrogen sustainably is water splitting, in which water molecules are decomposed into hydrogen and oxygen using an external energy input4,5. However, for water splitting to become commercially viable, efficient, stable, and cost–effective electrocatalysts are needed to overcome the sluggish kinetics of both the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) and the oxygen evolution reaction (OER)6–8.

Electrocatalysts reduce the overpotential required for water splitting by lowering the activation energy of these reactions9. While noble metals such as platinum–based materials (for HER) and iridium/ruthenium–based compounds (for OER) have demonstrated high electrocatalytic activity, their scarcity and high cost hinder their large–scale implementation10,11. Thus, research has increasingly focused on developing alternative, earth–abundant materials with bifunctional electrocatalytic capabilities capable of driving both HER and OER simultaneously12.

Mixed metal oxides, known for their structural stability and tunable electronic properties, have gained attention as promising materials for energy–related applications13,14. One such material, cadmium titanate (CdTiO3), has been extensively studied as a photocatalyst for HER and OER, where it has demonstrated high stability and promising photocatalytic activity15,16. These favorable characteristics, particularly its structural robustness under reaction conditions, motivate the investigation of CdTiO3 as a potential bifunctional electrocatalyst.

The structure of CdTiO3, with its highly ordered lattice composed of Cd2+ and Ti4+ ions, offers several advantages that could translate into good electrocatalytic behavior. Its capacity for surface electronic modulation, ability to provide abundant active sites, and high stability under reactive environments make it an attractive candidate for high–performance, low–cost electrocatalysts17. Additionally, CdTiO3 allows for incorporating dopants or defect engineering, which can further enhance its electrocatalytic performance by optimizing surface and electronic properties13,17.

While CdTiO3 has not yet been reported as an electrocatalyst, its demonstrated performance in photocatalysis and stability under operational conditions provide a compelling rationale for further exploration in the field of electrocatalysis15,16. This article aims to explore the electrocatalytic behavior of CdTiO3 in detail, focusing on its potential as a bifunctional electrocatalyst for OER and HER. We investigate the structural, electronic, and surface properties of CdTiO3, and suggest the mechanisms through which it may facilitate overall water splitting.

By examining the electrochemical performance of CdTiO3 and comparing it with other electrocatalysts, we aim to provide new insights into its potential for renewable electrocatalytic hydrogen production. This study seeks to contribute to the broader field of electrocatalysis and offers a foundation for future research into CdTiO3’s applications in sustainable energy technologies.

Experimental section

Materials

Cadmium (II) nitrate tetrahydrate (Cd(NO3)2·4H2O) as the cadmium source, tetrapropyl orthotitanate ((CH3CH2CH2O)4Ti) as the titanium source, ethylene glycol (HOCH2CH2OH), ethanol (CH3CH2OH), nitric acid (HNO3), and potassium hydroxide (KOH) were purchased from Sigma–Aldrich.

Preparation ofCdTiO3 electrocatalyst

A modified low–low-temperature sol–gel method was used to prepare nanostructured CdTiO3, where all quantities and conditions were optimized according to our previous works18,19. Initially, 8 mmol of cadmium (II) nitrate tetrahydrate was dissolved in 32 mmol of ethylene glycol, followed by the gradual addition of 8 mmol of alcoholic solution of tetrapropyl orthotitanate to the above solution. The resulting solution was stirred until a completely clear and homogeneous solution was obtained. Subsequently, one drop of nitric acid was added as a catalyst to the resulting solution, and then the solution was placed in an ultrasonic bath for 60 min. In the next step, the sample was left at room temperature (25 °C) for 24 h, and the resulting gel was then kept at 40 °C for 5 h. Afterward, it was heated in a controlled manner up to 170 °C. Finally, the sample was calcined at 550 °C for 5 h. These conditions have been optimized during a series of experiments to achieve good crystallinity.

Material characterizations

To explore the crystalline phase of the synthesized CdTiO3 sample, X–ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was performed using Bruker D8 Advance X–ray diffractometer. To investigate the morphology of the constructed CdTiO3 sample, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis was conducted employing a Philips CM120 HT instrument. The specific surface area and pore volume of the produced sample were determined utilizing Belsorp mini II. To examine the band gap of the assembled CdTiO3 sample, the UV–Vis diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (DRS) was employed using AvaSpec–2048–TEC spectrophotometers.

Electrochemical measurements

The thin film working electrode was designed utilizing the electrophoretic deposition (EPD) method, which we have already widely used for deposition of different materials from metal–organic frameworks20–23 to organic–inorganic hybrids24 and to different mixed oxides containing metal titanates18,19 metal molybdates25–27 and metal tungstates28–30. All electrochemical studies were carried out at 25 °C in the presence of a 1.0 M KOH electrolyte, employing a standard three–electrode cell setup. This included an Ag/AgCl electrode as the reference electrode, a platinum mesh as the counter electrode, and the electrophoretically deposited CdTiO3 electrode as the working electrode. Cyclic voltammograms (CVs) were recorded using Solartron Electrochemical Interface SI 1287, and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were conducted utilizing Solartron Phase Gain Analyzer SI 1260.

Results and discussion

During the sol–gel process, the reaction between nitrate ions and ethylene glycol produces a Cd–carboxylate complex. Also, the reaction between propoxy groups of tetrapropyl ortotitanate and ethylene glycol creates a network by producing propanol molecules. The details of such reactions have already been reported18,31,32. Then, by calcinating the prepared materials and producing NO2, H2O, and CO2, nitrate ions and hydrocarbon parts of the sample were removed, and nanostructured CdTiO3 was synthesized. The crystalline phase structure of the constructed CdTiO3 sample was analyzed using the XRD method, and the results are presented in Fig. 1. The acquired diffraction pattern for the as–prepared CdTiO3 sample aligns perfectly with the card number JCPDS: 29–027733, delivering no peaks attributable to cubic CdO, anatase TiO2, and rutile TiO2. This finding indicates that the synthesized CdTiO3 sample is of a high–purity nature34. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the assembled CdTiO3 sample demonstrates the diffraction peaks of (003), (101), (012), (104), (110), (015), (113), (021), (024), (116), (018), (214), and (300) facets at 2θ = 17.96°, 20.48°, 23.04°, 31.20°, 34.24°, 36.16°, 38.96°, 40.28°, 47.00°, 50.80°, 53.44°, 59.36°, and 61.32°, respectively. All diffraction peaks can be readily indexed to the ilmenite phase of CdTiO3, characterized by lattice parameters a = b = 5.237 Å and c = 14.834 Å. The mean crystallite size of the rhombohedral CdTiO3 was calculated utilizing the Debye–Scherrer equation, yielding a value of 41 nm. Also, the sharp peaks certify a good crystallinity for our prepared CdTiO3, which could utilize a better charge separation inside the CdTiO3, and thus, a better catalytic activity35.

The nitrogen adsorption–desorption curves of the prepared CdTiO3 are depicted in Fig. 3, which reveals an IV–type isotherm with an H1–type hysteresis loop (according to IUPAC classifications)36. The specific surface area, total pore volume, and mean pore diameter of nano–CdTiO3 were evaluated using the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) technique and were 10.14 m2 g−1, 3.45 × 10−2 cm3 g−1 and 15.12 nm, respectively. Based on the Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) method conducted to assess pore size distribution, as illustrated in Fig. S1, it can be concluded that the majority of particles possess a pore diameter of 2.12 nm. Consequently, the synthesized CdTiO3 sample is expected to exhibit a mesoporous structure37 which is in complete agreement with the results obtained from BET analysis.

The analysis of Mott–Schottky was performed to assess the electrical characteristics of the semiconductor CdTiO3. Given that the Mott–Schottky approach explores the connection between the capacitance of the space–charge layer (CSC) of a semiconductor and the operational potential42 we initially ascertained a potential region using the CV technique at a scan rate of 10 mV s−1 in the electrolyte of 1.0 M KOH solution, where no Faradaic process was observed, as portrayed in Fig. S3. To confirm that the detected behavior in Fig. S3 denotes the capacitive nature of nano–CdTiO3 electrode material, we utilized the CV studies within the identified potential window of −0.5 to + 0.25 V vs. Ag/AgCl to scrutinize the impact of diverse scan rates from 10 to 50 mV s−1 in 1.0 M KOH electrolyte (Fig. S4). As depicted in Fig. S4, an increase in the scan rate results in elevated anodic and cathodic current intensities. This observation suggests that such a phenomenon can be attributed to the capacitive behavior of the as–synthesized CdTiO3 sample. Ultimately, the Mott–Schottky studies were conducted for the CdTiO3 electrode material in 1.0 M KOH alkaline electrolyte at a frequency of 10 kHz, and the outcomes are illustrated in Fig. 5(A). However, it is crystal clear that determining and designating a linear range of this curve presents a considerable challenge. On the other hand, at such high frequencies, the capacitance of the space–charge layer is independent of the external potential. Therefore, within the range of potentials where space–charge layer charging transpires exclusively, the Nyquist curves must coincide precisely with each other. For this objective, the Nyquist curves were acquired for the CdTiO3 electrode material at a frequency range of 100 kHz to 0.1 Hz at diverse potentials in 1.0 M KOH aqueous solution (Fig. S5). As displayed in Fig. S5, the Nyquist curves within the potential range of −0.15 to −0.05 V vs. Ag/AgCl demonstrate a significant overlap, indicative of a non–Faradaic process. Consequently, this potential range was employed for the linear fitting of the Mott–Schottky curve, with the resulting data presented in Fig. 5(B). The positive slope of the Mott–Schottky plot in Fig. 5(B) indicates that the as–synthesized CdTiO3 sample is an n–type semiconductor. Furthermore, the horizontal intercept in Fig. 5(B) reveals the flat–band potential (EFB), which was determined to be −1.74 V vs. Ag/AgCl (−0.71 V vs. RHE) for the as–prepared CdTiO3 electrocatalyst. It is unequivocal that in n–type semiconductors, the EFB corresponds to the conduction band potential (ECB). Accordingly, the ECB for the CdTiO3 nanostructured electrocatalyst is approximately − 3.79 eV. Grounded in the estimated band gap energy and the calculated ECB, the valence band potential (EVB) for the nano–CdTiO3 electrocatalyst was computed to be −7.36 eV. Based on the experimental results obtained from the analyses, the experimental electronic scheme of the constructed CdTiO3 sample has been sketched and is shown in Fig. 5(C).

The electrochemical surface area (ECSA) is a critical parameter for assessing the intrinsic catalytic activity of electrode materials, as it reflects the number of electrochemically accessible active sites. Since direct measurement of ECSA is not feasible for non–flat electrodes, it can be estimated indirectly through the electrochemical double–layer capacitance (Cdl) in the non–Faradaic potential region. In this study, CV was conducted at various scan rates in a non–Faradaic window to determine the capacitive current response of the CdTiO3 electrode (see Fig. S4). As shown in Fig. S6, a linear relationship was observed between the current density and scan rate, and the slope of this linear fit corresponds to the Cdl value. The calculated Cdl of 1.9 mF cm−2 suggests a relatively large electrochemically active surface area, which likely contributes to the enhanced catalytic performance observed in both OER and HER processes.

To explore the electrocatalytic water splitting reactions on the surface of pure CdTiO3, a three–electrode system was employed in an alkaline environment. Initially, the electrocatalytic activity of CdTiO3 nanoparticles toward the OER was evaluated using the linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) technique at a scan rate of 10 mV s−1 in a 1.0 M KOH electrolyte (Fig. 6(A)). The thermodynamic potential for the OER, which is 1.23 V vs. RHE, is indicated by the dashed line in Fig. 6(A). From the LSV studies shown in Fig. 6(A), it can be observed that the bare electrode demonstrates an inferior electrocatalytic OER current density, revealing feeble performance for the OER. Moreover, the fabricated CdTiO3 electrode material for this study exhibits superior electrocatalytic activity, clearly indicating that the nano–CdTiO3 shows a better electrocatalytic response for OER compared to the bare electrode. A notable and continuous increase in current density around 1.40 V vs. RHE signifies outstanding oxygen evolution activity on the surface of the as–prepared CdTiO343. The acquired anodic polarization profile from the LSV experiment for the OER implies that the CdTiO3 electrode necessitates an overpotential of 270 mV to generate a current density of 10 mA cm−2, which is lower than the value newly documented for the IrO2 (η10 = 360 mV) benchmark electrocatalyst44.

In the context of investigating the kinetics of the electrocatalytic OER, we have plotted the Tafel curves in Fig. 6(B). A smaller Tafel slope corresponds to a faster kinetics reaction43. The Tafel slope for the as–synthesized CdTiO3 was evaluated to be 63 mV dec−1, which is smaller than the value reported for the IrO2 (76 mV dec−1) benchmark electrocatalyst44. The electrocatalytic performance of HER for the CdTiO3 catalyst was scrutinized in 1.0 M KOH aqueous solution using the identical three–electrode setup. The thermodynamic HER potential, which is 0.00 V vs. RHE, is marked by a dashed line (Fig. 7(A)). Based on the cathodic polarization curves presented in Fig. 7(A), it is evident that the CdTiO3 electrode exhibits superior hydrogen evolution activity compared to the bare electrode. The CdTiO3 electrocatalyst demands an overpotential of 320 mV to deliver a current density of 10 mA cm−2. The Tafel slope was calculated to be 79 mV dec−1 for the CdTiO3 catalyst, which is significantly lower than the Tafel slope for the bare electrode and even lower than the values reported for the platinum–based precious metal benchmark electrocatalysts (Fig. 7(B))48,49. This low Tafel slope (79 mV dec−1) implies faster HER kinetics on the surface of the CdTiO3 nanoparticles electrode, compared to other electrocatalysts that have been previously studied and reported (Table S1). To assess the long–term operational durability of the CdTiO3 electrocatalyst, chronopotentiometry tests were performed for both OER and HER in 1.0 M KOH solution. The measurements were conducted at constant current densities of + 10 mA cm−2 for OER and − 10 mA cm−2 for HER over a continuous period of 30 h. As presented in Fig. S7, the electrode exhibited stable potential profiles for both OER and HER throughout the 30 h testing period. For OER, the potential remained constant under anodic polarization, and a similar trend was observed for HER under cathodic conditions, indicating consistent catalytic performance throughout. These results clearly demonstrate the excellent electrochemical stability of CdTiO3 for overall water splitting. The observed long–term durability under both oxidative and reductive conditions highlights the promise of this material for future applications in sustainable energy conversion systems and practical alkaline electrolyzers.

Conclusion

In this work, a non–noble metal CdTiO3 electrocatalyst was successfully synthesized via a simple low–temperature sol–gel method. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report on the electrocatalytic overall water splitting performance of nanostructured CdTiO3. The material was characterized using XRD, TEM, BET, and DRS techniques. The CdTiO3 electrode demonstrated excellent bifunctional activity toward both HER and OER in alkaline media, requiring overpotentials of 320 mV and 270 mV at 10 mA cm−2, respectively, along with remarkable long–term durability. These results highlight the potential of CdTiO3 as a low–cost and efficient electrocatalyst for sustainable water splitting. Further studies are encouraged to explore the surface chemistry and catalytic mechanisms in greater depth.
