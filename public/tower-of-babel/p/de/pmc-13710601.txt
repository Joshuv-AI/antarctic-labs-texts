Side-to-side differences in hip bone mineral density and associated morphological factors
Authors: Keisuke Uemura, Yoshito Otake, Mazen Soufi, Sotaro Kono, Ryo Higuchi, Tomoki Asano, Hirokazu Mae, Tetsuro Tani, Kazuma Takashima, Yoshinobu Sato, Seiji Okada, Hidetoshi Hamada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSummaryIn the analysis of 82 participants using quantitative computed tomography (QCT) images, side-to-side differences in hip bone mineral density were 3.0%. Introduction

Osteoporosis is a chronic disease that leads to fragility fractures, which are closely associated with high mortality in older adults [1–3]. Osteoporosis is typically diagnosed by measuring bone mineral density (BMD) of the spine and hip using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA). In the clinical setting, hip BMD is often measured on a single side because bilateral analysis is time-consuming and costly. A strong correlation between the right and left hip BMD supports single-side measurement. However, several studies have reported measurable side-to-side differences in BMD [4]. These discrepancies may lead to the underdiagnosis of osteoporosis in approximately 2%–4% of individuals [5–7].

Previous studies reporting side-to-side differences in BMD have used bilateral DXA measurements and assessed the proportion of individuals whose side-to-side difference exceeded the least significant change (LSC) to account for DXA’s inherent measurement error. For instance, 47%–70% of individuals have exhibited differences exceeding the LSC in the total hip region [6–9]. However, information on the factors contributing to side-to-side differences in BMD is limited. Some studies have shown that patient background factors, such as age [8] and osteoarthritis [10], have an effect on BMD differences. However, the effects of morphological factors, such as bone structure and muscle mass, which are difficult to assess using DXA, remain uncertain.

Therefore, this study aimed to assess side-to-side differences in hip BMD using quantitative computed tomography (QCT) images and identify factors contributing to these discrepancies using a previously developed and validated automated deep learning-based method [11, 12].

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This prospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the participating institutions (approval number: 25110). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. A total of 110 volunteers who took part in a physical fitness testing event at the participating institution were enrolled.

CT acquisition

CT images of the bilateral total femurs were acquired using a Revolution CT scanner (GE Healthcare Japan, Tokyo, Japan), with a calibration phantom (B-MAS200, Kyoto Kagaku, Kyoto, Japan) placed beneath the participants. Imaging was performed using a tube voltage of 100 kVp, an in-plane pixel size of 0.684–0.977 mm, and a slice thickness of 0.625 mm. Image reconstruction was performed using filtered back projection with a standard kernel. The median radiation dose was 6.01 mSv (interquartile range [IQR]: 5.00–6.94 mSv).

BMD measurement from QCT images

BMD was automatically measured from the CT images using a previously developed and validated deep learning-based method with a correlation coefficient of 0.94 with the DXA measurements [12]. Briefly, a pretrained deep learning model was first used to automatically identify the femoral region and calibration phantom (Fig. 1a). Then, nine landmarks were identified in the proximal femur using another model: the head center, four points at the head-neck junction, the neck center, the tip of the lesser trochanter, and two points on the femoral shaft located 2 and 5 cm distal to the lesser trochanter [12, 13] (Fig. 1b). The femur volume was then rotated to a neutral position using the selected landmarks (Fig. 1b). After calibration with the phantom, the rotated images were projected anteroposteriorly [14] (Fig. 1c). Finally, the BMD measurement region was identified using the projected landmarks representing the total region in DXA measurements, referred to as CT-aBMD. All measurements were performed using MATLAB (version 9.8, The MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA).

Side-to-side comparison

CT-aBMD was measured for both sides and compared to calculate the correlation and side-to-side percentage differences in BMD. Although CT-aBMD measurements are fully reproducible (i.e., yield identical results when the same CT images are analyzed), no established LSC value is available for CT-aBMD. Therefore, LSC thresholds reported for DXA were used as reference values. Specifically, thresholds of 5.0%, defined in the International Society for Clinical Densitometry (ISCD) guidelines [15], and 1.8%, defined in the ISCD as the precision error and reported as the LSC in a previous study analyzing a Japanese cohort [9], were referenced for comparison. The number of individuals with a T-score of ≤ − 2.5 in the total hip region for each side was calculated. T-scores were standardized following the correction method recommended by the Japan Osteoporosis Society [1], in accordance with the ISCD guidelines [15].

Factor analysis

Factors previously identified to influence BMD were examined to determine whether they affected side-to-side differences in CT-aBMD. Particularly, the relationship between CT-aBMD and demographic characteristics, including sex, age, height, and body weight [16, 17], was analyzed. Additionally, the relationship between CT-aBMD and morphological factors, such as femoral anteversion and neck-shaft angle, was analyzed. Femoral anteversion was measured as the angle between the head-neck axis and the axis connecting the medial and lateral posterior condyles on the table-top plane. Neck-shaft angle was defined as the angle between the head-neck axis and the femoral shaft axis, which is the line connecting the points on the shaft located 2 and 5 cm distal to the lesser trochanter. Additionally, the correlation between side-to-side differences in BMD and gluteal muscle volume, which has been reported to affect BMD differences in patients with unilateral hip osteoarthritis [10], was examined. Gluteal muscle volume was automatically quantified from CT images using a pretrained deep learning model [18] (Fig. 1a). This model, based on a Bayesian U-Net architecture [13], was trained on manually segmented hip muscles from 50 patients. Its segmentation accuracy for gluteal muscles has been validated, with Dice coefficients ranging from 0.91 to 0.98 and average surface distances between 0.39 and 0.54 mm [18].

Factors were analyzed using bivariate and multivariate analyses with reference to the side exhibiting higher CT-aBMD. Specifically, each factor was categorized and compared between the higher and lower CT-aBMD sides. Differences in femoral anteversion and neck-shaft angle were calculated as follows: (value on the higher CT-aBMD side) − (value on the lower CT-aBMD side). For percentage differences in CT-aBMD and gluteal muscle volume, values were derived using the symmetry index [19]: [(value on the higher CT-aBMD side) − (value on the lower CT-aBMD side)] ÷ (mean value of both sides) × 100.

When feasible, participants were asked which leg they typically used as the stance leg during single-leg standing. The relationship between the stance leg and the side with higher CT-aBMD was then examined.

Statistical analysis

Data normality was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Non-normally distributed data were presented as median (IQR), and correlation was assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (rₛ). Between-group differences were examined using either the Wilcoxon rank-sum test or the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Multivariate analysis was conducted with a generalized linear model to identify factors associated with side-to-side %BMD differences. For stance-leg information, missing data were addressed using multiple imputation with logistic regression (m = 5) [20]. The stance leg was included in the generalized linear model as a categorical covariate with three levels: matching the side with higher CT-aBMD, not matching, and no preference.

All statistical analyses were performed using JMP (Student Edition Version 18, SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participants

Among the 110 volunteers, individuals with symptoms in the lower extremities or a history of fractures were excluded, as these factors may influence side-to-side differences in BMD [10, 21]. Specifically, 16 individuals were excluded due to knee pain, 6 due to hip osteoarthritis (Kellgren–Lawrence classification [22] of ≥ 3), and 6 due to a history of lower extremity surgery (including 4 cases of fractures, 1 Achilles tendon repair, and 1 anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction). No participants had neurological disorders. The final study cohort consisted of 82 individuals, including 20 men and 62 women, with a median age of 75 years (IQR: 66–81 years) (Table 1).

Side-to-side comparison (right and left)

A strong correlation was observed between the CT-aBMD values of both sides (rs = 0.93; p < 0.001). However, no significant difference was observed between the two sides (p = 0.23) (Table 2). The median absolute %BMD difference was 3.0% (IQR: 1.6%–5.8%), with differences exceeding 5% observed in 21 participants (26%) and exceeding 1.8% observed in 59 participants (72%). When the gluteal muscle volume, femoral anteversion, and neck-shaft angle were compared between the right and left hips, no significant differences were found (Table 2).

Side-to-side comparison (higher and lower CT-aBMD sides)

When hips were categorized into higher and lower CT-aBMD sides, gluteal muscle volume was significantly greater on the side with the higher CT-aBMD value (p = 0.04) (Table 3). No significant differences were found for the femoral anteversion and neck-shaft angle.

Factor analysis for side-to-side CT-aBMD differences

Bivariate analysis revealed that sex (women), body weight, and greater differences in gluteal muscle volume were significantly associated with larger side-to-side CT-aBMD differences (p = 0.01, 0.04, and 0.03, respectively) (Table 4). In the multivariate analysis using a generalized linear model, differences in gluteal muscle volume and femoral anteversion remained significantly associated with side-to-side CT-aBMD differences (p = 0.01 and p = 0.02, respectively) (Table 5). Discussion

This study evaluated side-to-side differences in BMD using QCT images and analyzed the factors associated with these differences. The results showed a median absolute %BMD difference of 3.0% in the total hip region, with 26% of participants exceeding the 5% threshold recommended in ISCD’s guidelines for DXA measurements. Furthermore, differences in gluteal muscle volume and femoral anteversion were associated with the magnitude of the BMD difference, with the side with smaller gluteal muscle volume and larger femoral anteversion having lower BMD. These findings underscore the importance of bilateral BMD measurements for accurately detecting hips with T-scores ≤ − 2.5, particularly when CT images are used to opportunistically screen for osteoporosis.

Comparison with previous studies reporting side-to-side BMD differences

Previous studies have reported strong correlations between the right- and left-side total hip BMD (r = 0.94–0.96) [5, 7, 9, 23]. These findings are consistent with those of our study (rs = 0.93). Additionally, the median hip BMD difference between the right and left sides in this study (0.007 g/cm2) was comparable to that reported by Chen et al. (0.001 g/cm2) [5]. In this study, 72% of the participants showed side-to-side differences greater than 1.8%. This finding is strikingly similar to that of a previous Japanese study that used an LSC of 1.8%, which showed that 69% of men and 71% of women exceeded this threshold [9]. Regarding T-scores, bilateral measurements increased the detection rate of T-scores of ≤ − 2.5 by an average of 2% compared with unilateral assessment. This rate may vary depending on cohort characteristics, such as sex and age distribution. However, this rate falls within the range reported in previous studies (1.8%–5.4%) [5–7].

Despite the smaller sample size compared with previous studies, our findings are consistent with those of previous studies and likely reflect trends in the general population, supporting the validity of the factor analysis for side-to-side differences in BMD.

Factor analysis

Multivariate analysis to identify factors influencing side-to-side differences in BMD revealed that larger differences in gluteal muscle volume and femoral anteversion were associated with greater BMD differences (Table 5).

Previous studies have shown that hip osteoarthritis and stroke can influence side-to-side differences in BMD due to disuse [10, 24, 25]. In the present study, gluteal muscle volume was greater on the side with higher BMD, suggesting that variations in mechanical loading may contribute to side-to-side differences in BMD. However, although prior studies have reported an effect of the dominant leg [26], our analysis did not demonstrate a significant association between stance-leg preference and BMD differences. As the gluteal muscles are involved in both single-leg stance and hip abduction, stance-leg preference alone may be insufficient to capture variations in muscle volume and BMD between sides. While the gluteal muscles were analyzed collectively based on their functional similarity, more detailed muscle-specific analyses—including thigh musculature—may provide further insight.

Previous studies have reported side-to-side differences of 4°–6° in femoral anteversion [27, 28]. Therefore, the association between femoral anteversion asymmetry and BMD asymmetry may warrant attention. However, given that the correlation between BMD difference and femoral anteversion difference was not significant in the bivariate analysis (rs = − 0.212; p = 0.06), small differences in femoral anteversion may be clinically negligible.

Application of the results for clinical intervention

In this study, side-to-side differences in total hip BMD exceeded 5% in 26% of individuals, and the proportion of individuals with T-scores of ≤ − 2.5 increased by 2% when bilateral measurements were performed. Factor analysis showed that differences in gluteal muscle volume and femoral anteversion were associated with side-to-side BMD differences. However, given that these parameters are difficult to assess before DXA scanning (i.e., CT scanning and analysis are needed), our results do not provide a direct method for determining which hip should be selected when only unilateral measurement is feasible.

Overall, the findings of this study support previous recommendations to perform bilateral DXA measurements whenever possible. Furthermore, when QCT images are available, such as in opportunistic screening, bilateral assessment should be routinely performed because it requires no additional time or effort and can improve the detection of individuals with osteoporotic-range BMD (i.e., T-score ≤ − 2.5).

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, this study only included relatively older individuals (median age: 75 years). The results may differ when younger individuals are included. Given that osteoporosis predominantly affects older adults, the lack of younger participants does not undermine the study’s overall significance. Second, although factors previously reported to influence side-to-side BMD differences were excluded and morphological parameters were examined, other potential contributors may exist. Therefore, further studies involving a larger sample size and exploring additional relevant parameters are needed to validate the results. Third, although the accuracy of QCT measurements has been validated against DXA-derived BMD, no established LSC is available for CT-aBMD measurements because repeated QCT acquisitions are limited by radiation exposure. Therefore, DXA-derived LSC thresholds were used as reference values in the present study. However, to assess whether this approach was reasonable, a post hoc analysis was performed using QCT images obtained before and after total hip arthroplasty in 30 patients. In the analysis of the unoperated side, the LSC for CT-aBMD was calculated to be 4.7%, which was lower than the ISCD-recommended value for DXA. However, postoperative CT images are subject to metal artifacts that may compromise measurement accuracy. Consequently, the LSC for CT-aBMD may have been overestimated and is likely lower if only preoperative QCT images were considered. Nevertheless, the proportions of participants exceeding the DXA-derived thresholds should be interpreted with caution because these thresholds were originally developed for DXA rather than CT-aBMD measurements.

Conclusions

This study showed a median side-to-side BMD difference of 3.0% in the total hip region. These BMD discrepancies were associated with differences in gluteal muscle volume and femoral anteversion, with the side with smaller gluteal muscle volume and larger femoral anteversion having lower BMD. Based on these findings, bilateral BMD measurements are recommended, particularly when using QCT images for opportunistic osteoporosis screening.
