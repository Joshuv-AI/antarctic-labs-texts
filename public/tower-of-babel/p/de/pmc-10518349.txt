Impaired metabolism predicts coronary artery calcification in women with systemic lupus erythematosus
Authors: Fanny Urbain, Maharajah Ponnaiah, Farid Ichou, Marie Lhomme, Clément Materne, Sophie Galier, Julien Haroche, Eric Frisdal, Alexis Mathian, Herve Durand, Micheline Pha, Miguel Hie, Anatol Kontush, Philippe Cluzel, Philippe Lesnik, Zahir Amoura, Maryse Guerin, Fleur Cohen Aubart, Wilfried Le Goff
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundPatients with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) exhibit a high risk for cardiovascular diseases (CVD) which is not fully explained by the classical Framingham risk factors. SLE is characterized by major metabolic alterations which can contribute to the elevated prevalence of CVD.MethodsA comprehensive analysis of the circulating metabolome and lipidome was conducted in a large cohort of 211 women with SLE who underwent a multi-detector computed tomography scan for quantification of coronary artery calcium (CAC), a robust predictor of coronary heart disease (CHD).FindingsBeyond traditional risk factors, including age and hypertension, disease activity and duration were independent risk factors for developing CAC in women with SLE. The presence of coronary calcium was associated with major alterations of circulating lipidome dominated by an elevated abundance of ceramides with very long chain fatty acids. Alterations in multiple metabolic pathways, including purine, arginine and proline metabolism, and microbiota-derived metabolites, were also associated with CAC in women with SLE. Logistic regression with bootstrapping of lipidomic and metabolomic variables were used to develop prognostic scores. Strikingly, combining metabolic and lipidomic variables with clinical and biological parameters markedly improved the prediction (area under the curve: 0.887, p < 0.001) of the presence of coronary calcium in women with SLE.InterpretationThe present study uncovers the contribution of disturbed metabolism to the presence of coronary artery calcium and the associated risk of CHD in SLE. Identification of novel lipid and metabolite biomarkers may help stratifying patients for reducing CVD morbidity and mortality in SLE.FundingINSERM and Sorbonne Université.

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is an autoimmune disease characterized by various systemic manifestations in young adults, particularly women. Patients with SLE display elevated risk of cardiovascular events and associated mortality.1 Surrogate markers of cardiovascular diseases (CVD), including carotid intima-media thickness (c-IMT)2 and presence of calcium in coronary artery,3, 4, 5 are increased in patients with SLE as compared to healthy individuals. However, mechanisms underlying increased cardiovascular risk in SLE are not fully understood. Although some well-known cardiovascular risk factors were shown to be more frequent in patients with SLE,6 the over-representation of CVD in SLE disease still remains unexplained by traditional Framingham risk factors.7,8 Thus, there is a critical need to find robust biomarkers to stratify patients according to their CVD risk in order to develop therapeutic approaches that reduce CVD morbidity and mortality in SLE.

Patients with SLE feature profound alterations in several metabolic pathways, including lipoprotein metabolism which is often affected in CVD.9 Dyslipidemia is occasionally detected in SLE, which may be characterized by an increase in circulating levels of triglycerides and low-density lipoprotein-cholesterol (LDL-C)10 but more frequently by reduced cholesterol content and cardioprotective activity of high-density lipoprotein (HDL).11, 12, 13 Thus, HDL from patients with SLE are dysfunctional exhibiting proinflammatory properties12 and a reduced capacity to promote cellular cholesterol efflux.13

Metabolomic and lipidomic approaches have revealed major systemic metabolic disturbances in SLE, which are not limited to lipid and lipoprotein metabolism but equally involve multiple metabolic pathways, including glycolysis, Krebs cycle, fatty acid oxidation, amino acid metabolism, lipid and eicosanoid biosynthesis and gut microbial metabolism.14, 15, 16, 17 Thus, metabolic alterations have been proposed to possess diagnostic and therapeutic value, with serum metabolites acting as robust predictors of SLE.14,18

Metabolomic and lipidomic studies in patients with CVD demonstrated that alterations in blood metabolome and lipidome were associated with atherosclerosis and CVD after adjustment for conventional Framingham risk factors,19 pointing out to the intimate link between altered metabolism and CVD. Moreover, few metabolites were previously identified as being associated with subclinical plaques20 and plaque progression in SLE.21 However, whether metabolic alterations are associated with the risk of CVD in SLE patients has not yet been investigated.

To address this question, metabolomic and lipidomic analyses were conducted in serum samples from 211 female patients with SLE who underwent a multi-detector computed tomography (CT) scan for coronary artery calcium (CAC) quantification, a robust predictor of coronary heart disease (CHD). The present study demonstrates that serum levels of several lipid species and metabolites were associated with CAC score in patients with SLE. Importantly, addition of these lipid species and metabolites to biological and clinical characteristics of patients improves prediction of the presence of coronary calcium and may thereby help predicting the development of CHD in SLE.

Methods

Patients and clinical data

Patients included in this study (211 female subjects with median age of 43.5 years [range 19–78 years]) followed at the French National Reference Center for SLE (Pitié-Salpêtrière Hospital, Paris, France) between January 2014 and December 2017. SLE was defined according to international recommendations (ACR).22 Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) was defined according to the revised Sapporo criteria.23 Each patient underwent a multi-detector CT scan (Siemens syngo.CT CaScoring) and CAC was quantified by means of the previously described Agatston scoring method.24 Duration of the disease, clinical involvements (skin, serositis, hematology, joints and nervous system), treatment regimens (steroids, methotrexate and hydroxychloroquine) and SLE variables (C3, anti-dsDNA, anti-nuclear and antiphospholipid (aPL) antibodies) are presented in Table 1. Disease activity was estimated by the Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Disease Activity Index (SLEDAI) score.25 The presence of cardiovascular risk factors, including hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, diabetes, obesity, smoking status, family or previous personal history of cardiovascular events and inflammation (US-CRP) was determined for all patients (Table 1). Patients were classified into 3 groups of coronary heart disease (CHD) risk according to the calcium score as defined by the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA) study26 (low, CAC = 0; moderate, 0 < CAC < 100, and high, CAC ≥ 100). For a 3-groups comparison study, the number of patients (n = 211) included in this study is sufficient as only 156 patients are needed with an effect size of 0.25 and a power (1-β error probability) of 0.80 with an alpha p of 0.05. Samples

A blood sample was collected at the day of the CT scan for each patient by venipuncture from the antecubital vein into sterile BD Vacutainer tubes containing clot activator. Serum was separated immediately by low-speed centrifugation at 2500 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C and was stored at −80 °C until use.

Biochemical analyses

Serum triglyceride (TG), total cholesterol (TC) and HDL-C concentrations were quantified with an autoanalyzer (Konelab 20) using reagent kits (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Fasting plasma LDL-C was calculated using the Friedewald formula.

Serum cholesterol efflux capacity assay

The human THP-1 monocytic cell line (RRID:CVCL_0006) was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and was maintained in culture in 5% CO2 at 37 °C in RPMI medium containing 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum, 2 mM l-glutamine and 80 U/ml penicillin/80 μg/ml streptomycin. THP-1 monocytes were grown in the presence of 50 ng/ml phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) for 3 days in order to induce their differentiation into macrophages. Cholesterol efflux capacity (CEC) of whole serum from human THP-1 macrophages was evaluated as previously described.27 Human THP-1 macrophages were cholesterol loaded for 24 h with 50 μg/ml [3H]cholesterol-labeled acetylated LDL (acLDL) (1 μCi/ml) in serum-free RPMI 1640 supplemented with 50 mM glucose, 2 mM glutamine, 0.2% BSA (RGGB) and 80 U/ml penicillin/80 μg/ml streptomycin. Macrophages were subsequently equilibrated in RGGB medium for an additional period of 16 h. Cellular cholesterol efflux to 2.5% whole serum was assayed for a 4-h chase period. Finally, the culture media were harvested and cleared of cellular debris by brief centrifugation. Cell-associated radioactivity was determined by extraction in hexane-isopropanol (3:2), evaporation of the solvent and liquid scintillation counting (Wallac Trilux 1450 Microbeta). The percentage of cholesterol efflux was calculated as 100 x (medium cpm)/(medium cpm + cell cpm). A standard serum sample obtained from a healthy normolipidemic donor was included in all the experiments and was used to calculate the relative cholesterol efflux of each sample.

Measurement of HDL capacity to acquire free cholesterol during LPL-mediated TRL lipolysis

Triglyceride-rich lipoproteins (TRL) were isolated from control plasma samples (Etablissement Français du Sang, EFS) by single-step ultracentrifugation (d < 1.016 g/ml) and labelled with fluorescent 23-(dipyrrometheneboron difluoride)-24-norcholesterol (TopF) (Avanti Polar Lipids) as previously described.28 Briefly, an evaluation of the transfer of TopF to HDL (as apoB-depleted serum) from SLE patients was performed by mixing TopF-labelled TRL (30 mg TG/dL) and apoB-depleted serum (3.3%) in Tris buffer (0.4 M, pH 8) on ice and followed by the addition of lipoprotein lipase (LPL) from Pseudomonas sp. (Sigma) at 37 °C as previously described.28 Reaction was stopped after 2 h by placing the mixture on ice and HDLs were isolated following the addition of apoB precipitant containing phosphotungstic acid and MgCl2, centrifugation and filtration. Fluorescence of TopF-containing HDL, reflecting the transfer of free cholesterol from TRL to HDL upon lipolysis, was measured using a Gemini Microplate Reader (Molecular Devices). Fluorescence of the standard TRL sample alone was measured at the TG concentration used in the assay and fluorescence values measured in HDLs were expressed as the percentage of fluorescence of such standard sample. A reference ApoB-depleted plasma sample obtained from one healthy normolipidemic plasma was used to normalize TopF transfer values obtained in SLE samples.

Lipidomic profiling

Materials

All internal standards were purchased from Avanti Polar Lipids (Alabaster, AL, USA). LC-MS grade or Ultra-Performance Liquid Chromatography grade solvents were used without further purification and obtained from Sigma–Aldrich (St Louis, MO, USA).

Extraction

Serum lipids were extracted using a modified Folch method.29 Briefly, 10 μl serum supplemented with a mixture of internal standards were extracted using 1600 μl of acidified methanol:0.05 N HCl (1:1 v/v) and 800 μl chloroform in the presence of an antioxidant, butylated hydroxytoluene. The lower organic phase was dried and lipids were reconstituted into 40 μl of LC-MS compatible solvent and were quantified by LC-MS/MS.

LC-MS/MS analysis

Lipids were quantified by LC-ESI-MS/MS using a Prominence UFLC (Shimadzu, Tokyo, Japan) and QTrap 4000 mass spectrometer (AB Sciex, Framingham, MA, USA) equipped with a turbo spray ion source (450 °C) combined with an LC20AD HPLC system, a SIL-20AC autosampler (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) and the Analyst 1.5 data acquisition system (AB Sciex, Framingham, MA, USA).

Quantification of phospholipids, sphingolipids and neutral lipids was performed in positive-ion mode. Sample (4 μl) was injected into a Kinetex HILIC 2.6 μm 2.1 x 150 mm column (Phenomenex, CA, USA). Mobile phases consisted of water and acetonitrile containing ammonium acetate and acetic acid. Lipid species were detected using scheduled multiple reaction monitoring (sMRM). Nitrogen was used as nebulization and collision gas. Air was used as exhaust gas.

Quantification

Lipids were quantified using thirty-seven calibration curves specific for 16 individual lipid subclasses and up to 12 fatty acid moieties. More abundant lipid species which displayed non-linear response in non-diluted extracts were quantified from a 20-fold diluted sample.

Data processing

An in-house R script adapted from Ejsing CS et al.30 was used to correct for isotopic contribution to MRM signals. A quality assurance (QA) strategy was applied to assess the performance of the analytical measurements. Lipid species displaying CV >30% across double measurements were discarded.

Lipidomic data have been deposited to Metabolomics Workbench under the ID#ST002732 (DatatrackID:4069).

Metabolomic profiling approach

Reference compounds and reagents

LC-MS grade reference solvents, acetonitrile (ACN) and methanol (MeOH) were from VWR International (Plainview, NY). LC grade ammonium formate and formic acid were from Sigma–Aldrich (Saint Quentin Fallavier, France). Stock solutions of stable isotope-labeled mix (Algal amino acid mixture-13C, 15N) for metabolomic approach were purchased from Sigma–Aldrich (Saint Quentin Fallavier, France).

Sample preparation

Eight volumes of frozen acetonitrile (−20 °C) containing internal standards (labelled mix of amino acids at 12.5 μg/ml) were added to 50 μl serum samples and vortexed. Samples were sonicated for 15 min, centrifuged for 2 min at 10,000 g at 4 °C and incubated at 4 °C for 1 h for slow protein precipitation. Samples were subsequently centrifuged at 20,000 g at 4 °C and supernatants transferred to another series of tubes and dried and frozen at −80 °C. Finally, samples were reconstituted with the starting mobile phase composition of the chromatographic column and then transferred to vials before LC-MS analyses.

Metabolomic analyses

LC-MS measurements were performed using pFPP, Discovery HS F5-PFPP column (5 μm, 2.1 × 150 mm, Sigma, Saint Quentin Fallavier, France). UPLC® Waters Acquity (Waters Corp, Saint-Quentin-en-Yvelines, France) and Q-Exactive mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, San Jose, CA) were used for metabolomic analysis as detailed by Garali et al.31 LC-MS raw data were first converted into mzXML format using MSconvert tool.32 Peak detection, correction, alignment and integration were performed using XCMS R package with CentWave algorithm33 and workflow4metabolomics platforms.34 Datasets obtained were log-10 normalized, filtered and cleaned based on quality control (QC) samples.35 The remaining features were annotated based on their m/z ratio and retention time using an in-house database as described previously,36 and also putatively annotated based solely on their m/z ratio using public databases such as the human metabolome database HMDB37 and the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes database (KEGG).38 The remaining unknown features were discarded from the dataset. Metabolomic data have been deposited to MetaboLights under the accession number#MTBLS7031.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using the R statistical software, version 4.1.3 (R foundation for Statistical Computing). Data are shown as median (25p–75p) for continuous variables and frequency (%) for categorical variables. Kolmogorov–Smirnov test for normality was performed. Comparisons of two groups were performed by a Mann–Whitney U test, while for three groups, Kruskal–Wallis test were performed. Relative risk analysis using modified Poisson regression for binary data was performed to determine the association between cardiac infiltration and the continuous or categorical variables. The statistical analysis of the lipidomics and metabolomics data were performed using Multi Experiment Viewer (MeV) statistical software package (version 4.9.0). Comparisons of the two groups were performed by a 2-tailed independent t-test and three groups by one-way ANOVA. Features were considered significant when the p value was below 0.05 after false discovery rate (FDR) correction.39 When the t-test/ANOVA returned an overall level of significance between the subjects, an agglomerative Hierarchical Clustering tree (HCL); Heatmap was produced. The features were clustered using complete linkage over the samples using Pearson correlation distance matrix.

Affected metabolic pathways were identified using the MetExplore version 2.30.10 web-based tool40 The metabolic network (source database: KEGG Map) of human species was based on 90 metabolic pathways including 1618 metabolites, 2067 reactions, 1456 enzymatic complexes, and 1456 genes.41 Metabolic networks are directed graphs, so it is possible to calculate compound importance based on relative betweenness centrality and out-degree centrality of any given compound from a pathway. Pathway impact was calculated as a sum of the importance measures of the matched metabolites normalized by the sum of the importance measures of all metabolites in each pathway corrected by false discovery rate (FDR).

Multivariable analysis was performed using logistic regression method for clinical-biological, lipidomic, and metabolomic data. The independent variables used in the multivariable analysis were chosen based on scientific literature and univariate significance with p input of 0.05 entry, p output 0.10 removal and with a cutoff of 0.5. The multivariable model was validated by bootstrapping methods and using the Omnibus Test of Model Coefficients & receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. Prognostic scores were developed for each model by calculating the coefficients of significant variables, along with the coefficients of the constant. The prognostic scores were plotted on an AUC-ROC curve to check for the sensitivity and specificity power of the models to predict the outcome. We deployed Random forest (RF) machine learning algorithm as a sensitivity analysis to validate the developed prognostic scores. RF uses an ensemble of classification trees, each of which is grown by random feature selection from a bootstrap sample at each branch, and made a 10-fold cross validation. RF analysis was performed using the randomForest R package. All the statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software, version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, MY, USA), MetaboAnalyst version 5.0 and R version 4.1.3.

Consistent with these observations, the classification of patients with SLE according to the risk of CHD (low, CAC = 0; moderate, 0 < CAC < 100 and high, CAC ≥ 100) revealed that age, disease duration, prevalence of hypertension, serum triglycerides and cholesterol (both total and LDL-C) were all significantly increased in parallel to CAC scores (Tables 1 and 2). It is noteworthy that age and disease duration were only partially correlated in patients with SLE (Pearson correlation: r = 0.393, p value < 0.0001; unstandardized β (5.227 (3.40–7.05 CI))). Although ultra-sensitive CRP was not a predictor of CAC in logistic regression analyses, significantly elevated CRP levels were detected in the group of patients with a CAC ≥ 100 in comparison to other groups (Table 1). By contrast, circulating levels of HDL-C and functionality of HDL, including the capacity to promote cholesterol efflux from macrophages (CEC) and to acquire free cholesterol during LPL-mediated TRL lipolysis (TopF transfer) were not different between CAC scores (Tables 2 and 3).

Taken together, our findings indicated that beyond traditional risk factors for CHD, including age and hypertension, disease activity and duration represented independent risk factor of CAC in patients with SLE.

Coronary calcium and circulating lipidome in patients with SLE

To determine whether circulating lipidome alterations was associated with CAC, lipidomic profiling was performed in sera from patients with SLE by LC-ESI-MS/MS. Heat map representation revealed that the abundances of 23 lipid species were significantly altered in patients with SLE with a moderate or high CAC score (Fig. 1a). Interestingly, among the 21 altered lipid species in total, 15 were enriched in patients with coronary calcification (Fig. 1b). Distribution of individual lipid species according to lipid classes using forest plot highlighted that this enrichment was largely specific to ceramides (Cer(d18:1/23:0), Cer(d18:1/25:0), Cer(d18:1/26:1), Cer(d18:2/26:0) and Cer(d18:2/26:1)), ceramide precursors, dihydroceramides (DHC), (Cer(d18:0/24:0), Cer(d18:0/24:1) and Cer(d18:0/26:1)) and to phosphatidylcholine (PC) (PC(32:1), PC(34:0), PC(36:5), PC(36:6), PC(38:5), PC(40:6), PC(40:7)) species whereas the abundance of only one phosphatidylglycerol (PG) (PG(34:1)) and one phosphatidylinositol (PI) (PI(38:3)) species was increased in patients with coronary calcification (Fig. 1c). Alterations in sphingolipids were chain length-dependent with enrichment in very long-chain fatty acids, such as 26:1 fatty acid in DHC and ceramides (both Cer(d18:1) and Cer(d18:2)) in patients with CAC > 0. It is noteworthy that elevated serum creatinine, a biomarker of kidney dysfunction, also discriminated patients with high and moderate CAC scores. This observation was in agreement with the higher percentage of patients with renal impairment in the former group (Table 1). Raw values for major metabolites associated with CAC were presented in Supplemental Table S1. Finally, logistic regression with bootstrapping analysis indicated that circulating levels of l-pipecolic acid, nicotinamide, salicylurate, 4-acetamidobutanoate and N-acetylputrescine were independent predictors of the presence of coronary calcium in patients with SLE (Supplemental Table S3).

Taken together, our findings indicated that the presence of coronary calcium in patients with SLE was associated with alterations in several metabolic pathways, including purine, arginine and proline metabolism, as well as in microbiota-derived metabolites.

Proposal of a prognostic score for CHD in patients with SLE

Logistic regression with bootstrapping of clinical and biological (Table 4), lipidomic (Supplemental Table S2) and metabolomic (Supplemental Table S3) variables were used to develop prognostic scores for CHD in SLE. The area under the ROC curve predicting the presence of coronary calcium was 0.86 for the clinical and biological variables, 0.78 for the lipidomic variables and 0.74 for the metabolomic variables. Interestingly, the combined score, including clinical, biological, lipidomic and metabolomic parameters, showed an AUC of 0.88 (p < 0.0001) consistent with a robust prediction of coronary calcium presence (Fig. 3 and Supplemental Table S4) and associated risk of CHD in SLE patients. A more thorough analysis indicated that lipidomic variables possessed a higher predictive value than metabolomic variables when combined or not to clinical and biological variables (Supplemental Figure S2 and Supplemental Table S5). Discussion

Analysis of circulating lipidome and metabolome in a large cohort of 211 women with SLE revealed that a specific SLE signature of altered metabolism could predict the presence of coronary artery calcium and the associated risk for CHD. This current study also put forward the involvement of microbiota-derived metabolites in the elevated risk of CVD in women with SLE and provides new information regarding the pathogenesis of CVD in SLE.

In agreement with previous studies, the present investigation demonstrates that age,3,42, 43, 44, 45 hypertension,42 BMI,42,45 disease duration43,44 and activity (SLEDAI)43 are robust independent predictors of the presence of coronary calcium in patients with SLE, confirming that these clinical variables may predict CHD in SLE. Our study also supports the notion that beyond traditional cardiovascular risk factors, parameters inherent to SLE contribute to the risk of CHD. However, surprisingly, we observed an inverse relationship between SLE activity (SLEDAI > 6) and CAC, which contrasts with previous studies reporting either an absence of association42,45 or a positive relationship.43 Overall, these findings suggest that the deleterious impact of disease activity on the presence of coronary calcification occurs primarily in young and less disease-exposed SLE patients. In contrast, in older patients with longer disease duration, the inverse association between disease activity and CAC may result from the circulating C3 level. This hypothesis is supported by the study of Manger et al., who reported that high C3 concentration was the most important CAC-associated factor in a cohort of female SLE.46

Compared to healthy individuals, circulating cholesterol levels were found either not altered4,47, 48, 49, 50, 51 or decreased3,42, 43, 44 in patients with SLE and were not associated with the presence of coronary calcium in SLE, although cholesterol concentration was positively associated with CAC progression in SLE patients during a 2-year follow-up.52 While circulating lipid concentrations related to apoB-containing lipoproteins (LDL-C and TG) increased with CAC scores, they failed to act as independent predictors of the presence of coronary calcium in our cohort of patients with SLE. Deterioration in the quality rather than the quantity of atheroprotective HDL is a hallmark of SLE and has been associated with atherosclerosis and surrogate biomarkers of CHD in patients with SLE.12,43,51,53,54 However, HDL-C levels, along with the capacity of HDL to promote cholesterol efflux from macrophages which is inversely associated with atherosclerosis,55 incidence of CHD56 and mortality,57 do not appear to contribute to the presence of coronary calcium in patients with SLE. A similar conclusion was reached when the capacity of HDL to acquire free cholesterol during LPL-mediated TRL lipolysis, a key function of HDL potentially underlying the relationship between HDL-C and CVD,28,58 was evaluated. Altogether, our study indicated that alterations of lipoprotein metabolism poorly explained the increased risk of CHD in women with SLE when CAC was used as a surrogate marker.

Our systemic lipidomic and metabolomic analyses provide new mechanistic clues regarding the contribution of SLE-related factors to the risk of CHD by identifying metabolites and lipids which may play an important role in the development of CVD in SLE. Among them, we found that circulating levels of ceramides as well as of their biosynthetic precursors, dihydroceramides, especially species with very long-chain fatty acids, were associated with the presence of coronary calcification and could predict CHD in female patients with SLE. Ceramides are involved in several cellular pathways contributing to atherosclerosis, including inflammation, apoptosis and cell death, and are known as robust prognostic indicators of major adverse cardiovascular events, including CHD, heart failure and cardiovascular death.59, 60, 61, 62, 63, 64 Hammad et al. recently reported that patients with SLE displayed elevated levels of circulating ceramides, including very long-chain ceramide species, in comparison to healthy individuals.16 In addition, further investigations from this group revealed that plasma concentrations of very long-chain ceramide species in patients with SLE were positively correlated with total plaque area assessed by carotid ultrasound measurements.17 Whereas long-chain ceramides, such as C16-ceramides, are proposed to primarily account for the adverse effects associated with this class of lipids, very long-chain ceramides are considered benign to date.61 Our present study suggests that very long-chain ceramides may be harmful in the context of SLE by exerting deleterious actions in the heart. In agreement with this hypothesis, overexpression of CerS2 in cardiomyocytes, the enzyme producing very long-chain ceramides, induced mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress and cell death.65 Because CerS2 is mainly expressed in the liver,66 we propose that very long-chain ceramides and dihydroceramides originate from de novo synthesis in the liver and are then transported in the circulation by lipoproteins. This mechanism is supported by a recent study showing that silencing of CerS2 in hepatocytes reduced circulating very long-chain ceramides.67 However, a common single nucleotide polymorphism in CERS2 is not associated with CAD in the Utah CAD study68 and CerS2 haploinsufficiency in mice was accompanied by elevated levels of certain acylcarnitines implicated in CVD69 suggesting a complex role of CerS2 in CVD. Very long-chain ceramide species containing a sphingadienine backbone (Cer(d18:2)) and less frequent fatty acids (C23 and C25) could result from the reduction of ceramides by the FADS3 desaturase70,71 and from branched chain amino acid metabolism in mitochondria72 combined to the action of fatty acid elongases (ELOVL1, 3 and 7),73 respectively. It is noteworthy that these ceramides species can also originate from the nutrition although isoforms are different (Cer(d18:2(4E, 14Z)) from FADS3 activity and Cer(d18:2(4E, 11Z)) from nutrition). Further investigations are needed to determine whether elevated expression of CerS2 occurs in SLE, which may represent a potential therapeutic target in this pathology.

In addition to ceramides, our study also identified several phosphatidylcholine species containing mono- (MUFA) and polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA) as well as phosphatidylserine as predictors of the presence of coronary calcification in patients with SLE, although with opposite effects. Interestingly, concentrations of such PUFA-containing phosphatidylcholine species were associated with cardiovascular events74 and death59 and their combination with ceramides in a risk score improved the prediction of CVD death in patients with CAD.59 However, we did not detect any alterations in plasmalogen and arachidonic acid-containing phospholipid species according to CAC in our cohort of SLE patients whereas these species were previously proposed to be associated with plaque progression in patients with SLE.21 Finally, a potentially protective action of phosphatidylserine regarding CAD in SLE is consistent with the role of this class of phospholipids in inhibiting inflammation, modulating coagulation, and enhancing HDL functionality.75 We thereby demonstrate that a score based on concentrations of selected phosphatidylcholine, phosphatidylserine and ceramide species possessed strong predictive value for the presence of coronary calcification and the associated risk of CHD in patients with SLE.

Patients with SLE exhibit an intestinal dysbiosis76 characterized by depleted diversity of gut microbiota.77 The present study shows that concentrations of several microbial metabolites were associated with the presence and the degree of coronary calcification in women with SLE. Among them, levels of metabolites derived from dietary phosphatidylcholine metabolism, such as γ-butyrobetaine and TMAO which promote atherosclerosis and predict CVD,78 were increased in parallel to CAC score in SLE. TMAO is an end product from an initial microbiota-related pathway that converts choline and carnitine from the diet to trimethylamine (TMA) which is then converted to TMAO in the liver. Interestingly, a reduction in fecal trigonelline, a metabolite inhibiting gut microbial choline metabolism and subsequent TMAO production, was observed in patients with SLE as compared to healthy individuals.79 In addition to microbiota-derived phosphatidylcholine metabolites, we identified hippurate, a product of microbial carbohydrate fermentation whose circulating levels are altered in SLE,80 as also being associated with CAC in women with SLE. Overall, our findings strongly suggest that disruption of microbial metabolism in SLE may contribute to the prevalence of CHD in this clinical condition.

Major alterations in the purine metabolism are detected in SLE which may underlie the impaired immune response in this autoimmune disease. For instance, reduced activity of purine 5′-nucleotidase, the rate-limiting catabolic enzyme for the hydrolysis of mononucleotides and deoxymononucleotides into their respective nucleosides or deoxynucleosides, was detected in lymphocytes from patients with SLE.81 Moreover, the activity of adenosine deaminase that hydrolyzes adenosine, a potent immunosuppressant, into inosine was increased in SLE and was correlated with disease activity.82 Analysis of fecal metabolome of patients with SLE revealed a decrease of metabolites involved in purine metabolism relative to healthy controls,79 linking gut microbiota to disturbed purine metabolism pathway in SLE. The present study further underlies the role of purine metabolism in the pathogenesis of SLE by reporting the association of this pathway with coronary calcification and its potential contribution to CHD in women with SLE. Purine metabolism is closely linked to arterial calcification83 and cardiovascular diseases.84 Indeed, the conversion of xanthine to uric acid is catalyzed by xanthine oxidase generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) that promote endothelial dysfunction and CVD. Uric acid was then proposed as an independent cardiovascular risk factor motivating the use of xanthine oxidase inhibitors to reduce major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE).84 We observed that women with SLE had an increased xanthine oxidase activity as a function of the CAC score, suggesting that xanthine oxidase inhibitors could represent a potential therapeutic option for reducing CHD in these patients. Further investigations are needed to determine whether the relationship between purine metabolism and CVD in SLE involves regulation of the immune response.

Amino acid metabolism is severely disrupted in SLE illustrated by the marked reduction of numerous amino acids, including arginine and proline, in sera from these patients in comparison to healthy controls.14 This disturbance at the systemic levels is reflected by the enrichment of arginine and proline, among other amino acids in fecal metabolome from SLE patients, highlighting the contribution of microbiota in the metabolic perturbations in SLE.79,80,85 Interestingly, the arginine/proline pathway was enriched in SLE women patients with coronary calcium in the present study suggesting that the activation of this metabolic pathway may be associated with CHD. In agreement with such a hypothesis, arginine activates mTOR signaling, a pathway disturbed in SLE86 and cardiovascular diseases.87 Although arginine is essential to preserve endothelial function through NO production, homoarginine which we found to be increased in women with coronary artery calcium was reported to compete with arginine and then impair NO synthesis.88 Metabolism of proline, a substrate for arginine production, has been recently reported to be increased in patients with acute coronary syndrome89 reinforcing the involvement of the arginine/proline pathway in the risk of CHD in patients with SLE.

Finally, the identification of metabolites acting as independent predictors of CAC led us to propose a score based on selected amino acids with strong predictive values for the presence of coronary calcium and the associated risk of CHD in women with SLE. Interestingly, the predictive value of this score is significantly improved when combined with clinical, biological and lipidomic variables.

To conclude, our study of a large number of women with SLE, highlights that beyond traditional risk factors, lipidomic and metabolic alterations in SLE are intimately linked to the presence of coronary calcium. The proposal of a coronary artery calcium prediction score including lipidomic and in a lesser extend metabolomic markers might help to stratify and manage patients at high risk of CVD.

Limitations

Further investigations in replicated cohorts of SLE patients as well as a follow-up of SLE patients included in the present study will be needed to validate the predictive and prognostic power of these markers for CHD.
