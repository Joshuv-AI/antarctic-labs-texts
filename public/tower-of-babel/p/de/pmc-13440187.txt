Unlocking miniature brilliance: micro/nanorobots for advanced dental theranostics
Authors: Yong Xia, Simin Li, Longwen He, Zihan Chen, Yukun Ma, Yuming Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOral diseases exert profound impacts on systemic health, posing substantial threats to global healthcare systems. The intricate anatomy, dynamic fluid clearance, and resilient biofilm barriers of the oral cavity collectively compromise the accessibility and efficacy of conventional therapies. Micro/nanorobots (MNRs), which perform precision tasks through active propulsion and programmable intervention, constitute an emerging paradigm for advanced dental theranostics (combined diagnosis and therapy). Over the past decade, MNRs have demonstrated particular promise in navigating confined oral anatomies, disrupting polymicrobial biofilms, and resolving inflammatory cascades. Mechanistically, MNRs promote oral tissue protection and regeneration through enhanced physical perturbation, targeted cell manipulation, and metabolic reprogramming. This review systematically evaluates the current progress of dental MNRs, and proposes translational challenges and prospects toward clinical implementation.Graphical abstract

Introduction

Oral diseases affect approximately 3.69 billion people worldwide and impose a substantial burden on global healthcare systems [1, 2]. The oral cavity presents multiple therapeutic barriers characterized by complex anatomical confinement, dynamic biological fluids, and dense polymicrobial biofilms. These multidimensional barriers span spatial scales from the nanostructure of biofilm matrix to millimeter-deep periodontal pockets, and temporal scales from rapid salivary washout to years of chronic biofilm persistence [3, 4]. Mechanical debridement cannot access restricted geometries, while passively delivered antimicrobials undergo rapid clearance and insufficient biofilm penetration due to diffusion-limited transport. Such limitations contribute to incomplete pathogen eradication, rapid biofilm re-establishment, and escalating antimicrobial resistance [5, 6]. Consequently, it is imperative to discover innovative strategies from passive protection to active propulsion in oral therapeutics.

Micro/nanorobots (MNRs) offer key advantages that directly address unmet needs in oral healthcare through active propulsion, environmental adaptation, and precision control [7–9]. Based on actuation mechanisms, MNRs are categorized into chemically driven (e.g., glucose, urea, and H2O2), externally powered (e.g., magnetic, ultrasound, and light fields), and biohybrid systems [10–12]. Unlike passive nanoparticles (NPs) relying on diffusion, MNRs actively navigate through confined oral spaces beyond the reach of conventional instruments [13–16]. Representative applications demonstrate deep penetration into dentinal tubules [17], navigation against gingival fluid flow in periodontal pockets [18], and pH-responsive targeting of acidic carious lesions [19] (Table 1). Another striking strength lies in their versatility, where MNRs integrate diagnostic sensing, biofilm disruption, and therapeutic cargo delivery into multimodal platforms for dental theranostics. For instance, near-infrared (NIR)-driven nanorobots with cascade catalytic capabilities autonomously penetrate biofilms and release nitric oxide to reduce bacterial infiltration, coupled with biomarker-responsive probes for simultaneous inflammatory diagnosis in diabetic periodontitis [20]. These breakthroughs position MNRs as promising tools for combating oral lesions—a field that has expanded rapidly over the past decade (Fig. 1).

Despite these advances, there is a lack of full-scale overviews regarding the advantages, mechanisms, and prospects of dental MNRs. The interdisciplinary nature of this field has led to varied terminology including micro/nanorobots, micro/nanobots, micro/nanoswimmers, and self-propelled particles [21]. To ensure clarity, we adopt MNRs as the unified term encompassing these diverse systems. This review systematically explores the rapidly advancing field of MNRs for dental theranostics, with emphasis on translational pathways toward clinical implementation. We first analyze MNR advantages over conventional dental therapeutics in addressing oral-specific barriers. Mechanistic insights into dental MNRs are summarized from the perspectives of enhanced perturbation, cell manipulation, and metabolic reprogramming. Finally, the practical design considerations and clinical translational challenges of MNRs are presented.

Oral therapeutic barriers

Addressing oral diseases requires understanding the unique barriers within the oral cavity (Fig. 2a). These multidimensional challenges encompass anatomical confinement that restricts access to diseased sites, biological fluid dynamics that disrupt therapeutic retention, and biofilm architecture that creates protective interphase barriers.

Anatomical confinement restricts therapeutic access

The oral cavity presents spatial bottlenecks ranging from millimeter-scale periodontal pockets to nanometer-scale dentinal tubules, each imposing distinct accessibility challenges. Enamel comprises tightly packed hydroxyapatite nanorods with an acellular structure and high brittleness. The occlusal surface of enamel features pits and elongated fissures with diameters ranging from 15 to 75 μm, trapping bacteria and food debris [29]. Dentinal tubules (0.9–2.5 μm diameter, 1–4 mm depth) exhibit outward fluid flow that rapidly clears therapeutic agents, preventing sustained desensitization [30, 31]. Root canals exhibit complex anatomies including intercanal anastomoses, lateral branches, isthmuses, C-shaped canals, and other developmental irregularities that harbor residual bacteria beyond the reach of chemical irrigants [32]. During the preparation of curved and calcified root canals, instruments may experience torsional and bending stresses, potentially resulting in step formation, canal perforation, or instrument separation [33]. In disease, periodontal pockets deepen to 3–12 mm and develop tortuous subgingival geometries inaccessible to scaling tools [34]. Beyond geometric constraints, the dense stromal extracellular matrix and specialized mucosal barriers in inflamed oral tissues create formidable penetration environments comparable to those in solid tumors [35]. Effective dental therapy requires strategies to navigate anatomical bottlenecks while overcoming dynamic biological barriers.

Biological fluids disrupt therapeutic retention

The oral cavity presents a unique fluid environment where saliva and gingival crevicular fluid (GCF) generate continuous shear forces that challenge therapeutic retention. These biological fluids rapidly clear therapeutic agents before effective action can occur. Basal salivary flow of 0.3–0.4 mL/min can increase up to 4–5 mL/min upon stimulation, creating continuous washout that limits drug residence time [36, 37]. GCF flows outward from periodontal pockets at rates of several microliters per hour in health, markedly increasing during inflammation to prevent passive penetration into subgingival sites [38].

The fundamental limitation of passive systems is their inability to generate propulsive force against these flows. Flow chamber studies reveal that biological fluids create distinct boundary layers with steep velocity gradients near surfaces, where therapeutic molecules experience both convective washout and limited diffusion into stagnant zones [39]. These fluid-mediated barriers reduce passive therapeutic residence time to minutes, which may be insufficient for effective antimicrobial exposure. Furthermore, the interaction between flowing biological fluids and surface-adherent structures generates interfacial zones with altered physicochemical properties that further impede therapeutic access [40].

Biofilm architecture creates protective interphase barriers

Oral tissues harbor over 700 bacterial species and more than 100 fungal and protozoal species that organize into polymicrobial biofilms. These microbial communities are encased within an extracellular polymeric substance (EPS) matrix that creates physical and chemical barriers to therapeutic penetration [41]. Biofilms colonize tooth surfaces and mucosal epithelium, initiating dental caries and periodontal diseases. As biofilms mature, acidic metabolic byproducts, proteolytic enzymes, and pro-inflammatory mediators converge to establish localized pathogenic niches that exacerbate tissue destruction [4, 42]. Chronic oral dysbiosis is also associated with increased risk of oral cancers [43]. Furthermore, the reach of oral microbes extends beyond the oral cavity, as periodontal dysbiotic immune responses can have systemic manifestations [44, 45].

The EPS matrix functions as a diffusion-limiting barrier through multiple mechanisms. Advanced three-dimensional oral biofilm models cultured on hydroxyapatite surfaces demonstrate spatial organization that resembles clinical dental plaque as shown in the Zürich multispecies model and related hydroxyapatite disk systems [46, 47]. The biofilm-fluid interface can form a distinct interphase zone with locally altered EPS organization and viscoelastic behavior compared to bulk biofilm or surrounding fluid [48]. The EPS-mediated diffusion barriers combined with metabolic dormancy in deep biofilm zones are associated with 10-1000-fold increased antibiotic tolerance [49].

In conclusion, the oral cavity’s unique combination of anatomical confinement, dynamic fluid flow, and resilient biofilm structures presents formidable challenges that defeat passive therapies. These unique conditions offer both hurdles and opportunities for developing innovative paradigms aimed at addressing complex biological traits and protecting oral niches.

Precise navigation and imaging-guided dental diagnostics

The heterogeneous distribution and nonuniform location of biofilms associated with oral diseases complicate accurate sampling, whereas conventional detection methods require complex equipment. MNRs demonstrate precise control and directed navigation, offering potential for future minimally invasive diagnostics [50]. Their strength lies in efficiently detecting oral pathogens, quantifying signal data, and visualizing lesion characteristics with clinically viable tracking systems to facilitate patient-specific treatments.

Unlike ordinary NPs that exhibit Brownian motion (random thermal motion of particles in fluid) in biofluids, MNRs continuously generate thrust in the low Reynolds number regime (where viscous forces dominate over inertial forces at microscale) and overcome the dominant viscous forces [51]. Navigation in certain directions, in 3D spaces, and in pathological environments enables MNRs to accurately locate hard-to-reach oral tissues while fulfilling detection accuracy standards. An illustrative example of controlled direction was applying catalytic antimicrobial robots (CARs) to identify microbial composition through a control algorithm for specific motion patterns. Through directed motion, biohybrid CARs traversed broad swathes of surfaces and moved over predetermined pathways to retrieve dental biofilms while preserving the integrity of adjacent regions (Fig. 3a) [23]. When navigating complex topographies and adhesive surfaces, micro/nanoscale systems may be lost or stuck. Autonomous navigation of MNRs in 3D spaces has been achieved by programmed magnetic guidance with sophisticated motion patterns, facilitating the precise localization of rugged oral tissues to inform clinical decisions [52]. Iron oxide nanoparticles (IONPs) demonstrated emergent behaviors, self-reconfiguring into kinematic chain-like structures to navigate through confined spaces and subsequently reassembling as swarms to capture bacterial, fungal, viral, and matrix components [53, 54]. Remarkably, swarming MNRs have been proposed to actively navigate tissue channels to cover the pathological microenvironment and collectively perceive and report physicochemical changes in unknown targets [55]. Considering distinctive dental pathologies, such as pH changes in dental caries, excess reactive oxygen species (ROS) in pulp diseases, and immune imbalance in periodontitis, multilocomotive MNRs will advance current oral diagnostic and care modalities by leveraging stimuli-responsive properties [56].

Apart from sample detection and quantification, MNRs may serve as contrast-enhancing or agent-carrying platforms for imaging guidance. When combined with existing imaging modalities such as ultrasound, radiology, and optical or magnetic imaging, these platforms facilitate improved lesion identification [57]. For instance, in vitro studies have demonstrated that incorporating bismuth oxide opacifiers within microrobots increased their radiopacity, enabling trajectory tracking under laboratory radiographic conditions that simulate clinical imaging techniques such as dental X-rays and cone-beam computed tomography [22]. Besides, optical biofilm imaging combined with radiographic tracking has been explored to visualize MNR navigation within tooth structures. Babeer et al. [54] illustrated that fluorescence-based biofilm detection and cone-beam computed tomography allowed real-time monitoring of microrobotic systems operating in root canal anatomies under ex vivo conditions (Fig. 3b). While these proof-of-concept studies demonstrate the feasibility of imaging-guided MNR navigation in simplified settings, real-time tracking remains to be validated in clinically relevant oral settings and requires further technological advancement.

Targeted microbial eradication to combat oral biofilms

The viscoelastic biofilms reinforced by the dense EPS are notoriously difficult to dismantle, resulting in recalcitrant infections from biofilm-induced oral diseases such as caries, periodontitis, and implant-associated infection [58]. The high degree of autonomy allows MNRs to efficiently traverse oral microenvironments, precisely controlling their trajectories to target microbes. Moreover, volant MNRs act as deep penetrators and retrievers of biofilm structures, achieving thorough eradication.

One prominent benefit of MNRs lies in mimicking the functions of toothbrush, floss, and mouthwash for efficient oral biofilm removal without resorting to antibiotics. Routine oral care typically involves manual brushing, followed by flossing and final rinsing, which primarily distresses people with limited mobility. Advanced MNR systems are capable of automating the entire process in a hands-free manner. Surface topography-adaptive robotic superstructures (STARS) could transform into bristle-like and floss-like shapes, enabling them to adhere to the corners and crevices of each tooth. The biofilm components (microbial cells and EPS matrix) were embedded in the extended and reassembled STARS bristles during the biofilm removal process and could be retrieved together with the collected bristles (Fig. 3c) [52]. Another study introduced Janus microrobots to inactivate potentially harmful Escherichia coli with H2O2 solutions, paving the way for the clinical implementation of MNRs in mouthwashes [59]. Janus Ag-MnO2 microrobots exhibited sterilization rates against both planktonic and biofilm-forming Porphyromonas gingivalis (98.1%) and Fusobacterium nucleatum (97.4%) in vitro with 5% H₂O₂ fuel over 30 min exposure [60]. However, the relatively high H₂O₂ concentration required may raise biocompatibility concerns.

Beyond the innate features of MNRs, the exploration of microbe-specific transport mechanisms represents a burgeoning field. MNRs are designed to introduce components with antimicrobial properties for a sophisticated or smart strategy, such as metallic fillers [61], stimuli-responsive materials [62], bioactive nanozymes [63], and antibiotics (Fig. 3d) [64]. The oral cavity is located in a superficial region of the human body, making it easily accessible to external light and magnetic fields, which facilitates the development of magnetoresponsive and photoresponsive materials. A hyperthermia-based bactericidal method was employed to inflict local damage, wherein the magnetic layer embedded within the nanorobots functions as a localized heat source. However, the heat is localized to a few micrometers and remains insufficient for the effective eradication of high bacterial loads within the dentinal tubules [17]. Interestingly, light-driven tubular nanorobots based on TiO2 decorated with Ag NPs degraded bacterial biofilm growth on commercial facial titanium miniplate implants [65]. This adaptability of MNRs aligns perfectly with the demands for managing oral fluctuation situations, signifying a substantial advancement over conventional approaches in terms of therapeutic accuracy and responsiveness.

Collectively, MNRs can precisely localize at oral sites, disrupt biofilm formation, and eradicate mature biofilms, thereby synergistically increasing antibiotic penetration and preventing recurrence. However, most current studies evaluate MNR efficacy against single-species biofilms under controlled conditions. Differential EPS composition, surface charge, and mechanical properties between bacterial and fungal biofilms may influence treatment outcomes. Future studies must assess MNR efficacy against clinically relevant polymicrobial biofilms to avoid overestimating therapeutic potential. Additionally, selective pressure on certain species could alter community composition, requiring assessment to prevent dysbiotic outcomes within the oral cavity.

Effective inflammation resolution to promote tissue regeneration

Inflammation is a fundamental defensive response to harmful stimuli, but excessive inflammatory activation is associated with irreversible damage to soft and mineralized dental tissues [67]. MNRs could invite opportunities for regenerating damaged dental structures and functional restoration under orthodontic, periodontal, and endodontic inflammation.

During orthodontic tooth movement, timely inflammation resolution prevents excessive tissue damage and accelerates post-inflammatory tissue remodeling. Tan et al. [26] developed nanorobots capable of biphasic inflammation modulation to facilitate tissue adaptation during force application, demonstrating the therapeutic potential of MNRs in managing force-induced inflammatory responses.

In periodontitis/peri-implantitis, the inflammatory cascade caused by subgingival biofilms provokes deep periodontal lesions, progressive bone degeneration, and eventual tooth/implant loss [68]. Current treatments, such as mechanical debridement/curettage, surgical tissue grafting, and adjunctive antibiotics, demonstrate variable efficacy and lack predictable regenerative outcomes, particularly in cases with significant bone loss [69]. For periodontitis management, nanorobots achieved enhanced biofilm penetration coupled with immune modulation toward tissue-protective phenotypes [70]. Janus Ga/Mg microrobots demonstrated effective anti-inflammatory capacity, restoring periodontal tissue integrity and reducing inflammatory cell infiltration in disease models (Fig. 4a) [18]. Similarly, MnO2-based microrobots achieved dual outcomes of biofilm disruption and bone regenerative capacity, restoring osseointegration in implant-associated infections [71–73]. Periodontitis/peri-implantitis is not solely linear to bacterial invasion, and these MNR strategies exemplify advances in reversing inflammatory states and relieving alveolar bone loss.

Endodontic inflammation can lead to irreversible damage to the dental pulp and may further progress into pulp necrosis and periapical inflammation without timely and effective treatment. Current therapeutic approaches, including root canal treatment and pulp-capping materials, demonstrate critical limitations in replicating natural pulp architecture and achieving timely reparative dentin formation, leaving pulp tissues vulnerable to reinfection [74]. To address this dilemma, Wang et al. [27] developed dual-fuel nanorobots capable of reducing pulp chamber congestion and inflammatory cell accumulation. This inflammation-resolving capacity facilitated subsequent tissue repair and protective barrier formation in pulp tissues (Fig. 4b).

These findings collectively indicate that MNRs possess the capacity to reshape inflammatory microenvironments and restore tissue homeostasis across multiple oral disease contexts. However, the clinical translation of these approaches faces challenges arising from inter-individual variability in immune status, baseline inflammation levels, and tissue-specific pathophysiology, necessitating personalized therapeutic strategies for optimal outcomes.

Design, propulsion, and therapeutic mechanisms of representative dental MNRs

Enhancing physical perturbation based on propulsion systems

Achieving locomotion that enables access to hard-to-reach areas is the first step in pathogen eradication and enhanced outcomes. The penetration of passive NPs is usually impeded by the tooth structure (e.g., narrow periodontal pockets and dental pulp) and salivary and GCF barriers, complicating dental interventions. Powered by chemical fuel and external fields, dental MNRs reach their target tract with a strong propulsive force, which facilitates the penetration of various physiological barriers.

Chemically driven MNRs

Chemical MNRs harness built-in energy for mechanical motion, which is typically based on concentration gradients produced by catalytic reactions or bubble generation induced by reactions with an environmental biofuel [77]. By exploiting gradients, tailoring reaction rates, and modulating the oral environment, these MNRs achieve accurate localization at pathological sites with sophisticated motion patterns.

Concentration-based fuel operates at the interface between particles and fluids, which induces movement that varies, depending on the fluid’s viscosity. The main approach is to enhance targeting effectiveness, thereby maximizing drug delivery and extending drug half-life. This mechanism generally utilizes the catalytic properties of precious metals, transition metals, and metal oxides to accelerate the decomposition of frequently used fuels such as H2O2 (Fig. 5a) [78]. As reported, the Pt layer on one side of the nanorobots catalytically decomposed H2O2 into H2O and O2, leading to a decreased H2O2 concentration on this side and providing the dynamics to deliver desensitization agents efficiently. The autonomous nanorobots moved in high-viscosity media at a speed of approximately 1.1 μm/s, thereby resisting the outflow of dentin fluid and entering the dentin tubules [75].

With respect to bubble-driven MNRs, catalytic metals mainly react with the acid and water to produce gaseous propellants (e.g., H2, O2, and NO). These biocompatible materials include Janus structures, which were fabricated by asymmetrically liquid metallic Ga on Mg microspheres. H2 bubbles produced by the magnesium–water reaction drove performance of up to 31.03 μm/s (pH 6.8), enabling penetration against GCF outward flow to reach Porphyromonas gingivalis biofilms at the bottom of periodontal pockets (Fig. 5b) [18]. Similarly, the generation and release of O2 bubbles serve as a power source, enabling MNRs to penetrate deep into orthodontic brackets [79], denture base [80], and periodontal pockets [60] to remove biofilms.

Intraoral propulsion of MNRs can be enhanced by increasing their catalytic activity, cascade reactions, and utilization of dual fuels. An alloying strategy can boost the catalytic activity of MNRs by modifying the electronic structure of the catalytic metal surface. Catalytically driven nanorobots with an alloy engine provided sufficient propulsion force while maintaining good targeting capability [81, 82]. In dual-fuel nanorobots, CaO2 could quickly generate H2O2 in the presence of H+, which reacted with L-Arg to boost the gradual accumulation and uneven distribution of NO bubbles. This cascade mechanism provides sequential propulsion through initial bubble-driven convection followed by NO diffusion (Fig. 5c) [27].

Overall, chemically driven MNRs leverage the in vivo chemical environment to achieve intelligent navigation, facile dispatching, and enhanced mass transfer. However, the lack of controlled directionality and speed of self-propulsion limits precise targeting in dental treatment [83]. With the optimized development of various chemical reactions and the incorporation of surface functionalization, these MNRs are expected to enhance stability in oral environments but also improve therapeutic precision.

Externally driven MNRs

Unlike chemical-driven methods, externally driven MNRs are steerable with precise motion trajectories through external forces such as magnetic or electric fields, light, or acoustic waves. These mechanisms offer advantages, such as wireless control, precise regulation, high biocompatibility, and penetration depth, to compensate for the constraints of existing treatment solutions.

Magnetic MNRs enable precise control and navigation by rotating, oscillating, and conical fields, which generally employ ferromagnetic materials. Various morphologies of magnetic MNRs, such as sphere-, rod-, tubular-, and helical-shaped MNRs, can form physical barriers against bacterial infiltration, mechanically destroy EPS and allow the loaded antibacterials to penetrate deep into the biofilm. Spherical microrobots with cohesive yet flexible structures exhibited self-reconfigurability as they rotated, bent, and interchanged. The localization of IONPs increased the magnetic moment and facilitated more dynamic and rapid orientation and positioning for physically (staircase-like with varying heights) and biologically (rugged and adhesive soft tissue) challenging topographies (Fig. 6a) [22, 63]. Guided by magnetic fields, spherical nanorobots achieved penetration depths of over 300 μm through the complex topography of dentin tissue (Fig. 6b) [84]. Another interesting configuration is the tubular Fe3O4HNT/PEI@Amp robot system with multimodal motion (ribbon and vortex motion mode) to avoid an obstacle and return to its original path. Under a rotating magnetic field, the vortex motion mode led to an apparent structural change in the sticky EPS of the biofilms colonized on the titanium mesh used for bone restoration [64]. Although the manipulation of magnetic objects and diverse magnetic fields for targeted moving velocity and direction is well understood, the long-term biocompatibility of magnetic particle-loaded MNRs needs further assessment.

Light-driven MNRs generate exceptional selectivity and locomotion primarily through photothermal, photocatalytic, or photoelectric motions. By manipulating the wavelength, intensity, and pattern of light, MNRs can reduce collateral damage to healthy oral tissues. The black TiO2/Ag nanorobots showed multiple motion behaviors across the UV to visible-light range, depending on the mutual orientation of the nanotubes concerning the metal target during deposition. Nanorobots displayed stop–go motion when the light was switched on–off to remove troublesome biofilms in infected metallic miniplates (Fig. 6c) [65]. More recently, NIR-propelled nanorobots combined photothermal and photodynamic effects with NO-mediated propulsion for deep biofilm penetration, achieving synergistic antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and osteogenic outcomes in peri-implantitis models (Fig. 6d) [85]. Similarly, an NIR-driven nanorobot performed thermophoresis propulsion to enhance the penetration ability and destroyed the dense EPS, thereby achieving precise diagnosis and therapy of chronic periodontitis [20]. While light is considered clean energy for driving MNRs and allows for ready MNR manipulation, the ability of MNRs to penetrate dental tissues may be limited.

Currently, most tactic MNRs for dental theranostics are driven by single actuation modes and may suffer from inadequate motility and difficulty in directional control. Combinatorial mechanisms of MNRs represent a practical approach to enhance adaptability across various dental conditions. Furthermore, the operation modes and efficiencies of biohybrid MNRs can be flexibly adjusted in terms of biocompatibility, autonomous motility, and deep tissue penetration. As multiple power sources coactuate MNRs, their application in integrated dental therapeutic platforms will continue to expand.

Manipulating cell behaviors to reshape microenvironment

MNRs can further modulate the oral microenvironment by engaging with host cells at both extracellular and intracellular levels to alter their biological behaviors [86, 87]. In this regard, MNRs primarily rely on surface chemotactic responses, functionalization with specific ligands, and meticulous shape design.

One prominent strategy involves endowing MNRs with dynamic targeting through chemotaxis to actively modify cell behaviors. Equipped with chemical sensors, MNRs detect molecules that are indicative of specific physiological cues. MnO₂ and PLGA microrobots demonstrated robust chemotaxis to target inflammatory sites characterized by activated macrophage accumulation (Fig. 7a) [60, 88]. In another study, a penetrating macrophage-based nanoformulation engaged P. gingivalis through the Toll-like receptor complex 2/1, thus disrupting the bacterial attachment to immune cells and inhibiting its evasion of the periodontal host immunological response [89]. Furthermore, helical hydrogel motors offered a high loading capacity and retained release of chemokines CXCL12, without the need for external forces. By leveraging these intrinsic chemical signals, the synthetic leader cell directs immune T-cell chemotactic migration [90].

Beyond chemotactic responses, MNRs are also typically functionalized with particular ligands capable of capturing cells [91]. In a compelling demonstration, DNA nanorobots have been engineered with fibroblast-targeting peptides incorporated onto an erythrocyte membrane. This modification enabled specific recognition and binding to periostin, a critical surface protein highly expressed on gingival fibroblasts. In response to intracellular glutathione, nanorobots ensured the responsive release of miR-1 − 3p without being degraded by intracellular enzymes, which inhibited the translation of pathogenic gene products to reduce fibroblast activation. Local injections of DNA nanorobots progressively decreased neutrophil chemotaxis and ameliorated the inflammatory level in periodontal tissues (Fig. 7b) [92]. Another DNA-based nanorobot specifically recognized CXCR2 receptors on the surfaces of neutrophils via an N-acetyl Pro-Gly-Pro peptide, targeting, hijacking, and regulating neutrophils [93]. Additionally, nanozyme assemblies have demonstrated avid and selective binding to fungal cells, particularly Candida albicans, exhibiting significantly reduced affinity for human gingival cells. This selectivity is attributed to interactions with cell wall O-mannan polysaccharides on the fungal surface, facilitating localized accumulation and targeted antifungal effects [66].

Taking advantage of the shape of MNRs to trap and release cells represents another approach for manipulating cell behaviors. Featuring a double helix, magnetically steerable microrobots were developed to transport and maintain stem cells while retaining their ability to commit to the osteogenic lineage (Fig. 7c) [94]. Another study devised a template-guided strategy to streamline the assembly process and allow multiple configurations, such as V-shaped, N-shaped, and 3D structures. These microrobots exhibited flexible utility for deterministic cell manipulation, including multicell transportation, excavation from deep holes, and precise positioning, outperforming simple microrobots [95]. Such beneficial designs appear to be a viable method for regulating cell behaviors to support pulp-dentin complex restoration and other forms of oral tissue regeneration.

Collectively, these micromanipulations offer excellent maneuverability and high precision for the attachment, activation, and infiltration of oral cells or resident microbes, standing as a prospective paradigm for oral disorders. From an alternative perspective, the interactive biointerfaces also encompass MNR phagocytosis and immunological clearance by macrophage uptake. Modulating the surface charge of MNRs has been explored to evade recognition by immune cells, enhancing the circulation time and targeting capability [96]. Instead of provoking inflammation, ideal dental MNRs should facilitate tailored adjustment of cell activities and achieve optimal integration with surrounding tissues during operation.

Reprogramming metabolism through orchestrating intrinsic signaling

Metabolism involves chemical reactions that provide energy and building blocks for cells [97]. Promising MNRs for metabolic reprogramming involve nutrient uptake and utilization, energy production, and the accompanied redox reactions at the subcellular levels to ultimately promote treating refractory oral lesions.

The uptake and utilization of nutrients such as carbohydrates, fatty acids, and amino acids coordinate metabolic networks to sustain cell viability. Nutrient deprivation strategies leveraging MNR-delivered enzymes have demonstrated efficacy in disrupting biofilm metabolism. Glucose oxidase-modified systems depleted glucose and oxygen to starve endodontic pathogens [98]. Laser-driven microrobots inhibited glycolytic pathways in Streptococcus mutans biofilms, reducing cariogenicity (Fig. 8a) [19].

MNRs are also tailored to optimize energy production to meet the specific metabolic demands of the oral microenvironment. Macrophage metabolic reprogramming represents a key mechanism for inflammation modulation. A microneedle drug delivery system loaded with nanorobots (MSN-LA@MNs) enhanced mitochondrial energy metabolism in force-loaded macrophages through targeted iNOS/NO signaling, restoring cellular respiration capacity and promoting clearance of apoptotic cell debris (Fig. 8b) [26].

Another notable contribution of dental MNRs lies in their ROS-regulation ability, which encompasses two complementary strategies. ROS-generating MNRs exploit oxidative stress to manage oral infections, while ROS-scavenging MNRs neutralize excess reactive species to treat inflammatory conditions. Several ROS-generating MNRs induce abundant and broadly distributed ROS (O2•−, HOO•, and ⋅OH) to combat fungi, bacteria, and even cancer cells. Transition metal oxide nanozymes catalyze H₂O₂ decomposition through surface redox cycling, wherein Mn³⁺/Mn⁴⁺ or Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺ redox couples facilitate electron transfer to generate ⋅OH and O2•− radicals (Fig. 8c) [99, 100]. Concurrently, S-nitrosothiol groups undergo ROS-mediated homolytic cleavage to release NO, which reacts with O2•− to form peroxynitrite (ONOO⁻), amplifying oxidative damage to bacterial membranes and DNA [101]. By adjusting the electromagnetic frequency and space-time management, the catalytic activity of the nanozyme-based microrobots increases substantially with motion, being 7 times higher in rolling motion and 6 times higher in gliding motion than in vibration. This action activated ROS generation and enhanced ROS diffusion, thereby erasing fungal biofilms at contaminated sites [66]. ROS-generating capabilities are further exemplified by photocatalytic MNRs employing ZnO: Ag [61], TiO₂ [65], and BiVO₄ [28], which generate electrons to produce O₂•⁻, HOO•, and •OH species. These systems have demonstrated efficacy against specific bacterial species including Streptococcus mutans and polymicrobial dental plaque on titanium surfaces [28]. Tethering MNRs to inflammatory environments benefits from rapid ROS scavenging as well as efficient elimination of the toxic reaction products simultaneously [102]. The CeO₂-based nanorobots exhibit robust catalase and superoxide dismutase-like activities, catalytically decomposing excess ROS and inhibiting NF-κB-mediated M1 macrophage polarization through redox-dependent signaling (Fig. 8d) [76]. Another microrobot attenuated inflammation through strengthened hydrogen diffusion concomitant with potentiated ROS neutralization [103]. On top of this, it provides active paradigms for a drug-free strategy competent in spontaneously eliminating causative triggers with minimal side effects.

As a result, the MNR-based regulation of cellular metabolism provides unrivaled opportunities for dental disease therapies. Although enzymes play a major role in controlling cellular metabolism, they currently garner insufficient attention in the context of dental micro/nanomedicine. Future research will continue to focus on precisely incorporating the structure and function of diverse enzymes into MNRs, along with uncovering distinct ROS species and their impacts on their differential biological effects across cell types.

These multifunctionalized MNRs lay the groundwork for noninvasive, autonomous, and real-time monitoring of therapeutic manipulation. Progress in this burgeoning field faces hurdles, necessitating comprehensive scrutiny and resolution to propel the field forward. Manufacturing and materials

The clinical translation of dental MNRs depends on precise material selection and fabrication techniques, which directly dictate efficacy and safety in the oral environment. The structure and behavior of oral tissues significantly differ, necessitating the judicious selection of translation-ready materials tailored to target tissues [104].

Fabricating dental MNRs requires techniques capable of precise control over size, shape, composition, and functionalization [105]. A variety of methods have been employed, including additive manufacturing (3D printing/molding), templated synthesis, solution-based chemical synthesis, and microfluidic/electrospray approaches. First, 3D printing allows for the creation of intricate, customized three-dimensional geometries for personalized dental requirements. For example, components such as actuation vessels and device bodies have been manufactured via low-force SLA 3D printers with high lateral resolution and biocompatible resins, offering high precision but limited scalability and higher per-unit costs for mass production. Second, templated synthesis approaches, such as atomic layer deposition and glancing angle deposition, offer high precision for tubular or helical structures, but involve multistep processes and template removal, limiting scalability. Third, solution-based chemical synthesis methods are highly versatile and cost-effective for producing large quantities of NPs. Various MNRs (dumbbell-shaped, spherical, and Janus) have been prepared via hydrothermal, coprecipitation, self-assembly, and sputtering methods, showcasing the flexibility in terms of material and shape generation, yet they may offer less precise control over the size distribution and particle aggregation. Fourth, microfluidic and electrospraying techniques enable the encapsulation of active agents (e.g., magnetic, catalytic, and therapeutic agents). However, their scalability for ultrahigh throughput can be hindered by complex setup requirements and potential clogging issues.

There remains potential for enhancing material biocompatibility, which could benefit from the exploration of compatible biomaterials, surface modification, and morphology optimization. First, translation-ready materials fall into two categories: intrinsically biocompatible components and those degrading into non-toxic byproducts. Representative examples include DNA-based constructs, metallic nanozymes, and biodegradable polymers. Second, surface modifications have been employed in the construction and functionalization of dental MNRs to reduce potential side effects. For example, the surfaces of Fe3O4 NPs are commonly modified with polyethylenimine for biocompatibility [28], while erythrocyte membrane coating prevents immune recognition and prolongs circulation [92]. Third, morphology optimization may be feasible for eliciting minimal adverse reactions within the complex oral biological environment. Spiky magnetic particles maintain dispersion in saliva and disrupt biofilms effectively, while smooth particles aggregate and poorly penetrate narrow spaces like interdental gaps or crevices between teeth [24]. Notably, the immunogenicity and genotoxicity of dental MNRs at the cellular and tissue levels demand rigorous efforts, particularly in understanding macrophage-mediated clearance kinetics and designing materials that balance therapeutic retention with safe elimination.

Future research should focus on production innovation, biocompatible materials, and balancing precision, scalability, and cost [106]. The encoding of different particles at the individual level through tailored surface patterns and batch functions can be integrated into the manufacturing process of an MNR, thus influencing its specific behavior. Advancing application requirements and desired characteristics will be combined through diverse approaches (e.g., bionic intelligent materials) to innovate dental MNRs with higher subtlety and specificity [107].

Actuation modes and motion control

The oral cavity presents a uniquely complex and dynamic environment, posing significant challenges to the navigation and localized delivery capabilities of these tiny machines. Factors such as strong fluid flow, varying viscosity, intricate anatomical structures, biofilm formation, and diverse tissue interactions necessitate the development of sophisticated control mechanisms. Unlike controlled laboratory settings, the in vivo environment introduces variables that can significantly alter a robot’s predicted trajectory and efficacy [108].

Depending on the actuation modes employed, dental MNRs must overcome a variety of challenges in their path toward clinical translation. Magnetic fields offer precise control with spatiotemporal tunability but efficiency decreases sharply with distance, making them impractical for deep periodontal pockets (> 5 mm) where field strength drops below therapeutic thresholds. Additional concerns include metal cytotoxicity, the need for specialized equipment, and safety risks for patients with implanted devices. Light-actuated MNRs enable precise targeting with decreased chemotoxicity but suffer from limited tissue penetration (1–2 mm for visible light, 3–5 mm for NIR), risking incomplete eradication and phototoxicity. Ultrasound technology, well-established in dentistry, offers deeper penetration than light but remains underexplored for acoustic MNR applications in oral scenarios [109]. Chemical MNRs face uncontrolled motion and fuel instability in saliva, where enzymes such as catalase and peroxidase degrade H₂O₂, antioxidants scavenge reactive species, and pH buffering neutralizes acid-based fuels [110]. To address fuel stability issues, endogenous signals, such as redox potential variations, could trigger targeted MNR activation for oral tissue regeneration. Ultimately, each driving mode involves inherent trade-offs, underscoring the need for application-specific strategies or hybrid approaches to achieve synergistic outcomes in dental theranostics.

In addition to feasible and reliable actuation systems, developing spatiotemporal control is another pivotal concern for enabling dental applications in complex saliva media or confined tooth spaces. Brownian motion poses an obstacle to both trajectory control and speed regulation of MNRs [111]. Therefore, research efforts should focus on systems that impart directionality, regulate speed, and enable precise prediction of locomotion within complex fluids and tissues. Spatiotemporal control strategies are predominantly based on physical boundaries, chemical gradients, and external energy inputs [112, 113]. Bioinspired designs combining remarkable biological MNRs enable precise spatiotemporal operations, such as sperm cells capable of navigating against blood flow and the bionic bacterial flagella that achieve back-and-forth propulsion [114, 115]. Beyond bioinspired approaches, artificial intelligence (AI) offers complementary strategies to enhance both design and control optimization of MNRs through AI-driven diagnostics and intelligent materials integration [116–118].

Consequently, realistic actuation possibilities and motion control must be clearly demonstrated to ensure their effectiveness in the oral cavity. Real-time monitoring of MNR operation requires combination of motion-control strategies with imaging techniques [119]. A recent study revealed that magnetically induced magnetosome chains with superferromagnetism exhibited superior sensitivity and resolution in deep tissue, outperforming current tracers [120]. As advancements continue, it becomes imperative to tailor imaging modalities with high-frequency imaging, high penetration depth, and resolution to match real-world clinical scenarios.

Laboratory testing and clinical translation

Translationally feasible MNRs demand extensive research and costly development to standardize methodologies for analyzing their clinical efficacy. To date, current evidence remains largely limited to in vitro and small-animal studies, and there are no ongoing/completed clinical trials on dental MNRs (retrieved from http://www.clinicaltrials.gov in May 2026), highlighting the substantial gap between preclinical research and clinical implementation.Comprehensive cost analyses comparing MNRs to conventional dental treatments remain limited at this early research stage.

To ensure the accuracy and reliability of multifunctional MNRs, laboratory testing and evaluation are of paramount importance. Clinical implementation requires dedicated equipment for MNR preparation and application (e.g., magnetic control systems, light sources), with treatment durations potentially ranging from minutes to hours depending on the indication. Clinicians would require specialized training in MNR handling, navigation control, and real-time monitoring, necessitating the development of standardized protocols and certification programs. Regulatory approval requires comprehensive evaluation including ISO 10993 biocompatibility testing across dental-specific endpoints such as enamel integrity, pulp viability, and gingival inflammation, large animal dental studies with realistic oral microbiome models, and phased clinical trials addressing safety, efficacy, and long-term tissue retention [121].

Currently, MNR design and fabrication are typically conducted in sterile laboratories, without involving large-scale production. For clinical translation, comprehensive considerations of aspects such as material safety and procurement, the reliability and efficiency of manufacturing processes, batch-to-batch consistency, and quality control measures are crucial to instill confidence in the medical community [122]. Recognizing and addressing these hurdles is vital for advancing the practicality and effectiveness of MNRs in real-world therapeutic scenarios.

Conclusions and outlook

MNRs have demonstrated particular promise in addressing the oral-specific barriers through precise navigation in confined anatomies, targeted biofilm eradication, and inflammation resolution. Among the platforms reviewed, magnetically driven MNRs currently appear most proximate to clinical translation for periodontal and endodontic indications, given their demonstrated efficacy, compatibility with existing dental imaging infrastructure, and the availability of FDA-approved magnetic formulations such as ferumoxytol. Despite rapid progress, there are still opportunities for improvement and interconnected obstacles to overcome in future research efforts.

From a translational standpoint, a particularly pressing impediment is the profound mismatch between the controlled conditions under which MNRs are currently validated and the dynamic reality of the living oral environment. Embedding safety-by-design principles into MNR fabrication from the earliest stages, validating performance in clinically relevant polymicrobial and large-animal models, and developing dental-specific endpoints will be prerequisites for regulatory advancement. Safe dosage thresholds for vulnerable populations such as older adults and immunocompromised patients also remain undefined and warrant dedicated investigation.

MNRs have evolved from simple structures to reconfigurable systems with cross-terrain adaptability for the heterogeneous oral landscape, yet current research mainly relies on qualitative assessment with insufficient quantitative correlation between MNR properties and biological function. Further work should elucidate interactions between MNR materials and oral environments to establish design-performance relationships that inform rational optimization. Microfluidic and lab-on-a-chip platforms may further accelerate MNR screening in physiologically relevant oral models [123].

At the biological level, current dental MNRs predominantly focus on biofilm eradication, yet indiscriminate antimicrobial action risks disrupting commensal communities and provoking secondary dysbiosis. Future MNR platforms should evolve toward ecological modulators capable of selectively suppressing pathobionts while preserving beneficial flora.

Beyond local oral applications, the interrelationships between oral diseases and systemic conditions further demand individualized MNR strategies aligned with precision medicine rather than a one-size-fits-all approach. A recent advance exemplifying this direction is the molecular robot, which activates osteogenesis only upon simultaneous detection of mesenchymal stem cell contact, acidic pH, and elevated ROS in diabetic periodontitis models [124]. Such microenvironment-responsive designs illustrate how MNRs can evolve from externally powered machines toward autonomous, self-gated therapeutic agents tailored to individual disease states.

Rapid advances in AI may support more intelligent MNR-enabled dental healthcare through improved motion control, autonomous navigation, and real-time treatment planning tailored to oral microenvironments. However, the integration of AI with MNRs faces hurdles in processing complex biomedical feedback and ensuring system reliability. Ethical frameworks addressing data privacy and informed consent will also need to be developed concurrently with technical advances.

In summary, this review links current progress with long-term goals for practical dental MNRs. With continued progress in fundamental understanding and technical innovation, MNRs may transform personalized oral healthcare from laboratory demonstrations into chairside practice.
