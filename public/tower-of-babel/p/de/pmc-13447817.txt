An eye tracking examination of fantasy television’s immediate influence on children’s executive functions
Authors: Sara Arian Namazi, Saeid Sadeghi, Mohammad Kiani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSeveral studies have investigated the immediate impact of viewing fantastical television (TV) programs on children’s cognitive function; however, the results have been inconsistent. This study experimentally investigates the immediate impact of TV content (high fantasy vs. low fantasy) on the executive functions (EFs), specifically visual attention and inhibitory control, of 65 children aged 5–7. Children were randomly assigned to view either high fantasy (HF) or low fantasy (LF) programs, and their EFs were assessed with eye-tracking pro/anti-saccade tasks before and after viewing the program. The results showed no significant impact of HF content on inhibitory control or visual attention after controlling for age, screen time habits, and EF-related difficulties. Bayesian analysis further supported these null findings. The absence of an effect may be attributed to the specific eye-tracking tasks used, the developmental stage of the participants, and the cultural context. These findings challenge the universality of the fantasy deficit, emphasizing the complex nature of media effects and the critical role of methodology, development, and culture in shaping cognitive outcomes.Keywords: Attention, Eye tracker, Fantasy, Inhibitory control, Preschool, TV programsSubject terms: Neuroscience, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

Before they can tie their shoes or write their names, many children can unlock a smartphone and navigate a world designed for adults, displaying a fluency often described as “digital nativity”1. Digital devices, including computers, smartphones, tablets, and televisions (TVs), have become integral to children’s daily routines2,3. Technology advancements have led to early and frequent screen exposure among Generation Alpha children, who often learn to navigate digital devices independently2. A survey of parents revealed that a percentage of children, 25% aged 0 to 2 and 58% aged 2–5, spend more time on digital devices than recommended4. Moreover, screen use generally increases with age, with nearly half of all children identified as “high media users”5, with recent statistics showing daily screen time increasing from approximately 2 h for 2- to 4-year-olds to over 3.5 h for children aged 5 to 86.

As screen-based technologies become ubiquitous in households7, concerns about their potential impact on children’s development have grown. While digital media provides opportunities for early learning and social interaction, unsupervised use has been linked to various physical and mental health challenges8,9. These include obesity, sleep disturbances, structural brain changes, and developmental delays in motor and language10,11.

Among the various digital platforms, TV gets particular attention due to its continued dominance in children’s media consumption. Despite the rise of newer digital media, TV remains a highly influential digital device7. Studies suggest that rather than being replaced, TV has adapted to the digital landscape, retaining its applicability12. For instance, a study found that watching TV and videos remains the primary reason children use screen devices, accounting for nearly three-quarters (73%) of their total screen time3. Watching TV is a complex cognitive process involving attention, encoding, and storage13. According to foundational cognitive theories like the multiple resource theory, these mental processes draw from a limited pool of cognitive resources14. This raises a critical concern that if watching certain types of content heavily taxes these resources, a child’s ability to perform subsequent cognitive tasks, such as learning in a classroom, could be immediately and negatively affected.

Building on these concerns, numerous studies have examined how various characteristics of TV content influence children’s cognitive development15,16. These investigations can be broadly categorized into long-term correlational studies and short-term experimental studies. The experimental investigation of these immediate and short-term effects is particularly valuable. It not only allows researchers to isolate potential causal mechanisms that, through repeated exposure, could accumulate into the long-term developmental outcomes observed in correlational work17 but is also important due to its direct ecological validity. For instance, with the increasing integration of TV programming into educational settings in countries like Iran, where preschools often include a TV program or cartoon time immediately preceding lessons, a deeper understanding of the immediate cognitive consequences of such programming is essential to ensure children are in an optimal state for subsequent learning activities18,19. This leads to the question of which types of content might have the most pronounced and immediate cognitive effects20.

One particularly influential form of TV content is fantasy, which includes events that defy physical laws (e.g., flying superheroes)20. The impact of such high fantastical (HF) content on cognitive abilities relates to humans’ innate understanding of physical laws. Even infants form fundamental concepts of how the world operates21, and when these expectations are violated, increased cognitive processing effort is required20. Cognitive models suggest that the demand for limited cognitive resources is greater for HF content than for more LF programming. This theoretical framework leads to a clear prediction that exposure to HF content may temporarily deplete the cognitive resources needed for goal-directed behavior13,22.

Consistent with these theoretical predictions, empirical research has begun to demonstrate specific cognitive consequences associated with exposure to HF TV content, particularly concerning executive functions (EF)15. EFs are cognitive processes that enable goal-directed behavior and adaptive responses to novel situations23. Given EFs’ diverse definitions and complex nature24, research has focused on investigating their components. One of the prominent models of EFs breaks them down into three core components: inhibitory control, working memory, and cognitive flexibility23,25.

Inhibitory control refers to the ability to suppress dominant, automatic, or prepotent responses in favor of goal-directed behavior. For instance, a child uses inhibitory control to keep their hands to themselves when they want to grab a toy, to wait their turn to speak, or to focus on a teacher’s instructions instead of a distracting noise outside. A key aspect of inhibitory control is top-down attentional control23,26–28, which selectively filters and prioritizes task-relevant information while suppressing interference from distractions29–31. For instance, a child employs top-down attention to focus on the teacher’s drawing on the whiteboard while actively inhibiting the impulse to look at a colorful but irrelevant poster on the wall. In contrast, bottom-up attention is automatically captured by a salient stimulus32, such as when a bright bird suddenly flies past the classroom window, drawing the child’s gaze regardless of their goals. These processes form part of cognitive control, the mechanism that enables adjustment of behavior in response to complex environmental demands33,34.

These self-regulatory skills are essential within the educational system, where a teacher-centered approach and highly structured learning demand children’s ability to exert inhibitory control and maintain focused attention19. Therefore, given the importance of inhibitory control for cognitive development in various areas, including academic learning and reasoning skills35,36, a large number of studies have examined the effects of HF content on this specific function. A review of the existing literature suggests that the majority of these studies report an immediate negative impact of HF exposure on inhibitory control16. For instance, Rhodes et al.37 indicated that brief exposure to HF content impaired inhibition, working memory, and cognitive flexibility in 5-6-year-olds. Similarly, Keşşafoğlu et al.38 showed that watching HF programming disrupted inhibitory control in 5-6-year-olds after only 8 min of exposure. Li et al.39 used eye tracking and fNIRS data to confirm that fantastical events demand greater cognitive resources, impairing EF in Chinese preschoolers. These immediate deficits are typically measured in the minutes directly following media exposure, as research indicates that even a 10-minute delay between viewing and testing can eliminate the negative impact38. These findings underscore the temporary but significant immediate cognitive effects of HF TV content across different cultures, highlighting its potentially disruptive nature in time-critical situations like a post-viewing classroom activity.

However, despite the growing body of research investigating how HF content influences inhibitory control, the findings remain inconsistent, and important gaps in understanding persist. Some studies have reported differing results, complicating the overall picture. For instance, Kostyrka-Allchorne et al.40 observed positive effects associated with fantastical features, while Hinten15 found no significant impact of HF content on inhibitory control in 5-7-year-olds. These discrepancies highlight the need for further research to understand the factors contributing to these inconsistent outcomes16.

Furthermore, only one study40 has explored the effects of HF programming on bottom-up attention, the automatic capture of attention by salient events. Since fantastical events are highly salient and visually stimulating, they are hypothesized to engage bottom-up processing22. However, this study did not find significant differences between children exposed to HF versus LF content, which leaves the question unanswered.

One possible explanation for these observed results lies in the methodological approaches used to measure EFs16. Assessing EFs in children involves a combination of performance-based cognitive tasks and behavioral rating scales. Behavioral rating scales depend on observations made by adults familiar with the child’s everyday behavior41,42, while cognitive tasks involve children completing standardized assessments under the supervision of trained professionals. To measure inhibitory control specifically, a range of tasks and instruments are used, each targeting different aspects of this function43. Among these, eye-tracking tasks have demonstrated greater sensitivity to age-related improvements in inhibition than some neuropsychological tests, suggesting their potential utility in assessing EF development in young children44. One such task is the anti-saccade paradigm, which measures both attentional and inhibitory processes (attentional control)45,46. This paradigm has also been employed in previous research examining the effects of digital device exposure on young children17,47.

Additionally, although existing studies have examined different cultural contexts, they have predominantly focused on Western and Chinese populations, and their generalizability to other populations remains uncertain. Cultural context shapes EF development, potentially moderating media effects. This is particularly relevant for an Iranian population. Recent research by Nejati et al.48 provides direct evidence for this, showing that Iranian preschoolers demonstrate significantly stronger baseline inhibitory control compared to their German peers, a difference attributed to cultural and educational styles emphasizing self-regulation. This finding challenges the assumption that all children will be equally susceptible to the cognitive disruption of HF media. It is plausible that children with more robust baseline inhibition are more resilient to such short-term effects.

Therefore, the present study aims to address these gaps by employing sensitive eye-tracking paradigms with Iranian children aged 5–7. This age range was selected as it represents a period of increasing screen use and the beginning of formal education, where potential media effects have direct relevance. We used a pro-saccade (gap) task to measure bottom-up visual attention and an anti-saccade task to measure inhibitory control, a well-established and sensitive paradigm for assessing these functions44,45. Based on the literature, we formulated two hypotheses. First, consistent with the majority of previous findings and the resource-depletion model37–39, we hypothesized that children exposed to the HF TV program would show impaired performance on the measure of inhibitory control (the anti-saccade task) compared to those exposed to the LF program. Second, based on the visually stimulating nature of HF content and its hypothesized engagement of bottom-up processing22,40, we also hypothesized that children exposed to the HF TV program would demonstrate enhanced performance on the measure of visual attention (the gap task) relative to the LF condition.

Method

Participants

Sixty-five typically developing children aged 5–7 years (M = 6.1 years, SD = 0.58 years, 32 females) participated in this study. These children were recruited from primary schools in Tehran, Iran. An initial 78 children were recruited, but 13 were excluded, 10 expressed unwillingness to complete the study and 3 were unable to demonstrate comprehension of the task instructions during the practice trials. The remaining 65 children were randomly assigned to either an HF (N = 32, M = 6.15 years, SD = 0.59 years) or an LF (N = 33, M = 6.05 years, SD = 0.58 years) TV program. Consistent with sample sizes used in previous studies, the study aimed to recruit approximately 30 participants per group. There was no significant difference in age between groups (t = 0.691, p = .492). All children had no history of psychiatric, neurological, or sensory-motor disorders, and had normal or corrected-to-normal vision. The majority of the children’s mothers held a bachelor’s (55.4%) or graduate degree (16.9%), while 26.1% had a high school education or less, and 1.5% had an associate degree. Similarly, most fathers held a bachelor’s (29.2%) or graduate degree (36.9%), with 4.6% holding an associate degree and 29.2% having a high school education or less. The study was approved by the Shahid Beheshti University Ethics Committee (IR.SBU.REC.1402.101) and was not preregistered.

Material

TV programs (HF and LF programs)

The selection of TV programs was anchored to the seminal study by Lillard et al.20, which not only was the first to experimentally investigate the immediate impact of fantastical content on children’s EFs but also established a methodological framework, including its specific programs and coding methods, that has been frequently employed in subsequent research. To allow for direct comparison while addressing potential confounds in the original study, we adopted a modified version of their design. Lillard et al.20 utilized four programs to examine both content (HF vs. LF) and pacing (fast vs. slow). As our primary research question focused specifically on the cognitive effect of fantastical content, we intentionally selected two slow-paced programs to separate this variable from the known confounding effects of fast pacing on EF.

For the HF condition, we used the “Flight of the Instrument Fairies” episode from Little Einsteins, and for the LF condition, we used the “All Tied Up” episode from Little Bill. These specific programs were chosen because they were also used in the Lillard et al.20 study, ensuring methodological continuity. This approach allows for a more direct comparison of findings across different assessment tools. To ensure validity for our Iranian sample, the programs were professionally dubbed in Persian and edited to match the 8-minute segments used in the original research.

To verify the classification of the programs, two independent coders followed Lillard et al.20 coding scheme to assess the frequency of fantasy elements and the rate of scene changes (by Shutter Encoder 18.5, 2024; Table 1). The Little Einsteins episode contained a high density of events that violate physical laws (e.g., impossible transformations, magical appearances), resulting in a high fantasy rate with 16 distinct fantastical events. In contrast, the Little Bill episode, which focuses on a 5-year-old’s daily life, contained only a single non-central fantastical element (a pair of talking shoes), yielding a very low fantasy rate with only one fantastical event.

Parent questionnaires

Parents completed a checklist and a questionnaire to provide information about their children’s prior screen time and pre-existing differences in EF-related difficulties.

Screen time checklist

Screen time habits were included as a covariate in line with previous studies49,50, given their predictive role in children’s cognitive and behavioral outcomes47,51,52. We used the checklist to ask parents about their children’s daily screen time, including TV/videos and gaming. Parents observed their children’s digital device use for 24 h on weekdays and weekends, using Sects. 17 and 18 of the Home Literacy Environment Log (HLE-log). The weighted average of children’s daily screen time was calculated by assigning weights to five weekdays and two weekend days.

The strengths and difficulties questionnaire (SDQ)

The child’s behavioral and emotional problems were assessed using the SDQ53, which has been considered indicative of EF-related difficulties due to its structural similarity to EF tasks20. Consistent with previous studies20,39, we included this measure as a potential confounding variable. The SDQ includes 25 items divided into five subscales: conduct problems, hyperactivity, emotional problems, peer problems, and prosocial behavior. Each item is rated on a three-point Likert scale from ‘not true’ to ‘certainly true.’ The total SDQ score is calculated by summing the first four subscales, providing an overall measure of behavioral and emotional problems. The internal consistency of the original English version was satisfactory (Cronbach’s α = 0.73; Goodman, 2001), and the Persian versions have demonstrated strong internal consistency for the total SDQ score (Cronbach’s α ranging from 0.70 to 0.85;54,55. In the present study, the internal consistency for the total SDQ score was also good (Cronbach’s α = 0.79).

Children EF assessment

To assess attention and inhibitory control (attentional control), we used pro-saccade (gap task) and anti-saccade tasks as designed by Amestoy et al.56, which are based on the visually guided saccade (VGS) paradigm. This paradigm measures oculomotor control and inhibition using exogenous cues57,58. Pro-saccade includes gap, step, and overlap tasks, of which the gap task was used in this study. The gap task provides a baseline measure of visual attention by requiring a simple reflexive eye movement, looking toward a target that appears on the screen. In contrast, the more cognitively demanding anti-saccade task measures inhibitory control by requiring children to suppress this natural, reflexive urge and instead generate a voluntary eye movement away from the target to its mirrored position (Fig. 1). This paradigm allows for a direct measure of the cognitive effort needed to inhibit a prepotent response.

Experimental Design of Gap and Anti-saccade Tasks. The figure illustrates the sequence of events within a single trial. (A) Fixation: All trials began with the child focusing on a central green moon. (B) Target Presentation: The moon disappeared, and after a 200 ms interval, a peripheral target (red star) appeared on either the left or the right side of the screen. (C) Gap Task Response: In the gap task, the child was instructed to look directly at the star. The white arrow indicates the correct saccade toward the stimulus. (D) Anti-saccade Task Response: In the anti-saccade task, the child was instructed to look away from the star to its mirrored position on the opposite side of the screen, thereby inhibiting the natural tendency to look at the target.

Procedures

Before the study, parents provided written informed consent for their children’s participation. Parents also completed questionnaires about their child’s behavior and media use at home. Each child was tested individually in a quiet, empty classroom at their school. The session lasted for approximately 15 min. During the warm-up, the researcher explained that she would play a few short games with the child. Then, as the pretest, each child randomly completed pro- and anti-saccade tasks (described in detail below). After that, they watched one of two TV programs on the same screen for approximately 8 min. Following the program, they completed the same set of tasks as the posttest (Fig. 2). Each child received a reward, such as a pencil or a notebook, after the experiment. Given the use of identical tasks for both pre- and post-tests, a learning effect is anticipated. However, since this effect is expected to be consistent across both the HF and LF TV program groups, any significant differences observed between groups can be attributed to the content of the TV programs rather than practice effects.

A schematic diagram of the study procedure.

The child was seated and placed their chin on a chinrest 65 cm away from the eye tracker. Tasks were presented in a 23-inch display (1920 × 1080 resolution and 144 Hz refreshing rate), synchronized with a Lenovo laptop, and conducted using a GP3 HD Eye Tracker (Gazepoint, Canada) with a sampling rate of 150 Hz. Each task was preceded by a 9-point calibration. A single trial for both tasks followed a precise, computer-controlled sequence: (1) Central Fixation: The trial initiated with a green moon fixation point (0.5 × 0.5 cm) appearing in the center of a black screen. This remained visible for a variable duration of 2000–3500 ms to ensure the child’s attention was centered. (2) Gap and Target Onset: The central fixation point disappeared. Following a 200 ms blank interval (the “gap”), a red star (0.5 × 0.5 cm) appeared as a peripheral stimulus. It was presented for 1000 ms, located 24 cm to either the right or left of the center. (3) Participant Response: The instructions, which were explained and practiced with the child beforehand, determined the required response for each task block. For the gap task, the instruction was to look at the peripheral target as quickly as possible (e.g., “As soon as you see the star, look right at it!”). For the anti-saccade task, the instruction required inhibiting the reflexive saccade, asking the child to look to the mirror-image location in the opposite direction of the target (e.g., “This time, it’s a trick! When the star shows up, I want you to look to the other side where there is nothing!”). The results of these tasks are obtained by examining the reaction time in shifting the gaze towards or away from the peripheral stimulus. The anti-saccade task demands greater cognitive effort, which makes its latencies longer than the gap task59. We extracted four oculomotor variables from each task: latency, gain, anticipatory, and erroneous saccades. Gap task results measure visual attention, while anti-saccade results and the anti-saccade effect (the delta between gap and anti-saccade latencies) measure inhibitory control.

Latency: Latency, or response time, refers to the delay up to the first non-anticipatory fixation. This includes the reaction time (i.e., the interval between the target stimulus’s appearance and the onset of the saccade) plus the saccade’s duration until fixation occurs.

This study provided four practice trials at the start of each task to ensure participant familiarization and comprehension. These trials were identical to the experimental ones (see Fig. 1); however, children’s performance on them was not included in the analysis. Following the practice trials, each task consisted of 15 experimental trials. The tasks collectively required approximately 8 min, and the order of tasks presentation was randomized across participants.

Data processing

Data analysis and eye-tracking preprocessing were performed using R programming version 4.3.1. All trial exclusion criteria and data handling procedures followed the established method outlined by Amestoy et al.56. Eye fixation parameters, including X and Y position, accuracy, stimulus presentation time, and fixation duration at the central and peripheral stimuli, were extracted. Dependent variables were then computed after automatically removing low-quality and invalid data. Trials in which participants failed to fixate on the central point (within a 4 cm tolerance) when the target stimulus appeared were excluded. This resulted in the exclusion of 7.59% and 9.85% of trials for the gap task, and 7.08% and 8.10% for the anti-saccade task, during the pre- and post-test, respectively. We then calculated latency and gain for non-anticipatory, non-erroneous saccades. An additional 1.13% and 0.31% of trials from the gap task, and 0.41% and 0% from the anti-saccade task (pre- and post-test), were excluded as outliers exceeding three standard deviations from the mean. For each trial, only latency and gain values from the first saccade reaching at least one-third of the target distance were considered.

Results

Preliminary analyses

The groups were first compared using analyses of means (Independent t-test) on the parent reporting data of children (Table 2). The results indicated that there were no significant group differences in screen time, the SDQ scores, visual attention scores in pre-test (latency, gain, anticipatory saccade, and erroneous saccade), and inhibitory control scores in pre-test (latency, anticipatory saccade, erroneous saccade, and anti-saccade effect). However, there was a significant difference in inhibitory control gain scores, which were higher in the LF program group (mean differences = 0.062). As shown in Table 2, children’s screen time (in minutes) and SDQ scores varied widely, with some children reporting up to 10 h of daily digital device use and SDQ scores as high as 29 out of 40, which is considered very high score on this measure of EF-related difficulties. To account for this variability, screen time and SDQ scores, along with the children’s age (in months), were included as covariates in the analysis.

Main analyses

All variables were analyzed separately using analyses of covariances (ANCOVA), with post-test scores as the dependent variables, TV program condition as the independent variable, and pre-test scores, children’s age, prior screen time, and EF-related difficulties (SDQ score) included as covariates. Statistical significance was determined at a p-value of < 0.05. According to Levene’s test, the variances meet the homogeneity assumption for visual attention (latency score: F = 0.485, p = .489; gain score: F = 0.060, p = .807; anticipatory saccade: F = 0.236, p = .629; and erroneous saccade: F = 1.190, p = .280) and inhibitory control (latency score: F = 1.040, p = .312; gain score: F = 0.193, p = .662; anticipatory saccade: F = 0.078, p = .769; erroneous saccade: F = 1.087, p = .301; and anti-saccade effect: F = 0.392, p = .533). Additionally, the normality of ANCOVA residuals was assessed using the Lilliefors test for normality. All residuals met the normality assumption except for the erroneous saccade variable in visual attention (D = 0.207, p = .007). To address this violation, the data for this variable were ranked prior to performing ANCOVA. Additionally, we found no significant interaction between the pre-test scores and the program group for any of our dependent variables, indicating that the assumption was met.

The ANCOVA analysis revealed that the main effect of the program groups was not statistically significant, indicating that there were no significant differences in visual attention (latency score: F = 0.803, p = 0.374, ηp2 = 0.014; gain score: F = 0.131, p = 0.719, ηp2 = 0.002; anticipatory saccade: F = 2.579, p = 0.114, ηp2 = 0.043; and erroneous saccade: F = 0.139, p = 0.710, ηp2 = 0.002) and inhibitory control (latency score: F = 0.008, p = 0.929, ηp2 = 0.0001; gain score: F = 3.100, p = 0.084, ηp2 = 0.051; anticipatory saccade: F = 0.248, p = 0.620, ηp2 = 0.004; erroneous saccade: F = 0.736, p = 0.395, ηp2 = 0.013; and anti-saccade effect: F = 0.084, p = 0.773, ηp2 = 0.001) post-test scores between the two TV program groups after controlling for pre-test, age, screen time and EF-related difficulties. Bayesian analyses

To further interrogate the null findings from the primary frequentist ANCOVAs and quantify the evidence for the null hypothesis, we conducted supplementary Bayesian analyses. For each dependent variable, a Bayesian ANCOVA was performed, mirroring the structure of the primary analyses. This compared a full model (including TV program group) against a null model (excluding group but retaining all covariates: pre-test score, age, screen time, and SDQ score). The resulting Bayes Factor (BF₁₀) indicates the evidence for the alternative hypothesis relative to the null hypothesis. A BF₁₀ below 1 provides evidence for the null, with values of 0.33–1 indicating anecdotal evidence and 0.1–0.33 indicating moderate evidence.

The results consistently supported the null hypothesis across all measures. For visual attention, evidence for the null was found for latency (BF₁₀ = 0.42), gain (BF₁₀ = 0.32), anticipatory saccades (BF₁₀ = 0.25), and erroneous saccades (BF₁₀ = 0.29; this analysis used rank-transformed data to match the frequentist approach). Similarly, for inhibitory control, evidence for the null was observed for latency (BF₁₀ = 0.28), gain (BF₁₀ = 0.30), anticipatory saccades (BF₁₀ = 0.26), erroneous saccades (BF₁₀ = 0.27), and the anti-saccade effect (BF₁₀ = 0.26).

To ensure robustness, we conducted a secondary analysis using change scores (post-test minus pre-test). A Bayesian ANCOVA was used to assess whether the TV program group predicted these change scores after controlling for age, SDQ score, and screen time. This analysis largely corroborated the initial findings, providing moderate evidence for the null hypothesis for change in visual attention gain (BF₁₀ = 0.27), anticipatory saccades (BF₁₀ = 0.25), and erroneous saccades (BF₁₀ = 0.25), as well as for change in inhibitory control latency (BF₁₀ = 0.29) and anticipatory saccades (BF₁₀ = 0.30). Anecdotal evidence for the null was found for change in visual attention latency (BF₁₀ = 0.78), inhibitory control erroneous saccades (BF₁₀ = 0.51), and the anti-saccade effect (BF₁₀ = 0.44). The change score for visual attention erroneous saccades was rank-transformed due to non-normality. A single exception was noted for the change in inhibitory control gain (BF₁₀ = 2.50), which provided anecdotal evidence for the alternative hypothesis.

Finally, to investigate potential overall effects of TV viewing or practice, we conducted Bayesian paired-samples analyses comparing pre-test and post-test scores across all participants, integrating the two groups. For most variables, the results provided moderate evidence for the null hypothesis, indicating no significant change from pre-test to post-test. This was the case for all visual attention measures, including erroneous saccades (BF₁₀ = 0.18), latency (BF₁₀ = 0.21), gain (BF₁₀ = 0.19), and anticipatory saccades (BF₁₀ = 0.19). For inhibitory control, there was moderate evidence for no change in anticipatory saccades (BF₁₀ = 0.20) and anecdotal evidence for no change in erroneous saccades (BF₁₀ = 0.39) and gain (BF₁₀ = 0.44). However, there was anecdotal evidence for a change in inhibitory control latency (BF₁₀ = 2.55) and the anti-saccade effect (BF₁₀ = 1.52), with mean latencies slightly decreasing at post-test, potentially suggesting a practice effect for this specific task. Collectively, the comprehensive set of Bayesian analyses provides strong, convergent evidence for a lack of immediate effects of program content on the measured cognitive functions.

Discussion

The current study examined the immediate impact of watching HF versus LF TV programs on the visual attention and inhibitory control of 5–7-year-old Iranian children. Contrary to our primary hypothesis and several previous studies20,37–39,49,50, and aligning with Hinten15 and Wang and Moriguchi60, our results indicated that the type of TV program did not significantly affect performance on either the gap task or the anti-saccade task. This null finding was further supported by a Bayesian analysis, which provided positive evidence for the null hypothesis, suggesting a genuine absence of an effect between the groups. This outcome challenges the assertion that brief exposure to HF content has a universally detrimental impact on EFs and suggests the effect is moderated by several key factors discussed below.

One potential explanation for these null findings lies in the specific nature of the inhibitory control task employed. This study used the anti-saccade paradigm, which measures a highly specific form of oculomotor inhibition requiring rapid, moment-to-moment cognitive control46,61,62. In contrast, studies that reported a negative HF effect often used broader, multi-component tasks. These include Stroop-based tasks, which tax interference control and require more sustained cognitive effort to resolve conflict20,37,39,50,63, or composite behavioral tasks like the Head-Toes-Knees-Shoulders (HTKS)18,38. Although all these tasks tap into the broader domain of inhibitory control, the constrained nature of the anti-saccade task differs significantly from these more complex measures. Consequently, the cognitive depletion from brief HF exposure may be insufficient to impair this foundational inhibitory mechanism, even if it affects performance on tasks with higher cognitive demands. This suggests the fantasy deficit effect is fragile and may not manifest when inhibition is measured with such specificity. Therefore, fantastical stimuli likely have varying impacts on distinct subcomponents of inhibitory control, a possibility that future research must address by directly comparing the sensitivity of these different paradigms.

The cultural context of our sample offers a compelling explanation for the null findings. Recent cross-cultural research indicates that Iranian preschoolers demonstrate stronger inhibitory control compared to their Western peers48. This observation of heightened EF aligns with an Iranian educational system that emphasizes structured learning and self-regulation from an early age. This stronger cognitive foundation may confer greater resilience to transient cognitive loads, such as those imposed by HF media. Thus, our results can be interpreted as a culturally-situated demonstration of resilience, where higher baseline EFs protect against the temporary depletion effects observed in other populations. Therefore, future studies are needed to conduct cross-cultural investigations to examine how TV content may differentially affect children across diverse cultural contexts.

Another factor to consider is the age of our participants (5–7 years). This period, marking the start of formal schooling, is characterized by significant EF development. While previous research suggests that the negative effects of HF are more pronounced in younger preschoolers (ages 3–5) whose inhibitory control is still nascent, our results challenge the assumption that 5–7-year-olds are universally susceptible. This aligns with a constructivist view of development64, whereby children’s evolving cognitive abilities shape their media processing. Older children are better equipped to critically evaluate and distinguish fantastical content from reality, reducing its disruptive potential. This cognitive maturation, potentially accelerated within a cultural context that fosters self-regulation, may provide the EF capacity necessary for resilience against brief media exposure. Therefore, our findings indicate that by age 5, children can possess a cognitive buffer, making the fantasy deficit effect less a universal phenomenon and more a function of developmental and contextual factors.

The pre-test/post-test design of this study also allows for an examination of potential practice or learning effects. An exploratory Bayesian analysis of performance changes from pre-test to post-test across the entire sample revealed a significant improvement in anti-saccade latency and effect, but no change in any other metric. This finding is interpreted as a task-specific learning effect65, where performance improves due to familiarity with the task demands. This improvement occurred equally in both the HF and LF groups, confirming that it did not confound the primary analysis of between-group differences. This analysis also serves to rule out a general effect of TV viewing, as exposure did not lead to a broad decline in EF performance. The pre-test may have even activated cognitive control networks, potentially creating a ceiling effect that masked subtle disruptions from the HF content.‬‬‬‬‬.

Finally, the null findings can be understood through the lens of exposure duration. Our study was designed to detect immediate effects following a brief eight-minute exposure, consistent with the methodology of prior experimental work20,38. The absence of an effect suggests that, for this age group and within this cultural context, such short-term exposure is insufficient to produce detectable changes in core EFs. This aligns with neuroplasticity principles66,67 and cultivation theory68, which posit that meaningful cognitive changes often result from cumulative and long-term exposure rather than single brief events. While longitudinal research has linked habitual screen time to EF outcomes47, our results indicate that these long-term correlations may not be driven by the kind of immediate temporary cognitive depletion proposed by the fantasy deficit hypothesis.

In our second hypothesis, we predicted that fantastical content, with its novelty and perceptual unpredictability, would enhance bottom-up stimulus-driven attention69,70. However, we found no group differences on the gap task, consistent with Kostyrka-Allchorne et al.31. The gap task is a direct measure of basic attentional disengagement and saccadic orienting71,72. The absence of an effect on this task indicates that an eight-minute exposure to HF content was insufficient to alter these fundamental orienting mechanisms. While it is possible that bottom-up priming from the TV show was too temporary to be measured, a more direct interpretation is that the content did not produce a meaningful change in this aspect of visual attention. Future research could employ tasks with competing distractors to investigate whether HF content affects more complex aspects of attentional control, but our results provide clear evidence against an effect on basic attentional orienting.

In addition, our findings revealed a significant age-related improvement in children’s inhibitory control accuracy (gain), consistent with a well-established body of developmental research25,50,73. As children grow older, their EFs tend to mature due to both neurobiological and experiential factors. This trend is supported by neurodevelopmental theories suggesting that the prefrontal cortex, which underlies EFs, undergoes prolonged maturation well into adolescence23. A relationship was also observed between visual attention accuracy and EF-related difficulties, such that higher EF-related difficulties scores were associated with greater accuracy in visual attention. However, this trend should be interpreted with caution due to the low number of participants exhibiting high levels of EF-related difficulties. It is possible that the observed association reflects sampling variability rather than a true population-level effect. From both methodological and psychometric perspectives, small subgroup sizes can introduce instability and limit the generalizability of findings.

Limitation

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, our pre-test/post-test design, while necessary for measuring change, introduces the possibility of practice effects. Indeed, our exploratory analysis confirmed a general improvement in anti-saccade latency from pre- to post-test, indicative of task learning. Although we statistically controlled for this by including pre-test scores as a covariate, and this learning effect occurred equally across both groups, it is a factor inherent to this study design. Second, our assessment of visual attention relied on the gap task, a measure of fundamental attentional disengagement and orienting. While this allowed for a direct test of whether HF content alters these basic mechanisms, our null finding does not rule out effects on other aspects of attention. A more comprehensive assessment in future research would benefit from including tasks that measure other aspects of attention, such as visual search tasks. Finally, although this study replicated aspects of Lillard et al.20 by using similar TV programs, it did not control for other influential features such as educational content or language complexity, which may have moderated the observed effects. Future studies need to carefully select their TV programs and consider other influential characteristics.

Conclusion

This study investigated the immediate impact of HF versus LF TV programs on Iranian children’s EF, specifically visual attention and inhibitory control, considering covariates such as age, EF-related difficulties, and screen time habits in children aged 5–7 years. Contrary to expectations, exposure to HF content did not significantly impact either inhibitory control or visual attention. This null finding, supported by Bayesian analysis, challenges the universality of the fantasy deficit. Instead, our results suggest the effect is fragile and highly moderated by methodological, developmental, and cultural factors. Additionally, the absence of significant effects on visual attention suggests that fantastical elements may not alter fundamental attentional orienting in this age group. Therefore, this study shifts the conversation from whether HF content is detrimental to a more nuanced investigation of for whom, under what measurement conditions, and in which cultural contexts such effects emerge. Future research should embrace this complexity by directly comparing tasks, ages, and cultures to build a more precise and ecologically valid model of how media interacts with the developing brain.
