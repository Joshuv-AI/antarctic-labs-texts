Electric scooter related injuries among children: a single institutional experience
Authors: Ali Abu-Kishk, Eran Kozer, Rony Beeri Berkowiz, Meital Ben-Ari, Ibrahim Abu-Kishk
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundElectric scooter (ES) use has become increasingly popular, particularly among children. However, few studies have characterized pediatric ES-related injuries. We present our experience with children hospitalized due to ES-related trauma.MethodsA retrospective review was conducted of 103 pediatric patients hospitalized with ES-related injuries between 2018 and 2024. Data collected included patient demographics, type of injury, helmet use, clinical presentation, inpatient management, and follow-up.ResultsES injuries accounted for approximately 3% of all pediatric trauma admissions. The mean age of patients was 11.3 ± 3.7 years, and 84 (81.5%) were male. Helmet use was documented in 55 (62.5%) of cases. No significant trend in patient age or injury incidence was observed over the study period. Orthopedic injuries were the most common (n = 73, 70.9%), followed by general pediatric surgical injuries (n = 11, 10.7%). Twelve patients (11.6%) required admission to the pediatric intensive care unit (PICU). Multiple injuries were observed in 14 patients (13.6%). A significant association was found between head trauma and lack of helmet use OR 23.8 (95% CI 3-200).ConclusionES-related injuries, particularly orthopedic trauma, are a common cause of hospitalization in children and are associated with notable morbidity. Helmet use was protective against head injury, underscoring the importance of safety measures.Keywords: Electric, Scooter, Injury, Pediatric, Helmet

Introduction

Electric scooters (ESs) have become an increasingly popular mode of transportation. In 2020, it was estimated that approximately 150,000 ESs were in use in Israel [1]. During the years 2018–2019, a 350% increase in ES-related injuries was reported in the country [1]. Similarly, a study analyzing data from the National Electronic Injury Surveillance System (NEISS) in the United States reported a more than 200% increase in ES-related injuries and hospital admissions between 2014 and 2018 [2].

ESs are predominantly used by children under the age of 15 years [3, 4]. Data from the United States NEISS found that the majority of ES injuries between 2013 and 2017 occurred in pediatric patients [5].

The most common injuries associated with ES use involve the limbs, head and neck, thorax, and abdomen. Limb fractures most frequently affect the radius, ulna, tibia, femur, and fibula [6–9]. A significant proportion of ES-related injuries also involve craniofacial fractures or soft tissue damage [10]. Traumatic brain injuries (TBIs), a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in children, are commonly reported in scooter-related incidents. Symptoms of TBI range from mild concussions to severe skull fractures and may result in long-term neurological deficits.

A retrospective study reported that children are more frequently injured while riding ESs compared to bicycles and are more likely to require major surgical interventions [11]. Moreover, ES-related injuries were significantly associated with a higher incidence of long bone fractures and paralysis compared to bicycle-related injuries, highlighting the potential severity of trauma associated with ES use in the pediatric population [11].

There are limited published studies describing pediatric ES-related injuries [12–14]. In this study, we present our institutional experience with children hospitalized due to ES injuries, focusing on the types and incidence of injuries, as well as potential preventive measures.

Methods

A retrospective review of medical records was conducted for all pediatric patients (aged ≤ 18 years) admitted to the pediatric division at Shamir (Assaf Harofeh) Medical Center due to ES-related injuries between January 2018 and December 2024. Inclusion criteria were based on the following ICD-9-CM codes: motor vehicle traffic accident involving other motor vehicles (E813.8), fall from scooter (E885.0), and fall from motorized mobility scooter (E884.3). Coding was independently verified by both a physician and a medical records clerk to ensure accuracy.

The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee at Shamir.

Medical Center (Assaf Harofeh) (Ref: 0033–25-ASF). Given the retrospective nature of the study a waiver of informed consent was received from The Ethics Committee. The medical center is an 850-bed teaching hospital serving an urban and rural population of approximately one million people. The Pediatric Division includes a 100-bed facility comprising general pediatric units, pediatric subspecialty services, general and orthopedic pediatric surgery units, a daycare clinic, and a general pediatric intensive care unit (PICU).

Patients were identified through the hospital’s computerized database. The study included individuals who sustained injuries while riding an ES, as well as bystanders injured after being struck by an ES. Injuries sustained during occupational use of ESs were excluded from the dataset.

Data analysis included patient demographics, type of injury, use of protective equipment, clinical presentation, inpatient management, and follow-up information.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS), version 19.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the study population. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables were compared using the Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney test, depending on data distribution. A P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Logistic regression analysis was performed to assess associations between head trauma and the variables age, gender, and helmet use.

Results

Between January 2018 and December 2024, a total of 103 children were admitted to the pediatric division due to ES-related injuries. These cases accounted for approximately 3% of all trauma-related pediatric admissions to our medical center during the study period. Helmet use was reported in 55 (62.5%) cases. An increase in both patient age and the number of admissions was observed during the first two years of the study; however, no significant trend was noted in subsequent years (Fig. 1). The mean age of the study population was 11.3 ± 3.7 years, and 84 (81.5%) were male. Four main mechanisms of injury were identified: falls 86 cases (83.5%), involvement with vehicles on the road 7 cases (6.8%), collisions with objects 6 cases (5.8%), and falls onto the scooter handlebar 3 cases (2.9%). The youngest patient (2 years old) was the only bystander injured after being struck by an ES. Among the seven patients hit by a vehicle, four (57%) required admission to the pediatric intensive care unit (PICU). Of the three patients who fell onto the scooter handlebar, three (75%) sustained abdominal injuries.

Fourteen orthopedic patients (13.6%) sustained two or more injuries, while the remainder had isolated injuries. Limb fractures were identified in 69 patients (67%), with upper limb fractures accounting for 35 cases (50.7%), followed by lower limb fractures in 24 patients (34.7%). Clavicle fractures occurred in 4 patients (5.7%), and pelvis and patellar fractures were observed in 2 patients each (2.9%). Head trauma was diagnosed in 15 patients (14.6%); among them, 7 (46%) had intracranial hemorrhage, and 4 (26%) sustained skull fractures. Helmet use data were available for 88 (85.4%) patients. Only one of the 15 (6.7%) patients with head trauma reported wearing a helmet, compared to 33 of 55 (60%) patients without head trauma (P < 0.001).

Abdominal trauma occurred in 9 patients (8.7%), with spleen injury in 4 (44%), retroperitoneal hemorrhage in 2 (22%), and kidney injury in 1 (11%). Only one patient with abdominal trauma required a blood transfusion, and none required abdominal surgery. Other injuries included facial trauma in 6 patients (5.8%), otolaryngologic injuries in 4 (3.9%), eye injury in 1 (1%), gynecologic injury in 1 (1%), and chest injury in 2 (1.9%); patients with chest injuries required chest tube insertion. No significant correlations were found between age, gender, type of injury, and length of hospitalization.

The mean duration of PICU hospitalization was 3.8 days (range 1–18), which was significantly longer than the mean length of stay in the general pediatric wards (2.3 days, P < 0.001). All female patients sustained injuries due to falls, and none required PICU admission. Logistic regression analysis of gender, age, helmet use, and head trauma showed a significant association only with helmet use OR 23.8 (95% CI 3-200), while gender and agewere not significant.

Patients were followed for an average of 7.5 months (range 6–9 months). There were no mortalities. Sequelae were observed in 19 patients (18.5%), including joint restriction in 10 patients, neurological sequelae in 6, fingertip amputation in 1, tooth loss in 1, and secondary infection in 1. Patients with neurological sequelae and joint restriction were referred for physiotherapy and rehabilitation.

Discussion

Electric scooter (ES) injuries are common among children, accounting for approximately 3% of all trauma-related pediatric hospitalizations. The majority of affected patients were under 15 years of age, with a predominance of males and orthopedic injuries. Upper and lower limb fractures represented approximately half and one-third of orthopedic injuries, respectively. Not wearing a helmet was significantly associated with head trauma, with nearly half of those cases involving intracranial hemorrhage. Approximately 12% of patients required admission to the pediatric intensive care unit, with a longer hospital stay compared to others. Half of the patients underwent surgical procedures, primarily orthopedic interventions. During the follow-up period, one-fifth of patients experienced sequelae. No clear trend in patient age or injury incidence was observed throughout the study period.

In recent years, ES-related injuries have increased exponentially [2, 5, 15–17]. Most reports in the medical literature focus on adult cases, where the predominant injuries are facial and extremity fractures [5, 15, 16, 18–21]. One pediatric study evaluating the epidemiology of admitted ES injuries compared to adults found that children had a higher rate of fractures but fewer facial injuries, despite a similar incidence of head trauma [13]. This study also identified helmet non-use as a key risk factor for pediatric head injuries, mirroring findings in adults. Our results align with these observations, with fractures accounting for over two-thirds of injuries and a significant association between head trauma and lack of helmet use. Additionally, our study documented a relatively low rate of facial injuries among children (5.8%).

In contrast to other studies, the present study did not observe an increasing trend in hospital admissions due to ES-related injuries over the study period. We hypothesize that recent traffic regulations introduced and updated in 2021 and 2024, respectively, may have contributed to this stabilization. These laws require ES riders to be at least 16 years old and mandate the use of protective equipment, including helmets [22].

The rate of polytrauma in our pediatric cohort (13.6%) was higher than previously reported in adults, where approximately 10% experienced multiple injuries related to ES use [19]. This difference may be attributed to children’s relative inexperience with motorized vehicles and reduced caution compared to adults. Similar findings have been reported in other pediatric study [13].

Despite age restrictions on ES riding in Israel, the mean age of participants in this study was under 16 years, consistent with national data [1] and other reports in the literature [3–5].

No female participants required PICU admission, which may reflect the smaller number of girls in the cohort and differences in injury mechanisms. All female patients sustained injuries from falls, suggesting a pattern of relatively low-energy trauma.

Regarding injury mechanisms, falls were predominantly associated with orthopedic injuries, while injuries involving vehicles were more likely to result in PICU admission, indicating greater severity. Falls onto the scooter handlebars mainly caused abdominal injuries. Similar to bicycle handlebar injuries, impacts from scooter handlebars can result in significant abdominal trauma even at low speeds due to direct force to the abdomen [23].

In adults, approximately 10–15% of patients with ES-related injuries require intensive care admission [24–26]. These studies reported helmet use rates of around 20% among injured patients. In the current pediatric study, 11.6% of participants required admission to the PICU. Despite a higher rate of polytrauma among children, this PICU admission rate is comparable to that of adults, which may be related to the higher rate of helmet use (62.5%) observed in our cohort. Previous studies of pediatric ES injuries have reported variable helmet usage rates, ranging from 0% [6] to 46% [27]. Although helmet use does not completely prevent head injuries, the strong association between lack of helmet use and head trauma in our study underscores the importance of this protective measure. This concern is heightened by the potential severity of pediatric head injuries and their long-term consequences [28].

Patients in this study were followed for a relatively short period; sequelae were observed in approximately 20% of cases, with no mortalities reported. Similar findings were reported in an adult study involving 104 patients, where 21% experienced complications related to prolonged hospitalization or surgical interventions [24]. Another study of 1,466 pediatric patients presenting to emergency departments with ES injuries reported that 216 (14.7%) were hospitalized; among them, 3 (1.4%) died, and 9 (4.1%) required rehabilitation care [29].

To illustrate the potential for residual disability, we describe two cases from our cohort. One unhelmeted teenager patient sustained a severe head injury following a collision with a car, resulting in intracranial hemorrhage and severe brain edema. The patient underwent craniectomy (Fig. 2), required mechanical ventilation for approximately one week, and developed seizures necessitating anticonvulsant therapy. He was subsequently transferred to a rehabilitation facility for two months. At discharge, he had regained full motor function; however, six months later, during skull reconstruction surgery, he continued anticonvulsant treatment and exhibited attention deficit disorder. Another teenager patient sustained a supracondylar distal humerus fracture from a fall (Fig. 3), necessitating surgical fixation. Despite physiotherapy, limited elbow extension and flexion persisted at six months post-injury.

Limitations

This study has several limitations, including its retrospective design, relatively small sample size, incomplete data in some cases, and a relatively short follow-up period. Additionally, helmet use was self-reported by participants or their parents, which may have introduced reporting bias. Despite these limitations, the study contributes valuable insights into the characteristics and sequelae of ES injuries in children. Moreover, the findings highlight the ongoing need for targeted preventive measures (Fig. 3).

Conclusions

ES injuries, particularly orthopedic injuries, are frequently observed among children hospitalized following trauma. These injuries are associated with substantial morbidity. Helmet use was found to be an effective protective measure against head injury.
