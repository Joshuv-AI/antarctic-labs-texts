Effects of high-dose selenium-enriched Saccharomyces cerevisiae on growth performance, antioxidant status, tissue fat content and selenium concentration, and selenoenzyme mRNA expression in chicks
Authors: Shansong Gao, Huiling Qiu, Fu Chen, Guoming Yang, Lele Hou, Jihong Dong, Wenxuan Dong
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSelenium-enriched Saccharomyces cerevisiae (SSC) as organic selenium (Se) has been shown to have better advantages and is approved for use in animal feed rather than inorganic Se, however, there is little available data on the toxic effects of SSC on poultry. The present study was conducted to investigate the effects of high-dose SSC on growth performance, antioxidant status, tissue fat content and Se concentration, and selenoenzyme mRNA expression in chicks. A total of 500, 1-day-old SPF chicks were randomly divided into 5 groups with 10 replicates of 10 chicks each. Group 1 served as a control and was fed a basal diet supplemented with 0.15 mg/kg Se from sodium selenite (SS), group 2 was fed the basic diet supplemented with 1.5 mg/kg Se from SS, while groups 3, 4, and 5 were fed the basal diet supplemented with 1.5, 5 and 10 mg/kg Se from SSC, respectively. The results showed that SS and SSC supplementation significantly affected the average daily feed intake (ADFI), feed/gain ratio (FCR), glutathione peroxidase (GPx) and superoxide dismutase (SOD) activities, total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC), malondialdehyde (MDA) content, tissue fat content and Se concentration, and GPx1 and GPx4 mRNA levels compared with the control group (P < 0.05). Compared with group 2, group 3 exhibited higher GPx and SOD activities, tissue Se concentration, and lower MDA content on d 30, and higher Se concentration, GPx1 mRNA levels in liver and breast muscle and GPx4 mRNA levels in liver and thigh muscle, and lower MDA content on d 60 (P < 0.05). The results of correlation analysis showed that high-dose SSC supplementation was positively correlated with AFDI, FCR, MDA content, and tissue Se concentration, and negatively correlated with GPx and SOD activities, T-AOC, GPx1 and GPx4 mRNA levels in tissues. In conclusion, up to 1.5 mg/kg Se from SSC in diet may be a safe concentration for chicks that exhibited better biological effects than SS, the toxic effects of high-dose SSC supplementation mainly exhibited growth decrease, peroxidation and lipid metabolism disturbance, and became stronger with the increase of dietary Se levels.Key words: selenium-enriched Saccharomyces cerevisiae, chick, growth performance, antioxidant function, fat content

INTRODUCTION

Selenium (Se), an essential trace element in humans and animals discovered in 1817, is an important part of more than 30 Se proteins and plays an antioxidant role in the body mainly through these enzymes to inhibit lipid peroxidation and remove excessive free radicals, thus protecting the membrane structure and function of organisms (Chen et al., 2020; Qiu et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2023). In China, Se deficiency is common due to the low Se level in the soil (Tan et al., 2002), which can cause Se deficiency in humans and animals through the food chain, and lead to a variety of diseases, such as exudative diathesis and pancreatic necrosis in chickens, heat necrosis and mulberry heart disease in pigs, and muscular dystrophy and white muscle disease in cows and sheep (Dinh et al., 2018; Li et al., 2014; Shi et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2020). Nowadays, inorganic Se supplementation in feed (0.1–0.2 mg of Se per kg of diet) has become the most important means to prevent and control Se deficiency in animals. Previous studies have found that Se is highly toxic, the safe intake range is narrow, and there is only a 10 to 20 times difference between dietary Se requirement and potentially toxic Se concentration (Lu et al., 2019; Raines and Sunde, 2011; Samo et al., 2020; Taylor et al., 2019). The bioavailability and pharmacological and toxicological effects of Se in animals are related to its chemical form (Arnaut et al., 2021; Han et al., 2017). In previous reviews of Se biokinetics, concerning potency variations, many studies have discovered that organic Se forms, exemplified by selenomethionine and Se-enriched yeast, exhibit superior bioavailability, reduced environmental pollution, lesser toxicity, a broader safety margin between optimal and toxic concentrations, and enhanced biological activity over inorganic forms, like selenite and sodium selenite (SS), with elemental Se in nanoparticle form positioned least bioavailable (Chen et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2014; Han et al., 2017; Hou et al., 2020; Jiang et al., 2009; Qiu et al., 2023; Silva et al., 2019). Therefore, the development of organic Se products with low toxicity, environmental protection and high efficiency has become a research hotspot.

Se-enriched yeast (SY), including Se-enriched Saccharomyces cerevisiae (SSC), was approved source of organic Se to be used as a feed additive for farm animals, with its higher absorption rate and safety, is a premium organic source of Se, including improvements in growth performance, antioxidant function, and meat quality by activating the glutathione and thioredoxin systems, and was approved by the Food and Drug Administration for use in diets (Chen et al., 2017; Hou et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2023). SSC enhances antioxidant capacity in broiler breast muscles and meat quality in goats' latissimus dorsi. By reducing oxidative damage, it extends meat shelf life and defends against inflammation from exogenous damage. It alleviates spleen inflammation, meat quality degradation, and apoptosis via regulating Nrf2 and inhibiting NF-κB pathways, boosting immunoglobulins and reducing proinflammatory cytokines (Zhang et al., 2024). Se exhibits dual roles as pro-oxidant and antioxidant, contingent on duration, dose, and oxidation state. Physiologically, it reduces ROS, boosts antioxidants, enhancing meat quality. Selenoproteins, crucial components of antioxidant enzymes like GPX, play a pivotal role in combating oxidative stress by scavenging free radicals and peroxides, while also modulating lipid metabolism, preventing lipid peroxidation, and reducing cardiovascular risks. Their antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties contribute to maintaining cellular health and immune function, emphasizing the significance of selenium intake for overall wellness (Gan et al., 2021; Malyar et al., 2021). However, excess Se generates ROS, harms antioxidants, reduces cell viability, causing cytotoxicity and anticarcinogenic effects (Gu and Gao, 2022). These dual actions challenge Se use in livestock. Most previous studies focused on dietary Se deficiency to an adequate level, and only a few feeding studies have examined the effects of super nutritional (≤1.0 mg Se/kg) and high Se intake (>1.0 mg Se/kg) on Se biomarkers (Cantor and Scott, 1975; Qiu et al., 2023; Taylor et al., 2019), and no significant changes were found in biomarkers such as enzymes when dietary Se level reached 1.0 mg Se/kg (Taylor and Sunde, 2016). When the Se level in feed and soil exceeds 3.0 mg/kg, a chronic toxic syndrome can be caused in animals. However, there are few reports on the biological activity of high doses of Se, and it is necessary to study the effects of high dietary Se levels on poultry to better characterize the effects of higher dietary Se levels. Therefore, the present study was conducted to evaluate the effects of high-dose SSC on growth performance, antioxidant function, fat content, Se concentration, and selenoenzyme expression in chicks to better characterize the susceptibility of chicks to high-dose organic Se.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Preparation of SSC

SSC was provided by the Institute of Animal Nutritional Metabolic and Poisoning Diseases of Qingdao Agricultural University. Its effective viable bacteria number was ≥1×1010 /g, and total Se content was ≥2.0 mg/kg, in which organic Se was not less than 90% (Chen et al., 2017; Hou et al., 2020). It was stored in a sterile container at 4°C before use.

Animals and Treatment

This project was approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Qingdao Agricultural University in accordance with the Guidelines for Animal Welfare Ethical Review of Laboratory Animals (GB/T35892-2018, National Standard of the People's Republic of China), and was conducted in accordance with the principles and specific guidelines of the Guidelines for Conservation and Use of Agricultural Animal Scientific Research Teaching (2020) (National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine. 2020. Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Washington, DC: The National Academies Press. https://doi.org/10.17226/5140.).

A total of 500 seven-day-old healthy Specific Pathogen Free (SPF) Chicks male White Leghorn chicks were purchased from the Poultry Research Institute of Shandong Academy of Agricultural Sciences and were randomly divided into 5 groups with 10 replicates of 10 chicks each. Group 1 served as the control group fed a basal diet supplemented with 0.15 mg/kg Se from SS (analytical grade, 97.0% pure). Group 2 was supplemented with 1.5 mg/kg Se from SS in the basal diet. Groups 3, 4, and 5 were supplemented with 1.5, 5, and 10 mg/kg Se from SSC in the basal diet, respectively. The experiment lasted for 60 d. Basal diets are formulated to meet the nutritional requirements of chicks (National Research Council, 1994), and their formulations and main components are listed in Table 1. Dietary Se addition was based on the calculated levels for each treatment. Water and feed were provided ad libitum during the experiment period of 60 d, all chicks were housed in individual pens within a closed and ventilated building, ensuring a pathogen-free environment with controlled temperature, humidity, and continuous light. Adequate space, Regular cleaning, disinfection, and monitoring of environmental parameters maintained hygiene and optimal conditions for chick growth. The animal's treatment, housing, and husbandry conditions adhered strictly to the experimental guidelines set forth by the National Laboratory Animal Standardization Technical Committee of China (SAC/TC 281), while the experimental protocols were crafted in accordance with the regulations for the utilization of experimental animals and were subsequently given the nod of approval by the Ethical Committee for Use of Laboratory Animals at Qingdao Agricultural University (China).

Growth Performance

On d 30 and 60, body weight (BW) and feed consumption (FC) were recorded, and the average daily weight gain (ADG), the average daily feed intake (ADFI) and feed/gain ratio (FCR) were calculated.

Sample Collection and Preparation

On d 30 and 60, 3 chickens were randomly selected from each replicate, and a total of 30 chickens were selected from each group. After blood samples were collected from the heart, plasma was fully separated and stored in an EP tube at -20°C for assay of antioxidant indicators. After these chickens were euthanized by dimethyl ether, liver, breast and thigh muscles were removed, 2.0 g of tissues were stored in liquid nitrogen for assay of the selenoprotein mRNA levels, and the remaining tissues were stored at -20°C for assay of fat content and Se concentration.

Laboratory Assay

Plasma antioxidant indicators, including glutathione peroxidase (GPx) activity, superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity, total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC), and malondialdehyde (MDA) content, were determined according to the instruction of the assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China).

The tissue fat content was determined by Soxhlet extraction. After the sample was fully dried and cooled, 2.0 g tissue was weighed, crushed in a mortar, wrapped with filter paper, put into a Soxhlet extraction tube, and extracted with anhydrous ether. The samples were roasted at 103-105°C, cooled in the dryer to room temperature and weighed. Blank determination should be packed in filter paper without a sample. Crude fat content (%) = fat weight ÷ sample weight × 100%.

The tissue Se concentration was determined by hydride generation-atomic fluorescence spectrometry (Hou et al., 2020). After 12 h of digestion with a mixture of perchloric acid and nitric acid (V: V = 1:1), the sample was digested on an electric heating plate. Se concentration in the samples was determined by an atomic fluorescence spectrometer (AFS-9330, Beijing Titan Instruments Co., Ltd., Beijing, China).

The mRNA levels of GPx1 and GPx4 in tissues were measured using a quantitative real-time reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) based on SYBR Green I (Chen et al., 2020; Hou et al., 2020). According to the sequences of β-actin, GPx1 and GPx4 in GenBank, Premier 5.0 was applied to design 3 pairs of primers, as shown in Table 2. Total RNA was extracted with TRizol reagent (Sangon Biotech Co., LTD.), and the AMV First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Sangon Biotech Co., LTD.) was used for reverse transcription to synthesize the cDNA template. RT-qPCR was performed on an ABI StepOnePlus Detection System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City CA). A relative quantification analysis of GPx1 and GPx4 mRNA levels was performed by the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Statistical Analysis

IBM SPSS 22.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk) was used for statistical analysis (Dong et al., 2024). All data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and analyzed using 1-way ANOVA to compare means and the multivariate analysis of the GLM procedure. The least significant difference (LSD) and Dunnett's T3 tests were used to determine differences between means. The replicate/cage was defined as the experimental unit for statistical analysis. P < 0.05 was considered a significant difference in all test results. The OmicStudio tool (https://www.omicstudio.cn) was used to draw cluster correlation heatmaps.

DISCUSSION

Effects of SSC on the Growth Performance of Chickens

Growth performance is an important index to evaluate the toxicity of various nutritional factors and toxic substances in feed to animals. Se-enriched yeast (SY), which contains SeMet and SeCys as the first and second most abundant Se species, accounting for approximately 60 to 85% and 2 to 4% of total Se respectively, is the most popular matrix for Se supplementation, exhibiting relative bioavailability values of 144% and 272% compared to Na2SeO3, based on Se and total SeMet content, respectively (Zhang et al., 2020b). In contrast to the few studies with no effect on production performance (Mariezcurrena-Berasain et al., 2022; Taylor et al., 2019), most of the previous studies showed that dietary Se supplementation at nutrient levels can improve the production performance of animals (Dong et al., 2023; Hou et al., 2022; Pan et al., 2011; Qiu et al., 2023). However, there are few studies on the effect of high-dose organic Se on production performance (Raines and Sunde, 2011; Taylor et al., 2019). Taylor and Sunde (2016) reported that a Se supply of 0.01-0.1 μg/g did not affect the BW of turkeys at 20 d. Taylor et al. (2019) found that dietary high Se supplementation had no significant effect on the growth of turkeys, but the final BW of turkeys supplemented with 2 and 5 μg/g Se was 30% and 26% lower than those of Se-adequate turkeys (0.4 μg Se/g), respectively. A study of weaned rats by Raines and Sunde (2011) showed that feeding 5 μg Se/g SS for 4 wk resulted in a 24% reduction in the final BW of rodents. Similarly, the results of this study showed that a high dose of Se could reduce the growth performance of chicks, which was manifested in increasing ADFI and FCR of chicks but did not affect BW and ADG. Correlation analysis also confirmed that ADFI and FCR exhibited a negative correlation with the Se level of SSC except for BW and ADG. These results suggested that the toxic effect of high-dose SSC on the growth performance of chicks was more manifested as an increase in feed intake than a smaller effect on BW, which ultimately led to an increase in feed coefficient. In addition, we found that the toxic effects of high doses of Se on growth performance were related to the species and age of the animal and the dose of Se. The tolerance of poultry to high Se levels is generally better than that of mammals, and previous studies have shown that 2 mg/kg (Chen et al., 2024) and 3 mg/kg (Zhang et al., 2024) Se supplements have no significant toxic effects on chickens. We found that growth performance of chicks showed good tolerance to less than 1.5 mg/kg Se and significant toxic effects to more than 5 mg/kg Se.

Effects of SSC on Fat Content in Chicken Tissues

The effect of dietary Se sources and levels on muscle fat content is still controversial. Previous studies were mostly limited to the effects of low doses of Se (≤1.0 mg/kg) on tissue fat content, while the effects of high-dose Se on tissue fat content were rarely reported. Previous studies showed that dietary Se sources and levels had significant effects on lipid metabolism in animals, dietary Se deficiency can lead to lipid metabolism disorders, and nutritional level of Se supplementation could help to improve lipid metabolism disorders in the liver and muscle of animals under stress conditions (Jing et al., 2023a; Jing et al., 2023b; Wang et al., 2023; Yu et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2022). Wang et al. (2010) reported that adding 0.5% and 1% selenomethionine (Se-Met) compound additives decreased back fat thickness of fattening pigs. Al-Quwaie (2023) reported that dietary bacterial Se nanoparticles significantly decreased the abdominal fat content by 24% and serum total lipids in broiler chickens compared to the control. Differently, Zhang et al. (2020a) reported that using 0.3 mg/kg of Se from organic Se or SS + Se-Met in fattening pig diets could increase the fat content of longissimus thoracis muscle, diet supplementation with SY or SS + Se-Met could increase back fat thickness and skin thickness, and SY could increase belly fat rat. These different results are most likely due to differences in the source of Se. However, previous studies of the same Se sources had produced completely different results, SY supplementation increased the fat content in the muscle of broiler ducks or in the thigh muscle of lambs (Mariezcurrena-Berasain et al., 2022), but it decreased fat content in chicken breast (Markovic et al., 2018). In the present study, 5 and 10 mg/kg Se from SSC reduced the fat content of muscle tissues to a certain extent, while 1.5 mg/kg Se concentration of SS and SSC supplementation did not affect the fat content of muscle tissues. We considered that Se level is an important factor affecting muscle fat content, and high-dose Se can lead to increased muscle fat content. However, we also noted that SSC supplementation resulted in a decrease in liver fat content on d 60 and did not affect liver fat content on d 30, compared with SS which did not affect liver fat content. These results suggested that long-term application of high-dose SSC can reduce fat content in liver tissue, and this effect may be mainly due to the regulatory effect of yeast, which may affect the fat accumulation by changing lipid metabolism, or Se may indirectly affect thyroid hormone, which plays an important role in lipid metabolism, leading to the decrease of fat accumulation (Ahmad et al., 2012). Correlation analysis also confirmed that the correlation between high-dose SSC levels and tissue fat content changed significantly at 30 ∼ 60 d. Leg muscle fat content changed from a positive correlation to a somewhat negative correlation with SSC dose growth. The negative correlation between liver fat content and SSC dose increases aggravated. We believe that short-term toxicity of high-dose SSC can significantly affect both muscle fat deposition and liver fat anabolic transport, resulting in liver fat deposition increasing with the increase of Se level. However, long-term (60 d) toxicity of SSC at high doses changed, with little change in the effect on muscle fat deposition, but a significant change in the effect on liver fat deposition, indicating that the liver, as a detoxification organ, plays a certain role in tolerating and compensating the fat deposition caused by high-dose Se toxicity, even if Se level increases, liver fat deposition does not increase. However, the mechanism by which dietary supplementation of high-dose Se leads to the decrease of fat content in muscle and liver remains to be further explored.

Effects of SSC on Se Content in Tissues

Se concentration in the liver and muscle represents the level of Se deposition in the body and is a key indicator for evaluating Se-enriched meat products. Se is distributed in all tissues and cells of the body after absorption and transportation, and its distribution state is different with different tissues, time, Se source and Se intake levels. With the increase of dietary Se source (SP or SS, Se-met source, etc.), Se concentration in muscle and organ tissues also increased (Cai et al., 2012; Deng et al., 2022; Pan et al., 2007; Zhou and Wang, 2011). Arnaut et al. (2021) reported that Se concentration in carcasses and feathers, as well as the Se retention, were linearly increased as supplemental Se levels increased. Liu et al. (2023) reported that the dietary supplementation of SY at varying levels resulted in a significant increase in Se concentration in the breast muscle, leg muscle, liver, and kidney of broiler chickens. Similarly, the present study demonstrated that the dietary supplementation of high-dose SS or SSC resulted in a significant increase in Se concentrations in breast and thigh muscles, and liver tissues, and Se intake was linearly increased as supplemental SSC levels increased. Furthermore, Se concentration in breast and thigh muscles and liver by SSC source was higher than those in SS. These findings aligned with several previous reports on the subject (Zhou and Wang, 2011). Gul et al. (2021) reported that broiler chicks fed on SY showed higher Se deposition as compared to other sources of Se. Deng et al. (2022) reported that dietary supplements with organic Se raised Se deposition in the liver, pancreas, breast muscle, and thigh muscle compared to inorganic administration. Organic Se sources, such as SY, are likely to be absorbed by active transport, are not specifically bound to proteins in place of methionine, and are preferentially absorbed and utilized by the body over inorganic Se (Schrauzer, 2003). However, the effect of hypertrophic Se level on Se deposition is less studied. In this study, high-dose Se could increase the Se concentration of tissues, the effect of organic SSC was better than that of SS, and its bioavailability was higher. Organic Se sources (Se-Met and SY) may therefore be easier to utilize in tissues than SS (Suzuki et al., 2005). In addition, with the addition of a high level of organic Se source, the tissue Se concentration of chicks also increased significantly. When the toxic level (more than 10–100 times the normal intake) was consumed, the tissue Se concentration would steadily increase with the increase of high-dose SSC. Correlation analysis also confirmed that Se concentration in liver and muscle tissues was positively correlated with high-dose SSC. With the increase of Se dosage and the extension of application time, the tissue Se concentration will gradually increase until the tissue Se concentration reaches a certain level, excretion and absorption keep pace, and the accumulation of Se in tissues will not further increase. However, the Se level of 10 mg/kg was still not balanced after 60 d. Therefore, we suggest that the toxic effects of high-dose SSC may require a longer time and higher Se concentrations in Se deposition in broiler tissues.

Effects of SSC on the Antioxidant Capacity of Chicks

Se plays an important role in the body's antioxidant defence system, and its antioxidation is mainly realized through selenase or selenoprotein (Chen et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2014; Hou et al., 2020). Se source and level are the main factors affecting the antioxidant status of animals. In the present study, the most commonly used indexes to evaluate the antioxidation of nutrients are the expression levels of GPx and SOD activities, T-AOC, MDA content and tissue selenoprotein mRNA levels. There have been different reports on the effects of adding inorganic Se (SS) and organic Se (SY) on antioxidant indicators. In both low (0.15 mg/kg) and high (1.0 mg/kg) doses of Se, most results showed that organic Se sources were superior to inorganic Se sources, and only a few results confirmed that inorganic Se sources were superior to organic Se sources. However, the effect of higher levels of Se sources on antioxidant indicators is less reported. Lu et al. (2019) reported that up to 3 mg/kg organic Se from SY can be used to supplement the diets for laying hens without adverse effects following 84-d administration. Samo et al. (2020) reported that compared with low concentrate diet, the high concentrate diet elevated MDA content with a concurrent decrease in GPx and SOD activities, while SY supplementation attenuated these changes and improved antioxidant status in colonic epithelium. Zeng et al. (2012) reported that dietary Se produced dose-dependent increases in GPx1 mRNA or activity in the pancreas, liver, and erythrocytes of dams. The 3.0 mg Se/kg diet decreased Selh (P < 0.01), Sepp1 (P = 0.06), and Sepw1 (P < 0.01), but increased Sels (P < 0.05) mRNA levels in the liver of the offspring, compared with the 0.3 mg Se/kg diet. Zhao et al. (2016) reported that the high-dose Se diet resulted in a moderate (20-50%) increase in GPx activities in both the liver and muscle. The high-dose Se induced gene expression of GPx3 in both the liver and muscle and elevated the GPx3 protein in the liver. Gene expressions of 8 selenoproteins in 6 tissues were decreased by both dietary Se deficiency and excess (3 mg Se/kg). Liu et al. (2012) reported that compared with those fed 0.3 mg Se/kg, pigs fed 3.0 mg Se/kg became hyperinsulinemic (P < 0.05) and had lower (P < 0.05) tissue levels of serine/threonine protein kinase. In the present study, both SS and SSC at 1.5 mg/kg level improved the antioxidant status of chicks, and increased the activities of GPx or SOD, T-AOC, GPx1 and GPx4 mRNA levels in tissues, but high-dose Se reduced the antioxidant status of chicks and did not show a better advantage. Previous studies had confirmed that GPx and SOD activities or T-AOC increased with the increase of dietary Se level, and reached a peak value when dietary Se level reaches a certain level, MDA content significantly decreased, selenoprotein level may be decreased when dietary Se level exceeds a certain level (1.0 mg/kg), and MDA content increased. Correlation analysis also confirmed that the level of high-dose SSC was significantly correlated with various antioxidant indicators, in particular, GPx and SOD activities or T-AOC were negatively correlated with the increase of high Se level. MDA content was positively correlated with the increase of high Se concentration, and selenoprotein expression at 10 mg/kg was significantly inhibited. These results indicated that the toxic effect of high-dose SSC on the antioxidant status of chicks was mainly lipid peroxidation toxicity.

CONCLUSION

The present results demonstrate that up to 1.5 mg/kg Se from SS and SSC can be used to supplement the diets for chicks without adverse effects following 60-d administration, while SSC supplementation exhibited better biological effects than SS supplementation. Differences in genetic background, growth rate, and digestive enzyme activity among various chick breeds, which could potentially influence their sensitivity and response to SSC, are accompanied by the toxic effects of high-dose SSC supplementation, notably growth retardation, peroxidation, and lipid metabolism disruption, these effects intensifying with increased dietary Se dosage. In addition, it is necessary to further study the effect of the effect of dietary high-dose SSC supplementation on lipid metabolism disorder and the production of Se-enriched meat products.
