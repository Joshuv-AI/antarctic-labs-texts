Minimum meal frequency and its associated factors among children aged 6–23 months in the cash crop district of Kercha, Southern Ethiopia: a community-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Jibril Jemal Ibrahim, Hiwot Hailu Amare, Mahlet Birhane Estifanos
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveDespite efforts to improve feeding practices globally, 48% of children do not meet the recommended minimum meal frequency. Thus, the aim of this study was to assess minimum meal frequency and its associated factors in children aged 6–23 months in cash crop districts of Kercha, Southern Ethiopia.DesignA community-based cross-sectional design was used to conduct this study from 29 February 2024 to 29 March 2024.SettingKercha district, Southern EthiopiaParticipantsA total of 543 children aged 6–23 months were selected using a multistage sampling technique.Primary outcomeThe primary outcome of this study was minimum meal frequency, defined as the percentage of children aged 6–23 months who consumed solid, semisolid or soft foods (including milk for non-breastfed children) at least the required number of times for their age in the previous day.Secondary outcomeThe secondary outcome of this study was factors associated with minimum meal frequency. Bivariable and multivariable multilevel mixed-effect logistic regression analyses were performed to find factors related to minimum meal frequency. Adjusted ORs (AORs) with 95% CIs and p values <0.05 were used to assess the strength and significance of the associations.ResultsA total of 539 (99.3%) children aged 6–23 months and their mothers participated in this study. The prevalence of minimum meal frequency was 62.3% (95% CI 58.1% to 66.4%) among children in the cash crop district of Kercha. The final model yielded an intracluster correlation coefficient of 7.3%, reflecting unexplained variations in the prevalence of minimum meal frequency among children at the kebele level. Factors associated with higher odds of meeting minimum meal frequency included being a first-born child (AOR 2.91, 95% CI 1.06 to 8.05), mothers (AOR 2.66, 95% CI 1.48 to 4.76) and fathers (AOR 3.43, 95% CI 1.98 to 5.95) with primary education, having mother attending postnatal visits (AOR 2.74, 95% CI 1.52 to 4.94), introducing additional food at 6 months (AOR 4.71, 95% CI 2.79 to 7.94), good maternal knowledge on child feeding (AOR 1.98, 95% CI 1.21 to 3.22) and living in the household with medium (AOR 2.98, 95% CI 1.59 to 5.53) and rich (AOR 2.39, 95% CI 1.37 to 4.18) wealth status.ConclusionsThe study revealed that only three-fifths of children met the recommended minimum meal frequency. Birth order, introducing additional foods at 6 months, parental education, maternal postnatal visits, good maternal knowledge on child feeding and wealth were linked to minimum meal frequency. To improve child nutrition, it is crucial to enhance maternal education, increase access to postnatal healthcare and educate parents about the importance of introducing additional foods at 6 months.Keywords: Child, EPIDEMIOLOGY, Ethiopia, Health, Community child health, Nutrition

Introduction

Infant and young child feeding practices are critical for ensuring optimal health and development during the crucial early years of life.1 The WHO identifies minimum meal frequency as a key indicator for evaluating these practices.1 Insufficient meal intake during the first 2 years can lead to irreversible consequences, including nutrient deficiencies that increase susceptibility to infections, developmental delays and even mortality.2 Furthermore, inadequate nutrition in early childhood is associated with poor educational outcomes, reduced productivity and diminished earning potential in later life, as well as a heightened risk of nutrition-related chronic diseases in adulthood.3

In low-income and middle-income countries, only 56% of children aged 6–23 months meet the minimum meal frequency.4 However, a recent systematic review revealed that this percentage is significantly lower in sub-Saharan Africa, where only 38.5% of children in this age group achieve the minimum meal frequency, and slightly higher in East Africa at 39.9%.5

In Ethiopia, various strategies have been implemented to improve infant and young child feeding practices with the goal of eradicating child undernutrition by 2030.6 However, community-level implementation remains inadequate. According to the 2019 mini Ethiopian Demographic and Health Survey (DHS), approximately half of children aged 6–23 months did not meet the minimum meal frequency requirements.7 This highlights a critical gap in efforts to combat child malnutrition, particularly in areas like Kercha district, which has been identified as a focal point due to its high child malnutrition rate of 25%.8

Despite existing research on minimum meal frequency among infants and young children in Ethiopia, studies have primarily focused on agrarian societies where food crops are predominant,8 9 pastoral communities10 11 and urban populations.12 13 Kercha district, characterised as a cash crop area, presents unique challenges. Unlike other agrarian or pastoral societies, the livelihoods in Kercha depend on seasonal production that lasts only a few months.14 Most agricultural products are sold shortly after harvest, leading to significant postharvest food loss due to a poor saving culture. Consequently, food insecurity is prevalent after the harvest season. Additionally, the region does not rely on the full cultivation of staple food crops alongside animal production as seen in other agrarian societies, further exacerbating accessibility issues. Moreover, livestock production in Kercha is relatively underdeveloped compared with pastoralist societies, where livestock is considered a valuable asset. Given the scarcity of studies focusing on cash crop societies in Ethiopia, there is a notable lack of information regarding the prevalence of minimum meal frequency and its determinants among children aged 6–23 months in this district. This research seeks to fill the existing knowledge gap and inform targeted interventions to improve child nutrition in this vulnerable population. Therefore, this study was aimed to assess minimum meal frequency and its associated factors in children aged 6–23 months in the cash crop district of Kercha, Southern Ethiopia.

Methods

Study setting and period

This study was conducted from 29 February 2024 to 29 March 2024 in the Kercha district of the West Guji zone, Southern Ethiopia, located 475 km from Addis Ababa and 38 km northeast of Bule Hora. The district consists of 28 kebeles and has a population of 265 034, primarily reliant on cash crops like coffee. There are approximately 15 133 children under the age of 2 in the district.

Study design and population

This research used a community-based cross-sectional study design. The source population comprised all children aged 6–23 months residing in the Kercha district. The study population included randomly selected children within this age range from specific kebeles in the district.

Eligibility criteria

Children aged 6–23 months with mothers or caregivers who had lived in the study area for at least 6 months included in the study. Children whose mothers or caregivers were seriously ill and unable to participate in interviews were excluded from the study. Although we intended to exclude children with serious illnesses, no such cases were identified during the data collection period.

Sample size determination and sampling technique

The required sample size for the first objective was determined using the single population proportion formula, based on the following assumptions: a meal frequency proportion of 68.9% in Wolaita Sodo town, South Ethiopia (p=0.689),12 a 95% CI, a margin of error of 5%, a design effect of 1.5 and a non-response rate of 10%. This calculation yielded a total sample size of 543. For the second objective, we calculated the sample size by considering various factors associated with minimum meal frequency, including postnatal care follow-up and the child’s current breastfeeding status. Using Epi Info version 7.2, the sample size calculated for postnatal care follow-up—considering a proportion of 81.7% in the exposed group and 64.5% in the unexposed group, with 80% power, a 95% CI, a design effect of 1.5 and a 10% non-response rate—was found to be 380. Additionally, for the current breastfeeding status of the child, with proportions of 59.6% in the exposed group and 9.6% in the unexposed group, along with 80% power, a 95% CI, a design effect of 1.5 and a 10% non-response rate, the sample size was also found to be 380. Therefore, we used a larger sample size calculated for the first objective for this study.

Additionally, we employed a multistage sampling technique to select the study participants. Initially, 9 kebeles were randomly chosen from a total of 28 kebeles in the Kercha district using a simple random sampling method. Following this, a census was conducted within the selected kebeles to identify children aged 6–23 months prior to data collection. We then applied population proportion sampling to determine the number of samples required from each kebele. Finally, children were selected through simple random sampling from each kebele, based on the sampling frame established during the census registration (figure 1).

Study variable

Dependent variable

Minimum meal frequency.

Independent variables

This study examines a range of sociodemographic and economic characteristics, including maternal age, marital status, maternal and paternal education levels and their occupation, maternal involvement in household decision-making, family size and wealth status. In this study, we also examined healthcare-related factors for mothers, including antenatal care visits, the place of delivery, postnatal care visits for the child and birth intervals. Furthermore, we investigated child-related characteristics such as the child’s age, sex, birth order, the timing of introducing complementary foods, current breastfeeding status and vaccination status. Finally, we assessed maternal knowledge regarding infant and young child feeding.

Operational definition

Minimum meal frequency: This metric measures the percentage of children aged 6–23 months who consumed solid, semisolid or soft foods (including milk for non-breastfed children) at least the minimum required number of times in the previous day.15 The minimum frequency is set at two times for breastfed infants aged 6–8 months, three times for breastfed infants aged 9–23 months and four times for non-breastfed children aged 6–23 months. The results are coded as ‘0’ for those who did not meet the age-specific minimum requirement and ‘1' for those who did.

Maternal knowledge of infant and young child feeding practices was assessed using a set of 10 standardised questions.16 17 We used a validated tool from a prior study.16 Responses were coded as ‘1’ for correct answers (yes) and ‘0’ for incorrect answers (no), resulting in a total score ranging from 0 to 10 points. Mothers who scored above the mean (6.6±1.9 SD) were categorised as having good knowledge, while those who scored below the mean were classified as having poor knowledge regarding feeding practices.16 17

Data collection tools and procedure

Data were gathered through face-to-face interviews using pretested structured questionnaires administered to a selected group of mothers with children aged 6–23 months. The data collection instrument consisted of five sections: sociodemographic and economic characteristics, maternal healthcare-related characteristics, child-related characteristics, knowledge characteristics and a 24-hour recall of infant and young child feeding practices. The meal frequency questionnaire was adapted from the WHO 2021 indicators for assessing infant and young child feeding practices.1 The other sections of the questionnaire were modified from the Mini Ethiopian DHS 2019 and relevant literature to better suit the local context.7 10 13 18 19 A team of eight nurses served as data collectors, supervised by two public health officers who oversaw the data collection process.

Data quality control

To ensure data quality, the questionnaires were initially prepared in English, then translated into the local language (Afan Oromo), and subsequently retranslated back into English by language experts to ensure consistency. Data collectors and supervisors underwent a 3-day training programme focused on the study’s objectives, the content of the questionnaires and the proper techniques for conducting structured interviews prior to the data collection phase. To reduce interviewer bias, data collectors were kept unaware of the study hypothesis. A pretest was carried out involving 5% of the sample, consisting of participants similar to those in the study population. The pretest took place in kebeles not included in the main study, 1 week prior to the actual data collection. Based on the results of this pretest, necessary adjustments were made to the questionnaires. Throughout the data collection period, supervisors conducted daily checks to verify the accuracy and completeness of the collected data.

Data processing and analysis

Data entry was performed using EpiData V.4.5, followed by transfer to SPSS V.26, and STATA V.17 software for analysis. The dataset was checked and cleaned prior to analysis. The household wealth index was calculated using principal component analysis based on 16 household asset variables recoded as binary (0=absent, 1=present). Households were classified into poor, medium and rich wealth status categories.

Descriptive statistics summarised the sociodemographic and economic characteristics of the population. Given our use of multistage sampling, which involved selecting kebeles, households and then individual children, we accounted for clustering effects by employing multilevel analysis. To identify factors associated with meeting minimum meal frequency, we conducted both bivariable and multivariable multilevel mixed-effect logistic regression analyses. For all factors, we initially performed a simple bivariable logistic regression that did not account for random effects, followed by a multilevel logistic regression model that incorporated the random effects at the kebele level.

We developed four consecutive models to analyse our data. In model I, we included only the outcome variable without any independent variables to determine the appropriateness of a random-effects model. We assessed multicollinearity and included only those variables with a variance inflation factor of less than 10. In model II, we incorporated individual child factors, such as sex, age, birth order, timing of introducing complementary foods, current breastfeeding status and vaccination status.

Model III expanded on this by including individual child variables from model II that had p values less than 0.25, as well as parent factors with p values under 0.25 identified through a bivariable multilevel mixed-effect logistic regression. These included child sex, age, birth order, timing of complementary food introduction, breastfeeding status, vaccination status and parental individual factors: maternal age, marital status, education levels of both parents, parental occupation, antenatal care follow-up, place of delivery, postnatal follow-up, birth interval and maternal knowledge regarding infant and young child feeding.

In model IV, we further included household factors—such as maternal involvement in household decision-making, family size and wealth status—while retaining the child and parent factors from model III with p values less than 0.25. Notably, we maintained the child’s sex across all models and included maternal age in model III despite its p value exceeding 0.25 to account for potential confounding.

We reported both crude ORs (COR) and adjusted ORs (AOR) with 95% CIs. Independent variables with p values less than 0.05 in the final model (model IV) were considered statistically significant associations with meeting minimum meal frequency. Model fit was assessed using −2 log likelihood (deviance), Akaike information criterion (AIC) and intracluster correlation coefficients (ICCs), with lower values of deviance and AIC indicating a better fit.

Patient and public involvement

In this study, we did not include patients or the public; instead, we focused exclusively on analysing data collected through surveys. Nevertheless, our aim is to contribute to the improvement of healthcare policies and enhance child health outcomes in the future.

Result

Sociodemographic and economic characteristics

Out of 543 children sampled, 539 aged between 6 and 23 months participated in the study, resulting in a response rate of 99.3%. Four participants declined to take part. The average age (±SD) of the mothers was 27.3±5.4 years. Among the mothers, 487 (90.4%) were married, and 328 (61%) were housewives. Notably, two-fifths of the mothers, 232 (43%), had not received formal education. In terms of wealth status, the households participating in this study were categorised as follows: 179 (33.2%) were classified as poor, 186 (34.5%) as medium and 174 (32.3%) as rich (table 1).

Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate the minimum meal frequency among children aged 6–23 months in the cash crop district of Kercha, Southern Ethiopia. In this study, we accounted for clustering effects at the kebele level and identified several factors significantly associated with achieving minimum meal frequency in children. These factors included birth order, the timing of complementary food introduction, the educational status of mothers and fathers, postnatal care visits, maternal knowledge of infant and young child feeding, and household wealth status. Additionally, we observed an ICC of 7.3%, indicating that 7.3% of the unexplained variation in the prevalence of minimum meal frequency among children can be attributed to kebele-level factors.

The findings indicated that 62.3% of children received the recommended minimum meal frequency for their age. This percentage is comparable to the 63% reported in a study conducted in the Dembecha district20; however, it is lower than the 72.2% observed in Dabat district,9 87.3% in India,21 69.4% in Ghana,22 68.4% in Bale zone23 and 68.9% in Wolaita Sodo town.12 The discrepancies in these findings may be attributed to differences in the study settings. This research was carried out in a cash crop rural area, while previous studies were conducted in predominantly agrarian and urban settings, where access to food and information about infant and young child feeding practices is more readily available. Such factors are likely to contribute to greater community awareness regarding minimum meal frequency practices.

In contrast, the rate of minimum meal frequency observed in this study was significantly higher than that reported in several other studies, including Burkina Faso (28.9%),24 Tanzania (40.3%),25 sub-Saharan Africa (41.2%),26 Dangila Town (50.4%),27 the Afar region (43.8%)28 and the North Shoa zone (33%).29 This discrepancy may be attributed to variations in study settings, particularly the socioeconomic status of respondents in the referenced areas compared with our study location. Additionally, previous studies often relied on DHS conducted within culturally diverse populations, which may have impacted child feeding practices. In contrast, our research was conducted in a relatively culturally homogeneous population, where similar feeding practices were more prevalent.

In this study, several factors were found to be associated with minimum meal frequency practices. First-born children were more likely to meet the minimum meal frequency requirements compared with those born in the fifth position or later. This finding is consistent with a previous study conducted in Ethiopia.30 One possible explanation for this finding is that mothers may feel less burdened when they have fewer children to care for. Moreover, first-born children often receive more focused attention, which can help ensure that mothers are able to provide adequate nutrition.

We found that children who began consuming additional foods at 6 months were more likely to meet the recommended minimum meal frequency compared with those who started either before or after 6 months. This finding aligns with research conducted in Malawi.31 One possible explanation for this trend is that by 6 months, children are developmentally ready, and their digestive systems are sufficiently mature to handle additional foods. This readiness may contribute to more consistent meal frequency. Conversely, children who begin eating complementary foods before 6 months may not be developmentally prepared, which could result in irregular meal patterns. Similarly, those who start after 6 months might miss critical developmental opportunities for establishing healthy feeding habits, potentially leading to inconsistencies in meal frequency.

Notably, children whose mothers had completed primary education were more likely to adhere to recommended minimum meal frequency compared with those whose mothers had no formal education. This finding aligns with research conducted in India,32 sub-Saharan Africa,26 Nigeria,33 the Dabat district9 and Wolaita Sodo town.12 A plausible explanation for this trend is that mothers with formal education are likely to possess better knowledge of infant and young child feeding practices and are more adept at understanding the information regarding the importance of maintaining a minimum meal frequency for their children.

Furthermore, the current study revealed that children whose fathers had completed primary education were more likely to adhere to the recommended minimum meal frequency compared with those whose fathers had no formal education. This observation aligns with findings from studies conducted in the Gimbichu district34 and Debre Berhan town.35 One possible explanation for this trend is that educated fathers may have a greater awareness of the importance of maintaining minimum meal frequency and are better equipped to comprehend the infant and young child feeding counselling provided by healthcare professionals.

Mothers who attended postnatal care follow-up visits for their child in this study were more likely to provide the recommended minimum meal frequency compared with those whose mothers did not attend. This finding is consistent with research conducted in Dangila town27 and Asayita district.28 One possible explanation for this finding is that mothers receive valuable counselling and guidance during postnatal care follow-up visits, enhancing their understanding of the significance of meal frequency for their child’s health and development.

The findings of this study indicate that mothers with a good knowledge of infant and young child feeding practices were more likely to adhere to the recommended minimum meal frequency compared with those with poor knowledge. This observation aligns with research conducted in Dembecha20 and Nigeria,33 which similarly found that mothers well-versed in infant and young child feeding guidelines are more prone to meet minimum meal frequency standards. One possible explanation for this finding is that knowledgeable mothers recognise the significance of appropriate meal frequency for their children’s growth and development, motivating them to ensure their children receive the necessary meals consistently.

In this study, children from households classified as medium or wealthy were more likely to achieve the required minimum meal frequency compared with those from poorer households. This finding aligns with research conducted in Ethiopia,36 in sub-Saharan Africa,5 Indonesia,37 India32 and Uganda.38 Similarly, studies conducted in Hadiya Zone and Dabat district9 found that households with medium and rich wealth status were more likely to offer the recommended minimum meal frequency for their children. This association could be attributed to the fact that households of medium and high wealth status may have more access to a variety of foods and resources to purchase the recommended minimum meal frequency for their children. Additionally, households with medium and rich wealth status are more likely to have food security and easily provide recommended meal frequency for their children.

This study focused on children in a cash crop district, addressing a relatively underexplored area of research. Moreover, we conducted a multilevel analysis to account for the clustering effects at the kebele level and calculated the ICC, which we have reported. However, there are several limitations to consider. First, the cross-sectional design of the study prevents the establishment of causal relationships between the outcomes and independent variables. This design captures data at a single point in time, making it impossible to determine whether changes in the independent variables precede or influence the outcomes. Additionally, recall bias could have affected the results, as mothers or caregivers of children aged 6–23 months might have difficulty accurately remembering certain details. The study also relied solely on a 24-hour recall method to evaluate minimum meal frequency, which may not accurately represent the typical eating patterns of children. Furthermore, social desirability bias could have affected the accuracy of the information provided, as mothers or caregivers might have tailored their responses to appear more favourable in the eyes of the researchers. Lastly, although the study was community-based and focused specifically on a cash crop region, its findings may not fully represent the broader population of Ethiopia. Nonetheless, they can still be applicable to similar cultural contexts.

Despite Ethiopia’s implementation of various national strategies, including the National Nutrition Programmes aimed at reducing nutrition-related issues, significant gaps persist in addressing child undernutrition. Many interventions have failed to yield improvements in childhood nutrition, as highlighted by multiple studies.39 In this study, key factors such as parental education levels, postnatal care visits, the timing of introducing complementary foods, maternal knowledge of infant and young child feeding practices and household wealth were linked to meeting the minimum meal frequency for children. Although there has been an improvement in the national literacy rate, parental education continues to have a profound impact on child nutrition, as education is often correlated with poverty levels. Thus, it is essential to combat poverty through enhanced maternal educational opportunities to improve child health.

Therefore, promoting access to healthcare for postnatal check-ups and providing parents with vital information about the importance of starting complementary feeding at 6 months is critical for improving child nutrition outcomes and reducing undernutrition. These efforts align with broader national and global nutrition goals, such as the WHO’s Global Nutrition Targets, which aim to reduce stunting and wasting in children under 5 years of age. By ensuring that families have the resources and knowledge they need, it can contribute to healthier populations and support the achievement of sustainable development goals related to health and well-being.
