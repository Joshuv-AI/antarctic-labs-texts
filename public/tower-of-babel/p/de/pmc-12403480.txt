Novel role of isosorbide as a biostimulant in enhancing plant growth and development in Arabidopsis thaliana
Authors: Raj Kishan Agrahari, Daisuke Aoki, Toru Fujiwara, Takehiro Kamiya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSustainable agriculture faces growing challenges in boosting food production while minimizing environmental impact, highlighting the need for innovative solutions. The "plastics to fertilizers" concept, which converts poly(isosorbide carbonate) (PIC) derived from plastic waste into urea and isosorbide, presents a promising approach, as we have previously reported (Abe in Green Chem 23:9030-9037, 2021). While urea’s role in plant nutrition is well established, the effect of isosorbide on plant growth and development remains largely unexplored. This study evaluates the impact of exogenous isosorbide treatment on Arabidopsis thaliana, aiming to unveil its potential as a biostimulant. Plants were grown in media with varying concentrations (0–10 mM) of isosorbide. Based on the optimal dose, determined by enhancements in shoot biomass and primary root length, we further analysed several parameters, including carbon and nitrogen content, carbon-to-nitrogen (C/N) ratio, nitrogen use efficiency (NUE), ionomic profiles, transcriptomic changes, and stress tolerance. Our results demonstrate that isosorbide treatment significantly promotes plant growth, with a significant increase in shoot biomass, improved C/N ratio, and enhanced NUE. Ionome analysis revealed altered distributions of essential elements in shoots and roots, indicating that isosorbide influence nutrient uptake and allocation. In addition, isosorbide enhanced plant growth under nitrogen deficiency and salt stress conditions. Transcriptomic analysis identified 447 differentially expressed genes in shoots and 327 in the roots, with significant enriched in pathways related to stress adaptation, metabolism, and hormonal regulation. Together, these findings provide novel insights into the biostimulant potential of isosorbide, highlighting its robust impact on plant growth and stress resilience while offering an innovative link between polymer recycling and sustainable agriculture.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07248-5.Keywords: Arabidopsis, Biostimulant, Isosorbide, Ionome, Abiotic stress, Transcriptome, Sustainable agriculture

Introduction

As the global population continues to grow and is projected by the UN to reach over 9 billion by 2050, pressure on agriculture systems to meet growing food demands intensifies [9]. At the same time climate change, soil degradation and demand for sustainable agriculture pose threat for global food security [21, 29, 31]. Moreover, traditional farming has intensified the depletion of natural resources and affected the environment; therefore, it is important to develop innovative strategies to support sustainable agriculture [13, 27]. Considering the scenario, there is an urgent need for innovative solutions that enhance global agricultural production without further depleting natural resources [4, 23]. The example of such progressive efforts is the EU's farm-to-fork policy, which aims to reduce the environmental impact of agricultural production by promoting strategies such as reducing the use of pesticides and fertilizers, encouraging organic farming, and promoting biodiversity [20, 28]. It also highlights environmentally friendly methods that reduce ecological effects while boosting agricultural productivity. Within this framework of emerging innovations, the combination of plastic recycling and agriculture offers a novel approach to sustainability. Recently, we reported a new approach, i.e., “fertilizer-from-plastics,” which not only provides an eco-friendly alternative to conventional plastic disposal but also contributes to sustainable agriculture by repurposing waste into valuable fertilizer products [1].

Under this emerging “Plastics to Fertilizers” approach, poly-isosorbide carbonate (PIC), a bio-based polycarbonate synthesized from isosorbide—a glucose-derived monomer—has gained attention as a sustainable alternative to petroleum-based polycarbonates due to its high transparency, thermal stability, and mechanical strength [11]. PIC undergoes chemical degradation via ammonolysis, yielding two key products: urea, a widely used nitrogen fertilizer [38], and isosorbide, a compound with promising potential in plant growth promotion [1]. Despite the clinical use of isosorbide derivatives in treating heart disease [35], its role in plant growth remains largely unexplored. Isosorbide is a sugar alcohol derivative belonging to the polyol class [17], characterized by the presence of multiple hydroxyl groups, such as those found in sorbitol and mannitol—well-known plant metabolites [2].

It has been extensively studied that sugar alcohols like sorbitol and mannitol enhance plant’s ability to adapt to various abiotic stress, including oxidative stress [33], salt [32], and drought [19]. Their well-known osmo-protectant role [25] stabilizes cellular structures and preserve water retention, assisting plants in overcoming various stress. Particularly, sorbitol helps with stress-induced metabolic changes, while mannitol enhances water retention in plant cells, limiting water loss under stress conditions. As sugar alcohols such as sorbitol and mannitol do, isosorbide may also play a role in enhancing plant growth by interacting with metabolic pathways that support growth and resilience. Understanding the role of isosorbide in enhancing plant growth and stress tolerance may improve our knowledge of sugar-derived chemicals in plant biology and highlight the compound's potential as a stress-reduction agent. This could pave the way for novel biostimulants that aid in crop adaptation to climate-related stressors, providing a viable approach to more robust agricultural systems.

In this study, we investigate isosorbide’s potential as a plant growth enhancer and stress tolerance agent. We assess the impact of this new biostimulant on plant physiology, nutrient absorption, and biomass accumulation. This research aims to bridge the gap between polymer recycling and sustainable agriculture by offering new perspectives on the diverse applications of bio-based materials.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

Arabidopsis thaliana accession Col-0 (used as the wild type (WT) in this study) was obtained from the Arabidopsis Biological Resource Center (Columbus, OH, USA). A single seed descent procedure was used to multiply the obtained seeds prior to experimental usage. For dose response studies, seeds were surface sterilized with 2% bleach for 5 min, rinsed with deionized water, and sown on MGRL nutrients medium [10] with 1% (w/v) sucrose (Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan), supplemented with 0, 2.5, 5 or 10 mM isosorbide (TCI, Tokyo, Japan), and solidified using 1% (w/v) gellan gum (Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan). After incubation for 2 days at 4 °C, the plates were placed vertically, and the plants were grown at 22 °C for 15 days under a 16-h light/8-h dark photoperiod. The plants were photographed after 8 days of growth, and primary root lengths were measured using ImageJ software. After 15 days, the shoots were cut, and fresh weights were measured.

Carbon and nitrogen ratio (C/N) assay and elemental analyses

Arabidopsis plants were grown on solid MGRL medium [10] supplemented with 0 or 2.5 mM isosorbide for 15 days. Shoots and roots were harvested, rinsed with deionized water, blotted dry with tissue paper, and dried in polypropylene tubes at 70 °C for 3 days in a hot-air oven. The dried samples were used for total C/N ratio analysis and elemental concentration measurements. The C/N ratio was determined using a CN analyzer (Vario MAX Cube, Elementar), which combusts the samples and automatically quantifies total carbon and nitrogen content. For elemental analysis, dried shoot and root samples were weighed and digested with nitric acid (HNO3) as described by Hashimoto et al. [12]. Briefly, 1 mL of HNO₃ was added to each sample in Pyrex tubes and heated at 110 °C until the acid evaporated; this step was repeated with an additional 1 mL of HNO₃. Subsequently, 1 mL of hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) was added, and the samples were heated until dry. The residues were dissolved in 2 mL of 0.08 N HNO₃ and diluted 15-fold with 0.08 N HNO3 containing 2 ppb indium as an internal standard. Elemental concentrations were measured using an inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometer (ICP-MS, Agilent 7800; Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA).

Low nitrogen and salt stress phenotyping

Sterilized seeds were sown on solid MGRL [10] medium (pH 5.8) containing 0 or 2.5-mM isosorbide, 1% (w/v) sucrose, and 1% (w/v) gellan gum. For low nitrogen (N) conditions, the medium was modified by adjusting the nitrate concentration without altering K concentration: 7 mM nitrate (control), 1.4 mM nitrate (1.4 mM KNO₃ and 5.6 mM KCl), 0.7 mM nitrate (0.7 mM KNO₃ and 6.3 mM KCl), and 0.14 mM nitrate (0.14 mM KNO3 and 6.86 mM KCl). The plates were incubated for 2 days at 4 °C, then placed vertically, and plants were grown at 22 °C for 15 days under a 16-h light/8-h dark photoperiod. Photographs were taken after 8 days, and primary root lengths were measured using ImageJ. On day 15, shoot fresh weight was measured. For salt stress, sterilized seeds were sown on solid MGRL medium (pH 5.8) containing 0 or 2.5 mM isosorbide, 1% (w/v) sucrose, supplemented with NaCl concentrations of 0, 50, 75, or 100 mM. Phenotypic measurements were performed as described for the low N experiment.

RNA-sequencing and differentially expressed gene analyses

Shoot and root tissues from Arabidopsis plants grown for 15 days on solid MGRL medium (pH 5.8) with 0 or 2.5 mM isosorbide, 1% (w/v) sucrose, and 1% (w/v) gellan gum were collected. Samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. Total RNA was extracted using the NucleoSpin RNA Kit (MACHEREY–NAGEL) and quantified with a Nanodrop Lite Spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The RNA-seq library was prepared using Lasy-Seq v1.1 protocol [24] and sequenced on the NovaSeqX Plus platform to generate 150-nt long paired-end reads. Around 8 million reads were obtained per sample. Raw data were pre-processed with quality control and trimming using fastp [5]. Clean reads were mapped to the TAIR10 reference genome (EnsemblPlants: https://plants.ensembl.org/index.html) using HISAT2 [16]. Gene-level counts were generated with featureCounts [18] using the appropriate annotation file. Gene expression profiles were evaluated using principal component analysis. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified with edgeR, using normalization, dispersion estimation, and exact tests. Genes with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 were considered significant. DEGs were annotated using TAIR10 descriptions and visualized via volcano plots. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis for up- and downregulated genes was conducted using clusterProfiler, based on biological process ontology, with a q-value cutoff of 0.05 for significance.

Results

Determination of optimal isosorbide concentration for Arabidopsis

The optimal concentration of isosorbide for enhancing Arabidopsis growth was determined by analysing its dose-dependent effects (0, 2.5, 5, and 10 mM) on plant using MGRL agar media supplemented with 1% sucrose over a 15-days period. To assess how different concentrations affected overall growth, primary root length and shoot fresh weight were measured. The results showed that plants treated with 2.5 mM isosorbide showed a significant increase in shoot fresh weight (Fig. 1A and B) and primary root length (Fig. 1C). In contrast, plant treated with 5 mM and 10 mM isosorbide showed reduced fresh weight and primary root length, indicating potential toxicity at higher concentration. These results suggest that 2.5 mM isosorbide is the optimal concentration for promoting growth in Arabidopsis.

Plants were grown in MGRL media with varying concentrations of isosorbide (0 mM, 2.5 mM, 5 mM, and 10 mM) for 15 days for shoot fresh weight analysis and 8 days for primary root length measurements. Representative images of plant shoots under each treatment are shown (B). Data represent as n = 21. Statistical significance was determined by Dunnett’s test, comparing each isosorbide treatment to the control (0 mM), with asterisks indicating significant differences (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ****p < 0.0001). Scale bar = 1 cm

Effect of isosorbide on carbon and nitrogen content, C/N ratio, and nitrogen use efficiency (NUE) in Arabidopsis

Sugar alcohols like sorbitol have been shown to enhance carbon enrichment and facilitate carbon–nitrogen assimilation in various plants [8, 26]. Therefore, it is important to determine whether isosorbide affects the carbon and nitrogen content in plants. Isosorbide treatment significantly altered carbon and nitrogen content in both shoot and root tissues of Arabidopsis grown for 15 days. In shoot tissues, nitrogen content (N%) significantly decreased, while carbon concentration (C%) increased substantially. This change resulted in an increased C/N ratio. Additionally, shoot NUE increased significantly, indicating improved NUE (Fig. 2A). These results suggest that isosorbide may promote a shift in carbon storage while optimizing nitrogen usage.

Effect of isosorbide on carbon and nitrogen content, C/N Ratio, and nitrogen use Efficiency (NUE). After 15 days of growth, plants were divided into shoots and roots for analysis of carbon content (C%), nitrogen content (N%), and C/N ratio and NUE. Panel (A) shows these parameters for shoot tissue, while panel (B) represents them for root tissue. Data are shown from four biological replicates. Statistical significance was evaluated by Student’s t-test, with asterisks indicating significant differences (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001)

In contrast, root tissues of isosorbide-treated plants showed reduction in both C% and N% (Fig. 2B). Despite the decrease in both carbon and nitrogen content, the C/N ratio and NUE were significantly higher compared to control (Fig. 2B). This implies that while isosorbide reduces the total carbon and nitrogen in roots, the increase in C/N ratio could result from reduced nitrogen retention or altered nitrogen allocation within the plant, rather than improved uptake or storage. Overall, these findings suggest that isosorbide alters carbon–nitrogen dynamics in Arabidopsis, with distinct effects on carbon and nitrogen metabolism in shoots and roots, particularly improving NUE and C/N ratio in both tissues.

Isosorbide-induced changes in the ionome of Arabidopsis shoots and roots

To further explore the influence of isosorbide on the accumulation pattern of different elements in the shoots and roots of Arabidopsis, ionome analysis was conducted. All elements analysed were within the standard curve range (Supplementary Fig. 1). Following this, significant changes in element accumulation patterns were observed in both shoots and roots of plants treated with isosorbide for 15 days. This analysis included essential macronutrients such as P, K, Ca, Mg, and S, along with essential micronutrients like Fe, Mn, B, Zn, Mo, Cu, and Ni. Additionally, non-essential elements including Na, Co, Li, Sr, Rb, Ge, and Se, as well as pollutants such as As, Cd, and Cs, were analysed to assess their variation in response to isosorbide. In the shoots, significant reductions were observed in Na, S, K, Co, Cu, and Mo levels compared to untreated plants, suggesting that isosorbide treatment alters translocation or accumulation of these elements in aerial parts (Fig. 3A). In contrast, in root, a distinct pattern was observed with significant reductions in B, S, and Mo (Fig. 3B). Interestingly, K levels in roots significantly increased, indicating a differential regulatory mechanism in response to isosorbide (Fig. 3B). These findings indicate tissue-specific changes in nutrient dynamics under isosorbide treatment, with reduced accumulation of several essential and non-essential elements in both shoots and roots. The changes observed in both nutrient and non-essential element profiles reflect a complex response of the plant ionome to isosorbide.

Exogenous isosorbide enhances plant growth under nitrogen-deficient and salt stress conditions

To determine whether exogenous isosorbide enhances plant growth under the influence of different environmental stressors, Arabidopsis plants were treated with isosorbide and exposed to nitrogen deficient and salt stress conditions. For nitrogen deficiency, plants were exposed to different amounts of nitrate (NO3⁻), which included 1.4 mM, 0.7 mM, and 0.14 mM as well as the control level of 7 mM. Under nitrogen-deficient conditions, isosorbide treatment significantly enhanced shoot fresh weight and primary root length (Fig. 4A), suggesting that isosorbide can stimulate growth in spite of a nitrogen deficiency, either by increasing the efficiency of nitrogen utilization or by altering metabolic pathways to maximize growth in the presence of low nitrogen supply.

Isosorbide’s robust effect on plant growth response under various stress conditions. The impact of isosorbide treatment on plant growth was assessed under nitrogen-deficient and salt-stress conditions. (A) Nitrogen Deficiency — plants were grown with varying nitrate (NO₃⁻) levels: 7 mM (normal), 1.4 mM, 0.7 mM, and 0.14 mM. Shoot fresh weight was measured after 15 days, and primary root length was measured after 8 days. Representative images of plant shoots for each nitrogen condition are included. (B) Salt Stress — plants were grown under NaCl concentrations: 0 mM, 50 mM, 75 mM, and 100 mM. Shoot fresh weight was recorded at 15 days, and primary root length was measured at 8 days. Representative images of plant shoots under each salt treatment condition are shown. Data are presented as n = 30 for nitrogen deficiency and n = 27–37 for salt stress. Statistical significance was assessed by Student’s t-test, with asterisks (*p < 0.05) indicating significant differences. Scale bar = 1 cm

Similarly, the impact of isosorbide was evaluated in salt stress conditions using concentrations of 0, 50, 75, and 100 mM of NaCl. Across all salt concentrations, exogenous isosorbide treatment significantly enhanced shoot fresh weight and primary root length, suggesting that isosorbide confers protection and stimulates growth even in the presence of severe salinity stress (Fig. 4B). Together, these findings indicate the potential of isosorbide as a growth-promoting agent that could mitigate the adverse effects of environmental stressors by robustly enhancing plant growth.

Transcriptomic response of Arabidopsis to exogenous isosorbide treatment

To understand the transcriptomic response of Arabidopsis treated with exogenous isosorbide, a comprehensive DEGs analysis was conducted on shoot and root tissues of Arabidopsis treated with or without isosorbide. Using an FDR threshold of < 0.05, 447 DEGs were identified in shoots (203 upregulated, 244 downregulated; Fig. 5A, Table S1 and S2), and 327 DEGs in roots (232 upregulated, 95 downregulated; Fig. 5B, Table S3 and S4).

Transcriptome analysis of Arabidopsis treated with isosorbide. Transcriptomic responses in Arabidopsis shoots and roots were analyzed based on data from three biological replicates, focusing on differentially expressed genes (DEGs), gene regulation patterns, and enriched gene ontology (GO) terms. Panel (A) shows the shoot tissue analysis, including a volcano plot of DEGs with significant upregulated and downregulated genes, as well as GO enrichment scatter plots for both categories. Panel (B) presents the root tissue analysis with a volcano plot and GO enrichment scatter plots for upregulated and downregulated genes. These analyses collectively reveal key biological processes influenced by isosorbide treatment in both tissues

Further GO enrichment analysis of biological processes in shoots revealed that the upregulated DEGs were predominantly associated with responses to oxidative stress and organic hydroxy compound metabolic processes, detoxification, plant organ senescence, organic hydroxy compound biosynthetic process, response to hypoxia. In contrast, the downregulated DEGs in shoots were significantly enriched in processes such as cellular responses to hypoxia, decreased oxygen levels, oxidative stress, and responses to jasmonic acid, fatty acids, water deprivation, and organic cyclic compounds (Fig. 5A). In roots, upregulated DEGs were enriched in biological processes related to responses to fungus, cellular responses to hypoxia, decreased oxygen levels, defence responses to bacteria, secondary metabolic processes, immune responses, salicylic acid signalling, and indole compound metabolism. In contrast, secondary metabolic process, response to oxidative stress, sulfur compound metabolism, secondary metabolic biosynthesis, response to metal ions, and alpha amino acid metabolism were associated to the downregulated DEGs in roots (Fig. 5B). These findings indicate that isosorbide treatment affects specific and tissue-dependent transcriptomic changes in Arabidopsis, resulting in notable changes to metabolic processes and stress response pathways, including oxidative stress, immunological responses, hormonal response and secondary metabolism.

Discussion

This study highlights the growing demand for sustainable alternatives to conventional plant growth stimulants. Isosorbide, derived from PIC degradation, offers a novel opportunity to reutilize waste byproducts for agricultural use, contributing to a more sustainable, circular approach to agriculture [1]. Our findings demonstrate that isosorbide promotes plant growth (Fig. 1), possibly by modulating carbon and nitrogen dynamics, ionome profiles, and gene expression, thereby improving NUE. These findings highlight its potential as a versatile biostimulant for enhancing stress resilience, especially under abiotic and nutrient-deficient conditions.

The role of isosorbide in optimizing resource allocation is supported by significant alterations observed in carbon and nitrogen metabolism. Specifically, shoot tissues had a higher carbon content and a lower nitrogen content, which suggests that isosorbide may enhance carbon storage while enhancing NUE (Fig. 2). These findings are consistent with previous studies that show the ability of sugar alcohols such as mannitol and sorbitol to enhance carbon metabolism under nutrient-limited conditions [22]. Conversely, the decrease in carbon and nitrogen content in roots suggests differential tissue responses, possibly influenced by shift in nitrogen availability in roots. This may be associated with changes in nutrient allocation. Such altered nutrient allocation can result from modifications in metabolic demands driven by environmental factors or treatment with growth regulators [7, 40]. This suggests that isosorbide may influence nutrient allocation by modulating the balance between carbon storage in shoots and NUE. Such regulations may involve both direct effects on metabolic pathways and indirect effects on resource distribution, contributing to enhanced shoot growth and regulated root nitrogen allocation depending on resource availability. Moreover, the significant changes in ionome profiles and variations in carbon and nitrogen content further support isosorbide’s role in regulating nutritional and ion homeostasis. Isosorbide likely modulates ion transport to maintain ion balance, as indicated by the reduction of essential macronutrients (K and S) and key micronutrients (Mo, Cu, and Co) in shoots (Fig. 3). Similar responses have been reported for other sugars alcohols, which influence ion transport under stress conditions, including nutrient deficiencies and osmotic imbalance [34, 36]. Interestingly, the increased potassium levels in roots suggest that isosorbide may affect K⁺ transport dynamics. Given potassium’s crucial role in solute transport and phloem loading [37], its accumulation in roots suggests that isosorbide may facilitate solute movement. However, a more detailed understanding of isosorbide uptake and its relationship with potassium accumulation may provide deeper insights into the mechanisms underlying isosorbide-induced growth enhancement.

Beyond nutrient regulation, isosorbide enhances plant resilience under nitrogen-deficient and salt stress conditions. Increased shoot biomass under nitrogen limitation suggests that isosorbide facilitates nitrogen acquisition and assimilation, thereby improving NUE. This is supported by our previous findings showing that isosorbide promotes growth when urea is the sole nitrogen source [1], reinforcing its capacity to enhance NUE. Likewise, the increase shoot biomass (Fig. 4) under salt stress suggests a protective effect, potentially linked to improved osmotic regulation and stress adaptation mechanisms. Similar to other sugar alcohols, such as mannitol, isosorbide may aid in maintaining water balance and reducing oxidative stress [32]. Transcriptomic data further supports this observation, showing that isosorbide activates oxidative stress response pathways in shoots, likely alleviating damage caused by reactive oxygen species. In contrast, in roots, it influences nitrate metabolism, optimizing nutrient acquisition and assimilation under stress. These findings are consistent with previous reports on sugar alcohols regulating metabolic responses to abiotic stress [3, 39].

Further tissue-specific DEGs were identified through transcriptome and GO enrichment analyses, revealing distinct molecular responses in roots and shoots that influence broader metabolic and signaling networks (Fig. 5). In shoots, isosorbide-induced upregulation of genes involved in oxidative stress response (GO:0006979), organic hydroxy compound metabolism (GO:1901615), and detoxification pathways (GO:0098754) suggests a role in enhancing redox balance and xenobiotic detoxification. Given that isosorbide is an aliphatic hydroxy compound, such transcriptional changes may reflect its intrinsic ability to modulate redox-regulated metabolic processes. These findings are consistent with the importance of detoxification and hydroxy compound metabolism in maintaining cellular homeostasis under stress [25]. Concurrently, downregulated genes in shoots were associated with jasmonic acid (JA)-mediated signaling (GO:0009753), water deprivation response (GO:0009414), and hypoxia response (GO:0001666), indicating suppression of stress-induced pathway that often limit growth. JA plays a key regulator for plant defence and stress adaptation, often promoting responses that limit biomass accumulation under abiotic stress [14] thus, its downregulation indicates a shift toward a growth-favourable transcriptional response. Similar effects have been reported for sugar alcohols that modulate JA-responsive genes to promote growth under environmental stress [3], supporting the notion that isosorbide may act as a growth-promoting agent. In roots, isosorbide triggered a distinct transcriptional pattern. Upregulated genes were enriched in fungal defence responses (GO:0009620), salicylic acid-mediated response (GO:0009751), and indole compound metabolism (GO:0042430), indicating enhanced biotic stress response and activation of secondary metabolism. Unlike shoots, genes related to oxidative stress response (GO:0006979) was significantly downregulated in roots, along with sulfur compound metabolism (GO:0006790) and secondary metabolite biosynthesis (GO:0019748). This tissue-specific contrast suggests that isosorbide differentially regulates redox balance and resource allocation—activating antioxidant pathways in shoots while reducing them in roots, possibly to shift resources toward nutrient uptake or interactions with beneficial microbes. These transcriptomic changes suggest that isosorbide directs a coordinated and tissue-specific molecular response, promoting detoxification and stress resilience in shoots while fine-tuning defence and metabolism in roots. Collectively, these findings support the potential of isosorbide as a plant biostimulant that modulates integrated stress adaptation mechanism.

Importantly, we observed a dose-dependent effect of isosorbide on growth: 2.5 mM enhanced shoot fresh weight and primary root length, while higher concentrations (5 and 10 mM) inhibited growth, indicating potential phytotoxicity. Such dose response patterns are typical for sugar alcohols and small molecule biostimulants. For example, mannitol and sorbitol promotes growth at low doses but induces osmotic stress and inhibit growth at higher concentrations [6, 15]. Similarly, alkamides modulate root architecture in a dose-dependent manner [30]. These results suggest that isosorbide functions as a biostimulant at low concentrations through beneficial signaling or osmotic effects, while excessive dose may disrupt metabolic or hormonal balance, consistent with a hormetic response. Together, these comprehensive physiological and molecular insights underscore isosorbide’s potential as a multifunctional biostimulant for sustainable crop improvement and highlight the importance of optimizing its dosage to ensure maximum benefits while avoiding adverse effects.

Conclusion

This study confirms isosorbide as a promising plant growth promoter. The findings highlight its potential as a biostimulant by regulating carbon and nitrogen metabolism, optimizing ion homeostasis, and promoting plant growth under nitrogen-limiting and salt-stress conditions. Furthermore, isosorbide’s role as a biostimulant aligns with sustainable agricultural practices through the utilization of PIC degradation products. These findings underscore the need for further exploration into its applications in crop improvement and environmental sustainability.
