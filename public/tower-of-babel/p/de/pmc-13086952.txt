A parameter estimation method for modelling proton exchange membrane fuel cell based on enhanced meta evolutionary differential evolution algorithm
Authors: Vivekananda Pattanaik, Shubhranshu Mohan Parida, Binaya Kumar Malika, Pravat Kumar Rout, Binod Kumar Sahu, Jyoti Ranjan Padhi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate estimation of unknown parameters in proton exchange membrane fuel cells (PEMFCs) is crucial for developing a precise and reliable model, which is necessary for further investigation, simulation analysis, and optimal control. However, conventional approaches often fail to provide satisfactory results due to high non-linearity, multi-variable nature, and strong nonconvexity associated with these problems. The hybrid variants either combine two or more algorithms or adaptively vary the most sensitive and dominant system parameters, which are found to achieve more desirable results than individual algorithms in solving the PEMFC parameter estimation. With this motivation, an enhanced meta-evolutionary differential evolution algorithm (EMEDEA) is proposed in this study to determine the optimal values of specific parameters in PEMFC based energy systems. The idea of achieving peak performance through highly sensitive hyperparameters, such as the mutation factor, crossover probability, and the selection of specific Differential Evolution (DE) strategies, is suggested by adopting dynamic parameter tuning or adaptive mechanisms. To make the study more practical and feasible for application under real-time conditions, a mathematical model of the PEMFC, considering all its nonlinearity and complexity, with different control variables, is used in this study. The sum of squared deviations between the experimental and estimated (or simulated) stack voltages is considered to formulate the objective function for the parameter estimation of the PEMFC system. Additionally, the performance of the proposed method EMEDEA as a solution to this problem has been simulated and tested, considering three different PEMFC stacks. The justification for the accuracy, effectiveness, and reliability of the proposed approach has been provided through comparison tables between experimental and computed stack voltages at each experimental stack current. The convergence graphs demonstrate the swift attainment of optimal values with the proposed approach. A comparative analysis with other prominent methods further confirms the accuracy and superiority of EMEDEA in solving the problem of PEMFC parameter estimation.

Introduction

Parameter estimation plays a crucial role in modelling and characterizing PEMFCs for their design and distribution system planning for control and protection. As the parameters are often hard to measure directly, and many cases are unavailable from manufacturers, to model the PEMFC, it is necessary to compute the unknown parameters accurately. These parameters are generally estimated by using experimental data. Using these data and the corresponding results, researchers and engineers aim to develop various models that reliably and accurately replicate the performance of PEMFCs under various operating conditions. To enable precise predictions of fuel cell performance, there is a need to estimate the parameters accurately. Additionally, the model also provides insights into internal electrochemical reactions and voltage losses. It ensures an accurate representation of polarisation (V–I) and power (P–V) characteristics1 of the PEMFC. The proper modelling also supports optimal system design and control. Apart from that, indirectly, the model enables engineers to refine the configurations of PEMFC gas channel architecture and catalyst layers. The development of robust control strategies for subsystems such as air supply and thermal management is dependent on the accurate modelling through the parameter estimation, which in turn enhances performance and facilitates better controllability. Primarily, precise parameter estimation enhances reliability by facilitating condition monitoring, fault detection, and degradation prediction for the PEMFC. These factors are critical and influential for assessing the operational lifespan of the fuel cell. Another significant possibility lies in reducing experimental costs. It is because accurate models lessen the need for extensive prototyping and testing, allowing performance evaluations to be conducted through simulations instead2. Lastly, parameter estimation enhances model robustness, contrary to operating uncertainties, inconsistent accuracy under variable temperature, pressure, and humidity conditions, referring to the characteristic of real-world applications, such as distribution system storage and electric vehicles.

Parameter estimation for modelling PEMFCs is a complex and challenging process due to its non-linear, dynamic, and multi-variable nature. PEMFC models involve numerous electrochemical and physical phenomena. This makes parameter estimation a multimodal optimisation problem with multiple local minima. This hinders the search for a global optimum. It is seen that many model parameters vary with operating conditions, such as temperature, pressure, and humidity. This, in turn, complicates the accurate prediction of polarization (v–i) characteristics curves needed for performance assessment.The presence of unknown parameters may lead to the development of empirical and semi-empirical models. These models contain parameters with unclear physical meanings or manufacturer data that is unavailable3. This necessitates optimization-based estimation approaches to avoid these limitations. The cons of experimental data further exacerbates the problem. The extracted (v-i) datasets are often small, inconsistent, or noisy. This may result in unreliable or non-generalizable model validations. It is found that the problem is not solved completely even when optimization yields a low error for a given dataset.This is because the estimated parameters may lack physical consistency, particularly when applied under different conditions during investigation and analysis4. There are also a few issues and challenges from the perspective of the optimization algorithm. Key challenges must be addressed during parameter estimation. Few issues like high computational cost, the risk of being trapped in local optima, slow convergence, and the need for careful tuning of algorithmic parameters are prominent ones. Dynamic and real-time estimation introduces further difficulties. It is found in many studies that focus only on static models. However, PEMFC parameters, such as ohmic resistance and electrochemical surface area, evolve dynamically. Real-time or online estimation remains computationally demanding5. However, it is critical for effective energy management. The nature of challenges also depends on the model types. The present study aims to formulate its objective by focusing on a solution to these challenges for parameter estimation in PEMFC.

Optimization techniques are extensively employed to estimate unknown parameters in PEMFC models. The principal objective is to minimize the error between the experimental voltage data and model’s predicted voltage output. The formulation is typically presented in a single-objective form. This, in turn, improves the accuracy of the PEMFC model for performance simulation and system analysis. The primary parameters estimated through optimization can be classified according to the voltage losses. This essentially represents the overall cell voltage. It is expressed as the Nernst (open-circuit) voltage minus three main drops4. They are activation, ohmic, and concentration voltage drops. The activation voltage drop parameters correspond to losses that occur at low current densities. It is due to the sluggish kinetics of electrochemical reactions at the electrode surfaces. Optimization techniques are utilized to estimate the semi-empirical coefficients. This defines the activation overvoltage as a function of operating temperature, oxygen concentration, and current density. The ohmic voltage loss parameters represent the losses. It results from ionic resistance in the electrolyte membrane and electronic resistance within the cell’s conductive components of the PEMFC. Two critical parameters are naturally optimized in this category. The first one is an empirical parameter related to the membrane’s water content. It influences ionic conductivity. The second one accounts for contact and electronic resistances between the gas diffusion layer, catalyst layer, and bipolar plates5. The concentration voltage loss parameters are also referred to as mass transport losses. It generally occurs at high current densities. This particularly occurred when reactant gas depletion causes a rapid decline in voltage. The diffusion coefficient or mass transport parameter needs to be optimized to model this phenomenon. In addition to these seven key empirical parameters, optimisation techniques are typically applied to determine operational and structural parameters. This is because these values also further enhance performance. Anode pressure (AP), cathode pressure (CP), and cathode stoichiometry (CS) are a few operational parameters that are optimised to achieve maximum power density and efficiency. For computational fluid dynamics (CFD)-based models, optimization also extends to structural parameters. The thickness and porosity of the gas diffusion and catalyst layers are parameters in this category that need to be estimated6. These enable a more comprehensive and realistic simulation of PEMFC behaviour under diverse operating conditions.

The estimation is done in a manner that best replicates the real-world performance of the PEM fuel cell. The primary goal of this optimization process is to minimize the error between the model’s predicted and experimentally measured output voltages7. The optimization results improve the accuracy, robustness, and predictive capability of the PEMFC model for design, simulation, and control applications. In all these optimisation approaches, the modelling process starts with the definition of the PEMFC model and its associated parameters need to be optimized for its modelling. This has been achieved in a manner that accurately describes the cell’s voltage–current (V–I) behavior with minimal deviation. An objective function is formulated as the sum of squared errors (SSE) between the experimentally observed voltages and model-predicted voltages8. The SSE value is a measure of model accuracy. The optimisation algorithm iteratively adjusts the unknown parameters within predefined limits. The system constraints explore the search space to minimise the SSE. Once the optimal parameter set is identified, it is used to simulate the PEMFC’s v-i polarization characteristics9. The resulting model output is validated by comparing it with experimental data to assess its fidelity and feasibility for various studies in control and distribution system planning. Optimization-based parameter estimation is crucial for developing high-fidelity PEMFC models. This ensures accurate performance predictions. It also enables system design enhancements. It also facilitates the development of advanced control strategies. Several critical limitations are associated with conventional parameter estimation methods. This includes slow convergence rates, susceptibility to local minima, and reduced adaptability. In addition, it may fail to deal with the nonlinear and multivariable nature of PEMFC systems. Furthermore, these recent advances in optimisation algorithms effectively overcome the limitations of conventional parameter estimation methods. As a result, optimization-driven modelling provides a more reliable and efficient framework. This enables subsequent performance evaluation, design optimization, and real-time control of fuel cell systems10. With this idea, the present study aims to develop a novel optimization method to deal with the parameter estimation problem in PEMFC.

Broadly, the parameter estimation techniques applied to PEMFCs can be categorized into two main categories. The first one is classical, and the second one is a meta-heuristic approach. Classical methods rely on mathematical optimization principles and are generally framed to minimize the error between the measured and modeled outputs, such as the least squares method (LSM). This method’s aim is to reduce the sum of squared deviations between the experimental and estimated cell voltages. The process is computationally simple and easy to implement. However, it is prone to convergence toward local minima when applied to highly nonlinear and multi-parameter PEMFC models11. This, in turn, results in suboptimal parameter identification. Similarly, other numerical and gradient-based optimization techniques, such as non-linear regression and iterative search algorithms, are also explored for PEMFC parameter estimation. However, these methods fail to provide better results due to their limitations, such as the intrinsic complexity, interdependence among parameters, and strong non-convex characteristics of PEMFC models. This limits their accuracy and robustness, particularly under varying operating conditions and complex conditions of all types of modelling12.

Meta-heuristic methods are predominantly effective in solving complex, non-linear, and multi-modal problems such as PEMFC parameter estimation. The differential evolution (DE) algorithm is applied through the operations of mutation, crossover, and selection. It is desirable for PEMFC modeling due to its simplicity, computational efficiency, and strong global search capability13. The genetic algorithm (GA) has proven effective for parameter estimation tasks, and is particularly effective for PEMFC parameter estimation due to its better convergence rate, stability, and accuracy14. Swarm intelligence algorithms outclass in balancing exploration and exploitation in complex search spaces. This makes them highly suitable for PEMFC parameter estimation. Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) is computationally efficient and easy to implement, even for the PEMFC parameter estimation15. However, the major limitation is premature convergence, particularly in high-dimensional or multi-modal problems. Recently, many evolutionary-based approaches offer unique exploration mechanisms and adaptive strategies that further enhance the reliability and precision of PEMFC parameter estimation16–18. The Walrus optimization algorithm (WaOA)1, Chaotic Harris Hawk Optimization (CHHO)7, Marine predator algorithm (MPA)11, Shark-smell optimization method (SSOM)17, Bonobo optimizers(BO)18 , Flower pollination algorithm (FPA)19, Salp swarm optimizer(SSO) 25, Grasshopper optimization algorithm (GOA)26, Shuffled frog leaping algorithm (SFLA)30, Cuckoo search optimizer (CSO), Artificial Hummingbird algorithm (AHA)39, Whale optimization algorithm (WOA)40 and Grey Wolf optimization (GWO)41, each offering unique exploration mechanisms and adaptive strategies that further enhance the reliability and precision of PEMFC parameter estimation. Also, other meta heuristics like Back-tracking search algorithm with Burger chaotic map (BSABCM)20, Atom-search optimizer (ASO)37, Enhanced transient search optimization (ETSO)42 , Lungs performance based optimization (LPO) 43, Modified Artificial Ecosystem Optimization (MAEO) 44, are also applied for estimation of PEMFC parameters. In addition to the above, improved versions of earlier techniques such as Quasi oppositional Bonobo optimizer (QOBO)18, Modified multi-group dna Genetic algorithm (MMDNA-GA)35, Repairable grey wolf optimization (RGWO)36, Cuckoo search with explosion operator (CS-EO)38 and Levy flight enabled-Artificial Hummingbird algorithm (L-AHA)39, in the form of adaptive control factor variation and hybrid-types are also proposed with improved search performance for parameter estimation of PEMFCs . Classical techniques have their own limitations of complexity in formulation, handling the constraints, and restricted area of flexibility, even when applying to the PEMFC parameter estimation6,21. However, all these approaches are not free from system parameter dependency and complexity in the hybrid approaches in terms of a coordinated way of searching the optimal values in both the exploration and exploitation stages of searching.

Hybrid algorithms have emerged as a powerful class of optimization techniques. They combine the complementary strengths of different meta-heuristic strategies to overcome the inherent limitations of individual algorithms. By integrating diverse search mechanisms, these methods achieve superior convergence speed, accuracy, and robustness. Collectively, these hybrid meta-heuristics, such as the JAYA algorithm and Nelder-Mead simplex (JAYA-NM), Reverse-Search Chaos Differential-Evolution Whale Optimization Algorithm (RSCDWOA), reflect a significant advancement in achieving accurate parameter estimation22,23. However, the hybrid algorithms despite their difficulty in formulation, have emerged as robust and feasible optimization techniques for solving many complex engineering problems. Also neural network based techniques are developed and implemented for estimation of PEMFC parameters45. These methods are also capable of accuarate estimation. There is a considerable possibility of developing a better and emerging technique with this hybrid formulation approach. Moreover, the stacked fuel cells can be used for energy management in distributed generation systems and transportation systems by the use of improved control techniques46,47.

The PEMFC has been widely studied for its potential applications in clean energy. However, significant research gaps remain, particularly in parameter estimation and optimization. The crucial gaps related to parameter estimation methods have been considered the primary motivation for this research. A PEMFC’s dynamics are highly nonlinear, and that makes the modelling more complex. Improved models are essential to accurately represent these nonlinear behaviors. Many models have been formulated with the assumption of fixed values for membrane resistance, catalyst activity, and mass transport coefficients. These are influenced by operating conditions such as temperature, humidity, and load variation. A sensitivity analysis is necessary to account for these variations. There is a need to quantify and reduce uncertainty in parameters for better modelling of the PEMFC. Real-time parameter estimation with dynamically adjusting the model as conditions change, such as load and human activity, is a critical research gap. There are two significant measurement difficulties are exising in terms of sensor limitations and limited observability, particularly in the case of specific internal parameters and variables. Lack of benchmark data and proper experimental data for validating parameter estimation techniques are the significant limitations to look after. From an estimation point of view, it is essential to develop an efficient optimization technique to accurately estimate the unknown PEMFC parameters. Although various authors suggest numerous approaches, a significant gap remains in improving techniques, particularly the hybrid approach. In this study, considering the available data, an attempt has been made to employ a data-driven approach based on the hybrid model for accurate parameter estimation of PEMFC.

The DE algorithm is primarily regulated by mutation scaling factor, crossover rates, and population size, the three control factors. The DE’s optimization process may stagnate if it fails to generate offspring solutions superior to their parents. This leads to a saturation in obtaining better results while moving from one iteration to the next. To avoid this stagnation, selecting an appropriate parameter configuration to improve DE’s search capabilities becomes vital. Secondly, to address the intricate challenges of parameter configuration in DE, researchers often descend towards two predominant strategies. The first one is parameter control, and the second one is parameter tuning. Parameter control is a dynamic approach in which the algorithm’s parameters are adjusted in real-time during its execution. In contrast, parameter tuning is a more static methodology. In this, the optimal configuration is established before the algorithm’s initiation. This perception is utilized to formulate a meta-evolutionary algorithm in this study. The concept optimizes the parameters of an evolutionary algorithm (EA) using another EA in this formulation. Three significant steps have been carried out. Firstly, with parameterised DE (PDE), a variant of DE that utilises augmented parameterisation, unlike traditional DE algorithms. This employs fixed mutation and crossover strategies. This concept develops a meta-level DE as an evolver to refine PDE’s hyperparameters iteratively. It is guided by performance feedback from multiple PDE instances acting as executors. Lastly, breaking away from the limitations of conventional parameter tuning, the EMEDEA is integrated with a GPU-accelerated computing framework24. This enhances MEDEA’s computational ability for facilitating swifter evaluations and algorithmic refinements. With these innovative ideas, EMEDEA offers an efficient and automated end-to-end approach to black-box optimization. This MEDEA approach is applied to parameter estimation in PEMFC.

Modelling

When optimisation techniques are applied to parameter estimation of the PEMFC, several assumptions are made as a simplification to the complex, nonlinear electrochemical and physical processes involved. The objective is to utilise these assumptions to develop a tractable mathematical model that the optimisation algorithm can use to match the experimental data accurately25. The general modelling assumptions related to the underlying semi-empirical model of the PEMFC are applied in the estimation process as follows.

For static (steady-state) models specifically

Steady-state operation: All governing equations are time-independent. These are formulated with a focus on stabilised operating conditions, thereby neglecting their dynamic characteristics.

Uniform current/temperature distribution: The system’s current density and prevailing temperature are assumed to be uniform over the active electrode area during steady operation.

Negligible anode overpotential: Activation losses at the anode are considered insignificant compared to cathodic losses; hence, only cathode kinetics are modelled in detail.

Constant gas compositions: Gas concentrations are assumed to be constant along the channels. Any gradients or depletion are neglected.

Negligible water dynamics: Water transport phenomena, such as back-diffusion and electro-osmotic drag, are not explicitly modeled in the mathematical expression, as they are considered non-sensitive parameters during steady operation.

Assumptions related to parameter estimation specifically:

The following assumptions are made for the simplified modelling.

Parameter constancy: All the semi-empirical parameters considered in the modelling are treated as constant across the I–V data range, despite actual variations with respect to temperature, pressure, and ageing that may occur during operation.

Objective function: Parameter estimation aims to minimize an error criterion. In this case, the error criterion is assumed in the form of the sum of squared deviations (SSD) between the experimental and estimated voltages.

Measurement accuracy: Experimental voltage, current, and pressure data are assumed to be accurate, reliable, and free of significant sensor bias and disturbances.

Independence of parameters: The parameters are assumed to be weakly correlated. This is because it is expected to ensure identifiability during the optimization process.

Neglected noise and hysteresis: Electrical noise, measurement delays, and hysteretic effects in transient regions are often ignored and not considered in system modeling.

Differential evolution algorithm and possible adaptations

DE is a population-based stochastic optimization technique. This technique includes mutation, crossover, and selection operations.

DE and its steps

The following steps are followed in DE31.

Step 1: Initialization: Initially, the candidate solutions xi, are randomly generated within the search space, considering the bounds as the limits of each variable.

Step -2: Mutation: For each solution xi, a mutant vector ui is created as follows.

It is typically performed either by specifying a predefined maximum number of generations or by establishing a fitness threshold.

Adaptive Differential Evolution (DE) algorithms are designed to modify key sensitive and dominant control factors such as the scaling factor (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F$$\end{document}), crossover rate (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$CR$$\end{document}), or even the strategy choice itself as the optimization progresses. These factors enable the algorithm to dynamically modify its behavior to suit the underlying characteristics of the problem, which later leads to its optimal value. These strategies were found to enhance performance on a wide variety of problem instances significantly. This is realized by allowing the parameters and strategies to self-adjust based on empirical performance during the run, rather than depending on fixed values throughout. In contrast, distributed DE influences multiple subpopulations or parallel frameworks. It often utilizes GPU acceleration to boost both computational efficiency and solution diversity to improve performance. This, in turn, makes the algorithms scalable and robust for large-scale optimization tasks even more complex, nonlinear and dynamic situations.

Meta-Evolutionary Algorithms (MetaEAs) raise the parameter optimization process to a meta-level for better search capability. In this case, the selection and tuning of control factors for EAs are themselves formulated as optimization problems. These are commonly solved by employing another EA as the meta-optimizer. This meta-evolutionary approach automates the search for suitable EA configurations. This, in turn, helps transcend manual tuning of control factors by searching for high-performing parameter sets across diverse optimization scenarios making the idea of hybrid approach formulation application in a constructive way32.

MetaDE utilizes EvoX24,32. This is a GPU-accelerated evolutionary computation framework. It is designed for efficient and scalable implementation of both meta-level and base-level EAs. Technically, EvoX harnesses the massive parallelism of GPUs through tensorized operations. It also integrates seamlessly with deep learning libraries, such as PyTorch, enabling rapid batch processing of populations and algorithmic states24. This framework supports distributed workflows, which is a modular, functional programming model. It facilitates flexibility and ease of algorithm development. Practically, this GPU acceleration results in significant speedups over traditional CPU implementations. EvoX’s architecture enables a more efficient approach to handling high-dimensional, complex optimization tasks. By this process, it makes it a robust backbone for MetaDE’s nested evolutionary search and adaptation processes. It can be concluded that a MetaEA uses one EA to optimize the hyperparameters of another EA.

The key limitation is that MetaEAs remain computationally expensive. This is mainly due to a lack of GPU acceleration. To overcome this, the paper employs EvoX. This is a distributed, GPU-based framework that enables large-scale evolutionary computation through functional programming, vectorization, and hierarchical state management. MetaDE is applied entirely on EvoX to exploit massive parallelism.

MetaDE

MetaDE functions as a two-level evolutionary optimization framework. In this process, one DE algorithm develops the parameters and strategies of another DE. The complete process can be segregated into two significant steps as follows. Firstly, it consists of Evolver a meta-level DE which optimizes the hyperparameters of DE24. The second part is an executor, a base-level DE that performs the actual problem optimization using these hyperparameters.

Step-1: Parameter representation:

For each executor, e.g., Parameterized DE, the parameter vector is fully defined, comprised of θ = [F, CR, bl, br, dn, Cs]. The F denotes the scaling factor controlling the mutation step size and bounds with [0,1]. The CR denotes the crossover probability controlling information mixing and bounds with [0,1]. The bl and br denote base vectors (left / right), and the set of {rand, best, pbest, current} is usually decided categorically. The dn denotes the number of difference vectors (1–4) and is generally an integer type. The Cs denotes the crossover scheme and set of {bin, exp, arithmetic} type, and is decided categorically.

Step-3: Meta-level learning.

Each evolver individual is allowed to encode one θi. That’s why it is a self-referential adaptation. It is due to the fact that this DE evolves its own control factors.

In MetaDE, the one-shot evaluation ensures that each Parameterized Differential Evolution (PDE) instance is executed only once with a fixed random seed. This reduces stochastic variance and ensures consistent comparisons among parameter configurations. The power-up strategy improves exploitation. Meanwhile, GPU parallelism allows all PDE executors to run simultaneously. It significantly boosts computational throughput, making large-scale meta-optimization highly efficient, robust and reliable.

Proposed enhancement in MetaDE (EMEDEA)

Two major modifications are suggested to enhance the MetaDE framework. The first is the concept of Opposition-Based Learning (OBL)33. The second one is the Self-Adaptive Parameter Boundaries34. Both the modifications to the basic MetaDE results improve search efficiency, convergence speed, and the robustness of the evolutionary process. The OBL mechanism is incorporated during the initialization and evolution phases. In this process, for every individual parameter vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x$$\end{document} in the evolver population, its opposite is generated as follows.27\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x^{\prime} = lb + ub - x$$\end{document}

Both \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x^{\prime}$$\end{document} are evaluated. The one with better fitness is retained for the next generation or iteration. This dual evaluation strategy safeguards a better exploration of the search space in both directions. This leads to the search process beginning and proceeds with a more diverse and symmetrically distributed population in both exploration and exploitation searching stages. It effectively covers both sides of the search space, reducing the likelihood of premature convergence and getting trapped in local minima.

The modification suggested is that, simultaneously, self-adaptive control factor boundaries dynamically regulate the permissible ranges of the DE control factors. Primarily, the most sensitive and dominant factors in the formulation are the mutation factor (F) and crossover rate (CR). It is done adaptively based on the historical performance of the population. When the algorithm detects an improvement in the region of a particular control factor, the bounds are contracted around that region to exploit it more intensively in both stages of searching. Conversely, when stagnation occurs, the bounds are expanded to encourage exploration in previously unexplored areas and improve the searching strategy. By integrating OBL and adaptive boundary adjustment into MetaDE, the algorithm attains a balanced interplay between global exploration and local exploitation. It is found that it results in faster convergence, improved solution quality, and enhanced stability across diverse optimization landscapes for optimal searching.

Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F^{(g)}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$CR^{(g)}$$\end{document} be the control factors. They are bound according to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F^{(g)} \in \left[ {F_{\min }^{(g)} ,F_{\max }^{(g)} } \right]$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$CR^{(g)} \in \left[ {CR_{\min }^{(g)} ,CR_{\max }^{(g)} } \right]$$\end{document} respectively. The idea implemented here is to adaptively shrink or expand these bounds according to historical improvement information. It is derived from the best fitness values or based on the success history of parameter usage.

Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_{best}^{(g)}$$\end{document} denote the best fitness at generation g. The fitness improvement is as follows.28\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta f^{(g)} = f_{best}^{(g - 1)} - f_{best}^{(g)}$$\end{document}

A normalized improvement rate can be expressed as follows.29\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r^{(g)} = \frac{{\Delta f^{(g)} }}{{\left| {f_{best}^{(g - 1)} } \right| + \in }}$$\end{document}where, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\in$$\end{document} denotes a small constant to avoid division by zero. This value reflects the amount of progress that has been made.

The parameter bounds for the next generation are updated as follows.30\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{\min }^{(g + 1)} = F_{\min }^{(g)} + \beta_{F} \left( {r^{(g)} - \overline{r}} \right)$$\end{document}31\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{\max }^{(g + 1)} = F_{\max }^{(g)} - \beta_{F} \left( {r^{(g)} - \overline{r}} \right)$$\end{document}32\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$CR_{\min }^{(g + 1)} = CR_{\min }^{(g)} + \beta_{CR} \left( {r^{(g)} - \overline{r}} \right)$$\end{document}33\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$CR_{\max }^{(g + 1)} = CR_{\max }^{(g)} - \beta_{CR} \left( {r^{(g)} - \overline{r}} \right)$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta_{F}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta_{CR}$$\end{document} denote learning rates controlling how aggressively the bound adapts. r denotes the moving average of recent improvement rates. It acts as a stability reference. The proposed formulation automatically shrinks the bounds when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r^{(g)} > \overline{r}$$\end{document}. That means the algorithm is improving rapidly. Conversely, it expands when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r^{(g)} < \overline{r}$$\end{document}. That means it acts during the stagnation stage. Adopting the updated dynamic bounds, new control parameters for each individual are calculated as follows.34\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{i}^{(g + 1)} = F_{\min }^{(g + 1)} + rand(0,1)\left[ {F_{\max }^{(g + 1)} - F_{\min }^{(g + 1)} } \right]$$\end{document}35\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$CR_{i}^{(g + 1)} = CR_{\min }^{(g + 1)} + rand(0,1)\left[ {CR_{\max }^{(g + 1)} - CR_{\min }^{(g + 1)} } \right]$$\end{document}

This ensures stochastic diversity. It achieves this by keeping the search adaptively focused on historically effective regions of the control factors space. The proposed adaptive formulation results in feedback-driven self-tuning of two sensitive factors, F and CR. When historical improvement is high, the algorithm automatically intensifies exploitation by narrowing the parameter boundary window. Conversely, when progress stalls, it ensures the enhancement of exploration by widening the permissible range. On the other hand, the proposed approach maintains an evolving balance between global and local search behaviour. This yields a faster convergence and higher robustness.

Critical findings and future scope of research

Despite the better performance of the proposed EMEDEA in accurately estimating the parameters of the tested PEMFC stacks as compared to other previously published techniques, still several research directions remain open for further exploration.

Dynamic and online estimation: The present study focused primarily on steady-state parameter estimation. However, the dynamic and online estimation of time-varying parameters remains a critical challenge for real-time operation. Online learning and self-updating during transient conditions are required, which would significantly enhance their applicability in energy management and fault-diagnostic frameworks.

Multi-objective and uncertainty-aware formulations: Multi-objective and uncertainty-aware formulations need to be focused on simultaneously. There is a need to improve robustness against noisy experimental data and unmodeled dynamics.

Hybrid data-driven and physics-based approaches: Hybrid data-driven and physics-based approaches have been successfully applied to many engineering problems and can be further investigated for PEMFC parameter extension. This could reduce computational complexity and enable faster convergence. It also preserves physical interpretability, and further support transfer learning across different PEMFC stack configurations.

Thermal, water, and degradation sub-models: The inclusion of thermal, water, and degradation sub-models within the optimization framework enables the solution to be more realistic and facilitates parameter estimation under coupled electro-thermo-chemical dynamics. Focusing on this direction could enhance long-term performance prediction and durability assessment.

Hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) and GPU-accelerated implementations: Future research should investigate the use of hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) and GPU-accelerated implementations of EAs. This will enable real-time control and monitoring of PEMFC systems in electric vehicles and microgrid environments.

Selection of the PEMFC model: The underlying mathematical model has a deep impact on the parameter estimation process. It is essential to decide, based on the application, whether to use an empirical, semi-empirical, data-driven, or mechanistic model. The selection must be made with some sort of justification to achieve an accurate result from it.

Impact of operating conditions: The values of empirical parameters are not fixed. They can change significantly with varying operating conditions and constraints, such as temperature, pressure, and humidity. Therefore, the experimental data range, coupling effects, degradation effects, and study on sensitivities need to be focused on during the study for better feasibility of the results obtained in real-time conditions.

Quality of experimental data: High-quality, reliable experimental data play a vital role in accurate parameter estimation. In this regard, sensor accuracy and placement, dynamic behavior, and statistical validation are vital for recovering accurate data for modeling.

Uncertainty and sensitivity analysis: Addressing the uncertainty of estimated parameters is a critical aspect of PEMFC model development for optimal parameter estimations. Sensitivity analysis, including quantitative analysis, ranking parameters, and coupled effects, is critical for accurate identification even under several uncertainties.

Conclusion

The research presented in this paper focuses on the inconsistencies and shortcomings of previous works/techniques conducted by various researchers, aiming to offer a concise and compelling optimization model for estimating the parameters of PEMFC using EMEDEA. A meta-heuristic algorithm, EMEDEA is proposed and found to be efficient in comparison and scalable for application to larger systems, higher complexity and non-linearities. The proposed approach successfully addressed the significant challenges of PEMFC parameter identification, including strong nonlinearity, multidimensionality, and nonconvex optimization characteristics. This has been achieved by integrating adaptive control of prominent DE hyperparameters(control factors), including the mutation factor, crossover probability, and strategy selection. The adaptive mechanism in EMEDEA dynamically adjusted these parameters during the optimization process in both exploration and exploitation searching stages. This has led to an enhancement in convergence speed, exploration–exploitation balance, and overall global search capability. The objective function is formulated based on the SSD for the estimating the unknown parameters of PEMFC stacks. The convergence graphs indicate the faster attainment of optimal parameters by the proposed EMEDEA and v-i characteristics curves show the accuracy of EMEDEA in following the experimental v-i data as close as possible.

Some limitations of the current study can be identified, including the complexity of constraint handling, the hybrid approach used to improve the algorithm, and the consideration of additional limitations by examining the bounds of variables. The current research can be further enhanced by considering the present study as the groundwork. By collecting more data on the operational conditions of PEMFCs, the proposed approach and model can be further examined, considering more complex factors in the estimation model, such as degradation and lifecycle effects, and improving the searching capability and reducing the workload of metaheuristic algorithms in handling constraints. Furthermore, this research can be extended by focusing on its real-time implementation for online parameter estimation and intelligent control applications in fuel cell-based energy systems.
