Deep learning-based Breast Imaging Reporting and Data System classification and establishment of diagnostic model in breast cancer diagnosis with automated breast ultrasound
Authors: Xinyi Zhou, Mingwang Xu, Gongning Luo, Xitian Liang, Fan Yang, Bo Li, Shanshan Sun, Xiaoshuan Liang, Wenjuan Peng, Zhao Liu, Yue Li, Wen Cheng, Qiucheng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDeep learning (DL) holds substantial promise for ultrasonography interpretation. However, there is limited research on the differential diagnosis of tumors in automated breast ultrasound (ABUS). This study constructs a breast cancer diagnosis model based on DL-adjusted Breast Imaging Reporting and Data System (D-BI-RADS) and determines its diagnostic performance.MethodsThis retrospective study evaluated 786 patients with 832 breast nodules who underwent ABUS examination, follow-up or surgery from November 2018 to January 2022. The three-dimensional (3D) Vision Transformer (ViT) DL diagnostic models (DMs) were established and ABUS BI-RADS was adjusted to D-BI-RADS. With univariable and multivariable logistic regression, incorporating ABUS features and D-BI-RADS, three breast cancer DMs were constructed. Area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was compared between DL, ABUS BI-RADS, and D-BI-RADS. In addition, the three DMs were verified in the validation set and test set.ResultsThe training, validation, and test data sets included 599 [mean age, 55±1.41 years; 351 (58.5%) malignant], 67 [mean age, 47.5±0.71 years; 40 (59.7%) malignant], and 166 [mean age, 61.5±4.95 years; 96 (57.8%) malignant] nodules. In the training data set, the D–BI-RADS showed improved performance in breast cancer diagnosis [AUC, 0.82; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.79–0.85; P<0.01] compared with A-BI-RADS. With BI-RADS category 4b (3.5) as the cut-off value for benign and malignant diagnosis, the D-BI-RADS showed improved performance (AUC, 0.919; 95% CI: 0.896–0.941). In addition, Logistic, Random Forest, and Support Vector Machine (SVM) methods achieved ideal performance in the test set (AUC, 0.93; 95% CI: 0.89–0.97; AUC, 0.934; 95% CI: 0.897–0.972; AUC, 0.938; 95% CI: 0.901–0.974; respectively).ConclusionsThe DL can be a useful adjunct guide to the adjustment of BI-RADS, and the established DMs achieved ideal diagnostic performance in breast cancer.

Introduction

Breast cancer has the highest incidence and mortality rate of malignant tumors in women (1). Early diagnosis and accurate assessment of lesions are the goals of various imaging modalities (2-4). Conventional ultrasound (CUS) is an essential tool for breast nodule detection and diagnosis in Asian women with higher breast density (5).

As a new ultrasonography technology, automated breast ultrasound (ABUS), with a three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction function, has been proven to overcome the shortcomings of CUS and plays a pivotal role in the screening and staging of breast cancer (6-9). The unique coronal reconstruction has been shown to improve the detection rate of breast cancer (10). Breast Imaging Reporting and Data System (BI-RADS®) (11,12) is used to standardize diagnostic characterization of breast lesions in ultrasound and help to determine benignity or malignancy. However, ABUS with at least 3 volume images and up to 3,000 two-dimensional (2D) images per patient may lead to diagnostic mistakes.

In recent studies, artificial intelligence (AI) has been expected to assist in image interpretation (13-17). Deep learning (DL), as a second reviewer, has been widely used in assistance of image diagnosis and prediction with lots of advantages (18,19). The transformer architecture has exhibited exceptional performance and scalability in natural language processing, establishing itself as a foundational model across various tasks. Recognizing these capabilities, researchers in computer vision have adapted transformers, notably through the development of Vision Transformer (ViT) models, to achieve significant advancements in vision-related tasks. It was reported that ViT would outperform convolutional neural networks (CNNs), achieve better results and save training computing resources when there were enough data for pre-training (20). Therefore, we hypothesized that ViT might also facilitate the interpretation of ABUS 3D images.

Current research focuses on enhancing diagnostic accuracy through DL by combining lesion characteristics with key technical approaches, including (I) transfer learning with pre-trained architectures (3), (II) interpretable AI frameworks that provide decision transparency (21), and (III) automated machine learning (22). Although these works promote technical development, they still lack a final, clinically grounded assessment in the form of a standardized BI-RADS category. In contrast, this study develops an integrated diagnostic model aimed at the intelligent calibration of preliminary BI-RADS assessments based on ABUS. Specifically, our model leverages a deep integration of a 3DViT and BI-RADS standards to generate a refined BI-RADS category. This approach is designed to significantly boost diagnostic accuracy, particularly at crucial clinical cut-off points.

The primary objective of our study was to establish a DL diagnosis model and compare the diagnostic performance of ABUS-based BI-RADS (hereafter, referred to as A-BI-RADS) and DL-adjusted BI-RADS (hereafter, referred to as D-BI-RADS). The secondary objective was to establish a comprehensive diagnostic model of breast cancer and identify its diagnostic performance. Consequently, this research provides a clinical tool that improves diagnostic accuracy and delivers a more reliable, effective risk category based on the BI-RADS standard, thereby reducing the risks of overtreatment and undertreatment to optimize clinical management. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD+AI reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2025-1-2707/rc).

Methods

Patients

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Harbin Medical University Cancer Hospital (No. KY2023-14). Due to the retrospective nature of the study, written informed consent from participants was waived. Patients with breast nodules between November 2018 and January 2022 in Harbin Medical University Cancer Hospital were retrospectively reviewed in this study. All patients were routinely screened by ABUS and/or CUS, unless there was significant skin rupture or tumor protrusion that affected image quality, CUS only. All the patients underwent more than a 2-year follow-up, or eventually underwent biopsy or surgical treatment. Complete pathological information was acquired after biopsy or surgery. Clinical information of patients and the ABUS features of the breast lesions were recorded. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (I) ABUS images with breast lesions; (II) lesions considered benign without surgery or biopsy were followed up in our hospital for more than 2 years; (III) lesions did not undergo biopsy or any treatment prior to the ABUS examination; (IV) images were clear for marking; and (V) complete data and clinical information. The exclusion criteria for the patients were as follows: (I) patients with pathological results neither benign nor malignant; (II) patients with no biopsy, surgery or no follow-up within 2 years; (III) without clear pathological confirmation; (IV) ABUS features of the breast lesions cannot be clearly seen when being affected by the shadow behind the nipple or with low-quality image; and (V) incomplete data and clinical information.

ABUS examination

The Invenia™ Automated Breast Ultrasound System (GE Healthcare, Sunnyvale, CA, USA) with an automated 6–14 MHz linear broadband transducer (covering volumes of 15.4×17.0×5.0 cm) was used in the study. The patients were placed similarly to those with CUS. Thus, anteroposterior, medial, and lateral orientation items of volume data were obtained, and, if required, superior and inferior orientations were performed additionally. After acquisition, all the images were sent to the workstation for review.

Datasets

A total of 832 breast lesions with 832 3D images from 786 patients (range, 20–81 years) were enrolled in this study. In these data, 566 of the 832 breast lesions have been previously reported (18). This prior article dealt with the development of a DL network (classification and automatic segmentation network) in the assistance of binary diagnosis of breast lesions, whereas in this study, we report the diagnostic performance of DL-adjusted BI-RADS classification and an established diagnostic model based on clinical data, image data and DL based on larger data. Clinical data and information for all patients were obtained from the electronic medical record. Seg3D2 software (Seg3D 2-2.4.4), a free volume segmentation and processing tool, was used by three other radiologists (not the three diagnostic radiologists) with more than 2 years of work experience to determine the boundary and shape of the lesions and to manually mark all of the lesions. The manually marked breast lesion volumes were used to help in network learning. The process of network learning is to simulate a doctor’s manual marking and to help in learning to extract the right shape information.

The training set and test set were selected by a random sampling method at 666 vs. 166. The training set was further randomly divided into a new training set and a validation set for model establishment at a ratio of 599:67. The performance of DL, A-BI-RADS, and D-BI-RADS was tested by the training set. The performance of breast cancer diagnostic models (DMs) was tested by the test set. Development of the DL

The work was performed on a server with dual Intel Xeon Silver 4114 central processing units (CPUs) (Intel Corp., Santa Clara, CA, USA), eight NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 graphics processing units (GPUs) (NVIDIA Corp., Santa Clara, CA, USA), and 256 GB of memory. The models were implemented in Python 3.10 using the PyTorch framework, and all computations were run on the Ubuntu 18.04 operating system with Compute Unified Device Architecture (CUDA) 11.4 support. We enhanced the original ViT model, which was designed for 2D image analysis, by modifying its input to accommodate 3D graphics. The structural diagram of the 3DViT network is presented in Figure 2.

Structure diagram of 3DViT network model for processing ABUS 3D images. 3DViT, three-dimensional vision transformer; ABUS, automated breast ultrasound; MLP, multilayer perceptron.

Initially, all labelled lesions were divided into training, validation, and test sets, with the original data dimensions being 865×608×330. We then segmented the data into appropriate sizes based on lesion dimensions and standardized them for network input. The network subdivides the image into numerous small blocks, treating each as a vector (referred to as Flattened Patches). A linear projection layer, acting as an embedding layer, is applied to embed each image segment into a corresponding vector. A Class Token is prefixed to this sequence of vectors, with each vector also receiving positional information. These vectors are then fed into the transformer encoder, and the output vector is derived by averaging all output token vectors. This output is subsequently processed by the multilayer perceptron (MLP) head module, a structural layer dedicated to classification tasks. After training, the model autonomously extracts shape information and other features of 3D image lesions to classify lesions as malignant or benign. The 3DViT network is trained on the training set, with classification performance iteratively verified and adjusted based on the validation set. Training is concluded once optimal classification performance is achieved. Finally, the model’s performance is evaluated on the test set, demonstrating its efficacy in 3D image analysis.

Then, we verified the performance of the 3DViT network on the training set, verification set and test set.

Image analysis

Three radiologists with 6 years, 5 years, and 1 year of work experience in ABUS were involved in the image interpretation. Only the primary ABUS images’ data, age, corresponding case number, and date of examination were visible to the radiologists to ensure the objectivity of the diagnosis. BI-RADS categorization is based on the same features as CUS, simultaneously adding 3D analysis by generating a coronal view. CUS features including shape (oval, round or irregular), margin (circumscribed, indistinct, microlobulated, angular or spiculated), orientation (parallel or nonparallel), echo pattern (hypoechoic, hyperechoic, isoechoic, anechoic, complex cystic and solid, or heterogeneous), posterior echogenicity (no posterior features, enhancement, shadowing, or combined pattern), calcification (presence or absence), and associated features in the BI-RADS lexicon. Architectural distortion, hyperechoic halo (presence or absence), spiculation (presence or absence) and retraction phenomenon (presence or absence) in the coronal view were added, the presence of which may be suggestive of malignancy (23,24). According to the American College of Radiology (ACR) BI-RADS, category 4a was generally defined as the boundary between benign and malignant diagnosis (6,11,25).

In the study, we upgraded A-BI-RADS category 3, 4a, or 4b to D-BI-RADS categories 4a, 4b, or 4c according to DL. Similarly, we reduced A‑BI‑RADS categories 4c, 4b, and 4a to D‑BI‑RADS categories 4a or 3. In general, according to the management decision of radiologists, BI-RADS categories 1, 2, and 3 are defined as negative for cancer and BI-RADS categories 4a, 4b, 4c, and 5 are defined as positive for cancer.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 27.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used for statistical analysis. Continuous variables are presented as means and standard deviations (SDs); categorical variables are presented as numbers and percentages. Differences between groups were analyzed using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. The accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) results of the six models were also calculated. Average precision (AP) represents the accuracy of the model for the positive samples. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were drawn. By calling the function in Scikit-Learn, the AP, precision-recall curves (PRC), and areas under the ROC curve (AUCs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. When treated as ordinal variables, the performance of the BI-RADS category in breast cancer diagnosis was also observed. To select the most important US features, a least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO)-penalized logistic regression model was built. The optimal cutoff values for breast cancer were identified using the highest Youden index. DeLong tests were applied for the comparison of AUC values between A-BI-RADS and D-BI-RADS. A heat map was drawn to show the correlation coefficients between important variables and pathology. P<0.05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant difference. Logistic, Random Forest, and Support Vector Machines (SVM) were used to evaluate the diagnostic performance.

Results

Case inclusion and grouping

Among the 832 breast lesions, there were 487 (58.53%) malignant lesions and 345 (41.47%) benign lesions. The maximum diameter (MD) was 0.3–7.6 cm. The data were further divided into a training set, a validation set and a test set. The training set included 599 cases with 351 malignant lesions and 248 benign lesions. The validation set included 67 cases with 40 malignant lesions and 27 benign lesions. The test set included 166 cases, in which malignant and benign lesions were 96 vs. 70. Discussion

Lack of experience in some radiologists or atypical manifestations of breast lesions may lead to diagnostic mistakes. Lots of benign lesions were always diagnosed as BI-RADS category 4a or even 4b, which led to more unnecessary core needle biopsies or mental burden for patients. Unfortunately, a great deal of category 4a nodules need to be recognized and explained to patients, which brought great challenges and burden to post-observation radiologists (26). Therefore, we built a DL network and explored the performance on 3D images. AP represents the accuracy of the model for the positive sample. The larger the AP is, the higher the comprehensive accuracy and recall result of the model for malignant lesions. Ultrasonographic images had a lot of noise interference. 3D images were more complex, which made it difficult for the DL network to fully learn with limited data. Therefore, according to the APs of DL, the performance was not ideal (0.52–0.55 in the test set) in this study.

However, D-BI-RADS classification got ideal diagnostic performance. The accuracy (84.3%) and AUC (0.82) of D-BI-RADS were higher than those of DL and A-BI-RADS. Higher specificity (65.8%) and PPV (79.2%) might help radiologists avoid unnecessary recalls of benign lesions. The result was similar to the previous study, in which radiologists achieved higher AUCs (0.801 and 0.800) with the assistance of DL (27). DL was a useful adjunct tool to adjust the BI-RADS classification to help make a precise diagnosis.

In addition, we analyzed those breast lesions with diagnostic differences between DL and A-BI-RADS. We found that breast density was positively correlated with diagnostic difference (P<0.05), and MD, indistinct margin and posterior features were negatively correlated with diagnostic difference (P<0.05). As breast density increased, the diagnostic differences became more pronounced. The denser the breast glands, the more noise interferes with the display of some breast lesions, which may lead to errors in the determination of DL and BI-RADS. The larger the diameter of breast nodules, the clearer the distinction of various information about the tumor, and the less the tumor affected by surrounding tissues, the easier it is for machine recognition. An indistinct margin was still one of the most important features in the diagnosis of breast cancer, and only 15.7% of breast cancers had a well-defined margin (28). Therefore, distinct margin was negatively correlated with diagnostic difference. Many benign lesions with posterior shadow patterns are easily misdiagnosed as malignant, which may lead to diagnostic differences. Lesions with posterior echo enhancement were mostly benign; therefore, the posterior echo enhancement caused the lowest diagnostic difference rate.

Moreover, we also found that when BI-RADS 4b was used as the cut-off value for benign and malignant diagnosis, the performance of A-BI-RADS and D-BI-RADS was significantly improved (AUC 0.869 and 0.919, P<0.01). D-BI-RADS4b got the highest accuracy (92.2%), specificity (89.9%) and PPV (92.5%). Based on these results, we wondered if we could further analyze category 4a nodules with the assistance of DL in the future to regrade those lesions to category 3 or 4b. Category 3 nodules avoid unnecessary needle biopsy, and category 4b nodules recommend necessary needle biopsy, mightily.

According to LASSO analysis, we established three diagnostic models and further explored the diagnostic effect of them. The results showed that in the verification set and the test set, the three AUC values respectively achieved satisfactory results: 0.966 (0.905–1) and 0.93 (0.89–0.97), 0.981 (0.955–1) and 0.934 (0.897–0.972), 0.967 (0.92–1) and 0.938 (0.901–0.974). It has been proven that D-BI-RADS classification adjusted by DL can improve the early detection and diagnosis of breast cancer from different aspects and is more conducive to clinical diagnosis and benefits patients.

It is important to acknowledge the limitations of this study. Firstly, due to the retrospective analysis and single-center study, our results may be biased. Secondly, in the diagnosis of benign and malignant nodules, it is difficult to define the boundary of non-mass nodules; therefore, some of these lesions are not included. Thirdly, ABUS cannot observe color Doppler information, therefore, in this study we did not investigate the role of color Doppler blood flow patterns in the diagnosis of breast cancer. Finally, in clinical practice, ABUS image quality is susceptible to variations in operator technique, device specifications, and patient characteristics, which may limit the model’s generalization performance.

Future work will focus on multi-center prospective validation and subsequently on expanding the model’s applications to non-mass lesions, integrating blood flow and elastography, and therapy prediction.

Conclusions

DL can be used as an adjunct guide to the adjustment of BI-RADS in this study, which is convenient and useful. Newly established diagnostic models showed ideal diagnostic performance in differential diagnosis of benign and malignant breast lesions.
