Ultrasonically activated peroxymonosulfate oxidation for advanced water treatment: Cavitation mechanism, free radical generation and process simulation
Authors: Lucheng Zhang, Mingqing Zhang, Gang Kang, Zihui Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe persistent presence of recalcitrant organic pollutants in aquatic environments highlights the urgent need for efficient and sustainable water treatment technologies. This study investigated the application of the ultrasonically activated peroxymonosulfate system for organic pollutant degradation, with a focus on elucidating its cavitation mechanism, radical generation, and multiphysics characteristics. Experimental results demonstrated that the US-PMS system is pH-tolerant, achieving 92.4% removal of the model pollutant (methylene blue) within 60 min under optimal reaction conditions, with a reaction stoichiometric efficiency of 41.7%. Radical quenching experiments and EPR confirmed that ·OH and SO4·- are the dominant radical species in the reaction. Based on the Keller-Miksis single-bubble model, the physical property changes during cavitation bubble collapse and their influence on radical activation were quantitatively analyzed, revealing the intrinsic link between ultrasonic cavitation and radical generation. Furthermore, COMSOL Multiphysics simulations identified a “bottom activation-top mixing” functional zone within the reactor. This zoning enhances mass transfer and PMS activation efficiency through the synergistic coupling of acoustic, flow, and thermal fields. These findings provide novel insights and theoretical foundations for optimising the US-PMS system and facilitating its scale-up application in industrial wastewater treatment.Keywords: Sonochemistry, Peroxynitrite, Water treatment, Cavitation bubble, Physical field changes

Introduction

Accelerating global industrialisation and urbanisation have resulted in the continuous discharge of significant quantities of organic pollutants into aquatic environments[1]. Owing to the limited efficiency of conventional water treatment processes in removing refractory organic compounds, these contaminants are frequently detected and accumulate in water bodies downstream of industrial zones and municipal wastewater treatment plants[2]. The severity of this issue is exemplified by the Mekong River Delta Basin, where studies report chemical oxygen demand (COD) concentrations reaching 472.98 mg/L, and persistent organic pollutants (POPs), including aldrin, dieldrin, and DDT, consistently exceeding 1 mg/L[3]. These organic pollutants exhibit significant environmental persistence, bioaccumulation potential, and multiple toxicological effects, posing serious threats to ecosystem integrity and human health even at trace concentrations[4]. Epidemiological and toxicological evidence indicate that chronic exposure to water contaminated with organic pollutants can lead to endocrine disruption, reproductive dysfunction, elevated cancer risks, and pulmonary, digestive, and dermatological disorders. Furthermore, they can adversely affect hematopoietic and immune functions and induce neurotoxicity and developmental toxicity[5,6]. Given the increasing urgency to mitigate these risks, the development of efficient and sustainable technologies for the removal of organic pollutants from water has become a critical focus in the fields of environmental engineering and science. Among various water treatment technologies, advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) have emerged as a promising solution to organic pollution challenges due to their high degradation efficiency and broad applicability. AOPs operate by generating highly reactive radicals that enable the rapid oxidation and often complete mineralization of a wide range of organic pollutants[7].

Ultrasound (US) technology has emerged as an efficient AOP for the treatment of organic pollutants in water, demonstrating outstanding performance. This technology synergistically combines radical oxidation, pyrolytic decomposition, and supercritical water oxidation mechanisms. It offers benefits such as rapid reaction kinetics, operational simplicity, compact design, and environmental friendliness, which have collectively propelled it to the forefront of recent research[8]. Acoustic cavitation, as the core mechanism of US oxidation, drives the degradation of organic compounds. When US waves propagate through a liquid medium, alternating positive and negative pressure cycles cause compression and expansion of the liquid. During the negative pressure phase, the average distance between molecules exceeds the critical distance required to maintain liquid integrity, allowing pre-existing structural defects (cavitation nuclei) to expand and form cavitation bubbles[9]. In the subsequent positive phase, the internal pressure of the bubbles is lower than the external environmental pressure, leading to rapid compression of the bubbles. Within 1 μs, the bubbles collapse dramatically, with their radius shrinking to less than 1 μm and a volume compression ratio of 105–106. During this process, the violent collapse of the bubbles generates extreme local conditions, including pressures of up to 1000 atm, vapor temperatures ranging from 4000 to 10,000 K, and bubble interface temperatures between 1000 and 1500 K[10]. At the moment of bubble collapse, the surrounding water molecules reach a supercritical state, significantly enhancing the mass transfer efficiency and reaction kinetics of organic pollutants due to their superior diffusivity and low dielectric constant[11]. When the cavitation effect acts on water molecules, a series of oxidative radicals (Eqs. (1), (2), (3), (4), (5)) is generated, promoting the oxidation and degradation of organic pollutants[12]. When it acts on organic pollutants, it induces the opening and breaking of chemical bonds, triggering pyrolysis reactions. Low-frequency US is widely used in industrial applications due to their cost-effectiveness, but its ability to generate ·OH is relatively weak. As a result, the standalone application of US for the treatment of organic pollutants in water often faces challenges such as low degradation efficiency and high operational costs. To overcome these limitations, the combination of US with oxidants has been developed as a more efficient water treatment method.

Peroxymonosulfate (PMS) is among the most extensively investigated oxidants in advanced oxidation processes due to its favorable properties. Although PMS exhibits inherent oxidative capacity in aqueous solution (Eqs. (6), (7)), its direct reaction kinetics with pollutants are considerably slower than radical-mediated pathways, limiting its efficacy in standalone applications[13]. However, when combined with US, the extreme conditions and physical field changes induced by cavitation act on the O-O bond in PMS, causing its cleavage and generating a series of highly reactive species with elevated redox potentials and longer half-lives, including sulfate radicals (SO4·−, E0 = 2.5–3.1 V), hydroxyl radicals (·OH, E0 = 1.8–2.7 V), and superoxide radicals (O2·−, E0 = -0.16 V) (Eqs. (8), (9), (10), (11), (12))[14]. This synergistic system has been proven to be an efficient and environmentally friendly water treatment technology, successfully applied to degrade various organic pollutants such as sulfamethazine[15], atrazine[16], and tetracycline[17].

However, current research on the US-PMS system predominantly focuses on pollutant degradation efficiency and reaction condition optimisation, often employing kinetic modelling, density functional theory (DFT) calculations, and radical identification to elucidate reaction pathways[18]. A critical limitation is that most studies treat ultrasound as a ‘black-box’ activation tool, largely overlooking two fundamental issues: (1) the dynamic coupling between PMS activation efficiency and cavitation, and (2) the contribution of extreme ultrasound-generated conditions (transient high temperature/pressure, microjets, acoustic streaming) to radical generation and mass transfer[15,19]. To address these research gaps, this study systematically investigates the enhancement mechanisms and multi-physical field synergy of US in PMS activation using methylene blue (MB) as a model organic pollutant. The unique contributions of this work are as follows: (1) By integrating reaction stoichiometric efficiency (%RSE) with single-factor experiments, we evaluate the practical degradation performance of the US-PMS system from the perspectives of energy utilization and oxidant activation efficiency for the first time, overcoming the limitation of conventional kinetic models that only reflect apparent reaction rates; (2) Based on the single-bubble dynamics model (Keller–Miksis equation), we quantitatively analyze the transient temperature, pressure, and radical yield during cavitation collapse, establishing a direct correlation between cavitation physical parameters and radical chemistry; (3) Using COMSOL Multiphysics-based multi-physics coupling simulations, we accurately reconstruct the spatiotemporal distribution of the acoustic, flow, and thermal fields within the reactor, unveiling the synergistic enhancement mechanism of bubble cluster behavior on mixing, mass transfer, and energy distribution in the reaction system. This study not only deepens the multi-scale understanding of the oxidation mechanism in the US-PMS system, breaking through the conventional single focus on “chemical pathways”, but also provides theoretical foundations and simulation support for the design of efficient and controllable sonoreactors, offering substantial guidance for engineering applications.

Result and discussion

PMS activation under different systems

The cavitation activation by US and direct oxidation by MB exhibit distinct contribution mechanisms to PMS consumption. This study systematically investigated the PMS consumption behaviour in four systems (PMS, PMS + MB, US + PMS, and US + PMS + MB) (Fig. 2), aiming to quantitatively analyse the synergistic/competitive effects between US physical fields and pollutant chemical oxidation on PMS activation pathways. Experimental results demonstrated that in the control system without US and MB, PMS exhibited excellent stability with only 5.9 % self-decomposition within 60 min, highlighting its advantage for industrial transportation and application. When MB was present, the PMS decomposition rate increased to 9.8 %, confirming that organic pollutants could slowly consume PMS through direct oxidation pathways. Notably, US introduction significantly altered the activation kinetics of PMS. During the initial reaction stage (0–5 min), the US + PMS system showed higher PMS consumption (0.40 mM) compared to the US + PMS + MB system (0.36 mM), attributed to the preferential self-quenching reaction between excess ·OH and SO4·- (generated by US cavitation) and PMS in the absence of pollutants, leading to non-productive PMS consumption. As the reaction progressed to 60 min, the US + PMS + MB system exhibited an additional 0.12 mM PMS consumption, resulting from MB acting as an efficient radical scavenger that continuously competed for radicals and directed PMS activation toward effective pollutant degradation pathways, while the self-quenching reaction in the US + PMS system gradually slowed down due to decreasing PMS concentration. These findings quantitatively elucidated the contribution mechanisms of different systems to PMS consumption, demonstrating the crucial influence of dynamic regulation through US-chemical synergy on improving pollutant degradation efficiency, which provides important guidance for the design of practical wastewater treatment processes.

Effect of experimental conditions

US power

US power intensity directly determines the acoustic energy input, which is a primary driver for activating PMS. Fig. 3(a) shows the degradation effect of MB under different US powers. When the US power is increased from 0 W to 60 W, the removal efficiency of MB increases rapidly from 30.4 % to 67.9 %, indicating that the input of US energy has broken through the cavitation threshold. The local transient extreme conditions generated by the collapse of cavitation bubbles can synergize effectively with PMS by breaking the O-O bonds of PMS, generating a large amount of ROS in the solution, thereby oxidatively degrading organic pollutants. As the US power further increases, the removal efficiency of MB also gradually improves (60 W vs 240 W = 67.9 % vs 92.4 %). Higher US power means stronger US energy input, which can more effectively activate PMS in the solution environment. In addition, higher US power generates more intense turbulence, thereby enhancing the mass transfer effect in the solution[21]. It is particularly worth noting that when the power exceeds 300 W, the degradation kinetics tend to stabilize (94.6 %), which may be due to the following reasons: (1) the concentration of organic pollutants in the solution environment decreases significantly, and continuous degradation faces the challenge of concentration effect, and the length of free radical diffusion path becomes a new rate-limiting step; (2) a large number of tiny bubbles caused by the sharp increase in power intensity will coalesce to form larger bubbles, resulting in a “shielding effect”, reducing the occurrence of cavitation and the transmission of energy during the collapse of cavitation bubbles, thereby reducing the activation efficiency of PMS[22]. Despite this, the %RSE remained relatively stable across all power levels. Increasing US power can enhance cavitation, activate more PMS molecules per unit time, and lead to an increase in ROS production; at the same time, the turbulence effect caused by high-intensity US significantly improves the mass transfer conditions of the gas–liquid interface, allowing the generated ROS to rapidly diffuse to the reaction phase and immediately react with organic pollutants. This dynamic balance mechanism of the two synergistic effects allows the absolute production of free radicals to increase, but they are not significantly quenched before being consumed, maintaining a high and stable %RSE across power levels.

Temperature

The reaction temperature significantly influences the solution’s viscosity and mass transfer coefficient. At lower temperatures, the solution exhibits higher viscosity and lower vapor pressure, conditions that can enhance cavitation generation within the reactor, thereby promoting organic pollutant degradation and PMS activation[23]. In addition, as the temperature rises, the concentration of dissolved oxygen in the solution decreases, which in turn reduces the concentration of ·OH[24]. Interestingly, the experimental results show that as the reaction temperature continues to rise (as shown in Fig. 3(b)), the degradation efficiency of MB shows a steady improvement, increasing from 80.9 % to 99.4 % as the temperature increases from 20 °C to 60 °C. This phenomenon can be explained from the perspective of molecular thermodynamics and fluid dynamics: (1) the increase in temperature leads to a decrease in the surface tension of the solution, reducing the energy barrier for the formation of cavitation nuclei and increasing the number density of cavitation bubbles[25]; (2) the increase in temperature significantly enhances molecular thermal motion, increasing the effective collision probability between pollutants and radicals; (3) the synergistic effect of thermal activation and US cavitation reduces the activation energy of PMS, increasing the generation rate of SO4·-. Based on the experimental data, it can be found that the increase in temperature leads to a significant decrease in %RSE: when the reaction temperature increases from 20 °C to 60 °C, the %RSE value after 60 min drops from 49.9 to 18.9 (a decrease of 62.1 %), indicating that although high temperatures can promote PMS activation, the generated ROS have a shorter lifespan due to the heat, making them more prone to ineffective quenching, which reduces their effective utilization. In addition, high-temperature operation requires additional energy to maintain the system temperature, increasing the overall treatment cost. In contrast, US-activated PMS systems operating at ambient temperature achieve a favorable balance, maintaining high %RSE and effective degradation efficiency without external heating. Therefore, practical implementation requires a comprehensive evaluation of degradation efficiency, %RSE, and energy consumption to identify the most economically viable temperature range.

pH

The degradation efficiency of MB in water by the US-PMS system under different initial pH values is shown in Fig. 3(c). The experimental results indicate that the system exhibits excellent degradation efficiency over a wide pH range of 3–11, which is mainly attributed to the differentiated transformation pathways of reactive species under different pH conditions. In a weakly acidic environment, protonation promotes the conversion kinetics of PMS anions (HSO5-) to SO4·-. Under weakly alkaline conditions, SO4·- reacts with OH– to generate ·OH via electron transfer (Eq. (10)). The higher redox potential and non-selective nature of ·OH enhance the thermodynamic driving force for oxidation[26]. However, at extreme pH values, performance declines. In strongly acidic media, excess H+ acts as a radical scavenger[27] (Eqs. (14), (15)). In strongly alkaline environments, the short lifetime of ·OH and self-quenching reactions among ROS reduce the effective radical concentration[28] (Eq. (16)). Notably, %RSE indicates that the conversion efficiency of oxidants to radicals remains stable across different pH conditions, suggesting that pH primarily affects the radical reaction pathways rather than activation efficiency. This pH resilience eliminates the need for stringent pH adjustment, a major limitation of conventional AOPs, thereby offering considerable economic and operational advantages. Nevertheless, practical application requires careful pH optimization, as the protonation state of pollutants (governed by their pKa) can significantly influence their reactivity and the selectivity of radical attack, particularly in complex wastewaters containing multiple pollutants.

PMS concentration

The initial concentration of PMS in the solution environment is closely correlated with the concentration of ROS generated upon its activation. Consequently, investigating the degradation efficiency of MB at different PMS concentrations is of paramount importance (Fig. 3(d)). As the PMS concentration increased from 1 to 3 mM, the MB degradation efficiency improved from 62.9 % to 92.4 %. Under effective US activation, higher PMS concentrations yield more ROS, increasing the collision frequency between radicals and pollutants and thereby enhancing the degradation process[29]. However, further increasing PMS to 4 and 5 mM resulted in diminishing returns, with marginal improvements in MB degradation. Excessive PMS can lead to an increase in solution viscosity, which significantly reduces the number of cavitation bubbles per unit volume and inhibits the occurrence of cavitation[30]. Additionally, PMS that is not activated promptly consumes SO4·- and ·OH in the system (Eqs. (17), (18)), reducing the effective oxidation capacity of active radicals before they oxidize organic pollutants. Finally, excessive SO4·- can undergo self-reactions in the solution (Eq. (19)), leading to a decrease in its concentration in the US-PMS system, which further hinders the effective degradation of organic pollutants[31]. Under fixed US irradiation conditions, the hydrodynamic characteristics inside the reactor remain constant, and the mass transfer diffusion rate does not change significantly with changes in PMS concentration. When the PMS dosage is increased from 1 mM to 5 mM, although the instantaneous generation rate of free radicals correspondingly increases, due to the mass transfer limitations of the reaction system, the excess ROS cannot contact the target pollutants promptly, leading to a significant increase in the proportion of free radical quenching reactions. This kinetic competition mechanism directly reflects the sharp decline in %RSE. After 60 min of reaction, the 1 mM PMS system still maintains a high efficiency of 68.7 %, while the 5 mM system’s %RSE drops sharply to 30.7 %. This phenomenon reveals that in practical engineering applications, it is necessary to follow the optimal oxidant dosage ratio to achieve the optimal balance between degradation efficiency and oxidant economy.

Effect of water quality conditions

Natural inorganic anions present in aquatic environments can react with ROS generated in the US-PMS system, thereby potentially inhibiting the oxidative degradation efficiency of organic pollutants. This study evaluated the effects of three common anions (Cl-, HCO3–, and H2PO4-) at various concentration on the degradation efficiency of MB, as shown in Fig. 4 (a)–(c). The results indicate that the presence of inorganic anions inhibited the degradation of MB in the US-PMS system to varying degrees, with the inhibitory effect following the order: HCO3–> H2PO4- > Cl-. When the concentrations of Cl-, HCO3–, and H2PO4- were 50 mM, the degradation efficiency of MB decreased to 89.6 %, 69.1 %, and 74.3 %, respectively. This phenomenon can be primarily attributed to the direct reaction of HCO3– and H2PO4- with ·OH and SO4·-, generating free radicals with lower oxidative capacity (Eqs. (20), (21), (22), (23))[32], thereby reducing the system's ability to degrade the target pollutant. Notably, Cl- exhibited the strongest inhibitory effect at a concentration of 10 mM (MB removal efficiency of 84.5 %), rather than at higher concentrations. This can be explained by the reaction of Cl- with ·OH and SO4·- to form chlorine radicals (Eqs. (24), (25), (26)) [33], which still possess certain oxidative capabilities and can participate in the degradation of organic pollutants, thereby partially offsetting the inhibitory effect at higher concentrations.

Removal performance of MB under different water quality conditions: (a) Cl-; (b) HCO3–; (c) H2PO4-; (d) HA. (Experimental conditions: US power = 240 W, T = 30℃, pH = 7, PMS = 3 mM, MB = 150 mg L-1).

In addition to the inhibitory effects of inorganic anions on the degradation of MB, naturally occurring organic matter in aquatic environments can also significantly influence the degradation process. As shown in Fig. 4(d), when the concentration of humic acid (HA) increased from 0 mM to 0.5 mM, the degradation efficiency of MB decreased by 25.0 %. This inhibitory effect is primarily attributed to the competition between HA and the target pollutant MB for ROS generated in the system[34,35]. Although HA partially consumes ROS, the MB molecules possess a lower degradation energy barrier and exhibit higher reactivity toward ·OH and SO4·-, making them more susceptible to oxidative degradation. Consequently, the radicals preferentially react with MB, thereby partially mitigating the competitive inhibition effect caused by HA. It should be noted that these water matrix components (inorganic anions and HA) do not significantly affect the activation efficiency of PMS itself, as confirmed by the consistent PMS decomposition rates observed in their presence (Fig. S3). In conclusion, when applying the US-PMS technology for degrading organic pollutants in practical water treatment, it is essential to comprehensively consider the co-existing influences of inorganic ions and dissolved organic matter in the actual water matrix. Systematic evaluation should be conducted to balance the need for pretreatment, operational costs, and overall degradation efficiency, in order to determine the optimal treatment strategy.

US-PMS system industrial application value

Among reported MB treatment technologies (Table 1), the US-PMS process effectively treats high-concentration MB within short durations, requiring minimal oxidant dosage and no pH adjustment. Furthermore, the absence of external catalysts simplifies the process and avoids hazardous waste disposal issues. As shown in Table 2, the US-PMS system achieves a %RSE of 41.7 %, significantly outperforming catalytic activation systems. Its advantages stem from the following mechanisms: (1) controlled PMS activation kinetics: the absence of exogenous catalysts results in a gentler activation rate of PMS, preventing local oversaturation of ROS in a short time and thereby reducing the ineffective loss caused by ROS self-quenching; (2) US enhancement of mass transfer: US cavitation can significantly enhance fluid mixing within the reaction system, optimize the spatial distribution of temperature and pressure, and thereby increase the effective collision frequency and reaction efficiency of ROS with pollutants. Thus, the %RSE of US-PMS surpasses most catalytic systems and is competitive with UV or thermal activation, highlighting its advantage in energy efficiency. Economic analysis shows that electricity and oxidant consumption constitute the primary operating costs. Taking MB degradation as an example, the comprehensive cost of the system for treating each milligram of pollutant is 0.00312 yuan (of which US energy consumption accounts for 43.2 % ≈ 0.00135 yuan mg−1, and PMS reagent accounts for 56.8 % ≈ 0.00177 yuan mg−1), with operating costs amounting to 4.32 yuan/L. As shown in Table S1, the operational cost of the US-PMS system is marginally higher than that of the heterogeneous catalytic activation system (γ-Fe2O3/CeO2: 3.78 yuan L-1). However, compared to other physical activation approaches, this system demonstrates outstanding cost-effectiveness: compared to ultraviolet activation systems (9.48 yuan L-1), thermal activation systems (5.87 yuan L-1), and electro-activation systems (15.9 yuan L-1), its costs are reduced by 54.4 %, 26.4 %, and 72.8 % respectively. The system has significant advantages over traditional processes in industrial applications: first, it can react efficiently under neutral conditions, eliminating the need for acid-base adjustment steps and related reagent inputs in traditional processes; second, solid PMS has excellent long-term stability (monthly decomposition rate < 5 %), reducing losses during transport and storage; additionally, the system does not require the use of noble metal catalysts and does not produce secondary hazardous solid waste, further reducing equipment maintenance and waste disposal costs. Therefore, the US-PMS system has certain cost-effectiveness and application potential in the field of industrial wastewater treatment.

Oxidation mechanism and degradation pathways of free radicals

To identify the primary ROS in the US-PMS oxidation system and to gain a deeper understanding of its reaction degradation mechanism, various ROS scavengers were introduced in this section to examine changes in the system's efficiency in degrading pollutants[34,36]. The experimental results are shown in Fig. 5(a). After the addition of Methanol and Tert-butanol to the US-PMS system, the degradation rate of MB plummeted from 92.4 % to 28.9 % and 54.1 %, respectively, indicating that ·OH and SO4·- play the most crucial roles in the degradation of organic pollutants in the US-PMS system. The addition of FAA to the system resulted in a 6.6 % reduction in the degradation efficiency of MB. This indicates that 1O2 is generated in relatively small quantities but still contributes to degradation. Additionally, the addition of BQ had a negligible effect on the degradation of MB (<3%), suggesting that ·O2– does not facilitate effective oxidation reactions in the US-PMS system. Notably, even after quenching most of the ROS within the reactor, the US-PMS system still demonstrated a certain ability to degrade organic pollutants (MB removal rate = 15.6 %). This indicates that the shear force and extreme conditions of high temperature and pressure inherent in the US system itself can cause the degradation of organic pollutants, but with low efficiency. Therefore, without the synergistic use of PMS, the degradation efficiency and economic benefits are low, limiting its industrial application value. With the aid of Electron Paramagnetic Resonance (EPR) testing, the ROS in the reaction system can be directly detected, thereby further analyzing the radical oxidation mechanism of the US-PMS system. As shown in Fig. 5(b), EPR accurately detected the adduct formed by the reaction of ·OH with DMPO, indicating the generation of a large amount of ·OH in the US-PMS system. In addition, the sextet peak splitting signal produced by the reaction of SO4·- with DMPO to generate DMPO-SO4 is also clearly visible. Therefore, US can effectively activate PMS to generate SO4·-. During the EPR test, no signals of the combination of ·O2– with DMPO and 1O2 with TEMP were detected, indicating that the non-radical pathway contributes only marginally in this system. This observation is consistent with the results obtained from the ROS quenching experiment. Although this radical-dominated oxidation mechanism has poor selectivity, it possesses broad-spectrum oxidation and deep mineralization capabilities, making it more suitable for treating industrial wastewater with complex composition and high ecological toxicity.

(a) Quenching experiments by Methonal, Tert-butanol, BQ and FFA; (b) EPR spectra. (Experimental conditions: US power = 240 W, T = 30℃, pH = 7, PMS = 3 mM, MB = 150 mg L-1).

The mineralization efficiency of MB by the US-PMS system was evaluated through TOC analysis. The results demonstrated a TOC removal rate of 71.3 % within 60 min of reaction, confirming the system’s significant mineralization capability. However, the residual TOC indicated the presence of short-chain organic intermediates in the system, which may pose potential ecological risks. Through HPLC-MS analysis, a plausible degradation pathway of MB was constructed (as shown in Fig. 6 and S4): ROS primarily attack the chromophore center of the dye molecule, leading to the progressive demethylation of methyl substituents on the amine groups, generating intermediate products with m/z 270, 256, and 228. Alternatively, ring-opening of the phenothiazine structure followed by oxidation of the sulfur atom represents another feasible initial reaction pathway, resulting in the formation of sulfoxide derivatives (m/z 303). Subsequent ring cleavage of the heterocyclic structure generates low-molecular-weight carboxylic acids and small S-/N-containing compounds. Notably, although some intermediates (e.g., sulfoxides) retain toxicity, ring-opening products are significantly less toxic than MB and are ultimately mineralized to harmless inorganic species (CO2, SO42-, and NO3–). These findings indicate that the US-PMS system not only effectively disrupts the structure of organic pollutants but also reduces ecological toxicity through deep oxidation, providing important insights for risk control in practical wastewater treatment.

Cavitation bubble dynamics in aqueous solutions

Physical properties of cavitation bubbles

Fig. 7(a) illustrates the nonlinear coupled dynamics between the driving sound pressure and bubble radius under a 40 kHz sinusoidal US drive. During the rarefaction phase, when the external pressure falls below the liquid's saturated vapor pressure, the vapor pressure inside the cavitation bubble and the pressure from dissolved gases become dominant, driving sustained bubble expansion until the radius reaches its maximum value of 2.4 × 10-5 m. This process leads to a significant accumulation of gas–liquid interfacial energy and vapor enthalpy within the bubble. As the acoustic cycle transitions to the compression phase, external high-pressure forces the bubble into an inertial collapse stage. The bubble radius undergoes drastic contraction within an extremely short time, reaching a minimum value of 5.2 × 10-7 m. Notably, the bubble exhibits distinct asymmetric rebounds after collapse, with progressively diminishing amplitudes, indicating that most energy has been dissipated through shock waves and thermal effects. This periodic expansion-collapse process generates transient extreme conditions that provide crucial energy conversion mechanisms in sonochemistry. The radial velocity of the cavitation bubble wall shows a close correlation with the radius change. In the expansion phase, the radial velocity is low, showing typical stable cavitation characteristics. While in the collapse phase, the bubble wall velocity increases sharply to over 900 m/s, breaking the quasi-static assumption and forming a local supersonic flow. Fig. 7(c) reveals the transient thermodynamic characteristics during the collapse of a cavitation bubble. As the bubble radius undergoes inertial collapse to the submicron scale, the entrapped gas experiences adiabatic compression, causing a rapid temperature increase from ambient conditions to 4064.2 K. The peak temperature precisely coincides with the minimum bubble radius, confirming the formation mechanism of cavitation hotspots. Subsequently, the temperature decay follows an exponential decline, accompanied by bubble rebound oscillations until stabilisation near the initial temperature. This transient high temperature not only directly pyrolyses organic pollutants but also induces homolytic cleavage of water molecules to generate radicals, while simultaneously promoting O-O bond activation in PMS, thereby achieving synergistic enhancement of pollutant oxidation degradation and AOP. During the expansion phase of the cavitation bubble, while its volume increases exponentially, the temperature remains nearly constant, leading to a drastic pressure drop inside the bubble to 4.44 × 102 Pa (Fig. 7(d)). As the bubble radius collapses, water vapor and other gas molecules are compressed into a van der Waals core, rapidly elevating the internal pressure to 2.35 × 109 Pa. This extreme pressure variation induces adiabatic heating, triggering the dissociation and activation of oxidants and water molecules, thereby generating a high flux of reactive radicals. Simultaneously, upon final collapse, the cavitation bubble generates microjets and shockwaves, which not only mechanically disrupt the molecular structure of organic pollutants, significantly enhancing degradation efficiency, but also intensify mass transfer and collision efficiency between pollutants and radicals.

Chemical changes in cavitation bubbles

Fig. 7(e) shows the dynamic changes in the number of gas molecules within a cavitation bubble. Due to the low diffusion coefficients of oxygen (O2: 1.76 × 10-9 m2 s−1) and nitrogen (N2: 1.85 × 10-9 m2 s−1), their diffusion time scale (approximately 0.01 s) is much longer than the US cavitation cycle, strongly inhibiting gas exchange at the bubble wall. Consequently, the quantities of O2 and N2 molecules remain nearly constant throughout the process. In contrast, water vapor exhibits significant non-equilibrium behaviour: during the expansion phase, the rapid increase in bubble volume causes the partial pressure of water vapor to drop sharply from the saturation vapor pressure, triggering rapid evaporation of the liquid. This drives an exponential influx of water molecules across the bubble wall. During the collapse phase, adiabatic compression rapidly elevates the temperature. Water molecules not only undergo supercritical condensation back into the liquid phase but also dissociate into highly reactive free radicals under extreme temperature and pressure, leading to a sharp decrease in vapor molecules. Fig. 7(f) illustrates the generation and concentration changes of reactive species within an US cavitation bubble. During the first collapse and the subsequent rebound collapse stages of the cavitation bubble, the extremely high temperature and high-pressure conditions promote the dissociation and activation of water vapor and oxygen inside the bubble, primarily generating high concentrations of oxidative substances such as O3, ·O, and H2O2. Subsequently, the concentration of these reactive species tends to reach a dynamic equilibrium. These substances degrade pollutants through two pathways: on one hand, O3 and H2O2 act as selective oxidants, preferentially reacting with unsaturated organic compounds (such as aromatic hydrocarbons and alkenes) by disrupting their π-bond structures to achieve initial degradation; on the other hand, under the continuous action of US, these oxidants can be further transformed into non-selective radicals like ·OH, achieving indiscriminate oxidation of various organic pollutants until complete mineralization. In contrast, the concentrations of reactive species such as ·H and ·HO2 are relatively low, contributing limitedly to the degradation process. It is noteworthy that the concentration of ·OH within the bubble is limited and gradually decreases, which may be due to its rapid diffusion into the bulk solution (2.8 × 10-9 m2 s−1). This release behaviour is beneficial for the degradation of non-volatile organic pollutants, which can be achieved through long-distance oxidation in the solution via liquid-phase radical chain reactions. The abundant nitrogen gas within the cavitation bubbles can also be activated and dissociated by US energy, forming a series of nitrogen-containing reactive species through oxidative pathways, with changes in their molecular numbers as shown in Fig. 7(g). Among these, NH3, NO2, and ·HNO3 are the primary chemical substances. Nitrogen-containing radicals can similarly engage in hydrogen abstraction reactions with organic pollutants, reducing their structural stability and thereby promoting the degradation of organic pollutants. It is noteworthy that this pathway may generate nitro/nitroso compounds, potentially leading to toxic intermediates. Therefore, it is necessary to extend the US treatment time or combine it with the use of oxidants to achieve complete mineralization of the pollutants.

PMS activation

PMS activation is the key to the degradation of organic pollutants in the US-PMS system. Fig. 7(h) illustrates the activation of PMS, the consumption of oxidants, and the generation of radicals under the extreme conditions of US cavitation bubbles. During the US cycle, the extreme conditions generated by the collapse of cavitation bubbles efficiently activate the primary form of PMS (HSO5−) through a triple mechanism: (1) mechanical shear force of cavitation microjets directly breaks the O-O bond of HSO5−; (2) thermal dissociation induced by transient high temperatures; (3) strong electric field at the cavitation interface promotes electron transfer pathways. These three synergistic effects cause a sharp decrease in the number of HSO5− molecules, leaving only 5.1 ‰ while triggering complex radical chain reactions. Initially, SO4·−, as the primary reactive species, rapidly reaches its peak, indicating the preferential generation of SO4·− during the PMS activation process. The S2O82− produced by the activation of PMS in the US cavitation field acts as a key intermediate, and its dynamic changes in concentration profoundly reveal the unique mechanism and environmental advantages of US activation of PMS. During the initial bubble collapse, abundant SO4·− radicals self-couple to rapidly form S2O82−. Subsequently, S2O82− acts as a “radical reservoir” and reacts with newly generated ·OH in the system, not only maintaining the continuous supply of SO4·− but also forming a unique radical recycling network. During the collapse phase, a large number of water vapor molecules enter the cavitation bubbles and react with S2O82−, causing a slight fluctuation in its concentration. Simultaneously, dissolved oxygen drives the conversion of SO4·− to SO5·− through oxidative pathways. Although the latter has a lower concentration and weaker oxidative capacity, its disproportionation reaction can effectively replenish dissolved oxygen, allowing the system to maintain continuous oxidative capacity.

Process modelling

Cavitation bubble evolution

Through COMSOL Multiphysics field simulation, the nonlinear dynamic behaviour of cavitation bubbles in the US reactor is precisely and intuitively revealed (Fig. 8(a)). The simulation results show that within the US cycle (0–35 μs), the cavitation bubble undergoes four typical evolutionary stages: (1) the compression phase (0–17 μs), where positive pressure compresses the bubble, maintaining it near its initial radius; (2) the expansion phase (17–23 μs), where negative pressure drives the cavitation bubble to expand exponentially, achieving a 216-fold volume increase; (3) the collapse phase (23–25 μs), where the cavitation bubble undergoes ultrafast implosion within 2 μs, reducing its radius by 62 % and generating extreme physical conditions; (4) the oscillation phase (25–35 μs), where the cavitation bubble gradually dissipates energy through damped oscillation. This dynamic process explains the mechanism of PMS activation by US at the microscale: during the expansion of the cavitation bubble to its maximum size, Marangoni flows (driven by surface tension gradients) promote the directional migration of PMS molecules toward the gas–liquid interface. The subsequent collapse phase generates two key activation pathways: (1) thermal decomposition: the transient high temperature provides sufficient energy to excite vibrational modes of PMS molecules, facilitating O-O bond cleavage; (2) mechanical shear: hydrodynamic stress at the gas–liquid interface directly exerts shear forces on PMS molecules, inducing conformational strain and mechanical cleavage of the O-O bond. In addition, the shock waves generated by the collapse of the cavitation bubbles can promote the rapid diffusion of newly generated ROS, effectively avoiding their self-quenching and improving their effective utilization.

Sound field distribution

Fig. 8(b) illustrates the three-dimensional distribution characteristics of the steady-state sound pressure field within the reactor. The simulation results reveal that the sound pressure field exhibits a standing wave distribution pattern, with high-intensity negative pressure concentrated in the front region of the US transducer. The acoustic energy is focused in this area, stretching and disrupting the originally dense solution structure, thereby inducing cavitation phenomena. Consequently, the front region of the US transducer is a critical zone for the occurrence of US cavitation and PMS activation. Outside the core negative pressure region, there is a distinct gradient distribution of sound pressure. An adjacent positive pressure ring band regulates the dynamics of cavitation bubbles through two mechanisms: on one hand, radiation forces create a three-dimensional potential well that constrains the movement of cavitation bubbles, prolonging the residence time of ROS generated during activation; on the other hand, the positive pressure gradient induces non-spherical oscillations of the cavitation bubbles, enhancing the mechanical shear effect during collapse. A secondary negative pressure zone envelops the positive pressure region. Although the sound pressure in this zone is below the cavitation threshold, its periodic pressure fluctuations enhance mass transfer through two pathways: first, by exciting micro-scale acoustic streaming, which reduces the thickness of the boundary layer; second, by inducing directional migration of solute molecules. This gradient sound pressure field establishes a dual-functional “activation-transport” reaction system: in the core region, the extreme conditions generated by the collapse of cavitation bubbles activate PMS, while the confinement effect of the positive pressure region extends the half-life of ROS; in the transition zone, the micro-scale acoustic streaming driven by the secondary negative pressure and the macro-scale convection induced by the positive pressure gradient form multi-scale mixing, improving the mass transfer coefficient of pollutants.

Flow field distribution

Fig. 8(c) displays the distribution of the flow field inside the reactor, as simulated using COMSOL. Under the specified reaction conditions (US power of 240 W), the simulation results indicate that the maximum flow velocity within the reactor reaches 0.268 m/s. Due to the continuous vibration of the US transducer, a high-speed flow zone forms at its front, extending vertically downward to the bottom of the reactor. This region is characterized by densely packed velocity contour lines and significant gradient changes, exhibiting a typical turbulent vortex structure. Within this zone, high-speed vortices effectively disrupt concentration gradients through periodic stretching and folding, enhancing micromixing and reducing diffusion limitations. This significantly enhances the effective contact efficiency between oxidants and organic pollutants, thereby optimizing the mixing of fluid microclusters and the mass transfer efficiency. Furthermore, near the reactor walls, the acoustic streaming effect induced by US vibrations forms a turbulent boundary layer, which further increases the non-uniformity of the velocity distribution. Concurrently, the centrifugal instability effect aids in the entrainment of pollutants from the wall region into the mainstream area, effectively preventing the deposition of pollutants at the solid–liquid interface and ensuring mass transfer efficiency during the operation of the US generator. Upon reaching the top of the reactor, the fluid’s kinetic energy gradually dissipates, causing the flow velocity to decrease and forming a relatively stable turbulent mixing layer. This region is critical for the thorough mixing of oxidants, ROS, and organic pollutants, playing a key role in degradation.

Temperature field distribution

The simulation results for the temperature distribution inside the reactor are shown in Fig. 8(d). Heat generated by the oscillation of the US transducer is the primary heat source within the reactor. Under steady-state operation, the highest temperature increase, reaching 449 K, occurs in the region at the front end of the US transducer. The reactor exhibits a significant temperature gradient distribution, with high-temperature areas primarily located in three regions: the top of the US transducer, the bottom impact zone, and the upper mixing zone. This distribution is highly coupled with the flow field structure—the high-speed downward flow transports heat to the bottom, while the turbulent mixing layer achieves uniform heat distribution in the upper part of the reactor. The elevated temperature increases the diffusion coefficient of pollutants by enhancing molecular thermal motion and reducing solution viscosity. This not only increases the collision frequency between radicals and pollutants but also lowers the nucleation energy barrier for cavitation bubbles, promoting higher bubble number density and enhancing collapse intensity. Simultaneously, the high-temperature zones further increase the activation rate of PMS through thermal activation pathways, leading to the generation of higher concentrations of ROS in the solution environment. However, compared to the transient extreme temperatures from cavitation collapse, this steady heating is relatively mild and contributes less to PMS activation and reaction rate enhancement. In industrial applications, actively heating the reactor not only increases energy consumption but also accelerates equipment ageing. Therefore, the optimal operational strategy is to utilize the US self-heating effect to maintain a mild temperature rise in the reactor without additional energy consumption, thereby improving reaction conditions, enhancing the activation efficiency of PMS, and increasing the degradation rate of organic pollutants.

Chemical field distribution

Fig. 8(e) displays the distribution of contaminant concentration within the reactor, as simulated using COMSOL. The simulation results reveal a significant heterogeneous spatial distribution of pollutants within the reactor. High-concentration regions, shaped by the competition between mass transfer and turbulent diffusion, form an inverted ‘U’ pattern near the reactor top, while the low-concentration regions are primarily located in the middle and lower sections, forming a distinct vertical concentration stratification. Specifically, the light green area at the bottom of the reactor corresponds to the intense cavitation zone. The dense collapse of cavitation bubbles here generates strong micro-jets, effectively promoting the rapid degradation of contaminants, resulting in lower pollutant concentrations. The red, wavy region at the top reflects the dynamic characteristics of the turbulent mixing layer. Although the vortex structures in this area enhance mixing, they also lead to a relative enrichment of pollutants. Consequently, this region is the primary area for ROS-mediated mineralization of contaminants. This gradient distribution of concentration, ranging from light green at the bottom to red at the top, intuitively reveals the functional zoning characteristics of “bottom activation − top mixing” within the US reactor, providing important guidance for engineering optimization. Furthermore, the contaminant concentration at the top can serve as an indicator for process control. When the concentration at the top meets the discharge standard, the overall reactor removal efficiency is also compliant. Utilizing this correlation can reduce online monitoring costs while ensuring stable, compliant effluent quality.

Conclusion

This study integrates single-bubble dynamics modeling, COMSOL Multiphysics simulations, and experimental validation to establish a novel cavitation-based mechanism for PMS activation, overcoming key limitations of conventional AOPs. Experimentally, the system achieved 92.4 % degradation of the model pollutant at neutral pH without an external catalyst, avoiding secondary pollution risks from sludge or metal leaching. In terms of theoretical analysis, the Keller-Miksis model quantitatively revealed the extreme conditions during cavitation bubble collapse, elucidating how the synergistic effects of thermal and shear forces cleave the O-O bond in PMS, subsequently generating ·OH, SO4·- and cavitation-induced nitrogen–oxygen radicals, thereby refining the radical reaction mechanism within the US-PMS system. Concurrently, COMSOL Multiphysics coupling simulations accurately captured the spatial distribution characteristics of the acoustic, flow, and temperature fields, and validated the “bottom activation-top mixing” reactor zoning theory. Based on these findings, optimizing the position and power of the US transducer can achieve optimized US energy distribution and radical utilization efficiency. This discovery provides crucial theoretical support and quantifiable design parameters for the industrial-scale upscaling of the US-PMS system.

While this study elucidates key mechanisms in the ultrasound-activated PMS process through a multi-scale approach, certain limitations should be acknowledged. First, all experiments were conducted using synthetic wastewater at laboratory scale, which possesses a relatively simple water chemistry matrix. Future work must validate the system's adaptability and degradation efficacy in real wastewater of complex composition. Second, although the single-bubble dynamics model employed in this work quantitatively reveals the extreme physicochemical conditions during cavitation collapse and its contribution to radical generation, actual ultrasonic reactors involve multibubble populations and their interactions. Future studies should incorporate population balance models or multiscale simulation methods to account for bubble–bubble interactions, coalescence, and breakup behavior, thereby enabling more accurate predictions of cavitation yield and activation efficiency at the reactor scale. Lastly, while COMSOL simulations reveal synergistic acoustic, flow, and thermal field distributions, providing a basis for optimization, practical scale-up requires solving challenges like transducer arrangement, energy efficiency, and economic feasibility. Subsequent research will focus on reactor structure optimization and pilot-scale validation based on these physical field characteristics.
