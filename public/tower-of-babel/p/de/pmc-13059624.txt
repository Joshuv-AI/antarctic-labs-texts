Development and validation of the engaging in life scale: long and short forms among Chinese adults with emotional distress
Authors: Dongyang Chen, Shixing Feng, Yu Leng, Guangyu Zhou, Stefan G. Hofmann, Xinghua Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Engaging in life has been proposed as a potential transdiagnostic mechanism underlying emotional disorders, underscoring the need for reliable assessment. This study conducted a multi-stage development and validation of the Engaging in Life Scale (ELS). Participants were 2,420 Chinese adults aged 18–65 recruited from the prescreening phase of a randomized controlled trial on emotional distress, ensuring inclusion of individuals with varying symptom levels. An initial item pool was generated through qualitative interviews and expert review, then refined via item analysis and exploratory factor analysis, yielding a 17-item scale (ELS-17) encompassing four dimensions—work/study, socializing, leisure, and sleep routine. Confirmatory factor analysis in an independent subsample supported this four-factor structure, with high reliability (α = 0.83–0.91) and satisfactory construct validity (CR = 0.87–0.93; AVE = 0.64–0.78). Multidimensional item response theory indicated strong measurement precision and measurement invariance across gender, age, and distress severity. To enhance applicability, a short form (ELS-8) was derived using the ant colony optimization (ACO) algorithm, retaining two items per dimension while preserving model fit, reliability, and criterion validity. The ELS-8 showed excellent fit and very high correspondence with the ELS-17 (r = 0.98). Overall, the ELS provides a theoretically grounded and psychometrically sound measure of engaging in life in both long and short forms, offering a solid foundation for future research on transdiagnostic mechanisms underlying emotional disorders.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04176-3.

Introduction

Engaging in life is defined as the investment of one’s time and energy into daily activities, including work, study, socializing, leisure, and a normal sleep routine [27]. It reflects an individual’s behavioral action to initiate and sustain activities in life, thereby supporting adaptive functioning and the restoration of normal life. Specifically, within this behavioral framework, a ‘sleep routine’ is conceptualized not as the physiological process of sleep itself, but as the active behavioral management of one’s life rhythm. Maintaining consistent sleep–wake cycles despite psychological strain is viewed as a form of intentional self-regulation and a foundational domain of engaging in life, distinct from purely biological sleep markers. A growing body of research suggests that higher levels of life engagement are associated with better mental health outcomes, whereas insufficient engagement may represent a critical vulnerability factor for emotional disorders [9, 12, 30, 40]. Conceptually, engaging in life shares lineage with established behavioral frameworks but focused on a distinct mechanism. While Behavioral Activation (BA) emphasizes increasing contact with positive reinforcement (reward-seeking) to reverse depression [19], and Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) focuses on value-congruence [3, 14], the present construct-grounded in the Mindfulness Intervention for Emotional Distress (MIED)-emphasizes an acceptance-based capacity [27]. Similar to “doing what needs to be done” in Morita therapy [16, 24], it captures the intentional maintenance of functional routines despite the presence of distress, regardless of immediate reward or symptom reduction. Accordingly, it has been proposed as a potential transdiagnostic mechanism and treatment mediator for emotional disorders [27], yet its measurement remains insufficiently developed.

An earlier version of this construct, called life engagement, was defined as “the extent to which individuals are involved in activities that are personally valued” [35] and was later broadened to encompass cognitive vitality, motivation, reward seeking, and the capacity to experience pleasure, thereby reflecting functional outcomes such as fulfillment, well-being, and a sense of meaning in life [3, 44]. Based on this conceptualization, several instruments have been developed, including the Life Engagement Test (focusing on meaning and goal-directedness) [35], measures of valued living such as the Engaged Living Scale (emphasizing the congruence between behavior and values) [39], and adapted symptom subscales (e.g., PANSS-14 Life Engagement Subscale, IDS-SR10 Life Engagement Subscale) [37, 41]. However, a distinct boundary exists between these constructs and the present operationalization. These existing tools primarily capture the experiential aspects of meaning, value-congruence, or symptom-releated functoning, rather than the behavioral dimension central to engaging in life-namely, the functional capacity to initiate and sustain activities even under distress.

Within the MIED diamond model, engaging in life is defined as behavioral action to sustain daily activities despite emotional or physical discomfort—a transdiagnostic mechanism that supports adaptive functioning across emotional disorders. Positioning this construct within distress contexts enables assessment of individuals’ behavioral level to remain behaviorally active under real-world psychological strain, providing a more ecologically valid and mechanism-oriented conceptualization than existing meaning- or satisfaction-based approaches. This contextualized behavioral framing enhances both theoretical clarity and clinical applicability, making the construct particularly relevant for transdiagnostic and intervention research.

Despite the clear theoretical framework provided by the MEID model, a critical measurement gap remains. Existing instruments generally fall into two categories: (1) Subscales with symptom specific measures, such as Life Engagement Subscale of the Inventory of Depressive Symptomatology (IDS-SR10), which are embedded within disorder specific assessments and may not capture the transdiagnostic nature of engagement across broader emotional disorders; or (2) Global outcome measures, such the Life Engagement Test (LET), which primarily assess cognitive evaluation (e.g., sense of purpose) or subjective satisfaction rather than specific behavioral maintenance. Crucially, neither category adequately captures the core construct of engaging in life: the capacity to maintain behavioral routines (including sleep) despite the presence of emotional distress. Consequently, there is a lack of a validated, transdiagnostic instrument that operationalizes this construct as a multidimensional behavioral process suitable for quantification in clinical settings.

Beyond theoretical precision, methodological rigor and feasibility are critical for developing a psychometrically sound and practically useful scale. Short-form versions are especially valuable for large-scale screening and longitudinal monitoring. Recent evidence indicates that the ant colony optimization (ACO) algorithm provides an efficient, multidimensional approach for deriving short forms by simultaneously optimizing reliability, validity, and model fit [29, 36].

To address this gap, the present study aimed to develop and validate the Engaging in Life Scale (ELS). We adopted a unified, multi-stage psychometric framework to answer the following three research questions: 1) What is the underlying latent factor structure of the ELS items, and does it align with the theoretical domains of the MIED framework? 2) Does the ELS demonstrate robust reliability, measurement invariance across gender, and convergent/discriminant validity against established measures? 3) Can an efficient short form (ELS-8) be derived using Ant Colony Optimization without compromising the scale’s structural integrity or construct coverage?

Methods

Participants and procedure

Data were drawn from the prescreening phase of a large-scale randomized controlled trial (RCT) on emotional distress conducted at Peking University. During this phase, all participants completed a self-report screening questionnaire prior to randomization and intervention. For the present psychometric analyses, the entire prescreening dataset was used independently of the subsequent RCT process; thus, the sample was not restricted by the RCT’s eligibility criteria, and all data were collected before any intervention. Participants were recruited through a WeChat-based online advertisement directing respondents to the Wenjuanxing survey platform. A total of 2,998 responses were obtained, and after excluding attention-check failures, abnormal completion times (< 3 min or > 30 min), and duplicate entries, 2,420 valid cases were retained (validity rate = 80.7%). The final sample comprised 2,025 females (83.7%), with an age range of 18–65 years (M = 33.15, SD = 9.38). See more demographic characteristics in the Supplementary Table 1.

To reduce overfitting and allow cross-validation, the dataset was randomly divided into two equal halves (n = 1,210 each) using R with a fixed random seed for reproducibility. The development half was used for item analysis and exploratory factor analysis (EFA), while the validation half was used for confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), reliability estimation, and criterion-related validity testing. Analyses requiring larger sample sizes—such as multidimensional item response theory (MIRT) modeling and measurement invariance across gender—were conducted on the full sample (N = 2,420) to maximize statistical stability. In the final stage, the ant colony optimization (ACO) algorithm was applied to derive the short form (ELS-8) in the development half, and its structure and validity were cross-validated in the validation half.

Ethical approval was granted by the Committee for Protecting Human and Animal Subjects, School of Psychological and Cognitive Sciences, Peking University (approval number: #2024–10–14).

Item generation and exploratory factor analysis

Item development and revision

Item generation was guided by the MIED diamond model of psychopathology [27], with particular emphasis on the core construct of engaging in life. This model highlights behavioral engagement across four major life domains—work/study, socializing, leisure, and sleep routine—as a key transdiagnostic mechanism of emotional disorders. Guided by this framework, four preliminary domains were specified and later examined as potential latent dimensions of engaging in life through factor analyses.

The initial item pool was developed through semi-structured interviews with adults experiencing emotional distress, focusing on their daily behavioral expressions of engaging in life. Interview transcripts were analyzed using open coding and categorization to identify key behavioral themes, which served as the basis for preliminary items. These items were refined through expert discussions with MIED-certified instructors and clinical supervisors specialized in psychological counseling and psychotherapy. Building on this process and clinical observations, the development team—comprising a doctoral student in clinical psychology, a master’s student in clinical psychology, and a licensed supervisor certified by the Chinese Psychological Society (also an active researcher in emotional distress)—expanded and systematized the pool to 30 items. All items were rated on a five-point Likert scale (1 = not at all true, 5 = completely true), with 50% reverse-scored to minimize response bias.

One senior researcher specializing in emotional distress and three doctoral students in clinical psychology independently reviewed the items for theoretical alignment and clarity of wording. To ensure theoretical rigor and coverage, three MIED-certified supervisors familiar with the diamond model of psychopathology provided independent revision suggestions. To further evaluate comprehensibility and face validity, six adults with emotional distress participated in cognitive interviews, which assessed participants’ understanding of the instructions and items. Finally, a pilot online survey with 130 adults aged 18–65 experiencing emotional distress (112 valid responses) provided preliminary evidence of feasibility and internal consistency (Cronbach’s α), serving as an initial psychometric evaluation before large-scale administration.

Data analysis

All analyses were conducted in R 4.4.1. Item analysis and EFA were performed using the psych [33] and GPArotation [4] packages.

First, item analysis was conducted in the test set, including: (1) extreme group t-tests: participants were ranked by total score, and the top and bottom 27% were compared for each item using independent-samples t-tests; items with nonsignificant results were removed [43],(2) Corrected Item–Total Correlation (CITC): items with CITC < 0.40 were removed [47],and (3) internal consistency contribution: Cronbach’s α was computed with and without each item, and items were removed if α increased meaningfully after deletion, indicating that the item weakened internal consistency [42].

Second, items retained from item analysis were subjected to EFA. Prior to extraction, data adequacy was assessed using the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) metric and Bartlett’s test of sphericity; KMO ≥ 0.70 and a significant Bartlett’s test (p < 0.05) indicated suitability for factor analysis [2, 21]. Given that items were ordinal, a polychoric correlation matrix was used. The number of factors was determined based on parallel analysis, scree plot, the eigenvalue > 1 rule, and theoretical considerations. Factors were extracted using principal axis factoring and rotated with oblimin rotation. Item deletion criteria were: (1) primary loading < 0.32 [46],(2 cross-loading gap < 0.20 [17],(3) communality (h2) < 0.20 [46].

Confirmatory factor analysis and validation

Measures

Engaging in life scale-17 (ELS-17)

The ELS-17, developed in the EFA stage, comprises 17 items across four domains (work/study, leisure, socializing, and sleep routine) rated on a five-point Likert scale (1 = not at all true, 5 = completely true). Item 12 is directly scored, while the others are reverse scored, yielding a total score of 17–85, with higher scores indicating that the individual is more engaged in life. The EFA stage provided preliminary psychometric evidence for the scale. The ELS was originally developed and validated in Chinese. To facilitate international dissemination, an English reference version (provided in the Supplementary Materials) was produced using a hybrid AI-assisted expert review procedure. Initial translations were generated using neural machine translation tools to ensure linguistic fluency. Subsequently, the lead author and a bilingual clinical psychologist critically reviewed and refined the items. This process prioritized conceptual equivalence over literal translation, ensuring that the English phrasing accurately reflected the behavioral constructs of the MIED framework. Researchers intending to use the English version for empirical data collection are encouraged to conduct further psychometric validation.

Life engagement test (LET)

The LET [35] assesses purpose in life, defined as the extent to which individuals engage in valued activities. Sample items include: “I value my activities a lot” and “There is not enough purpose in life”. It contains six items rated on a five-point scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree), producing total scores of 6–30. The LET has demonstrated good psychometric properties and was included to assess convergent validity. In the current study, the scale demonstrates satisfactory internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.77).

The satisfaction with life scale (SWLS)

The SWLS [10] evaluates the cognitive component of subjective well-being via five items rated on a seven-point scale. Representative items include: “In most ways my life is close to my ideal” and “So far I have gotten the important things I want in life”. Scores range from 5 to 35, with higher values indicating greater life satisfaction. The scale has been validated across cultures [1] and was used to test convergent validity. The Cronbach’s α coefficients in the present sample was 0.91.

WHO-5 well-being index (WHO-5)

The WHO-5 measures subjective well-being over the past two weeks using five positively worded items rated on a six-point scale (0–5). Total scores (0–25) reflect overall well-being, with higher scores indicating better outcomes. Example items are: “I have felt cheerful and in good spirits” and “My daily life has been filled with things that interest me.”. The WHO-5 has demonstrated sound reliability and validity [38] and was also used to examine convergent validity. The Cronbach’s α coefficients in the present sample was 0.93.

The Kessler psychological distress scale (Kessler-10)

The Kessler-10 [23] consists of 10 items rated on a five-point scale (1 = none of the time, 5 = all of the time), yielding scores from 10 to 50, with higher scores reflecting greater psychological distress. Sample items include: “In the past 4 weeks, about how often did you feel tired out for no good reason?” and “In the past 4 weeks, about how often did you feel that everything was an effort?”. The K10 has well-established reliability and validity and was used to assess discriminant validity. The Cronbach’s α coefficients in the present sample was 0.92.

The short form perceived stress scale (PSS-SF)

The four-item PSS-SF [25] measures perceived stress over recent weeks. Items are rated on a five-point scale (0 = never, 4 = very often), with total scores ranging from 0 to 16. Example items include: “In the last month, how often have you felt that you were unable to control the important things in your life?” and “In the last month, how often have you felt difficulties were pilling up so high that you could not overcome them?”. This brief version has psychometric properties comparable to longer forms and was used to assess discriminant validity. The Cronbach’s α coefficients in the present sample was 0.82.

Data analysis

All analyses were conducted in R 4.4.1, using the packages lavaan [34], semTools [20], psych [33], and mirt [5].

To further examine the psychometric properties of the ELS-17, we conducted CFA using the weighted least squares mean and variance adjusted estimator (WLSMV), which is appropriate for ordinal categorical data. Model fit was evaluated with commonly recommended indices: the comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker–Lewis index (TLI), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR). Following conventional cutoffs, CFI and TLI ≥ 0.95 were considered indicative of good fit, whereas RMSEA < 0.06 and SRMR < 0.08 indicated excellent fit [18]. Construct validity was further examined based on the CFA results: convergent validity was assessed using composite reliability (CR ≥ 0.70) [22]and average variance extracted (AVE ≥ 0.50) [13], while discriminant validity was assessed using the heterotrait–monotrait ratio (HTMT), with HTMT < 0.85 indicating satisfactory discriminant validity [15]. To further rigorously examine the structural validity and rule out alternative explanations, we compared the hypothesized four-factor structure with three alternative models in the validation set: a one-factor model, a second-order model, and a bifactor model.

For reliability, we adopted both classical and structural approaches. Internal consistency was evaluated with Cronbach’s α, and omega coefficients (ω) derived from CFA. To complement these indices with evidence from modern measurement theory, we fitted a MIRT model to the full sample using the graded response model (GRM) [32]. Within this framework, test information curves were generated for θ values ranging from − 3 to + 3, and marginal reliability was calculated from posterior estimates and standard errors to evaluate measurement precision across the latent continuum [11].

For criterion-related validity, correlations were computed between the ELS total score and several external measures, including the LET, SWLS, and WHO-5 (theoretically expected to be positive) as well as the Kessler-10 and PSS-SF (theoretically expected to be negative). To obtain more robust results, Pearson’s correlation coefficients were reported, and the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure was applied to control the false discovery rate (FDR) in multiple testing.

In addition, MI across gender was tested via multi-group CFA, also using WLSMV estimation. Four increasingly constrained models were fitted sequentially: configural, metric, scalar, and strict invariance, corresponding to constraints on factor structure, factor loadings, item thresholds, and residual variances, respectively. Invariance was considered supported when the difference between nested models met the criteria (ΔCFI ≤ 0.01 and ΔRMSEA ≤ 0.015) [6, 7]. The strict model was additionally estimated under the theta parameterization, as residual variances are not identifiable under the delta parameterization.

Short-form derivation via ACO

All analyses were conducted in R 4.4.1, primarily using the ShortForm package [31] and the lavaan package [34].

Development of the short versions of the scale

Building on the long-form scale established in the previous stage, we applied the ACO algorithm to derive a short form. Candidate items were restricted to a minimum of two per factor, with the target short form consisting of two items per factor. A detailed description of the ACO algorithm, including its theoretical rationale and iterative search mechanism, is provided in the Supplementary Materials (see Supplementary Methods: Ant Colony Optimization). The algorithm was configured with 40 ants, a pheromone evaporation rate of 20% (i.e., 80% retention), 60 iterations, and a maximum of 2400 runs. Compared with previous applications using 20 ants, 10% evaporation, 50 iterations, and a maximum of 1000 runs [45], our larger item pool warranted increasing both the number of ants and the iterations to enhance search coverage and solution stability.

Optimization was guided by CFA model fit, with CFI, RMSEA, and SRMR as selection criteria (thresholds: CFI > 0.95, RMSEA < 0.06, SRMR < 0.08;[18]. Distinct from prior applications of ACO [45], we additionally incorporated regression paths from the short-form factors to external mental health indicators during optimization. This procedure served as a supplementary constraint to ensure that the short form retained theoretically expected criterion-related associations, rather than solely optimizing internal measurement properties.

To account for the stochastic nature of ACO, the optimization was repeated with 10 different random seeds [45]. Item selection frequencies across runs were tallied, and the most frequently selected items were prioritized. In the event of ties, decisions were based on the primary factor loadings, with items showing higher loadings prioritized. This procedure yielded a short form with two items per factor.

Reliability and validity of the short form

To validate the short form, the ACO-derived model was first fitted in the training set to confirm convergence and acceptable fit, and then cross-validated in the validation set. Model fit was assessed using conventional indices (CFI, TLI, RMSEA, SRMR), and construct validity was examined through CR, AVE, and HTMT [13, 15, 18]. Multi-group CFA on the combined sample further tested configural, metric, scalar, and strict invariance across gender, following the criteria used in the previous stage [6],the strict model was additionally estimated under the theta parameterization.

Reliability was evaluated using Cronbach’s α and omega coefficients (ω) for the total scale, while factor-level consistency was estimated via polychoric correlations, given the two-item structure of each factor. To supplement these indices from a modern psychometric perspective, a GRM [32] was fitted in the combined sample using the mirt package, yielding test information curves and marginal reliability.

Criterion-related validity was examined in the validation set by correlating the short-form total score with external scales. Positive associations were expected with the LET, SWLS, and WHO-5, and negative associations with the K10 and CPSS. Pearson correlations were computed with Benjamini–Hochberg correction, and consistency between short- and long-form total scores was assessed to evaluate representativeness.

Results

Item development and exploratory findings

Item revision and retention

The initial pool included 30 items. To better capture the target construct, item wording was revised to explicitly reflect the impact of emotional or physical distress on engaging in life. For example, “Compared with most people around me, I spend time on work/study” was revised to “I need to spend considerable time dealing with emotional and physical distress, which forces me to reduce my study or work activities.” Expert review and peer feedback led to the removal of six items due to redundancy or weak construct relevance. Based on supervisory input, the “leisure and entertainment” dimension was broadened to “leisure (physical activities and hobbies)” to encompass activities such as painting, music, and reading. Cognitive interviews indicated that most items were comprehensible, though several were lengthy, overly technical, or ambiguous; nine items were therefore simplified. For instance, “I need to spend considerable time dealing with emotional and physical distress, making it difficult to maintain a normal routine” was shortened to “I need to spend considerable time dealing with emotional and physical distress, which prevents me from maintaining a reasonable routine.” The resulting scale comprised 24 items across four dimensions (work/study, socializing, leisure, and sleep routine). A pilot study supported its reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.88), providing preliminary justification for large-scale administration.

Item analysis

The extreme group comparison method (assessing item discrimination) revealed significant differences between the high-scoring (top 27%) and low-scoring (bottom 27%) groups on any item; thus, no deletions were made based on t-test results. In contrast, the Corrected Item–Total Correlation (CITC) analysis identified four items (ELS4 = 0.094, ELS10 = 0.123, ELS16 = 0.052, ELS22 = 0.182) with values below the 0.40 threshold. Internal consistency analysis further showed that removing these items increased Cronbach’s α, supporting their exclusion. Accordingly, a total of four items (ELS4, ELS10, ELS16, ELS22) were eliminated during item analysis.

Exploratory factor analysis

The KMO measure was 0.94, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (χ2 = 18,302.33, df = 190, p < 0.001), confirming excellent sampling adequacy for factor analysis. Eigenvalue analysis indicated four factors above the Kaiser criterion (10.45, 1.62, 1.46, 1.06), with subsequent eigenvalues below 1 (e.g., 0.93 for the fifth). Parallel analysis suggested up to five factors, whereas the scree plot showed a distinct elbow after the fourth (See Supplementary Fig. 1 and Fig. 2 for details.). Balancing these statistical criteria with theoretical considerations, a four-factor solution was retained. The four-factor solution corresponded closely to the hypothesized life domains, supporting their interpretation as distinct but related dimensions of engaging in life. Three items (ELS6, ELS18, ELS24) were then removed due to cross-loading gaps < 0.20. The final model accounted for 63.6% of the variance, with item loadings ranging from 0.54 to 0.92, all salient on their intended factors. Confirmatory factor analysis, reliability, validity, and measurement invariance

Confirmatory factor analysis

The results of the CFA indicated that the four-factor model of ELS-17 fit very well, with χ2 = 474.73, CFI = 0.998, TLI = 0.997, SRMR = 0.029, and RMSEA = 0.052(90% CI [0.047,0 0.056]). To further verify the structural stability, we tested alternative models (one-factor, second factor, and bifactor models) in the validation set. The results confirmed that the four-factor correlated structure was superior to the unidimensional model and comparable to the second-order model (see Supplementary Table 10 for detailed comparisons).

Reliability analysis

Reliability analyses indicated strong internal consistency for the ELS-17 in the validation sample. The total scale demonstrated excellent reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.94), and subscales showed acceptable to good levels (α = 0.83–0.91). Omega coefficients (ω = 0.84–0.91) yielded comparable results. Consistent with these findings, MIRT-based marginal reliabilities further indicated good measurement precision across the latent continuum (see Supplementary Table 2 for details.). Test information curves (Supplementary Fig. 3) indicated that measurement precision was highest around average latent trait levels (θ ≈ 0) for Work/Study and Socializing, followed by Leisure, whereas the Sleep routine factor provided comparatively lower information but still adequate precision across the continuum.

Construct validity test

Construct validity was supported by both convergent and discriminant evidence. Convergent validity was indicated by high composite reliability (CR = 0.87–0.93, all > 0.70) and satisfactory average variance extracted (AVE = 0.64–0.78, all ≥ 0.50). Discriminant validity was confirmed by HTMT values, with all inter-factor correlations below 0.85, demonstrating clear distinctiveness among the four factors. Detailed results are presented in Supplementary Table 2 and Supplementary Table 3.

Criterion related validity test

Pearson correlations were computed between ELS-17 scores and external measures (Table 2). Effect sizes were interpreted following Cohen’s guidelines [8]: small (r ≥ 0.10), medium (r ≥ 0.30), and large (r ≥ 0.50). As expected, convergent validity was supported by large positive correlations with the LET, SWLS, and WHO-5 (ranging from 0.48 to 0.61), all statistically significant after FDR correction. Reliability and measurement precision

The short form showed satisfactory total-scale reliability (α = 0.904). Factor-level consistency was acceptable for four factors (α = 0.788–0.875; ω = 0.800–0.877). Graded response models yielded marginal reliabilities of 0.658–0.734 for the four factors (details are provided in Supplementary Table 5). Test information curves (Supplementary Fig. 4) resembled those of the long form (Supplementary Fig. 3), although overall measurement precision was reduced due to item reduction.

Criterion-related validity

The short form demonstrated strong criterion-related validity. Convergent validity was reflected in positive correlations with life engagement, life satisfaction, and well-being, whereas discriminant validity was supported by negative correlations with psychological distress and perceived stress (all p < 0.001; see Supplementary Table 7). The short and long forms were highly consistent across both training and validation sets (r = 0.98, p < 0.001), confirming that the short form preserves the theoretical correlational pattern of the long version.

Discussion

The present study developed and validated the Engaging in Life Scale (ELS) to operationalize a core dimension of the Mindfulness Intervention for Emotional Distress (MIED) framework [27]. Through a multi-stage psychometric process, we established the 17-item long form (ELS-17) and an efficient 8-item short form (ELS-8). Both versions demonstrated robust psychometric properties, supporting the ELS as a reliable, valid, and transdiagnostic tool for assessing the capacity to maintain functioning across work/study, socializing, leisure, and sleep domains.

Across all analytic stages, results converged to support a four-dimensional representation of engaging in life—work/study, socializing, leisure, and sleep routine—each reflecting a distinct but interconnected facet of daily engagement. Although parallel analysis initially suggested a five-factor solution, the four-factor structure demonstrated superior theoretical coherence [26] and empirical interpretability. Furthermore, confirmatory comparison with alternative models empirically supported the multidimensional nature of the construct and the robustness of the four-factor solution. This configuration aligns closely with the MIED framework [27], which emphasizes the degree of engagement across major life domains as a key transdiagnostic process. The retained items showed strong loadings on their intended dimensions, indicating that each factor captured a substantive and theoretically meaningful aspect of engaging in life.

Regarding construct validity, the ELS showed expected associations with well-being and distress. Notably, the strong inverse correlation with the Kessler-10 warrants theoretical clarification. While this suggests shared variance (~ 50%), it reflects the MIED premise that high distress is a primary barrier to engagement. Crucially, however, the constructs remain distinct: symptom measures assess internal emotional states, whereas the ELS assesses external behavioral maintenance. The unique variance indicates that the ELS captures a specific resilience mechanism—the capacity to act despite distress—that is not fully explained by symptom severity. Conceptually, this behaviorally anchored approach advances beyond meaning- or satisfaction-based instruments [35]. By focusing on the functional execution of routines (including the deliberate behavioral regulation of sleep) rather than subjective symptoms, the ELS offers a more mechanism-oriented and ecologically valid measure of functioning under strain.

The derivation of the ELS-8 via Ant Colony Optimization (ACO) successfully balanced brevity with structural integrity. The short form retained the four-factor structure and showed excellent model fit. The "perfect" fit indices in the validation set, while partly attributable to the algorithm's maximization strategy and model parsimony, were supported by the stability of cross-validation results. Interestingly, the ACO algorithm converged on a solution consisting entirely of negatively worded items. This data-driven selection likely minimizes "method effects" often associated with mixed-wording scales [28] and aligns with clinical utility, as reporting specific functional impairments (e.g., "distress stopped me from working") is often cognitively more direct for distressed individuals. Despite the parsimony of having only two items per factor, the high composite reliability and correlation with the long form confirm that the ELS-8 captures the core variance of the construct without significant information loss.

Both versions achieved maximal measurement precision around average levels of the latent trait. While precision naturally decreased at the extremes-limiting discrimination for individuals with profound impairment or superior functioning-this profile aligns with the scale’s clinical utility, prioritizing sensitivity within the mild-to-severe distress range over the asymptomatic tails. Additionally, the ‘Sleep Routine’ factor yielded lower information than other domains. This likely reflects the physiological complexity of sleep regulation compared to purely voluntary behaviors (e.g., socializing), introducing more measurement noise, yet sleep remains a theoretically essential indicator of life rhythm stability.

Based on the psychometric findings, we propose three key recommendations for the application of the ELS. First, regarding clinical assessment, the long form (ELS-17) is recommended for comprehensive intake evaluations and treatment planning. Its four-dimensional structure allows clinicians to identify specific behavioral deficits and tailor interventions. Second, for screening and monitoring, the short form (ELS-8) offers a time-efficient alternative ideal for large-scale epidemiological surveys or session-by-session monitoring in clinical trails. Its high correlation with the full scale ensures that it captures the core construct of engaging in life with minimal burden. Third, for transdiagnostic research, we recommend using the ELS to investigate engaging in life as a mechanism of change. Unlike symptom-based measures, the ELS captures the functional capacity to act despite distress, making it a valuable tool for studying treatment processes within transdiagnostic contexts.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the analyses were based on cross-sectional data, leaving the temporal stability (e.g., test–retest reliability), sensitivity to therapeutic change, and predictive validity of the scale unexamined. Additionally, although the large sample was randomly split for development and validation, both studies were drawn from the same population source; Replication in independent samples is required to confirm robustness.

Second, regarding generalizability, the sample was recruited from a single cultural context and consisted exclusively of help-seeking individuals experiencing emotional distress, resulting in a predominantly female (83.7%) sample without a healthy control group. While our multi-group analyses supported strict measurement invariance across gender, age, and distress severity levels—suggesting the scale assesses the same construct with comparable psychometric properties across these subgroups—the absence of healthy controls and the reliance on a single cultural setting restrict broader applicability. Consequently, findings primarily reflect the behavioral patterns of distressed individuals within this specific cultural framework. Future research should prioritize recruiting healthy controls, balanced gender ratios, and diverse cultural samples to establish the scale’s normative validity and cross-cultural universality.

Finally, a methodological limitation concerns the ant colony optimization (ACO) algorithm. To ensure clinical relevance, we incorporated regression paths to external mental health indicators during item selection. Consequently, the criterion-related validity coefficients and the “perfect” fit indices observed for the ELS-8 in the validation set may partly reflect the algorithm’s maximization strategy. While cross-validation supported structural stability, these indices should be interpreted with caution. Future research using independent samples is necessary to confirm these associations without the influence of the selection algorithm.

Conclusion

This study developed and validated the Engaging in Life Scale (ELS) as a theoretically grounded and psychometrically robust measure. The 17-item long form (ELS-17) showed a stable four-factor structure, high reliability, and strong validity, while the 8-item short form (ELS-8) derived via the ant colony optimization algorithm retained comparable psychometric quality. The ELS conceptualizes engaging in life as a behavioral action to sustain daily functioning despite distress, offering an efficient, mechanism-oriented tool for investigating adaptive functioning and transdiagnostic processes relevant to emotional disorders.
