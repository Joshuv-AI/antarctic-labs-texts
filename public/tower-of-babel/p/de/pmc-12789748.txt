Step Forward in Rheumatic Valve Disease
Authors: Varun Bhatia, Edwin Francis, Rajiv Chandrashekharan, Annu Jose
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRheumatic heart disease remains prevalent in low-income countries, often leading to combined severe mitral stenosis (MS) and aortic stenosis (AS). Although percutaneous transmitral commissurotomy (PTMC) is the standard treatment for severe MS with suitable anatomy, rheumatic AS is typically excluded from major transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) trials because of its noncalcific nature.Case SummaryWe present a 72-year-old woman with severe rheumatic AS and MS who underwent simultaneous PTMC and TAVI. PTMC was performed via the right femoral vein using a 25-mm Accura balloon, followed by TAVI with a self-expanding Evolut-R valve, optimizing hemodynamics for her small aortic annulus.Take-Home MessagesSimultaneous PTMC and TAVI can be a safe and effective alternative to surgery in selected patients, offering a less invasive option for managing combined rheumatic valve disease. This case demonstrates its feasibility; however, further studies are needed to assess long-term outcomes.Key words: mitral and aortic stenosis, rheumatic valvular disease, TAVI and PTMC

Percutaneous transmitral commissurotomy (PTMC) is the standard interventional treatment for patients with severe mitral stenosis (MS) and suitable anatomy.1 Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) is the treatment of choice for intermediate and high surgical risk patients with severe calcified aortic stenosis (AS).2 Recently, TAVI has been approved in low-risk patients as well.3 However, in rheumatic heart disease–associated severe AS, surgical valve replacement has been the mainstay of treatment given the younger age of these patients and the lower degree of valve calcification.4 Therefore, TAVI use in rheumatic AS is not widespread and is yet to be fully investigated, except in limited case series and case reports.

We describe a case of a 72-year-old woman in whom simultaneous TAVI and PTMC was the chosen technique to treat combined severe rheumatic AS and MS in a single procedure.

History of presentation

A 72-year-old woman presented with gradually worsening exertional dyspnea and exercise intolerance of 2 years’ duration. She was hypertensive and diabetic on regular medications.

Investigation

Transthoracic echocardiography (Figure 1) showed classic features of rheumatic valvular heart disease with severe AS and severe MS with normal left ventricular function and mild pulmonary artery hypertension. The gradient across the aortic valve was peak gradient/mean gradient (Pg/Mg) of 80/68 mm Hg (Figure 2). Morphologically, it was a bicuspid aortic valve with left and right cusp fusion. The anterior mitral leaflet was doming, and the posterior mitral leaflet showed restricted mobility, which is the classic description for rheumatic MS. The planimetry area of the mitral valve was 1.3 cm2, suggestive of severe MS. There was moderate subvalvular thickening and fusion and specks of calcification on the valve leaflets. Mitral commissures were however free of calcification. Transmitral gradient was Pg/Mg of 14/7 mm Hg at a heart rate of 70 beats/min. There was a trace of mitral regurgitation. Mitral valve appeared suitable for PTMC.

Computed tomography aortogram (Figure 3) revealed bicuspid aortic valve with left right commissural fusion with a fibrotic raphe with calcium at the noncoronary cusp; aortic annulus was small (area: 284 mm2), left coronary cusp sinus of Valsalva (SOV) was 29.8 mm, right coronary cusp SOV was 28.8 mm, and noncoronary cusp SOV was 28.9 mm. The SOV were adequate and roomy. The left coronary ostia height was 10 mm, and right coronary ostia height was 12.6 mm. Bilateral iliofemoral was of adequate size and did not show any tortuosity. The aortic valve was deemed suitable for TAVI. The Society of Thoracic Surgeons score for this patient was 5.96%, which classified our patient in the intermediate-risk group. The Wilkin score, which predicts the procedural success for mitral valvuloplasty based on valve structure, was 8.

Management

This case was discussed by the heart team and a decision was made to consider a fully percutaneous treatment approach by planning to do PTMC followed by TAVI in the same setting.

The procedure was done under general anesthesia because transesophageal echocardiography guidance was used. The plan was to do PTMC before the TAVI procedure in the same setting, because in case of severe mitral regurgitation due to chordal rupture or anterior mitral leaflet tear post-PTMC, the patient could be to sent for surgical double valve replacement. PTMC was done through right femoral vein with a 25-mm Accura balloon (Figures 4A to 4C, Videos 1 and 2). Post-PTMC echocardiogram showed nicely opened commissures with a planimetry measured valve area of 1.8 cm2. Mitral gradients fell from Pg/Mg 14/7 to 12/4 mm Hg. There was only mild mitral regurgitation (Figures 4D and 4E). Because the PTMC result was good, we decided to proceed with TAVI. Predilation of the aortic valve with a 16- × 40-mm balloon was done because the aortic valve was bicuspid with fibrotic raphe (Figure 5A). Our case was suitable for both balloon and self-expandable transcatheter heart valves (THVs). However, the self-expandable supra-annular THV EVOLUT R 23 (Videos 3) was selected and deployed, achieving commissural alignment in view of the small annulus (perimeter: 19.6 mm; area: 284 mm2) (data from the SMART [Self -Expanding or Balloon -Expandable TAVI in patients with a small aortic annulus] trial) to achieve better hemodynamic performance (Figures 5B to 5D). Even though our case had a borderline left coronary ostial height of 10 mm, we did not prefer to use coronary protection while doing TAVI because the left coronary cusp SOV was 29.8 mm, which is adequate and roomy with low risk of potential coronary obstruction. If required, postdilation with an 18-mm balloon was planned. Good valve deployment was achieved with a 3- to 4-mm depth and good commissural alignment (Figure 6). There was no paravalvular aortic regurgitation or significant gradient. The femoral access site was closed with the hybrid technique using 1 Proglide suture (Abbott) and 1 Obtura (Meril) (collagen plug–based device), and proper hemostasis was achieved. General anesthesia was reversed, and extubation done on the table. No neurologic complications were noticed.

Follow-Up

The patient is doing well on follow-up (9 months) with no symptoms, with well-functioning aorticprosthesis and mild residual MS and mild regurgitation.

Discussion

The combination of severe MS and AS is typically a sequela of rheumatic heart disease. The combination of stenoses across the mitral and aortic valves induces a low-flow state. The presence of MS causes underestimation of aortic valve gradients because of a reduction in left ventricular preload (ie, low-flow, low-gradient AS).5 Thus, the combination of MS and AS may lead to delayed therapy, progressive cardiac damage, and subsequently higher procedural risks. The severity of MS using echocardiographic transmitral gradients and mitral valve area (MVA) may be inaccurate in patients with severe AS. MVA is often overestimated with the use of pressure half-time because of impaired left ventricular diastolic function prolonging the pressure half-time.6 Planimetry of the mitral valve is considered a more reliable parameter in presence of AS. Double valve surgery is recommended for patients who undergo aortic valve surgery with MVA <1.5 cm2.7 However, combined mitral and aortic replacement significantly increases mortality risk compared with single-valve intervention.

Rheumatic AS causes severe morphologic distortion of the valve because of postinflammatory commissural fusion and fibrous thickening with limited calcification.8 Unlike the fusion on the bicuspid valve, the fusion caused by rheumatic change seems more fibrotic if not calcified. TAVI for noncalcified lesions is an off-label application. After low-risk trials, TAVI has now become the standard and alternative treatment for patients with symptomatic calcific AS across the entire spectrum of surgical risk.8 Rheumatic AS has been excluded from major TAVI studies because of the predominantly noncalcific pathology of the rheumatic aortic valve. There have been case reports and case series showing successful implantation of the THV even in patients with and without significant leaflet calcification. PTMC is the treatment of choice for severe rheumatic MS, but is reserved for patients with suitable valve anatomy without significant mitral regurgitation.1

Our case demonstrated success of combining interventional treatment at the same sitting for both mitral and aortic valves. A self-expandable THV EVOLUT R 23 THV was used in view of a small annulus (perimeter diameter: 19.6 mm; area diameter: 284 mm2) (data from the SMART trial) to achieve better hemodynamic performance. Currently, there is no consensus regarding balloon or self-expandable THV selection for rheumatic AS with less calcification.

Rheumatic heart disease is still prevalent in many parts of the world, mainly in low-income countries.9 The subject of the potential role of TAVI in patients with rheumatic heart disease is important.

Post-PTMC restenosis is very low in middle-aged and older adults if both commissures are opened well, because recurrent rheumatic fever is rare in this age group. Doing PTMC before TAVI gives us the opportunity to send the patient for surgery in case of a severe mitral regurgitation due to chordal rupture or anterior mitral leaflet tear. Redo TAVI and, if necessary, redo PTMC (rarely needed) are options for lifetime management of patients with this combined rheumatic valve disease.

Conclusions

This case highlights the feasibility and success of simultaneous PTMC and TAVI in a patient with severe rheumatic MS and AS. By performing PTMC first, the procedural strategy ensured safety in case of potential mitral regurgitation, allowing a transition to surgery if needed. The use of a self-expandable THV provided excellent hemodynamic results in a small aortic annulus. At 9-month follow-up, the patient remains asymptomatic with well-functioning valves. This approach may serve as a viable alternative to surgery in select patients with combined rheumatic valve disease.
