Investigating the Similarity and the Combination of Muscle Synergies During Multi and Uniplanar Upper Limb Motions by Musculoskeletal Model: An Experimental and Computational Study
Authors: Morteza Farrokhnia, Seyyed Arash Haghpanah, Esmaeal Ghavanloo, Abbas Karami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsUnderstanding how the central nervous system generates muscle activation patterns for performing diverse movements remains a significant challenge in motor control. This study aims to evaluate the similarities and combinations of complex multiplanar upper limb movements in relation to the synergies of basic motions, using both experimental and computational approaches.MethodsThree healthy participants performed both proprioceptive neuromuscular facilitation (PNF) patterns and basic upper limb motions. Electromyography (EMG) and kinematic data were recorded simultaneously. A verified musculoskeletal (MS) model, calibrated with computed muscle control (CMC) results and processed EMG data, was used to estimate muscle activations. Muscle synergies and corresponding activation coefficients were extracted using non‐negative matrix factorization (NNMF), and variance accounted for (VAF) metrics were applied to determine the optimal number of synergies. Spearman's rank correlation and least squares optimization were employed to assess the similarity and combination of synergy patterns between motion types.ResultsFour muscle synergies were identified for both basic and complex movements. A shared synergy with correlation values exceeding 0.5 was observed across both types of movement. The composition of each complex motion synergy usually requires the merging of three basic motion synergies.ConclusionsThe findings suggest that complex upper limb movements are constructed through combinations of fundamental motor modules. This supports the concept of modular control in human motor coordination.Keywords: correlation coefficient, electromyography data, merging and fractionation, motor control

Introduction

Proprioceptive neuromuscular facilitation (PNF) patterns are complex multiplanar motions widely applied in rehabilitation after neurological disorders such as stroke and cerebral palsy [1]. These patterns are also used to improve upper extremity range of motion in patients with neural impairments [2], enhance muscle function, and increase joint stability [3]. Furthermore, PNF‐based training has been shown to improve postural balance in individuals with chronic ankle instability [4, 5, 6] and to positively influence balance and gait in stroke patients.

The central nervous system (CNS) simplifies the control of such complex movements by activating a reduced set of coordinated muscle activation patterns, known as muscle synergies [7, 8]. Through the recruitment of a small number of synergies, the CNS can generate a wide range of motor tasks [9]. While the determination of muscle coordination and activation patterns has been studied extensively, the underlying principles in multi‐planar upper limb motions remain incompletely understood [10].

Recent studies have expanded the understanding of muscle synergies in upper‐limb movements. For instance, Scano et al. provided a comprehensive spatial mapping of muscle synergies in highly variable upper‐limb movements, demonstrating that a limited number of synergies underlie a wide variety of tasks, even in contexts of high variability [11]. Similarly, Masiero et al. focused on wrist kinematics during daily activities, showing that wrist trajectories can be described using a few functional principal components, which could simplify robotic control and therapy design [12]. Furthermore, Baracca et al. analyzed upper‐limb movements in the Cartesian domain, revealing that certain spatial directions are associated with more complex motion evolution, which has implications for robotics and neuroscience [13]. Baracca et al. extended this work by estimating whole‐body muscular activation from an optimal set of scarce electromyographic recordings, providing insights into how limited EMG data can be used to reconstruct muscle activity patterns across the body [14].

In addition to kinematic studies, wearable systems have been developed to optimize sensing and track upper‐limb motion. Bonifati et al. introduced a multi‐modal under‐sensorized wearable system that effectively reconstructs the musculoskeletal state of the upper limb using minimal sensors, achieving high accuracy in joint and muscle state estimation [15]. Similarly, Rook et al. proposed an inverse synergy‐based approach for wearable electromyography systems, enabling accurate muscle activity estimation with reduced sensor requirements, and more recently, extended this concept to personalized musculoskeletal trunk models, demonstrating how inverse synergy‐based EMG methods can generalize across different anatomical regions for rehabilitation and robotics applications [16].

Dimension‐reduction techniques such as principal component analysis (PCA), independent component analysis (ICA), and non‐negative matrix factorization (NNMF) have been used to identify synergy structures. While PCA and ICA suffer from constraints such as orthogonality and statistical independence, NNMF offers the advantage of decomposing electromyographic (EMG) data into non‐negative temporal and spatial components [17], which aligns well with the physiological interpretation of muscle activation patterns.

Musculoskeletal modeling has further advanced the ability to study muscle coordination, particularly for deep or inaccessible muscles. Subject‐specific upper limb models [18, 19, 20] have been validated against experimental EMG data, enabling the estimation of muscle activation patterns across multiple joints and degrees of freedom. Saito et al. identified nine basic units of muscle synergies across 24 upper‐limb manipulation tasks, demonstrating how the CNS flexibly combines these units to meet different mechanical demands [21]. Similarly, Chen et al. explored synergetic similarities and dissimilarities in six upper‐limb movements, confirming the modular structure of the CNS and identifying shared and specific synergies [22]. Cai et al. analyzed muscle synergies during joystick manipulation tasks, showing how long‐term training improves manipulation performance and stability of synergy compositions [23]. Furthermore, Brambilla and Scano used OpenSim simulations to compare muscle synergies extracted from 12 versus 32 muscles, revealing that additional synergies capture patterns missed with fewer muscles [24]. Wang et al. investigated muscle synergies during robot‐assisted circle‐drawing tasks, revealing high structural similarity among healthy subjects and potential applications for motor function assessment [25]. Finally, Wei et al. conducted a systematic review on continuous motion intention prediction using sEMG for upper‐limb rehabilitation, emphasizing the integration of muscle synergy features for precise motion prediction [26].

These studies highlight the importance of synergy analysis in understanding motor control and improving rehabilitation protocols. However, the integration of synergy analysis into multi‐planar upper limb tasks, especially combining basic and complex patterns such as PNF, remains limited. The present study aims to fill this gap by investigating shared and specific synergies between basic shoulder motions (flexion/extension, abduction/adduction, and axial rotation) and two complex PNF patterns (type 1 and type 2). EMG and kinematic data were experimentally recorded, and a validated musculoskeletal (MS) model—verified via computed muscle control (CMC) and EMG comparison—was used to estimate activations of additional muscles. Synergies were extracted using the NNMF method, and their similarities and combination coefficients were quantified using correlation analyses. Understanding these relationships provides insight into how the CNS organizes upper limb motor control and can guide the development of targeted neurorehabilitation protocols. In view of the above remarks, we have pursued three main objectives in this paper:

Methods

Experimental Protocol

In this study, three right‐handed subjects (three men; range 25–35) participated. All participants had no musculoskeletal disorders and no surgery in the upper extremities. In addition, they had no neurological and musculoskeletal disorders and were informed about the consent to the works in experiments. The experiments were designed for three basic motions and two complex motions. The basic tasks included flexion/extension, abduction/adduction and shoulder axial rotation, and the complex motions are PNF type 1 and type 2. The Schematic of PNF patterns is shown in Figure 1.

Schematic of PNF patterns. a) Diagonal 1 b) Diagonal 2.

The EMG signals were recorded by a 10‐channel TrignoTM Wireless EMG system at 1000 Hz (Delsys Inc., Natick, MA, USA.) equipped with parallel‐bar Ag/AgCl electrodes with a fixed inter‐electrode distance of 10 mm. Ten muscles around the shoulder and scapula were recorded including anterior (AD), middle (MD), posterior deltoids (PD), infraspinatus (Infra), latismus dorsi (LD), pectoralis major clavicular head (PM‐clav), pectoralis major sternal head (PM‐stern), superior trapezius (TS), and Biceps (Bic). These muscles are superficial and dominant [19]. The EMG sensors were positioned at the midpoint of each muscle in accordance with the Surface Electromyography for the Non‐Invasive Assessment of Muscles (SENIAM) instructions [27]. All the original EMG signals are preprocessed to extract the effective EMG signals. The Butterworth high‐pass filter (fourth order and cutoff frequency of 50 Hz) was applied to raw EMG signals to filter and then rectify. The Butterworth low‐pass filter (fourth order and cutoff frequency of 4 Hz) was applied to obtain the EMG envelope. Then each EMG channel was normalized with respect to the maximum observed values [22]. The kinematic data were measured by 12 cameras (ProReflex, QualisysR Ltd., Sweden) at 100 Hz sampling frequency to track the markers fixed to the skin. The twelve bony landmarks are incisura jugularis (IJ), processus xiphoideus (PX), 7th cervical vertebra (C7), 8th thoracic vertebra (T8), sternoclavicular (SC), acromioclavicular (AC), angulus acromialis (AA), medial epicondyle (EM), lateral epicondyle (EL), radial styloid (RS), and ulnar styloid (US). The bony landmarks were attached to the skin based on the international society of biomechanics (ISB) [28]. Figure 2 shows the electromyography and the kinematic measurement device attached to the skin based on the protocol.

Kinematic and electromyography measurement devices in the experiment.

Upper Limb Musculoskeletal Model

In this study, thoracoscapular shoulder model developed by Seth et al. [19] and is validated by measured and processed EMG data. The advantage of using this model is the feasibility to be scaled to other subjects including the large thoracoscapular muscles and uncoupled movement of the scapula with respect to the humerus by defining the translation and rotation of the scapula on the simulated ellipsoid‐shaped surface of the thorax. Using the Qualysis cameras, each task was simulated in the OpenSim [29]. This model includes thorax, scapula, clavicle, and humerus (Figure 3), seven joints and thirty‐three muscle bundles. The scapulothoracic joint is modeled as the closed chain, and the scapula motion is modeled by the fixed scapula points constrained to an ellipsoid surface and it causes four degrees of freedom including scapula abduction/adduction, elevation/depression, upward rotation, and internal rotation (winging).

Spearman Correlation

To investigate the similarities, the Spearman correlation analysis is employed to assess the coefficient r and the p‐value to indicate the similarity and significance of synergies during basic and complex motions. The Spearman rank correlation is a criterion. It will be used when the relationship between variables is not linear. The Spearman rank correlation coefficient ranges from −1 to 1.

The p‐value associated with the Spearman rank correlation coefficient indicates the monotonic relationship existed between the two variables. If the p‐value is less than the significance level (0.05) there is a statistically significant monotonic relationship between two variables.

In line with prior validation studies, the main criterion for model validation was the temporal alignment and consistency of muscle activation profiles rather than the absolute amplitude scaling, which can be affected by inter‐individual variability and normalization procedures. To address this, we used the root mean square error (RMSE) as a quantitative metric, and an RMSE threshold of 0.2 has been widely considered acceptable for verifying musculoskeletal model predictions against EMG data [34, 35]. In our study, the RMSE values for all investigated motions were below this threshold (see Table 1), which indicates that the temporal patterns of the simulated and experimental activations were in good agreement. Therefore, despite some amplitude discrepancies, the overall alignment of activation timing and the RMSE values below 0.2 confirm an acceptable agreement between the musculoskeletal model and the experimental measurements.

Discussion

The similarity between synergy patterns and the activation coefficients is performed between the basic and complex motions by the correlation coefficients analysis to consider the special and shared synergies. Based on the results, it is realized that similar trends are seen in temporal patterns of basic and complex motion in different individuals as the CNS controls human movement by selecting different groups of muscles in similar synergy patterns in different types of motion. It means that the muscles cooperation may be different in the significantly similar synergy patterns between basic and complex motions that occur in multiplanes. The shared and the special synergies are recognized by applying the correlation coefficients between different types of motions. The correlation coefficient of the synergy pattern 3 (C3) is more than 0.4 in Table 2 for all groups of movements which indicates all movements share the synergy pattern of C3. The highly significant synergy pattern similarity (Table 2) has mostly existed between the group of PNF1‐Abd, module C1 has the correlation of 0.52 with 95% significant correlation and the module C4 has the correlation of 78% with 99% significant correlation. Also, the group of PNF2‐Abd and PNF ‐Flex have significant similarity in three modules and the group of PNF2‐Flex has significant similarity in two modules that showed the group of PNF1‐Abd has more similar patterns with respect to the other motions. In the following, the correlation coefficients were performed to consider the similarity of the activation coefficients in the shared and special synergies. Table 3 shows that the weight coefficient of synergy 3 (W3) has more correlation with other weight coefficients in different groups of motions. The dominant muscles of synergy 3 in PNF type 2 pattern are TS, SA, MD and the Infra. The SA and TS muscles cooperate with other muscles to preserve the scapula against the thorax to stabilize the upper limb motion under synergy 1 of the flexion motion and The MD and Infra muscles cooperate with other muscles in the concentric phase of upper limb motion under the synergy 3 of the abduction motion. Also, Triceps and T‐Major cooperate with other muscles in the eccentric phase of upper limb motion under the synergy 2 of the flexion and abduction motion. Therefore, the synergy 3 of PNF type 2 seems to be the combination of synergy 1 of the flexion motion and synergy 3 of the abduction motion. To investigate the merging coefficients systematically, the nonnegative least square optimization was used. The results in Table 4 show the merging coefficients of each complex motion synergy with respect to basic motion synergies as it shows synergy 3 of the PNF1 has been merged by synergy 4 of the flexion motion and synergy 2 of the abduction motion (Figure 14) and synergy 1 of the PNF1 has been merged with the low value activation coefficients of the basic motions.

According to the Table 2, Synergy 3 appears to serve as the shared synergy among the muscle synergy patterns. The similarities between muscle activity coefficients in synergy 3 of the PNF1 and PNF2 with other synergies of basic motions in Table 3 demonstrate the combination of basic motions synergies according to the Table 4.

On the other hand, Table 3 demonstrates that the muscle activation weighting coefficients of Synergy 1 in PNF1 and PNF2 are similar to those observed in fewer number of basic motion synergies. Furthermore, the combination coefficients reported in Table 4 are comparatively lower values for Synergy 1, which indicates of being special Synergy in PNF1. As Table 4 shows, the synergies weight coefficients of complex motions may be merged by three, two of the basic synergies or the other basic motions in addition to flexion, abduction, and axial rotation motions.

The focus of this study was primarily on developing and validating the proposed methodology, rather than conducting a statistical or population‐level analysis. Specifically, we aimed to investigate whether complex upper limb movements could be decomposed into combinations of primitive movements at the level of muscle synergies and movement patterns. Although the small sample size may limit the extent to which the findings can be generalized to a broader population, it enabled us to perform detailed, subject‐specific evaluations and refine the model under controlled experimental conditions. These results serve as an initial proof‐of‐concept and provide a foundation for future studies involving larger and more diverse participant groups to validate and extend the applicability of the proposed approach.

While subject‐specific scaling and experimental data integration were applied, the modeling framework may still not fully capture the variability in coactivation patterns across individuals. This reduced variability could influence synergy extraction results, potentially leading to an oversimplification of neural control strategies. Such limitations should be considered when interpreting the generalizability of the findings. Also, measuring all muscle activations is challenging due to crosstalk and the presence of deep muscles. Therefore, an optimization approach was used to estimate all muscle activations involved in upper limb motions.

Conclusion

The purpose of this study was to investigate the similarity and the combination of synergies under different movements. The muscle synergies from three basic and two complex upper limb motions were extracted by the verified musculoskeletal model to compute the muscle activations. Four synergy modules under the complex and basic upper extremity movements were discovered and were appropriate to discover the EMG signal characteristics. The shared and special synergies were identified by applying the Spearman correlation between synergy patterns and activation coefficients. The 3rd synergy of the complex motions was recognized as the shared synergy and the group pf PNF2‐Abd had the highest similarity between all groups of motions based on the correlation analysis. Therefore, the merging coefficients of the activations in basic motion synergies were computed to compose the muscle activations in complex motion synergies. Based on the results, the activation coefficients of the most complex motion synergies are the combination of the basic synergies that are considered in the research. Therefore, the nervous system employs combinations of shared and specific synergies to generate diverse motions, which can be applied in prosthetic control and neurorehabilitation. Future studies should involve larger and more diverse cohorts, examine a broader range of multi‐planar and functional upper‐limb tasks, and integrate advanced musculoskeletal modeling with wearable sensing technologies. In addition, longitudinal clinical studies are needed to translate synergy‐based metrics into individualized rehabilitation protocols and to assess the effectiveness of interventions such as PNF‐based therapy and robotic assistance.
