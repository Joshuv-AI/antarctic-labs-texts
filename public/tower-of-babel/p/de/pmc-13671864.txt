Myelinated Retinal Nerve Fiber Layer Mimicking Retinal Detachment in a Patient with Vitreous Hemorrhage: A Case Report
Authors: Bo Ree Kim, Soo Chang Cho
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

The myelinated retinal nerve fiber layer (MRNFL) is a benign anomaly in which the retinal nerve fibers anterior to the lamina cribrosa are myelinated. Optic nerve myelination does not extend beyond the lamina cribrosa, as local tissue factors prevent oligodendrocyte migration and myelination beyond the prelaminar space [1]. MRNFL occurs in 0.57% to 1% of the population and appears as white, feathery patches on the retinal surface [2]. When vitreous hemorrhage (VH) obscures fundus visualization, the MRNFL may be misinterpreted as retinal detachment (RD), complicating the differential diagnosis.

We report a case of a 55-year-old female patient with no relevant medical or trauma history who presented to our hospital with a 1-day history of blurred vision in her right eye. Five days earlier, the patient developed floaters and visited clinic A, where no specific abnormalities were observed. One day before presentation, the patient visited clinic B because of blurred vision and was suspected of having RD beneath VH (Fig. 1A). A few hours before presentation, the patient visited clinic C, where superior RD was suspected, and the patient was referred to our institution for emergency surgery. Dilated fundus examinations were performed at both clinics B and C. Written informed consent for the publication of the research details and clinical images was obtained from the patient.

Initial, intraoperative, and postoperative findings in the right eye of a 55-year-old female patient with myelinated retinal nerve fiber layer (MRNFL) accompanied by dense vitreous hemorrhage (VH), mimicking retinal detachment (RD). (A) One day before presentation to our hospital, fundus photography (FP) performed at clinic B revealed dense VH in the right eye. A yellowish-white lesion (arrow) in the superotemporal mid-periphery and the optic disc (arrowhead) were faintly visualized. (B) At the initial visit to our hospital, FP revealed a yellowish-white lesion (arrow) in the superotemporal mid-periphery, partially obscured by VH. (C) Ultrasonography at the initial visit revealed no definite evidence of RD. (D) An optical coherence tomography (OCT) scan through the fovea revealed no abnormal findings at the initial visit. (E) An OCT scan through the superotemporal yellowish-white lesion revealed a thickened, hyperreflective retinal nerve fiber layer (arrow) at the initial visit. (F, G) Two weeks after the initial visit, FP revealed worsening VH, graded as grade 4, and ultrasonography revealed increased vitreous opacity without evidence of RD. (H) Intraoperative findings during vitrectomy revealed focal vitreoretinal traction over the retinal vessels in the MRNFL (arrow). The intraoperative video still was rotated 180° to maintain consistency in the superior–inferior orientation across the figure panels. (I–K) Fifteen months after vitrectomy, FP revealed a clear view of the MRNFL (arrow), and the retina remained stable and attached. Endolaser photocoagulation scars around the focal traction site (arrowhead) were also identified. OCT scans through the fovea showed no abnormal findings. (L) Sixteen months after vitrectomy, fluorescein angiography revealed localized hyperfluorescence from endolaser photocoagulation scars, without other retinal vascular abnormalities in the MRNFL. The arrow indicates the endolaser photocoagulation scars.

At the initial examination, the best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was 0.2. Slit-lamp examination revealed moderate cataract, and fundus examination was limited by VH and cataract. A yellowish-white lesion was observed in the superotemporal mid-periphery. Careful dilated fundus examination through relatively clear media revealed normally coursing, non-tortuous retinal vessels overlying the lesion. The retina appeared flat (Fig. 1B). Ultrasonography showed no evidence of RD (Fig. 1C). Optical coherence tomography (OCT) scans of the visible portion of the lesion revealed a thickened, hyperreflective retinal nerve fiber layer (Fig. 1D, 1E). We diagnosed the patient with VH accompanied by an MRNFL. We inferred that the MRNFL beneath the VH was misinterpreted as bullous RD at the referral clinics. The patient was closely monitored for spontaneous absorption of VH.

Two weeks after the initial visit, the BCVA decreased to finger counting, and the VH worsened (Fig. 1F). Ultrasonography revealed increased vitreous opacity without RD (Fig. 1G). As the VH worsened, the patient underwent vitrectomy with VH removal, cataract surgery, and endolaser photocoagulation 15 days after the initial visit. Intraoperatively, no retinal tears or detachments were observed. Although no definite bleeding focus was identified, focal vitreoretinal traction over the retinal vessels was observed in the MRNFL (Fig. 1H), likely causing the VH. At the 15-month postoperative follow-up, the uncorrected visual acuity was 1.0, and the retina remained stable (Fig. 1I–1K). Fluorescein angiography showed localized hyperfluorescence from endolaser photocoagulation scars without other abnormal vascular findings (Fig. 1L).

This report involved a patient who was referred for surgical intervention after RD was suspected at two clinics following dilated fundus examination. This demonstrates that the MRNFL with dense VH can mimic RD. Although several reports have described the coexistence of the MRNFL and VH [3–5], to the best of our knowledge, no prior report has described the MRNFL with dense VH mistaken for RD. Retinal vascular abnormalities in the MRNFL area, such as telangiectasia, branch retinal vessel occlusion, and neovascularization may lead to VH [3–5]. However, none of these vascular abnormalities were identified in our report. Instead, intraoperative findings suggested that VH may have resulted from vitreoretinal traction near retinal vessels.

Careful dilated fundus examination is essential for differentiating MRNFL with dense VH from RD, while multimodal imaging plays a complementary role. Meticulous dilated fundus examination provides important diagnostic clues. In non-obscured areas, findings such as a characteristic yellowish-white lesion, a relatively flat retina, and normally coursing, non-tortuous retinal vessels may help exclude RD, as in this case. Multimodal imaging is also valuable. Ultrasonography is useful for detecting RD; however, false-negative results should be considered. Even with VH, serial OCT imaging through the visible areas can provide additional evidence by showing a thickened, hyperreflective retinal nerve fiber layer, thereby supporting a diagnosis of MRNFL rather than RD.

In conclusion, MRNFL with dense VH can mimic RD. Careful examination of the dilated fundus is essential for an accurate differential diagnosis, with multimodal imaging serving a complementary role.
