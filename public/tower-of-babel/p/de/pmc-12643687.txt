Evaluation of portal venous flow velocity before and after direct acting antiviral therapy in chronic hepatitis C: A cohort study
Authors: Hale Çolakoğlu Er, Ramazan Erdem Er, Abdullah Emre Yildirim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to evaluate the changes in portal venous flow velocity before and after direct-acting antiviral (DAA) therapy in patients diagnosed with chronic hepatitis C (CHC). A total of 53 CHC patients (36 males, 17 females; mean age 61.8 ± 7.7 years) treated with DAA at the Gastroenterology Department of our institution were included, along with 55 healthy controls matched by age and sex. Portal vein flow velocity was measured using Doppler ultrasonography, before and after treatment. Biochemical parameters, aspartate aminotransferase to platelet ratio, and fibrosis-4 scores were also analyzed. The mean pretreatment portal vein velocities in patients before treatment were 12.1 m/s and 17.5 m/s in the control healthy individuals (P < .001). DAA therapy resulted in a 100% sustained viral response. The posttreatment velocity increased significantly to 18 m/s (P < .001). Aspartate aminotransferase and alanine aminotransferase levels were significantly decreased (P < .001), while serum albumin levels were increased (P = .008). Although the platelet count increased, the change was not statistically significant (P = .232). Significant improvements were observed in aspartate aminotransferase to platelet ratio and fibrosis-4 scores (P < .001). The cirrhotic and non-cirrhotic subgroups demonstrated significant increases in portal flow velocity and decreased fibrosis values. DAA therapy in CHC patients also improved portal hemodynamics and noninvasive fibrosis markers, suggesting a broader impact on liver health and portal circulation.

1. Introduction

The introduction of direct-acting antiviral agents (DAAs) has revolutionized the treatment of chronic hepatitis C virus (HCV) infections.[1] These agents interfere with the viral replication cycle and have largely replaced interferon-based therapies that are less effective and often poorly tolerated. DAAs achieve sustained virologic response (SVR) rates exceeding 90%, even in treatment-naïve patients, and offer advantages such as oral administration and shorter treatment durations. HCV treatment has evolved from interferon-based regimens, which relied on stimulating the host immune response, to targeted antiviral approaches. Interferon and ribavirin combinations were once the standard of care but were associated with modest efficacy and significant adverse effects. DAAs, in contrast, directly inhibit viral proteins such as the NS3/4A protease, NS5A replication complex, and NS5B RNA-dependent RNA polymerase, effectively blocking viral replication. The combination of DAAs targeting different sites within the HCV life cycle results in high cure rates, shorter treatment durations, and better tolerability. Understanding these therapeutic mechanisms provides a crucial background for evaluating posttreatment changes in hepatic hemodynamics and fibrosis regression. DAAs are categorized into several classes according to their molecular targets: NS3/4A protease inhibitors (such as simeprevir and glecaprevir), NS5A inhibitors (including ledipasvir, daclatasvir, and velpatasvir), and NS5B polymerase inhibitors (such as sofosbuvir). In non-cirrhotic and treatment-naïve patients, short-duration combinations of DAAs often achieve cure rates above 95%. In contrast, cirrhotic or previously treated individuals may require extended treatment durations or the inclusion of multiple DAA classes to prevent relapse. The selection and combination of DAA regimens are therefore tailored according to the degree of liver fibrosis and treatment history to maximize therapeutic outcomes.[1–3]

Ultrasonography and Doppler ultrasonography are noninvasive and widely used imaging modalities for evaluating liver diseases, including those in patients with chronic HCV.[4,5] Portal hypertension is a common complication of CHC (chronic hepatitis C), and measuring portal vein flow velocity has been proposed as a useful tool for assessing disease severity and monitoring treatment effects. Although Doppler studies have evaluated interferon-based regimens, there is limited literature assessing portal hemodynamics before and after DAA treatment. This study aimed to assess changes in portal vein velocity and noninvasive fibrosis scores in CHC patients receiving DAA therapy.

2. Materials and methods

Patients aged between 18 and 75 years who were diagnosed with CHC infection confirmed by positive HCV RNA and who received a complete course of DAA therapy were included in this study. Only those with available pre- and posttreatment laboratory and Doppler ultrasonography data and without co-infection with hepatitis B virus or hepatitis delta virus were enrolled. The control group consisted of age- and sex-matched healthy individuals with normal liver function tests, negative viral hepatitis markers, and no history of chronic liver disease. Patients under 18 years of age with a body mass index > 40, hepatocellular carcinoma, liver lesions, Wilson disease, autoimmune hepatitis, alcoholic liver disease, primary biliary cholangitis, primary sclerosing cholangitis, or co-infection with hepatitis B virus or hepatitis delta virus were excluded. As patients were invited to a follow-up after treatment completion, post treatment data collection was possible for all those with preexisting baseline values.

All patients provided written informed consent. The study was performed in accordance with the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the local ethics committee.

The diagnosis of liver cirrhosis was established based on a combination of clinical, biochemical, and imaging criteria. Imaging findings consistent with cirrhosis included an irregular liver surface, coarse parenchymal echotexture, and splenomegaly as observed on ultrasonography. In addition, the child–pugh classification was used to assess the degree of hepatic functional impairment in cirrhotic patients. The power analysis determined that the difference of 2.4 ± 3.9 in portal vein velocities between pre- and posttreatment in the hepatitis C group was statistically significant. Using G*power 3.1.9.2 software (HHU, Düsseldorf), with an alpha level (α) of 0.05 and a power of 80% (1-β), the minimum number of participants required in each group was calculated to be 43.

The HCV genotype was also determined and HCV RNA values were measured. Thirty-one patients were non-cirrhotic and 22 had child–pugh class A cirrhosis. HCV genotype 1a was found in 7 patients and genotype 1b in 46 patients. Five patients were treatment-naïve, and 48 had received prior ribavirin and pegylated interferon therapy. Twenty-six patients were treated with sofosbuvir–ledipasvir for 24 weeks), and 27 with ombitasvir–paritaprevir–ritonavir plus dasabuvir for 12 weeks. Patient characteristics are shown in Table 1.

Portal vein flow velocity was assessed using Doppler ultrasonography before and 24 weeks after the treatment. Biochemical markers, aspartate aminotransferase to platelet ratio (APRI), and fibrosis-4 (FIB-4) scores were evaluated pre- and posttreatment. SVR was confirmed by undetectable HCV RNA 24 weeks post-treatment.

Measurements were performed using a 1 to 6 MHz convex probe on a Siemens Acuson S2000 ultrasound device. Doppler ultrasound were conducted by an expert radiologist with 15 years of experience in abdominal and Doppler ultrasound. For portal blood flow velocity analysis, the gate size was set smaller than the lumen of the vessel to minimize noise. In addition, the angle of the ultrasound beam relative to the vessel was set to <60°. Doppler signals obtained by pulse-wave Doppler ultrasonography. The waveforms were traced between 2 cardiac cycles, and the mean velocities were automatically calculated. This procedure was repeated 5 times, and the mean velocity value was used. The examination commenced with Doppler ultrasound performed with patients in a supine position. In order to minimize bias, the radiologist responsible for performing the measurements was kept blind to patient clinical characteristics and laboratory findings.

Descriptive statistics were used for quantitative variables, including mean ± standard deviation, median, minimum, and maximum values, while frequency and percentage were used for qualitative variables. The Shapiro–Wilk test assessed normality. For normally distributed data, analysis of variance was conducted to compare the groups, while the Student t test evaluated qualitative variables across groups. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 21.0 for Windows (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY), with a P-value < .05 considered statistically significant.

This prospective study included 53 patients (mean age 61.8; 36 males, 17 females) diagnosed with CHC who received DAA therapy at the Gastroenterology Department of our institution over a 6 month period. Based on laboratory findings, clinical assessments, and imaging results, 31 patients were classified as non-cirrhotic, while 22 patients were diagnosed with child–pugh class A cirrhosis. Regarding HCV genotypes, 7 patients exhibited genotype A, and 46 patients exhibited genotype B. Prior to enrollment, 5 patients were treatment-naïve for HCV, whereas 48 patients had prior experience with ribavirin-pegylated interferon therapy. A subset of 26 patients received sofosbuvir–ledipasvir for a 24-week duration, and 27 patients were administered ombitasvir–paritaprevir–ritonavir with dasabuvir for 12 weeks. However, portal vein velocity was significantly lower in the patient group (12.12 ± 2.81) compared to the control group (19.04 ± 3.55, P < .001). Pretreatment, portal vein velocities in both non-cirrhotic (12.56 ± 2.89) and cirrhotic patient groups (11.51 ± 2.69) were significantly lower than those of healthy controls (19.04 ± 3.55, P < .001 for both). Following treatment, portal vein velocities in the non-cirrhotic group normalized to levels comparable with the control group (non-cirrhotic patient group: 18.76 ± 4.38; control group: 19.04 ± 3.55; P = .75). Although portal vein velocities in the cirrhotic group increased significantly compared to pretreatment values, they remained significantly lower than those of the control group (cirrhotic: 16.34 ± 4.45; control group: 19.04 ± 3.55; P < .007). 4. Discussion

In this prospective study, we demonstrated that DAA therapy in patients with CHC not only achieved a 100% SVR, but also resulted in significant improvements in portal venous hemodynamics and noninvasive fibrosis markers. The increase in portal vein flow velocity and the reduction in APRI and FIB-4 scores suggest that DAA therapy contributes to the reversal or regression of liver fibrosis and a decrease in intrahepatic vascular resistance.

Previous research has mainly emphasized the high SVR rates associated with DAAs and normalization of aminotransferase levels. While virologic suppression is a crucial goal, it is equally important to understand functional and structural hepatic recovery following viral eradication. Recent evidence suggests that hepatic inflammation and fibrosis may regress postSVR, leading to improvements in hepatic perfusion and portal hemodynamics.[6–9]

Portal hypertension, which is an important determinant of morbidity in liver disease, is usually assessed using the hepatic venous pressure gradient, which is invasive and not widely available. Doppler ultrasonography, particularly the measurement of portal vein velocity, offers a feasible alternative for estimating portal pressure changes noninvasively. In this study, we observed a statistically significant increase in portal vein velocity after DAA therapy, aligning with the hypothesis that fibrosis regression reduces vascular resistance and improves flow.

Studies using transient elastography and acoustic radiation force impulse imaging have also shown reduction in liver stiffness after DAA therapy, which may parallel fibrosis improvement.[10,11] However, stiffness measurements alone may be influenced by inflammation or congestion, making Doppler-based assessments a valuable adjunct.

Our subgroup analysis showed that even patients with child–pugh A cirrhosis had significant hemodynamic and biochemical improvements. This indicates that patients with compensated cirrhosis may benefit both virologically and physiologically from DAAs. Increased albumin levels in patients with cirrhosis likely reflect enhanced synthetic function and reduced hepatocellular stress.[12–15] Although the increase in platelet count was not statistically significant, a trend toward improvement was evident and may become more pronounced with longer follow-up.

One important aspect of this study was the use of portal vein velocity as a biomarker of response. Although underutilized, this parameter offers insight into intrahepatic circulation and could serve as a surrogate for early fibrosis reversal. Larger studies correlating portal hemodynamics with liver stiffness, histology, and clinical outcomes, such as variceal bleeding or decompensation, are warranted.

Notably, hemodynamic changes occurred within 24 weeks after completing the therapy. However, whether these improvements are sustained over time remains unclear. Longitudinal follow-up using multimodal imaging and biochemical indices would be valuable for assessing the long-term benefits of viral eradication.

Our study has some limitations. First, the patient population was small. Second, the patients did not undergo biopsy. Studies involving a larger patient population with biopsy confirmation will contribute to the understanding of this topic.

5. Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that DAA therapy in patients with chronic hepatitis C leads to a 100% SVR and significant improvements in portal venous hemodynamics, as evidenced by increased portal vein flow velocity. These changes are accompanied by improvements in noninvasive markers of liver fibrosis (APRI and FIB-4 scores).
