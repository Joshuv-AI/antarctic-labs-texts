Background

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a serious global health problem. According to The Lancet reports, the deaths of AMR reached more than 1.2 million people. If AMR is not remedied, it is estimated that deaths will be over 10 million people annually by 2050 [1]. Since progress in antimicrobial drug development has slowed, other solutions should be investigated [2]. The most notable ones are the use of metal-based nanoparticles and metal nanomaterials in aPDT/aPTT therapy. Among metallic nanoparticles, AgNPs have been recognized for their greater antimicrobial activity [2], the strongest localized surface plasmon resonances (LSPR) across a broad wavelength range, and high electrical conductivity [3]. Also, the biofunctionalization of AgNPs is widely employed in biomedical applications, including biosensing and therapy [4]. The interaction of metal nanoparticles with biomolecules, including oligonucleotides, is a significant point in nanobiotechnology. According to studies, DNA bases can act as molecular linkers and conjugate to AgNPs. The strength of conjugation of AgNPs with DNA bases is in the order of C (cytosine) > G (guanine) > A (adenine) > T (thymine). Exocyclic nitrogen of C, G, and A structures leads to a higher binding affinity of these three bases than T. The interaction between C and AgNPs is stronger than that between G and A. However, G and A can produce more canonical structures than ‘’C’’, after combining with AgNPs [5]. Another study reported the strengths of AgNPs’ interactions with DNA bases based on binding constants. According to this point, G interacts with AgNPs more strongly than other nucleobases [6].

Furthermore, guanine-rich DNA or RNA sequences innately tend to form noncanonical secondary structures called G-quadruplexes. These secondary structures are composed of two or more stacked square planar arrangements called “G-tetrad”. To generate a G-tetrad, four G bases are connected via Hoogsteen hydrogen bonding. G-quadruplex structures participate in biological functions, including the inhibition of DNA replication, transcription, and translation. Also, they are used as nanovehicles, antibacterial agents, DNAzymes, and detection probes in biosensors, such as photoelectrochemical biosensors [7, 8]. Poly-A was employed to design sensitive and low-cost electrochemical DNA biosensors [9]. Poly A has been used to bind AgNPs in a label-free electrochemical sensor to detect amyloid β oligomers, a biomarker for early diagnosis of Alzheimer’s disease [10]. AgNPs and AgNPs-based structures, such as AgNPs-conjugated oligonucleotides, can be applied as a reservoir of silver cations that release them to fight bacteria [11].

AgNPs exhibit antimicrobial properties comparable to those of their bulk counterparts, enabling effective targeting of diverse pathogenic microorganisms [12]. AgNPs are synthesized using various methods, including chemical, physical, and biogenic approaches. Chemical and physical processes are associated with several limitations, such as high cost and the need for high temperatures and pressures [13]. Biogenic methods are more reliable, environmental, quick, and efficient. Biosynthesized AgNPs exhibit different activities depending on the techniques and biomaterials used. Biological materials used to generate biogenic AgNPs include extracts of fungi, lichens, bacteria, and animal blood serum, as well as algae and various plant components, such as leaves and seeds [2, 13]. R.crispus is an abundant medicinal herb in Iran and other Asian countries. R.crispus contains flavonoids and anthraquinones. This plant possesses antioxidant, antifungal, and antimicrobial activities [14].

In this work, we synthesized AgNPs using a biological method with extracts from R. crispus seeds, called RC-AgNPs. Also, we synthesized AgNP-based nanoplatforms (RC-AgNP @ poly-G) by incubating RC-AgNPs at different concentrations with a constant poly-G concentration. We investigated the antibacterial activity of RC-AgNPs and RC-AgNP @ poly-G, against Escherichia coli, Pseudomonas aeruginosa (gram-negative strains), and Staphylococcus aureus (gram-positive strain). Also, the aPDT/aPTT effects of these nanostructures were evaluated. Based on the LSPR features of AgNPs, their antimicrobial effect, and their high binding affinity to poly (A) and poly (G), we can employ RC-AgNPs conjugates with poly (A) and poly (G) as electrochemical DNA biosensors and theranostic nanoplatforms in future work.

Materials and methods

Preparation of R.crispus seed extract, AgNPs biosynthesis, and RC-AgNP @ poly-G synthesis

R.crispus seeds were collected in the Khorramabad District, Tonekabon County, Mazandaran Province, Iran. This plant was recognized with the help of the PlantNet app [15, 16] (Supplementary Fig. S1). R.crispus has also been registered in the herbarium of the Faculty of Traditional Medicine, Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences and Health Services under voucher archive 3684. To prepare the extract, the plant seeds were boiled in distilled water and then filtered through standard filter paper. To generate RC-AgNPs, 200 milliliters (ml) of filtrate extract was blended with 200 ml of a prepared 2 millimolar (mM) AgNO3 solution at room temperature, and the final concentration of AgNO3 was 1 mM. To examine the appearance of AgNPs, we took photographs over a few days. To synthesize RC-AgNP @ poly-G, variable concentrations of RC-AgNPs were mixed with a constant concentration of poly-G (30 nucleotides). The prepared RC-AgNP @ poly-G nanostructures were incubated at room temperature for 3 h. After incubation, further analysis was accomplished. What is intended in this project is, first, to investigate the antibacterial effect of biosynthetic AgNPs at different concentrations. Then, the aim is to investigate the antibacterial effects of adding poly-G at a concentration of 0.5 µM to biosynthetic AgNPs. Concentrations of poly-G above or below 0.5 µM were not selected. Because higher concentrations require greater consumption of poly-G, and at lower concentrations, it is more difficult to manipulate.

Characterization

UV-visible spectroscopy analysis of RC-AgNPs and RC-AgNP @ poly-G was performed using a DeNovix DS-11 + spectrophotometer. The generation and stability of RC-AgNPs were monitored by measuring absorbance over 300–700 nm. The interaction of poly-G with RC-AgNPs to synthesize RC-AgNP @ poly-G was studied by Ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) spectroscopy. To determine the functional group of RC-AgNPs and RC-AgNP @ poly-G, FT-IR spectroscopy (model Perkin Elmer Spectrum RX1 FT-IR spectrophotometer) was performed in the 400–4000 cm− 1 range. The hydrodynamic size and surface charge of RC-AgNPs and RC-AgNP @ poly-G were measured by the SZ-100z Dynamic Light Scattering & Zeta potential analyzer (company, Horiba Jobin Yvon). The morphology of RC-AgNPs was identified by TEM (Philips CM-120). The crystalline structures of RC-AgNPs were investigated by XRD (Bruker D8 ADVANCE). The morphology of treated bacteria with RC-AgNPs was examined using SEM (AIS 2100, Seron Technology, Korea).

Entropy and temperature studies of RC-AgNP @ poly-G

For this subject, the poly-G secondary structure was investigated using the Vienna RNA secondary structure server [17], at 25°, 37°, and 40° C. Also, the UV-visible absorbance of RC-AgNP @ poly-G was measured at the indicated temperatures.

Antibacterial assay of nanostructures

The antibacterial effect of RC-AgNPs was tested in concentrations of 2.5, 5, 10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 µg/ml (µg/ml). The antibacterial effect of RC-AgNP @ poly-G was tested in a constant concentration of poly-G (0.5 micromolar (µM)) and RC-AgNPs in concentrations of 2.5 µg/ml, 5 µg/ml, 10 µg/ml, and 20 µg/ml. Antibacterial activity has been studied against E. coli (ATCC 43894), P. aeruginosa (ATCC 47085), and S. aureus COL. To evaluate the antimicrobial effect, we selected the turbidimetric determination, “Bacterial growth curve method [11]. Bacteria were inoculated into Luria-Bertani (LB) medium and incubated overnight at 37° C. After complete growth, the bacteria were adjusted to an OD of 0.1 and treated with the indicated concentrations of nanostructures. Bacterial turbidity was measured to OD600 nm after overnight incubation at 37°C.

PDT effect of nanostructures

We investigated the PDT effect of RC-AgNPs and RC-AgNP @ poly-G at the mentioned concentrations. Supplementary Fig. S2 shows the light source used for PDT. Bacteria with an OD600 of 0.1 were treated with different concentrations of nanostructures and incubated under photostimulation and non-photostimulation conditions. The irradiation intensity and duration (520 nm wavelength) were 4.75 milliwatts and 2 h, respectively. The OD600 of bacteria was measured after overnight incubation at 37° C. This test attempts to determine whether nanostructures synthesized without a photosensitizer have PDT properties. The bacteria were incubated at 37 °C for one to two hours before light irradiation to reach an OD600 of 0.1. Then, they were exposed to light for 2 h, and after overnight incubation, the OD of the bacteria was measured at time 20 of the antibacterial assay.

PTT effect of nanostructures

The PTT effect of RC-AgNPs and RC-AgNP @ poly-G was studied at the mentioned concentrations. Direct heat was used to induce PTT. The treated and untreated bacteria were incubated at 40 °C for 2 h. The bacteria were then incubated at 37 °C. The OD600 of bacteria was measured after overnight incubation. Then, the percentage of bacterial reduction in the PTT condition (40 °C) was compared with that in the control condition (non-PTT, 37 °C). It should be noted that the bacteria treated with nanostructures and the untreated bacteria were exposed to both 40 °C and 37 °C.

Statistical analysis

Prism software was employed to perform statistical analysis. All obtained data were presented as the mean ± standard deviation of the four replicates. One-way analysis of variance (p-value < 0.0001) and student’s t-test (*: 0.0201 ≤ p-value ≤ 0.041 **: 0.0011 ≤ p-value ≤ 0.0096 ***: 0.0003 ≤ p-value ≤ 0.0008) were carried out to compare the groups.

The first characterization method for nanoparticle formation is color change. Biosynthesis of RC-AgNPs was detected by changes in color from the yellow color of R. crispus seed extract to the yellowish-brown color of RC-AgNPs (Supplementary Fig. S3).

Formation of AgNPs was traced spectrophotometrically. The maximum wavelength (λ max) at 455–472 nm confirmed the production of nanoparticles.

to investigate RC-AgNPs stability, the UV-visible absorbance of biosynthesized nanoparticles was monitored over 99 days at different time intervals. Absorption increased in 99 days. The nanoparticles showed the maximum absorption on the last day (Supplementary Fig. S4). UV-visible spectroscopy analysis of RC-AgNPs @ poly-G absorption was measured at four concentrations of nanoparticles and 0.5 µM of poly-G. λ max of RC-AgNP @ poly-G were 440 and 450 nm in concentrations (2.5, 5, and 10 µg/ml) and 20 µg/ml of RC-AgNPs, respectively (Fig. 1).

Discussion

To investigate the appearance of RC-AgNPs, images of the nanoparticle solution were captured over a few days. The color of the R. crispus extract solution changed after AgNO3 was added. (Supplementary Fig. S3) shows color changes after 1 h, 6 h, 1 day, and 2 days, indicating the presence of AgNPs.

The plasmonic properties of AgNPs are followed by UV-visible spectroscopy analysis. The peak between 400 and 500 nm is the surface plasmon resonance (SPR) of AgNPs. SPR absorption spectroscopy reveals the chemical, physical, and optical properties of nanoparticles, such as size and stability. Sizeable particles have broader peaks, whereas tinier particles have sharper peaks [13, 19]. SPR spectrum of RC-AgNPs was a broad peak with λmax between 455 and 472 nm. The stability of RC-AgNPs was assessed for 99 days. The absorbance peak increased over time. The absorption peak remained almost constant from the 13th day to the 99th day (Supplementary Fig. S4).

UV-visible spectroscopy analysis of RC-AgNP @ poly-G is shown in Fig. 1. Oligonucleotides can be used as templates for nanoparticle organization [5]. Absorption peaks of RC-AgNP @ poly-G have changed in every four concentrations of RC-AgNPs (2.5, 5, 10, and 20 µg/ml). Changes in the peaks of RC-AgNPs @ poly-G relative to RC-AgNPs and poly-G confirmed the interaction of poly-G with RC-AgNPs. Also, it shows that poly G has been applied as a scaffold for the accumulation of biosynthetic AgNPs.

Figure 2 and Supplementary Fig. S5 present the temperature and entropy results for RC-AgNP @ poly-G. Temperature variations can alter SPR spectra [19]. In this study, the maximum absorbance at 25°, 37°, and 40° C has been varied while the λmax remains constant at each temperature (Supplementary Fig. S5). In computational studies, the stability of the poly-G secondary structure was investigated using MFE and centroid structures. In other words, MFE and the centroid method are dynamic programming algorithms for the evaluation and prediction of the optimal structures of biomolecules, such as the prediction of the most favorable secondary structure of ssDNA and ssRNA [17]. The thermodynamic stability and entropy of the hairpin probes used in a biosensor designed in 2022 were evaluated. Results from the Vienna RNA secondary structure server indicated that the hairpin probes were stable [20]. Figure 2 represents computational studies. According to the MFE structure (Fig. 2a) and centroid structure (Fig. 2a), the free energy was − 0.00 kcal/mol at 25, 37, and 40° C. Consequently, the poly-G secondary structure remained stable at these three temperatures. Based on these results, the effect of temperature variations on the absorbance of RC-AgNPs @ poly-G can be attributed to the RC-AgNPs.

Furthermore, the stability of poly-G demonstrates that this oligonucleotide can serve as a supporting framework across these three distinct temperatures. In other words, these 3 temperatures do not reduce the capacity of poly-G to host RC-AgNPs. However, due to the existence of a G-rich strand of the oligonucleotide, the potential of G-quadruplex formation was examined at 25, 37, and 40 °C, using the prediction algorithm of the MFE method. G-quadruplex secondary structures are represented in a positional entropy form (Fig. 2c). Based on the thermodynamic prediction results, the free energy of the thermodynamic ensemble at 25, 37, and 40° C was − 110.43, -90.85, and − 85.96 kcal/mol, respectively. According to the MFEs, the most stable state and the lowest entropy of the G-quadruplex was observed at 25° C. The MFEs obtained from (Fig. 2c) are more stable than the MFEs obtained from (Fig. 2a), indicating the tendency of poly-G to form a G-quadruplex. Consequently, the probability of G-quadruplex formation increases at all three temperatures within RC-AgNP @ poly-G. G-quadruplexes can serve as scaffolds for nanoparticle assembly [21]. According to the results of the antibacterial assays described below, RC-AgNPs exhibit a low antibacterial effect at low concentrations, such as 2.5 µg/ml. The interaction of these nanoparticles with poly-G increases antibacterial efficacy at lower concentrations. In other words, one factor contributing to the reduction in the effective antibacterial concentration can be the potential for G-quadruplex formation.

According to the FT-IR graph, the R. crispus seed extract spectrum shows 1636.73 cm− 1 shifted to 1630.29 and 1638.85 cm− 1 in the RC-AgNPs and RC-AgNP @ poly-G spectra, respectively. These wave numbers are in the regions 1632–1638 and 1630–1680 cm− 1, corresponding to the NH2 groups of the amide I band [22] and C = O stretching of the amide I band [23], respectively. In the RC-AgNPs spectrum, the appearance band around 3446.47 cm− 1 was attributed to the N-H absorption peak [24]. The peak at 3446.47 cm− 1 shifted to 3427 cm− 1 in the RC-AgNP @ poly-G spectrum, corresponding to O–H or N–H groups [25]. Peak at 2371.18 cm− 1, assigned to O = C=O stretching vibration [26], and 2926.62 cm− 1 disappeared in RC-AgNP @ poly-G spectrum, (Fig. 3a).

According to the XRD assay, the general peak corresponds to the silver (Ag0) standard XRD card of JCPDS 04-0783 and the Chlorargyrite (AgCl) standard XRD card of JCPDS 31-1238 with cubic structures in (Fig. 3b).

Based on TEM analysis (Fig. 4a and b), RC-AgNPs manifest spherical shapes. Some of them have irregular shapes (Fig. 4a). The size distribution histogram of the TEM image (Fig. 4b) presents the average core size of RC-AgNPs.

ZP and DLS measurements of RC-AgNPs and RC-AgNP @ poly-G have been shown in (Fig. 4c and d). The average hydrodynamic size of RC-AgNP @ poly-G is higher than the average hydrodynamic size of RC-AgNP, which confirms the interaction of poly-G with RC-AgNP. PDIs more than 0.1 indicate polydispersity of nanoparticles. PDIs less than 0.1 are monodisperse nanoparticles [27]. According to the PDI presented in (Fig. 4c), RC-AgNPs and RC-AgNP @ poly-G are polydisperse. Given the positive ZP of RC-AgNPs, they can be investigated as nanocarriers in future nano-drug delivery systems, such as gene delivery. Also, studies have shown that cationic AgNPs exhibit potent antibacterial activity against all bacterial strains [28], confirming the results of the antimicrobial assays reported here.

Antibacterial assays of RC-AgNPs have shown greater efficacy against gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria at high concentrations of biogenic AgNPs (20, 30, 40, and 50 µg/ml). RC-AgNPs have a less antibacterial effect in low concentrations (2.5, 5, and 10 µg/ml) on every three bacteria (Fig. 5). PDT and PTT performances of RC-AgNPs were evaluated on the rate of antibacterial effect enhancement.

The PDT effect of nanoparticles increased antibacterial effect against E. coli (in concentrations 20, 30, and 40 µg/ml) and P. aeruginosa (in concentration 20 µg/ml) (Fig. 6). In S. aureus, %BR didn’t increase in any concentrations. This effect could be attributed to the differences in the cell walls of gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria. Gram-positive bacteria possess a thick layer of peptidoglycan (~ 20–80 nm) in the cell wall, while gram-negative bacteria have a cell wall with a thin layer of peptidoglycan (~ 7–8 nm) [29].

RC-AgNPs exhibited low antibacterial activity at concentrations of 2.5, 5, and 10 µg/ml. RC-AgNP @ poly-G, an AgNP-based nanoplatform, showed significant antibacterial activity in the mentioned concentrations. RC-AgNP @ poly-G showed enhanced antibacterial activity at 20 µg/ml.

The antibacterial effect was studied at a constant poly-G concentration (0.5 µM) and variable RC-AgNPs concentrations (2.5, 5, 10, and 20 µg/ml). RC-AgNP @ poly-G enhanced the %BR in all three bacteria, E. coli (the concentrations of 5 and 20 µg/ml), S. aureus (the concentrations of 2.5, 5, 10, and 20 µg/ml), and P. aeruginosa (the concentrations of 5, 10, and 20 µg/ml) (Fig. 7).

PTT performance of RC-AgNPs increased %BR in all three bacteria, E. coli (the concentrations of 20, 30, and 40 µg/ml), S. aureus (the concentrations of 20 and 30 µg/ml), and P. aeruginosa (the concentration of 20 µg/ml) (Fig. 8a, b, and c). According to reports, a temperature increase increases the rate of Ag+ release from AgNPs. Ag+ induces oxidative stress by generating endogenous reactive oxygen species; it has efficient antibacterial activity [30].

The PDT and PTT performances of RC-AgNP @ poly-G were tested. AgNPs have been thoroughly examined because of their distinctive properties, such as photothermal, optical, and electrical [31], LSPR, surface-enhanced Raman scattering, antibacterial, and antifungal [32]. The PDT effect of RC-AgNP @ poly-G did not affect the %BR in the used bacteria. PTT performance of RC-AgNP @ poly-G just revealed antibacterial activity against E. coli (the concentrations of 2.5 and 5 µg/ml) (Fig. 8d). Poly-G was applied as a polymeric material for the accumulation of biogenic AgNPs and increased antibacterial effect at concentrations of 2.5, 5, 10, and 20 µg/ml. In general, PDT can exert its effects through two distinct mechanisms, namely type I and type II. In the type II mechanism, the photosensitizer is promoted to the singlet excited state (S1) upon absorbing light at a specific wavelength, and then to the long-lived triplet excited state (T1) via intersystem crossing (ISC). In this case, energy transfer from the triplet photosensitizer to ground-state molecular oxygen produces singlet oxygen. In contrast, the type I mechanism involves the transfer of electrons from the excited photosensitizer (in the singlet or triplet state) to molecular oxygen. The type I mechanism can generate reactive oxygen species and other free radicals. These reactive oxygen species induce damage to vital cellular components, leading to cellular damage and ultimately to the death of target cells [33].

Poly-G as a scaffold induces the accumulation of RC-AgNPs at low concentrations, thereby elevating the efficacy of Ag+ release and, consequently, enhancing antibacterial activity while reducing the effective antibacterial concentration. Consequently, RC-AgNP @ poly-G can be regarded as a patient-oriented nanostructure with minimal toxicity. Also, given the affinity of AgNPs for nucleotide G [5], poly-G can act as a nanocarrier for these biosynthetic AgNPs.

Studies have shown that the interaction of AgNPs with polymeric materials offers benefits, including low cytotoxicity, controlled release of Ag+ ions, and strong antibacterial activity [28]. Also, AgNPs can eliminate bacterial membrane permeability and disrupt their structure [34]. According to findings from entropy and temperature studies of RC-AgNP @ poly-G, the possibility of G-quadruplex formation within RC-AgNP @ poly-G is considerably elevated. The possibility of G-quadruplex formation may be another factor contributing to the augmentation of the antibacterial effect. Based on extensive research, the G-quadruplex can reduce DNA replication, transcription, and translation [8].

SEM analysis was performed to investigate the morphological effects of the treated bacteria. RC-AgNPs exhibited high antibacterial performance at a concentration of 40 µg/ml against E. coli, S. aureus, and P. aeroginosa. The %BR was %62.65, %89.99, and %92.42 in E. coli, S. aureus, and P.aeroginosa, respectively (Fig. 5). SEM images of P. aeroginosa confirmed the %BR of %92.42 against these bacteria. Figure 4e and f show untreated P. aeroginosa, which is flawless and intact. In comparison, RC-AgNPs (at a concentration of 40 µg/ml) showed a bactericidal effect against P. aeroginosa (Fig. 4f).

In this study, R. crispus seeds were used to synthesize AgNPs. According to a previous study published as a conference paper, the plant’s seeds have a greater antimicrobial effect than its leaves, roots, or stems. They also showed greater stability over 92 days than the plant’s leaves [35]. For this reason, the plant’s seeds were used to synthesize AgNPs. In recent years, no article has reported the use of R. crispus seeds for the biosynthesis of AgNPs. However, in a 2020 conference paper, R. crispus leaves were used for the biosynthesis of AgNPs, and their antibacterial activity was investigated [36]. In this study, we examined the antibacterial effect of biosynthetic AgNPs and analyzed their interaction with poly-G. As mentioned, AgNPs can accumulate on poly-G due to their affinity for G nucleotides [5]. AgNPs-conjugated oligonucleotides can serve as a reservoir of silver cations, releasing them to fight bacteria [11].

Based on entropy and temperature studies of RC-AgNP @ poly-G, we investigated the possibility of G-quadruplexes formation. The results indicated that poly-G tends to form G-quadruplexes (Fig. 2). G-quadruplexes can be used as nanocarriers for AgNPs [7, 8]. Based on the antibacterial results (Fig. 7), RC-AgNP @ poly-G showed significantly higher %BR than RC-AgNP, especially against S. aureus. That is, the interaction of poly-G with RC-AgNP provided a significant antibacterial effect at lower concentrations. In recent studies, no paper was found in which AgNPs were synthesized with R. crispus seeds and interacted with poly-G. However, in 2014, a study used poly-G as an activator for silver nanoclusters [37]. In this study, we tested aPTT using direct heating rather than near-infrared (NIR) light. In general, in PTT, the photothermal agent increases local temperature upon irradiation and ultimately causes cell damage.

In most studies, near-infrared (NIR) light was used for PTT [38]. RC-AgNPs showed aPTT effect against E. coli, S. aureus, and P. aeruginosa at 40 °C, while RC-AgNP @ poly-G showed aPTT effect only against E. coli at this temperature. It should be noted that the growth of untreated bacteria with nanostructures was not affected by raising the temperature from 37° to 40 °C. At the same time, an aPTT effect was observed in bacteria treated with RC-AgNPs. Therefore, the observed decrease in bacterial growth at 40 °C can be attributed to the added nanoparticles. In other words, by increasing the temperature by 3 degrees, the nanostructures raised the local temperature. Using increased temperature rather than near-infrared (NIR) light eliminates the need for a laser for light irradiation, making the approach economically viable (Fig. 8 and supplementary Fig. S6). Another critical issue in this project’s PTT case, which used direct heat, is that studies have shown that direct heat can also activate the immune system, which helps treat infections. In one study, heat stress was shown to induce innate immune responses and significantly reduce the intracellular survival of Brucella abortus in vitro and in vivo. Heat exposure also increased pro-inflammatory cytokines, reactive oxygen species accumulation, and apoptosis [39]. The aPDT effect of the synthesized nanostructures was also investigated. RC-AgNPs alone (without combining with other photosensitizers) showed aPDT effect against E. coli and P. aeruginosa. According to most studies, AgNPs have been used in combination with other photosensitizers for PDT [38].

A point should be made about the nanoparticles’ size. The average size obtained from TEM for biogenic AgNPs, namely RC-AgNPs, is 27.13 ± 11.63 nm, and the average hydrodynamic size of these nanoparticles is 214.33 ± 2.211 nm. The ratio of these two average sizes is approximately 8 times. The 8-fold increase in the average hydrodynamic size relative to the TEM-observed average size can be attributed to the presence of biomolecules in the plant seed extract used, which can act as a coating agent for the nanoparticles. In various studies, phytochemicals in plant extracts, especially those from seeds, have been used as coating agents [40].

In the continuation of this project and in future work, these designed nanostructures will be tested in cancer cell lines, including MCF-7 cells, to investigate the effects of these nanostructures and PDT on telomerase activity. The telomerase is activated in cancer cells, thereby inhibiting telomere shortening [41].

Conclusions

To conclude, this study demonstrated the use of biological methods to produce nanostructures to combat AMR. Biogenic AgNPs were produced from R. crispus seed extracts. Due to the binding affinity of DNA bases to AgNPs, AgNPs-mediated hybrid nanostructures were designed via the interaction of poly-G with biogenic AgNPs. The antibacterial properties of these nanostructures were investigated at different concentrations against gram-negative and gram-positive bacteria. The PDT and PTT effects of these nanoparticles were also evaluated. RC-AgNPs exhibited potent antibacterial activity at high concentrations (20, 30, 40, and 50 µg/ml). They exhibit synergistic antibacterial activity against gram-negative and gram-positive strains. Whereas the PDT performance of RC-AgNPs increased the %BR against gram-negative strains. The antibacterial activity of RC-AgNPs was low in concentrations of 2.5, 5, and 10 µg/ml. In comparison, RC-AgNPs @ poly-G had an enhancing effect at these concentrations. Due to the LSPR feature, strong interaction with DNA bases, and the low toxicity of biogenic AgNPs, RC-AgNPs-conjugated oligonucleotides are promising candidates for future studies as nanotheranostic platforms for the treatment and diagnosis of infectious and non-infectious diseases.
