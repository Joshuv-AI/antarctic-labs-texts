Prognostic factors and prognostic risk score of 30-day all-cause mortality in thyroid storm
Authors: Pakpoom Chantrapitak, Worapaka Manosroi, Sittikul Thipbumrung, Pichitchai Atthakomol
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThyroid storm is a life-threatening endocrine emergency with high mortality. Existing diagnostic score primarily focuses on diagnosis rather than predicting outcomes. This study aimed to identify prognostic factors and develop a practical clinical prognostic score for 30-day all-cause mortality in patients with thyroid storm.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted at a tertiary care center in Thailand. Patients diagnosed with thyroid storm were included. To identify prognostic factors of 30-day all-cause mortality, clinical and biochemical variables were analyzed using multivariable logistic regression analysis presented with odds ratio (OR). A prognostic risk score was developed and internally validated using bootstrapping. Statistical significance p-value was set at < 0.05.ResultsA total of 115 patients were included, with a 30-day mortality rate of 13%. Significant prognostic factors of 30-day mortality included male sex (OR 4.34), hypertension (OR 2.54), SOFA score ≥ 2 (OR 9.93), and GCS ≤ 10 (OR 1.95). Based on these four variables, a weighted scoring system (total 8 points) was developed. The model demonstrated good discriminatory performance with an apparent AUC of 0.81, which remained robust at 0.79 (95% CI 0.69–0.91) after internal validation via bootstrapping.ConclusionThe prognostic factors and proposed clinical prognostic score, incorporating simple and readily available parameters, provides an effective tool for early risk stratification in thyroid storm. This score can facilitate prioritized intensive care admission. Further external validation is warranted to confirm its generalizability across diverse clinical settings.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12902-026-02348-1.Keywords: Thyroid storm, 30-day mortality, Predictive score, Predictors

Introduction

Thyroid storm is a rare but life-threatening complication of thyrotoxicosis [1]. The diagnosis of thyroid storm primarily relies on a combination of clinical manifestations and biochemical findings. Currently, two major diagnostic frameworks are widely utilized: the Japan Thyroid Association (JTA) criteria and the Burch-Wartofsky Point Scale (BWPS) [2, 3]. Despite advancements in therapeutic interventions, the mortality rate associated with thyroid storm remains high, estimated between 10% and 50% [4] .

Early intervention is critical for improving prognosis in thyroid storm [5]. Consequently, current guidelines advocate for prompt, aggressive management based on clinical suspicion rather than awaiting definitive diagnostic criteria [2, 6].

Identifying prognostic factors for thyroid storm mortality can assist physicians in determining the optimal timing for aggressive intervention and the early allocation of resources, such as intensive care unit (ICU) admission. Previous studies from Japan, Taiwan, and Germany have identified several key prognostic factors, including advanced age, multiple comorbidities, higher Japan Coma Scale (JCS) scores at admission, the type of antithyroid drug used, and the requirement for supportive therapies such as beta-blockers, mechanical ventilation, or hemodiafiltration [7–9].

Although several prognostic factors for mortality in thyroid storm have been identified, their generalizability across different populations remains unclear. Most existing studies were conducted in high-resource settings, where certain treatments may not be universally available in regions with limited medical infrastructure. Furthermore, many identified prognostic factors for mortality have not yet been integrated into a scoring system as existing scoring systems primarily focus on diagnosis rather than predicting outcomes. Therefore, this study aimed to identify prognostic factors and develop a prognostic risk score for 30-day all-cause mortality in thyroid storm patients within a resource-constrained setting.

Materials and methods

Study design and eligibility

A 10-year, retrospective observational study was conducted including adult patients diagnosed with thyroid storm and admitted to a tertiary care center between March 1, 2014, and March 1, 2024. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Faculty of Medicine, Chiang Mai University (Ethical Number: MED-2567-0233; approved on July 8, 2024). Due to the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the committee.

Electronic medical records were initially screened for ICD-10 codes E05.5 (thyroid crisis or storm) and E05.9/E05.90/E05.91 (thyrotoxicosis, unspecified with or without thyrotoxic crisis/storm). Inclusion criteria consisted of adult patients (aged ≥ 18 years) with a clinical suspicion of thyroid storm who met either a BWPS score of ≥ 25 or the JTA criteria for definite diagnosis at admission. This combined criterion was chosen to maximize diagnostic sensitivity, ensuring the inclusion of patients with both impending and definite thyroid storms, as early recognition is critical in clinical practice. Patients with active malignancy, incomplete medical records, specifically those missing key predictors or outcomes such as thyroid function tests at admission, or 30-day mortality status were excluded from the analysis.

Clinical and laboratory data were extracted from electronic medical records, covering both the pre-admission period and the duration of inpatient care. Baseline demographic information and clinical characteristics were collected, including current antithyroid medication use, duration of treatment discontinuation (in patients with known hyperthyroidism), and any history of prior thyroid storm or thyrotoxicosis requiring hospitalization. Clinical status at admission was documented, comprising comorbidities, vital signs, admission location, and length of hospital stay. Severity and diagnostic scores were also recorded, specifically the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) [10], Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) [11], BWPS, and JTA categories. Data on laboratory investigations at admission, including basic biochemistry, thyroid and liver function tests, and serum random cortisol levels, were collected.

Cardiovascular disease was defined as a history of ischemic heart disease, heart failure, or structural heart disease documented in the medical record. Chronic kidney disease (CKD) was defined as a documented diagnosis of CKD or an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 60 mL/min/1.73 m² for at least 3 months prior to admission, when available. Active malignancy was defined as any solid or hematologic malignancy that was either newly diagnosed, under active treatment, or with evidence of active disease at the time of admission.

The primary outcome was 30-day all-cause mortality, defined as death from any cause within 30 days of admission. For patients discharged alive or those who returned home for palliative care, survival status at 30 days was verified through telephone follow-up with the patients’ families or by reviewing hospital electronic medical records.

At our institution, the treatment protocol for thyroid storm aligned with international management guidelines, which comprised the administration of antithyroid drugs, beta-blockers, and corticosteroids, alongside tailored supportive care. Management also prioritized the concurrent treatment of active comorbidities and acute precipitating illnesses during the admission [2]. Regarding criteria for ICU admission, it was determined based on clinical severity—including hemodynamic instability, respiratory failure, altered consciousness, or the immediate requirement for organ support (e.g., mechanical ventilation or vasopressor therapy)—subject to clinical evaluation and approval by a critical care physician.

Assay methods

Serum cortisol levels were measured by an electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (ECLIA) using an Elecsys model 1010 (Roche Diagnostic, Laval, Quebec). The intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation for serum cortisol were < 10%. Serum FT4 (normal reference range, 0.93–1.71 ng/dL), serum FT3 (normal reference range, 2.04–4.40 pg/mL) and TSH (normal reference range, 0.27–4.2 µIU/mL) were measured by ECLIA (Elecsys assay, Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany) with intra- and inter-assay variation of 1.9% to 8.2%.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using STATA software version 15.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). Categorical variables are reported as counts and percentages, while continuous variables are presented as means ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) for non-normally distributed data. For univariable analysis, the independent t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used for continuous variables, and Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

To identify independent prognostic factors of 30-day mortality, a multivariable logistic regression model was employed using the clustered sandwich estimator. This approach accounted for potential intra-cluster correlations in clinical management and patient severity within each ICU admission. Results are reported as odds ratio (OR) with 95% confidence interval (CI). To minimize the risk of model overfitting—especially given the rarity of thyroid storm—the number of predictors included in the model was restricted based on the final sample size. Candidate variables were selected presumptively based on clinical relevance, pathophysiological rationale, and prior literature, rather than relying solely on statistical significance in univariable analysis.

Item scores were derived by transforming the beta-coefficients from the multivariable logistic regression model. The coefficient for each factor was divided by the smallest coefficient in the model and rounded to the nearest 0.5. These item scores were then summed to calculate a total risk score. The study population was subsequently categorized into two risk levels: low-risk and high-risk for 30-day all-cause mortality. The optimal cut-off point was determined based on the levels yielding the lowest positive likelihood ratio (LR+) for the low-risk group and the highest LR + for the high-risk group, respectively. The predictive ability of the scoring system was presented by an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AuROC). Internal validation was performed using a bootstrapping technique with 1,000 resamples. Within each bootstrap replicate, the multivariable model was re-fitted, accounting for ICU admission clustering. The final performance was reported as an optimism-corrected concordance index (C-index) with its 95% confidence interval.

The sample size was determined based on prognostic factors for mortality identified in a prior Japanese study [7]. Among the statistically significant predictors of in-hospital mortality, the non-use of antithyroid drugs—which yielded the largest required sample size—was selected for the calculation. Using an odds ratio (OR) of 2.81 from a multivariable logistic regression model, with a significance level of 0.05 and 80% power, the initial estimate was adjusted using the variance inflation factor (VIF) to account for the multivariable nature of the reference model. The final calculated sample size required for this study was 110 cases.

Results

Baseline demographic and biochemical data

A study flow is shown in Fig. 1. A total of 115 patients with thyroid storm were enrolled in the study. During the 30-day follow-up period, the 30-day mortality rate was 13% (n = 15). Of these, 10 deaths were classified as in-hospital mortality, with the primary causes being septic shock (n = 6) and multi-organ failure (n = 4). The remaining 5 deaths occurred post-discharge within the 30-day follow-up period. The mean age of the cohort was 48.3 ± 20.3 years, with no significant difference observed between survivors and non-survivors. Although most of the total population was female (59.2%), a higher proportion of males was observed among those who died compared to survivors (61.1% vs. 37.3%; p = 0.058).

Study flow

Regarding the etiology of thyroid storm, a definitive diagnosis of Graves’ disease could not be confirmed for all 115 patients because specific diagnostic tests, including TSH-receptor antibody (TRAb) levels, thyroid ultrasonography, and thyroid scintigraphy, were not uniformly available. Regarding comorbidities, hypertension (22.5%) and diabetes mellitus (15.0%) were the most prevalent conditions in the study population. Active malignancy was significantly more common in the mortality group compared to survivors (16.7% vs. 2.0%; p = 0.024), whereas other comorbidities showed no significant differences between groups. The use of antithyroid medications at presentation, including methimazole (MMI) and propylthiouracil (PTU), did not differ significantly between the two groups. Similarly, the duration of treatment discontinuation and history of previous thyroid storm episodes were comparable.

Patients who died were more frequently admitted to the ICU compared to survivors (88.9% vs. 55.9%; p = 0.008). Markers of disease severity were generally higher among non-survivors. Although the mean BWPS score was higher in the mortality group (60.6 ± 17.6 vs. 53.9 ± 14.9), the difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.092). In contrast, the mean GCS score was significantly lower (12.5 ± 3.9 vs. 14.3 ± 2.2; p = 0.002) and the median SOFA score was significantly higher (5 (IQR 3–7) vs. 2 (IQR 1–4); p < 0.001) in the mortality group. Regarding biochemical parameters, baseline levels of FT4, FT3, and TSH did not differ significantly between the two groups. Similarly, no significant differences were observed in serum sodium, potassium, bicarbonate, or random serum cortisol levels. Prognostic factors of 30-day all-cause mortality in thyroid storm

A multivariable logistic regression analysis, incorporating four pre-specified prognostic factors and clustered by ICU admission, was constructed to identify independent factors associated with 30-day all-cause mortality. The four significant prognostic factors were: male sex (OR 4.34; p = 0.006), hypertension (OR 2.54; p = 0.021), SOFA score ≥ 2 points (OR 9.93; p = 0.007), GCS ≤ 10 points (OR 1.95; p = 0.005). Prognostic risk score for 30-day all-cause mortality in thyroid storm

Four independent predictors were identified and assigned point values based on their respective beta-coefficients from the multivariable logistic regression analysis. The assigned scores for each factor were as follows: male sex (2 points), hypertension (1.5 points), SOFA ≥ 2 points (3.5 points) and GCS ≤ 10 (1 point). The total risk scores ranged from 0 to 8. The total scores were subsequently classified into two risk categories. A low-risk group was defined by a score of 0–4, while a high-risk group was defined by a score of 4.5-8. Patients in the high-risk group demonstrated a significantly higher proportion of 30-day all-cause mortality compared to those in the low-risk group (25% vs. 4.5%). Using a cut-off score of ≥ 4.5 to define the high-risk group, 12 out of 15 non-survivors were correctly identified, yielding a sensitivity of 80.0% with LR + of 2.22 (95% CI, 1.54–3.22). Concurrently, 64 out of 100 survivors were correctly classified into the low-risk group, providing a specificity of 64.0% with LR + of 0.31 (95% CI, 0.11–0.87). Discussion

This retrospective cohort study identified several clinical and biochemical variables associated with 30-day all-cause mortality in patients with thyroid storm. The final prognostic scoring system, which incorporates these factors, demonstrated high performance with an AuROC of 0.81. The overall 30-day mortality rate in this study was 13%, which is comparable to the 10.7% reported in the Japanese nationwide survey [12]. The primary outcome using 30-day all-cause mortality in the present study aligns with the 30-day thyroid storm-associated mortality definition utilized in a previous German study [8]. This consistency supports the clinical relevance of using a 30-day outcome window, which provides a more comprehensive assessment of mortality than relying solely on in-hospital death.

Several independent prognostic factors identified in this study including male sex, hypertension, higher SOFA score, and lower GCS are consistent with previous reports [9, 12]. These findings highlight that mortality in thyroid storm is driven more strongly by the severity of systemic decompensation and multi-organ dysfunction than by the degree of thyroid hormone excess alone. Similarly, more recent observational studies have continued to identify clinical severity and organ dysfunction as central determinants of outcome in thyroid storm, further supporting the clinical relevance of the predictors identified in our study [1]. Male sex was also associated with higher mortality in the present cohort. While thyroid storm is more prevalent in females, sex-based disparities in outcomes have been observed in prior epidemiologic studies, including a Taiwanese nationwide cohort [9]. The underlying mechanism remains unclear; however, it may relate to differences in comorbidity profiles, delayed clinical presentation, or greater disease severity at admission. Due to the relatively small number of events in this subgroup, these findings should be interpreted with caution.

A SOFA score ≥ 2 was independently associated with increased risk of 30-day mortality. This finding aligns with the Sepsis-3 consensus, which identifies a SOFA score ≥ 2 as a marker of clinically significant organ dysfunction and increased mortality [13]. Although thyroid storm is distinct from sepsis, both conditions share a common pathway of severe systemic decompensation, characterized by hemodynamic instability and intense metabolic stress. Consequently, these results support the clinical utility of the SOFA score as an adjunctive prognostic tool in the management of thyroid storm.

Neurologic impairment, as reflected by a GCS ≤ 10, was significantly associated with higher mortality. This finding is consistent with Japanese nationwide data, which identified central nervous system dysfunction as a critical predictor of poor outcomes in thyroid storm [12]. In terms of pathogenesis, altered consciousness may result from severe metabolic derangement or cerebral hypoperfusion, both of which are biologically linked to a worse prognosis. Among baseline comorbidities, chronic hypertension represent pre-existing vulnerabilities, predisposing patients to adverse outcomes during critical illnesses, such as severe thyrotoxicosis [14, 15].

The prognostic risk score developed in this study utilizes simple, practical parameters readily available in routine clinical practice, demonstrating good predictive accuracy. This performance was further confirmed by internal validation using bootstrapping, which yielded consistent results. This prognostic scoring system provides a valuable tool for clinical decision-making, particularly in resource-limited settings with restricted intensive care capacity. By effectively stratifying patients based on mortality risk, the score assists clinicians in identifying those who require immediate triage to the ICU versus those who may be safely monitored in a high-dependency or general ward setting. Furthermore, it serves as an objective communication tool, facilitating standardized discussions between emergency physicians, endocrinologists, and intensivists to ensure a standardized approach to patient management.

This study has several strengths. To our knowledge, it is among the first to develop a clinical prognostic score specifically focused on mortality outcomes in thyroid storm. Unlike previous studies limited to in-hospital mortality, we utilized 30-day all-cause mortality to better capture clinically significant short-term outcomes following hospital discharge. Additionally, by incorporating both thyroid-specific severity measures and general organ dysfunction markers, we provided a more comprehensive assessment of mortality-associated factors. Notably, all prognostic variables included in our score are readily obtainable in routine clinical practice.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective, single-center design may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of our findings. Moreover, certain established prognostic factors identified in international cohorts and recent guidelines (such as left ventricular ejection fraction, central nervous system symptoms and degree of fever) could not be incorporated into our scoring system due to inconsistent documentation in the medical records. Similarly, although systemic corticosteroids were utilized, the retrospective nature of our data precluded a detailed, time-dependent analysis of corticosteroid dosing adequacy and its direct impact on patient outcomes. Second, the relatively small number of mortality events restricted model stability and raised the possibility of overfitting, warranting a cautious interpretation of the predictive results. Third, several continuous variables were dichotomized using clinically pragmatic thresholds; while this enhances clinical interpretability, it may reduce statistical power and obscure more complex dose-response relationships. Fourth, treatment-related confounding, such as variations in ICU-based interventions, cannot be entirely excluded. Fifth, due to the retrospective nature of the study, standardized thyroid volume measurements were not consistently available and therefore could not be analyzed. Sixth, the exact underlying etiology of thyrotoxicosis (e.g., Graves’ disease versus other causes) could not be definitively determined for all patients due to the retrospective nature of the data and the unavailability of TRAb testing, thyroid ultrasound, and thyroid scans in several cases. Finally, residual confounding from unmeasured triggers or concomitant illness severity may have influenced the observed associations, highlighting the need for further external validation in larger, multi-center prospective studies.

In conclusion, the present study identifies several clinical and biochemical predictors that, when integrated into a practical scoring system, effectively stratify patients at high risk of 30-day all-cause mortality in thyroid storm at an early stage. These findings provide the basis for future large-scale, prospective studies aimed at validating and refining a practical prognostic model for thyroid storm across diverse clinical settings.
