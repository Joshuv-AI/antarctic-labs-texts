Breaking the denoising dilemma in NMR spectroscopy by blind source separation
Authors: Haolin Zhan, Yueyao Li, Yongqi Yuan, Yuqing Huang, Zhong Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy serves as a fundamental analytical technique finding valuable applications across chemistry, biology, and materials science, yet its widespread utility is frequently constrained by its inherently low sensitivity and concomitant noise, making noise reduction a critical processing step. Existing denoising methods typically impose a difficult trade-off, namely, traditional iterative algorithms are interpretable and widely adoptable but time-consuming, while deep learning approaches achieve fast denoising and better performance at the cost of generalizability and interpretability. To break this dilemma, we propose a denoising protocol, namely CCA-NMR, to unlock clean signals in noisy NMR spectra. This protocol exploits the idea of blind source separation to separate noise and real signals based on autocorrelation coefficients, delivering good noise reduction, fast computational time, and decent universality. Owing to these advantages, it enables reliable sensitivity benefits and recovers genuine peaks obscured by noise, thus reducing the necessary experimental acquisition times and facilitating subsequent quantitative analysis. Its performance is evidenced through experimental validation across a diverse range of NMR platforms, including relaxation and diffusion Laplace NMR, multidimensional protein NMR, and time-resolved NMR during real-time electrocatalytic reactions. As a consequence, these findings substantially expand the practical applicability of NMR spectroscopy, and indicate its considerable potential for broad chemical and biomedical applications.

Introduction

Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy, including Fourier NMR1–3 and Laplace NMR,4–6 capitalizes on its robust non-invasive ability to identify distinct chemical environments, thus enabling precise information extraction at the molecular level. This encompasses the sample's composition, concentration, structure, and dynamic properties, making it an increasingly influential tool across a wide range of scientific disciplines. However, these advantages are regretfully counterbalanced by a significant limitation of inherently low detection sensitivity, as a consequence of the small Boltzmann polarization of nuclear spins. Thus, compared to other analytical techniques like mass spectrometry or infrared spectroscopy, NMR requires greater signal averaging or sample concentration. This sensitivity constraint manifests as substantial noise in the resulting spectra. Such noise not only distorts signal amplitudes but can also adversely affect other critical signal characteristics, including frequency and phase. Consequently, the detection of weak peaks and low-concentration molecules is hampered, and the accuracy of spectral interpretation is compromised, posing a challenge to the broader applications of NMR spectroscopy.

In standard one-dimensional (1D) NMR, the sensitivity issue can often be mitigated by direct signal averaging through repetitive transient scans. However, this approach becomes increasingly inadequate for experiments inherently requiring extended acquisition times. A prominent example is multi-dimensional NMR in protein analysis in solution under physiological conditions,7 in which low-natural-abundance nuclei are frequently employed as probes and indirect-dimension sampling causes practical issues of increased sampling burden, quadrature/hypercomplex acquisition penalties, relaxation and transfer losses, and total acquisition-time requirements. Moreover, Laplace NMR necessitating the acquisition of a full relaxation/diffusion-edited NMR array8–10 also compounds this challenge. Here, the quantification of spectral peaks is initially difficult, and noise in each individual spectrum propagating into the final measurement errors exacerbates this problem, critically undermining the measurement accuracy of relaxation (T1/T2) or diffusion distributions. Another critical scenario is real-time in situ reaction monitoring.11 Practical time constraints limit the extent of feasible signal averaging, while simultaneously, the gradual generation of products and their limited cumulative concentrations hinder the signal acquisition and quantification. In summary, NMR spectra acquired under such conditions are noisier, and signal averaging is further limited by time constraints.

Extracting meaningful structural, kinetic or mechanistic information from such noisy data has therefore become a non-trivial task in NMR analysis. Given these constraints, the development of effective methods for NMR noise reduction becomes essential. The goal of such denoising is to unlock clean signals from noisy spectra, approximating the underlying true noiseless signals as accurately as possible. This process entails not only suppressing the noise components but also faithfully preserving all true spectral information inherent to the original signal, also referred to as “noise filtering”. Building upon this goal, a fundamental approach lies in hardware advancement, which includes optimizing probe and coil design,12 increasing the field strength of the main magnet,13,14 and implementing cryogenic detection systems.15 However, such hardware-based enhancements entail substantial financial investment. Moreover, hyperpolarization advancements16–20 have been introduced to deliver significant sensitivity gains. While these techniques can mitigate noise-related limitations to some extent, they also impose constraints, including the need for specialized hardware, complex experimental setups, and highly skilled operators. Alternatively, a complementary strategy involves the development of post-acquisition denoising algorithms. Various computational methods have been developed to reduce noise, such as NASR21 using resampling, CoSeM22 based on compressed sensing, and CHORD23 and CHORD-V24 utilizing a low-rank Hankel model, among others. Although generally applicable across multiple scenarios, these algorithms often yield limited performance gains and suffer from time-consuming iterative computations, limiting their practicality especially when processing the large spectral arrays common in the aforementioned challenging scenarios. In parallel, deep learning approaches,25 such as DN-Unet26 and LD-Net,27 have demonstrated considerable success in noise suppression and signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) enhancement for both 1D heteronuclear and 2D protein NMR data. While deep learning models benefit from fast inference and superior performance, they are constrained by insufficient generalization when applied to data outside their training distribution, as well as limited adaptability across diverse NMR experimental setups. Consequently, there remains a pressing need for a denoising method that not only delivers superior performance and computational efficiency, but also exhibits strong generalization capability, enabling reliable applications across a wide range of NMR experiments.

Herein, we introduce a denoising approach for NMR spectroscopy to meet the above demands, based on canonical correlation analysis,28,29 referred to as CCA-NMR. The CCA-NMR framework treats noise reduction in NMR arrays as a blind source separation problem, distinguishing genuine signals from noise based on differential autocorrelation coefficients. It enables simultaneous denoising of arrayed NMR experiments within a single computation by identifying underlying components that exhibit maximal autocorrelation within the spectral channels. As a result, this approach allows one to filter out noise while preserving the desired spectral content, thus shortening the experimental time, which is valuable for Laplace NMR and multidimensional NMR experiments that initially demand prolonged acquisition. Benefitting from the robust suppression of unwanted experimental noise, CCA-NMR enables the recovery of minute signals from low-concentration components and benefits subsequent quantitative analysis. Its performance has been adequately validated across multiple experimental platforms such as relaxation and diffusion Laplace NMR, multidimensional protein NMR, and time-resolved NMR spectroscopy.

Results and discussion

Noise reduction in Laplace NMR analysis

In a natural sample system of British stout, the original diffusion spectra (Fig. 1A), acquired using an internally encoded selective TOCSY (iDESTO) sequence,30 exhibit substantial noise, particularly in the later spectral channels, which nearly obscures useful information and impedes the extraction of meaningful signals. As shown in Fig. 1B, CCA-NMR achieves simultaneous noise reduction across all spectral channels without obviously compromising spectral quality or losing critical information. Notably, CCA-NMR successfully recovers weak signals that are otherwise buried in noise, facilitating unambiguous spectral interpretation, peak intensity quantification, and subsequent diffusion analysis. Beyond improving regions where signals are already visible above the noise floor, CCA-NMR leverages information from high-SNR channels to identify and reconstruct genuine peaks that are significantly below or even completely submerged in noise, as exemplified by the 32nd spectrum in Fig. 1.

Schematic diagram of CCA-NMR and experimental results of diffusion Laplace NMR analysis of a British stout sample. (A) Original diffusion spectral array acquired. (B) Denoised diffusion spectral array obtained through CCA-NMR. The 1st, 7th, 12th, 17th, 22nd, 27th and 32nd spectra are displayed for a total of 32 spectral channels, and their noise reduction factors are 1.61, 2.59, 2.69, 2.33, 3.07, 3.54, and 3.11, respectively.

Another CCA-NMR denoising example is further evidenced on a diffusion NMR dataset of a mixture containing pyridine, 2,3-dihydrofuran, and n-propanol. In this case (Fig. S1A), Gaussian white noise with standard deviations of 0.02 is added to the normalized high-SNR dataset to obtain a noisy spectral array. Similarly, CCA-NMR provides well-denoised spectra and enables the recovery of peaks originally obscured by noise across all spectral channels. Expanded spectral regions (Fig. S1B) clearly show the details of noise suppression and the restoration of weak signals. In addition, the high-SNR experiment data and residuals between the denoised spectra and high-SNR references were provided, and two quantitative measures, root mean square error (RMSE) and Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC), were calculated, for direct comparison of peak height, position, and linewidth against ground truth. The quantitative and visual results evidence that the noise filter preserves high spectral fidelity.

Another example of relaxation Laplace NMR denoising is demonstrated on a chemical solution of ethyl 3-bromopropanoate and acetone.31 In the noisy T2 spectral array (Fig. 2A), signals in heavily noise-affected channels, such as the 9th spectrum, are nearly obscured. Through effective noise suppression, the CCA-NMR protocol produces a clean spectral array (Fig. 2B), enabling the recovery, identification, and quantification of all meaningful signals. In this case, all peaks originally buried in severe noise are clearly recovered in the denoised array. Moreover, the advantage of CCA-NMR is evident not only in highly noisy channels but also in originally cleaner ones. For instance, in the first spectrum, CCA-NMR also reveals the finer spectral details such as the coupling splitting structure of peaks i and ii (see expanded views). For these data, components with autocorrelation coefficients below the mean value were removed across all 10 relaxation-encoded channels. Without requiring prior information or parameter tuning, an average SNR gain of 3.03 ± 2.12 was achieved for all spectral channels. Benefiting from this noise suppression, CCA-NMR also substantially improves the accuracy of measured T2 relaxation times. Specifically, compared to the original noisy data, CCA-NMR reduces the average error from (0.52 ± 0.36) to (0.30 ± 0.05) s.

The consistent performance in noise reduction, peak recovery, and subsequent quantitative analysis across three Laplace NMR datasets demonstrates the robustness and universality of the CCA-NMR method to diverse Laplace NMR experiments. Consequently, CCA-NMR establishes itself as a general tool for fast, sensitive, and reliable Laplace NMR analysis, particularly for samples containing low-concentration species or spectra with wide dynamic ranges.

Multi-dimensional protein NMR denoising

Beyond the Laplace NMR applications described above, the proposed CCA-NMR protocol also demonstrates robust performance in Fourier-transform NMR, particularly in the challenging context of multidimensional protein NMR. Such experiments inherently require extended sampling along indirect evolution dimensions, resulting in exponentially increased acquisition times and limiting the feasibility of signal averaging. This is illustrated using the 2D 1H–15N HSQC data of GB1 protein.32,33

In this example (Fig. 3), the resulting 2D spectrum is encoded across multiple 15N channels, making it suitable for filtering out low-autocorrelation components and achieve desired noise suppression. As shown in Fig. 3A, nearly all peaks in the noisy 2D spectrum are obscured by severe noise, impeding the extraction of atomic-level information associated with protein structure and spatial conformation. Applying CCA-NMR yields a clean 2D spectrum (Fig. 3B) in which interfering noise and artifacts are substantially suppressed while all genuine signals are preserved. In the context of increasing signal channels and enlarged data sizes (N = 8192 and K = 512), the entire computation takes only hundreds of microseconds on a standard personal computer, demonstrating its computationally efficient advantage. Provided that an autocorrelation threshold is set to a value approaching the mean of autocorrelation coefficients (Fig. S2), quantitative statistics reveal an average noise reduction factor of 11.60 ± 4.78 across all 15N traces, and the maximum noise reduction reaches 37.56-fold. This result further supports the phenomenon that noise suppression becomes more pronounced as the number of signal channels (K) increases. The corresponding time savings exceed three orders of magnitude, and this advantage would be even more significant in 3D or higher-dimensional protein NMR experiments. Moreover, comparison with a high-SNR reference spectrum (Fig. 3C) confirms that CCA-NMR faithfully recovers spectral content while effectively filtering noise. The four representative traces highlighted in Fig. 3D visually illustrate the benefits of CCA-NMR denoising. Specifically, even weak signals approaching the noise level, such as the minute peak indicated by the arrow in trace 4, remain clearly identified and recovered following denoising. Consequently, these results establish CCA-NMR as a powerful and efficient tool for rapid, faithful noise reduction in multidimensional NMR experiments.

Applications in time-resolved NMR experiments

To further demonstrate its broad applicability, the CCA-NMR denoising protocol was applied to time-resolved NMR spectroscopy for real-time monitoring of electrochemical reactions. The in situ electrochemical NMR (EC-NMR) setup used here follows a previously reported design34–36 (Fig. 4A). Owing to inherent NMR sensitivity limitations and experimental noise, reaction products typically must accumulate to detectable levels, which hinders the timely response to transient intermediates or trace products. Additionally, apart from spatiotemporal magnetic-field variations, standard 1D NMR (Fig. 4B) suffers from peak overlaps, specifically between peak 1 from the 1-butanol reactant and peak a from the butyric acid product, complicating the identification and quantification of the product peaks. A previously introduced spatially selective pure shift NMR variant37 (Fig. 4B) resolves this overlap by suppressing both field-inhomogeneity effects and homonuclear J-couplings, but at the cost of reduced sensitivity and extended acquisition time. Consequently, the resulting time-dependent spectra may exhibit pronounced noise. For example, Fig. 4C illustrates a noisy spectral array with insufficient sensitivity to monitor reaction progress. In this case, the denoised time-resolved spectral array obtained through CCA-NMR (Fig. 4D) maintains both high resolution and enhanced SNR, facilitating clear spectral interpretation and subsequent intensity quantification. This example confirms the feasibility and compatibility of CCA-NMR for time-resolved NMR spectroscopy and highlights its potential for real-time monitoring of dynamic chemical and electrochemical processes.

In situ observation of 1-butanol electrooxidation on Pt/C at 1.2 V (vs. SCE). (A) The schematic EC-NMR setup and the highlighted oxidation pathway. (B) Standard 1D NMR and 1D spatially selective pure shift NMR with heavy noise at the reaction moment of 275 min. (C) Noisy time-resolved pure shift NMR spectra at 18 reaction moments. (D) Denoised time-resolved spectral array obtained through CCA-NMR.

Discussion

Unlike conventional denoising algorithms that rely on iterative computations and predefined regularity constraints to process individual spectra, the CCA-NMR protocol leverages the inherent autocorrelation difference between genuine signals and noise across multiple spectral channels, enabling efficient noise reduction of an entire spectral array within a single computation. It is specifically designed for arrayed NMR experiments, such as those involving repetitive acquisitions or inherently long measurement times, where extensive signal averaging via repeated transients is impractical. This objective aligns with that of recent approaches like PE-NMR38 and SE-PSNMR39 employing principal component analysis (PCA). Nevertheless, there exists a conceptual distinction between CCA-NMR and PE-NMR. Specifically, PE-NMR seeks and retains components that maximize variance across the spectral array to implement noise reduction. This variance-maximization criterion does not explicitly prioritize the preservation of weak peaks. In contrast, CCA-NMR, based on canonical correlation analysis, seeks components that maximize autocorrelation to perform the blind source separation of informative components and unwanted noise. Beyond improving regions where signals are already visible above the noise floor, CCA-NMR facilitates the recovery of genuine peaks that are significantly below or even completely submerged in the noise, by sharing information from noiseless channels.

Detailed comparative evaluation across three datasets were conducted to highlight the advantage of CCA-NMR. For the British stout dataset (Fig. S3), where no noise-free reference spectrum is available, we adopted the downstream measurement error of diffusion coefficients as the quantitative criterion. The quantitative results and visual comparisons have been given in Table 1. Mainstream deep-learning methods, trained on data without spin–spin coupling splitting, are unsuitable for denoising crowded spectral regions and were therefore not included in this comparison. In this case, CCA-NMR achieves superior denoising performance, delivering an SNR gain of 4.73 ± 2.86, compared to the iterative CoSeM algorithm (3.20 ± 0.09) and the recent PE-NMR method (4.35 ± 2.11). Effective noise suppression further delivers clean spectra with well-defined peaks, minimizing interference from experimental noise. Consequently, compared to the original noisy data, the measurement error of diffusion coefficients (b values) is reduced from (0.27 ± 0.18) to (0.11 ± 0.07) × 10−10 m2 s−1, while it is (0.15 ± 0.15) and (0.13 ± 0.12) × 10−10 m2 s−1 for PE-NMR and CoSeM, respectively. In addition, regarding the computational efficiency, CoSeM spends over 3.3 hours to process a total of 32 NMR spectra on a standard computer, while CCA-NMR accomplishes simultaneous denoising of the full spectral array within merely 0.15 s. It is worth noting, however, that PE-NMR offers the highest computational efficiency, being approximately twice as fast as CCA-NMR.

For the relaxation Laplace NMR dataset of a mixture of ethyl 3-bromopropanoate and acetone, the comparison results against CoSeM, PE-NMR, and two deep learning methods, i.e., DN-UNet and LD-Net, are shown in Fig. S4. In denoised 8th spectra (Fig. S4A), the proposed CCA-NMR achieves decent noise reduction, and recovers peaks submerged in noise, although it may somewhat overestimate the peak intensities of weak peaks, resulting in different relative intensities of the strong peak around 3.4 ppm and the weaker peaks. Nevertheless, compared to CoSeM and PE-NMR, CCA-NMR still yields superior quantitative measures, specifically, a higher SNR gain (3.50 vs. 2.32 and 2.35), a lower RMSE (3.40 × 10−3vs. 8.05 × 10−3 and 6.57 × 10−3), and a higher PCC (0.789 vs. 0.634 and 0.627). Although the two deep-learning methods DN-UNet and LD-Net provide markedly higher quantitative metrics than the traditional approaches, their visual inspection reveals that only the strongest peaks are recovered in the displayed 8th spectra and peak splitting is lost for all other weaker peaks, as indicated by the black dashed circles. A more serious issue is observed in the 10th spectrum (Fig. S4B), where nearly all meaningful signals were initially obscured by noise, and CCA-NMR successfully removed the noise while preserving the true signals, whereas CoSeM failed despite longer computation times. Similarly, all weak peaks are entirely suppressed in denoised spectra for DN-UNet and LD-Net. This suggests that existing deep-learning algorithms, which rely heavily on feature learning from training data, may not be suitable for recovering spectral peaks that are almost completely submerged in noise. For another diffusion Laplace NMR dataset of a mixture of pyridine, 2,3-dihydrofuran, and n-propanol, the comparison results are provided in Fig. S5. For the 19th spectrum (Fig. S5A), only CCA-NMR and PE-NMR recover all spectral peaks, whereas CoSeM, DN-UNet, and LD-Net yield clean baselines at the cost of losing peak-splitting information and weak peaks. For the 20th spectrum (Fig. S5B), CCA-NMR demonstrates an even more pronounced advantage, as it is the only method that successfully recovers all peaks with decent fidelity. A quantitative comparison of these two datasets is provided in Table S1.

It should be noted that while CCA-NMR can recover genuine peaks completely obscured in noisy channels by leveraging information from cleaner ones, its ultimate capacity remains bounded by the intrinsic detection limits of the instrument. Furthermore, the method relies on the selection of an autocorrelation threshold to separate meaningful components from unwanted noise. As this threshold is enlarged, fewer components are retained and noise suppression increases; however, setting the threshold too high may eliminate subtle but informative variations across spectral channels. Therefore, appropriate threshold selection is critical for balancing noise removal with signal preservation. In the absence of prior knowledge, it is recommended to use a threshold slightly lower than the mean autocorrelation coefficient as a practical default, which consistently maintains an effective trade-off between denoising performance and spectral fidelity.

As seen in Fig. S6, threshold sensitivity analyses were further conducted across four major datasets. The quantitative evaluation of RMSD, PCC, and noise reduction factors indicates that the threshold selected by the default mean autocorrelation coefficient (marked in pink) yields satisfactory performance in most scenarios. In addition, we further performed a detailed case study on the GB1 protein NMR dataset using six candidate threshold values, i.e., 0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.4, and 0.6, and the results are shown in Fig. S7. At the default threshold (the mean autocorrelation coefficient is approximately 0.07), thresholds in the vicinity, specifically 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, and even 0.4, preserve features with a genuine autocorrelation structure while effectively suppressing random fluctuations, resulting in reasonably denoised spectra. When a much smaller threshold is applied, noise components are inadequately suppressed, leading to residual noise in the spectra (Fig. S7A). Conversely, a higher threshold produces stronger suppression by retaining only components with higher autocorrelation coefficients. Although this yields cleaner spectra, it also risks incorrectly suppressing desired modulation features; for instance, as indicated by the red arrows in Fig. S7F, certain peaks indicated by red arrows are unfortunately lost under an overly aggressive threshold. In addition, we also identify specific conditions under which the default threshold may fail. In particular, failure occurs when the number of retained components falls below the number of modulation components. In an extreme case where only a single component is retained, all modulation information would be lost, rendering the spectra across the array identical and thus unsuitable for downstream analysis.

The lag-1 autocorrelation assumption is indeed an implicit yet fundamental premise of our method, and its dependence on data acquisition and processing conditions also warrants thorough investigation. Thus, we have conducted a series of stability experiments and analyses on the diffusion Laplace NMR dataset of a mixture of pyridine, 2,3-dihydrofuran, and n-propanol, in terms of zerofilling dependence, apodization functions, and lag parameters. Specifically, regarding zerofilling factors ranging from 1× to 16×, the quantitative evaluation of RMSD, PCC, and noise reduction factors has been summarized in Table S2. Similarly, quantitative results with Gaussian window factors ranging from 0 to 8, and four lag parameter candidates, i.e., lag-1, lag-2, lag-5, and lag-8, have been summarized in Tables S3 and S4. Apart from the quantitative comparison, visual comparison for the 19th spectrum is also provided in Fig. S8. Both quantitative and visual results indicate that CCA can provide decent noise suppression and spectral fidelity under different zerofilling, apodization, spectral resolution, and lag parameter conditions. Through the above tests, the results evidence that CCA-NMR performance is stable with respect to zerofilling, apodization, spectral resolution, and lag parameters. In addition, the details of zerofilling factors, apodization functions, or spectral widths used for all major datasets are summarized in Table S9, and no datasets adopted additional zerofilling.

As highlighted by the examples above, the proposed CCA-NMR protocol exhibits broad compatibility across a variety of NMR experiments that involve multiple signal channels, including Laplace NMR, multidimensional NMR, time-resolved NMR, and other modulation-arrayed experiments (e.g., those varying temperature40 or phase41 or others). In addition, CCA-NMR also presents decent application potential for high-dimensional NMR datasets and reaction monitoring in benchtop NMR,42,43 where both processing speed and denoising efficacy are critical. Moreover, this method in principle would also be applicable to nuclei other than 1H, provided that the modulated spectra exist in the form of an array.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we have developed a denoising protocol for NMR spectroscopy by reformulating canonical correlation analysis to enable noise reduction and facilitate quantitative measurement. This protocol allows one to effectively filter out noise while preserving desired spectral information, thus substantially shortening experimental times. It simultaneously overcomes the drawbacks of both iterative denoising algorithms and deep learning models, delivering good noise reduction, fast computational time, and broad compatibility. Experimental results validate its robust performance across multiple experimental platforms, including relaxation and diffusion Laplace NMR, 2D protein NMR, and time-resolved NMR spectroscopy. This work thus represents a fundamental methodological advancement in NMR, and is poised to boost a wide range of applications in chemical and biomedical analysis.
